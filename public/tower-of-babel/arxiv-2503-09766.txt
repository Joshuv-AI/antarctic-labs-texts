Frog model on ℤ \mathbb{Z} with random survival parameter Thanks: Research supported by CAPES (88887.676435/2022-00), FAPESP (23/13453-5)
Gustavo O. de Carvalho, Fábio P. Machado

Abstract
. We study the frog model on ℤ \mathbb{Z} with geometric lifetimes, introducing a random survival parameter. Active and inactive particles are placed at the vertices of ℤ \mathbb{Z} . The lifetime of each active particle follows a geometric random variable with parameter 1 − p 1-p , where p p is randomly sampled from a distribution π \pi . Each active particle performs a simple random walk on ℤ \mathbb{Z} until it dies, activating any inactive particles it encounters along its path. In contrast to the usual case where p p is fixed, we show that there exist non-trivial distributions π \pi for which the model survives with positive probability. More specifically, for π ∼ B ​ e ​ t ​ a ​ ( α , β ) \pi\sim Beta(\alpha,\beta) , we establish the existence of a critical value β = 0.5 \beta=0.5 , that separates almost sure extinction from survival with positive probability. Furthermore, we show that the model is recurrent whenever it survives with positive probability.

1.
Introduction and results

We study the frog model, a branching random walk process often associated with the spread of a rumor or a disease in a population. Initially, a random number of particles are placed at each vertex of a connected graph
\mathcal{G}
, where one vertex is designated as the root. At time 0, particles at the root are active, while all others are inactive. Active particles perform a simple symmetric random walk on
\mathcal{G}
, activating any inactive particles they encounter along their path.

At every time step, each active particle may die with probability
1-p
before jumping,
in which case it is removed from the model. Consequently, the lifetime of each active particle follows a geometric distribution with parameter
1-p
. The case where particles never die can be incorporated by setting
p=1
.

There has been considerable interest in identifying conditions under which the probability of survival is positive, i.e., ensuring that at all times at least one active particle remains. In
[
1
]
, the authors proved that the process undergoes a phase transition in the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
and in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the
d
-homogeneous tree, for
d\geq 3
, whereas in
\mathbb{Z}
, they showed that, under mild conditions on the initial number of particles per vertex, there is no phase transition.

Following this initial work, several studies have further investigated
p_{c}
, the critical parameter, in graphs where a phase transition occurs
[
4
,
9
,
10
,
5
]
.
In
[
2
]
, the authors show that the frog model can survive in
\mathbb{Z}
with positive probability, provided that either the random walks have a drift or the survival parameter
p=p_{x}
varies appropriately with the initial position
x\in\mathbb{Z}
of each particle.

In this paper, we construct a particle-wise disorder setup to achieve survival with positive probability in
\mathbb{Z}
. This serves as an illustration of how introducing randomness into a parameter or the environment can significantly alter the properties of a model.

Other properties of the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
have also been studied. In
[
6
,
2
]
, the authors analyze the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
with drift and other variations, primarily focusing on recurrence versus transience, i.e., whether there are particles returning to the root infinitely often. In this paper, we further prove that, given survival, the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
is recurrent.

We now formally define the frog model. Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and define
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
Let
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
\{L_{x,i}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},i\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
\{\pi_{x,i}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},i\in\mathbb{N}}
be independent collections of i.i.d. random variables. Let
\{(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},i\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
S^{x,i}_{0}=x
, be a collection of independent simple symmetric random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
.

For each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\eta_{x}
denotes the initial number of particles at vertex
x
.
For each particle
i
(
i\in\{1,\dots,\eta_{x}\}
) starting at vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}
:

•

(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
denotes its random walk;

•

\pi_{x,i}
is its survival parameter;

•

L_{x,i}
is its lifetime, given by the conditional probability

P(L_{x,i}=k|\pi_{x,i}=p)=(1-p)p^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{(k\in\mathbb{N}_{0})}.

We denote by
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)
the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
, where
\pi
represents the survival parameter distribution and
\eta
determines the initial number distribution of particles per vertex.

Definition 1.1
.

A realization of the frog model is said to survive if at every time step there is at least one active particle. Otherwise, it dies out.

It is also useful to describe the survival of the frog model in terms of an oriented percolation model. Let

A(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:S^{x,i}_{n}=y\text{ for some }i\in\{1,...,\eta_{x}\}\text{ and }n\in\{0,...,L_{x,i}\}\}

be the set of vertices that can potentially be visited by a particle initially placed at
x
. The vertices in
A(x)
will be visited if and only if
x
is visited and its particles are activated.

Therefore, a realization of the frog model survives if and only if there exists an infinite sequence of distinct vertices
0=x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\dots
in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
x_{j}\in A(x_{j-1})
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
a\vee b
denote
\max\{a,b\}
. Under the mild condition
E[\log(1\vee\eta)]<\infty
,
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
shows that if
P(\pi=p)=1
for some
p\in(0,1)
, then

P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]=0.

(1.1)

In fact, the previous result assumes that
\pi
is almost surely constant. Introducing randomness in
\pi
may cause (
1.1
) to fail in some cases. We first present trivial survival results for certain distributions
\pi
.

If
P(\pi=1)>0
, it is straightforward to see that
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0
whenever
P(\eta=0)<1
, as each particle has a chance to live forever and perform an infinite random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, if there exists
a<1
such that
P(\pi\in[0,a])=1
, then
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]=0
whenever
E[\log(1\vee\eta)]<\infty
. This follows from coupling the processes in such a way that
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)
is dominated by
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},a,\eta)
and applying (
1.1
) with
p=a
.

Among distributions
\pi
that do not exhibit the aforementioned trivialities, we focus in particular on the Beta family.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that
\pi\sim Beta(\alpha,\beta)
, where
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
, if its density function is given by

f_{\pi}(x)=\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\mathbbm{1}_{(x\in(0,1))},

where
B(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx
is its normalizing constant.

This family encompasses a wide variety of distributions in
(0,1)
that are mathematically well-suited for our calculations. However, it is worth noting that survival results can be easily extended through couplings to stochastically larger (or smaller, in the case of almost sure extinction) non-Beta distributions.

We now state theorems describing the survival and extinction of the frog model as a function of

\alpha
and
\beta
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider that
\pi\sim Beta(\alpha,\beta)
and
E(\eta)<\infty
. If
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0.5
, then

P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]=0.

Theorem 1.4
.

Consider that
\pi\sim Beta(\alpha,\beta)
and
P(\eta=0)<1
. Then,

P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0

in any of the following cases:

•

\alpha>0
and
\beta<0.5

•

\alpha>\alpha_{0}
and
\beta=0.5
, where
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{0}(E(\eta))\geq 0
is a function defined for
E(\eta)\in(0,+\infty)\cup\{+\infty\}
given by

\alpha_{0}(E(\eta)):=\inf\{\alpha>0:B(\alpha,0.5)<E(\eta)\sqrt{2}\}.

1
summarizes the results of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
in a phase diagram for cases where
\eta
satisfies
P(\eta=0)<1
and
E(\eta)<\infty
. Note that in Theorem
1.4
,
\alpha_{0}=0
when
E(\eta)=\infty
, which makes the diagram complete in this case.

. The shaded part represents the region where

We now give special attention to distributions for which
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0
, which are not necessarily from the Beta family. In such cases, we can also analyze the recurrence of the model.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta
)) be the number of times vertex 0 is visited in
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)
. We say that
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta
) is recurrent if

P[V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta))=\infty]>0.

It is straightforward that

P[V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta))=\infty]\leq P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]

and that the model is non-recurrent whenever

P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]=0
.

A natural question is whether
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)
is recurrent whenever
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0
. The next theorem provides a general answer to this question, highlighting the strong relationship between survival and recurrence.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
E(\eta)<\infty
and
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0
, then

P[V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta))=\infty|\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]=1.

Remark 1.7
.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
relies on couplings and previous results on closely related rumor processes.
Indeed, as another application, the same arguments used in our proof can also be applied to the rumor process defined in
[
11
]
to show that the rumor reaches vertex zero infinitely often, almost surely, given its survival.
Moreover, Theorem
1.6
also holds for the frog model with a general lifetime described by an arbitrary random variable
L
, which need not follow a geometric distribution with parameter
\pi
, as considered in Theorems 1.13 and 1.14 of
[
1
]
.

Since visiting vertex
0
infinitely often is only possible when the model survives, Theorem
1.6
also implies that if
E(\eta)<\infty
, then

P[V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta))=\infty]=P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}].

Thus, Theorem
1.6
implies that the hatched region in Figure
1
also represents recurrence. However, the result is more general, as it applies to any
\pi
, rather than only those from the Beta family.

The key idea of the proof is to identify necessary conditions on the distribution
\pi
so that

P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0.

These conditions, in turn, imply that, with probability 1, there will be potential for infinitely many particles to return to the root. When survival occurs, this potential is equivalent to

V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta))=\infty.

1.3
,
1.4
, and
1.6
. Section
2.1
introduces models that serve as useful couplings for the frog model. Section
2.2
presents auxiliary lemmas, while Section
2.3
contains the main proofs of the theorems.

2.1.
Coupling with rumor processes

We begin by defining models that will later be used in a coupling argument to assist in proving our theorems.

We consider the rumor model described in
[
11
]
. Let
\{I_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a collection of i.i.d. non-negative random variables representing the radius within which each vertex propagates the rumor. We let
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{0}}:=\{0\}
and define recursively for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
:

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1}:=\bigcup_{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}\{z\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{n}:|z-u|\leq I_{u}\}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{A}_{n+1}:=\mathcal{A}_{n}\cup\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1}.

(2.1)

Here,
\mathcal{A}_{n}
represents the set of vertices that are aware of the rumor at time
n
, while
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
consists of vertices that first receive the rumor at time
n
and propagate it at time
n+1
. Thus, each vertex
z\in\mathbb{Z}
, upon receiving the rumor at time
n
, attempts to propagate it at time
n+1
to its neighbors that have not yet received it and are within a distance of at most
I_{z}
.

We say that the rumor percolates if
|\cup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{n}|=\infty
, meaning that the rumor reaches infinitely many vertices. The percolation event is also equivalent to
\cap_{n\geq 0}\{\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}\neq\emptyset\}
, which ensures that at every time step at least one vertex continues propagating the rumor.

We refer to this model as the
bi-directional firework process
. The usual (uni-directional) firework process
[
7
]
can be defined similarly by modifying, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the definition of
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1}
in (
2.1
) to

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1}:=\bigcup_{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}\{z\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{n}:u<z\leq u+I_{u}\}

while keeping all other definitions unchanged. Thus, the uni-directional firework process behaves similarly to the bi-directional one, but spreading occurs in only one direction, and negative vertices play no role. We denote the firework and bi-directional firework processes by
\text{FW}(I)
and
\text{BFW}(I)
, respectively, where
I
is the radius random variable.

When both processes share the same radius distribution
I
, a simple coupling argument shows that

P(\text{FW}(I)\text{ percolates})\leq P(\text{BFW}(I)\text{ percolates}).

However, the conditions on
I
for percolation to occur with positive probability are identical for both models, as established in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
7
]
Theorem 2.1 and
[
11
]
Proposition 3.1)
.

We have that

P(\text{FW$(I)$ percolates})>0\iff P(\text{BFW$(I)$ percolates})>0\iff\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=0}^{n}P(I\leq i)<\infty.

In particular, we consider the case where
I
is defined as a function of two other random variables.

Let
\{N_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{R_{z,i}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z},i\in\mathbb{N}}
be independent collections of i.i.d. random variables, where
N_{z}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the number of spreaders at vertex
z
and
R_{z,i}\geq 0
denotes the radius of the
i
-th spreader at vertex
z
. We define
I_{z}
as the total propagation radius of the rumor at vertex
z
, considering all its spreaders:

I_{z}:=\max_{i\in\{1,...,N_{z}\}}R_{z,i}.

(2.2)

In this setup,
P(I\leq i)
can be directly determined from the distributions of
N
and
R
. Furthermore, by combining Lemma
2.1
with Kummer’s test for series convergence (see
[
3
, Proposition 3.2]
), we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider (
2.2
). The following items hold:

(i)

If
E(N)<\infty
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}nP(R\geq n)<1/E(N)
, then

P(\text{FW$(I)$ percolates})=P(\text{BFW$(I)$ percolates})=0.

(ii)

If
\liminf_{n\to\infty}nP(R\geq n)>1/E(N)
(where
1/E(N):=0
when
E(N)=\infty
), then

P(\text{FW$(I)$ percolates})>0\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581ptP(\text{BFW$(I)$ percolates})>0.

We now introduce variables for the frog model. These definitions will be useful for constructing a coupling with the firework process. Let

D_{z,i}^{\rightarrow}:=\max_{n\in\{0,...,L_{z,i}\}}\{S^{z,i}_{n}-z\}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636ptD_{z,i}^{\leftarrow}:=\max_{n\in\{0,...,L_{z,i}\}}\{z-S^{z,i}_{n}\}

(2.3)

represent the maximum distance reached by the
i
-th particle starting at
z
in the rightward (positive) and leftward (negative) directions, respectively.

Additionally, define

D^{*}_{z,i}:=\max_{n\in\{0,...,L_{z,i}\}}\{|S^{z,i}_{n}-z|\}=D_{z,i}^{\rightarrow}\vee D_{z,i}^{\leftarrow}

as the maximum displacement of the
i
-th particle from
z
in either direction.

Both
\{D^{\rightarrow}_{z,i}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z},i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{D^{*}_{z,i}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z},i\in\mathbb{N}}
form collections of i.i.d. random variables. We denote their representative random variables by
D^{\rightarrow}
and
D^{*}
, respectively.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
8
]
, Lemma 2.1)
.

For every
z\in\mathbb{Z}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(0,1)
,

P(D_{z,i}^{\rightarrow}\geq n|\pi_{z,i}=p)=\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-p^{2}}}{p}\bigg)^{n}

We can now establish a connection between the frog model and FW
(I)
and BFW
(I)
, both defined with
I
as a function of
N
and
R
, as in (
2.2
).

On the one hand, when
N_{z}=\eta_{z}
and
R_{z,i}=D_{z,i}^{*}
, BFW
(I)
dominates
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)
, meaning that

P[\text{BFW$(I)$ percolates}]\geq P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}].

This dominance holds because we overestimate the number of vertices visited by each particle’s random walk.

On the other hand, when
N_{z}=\eta_{z}
and
R_{z,i}=D_{z,i}^{\rightarrow}
, FW
(I)
is dominated by
\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)
, meaning that

P[\text{FW$(I)$ percolates}]\leq P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}].

This dominance holds because we consider only the rightward portion of each particle’s random walk.

These relationships, together with Lemma
2.2
, lead to the following result.

Lemma 2.4
.

The following items hold:

(i)

If
E(\eta)<\infty
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}nP(D^{*}\geq n)<1/E(\eta)
, then

P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]=0.

(ii)

If
\liminf_{n\to\infty}nP(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)>1/E(\eta)
(where
1/E(\eta):=0
when
E(\eta)=\infty
), then

P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0.

2.2.
Auxiliary lemmas

Lemma 2.5
.

Consider the beta function
B(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx
defined for
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
. Then,
B(\alpha,\beta)
is decreasing in
\alpha
and
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}B(\alpha,\beta)=0
for any
\beta>0
.

Proof.

The fact that
B(\alpha,\beta)
is decreasing in
\alpha
follows directly from its integral representation. We now proceed with the proof of the second statement.

Fix
\beta>0
. Note that
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}B(\alpha,\beta)
exists because
B(\alpha,\beta)
is positive and decreasing in
\alpha
. Instead of dealing directly with a continuous limit, we use the discrete limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}B(n,\beta)
, where
n
approaches infinity along the sequence of integers. Since both limits must coincide, it is sufficient to focus only on the discrete one and prove that
\lim_{n\to\infty}B(n,\beta)=0
.

We use a well-known recursion property of the beta function. Namely, for all
x>0
and
y>0
, we have

B(x+1,y)=B(x,y)\frac{x}{x+y}.

Applying this property recursively, we obtain for
n\in[2,+\infty)\cap\mathbb{N}
that

B(n,\beta)=B(1,\beta)\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}\frac{1+i}{1+i+\beta}.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
1/k\leq\beta
. We apply a telescoping product (i.e., a product
in which some numerators cancel out corresponding denominators) to obtain that

\displaystyle\bigg(\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}\frac{1+i}{1+i+\beta}\bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}\bigg(\frac{1+i}{1+i+1/k}\bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1+i+j/k}{1+i+(1+j)/k}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}\frac{1+i}{2+i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

Since
B(1,\beta)<\infty
, we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}[B(n,\beta)]^{k}=0
. Therefore,
\lim_{n\to\infty}B(n,\beta)=0
and the proof is complete.

\square

Lemma 2.6
.

For every
1<\gamma<2
, the following items hold

(i)

\lim_{n\to\infty}{(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}})^{-n}}=1

(ii)

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\frac{(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma/2}})^{n}}{{(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}})^{n}}}=0

Proof.

(i)

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. By the classic limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-\frac{1}{n})^{-n}=e
, we have for sufficiently large
n
that

[(1-\epsilon)e]^{n^{1-\gamma}}\leq\bigg[\frac{1}{(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}})^{n^{\gamma}}}\bigg]^{n^{1-\gamma}}\leq[(1+\epsilon)e]^{n^{1-\gamma}}.

(2.4)

The proof follows directly from (
2.4
) by realizing that
\big[\frac{1}{(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}})^{n^{\gamma}}}\big]^{n^{1-\gamma}}={(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}})^{-n}}
and that
\lim_{n\to\infty}[(1-\epsilon)e]^{n^{1-\gamma}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}[(1+\epsilon)e]^{n^{1-\gamma}}=1
.

(ii)

By item (i), it is enough to show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\bigg(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma/n}}\bigg)^{n}=0

to conclude the proof.

Using
\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-\frac{1}{n})^{n}=e^{-1}
, we have for sufficiently large
n
that

\bigg[{\Big(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma/2}}\Big)^{n^{\gamma/2}}}\bigg]^{n^{1-\gamma/2}}\leq[(1+\epsilon)e^{-1}]^{n^{1-\gamma/2}}

(2.5)

for any
\epsilon>0
, but we consider specifically
\epsilon
such that
(1+\epsilon)e^{-1}<1
.

Since
[(1+\epsilon)e^{-1}]^{n^{1-\gamma/2}}
decays exponentially fast, we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}n[(1+\epsilon)e^{-1}]^{n^{1-\gamma/2}}=0
and the proof is complete by (
2.5
).

\square

2.3.
Proof of Theorems

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Note that
\{D^{*}_{x,i}\geq n\}=\{D^{\rightarrow}_{x,i}\geq n\}\cup\{D^{\leftarrow}_{x,i}\geq n\}
and that
D^{\rightarrow}_{x,i}
and
D^{\leftarrow}_{x,i}
are identically distributed. Therefore, by subadditivity,

P(D^{\leftarrow}_{x,i}\geq n)=P(D^{\rightarrow}_{x,i}\geq n)\geq\frac{1}{2}P(D^{*}_{x,i}\geq n).

(2.6)

So, the proof is complete by Lemma
2.4
(i) if we show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}nP(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)=0.

Recall that
P(D_{x,i}^{\rightarrow}\geq n|\pi_{x,i}=p)=(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-p^{2}}}{p})^{n}
by Lemma
2.3
and note that
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}=\sqrt{(1+x)(1-x)}\geq\sqrt{1-x}
for
x\in(0,1)
. Therefore, for any
z\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle nP(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{z}n\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}\bigg)^{n}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}dx

(2.7)

\displaystyle+\int_{z}^{1}n\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}\bigg)^{n}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}dx

The idea is to choose
z=z(n)\in(0,1)
such that we are able to show that both integrals in (
2.7
) go to 0 when
n\to\infty
. Note that
(\max\{\frac{1}{\beta},1\},2)
is a non-empty interval as
\beta>0.5
and let
z:=1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}}
with
\gamma\in(\max\{\frac{1}{\beta},1\},2)
.

Since
\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}
is an increasing function of
x\in(0,1)
, we use Lemma
2.6
(ii) to conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}n\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}\bigg)^{n}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx}{B(\alpha,\beta)}dx

\displaystyle\leq n\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-z}}{z}\bigg)^{n}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx}{B(\alpha,\beta)}dx

\displaystyle\leq n\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-z}}{z}\bigg)^{n}

\displaystyle=n\frac{(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma/2}})^{n}}{{(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}})^{n}}}\rightarrow 0.

We have that
\big(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}\big)^{n}x^{\alpha-1}\leq x^{\alpha-1-n}\leq z^{\alpha-1-n}
for any
x\in(z,1)
and
n>\alpha-1
. Moreover,
1-\gamma\beta<0
as
\gamma\in(\max\{\frac{1}{\beta},1\},2)
. Therefore, we take a sufficiently large
n
and use Lemma
2.6
(i) to conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{1}n\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}\bigg)^{n}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{nz^{\alpha-1-n}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\int_{z}^{1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{nz^{\alpha-1-n}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\frac{(1-z)^{\beta}}{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{1-\gamma\beta}(1-\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}})^{\alpha-1-n}}{B(\alpha,\beta)\beta}\rightarrow 0,

completing the proof.

\square

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
c_{1}>c_{2}>...>c_{k}\geq 0
be a sequence of constants and
z_{i}=z_{i}(n):=1-\frac{c_{i}}{n^{2}}
for
i\in\{1,2,...,k\}
. Let

I_{n,\alpha,\beta}(i):=n\int_{z_{i}}^{z_{i+1}}\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\bigg)^{n}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}dx.

By Lemma
2.3
and the superadditivity property of the limit inferior,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}nP(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}I_{n,\alpha,\beta}(i)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\liminf_{n\to\infty}I_{n,\alpha,\beta}(i).

(2.8)

For any
0<z\leq x\leq 1
and
n\geq\alpha-1
, we have that

\bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\bigg)^{n}x^{\alpha-1}\geq\frac{\big(1-\sqrt{(1-z)(1+z)}\big)^{n}}{x^{n+1-\alpha}}\geq\big(1-\sqrt{2(1-z)}\big)^{n}.

So, for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle I_{n,\alpha,\beta}(i)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n\big(1-\sqrt{2(1-z_{i})}\big)^{n}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\int_{z_{i}}^{z_{i+1}}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{n\big(1-\sqrt{2(1-z_{i})}\big)^{n}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\bigg[\frac{(1-z_{i})^{\beta}}{\beta}-\frac{(1-z_{i+1})^{\beta}}{\beta}\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(c_{i}^{\beta}-c_{i+1}^{\beta})}{B(\alpha,\beta)\beta}n^{1-2\beta}\bigg(1-\frac{\sqrt{2c_{i}}}{n}\bigg)^{n}.

When
\beta<0.5
, we have by
(\ref{eq:i_int})
that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}I_{n,\alpha,\beta}(1)=\infty
for any choice of
c_{1}>c_{2}\geq 0
. We combine (
2.8
) and Lemma
2.4
(ii) to conclude that
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0
for any value of
E(\eta)\neq 0
.

Now we deal with the case
\beta=0.5
. By (
2.9
), we have that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}I_{n,\alpha,0.5}(i)\geq\frac{2(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\sqrt{c_{i+1}})}{B(\alpha,0.5)}e^{-\sqrt{2c_{i}}}.

(2.10)

By Lemma
2.5
, we have that
B(\alpha,0.5)
is decreasing in
\alpha
with
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}B(\alpha,0.5)=0
. This implies that
\alpha_{0}(E(\eta)):=\inf\{\alpha>0:B(\alpha,0.5)<E(\eta)\sqrt{2}\}
is well-defined for
E(\eta)\neq 0
(it is the infimum of a non-empty set). For all
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
, this also implies that
B(\alpha,0.5)<E(\eta)\sqrt{2}
and that there exists
\gamma=\gamma(\alpha)<1
such that

B(\alpha,0.5)<\gamma E(\eta)\sqrt{2}.

(2.11)

Note that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{i\sqrt{2}}{m}}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{m}}}{m\big(1-e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{m}}\big)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

where
\lim_{m\to\infty}m(1-e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{m}})=\sqrt{2}
can be calculated by the L’Hospital rule. So, as
\gamma(\alpha)<1
, there exists
m
and
k
such that

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}e^{-\frac{i\sqrt{2}}{m}}>\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{2}}.

(2.12)

Consider such
m
and
k
and take
c_{i}=(\frac{k-i}{m})^{2}
for

. We combine (
2.10
), (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) to obtain for
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
that

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\liminf_{n\to\infty}I_{n,\alpha,0.5}(i)\geq\frac{2}{B(\alpha,0.5)}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}e^{-\frac{i\sqrt{2}}{m}}>\frac{1}{E(\eta)},

where
\frac{1}{E(\eta)}:=0
when
E(\eta)=\infty
. Again, we use (
2.8
) and Lemma
2.4
(ii) to conclude that
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}]>0
for
\beta=0.5
and
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
.

\square

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Since
E(\eta)<\infty
and
P[\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)]\text{ survives}]>0
by assumption, we have by Lemma
2.4
(i) that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}nP(D^{*}\geq n)\geq\frac{1}{E(\eta)}.

Therefore, there exists an infinite increasing sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
a_{i}P(D^{*}\geq a_{i})\geq\frac{1}{2E(\eta)}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. It is possible to create a subsequence
(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that, in addition to
b_{i}P(D^{*}\geq b_{i})\geq\frac{1}{2E(\eta)}
, we also have that
b_{i+1}\geq 2b_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
P(D^{*}\geq j)\geq P(D^{*}\geq b_{i+1})\geq\frac{1}{2E(\eta)b_{i+1}}
holds for every
j\in\{b_{i}+1,...,b_{i+1}\}
, since
P(D^{*}\geq j)
is non-increasing. So, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(D^{*}\geq n)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=b_{i}+1}^{b_{i+1}}P(D^{*}\geq j)

(2.13)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b_{i+1}-b_{i})}{2E(\eta)b_{i+1}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4E(\eta)}=\infty.

We use (
2.6
), (
2.13
) and the independence between
\eta_{x}
and
D^{\leftarrow}_{x,1}
to conclude that

\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}P(\eta_{x}\geq 1,D_{x,1}^{\leftarrow}\geq x)\geq\frac{P(\eta\geq 1)}{2}\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}P(D_{x,1}^{*}\geq x)=+\infty

and, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, that

P(\eta_{x}\geq 1\text{ and }D_{x,1}^{\leftarrow}\geq x\text{ for infinitely many }x\in\mathbb{N})=1.

(2.14)

Under the event (
2.14
), there exist infinitely many vertices in
\mathbb{N}
that contain at least one particle capable of taking enough steps to the left to revisit vertex 0.

Thus, given that the frog model survives on the positive side (i.e., every vertex
x\in\mathbb{N}
is visited by some particle), it follows that

V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta))=\infty

with probability 1.

Moreover, by (
2.14
) and symmetry, we have that

P(\eta_{x}\geq 1\text{ and }D_{x,1}^{\rightarrow}\geq-x\text{ for infinitely many }x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0})=1.

Similarly to the previous case, given that the frog model survives on the negative side (i.e., every vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0}
is visited by some particle), it follows that

V_{0}(\text{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta))=\infty

with probability 1.

This completes the proof, as the survival of the frog model implies survival on either the positive or the negative side.

\square
