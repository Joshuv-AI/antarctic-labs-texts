Heterogeneously integrated photonic terahertz transmitter on thin-film lithium niobate
Authors: Luyu Wang, Hanke Feng, Liga Bai, Zhouze Zhang, Zhe Ding, Yifan Wu, Linze Li, Dingran Gu, Tianyu Long, Lu Zhang, Xianbin Yu, Zhidong Lyu, Cheng Wang, Baile Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTerahertz (THz) communication unlocks massive capacity for future data-intensive networks. While photonics-based THz systems offer broad bandwidths, their reliance on discrete optical assemblies dictates substantial coupling losses and bulky footprints. Achieving complete on-chip integration without degrading the bandwidth or efficiency of discrete optoelectronic components remains a central challenge. Here, we demonstrate an integrated photonic THz transmitter by heterogeneously bonding an InP-based uni-traveling-carrier photodiode (UTC-PD) to a thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) modulator chip. This architecture successfully merges TFLN’s excellent electro-optic response with robust III-V THz generation, featuring an integrated photodiode with a benchmark 3-dB bandwidth of 240 GHz and 0.67 A/W internal responsivity. In sub-THz wireless links, the transmitter delivers 40 Gbit/s at 150 GHz and 32 Gbit/s at 200 GHz using NRZ or PAM4 formats, establishing a scalable pathway for next-generation wireless interconnects.Subject terms: Integrated optics, Optoelectronic devices and components, Terahertz optics

Introduction

The explosive growth of data-intensive applications in artificial intelligence (AI) and immersive media is pushing wireless networks to scale their capacity, latency, and interconnectivity1,2. To address this demand, the terahertz (THz) band (0.1–10 THz) has emerged as a promising solution for ultrabroad bandwidth wireless in 6G and beyond networks3,4. Within this framework, THz transmitters, responsible for generating THz carrier signals and modulating baseband data onto the carrier, play a critical role5,6. Over the years, conventional electronic schemes based on solid-state multiplier chains and mixers have been the primary method for THz generation. However, these approaches are fundamentally constrained by the nonlinear nature of harmonic up-conversion and limited bandwidth of analog electronics, which limit their applicability in broadband and power-efficient THz links7,8.

Photonics-assisted approaches have proven to be a compelling alternative, offering sufficient bandwidth, low phase noise, and flexible frequency tunability9–11. In such systems, high-speed photodiodes (PD) play a central role through optical heterodyning, in which two narrow-linewidth lasers generate a THz beating signal that is converted into the THz domain without transistor-related bottlenecks6,10. Among various PD technologies, the uni-traveling-carrier photodiode (UTC-PD)12 is characterized by its electron-dominated transport mechanism. By utilizing only electrons as the active carriers, UTC-PDs can achieve ultrahigh bandwidth while maintaining a linear response even under high-power injection, establishing them as an effective choice for broadband THz emitters13–15. Leveraging these capabilities, UTC-PDs have recently enabled ultra-high-speed wireless links exceeding 100 Gbit/s in the sub-THz regime10,16,17. However, most of such systems still rely on discrete optoelectronic components that typically necessitate bulky supporting equipment, including high-power lasers and multiple optical amplifiers, to compensate for the substantial coupling and transmission losses18. Such configurations severely limit the scalability and flexibility required for large-scale deployment in future 6G networks, necessitating the on-chip integration of UTC-PDs with other essential photonic components to develop compact, energy-efficient THz front-ends.

While monolithic integration on Indium Phosphide (InP) platforms—which aims to consolidate lasers, modulators, and photodetectors onto a single chip—has been proposed for THz transmitters9,19, practical communication demonstrations using this intrinsic approach remain hindered by fundamental bottlenecks. Specifically, conflicting epitaxial requirements for different active components severely compromise the overall performance. Moreover, InP platforms face intrinsic physical limitations, including pronounced propagation losses from free-carrier absorption20 and a nonlinear electro-optic response that degrades high-frequency modulation fidelity21.

To overcome these constraints, heterogeneous integration offers a path to combine InP-based components with low-loss, broadband photonic platforms that support advanced modulation and signal routing22–24. Among these platforms, thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) is particularly attractive due to its strong electro-optic (EO) properties with low optical loss and excellent scalability. These characteristics enable sub-THz EO modulators operating at CMOS-compatible voltages, which facilitate energy-efficient Tb/s data throughput25,26. At the same time, the expanding availability of foundry-level processes for TFLN has enabled large-scale integration of diverse low-loss components, providing a promising basis for integrated THz photonics27–29.

Recent efforts have explored the integration of UTC-PDs with TFLN, including heterogeneously integrated MUTC-PDs on TFLN exhibiting 80 GHz bandwidth and 0.6 A/W responsivity30, as well as wafer-level InP-TFLN integration reaching 140 GHz bandwidth and 0.4 A/W responsivity31. However, the performance scaling in these works highlights a fundamental physical trade-off inherent in high-speed photodetectors: achieving a higher bandwidth requires a thinner absorption layer and a smaller device footprint to minimize carrier transit time and RC capacitance, which inevitably severely degrades the optical absorption efficiency32. While these existing devices have enabled baseband optical communication experiments, their bandwidths still fall short of the requirements for THz communication, and scaling to higher bandwidths inevitably results in a reduction in responsivity. Moreover, the large refractive index contrast between the TFLN waveguide and the III–V layers results in a severe optical mode mismatch, which further compromises the coupling efficiency. Therefore, realizing efficient on-chip THz generation on TFLN demands advanced device architectures capable of simultaneously overcoming the heterogeneous coupling bottleneck while pushing the bandwidth of the PD into the sub-THz regime.

In this work, we address these unmet demands by developing a heterogeneously integrated THz transmitter on the TFLN platform, comprising a high-speed TFLN modulator for data encoding and a 240-GHz bonded InP UTC-PD for THz generation. By utilizing a dual-step matching layer configuration, we achieve efficient optical field coupling between the low-index TFLN waveguide and the InGaAs absorber of the UTC-PD. Combined with a bandgap-engineered epitaxial design, the UTC-PD achieves a 3-dB bandwidth of 240 GHz and an internal responsivity of 0.67 A/W—representing the highest bandwidth-efficiency product among heterogeneously integrated photodiodes to date. System-level performance is demonstrated through sub-THz wireless links, where 40 GBaud NRZ at 150 GHz carrier frequency and 16 GBaud PAM4 at 200 GHz are achieved. This multi-band and multi-format operational versatility confirms that this work provides a scalable hardware foundation for future ultra-broadband photonic-wireless networks.

Results

Chip design and fabrication

Figure 1a presents a schematic of the proposed integrated THz photonic transmitter and its potential application scenarios. The transmitter enables direct on-chip THz generation via optical heterodyning and data encoding via electro-optic modulation, through the co-integration of high-speed TFLN modulators and heterogeneously bonded UTC-PDs. Specifically, two continuous-wave (CW) laser tones are delivered to each transmitter unit. To support high-linearity signaling for advanced modulation formats (e.g., PAM4), a single sub-carrier (SSC) modulation scheme is adopted. Under this configuration, only one tone is modulated by the EO modulator to encode baseband data, while the other remains unmodulated as an optical local oscillator. These two tones are then combined via a multi-mode interferometer (MMI) coupler and fed into the UTC-PD for photomixing. The generated signal is then directly radiated via a THz antenna for wireless transmission. This scalable scheme is particularly suited for high-capacity, short-range indoor networks serving multiple users, with potential applications spanning extended reality (XR), wireless data center interconnects, smart manufacturing, and holographic communication systems33–37.

A thick DVS-BCB overcladding passivates the UTC-PD sidewalls and provides a planar dielectric surface for electrode formation. The resulting non-suspended electrode configuration ensures stable high-frequency performance and improved structural robustness. Detailed fabrication process is discussed in the Methods. The optical image in Fig. 1c shows the compact and scalable chip layout, where dense arrays of EO modulators and heterogeneously integrated UTC-PDs are patterned on a 1 × 1.5 cm2 TFLN chip. The fabrication flow is fully compatible with wafer-scale processing, enabling scalable integration of complex THz photonic circuits.

The UTC-PD here retains the core active-layer design of our previous epitaxial structure39 with targeted modifications to enhance responsivity. As shown in the cross-section diagram in Fig. 2c, the epitaxial design incorporates a 180 nm partially depleted In0.53Ga0.47As absorption layer, which enables sufficient optical absorption while maintaining a high internal electric field at the heterojunction interface. To enhance velocity overshoot and suppress carrier accumulation, a 30 nm n-doped InP cliff layer (2 × 1017 cm−3) is inserted at the absorption-drift interface. This region locally intensifies the electric field, preventing collapse under high optical injection and maintaining an appropriate electric field within the 220 nm InP collector to support electron transport with overshoot velocity. A 100 nm heavily n-doped InP layer serves as the n-contact. More details are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Efficient optical coupling between the TFLN waveguide and the III–V photodiode is critical for achieving high responsivity, especially in compact device footprints. However, a key challenge arises from the significant refractive index contrast between LN (n ≈ 2.13) and the InGaAs absorption layer (n ≈ 3.59). Without additional matching structures, direct coupling from the LN waveguide into the PD absorption layer demands an extended interaction length, which is incompatible with compact integration22.

To address this issue, we introduce a dual-step optical mode-matching structure that gradually bridges the refractive index gap between the underlying LN (n ≈ 2.13) and InGaAs (n ≈ 3.59). As evidenced by the false-color SEM image in Fig. 2b and the epitaxial cross-section in Fig. 2c, the InP (n ≈ 3.18) collector and n-contact region of the UTC-PD are etched into two precisely defined steps. This cascaded morphology creates an intermediate effective-index gradient that enables smooth mode evolution from the TFLN waveguide into the high-index absorber.

To evaluate and optimize this coupling design, finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations were performed. The simulated optical intensity profile (Fig. 2f) serves as a visual guide to delineate the functional role of each step. Specifically, the first step (located between dashed lines 1 and 2 in Fig. 2f), with an etch depth of 100 nm, is designed to match the effective index of the TE mode in the LN waveguide. Its lateral length (2.2 μm) is optimized according to the phase-matching condition for directional coupling. Its lateral length (2.2 μm) is optimized according to the phase-matching condition for directional coupling. The second step (located between dashed lines 3 and 4 in Fig. 2f), etched to a depth of 300 nm and extended by 0.8 μm, accelerates energy transfer into the absorber region while minimizing reflections. Concurrently, the cross-sectional profiles in Fig. 2i (marked 1–5) visually confirm this successive upward migration, culminating in the optical power being predominantly confined within the InGaAs layer (beyond dashed line 5) for high-efficiency absorption.

Compared with devices without (Fig. 2h) or with a single-step matching layer (Fig. 2g), the optical mode in the dual-step design undergoes a faster and more continuous spatial transition within a total coupling length of only 3 μm. Figure 2j shows the simulated absorption efficiency as a function of propagation length. The dual-step design yields a simulated quantum efficiency of 56.46% (responsivity of 0.71 A/W) for an active region length of 7 μm. For comparison, we overlay curves for single-step and no-matching configurations, both of which see significantly slower absorption rate, confirming the critical role of dual-step matching in achieving compact, high-efficiency integration.

UTC-PD characterization

The frequency response of the UTC-PD is characterized using an optical heterodyne system (Methods and Supplementary Note 3), with all measurements conducted in situ on the fully integrated transmitter chip. As shown in Fig. 3a, the fabricated 2 × 7 μm2 PD achieves a 3-dB electrical bandwidth of 240 GHz at a photocurrent of 1 mA. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the highest bandwidth reported thus far for heterogeneously integrated PDs on TFLN platforms. The output power remains broadly uniform from DC to 180 GHz, which is essential for broadband communication as it minimizes amplitude distortion and simplifies digital equalization40.

To further evaluate the saturation characteristics of the PD, the output RF power is measured directly using a GSG probe and a THz power meter (Methods and Supplementary Note 3) at 150, 180, and 210 GHz, as shown in Fig. 3b. At 150 GHz, the device delivers a peak output RF power of −9.3 dBm at a photocurrent of 2.8 mA. At higher frequencies, the maximum achievable output power decreases, consistent with the device’s frequency response roll-off. The achieved output power is sufficient for centimetre- to metre-scale THz links, where sub-milliwatt levels are typically adequate due to focused beams and close transceiver spacing9,41. For longer-distance or higher-frequency links, external PAs can be employed to further boost the THz signal.

Figure 3c shows the measured DC current-voltage (I-V) characteristics of the fabricated UTC-PD. At a working reverse bias of −1 V, the dark current remains around 100 pA at room temperature, reflecting high-quality epitaxial growth and effective sidewall passivation achieved through dry etching and surface treatment. Such low leakage current is essential for THz photomixing, as it reduces the shot-noise floor and consequently enhances the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)42. To assess the wavelength tolerance and suitability for telecom-band operation, we measured the responsivity of the PD across a broad wavelength range from 1490 nm to 1640 nm. At a wavelength of 1550 nm, the measured external responsivity is 0.21 A/W. By factoring in the measured −5 dB coupling loss between the lensed fiber and the TFLN waveguide facet, this yields a consistent internal responsivity of 0.67 A/W across multiple devices. Moreover, this internal responsivity shows a minimal variation of less than ±10% across the entire C+L-band, as shown in Fig. 3d. Such broadband optical absorption behavior facilitates flexible transmitter design and wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) compatibility, allowing this UTC-PD to support multichannel or dynamically reconfigurable THz links.

THz wireless communication

A proof-of-concept experiment is conducted to validate wireless transmission enabled by the TFLN photonic integrated transmitter. As shown in Fig. 4a, two tunable lasers are amplified by Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFA) and coupled into the TFLN photonic chip via a fiber array. The on-chip TFLN modulator exhibits an EO 3-dB bandwidth exceeding 67 GHz (Fig. 3e) and a low half-wave voltage of 2.5 V. Driven by an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG) through an RF probe, the modulator is used to encode NRZ and PAM4 signals onto one of the optical carriers. The integrated UTC-PD then directly generates terahertz signals for wireless transmission, through heterodyne mixing between the two carriers. The THz output is delivered to a probe-contacted antenna without additional power amplifier, owing to the sufficient output power of the transmitter chip. The signal is received by an antenna after a 0.2 m wireless link, amplified by a frequency-matched low-noise amplifier (LNA), and down-converted to intermediate frequency (IF) with a corresponding subharmonic mixer driven by a frequency-multiplied Local Oscillator (specifically utilizing multiplication factors of ×12 and ×16 for the 150 GHz and 200 GHz bands, respectively). The specific LO is dynamically optimized to ensure the entire signal spectrum is captured by a real-time digital storage oscilloscope (DSO) and processed offline using digital signal processing (DSP) algorithms.

The transmission performance is summarized in Fig. 4b–d, with the photocurrent of the UTC-PD maintained at 1 mA. To validate the broadband capability of this transmitter, wireless communication experiments were conducted across a wide carrier frequency range from 120 GHz to 200 GHz. While the system demonstrates robust data transmission across the entire tested spectrum (detailed results for other frequency bands are provided in Supplementary Note 4), we focus our evaluation here on both NRZ and PAM4 modulation formats at representative carrier frequencies of 150 GHz and 200 GHz. As shown in Fig. 4b, the system achieves high-speed data transmission with BER remaining below the soft-decision forward error correction (SD-FEC) threshold. Notably, at 150 GHz, we achieve transmission rates up to 40 Gbit/s for both NRZ and PAM4 signals. Even at a carrier frequency of 200 GHz, the system robustly supports high-speed transmission of 32 Gbit/s for both modulation formats. Open eye diagrams are observed across these high data rates. Specifically, Fig. 4c illustrates the recovered NRZ and PAM4 eyes at 150 GHz, while Fig. 4d displays the recovered eyes at 200 GHz for both modulation formats. The gradual narrowing of the eyes with increasing baud rate is consistent with a reduced electrical SNR. Nevertheless, the achieved data rates show potential of compatibility with standards such as IEEE 802.15.3d, demonstrating the transmitter’s applicability in multi-channel and multi-user scenarios. The achieved performance thus represents a conservative lower bound, with the integrated device’s inherent bandwidth supporting significantly higher data rates. Overall, these results establish the feasibility of chip-scale THz wireless links using a heterogeneously integrated TFLN platform, while also indicating substantial headroom for further enhancement in both baud rate and carrier frequency.

Discussion

This work demonstrates a TFLN-based integrated THz photonic transmitter, in which a high-speed modulator is co-integrated with a heterogeneously bonded InP UTC-PD. System-level validation through high-speed wireless links using distinct modulation formats (NRZ and PAM4) at representative carrier frequencies of 150 GHz and 200 GHz confirms the functionality of this architecture. The demonstrated performance is enabled by the excellent EO performance of the TFLN platform and the integrated UTC-PD capability of operating in the THz regime. Table 1 summarizes the reported waveguide-based heterogeneously integrated high-speed PDs, showing that our device outperforms all previous demonstrations in 3-dB bandwidth, yielding the highest bandwidth-efficiency product to date. This advance arises from an optimized epitaxial design featuring a dual-step matching layer, which ensures efficient optical coupling while maintaining ultra-fast response.

At the system level, although dual-subcarrier modulation (DSC), where both optical tones are combined prior to simultaneous modulation, theoretically enhances the output power for basic intensity formats like NRZ43, it introduces severe nonlinear distortions for multi-level signals. We therefore adopted the SSC architecture, which provides superior linearity, yields lower bit error rates for PAM443, and naturally scales to more complex, higher-order modulation formats.

Despite the achieved high-speed data transmission, the current system has not yet fully exploited the intrinsic capabilities of the >60 GHz modulator and the 240 GHz UTC-PD. From a practical link-design perspective, the overall transmission capacity is dictated by the complete end-to-end RF/THz signal path rather than the intrinsic bandwidth of the integrated photonic transmitter alone. As evaluated via multi-tone probing (detailed in Supplementary Note 5), this cascaded response suffers from frequency-dependent amplitude roll-offs and localized ripples induced by the modulator RF drive chain, THz probe-to-antenna coupling, subharmonic down-conversion, and LO multiplication. Furthermore, even with amplifiers integrated, the substantial free-space THz propagation loss of more than 60 dB over the 0.2 m wireless link significantly degrades the received signal-to-noise ratio. Consequently, these cascaded impairments collectively reduce the usable signal bandwidth and constrain the maximum achievable data rate. To overcome these limitations, future implementations can be optimized through hardware enhancements—such as employing flatter-gain broadband LNAs, lower-conversion-loss mixers, lower-loss waveguide transitions, and high-gain antennas—alongside calibrated RF pre-emphasis and high-efficiency digital signal processing (DSP) algorithms to equalize the residual link response. Building on these strategies, the proposed photonic transmitter architecture possesses the potential to scale towards kilometer-level THz wireless communications44,45.

Building on this architecture, further enhancements in system capacity can also be realized through component-level improvements. On the modulator side, the TFLN platform enables I/Q modulation46 for advanced modulation formats such as 16QAM and QPSK, as well as on-chip injection-locking schemes47,48 that can enhance signal purity. On the PD side, refined optical-field engineering can improve the spatial uniformity of absorption without compromising responsivity. Advances in epitaxial design are expected to extend the operation to 300 GHz.

The demonstrated THz transmitter is highly compatible with other high-performance photonic components already realized on the TFLN platform, such as microcomb sources for phase-coherent carriers49, optical true-time delay lines for THz beamforming50. In addition, heterogeneous laser integration on TFLN offers a pathway toward self-sufficient optical front ends22,51, enabling fully monolithic transmitter architectures. Integrating these elements with the presented transmitter could yield comb-driven, multi-wavelength THz links featuring high integration density and multi-user connectivity, paving the way for practical deployment of compact, high-capacity wireless systems.

Methods

TFLN chip fabrication

Devices are fabricated on a commercially available x-cut TFLN wafer (NANOLN) consisting of a 500 nm LN, a 4.7 μm buried SiO2 layer, and a 500 μm silicon substrate. A SiO2 layer is first deposited on the TFLN wafer by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) to serve as an etching hard mask. Optical waveguides and Mach-Zehnder interferometers (MZIs) are then patterned using UV stepper lithography, processed die by die (1.5 cm × 1.5 cm) with a resolution of 500 nm. The exposed photoresist patterns are subsequently transferred to the SiO2 layer via a standard fluorine-based etching process and further into the LN layer using an optimized Ar-based inductively coupled plasma reactive-ion etching technique. The LN etch depth is ~250 nm, leaving a 250 nm thick slab. After removing the residual SiO2 mask and redeposited materials, the wafer undergoes an annealing step. A sequence of second, third, and fourth lithography and lift-off processes is then performed to form the microwave electrodes, heater, and metal interconnects/pads, respectively. To enhance EO bandwidth, advanced slotted electrodes are implemented with a 5 μm slot length and 2 μm slot width. The positive and negative electrodes are separated by a 5 μm gap to ensure strong EO coupling while suppressing metal-induced optical loss.

Adhesive bonding and UTC-PD fabrication

After the patterning of the TFLN chip and EO modulater fabrication, III–V epitaxial dies were then bonded directly onto TFLN surface using an ultra-thin DVS-BCB adhesive process. Following the bonding process, Ti/Pt/Au layers were deposited to form p-contacts. The main MUTC structure was then patterned using a combination of ICP-RIE dry etching and wet etching (H3PO4:HCl = 3:1). Meanwhile, excess III–V material was removed to expose LN waveguides, ensuring low-loss propagation across the hybrid platform. Following the etching steps, GeAu/Ni/Au layers were deposited as n-contact metals, and a rapid thermal annealing process at 360 ∘C was conducted to ensure good ohmic contact. A 4 μm thick BCB layer was then applied to passivate the sidewalls of the MUTC structure and provide stable support for the subsequent electrodes made of Ti/Au layers. Detailed fabrication process flow can be found in the Supplementary Note 1.

RF measurements of the UTC-PD

The frequency response and saturation characteristics of the UTC-PD were examined using an optical heterodyne setup. Two tunable lasers were used to generate single-frequency beat signals covering a range from Megahertz to Terahertz. For frequencies ranging from DC to 110 GHz, the RF signal was captured with the Rohde & Schwarz power meter (NRP2) via a GSG Model 110H probe. For frequencies above 110 GHz, a VDI power meter (PM5B), together with a set of waveguide probes covering the 110-325 GHz range, was utilized to evaluate the generated terahertz power. The detailed schematics of two measurement setups can be found in Supplementary Note 3.

THz wireless communication setup

The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 4a. An arbitrary waveform generator is used for message loading with a sample rate of 92 GSa/s and a maximum effective bandwidth of 32 GHz. To optimize the optical mixing performance of the UTC-PD, the gains of the EDFAs are carefully adjusted to equalize the power of the optical LO and carrier, which is achieved by balancing the photocurrents generated by each optical tone incident to the UTC-PD. The photocurrent of the UTC-PD is maintained at 1 mA to avoid saturation and potential device damage. The generated THz signal is transmitted over a 0.2 m wireless link, corresponding to a total free-space path loss of 62.0 dB at 150 GHz and 64.5 dB at 200 GHz. The LNA matched to the specific operational bands are employed to compensate for the high loss of the terahertz signal across different tested carrier frequencies. Subsequently, the amplified signal is down-converted using a subharmonic THz mixer. As depicted by the multiplier block (×N) in Fig. 4a, a local oscillator multiplication factor of N = 12 is utilized for carrier frequencies up to 160 GHz, whereas N = 16 is utilized for the 200 GHz band characterization. The IF signal is captured by a DSO is used with a sample rate of 160 GSa/s and a maximum effective bandwidth of 59 GHz.

Digital signal processing procedure

The digital signal processing (DSP) procedure is structured as follows. At the transmitter, the source bit sequence is generated via offline digital signal processing (DSP) and directly mapped into corresponding NRZ and PAM4 symbols. Subsequently, pulse shaping and resampling are applied to effectively confine the signal bandwidth and match the sampling rate of the arbitrary waveform generator (AWG). Based on optimal testing, the roll-off factor is set to 0.6 for 20 GBaud and 25 GBaud signals, and 0.3 for all other symbol rates. At the receiver, initial timing recovery is performed using the Gardner algorithm to correct impairments from system components. Subsequently, feed-forward equalization (FFE) and decision feedback equalization (DFE) are applied to compensate for channel distortion. Specifically, the tap allocations are optimized according to the carrier frequencies and modulation formats: at 150 GHz, 80 FFE and 50 DFE taps are utilized for NRZ signals, while 70 FFE and 30 DFE taps are employed for PAM4 signals; at 200 GHz, 60 FFE and 30 DFE taps are applied for both modulation formats.
