Estimating the True Distribution of Data Collected with Randomized Response
Carlos Antonio Pinzón 1 , Ehab ElSalamouny 1,5 , Lucas Massot 3 , Alexis Miller 4 , Héber Hwang Arcolezi 2 , Catuscia Palamidessi 1 1 INRIA Saclay, France 2 INRIA Grenoble, France 3 École Polytechnique, France 4 Ecole Normale Supérieure de Lyon, France 5 Suez Canal University, Egypt

Abstract
Randomized Response (RR) is a protocol designed to collect and analyze categorical data with local differential privacy guarantees. It has been used as a building block of mechanisms deployed by Big tech companies to collect app or web users’ data. Each user reports an automatic random alteration of their true value to the analytics server, which then estimates the histogram of the true unseen values of all users using a debiasing rule to compensate for the added randomness. A known issue is that the standard debiasing rule can yield a vector with negative values (which can not be interpreted as a histogram), and there is no consensus on the best fix. An elegant but slow solution is the Iterative Bayesian Update algorithm (IBU), which converges to the Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) as the number of iterations goes to infinity. This paper bypasses IBU by providing a simple formula for the exact MLE of RR and compares it with other estimation methods experimentally to help practitioners decide which one to use.



1
Introduction

Local differential privacy (LDP)
[
9
]
is a framework for providing privacy guarantees when collecting data from a set of users.
It removes the need for trust in the correct management of whoever collects the data and is typically used for automated telemetry.
The essence of LDP is to introduce controlled uncertainty right before the users transmit their data to avoid the data collector from receiving the exact specific information of each user.

The level of uncertainty or noise introduced by LDP is controlled by a parameter
\epsilon>0
, varying from a high privacy regime with
\epsilon\approx 0
(large uncertainty) to a low privacy regime with
\epsilon\gg 1
(low uncertainty).
The organization that collects and processes the data can not be certain about the specifics of any user in particular, but it can still derive statistical estimations.
These estimations become more precise as
\epsilon
increases and as the size of the user group grows.

One of the simplest and most fundamental LDP mechanisms is the Randomized Response
[
16
]
(also called
K
-ary RR, kRR, or simply RR), introduced firstly without any connection to LDP in
[
21
]
.
In a nutshell, RR reports either the truth with some fixed probability or a random value, picked uniformly.

Despite its simplicity, RR has been the subject of extensive research as it is the building block of more advanced mechanisms like Symmetric Unary Encoding and Local Hashing
[
20
]
as well as longitudinal protocols, e.g. RAPPOR
[
13
]
, d-bitFlipPM
[
7
]
, Longitudinal Local Hashing
[
3
]
.

Moreover, RR is known to be optimal among all LDP mechanisms for small domains or large values of
\epsilon
. Namely, whenever the domain size
K
satisfies
K<3e^{\epsilon}+2

[
20
]
.
Large values of
\epsilon
are particularly relevant in light of recent advances in Shuffle differential privacy
[
12
,
5
]
, where a shuffler is applied after local randomization to strip metadata and randomly permute the reports.
This added layer of anonymity enables privacy amplification, meaning that stronger central-DP guarantees can be achieved from weaker local ones. The growing use of Shuffle DP in real-world scenarios heightens the importance of high
\epsilon
regimes, and therefore, of RR.

Concerning statistical estimations, we focus on estimating the original data distribution, which is arguably the most fundamental statistic. To this purpose, typically the analytics server constructs a histogram of reported data, normalizes it to obtain a distribution, and uses a debiasing linear correction rule (
Inv
) to compensate for the added noise.
The resulting vector is proven to be unbiased, but it may not be a valid distribution, as it may have some negative entries.

In order to obtain a distribution, two simple workarounds have been proposed. The first,
InvN
, sets to
0
all the negative entries of the vectors, and renormalizes it. The second,
InvP
, projects the vector on the closest point on the simplex in terms of Euclidean distance.

A different approach, called iterative Bayesian update (IBU), was proposed by
[
1
]
. IBU produces an estimate of the original distribution
using an iterative algorithm that converges to the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) as the number of iterations grows
[
10
]
.
In this sense, IBU is well justified mathematically. However, the asymptotic nature of the algorithm makes it less attractive. Another drawback is the missing theory about the number of iterations or the stop condition that guarantees a certain proximity to the MLE.

Our first objective is to enhance the efficiency of MLE computation. To this aim, we propose
MLE*
, based on a mathematical formula that can be computed quickly, and which provides the exact MLE, thus bypassing the issue about the stop condition.

The second objective is to compare these estimators. One may think that, since the MLE is the “most likely” estimate, it would also be the most precise. Furthermore, the MLE is known to minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the empirical distribution (i.e., the normalized histogram output by RR) and the ideal distribution, obtained by multiplying the input distribution by the channel matrix that represents the RR noise. However, for practical purposes, the Kullback-Leibler divergence may not be the most important utility metric. Indeed, the precision of an estimation is typically measured in terms of mean square error (MSE). In this paper, we compare the MSE precision of the MLE,
InvN
and
InvP
. It turns out that, surprisingly, the precision of
InvN
and
InvP
“flip” depending on the distribution being more or less concentrated, while that of the MLE is always in between.

Other important terms of comparisons we investigate in this paper are efficiency, consistency, and unbiasedness. The methods that we consider are summarized in Table
1
.

In summary, the contributions of this paper are:

1.

We provide a simple formulation for the MLE, prove its correctness formally, and validate it empirically. These formulas coincide with
[
16
]
, Supplementary Material, Section F.

2.

We propose an algorithm that computes the MLE and is significantly more efficient than the state-of-the-art solution (IBU).
In addition, our algorithm gives an exact solution, whereas IBU provides only an approximation because the number of iterations is necessarily finite.

3.

We theoretically and experimentally compare MLE with other estimation methods to clarify their trade-offs, with the objective to help developers choose which one to use.

2
Preliminaries

This section formulates the estimation problem, presents the state-of-the-art estimators from related work.

2.1
Problem formulation

There are
N
users
u=1..N
, each of which has a secret value
x_{u}
from a set of
K
categories labeled as
\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
.
The secret value can represent, for example, the user’s browser homepage, the number of times they click on a specific button, or the emoji they use most frequently.

An organization, referred to as the data collector or the analyst, is interested in finding out the most frequent categories of the population as a whole (not user by user), and more generally, they are interested in estimating the proportion
\theta_{i}
of users
u
taking value
x_{u}=i
for every
i\in{\left\{1..K\right\}}
.
This corresponds to finding a vector
\theta\in\Delta
where the simplex
\Delta
is the space of distributions over
\{1..K\}
, i.e.,
\Delta:={\left\{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}:\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=1,\theta_{i}\geq 0\right\}}
.

To avoid violating the privacy of the users, the data collector will not collect
x_{u}
directly.
Instead, they will use a
mechanism

\mathcal{M}
(a function whose output is influenced by randomness, also known as a channel in information theory), whose purpose is to deliberately transform the input data
x_{u}
into some
y_{u}:=\mathcal{M}(x_{u})
with controlled randomness to hide its true value while still providing some information about it.
One sample per user is measured.

We suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is an instance of RR, and address the problem of how the analyst efficiently
estimates the unknown distribution
\theta\in\Delta
using the outputs
(y_{u})_{u=1}^{N}
and
the structure of the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
.

2.2
LDP and the RR mechanism

Local Differential Privacy (LDP)
[
9
]
is a strong privacy protection
ensured by the user-side mechanism by setting a formal bound on how much information is
revealed about the true input.
Formally, a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
with discrete output space satisfies
\epsilon
-LDP for some
\epsilon>0
(the smaller the more private) if for all inputs
x,x^{\prime}
and outputs
y
, it holds that

\Pr(\mathcal{M}(x)=y)\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr(\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime})=y).

This protects the unknown input because upon observing any output
y
, the degree to which any candidate input
x
is more likely than any other
x^{\prime}
is limited by
\epsilon
.

The randomized response (RR) mechanism takes an input
x\in{\left\{1..K\right\}}

and reports
\mathcal{M}(x)
from the same domain such that

\Pr(\mathcal{M}(x)=y)=\begin{cases}p&\text{ if }y=x\\
q=\frac{1-p}{K-1}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

(1)

That is,
\mathcal{M}(x)
returns
x
with probablility
p
, and otherwise outputs some
x^{\prime}\sim\text{uniform}({\left\{1,...,K\right\}}\setminus{\left\{x\right\}})
. Equivalently, it returns
x
with probability
p-q
,
and otherwise returns some
x^{\prime}\sim\text{uniform}({\left\{1,...,K\right\}})
.
The RR was first introduced by
21
for the binary case (
K=2
), as a survey technique for eliminating evasive answer bias.
More recently, RR has gained increased attention in the context of LDP, since it
satisfies
\epsilon
-LDP for
\epsilon=\log(p/q)
.

2.3
Estimators based on expectation

Let
\phi\in\Delta
denote the normalized histogram of the observed

y_{u}
for
u=1..K
, after applying RR parametrized by some known value
p
.
Note that the expected value of
\phi
is
\phi_{i}=q+(p-q)\theta_{i}
.
Based on this observation,
16
derived a simple and unbiased estimator, which we call the
linear inversion
estimator (
Inv
)

\hat{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{Inv}}:=\texttt{Inv}(\phi)_{i}:=\frac{\phi_{i}-q}{p-q}.

(2)

Nevertheless, the constraint
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{Inv}}\in\Delta
may fail to hold,
since (
2
) may produce negative values.
This occurs very often, as detailed later in Section
5.1
.
To overcome this issue, the following two post-processing solutions have been proposed in the literature.

1.

Normalization (
InvN
):
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvN}}_{i}\propto\max(0,\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{Inv}}_{i})
, i.e., set to zero the negative components and rescale.

2.

Projection (
InvP
):
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}}:=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\theta}{\|\theta-\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{Inv}}\|}
, where
\theta
varies in the set of valid distributions.

These estimators are valid (they always produce distributions), and they are fast.

InvN
is (clearly)
O(K)
and
InvP
can be implemented in
O(K\log K)
.
However, their design is a post-processing workaround without formal guarantees.

3
Estimators Based on Likelihood

Let
\mathbf{MLE}(\phi)
be the set of all MLEs. That is

\mathbf{MLE}(\phi):=\operatorname*{arg\,max}^{\text{(set)}}_{\theta\in\Delta}\Pr(\phi|\theta),

(3)

where
\Pr(\phi|\theta)
denotes the probability that the normalized histogram of the
random variables
Y_{u}
for
u=1..N
, is
\phi
provided
that the distribution for
x_{u}
is
\theta
and
Y_{u}\sim\mathcal{M}(x_{u})
.
Based on the theory of the Expectation-Maximization algorithm
[
6
]
,
it has been shown
[
1
,
2
,
10
]

that one can asymptotically approach an MLE using an algorithm known as
Iterative Bayesian Update (IBU). This algorithm is applicable to any discrete mechanism
with a finite channel matrix
C_{i\,j}:=\Pr(\mathcal{M}(i)=j)
of size
{K_{\text{in}}}\times{K_{\text{out}}}
.

IBU starts with a fully supported prior distribution
\hat{\theta}^{(0)}
, by default
\hat{\theta}^{(0)}:=(1/{K_{\text{in}}},1/{K_{\text{in}}},...,1/{K_{\text{in}}})
, and repeatedly updates
\hat{\theta}^{(t)}
into
\hat{\theta}^{(t+1)}
using what is known as Jeffrey’s update rule
[
15
,
19
]
:

\hat{\theta}^{(t+1)}_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{\text{out}}}\frac{\phi_{j}\;\hat{\theta}^{(t)}_{i}C_{i\,j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{in}}}\hat{\theta}^{(t)}_{k}C_{k\,j}}.

(4)

The most important property of IBU is that
\hat{\theta}^{(t)}
converges to
\hat{\theta}^{*}
for some
\hat{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbf{MLE}(\phi)
as
t\to\infty

[
10
]
.
The complexity of this procedure for a fixed large number of iterations
t={N_{\text{iters}}}
is
O({K_{\text{in}}}\,{K_{\text{out}}}\,{N_{\text{iters}}})
, because at each time step
t
, all denominators (for every
j
) can be cached in
O({K_{\text{in}}}\,{K_{\text{out}}})
and then
\hat{\theta}^{(t+1)}
can be computed in
O({K_{\text{in}}}\,{K_{\text{out}}})
.

In the particular case of RR, where
{K_{\text{in}}}={K_{\text{out}}}=K
, the algorithm can be accelerated from
O(K^{2}\,{N_{\text{iters}}})
to
O(K\,{N_{\text{iters}}})
using symmetries. Precisely, the update step (
4
) can be simplified to the following,
which takes only
O(K)
.

\displaystyle s^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\phi_{i}}{q+(p{-}q)\,\hat{\theta}^{(t)}_{i}}

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{(t+1)}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\hat{\theta}^{(t)}_{i}\left(q\,s^{(t+1)}+\frac{(p{-}q)\,\phi_{i}}{q+(p{-}q)\,\hat{\theta}^{(t)}_{i}}\right).

We will denote this procedure by
{\texttt{IBU}}(\phi):=\hat{\theta}^{({N_{\text{iters}}})}
for some fixed
{N_{\text{iters}}}
.

Lastly,
22
propose an algorithm that optimizes a regularized version of the likelihood by merging small values together.
Their algorithm runs in
O(K^{2}\log(K)\,{N_{\text{iters}}})
and uses a smoothing factor, which, for some practical experiments, gives better results than a pure MLE.

14
address the estimation under central (not local) differential privacy, and
17
propose an approximate iterative method (like IBU).

4
Formula for the MLE

This section starts with a derivation of the formula for the MLE, which coincides in different notation with that of
[
16
]
, Supplementary Material, Section F, and then provides the pseudocode to compute it and a summarizing sketch of the detailed self-contained proof in the Supplementary Material.

Consider a RR mechanism, defined with
p>0
and
q=\frac{1-p}{K-1}
as in (
1
).
For any observed distribution
\phi
and some threshold
\tau\in[\min_{i}\phi_{i},\max_{i}\phi_{i}]
, define
\phi^{\tau}
as

\phi^{\tau}_{i}=\begin{cases}q&\text{if }\phi_{i}<\tau\\
c_{\tau}\,\phi_{i}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad c_{\tau}=\frac{1-\sum_{\phi_{i}<\tau}q}{\sum_{\phi_{i}\geq\tau}\phi_{i}}.

Notice that
\sum_{i}\phi^{\tau}_{i}=1
by the definition of the constant
c_{\tau}
.
Observe also that the denominator in
c_{\tau}
is non-zero because

\tau\leq\max_{i}\phi_{i}
.
For this threshold transformation, notice that

\texttt{Inv}(\phi^{\tau})_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\phi_{i}<\tau\\
\frac{c_{\tau}\phi_{i}\ -q}{p-q}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(6)

The vector
\texttt{Inv}(\phi^{\tau})
sums up to
1
, but it may contain negative values for small
\tau
.
The proposed estimator, which we call
{\texttt{MLE*}}(\phi)
, is precisely
\texttt{Inv}(\phi^{\tau^{*}})
where

\tau^{*}
is the smallest threshold
\tau
for which
\texttt{Inv}(\phi^{\tau})
does not contain negative values:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}:={\texttt{MLE*}}(\phi):=\texttt{Inv}(\phi^{\tau^{*}}),

(7)

\displaystyle\tau^{*}:=\min{\left\{\tau\;\middle|\;\forall i,\,\phi_{i}{<}\tau\vee\;c_{\tau}\,\phi_{i}{\geq}q\right\}}.

Algorithm
1
computes
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}
for 1-indexed arrays, and we give
a zero-indexed Python implementation in the Supplementary Material.
The following invariant holds during the loop:
s
is the suffix sum
s=\sum_{i>k}\phi_{{\sigma({i})}}
.
After the loop,
i
is the number of zeros in the output
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}
and
\tau^{*}=\phi_{\sigma(i)}
.
The main loop that keeps track of
s
and finds the threshold
\tau^{*}
is
O(K)
,
therefore, the algorithm as a whole is
O(K\log K)
because it sorts the indices of

We conclude this section by showing that
MLE*
is the unique MLE of the RR mechanism, and we show a theoretical application of this result.

Theorem 1
.

The MLE for the RR mechanism is unique and given by the proposed formula
MLE*
.

Proof sketch.

The complete proof is given in Theorem
(Supplementary Material). The following is a sketch of it.

Suppose that
\hat{\theta}
is an MLE.
It can be shown using the Lagrange Multipliers method that for every category
i
, either
\hat{\theta}_{i}=0
or
\hat{\theta}_{i}=\frac{\phi_{i}}{\lambda}-\frac{q}{p-q}
, where the value of
\lambda
is such that the total sum is 1, i.e.
\lambda:=\frac{(p-q)\sum_{\hat{\theta}_{i}>0}\phi_{i}}{1-|{\left\{i:\hat{\theta}_{i}{=}0\right\}}|q}
.
This is proven in Theorem
(Supplementary Material).
With this result, the problem of computing all the components of
\hat{\theta}
is reduced into identifying the components
i
in which
\hat{\theta}_{i}=0
because
\lambda
and all the other components can be computed with the given formulas.
The rest of the proof is devoted to identifying the zero-valued components.

As shown in Theorem
(Supplementary Material), the entries in
\hat{\theta}
are
monotonic with respect to the entries in
\phi
in the sense that
\hat{\theta}_{i}\leq\hat{\theta}_{j}
if and only
if
\phi_{i}\leq\phi_{j}
. This observation follows by contradiction: if
\hat{\theta}
was not monotonic with
respect to
\phi
, then the order of two entries can be flipped, resulting in a new
\hat{\theta}^{\prime}
that
has a higher likelihood.

Therefore, the zeros of
\hat{\theta}
must occur in the positions of the smallest
n
components of
\phi
.
In other words, letting
\theta^{[n]}
be the result of applying the aforementioned formula obtained via Lagrange Multipliers assuming that the zeros occur in the positions of the smallest
n
values of
\phi
, then the MLE must be
\theta^{[n]}
for some
n\in{\left\{0,...,K-1\right\}}
whose value is unknown so far.

Finally, to find the value of
n
, let
e_{i}
denote the value of the
i
’th smallest component of
\phi
and define
g(n):=(1-nq)e_{n+1}-q(\sum_{i>n}e_{i})
for
n<K
, then the desired
n
must satisfy
g(n)\geq 0
and must be as small as possible.
This is shown in Lemmas
and
(Supplementary material).
The threshold
\tau^{*}
in Equation (
7
) corresponds to
e_{n+1}
.

In summary,
\hat{\theta}=\theta^{[n]}
, so the MLE is unique.

\square

5
Theoretical Comparison

This section compares validity, unbiasedness, MSE, consistency and complexity for the estimators of interest:
MLE*
,
Inv
,
InvN
and
InvP
.

5.1
Validity

By validity, we denote the property that the returned estimates are always guaranteed to be valid distributions.

Inv
guarantees
\sum_{i}\hat{\theta}_{i}=1
but not
\hat{\theta}_{i}\geq 0
, therefore, it is invalid.
The other three estimators are valid by design.

The problem that
Inv
produces vectors with negative values occurs very frequently in practice.
Indeed, for a population with
\theta_{i}\approx 0
for some
i
, the probability of
\hat{\theta}_{i}<0
equals that of
\phi_{i}<q
, which is mostly governed by the probability of a Binomial(
N-1,q
) not exceeding
Nq
.
This value is close to
1/2
and therefore non-negligible regardless of
N
.

5.2
Unbiasedness

It was shown in
20
, Theorems 1 and 2, that
Inv
is unbiased and has element-wise variance given by

\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{i})=\frac{q(1-q)}{N(p{-}q)}+\theta_{i}\frac{(p{-}q)(1-2q-(p{-}q))}{N(p{-}q)},

(8)

which follows from the observation that
\phi_{i}
follows a Multinomial distribution.

InvN
,
InvP
and
MLE*
, however, are biased for certain
\theta
, and this is an inevitable consequence of being valid.
For instance, fix
\theta=(1,0,...,0)
and vary
\phi
.
The realizations of the random vectors
\phi
and
\hat{\theta}
are points scattered around
\phi^{\prime}=((p{-}q),q,...,q)
and
\theta
respectively.
Some of the points around
\theta
are outside the valid region and some are inside, but they balance and we have
E(\hat{\theta})=\theta
.
If we apply any rule
f
that moves invalid points to the valid region and keeps valid points as they are, then
E(f(\hat{\theta}))
will necessarily fall in the (strict) interior of the valid region.
Thus, since
\theta
is in the border, not in the interior, we obtain
E(f(\hat{\theta}))\neq\theta
.
A generic proof for this fact can be found in
4
.

5.3
Complexity

In terms of computational speed,
Inv
,
InvN
and
InvP
are
O(K)
while
IBU
is
O(K\,{N_{\text{iters}}})
and
MLE*
is
O(K\log K)
(cost of sorting the indices).
The larger cost of
MLE*
above
O(K)
is very low relative to the gained guarantees: the output is valid, it produces the exact MLE, and the complexity difference is sub-polynomial.

5.4
MSE, TV and consistency

The Mean Squared Error is defined as
\text{MSE}_{\theta}(\hat{\theta}):=E_{\phi|\theta}({\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|}^{2})
, where
\hat{\theta}
is fixed to one of the four estimators.
As shown in Theorem
(Supplementary Material), the four estimators are consistent because
\text{MSE}_{\theta}(\hat{\theta})\in O(K/N)
, which converges to
0
as
N\to\infty
.
This implies that other measures, like the Total Variation
\text{TV}_{\theta}(\hat{\theta}):=\frac{1}{2}E_{\phi|\theta}({\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|}_{1})
also converge to
0
as
N\to\infty
.
Alternatively, the consistency of
MLE*
can be proven using the sufficient conditions derived by
11
for the consistency of MLE for generic privacy mechanisms, of which RR is a particular case.

6
Experiments

In this section, we describe our experimental setup, present key results, and discuss the implications of our findings. The code is available at
[
18
]
.
Our experiments pursue two main objectives:

(1)
validating the correctness of our
MLE*
estimator by comparing it against the
IBU
approach under the RR mechanism, and
(2)
evaluating the performance and robustness of
MLE*
compared to two standard baselines,
InvP
and
InvN
.
Specifically, while
InvP
and
InvN
perform well in distribution-specific settings,
MLE*
offers a consistently strong performance across a wide range of scenarios, making it a robust and reliable choice when the true data distribution is unknown.

6.1
General Setup

We conduct an extensive empirical evaluation to compare the performance of three estimators under the RR mechanism:

MLE*
,
InvP
, and
InvN
.

Experiments are designed to assess robustness across a wide range of configurations.
Specifically, we vary:

•

Privacy budget
\epsilon\in\{1,2,\ldots,9,10\}
;

•

Sample size
N\in\{10^{2},10^{3},10^{4},10^{5},10^{6}\}

7
Conclusion

We addressed several limitations in frequency estimation under randomized response.

First, we derived a formula for the Maximum Likelihood Estimator and proved its correctness both theoretically and empirically. We also proved the uniqueness of the MLE.
Our derivation corroborates the results of
[
16
]
, Supplementary Material, Section F, and, since the formula does not involve limits of any kind, it can be used to better understand the analytical properties of the MLE.
Second, we proposed an algorithm,
MLE*
, that computes the MLE in
O(K\log K)
using the formula we found, vastly outperforming the iterative procedure IBU in speed while also providing an exact output as opposed to an approximation.
Third, we conducted extensive empirical comparisons of the most prominent valid estimators (
InvN
,
InvP
and the MLE using
MLE*
) to shed light on their behavior and trade-offs under different scenarios.
We found that in terms of mean squared error, the projection method
InvP
outperforms
InvN
on average when the unknown target distribution is highly concentrated and vice-versa.
Furthermore, while
InvP
and
InvN
outperform each other under specific conditions,
MLE*
consistently stays in between, which makes it robust in that it is never the worst of the three.
Its validity, robustness, and efficiency make
MLE*
a reliable default choice when the data distribution is unknown and performance under uncertainty matters.

Since many practical mechanisms are built upon or inspired by RR, an interesting direction for future research is to extend our analysis of the formula of
MLE*
to other LDP frequency estimation protocols
[
20
]
, in particular to
longitudinal LDP protocols

[
13
,
3
,
7
]
, which protect users’ data across repeated collections and are highly relevant in real-world telemetry and monitoring applications.
Another future direction is to combine different estimators to enrich the analysis. Finally, a bias-variance is included in the code repository
[
18
]
.

A
Code for reproducibility

The implementation of the estimators and the code needed to reproduce all the results and plots presented in this paper can be found in the Zip file of Supplementary Material.

For quick reference, a simple implementation in Python (zero-indexed) of
MLE*
and
IBU
is presented below.

import numpy as np

def MLE(phi, p: float):
phi = np.asarray(phi)
K = len(phi); q = (1-p) / (K-1)
assert p >= q and q >= 0
sig = np.argsort(phi)
i = 0; s = 1
while i<K and q*s > (1-i*q) * phi[sig[i]]:
s -= phi[sig[i]]; i += 1
lam = (p - q) * s / (1 - i*q)
theta = np.zeros(K)
theta[sig[i:]] = phi[sig[i:]]/lam - q/(p-q)
return theta

def IBU(phi, p: float, n_iters=40000):
phi = np.asarray(phi)
K = len(phi); q = (1-p) / (K-1)
d = p - q
theta = np.ones(K) / K
for i in range(n_iters):
Z = phi / (q + d * theta)
theta = theta * (q * np.sum(Z) + d * Z)
return theta

B
Proof and derivation of the formula

Let
\Delta
be the set of all probability distributions over
K
categories, i.e., the simplex
\Delta={\left\{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}:\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=1,\theta_{i}\geq 0\right\}}
.

For
\theta\in\Delta
, the vector
{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)\in\Delta
denotes the expected compound distribution of the output
Y\in{\left\{1..K\right\}}
of the randomized-response mechanism
{\mathcal{M}}
(governed by some fixed
p
and
q
) when the input
X
is sampled from
\theta
, i.e., if
X\sim\theta
and
Y\sim{\mathcal{M}}(X)
, then
{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)_{y}=p_{Y}(y)=\sum_{x}\theta_{x}\;p_{Y|x}(y)=q+(p-q)\theta_{y}
.

If
\phi\in\Delta
is the empirical histogram of
N
observed outputs
y_{i}\in\{1..K\}
coming from
{\mathcal{M}}
, then the
likelihood
of some
\theta\in\Delta
being the original input distribution given
\phi
is
L(\theta|\phi):=p(\phi|\theta):=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{Y}(y_{i})
.
If
\theta
maximizes the likelihood in
\Delta
, we say that
\theta
is a
Maximum Likelihood Estimator
(MLE) for
\phi
.

For
\phi,\theta\in\Delta
the
Kullback-Leibler divergence
is given by
D_{KL}(\phi,\theta):=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\phi_{i}\log\left(\frac{\phi_{i}}{\theta_{i}}\right)
, with convention
0\log\frac{0}{0}=0
.

To begin, we prove that Maximizing likelihood is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence.

Theorem 3
.

Maximizing likelihood is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence in the following sense:

\operatorname*{arg\,max}^{\text{(set)}}_{\theta\in\Delta}L(\theta|\phi)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}^{\text{(set)}}_{\theta\in\Delta}D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta))

Proof.

For fixed
\phi
, let
f(\theta):=\sum_{y}\phi_{y}\log{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)_{y}
.
Notice that
D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta))=\sum_{y}\phi_{y}\log\phi_{y}-f(\theta)
and

\displaystyle L(\theta|\phi)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{Y}(y_{i})=\prod_{y=1}^{K}p_{Y}(y)^{|{\left\{i:y_{i}{=}y\right\}}|}

\displaystyle=(\prod_{y}p_{Y}(y)^{\phi_{y}})^{N}=\exp\left(N\sum_{y}\phi_{y}\log p_{Y}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(N\,f(\theta)\right)

So,
D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta))
is minimized if and only if
f(\theta)
is maximized, which is equivalent to maximizing
L(\theta|\phi)
.
∎

We now use the Lagrange Multipliers method to derive a formula that characterizes any given MLE.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\phi,\theta\in\Delta
and let
\lambda:=\frac{(p-q)\sum_{\theta_{i}>0}\phi_{i}}{1-|{\left\{i:\theta_{i}{=}0\right\}}|q}
.
If
\theta
minimizes
D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta))
in
\Delta
, then, for every
i
, either
\theta_{i}=0
or
\theta_{i}=\frac{\phi_{i}}{\lambda}-\frac{q}{p-q}
.

Proof.

To apply the theorem of the Lagrange multipliers method, we will split the constraint
\theta_{i}\geq 0
into
\theta_{i}=0
for some
i
and
\theta_{i}=e^{u_{i}}
for some others in the following sense.
List the indices
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}
(here
m<K
) and
\beta_{1},...,\beta_{K-m}
with
\theta_{\alpha_{i}}>0
and
\theta_{\beta_{i}}>0
.
For every vector
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, let
\theta=f(u)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
be given by
f(u)_{\alpha_{i}}=e^{u_{i}}
and
f(u)_{\beta_{i}}=0
.

The minimizer
u^{*}
of
\ell(u):={\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\phi,\theta)
constrained to
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=1
can be found with the Lagrange multipliers method:
(u^{*},\lambda^{*})
must be a critical point of the Lagrange function
\mathcal{L}(u,\lambda)=\ell(u)-\lambda(1-\sum_{i}\theta_{i})
.
Notice that

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{i}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\theta_{\alpha_{i}}}}\frac{\partial{\theta_{\alpha_{i}}}}{\partial u_{i}}=\left(-\frac{\phi_{\alpha_{i}}(p-q)}{q+(p-q){\theta_{\alpha_{i}}}}+\lambda\right){\theta_{\alpha_{i}}},

and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda}=1-\sum_{i}\theta_{i}
.
Therefore, from
\nabla_{u,\lambda}\mathcal{L}(u,\lambda)|_{u^{*},\lambda^{*}}=0
and the fact that
{\theta_{\alpha_{i}}}>0
by definition, it follows that the minimizer
\theta:=f(u^{*})
is given by
\theta_{\alpha_{i}}=\frac{\phi_{\alpha_{i}}}{\lambda^{*}}-\frac{q}{p-q}
and
\theta_{\beta_{i}}=0
, and
\lambda^{*}=\frac{(p-q)\sum_{\theta_{i}>0}\phi_{i}}{(p-q)+|{\left\{i:\theta_{i}{>}0\right\}}|q}
.
Finally, from
p+(K-1)q=1
, the denominator becomes simply
1-|{\left\{i:\theta_{i}{=}0\right\}}|q
.
∎

With Theorem
4
, the problem of computing all the components of
\hat{\theta}
is reduced into identifying the components
i
in which
\hat{\theta}_{i}=0
because
\lambda
and all the other components can be computed with the given formulas.
The rest of the proof is devoted to identifying the zero-valued components.

The problem might be reduced even further because, as shown in Theorem
5
, permuting
\phi
simply permutates the MLE.
Hence, if we knew how to find an MLE for every sorted
\phi
, we could derive a method to find an MLE for every
\phi
simply by permutating the input and then the output in the same way.

Theorem 5
.

(Permutations can be abstracted)
Fix some
\phi\in\Delta
satisfying
\phi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\phi_{K}
, some permutation
\sigma:{\left\{1..K\right\}}\to{\left\{1..K\right\}}
, and let
f_{\sigma}:\Delta\to\Delta
be the permutation function
f_{\sigma}(\theta)_{i}=\theta_{\sigma(i)}
.
If
\theta
is an MLE for
\phi
, then
f_{\sigma}(\theta)
is an MLE for
f_{\sigma}(\phi)
(and vice-versa via
f_{\sigma^{-1}}
).

Proof.

Notice that
{\mathcal{M}}(f_{\sigma}(\theta))_{i}=q+(p-q)f_{\sigma}(\theta)_{i}=q+(p-q)\theta_{\sigma(i)}={\mathcal{M}}(\theta)_{\sigma_{i}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle D_{KL}(f_{\sigma}(\phi),{\mathcal{M}}(f_{\sigma}(\theta)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\phi_{\sigma(i)}\log\frac{\phi_{\sigma(i)}}{{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)_{\sigma(i)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\phi_{j}\log\frac{\phi_{j}}{{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)_{j}}

\displaystyle=D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)).

∎

Furthermore, as shown in Lemma
1
and Theorem
6
, the entries in
\hat{\theta}
are monotonic with respect to the entries in
\phi
.

Lemma 1
.

(Monotonicity)
If two distributions
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Delta
differ only at two swapped positions
j
and
k
with
a:=\theta_{j}=\theta^{\prime}_{k}<b:=\theta_{k}=\theta^{\prime}_{j}
, and
\phi_{j}<\phi_{k}
then
D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta))<D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{\prime}))
.

Proof.

Let
\delta:=D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta))-D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{\prime}))
.

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\phi_{i}\log\frac{q+(p-q)\theta^{\prime}_{i}}{q+(p-q)\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\phi_{j}\log\frac{q+(p-q)b}{q+(p-q)a}+\phi_{k}\log\frac{q+(p-q)a}{q+(p-q)b}

\displaystyle=(\phi_{j}-\phi_{k})\log\frac{q+(p-q)b}{q+(p-q)a}.

Since
\phi_{j}<\phi_{k}
and
b>a
, we have
\delta<0
.
∎

Theorem 6
.

(Monotonicity)
If
\theta
is an MLE for
\phi
, then for any
i,j\in{\left\{1..K\right\}}
, if
\phi_{i}\leq\phi_{j}
then
\theta_{i}\leq\theta_{j}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Lemma
1
.
∎

Due to Theorem
6
, if
\hat{\theta}
is an MLE for
\phi
, then the components of
\hat{\theta}
preserve the ordering (by value) of the corresponding components in
\phi
.
Therefore, the zero components of
\hat{\theta}
must align with the positions of the
n
smallest components of
\phi
, for some unknown
n\in{\left\{0,...,K-1\right\}}
.
In particular, when
\phi
is sorted, the zeros of
\hat{\theta}
must occur in the first
n
coordinates.
This largely reduces the search space for
\hat{\theta}
, because if we let
\theta^{[n]}
be the result of applying the formula of Theorem
4
(Definition
1
), then it must hold that
\hat{\theta}
equals
\theta^{[n]}
for some
n
(Lemma
2
).

Definition 1
.

Fix some
\phi\in\Delta
satisfying
\phi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\phi_{K}
, and some
n\in{\left\{0..K-1\right\}}
.
Define
\lambda^{[n]}:=\frac{(p-q)\sum_{i>n}\phi_{i}}{1-nq}
, and let
\theta^{[n]}
be the
K
-dimensional vector given by

\theta^{[n]}_{i}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }i\leq n\\
\frac{\phi_{i}}{\lambda^{[n]}}-\frac{q}{p-q}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Notice that
\sum_{i}\theta^{[n]}_{i}=1
, but it is not necessarily the case that
\theta^{[n]}\in\Delta
because
\theta^{[n]}_{i}<0
may occur.
Notice also that

{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n]})=\begin{cases}q&\text{ if }i\leq n\\
\frac{(p-q)\phi_{i}}{\lambda^{[n]}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma 2
.

Fix some
\phi\in\Delta
satisfying
\phi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\phi_{K}
.
If
\theta
is an MLE for
\phi
, then there exists some
n\in{\left\{0,...,K-1\right\}}
such that
\theta=\theta^{[n]}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6
, we have
\theta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{K}
.
Let
n=|{\left\{i:\theta_{i}=0\right\}}|
.
Notice that
\theta_{i}=0
iff
\theta^{[n]}_{i}=0
, because (
\rightarrow
) if
\theta_{i}=0
, then
i\leq n
and
\theta^{[n]}_{i}=0
, and (
\leftarrow
) if
\theta_{i}>0
, then
i>n
and
\theta^{[n]}_{i}>0
.
From Theorem
4
, it follows that
\theta_{i}=\frac{\phi_{i}}{\lambda}-\frac{q}{p-q}=\theta^{[n]}_{i}
for all
i>n
.
∎

In order to determine the value of
n
for which the MLE is
\theta^{[n]}
(still assuming sorted
\phi
), we first prove some properties of
\theta^{[n]}
for arbitrary
n
in Lemma
3
.

Lemma 3
.

Fix some
\phi\in\Delta
satisfying
\phi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\phi_{K}
, and some
n\in{\left\{0..K-1\right\}}
.
Let
g(n):=(1-nq)\phi_{n+1}-q(\sum_{i>n}\phi_{i})
.
The following assertions hold:

(a)

\theta^{[n]}\in\Delta
if and only if
g(n)\geq 0
.

(b)

For
n>0
, if
\theta^{[n-1]}\in\Delta
, then
\theta^{[n]}\in\Delta
.

(c)

For
n>0
, if
\theta^{[n-1]}\in\Delta
, then
\lambda^{[n-1]}\geq\lambda^{[n]}
.

(d)

For
n>0
, if
\phi_{n-1}=\phi_{n}
then
g(n)=g(n-1)
.

(e)

For
n>0
, if
g(n-1)=0
then
\theta^{[n-1]}=\theta^{[n]}
.

Proof.

(a) Let
\theta=\theta^{[n]}
and
\lambda=\lambda^{[n]}
.
We have
\theta\in\Delta
iff
\frac{\phi_{i}}{\lambda}\geq\frac{q}{p-q}
for all
i>n
.
This holds for all
i>n
iff it holds for
i=n+1
since
\phi_{n+1}\leq\phi_{n+2}\leq\cdots\leq\phi_{K}
.
So,

\displaystyle\theta\in\Delta

\displaystyle\iff\frac{\phi_{n+1}}{\lambda}\geq\frac{q}{p-q}

\displaystyle\iff\phi_{n+1}(1-nq)\geq q\Sigma_{i>n}\phi_{i}

\displaystyle\iff g(n)\geq 0.

(b) Since
\phi_{n+1}\geq\phi_{n}
,

\displaystyle g(n)-g(n-1)

\displaystyle=(1-nq)\phi_{n+1}-(1-(n-1)q)\phi_{n}

\displaystyle-q(\Sigma_{i>n}\phi_{i}-\Sigma_{i>n-1}\phi_{i})

\displaystyle=(1-nq)(\phi_{n+1}-\phi_{n})\geq 0

(c) We have
g(n-1)\geq 0
from
\theta^{[n-1]}\in\Delta
, and

\displaystyle\textnormal{sign}(\lambda^{[n-1]}-\lambda^{[n]})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{sign}(\frac{\Sigma_{i>n-1}\phi_{i}}{1-nq+q}-\frac{\Sigma_{i>n}\phi_{i}}{1-nq})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{sign}(\underbrace{(1-nq)(\Sigma_{i>n-1}\phi_{i})-(1-nq+q)(\Sigma_{i>n}\phi_{i})}_{\delta}),

and

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=(1-nq)\phi_{n}-q\sum_{i>n}\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=(1-(n-1)q)\phi_{n}-q\sum_{i>n-1}\phi_{i}=g(n-1)\geq 0,

so
\lambda^{[n-1]}\geq\lambda^{[n]}
.

(d) If
\phi_{n+1}=\phi_{n}
, from (b) one obtains
g(n)-g(n-1)=(1-nq)(\phi_{n+1}-\phi_{n})=0
.

(e) If
g(n-1)=0
then
\delta=0
and thus
\lambda^{[n-1]}=\lambda^{[n]}
which means that
\theta^{[n-1]}=\theta^{[n]}
.

∎

We now characterize the value of
n
using the aforementioned properties.

Lemma 4
.

(
n
is as small as possible)
For
n>0
, if
\theta^{[n-1]}\in\Delta
then
\theta^{[n]}\in\Delta
and
D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n-1]}))\leq D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n]}))
.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{i}:=\phi_{i}\log\frac{{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n-1]})_{i}}{{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n]})_{i}}
.
Notice that
\delta_{i}\geq 0
for all
i>n
, because

•

For
i<n
,
{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n-1]})_{i}=q={\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n]})_{i}
, so
\delta_{i}=0
.

•

For
i=n
, since
\theta^{[n-1]}\in\Delta
, when
\theta^{[n-1]}_{i}\geq 0
, hence
{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n-1]})_{i}\geq q={\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n]})_{i}
.

•

For
i>n
,
\delta_{i}=\phi_{i}\log\frac{\lambda^{[n-1]}\phi_{i}}{\lambda^{[n]}\phi_{i}}
, in which case
\delta_{i}\geq 0
because
\lambda^{[n-1]}\geq\lambda^{[n]}
(Lemma
3
).

Therefore, for
\delta:=D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n]}))-D_{KL}(\phi,{\mathcal{M}}(\theta^{[n-1]}))
, we have

\delta=\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\geq 0
.
∎

Finally, everything is put together in Theorem
7
.

Theorem 7
.

(Wrap up)
Fix any given distribution
\phi^{\prime}\in\Delta
and consider the following procedure.

1.

Let
\sigma
be an arg-sort permutation for
\phi^{\prime}
, so that
\phi^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\phi^{\prime}_{\sigma(K)}
.

2.

Let
\phi\in\Delta
be given by
\phi_{i}:=f_{\sigma}(\phi)_{i}=\phi^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)}
.

3.

Let
n
be the smallest integer such that
g(n)\geq 0
, where
g(n):=(1-nq)\phi_{n+1}-q(\sum_{i>n}\phi_{i})
.

4.

Let
\theta^{[n]}
be the vector defined in Definition
1
for
\phi
and
n
.

5.

Let
\theta^{\prime}\in\Delta
be given by
\theta^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)}=\theta^{[n]}_{i}
.

Then
\theta^{\prime}
is the unique MLE for
\phi^{\prime}
.
Alternatively, for pedagogic reasons,
\theta^{[n]}
can also be presented as

\theta^{[n]}_{i}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\phi_{i}\leq\tau:=\phi_{n}\\
\frac{\phi_{i}}{\lambda}-\frac{q}{p-q}&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

By Theorem
5
, the search for an MLE
\theta^{\prime}
for
\phi^{\prime}
can be reduced to the search for an MLE
\theta
for
\phi
, via
\theta_{i}=\theta_{\sigma(i)}
.
Since
\phi
is sorted, by Lemma
2
, there exists some
n\in{\left\{0..K\right\}}
such that
\theta=\theta^{[n]}
.
By Lemma
4
, the set
{\left\{n:\theta^{[n]}\in\Delta\right\}}
is a non-empty suffix of
{\left\{0..K-1\right\}}
, and the optimal
n
is the smallest in this set.
Regarding the alternative presentation, Lemmas
4
and
3
(Part (d)) imply that the optimal
n
satisfies
n=0
or
\phi_{n-1}\neq\phi_{n}
.
This means that
i\leq n
iff
\phi_{i}\leq\tau:=\phi_{n}
, so the two definitions are equivalent.
∎

Theoretical use of the formula

Theorem 8
.

If the smallest entry in
\phi
takes value
v<q
, and the second smallest is at least
(Kq-v)/(K-1)
, then
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}}
,
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}
and
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvN}}
are collinear, with
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}
in the middle.

Proof.

Let
n
be the coordinate of the smallest component
\phi_{n}<q
.
We have
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}}_{n}=\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvN}}_{n}=\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}_{n}=0
, and the remaining components are given by the following formulas.

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}\frac{1}{p-q}-\frac{(Kq-\phi_{n})}{(K-1)(p{-}q)}

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvN}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}\frac{1}{p-\phi_{n}}-\frac{q}{p-\phi_{n}}

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}\frac{1-q}{(p{-}q)(1-\phi_{n})}-\frac{q}{(p{-}q)}

Letting
t:=\frac{1-\phi_{n}}{p-\phi_{n}}
, it can be shown that
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvN}}-\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}}=t(\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}-\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}})
.
Therefore, the three estimators are collinear.
Furthermore, since
1-\phi_{n}>p-\phi_{n}
, then
t>1
, which implies that
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}
is between
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvN}}
and
\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}}
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

The four estimators (
Inv
,
InvP
,
InvN
and
MLE*
) are continuous, i.e. the function
\phi\mapsto\hat{\theta}
is continuous where
\hat{\theta}
is the result under each respective estimator.

Proof.

The estimator
Inv
is a linear transformation of the distribution
\phi
,
and is therefore continuous. By definition,
InvP
can be written as

{\texttt{InvP}}(\phi)=f(\texttt{Inv}(\phi))
, where
f(v)
is the projection of
v\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

onto the set of distributions over
\{1,2,\dots,K\}
. Note that
f
is
a continuous mapping since that set is closed and convex.
Therefore
InvP
is continuous by the continuity of both
Inv
and
f
.

InvN
can be written as
{\texttt{InvN}}(\phi)=f(\texttt{Inv}(\phi))
for the
postprocessing function
f:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
(that transforms a vector
into a valid distribution) via

f(v)_{i}=\frac{\max(0,v_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\max(0,v_{i})}
.
Note that the numerator and the denominator are continuous functions of the vector
v
and the dominator is always positive, so
f
is continuous.
Hence, by the continuity of
f
and
Inv
,
InvN
is continuous.

Lastly we want to prove that
h(\phi):=\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{MLE*}}
is continuous. Using Theorem
5
we can suppose that the entries of
\phi
are increasing. If we recall the notation of
g
in Lemma
3
and partition the domain of
\phi
as
\Delta=\cup_{n=0}^{K-1}A_{n}
where
A_{n}=\{\phi:g(n)\geq 0\ \land g(n-1)<0\}
, then
h
can be expressed as a piecewise continuous function taking value
\theta^{[n]}
in each set
A_{n}
.
For
n
fixed,
h
is continuous in
A_{n}
, therefore, the continuity of the MLE needs only to be tested at the borderline points
\phi
where
g(n)=0
.
It was shown in Lemma
3
(e) that
{\theta^{[n]}}=\theta^{[n+1]}
, so
h
is continuous in
\Delta
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

If
\hat{\theta}:=h(\phi)
is an estimator for
\theta\in\Delta
and
h
is continuous and satisfies
h(\phi^{*})=\theta
, where
\phi^{*}_{i}:=(p{-}q)\theta_{i}+q
, then
\hat{\theta}
is consistent.

Proof.

Suppose
h:\Delta\to\Delta
is continuous and fix
\theta\in\Delta
.
For every
n
, let
X^{(n)}
be a random multiset of
n
values sampled from
{\left\{1..K\right\}}
with distribution
\theta
, let
Y^{(n)}
be the multiset of randomized values after applying RR to each value in
X^{(n)}
, and let
\phi_{n}\in\Delta
be the histogram of
Y^{(n)}
.
This forms a sequence of random variables
\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)},...\in\Delta
.

Y^{(n)}
follows a categorical distribution governed by
\Pr(Y^{(n)}=y\,|\theta)=p\theta_{y}+q(1-\theta_{y})=\phi^{*}_{y}
, which is independent of
n
.
Therefore
\phi^{(n)}
follows a a Multinomial distribution, so the law of large numbers for Multinomial distributions guarantees that
\phi^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{p}}}{{\to}}\phi^{*}
(componentwise convergence in probability).

Using the Continuous Mapping Theorem,
since
h
is continuous, then
h(\phi^{(n)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{p}}}{{\to}}h(\phi^{*})=\theta
.
In addition,
{\|\phi^{(n)}\|}
is bounded because
\phi^{(n)}\in\Delta
, so convergence in probability or in distribution to a constant is equivalent to convergence in L2.
Therefore,
{\|h(\phi)-\theta\|}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{p}}}{{\to}}0
.
∎

Theorem 9
.

The four estimators (
Inv
,
InvP
,
InvN
and
MLE*
) are consistent.
Moreover, for
Inv
and
InvP
, the rate of L2-convergence is
O(K/N)
.

Proof.

The consistency of the four estimators is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
5
and
6
.

For
\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{Inv}}
, from Equation (
8
) and its unbiasedness, we have
\text{MSE}_{\theta}(\hat{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}E((\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{i})
, which yields
\text{MSE}_{\theta}(\hat{\theta})=\frac{Kq(1{-}q)+(p{-}q)(1{-}2q{-}(p{-}q))}{N(p{-}q)}
.

For
\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{InvP}}
, let
\hat{z}=\hat{\theta}^{\texttt{Inv}}
.
By definition, we have
{\|\hat{z}-\hat{\theta}\|}\leq{\|\hat{z}-\theta\|}
.
So
{\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|}\leq{\|\hat{\theta}-\hat{z}\|}+{\|\hat{z}-\theta\|}\leq 2{\|\hat{z}-\theta\|}\in O(K/N)
.
∎
