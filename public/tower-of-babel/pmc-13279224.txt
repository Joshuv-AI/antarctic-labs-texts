Long‐Term Outcomes of Antithrombotic Treatment in a Cohort of 864 Patients With Fontan Circulation
Authors: Ayse‐Gül Öztürk, Mikael Dellborg, Anna Damlin, Kok Wai Giang, Zacharias Mandalenakis, Peder Sorensson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn effective antithrombotic strategy for patients with univentricular heart is required. We determined the prevalence of antithrombotic treatment and survival in patients with Fontan circulation, with secondary outcomes mortality, cardiovascular disease, acute myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke.MethodsWe retrospectively included patients with univentricular heart born in Sweden between January 1, 1970, and December 31, 2017. Data from the National Patient Register, the Cause of Death Register, and the Prescribed Drug Register were used. Patients who underwent total cavopulmonary connection surgery, resulting in Fontan circulation, were included and divided into groups based on treatment: anticoagulation, antiplatelets, and no antithrombotic therapy.ResultsIn total, 864 patients (median [interquartile range] 14.4 [3.9–24.9] years follow‐up) were included, 511 of whom (59%) were men. Thirty‐six percent received no antithrombotic treatment, and the mortality rate during the follow‐up was 58%. Among the patients treated with antithrombotic treatment, 75% received antiplatelets, with a 4% mortality rate. The mortality incidence rate was 2.40 (95% CI, 1.49–3.67) for antithrombotic treatment and 49.42 (95% CI, 42.50–57.15) without. The hazard ratio for mortality was 22.32 (95% CI, 14.04–35.48) without antithrombotic treatment. The incidence rate of ischemic stroke was 2.45 (95% CI, 1.52–3.75) with antithrombotic treatment and 2.47 (95% CI, 1.13–4.68) without. The incidence rate of acute myocardial infarction was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.25–1.50) with antithrombotic treatment and 2.76 (95% CI, 1.32–5.07) without treatment.ConclusionsTreatment with antiplatelets was associated with lower mortality compared with no antithrombotic treatment.

The prognosis for neonates born with congenital heart disease has improved over the past 50 years. 1 Since the introduction of Fontan surgery, the outcomes in this patient group have improved, and patients are now increasingly surviving into adulthood, 2 although various long‐term postoperative complications have been reported. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

Thrombosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD), including cerebrovascular events, are major causes of morbidity and mortality following the Fontan procedure in patients with univentricular heart (UVH). 5 , 7 , 8 These conditions are most prevalent in the first year after Fontan surgery, remain elevated in subsequent years, 9 and result in a considerable proportion of late‐term mortality. 10 Longitudinal studies have indicated that the cumulative incidence of CVD, including cerebrovascular events, in patients operated with Fontan surgery over a decade ranges from 2% to 25% 11 with an incidence rate of 3.5/100 patient‐years in those not receiving thromboprophylaxis. 12 Little is known about the optimal thrombotic prevention strategy following Fontan surgery. International adult congenital heart disease guidelines recommend anticoagulation for patients with Fontan with a history of atrial thrombus, atrial arrhythmias, or thromboembolic events. 13 Center‐based differences in anticoagulation use in patients with UVH are common. However, these recommendations are primarily based on expert opinion.

The primary aims of this study were to evaluate the prevalence of antithrombotic treatment and the survival probability in patients with Fontan circulation. The secondary aims were to determine outcomes in mortality, CVD, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), and ischemic stroke (IS).

METHODS

Data can be accessed from the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare and Statistics Sweden by submitting a request to the respective data providers, contingent upon meeting legal and regulatory requirements and receiving approval from the Swedish Ethical Review Authority.

Data Sources

During the study period between 1970 and 2017, patients with UVH were identified using data from the Swedish NPR (National Patient Register), the Cause of Death Register, and the NPDR (National Prescribed Drug Register). Each individual is assigned a unique personal identity number, which allows the linking of different register data. 14 All Swedish residents have access to publicly funded inpatient and outpatient health care. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

The NPR, which has been in operation since 1964, has had complete coverage since 1987 because all hospitals are required to report to the register. The NPR has been collecting data on outpatient specialist care since 2001, and the outpatient section has high (>80%) coverage of all somatic specialized outpatient visits provided in the public health care system. 15 The NPR relies on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) for diagnoses.

The Cause of Death Register, managed by the National Board of Health and Welfare, has provided detailed information on Swedish citizens' deaths since 1961, including cause, age, sex, place of death, and municipality of residence.

The NPDR is the official source of drug statistics in Sweden, with the pharmaceutical register established in July 2005. This register contains all prescribed drugs dispensed at pharmacies, and contains vital information, such as medication name, strength, pack size, prescription codes, dispensing date, and the patient's personal identification number. 16

Study Population

A total of 5075 patients with potential UVH physiology were identified. Out of these, 864 individuals born in Sweden who completed Fontan circulation were included. Patients with Fontan circulation were identified using ICD diagnosis codes and procedure codes. Patients born outside Sweden were excluded due to incomplete data and because of significant immortal time bias before coming to Sweden. Patients who died before or during the completion of the Fontan circulation were excluded. Additionally, patients who underwent ventricular septum defect closure and consequently achieved a 2‐chambered circulation were also excluded. Only patients who survived until the completion of Fontan circulation (n = 864) and were registered with the procedures related to Fontan surgery were included in the analysis (Figure S1).

Patients' data were sourced from the NPR and the Cause of Death Register to identify individuals with UVH who were born between January 1, 1970, and December 31, 2017.

UVH was defined as any of the following congenital heart defects: hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS), pulmonary atresia with an intact ventricular septum, tricuspid atresia, hypoplastic right heart syndrome, double inlet left ventricle, or double outlet right ventricle (Table S1). Patients with double outlet right ventricle commonly undergo surgery to establish a 2‐chamber system. The patients who were surgically treated with total cavopulmonary connection and ultimately underwent the Fontan procedure were included. A diagnosis was considered valid if a patient had at least 1 registered ICD code diagnosis of a congenital heart defect categorized as UVH during an outpatient visit, hospital discharge, or on a death certificate between 1970 and 2017. The follow‐up time began from birth, and data were collected until death or at the end of the study period on December 31, 2017. All personal identification numbers were anonymized in the final data set.

The study population, consisted of 864 patients, was divided into 3 groups based on their medication: anticoagulation therapy (but no antiplatelets), antiplatelet therapy (but no anticoagulants), and no anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy (no antithrombotic medication). Two additional groups were established: one consisted of individuals who received treatment with antiplatelet and anticoagulation agents simultaneously, and the other comprised those who had switched medications from antiplatelets to an anticoagulation agent or vice versa. The 2 latter groups were not included in the comparisons between anticoagulation therapy, antiplatelet therapy, and no anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy. Ninety individuals (16%) received both forms of antithrombotic medication. These patients were considered to have been treated with antithrombotic treatment, although the type of treatment could not be further described, and they were not included in the comparisons between treatment and no treatment.

The definition of medication treatment is at least 3 prescriptions of medications, corresponding to a minimum treatment duration of 9 months. The specific drugs included in each group are shown in Table S2. The medication registry data are available from 2005, and sensitivity analyses was conducted starting from 2006, with a follow‐up period of 11 years. The follow‐up period for treatment with medication is on average 6.4 years. Cardiovascular events, namely AMI, IS, and cerebral hemorrhagic events, such as hemorrhagic stroke and subarachnoid hemorrhage, were recorded. Hemorrhage was defined as epidural, subdural, or subarachnoid hemorrhage, hypovolemia, anemia, or transfusions.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic data are presented as descriptive statistics to show long‐term mortality in patients with UVH. In descriptive statistics, continuous variables are shown as the mean±SD and categorical variables as the number and percentage. We conducted 3 separate sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded all individuals who died before 2006, resulting in a population of 735 patients. The second analysis included only individuals born from 2006 to 2017 (289 patients). Finally, all deaths occurring before the age of 2 were excluded. The incidence rate is reported per 1000 person‐years. CIs for the incidence rate were estimated with Poisson regression. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the survival probability with a 95% CI. The stratified Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. The Cox proportional hazards model was adjusted for variables including gender, birth year, and age at the time of the first intervention. Statistical analyses were performed with R software version 4.3.2 for data management. 17

Ethics

The Gothenburg Regional Research Ethics Board approved the study, which was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki (Gbg 912‐16, T619‐18). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology initiative recommendations.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Cardiovascular Morbidity

Patients on antithrombotic medication were associated with lower incidence of CVD (eg, AMI) than those without antithrombotic medication (Table 2). The incidence rate for AMI was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.25–1.50) in those with antithrombotic treatment and 2.76 (95% CI, 1.32–5.07) in those without antithrombotic treatment.

The lowest risk of developing CVD was observed in patients who received antithrombotic treatment (Table 3). In patients with non‐HLHS‐UVH, the HR for CVD was 1.88 (95% CI, 0.99–3.56) for patients with no antithrombotic treatment. In patients with HLHS, the corresponding HR was 4.94 (95% CI, 1.91–12.76).

The cumulative incidence of CVD in the different anticoagulation and antiplatelet medication groups is shown in Figure S4. There was no association between the different treatment options and outcomes. Patients on antiplatelets seemed to have a lower cumulative incidence of IS than those without antithrombotic medication (Figure S5). Furthermore, patients on antiplatelets were associated with lower incidence of AMI than those on anticoagulants or those without antithrombotic treatment (Figure S6).

Sensitivity Analyses

A sensitivity analysis was conducted on patients without antithrombotic treatment for the entire population, those who were excluded because they died before 2006, and those who were born in 2006 or later (Table S5). An increased risk of mortality was observed in all groups. Additionally, a higher incidence rate of events was observed in the absence of antithrombotic treatment in the cohort excluding patients with UVH who died before 2006 (Table S6).

A sensitivity analysis of patients who were born in 2006 or later showed that approximately three quarters were treated with antiplatelets, 5 (2%) received anticoagulants, and 44 (21%) antiplatelet and anticoagulant (Table S7). Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis of the incidence rate of events in patients with UVH who were born in 2006 or later were associated with higher incidence rate of mortality in those without antithrombotic treatment, incidence rate 187.17 (95% CI, 131.79–257.99) than in those with antithrombotic treatment, incidence rate 3.16 (95% CI, 1.03–7.38) (Table S8). The risk of CVD and IS was also associated with higher in patients without antithrombotic medication than in those with antithrombotic medication.

A sensitivity analysis of mortality and cardiovascular events after including all patients with and without antithrombotic medication who died before 2006 among non‐HLHS‐UVH and HLHS was performed (Table S9). The results are based on analysis assuming that all patients who died before 2006, when the NPDR was established, had received treatment. The risk of mortality is nearly 7 times higher for patients with HLHS compared with those with non‐HLHS‐UVH.

Patients undergoing anticoagulant therapy demonstrated significantly higher rates of adverse outcomes compared with those receiving antiplatelet therapy (Table S10). Furthermore, the prevalence of complications including mechanical valve replacement, CVD, AMI, and IS was substantially greater in the anticoagulant group.

The majority of fatalities occurred during infancy. In Tables S11 and S12, all deaths occurring before the age of 2 were excluded. The analysis was associated with increased risk of mortality and no significant difference in morbidity between the cohort that received antithrombotic medication and those who did not receive any treatment (Figure S7).

DISCUSSION

By incorporating a nationwide cohort, robust data validation, and long‐term follow‐up, this study provides an assessment of the effects of antithrombotic treatments on patients with UVH after Fontan surgery. In addition, our study provides insights into the effect of antithrombotic treatment on mortality, CVD, AMI, and IS in the long term.

Antithrombotic Therapy in UVH

Patients on antiplatelets were associated with lower mortality and cardiovascular complications after Fontan surgery than those on anticoagulants or without antithrombotic therapy. A cohort study of 210 patients born before 1985 who underwent Fontan surgery at Boston Children's Hospital compared the efficacy of aspirin (24.3% of patients) and warfarin (25.7% of patients) with no treatment in relation to thromboembolic events. 18 Consistent with our results, the lack of aspirin or warfarin prophylaxis was significantly associated with a higher thromboembolic event rate than that with aspirin or warfarin (HR, 8.5 [95% CI, 3.6–19.9], P<0.001). However, no significant difference was observed between the efficacy of aspirin and warfarin. An international, multicenter, randomized controlled trial conducted on 111 patients with UVH compared the effectiveness of acetylsalicylic acid and warfarin after the Fontan procedure. 19 However, this trial showed no significant difference in thrombotic complications, suggesting that these treatments were equally effective in thromboprophylaxis after the Fontan procedure. In our study, the observations of AMI and IS are characterized by wide CIs, reflecting considerable uncertainty stemming from the small number of events. Additionally, the differences observed in hazard ratios are modest, likely due to the limited data available.

The timing of surgery in the UVH cohort likely varied from 1970 to 2017 due to advances in neonatal care, surgical methods, and treatment strategies over time. 2 Significant advancements have occurred, potentially impacting optimal surgical timing and patient outcomes. These evolving practices could have influenced patient survival, with different implications depending on the era of treatment, thus may have impacted survival trends throughout the study.

In the cohort receiving anticoagulants, 48 patients out of 864, the survival rate was not as favorable as expected compared with the cohort receiving antiplatelet therapy. Patients receiving anticoagulant therapy exhibit significantly higher rates of adverse outcomes compared with those on antiplatelet therapy (Table S10). Complications such as mechanical valve replacement, CVD, AMI, and IS are also markedly more prevalent within the anticoagulant group. These differences may reflect greater disease severity or underlying comorbidities that necessitated anticoagulant use.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of our study is the use of the NPR and the Cause of Death Register in Sweden. The NPR, which is known for its extensive validation and nearly complete coverage of all hospital care related to congenital heart disease, serves as a robust foundation for our research. The inclusive Swedish health care system, which provides universal, no‐cost access to health care, greatly increases the comprehensiveness of our study. Additionally, mandatory participation in registries for health care providers, as dictated by law, ensures negligible loss to follow‐up.

This study is observational in nature, which may limit the ability to establish causal relationships. Additionally, there is a potential for unmeasured confounding factors that could influence the observed outcomes, notwithstanding the adjustments made in the analysis. The study has some limitations inherent in registry‐based research. The potential for incorrect coding and classification of congenital heart defects in any study reliant on ICD codes is a concern. The absence of access to clinical data limited our ability to validate diagnoses directly through hospital records. However, the specialized nature of Swedish tertiary care adult congenital heart disease centers, where patients with UVH are predominantly diagnosed, treated, and followed up, minimizes the risk of misclassification. The evolution of ICD classification from its eighth to tenth versions introduces an additional layer of complexity, potentially affecting the accuracy of diagnosing UVH. Nonetheless, the validation rate for diagnoses within the Inpatient Register is notably high (85%–95%). 20 , 21

Our definition of UVH excluded certain uncommon congenital heart defects potentially classifiable as UVH, such as severe cases of unbalanced atrioventricular septal defects. Fontan fenestration and different types of Fontan circulation are potential confounders that can affect medical management and clinical outcomes. These factors were not captured in the available data. Another limitation is the lack of indications or patient‐specific dosage for antithrombotic therapy.

A substantial proportion of mortality occurred during infancy or early childhood. As a result, many of these patients may be inherently categorized as “untreated,” given that their deaths often precede the initiation of any therapeutic intervention. Considering the potential influence of survivor bias, the observed lower mortality rate within the “treated” cohort may be attributable to the fact that these individuals have survived sufficiently long to receive treatment. Sensitivity analyses, which exclude very early mortalities, demonstrate that the observed differences in outcomes persist, indicating that the treatment effect extends beyond the neonatal and early childhood periods. However, this approach may not have captured the full spectrum of the original cohort's outcomes. The second sensitivity analysis, which included only individuals born between 2006 and 2017, allowed for a broader perspective on current treatment patterns.

Finally, the Outpatient Register's initiation in 2000 created a historical data void, precluding the identification of patients treated on an outpatient basis before this date. Therefore, data from outpatient clinics and primary care settings remain unaccounted for in our study. The NPDR's establishment on July 1, 2005 and the subsequent inclusion of patients in our study beginning on January 1, 2006 further limit the temporal scope of our follow‐up. Despite these limitations, the specialized follow‐up care provided by congenital heart units—a facet likely to ensure registration in the Swedish NPR—indicates the careful monitoring of children and adults with UVH, including the tracking of mortality through the Cause of Death Register.

CONCLUSIONS

Patients treated with antiplatelets or anticoagulants had an overall higher survival rate than those with no antithrombotic treatment, and patients on antiplatelets had the lowest mortality. This finding applies to patients with UVH, especially those with HLHS. The risk of mortality was 22 times higher without any therapy. Furthermore, antiplatelet therapy seemed to be associated with a reduction in the occurrence of CVD. These findings indicate that patients with UVH derive benefits from receiving treatment with antithrombotic drugs, especially antiplatelet agents, suggesting a need for more distinct guidelines.
