Investigating phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, and pomological traits in highbush blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.) cultivars across ripening stages
Authors: Ömer Faruk Bilgin, Azam Akbari, Doğan Ergün, Salih Kafkas, Nesibe Ebru Kafkas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically investigated phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, and pomological traits in five highbush blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.) cultivars—“Cargo”, “Ozarkblue”, “Blue Ribbon”, “Volar”, and “Topshelf” across three distinct ripening stages (green, pink, and fully ripe). Significant cultivar-dependent variations were observed throughout fruit development. Total phenolic content declined progressively during ripening in all cultivars, with “Cargo” exhibiting the highest values at early stages. Phenolic composition shifted markedly with maturation: chlorogenic acid dominated unripe fruit (particularly in “Topshelf”) and declined sharply toward ripeness, while ellagic acid and flavonoids (myricetin, quercetin, catechin) showed more stable or increasing trends. This transition reflects the biochemical shift from defense-related phenolics to color-associated compounds during fruit development. Antioxidant activity, measured by DPPH and FRAP assays, was highest in unripe fruit, with “Ozarkblue” demonstrating peak capacity. Conversely, total anthocyanin content increased dramatically during ripening, reaching maximum levels at full ripeness, with “Cargo” accumulating the highest concentrations. Pomological traits also varied considerably: “Ozarkblue” produced the largest berries (4.75 g), while “Volar” and “Topshelf” maintained higher acidity, and “Cargo” achieved superior sweetness (12.5 °Brix). Multivariate analysis confirmed that ripening stage was the primary driver of biochemical variation, while genotype determined the magnitude of these changes. These findings provide practical guidance for optimized harvest timing: early-harvested “Ozarkblue” for antioxidant-rich functional products, and fully ripe “Cargo” for anthocyanin-rich fresh market applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09037-0.

Introduction

Blueberries (Vaccinium corymbosum L.), members of the Ericaceae family, are widely recognized for their vibrant blue pigmentation and pleasant flavor, largely attributed to their high anthocyanin content [1, 2]. Although originally native to North America, their cultivation has expanded globally due to increasing consumer awareness of their numerous health benefits [3, 4]. These health-promoting effects stem primarily from blueberries’ rich content of phenolic compounds, including phenolic acids, tannins, stilbenes, lignans, and a wide array of flavonoids such as anthocyanins, flavonols, and flavan-3-ols [5]. Notably, anthocyanins alone can account for up to 70% of the total phenolic content, highlighting their major contribution to bioactivity [5, 6]. These compounds have been extensively studied for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, neuroprotective, and anticancer properties [7–9]. Advanced analytical techniques, such as HPLC-DAD-ESI-MSⁿ, have identified various glycosylated anthocyanins—mainly malvidin, delphinidin, petunidin, cyanidin, and peonidin derivatives—that are responsible for the deep blue-purple color and antioxidant potency of ripe fruit [10, 11]. Additionally, blueberries contain other health-promoting phenolic compounds such as proanthocyanidins and phenolic acids like chlorogenic, p-coumaric, and ferulic acids [6, 12]. The phenolic composition of blueberries is highly dynamic and varies considerably throughout fruit development and ripening. In early ripening stages, phenolic acids and flavan-3-ols (e.g., catechin and epicatechin) are more abundant, whereas anthocyanin levels sharply increase as the fruit matures, reflecting a physiological transition towards pigmentation and oxidative protection [13–15]. Flavonols such as quercetin and myricetin may remain relatively stable or show moderate declines [16]. These ripening-related changes influence both the nutraceutical quality and sensory characteristics of the berries. Furthermore, anthocyanin accumulation can continue even after harvest, indicating that postharvest conditions also impact the fruit’s antioxidant properties [17, 18]. Simultaneously, ripening affects key pomological traits such as fruit size, skin color, texture, sugar content, acidity, firmness, and yield—critical parameters for both market value and consumer acceptance [19–21]. Several studies suggest that total phenolic content and antioxidant activity often increase during ripening, although peak levels may vary among cultivars and occur at intermediate stages [14, 15]. Beyond environmental influences, considerable genotypic variation exists among highbush blueberry cultivars regarding phenolic composition, anthocyanin levels, and antioxidant activity [22–25]. These traits exhibit moderate to high heritability, making them promising targets for breeding programs. Moreover, phenolic compounds—especially anthocyanins—have been associated with reduced risk of chronic diseases such as obesity, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes [26, 27].

Despite the considerable body of research on blueberry health benefits, several critical knowledge gaps remain. First, while total phenolic and anthocyanin contents have been extensively studied, limited attention has been given to profiling individual phenolic compounds and tracking their dynamic changes across distinct ripening stages. Second, although cultivar effects on phenolic composition are well-documented, the interactive effects of genotype and developmental stage have rarely been examined under standardized field conditions using comprehensive analytical approaches. Third, the integration of detailed phenolic profiling with key pomological traits (size, color, acidity, sweetness) has been insufficiently explored, limiting our understanding of how biochemical and quality attributes co-develop during fruit maturation.

The present study addresses these gaps by systematically analyzing individual phenolic compounds (via HPLC), total anthocyanin content, antioxidant activity, and key pomological attributes in five highbush blueberry cultivars—“Cargo”, “Ozarkblue”, “Blue Ribbon”, “Topshelf”, and “Volar”—across three distinct ripening stages. The findings aim to provide deeper insights into cultivar-specific phenolic transitions and quality development, supporting optimized harvest timing, postharvest handling, and targeted breeding strategies. Ultimately, such data are essential for maximizing both the health benefits and commercial potential of blueberries.

Materials and methods

Plant Material

Freshly harvested fruits of five different highbush blueberry cultivars (“Cargo,” “Ozarkblue,” “Blue Ribbon,” “Volar,” and “Topshelf”) were collected from a blueberry farm located in Chayuan Village (28.0867° E, 38.4866° N), Manisa, Ege Province, Türkiye. Plant material was obtained from Safir Bey Farm (Salihli, Manisa, Turkey). Formal identification of all cultivars was performed by Ibrahim Yavuklu, an agricultural engineer with six years of specialized experience in blueberry cultivation at Safir Bey Farm. All samples were cultivated specimens; no wild plant material was collected, and no special permissions were required.

Experimental design

The field experiment was arranged as a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replicates per cultivar, each replicate consisting of five plants. All laboratory biochemical analyses were conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD). The harvest took place in June 2024 at three different ripening stages (green, pink, and ripe), as indicated in Fig. 1.

Visual representation of the three ripening stages—green, pink, and ripe—of the ‘Cargo’ highbush blueberry cultivar

Each biological replicate consisted of pooled fruit samples from seven plants (10 fruits per plant, total 70 fruits per replicate). Three independent biological replicates were prepared for all analyses. For spectrophotometric assays (total phenolic content, total anthocyanin content, DPPH radical scavenging activity, and FRAP), each biological replicate was analyzed in technical triplicate. Therefore, values for these assays represent the mean of nine measurements (3 biological replicates × 3 technical replicates) ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For HPLC phenolic profiling, each biological replicate was analyzed once due to the high cost and time requirements of the analysis.

The collected fruit samples were labeled and placed into cool storage containers with ice boxes before being transported to the Instrumental Analysis Laboratory of Cukurova University, Faculty of Agriculture, Department of Horticulture for pomological and biochemical investigations.

Chemicals

The experiment utilized various chemicals, including Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, sodium carbonate, acetonitrile, potassium peroxide sulfate, ethanol, methanol, calcium chloride, gallic acid, myricetin, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, ellagic acid, quercetin, kaempferol, cyanidin 3-glucoside, sodium nitrite, aluminum chloride, sodium hydroxide, catechin, Trolox, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), potassium chloride, sodium acetate, and cyanidin-3-glucoside.

All chemicals and standards (e.g., gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, catechin, DPPH, Folin–Ciocalteu reagent) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and all solvents were of HPLC grade (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

Morphological characteristics

The analyses were conducted in nine replicates to evaluate key pomological traits. Fruit width (mm) and height (mm) were measured using a digital caliper with a precision of 0.01 mm. Fruit weight (g) was determined using a precision scale with an accuracy of 0.001 g.

Fruit skin color measurement

The color of the fruit skin was measured with a chromameter and represented in the CIELAB color space using the coordinates: L*, a*, b* [28, 29]. For each replicate, color was measured at 30 fruits at three randomly selected locations around the mid-region of the fruit, between the calyx and stem. The L* value indicated the lightness of the color, on a scale from 0 to 100, with 0 as dark and 100 as bright. If a* was negative, the color was blue-green; if positive, red-purple. For b*, if negative, the color was blue; if positive, yellow. Additionally, hue angle and chroma were calculated as h°= tan-1(b*/a*) and C* = (a² + b²)½, respectively [30]. Hue direction: h°= 0° was red-purple; 90° was yellow; 180° was blue-green; and 270° was blue [30]. Chroma (C*) measured the closeness of the color to gray and the intensity or saturation of the color [31].

TSS and acidity measurement

Total soluble solids (TSS) were measured using juice from nine randomly selected fruits per cultivar with a digital refractometer (PAL-BX|ACID4, Atago, Tokyo, Japan), and the results were expressed in °Brix. Titratable acidity (TA) was determined by titration of the same juice (n = 10) with 0.1 N NaOH until pH 8.1 was reached. Results were expressed as a percentage of citric acid equivalent [32].

Determination of antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The free radical scavenging activity of the fruit extracts was determined using the DPPH assay according to the method described by [33] with slight modifications. Briefly, 0.1 mL of the extract was mixed with 2.4 mL of DPPH solution (9 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L in methanol) and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 518 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Results were expressed as micromoles of Trolox equivalents per 100 g of fresh weight (µmol TE/100 g FW).

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) of the extracts was measured following [34]. The FRAP reagent was prepared by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl₃·6 H₂O in a 10:1:1 ratio. In this assay, 100 µL of the extract was added to 3 mL of FRAP reagent and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Absorbance was recorded at 593 nm. The antioxidant capacity was calculated using a Trolox standard curve and expressed as µmol Trolox equivalents per 100 g of fresh weight (µmol TE/100 g FW).

Preparation of blueberry extracts

Phenolic compounds were extracted using a solvent system optimized for blueberry anthocyanins: methanol: water: trifluoroacetic acid (70:29.9:0.1, v/v/v) as described by [35]. Trifluoroacetic acid was used as an ion-pairing agent to improve chromatographic resolution of anthocyanin peaks, and was preferred over formic acid for its superior peak sharpening properties in methanol-based extraction systems. Briefly, 2 mL of the homogenized blueberry sample was mixed with 3 mL of 70% methanol containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, sonicated for 20 min, and centrifuged at 5,500 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and used for HPLC analysis.

Determination of total phenolics

The total phenolic content of the extracts was determined according to the Folin–Ciocalteu (FC) method [36]. The protocol summarizes that 0.1 mL of extract was mixed with 0.1 mL of FC reagent and 0.9 mL of water for 5 min, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 7% (w/v) Na₂CO₃ and 0.4 mL of water for another 30-minute incubation. At the last step, absorbance was measured at 765 nm. Blank solution (water and reagents) was used for calibration. Total phenolic content was reported in milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per 100 g of fresh fruit as measured with a standard calibration curve for gallic acid. All analyses were conducted in triplicate.

Determination of monomeric anthocyanins

Total monomeric anthocyanin content was determined using the pH-differential method [37]. For the analyses, 1 mL of the extract sample was split between two volumetric flasks. One flask was filled with a KCl solution at pH 1 and the second flask was filled with a CH₃COONa solution at pH 4.5. The pH-differential solutions were left at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 520 nm and 700 nm for both solutions. The results were reported as mg of cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3GE) per 100 g of fresh sample. Each analysis was performed in triplicate.

Analysis of the phenolic compounds using HPLC

Phenolic compounds were analyzed by HPLC based on [38], with modifications to column, acid modifier, flow rate, and detection wavelengths. Separation was performed on an Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) at 30 °C using solvent A (2.5% formic acid in water) and solvent B (2.5% formic acid in acetonitrile) at 1.0 mL/min. The gradient program was: 0–3 min, 10% B; 3–6 min, 10–30% B; 6–8 min, 30% B; 8–12 min, 30–50% B; 12–17 min, 50% B; 17–25 min, 50–90% B; 25–35 min, 90–10% B; 35–40 min, 10% B (re-equilibration). Detection was performed at 260 nm (gallic, syringic, and ellagic acids), 320 nm (chlorogenic, caffeic, p-coumaric, and ferulic acids), 360 nm (myricetin, quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin, naringenin, and catechin), and 520 nm (cyanidin-3-glucoside and pelargonidin-3-glucoside). Samples were filtered (0.45 μm) and injected (20 µL). Compounds were identified by retention time and UV spectra comparison with authentic standards. Calibration curves for each compound were constructed using five concentrations prepared from 1 mg/mL stock solutions in methanol (or methanol: DMSO 9:1 for ellagic acid due to its limited methanol solubility). All stock solutions were sonicated for 15 min and visually inspected for clarity before dilution.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Minitab 22 and R software (version 4.4.2). Two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test (p ≤ 0.05) was used for mean comparisons (Minitab 22). Pearson correlation analysis was conducted using the corrplot package (R) to assess relationships among traits.

Fresh weight showed the most dramatic developmental shift. ‘Ozarkblue’ reached 4.75 ± 0.13 g at ripeness, nearly a 380% increase from its initial weight, underscoring its superior biomass accumulation. This trend aligns with [41], who linked fruit weight gain to changes in carbon and nitrogen dynamics during blueberry development.

In contrast, fruit height increased more moderately. ‘Ozarkblue’ and ‘Topshelf’ showed the highest final heights (15.67 ± 0.46 mm and 15.08 ± 0.64 mm, respectively), supporting previous findings that vertical growth patterns contribute to cultivar-specific fruit quality traits [42].

Overall, these morphological changes illustrate the strong influence of genotype on fruit growth trajectories, likely driven by differences in cell division and expansion regulation—factors critical to both consumer appeal and commercial value.

Acidity and soluble solids

Ripening significantly influenced titratable acidity (TA) and total soluble solids (TSS) across cultivars (Table 1). TA declined during ripening in all genotypes, reflecting expected patterns of organic acid metabolism [43]. However, the rate and extent of decline were cultivar-dependent. ‘Volar’ exhibited the highest initial acidity (0.45 ± 0.01%) and retained relatively high levels at full ripeness (0.21 ± 0.01%), suggesting distinct regulation of acid degradation. These trends align with [44], who reported genotype-specific variations in organic acid decline, particularly in citric acid.

In contrast, TSS values generally increased with ripening, consistent with sugar accumulation during fruit maturation [45]. ‘Cargo’ showed the highest TSS at the ripe stage (12.50 °Brix), followed by ‘Blue Ribbon’ (11.00 °Brix) and ‘Ozarkblue’ (10.50 °Brix). Interestingly, ‘Topshelf’ displayed an atypical trend with an initial drop in TSS at the pink stage before recovering at full ripeness, possibly due to temporary metabolic shifts or environmental influences. These inter-cultivar differences suggest genetic variation in carbohydrate metabolism and sugar storage capacity, which are critical for breeding programs aimed at improving fruit quality. Moreover, the correlation between sugar accumulation and morphological development during early maturation offers practical insights for determining optimal harvest timing.

Fruit skin color measurement

Changes in color parameters—including lightness (L*), chroma (C), hue angle (h°), and the a* and b* coordinates—effectively tracked ripening progression in blueberry cultivars (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 1, the fruit transitioned from green to pink to dark blue as ripening advanced. Lightness (L*) declined consistently across all cultivars, reflecting chlorophyll breakdown and anthocyanin accumulation. ‘Volar’ exhibited the most pronounced reduction, from 47.68 ± 0.41 to 31.85 ± 1.63, aligning with findings by [46].

The a* values increased during ripening, indicating a shift from greenish to reddish tones, while b* values became more negative—particularly in ‘Ozarkblue’—indicating enhanced blue pigmentation. This trend corresponds with flavonoid accumulation, as described by [47], and the rise in delphinidin-based anthocyanins during ripening [15].

Chroma (C*), representing color intensity, peaked in ‘Ozarkblue’ during the pink stage, whereas hue angle (h°) declined in all cultivars, with ‘Topshelf’ showing the sharpest decrease (from 277.37° to 199.99°). These color changes mirror the progression of pigment biosynthesis during ripening [48].

Overall, skin color parameters not only distinguished ripening stages but also reflected underlying biochemical shifts. As noted by Routray and Orsat (2011), such non-destructive traits offer valuable indicators of phytochemical content, aiding in optimal harvest decisions and cultivar evaluation.

Bioactive compounds

Antioxidant activity during ripening

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity, expressed as µmol Trolox equivalents per 100 g fresh weight (µmol TE/100 g FW), exhibited a cultivar-dependent trend across ripening stages (Table 3, Fig. 2). In most cultivars, antioxidant capacity peaked at the green stage and declined progressively during ripening. Notably, ‘Ozarkblue’ displayed the highest DPPH activity at the green stage (1619.01 ± 7.15 µmol TE/100 g FW), followed by ‘Cargo’ (1364.01 ± 24.13 µmol TE/100 g FW) and ‘Blue Ribbon’ (938.71 ± 39.40 µmol TE/100 g FW). Although ‘Cargo’ maintained a high value at the ripe stage (1350.54 ± 42.41 µmol TE/100 g FW), a sharp decrease was observed at the pink stage (478.52 ± 35.26 µmol TE/100 g FW). In contrast, cultivars like ‘Topshelf’ and ‘Volar’ exhibited significantly lower antioxidant capacity at the pink stage (219.71 ± 6.80 µmol TE/100 g FW and 145.09 ± 1.77 µmol TE/100 g FW, respectively), with only modest recovery at full ripeness. These patterns align with findings by [13], who reported that phenolic transformations during ripening can alter antioxidant potential. The observed differences among cultivars support the hypothesis that antioxidant capacity is influenced by both ripening stage and genetic background [22]. Such genotype-dependent variation in phenolic content and antioxidant activity has also been documented across diverse edible fruit species [49], in strawberry cultivars exhibiting differential antioxidant responses [50], and in raspberry cultivars showing cultivar-specific phenolic accumulation [51].

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

FRAP values showed dramatic decline during ripening across most cultivars (Table 3). Green fruits exhibited the highest antioxidant capacity, with “Ozarkblue” reaching 2265.37 ± 16.09 µmol TE/100 g FW, while ripe fruits dropped to ~ 1000 µmol TE/100 g FW. This observed reduction in antioxidant capacity during ripening may be attributed to a shift in phenolic composition, as immature fruits are rich in phenolic acids and flavonoids, while ripening leads to an accumulation of anthocyanins [52]. “Blue Ribbon” peaked at the pink stage (2079.47 ± 22.65 µmol TE/100 g FW.) rather than green, indicating genetic variation in antioxidant timing. This pattern aligns with research showing that phenolic profiles and antioxidant dynamics vary significantly among genotypes during ripening [3, 53]. The cultivar-specific variation in anthocyanin accumulation observed here agrees with earlier findings on the genetic control of anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways [54]. The dramatic cultivar-dependent increase in anthocyanin content during ripening, particularly the substantial accumulation in ‘Cargo’, reflects coordinated transcriptional activation of the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway. This pathway involves sequential enzymatic steps catalyzed by chalcone synthase (CHS), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), and UDP-glucose: flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT) [55]. In blueberry, this pathway is controlled by MYB-type transcription factors, particularly VcMYBA, which activates anthocyanin biosynthetic genes in ripening fruit [56]. Allelic variation in regulatory genes likely underlies the striking cultivar differences observed, with anthocyanin content being a moderately heritable trait suitable for breeding selection [57]. These findings have practical implications for determining optimal harvest timing based on intended use. For example, cultivars such as “Cargo” and “Ozarkblue” may offer higher antioxidant activity at earlier stages, whereas fully ripe fruit provides maximal anthocyanin content. This trade-off underscores the importance of developmental stage selection for targeted functional or fresh-market applications, in line with the recommendations by [58].

Phenolic profile during ripening

The examination of total phenolic content (TPC) indicated substantial variation among cultivars throughout the maturation process (Table 3, Fig. 4). “Cargo” exhibited an exceptionally high TPC at the green stage (897.11 ± 5.90 mg GAE/100 g FW), which significantly dropped at the pink stage (346.44 ± 0.52), followed by a moderate increase at the ripe stage (488.13 ± 1.51). In contrast, “Topshelf” demonstrated a continuous decline in TPC from 514.11 ± 4.36 to 106.15 ± 2.25 mg GAE/100 g FW across ripening stages. These cultivar-specific trends may reflect a dynamic balance between phenolic biosynthesis and degradation processes during fruit development, as supported by [59]. Parallel HPLC-DAD analysis of individual phenolic compounds revealed distinct shifts in phenolic acid, flavonoid, and anthocyanin profiles across ripening stages and cultivars (Table 4, Fig. 5). Chlorogenic acid emerged as the most abundant phenolic acid. Notably, “Topshelf” showed a remarkable increase from 30.62 mg/100 g FW (green) to 176.59 mg/100 g FW (ripe), aligning with [13], who observed similar accumulation patterns during ripening. The exceptionally high chlorogenic acid accumulation observed in ‘Topshelf’ at the green stage (176.59 mg/100 g FW) suggests a cultivar-specific upregulation of phenylpropanoid pathway enzymes, particularly phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA: quinate hydroxycinnamoyltransferase (HQT). Genetic variation in HQT expression has previously been identified as a key factor driving differences in chlorogenic acid levels among plant species [60]. This preferential accumulation in unripe fruit likely serves critical physiological functions, including photoprotection, antioxidant defense, and deterrence against herbivores [61]. The subsequent decline observed during ripening may reflect enzymatic degradation or metabolic redirection, as developmental priorities shift from chemical defense to pigmentation. Other cultivars showed more moderate or stable chlorogenic acid levels, indicating possible differences in regulation of phenolic metabolism. Among anthocyanins, cyanidin-3-glucoside displayed distinct accumulation patterns. In line with the total anthocyanin results, HPLC analysis confirmed that individual anthocyanins (cyanidin-3-glucoside and pelargonidin-3-glucoside) were not detected (nd) or were present only in trace amounts at the green stage (Table 4). In most cultivars, their concentrations increased significantly during ripening. However, a notable exception was observed for cyanidin-3-glucoside in ‘Ozarkblue’ (4.34 to 1.25 mg/100 g FW) and ‘Blue Ribbon’ (3.64 to 1.58 mg/100 g FW), where C3G levels decreased from the pink to the fully ripe stage despite concurrent increases in total anthocyanin content. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by a shift in anthocyanin biosynthetic flux during the final ripening stage. As fruit matures, the metabolic pathway increasingly channels dihydroflavonol precursors through flavonoid 3’,5’-hydroxylase (F3’5’H) toward tri-hydroxylated anthocyanins (delphinidin, petunidin, and malvidin glycosides), rather than through flavonoid 3’-hydroxylase (F3’H) toward cyanidin [55]. Additionally, existing C3G may be directly methylated to peonidin-3-glucoside by anthocyanin O-methyltransferase (AOMT) [53]. These derivative anthocyanins are detected by the pH-differential method as total monomeric anthocyanins but were not individually quantified in the current HPLC analysis, which targeted only C3G and pelargonidin-3-glucoside. The cultivar-specific nature of this pattern — pronounced in ‘Ozarkblue’ and ‘Blue Ribbon’ but not in ‘Cargo’ — likely reflects differential expression of F3’5’H and AOMT genes among genotypes, consistent with the genetic regulation of anthocyanin decoration reported by [56]. Interestingly, while total anthocyanin content increased with ripening in all cultivars, individual C3G levels decreased from the pink to ripe stage in ‘Ozarkblue’ and ‘Blue Ribbon’. This may be attributed to the enzymatic conversion of cyanidin-3-glucoside to methylated (peonidin) and hydroxylated (delphinidin, malvidin) anthocyanin derivatives during final ripening, which are detected by the pH-differential method but were not individually quantified in the current HPLC analysis.

Multivariate analysis

Correlation analysis

The findings of the correlation analysis indicated complex relationships among biochemical traits during the ripening of blueberry fruit (Fig. 6). Total anthocyanin content (TAC) showed strong positive correlations with several phenolic compounds, most notably with ellagic acid (r = 0.58, p < 0.01) and TSS (r = 0.70, p < 0.01). This finding supports the work of [13], which reported concurrent increases in anthocyanins alongside phenolic acids during blueberry ripening. Physical parameters (width, height, and weight) demonstrated exceptionally strong intercorrelations (r = 0.89–0.97, p < 0.01), confirming the coordinated nature of fruit dimensional development. These physical dimensions also showed strong positive correlations with naringenin (r = 0.65–0.75, p < 0.01), suggesting coordination between fruit size and flavonoid accumulation during maturation, as documented by [15] in other Vaccinium species.

Moderate to strong positive correlations were observed among flavonoids, with chlorogenic acid showing notable relationships with catechin (r = 0.69, p < 0.01) and kaempferol (r = − 0.30, p < 0.01), indicating possible shared biosynthetic control mechanisms. This suggests that different classes of phenolic compounds contribute differently to total phenolic content at various ripening stages. Such complexity in relationships is expected, given the dynamic nature of phenolic metabolite changes during fruit development [68].

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed clear trends in the biochemical and morphological characteristics of blueberry cultivars across ripening stages. The first two principal components explained 55.8% of the total variation, with PC1 accounting for 39.9% and PC2 for 15.9% of the variance (Fig. 7). This level of explained variance typically reflects the interrelated nature of the traits measured, which is common for fruit quality attributes. The biplot indicated clear separation across ripening stages along PC1 (Fig. 7), suggesting that maturity stage was the primary factor differentiating fruit traits. Fruits from the green stage were distinctly separated from those at the pink and ripe stages, which were associated with higher titratable acidity (TA) and antioxidant capacity measures (FRAP, TPC). This separation corresponds with the underlying biochemical changes during fruit ripening, characterized by the metabolism of organic acids and the accumulation of sugars [43].

Principal component analysis (PCA) based on the phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, and pomological traits of five highbush blueberry cultivars at different ripening stages (green, pink, and ripe)

The phenolic compounds showed contrasting patterns of association with ripening stages. Chlorogenic acid and catechin were more closely associated with the green stage, consistent with studies reporting reductions in these compounds during ripening [69]. Anthocyanins, including cyanidin-3-glucoside, along with syringic acid and myricetin, showed stronger associations with pink and ripe fruits, as expected, since these compounds accumulate as fruit ripens [13]. Morphological traits (width, height, and weight) displayed positive associations with ripening progression, clustering closer to intermediate ripening stages in the biplot, while also showing association with naringenin and TAC. This synchronization of morphological and biochemical traits as berry fruit matures reflects relationships reported in previous blueberry research [42].

Antioxidant capacity measures (DPPH, FRAP) and total phenolic content (TPC) exhibited distinct positioning in the biplot, with FRAP and TPC clustering together and showing stronger association with the green stage, while DPPH was positioned closer to intermediate ripening stages. Their placement suggests that different phenolic compounds may be primarily responsible for antioxidant activity at specific ripening stages, supporting previous conclusions about the dynamic nature of antioxidant capacity during fruit development [70].

The PCA results present a clear separation of ripening stages, highlighting the considerable impact of harvest timing on the phytochemical profile of blueberries. This finding is especially important for decisions about harvest timing, depending on the desired quality attributes for fresh consumption or processing. Furthermore, the strong association of specific phenolic compounds with particular ripening stages is also valuable for breeding programs aiming to enhance specific nutritional or functional properties.

Hierarchical cluster analysis

The hierarchical clustering analysis based on phytochemical and physical characteristics across the five blueberry cultivars established unique class patterns of compound accumulation and fruit development during the ripening process. The corresponding heatmap displays distinct clustering patterns that provide insights into the natural dynamics of fruit development and highlight the array of bioactive compounds that evolve during ripening (Fig. 8). Hierarchical clustering identified groupings for several different phenolic derivatives linked with traits that showed correlated increases or decreases in bioactive compound concentration over time. The specific compounds that clustered together included antioxidant activity measures (FRAP, DPPH, and TPC) forming the uppermost cluster, followed by anthocyanin-related compounds (myricetin, TA, syringic acid, and cyanidin-3-glucoside), phenolic acids (chlorogenic acid and catechin), physical properties and flavonoids (naringenin, height, width, weight, and TSS), and additional phenolic compounds (TAC, ellagic acid, and kaempferol). Combining bioactive compounds into distinct clusters throughout fruit development highlights the connection between fruit development and associated biochemical changes observed in developing blueberry fruit.

Fig. 8Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) of five highbush blueberry cultivars at different ripening stages, based on combined data from phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, and pomological traits

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) of five highbush blueberry cultivars at different ripening stages, based on combined data from phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, and pomological traits

Three distinct cultivar clusters emerged reflecting the developmental process. The early development cluster included all green-stage cultivars (“Volar Green,” “Cargo Green,” “Ozarkblue Green,” and “Blue Ribbon Green”), characterized by high FRAP, DPPH, TPC values, and elevated chlorogenic acid and catechin content. The intermediate ripening cluster comprised pink-stage cultivars (“Blue Ribbon Pink,” “Ozarkblue Pink,” “Cargo Pink,” and “Volar Pink”), exhibiting intermediate characteristics. “Topshelf Green” and “Topshelf Pink” showed unique positioning, demonstrating cultivar-specific differences as reported by [71]. The fully ripe cluster included all ripe-stage cultivars, clearly separated by enhanced physical parameters and developed TSS values. “Blue Ribbon Ripe,” “Topshelf Ripe,” and “Cargo Ripe” clustered tightly, reflecting similar ripening characteristics. This cluster demonstrated progressive cyanidin-3-glucoside increases, coinciding with anthocyanin biosynthesis as described by [72] and [65], supporting genetic influence findings by [13].

DPPH radical scavenging activity, a measure of antioxidant activity, was highly correlated with total phenolic content, especially in the early development cluster. This finding supports [16], who reported strong relationships between phenolic compounds and antioxidant properties in blueberries. Total Anthocyanin Content (TAC) showed different patterns throughout ripening, with the greatest increases observed in “Blue Ribbon Ripe” and “Cargo Ripe” as they approached the final stages of ripening. These observations are consistent with [73], who studied blueberry polyphenols.

An assessment of physical characteristics showed similar developmental patterns for all cultivars, with increasing height and width through ripening. The “Ozarkblue” cultivar exhibited the largest final fruit size, while Total Soluble Solids (TSS) showed a steady increase during ripening across all cultivars. Interestingly, “Blue Ribbon Ripe” exhibited the highest final TSS content, which is consistent with the patterns reported by [42] regarding blueberry quality parameters.

The distinct separation of developmental stages for all cultivars indicates that ripening stage has a greater impact on biochemical profiles than cultivar differences. However, the presence of sub-clusters within each major cluster shows that genetic factors also play a role in determining the final composition and concentration of bioactive compounds. The comprehensive phenolic profiling presented here reveals substantial genotypic diversity among highbush blueberry cultivars. Such cultivar-dependent variation likely arises from multiple mechanisms: (1) genetic polymorphisms in structural genes encoding biosynthetic enzymes, affecting enzyme activity or expression levels; (2) differential expression of transcription factors regulating metabolic pathway flux; (3) variation in precursor availability and substrate channeling; and (4) differences in cellular compartmentation and transport of phenolic compounds [74]. These findings have important implications for breeding programs, as the strong cultivar effects indicate that selection for enhanced phenolic profiles is feasible. The identification of high-performing genotypes (e.g., ‘Ozarkblue’ for antioxidant capacity, ‘Cargo’ for anthocyanins, ‘Topshelf’ for chlorogenic acid) provides both breeding parents and commercial targets for functional food applications.

This information could be valuable for both cultivation and processing. The higher phenolic content and antioxidant activity in the early development cluster, although not ideal for fresh fruit consumption, suggests potential value in utilizing green fruit extracts for nutraceutical purposes, as proposed by [75]. Furthermore, the gradual alterations of bioactive compounds during ripening provide opportunities for targeted harvest strategies based on bioactive compound accumulation. Cultivar variation is highly relevant for breeding programs, as highlighted by [75], since breeding can enhance phytochemical properties. The distinct clustering patterns shown in this study contribute additional insights for determining ideal harvest timing and guiding cultivar selection, from cluster to specific end-use application, with multiple quality parameters being coordinated during ripening.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of phenolic composition, antioxidant capacity, and pomological characteristics across three ripening stages in five highbush blueberry cultivars—“Cargo,” “Ozarkblue,” “Blue Ribbon,” “Topshelf,” and “Volar”—grown under uniform field conditions. The results demonstrated significant genotypic and developmental variation in individual phenolic compounds, particularly anthocyanins, flavonols, and phenolic acids, as well as in total anthocyanin content. Antioxidant activity, measured using both DPPH and FRAP assays, increased markedly with ripening, correlating strongly with anthocyanin accumulation, especially in cultivars “Cargo” and “Blue Ribbon.” Color parameters (L*, a*, b*) shifted progressively from green to blue hues with ripening, reflecting pigment accumulation, and were found to be cultivar-dependent. Pomological traits such as fruit size, weight, and shape index also varied significantly between genotypes and ripening stages, with “Topshelf” and “Ozarkblue” producing larger fruits at commercial maturity. These findings underscore the strong influence of both genotype and ripening stage on blueberry quality attributes and suggest that targeted cultivar selection and optimal harvest timing can be used to maximize nutritional and commercial value. While this study addressed key phytochemical and physical traits, it did not encompass sensory properties, postharvest stability, or sugar–acid balance, which are also crucial for consumer acceptance. Future research should include broader quality assessments across multiple seasons and environments to validate these results and support breeding programs aiming at cultivars with superior health benefits and market potential.
