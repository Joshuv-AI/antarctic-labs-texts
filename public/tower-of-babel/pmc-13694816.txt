Chronotherapy for retinopathy: Unraveling the mystery of Rev-erbα in the circadian rhythm
Authors: Cheng-kang Qu, Hao Wu, Zi-xu Huang, Yi-wen Wang, Ke-xin Wang, Si-Yu Li, Ning Pu, Jie-hao Zhang, Shen-ao Ding, Chen-xu Zhang, Zong-Ming Song, Ye Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDisruption of the retinal circadian rhythm is a common manifestation of various retinal diseases, characterized by abnormal expression of clock genes and impaired light signaling in intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs). This review aims to elucidate the mechanisms of circadian disruption in retinal pathologies, explore the interrelationship between clock genes and ipRGCs, and delineate the therapeutic potential and directions of chronotherapy for retinopathies. Through a systematic retrieval and analysis of literature on retinal diseases and circadian rhythms from databases including PubMed and Web of Science, this review reveals that Rev-erbα and melanopsin form an antagonistic system within the retina, regulating the intraocular dopamine-melatonin rhythm and maintaining retinal homeostasis. Disruption of this rhythmic system leads to oxidative stress, inflammation, and dysregulation of emotional responses. Moreover, chronotherapy, as an intervention leveraging intrinsic temporal patterns, holds significant promise for treating retinopathies associated with circadian misalignment.

Introduction

Circadian rhythm orchestrates the time coordination of diverse physiological and behavioral processes within a 24-h period. It is accomplished through a series of intricate gene expression programs. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) functions as the central pacemaker of circadian rhythms in mammals, receiving photic cues from the retina, autonomously generating circadian oscillations, and transmitting rhythmic signals throughout the body.1 Dysfunctions of the circadian clock or its associated proteins are implicated in various pathological conditions, such as abnormal hormone secretion, oxidative stress, mood disorders, and inflammatory reactions.2,3 These disturbances in circadian rhythms can also impact the metabolic and renewal capabilities of retinal cells, such as the phagocytosis of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), regeneration of photoreceptor outer segment discs, and photosensitive protein synthesis.4,5 Clock genes contribute to balanced circadian rhythms in organisms and synchronize normal physiological functions, thus slowing down the aging process of the retina.6,7 In mammals, the transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 promote the expression of genes encoding Period (Per) and Cryptochrome (Cry). Subsequently, the protein products derived from PER and CRY exert negative regulatory effects on CLOCK and BMAL1 in the feedback loop.7 Nr1d1 is a vital gene involved in modulating circadian rhythms, and its encoded protein REV-ERBα plays a crucial role in maintaining a constant internal environment.8 For instance, REV-ERBα functions as a transcriptional repressor of Bmal1 through binding to the RORE cis-elements in the promoter region, thereby modulating visual information processing.5,9 Moreover, REV-ERBα can also regulate both metabolic activity and immune-inflammatory reactions.2,10 Thus Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) are distinct from classic imaging photoreceptors, such as rods and cones, which are located in the outer nuclear layer (ONL). They fulfill a unique role in non-image-forming processes.17 The passage of light through ipRGCs is implicated in diverse physiological activities, such as adjusting emotion, synchronizing the circadian rhythms, monitoring seasonal changes, and regulating sleep depth.18,19 Irregular light exposure can disrupt ipRGC signal transmission and cause disturbances in circadian rhythms and sleep patterns, ultimately resulting in mood and cognitive impairments.20 The pupillary light reflex (PLR) test has been widely used to evaluate the function of ipRGCs.21 It also serves as a diagnostic tool to quantify the visual impairments of the retina. In clinical practice, the PLR test has been used for the diagnosis of retinal diseases, such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy (DR).22,23 Melanopsin, a type of photosensitive protein present in ipRGCs, was first identified in the melanophore of Xenopus laevis.24,25 The melanopsin signaling pathway is crucial for non-image-forming visual functions, which encompass light-induced physiological responses independent of image formation.17,26 The mRNA level of melanopsin exhibits rhythmicity in response to changes in light intensity.27 In addition, light can directly or indirectly affect the sleep and alertness of organisms through a specialized circadian rhythm pathway, with melanopsin potentially playing a crucial role during this process.20,28,29 Some empirical research has shown that the absence of melanopsin can induce profound alterations in the sleep patterns of mice30 (Figure 1).Figure 1Disruption of circadian rhythm and diseaseRetina is not only a light receptor but also acts as the initiator of circadian rhythm. Disruption of circadian rhythm and disease

Retina is not only a light receptor but also acts as the initiator of circadian rhythm. When ocular clock malfunction occurs, it leads to a series of pathological processes, including inflammation, hormone secretion disorders, and cell self-renewal disorders.

Chronotherapy is a therapeutic strategy based on the characteristics of the human circadian rhythm. By precisely timing interventions such as light exposure, drug administration, or behavioral modifications, it aligns treatment with the biological clock to enhance therapeutic efficacy and minimize side effects.31 Through the retina, which serves as a critical gateway for light signal input, chronotherapy modulates physiological rhythms, including hormone secretion and the expression of core clock genes (e.g., Rev-erbα and Bmal1). As integral components of circadian rhythms, Rev-erbα and melanopsin collectively contribute to the balance of physiological activities. For instance, Rev-erbα and melanopsin jointly modulate the sensitivity of the ipRGCs pathway, thereby regulating light-evoked activity.5 Moreover, targeting the Rev-erbα and melanopsin signaling pathway may provide a novel strategy for the treatment of retinal diseases by delaying the aging process.1,8,32 Additionally, the retina serves as one of the most readily observable portals for circadian rhythm manifestation, and its circadian rhythm expression aligns closely with that of the central nervous system.33

The role of rev-erbα and ipRGCs in the circadian rhythm

Rev-erbα-mediated circadian rhythm regulation

Rev-erbα is involved in regulating circadian rhythm and metabolic activities during retinal development.10,34 Its extensive distribution ensures normal rhythmicity in different tissues, allowing organisms to adapt to periodic environmental changes while maintaining stable vital signs.5,32,35 Experimental knockdown of Rev-erbα results in profound abnormalities in the retina, such as RPE cell dysfunction, oxidative stress, and sensitivity to ambient light levels.36 On closer inspection, researchers find that REV-ERBα can regulate the transcription and translation of Per and Cry.37 This study demonstrates that all five orphan nuclear receptors, including REV-ERBα, REV-ERBβ, RORα1, RORβ, and RORγ, bind to a common RORE2 site on the Bmal1 promoter. Over-expression of REV-ERBα significantly inhibits the expression level of Bmal1.38 Researchers also detect elevated levels of REV-ERBα prior to the lowest point of Bmal1 oscillations, suggesting that REV-ERBα acts as a transcriptional repressor in regulating Bmal1 expression.39 This hypothesis is further supported by another experiment using the Rev-erbα−/− mice, in which the amplitude of Bmal1 oscillation reduces significantly due to enhanced Bmal1 expression in the SCN during the anticipated trough period.38

The mechanism of Rev-erbα in neuroinflammation, dopaminergic neuron regulation, and retinal activity shows a high degree of context dependence. It involves both Bmal1-dependent pathways and clear Bmal1-independent mechanisms, with significant differences in mechanisms across different tissues, cell types and pathological models. Among them, the Bmal1-independent pathway plays a dominant role in the physiological regulation of the retina. In retinal activity, Rev-erbα enhances light sensitivity in low-light environments by regulating the expression of melanopsin in ipRGCs, influencing the pupillary reflex, negative masking behavior, and electrophysiological responses.5 This mechanism is clearly independent of Bmal1 and is achieved through the melanopsin-mediated rod pathway.5 The absence of Rev-Erbα does not cause changes in retinal structure or major cell markers. In neuroinflammation, Rev-erbα acts on microglia in the central nervous system and significantly reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNFα induced by LPS by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway.40 In the regulation of dopaminergic neurons, Rev-Erbα participates in the regulation of light signal transmission to the dopaminergic system by up-regulating the expression of melanopsin in ipRGCs, thereby affecting intraocular dopamine secretion and related visual functions, such as light adaptation and refractive development.41 Therefore, Rev-erbα has emerged as a critical modulator of retinal activity, revealing an expanding spectrum of function. In this context, modulating the Rev-erbα expression under pathological conditions may hold promising therapeutic potential for retinopathy (Table 2). The regulatory role of ipRGCs in circadian rhythms

ipRGCs are the fundamental unit of the non-image-forming visual system in the retina. They possess a unique ability to receive both light and dark signals, thus play a vital role in regulating the human circadian clock.24,45 It is noteworthy that ipRGCs are not a singular cell type but rather a diverse group of cells with distinctive morphological and physiological characteristics. Currently, six subtypes of ipRGCs (M1-M6) have been identified in the mammalian retina.46 Melanopsin serves as the definitive marker protein for all subtypes of ipRGCs. Notably, M1 ipRGCs exhibit the highest level of melanopsin expression, followed by M3 and M2 subtypes, while M4-6 ipRGCs express lower levels.26 Melanopsin signaling plays an indispensable role in the non-image-forming visual functions of the retina. It is implicated in various physiologic activities including PLR, vascular development as well as the secretion of dopamine and melatonin.17,47,48 Researchers have detected abnormal melanopsin expression in the ipRGCs during the process of various pathologies, such as AMD, DR, and glaucoma.22,49,50 Additionally, melanopsin is also closely correlated with neurological conditions such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimers’ disease.1

Recently, it has been shown that the light signals received by ipRGCs are involved in regulating circadian rhythms. The light information received by ipRGCs is conveyed to the hypothalamic SCN via the retinohypothalamic tract (RHT). The SCN integrates these light signals through the rhythmic changes in the electrical activity of its neuronal network, and then regulates the transcription-translation feedback loop of core clock genes. Ultimately, it coordinates the rhythms of peripheral clocks throughout the body through vasoactive intestinal peptide and arginine vasopressin.51,52,53 Rodent clock rhythms are most susceptible to light with a wavelength of 470 nm, which coincides with the properties of melanopsin.54,55 Research shows that the peak of the action spectrum of melanopsin (λmax) is located in the short-wavelength blue light region, approximately 480 nm, making it highly sensitive to light with wavelengths around 470 nm.56 Specifically, melanopsin, as the key photosensitive pigment of ipRGCs, is closely related to neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and alzheimer’s disease (AD) by regulating non-image-forming visual functions including circadian rhythms, pupillary light reflex, and sleep-wake cycles. Studies have shown that the amplitude of melanopsin-mediated pupillary light reflex (PIPR) after light exposure is significantly reduced in patients with PD, and the density of melanopsin-positive cells in the retina is significantly decreased, indicating anatomical and functional damage, which is directly associated with common non-motor symptoms such as sleep disorders and circadian rhythm disturbances in these patients.57 The degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and the loss of synaptic connections with melanopsin cells may further exacerbate the dysfunction of this system.58 In AD, although some meta-analyses show that PIPR impairment is not as significant as in PD, abnormalities in the melanopsin system are still observed, with dendritic structures possibly being affected in the preclinical stage, and melanopsin gene polymorphisms interacting with brain atrophy, cognitive function, and sleep characteristics.59 Additionally, the assessment of melanopsin function has been proposed as a potential biomarker for early identification of neurodegeneration or monitoring cognitive decline, while phototherapy strategies targeting melanopsin have also been explored to improve sleep rhythms in patients with PD.60

Further investigations have revealed that glutamate serves as the primary neurotransmitter for transmitting light information to the SCN via the RHT.61 Additionally, pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), a member of the vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP)/secretin/glucagon family, is co-localized with glutamate in the RHT of rats. Previous studies have shown that ipRGCs directly project to VIP neurons in the SCN, and these VIP neurons receive synaptic inputs from both axon-dendrite and axon-soma synapses from the retina.62,63 VIP neurons are intensively distributed in the core region of the SCN. They play a role in altering the phase of SCN circadian rhythms and inducing calcium flow in the SCN, thereby adapting the circadian clock to light phase changes.64,65 In part, the phase-shifting ability of light is thought to involve glutamate release from ipRGCs to N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors on SCN neurons, whereas rats lacking PACAP-1 receptors exhibit abnormal phase shifts and reduced light induction of the Per.66,67 VIP can also cause a phase shift similar to light, so that light signals will be propagated through SCN neurons.61 Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that mice lacking VPAC2 receptors, which facilitate both VIP and PACAP signaling pathways, exhibit severe disruptions in their circadian rhythm.68

Crosstalk between melanopsin and rev-erbα

Melanopsin and Rev-erbα jointly modulate the circadian rhythms and maintain retinal homeostasis. Melanopsin not only functions as a photosensitive pigment, but also can influence clock genes.69 The interaction between melanopsin and circadian clock genes is a one-way, indirect regulatory relationship: Melanopsin initiates neural and molecular signaling pathways, such as the SCN projection and cyclic adenosine monophosphate- cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), ultimately influencing the transcriptional rhythm and phase of circadian clock genes.70,71 As the photopigment of ipRGCs, melanopsin senses blue light and transmits the light signal through the RHT to the SCN, thereby affecting core clock genes. Moreover, the downstream pathway activated by melanopsin, cAMP-CREB, can act on the promoter of circadian clock genes, thereby influencing the expression of clock genes.

The absence of melanopsin results in periodic fluctuations in REV-ERBα levels over a 24-h period, while the absence of REV-ERBα leads to increased expression of melanopsin. For instance, the retina of Rev-erbα−/− mice shows a significantly higher abundance of ipRGCs (62% more than the wild type), as well as increased melanopsin immunostaining intensity in individual ipRGCs.5,72 In light-evoked behavioral tests, the Rev-erbα−/− mice exhibit increased sensitivity to negative masking under low illumination conditions (0.1 lux) compared with the wild type littermates.5 The specific molecular regulatory mechanism underlying Rev-erbα and melanopsin remains elusive. Light stimulus is detected by ipRGCs and transmitted along the retinohypothalamic tract (RHT) to the hypothalamic SCN, thereby modulating circadian rhythms. Melanopsin, a photosensitive protein present in ipRGCs, enables the detection of light stimulus. Upon transmission to the RHT terminal, the light signal triggers the release of pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP), and glutamate. These molecules subsequently bind to their respective receptors on SCN neurons, leading to changes in intracellular Ca2+ concentration and activation of MAPK, PKA, PKC, and CaMK signaling pathways. This cascade results in phosphorylation of cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (CREB), which binds to the cAMP response element (CRE) and promotes transcriptional expression of Per. Additionally, the Clock/Bmal1 complex can also modulate the Per expression independently of this pathway. Clock genes not only exert negative feedback repression on their own expression, but also regulate each other’s activity.

The circadian rhythm is primarily regulated by a transcription-translation feedback loop, wherein Bmal1 and Clock form a heterodimer that binds to the E-box in the promoter region of clock-controlled genes, thereby initiating the expression of downstream clock genes such as Per and Rev-erbα.43 During this process, Bmal1 and Clock exert a negative feedback impact by inhibiting their own expression.73 The downstream clock genes can also modulate the expression of Bmal1 and Clock, and vice versa. Among them, Per, Cry, and Rev-erbα all exert a negative influence on Bmal1 expression, while RORs positively regulate Bmal1 expression.38 In the SCN, Bmal1 expression is up-regulated during the dark phase and peaks at midnight, while Per exhibits high expression levels during the light phase. These rhythmic oscillations also exist in the peripheral tissues of the body.74 However, the highest peak of Rev-erbα expression is detected at a later time point.43 Studies have demonstrated that Rev-erbα expression complies with its own cycle, with the weakest level observed during early night and the strongest level seen in early morning. The rhythmic oscillations should be ascribed to the modulation by Bmal1 and Per.75

The expression of clock genes can also be regulated by light stimulus through the melanopsin-mediated light signaling pathway.76 When there is sufficient external light, this signaling pathway can phosphorylate the cyclic-AMP response binding protein (CREB), and bind to the cAMP response element (CRE) in the SCN, thereby promoting the transcription of the Per.43,77 This regulation pattern is independent from the way of regulatory pathways mediated by ipRGCs because these clock genes can still maintain some rhythmicity even in the absence of light.75 Studies have also shown that neurotransmitters PACAP and glutamate can regulate circadian rhythms through the melanopsin-mediated light signaling pathway.43 Compared with wild-type (WT) mice, early night light stimulation resulted in a more pronounced phase delay and a significant reduction in light-induced Per expression in PAC1−/− mice. Late night light stimulation induced similar levels of Per expression in the SCN of both WT and PAC1-/- mice, but PAC1 mice still exhibited a phase delay. These findings underscore the crucial role of light stimulus in regulating the expression of clock genes.10

The inseparable relationship between Rev-erbα and melanopsin is further confirmed by gene knockout experiments. Rev-erbα−/− mice manifest augmented light sensitivity, as evidenced by shortened electroretinogram wave latencies, the presence of an atypical humped b-wave, scotopic threshold responses, and enhanced pupil constriction.5 Notably, accumulating evidence suggests that Rev-erbα and melanopsin synergistically participate in multiple physiological functions of the retina, encompassing neurotransmitter secretion (such as dopamine and melatonin), inflammatory responses, antioxidant activity, and cellular metabolic renewal.2,5,69,78,79 However, disturbances of these functions would impair retinal homeostasis, lead to the development of retinopathy, as well as symptoms such as insomnia and emotional dysregulation.80,81,82 This further emphasizes the crucial importance of investigating the intricate correlation between Rev-erbα and melanopsin. Notably, current research has only observed an indirect functional connection between Rev-erbα and melanopsin in the regulation of retinal photosensitivity and the circadian rhythm pathway, but lacks direct evidence at the molecular level, such as protein-protein interactions, transcriptional regulation, and signal pathway crosstalk.

Chronotherapy based on the base of rev-erbα

Chronotherapy related to the retina

Chronotherapy is an intervention strategy based on the principle of the biological clock within the human body. By adjusting the rhythm of treatment time or intervention methods to synchronize with the internal circadian rhythm, it can optimize therapeutic efficacy, reduce side effects, and improve prognosis.31 Its core lies in leveraging the periodicity of gene regulation by the biological clock to administer drugs, conduct behavioral interventions, or implement biological regulation at specific time windows to match the circadian fluctuations of physiological functions. Previous studies on chronotherapy mainly focused on identifying time-sensitive therapeutic windows for drugs to achieve optimized therapeutic outcomes. For instance, in cancer chemotherapy, administering drugs during periods when tumor cells are particularly active in proliferation can significantly enhance their killing efficiency.83 For glucocorticoids, taking them in the morning better mimics the natural secretion rhythm of cortisol in the human body, thereby reducing interference with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis.84 Additionally, cardiovascular morbidity and mortality can be significantly reduced by adjusting the duration of antihypertensive medication.85 However, researchers find that therapeutic time windows can only temporarily alleviate symptoms for these diseases involving disruptions in the master biological clock, and resetting the biological clock to its normal state is more crucial. Previous research has explored the possibility of treating circadian rhythm disorders by intervening in molecules involved in the circadian rhythm. For instance, phototherapy is performed to modulate melatonin secretion, thereby improving sleep disorders and mood in patients.86,87 In addition, cordycepin extracted from fungi has been found to cause a phase shift in the daily oscillatory expression of core circadian clock genes such as Per and Cry, thereby reducing the time needed to recover from jet lag by 50%.88 Accordingly, chronotherapy may directly intervene in the transcription-translation feedback loop of circadian genes, providing new insights for the treatment of circadian rhythm disorders.

As the starting point for receiving light signals, retina exhibits different characteristics from other target organs. On the one hand, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), as the main biological clock regulator, can regulate the metabolic processes within the retina and the function of inner retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) through the autonomic nervous system and hormone secretion, thereby influencing the biological clock within the retina. On the other hand, the independent biological clock within the retina can modulate the secretion of intraocular dopamine and melatonin, thereby affecting the function of ipRGCs, and ultimately having an impact on the biological clock rhythm of the SCN. Studies have shown that in the blind population, the occurrence of sleep disorders is closely related to the disorder of melatonin clock rhythm caused by retinal dysfunction.86 In certain central nervous system diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease, the ipRGC function of these patients is impaired, and a series of circadian clock disorders occur in succession.59,89 In the treatment of degenerative retinopathy accompanied by circadian rhythm disorders, restoring the ipRGCs function is of vital importance. Currently, numerous studies have focused on clock genes and their related molecular pathways as potential therapeutic targets. The regulation of melatonin and dopamine is the most widely used therapeutic strategy. For instance, modulating melatonin secretion by phototherapy can correct sleep disorders and depression caused by clock rhythm disorders.90 Another study has also verified that in a state of constant darkness, dopamine exerts its effect through dopamine D2 receptors located on these ipRGCs, thereby increasing the levels of melanopsin transcripts.91 Encouragingly, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating clock gene expression are currently under investigation. For instance, Rev-erbα provides a novel therapeutic means for retinal degenerative diseases (especially dry AMD) through multiple mechanisms such as antioxidant effects and regulation of mitochondrial function.4 Despite challenges such as delivery technology and individual differences, with the development of Rev-erbα agonists and the optimization of chronotherapy, its clinical application prospects are promising. In the future, it is necessary to combine precision medicine and interdisciplinary technologies to promote the translation from experimental evidence to clinical treatment.

Dopamine and circadian rhythms in retina

Dopamine is an essential neurotransmitter that is closely linked to circadian rhythms and exerts significant influence on neural activities.69 A complete dopaminergic system exists within the retina. Dopamine is mainly synthesized and released by dopaminergic amacrine cells (DACs) in the retina, and its activity is regulated by light and the circadian rhythm.41 Retinal dopamine regulates light adaptation, visual signal processing, and non-image-forming visual functions by acting on D1-type receptors.92 Multiple animal and clinical studies have shown that changes in retinal dopamine levels can occur without significant alterations in central dopamine. The retinal dopamine abnormalities in PD may have dual sources, but the primary evidence from the eye is stronger.93,94 Studies have demonstrated the presence of α-synuclein (aSyn) in the retinas of patients with PD, leading to the loss of dopaminergic cells, which represents a primary pathological change in the retina.93 The dopamine abnormalities in DR also tend to originate locally in the eye. A decline in retinal dopamine levels occurs early in diabetes, preceding microvascular lesions, and is associated with oxidative stress and ferroptosis.95

Recently, the single-cell polymerase chain reaction (SC-PCR) analysis has detected the steady-state mRNA of all six core clock genes (Clock/Bmal1/Rev-erbα/RORα/Per/Cry) in 30% of dopaminergic amacrine cells, while the minimum expression is found in photoreceptors.69 These findings highlight the existence of a photoreceptor-independent circadian clock within inner retinal circuits. Furthermore, dopamine can modulate the signaling transmission in retinal neural networks under different illumination conditions and contribute to the process of light/dark adaptation.96 Continuous exposure to light stimulus leads to up-regulated retinal dopamine levels due to the activation of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), a rate-limiting enzyme in dopamine synthesis.97 ipRGCs can communicate with dopaminergic neurons through gap junctions, providing continuous excitatory stimulus to these dopaminergic neurons.98,99 In a previous experiment, researchers showed that stimulating the optic nerve head using blue light can significantly upregulate ocular dopamine levels in rabbits.100 The ipRGC axons containing melanopsin are always located at the optic nerve head.101 Given the fact that the most responsive wavelength for melanopsin falls within the blue light spectrum, it is highly possible that ipRGCs may be capable of regulating the retinal dopamine level through melanopsin signaling.102 Additionally, melanopsin not only regulates dopamine secretion, but also affects the activity of TH in the eye. Studies have experimentally demonstrated that TH mRNA expression is inhibited in melanopsin knockout mice, but TH activity and dopamine content are not altered after light induction.72,97 These results indicate that melanopsin-mediated light signal can promote the secretion of dopamine in the retina. Conversely, the dopamine released by amacrine cells can influence melanopsin synthesis in ipRGCs.1,91 Amacrine cells release a variety of neurotransmitters, including dopamine, acetylcholine, and various neuropeptides.103 The majority of amacrine cells are inhibitory neurons that utilize GABA or glycine as their primary neurotransmitter.104 Notably, there exists a subset of amacrine cells known as DACs, which predominantly employ dopamine as their neurotransmitter.1 Dopamine exerts regulatory control over melanopsin translation through activating the D2 dopamine receptors on ipRGCs.91 Dopamine secreted by DACs exerts regulatory control over melanopsin mRNA transcription and translation in ipRGCs through D2dopamine receptors.91

Rev-erbα also participates in modulating rhythmic dopamine secretion. Researchers find that the use of the Rev-erbα inhibitor SR8278 causes an increase in brain dopamine levels and then induces manic-like behavior.80 Furthermore, REV-ERBα competes with nuclear receptor-related 1 protein (NURR1) to suppress TH gene transcription, which contributes to dopamine secretion.8 In dopaminergic neurons, rhythmic expression of the TH gene is modulated through competition between the circadian nuclear receptor REV-ERBα and NURR1, another nuclear receptor that binds to the same cis-element (R/N sites).8 In a Parkinson’s disease mouse model induced by injection of 6-OHDA, inhibiting Rev-erbα expression with SR8278 can increase the content of intraocular dopamine and retard disease progression.8 However, a new study shows that astrocytes with low Rev-erbα expression are associated with the apoptosis of dopaminergic neurons. In contrast, a Rev-erbα agonist could attenuate the activation of astrocytes and reduce the damage to dopaminergic neurons.105 These findings suggest that Rev-erbα regulates dopamine through multifarious approaches, including endocrine regulation, inflammatory reactions, and apoptosis pathways. As a modulatory component of the circadian rhythm, the connection between Rev-erbα and the neuroendocrine pathway is particularly significant. To explore how Rev-erbα influences the circadian rhythm through the hormone secretion pathway, researchers should conduct an in-depth exploration of the dopamine-melatonin loop and the Rev-erbα-melanopsin loop.

Melatonin affects the secretion of dopamine in the retina

Melatonin, a hormone widely present in vertebrates, is regulated by the light signals from the environment. The secretion of melatonin follows a strict 24-h rhythm. The SCN regulates the enzymatic activity of the pineal body through neural output pathways, ensuring that melatonin is synthesized and released only under dark conditions at night, thus serving as the “darkness hormone” of the organism.106,107 Multiple studies have confirmed that retinal photoreceptors can autonomously synthesize melatonin at night, and this process is regulated by light and is relatively independent of the pineal gland rhythm.108 Under physiological conditions, the pineal gland is the main source of circulating melatonin, but the melatonin synthesized in the eye mainly acts on local tissues and has a limited contribution to the systemic level.108 Under pathological conditions, such as DR or glaucoma, local melatonin synthesis in ocular tissues may be upregulated or downregulated to cope with oxidative stress, inflammation, or neural damage.109,110 Melatonin participates in a wide variety of retinal activities, including visual adaptation, cellular development, and neuroprotection.111,112 Melatonin functions primarily through binding to its specific receptors, which are present in multiple subtypes within retinal cells. Abnormal secretion of melatonin can lead to various retinal diseases.113 In patients with glaucoma, damage to ipRGCs will inevitably lead to lower melatonin expression.73 This melatonin deficiency exacerbates the pathological progression by impairing antioxidant capacity and disturbing the intraocular pressure regulation.73 In patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), the level of melatonin in the aqueous humor is significantly elevated, while the level in the serum is markedly decreased.109 This “increase in aqueous humor and decrease in serum” dissociation strongly suggests that the changes in ocular melatonin are not driven by alterations in pineal gland secretion, but rather by abnormal regulation of local synthesis within the eye.109 In patients with high intraocular pressure and in animal models of glaucoma, the concentration of melatonin in the aqueous humor is significantly higher than in normal controls and further increases with rising intraocular pressure.110 This local concentration increase that is in sync with the dynamic changes in intraocular pressure is difficult to explain by fluctuations in pineal gland secretion and is more likely to reflect an enhanced responsive synthesis by ocular tissues in response to pathological stimuli.

Thus far, the circadian rhythm of melatonin secretion has garnered considerable scientific interest. The secretion of melatonin can be influenced by light, particularly exhibiting higher levels during periods of low light intensity.114 Typically, light intensity is stronger during daytime and weaker at night, thereby eliciting rhythmic fluctuations in melatonin secretion throughout 24 h.114 However, the dopamine cycle exhibits an intriguing pattern in contrast to melatonin. Dopamine primarily acts as a light-induced signaling molecule, so its secretion reaches a maximum level during the daytime to ensure the optimal vitality of the body.115 In contrast, melatonin is predominantly secreted during nighttime to facilitate dark-adaptive physiological activities.116 The prevailing consensus is that melatonin inhibits dopamine secretion through its action on melatonin receptors, while dopamine inhibits melatonin synthesis and release via its action on D2 receptors.117,118 In addition, melatonin affects clock gene expression. For instance, melatonin can enhance the expression of Bmal1, thereby participating in the regulation of circadian rhythms.69

However, as our understanding of circadian rhythms deepens, it has been discovered that the regulation of melatonin and dopamine secretion is closely correlated with light signals, because secretion disorders and ipRGC deletion often coexist. For instance, in glaucoma, the destruction of ipRGCs leads to reduced melatonin expression.73 Moreover, the secretion of dopamine increases significantly, which may be ascribed to the weakened inhibitory effect of melatonin.96 As a shared regulatory site for both melatonin and dopamine, the clock gene Rev-erbα holds significant therapeutic potential. SR9009 is an agonist of Rev-erbα that can be developed to reduce the intraocular dopamine level via up-regulating Rev-erbα expression. Researchers proposed that the decrease in dopamine levels may indirectly promote the secretion of melatonin, thereby reducing intraocular pressure, alleviating glaucoma lesions, and circadian rhythm disorders.119,120

Strong light exposure at night can significantly suppress melatonin secretion, a process mainly driven by ipRGCs. ipRGCs convey light-dark information to the SCN, triggering signal transduction and thereby regulating the activity of the pineal gland.121 Melatonin secretion can be influenced by melanopsin expression and the physiological state of ipRGCs.122 Extensive research has demonstrated that melanopsin can inhibit the melatonin release from the pineal gland. Studies have also shown that light signals, primarily driven by melanopsin, exert inhibitory effects on melatonin secretion.123 Among different colors of light, blue light has the strongest inhibitory effect on melatonin due to the higher sensitivity of melanopsin to its wavelength band.123 In addition, melatonin is closely related to the growth, development, and refractive control of the eyeball. Prolonged exposure to bright light or blue light at night may result in reduced melatonin secretion and excessive eye growth, thereby increasing the risk of myopia.124 In particular, the deficiency of dopamine secretion is also considered a significant cause of myopia.99 In this context, melatonin and dopamine may jointly modulate the progression of myopia, whose initiation factor is likely to be the blue light signal received by ipRGCs.116 Furthermore, in mice lacking rod cells, the synthesis process of melatonin loses its intrinsic circadian rhythms, which should be attributed to the disrupted signal transmission between rod cells and ipRGCs.69 Additionally, melatonin may influence dopamine secretion by regulating DACs through the melatonin receptors on ipRGCs.91,98,125 Therefore, it is reasonable to modulate the expression level of melanopsin to treat melatonin- or dopamine-related disorders.

Taken together, we can propose the following hypotheses based on the crosstalk of Rev-erbα and melanopsin in the dopamine-melatonin loop: Rev-erbα is an important component of clock genes and serves as the commander that regulates circadian rhythm. Melanopsin is an important protein that senses external light signals and resets the endogenous clock according to the intensity of external light. Experiments have shown that activation of Rev-erbα leads to a reduced amount of melanopsin and weakens the blue light signal received by ipRGCs.5,126 When the SCN receives insufficient blue light signals, it “asks” the pineal gland to secrete more melatonin and suppresses dopamine function.72,127,128 At this point, the circadian rhythm shifts toward the night rhythm. On the contrary, when Rev-erbα is knocked out or inhibited, the content of melanopsin increases, and the response of the non-visual imaging system is enhanced.5 The blue light signal is transmitted to the pineal gland through the same pathway, which inhibit the secretion of melatonin, and enhance the content of dopamine, At this point, the circadian rhythm changes to the day rhythm.100,123 During this process, dopamine and melatonin, as rhythmically secreted hormones, participate in the regulation of melanopsin. Specifically, REV-ERBα can competitively inhibit NURR1, thus lowering dopamine levels, while dopamine can promote the transcription of melanopsin mRNA.8,91 On one hand, the reduced melanopsin level leads to increased secretion of melatonin and the activation of Bmal1, which in turn promotes the expression of Rev-erbα.129 On the other hand, melatonin acts on the melatonin receptors, further increasing dopamine reuptake and reducing dopamine concentration in the synaptic cleft.130 In the mammalian retina, dopamine receptors and MT1/MT2 melatonin receptors form heterodimers through co-immunoprecipitation.108,131 This structure directly mediates the cross-regulation of melatonin and dopamine signals. Dysregulated circadian rhythms in retinopathy

Retinal dopamine is involved in t resetting the retinal circadian clock. It also acts as a regulator in the adaptive reaction to light conditions.41 However, in retinopathy, intraretinal dopamine secretion is disturbed due to destruction of retinal architecture. Thus far, abnormal dopamine secretion has been detected in DR, RP, and Parkinson’s disease related retinopathy. Other than the non-motor impairments, Parkinson’s disease also affects the visual system and causes retinal damage, including the loss of retinal ganglion cells, thinning of the retinal layers, and dysfunction in dopaminergic circuits.132 Researchers show that retinopathy caused by Parkinson’s disease should be primarily attributed to the reduced dopamine level in the retina.1 Concurrently, the number of ipRGCs in patients with Parkinson’s disease reduces significantly.133 The abnormalities in ipRGCs may cause circadian rhythm disturbances in patients with Parkinson’s disease, potentially contributing to the occurrence of sleep and emotional disorders.9 Furthermore, recent studies have revealed that patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus accompanied by diabetic retinopathy exhibit diminished ipRGC function, as evidenced by reduced levels of 6-sulfatoxymelatonin in nocturnal urine and the failure to detect the dim light melatonin onset (DLMO) in evening saliva samples. These findings provide preliminary evidence that ipRGC dysfunction may disrupt melatonin regulatory mechanisms, thereby potentially affecting the circadian rhythm system.134,135

DR is a common diabetic complication characterized by the leakage and obstruction of retinal microvessels.136 This condition stands as one of the primary etiologies for visual impairment and blindness. There is a robust correlation between DR and circadian rhythm, which encompasses disruptions of the dopaminergic system.41,137 Dopamine plays a pivotal role in regulating vascular constriction and dilation. It can inhibit oxidative stress, inflammatory reactions, and mitigate macular edema, all of which contribute to the progression of DR.125 Impaired the dopaminergic system and abnormal circadian rhythm have been found in patients with DR.45 Patients with DR manifest abnormal light-regulated circadian symptoms, such as sleep disturbances.136 It is hypothesized that DR pathology may affect the function of ipRGCs, thus aggravating the dopamine deficiency. Remarkable transcriptional changes of core clock genes are also detected in the retinas of DR mouse models. These changes include the loss of rhythmic expression of Cry2, Per1, and Npase2, as well as a weakening of the oscillation of Rev-erbα at peak times.138 Meanwhile, DR can elicit morphological alterations in ipRGCs. Specifically, research findings indicate that patients with diabetic retinopathy exhibit impaired function of ipRGCs, as evidenced by a reduction in the amplitude of the PIPR.49 Clinicians have detected a significant reduction in melanopsin expression in patients with DR, accompanied by decreased intraretinal dopamine levels.49,139 In addition, the red light (λ = 633) and blue light (λ = 463) are applied to stimulate the pupil reaction of patients with DR. It is observed that the decline in dopamine level is more pronounced under blue light exposure, while low-intensity red light therapy can inhibit the early stages of DR.140 These findings imply that DR pathology may affect the dopamine level through modulating the melanopsin signaling in ipRGCs. Conversely, the dysregulated clock gene expression may act as a contributing factor to the pathogenesis of DR.136 One of the well-studied clock genes is Bmal1, which alters various signaling pathways through its interaction with E-box.141 The Bmal1 deficiency in DR can mitigate the expression of downstream antioxidant elements, impair mitochondrial metabolic function, and accelerate DR progression.142,143 It is speculated that inhibiting Rev-erbα may enhance melanopsin synthesis in ipRGCs, thereby promoting dopamine secretion.9,44 Further elucidation of the specific molecular mechanism underlying the Rev-erbα-melanopsin-dopamine system is warranted.

Rev-erbα regulates oxidative stress through the Nrf2 pathway

AMD is a degenerative retinopathy primarily affecting the macula of the elderly population. It is characterized by the progressive death of the photoreceptors and RPE, leading to central vision loss.36 RPE is a highly specialized epithelial cell layer that interacts with the apical surface of the photoreceptors, the basal side of Bruch’s membrane, and the choroidal vasculature to maintain normal physiological activity of retina.144 At the initial stage of AMD, the lipofuscin accumulates in the retina tissue due to cellular senescence. With the continuous accumulation of lipofuscin, RPE cells become increasingly susceptible to oxidative damage, which impairs their ability to phagocytose and renew, ultimately resulting in RPE atrophy.36 Notably, RPE, similar to ipRGCs, possess distinctive ocular clock regulation mechanisms that contribute to the refinement of visual sensitivity.69 A pioneering study shows that the Rev-erbα expression in the retinas of patients with AMD decreases significantly, and this down-regulation is correlated with the severity of the macular lesion.36 Studies have shown that the application of Rev-erbα agonist SR9009 enhances phagocytic activity in normal ARPE-19 cells, as well as increases the expression of clock-controlled autophagy genes.4 Genes associated with circadian phagocytosis, such as integrin beta subunit (ITGB5), protein S (PROS1), and protein tyrosine kinase 2 (PTK2), are shown to be involved in the recognition and engulfment stages of phagocytosis in RPE.145,146 Intriguingly, the continuous increase in ITGB5 and PROS1 expression, rather than PTK2, is observed in senescent ARPE-19 cells over a 24-h period. In addition, the pharmacological activation of Rev-erbα enhances the phagocytic activity of normal ARPE-19 cells, while simultaneously upregulating ITGB5 and PROS1 gene expression.4,53,146 These findings suggest that the altered rhythmicity of clock gene expression enhances the circadian phagocytic activity of senescent RPE cells by influencing the phagocytosis-related gene expression.4 Nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a transcription factor that plays a vital role in the body’s response to oxidative stress.147 Normally, Nrf2 binds to Keap1 to facilitate its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation by the proteasome. However, under conditions of oxidative stress, ROS can disturb Nrf2 ubiquitination, leading to its translocation into the nucleus and the subsequent transcription of antioxidant components.148 Numerous studies have demonstrated that Nrf2 can effectively combat oxidative stress-related diseases caused by aging and light stimulus.147 A recent study shows that REV-ERBα alleviates RPE degeneration through Nrf2 signaling.4 Pharmacological activation of Rev-erbα expression has been shown to protect RPE from oxidative damage both in vivo and in vitro.4 Rev-erbα directly promotes the transcription of Nrf2 as well as its downstream antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) to protect against oxidative damage.36 Conversely, Rev-erbα knockout specifically in the RPE can lead to accumulated oxidative stress and RPE death in aged mice.36 On closer inspection, it is found that Rev-erbα promotes Nrf2 expression by inhibiting intermediate transcriptional repressors such as NFIL3/E4BP4 or through the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway.15 (Figure 4).Figure 4Rev-erbα alleviates the oxidative damages in retinasNrf2 is an important transcription factor that promotes the expression of downstream antioxidant enzymes. Excessive exposure to light is regarded as an etiological factor for retinal aging.149 Retina is more vulnerable to shorter-wavelength light, which causes primarily photochemical damage.150 Specifically, blue light has been shown to induce photoreceptor death and neural gliosis, resulting in degenerative retinopathy.151 Blue light can penetrate deep into the retina and cause internal damage to ipRGCs. ipRGCs plays a pivotal role in regulating the body’s circadian rhythms and sleep cycle.152 A clinical study reveals that patients with AMD have poor ipRGC responsiveness, lower sleep efficiency, and a higher likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms.153 Additionally, light exposure not only induces the overproduction of ROS, but also aggravates the ipRGCs-mediated inhibitory effect on melatonin secretion. Melatonin can remove surplus ROS and bolster the antioxidant enzymes. Conversely, melatonin deficiency will accelerate the degenerative process of AMD.154 The damage to ipRGCs may interrupt the transmission of the melatonin secretion signal. Melatonin deficiency eventually contributes to the imbalance of light rhythm. Researchers have shown that melanopsin expression is downregulated in the retinas of patients with AMD.22 Similarly, the expression of Rev-erbα also decreases significantly in the retinas of AMD mouse model.36 Currently, the utilization of Rev-erbα agonists has achieved preliminary success in protecting against oxidative stress.155 However, current research lacks data about the melanopsin-mediated circadian pathways and clock gene expression patterns in patients with AMD both pre- and post-treatment. Further investigations are necessary to elucidate this matter.

It is worth noting that Rev-erbα plays a complex dual role in the regulation of oxidative stress, which is highly dependent on tissue type, disease model, target site, and background of signaling pathways. Rev-erbα is not a direct antioxidant but indirectly affects the outcome of oxidative stress through transcriptional regulatory networks, such as the Nrf2 pathway, ferroptosis-related genes, and inflammatory factors. In the vast majority of cases, Rev-erbα can directly activate the Nrf2 pathway, upregulate antioxidant enzymes such as SOD1 and catalase, and inhibit oxidative stress.156 However, in a few cases, such as oxidative stress caused by ferroptosis, the decreased expression of Rev-erbα can inhibit the transcription of anti-ferroptosis genes, thereby reducing oxidative stress damage.157

Rev-erbα-melanopsin rhythms and emotional disease

Retinopathy not only exerts direct impacts on the visual function of patients, but also affects their emotional regulation ability.158,159 The retina serves as the primary receptor for light signals, and it has been established that mood-related regulatory information is transmitted through ipRGCs.160 Melanopsin, synthesized by ipRGCs, acts as a pivotal molecule in receiving the mood-regulatory signals. Any disruption of ipRGCs or melanopsin can lead to psychiatric or psychological disorders.20 Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a mood disorder that coexists with various retinopathies. Ocular diseases such as glaucomatous optic neuropathy, Parkinson’s disease related retinopathy, and AMD can induce emotional disturbances closely resembling SAD.78 Studies have verified a close relationship between SAD etiology and ipRGC malfunction. As a recent methodological advancement, the Post-illumination pupil response (PIPR) has been used to measure ipRGC responses and to quantify the light responsiveness of humans.161 In patients with AMD, melanopsin-mediated PIPR is significantly correlated with sleep efficiency, suggesting that ipRGC dysfunction may affect sleep by reducing light input to the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the ventrolateral preoptic area.153 The PIPR also decreases significantly from summer to winter in patients with SAD.162 In particular, seasonal variation in the melanopsin-driven nonvisual responses to light may serve as a risk factor for developing SAD. Excitingly, phototherapy is proven effective for treating SAD by compensating for the photosensitivity loss due to melanopsin deletion.162 Furthermore, the longer duration of melatonin release is another risk factor for impaired photosensitivity in patients with SAD.163 The delayed phase and prolonged duration of melatonin secretion in patients with SAD may contribute to the manifestation of narcolepsy and late awakening symptoms associated with this condition.164 These findings align with the fact that melanopsin-driven retinal responses are attenuated due to the inhibitory effect of light stimulation on melatonin secretion. In addition, the loss of ipRGCs is proven to cause SAD. For example, the presence of glaucoma is associated with an elevated susceptibility to mood and sleep disorders. Rev-erbα-melanopsin rhythms and retinal inflammation

Retinal inflammation is a pathological hallmark of diverse retinopathies, including uveitis, AMD, and DR. Chronic inflammation leads to retinal damage and functional deterioration, accompanied by the development of neovascularization and fibrosis.43 Currently, corticosteroids are widely used as the primary therapeutic option for managing retinal inflammation. However, their prolonged usage will cause systemic and ocular complications such as glaucoma and cataracts.168 Therefore, there is an urgent need to search for safe therapeutic approaches to address retinal inflammation.43 Enthusiastically, studies have shown that Rev-erbα can alleviate retinal inflammation by regulating the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), a family of transcription factors that are widely present in diverse cell types (Figure 6). In the retina, the NF-κB signaling pathway participates in numerous physiological and pathological activities, including neuroprotection, angiogenesis, inflammation, immune regulation, and cell apoptosis.169 Inhibiting NF-κB activity can reduce the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α and alleviate the pathological changes in DR, glaucoma, optic neuropathy, and AMD.43,170 REV-ERBα deficiency exacerbates the production of pro-inflammatory factors and toxin-induced inflammation through the NF-κB pathway.43 Besides, Rev-erbα can also inhibit the circadian oscillation of the NLR family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome in the retina [1,107]. Correspondingly, inhibition of Rev-erbα not only facilitates the transition of microglia from an M1 phenotype to an M2 phenotype, but also inhibits the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in the central nervous system.77 Furthermore, the Bmal1 or Rev-erbα defects can promote the expression of complement genes C4b and C3 in astrocytes. These complements will activate the microglia and enhance synaptic phagocytosis.171 On the other hand, Rev-erbα agonist SR9009 demonstrates inhibitory effects on microglial activation in the nigrostriatal system of mice.77 Interestingly, pharmacological stimulation of Rev-erbα can also mitigate the retinal inflammation induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and hinder the activation of microglia.172 In primary retinal microglia cultures stimulated with LPS, the NF-κB signaling pathway is inhibited by SR9009, resulting in reduced expression of inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, SR9009 administration leads to increased levels of CD206 (a marker for the M2 phenotype), higher proportions of ramified microglia, and reduced expression of Hmga2 (a pro-inflammatory gene).43 To sum up, Rev-erbα exerts anti-inflammatory effects on the retina and, more broadly, on the entire nervous system, making it an intriguing subject for further investigation into its underlying mechanism. Moreover, ipRGCs can affect retinal neuroinflammation through modulating melatonin secretion.173 The clock rhythm-dependent expression product, melatonin, exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.174 Melatonin activates genomic effects mediated by RORα nuclear receptors and attenuates NF-κB activation through SIRT1 deacetylation. Translational perspectives and limitations of chronotherapy therapy

The discovery of circadian rhythm has led to more profound insights into the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases. However, most of the current research on clock gene therapy is restricted to cell and animal experiments, and its implementation into the clinic needs to be further explored. At present, treatments for ipRGCs have been initially applied in clinical practice, such as blue light therapy, which can significantly improve the quality of life of patients with Parkinson’s disease, depression, and sleep disorders.175 However, for patients with severe retinal ganglion cell layer damage, the reduction of ipRGCs may not be restored to receive sufficient light signals. Furthermore, when the transmission of light signals is affected, the expression of Rev-erbα may decrease not only in the retina but throughout the body. This could exacerbate circadian rhythm disorders and pathological conditions. For instance, age-related lens yellowing significantly reduces the transmission of blue light, thereby reducing the activation of melanopsin-positive ipRGCs and affecting the expression of clock genes.176 Patients with cataracts, due to the reduced transmission of blue light, may experience circadian rhythm disorders and a decline in sleep quality.176,177 Therefore, chronotherapy may be a new solution to deal with this kind of disease.

Chronotherapy is based on the biological clock, which involves determining the optimal timing for drug administration within a precise circadian time window in order to enhance pharmacokinetic properties, improve drug efficacy, and minimize toxicity.178 It plays a crucial role in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases (such as hypertension) and autoimmune diseases (such as rheumatoid arthritis) that are influenced by circadian rhythm.179 Small molecule clock regulators (SMMCCs) refer to a class of chemicals capable of modulating the biological clock in organisms. These small molecules can function as agonists, inverse agonists, or antagonists by interacting with core or non-core proteins of the circadian clock system, thereby influencing its physiological effects.180 SR9009 and SR9011 act as selective agonists for Rev-erbα, while SR8278 functions specifically as an antagonist for Rev-erbα.181 Treatment using SMMCCs enables direct regulation of the circadian rhythm and optimization of drug therapy outcomes (Table 3). For instance, alterations in endogenous clock activity affecting dopamine synthesis in human striatal precursors may result in poor response to conventional therapeutic doses among patients with Parkinson’s disease.191 The utilization of SMMCCs for drug delivery allows fine-tuning of the patient’s dopamine system. By identifying and utilizing biomarkers associated with circadian rhythms, more personalized treatment options can be offered to individuals with Parkinson’s disease.192 In these patients with impaired ipRGCs, such as optic nerve degeneration caused by Alzheimer’s disease, the amount of melanopsin is not sufficient to output sufficient light signals to the SCN.193 Phototherapy may not be effective for these severe conditions. Admittedly, there are enormous challenges in the transformation of SMMCC into clinical application. Firstly, most studies focused on cell and animal experiments, without sufficient clinical treatment data. Moreover, as the circadian clock is closely related to drug metabolism, the use of SMMCC may affect the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) process of co-administered drugs. These disturbances would affect the efficacy and safety of drugs. Rev-erbα has been suggested to regulate the drug-metabolizing enzymes such as Cyp2b10 and Ugt1a9.194 The alterations in the body’s circadian rhythm caused by SMMCC may affect the liver’s rate of drug metabolism, thereby increasing or decreasing the efficacy of the drug.195 Therefore, further clinical studies are needed to evaluate the SMMCC-mediated effects on the efficacy of other co-administered drugs.

In the chronotherapy for retinal-related diseases, multiple key factors significantly influence the effectiveness and safety of treatment strategies. First of all, the diversity of ipRGC subtypes constitutes a core challenge. The human retina contains approximately 7000 melanopsin immunoreactive RGCs. Relatively few of these are M3 cells, which are not added to the M5 subtype and are newly labeled with “gigantic M1 (GM1)” and “gigantic displaced M1 (GDM1)” in addition to the M1 subtype.196,197 The differences in the phenotypes and function of ipRGCs may affect the outcome of chronotherapy. For instance, the Brn3b-positive M1 subtype mainly projects to the anterior nucleus of the superior colliculus to mediate the pupillary light reflex, while the Brn3b-negative M1 subtype projects to the SCN to govern circadian rhythm light synchronization.198 The M6 subtype, as the most abundant type, has a unique double-layer dendritic structure. Different subtypes are coupled with various non-myelinated cells through gap junctions and may release different neuromodulators, such as PACAP and glutamate, thereby exerting diverse regulatory effects on local retinal circuits.199 This functional heterogeneity implies that light stimulation or drug intervention needs to precisely target specific subtypes; otherwise, it may not effectively regulate the target pathway.200 Secondly, the differences in chronotype among individuals directly affect the choice of treatment timing. Research shows that patients with AMD and glaucoma have significant changes in their circadian preferences, and the melatonin secretion rhythm and ipRGC function of patients with DR also show individualized abnormalities.201,202 If drug administration or light exposure time is not customized according to the individual’s biological clock phase, it may weaken the therapeutic effect or even exacerbate the rhythm disorder. The differences in drug sensitivity caused by genetic background also need to be taken seriously. The retinal autonomous biological clock is regulated by core clock genes such as Clock and Bmal1, and their polymorphisms or mutations, such as the P23H rhodopsin mutation, can change the retinal response to light stimulation or drugs.203 For example, abnormal clock gene function not only affects the survival of photoreceptors but may also amplify oxidative stress and metabolic pathway disorders, causing the same dose of phototherapy or drugs to produce different effects in different genetic backgrounds.204 In addition, the expression differences of subtype-specific transcription factors of ipRGC further increase the complexity of individualized treatment. Regarding long-term risks, abnormal light exposure or improper circadian rhythm intervention may disrupt the autonomous clock in the retina, leading to transcriptional rhythm disorders, accumulation of reactive oxygen species, and neuroinflammation. Although conventional therapies such as high-dose anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) do not directly damage ipRGCs, they may cause retinal inflammation and death of common RGCs, suggesting that chronotherapy requires a balance between short-term benefits and long-term maintenance of retinal homeostasis.205 Notably, ipRGCs are damaged early in various neurodegenerative diseases, and the degree of their functional preservation can serve as a biomarker for disease progression, providing an objective indicator for the efficacy monitoring of chronotherapy.205,206 In addition, the clock rhythm variability among different species is also a major obstacle limiting the clinical translation. For instance, the oscillation period of human developmental rhythm is approximately three times longer than that of mice, which may lead to differences in the timing, dose, and side effects of SMMCC administration between patients and animal experiments.207 It is worth noting that the acrophase of Rev-erbα is not synchronized between nocturnal animals and diurnal humans, and these pathways may also be tissue-dependent. A recent study has shown that the expression peak of Rev-erbα in diurnal humans occurs in the early morning, in sync with awakening and the initiation of daytime activities. Its rhythmic intensity and phase are highly dependent on the actual natural light exposure and the amount of daytime physical activity an individual receives, and are regulated by genetic background.208 In contrast, the peak of Rev-erbα in nocturnal rodents is located in the middle of the experimental light period, mainly driven directly by artificial light-dark cycles, with relatively minor contributions from behavior.208,209 Therefore, the feasibility of chronotherapy for circadian rhythm disorders needs to be reassessed based on the endogenous biological clock within the human body.

Conclusions

The dysregulation of the retinal circadian clock is associated with various retinal diseases, including AMD, DR, and retinopathy of prematurity, and so forth. Chronotherapy targeting the retinal circadian clock may act as a potential treatment strategy for these diseases. The pathologic process of degenerative retinopathies is frequently accompanied by disturbances in circadian rhythms, leading to abnormal hormone secretion, oxidative stress, mood disorders, and inflammatory damage. Rev-erbα and melanopsin play critical roles in regulating circadian rhythms. Rev-erbα participates in the retinal inflammatory response and autophagy, while also adjusting the expression of other clock genes. Melanopsin is a photosensitive protein that modulates mood and hormone secretion upon receiving light signals. They jointly regulate dopamine and melatonin secretion which are involved in antioxidant defense and emotional activities. In degenerative retinopathy, the death of ipRGCs disturbs the transmission of rhythmic signals to the SCN and causes abnormal clock gene expression. Even with effective control of primary retinal lesions, secondary clock disruption still induces chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and mood disorders. The clock rhythm disorder can be corrected by adjusting the light intensity or clock gene expression. Chronotherapy, which targets the retina’s internal circadian system, provides a new approach to improve visual function and retard retinal disease progression. Future developments in drug delivery timing, personalized time-based treatments, and integration with emerging technologies would show strong potential for managing visual impairments by aligning retinal and external rhythms.
