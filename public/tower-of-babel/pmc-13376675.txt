Iron availability modulates bacteria carbon cycling in the mesopelagic
Authors: Travis Mellett, Patrick Monreal, Korinna Kunde, Lauren E Manck, Kelsy Cain, François Ribalet, Matthew J Church, E Virginia Armbrust, Randelle M Bundy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mesopelagic zone is an important boundary between the surface and the deep ocean. Heterotrophic bacteria in this region impact the attenuation of organic carbon (Corg) through its consumption and conversion back to inorganic carbon. While iron (Fe) is known to limit primary production, the impact of Fe on mesopelagic heterotrophic bacteria growth and Corg degradation is less understood. This study demonstrates serial Fe-limitation and Fe-Corg colimitation of bacteria growth within mesopelagic waters across an oligotrophic and a productive oceanic region. The addition of Fe increased the total Corg demand of mesopelagic bacteria. When Corg was supplied without additional Fe, bacterial growth efficiencies declined and production of Fe-specific chelators (siderophores) increased. Mesopelagic bacteria exhibited cellular Fe quotas substantially higher than those previously reported in surface waters and showed high plasticity in intracellular Fe when Corg stimulated growth. These findings reveal a dynamic coupling between Fe and Corg availability that may regulate bacterial metabolism in parts of the mesopelagic, with implications for basin-scale differences in Corg remineralization and Fe cycling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49839-8.Subject terms: Biogeochemistry, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The mesopelagic zone (~ 200–1000 m), often called the “twilight zone”, is the region between the sunlit surface ocean and deep sea where light is insufficient to support photosynthesis. It is characterized by increasing pressure, higher availability of inorganic nutrients, and decreasing temperatures. This region represents an important transition zone between the surface ocean where phytoplankton fix inorganic carbon, including carbon dioxide (CO2), into organic carbon (henceforth “Corg”) and the deep ocean where carbon can remain sequestered for long timescales. The organic matter generated in the surface reaches the mesopelagic and deep ocean through gravitational settling of particles, zooplankton migration and excretion, and the physical mixing of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) below the winter mixed layer1,2. Most of the Corg produced in the surface ocean is degraded above or within the upper mesopelagic (< 500 m), where it may be returned to the atmosphere as CO2 on seasonal or decadal timescales. However, if Corg persists through the mesopelagic to be remineralized in the deep bathypelagic (1000–4000 m) and abyssal (> 4000 m) ocean, it can be sequestered from the atmosphere for centuries2–4. Thus, understanding the factors that control the degradation of Corg in the mesopelagic are directly connected to the mechanisms governing its delivery to the deep ocean and its long-term storage in the global ocean.

Heterotrophic bacteria are important contributors to the consumption of organic matter5,6 in the mesopelagic, thereby influencing the transfer efficiency of Corg to the deep ocean7. Although bacteria biomass and growth can be limited by the availability of labile Corg substrates, iron (Fe) can also impact growth. Fe is a critical micronutrient because it is a cofactor in numerous enzymes. Up to ~ 90% of cellular Fe in heterotrophic bacteria may be contained in respiratory enzymes (e.g. cytochrome b1 and oxidase complexes)8,9. When Fe availability is insufficient, bacteria may bypass some Fe-intensive enzymatic pathways, altering respiration efficiency9–12 and Corg cycling13,14. Where Fe concentrations are exceedingly low, some bacteria produce and secrete Fe-specific chelators called siderophores. Siderophore synthesis is tightly regulated by intracellular Fe availability15 and typically increases under Fe stress, whether due to low Fe availability or poor Fe bioavailability16. Elevated siderophore concentrations in seawater have been used as a biomarker for bacterial Fe stress17.

Growing evidence suggests that Fe availability may influence heterotrophic bacterial metabolism in the mesopelagic. Dissolved Fe concentrations in the mesopelagic zone are low relative to macronutrients nitrate (N) and phosphate (P), reflecting the deeper regeneration length scale of Fe compared to N and P17. Multiple recent lines of evidence point to high bacterial Fe demand, and possible Fe stress, in this region. Elevated siderophore concentrations have been observed throughout the mesopelagic in the central Pacific17,18, and an increased abundance of siderophore cellular transport proteins has been observed compared to surface waters19. Additionally, bacterial Fe uptake rates and cellular quotas were observed to be higher in the mesopelagic than in the surface in one study20. Together, these findings indicate substantial Fe demand by bacteria in the mesopelagic. However, the drivers for elevated Fe demand and its consequences for bacteria growth and Corg cycling remain poorly understood.

In this study, we provide experimental evidence that Fe availability influences the growth and Corg consumption of mesopelagic bacteria. We conducted a series of field experiments on two research expeditions (called Gradients 4 and 5; Fig. 1) spanning the eastern subtropical North Pacific to the equatorial upwelling zone. Experiments were conducted in the low-productivity waters of the subtropical gyre and the higher productivity waters of the equatorial upwelling zone, encompassing nearly an order of magnitude difference in particulate organic carbon flux21. We consistently observed either co-limitation of Fe and Corg or serial limitation of Fe when Corg was added to heterotrophic bacteria growth. In Corg replete but Fe-stressed conditions, mesopelagic bacteria produced more siderophores per cell and exhibited lower growth efficiencies. Mesopelagic bacteria had elevated cellular Fe quotas compared to bacteria in the surface ocean as observed in another study20, consistent with high Fe demand and cellular storage. Plasticity in cellular Fe quotas in response to additions of Corg indicated mesopelagic bacteria may use Fe storage to grow quickly when Corg was available. Overall, these experiments shed insights into how and why mesopelagic bacteria may become Fe stressed, and how their growth could impact the mesopelagic Fe and Corg cycles.

Results

Heterotrophic bacteria growth response

Fe and Corg addition experiments were conducted at several depths in mesopelagic waters (Fig. 1), with the goal of testing whether Fe availability altered heterotrophic bacterial growth and Corg consumption. Seawater was amended with either 1 nM 57Fe (+ Fe), Corg in the form of glucose (+ Glu) or glucosamine (+ Gluam), and both 57Fe and Corg (+ FeGlu and +FeGluam). Experiments were conducted with either high Corg availability (60 µM Corg) or lower Corg availability (10 µM Corg). These model organic substrates were chosen to explore whether Fe had an impact on heterotrophic bacteria growth when there was differences in the relative availability and lability of Corg.

The availability of Fe consistently stimulated mesopelagic heterotrophic bacteria growth and biomass in experimental treatments that had been amended with Corg. Addition of 1 nM Fe alone did not significantly alter bacteria growth rates from control treatments. When Fe was amended along with high concentrations of glucose (60 µM Corg), Fe and Corg together significantly increased bacteria cell abundances, net growth rates, and bacteria biomass compared to additions of Corg alone (Fig. 2a,c). In the series of experiments at lower Corg concentrations (10 µM Corg), cell abundances, growth rates, and bacteria biomass were comparable for both + Glu and +FeGlu treatments (Fig. 2b,d). Altogether, the addition of Fe with glucose significantly stimulated bacterial growth compared to glucose alone (Wilcoxon p < 0.001, n = 21). However, the growth response from Fe and Corg addition varied based on the amount added, the substrate (glucose versus glucosamine), location (gyre versus equator), and depth (Figs. S1, S2).

Within the subtropical gyre, there were significant differences in the response of the bacteria community within a relatively narrow depth range (200–450 m) of the mesopelagic. For example, in experiments conducted using water from 200 m, there was no significant growth response in the + Glu treatment compared to the unamended control (Fig. S1). However, growth rates and total bacteria biomass significantly increased in the +FeGlu treatment (p < 0.0001). These results are consistent with Fe-C colimitation of heterotrophic bacteria growth. Slightly deeper (250 m), there was a bacteria growth response in both the + Glu and +FeGlu treatments relative to the control (p < 0.0001), but there was no significant difference between the Corg alone and combined Fe and Corg treatments, consistent with Corg limitation. At the deepest depth (450 m), both + Glu and +FeGlu showed an increase in bacteria biomass compared to the control (p = 0.0008 and p < 0.0001, respectively), but +FeGlu resulted in 60-fold greater bacteria biomass and 4-fold greater net growth rate compared to + Glu (p < 0.0001; Fig. S1), indicating serial Fe limitation.

Similar patterns in the bacteria growth response were observed in the experiments conducted at the Equator. In treatments with high concentrations of glucose (60 µM Corg), total bacteria biomass and net growth rates were significantly greater than the control (p < 0.0001). However, the +FeGlu treatment had significantly higher bacteria biomass and net growth rates compared to + Glu (100 m, p = 0.0007; 300 m p = 0.0098; Fig. S2), consistent with serial limitation of Fe. In the experiments with lower Corg additions (10 µM C), the biomass response was significantly greater in + Glu and +FeGlu treatments than in the controls (p < 0.0001). However, no significant difference in bacterial biomass and net growth rates was observed between + Glu and +FeGlu treatments at lower Corg amendments (Fig. 2b,d), indicating Fe did not impact bacteria growth at low Corg additions. The bacteria biomass response in the glucosamine (+ Gluam) treatments was not significantly different from the controls at either depth, but at the shallowest depth (100 m) the +FeGluam treatment had significantly higher bacterial biomass compared to the control (p = 0.0037; Fig. S2), indicating Fe-Corg co-limitation of growth in these treatments.

In all the experiments, large, fast-growing bacteria were those that responded to nutrient addition. At the onset of the experiments, biomass of both small and large bacteria (see “Flow cytometry” methods) was comparable (equivalent to biomass concentrations of 1–3 µg C L− 1). However, large bacteria became dominant in treatments amended with Corg, with biomass increasing to 10–250 µg C L− 1 (Fig. S3). These results suggest the biological response was dominated by high nucleic acid, copiotrophic bacteria (e.g. Alteramonas macleodii22,23, Psuedoaltermonas19, often associated with particles and high nutrient environments.

Bacterial metabolic response

Estimates of bacteria carbon demand and growth efficiency gave additional insights into the effect of Fe addition on growth and metabolism (see “Methods”). When the dissolved organic carbon (DOC) that was added was consumed during each experiment, it could either be respired by the bacteria or incorporated into new bacterial biomass. Together these two processes represent the total Corg demand (see Total Carbon Demand methods). In the subtropical gyre, total Corg demand typically increased when Fe was added (Fig. S4). At the Equator, there was only a subtle increase in the total Corg demand when Fe was added at 100 m, but it was not statistically significant (Fig. S5). Total Corg demand along with estimated bacterial biomass from the experiments, enabled us to calculate bacterial growth efficiencies (BGE; see “Methods”), or the fraction of Corg consumed that is converted to biomass. In our experiments, BGE averaged 0.15 in the + Glu treatments, and 0.35 in the +FeGlu treatments (p = 0.097, n = 37; Fig. 3a). BGEs in the + Glu varied depending on how much Corg was added, with treatments amended with low concentrations of Corg having BGE ≥0.35, while BGE were < 0.2 in the higher Corg treatments (p = 0.002, n = 28). In contrast, BGE in the +FeGlu treatments were similar and ranged from 0.3 to 0.4 across all experiments (Fig. 3a).

Siderophores, biomarkers for potential Fe stress17, were measured in-situ and at the end of each experiment. In-situ average concentrations of siderophores during the Gradients 4 cruise were similar across depth and location (5.1 ± 1.7 pM; Table S2). During the Gradients 5 cruise, siderophore concentrations in the mesopelagic of the Equator were higher, averaging 20 ± 1.9 pM. Siderophore concentrations in the experiments were greater than in-situ concentrations when glucose stimulated bacterial growth (+ Glu, +FeGlu; Fig. S6). Typically, higher concentrations were observed in + Glu treatments compared to +FeGlu, when normalized to bacteria cells (Fig. 3b; Table S2), but were not statistically significant. Chemical characterization of siderophores from select gyre (250 m) and Equator (300 m) experiments during Gradients 4 revealed that the + Glu treatment had higher concentrations and a greater diversity of siderophores compared to +FeGlu treatments (Fig. S6a,c). Siderophores like fimsbactins, amphibactins, and several novel siderophores were all present in high proportions of the total siderophore pool (Fig. S6b,d). Fimsbactins and/or amphibactins comprised more than half of the siderophores in the + Glu treatments, while an unknown siderophore (m/z = 968.342) dominated (34–47%) of the siderophore pool in the +FeGlu treatments.

Particulate Fe and P

Biogenic particulate (> 0.2 μm) Fe and P concentrations were quantified to estimate heterotrophic bacterial cellular quotas and quantify the amount of labeled 57Fe uptake by cells (see Particulate biogenic Fe and P methods). In the unamended control treatments, concentrations of particulate Fe ranged from 0.15 to 0.3 nM in the Gradients 4 experiments and 0.3 to 0.76 nM in the Gradients 5 experiments (Figs. S7, S8). In-situ particulate P concentrations were typically < 10 nM, but as high as 28 ± 7.5 nM across both cruises (Figs. S7, S8).

There was a positive relationship between particulate P and bacterial biomass (R2 = 0.83, n = 103, p < 0.0001; Fig. S9a), consistent with bacterial biomass driving changes in the particulate P pool. Similarly, increases in particulate 57Fe correlated with changes in bacterial biomass (R2 = 0.76, n = 48, p < 0.0001; Fig. S9b) in treatments with 57Fe amendments (+ Fe, +FeGlu, +FeGluam). Approximately 20–30% of the added Fe was found in the particulate Fe pool by the end of the experiments. In contrast, the particulate 56Fe pool showed no significant relationship with bacterial biomass (n = 102, p = 0.6; Fig. S9c). Hence, changes we observed in the total particulate Fe pool over time in the experiments were attributable to uptake of the added 57Fe (Fig. S9d) and are consistent with the incorporation of 57Fe and P into bacterial biomass. We used the biogenic particulate Fe, P, and C (see “Flow cytometry” methods) measurements to estimate cellular quotas of the bacteria in-situ and in our experiments. Assuming all biogenic Fe was sourced from bacteria (assumed as a maximum quota), bacteria Fe quotas from in-situ mesopelagic waters ranged from 577 to 3214 µmol Fe: mol C (or 40–170 mmol Fe: mol P; Fig. 4a; Fig. S10). In the experimental treatments, the estimated quotas showed a range spanning three orders of magnitude, indicating high plasticity with bacterial growth (Fig. 4a). The Fe: C quotas declined as bacteria carbon biomass accumulated, and were lowest at the highest bacteria biomass concentrations, with Fe: C ~ 10 µmol: mol (Fig. 4a).

Discussion

Iron and organic carbon influence bacterial growth in mesopelagic waters

These experiments indicated bacterial growth in the mesopelagic in this region may be either co-limited by Fe and Corg or serially limited by Fe. Most previous research has focused on how Fe availability impacts phytoplankton growth24–26, and those studies which have examined the Fe requirements of heterotrophic bacteria have often focused on surface waters and the competition between bacteria and phytoplankton for Fe9,13,27–31. Few studies have investigated how Fe impacts bacterial growth within the mesopelagic zone, where emerging evidence suggests Fe stress may occur, or there is a high demand for Fe17–20. Our experiments provide a mechanistic understanding about how Fe impacts bacteria in the mesopelagic zone across a biogeochemical and productivity gradient in the subtropical North Pacific.

The bacterial growth response varied across depths and regions but largely demonstrated an effect of serial Fe limitation on heterotrophic bacteria growth. Serial Fe limitation was observed in most experiments, whereas Fe-Corg co-limitation was detected only at the shallowest mesopelagic depth within the subtropical gyre (200 m). Within the gyre, two of the three depths showed a strong separation between +FeGlu and + Glu treatments, while the intermediate depth (250 m) showed a response to Corg alone. The vertical heterogeneity of the growth response within the mesopelagic is notable, and could be related to small differences in the relative availability of Fe and Corg, or distinct microbial communities at each depth. For example, the accumulation of nitrite at the end of the experiment at 250 m suggests that a distinct microbial community may have responded at this depth compared to the others (Fig. S11). While there were slight differences in the bacteria growth response to Fe and Corg addition with depth, the most common observation was that added Fe only impacted bacteria growth when sufficient Corg was present.

The evidence of serial Fe limitation in our experiments indicates that mesopelagic bacteria growth is primarily limited by Corg availability. Similar dynamics have been observed in several studies from surface waters and in a similar study in the upper mesopelagic32. In Southern Ocean surface waters, bacterial production did not increase with the addition of Fe but did increase when Fe and Corg were added together13,27. Although Fe did not have a primary effect on bacteria growth in our experiments, the overall low initial dissolved Fe concentrations in our experiments and the lack of significant 56Fe uptake indicated the in-situ Fe was not bioavailable. Although there was no growth response to Fe alone, the addition of bioavailable Fe increased the total carbon demand (Figs. S4, S5) and bacteria growth efficiencies (Fig. 3a). The lower growth efficiencies we observed in the absence of amended Fe could be due to several possibilities. For example, the bacteria community could be using alternative Corg metabolic pathways, such as the glyoxylate shunt, which has been shown to decrease growth efficiency in low Fe conditions11. Copiotrophic bacteria have also been estimated to allocate as much as 20–30% of cellular Corg to siderophore production under low Fe conditions12, and the cell-normalized siderophore concentrations we measured were consistently higher in the + Glu treatments compared to +FeGlu (Fig. 3b; Table S2), which could also decrease BGE. While we detected no significant relationship between cell-normalized siderophore production (p = 0.8, n = 18) or total siderophore concentrations (p = 0.43, n = 18) and BGE, siderophore synthesis may nonetheless represent an important metabolic cost by bacteria in the mesopelagic. The low Fe conditions of the mesopelagic could also explain seasonal changes in siderophore concentrations coinciding with spring and summer blooms and the availability of Corg in the subtropical Pacific17,18.

High and plastic iron quotas of mesopelagic bacteria

One of the most surprising results from our experiments were the high estimated in-situ Fe quotas of mesopelagic bacteria, and the huge range in Fe quotas we observed across a gradient in Corg availability (Fig. 4). In heterotrophic bacteria, cellular Fe is incorporated into essential enzymes involved in respiration and Corg metabolism, as well as proteins associated with oxidative stress defense (e.g. superoxide dismutase, catalase-peroxidase) and storage proteins (e.g. bacterioferritin19. Fe is also required for key enzymes mediating nitrogen transformations in the mesopelagic and oxygen minimum zones (e.g. nitrite oxioreductase33). Estimating heterotrophic bacteria Fe quotas in natural systems is challenging, and relatively few estimates exist (Table S3). In marine systems, quotas have been estimated using two primary approaches: (1) uptake measurements with radiolabeled 55Fe9,11,20,27, and (2) bulk metal analyses of biomass19 (Table S3). Here, we combined aspects of both methods. We traced the incorporation of amended 57Fe into biomass, and measured bulk particulate Fe and P in our incubations. As an initial approximation, we assumed all labile particulate Fe and P was associated with bacterial biomass. This likely overestimates true cellular quotas, as the bulk measurements may include detrital and authigenic Fe pools34, though the authigenic pools are likely minimal in this study region35. Accordingly, our in-situ particulate-based estimates likely represent maximum Fe quotas for bacteria (Fig. 4a). The maximum in-situ quotas ranged from 577 to 3214 µmol Fe: mol C (or 40–170 mmol Fe: mol P). These estimates were higher, but also overlap with previous culture studies and field experiments which have reported highly variable cellular Fe quotas, spanning from 0.43 ± 0.1 µmol Fe: mol C to as high as 591 ± 208 µmol Fe: mol C11,27 (Table S3). Our estimates also overlap with recent measurements of short-term 55Fe uptake measurements from mesopelagic bacteria20, consistent with elevated Fe demand in this region.

The in-situ Fe quotas exhibited substantial plasticity in response to bacterial growth, spanning nearly three orders of magnitude across experiments (Fig. 4a). This relationship resembled a power-law, commonly used to describe macromolecule size-scaling relationships with biomass36,37. To better constrain cellular Fe requirements, we modeled quotas as a function of observed net cell divisions using an exponential decay framework (see “Methods”). This approach distinguishes between a storage quota (Qs), representing Fe that can support growth without additional Fe uptake from the environment, and a maintenance quota (Qo), representing the quota sustained and maintained when Fe and Corg are replete (Qo; Fig. 4b). Across all experiments, the mean storage quota (Qs) based on the model was estimated to be equal to 687 ± 22 µmol Fe: mol C (Fig. 4b). This value is at the lower end of our particulate-based maximum estimates but remains substantially higher than most culture-derived quotas from surface ocean bacteria (Table S3). When compared to the in-situ total labile particulate Fe pool, these estimates suggest up to ~ 50% of in-situ labile particulate Fe in the mesopelagic water column could reside within bacteria biomass. This indicates mesopelagic bacteria may store Fe intracellularly, representing a potentially important biogenic Fe reservoir and an underappreciated sink of dissolved Fe in the mesopelagic ocean.

The high Fe quotas observed in mesopelagic bacteria are strong evidence for Fe storage and the plasticity in quotas provide a basis to interpret the serial Fe limitation observed in these experiments. For example, the decay model indicates that on average approximately three cell divisions (ln(Qs/Qo) = 3.46) could be supported by stored Fe before additional Fe uptake is required (Fig. 4b). The nonlinear decline in quotas with increasing biomass in Corg-amended treatments (Fig. 4a) suggests mesopelagic bacteria may engage in luxury uptake when Fe is available, subsequently drawing down stored Fe during periods of enhanced Corg supply. High Fe storage capacity has been observed in the cultured marine bacteria Pseudoalteromonas, with an elevated Fe quota of 84 µmol Fe: mol C19 attributed to multiple copies of the Fe storage protein bacterioferritin19. Similarly, Escherichia coli was observed to increase Fe acquisition and Fe stress proteins38 under low Corg availability, consistent with luxury consumption to maximize fitness across fluctuating environments39. In the mesopelagic, where labile Corg is scarce and inputs are episodic (i.e. export events, particle hotspots, cellular lysates), intracellular Fe storage could confer a competitive advantage. Although luxury Fe uptake and Fe storage has been documented in some pennate diatoms in coastal upwelling systems40 and in Antarctic sea-ice diatoms41, comparable evidence in marine bacteria has not previously been described to our knowledge.

Variations in growth efficiency and siderophore production across Corg treatments suggest a transition to Fe stress as intracellular quotas decline (Fig. 4c). The modeled maintenance quota (Qo) in Fe-and Corg-replete treatments (+ FeGlu) was 21.7 ± 3.1 µmol Fe: mol C (Fig. 4b; red shading). This value may represent a physiological Fe-stress threshold, below which bacteria must allocate increasing metabolic resources toward Fe acquisition (Fig. 4c). Notably, this estimate is near thresholds reported from similar experiments conducted in surface waters in the Southern Ocean (37 µmol Fe: mol C27) and from metagenomic analyses in the California Current (50 µmol Fe: mol C31). Such thresholds provide useful environmental context for interpreting our experimental results and assessing the potential for bacteria Fe stress in mesopelagic bacteria.

Framing the experimental results in a broader oceanographic perspective

The results from these experiments demonstrated that the relative availability of Fe and Corg impacted bacteria growth within the mesopelagic. However, it is unknown if the observations from these experiments apply more widely to the mesopelagic throughout the Pacific Ocean, or other ocean basins, and whether the complexity of organic substrates, differences in Fe requirements of free-living versus particle-associated bacteria, or other factors, would change the findings demonstrated here42. Particle interceptor trap data has not observed a clear relationship between Fe-limited ocean regimes and particulate carbon flux, with thermocline depth considered the primary factor controlling microbial mediated flux43,44. Similarly, pressure is known to slow metabolic rates of particle attached and free-living bacteria in the deep ocean45, and this was not explicitly controlled in our experiments. These factors will be very important to consider in on-going studies. In this section, we make some hypotheses for where the relative availability of Fe to Corg may have an impact on bacteria growth, with implications for both the cycling of Fe and carbon. We will stress however, that these are simplifications, though useful for generating hypotheses about possible locations of bacteria Fe stress, and for comparing to other published oceanographic observations.

Using profiles from two representative ocean basins sampled by the GEOTRACES (Pacific and Atlantic), we can estimate water column Fe: Corg ratios, reflecting their relative availability to microorganisms. The marine organic matter pool consists of both particulate and dissolved organic matter, and while there are important differences between the physical behavior and composition of these operationally defined pools, the dissolved inventory dominates the total pool and is useful for our first-order calculations. If we assume that the fraction of DOC that declines exponentially within the mesopelagic is representative of the semi-labile fraction of Corg that is likely available for uptake (Fig. 5a), then we can estimate the change in Corg availability with depth (see “Methods”). Combined with the increases in dissolved Fe with depth observed in these same profiles, we can generate an estimate of Fe: Corg availability in the upper mesopelagic at these two representative open ocean stations (Fig. S5b). This Fe: Corg gradient varies by nearly an order of magnitude between the North Atlantic station and the South Pacific station, due largely to basin-scale differences in dissolved Fe. Ratios range from ~ 2–8 µmol Fe: mol C in the surface to 60–390 µmol Fe: mol C at 500 m depth (Fig. 5b). Interestingly, this range is comparable in magnitude to the estimated Fe quotas of bacteria in our experiments.

Using our estimate of the relative Fe: Corg availability in the water column and the Fe stress thresholds derived in this study (Fig. 4b) and others27,31 (20–50 µmol Fe: mol C) allows us to estimate a hypothetical depth horizon where heterotrophic bacteria may become Fe-stressed in these two representative oceanographic stations. If we only consider the mesopelagic, the depths where Fe: Corg availability falls below this threshold we would hypothesize that heterotrophic bacteria would be susceptible to Fe stress, whereas depths above this threshold would imply Corg limitation. Based on data from the South Pacific station, we estimate that heterotrophic bacteria would be Fe-stressed from the beginning of the mesopelagic zone (~ 120 m) to ~ 400 m (Fig. 5b). In contrast, in the North Atlantic station, we find the estimated water column Fe: Corg availability crosses the Fe-stress threshold value within the photic zone at ~ 100 m, so we would hypothesize that heterotrophic bacteria would not be Fe-stressed in the mesopelagic in this region.

These hypotheses are useful, because we can compare this simple prediction to existing water column siderophore data from the GEOTRACES program, as peaks in siderophore concentrations in the water column should indicate Fe stress. Siderophore samples binned in 50 m depth intervals from the entire GP15 meridional section in the Pacific17 show a gradient in the upper mesopelagic and a maximum within the 350–400 m depth range (Fig. 5b; Table S4), around the same horizon where our estimated water column Fe: Corg availability would suggest heterotrophic bacteria transition from Fe-stress to persistent Corg limitation. This is suggestive, and is consistent with the other lines of evidence from the Pacific that bacteria in the mesopelagic are rapidly taking up Fe20 and that there are “hotspots” of siderophores throughout the upper mesopelagic in the North Pacific Ocean17.

The collective results from these experiments indicate Fe availability could impact marine bacteria growth in the mesopelagic and thus both impact our understanding of the Fe and Corg cycles in the mesopelagic. Nearly 90% of the Corg that sinks below the euphotic zone is thought to be consumed within the mesopelagic waters46, and transformed into microbial biomass and dissolved organic or inorganic carbon. The growth rates and efficiencies of heterotrophic bacteria in the mesopelagic impact the attenuation of Corg passing through the twilight zone, it is important to understand how the relative availability of other nutrients within this zone might impact the attenuation of carbon with depth. Amongst other factors controlling microbial growth in the mesopelagic such as temperature and pressure, Fe availability may be a factor that should be considered in the future. Significant uptake of dissolved Fe by heterotrophic bacteria in the mesopelagic has not been previously considered and could potentially impact vertical distributions of dissolved Fe and its regeneration length scale. The relationship between the relative Fe and Corg availability in the mesopelagic is important to constrain, particularly for future marine CO2 removal strategies employing Fe additions, as this could ultimately impact heterotrophic bacteria growth and respiration in the twilight zone and the fate of any exported Corg.

Methods

Incubation setup and design

Three incubation experiments from multiple depths within the mesopelagic were conducted during the Gradients 4 (Nov–Dec 2021) and Gradients 5 (Jan–Feb 2023) research cruises aboard the R/V Thomas G. Thompson. Both cruises crossed large natural biogeochemical gradients between the North Pacific subtropical gyre to the Equatorial upwelling zone (Table S1). Water was collected from stations spanning biogeochemical gradients, from the subtropical gyre (low nitrate, elevated Fe) to the north and equatorial upwelling region (elevated nitrate, low Fe) to the south (Fig. 1). All experiments were conducted using water collected below the deep chlorophyll maximum, where light was <1% surface flux of photosynthetic active radiation. During the Gradients 4 cruise, three experiments were conducted in the subtropical gyre at depths of 200, 250, and 450 m. The upper depth two depths are positioned within Subtropical Underwater, with the deepest experiment conducted within North Pacific Intermediate Water47. Two additional experiments were conducted in the equatorial waters at depths of 100 and 300 m (Fig. 1). During the Gradients 5 expedition, experiments were conducted at the Equator at 100 m and 300 m. Both of these depths are conducted within Equatorial Surface Waters and the shoaling of the Equatorial Undercurrent. Although dFe data is not available for all experiments, in-situ concentrations were typically all < 0.6 nM.

Water was collected at each station using 12-L Go-Flo (General Oceanics) bottles mounted to an epoxy coated trace-metal-clean rosette system. Bottles were moved into a Class-100 clean van prior to sampling. Approximately 1 L of seawater from each depth was subsampled for subsequent determinations of bacterial cell abundances (2 mL) and particulate trace metals and phosphorus in the initial conditions. Seawater for particulate matter concentrations were filtered on an acid-cleaned Teflon filtration rig (Savillex Corporation) with 0.2 μm polycarbonate track-etched (PCTE) filters. Filtrate from particulate samples was used for subsequent analyses of dissolved organic carbon (DOC), nutrients (nitrate + nitrite, phosphate, silicate, ammonium), and dissolved siderophores. To set-up the experimental treatments, seawater was prefiltered into 1 L polycarbonate bottles using an acid-cleaned 7 µm nylon mesh to remove large zooplankton. The incubation bottles were amended with 1 nM 57Fe, and/or 10 µM or 60 µM C in the form of glucose (Glu) or glucosamine (Gluam; 10 µM only), cleaned of trace-metals using Chelex-100 resin prior to amendment. All treatments were performed in triplicate. Bottles were incubated in the dark at 16 °C and harvested after 48-92 hours (Table S1). Incubations were terminated by subsampling for nutrients, DOC, dissolved siderophores, particulate Fe and phosphorous (P), and cell abundances at the end of the experiment. All measurements were sampled in triplicate by treatment with the exception of dissolved siderophores, which were pooled together with 1/3 of each triplicate to form a single sample per treatment.

Nutrients and DOC

Filtrate (0.2 µm PCTE) from the incubation experiments was subsampled into 30 mL low density polyethylene bottles for subsequent determination of nutrient concentrations. Additional filtrate was aliquoted into 30 mL glass bottles and frozen at − 20 °C for quantification of DOC concentrations. Analyses were conducted at the University of Washington School of Oceanography Marine Chemistry laboratory. Quantification of inorganic nutrients (phosphate, silicate, nitrate + nitrite, and ammonia) were measured colorimetrically on a Seal Analytical AA3 following Joint Global Ocean Flux Study (JGOFS) protocols48. Dissolved organic carbon was analyzed with a Shimadzu TOC-Vcsh DOC analyzer following JGOFS protocols48.

Flow cytometry

Seawater sampled for flow cytometric analyses of cell abundances was fixed to a 0.25% final concentration of glutaraldehyde and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Flow cytometry measurements were made on a BD Influx flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) equipped with two 10% neutral density filters, a 488 nm laser, and small particle detector option, and data collection was triggered by forward light scatter. One micron beads were added to each sample as an internal standard (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples for heterotrophic bacteria were run both unstained and stained with SYBR Green I (1:10,000 final dilution), incubated for at least 25 min on ice in the dark, prior to analyses. Additional unstained samples were analyzed to determine concentrations of chlorophyll-containing picocyanobacterial cells. Unstained samples were sequentially gated using the FCSplankton package in R (https://github.com/fribalet/FCSplankton). Synechococcus cells were manually gated based on forward scatter and florescence of phycoerythrin (580/30 nm emission). Prochlorococcus cells were manually gated on forward scatter and chlorophyll fluorescence (692/40 nm emission) using the set_gating_params() function. Heterotrophic bacteria (together with Prochlorococcus) cells were enumerated based on manual gating of forward scatter and SYBR Green fluorescence (530/40 nm emission). To deconvolve heterotrophic bacteria from Prochlorococcus, the particle size distributions of the unstained Prochloroccocus cells were used to correct the particle size distributions of stained heterotrophic bacteria + Prochlorococcus gated cells. This was done by binning each gated population by forward light scatter values and removing the amount of unstained Prochlorococcus cells per forward light scatter bin from the stained bacteria + Prochlorococcus bins, assuming staining does not significantly alter forward light scattering.

Cell size was inferred from individual particles based on flow cytometry light scatter measurements and the application of Mie light scatter theory to a simplified optical model49. This approach was calibrated using a set of polystyrene calibration beads of known refractive index and cell diameter, showing good agreement with Mie-predicted particle diameter. Assuming spherical particles, these measurements were converted to carbon quotas using the equation pgC cell−1 = 0.261 × Volume0.860 (Protist plankton < 3000 μm3)50. Although not explicitly tested on heterotrophic bacteria, these quota estimates align well with those from cyanobacteria phytoplankton cultures49 (see Ribalet et al. 2019 for details). The equivalent spherical diameters (ESD) of bacteria were determined using a threshold ESD of 0.475 μm for discriminating between small and large bacteria populations (Fig. S3a).

Particulate biogenic Fe and P

Each 1 L incubation bottle was filtered using an acid-cleaned Teflon filtration rig (Savillex Corporation) and 0.2 µm polycarbonate track etched (PCTE) filters that were cleaned in 10% (v/v) trace metal clean nitric acid, and 10% (v/v) trace metal clean hydrochloric acid, and held in a solution of pH 1.8 Milli-Q water until use. After filtering, the PCTE filters were folded, placed in a 2 mL polypropylene vial (Fisher Scientific) and frozen at − 20 °C until analysis. The filters were processed using a modified method from Berger et al.34 to target biogenic Fe and P within particles. The leach solution was 0.02M hydroxylamine hydrochloride and 25% (v/v) trace metal grade glacial acetic acid. Filters were thawed and placed in trace metal cleaned 15 mL falcon tubes, 5 mL of leach solution was added, and the tubes were placed in a 90 °C water bath for 10 min. After 10 min the tubes were removed and allowed to cool for 3 h and 50 min before the solution was syringe filtered through a 0.2 µm PTFE filter into a 10 mL PTFE vial (Savillex Corporation). Five mL of Milli-Q was used to rinse the tube and was filtered through the same syringe filter into the vial for a final volume of 10 mL. The vials were left on a hot plate in a positive pressure trace metal clean hood at 125 °C overnight to evaporate the supernatant. The following morning, the dried residue in each vial was dissolved with 15 mL 1 N HNO3 (Optima, Fisher Scientific) containing 1 ppb internal standards of indium and rhodium. The solutions were quantified for trace metals and phosphorus using a method of standard addition on an Element 2 XR at the University of Washington TraceLab in medium resolution and counting mode.

Dissolved siderophores

The remaining volume from the cellular quota filtration step was pooled from each treatment replicate for a single 1 L siderophore sample. The pooled filtrate was passed over a solid phase extraction (SPE; Bond Elut ENV, Agilent Technologies) column, and the columns were frozen (− 20 °C) until analysis. In the lab, SPE columns were extracted with methanol (Optima or distilled) and evaporated using a vacuum concentrator (SpeedVac, Thermo Scientific) to < 1 mL. The concentration and identity of discrete Fe-containing compounds, or putative siderophores, was determined using liquid chromatography (LC) with a combination of inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), following previously described methods51. Briefly, 50 µL sample aliquots spiked with 5 µM cyanocobalamin (Sigma-Aldrich) were injected through a ZORBAX-SB C18 trap column and then a ZORBAX-SB C18 working column (0.5 × 35 mm, 5 µm; Agilent Technologies) using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 LC system at a flow rate of 40 µL/min at 30 °C.

The chromatography had an initial 20 min gradient from 95% solvent A (Milli-Q with 5 mM ammonium formate; Sigma-Aldrich) and 5% solvent B (methanol with 5 mM ammonium formate) to 90% solvent B, followed by a 10 min isocratic step of 90% solvent B, then a 5 min gradient from 90% to 95% solvent B, an isocratic 5 min step at 95% solvent B, and a 15 min conditioning step at 5% solvent B prior to injecting the next sample. From the LC, samples were introduced into either the ICP-MS (iCap RQ; Thermo Scientific) in kinetic energy discrimination (KED) mode or the ESI-MS (Q-Exactive HF Orbitrap; Thermo Scientific), with MS1 scans collected in full positive mode with 120,000 mass resolution, 200–2000 m/z mass range, 1E6 AGC target, and 100 ms maximum injection time. MS2 scans were collected in data-dependent MS2 (dd-MS2) mode with 30,000 mass resolution, 1.0 m/z isolation window, 2E4 AGC target, 100 ms maximum injection time, 35% collision energy, and an in-house inclusion list containing the apo- and metal-bound masses of ~ 600 known Fe-metallophores. See Park et al. (2023) for additional details51.

The data from the LC-ICP-MS and LC-ESI-MS analyses was used to identify 56Fe and 57Fe peaks in LC-ICP-MS chromatograms using custom R scripts. Individual peak areas were calculated with standard curves of the siderophore ferrioxamine E (R2 > 0.99; Sigma-Aldrich). Following peak quantification, LC-ESI-MS data was used to identify masses (m/z) with similar retention times as LC-ICP-MS peaks. Raw data files were converted to mzXML format using MSConvert (Proteowizard), and processing was carried out with in-house R scripts based on previously-published workflows18,52,53. For each peak, the R package RaMS54 was used to subset data structures to a time window of ± 75 s of the peak’s ICP-MS retention time. Then, extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) were iteratively generated in 0.005 m/z increments for m/z’s 300–1800 and a peak shape score was calculated based on the integral of the squared intensity as a function of time35. Candidate masses with good scores were further investigated by searching for and evaluating the peak shape of apo masses, 13C isotopologues and Fe isotopologues. Candidate masses were identified as siderophores by the presence of two LC-ESI-MS EIC peaks at the LC-ICP-MS retention time of Fe, with Δm/z equal to the mass difference of the two most abundant Fe isotopes (56Fe/54Fe) and intensity ratios similar to the natural abundance ratios of the two isotopes.

Bacteria growth efficiency and total carbon demand

Bacteria growth efficiency (BGE) and total carbon demand were based on DOC consumption and estimates of bacterial biomass from each treatment. This calculation assumes that all DOC added to the incubation but not observed in the final sample was consumed by bacteria and represents the total carbon demand for each incubation. The total carbon demand represents the in-situ DOC concentrations from the depth of each incubation plus the DOC spike (+ 10 µM low C, + 60 µM high C) minus what was remaining at the end of the incubation (DOC final).

BGE was calculated using the total carbon demand and the biomass estimates measured at the end of each experiment using flow cytometry samples (see “Flow cytometry” for details), representing the fraction of total carbon demand which was converted into biomass (BGE = biomass production/total carbon demand). Statistical analyses

To examine whether bacterial carbon biomass varied significantly across experimental treatments, sampling depths, and incubation conditions, we employed a generalized linear model (GLM) approach. Initial data exploration revealed that bacterial biomass values were highly right-skewed, violating the normality assumption required for standard linear models. A log transformation improved but did not fully resolve this issue (Shapiro–Wilk test: W = 0.90, p-value < 0.0001). Therefore, we fitted a GLM with a Gamma distribution and log link function to mitigate right-skewed positive data. The model included treatment, depth, and incubation as predictors, as well as their interactions. Diagnostic plots of residuals versus fitted values confirmed that variance was relatively constant across the range of fitted values, validating our model choice. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means with Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) adjustment to identify specific differences between treatments at each depth and incubation combination.
