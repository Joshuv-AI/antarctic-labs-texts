Construct validity of primary trait writing rubrics based on assessment use argument (AUA) validation framework
Authors: Nurul Fariena Asli, Mohd Effendi Ewan Mohd Matore, Melor Md Yunus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In assessing performance-based language assessment, the use of a valid and reliable scoring rubric is crucial to minimize measurement errors that become threats in the rating process. The validation process of rubrics which previously was based on qualitative data is not satisfying since empirical evidence is not present. Thus, drawing on the Assessment Use Argument (AUA) Validation Framework, this study aims to search for evidence to prove a claim which is Primary Trait Writing (PTW) rubrics for students self assessment activities are relevant to the construct being measured. Based on that, two warrants and one rebuttal were derived to prove the claim. The participants consisted of 149 secondary school students in a state in Malaysia and three facets identified in the study were the examinee (149 students), the rater (149 students and 3 teachers), and the trait (Content, Format, Cohesive Device, and Sentence Fluency). Many Facet Rasch Model was employed to search for evidence in approving the warrants and rejecting the rebuttal. Based on the statistical results, evidence has shown that PTW rubrics successfully discriminated between students' writing ability, and fulfilled the six basic conditions of rating scale effectiveness to a certain extent where the Cohesive Device trait became the primary concern. In addition, the fit statistics for all traits demonstrated internal consistency and the high-reliability index portrayed the criteria were well differentiated in terms of difficulty level. Thus, all evidence had shown that PTW rubrics obtained construct validity where the warrants were supported, and the rebuttal was rejected which led to the acceptance of the claim. The implication of this study highlights the importance of validating assessment rubrics to ensure their internal validity and the use of MRFM in providing comprehensive analyses of the strengths and weaknesses of the developed rubrics. The use of a primary trait type scoring rubric as a tool in students’ self-assessment activities must be highlighted as more current studies are focusing on holistic and analytic scoring. Therefore, it is suggested for future research to expand the use of the Primary Trait rubrics in other type of essays and to be used in peer-assessment activities. Introduction

Assessing writing assessments is a delicate task where the research community has increasingly emphasized scoring rubrics as a key component to minimize measurement errors that can threaten the rating process. Recent studies have demonstrated the great potential of scoring rubrics for assessing students' second language (L2) writing skills [[1], [2], [3]]. A scoring rubric is a collection of criteria that includes descriptions of different performance levels [4]. It is specifically designed for evaluation, outlining the expectations for those who utilize it. Furthermore, it serves as the standard for grading evaluations [5], intentionally employed to help students achieve specific goals. Additionally, rubrics offer detailed explanations of different levels of excellence in performance quality based on criteria [6]. Unlike rating scales, rubrics do not supply performance-level explanations alongside the criteria. Rating scales are typically broad in scope and can be understood within a specific context. Rubrics originate from grading scales but are tailored for particular uses, especially in performance-based assessments. Three basic categories of grading rubrics employed in writing assessment are holistic, analytic, and primary trait. A holistic rubric yields a cumulative score based on an extensive assessment of a student's performance, evaluating all criteria collectively rather than separately [6]. Conversely, an analytic rubric deconstructs the writing task into various criteria or dimensions, each evaluated separately, so offering a more detailed evaluation of a student's strengths and weaknesses [7]. A primary trait rubric emphasises evaluating the degree to which a certain writing task achieves its main objective or defining feature [8], prioritizing the most critical element of the writing over a wide range of criteria. Extensive research has been conducted using holistic and analytic rubrics; however, the primary trait writing rubric is not commonly used in research despite being considered more appropriate for L2 learners [[9], [10], [11]]. It shows that far too little attention has been paid to explore the effectiveness of primary trait scoring rubrics even though it seems to be most suitable for L2 learners.

Southeast Asian nations, especially Malaysia, have included the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) into their educational frameworks to evaluate the English ability of second language learners [12]. In 2017, the Malaysian Ministry of Education (MOE) instituted the CEFR to improve English language proficiency among Malaysian students and to create a benchmark aligned with worldwide norms [13]. The application of CEFR in language education necessitates substantial modifications in pedagogy, learning methodologies, and evaluation practices inside the classroom, as CEFR prioritizes learner autonomy and self-direction, allowing students to evaluate and oversee their language development. Thus, the Malaysian English Language Roadmap 2015–2025 was formulated to contextualise the CEFR within the Malaysian educational framework, highlighting the cultivation of students capable of independent learning and self-awareness regarding their strengths and limitations [14]. With these changes, teachers have the responsibility to conduct self-assessment activities in the classroom. However, there is a demand for validated scoring rubrics for classroom assessments due to the unavailability of a prescribed scoring method for School-Based Assessment (SBA) [11]. Additionally, teachers often resist conducting assessments as they may feel they lack the competence to develop valid scoring procedures [14]. This shows that the traditional method of assessing students has shifted to performance-based assessment, where students are responsible for assessing their progress.

One of the issues faced is that students are rarely tasked with self-assessment due to a lack of faith in students as evaluators [15]. Therefore, there is a dire need to provide students with a valid and reliable rubric to minimize measurement errors that may arise during self-assessment [12]. In this context, an Assessment Use Argument (AUA) can be utilized to verify that the rubric for self-assessment is valid, reliable, and suitable for its intended purpose—helping students evaluate their performance. Supporting the rubric with evidence and reasoning through an AUA validates its application in enabling students to assess their learning. Given the effectiveness of scoring rubrics in assessing L2 writing skills, they are also recognized as a method of self-assessment used by educators throughout the instructional process [16]. Scoring rubrics offer students a set of criteria and dimensions that clearly outline the expectations for a given task, as well as a description of performance levels associated with each dimension [17]. An effectively constructed rubric can clarify exemplary work and assist students in self-monitoring and self-assessment [18,19]. Rubrics are regarded as a useful instrument in the instruction and acquisition of second language writing, since they enable students to employ critical thinking and evaluative abilities to judge the quality of their work based on stated criteria [20]. Rubrics are notable for their ability to clarify teachers' expectations, improve students' comprehension of learning objectives, and provide feedback to identify strengths and weaknesses, ultimately guiding students toward self-assessment [21].

The significance of this study lies in the current changes in the education system towards performance-based assessment, where student-centred learning is emphasized. Students are expected to be able to be responsible for their learning and engage in self-assessment, so the empirically validated rubrics become more prominent than before. Lacking validation increases the risk of having unreliable and invalid assessments, which leads to inaccurate assessments of student's performance, which will hinder their progress. In terms of the language assessment field, the evaluation of writing skills is very subjective; thus, the need for empirically validated rubrics will make the assessment consistent and objective. As for the increasing demands for self-assessment, well-validated rubrics provided to students will ensure the fairness and accuracy of their self-assessment practices. Thus, it is undeniable that rubrics' empirical validation is crucial to address the issues of measurement errors and educational demands and on the other hand, providing support for self-assessment practices. The Many Facet Rasch Model (MFRM) can be used to validate these rubrics by providing the measures for different facets of the scoring process: (a) the rubric traits, (b) examinees and (c) raters which are crucial in minimizing subjective bias and also produces more reliable and valid measurements. include this also at suitable place in the textpractices.

The Primary Trait Writing (PTW) rubric also has an innovative component which focuses on important features associated with the composition task, enabling comprehensive assessment. Compared to holistic rubrics, this focused method offers more precise perspectives on certain areas of weaknesses and strengths. In order to guarantee that the characteristics evaluated fairly represent high-quality writing, PTWRs are frequently created via extensive study and validation procedures. This scientific underpinning boosts their reputation and trustworthiness. With regard to self-evaluation tools, PTW rubric enables students to appraise their individual work in accordance with precise standards, encouraging self-control and taking charge of their education. This creative application of rubric inspires students to consider their work from a critical standpoint. PTW rubric can be modified to accommodate different types of students, goals, and written genres. This adaptability enables teachers to customize exams to fit a range of learning environments and requirements. By closely aligning these rubrics with certain curriculum objectives and competences, assessments are made to be both meaningful and pertinent. This creative alignment strengthens the assessment's instructional goal.

Through an examination of the inclusive evaluation, PTW rubric can be created with ethnic and situational factors in mind, increasing their relevance and equity for a range of educational populations. The requirement for inclusion in assessments is addressed by this novel idea. PTW rubric give educators and pupils an analogous structure for talking about good writing through collaboration. By fostering communication and mutual comprehension among instructors, this cooperative feature enhances teaching strategies. Additionally, PTW rubric assessment of pupil achievement yields useful information that can guide curricular modifications and teaching tactics. This invention encourages an upward trajectory of ongoing enhancement in both instruction and learning. PTW rubric makes it possible to examine the writing procedure as well as the finished work to gain an in-depth comprehension. Rather than focusing only on evaluating final products, this dual approach enables students to improve their writing abilities as they progress. When examined through the prism of the argumentation assessment, the creative features of PTW rubric emphasize how well they promote relevant assessment procedures. Through trait-based learning, research-based evaluation, and increased student agency, PTW rubric serve the needs of modern education while encouraging an atmosphere of introspection and ongoing development.

Objectives of the study

Consequently, the current quantitative study offers fresh insight into how a scoring rubric can be examined for self-assessment activities in the classroom where it is aimed that the chosen type of rubric as well as the traits and descriptors are measuring what they are supposed to measure. Thus, the objective of this study is to validate the construct validity of the Primary Trait Writing rubrics using the Assessment Use Argument (AUA) validation approach. - Meanwhile, the two main research questions guiding this study are: (1) to what extent do the criteria in PTW rubrics effectively discriminate between different levels of students’ writing abilities? (2) what empirical evidence supports the relevance and representativeness of the content assessed by PTW rubrics? These research questions provide evidence that AUA needs to accept the warrants and reject the rebuttal for this study as it will be used to prove the claim that PTW rubrics are relevant to the construct being measured. Detailed explanations are provided in section 2.3.

Literature review

This aspect of the review of literature presents and discusses key literature as it relates to, and links discussed in the existing area of research.

Many facet Rasch Model

The Many-Facets Rasch measurement (MFRM) model was introduced in the late 1990s. It is an extension of the Rasch Model where more than two facets can be calibrated at a particular time [22]. The scores from all the facets are calibrated using MFRM in order to validate the scoring rubrics and based on the context of this study, MFRM is used to search for the evidence where the rubrics are able to differentiate between students' level of performance. There are several previous studies in various contexts had been using MFRM to prove the criteria of their developed rubrics were able to effectively discriminate student's proficiency levels. A study by [1], focusing on validation of an analytic rubric for Spanish academic writing and the result showed that the analytic criteria effectively distinguished between students with different language proficiency levels and the results are in line with a study by Refs. [23,24] which investigate the reliability and validity of a rubric developed for a Foreign Language (EFL) writing test. Meanwhile, in assessing second language learners writing skills [[25], [26], [27], [28]], had utilized MFRM in exploring rater behavior and validating scoring rubrics for second language writing assessments and the results of the study portrayed that the developed rubric effectively distinguished between students with high and low ability in writing skills. It shows the scarcity of previous studies which have investigated the ability of self-assessment scoring rubrics effectiveness in distinguishing students writing performance.

MFRM is also being used to analyze the functioning of each response category in rating scales using \ six basic conditions for rating scale effectiveness [29]. The criteria for assessing the efficiency of rating scale categories, as outlined by [29], consist of six fundamental characteristics or indicators that must be met for a rating scale to be deemed of good quality (a) min of 10 observations for each category used category frequency (b) average category measures that increase monotonically (c) the outfit mean square statistics less than 2.0 (d) Rasch-Andrich category thresholds that increase monotonically (e) Rasch-Andrich category thresholds should be 1.0 to 5.0 logit apart (f) the shape of the probability curves peaked for each category. Studies on rating scales using the six basic conditions varied in different context such as in rater-mediated assessment by Refs. [30,31] which displayed all the six basic conditions are fulfilled using validated scoring rubrics. The results showed distinguishable peaks for each rubric criterion which are in line with studies in rubric validation by Refs. [23,25]. However, it is also common to have one or two of the basic conditions which are not fulfilled in developing the scoring rubrics as being shown in studies conducted by Ref. [32] where the results portrayed the distance between category threshold is problematic. Interestingly, study by Ref. [25] on validation of a scoring rubric used in the assessment of L2 writing electronic portfolios found that the first basic condition is not fulfilled where the first category of the rubric is only being used 4 times, but the peak of the category threshold is still distinguishable. However, those rubrics from Refs. [25,32] are not considered as ineffective as the other 5 basic conditions are still fulfilled.

Most of the studies mentioned before used Rating Scale Model and there has been little discussion about another function of FACETS where it also provides Partial Credit Model analysis. The Partial Credit Model (PCM) is a unidimensional model for the analysis of responses recorded in two or more ordered categories [33]. It is a generalization of the dichotomous Rasch Model and provides estimates of person locations, item difficulties, and thresholds specific to each item. Therefore, PCM provides the function of determining each item its rating scale structure, which allows for different thresholds for different items. It has sparked the interest of this study to embark on the journey in validating the construct validity of the developed rubrics using PCM.

Writing assessment

Writing is a crucial ability that demonstrates student's proficiency in second language acquisition. Two forms of assessments are utilized in evaluating students writing performance: formative and summative assessments. The summative writing assessment is typically associated with high-stakes tests, including IELTS and SAT for overseas assessments, as well as high-stakes examinations in Malaysia, such as SPM and STPM. The purpose of summative examination is to assess students' performance at the conclusion of the learning process. Conversely, the implementation of formative assessment in writing will increase students' mastery of writing skills, hence benefiting their performance in summative assessments. The mechanics of writing encompass content, organisation, coherence, vocabulary, and language elements. With the alignment of CEFR in the education system primarily in Malaysia, CEFR 'can do' statements to assess students writing proficiency have been proposed to be used in the classroom [34] has developed a self-assessment grid (SAG) for evaluating students' writing, which students can utilize for their self-assessment. A number of researches on writing assessments concentrate on several components essential for evaluating students' writing performance. Research by [2] examines four components of writing: content, organisation, language use, and mechanics. All these writing components generally exhibited strong performance, contrasting with a study by Ref. [25] that developed scoring rubrics for L2 writing electronic portfolios utilizing the identical writing components as [2]. A separate study by Ref. [35] examines task achievement, coherence and cohesion, language use, and range and accuracy to create a local CEFR-based writing checklist for a thorough evaluation of writing competency. Additionally [32], highlights content coverage, correctness, clarity, and language proficiency in formulating their evaluation systems to assess the effectiveness and precision of a summary in representing the original text while ensuring clear and coherent language usage. In summary, as these studies are grounded in the CEFR, the majority of the evaluated writing components pertain to the four criteria (Content, Communicative Achievement, Organisation, and Language) established by the CEFR assessment grid [35] asserted that all four criteria underwent a comprehensive validation process by a test development team for CEFR, rendering their applicability indisputable. Consequently, this study employed the four criteria as guidelines for selecting features for the PTW rubrics.

Primary trait scoring Rubric

The primary trait scoring is one of the effective rating scales for evaluating writing, particularly for L2 learners [36]. Even though it is claimed to become another potential tool to be used in assessing students' performance, a dearth of studies found regarding the primary trait scoring rubric. To date, there are a few studies that have been found in literature where the primary trait scoring rubric is used. A recent study [37] investigated students' writing performance of 46 English Education Study Program students using a primary trait scoring rubric. The results showed that 98 % of the students were able to write a topic sentence portraying their ability to write compare and contrast paragraphs. With the use of a primary trait scoring rubric, specific data was gained whereas among the students, 52 % of them were still unable to develop the ideas in the paragraph effectively. The data helped the lecturer to realize the weaknesses of each student and proper planning was made to improve students' ability in the areas of weaknesses. In a study conducted by Ref. [11], the researchers investigated the preferences of ESL teachers regarding the most appropriate scoring method for assessing essay writing in the classroom. One of the findings revealed that the primary trait scoring rubric allows teachers to focus on evaluating students' essays and is less time-consuming compared to holistic and analytic scoring methods. Providing a more explicit and thorough explanation of a student's writing skills for a particular assignment, together with a precise score, makes it simpler to evaluate the students' essays [9]. The scoring rubric directs the raters to concentrate their attention solely on a specific aspect of discourse in a written piece. The scoring for the specific characteristic is intensified and focused solely on the writing job mentioned in the question. It is appropriate for use in evaluating students' performance in the classroom and may vary across different teachers [11].

In contrast, a previous study revealed that a notable drawback of the primary trait scoring rubric is the difficulty primary raters face in just concentrating on the designated trait, since they may inadvertently incorporate additional qualities into their scoring process [38]. Primary raters are experienced assessors who cannot suppress their expertise when evaluating students' writing. Therefore, the reason why most studies only utilize the holistic and analytic scoring rubric is due to their reliance on expert raters in their research. Inexperienced raters may find the primary trait scoring rubric to be appropriate, especially when assessing ESL learners. It is advantageous to concentrate solely on one issue while evaluating work [39]. asserted that the major characteristic scoring rubric is very appropriate for classroom implementation as it allows teachers to tailor the rubrics to assess certain traits they wish to evaluate in learners. Hence, the investigation of the primary trait scoring rubric is considered a legitimate area of research, as L2 learners require a highly targeted rubric that concentrates on one specific aspect at a time during their language acquisition process. Second language learners require a highly explicit and comprehensive instructional resource, as suggested by Ref. [9], that can offer valuable feedback to enhance their writing proficiency in the target language. Furthermore, given that the raters consist of novice students who are also L2 learners, employing primary trait scoring makes it more convenient for them to evaluate their work. This is because the primary trait scoring rubric offers a clear description of the main feature of performance [39] that has to be evaluated. This allows raters to focus only on the specific aspect while analyzing the essays.

Besides, the uniqueness exists when the rubric provides a distinctive style of writing assessment that sets itself apart from other approaches in multiple significant ways. The goal and value of assessment in influencing instruction and student learning outcomes are emphasized by PTW. In contrast to conventional rubrics, which might only evaluate the finished result, PTW combined with AUA consider how assessment influences teaching strategies and fosters students' long-term growth. Secondly, the main characteristics of writing—such as structure, content, style, and conventions—are the focus of PTW. These characteristics are seen to be fundamental to good writing and form the basis for assessing student work. This method is in contrast to holistic rubrics, which could offer overall marks without dissecting individual literary components. PTW are based on core qualities, but they also consider the general cohesiveness and standard of the written writing, maintaining a holistic viewpoint. This harmony permits an exhaustive assessment that recognizes the writing's particular merits and faults as well as its general efficacy.

PTW are intended to be tightly aligned with educational goals, in contrast to previous rubric methods that might be considered static or disengaged from classroom procedures. This alignment makes sure that input from assessments may be used immediately to enhance instructional techniques and student learning objectives. PTW based on AUA acknowledge evaluation as a dynamic, continuing process as opposed to a singular event. In order to promote continuous development in teaching and learning, this viewpoint urges educators to regularly enhance evaluation procedures based on student requirements and instructional goals. Because they place a strong emphasis on the integration of fundamental qualities, congruence with educational objectives, and the argumentative explanation of assessment judgments, PTW based on AUA constitute an important advancement of written assessment. By encouraging a deeper knowledge of how evaluation might improve methods of instruction and pupil-generated outputs gradually, this method goes beyond conventional rubric structures.

Assessment use argument (AUA)

The Assessment Use Argument (AUA) was initially proposed by Bachman and Palmer in 2005 and further enhanced in 2010. The Assessment Use Argument (AUA) is a validity paradigm in language testing that focuses on evaluating and documenting the degree of precision and suitability in the interpretation and use of test scores [40]. The framework provides a structured approach for test development and builds links between inferences made from the expected and actual results, decisions, and interpretations of a test taker's language skills, as well as assessment records and scores. Bachman establishes a connection between these factors and the performance in assessments, as well as the particular actions involved in the assessment. The term refers to a theoretical structure that guides the creation and application of a specific language evaluation, encompassing the interpretations and applications derived from the assessment [40]. Recently, language testing researchers [[40], [41], [42], [43]] have shifted the traditional way of validation to argument-based validation based on Toulmin's [44] structure as the basis. Thus, AUA utilizes Toulmin's methodology for practical reasoning as a foundation for expressing an AUA. The process of substantiating the selection of a specific language assessment or rubric for this research involves (i) articulating the claims, warrants, and rebuttals in an AUA and (ii) providing backing to support the warrants and weaken the rebuttals in the AUA [40].

The utilization of AUA in rubric validation seems to be the catalyst in enhancing student directed learning. The connection between current educational demands, the rise of student-centred learning, and the potential pitfalls of unvalidated rubrics exists on the assessment practices. For current educational demands, modern education prioritizes critical thinking, creativity, and effective communication skills. Students are expected to articulate their ideas clearly and persuasively across various contexts. There's a shift towards competency-based models that require students to demonstrate mastery of specific skills, making valid and reliable assessments essential. The connection towards rise of student-centred learning happened through active engagement. The student-centred learning encourages active participation, where learners take ownership of their education. This approach fosters self-directed learning, making self-assessment a vital component of the process. With diverse learning styles and paces, student-centred approaches often rely on rubrics to guide students in understanding their progress and areas for improvement.

The unvalidated rubrics may lack clarity and specificity, leading to varied interpretations of what constitutes quality writing. This inconsistency can confuse students and educators alike. When students use unvalidated rubrics to assess their own work, they may misjudge their abilities. If the criteria are vague or poorly defined, students might overlook significant flaws or inflate their strengths, undermining their learning progress. Besides, if students perceive that their self-assessments based on unreliable rubrics do not align with formal evaluations, it can erode their trust in the assessment process and diminish their motivation.

The validated rubrics using AUA are able to assist L2 learners to cater current educational demands by providing clear, consistent criteria that align with critical thinking and communication skills. By developing well-defined traits and empirical support, those validated rubrics empower students to engage in meaningful self-assessment. They can accurately identify their strengths and areas for improvement, fostering a genuine understanding of their writing capabilities. Other than that, students can take greater ownership of their learning, setting realistic goals and tracking their progress based on reliable feedback as those rubrics do not only support accurate self-assessment but also ensure that students are equipped with the tools they need to succeed. Conversely, unvalidated rubrics pose significant risks that can hinder student growth and undermine trust in the assessment process. Due to that, teachers can fullfil current demands, foster student learning, and promote a culture of reflective assessment.

To make it more focused, Bachman has used the six test usefulness qualities (reliability, construct validity, authenticity, interactiveness, impact, and practicality) in AUA as the quality control throughout the process of development and validation in a language test. Even though these qualities were developed for evaluating test usefulness, they can be used to evaluate rubrics [25]. However, only construct validity is being the focus in this paper due to limited space available [45]. define construct validity as the extent to which test scores can be interpreted as a measure of the underlying construct the test was designed to assess. Thus, the scores from the writing assessment are calibrated and the findings being used to evaluate the effectiveness of the rubrics based on [29] basic conditions in evaluating rating scale. The criteria and descriptors from the PTW rubrics were examined to develop the argument which becomes the evidence in rejecting or accepting the claim made for construct validity. It is to prove whether the rubrics are relevant to its intended purpose. AUA was applied in this study as it acted as a specific guide in the validation framework that suited the characteristics of a novice researcher. Methodology

Individuals involved in the field of language testing and assessment should utilize the most appropriate psychometric theory to deliver assessment outcomes that are highly objective, valid, and equitable for examinees [47]. The rationale of this study utilizes MFRM compared to other models such as Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Generalizability Theory (G-Theory) is due to its nature in providing thorough analysis to determine the validity of the PTW rubrics. MFRM provides statistical measures (a) rating scale effectiveness (b) separation and reliability indices (c) infit and outfit statistics (d) category functioning for each trait using Partial Credit Model and (e) point biserial correlation which can be used to find evidence to prove the construct validity of the developed rubrics. As for CTT, it is not suitable to be used in this study since it only focuses on basic reliability estimate and do not provide details for item level [48]. There is no consideration of how examinees perform on individual items thus it becomes less effective in analysing the performance of the rubric criteria or discriminate the students’ level of writing ability. Meanwhile G-Theory is focusing on the sources of measurement errors such as rater bias [49] therefore it has lack ability to explore whether the rubric trait reflects the underlying construct. Thus, the rationale for using MFRM over CTT and G-Theory in this study lies in its capacity to handle the multiple facets involved in performance-based writing rubrics and its ability to provide stronger empirical evidence of rubric validity.

To address the proposed research objective, a quantitative approach was applied using the Many Facet Rasch Model to search for evidence to support and reject the rebuttal for this study. There were 4 facets involved primarily students, raters (student and teacher raters, traits (rubric criteria), and rater type (self-assessment and teacher assessment). However, this paper will not address the rater type as it is not the main emphasis. Since this study involves raters, Many Facet Rasch Model (MFRM) was employed to analyze the data as it facilitates the inclusion of additional facets, like raters, test items, or task complexity, that may influence the measuring process. Each component can be evaluated independently, facilitating a more comprehensive analysis of the rubrics. By adopting MFRM as the tool in measuring the construct validity of the 4 Primary Trait Writing Rubrics, firstly, this study seeks to evaluate the global modal fit and unidimensionality of the rubrics to prove that it successfully calibrated. Secondly, to determine the construct validity of the PTW rubrics in terms of their ability to differentiate students’ abilities, fit statistics, separation indexes, and category functioning where the 6 basic conditions of rating scale effectiveness proposed by Ref. [29] were employed. To gain more satisfaction on the scale effectiveness, the Partial Credit Model (PCM) which is a part of the analysis in MFRM was conducted to search for the category functioning of each trait.

Sampling and participants

This study was conducted in the context of form four students (16 years old) will take the Sijil Pelajaran Malaysia (SPM) examination in form five. SPM is a high-stakes examination akin to the GCSE, O Level, or IGCSE in other nations. One of the subjects assessed in SPM is the English Writing Paper (1119), with questions pitched at the A2 to B2 CEFR levels. Essay writing is a crucial component of the SPM examination, since it assesses the examinee's proficiency in the English language, analytical thinking capabilities, and ability for constructing coherent arguments. Preparation for the SPM intensifies in Form 4 to ensure optimal results; thus, their participation in this research is essential. The participants were comprised of 149 students from 6 secondary public schools in Malaysia, together with 3 teacher raters who serve as examiners for a high-stakes examination in Malaysia. The targeted participants were 150 students however, 1 student did not give a rating to his essay, and it was excluded from being analyzed.

The study employed purposive sampling to categorize participants into three proficiency levels based on their performance level (TP) in writing skills awarded by their teachers for performance-based assessment in the classroom. TP is given based on the ‘can do’ statements outlined by CEFR and is being used to assess students' language skills. Since TP results are considered private and confidential, the participants were chosen by the teachers based on the TP required by researcher. Therefore, researcher has limited accessibility in choosing the demographic background of the students. The criteria for participation were specified for several reasons. The researcher only selected national secondary schools with the assumption that all students have received identical training and have been exposed to the same new syllabus. In addition, the researcher chose to recruit students based on their proficiency (3 categories) in writing to ensure a diverse representation of all skill levels in this study. Instrument

The first instrument consisted of an essay task designed for students to create their own essay, with a difficulty level aligned with the A2 CEFR standard. Another instrument involved was a sample essay where it was used for the purpose of rater-training. In addition, the primary tools employed were the third draft of the PTW rubrics, which had undergone revisions by experts throughout the rubric development stage to ensure content validity. These rubrics were then administered to participants during the pre-trialing and trialling stages to assess face validity as well as construct validity. During the rubric development stage, three experts were selected to validate the initial version of PTW rubrics using a metarubric developed from (17), which is purported to be a rubric utilised for assessing the quality of a proposed rubric. The second version of the PTW rubrics was created based on the feedback provided by the experts. Prior to implementing the rubrics in the trial phase, a preliminary stage was carried out to verify the clarity and suitability of the rubrics for students. The selected participants consisted of 20 students who possessed identical qualities to the intended subjects. The objectives were to assess (a) the level of familiarity with the vocabulary employed, (b) the appropriateness of the font size, and (c) the arrangement of the rubric. Subsequent to that, more revision was carried out, resulting in the development of the third version of the PTW rubrics which were used for the study in this paper. Even though the content of the rubrics was validated by the experts, empirical evidence is required to provide support for the warrants and rebuttals in the validity argument. There were 4 traits used in the rubrics (a) content (b) format (c) cohesive device (d) sentence fluency. Apart from that, an essay task and 149 essays written by participants were also used as the instruments in this study.

Warrant 1: the criteria successfully discriminate between students’ writing ability

The results demonstrate the construct validity of PTW rubrics. This is clear as the criteria effectively distinguish between students of varying abilities, as indicated by the student's measurement report and the effectiveness of the rating scale. The text highlights the discriminatory capability of the scales which can guarantee the effectiveness of PTW rubrics as tools for student self-assessment as it has yielded the same results as the previous studies [1,[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. Previous research by Ref. [23] has also employed the MFRM and successfully prove that the developed rubrics are able to validate scoring rubrics and portrayed the current research reveals an intriguing finding: the separation index of 6.33 indicates the presence of six distinct levels of students' writing abilities, aligning with the six levels of performance standard (TP) employed in the English Language Curriculum. The discovery has the potential to enhance the existing body of knowledge on the use of PTW rubrics for self-assessment exercises in the classroom as they are able to discriminate the students according to the performance standards (TP) used in the curriculum thus conform to the first research question. It can assist educators in accurately evaluating students' proficiency, specifically in the domain of writing, by employing these rubrics.

In addition, the research reveals a strong reliability index, suggesting that students with varying writing abilities may be accurately identified. Furthermore, the results of the Chi-square test demonstrate that students with diverse writing performances can be effectively differentiated. The advantage of utilizing MFRM becomes apparent when comparing the estimated measures generated by MFRM with their corresponding positions on the Wright Map. This analysis accurately places students in their appropriate locations on the map, with highly proficient students predominantly positioned at the top and low proficient students predominantly positioned at the bottom. Therefore, the map serves the objective of visually presenting a framework for comprehending the significance of scores obtained from the evaluation [51]. However, this study also found that several students who were classified as highly proficient were positioned in the center or bottom of the Wright map, and vice versa. Hence, teachers should be alerted to the fact that students' writing proficiency might vary, and numerous elements could impact a student's ability at any given moment.

Although the rubrics effectively differentiated between students' writing skills, it was clear from the category threshold and probability curve that the lowest portion of the PTW rubrics was underutilized. This observation might be used as evidence to refute the first argument. The distance between Category Thresholds is narrow, yet, the category frequency satisfies the condition of having more than 10 observations, including the lowest categories (Category 0 and 1). In contrast to earlier studies, which typically show a peak in the probability curve when there are more than 10 observations per category [30,31]. [53] advised against prematurely merging categories in cases where researchers have limited samples of raters and/or examinee performances (or goods) and have not thoroughly explored the scale's potential applications. It can be inferred that merging or reducing the PTW rubric categories is unnecessary. Instead, the number of samples should be raised to provide a more accurate representation of the peaks in the probability curve. The discussions in this section shows evidence based on MFRM results that the criteria or traits in PTW rubrics are able to discriminate student's ability. Even though there are a few issues being brought up, the overall results are still responding to the first research question.

Warrant 2: the rubrics provide empirical and substantive evidence of the relevance and representativeness of the content

Next, the discussion focuses on warrant 2 to answer research question 2, which examines the measures of separation indices, the point-biserial correlation for each criterion, and the category functioning proposed by Ref. [29] to assess the effectiveness of the rating scale for each trait. The purpose of this evaluation is to determine if the rubrics can demonstrate the relevance and representativeness of the content, leading to the potential rejection of the rebuttal. The results indicate that a high trait separation ratio corresponds to a greater distinction between the traits in terms of model precision. Additionally, a high separation index suggests that the components are measuring different aspects of the ability [32]. These findings provide strong evidence to support warrant 2. The disparities in the challenges posed by these four traits are statistically significant. More specifically, in this study, the trait of Cohesive Device proved to be the most challenging to achieve a high score, which contradicts the findings of recent studies on writing components [2,35] Both tests revealed that achieving a high score in Organisation, which includes cohesive devices, was rather effortless.

Further analysis was done to explore the findings of both situations. Specifically, the study conducted by Ref. [35] focused on evaluating the organization of the text by testing the use of coordinating conjunctions, also known as simple linkers. Meanwhile, this current study specifically examined the usage of Cohesive Devices, which are advanced linkers commonly employed in essay writing. Hence, this elucidates the discrepancy between the outcome and the findings from Ref. [35]. Furthermore, one could contend that the goal is assessing proficiency at the secondary school level, which necessitates the use of only basic connective words. Nevertheless, the researcher reached a consensus to include Cohesive Device in the rubrics based on the insights gained from an intuition-driven approach to rubric construction. Regarding the Format trait, students may have found it the most straightforward due to their prior exposure to essay formats, such as the email-style essay, which they have been practicing since early secondary school. It aligns with the results indicating that there were fewer than 10 observations for Category 0 in the Format trait. This suggests that raters seldom assigned a rating of 0, as most participants received higher scores for this trait.

The results of the point-biserial correlation analysis indicate that all traits were measured within a unidimensional construct, and the traits assessed in the PTW rubrics demonstrated internal consistency. Furthermore, it demonstrates that all of the criteria are jointly contributing to the definition of the measured construct, so undeniably supporting warrant 2. In addition, the findings on the functioning of each trait category also provided evidence for the relevance and representativeness of the content in PTW rubrics. Out of the six basic conditions for rating scale effectiveness, Content, Format, and Sentence Fluency traits failed to meet only one of the conditions which was not significant. As previously mentioned, the Format had fewer than 10 observations for Category 0, but the peaks were observed for all categories. The Rasch model allows for the estimation of the probability of a participant selecting a specific response category based on their abilities and the difficulty of the item [54].

The issue with Content and Sentence Fluency is the distance between category thresholds, while the probability peaks for each category are still discernible. The MFRM suggests that the intervals between category thresholds should be 1.0 to 5.0 logits and that the peaks of all category scores should be easily recognizable. These findings indicate that the categories are clearly defined and can be easily differentiated from each other, resulting in a rating scale of excellent quality [31]. The distance for both traits is approaching 1.0, indicating that the problem is not significant. Therefore, the warrant is still valid. Regarding the trait of a Cohesive Device, two basic conditions are not fulfilled. The distance between the category thresholds for Category 1 and Category 3 is less than 1.0, leading to the presence of redundant categories in the probability curve. The findings are in contrast to most studies, which found distinct category peaks for all criteria [23,25,31].

However, a previous study by Ref. [32] appears to have the same findings as this study where certain categories in the component seemed problematic (distance between category threshold) as they defined much fewer wide intervals on the latent variables than the other categories in these components. The study raised concern about the participants in the study who were at the intermediate level which had restricted the variability of scores that ideally should be reflected in the scoring categories. As for this context of the study, the participants are form 4 students with different levels of proficiency so they should be able to use Cohesive Device in their essays. Thus, the inclusion of the Cohesive Device trait in the PTW rubrics is valuable in helping teachers and students increase their knowledge on the subject. Overall, the problem does not lie with the developed rubrics but with external factors (the students and teacher). This is similar to the study by Ref. [25] as the raters also had difficulties with the use of cohesive markers as they lacked exposure to the usage based on the interviews conducted to obtain the feedback from participants It indicates that rubrics cannot be used easily if the users do not have prior knowledge of the evaluated traits. In this context of the study, the rater training conducted before the actual study was crucial and it is believed that it helped to minimize the rating errors due to certain circumstances, but problems will still occur.

Rebuttal: there are effects of construct-irrelevant variance and construct under-representation

It is necessary to examine any characteristics of the rubrics that led to variations in the raters' performance or contributed to the lack of effectiveness of the rubrics [25]. Thus, the investigation to determine any construct-irrelevant variance and construct under-representation that might have affected the construct validity of PTW rubrics was conducted using fit statistics of each trait, item separation and reliability, and rating scale functioning as well. Based on the discussions for Warrant 1 and Warrant 2, some effects of construct-irrelevant variance and construct underrepresentation were detected however, they are insignificant as other measures were pointing in the good direction. Firstly, warrant 1 is accepted as the evidence to support the claim that PTW rubrics are relevant to the construct being measured as the discrimination power of the PTW rubrics is proven. Secondly, for Warrant 2, even though the trait Cohesive Device weakens the strength of the argument, it can be said that the data supports the warrant that the rubrics provide empirical and substantive evidence of the relevance and representativeness of the content particularly based on the comparison with relevant literature. Lastly, the fit statistics score is close to 1, indicating a perfect fit for the item. This suggests that the observed ratings and expected ratings were quite similar. Due to all the evidence, rebuttal 2 is rejected which makes it clear that PTW rubrics achieved the construct validity as it responded positively to the research questions based on the approval of warrant 1 and warrant 2.

Conclusion

This quantitative analysis concludes that, despite certain concerns that may compromise the rubric's validity, it is deemed valid for self-assessment among secondary school students. The application of MFRM in this study offers compelling evidence for the validity of the constructs, achieved through a thorough investigation of the traits. There are several implications for educational practice gained from this study. First, the use of validated rubrics is crucial to ensure the measurement accuracy of the self-assessment in the classroom used by students. The rubrics help to facilitate more accurate evaluation on students' writing performance and minimizing the bias in evaluating the students' work. Consequently, teachers can assess each student's performance and determine their level of proficiency. In addition, with the accurate evaluation using the validated rubrics, reliable and meaningful feedbacks obtained which are clear and practical enable students to engage in self-reflection. Students are able to identify on their strengths and weaknesses and are able to focus on the area that they need to improve. This eventually, will foster leaner autonomy and train students to become responsible in their own learning. Lastly, the integration of rater training in this study has given positive impact as it improves the assessment uniformity across various raters. Teachers can conduct rater training to train students to become better self-assessor and align their thinking on how teachers are assessing their work. It increases the reliability of the self-assessment and students can practice it on their own without the presence of a teacher. Thus, the goal of producing learners who can be responsible for their own learning is achievable.

These are the practical implications of PTW validated rubrics based on AUA in educational settings by focusing on specific traits or elements that are critical to the writing task. By outlining the qualities (such as structure, clarity, and reasoning) that are most crucial for the writing assignment, PTW validated rubrics offer a clear framework. This explanation helps both evaluators and students comprehend expectations. Standardized characteristics allow assessors to apply the same standards to all submissions, guaranteeing dependability and fairness. This will support writing assessments that are consistent. Through opportunities for self-assessment, when students utilize the rubric to evaluate their own work against predetermined traits, PTW can improve student learning by encouraging self-regulation and reflection. In order to help students, realize where they need to develop, teachers can also provide tailored feedback based on particular features. An additional pragmatic consequence is the conformity to educational goals. In order to ensure that assessments are meaningful and related to learning objectives, PTW can be created to fit with certain curriculum goals and learning outcomes. Students can set personalized writing goals that correspond with their academic needs by knowing which attributes are valued. Teachers collaborated with one another by sharing rubrics, which might result in collaborative assessment methods where teachers talk about student work and outcomes using a common framework. By establishing a shared vocabulary around writing assessment, PTW can help instructors have fruitful conversations about writing practices and standards. The practical implications also benefit various learners, as educators can change the rubric's characteristics to suit varying ability levels or provide scaffolding to meet the requirements of diverse learners. Assessments can be made more inclusive and equitable by taking cultural differences in writing into account while developing the validated rubrics. Analyzing evaluation data from PTW can help with the ongoing improvement of writing teaching by highlighting common areas of weakness within a student group and guiding focused interventions. Additionally, educators can participate in professional development aimed at enhancing their teaching techniques, honing their assessment abilities, and utilizing rubrics more skilfully. In addition to enhancing student learning and offering focused feedback, they also assist teachers in coordinating assessments with learning objectives and promoting insightful dialogues about writing. All things considered, PTW help make writing assessment more methodical and thoughtful, which is advantageous for teachers and students alike.

Several limitations need to be highlighted for this study since this is the first one applying MFRM to examine the quality of self-assessment scoring rubrics for secondary school students. Firstly, this study only provides the initial evidence of scoring rubrics for construct validity measures. It does not include comprehensive reliability measurements, such as rater analysis. Without analyzing the consistency of assessments across different raters, it is difficult to gauge the reliability of the rubrics. The alignment between self-assessment and teacher ratings is crucial to ensure that the rubrics consistently measure what they are intended to measure in order to claim that PTW rubrics are relevant and valid to be used in self-assessment activities. Second, the current study predominantly concentrates on self-assessment which is a narrow scope in assessing students' writing skills which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Even though the rubrics are designed for self-assessment, it does not yet explore if they manage to function well in peer assessment or teacher assessment contexts. Gathering validity evidence from the three components (self-assessment, peer assessment and teacher assessment) will enhance the validity of the developed rubrics. All in all, it can be stated that the current study needs for more evidence to ensure the rubrics are valid, meaning they measure the intended construct accurately. Lack gathering of evidence verify that the rubrics need more analysis to accurately reflect the overall students’ writing performance.

For future research, the identified limitations necessitate where more research using reliability measurements, including rater analysis, as the rubrics are intended for self-assessment. It is essential to evaluate the efficiency of self-assessment and the alignment of self-evaluations with teacher ratings, since this impacts the validity and reliability of the established rubrics. Therefore, it can be one of the pieces of evidence in ensuring the rubrics are measuring what it supposed to measure. Instead of only focusing on using the PTW rubrics during self-assessment activities, future research should be conducted using peer assessment activities as well and the results can be analyzed based on the reports on self-assessment, peer assessment and teacher assessment. This will provide deeper insights on the effectiveness of PTW rubrics in enhancing learners' autonomy, consistency and accuracy in their writing assessment. Both limitations highlight a wider scope of the study which demonstrates that PTW rubrics can be applied in different assessment methods, which leads to a more comprehensive assessment of students’ writing performance.
