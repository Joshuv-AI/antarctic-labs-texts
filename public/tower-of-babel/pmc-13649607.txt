Endoscopic Evacuation Under Local Anesthesia for Subacute Lobar Intracerebral Hemorrhage in Older Adults
Authors: Norichika HASHIMOTO, Makoto ISOZAKI, Hatsuji UNO, Tetsuro TSUJI, Takahiro YAMAUCHI, Toshiaki KODERA, Ryuhei KITAI, Ken-Ichiro KIKUTA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Minimally invasive surgery for intracerebral hemorrhage is typically performed in the acute phase; however, in frail older adults, the risks of general anesthesia may outweigh the benefits of an early intervention. This study evaluated a “consistency-guided” strategy, in which hematoma evacuation is delayed until liquefaction allows endoscopic removal under local anesthesia. We retrospectively analyzed patients aged ≥70 years with lobar hemorrhages of ≥30 mL, excluding deep-seated types. Patients were categorized into 3 groups: subacute endoscopic surgery performed between days 7 and 14 based on hematoma consistency, acute craniotomy performed for neurological deterioration, and conservative management. The hematoma evacuation rate, operative metrics, complications, and functional outcomes were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale at 90 days and Functional Independence Measure gain. Among the 43 patients (endoscopic: 18, craniotomy: 11, conservative: 14), baseline hemorrhage severity was comparable across groups, with mean intracerebral hemorrhage scores of 3.07, 2.27, and 2.64, respectively. The endoscopic group achieved a high evacuation rate (median 88%) with significantly shorter operative times than the craniotomy group (50 vs. 180 min) and had no postoperative respiratory complications. Although the median 90-day modified Rankin Scale score was 5 across all groups, the endoscopic group demonstrated significantly greater Functional Independence Measure gain compared with conservative management, particularly among patients with hematoma volumes ≥40 mL. Delayed endoscopic evacuation under local anesthesia appears to be a feasible and safe strategy for older adults with frailty and may contribute to improved functional recovery in this population.

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in older adults has become an increasingly critical challenge in aging societies worldwide.1-3) Lobar ICH is particularly common in this population, which is often characterized by frailty, multiple comorbidities,4,5) and heightened vulnerability to anesthesia-related complications. In such patients, the potential benefits of hematoma evacuation must be carefully weighed against the physiological risks associated with acute-phase surgery under general anesthesia (GA). Although international guidelines recommend early surgical intervention for supratentorial lobar ICH ≥30 mL,6-8) the applicability of these recommendations to the “oldest-old” remains uncertain, as major randomized trials have largely excluded patients aged >80 years or those with poor baseline functional status.9-13)

Recent minimally invasive surgical trials, including MISTIE III10) and the landmark ENRICH trial,11) have demonstrated the benefits of early hematoma evacuation in preventing secondary injury from blood toxicity. However, these protocols typically require GA and exclude patients with significant pre-existing disabilities, limiting their generalizability to the frail geriatric populations encountered in real-world practice. In patients with frailty, the systemic stress of GA and acute-phase physiological instability may outweigh the benefits of immediate clot removal. Furthermore, age-related cerebral atrophy in older adults often preserves intracranial compliance,5) providing greater tolerance to the mass effect. This unique physiological context creates a “therapeutic window,” allowing surgeons to prioritize systemic safety over immediate decompression.

We propose a “consistency-guided” approach that converts a high-risk acute surgery into a lower-risk subacute procedure. Hematomas in older adults typically undergo progressive softening beginning around day 7 and liquefy after approximately day 10.14) Waiting for liquefaction facilitates simple aspiration with minimal parenchymal manipulation. Consequently, endoscopic evacuation can be performed under local anesthesia, thereby avoiding airway manipulation and hemodynamic instability. While early surgery aims to minimize toxic edema, our strategy prioritizes the tolerability of surgical intervention in patients who would otherwise be deemed unfit for conventional surgeries. This approach aligns with the goals of minimally disruptive neurosurgical practices in older adults.15-20)

Despite these potential advantages, evidence supporting delayed local anesthesia-based endoscopic evacuation remains limited. Critics may argue that delayed surgery misses the window to prevent edema; however, the actual clinical benefit for patients with frailty who survive the initial ictus remains unclear. Additionally, evaluating efficacy in this demographic is challenging due to the “ceiling effect” of the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). The Functional Independence Measure (FIM) may better capture subtle but clinically meaningful improvements in independence,21) particularly in geriatric rehabilitation-oriented care settings.

This study aimed to evaluate the safety and clinical utility of consistency-guided endoscopic hematoma evacuation under local anesthesia in patients aged ≥70 years with lobar ICH. We specifically examined whether this strategy could serve as a practical alternative for patients excluded from standard acute surgical protocols. By comparing this strategy with conventional craniotomy and conservative management, and by assessing functional outcomes using the FIM, we sought to identify prognostic factors and determine whether this “strategic delay” offers a viable alternative for frail older adults.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Fukui General Hospital, a single secondary emergency care center in Fukui, Japan, which provides continuous 24-hr emergency services. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval no. 2019-36) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for the surgery was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives. Because of the retrospective and observational nature of this study, clinical trial registration was not applicable.

Patient selection and treatment allocation

Consecutive patients aged ≥70 years who were admitted with spontaneous lobar ICH between April 2015 and April 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. The inclusion criteria were a hematoma volume of at least 30 mL confirmed by computed tomography, a cortical or subcortical lobar location, and the presence of focal neurological deficits, such as hemiparesis. Patients were excluded if they showed signs of impending herniation (defined as severe consciousness disturbance with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 5 or less or pupillary asymmetry), had a history of decompressive craniectomy, or had underlying vascular lesions such as moyamoya disease or arteriovenous malformations. The ICH score was calculated for all patients to characterize the baseline severity.

Patients were stratified into 3 treatment cohorts based on their clinical status, urgency of decompression, and suitability for GA. Patients who were neurologically stable but considered suitable for GA were evaluated individually. Some patients underwent acute craniotomy based on clinical judgment, while others were observed until hematoma softening. The endoscopic surgery group comprised patients treated in the subacute phase (7-14 days after onset); this strategy was specifically selected for patients who remained neurologically stable but were deemed high-risk for GA because of advanced age, frailty, or severe comorbidities. The craniotomy group included patients treated in the acute phase (0-3 days after onset) who required urgent decompression due to rapid neurological deterioration or a significant mass effect. Finally, the conservative management group consisted of patients with advanced frailty, severe systemic comorbidities, or poor premorbid functional status, for whom surgical intervention was judged inappropriate by the attending physicians and family members. In older adults, age-related cerebral atrophy often preserves intracranial compliance;5) consequently, some patients demonstrate relatively greater tolerance to acute mass effect. This physiological characteristic allows for a safe observational period until hematoma softening occurs, making subacute intervention under local anesthesia feasible in selected cases. Surgical procedure

Consistency-guided endoscopic evacuation was performed with the patient in the supine or lateral position to optimize access to the hematoma. Local anesthesia was induced by scalp infiltration with 0.5% lidocaine, combined with intravenous diazepam (5 mg), pentazocine (15 mg), and dexmedetomidine to maintain spontaneous respiration. A transparent surgical drape (Skeleton Drape; Medline Industries, Inc., Northbrook, IL, USA) was used to allow continuous monitoring of the patient's breathing and movements.

A 3-cm linear scalp incision was made, and a burr hole was created at the point where the hematoma was closest to the cortical surface. The entry site was chosen to provide the shortest trajectory to the hematoma center, maximizing aspiration efficiency. The bone window was enlarged as needed to increase sheath maneuverability. A rigid neuroendoscope (HOPKINS Telescope 0°; Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) and a transparent sheath (Neurosheath; Medikit Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) were employed. After sheath insertion and confirmation of liquefied hematoma efflux, the sheath was fixed.

Under direct visualization, the hematoma was evacuated as completely as possible using suction. Hemostasis was achieved using electrocoagulation with dedicated suction–coagulation devices (Fujita Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Artificial cerebrospinal fluid (Artcereb; Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was continuously irrigated to maintain a clear surgical field. After hemostasis, a powdered microfibrillar collagen hemostat (Avitene; Davol Inc., Warwick, RI, USA) was applied, and the cavity was irrigated again with Artcereb to ensure definitive hemostasis. The access tract was then lined with oxidized regenerated cellulose soaked in fibrin glue. This technique is consistent with previously reported methods.22-24) No patient required conversion to GA. Data collection and outcome measures

Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, admission GCS score, and Charlson Comorbidity Index, were extracted from the medical records. Hematoma volume was calculated using the ABC/2 method (A, largest diameter; B, perpendicular diameter; C, vertical extent). Functional outcomes were assessed using the mRS at 90-days and the FIM outcomes. To quantify functional recovery, the FIM gain was defined as the difference between the FIM scores at rehabilitation admission and discharge.21) The FIM consists of 18 items covering motor and cognitive domains, with total scores ranging from 18 to 126. FIM scores at rehabilitation admission and discharge, and FIM gain, were compared among the endoscopic, craniotomy, and conservative treatment groups. Because FIM assessment is not applicable to deceased patients, analyses of total, motor, and cognitive FIM scores and FIM gain were performed only for patients who survived until rehabilitation admission. Accordingly, no missing data were present for the FIM score changes. All functional assessments were performed by rehabilitation staff who were blinded to the treatment allocation.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance when the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were satisfied, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Multivariate ordinal logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of 90-day functional outcomes. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model, and the proportional odds assumption was verified using the Brant test. To address potential baseline imbalances among treatment groups, age, sex, admission GCS score, hematoma volume, and treatment modality (endoscopic vs. others) were included the multivariate model as covariates.

As an exploratory analysis, we evaluated whether hematoma volume could predict clinically meaningful improvement, defined as an FIM gain ≥10, using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. This analysis showed limited discriminative ability and did not identify a clinically useful cutoff.

Additionally, we performed ROC analysis using the 90-day mRS (0-3 vs. 4-6) as a primary functional outcome (Supplementary Figure 2A). Based on the ROC-derived threshold, we used 40 mL as a practical cutoff for subsequent comparisons of the mRS and FIM gain between the treatment groups (Supplementary Figure 2B).

Analyses were conducted using EZR (version 1.68).25) Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power.26)

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed using a ROC-derived hematoma volume cutoff of 40 mL. Functional outcomes between the endoscopic and conservative treatment groups were compared using the 90-day mRS and FIM gain in patients with hematoma volumes ≥40 mL. Additional exploratory comparisons using other hematoma volume thresholds (≥30, ≥50, and ≥60 mL) are presented in Supplementary Table 2A and B.

Results

Hematoma evolution and timing of surgery

The hematoma consistency followed a predictable time-dependent pattern: solid on day 0, firm on days 1-6, soft on days 7-9, and liquefied on day ≥10. In the endoscopic group, patients who underwent surgery on or after day 7, corresponding to the soft or liquefied phases, achieved superior evacuation rates. Importantly, no postoperative respiratory complications occurred in this subgroup, supporting the safety of the delayed strategy. Although specific intracranial pressure data were not collected, the absence of rapid neurological deterioration during the waiting period in this oldest-old cohort suggests that age-related cerebral atrophy may have preserved intracranial compliance, allowing safe observation until hematoma softening occurred.5)

Consistent with this time-dependent evolution, patient allocation followed the flow shown in Figure 1. Of the 50 consecutive patients screened, 7 were excluded for impending herniation, leaving 43 patients for the consistency-guided strategy. Immediately after arrival, 8 patients underwent emergency craniotomy because of rapid neurological deterioration. The remaining 35 patients were neurologically stable and considered suitable for GA. Among them, 3 patients experienced neurological deterioration within the first 3 days and underwent acute craniotomy, whereas the remaining 32 patients were stable and remained observation until day ≥7. At reassessment, 18 patients with hematoma softening underwent endoscopic evacuation under local anesthesia, while 14 were managed conservatively (5 based on clinical judgment favoring non-operative care and 9 because the family declined surgery). The low incidence of rapid deterioration during the observation period supports the feasibility and safety of this delayed, consistency-guided approach in the oldest-old population.

Baseline characteristics and surgical parameters

The baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. A significant age difference was observed among the groups (p < 0.0001), with the endoscopic cohort being the oldest (mean age, 84.4 ± 4.7 years). Despite this advanced age, the admission GCS score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and hematoma volume did not differ significantly from those of the other groups. Addressing the comparability of disease severity, the ICH score was similar across groups (endoscopic, 3.07 ± 0.87; craniotomy, 2.27 ± 0.90; conservative, 2.64 ± 0.84; p = 0.97), confirming that the treatment allocation was not biased by the initial hemorrhage severity. Although not statistically significant, the endoscopic group had the highest mean ICH score among the 3 cohorts, indicating that these patients achieved functional recovery despite having more severe baseline characteristics. Because FIM outcomes were evaluated only in patients who survived until rehabilitation admission, these analyses excluded deceased patients. Among the survivors, there were no significant differences in the total, motor, or cognitive FIM scores among the groups at rehabilitation admission or discharge. Similarly, the change in FIM scores from admission to discharge, including the total, motor, and cognitive components, did not differ significantly among the 3 groups. Across all treatment groups, motor FIM gain consistently exceeded cognitive FIM gain, indicating that functional recovery in this older cohort was primarily driven by improvements in basic motor abilities rather than cognitive domains.

Based on the ROC analysis using the 90-day mRS, a hematoma volume of 40 mL was identified as the optimal cutoff value, as shown in Supplementary Figure 2A and B. Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical utility of consistency-guided endoscopic hematoma evacuation under local anesthesia in older adults with spontaneous lobar ICH. By incorporating a time-dependent model of hematoma consistency, we identified the subacute phase, specifically after day 7, as the optimal window for minimally invasive intervention. This temporal evolution of hematoma consistency, from solid to firm, soft, and eventually liquefied, is consistent with established pathophysiological descriptions27) and aligns with prior reports,14) suggesting that hematomas naturally soften during this period, facilitating safer and more efficient evacuation. Crucially, we demonstrated that delayed intervention may allow effective clot removal once liquefaction has progressed, minimizing the surgical trauma associated with the manipulation of solid clots in the acute phase. Importantly, endoscopic evacuation in the subacute phase differs fundamentally from conventional acute hematoma removal, as the procedure primarily involves short-duration aspiration of liquefied components rather than mechanical manipulation of solid clots. In this context, evacuation after hematoma softening may provide earlier decompression with minimal physiological stress under local anesthesia, potentially attenuating ongoing secondary injury and facilitating earlier mobilization and rehabilitation in frail, older adults.

Importantly, prior studies have demonstrated that minimally invasive evacuation has historically been performed in the subacute phase. A classic study by Yamanaka and Satoh showed that stereotactic aspiration for putaminal hemorrhage was typically performed around day 7, when the hematoma had softened sufficiently for safe removal under local anesthesia.28) More recently, Eto et al.29) demonstrated that stereotactic evacuation performed in the subacute phase (mean 13.6 days) achieved a high removal rate and improved FIM scores, particularly in cognitive domains. These findings support the concept that the subacute phase provides a physiologically favorable window for minimally invasive clot evacuation, which aligns directly with our consistency-guided strategy. In our cohort of very old and frail patients, motor FIM gain exceeded cognitive gain across all treatment groups, suggesting that motor function may remain more responsive to rehabilitation than cognitive domains in this population.

The rationale for delaying surgery in this specific cohort differs fundamentally from the “early evacuation” strategies advocated for younger patients in trials such as ENRICH.11) The “oldest-old” population frequently exhibits significant age-related cerebral atrophy,5) providing greater intracranial compliance. Consequently, many of these patients remain neurologically stable during the acute phase despite having hematoma volumes that would mandate urgent decompression in younger adults. This physiological reserve allows for a safe observation period until the hematoma softens. Furthermore, unlike simple aspiration via a biopsy needle, endoscopic surgery allows for the management of septations, and partially organized components often found in subacute hematomas, ensuring complete evacuation under direct visualization of the hematoma.15-20,30)

In terms of functional outcomes, multivariate analysis identified older age, lower admission GCS score, and larger hematoma volume as independent predictors of poor outcome, consistent with previous studies.31) Although the median mRS score was 5 across all groups, this likely reflects the well-recognized “ceiling effect” of the mRS in the very old patients rather than treatment inefficacy.21) The lack of difference in ICH scores among the groups suggests comparable baseline severity; therefore, the uniformly poor mRS outcomes are more plausibly attributed to the intrinsic frailty and low neurological reserve of this population rather than to the surgical timing or modality itself. Furthermore, after adjusting for baseline characteristics in the multivariate ordinal logistic regression model, treatment modality was not independently associated with 90-day mRS outcomes, confirming that the lack of group differences was not attributable to imbalances in baseline severity. Although the hematoma location differed among the groups, primarily due to a higher proportion of occipital hemorrhages in the endoscopic cohort, this imbalance did not appear to influence treatment allocation or outcomes, as the baseline severity and functional measures were comparable across the groups.

The absence of deep hemorrhages in our cohort does not imply a favorable functional prognosis. Lobar ICH in older patients is frequently associated with extensive cortical injury and underlying pathologies such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy,4) which, combined with age-related comorbidities, severely limits recovery potential.

Herein, ROC analysis using the 90-day mRS (0-3 vs. 4-6) demonstrated moderate discriminative ability (area under the curve = 0.723) and suggested approximately 40 mL as a practical hematoma volume threshold. This value is broadly consistent with previous reports indicating that hematoma volumes of approximately 40 mL are associated with unfavorable outcomes, supporting the plausibility of this cutoff in our cohort. Conversely, the exploratory ROC analysis using FIM gain ≥10 showed limited discriminative performance and did not identify a clinically meaningful cutoff, likely reflecting the characteristics of FIM gain in very old and severely affected patients, in whom functional improvement is often modest; additionally FIM gain cannot be assessed in those with severe disability or death.

Nevertheless, when applying the 40 mL threshold derived from the mRS-based ROC analysis, patients with hematoma volumes <40 mL demonstrated significantly greater FIM gain during rehabilitation. Although FIM gain was not suitable for determining an independent cutoff by ROC analysis, it still captured clinically relevant differences in functional recovery when an externally derived threshold was applied. Taken together, these findings suggest that a hematoma volume of approximately 40 mL may represent a reasonable and clinically relevant threshold associated with both global outcomes and functional recovery in this population. These considerations provide the context for interpreting the subgroup findings based on hematoma volume.

Notably, while the mRS failed to detect differences between treatment groups in the overall cohort, the FIM revealed potential functional advantages of the endoscopic approach in specific subgroups. In patients with hematoma volumes ≥40 mL, the endoscopic group was associated with greater FIM gain compared with the conservative group (p = 0.04; Supplementary Table 2B). This finding highlights the limited sensitivity of the mRS in capturing subtle but clinically meaningful recovery (e.g., ability to eat or transfer with assistance) in older adults and supports the complementary use of the FIM to evaluate the true value of palliative or function-preserving surgery.21) In this population, even a small increment in FIM gain can reduce the burden of care and facilitate rehabilitation, justifying the role of minimally invasive surgery under local anesthesia. Although the minimal important change of the FIM has been reported to be substantially larger in general geriatric stroke populations, these thresholds may not be directly applicable to very old, frail patients with severe ICH, in whom even modest functional changes can meaningfully affect caregiving burden and rehabilitation potential.32)

Regarding patient selection, conservative management in this study was not a proxy for “withdrawal of care” but was selected for patients deemed medically unsuitable for surgery due to severe frailty or comorbidities. Conversely, the endoscopic group consisted of neurologically stable patients who were high-risk candidates for GA. Among neurologically stable patients who were considered suitable for GA, a small subset underwent acute craniotomy based on clinical judgment (n = 3), whereas the remaining patients were observed until day 7 for reassessment of hematoma consistency. This stepwise allocation ensured that acute craniotomy was applied not only to patients with rapid neurological deterioration but also to selected stable patients when early intervention was considered appropriate. The low rate of deterioration among the remaining patients indicates that this consistency-guided strategy can be implemented safely in the oldest-old population.

The decision to delay surgery, even in patients with hematoma volumes ≥60 mL, was strictly based on neurological stability afforded by the increased intracranial compliance.5) These patients underwent rigorous monitoring to ensure that the delay did not compromise their safety. This strategy reflects an approach tailored to the distinct clinical physiology of older adults, distinguishing it from the management of younger patients, where rapid herniation is a primary concern.

Endoscopic evacuation has been successfully performed under local anesthesia using a rigid endoscope and transparent sheath.22) As all targets were superficial lobar lesions, neuronavigation was not routinely required. The combination of local anesthesia and light sedation (e.g., dexmedetomidine) maintains spontaneous respiration, offering a viable surgical option for patients who would otherwise be considered inoperable under GA.

Major randomized trials, such as STICH,9) MISTIE III,10) and ENRICH,11) primarily enrolled younger or “young-old” patients and focused on acute-phase interventions under GA. In contrast, the present study proposes a practical and sustainable treatment model tailored to older adults with frailty,23) a demographic often excluded from major trials.12,13) Therefore, consistency-guided endoscopic evacuation during the subacute phase may represent a valuable alternative pathway, bridging the gap between conservative care and high-risk acute surgery for this growing patient population.

This study has some limitations, including its retrospective single-center design, small sample size, and reliance on time-based rather than quantitative measures of hematoma consistency. Furthermore, although our study demonstrated the safety and potential efficacy of subacute endoscopic evacuation under local anesthesia, we did not directly compare these outcomes with those of acute endoscopic evacuation under GA. Given the high risks associated with GA in frail older adults, our subacute approach offers a practical alternative. Future prospective studies comparing subacute local anesthesia interventions with acute GA interventions are warranted to definitively establish the optimal timing and modality for this vulnerable population.

Additionally, future studies should incorporate objective metrics, such as computed tomography density (Hounsfield units) or intraoperative suction resistance, and employ prospective designs with propensity score adjustments to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Consistency-guided endoscopic hematoma evacuation under local anesthesia is a safe and feasible treatment strategy for older adults with spontaneous lobar ICH, particularly when strategically timed during the subacute phase to leverage hematoma softening. This study demonstrates that even the “oldest-old” population—a cohort largely excluded from major surgical trials and often unsuitable for acute-phase interventions—can benefit from this minimally invasive and physiologically tailored approach. While the prognostic ceiling imposed by extensive primary brain injury limits recovery in massive hemorrhages, functional improvements are evident in patients with moderate-to-large hematomas. Notably, the FIM was more sensitive than the mRS in detecting clinically meaningful recovery, underscoring the necessity of using outcome measures that capture granular functional gains relevant to geriatric care. Collectively, these findings support a sustainable, resource-efficient neurosurgical pathway tailored to older adults with frailty, addressing the critical therapeutic gap in our aging society.

By days 7-9, the hematoma softens and the mass effect peaks, making endoscopic evacuation under local anesthesia feasible. After day 10, the hematoma liquefies and the mass effect declines.

Receiver operating characteristic curve for predicting poor functional outcomes using hematoma volume. The receiver operating characteristic curve demonstrates the discriminatory ability of hematoma volume for predicting poor functional outcome (modified Rankin Scale 4-6 at 90 days). The area under the curve was 0.723 (95% confidence interval, 0.539-0.906). The optimal cutoff identified by the Youden index was 39 ml.

Sensitivity and specificity curves identifying the optimal hematoma volume cutoff. Sensitivity and specificity for predicting poor functional outcome (modified Rankin Scale 4-6 at 90 days) are plotted across hematoma volume thresholds.
