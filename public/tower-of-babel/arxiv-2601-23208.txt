A Random Matrix Theory of Masked Self-Supervised Regression
Arie Wortsman, Federica Gerace, Bruno Loureiro, Yue M. Lu

Abstract
In the era of transformer models, masked self-supervised learning (SSL) has become a foundational training paradigm. A defining feature of masked SSL is that training aggregates predictions across many masking patterns, giving rise to a joint, matrix-valued predictor rather than a single vector-valued estimator. This object encodes how coordinates condition on one another and poses new analytical challenges. We develop a precise high-dimensional analysis of masked modeling objectives in the proportional regime where the number of samples scales with the ambient dimension. Our results provide explicit expressions for the generalization error and characterize the spectral structure of the learned predictor, revealing how masked modeling extracts structure from data. For spiked covariance models, we show that the joint predictor undergoes a Baik–Ben Arous–Péché (BBP)-type phase transition, identifying when masked SSL begins to recover latent signals. Finally, we identify structured regimes in which masked self-supervised learning provably outperforms PCA, highlighting potential advantages of SSL objectives over classical unsupervised methods.



1
Introduction

Self-supervised learning (SSL) — a training paradigm in which models learn useful representations from unlabeled data by exploiting the data itself as a source of supervision — has emerged as a foundational component of the recent success of transformer architectures. By avoiding the need for manual annotations, SSL retains many of the benefits traditionally associated with supervised learning while avoiding reliance on labeled data. Consequently, SSL is widely adopted as a pretraining paradigm for learning general-purpose representations that substantially accelerate the optimization of downstream tasks, especially in data-scarce settings.

A canonical example of a self-supervised learning task is masked language modeling (MLM), in which a neural network is trained to predict masked tokens in text using the remaining tokens as contextual information
(
16
;
27
;
47
;
9
;
44
)
. For example, given the sentence “
The capital of France is Paris
”, a typical MLM task would be to teach the model to infer that we are speaking about the capital of a country from the context “France” and “Paris” from the masked sentence “
The [MASK] of France is Paris
”. Masked language modeling is the core training framework of LLMs in the BERT family
(
17
;
34
)
, where it is used to train state-of-the-art NLP encoders for which bi-directional context is crucial, as well as in vision transformers
(
6
;
26
)
.

Recent works
(
50
;
49
)
have numerically shown that, when trained with MLM, transformers learn the conditional distribution of a missing token given the observed ones by sequentially inferring positional and semantic relationships across the entire text. However, several fundamental questions remain open. For instance:
How much data is required to achieve good generalization performance in MLM? How does this performance depend on the underlying structure of the data, and in particular on the correlations among tokens?

In this work, we investigate masked self-supervised learning in its simplest declination: learning real-valued sequence data with a matrix-valued linear predictor. More precisely, given
n
real-valued sequences
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
corresponds to the length of the sequence, we train a family of ridge-regularized linear predictors
X\mapsto X\hat{A}
under the constraint that no coordinate of the sequence can be used to predict itself (this will be made precise in the next section). In this context,
our main contributions
are:

•

Asymptotic limit for training and generalization errors:
We derive a sharp asymptotic characterization of the training and generalization performance of the aggregate predictor
\hat{A}
in the high-dimensional regime where
n,d\to\infty
with a fixed ratio
\nicefrac{{n}}{{d}}\to\alpha>0
. The resulting asymptotic limit depends only on the population covariance of the data, yielding a precise and interpretable description of which structural properties of the data enable successful generalization in masked self-supervised regression (SSR).

•

Deterministic equivalent for the predictor:

Beyond performance metrics, we establish a high-dimensional deterministic equivalent for (the resolvent of) the matrix-valued aggregate predictor
\hat{A}
itself, which allows us to characterize how self-supervised learning encodes and exploits the underlying data geometry. Technically, this result relies on the analysis of a random matrix defined as an aggregate of a diverging number of correlated predictors. This matrix does not belong to standard random-matrix ensembles, and our analysis may be of independent interest.

•

Spiked vs AR(1):

We further provide an in-depth analysis of two prototypical statistical models. In a
spiked covariance model
, we show that principal component analysis (PCA) strictly outperforms masked self-supervised learning, and we establish a BBP-type transition in the asymptotic spectrum of
\hat{A}
occurring at the same threshold as the corresponding transition for the sample covariance matrix. In sharp contrast, for a
first-order autoregressive model
, we prove that masked self-supervised regression can strictly dominate PCA in performance if the number of directions in PCA is not close to the dimension. We further provide a detailed analysis of the effective inductive bias induced by strong temporal correlations, elucidating the mechanisms by which self-supervision leverages sequential structure.

Together, our results elucidate the mechanisms by which masked self-supervised learning exploits correlations in the training data, and provide a principled comparison between SSR and classical spectral approaches, revealing their respective strengths and limitations in the high-dimensional regime.

2
Setting

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote
n
data points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
X=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
denote the data matrix. Here, each
x_{i}
can be thought as a sequence of (real valued) tokens or pixels of length
d
, in the case of text or image data, for instance.

As motivated in the introduction, we are interested in the problem of masked self-supervised learning: given a sequence
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an arbitrary coordinate
k\in[d]
(the “
mask
”), the goal is to predict the masked coordinate
x_{k}
from the remaining
[d]\setminus\{k\}
coordinates. Here, we focus on the simplest instance of this problem where we perform self-supervised ridge regression with regularization
\lambda>0
:

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{k}

\displaystyle=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
a_{k}=0\end{subarray}}{\argmin}\frac{1}{n}||X_{k}-Xa||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||a||_{2}^{2},

(2.1)

where the constraint
a_{k}=0
makes sure that we do not use the
k
th coordinate to predict itself.

Remark 1
.

The problem in
eq.

2.1
corresponds exactly to the regularized maximum likelihood problem for the case in which the sequence
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is jointly Gaussian and the variance is fixed.

Equation

2.1
is a strictly convex problem for
\lambda>0
— denote by
\hat{a}_{k}
its minimizer for each
k\in[d]
. We can define the following aggregate prediction matrix:

\hat{A}:=[\hat{a}_{1},\hat{a}_{2},\dots,\hat{a}_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

(2.2)

Note that by construction
\mathrm{diag}(\hat{A})=0
. This matrix, which we will refer to as the
self-supervised ridge matrix
(SSR matrix), contains aggregated information about the correlations in the training data
X
.

Remark 2

(Relationship with factored attention)
.

As discussed in
(
50
)
, for this real-valued sequence task the matrix-valued predictor
\hat{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
can be interpreted as the attention map of a single layer of
factored self-attention
, with an untrained value matrix fixed to the identity. Despite its simplicity, factored attention has been empirically shown to outperform standard attention mechanisms in some contexts, including protein contact prediction
(
8
)
and the approximation of ground states of many-body quantum systems
(
56
;
51
;
57
)
.

In the following, we will be mainly interested in two questions: (i) what properties of the data are captured by the SSR matrix
\hat{A}
? (ii) How well does
\hat{A}
perform reconstruction of the masked entries at a fixed training data budget, as quantified by the total reconstruction population risk:

L(\hat{A})=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\|x-\hat{A}^{\top}x\|^{2}\right],

(2.3)

where the expectation is taken over a new sequence
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is not in the training set.

Remark 3
.

The total reconstruction error is also commonly used as the generalization error in other unsupervised learning tasks, in particular for PCA [see, e.g
(
48
)
and
(
21
)
].

Complementary, we also define the total reconstruction empirical risk:

\hat{L}_{n}(\hat{A})=\frac{1}{nd}||X-XA||^{2}_{F},

(2.4)

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is the training data matrix in
Equation

2.1
. We will refer to
Equation

2.3
and
Equation

2.4
as the generalization and training errors, respectively.

Our main results provide sharp answers to both questions (i) and (ii) in the
high-dimensional limit
, where the number of samples
n
and the sequence length
d
grow to infinity proportionally, with
\nicefrac{{n}}{{d}}\to\alpha=\Theta_{d}(1)
denoting the sample complexity. More precisely, we will derive a
deterministic equivalent
for the resolvent of the SSR matrix
\hat{A}
and asymptotic limits of the corresponding risks
L(\hat{A})
,
\hat{L}_{n}(\hat{A})
. These results allow us to precisely characterize how the aggregated predictor encodes the geometry of the underlying data distribution, and how this geometry governs the total reconstruction error.

3
Main Results

Our first result concerns the structural properties of the aggregated predictor matrix
\hat{A}
defined in
eq.

2.2
. While vector-valued ridge estimators of the form
eq.

2.1
have been extensively analyzed in the random matrix theory literature, the aggregate estimator studied here exhibits fundamentally different behavior. In particular, although each coordinate-wise minimizer
\hat{a}_{k}
solves a standard ridge regression problem, all such estimators are trained on the same data matrix
X
, inducing strong statistical dependencies across coordinates. As a consequence, existing RMT results that treat each
\hat{a}_{k}
in isolation are insufficient to characterize the joint behavior of the matrix-valued estimator
\hat{A}
, its spectrum, and the associated reconstruction risk. A global analysis that explicitly accounts for these correlations is therefore required. To that end, we first derive a joint expression for the minimizer
\hat{A}
:

Lemma 3.1
.

For
\lambda>0
, let
\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denote the sample-covariance matrix and let

Q(\lambda)=(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}

denote the resolvent of
\hat{\Sigma}
. Then

\hat{A}=I_{d}-Q(\lambda)\Lambda,\qquad\Lambda=[\mathrm{diag}(Q(\lambda))]^{-1}.

We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for the proof.

Remark 4
.

Note that the matrix
\hat{A}
is, in general, not symmetric as
Q(\lambda)
and
\Lambda
do not commute in general. However, by applying the similarity transformation
S\to\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}S\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}
to the matrix
Q(\lambda)\Lambda
, we obtain the matrix
\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}
, which is a symmetric matrix. Then
\hat{A}
has the same spectrum as
I_{d}-\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and therefore has a real-valued spectrum.

Lemma

3.1
shows that
\hat{A}
depends on the data only through the resolvent of the empirical covariance matrix. This representation naturally suggests an approach based on random matrix theory. The main technical challenge, however, is that this dependence appears through the product
Q(\lambda)\Lambda
, where
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix defined as a nonlinear function of the resolvent. Although
\Lambda
admits a deterministic equivalent in the high-dimensional limit, its finite-dimensional dependence on
Q(\lambda)
precludes a direct application of standard random matrix results. Our analysis therefore proceeds by first establishing a sharp concentration of
\Lambda
around its deterministic limit, and then leveraging a linearization argument to control the resulting structured product and characterize the behavior of the aggregated predictor.

3.1
The Generalization and Training Errors

With the description of
\hat{A}
in
Lemma

3.1
, we can characterize the generalization properties of the SSR predictor. A first natural statistical question is whether this is a consistent estimator, i.e. whether the estimator
\hat{A}
reaches vanishing generalization error in the classical statistical limit where
n\to\infty
at fixed
d=\Theta_{n}(1)
. The answer is negative: To see this, recall that given
k\in[d]
, we can always decompose

x_{k}=\mathbb{E}[x_{k}|x^{(-k)}]+(x_{k}-\mathbb{E}[x_{k}|x^{(-k)}])
, and
\mathbb{E}[x_{k}|x^{(-k)}]
is the best estimator of
x_{k}
given the rest of the coordinates. Then, if
x_{k}-\mathbb{E}[x_{k}|x^{(-k)}]
is non-zero, there will always be a part that is not predictable by the rest of the coordinates. As an example, if the data were sampled from a Gaussian distribution with a full-rank covariance, then
x_{k}-\mathbb{E}[x_{k}|x^{(-k)}]
is always non-zero.

Lemma 3.2

(Approximation error)
.

Assume
\Sigma:=\mathbb{E}[xx^{\top}]\succ 0
. Then, the approximation error is given by:

\displaystyle L_{\rm App}:=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\\
{\rm diag}(A)=0\end{subarray}}{\min}L(A)=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma^{-1}[\diag(\Sigma^{-1})]^{-2})=\dfrac{1}{d}\sum_{k\leq d}\frac{1}{(\Sigma^{-1})_{kk}}.

(3.1)

and it is attained by
A_{\mathrm{App}}:=I_{d}-\Sigma^{-1}[\diag(\Sigma^{-1})]^{-1}
.

Remark 5
.

The approximation error defined above is the best achievable error in the SSR hypothesis: a linear predictor in the form of a square matrix with zero-diagonal.

Remark 6
.

For Gaussian Data, the Approximation error coincides with the sum of the Bayes errors for each coordinate prediction in
Equation

2.1
. To see this, note that for each
k
, we have that the Bayes error of the predictor
\hat{a}_{k}
in
Equation

2.1
is achieved by the estimator
\mathbb{E}\left[x_{k}|x_{-k}\right]
. When
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, for
\Sigma\succ 0
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[(x_{k}-\mathbb{E}\left[x_{k}|x_{-k}\right])^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Var}(x_{k}|x_{k-1})=\dfrac{1}{(\Sigma^{-1})_{k,k}},

(3.2)

and the sum of the Bayes errors for each coordinate becomes
the expression in
Lemma

3.2
.

\Sigma=I_{d}

Lemma

3.2
shows that in general the lowest achievable error is non-zero. Moreover, note that in the particular case where
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix, the best possible estimator is
\hat{A}\equiv 0
. This is intuitive, as when coordinates are not correlated there is no information on a coordinate in the remaining. This highlights that structure is fundamental for successful SSR, and raises the question of what properties of the data geometry are particularly important. To get an intuition for this question, consider the spectral decomposition of the population covariance
\Sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}u_{k}u_{k}^{\top}
, and note the approximation error can be rewritten as:

L_{\mathrm{App}}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}u_{k}^{\top}[\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma^{-1})]^{-2}u_{k}.

(3.3)

Taking inspiration from the case of Gaussian data, we have that for
k\in[d]
,
\mathrm{Var}(x_{k}|x_{-k})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{(\Sigma^{-1})_{kk}}}
. Hence, the matrix
[\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma^{-1})]^{-1}
can be interpreted as a measure of how predictable (or explainable) each coordinate is with respect to the rest. With this interpretation, we see that the quadratic form
u_{k}^{\top}[\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma^{-1})]^{-2}u_{k}
penalizes the magnitude of the vectors
u_{k}
with this variance. Therefore, this quantity is lower when the coordinates of the eigenvectors are delocalized, and
\mathrm{Var}(x_{k}|x_{-k})
is large for each
k\in[d]
.

Now that we understand what the best achievable error is, we move to addressing the performance of SSR at finite sample complexity. Our first main result is to show that the risk achieved by the SSR predictor
L(\hat{A})
admits a fully deterministic characterization in the proportional high-dimensional limit where
d\to\infty
with
n=\Theta(d)
, depending only on the population covariance
\Sigma
, the asymptotic ratio
\nicefrac{{n}}{{d}}\to\alpha
and the regularization strength
\lambda>0
.

Theorem 1

(Asymptotic Limit of the risk)
.

Let
X=Z\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, with
\Sigma\succeq 0
, and
\|\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}
bounded, and
Z
having independent entries, with mean
0
, variance
1
, and
4+\varepsilon
-th bounded moments. Let
\mathrm{df}_{1}^{\Sigma}:=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma(\Sigma+\kappa I_{d})^{-1})
and
\mathrm{df}_{2}^{\Sigma}:=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma^{2}(\Sigma+\kappa I_{d})^{-2})
denote the degrees of freedom. Let
\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)
denote the (unique) solution of the self-consistent equation:

\kappa=\lambda+\frac{\kappa}{n}\mathrm{df}_{1}^{\Sigma}(\kappa).

(3.4)

Then, as
n,d\to\infty
at fixed
\nicefrac{{n}}{{d}}\to\alpha=\Theta_{d}(1)
, the normalized generalization error converges in probability to:

\displaystyle L(\hat{A})

\displaystyle\to L_{1}\left(1+\dfrac{\mathrm{df}^{\Sigma}_{2}(\kappa_{\star}(\lambda))}{n-\mathrm{df}^{\Sigma}_{2}(\kappa_{\star}(\lambda))}\right)

where

\displaystyle L_{1}=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\bar{D}^{2}(\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)I_{d})^{-1}\right],

and

\displaystyle\bar{D}=\bar{D}(\Sigma,\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)):=[\mathrm{diag}((\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)I_{d})^{-1})]^{-1}.

Remark 7
.

It is important to highlight that computing this asymptotic limit from existing limits in the literature for the minimizer
\hat{a}_{k},k\in[d]
(e.g.,
(
18
;
58
;
25
)
) would require summing a diverging number of individual formulas, one for each
k\in[d]
. Instead,
Theorem

1
gives us a closed formula for the loss associated with the aggregate predictor.

To get some intuition for the formulas in
Theorem

1
, it is instructive to consider the ridge-less limit
\lambda\to 0^{+}
when
\alpha>1
. In this case,
\kappa(\lambda)\to 0
, so
L_{1}\to L_{\mathrm{App}}
defined in
Lemma

3.2
, which is independent of
n
. Then:

L(\hat{A})-L_{1}\sim L_{1}\dfrac{\mathrm{df}_{2}(0)}{n-\mathrm{df}_{2}(0)}=L_{\rm App}\dfrac{1}{\nicefrac{{n}}{{d}}-1}.

(3.5)

From the above, we can see an explicit dependence of the generalization error on
\alpha
. More generally, we can study how
L(\hat{A})
converges to the approximation error by computing the degrees of freedom, which depend only on the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
. Then, the general performance of the SSR estimator will be determined by
L_{\mathrm{App}}
, which is a highly structure-dependent quantity (as described above), and the speed at which the convergence will happen depends on the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
.

As noted in Remark

\hat{A}
by studying the eigenvalues of the matrix
\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}

\Lambda=[\mathrm{diag}(Q(\lambda))]^{-1}

\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}

\hat{A}

Theorem 3
.

For
\lambda>0
and
\alpha=\frac{n}{d}
, let
\tilde{m}(\lambda)
be the unique solution of the self-consistent equation:

\tilde{m}(\lambda)=\left(\lambda+\dfrac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Sigma(\tilde{m}\Sigma+I_{d})^{-1}\right)\right)^{-1}.

(3.6)

For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, let
\chi
be the solution to the self-consistent equation

\chi=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Sigma(-\lambda I_{d}-\frac{1}{1-\chi}\Sigma+\bar{D}/z)^{-1}\right).

(3.7)

where
\bar{D}
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\bar{D}_{k,k}=\frac{\lambda}{(\tilde{m}(\lambda)\Sigma+I_{d})^{-1}_{k,k}}
. Then, the matrix:

\mathcal{G}(z)=\left[\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{1-\chi}\Sigma+\lambda I_{d}\right)^{-1}\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}-zI_{d}\right]^{-1}

(3.8)

is a deterministic equivalent for the resolvent:

G(z):=(\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}-zI_{d})^{-1}.

(3.9)

As an immediate consequence, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, the Stieltjes transform of the empirical spectral measure of
\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}
evaluated at

\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathcal{G}(z)
.

for details of this model.)

3.2.1
Universality of the spectrum

An interesting consequence of
Theorem

3
arises when we focus on the case where the covariance
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix and we consider the ridge-less limit
\lambda\to 0^{+}
. From
Lemma

3.2
, we know that this task is equivalent to simply fitting noise.

Corollary 1
.

Assume
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix. Then, for
\alpha>1
and
\lambda\to 0
, the deterministic equivalent for the resolvent in
Theorem

3
is independent of
\Sigma
:

\mathcal{G}(z)=\dfrac{1}{(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})(1-\chi)-z}I_{d},

where
\chi
solves:

\chi^{2}+(z-1)\chi+\dfrac{z}{\alpha-1}=0.

(3.10)

In particular, the spectral density of the SSR matrix
\hat{A}
is supported in the interval
\left[\frac{-2}{\sqrt{\alpha}-1},\frac{2}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}\right]
.

The fact that
\mathcal{G}(z)
is independent of the values in
\Sigma
means the asymptotic spectrum in the ridge-less limit is universal. The right of
Figure

2
illustrates this universality for the family of diagonal power-law covariances parametrized by an exponent
\beta>0
. Despite the heavy-tailed spectrum of the population covariance, as
\lambda
approaches
0^{+}
the different spectra collapse to the same universal shape for
\alpha>1
.

As a sanity check for our results on the spectrum of the SSR matrix, consider the simplest possible setting of i.i.d. isotropic Gaussian data
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. In this case, the asymptotic spectral density of
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}X^{T}X
is the Marchenko-Pastur law,
\tilde{m}(\lambda)
is the solution of

-\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}\tilde{m}(\lambda)^{2}-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}+\lambda)\tilde{m}(\lambda)+1=0.

(3.11)

Then in this case, the spectral density of the SSR matrix
\hat{A}
is just a transformation of the Marchenko-Pastur distribution. To illustrate this,
Figure

3
shows the empirical spectral density of
\hat{A}
with the deterministic spectral density stated in
Theorem

3
. In the case where
\alpha<1
, there are fluctuations around the atom on the left. These fluctuations decrease as
d
goes to infinity.

Case Studies

In the last section, we showed that the high-dimensional limit of the SSR predictor can be characterized by a deterministic equivalent depending on the data only through the properties of the population covariance
\Sigma
. We now turn to an in-depth discussion of two particular examples of structured data which are popular in the machine learning literature, corresponding to two different types of tasks: one in which the coordinates of
x
are correlated through an underlying low-dimensional structure, and one in which
x
follows an autoregressive structure. Since we are considering a linear SSL, a natural linear unsupervised learning benchmark is PCA. For the reader’s convenience, we recall that the PCA estimator with
p
principal components (or simply,
p

A^{\mathrm{PCA}}_{p}=\arg\min_{A\succ 0,\mathrm{rank}(A)=p}\dfrac{1}{dn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-Ax_{i}\|^{2}.

(4.1)

In this section, we consider the case where
\Sigma=I_{d}+\theta vv^{\top}
, with
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
\theta\geq 0
. Known as the
spiked covariance model
, it is a popular model in the statistics literature for a situation where one needs to learn a signal
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in otherwise noisy data
(
19

.

The first question we want to answer in this setting is: What is the performance of
\hat{A}
compared to PCA in the task of reconstructing a random vector with covariance given by
\Sigma=I_{d}+\theta vv^{\top}
? How does it change depending on the signal-to-noise ratio
\theta
? We first answer this question in the classical limit
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider centered data with distribution satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem

1
, and covariance
\Sigma=I_{d}+\theta vv^{\top}
, for
\theta\geq 0
and
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Let
L(\mathrm{SSR})
denote the infinite-data generalization error in
eq.

2.3
for Self-Supervised Ridge Regression, and
L(\mathrm{PCA}_{p})
denote the infinite-data generalization error for PCA with
p
directions. Then, for any values of
\theta,\lambda
and
p\geq 1
, we have:

L(\mathrm{PCA}_{p})<L(\mathrm{SSR}).

This lemma gives us insight on how strong the correlation in the data needs to be in order for SSR to achieve a reasonable performance. Recall that for isotropic data
\Sigma=I_{d}
the best SSR matrix is
\hat{A}=0
, since there is no correlation in the data. We can see the spike
\theta vv^{\top}
as a perturbation of this case, which introduces correlations between the entries of
x
. Indeed, for a constant SNR
\theta=\Theta_{d}(1)
, the correlations are vanishing in
d
(because
\|v\|_{2}=1
) so the correlations are rather weak in the high-dimensional limit.
Figure

4
illustrates this by comparing the empirical error of PCA with the asymptotic limit of the generalization error in
Theorem

1
for different values of the SNR
\theta
in the large
n
limit. As it can be seen, even the approximation error in this case is not better than
L(\hat{A})=1
, which corresponds to no reconstruction.

We now turn to the question of whether signatures of the low-dimensional structure of
\Sigma
can be detected in the spectrum of the SSR matrix
\hat{A}
. Recall that the sample covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}
in this model undergoes a Baik-Ben Arous-Peché (BBP) transition
(
5
)
: the spectrum of
\hat{\Sigma}
will not identify the spiked direction
v
unless
\theta>\theta^{\star}
, for some critical value
\theta^{\star}>0
. In other words, under certain values of
\theta
the spectral distribution of
\hat{\Sigma}
is no different than if the model was sampled from pure noise (isotropic data). As
\theta
is increased, there exists a value
\theta^{\star}
after which an isolated eigenvalue pops out of the Marchenko-Pastur bulk of
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}
.

Since the matrix
\hat{A}
is related to the sample covariance through the resolvent
Q(\lambda)
, a natural question is whether a similar BBP transition can be observed for
\hat{A}
. Building on the deterministic equivalents found in
3
and
Corollary

1
, we prove the following result concerning this phase transition.

Proposition 1
.

Let
s_{1},s_{2}
denote the top two eigenvalues of the SSR matrix
\hat{A}
in the ridgeless limit, and let
\xi
be the solution of
Equation

3.10
. Then

s_{1}\xrightarrow{n,d\to\infty}\begin{cases}1-z^{\ast}&\text{if }\theta>\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\\
\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{\alpha}}&\text{otherwise },\end{cases}

(4.2)

where
z^{\ast}
solves equation:

\frac{z\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}=\frac{1}{1+\theta},

(4.3)

for
\chi
solving
eq.

B.44
,
and
s_{2}\xrightarrow{n,d\to\infty}\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{\alpha}}
.

Remark 8
.

The classical BBP transition for the covariance also occurs at
\theta^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
, so the phase transition occurs at the same SNR in both models. The second eigenvalue
s_{2}
converges to the top of the bulk in the case where
\Sigma=I_{d}
(see
Corollary

1
).

is illustrated in

As a second example, we now consider a case in which

(4.4)

where
\varepsilon_{k}
are i.i.d centered random variables with variance

, and

is a parameter. Note that the

The covariance of AR(k) processes are Toeplitz matrices (see, e.g Appendix 3 in

)

such that its entry

x_{j}
and
x_{k}
that decays exponentially as
|j-k|
grows. The spectrum and properties of this matrix have been studied, for example, in
(

and
(
43
)
. Our first result for this setting shows that unlike the spiked covariance case, in the population limit SSR outperforms PCA unless the number of directions

. This is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Consider centered data with distribution satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem

1
, and covariance of the form in
Equation

4.5
, with a fixed parameter
\rho\in(0,1)
. Let
L(\mathrm{SSR})
and
L(\mathrm{PCA}_{p})
denote the population limit of the generalization error for self-supervised ridge regression and PCA with
p
directions, respectively. Let
\gamma:=\frac{p}{d}
. Then, as
d
grows to infinity and
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, we have

L(\mathrm{PCA}_{p})<L(\mathrm{SSR}),

(4.6)

if and only if

\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\dfrac{(2\rho)^{2}}{(1+\rho)^{2}+(1-\rho)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)\leq\gamma.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we studied masked self supervised models in their simplest declination: self-supervised ridge regression. We derived asymptotic limits for the generalization error and the asymptotic spectral distribution of the matrix-valued SSR estimator. With this characterization, we proved the importance of structure in the data for this type of tasks, and tested this on two classical statistical models: the spiked covariance model and an auto-regressive process of order
1
.

(A.1)

\hat{a}_{k}

Equation

2.1

\hat{A}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\\

(A.2)

A.1
Proof of
3.1

We solve this problem by imposing the KKT conditions in

A.2

g_{l}(A)=A_{l,l}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(A)

\displaystyle=F(A)-\sum_{l=1}^{d}\lambda_{l}g_{l}(A)=F(A)-\mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda A)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left((I_{d}-A^{T})X^{T}X(I_{d}-A)\right)+\lambda\mathrm{Tr}(A^{T}A)-\mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda A)

(A.4)

\frac{\partial}{\partial A}\mathcal{L}(A)=-\dfrac{2}{n}X^{T}X+\dfrac{2}{n}X^{T}XA+2\lambda A+\Lambda,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial A}=-\frac{2}{n}X^{T}X+\frac{2}{n}(X^{T}X+\lambda I_{d})A+\Lambda=0.

(A.6)

Denote

(A.7)

In order to obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}(\hat{A})=\mathrm{diag}\left((\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{d}))^{-1}(\hat{\Sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\Lambda)\right)=0

\displaystyle\iff\lambda_{k}Q({\lambda})_{k,k}=-2Q({\lambda})_{k,:}^{T}\hat{\Sigma}_{k,:}

(A.10)

where we used the fact that

(A.11)

\hat{A}=Q(\lambda)(\hat{\Sigma}-D(\lambda)).

(A.12)

By adding and subtracting the

\hat{A}=I_{d}-Q(\lambda)(D(\lambda)+\lambda I_{d}).

(A.13)

Note that

\displaystyle D(\lambda)+\lambda I_{d}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\diag(Q(\lambda)\hat{\Sigma})}{\diag(Q({\lambda}))}+\lambda I_{d}

(A.14)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\diag(Q(\lambda))}\left(\diag(Q(\lambda)\hat{\Sigma})+\lambda\diag(Q(\lambda))\right)

(A.15)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\diag(Q(\lambda))}.

(A.16)

Hence, we finally write:

\hat{A}=I_{d}-Q(\lambda)[\diag(Q(\lambda))]^{-1}.

(A.17)

Appendix B
The Spectrum of
\hat{A}

With the results in Appendix
A
, we
we can proceed to study the spectrum of the SSR matrix
\hat{A}
, given by:

\hat{A}:=I_{d}-Q(\lambda)\Lambda,\quad\Lambda=[\diag(Q(\lambda))]^{-1}

(B.1)

The first challenge we note is the second term: We have the product of
Q(\lambda)
with a diagonal matrix
\Lambda
, which are both random but not independent. We will overcome this complication by leveraging the fact that the diagonal of the resolvent is, under mild conditions, very concentrated. This is where we will first apply
Equation

A.18
. With this, we will be able to replace the diagonal matrix
[\diag(Q(\lambda))]^{-1}
by a deterministic matrix
\bar{D}
, and study the matrix
I_{d}-Q(\lambda)\bar{D}
instead of
\hat{A}
.

B.1
The non-isotropic case - Proof of
Theorem

3

In what follows, we directly study the spectrum of
\hat{A}
. Since the diagonal entries of
Q
are nonnegative, we only need to study the spectrum of the matrix

\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(B.2)

where, by
Equation

A.18

\bar{D}_{k,k}:=\dfrac{\lambda}{[(I_{d}+\tilde{m}(\lambda)\Sigma)^{-1}]_{k,k}}

(B.3)

To make notation simpler, denote
\tilde{D}=\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. For any
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, we aim to obtain a deterministic equivalent of the resolvent

G(z)=\left(\tilde{D}Q(\lambda)\tilde{D}-zI_{d}\right)^{-1}.

(B.4)

To that end, we use the linearization trick. Note that:

\left(\tilde{D}Q(\lambda)\tilde{D}-zI_{d}\right)G(z)=I_{d}.

(B.5)

Now define
G_{1}=Q(\lambda)\tilde{D}G(z)
, so
\tilde{D}G(z)+(-\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{d})G_{1}=0
. We get:

\tilde{D}G_{1}-zG(z)=I_{d}.

(B.6)

Putting these equations together, we get the following linear system of equations:

\begin{pmatrix}-zI_{d}&\tilde{D}\\
\tilde{D}&-\hat{\Sigma}-\lambda I_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}G(z)\\
G_{1}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(B.7)

Let
H
be the block matrix:

H=\begin{bmatrix}-zI&\tilde{D}\\
\tilde{D}&-\hat{\Sigma}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}.

(B.8)

Then:

\displaystyle G(z)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}G(z)\\
G_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}H^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}\\
0\end{pmatrix}=[H^{-1}]_{1,1}.

(B.9)

We can also compute
H^{-1}
via matrix block-inversion:

\displaystyle H^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}G(z)&G(z)\tilde{D}Q\\
Q\tilde{D}G(z)&(\frac{\tilde{D}^{2}}{z}-\hat{\Sigma}-\lambda I)^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

(B.10)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}G(z)&G(z)\tilde{D}Q\\
Q\tilde{D}G(z)&-Q+Q\tilde{D}G(z)\tilde{D}Q\end{bmatrix}.

(B.11)

Note that

H=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}-zI_{d}&\tilde{D}\\
\tilde{D}&-\lambda I_{d}\end{pmatrix}}_{H_{0}:=}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-\hat{\Sigma}\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{random part}}.

(B.12)

The idea is to get an approximation of
\mathbb{E}[H^{-1}]
and use it as a deterministic equivalent for
H
. For this, we first use the fact that
HH^{-1}=I_{2d}
:

\displaystyle H^{-1}H=I_{2d}\iff H^{-1}H_{0}+H\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-\hat{\Sigma}\end{pmatrix}=I_{2d}.

(B.13)

Now, since
\hat{\Sigma}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}
, we can define
\tilde{x}_{i}=(0_{d},x_{i})
, where
0_{d}
denotes the
d
-zeros vector, and we will get:

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-\hat{\Sigma}\end{pmatrix}=-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{T}.

(B.14)

Then
Equation

B.13
becomes:

H^{-1}H_{0}-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H^{-1}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{T}=I_{2d},

(B.15)

and taking expectation, since
H_{0}
is deterministic:

\mathbb{E}[H^{-1}]H_{0}-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[H^{-1}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{T}\right]=I_{2d}

(B.16)

Now, we define the leave-one-out version of
H
. For each
i\in[n]
, let

H_{\setminus i}=H_{0}-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j\not=i}\tilde{x}_{j}\tilde{x}_{j}^{T}.

(B.17)

Then, by the Sherman-Morrison formula we have that:

\displaystyle H^{-1}

\displaystyle=H_{\setminus i}^{-1}-\dfrac{H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{T}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}}{1-\frac{1}{n}\tilde{x}^{T}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{x}_{i}}.

(B.18)

Then, we can write the second term in
Equation

In this section we study the case where the covariance of the data is given by

, for
\theta\geq 0
,
\|v\|_{2}=1
. It will be useful to denote

In order to compute the numerator in

D.5

\Sigma
and

will be an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\dfrac{1+\theta}{(\tau+\theta)^{2}}

Then, we get:

and

\displaystyle a+2b-b^{2}v_{\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\theta(\tau^{2}-2\tau-\theta)}{(\tau+\theta)^{2}}+2\dfrac{\theta}{\tau+\theta}-\dfrac{\theta^{2}}{(\tau+\theta)^{2}}v_{\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\theta}{(\tau+\theta)^{2}}\left((\tau^{2}+\theta(1-v_{\ell}^{2})\right).

(D.25)

\tilde{\Sigma}_{i,j}=\rho^{\min\{|i-j|,|i-j+d|,|i-j-d|\}}.

(D.34)

Note that this only changes the element in the border of
\Sigma
. From now on, we will work with denote
\Sigma=\tilde{\Sigma}
, knowing that asymptotically both matrices share the same spectrum (see
46
, Appendix 3).

This modified version of
\Sigma
can be diagonalized by the Fourier transform, and the eigenvector are given by:

[v_{k}]_{\ell}=\exp\frac{2\pi ik\ell}{d},

(D.35)

where for each
k
, note that both the real and complex parts of
v_{k}
are eigenvectors.
As
d
grows, we can approximate the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
by:

E(x)=\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\pi x)},0\leq x\leq 1.

(D.36)

More precisely, for
\ell\in[d]
,
\lambda_{\ell}\sim E(\frac{\ell}{d})
. Now, recall we want to know the value of
Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}
, for
\ell\in[d]
. However, since
\Sigma
is now a circulant matrix, we have that
Q(\lambda)=(\Sigma+\lambda I)^{-1}
is a circulant matrix. Hence, all of it’s diagonal entries are the same. This way, we can write:

Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}=\dfrac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}Q(\lambda)_{k,k}=\dfrac{\mathrm{Tr}(Q(\lambda))}{d}.

(D.37)

Then, for large
d
:

Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}\sim\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{1}{\lambda+E(x)}dx=m_{\Sigma}(-\lambda),

(D.38)

where
m(\cdot)
is the Stletjes transform. Using the fact that

t_{\Sigma}(z)=-zm_{\Sigma}(z)-1\implies m_{\Sigma}(z)=-\dfrac{t_{\Sigma}(z)}{z}-\dfrac{1}{z},

(D.39)

for
t_{\Sigma}(z)
being the T-transform (
46
, Chapter 11) , we can use (
46
, Equation A.33) to get:

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{1}{\lambda}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}

(D.40)

where
E_{+}=\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}
,
E_{-}=\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}
. Then:

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)^{2}_{\ell,\ell}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{d}{d\lambda}Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}

(D.42)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{d}{d\lambda}\left(\dfrac{1}{\lambda}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}\right)

(D.43)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{2}}-\dfrac{\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}+\dfrac{\lambda(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}}{\lambda^{2}(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}

(D.44)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{2}}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{2}\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}-\dfrac{(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2\lambda(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{3}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(D.45)

Then:

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}-\lambda Q(\lambda)^{2}_{\ell,\ell}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}-\left(\dfrac{1}{\lambda}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}-\dfrac{(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{3}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)

(D.46)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{3}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{3}{2}}}

(D.47)

and:

\displaystyle\dfrac{Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}-\lambda Q(\lambda)^{2}_{\ell,\ell}}{[Q(\lambda)_{\ell,\ell}]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\dfrac{(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{3}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{3}{2}}}}{\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}\right)^{2}}

(D.48)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\dfrac{(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{3}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{3}{2}}}}{\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}-1}{\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}}\right)^{2}}

(D.49)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}-1\right)^{2}}.

(D.50)

With this, we conclude

L(\mathrm{SSR})\sim\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}-1\right)^{2}},

(D.51)

where we recall
E^{+}=\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}
and
E^{-}=\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}
. Let

f(\lambda,\rho):=\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}-1\right)^{2}}

(D.52)

so that

L(\mathrm{SSR})\sim f(\lambda,\rho).

(D.53)

As a sanity check, note that if we take
\rho\to 0
, we get

f(\lambda,\rho)\to 1
, which we know is the case for an isotropic covariance.

On the other hand, we can compute the approximation error for PCA for a number of direction
p
as follows. Assume that
\frac{p}{d}:=\gamma
, as
d
grows to infinity. Then, asymptotically, the approximation error of PCA will be:

L(\mathrm{PCA})=1-\dfrac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\gamma d\rfloor}\lambda_{j}\sim\int_{\gamma}^{1}E(x)dx.

(D.54)

By computing this integral, we get:

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}^{1}E(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma}^{1}\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\pi x)}dx

(D.55)

\displaystyle=(1-\rho^{2})\int_{\gamma}^{1}\dfrac{1}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\pi x)}dx

(D.56)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(1-\rho^{2})}{(1-\rho^{2})}\left(1-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\dfrac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right)\right)

(D.57)

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(E^{+}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right),

(D.58)

so

L(\mathrm{PCA})\sim\left(1-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(E^{+}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right)\right).

(D.59)

With this, we conclude that, asymptotically in
d
,

L(\mathrm{PCA})<L(\mathrm{SSR})\iff\left(1-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\dfrac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right)\right)\leq f(\lambda,\rho).

(D.60)

Now, we take the limit as
\lambda\to 0
for
f(\lambda,\rho)
. Let
F(\lambda)=\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+(E^{+}+E^{-})\lambda+1}
. Then:

\displaystyle f(\lambda,\rho)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2(\lambda+E^{-})^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda+E^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sqrt{(\lambda+E^{-})(\lambda+E^{+})}-1\right)^{2}}

(D.61)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})}{2F(\lambda)\left(F(\lambda)-1\right)^{2}}.

(D.62)

Now:

F(\lambda)-1=\dfrac{F(\lambda)^{2}-1}{F(\lambda)+1},

(D.63)

so:

\displaystyle f(\lambda,\rho)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})(F(\lambda)+1)^{2}}{2F(\lambda)\left(F(\lambda)^{2}-1\right)^{2}}=\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})(F(\lambda)+1)^{2}}{2F(\lambda)\lambda^{2}\left(\lambda+(E^{+}+E^{-})\right)^{2}}

(D.64)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(2\lambda+E^{+}+E^{-})(F(\lambda)+1)^{2}}{2F(\lambda)\left(\lambda+(E^{+}+E^{-})\right)^{2}}.

(D.65)

By noting that
F(\lambda)\to 1
as
\lambda\to 0
, we get:

f(\lambda,\rho)\to\dfrac{2}{(E^{+}+E^{-})}.

(D.66)

Then, for any fixed
\rho\in(0,1)
, as
\lambda\to 0
we get:

L(\mathrm{PCA})<L(\mathrm{SSR})\iff\left(1-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\dfrac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right)\right)\leq\dfrac{2}{(E^{+}+E^{-})}

(D.67)

Recall that
E^{-}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{E^{+}}}
, so:

L(\mathrm{PCA})<L(\mathrm{SSR})\iff\left(1-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(E^{+}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right)\right)\leq\dfrac{2E^{+}}{(E^{+})^{2}+1}

(D.68)

Now, the LHS of this inequality is decreasing in
\gamma
. This means that, for a fixed value of
E^{+}(\rho)
, there exists a minimum value
\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)
such that
L(\mathrm{PCA})<L(\mathrm{SSR})
for all
\gamma\geq\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)
. To find this value, we have:

\displaystyle\left(1-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(E^{+}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right)\right)\leq\dfrac{2E^{+}}{(E^{+})^{2}+1}

(D.69)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}\left(1-\frac{2E^{+}}{(E^{+})^{2}+1}\right)\leq\arctan\left(E^{+}\tan\dfrac{\pi\gamma}{2}\right)

(D.70)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{1}{E^{+}}\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\left(1-\frac{2E^{+}}{(E^{+})^{2}+1}\right)\right)\right)\leq\gamma

(D.71)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{1}{E^{+}}\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{(E^{+}-1)^{2}}{(E^{+})^{2}+1}\right)\right)\right)\leq\gamma

(D.72)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\dfrac{(2\rho)^{2}}{(1+\rho)^{2}+(1-\rho)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)\leq\gamma

(D.73)

Note that
\gamma
increases as
\rho
moves closer to
1
. This means that, as the covariance matrix becomes more structured (or, in terms of the AR(1) model, the dependence on the past is stronger), PCA needs a growing number of directions to achieve the same generalization error as self-supervised Ridge Regression.

Appendix E
Generalization Error for Toeplitz Matrices for finite
\alpha=\frac{n}{d}

Recall that, by
Theorem

1
, we have the following asymptotic limit for
L(\hat{A})
:

\displaystyle L(\hat{A})

\displaystyle\to L_{1}\left(1+\dfrac{\mathrm{df}^{\Sigma}_{2}(\kappa_{\star}(\lambda))}{n-\mathrm{df}^{\Sigma}_{2}(\kappa_{\star}(\lambda))}\right)

(E.1)

where

\displaystyle L_{1}=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\bar{D}^{2}(\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)I_{d})^{-1}\right],

(E.2)

\displaystyle\bar{D}=\bar{D}(\Sigma,\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)):=[\mathrm{diag}((\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)I_{d})^{-1}))]^{-1},

(E.3)

and
\kappa_{\star}(\lambda)
satisfies the self-consistent equation:

\kappa=\lambda+\frac{\kappa}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\Sigma(\Sigma+\kappa I_{d})^{-1}\right\}.

(E.4)

We will first compute the degrees of freedom for any
\kappa>0
:

\mathrm{df}_{2}(\kappa)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Sigma^{2}(\Sigma+\kappa I_{d})^{-2}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\dfrac{\lambda_{\ell}^{2}}{(\lambda_{\ell}+\lambda)^{2}},

(E.5)

for
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}
eigenvalues of
\Sigma
,
and for any
\kappa>0
. Recall that, as we saw in the last section, as
d
grows we can approximate the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
by:

E(x)=\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\pi x)},0\leq x\leq 1.

(E.6)

More precisely, for
\ell\in[d]
,
\lambda_{\ell}\sim E(\frac{\ell}{d})
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{df}_{2}^{\Sigma}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\dfrac{\lambda_{\ell}^{2}}{(\lambda_{\ell}+\lambda)^{2}}

(E.7)

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{E(x)^{2}}{(E(x)+\kappa)^{2}}dx.

(E.8)

Developing this integral we get:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{E(x)^{2}}{(E(x)+\kappa)^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\pi x)}\right)^{2}}{\left(\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\pi x)}+\kappa\right)^{2}}dx

(E.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\pi x))\right)^{2}}dx

(E.10)

\displaystyle=(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2})-2\kappa\rho\cos(\pi x))\right)^{2}}dx.

(E.11)

To compute this last integral, we recall that, by Equation A.31 in
46
:

\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{dx}{c-d\cos(\pi x)}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{c-d}\sqrt{c+d}}.

(E.12)

Then:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{dx}{(c-d\cos(\pi x))^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{c}\dfrac{dx}{c-d\cos(\pi x)}

(E.13)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{c}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{c-d}\sqrt{c+d}}

(E.14)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sqrt{\dfrac{c+d}{c-d}}+\sqrt{\dfrac{c-d}{c+d}}}{(c-d)(c+d)}

(E.15)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{c}{\left((c-d)(c+d)\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(E.16)

Then, if we go back to
Equation

E.11
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{E(x)^{2}}{(E(x)+\kappa)^{2}}dx

\displaystyle\sim(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2})-2\kappa\rho\cos(\pi x))\right)^{2}}dx

(E.17)

\displaystyle=(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2})}{\left((1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2})+2\kappa\rho)(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2})-2\kappa\rho)\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

(E.18)

\displaystyle=(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2})}{\left((1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho)^{2})(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1-\rho)^{2})\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(E.19)

Then:

\mathrm{df}_{2}^{\Sigma}(\kappa)\sim d(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\dfrac{1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2})}{\left((1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho)^{2})(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1-\rho)^{2})\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(E.20)

Note that, taking
\rho\to 0
, we get
\mathrm{df}_{2}(\kappa)\to\dfrac{d}{(1+\kappa)^{2}}
, which corresponds to the isotropic case. Taking
\rho\to 1
, for the numerator the leading order is

(1-\rho^{2})^{2}(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho^{2}))\sim 2\kappa(1-\rho^{2})^{2},

(E.21)

while for the denominator is:

\left((1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1+\rho)^{2})(1-\rho^{2}+\kappa(1-\rho)^{2})\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\sim(4\kappa(1-\rho^{2}))^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(E.22)

Then, when
\rho\to 1
, by
Equation

E.21
and
Equation

E.22
:

\mathrm{df}_{2}^{\Sigma}(\kappa)\sim d\dfrac{2\kappa(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}{(4\kappa(1-\rho^{2}))^{\frac{3}{2}}}\sim d\dfrac{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}{4\sqrt{\kappa}}.

(E.23)

Now, as
\lambda\to 0
,
Equation

E.1
and
Equation

E.3
become:

\displaystyle L(\hat{A})

\displaystyle\to L_{1}\left(1+\dfrac{\mathrm{df}^{\Sigma}_{2}(\kappa_{\star}(0))}{n-\mathrm{df}^{\Sigma}_{2}(\kappa_{\star}(0))}\right)

(E.24)

where

\displaystyle L_{1}=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\bar{D}^{2}(\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(0)I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(0)I_{d})^{-1}\right],

(E.25)

\displaystyle\bar{D}=\bar{D}(\Sigma,\kappa_{\star}(0)):=[\mathrm{diag}((\Sigma+\kappa_{\star}(0)I_{d})^{-1}))]^{-1}.

(E.26)

For
n<d
, we have
\alpha<1
and
\kappa(0)>0
, but is very close to
0
when
\alpha\to 1
. Therefore in this case,
L_{1}
is almost constant when
\alpha
is close to
1
. Then, as
\alpha\to 1

L(\hat{A})\sim C(1+\dfrac{1}{\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}_{2}^{\Sigma}(\kappa(0))}-1}),

(E.27)

and from
Equation

E.23
:

L(\hat{A})\sim C(1+\dfrac{1}{\dfrac{4\alpha\sqrt{\kappa(0)}}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}-1}).

(E.28)

Then, as
\rho\to 1
, the rate at which the generalization error goes to infinity when
\lambda\to 0
changes, becoming slower.

Appendix F
BBP Transition - Proof of
Proposition

1

In what follows, we study the case where the population covariance takes the form of

\Sigma=I_{d}+\theta vv^{\top},

(F.1)

where
\Sigma_{0}
is the “bulk” (diagonal) matrix and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a unit-norm vector.

By the same argument we did in Appendix
B.2
for the case where there is no spake, we have the following deterministic equivalent for the resolvent:

\bar{Q}(\lambda)=(\dfrac{1}{\nu+1}I_{d}+\lambda I_{d})^{-1},

(F.2)

and
\nu
satisfies:

\nu=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(\dfrac{1}{\nu+1}+\lambda I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right).

(F.3)

Taking
\lambda\to 0
, when
\alpha>1
we get that
\nu_{\mathrm{isotropic}}=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}
, and the diagonal matrix
\bar{D}
becomes:

\bar{D}_{\mathrm{isotropic}}=\dfrac{1}{\nu+1}I_{d}.

(F.4)

Now, if we add a rank-one update
\theta vv^{T}
to a diagonal matrix by
Equation

F.3
we have that

\mathrm{\nu}_{\mathrm{spike}}=\mathrm{\nu}_{\mathrm{isotropic}}=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}.

(F.5)

Then, as
\lambda\to 0
, the deterministic equivalent for the resolvent is:

\displaystyle\bar{Q}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\dfrac{1}{\nu+1}\Sigma+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}

(F.6)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}(I_{d}+\theta vv^{T})^{-1}

(F.7)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}(I_{d}-\dfrac{\theta}{1+\theta}vv^{T}).

(F.8)

Then, in this case, for each
\ell\in[d]
:

\displaystyle(\bar{D}_{\mathrm{spike}})_{\ell,\ell}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathrm{diag}(\bar{Q}(\lambda))\right]^{-1}_{\ell,\ell}

(F.9)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\left[\mathrm{diag}(I_{d}-\dfrac{\theta}{1+\theta}vv^{T})^{-1}\right]^{-1}_{\ell,\ell}

(F.10)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\left(1-\dfrac{\theta}{1+\theta}v_{\ell}v_{\ell}\right)^{-1}.

(F.11)

Remark 9
.

Note that if the vector
v
is delocalized (with respect to
I_{d}
), then the second term is asymptotically negligible. On the other hand, if it’s localized (such as
v=e_{k}
, for some
k\in[d]
, then this term is not negligible.

We can now examine the potential spike in our problem. Recall the matrix
\hat{A}
can be written as:

\hat{A}=I-Q[\diag(Q)]^{-1}

(F.12)

As we saw before, we have that

\|\hat{A}-(I_{d}-Q(\lambda)\bar{D})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0,

(F.13)

as
d
grows. Therefore, for the purpose of this section we can study
I_{d}-Q(\lambda)\bar{D}
instead of
\hat{A}
. The eigenvalues of this matrix are in a one-to-one correspondence with those of

\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(F.14)

Specifically, if
v
is an eigenvector of
\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}
with eigenvalue
s
, then
\bar{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v
is an eigenvector of
I_{d}-Q(\lambda)\bar{D}
with eigenvalue
1-s
. In what follows, we study
\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

We will focus on the case where the spike
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is generic, i.e
\|v\|_{\infty}=o_{d}(1)
. Then, in this case, the diagonal matrix
\bar{D}
is the same as the diagonal case:

(\bar{D}_{\mathrm{spike}}^{\frac{1}{2}})_{\ell,\ell}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}I_{d},

(F.15)

and the spike does not modify it.

In the isotropic case, as stated in
Corollary

1
, the bulk of the spectrum of
\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is bounded in the interval:

I:=\left[\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1},\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}\right].

(F.16)

The spike will appear in the left of
I
. By
theorem

3
, a deterministic equivalent for the resolvent

G(z):=(\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q(\lambda)\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}-zI_{d})^{-1},z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(F.17)

is given by:

\mathcal{G}(z)=\left(\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{1-\chi}\Sigma+\lambda I_{d}\right)^{-1}\bar{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}-zI_{d}\right)^{-1},

(F.18)

where
\chi
solves:

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Sigma(-\frac{1}{1-\chi}\Sigma+\bar{D}/z)^{-1}\right)

(F.19)

Replacing
eq.

F.15
in
eq.

F.18
we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{1-\chi}(I_{d}+\theta vv^{T})\right)^{-1}-zI_{d}\right)^{-1}

(F.20)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}\left((I_{d}+\theta vv^{T})^{-1}-\dfrac{z\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}I_{d}\right)^{-1}

(F.21)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}\left(\left(1-\dfrac{z\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}\right)I_{d}-\dfrac{\theta}{\theta+1}vv^{T}\right)^{-1}

(F.22)

The spike appears at
z
such that this matrix is singular. This happens when:

\displaystyle\left(1-\dfrac{z\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}\right)-\dfrac{\theta}{\theta+1}=0

(F.23)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\dfrac{z\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}=1-\dfrac{\theta}{\theta+1}

(F.24)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\dfrac{z\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\chi)}=\dfrac{1}{\theta+1}.

(F.25)

Note that for
\theta=0
, the left-limit of the bulk is
\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}
, and in this case,
\chi_{0}
solves the quadratic equation:

\chi^{2}_{0}+(z-1)\chi+\dfrac{z}{\alpha-1}=0.

(F.26)

Note that, as
n
and
d
grow to infinity, the solution of
Equation

F.19
and
Equation

F.26
are asymptotically the same, as the spike will not change the normalized trace in the limit. Then, at
z=\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}
we have:

\displaystyle\chi_{0}^{2}+\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}-1\right)\chi_{0}+\dfrac{\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}}{\alpha-1}=0

(F.27)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\chi_{0}^{2}-\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}\chi_{0}+\dfrac{1}{(\sqrt{\alpha}+1)^{2}}=0

(F.28)

\displaystyle\implies\chi_{0}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}.

(F.29)

Then,
Equation

F.25
, for the critical
\theta_{c}
becomes:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha}-1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1}\alpha}{(\alpha-1)(1-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}+1})}=\dfrac{1}{\theta_{c}+1}

(F.30)

\displaystyle\implies\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{(\sqrt{\alpha}+1)}=\dfrac{1}{\theta_{c}+1}

(F.31)

\displaystyle\implies 1+\theta_{c}=1+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}},

(F.32)

so we conclude
