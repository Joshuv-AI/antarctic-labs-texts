A trans-therapeutic signature of recovery in internet gaming disorder: From shared network reorganization to molecular underpinnings
Authors: Ningning Zeng, Jiejie Fu, Shuang Li, Haoseng Ni, Qinxuan Li, Xin Luo, Meiting Wei, Zhoukang Wu, Liangjiecheng Huang, Min Wang, Guangheng Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsA variety of self-regulation interventions have shown efficacy in treating Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD), but their shared and unique neurobiological mechanisms remain unclear. This study aims to identify a common, trans-therapeutic neural signature of recovery and to delineate the specific connectivity fingerprints of five different self-regulation interventions for IGD.MethodsThis secondary analysis pooled resting-state fMRI data from 269 individuals with IGD who participated in one of five randomized controlled trials of self-regulation-based interventions: Approach Bias Modification (ApBM), Retrieval-Aversive Conditioning (R-AC), Progressive Aerobic Training (PAT), face-to-face Mindfulness Meditation (MM), and a Digital (online) mindfulness intervention. Participants were diagnosed with IGD according to DSM-5. Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression was used to identify a shared connectivity pattern associated with clinical improvement and similarity network fusion to define intervention-specific connectivity fingerprints. Imaging-transcriptomic analysis was further used to explore the molecular correlates.ResultsAll interventions were associated with significant reductions in IGD severity. PLS analysis identified a shared connectivity pattern predictive of treatment response, characterized by a rebalancing of Subcortical Network (SCN) connectivity along the sensorimotor-association axis. This shared pattern was linked to gene expression profiles related to cellular stress and neuroplasticity. Each intervention also induced a unique connectivity fingerprint, targeting distinct neural circuits.Discussion and ConclusionsDiverse self-regulation interventions for IGD converge on a shared therapeutic pathway involving the large-scale reorganization of cortico-subcortical circuits. These findings identify a robust neurobiological phenotype of recovery, offering a potential biomarker for treatment response and a target for future circuit-based therapies.Keywords: internet gaming disorder, randomized clinical trials, brain network, neuroplasticity, mindfulness intervention

Introduction

Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) has been recognized as a pressing global mental health concern, characterized by repetitive and persistent gaming that leads to significant functional impairment (King & Delfabbro, 2014). The disorder affects a substantial portion of the population, with an estimated global prevalence of 8.8% among adolescents and 10.4% among young adults (Gao, Wang, & Dong, 2022). The consequences of IGD extend beyond mere excessive screen time, manifesting as a complex syndrome associated with profound neurocognitive impairment (Weinstein & Lejoyeux, 2020), executive dysfunction (Wang, Dong, Zheng, Du, & Dong, 2020), and a heightened risk for emotional disorders (Wang et al., 2022), alongside other severe physical and psychosocial problems (S. Liu, Potenza, et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021). This multifaceted nature of the disorder underscores that effective interventions must consider not only its psychological and behavioral symptoms but also the remodeling of its underlying neurobiological mechanisms.

The past decade of neuroimaging research has revealed that IGD is associated with dysregulation across distributed brain networks, particularly in the interaction between subcortical and cortical systems. Central to the disorder's pathophysiology is the Subcortical Network (SCN), which includes key reward-processing hubs like the striatum and is integral to generating craving and motivational states. In parallel, several large-scale cortical networks are implicated in the top-down regulation of these subcortical drives. For instance, the SCN showed abnormal hyperconnectivity with the Frontoparietal Network (FPN), which is critical for cognitive control and goal-directed behavior (Z. Li, Luo, et al., 2025). This excessive connectivity, particularly a heightened coupling between the striatum and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in response to gaming cues, is thought to hijack cognitive resources, prioritizing game-related information processing over adaptive, goal-directed actions (Wang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2025). The Default Mode Network (DMN) also showed a nuanced pattern of dysconnectivity with the SCN (L. Wang, Simkute, & Griskova-Bulanova, 2023). The functional connectivity (FC) has shown to be weakened between the SCN and the DMN's anterior regions, disrupting value-based decision-making and self-regulation (Dong et al., 2021; Lee, Namkoong, Lee, & Jung, 2021). Conversely, FC is strengthened with the DMN's posterior regions, a change linked to intensified rumination on gaming memories and craving (Ding et al., 2013; Lee, Lee, Lee, & Jung, 2017). Furthermore, the dysconnectivity of the SCN in IGD also extends to areas such as the sensorimotor and attention networks (Chen et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022). These combined effects constitute a picture of functional imbalance between the cortical-subcortical systems in IGD, and therefore reshaping this circuit has become a major goal of IGD interventions.

The neuroscientific model of IGD internalizes this imbalance between neural systems as a fundamental failure of self-regulation, which occurs when top-down control mechanisms are unable to appropriately control the influence of bottom-up reward-related information (Dong & Potenza, 2014). The primary intervention for this regulatory failure is to consider training and enhancing the brain's inherent self-regulatory abilities. Recent umbrella reviews indicate that among various treatments, behavioral self-regulation approaches demonstrate clinical efficacy for internet-related addictions (Carvalho et al., 2025), with integrated psychological and exercise programs yielding the largest effect sizes (Zhang, Lan, Song, & Wang, 2026). Unlike interventions that rely on medication or neuromodulation, this approach primarily includes modalities such as cognitive-behavioral therapies, mindfulness meditation, and physical exercise(Lindenberg, Kindt, & Szász-Janocha, 2022; Lu et al., 2025; Nuske, Hides, & King, 2025; Xu et al., 2024). Their principle is not to act directly upon a neural target, but to train and strengthen the brain's own top-down control systems, thereby equipping individuals with durable skills to manage maladaptive behaviors (Reangsing, Wongchan, Trakooltorwong, Thaibandit, & Oerther, 2025).

At the neural level, cognitive training has been shown to weaken compulsive gaming by reshaping frontostriatal pathways, specifically by reducing dorsal striatum activation during craving regulation (Wu et al., 2022). Similarly, mindfulness meditation can decrease craving by reducing cue-related activation in the lentiform nuclei and insula, while simultaneously increasing FC between the middle frontal gyrus and the striatum (Ni et al., 2024). It can be said that the neurobiological mechanisms of these self-regulatory therapies are an active area of ​​research.

However, the clinical efficacy of these interventions exhibits significant individual variability, and the underlying factors moderating these outcomes have yet to be fully elucidated. Although it is theoretically postulated that these therapies facilitate clinical improvement by engaging different neurobiological pathways, overlap may exist among these hypothesized mechanisms (Dong, Dai, & Potenza, 2024). Critically, there is a scarcity of empirical research that directly compares the convergent and divergent effects of these different therapies on brain network connectivity within a unified framework. Their shared therapeutic pathways and unique neural fingerprints remain largely to be elucidated.

To address these gaps, this study retrospectively analyzes resting-state fMRI data from five distinct self-regulation interventions for IGD: Approach Bias Modification (ApBM), Retrieval-Aversive Conditioning (R-AC), Progressive Aerobic Training (PAT), and Mindfulness Meditation delivered in both digital and offline (MM) formats. We employed a comprehensive behavior-brain-gene model to investigate both the specific and shared outcomes of these therapeutic modalities. Specifically, at the behavioral level, we compared the clinical effectiveness of each intervention. At the neural level, we used a similarity network diffusion framework to identify the unique connectivity fingerprints for each therapy, and Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression to delineate a common, trans-therapeutic connectivity pattern associated with clinical improvement. Finally, we integrated imaging-transcriptomic analysis to explore the gene expression profiles potentially underlying this shared neural signature. We hypothesized that: 1) each intervention would be associated with a unique connectivity fingerprint, reflecting its specific mechanism of action on distinct neural networks; 2) the shared therapeutic effect of these self-regulation interventions would manifest as a large-scale remodeling of the cortico-subcortical system, particularly involving the SCN and FPN; and 3) this common neural pattern would be supported by an underlying genetic architecture involving pathways previously implicated in the neurobiology of addiction.

Methods

Participants

To elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying recovery from Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD), this study retrospectively aggregated resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) and demographic data from four independent randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The protocols for these overarching trials were prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration numbers: ChiCTR2300077831, ChiCTR2500095444, ChiCTR2400090834, and ChiCTR2300075869). Ethical approvals were granted by the respective institutional review boards, and all enrollees provided written informed consent prior to participation. Notably, the final sample sizes analyzed in the present study represent a specific rs-fMRI subset of participants retrieved directly from these primary trial databases.

Participants across studies were primarily young adults recruited from university populations in China via advertisements and online screening questionnaires. The unified inclusion criteria for an IGD diagnosis required participants to have a score of ≥50 on the Internet Addiction Test (IAT) (Widyanto & McMurran, 2004) and to meet at least five of the nine diagnostic criteria for IGD from the DSM-5 (Regier, Kuhl, & Kupfer, 2013). Key exclusion criteria were consistent across the studies and included a history of other major psychiatric or neurological disorders, comorbid depression, substance abuse or dependence, prior or ongoing intervention for IGD, and any contraindications for MRI scanning.

This retrospective analysis included a total of 269 participants with IGD who were assigned to one of the five primary intervention or control groups across the studies. The final sample for each intervention was as follows: 1) 30 participants in the ApBM group and 27 participants in control groups; 2) 30 participants in the R-AC group and 22 participants in control groups; 3) 33 participants in the PAT group and 30 participants in control groups; 4) 31 participants in the digital mindfulness meditation (Digital Intervention) group, 31 participants in the MM group and 35 participants in control groups. Demographic information for all studies is provided in Tables S1–S4 in the Supplementary Materials.

Interventions

Detailed information regarding the intervention can be found in the supplementary materials and is briefly summarized here.

Approach bias modification

This intervention is based on the dual-process model of addiction, aiming to reduce automatic approach tendencies toward gaming cues by retraining motor responses from approach to avoidance, thereby weakening the link between cues and impulsive gaming behavior (Wei et al., 2025). To achieve this, participants assigned to the ApBM group completed five training sessions over a 10-day period, with a maximum of one session per day. Each 20-min session involved a joystick-operated Approach-Avoidance Task (AAT). The task required participants to “push away” (avoid) 90% of gaming-related images while “pulling” (approaching) only 10%. The sham-AAT control group followed the same schedule, but gaming images were pushed and pulled with equal 50% probability, providing no specific bias training.

Retrieval-aversive conditioning

The principle of this intervention is to disrupt the reconsolidation of rewarding game-related memories; by introducing an aversive stimulus after memory retrieval, it aims to weaken the positive associations with gaming cues and reduce subsequent craving (Li, Li, Xu, Jiang, & Dong, 2026). The intervention was administered in five daily sessions over five consecutive days, with each session lasting approximately 40 min. Each session consisted of three phases: memory retrieval (playing a gaming video), memory reconsolidation (10 min of quiet rest), and aversive conditioning (systematically pairing gaming images with aversive images). The control group underwent an identical procedure, but neutral images were substituted for the aversive ones.

Progressive aerobic training

The intervention aimed to improve cognitive control over gaming urges by increasing participants' aerobic fitness (Q. Li, Luo, et al., 2025). The PAT intervention involved 20 supervised treadmill sessions conducted over a four-week period. Each session lasted 40 min. The training intensity was progressive: the first 10 sessions were conducted at 46% of the participant's estimated maximal oxygen uptake, which was increased to 55% for the subsequent 10 sessions. The control group performed free training for the same duration and frequency but without any prescribed structure or intensity level.

Mindfulness meditation (MM and digital intervention)

Mindfulness-based interventions aim to improve IGD by enhancing present-moment awareness and nonjudgmental acceptance of internal states (Dong et al., 2025; Ni et al., 2024). Two forms of this intervention were delivered over a four-week period, consisting of eight group sessions (two per week), with each session lasting between 2.5 and 3.5 h.

The offline Mindfulness Meditation (MM) was a group program led by professional instructors. Core practices included guided body scans, mindful breathing, and observing internal experiences (e.g., cravings, thoughts) without judgment to help participants disengage from automatic, impulsive reactions to gaming cues.

The Digital Intervention adapted this course for a remote group setting through a structured, interactive online platform. The curriculum was divided into 8 modules delivered bi-weekly, with content including video-based instructions, guided audio meditations, and online forums for group discussion and instructor feedback.

Both mindfulness groups were compared against a common active control group that participated in progressive muscle relaxation, following the identical session schedule and duration.

fMRI acquisition and analysis

IGD subjects completed resting-state fMRI scans at baseline and at the end of the intervention period. Details of fMRI acquisition, preprocessing were provided in the supplemental materials. Time series were extracted from the preprocessed data and functional connectivity (FC) matrices were calculated using the Brain Connectome Atlas (BNA), which divides the whole brain into 246 regions of interest (ROIs) covering 8 functional networks. Each subject contains two 246*246 FC matrices, which are then subtracted (post-pre) to obtain the difference matrix (ΔFC).

To quantify the Z scores of the individual ΔFC matrix of the intervention groups, we used Gaussian process regression (GPR) for normative modeling (Rutherford et al., 2022). The model was trained on all control group samples to predict the edges of the ΔFC matrix, with age, sex, intervention mode, and site effect as covariates. The trained model was then applied to a test set of 155 intervention group participants to calculate a Z score quantifying individual ΔFC deviation from the normative prediction. Each Z-transformed ΔFC matrix describes the deviation in ΔFC of the intervention individual compared to the control group. A larger absolute value of the Z value indicates a greater change in FC before and after the intervention compared to the control group.

Supervised multi-topology network cross-diffusion

To discover the unique effects of different interventions on brain network connectivity, we adopted a supervised multi-topology network cross-diffusion (SM-netFusion) framework to estimate representative and discriminative network edges (Mhiri, Mahjoub, & Rekik, 2020). Compared to other Similarity Network Fusion techniques, this framework uses supervised learning to derive a weighted combination of optimal topological diffusion kernels from multiple network topological features. This standardization can capture the shared networks between individuals at different topological scales, thereby improving the representativeness and centrality of the group map (Bessadok, Mahjoub, & Rekik, 2021).

The input of SM-netFusion is the individual connectivity matrix. Given that the network topology may be affected by negative values, we divided the Z-transformed matrix into positive and negative connectivity matrix, and flipped the negative one. All matrices were then binarized, and we set the edges (set to 1) with Z > 1.96 (corresponding to p = .05) to be retained, while the rest of the edges were set to 0. To explore the underlying data distribution within each intervention group, SM-netFusion performed a feature extraction and clustering step. The two matrices for subjects within the group were vectorized separately and concatenated into a feature matrix for each intervention group. The Single-Cell Interpretation with Multiple Kernel Learning framework was then employed to cluster participants with similar connectivity patterns into non-overlapping subgroups and assign a unique cluster label to each participant. For each participant's positive and negative sparse matrix, SM-netFusion calculated multiple topological measures, including degree, closeness, and eigenvector centrality, to capture both local and global network properties. In the supervised learning step, the subjects' cluster labels are used to learn the optimal weighted combination of these topological features. This process generates “normalization kernels” that reflect the inherent subgroup structure of the data, and these kernels are used to normalize the original sparse matrix. These normalized networks then underwent an iterative cross-diffusion process, where information is shared across similar networks to reinforce common connectivity patterns and reduce idiosyncratic noise. All the refined individual networks were fused (averaged) to produce a single, highly representative, and centered brain network atlas for each intervention condition. Finally, we employed an analysis of variance strategy suitable for multi-class comparisons to identify the “connectivity fingerprints”. We calculated the variance of each edge in five representative network graphs that was most informative in discriminating the unique effects of each intervention. Ultimately, we selected the 0.1% (30) edges with the highest variance as the final discriminative connectivity fingerprints, representing the neural pathways most significantly modulated by the different interventions.

Neuroimaging transcriptomic analysis

To elucidate the molecular underpinnings of the common neural connectivity patterns observed across the interventions, we integrated our findings with transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) (Hawrylycz et al., 2012). The data was processed using the standardized abagen toolbox workflow. This procedure mapped the expression profiles of 15,633 genes onto the 246 regions of our BNA atlas. We then employed a gene differential covariance network analysis to quantify each gene's influence (Z. Liu, Potenza, et al., 2021). A reference covariance network was first established from the full gene set based on inter-regional Spearman correlations. Next, we systematically created perturbation networks, each time omitting a single gene from the dataset. The Z-scored difference was calculated between the reference and each perturbation network. Finally, these difference matrices were vectorized, yielding a comprehensive gene differential covariance matrix (30,135 edges × 15,633 genes) that provided a transcriptomic profile for our brain connectivity phenotype.

Statistical analysis

Published studies reported the effectiveness of interventions in RCTs. We present demographic information and statistical significance of the intervention for each study in Tables S1–4 (see the Supplementary Material). Here we just compared intervention response rates across intervention groups using one-way analysis of variance, with age and sex as covariates. Simple-effects analyses were performed using a Bonferroni correction to control for false-positive results.

We applied partial least squares regression (PLS) to identify a shared multivariate feature pattern that maximally covaried with response rate of IAT ([pre-post]/pre) across all intervention individuals. The Z-transformed ΔFC matrix of the intervention individuals was vectorized and used as the independent variable, while the IAT response rate served as the dependent variable. To control for the risk of overfitting associated with high-dimensional neuroimaging data, we utilized non-parametric permutation testing. By shuffling the clinical behavioral labels 5,000 times, we generated a null distribution of the covariance. The resulting empirical p-value indicates the probability of observing the extracted PLS component by chance, serving as a robustness metric for the model. Finally, we report the correlations between PLS components and clinical variables and visualize the top 1% of edges in terms of PLS ​​weight (discriminating between positive and negative).

We also utilized PLS regression to map the relationship between spatial gene expression patterns and the shared brain connectivity phenotype. In this model, the gene differential covariance network served as the predictor variable, with the common connectivity pattern as the response variable. The statistical significance of the PLS components was assessed by permutation tests (5,000 iterations) preserving spatial autocorrelation, and weight rankings of the PLS components were generated. To functionally annotate these findings, we conducted a gene set enrichment analysis. The top 1,000 positively-weighted and bottom 1,000 negatively-weighted genes were submitted to the Metascape platform. This platform performed pathway and process enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome gene set libraries to identify the biological functions of the most influential genes. Significantly enriched gene terms were identified by a p-value <0.01 with FDR correction, a minimum count of 3, and an enrichment factor >1.5.

Ethics

All original studies received ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards. Written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to their inclusion. The trial protocols for studies were preregistered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration numbers: ChiCTR2300077831, ChiCTR2500095444, ChiCTR2400090834, and ChiCTR2300075869).

Results

Evaluating the effectiveness of different interventions

All groups showed remission on clinical scores after the intervention, while one-way ANOVA revealed differences in response rates among intervention modalities (IAT: F(4,150) = 15.823, p < .001; DSM: F(4,150) = 6.262, p < .001). Simple effects analysis showed that MM had the highest response rates for both IAT (vs ApBM, t = 6.455, p < .001; vs R-AC, t = 4.933, p < .001; vs PAT, t = 4.343, p < .001, Fig. 1A) and DSM (vs ApBM, t = 5.194, p < .001; vs R-AC, t = 3.441, p = .007, Fig. 1B). For Digital MM, we observed that it also demonstrated a superior improvement effect on the IAT (vs ApBM, t = 6.080, p < .001; vs R-AC, t = 4.434, p = .001; vs PAT, t = 3.776, p = .003).

Furthermore, we compared the symptom improvement effects across the various control groups. Intervention-specific changes in functional connectivity

The SM-netFusion analysis identified the top 30 discriminative edges for each intervention, revealing specific fingerprints of neural change. These distinct connections highlight the unique impact of each therapeutic modality relative to the normative baseline. While the unique fingerprints exhibited notable spatial heterogeneity, the FPN frequently emerged as a modulated cortical hub across several modalities, albeit with diverse reconfiguration patterns. Specifically, the ApBM fingerprint was characterized by enhanced connectivity within the Somatomotor Network (SMN) and between the SMN and Dorsal Attention Network (DAN), alongside attenuated connectivity between the FPN and Default Mode Network (DMN). R-AC induced a distinct pattern, strengthening connections between the FPN and DMN, while concurrently reducing functional coupling between the Ventral Attention Network (VAN) and both the FPN and DMN. The physical and mental training modalities demonstrated their own unique FPN-centric reconfigurations: PAT primarily enhanced intra-FPN connections and FPN-DMN coupling, while attenuating connections between the FPN and attention networks (DAN and VAN). Offline MM induced a concentrated topological reconfiguration within the FPN itself (displaying concurrent positive and negative edge changes), alongside heterogeneous alterations in FPN coupling with the DMN, DAN, and Limbic networks. Finally, the Digital Intervention was characterized by enhanced connectivity between the FPN and widespread networks (DMN, Limbic, VAN), while concurrently decreasing connectivity within and between attention and motor networks (DAN-VAN, DAN-SMN). Collectively, different therapies utilize unique, heterogeneous cortical pathways to initiate cognitive control, highlighting the necessity of exploring a downstream, trans-therapeutic outcome signature (Fig. 2). Intervention-shared changes in functional connectivity

PLS analysis identified a significant multivariate brain connectivity pattern (PLS1) that was strongly associated with the IAT intervention response rate across all interventions. This first component explained 71% of the variance in the IAT response rate, and the individual subject weights for this component were significantly correlated with their IAT response rate (r = .843, pperm = .012, Fig. 3A). The structure of this brain pattern is defined by two opposing sets of connections based on their PLS1 weights (Fig. 3B). The positively-weighted portion of the pattern was primarily characterized by connections between the SCN/DAN and primary sensory networks (Visual and SMN). Conversely, the negatively-weighted portion of the pattern was defined by connectivity within attention network (DAN-VAN), as well as hypoconnections between the SCN and higher-order cognitive networks. These results appear to reflect a rebalancing of SCN connectivity patterns along the sensorimotor-associative (SA) axis, while also demonstrating functional segregation within the attention network.

Enrichment analysis

We further assessed the spatial associations between changes in shared brain connectivity and nodal gene expression profiles. PLS1 for gene expression explained 1.6% of the variance, and its resulting gene expression map was significantly correlated with the Shared connectivity phenotype (r = .127, pperm < .001). The analysis of the top-ranking (positively-weighted) genes identified a predominant enrichment for pathways related to cellular stress. The most statistically significant term was ‘Cellular responses to stress’ (Reactome Gene Sets, Fig. 4A), indicating a strong association with molecular cascades activated by adverse conditions. This theme was corroborated by the significant enrichment of related processes, including ‘response to oxidative stress’ and ‘cellular response to hormone stimulus’ (GO Biological Processes), which are integral to the biological stress system.

In contrast, the bottom-ranking (negatively-weighted) genes were overwhelmingly enriched for processes related to neuroplasticity and synaptic function. The most significant terms in this set were directly involved in neural communication, including ‘modulation of chemical synaptic transmission’ and ‘synaptic signaling’ (GO Biological Processes, Fig. 4B). These functional terms were supported by pathways essential for building and maintaining synaptic architecture, such as ‘positive regulation of cell adhesion’ and ‘glycoprotein biosynthetic process’ (GO Biological Processes).

Robust analysis

The primary rationale for pooling all control groups to train the normative model was to establish a robust universal baseline of non-specific trial effects while controlling for age, sex, site, and modality covariates. To ensure this pooled baseline did not introduce bias, particularly from control conditions that also exhibited symptom reduction, we conducted two supplementary validation analyses. First, an evaluation of extreme edge proportions (|Z| > 1.96) confirmed that neural alterations in active intervention groups were greater than those in active control groups (F = 3.445, p = .003). Second, an independent within-cohort standardization approach (Z-scoring relative to cohort-specific controls only, followed by binarization) yielded highly consistent topological network alterations (mean subject-level Spearman r = 0.617 compared to the pooled model). Detailed results and visualizations of these robustness analyses are provided in Supplementary Materials (Fig. S1).

Discussion

This study directly compared the changes in brain network connectivity in patients with IGD who underwent one of five different self-regulation interventions. We found that a significant, shared pattern of therapeutic change was a rebalancing of the SCN's connectivity along the SA axis, a pattern that was strongly correlated with clinical improvement across all groups. Further analysis revealed that this common neural signature is associated with a specific molecular foundation, linked to the expression of gene pathways involved in both cellular stress and neuroplasticity. In contrast to this shared therapeutic pathway, each intervention also induced a unique connectivity fingerprint, highlighting distinct mechanisms of action. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify a common, trans-therapeutic neural connectivity phenotype for self-regulation interventions in IGD. This discovery provides a potential biomarker for tracking intervention response and offers a valuable target for the development of future therapies aimed at specific neural circuits.

Our central finding points to a therapeutic mechanism that extends beyond localized effects, reflecting a broad and integrative reorganization of brain connectivity. Across interventions, we consistently observed a large-scale rebalancing of SCN connectivity along the SA axis. The SA axis represents a fundamental organizing principle of the human cortex, forming a hierarchical gradient from primary sensorimotor areas to transmodal association cortices that governs normative brain development and function (Luo et al., 2024; Sydnor et al., 2023). Specifically, a better clinical outcome was associated with a systematic shift in SCN connectivity along this gradient: we observed strengthened coupling with networks at the sensorimotor pole (Visual and SMN) and, concurrently, a pronounced decoupling from networks at the association pole. Thus, the shared therapeutic effect may be not a change in a single, isolated connection, but rather a holistic reorganization of cortico-subcortical communication along a well-established macroscale gradient. This large-scale rebalancing may reflect a dual process essential for recovery: the enhanced SCN-sensorimotor coupling could ground the individual in real-world sensory input (Liu et al., 2025; Mohammed & Hollis, 2018), while the decoupling from association area may liberate executive functions from the pervasive influence of subcortical reward signals (Dong, Wang, Zhang, Du, & Potenza, 2019; Dong et al., 2021; M. Wang, Simkute, & Griskova-Bulanova, 2023). The effective interventions for IGD appear to facilitate a shift towards a more mature and adaptive connectivity profile, guided by the brain's intrinsic hierarchical architecture.

Although cognitive behavioral therapy was not evaluated in the study's cohort, given its widespread use in IGD, it is necessary to infer its implications. Cognitive behavioral therapy is classically viewed as a top-down prefrontal intervention. Recent neuroimaging reviews evaluating treatments for internet gaming disorder indicate that successful psychotherapy normalizes cortical-subcortical connectivity (Ayala-Rojas et al., 2025; Sharma & Weinstein, 2024). Specifically, psychotherapy alters intrinsic connectivity distribution in regions associated with habit execution and reward processing, including the sensorimotor and subcortical networks (L. Liu, Potenza, et al., 2021). Therefore, the rebalancing of sensorimotor and subcortical structures may encompass cognitive behavioral therapy, representing the neural commonality of self-regulation interventions.

Another finding was the decoupling between the DAN and VAN. According to the neurobiological model of addiction, it is described as a state characterized by impaired cognitive control, centered on dysregulation of salience attribution (Goldstein & Volkow, 2011). In this state, addiction-related cues are assigned excessive salience, leading to a persistent hypersensitivity in the VAN, responsible for bottom-up, stimulus-driven responses. This persistent cue-driven VAN activation, in turn, functionally suppresses the activity of the DAN, responsible for top-down, goal-directed attention (Corbetta & Shulman, 2002). The observed therapeutic decoupling between these two networks likely represents a restoration of functional segregation. Such rebalancing may diminish the automatic capture of attentional resourcrs by external gaming cues, thereby facilitating more adaptive allocation of cognitive control.

We further found that the shared connectivity pattern was spatially associated with a unique transcriptional profile. The strong positive correlation with genes for cellular stress is consistent with studies conceptualizing addiction as a state of chronic allostatic load and neuroinflammation, suggesting the intervention works by mitigating this underlying physiological stress (Uhl, Koob, & Cable, 2019; G. Wang, Simkute, & Griskova-Bulanova, 2023). Also, the inverse relationship with genes essential for synaptic transmission and plasticity may suggest the therapeutic mechanism is not a simple promotion of synaptic remodeling, but perhaps a stabilization of circuits subjected to the maladaptive plasticity inherent in addiction (Lüscher & Malenka, 2011). Therefore, one possible explanation is that cognitive intervention first restores the homeostasis of highly reactive, stress-sensitive brain regions. This restoration likely creates a more favorable molecular environment for subsequent, healthier neuroplastic changes to consolidate recovery. By linking network-level changes with molecular data, these findings provide a multiscale explanation for the biological effects of shared connectivity patterns.

Beyond the shared therapeutic signature, our analysis also revealed that each intervention induced a unique connectivity fingerprint, highlighting distinct neurobiological starting points for recovery. Interestingly, there are similarities between PAT and MM fingerprints, with both being uniquely characterized by complex heterogeneous patterns of variation within the FPN. Given that one intervention is physical and the other is mental, one potential explanation of this convergence lies in their shared impact on cognitive control, a core function of the FPN (Cole et al., 2013). Both aerobic exercise and mindfulness meditation are independently known to enhance executive functions such as attentional control and response inhibition (Talukdar et al., 2018; Taren et al., 2017). The observed heterogeneous changes within the FPN may therefore reflect a flexible reorganization of this network to support more efficient top-down regulation in both contexts. Another possible shared mechanism is the enhancement of interoception, or body awareness. MM explicitly trains attention to internal sensations (e.g., breath, bodily feelings) (Farb, Segal, & Anderson, 2013), while PAT increases the salience of physiological signals (e.g., heart rate, respiration) (Amaya et al., 2021).

However, while individual interventions induced distinctive topological reconfigurations frequently involving the FPN, our cross-cohort PLS analysis revealed that the trans-therapeutic signature of symptom improvement was anchored in subcortical-sensorimotor rebalancing rather than intra-FPN connectivity. This anatomical divergence likely reflects the distinction between the initiation of therapy and the ultimate mechanism of recovery. Different self-regulation modalities utilize heterogeneous cortical pathways as top-down entry points to engage executive control. However, the magnitude of these specific cortical changes does not necessarily scale linearly with universal clinical outcomes across all intervention types (Ji et al., 2026; Mestre-Bach & Potenza, 2023). Instead, the diverse cortical initiations appear to converge upon a shared downstream target: resolving the bottom-up dysregulation within the SCN.

Finally, MM demonstrated a significantly higher response rate compared to the other four modalities. One reason for this superiority may be MM's holistic nature; as an intervention, it is not limited to a single mechanism but concurrently trains multiple facets of self-regulation, including attentional control, emotional regulation, and interoceptive awareness (Tang, Hölzel, & Posner, 2015). This multi-component approach may more effectively address the complex neurocognitive deficits underlying IGD compared to other interventions. Meanwhile, compared with digital intervention, offline teaching contact may make it easier for subjects to engage in training. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the interventions were not matched for their parameters.

Limitations

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while our analytic framework utilized control groups to isolate intervention-specific variance, the shared connectivity signature may still capture common psychological phenomena inherent across active clinical trials. Therefore, this trans-therapeutic signature should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, our findings are specific to IGD. Although the identified mechanisms involving reward and executive control circuits are broadly relevant to addiction, the specific connectivity fingerprints may not generalize to other subtypes. Also, we were unable to quantitatively link the intervention-specific connectivity fingerprints to specific psychological processes. The individual-level network features extracted by the SM-netFusion framework are binary, and the pooled retrospective dataset lacks modality-specific psychological metrics across all cohorts. Future studies incorporating diverse cognitive assessments are needed to directly map these distinct neural fingerprints to specific psychological functions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that diverse self-regulation interventions for IGD, despite their unique neural starting points, converge upon a common therapeutic pathway. This shared mechanism involves a large-scale rebalancing of subcortical connectivity along the brain's hierarchical sensorimotor-association axis, a pattern strongly predictive of clinical improvement. Furthermore, we link this network-level reorganization to a specific molecular signature, involving a shift from gene expression profiles associated with cellular stress to those supporting neuroplasticity. These findings identify a robust, trans-therapeutic neurobiological phenotype of recovery in IGD, offering a potential biomarker for tracking intervention response and a valuable target for developing future circuit-based therapies.
