Very Late–Onset Cancer Therapy–Related Cardiac Dysfunction After More Than 100 Courses of Anti-HER2-Based Therapy for Breast Cancer
Authors: Masayuki Kikuchi, Hirokazu Matsushima, Takahiro Uchida, Rika Miyabe, Hidenori Kita, Tsunehiro Shintani
Published: 2026

Cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) is a well-recognized complication of anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) therapy, typically occurring during or shortly after treatment initiation. However, very late–onset CTRCD after prolonged exposure remains extremely rare. We report the case of an 83-year-old woman with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer who developed acute decompensated heart failure after more than 100 courses of anti-HER2-based therapy. This case highlights the importance of long-term cardiac surveillance, even in patients who have tolerated therapy for extended periods without apparent cardiotoxicity.

Case

An 83-year-old woman underwent mastectomy and axillary lymph node dissection for left breast cancer 10 years previously (pT3N1M0, pStage IIIA, invasive ductal carcinoma, estrogen receptor 0, progesterone receptor 0, HER2 3+, Ki-67 labeling index-67 70%). Two years after surgery, multiple liver metastases were detected. She received trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and docetaxel for 1 year, followed by trastuzumab and pertuzumab for the next 7 years. Other than advanced age, no apparent major cardiovascular risk factors were identified from the available clinical history. She had no known prior cardiac disease, anthracycline exposure, or left-sided radiotherapy.

Before initiation of anti-HER2 therapy in August 2016, transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function, with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 66% by the Teichholz method, normal left ventricular contraction without regional wall motion abnormalities, no left atrial enlargement or left ventricular dilatation, and only mild mitral, aortic, and tricuspid regurgitation. Serial echocardiographic surveillance was performed every 6 months during prolonged anti-HER2 therapy. During routine surveillance, LVEF was assessed primarily using the Teichholz method in accordance with institutional practice. When a decline in cardiac function was suspected, additional evaluation using the Simpson’s biplane method was performed. LVEF remained preserved at 59% in August 2022 and 57% in February 2023, without regional wall motion abnormalities. Baseline transthoracic echocardiography after the 100th course of trastuzumab and pertuzumab also showed an LVEF of 57% by the Teichholz method, without left ventricular wall motion abnormalities or moderate-or-greater valvular disease.

Two weeks after the 111th course of trastuzumab and pertuzumab, she presented to our emergency department with dyspnea. On arrival, her blood pressure was 132/84 mm Hg, heart rate was 94 beats/min, and oxygen saturation was 96% while receiving 3 L/min oxygen. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm without ST-segment elevation. Laboratory testing showed an elevated N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide level (1030 pg/mL) with a normal creatine phosphokinase level (153 U/L). Serum M-protein and urinary Bence Jones protein were absent. Her soluble interleukin-2 receptor level was 351 U/mL and her angiotensin-converting enzyme level was 6.6 U/L. Transthoracic echocardiography at presentation showed marked deterioration of left ventricular systolic function, with an LVEF of 29% by the Teichholz method and 25% by the Simpson method, diffuse severe hypokinesis, and markedly reduced global longitudinal strain (–9.2%). Moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation, moderate aortic regurgitation, and moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation also were noted (Fig. 1B-D). The patient was diagnosed with acute decompensated heart failure, and treatment was initiated with intravenous furosemide (20 mg/d), spironolactone (25 mg/d), and telmisartan (20 mg/d). Four days later, furosemide was switched to oral azosemide (30 mg/d). Three days later, telmisartan was switched to losartan potassium (25 mg/d), and bisoprolol fumarate (0.3125 mg/d) was added.

Ten days after treatment initiation, pleural effusions had improved (Fig. 1E), and the patient had lost 5 kg of body weight. Coronary angiography showed no significant coronary artery stenosis (Fig. 1F,G). Left ventriculography demonstrated diffuse hypokinesis, and LVEF had improved to 37%. Four days after cardiac catheterization, she was discharged on azosemide (15 mg/d), spironolactone (25 mg/d), losartan potassium (12.5 mg/d), and bisoprolol fumarate (0.3125 mg/d).

Follow-up echocardiography performed 3 months after discharge showed substantial recovery of cardiac function, with an LVEF of 56% by the Simpson method and 60% by the Teichholz method, together with improvement in global longitudinal strain to –16.1%. No more than mild-to-moderate valvular regurgitation was present. Improvement was maintained on subsequent follow-up, with an LVEF of 62% and global longitudinal strain (GLS) of –15.3% in September 2024, and an LVEF of 64% and GLS of –20.5% in January 2025. Anti-HER2 therapy was discontinued. Discussion

Approximately 20%-25% of metastatic breast cancers overexpress HER2, which is associated with an unfavourable prognosis.1 Anti-HER2-based therapy, including trastuzumab and pertuzumab, has substantially improved outcomes in patients with HER2-positive breast cancer;1,2 however, it is also associated with a risk of cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD). To the best of our knowledge, symptomatic CTRCD developing after more than 100 courses of anti-HER2-based therapy is extremely rare.

CTRCD is broadly categorized into symptomatic and asymptomatic forms. According to the 2022 European Society of Cardiology cardio-oncology guidelines, asymptomatic CTRCD is defined on the basis of changes in LVEF, GLS, and cardiac biomarkers, whereas symptomatic CTRCD is defined by the development of heart failure symptoms with accompanying cardiac dysfunction.3 In the present case, the patient developed acute decompensated heart failure with a marked reduction in LVEF and was therefore considered to have severe symptomatic CTRCD.

Anti-HER2 therapy–related cardiac dysfunction is generally considered to occur relatively early after treatment initiation, and trastuzumab-associated cardiotoxicity is often regarded as potentially reversible, in contrast to anthracycline-related myocardial injury.4 However, the present case suggests that clinically significant CTRCD also may become apparent after prolonged exposure to anti-HER2-based therapy. The mechanism underlying this very late onset remains uncertain. One possible explanation is that subclinical myocardial dysfunction gradually accumulated during long-term HER2-targeted therapy and became overt only after a decline in cardiac reserve associated with advanced age. In addition, because guideline-based surveillance parameters such as Simpson's biplane LVEF and GLS were not routinely assessed before symptom onset, preceding asymptomatic dysfunction may not have been detected. Cardiac biomarkers were not monitored routinely during the course of anti-HER2 therapy and were obtained only after the onset of heart failure symptoms, which may have limited the ability to detect earlier subclinical cardiac dysfunction.

Risk factors reported for CTRCD during anti-HER2 therapy include older age, prior cardiac disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking, prior anthracycline exposure, and left-sided radiotherapy.4,5 In the present case, no apparent major risk factors other than advanced age were identified from the available clinical history. In addition, no apparent “second hit” factors, such as uncontrolled hypertension, recent infection, or clinically significant arrhythmia, were identified around the time of heart failure onset, based on the available clinical information. Serial echocardiographic surveillance performed during prolonged anti-HER2 therapy demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function before heart failure onset, with an LVEF of 59% in August 2022 and 57% in February 2023, without regional wall motion abnormalities. At presentation in March 2024, however, LVEF had declined to 25% by the Simpson method, and GLS to –9.2%, followed by substantial recovery after heart failure treatment and discontinuation of anti-HER2 therapy.

The optimal surveillance strategy for patients receiving prolonged anti-HER2 therapy remains uncertain. Although many patients undergo close monitoring early after treatment initiation, this case highlights the fact that clinically significant CTRCD may occur even after many years of apparently well-tolerated therapy. Therefore, continued cardiac surveillance should be considered in patients receiving long-term anti-HER2-based therapy, particularly in older adults. Guideline-based assessment using biplane Simpson's LVEF and, when available, GLS may help detect asymptomatic dysfunction earlier and allow timely modification of therapy before overt heart failure develops. Further studies are needed to clarify the incidence, predictors, and optimal surveillance intervals for very late–onset CTRCD during prolonged anti-HER2 therapy.

When symptomatic heart failure develops during prolonged anti-HER2 therapy, alternative etiologies also should be considered carefully. Myocarditis also should be considered as a potential differential diagnosis in cases of acute-onset left ventricular dysfunction during cancer therapy. In the present case, endomyocardial biopsy and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging were not performed; therefore, myocarditis cannot be excluded definitively. However, the absence of preceding infectious symptoms, lack of regional wall motion abnormalities, and the clinical course with recovery of left ventricular function following discontinuation of anti-HER2 therapy and initiation of heart failure treatment were more consistent with cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction. In this case, coronary angiography showed no significant coronary artery stenosis. In addition, no evidence of severe baseline valvular disease was present, and laboratory evaluation did not suggest cardiac sarcoidosis or cardiac amyloidosis. These findings supported the clinical impression that this episode was consistent with late-onset CTRCD associated with prolonged anti-HER2-based therapy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Baseline surveillance before symptom onset relied primarily on Teichholz-based assessment and did not include Simpson's biplane LVEF, 3-dimensional LVEF, or routine GLS assessment. In addition, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was not performed. Therefore, the diagnosis of late-onset CTRCD associated with prolonged anti-HER2-based therapy cannot be considered definitive. However, the marked decline in LVEF during long-term anti-HER2 therapy, the absence of significant coronary artery stenosis, the lack of other clear alternative causes, and the subsequent recovery of left ventricular function after treatment and discontinuation of anti-HER2 therapy were all consistent with this diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case suggests that very late–onset CTRCD may occur during prolonged anti-HER2-based therapy, even after more than 100 treatment courses. Continued cardiac surveillance should therefore be considered during long-term anti-HER2 therapy, and guideline-based assessment using Simpson's LVEF and GLS may help detect dysfunction before overt heart failure develops.
