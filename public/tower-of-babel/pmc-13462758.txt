Synergistic application of nano-silicon and Streptomyces albogriseolus enhances maize salinity tolerance by modifying soil silicon fractions and ionic homeostasis
Authors: Pooria Pezeshknejad, Reza Ghorbani Nasrabadi, Hassan Etesami, Farhad Khormali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalinity adversely affects the physiological and biochemical processes in maize (Zea mays L.), leading to significant yield losses. This study aimed to evaluate the combined effects of nano-silicon (nano-Si) and halotolerant Streptomyces albogriseolus (S. albogriseolus) on the morphological, physiological, biochemical characteristics of maize, and Si fractions under salinity stress. A factorial pot experiment was conducted using three salinity levels (1.4 (control), 5, and 8 dS m−1), four nano-Si concentrations (0, 100, 200, and 400 mg kg−1), and inoculation with or without S. albogriseolus. Both nano-Si and S. albogriseolus improved plant performance; however, their combined application produced the strongest mitigation effect. Under severe salinity, co-application of nano-Si and S. albogriseolus increased shoot dry weight (55%), root dry weight (78%), shoot length (51%), and root length (82%), reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) (35%), enhanced catalase (CAT) activity, reduced Na accumulation (19%), and restored ionic homeostasis N (41%), P (25%), K (22%), and Si (28%), thereby improving the K/Na ratio. This treatment also enhanced the plant-available soil Si pools (soluble and exchangeable fractions). This integrated approach offers a sustainable and environmentally friendly strategy for enhancing maize resilience and productivity under saline stress.Keywords: Nano-silicon, Streptomyces albogriseolus, Silicon fractions, Ionic homeostasis, Antioxidant enzymes

Introduction

Soil salinity is a significant environmental constraint that poses a serious threat to the sustainability of agricultural production and global food security1. In recent decades, soil salinity has reduced global crop yields by more than 50%, and is considered a major limitation to crop productivity in arid and semi-arid regions of the world. Salinity stress affects plant growth by altering osmotic pressure, inducing ion toxicity, and disrupting the soil biodiversity. Moreover, prolonged and extensive use of chemical fertilizers to maximize crop yields has resulted in soil and water pollution, a decline in soil biodiversity, and severe ecological damage2,3. Excessive accumulation of salt ions in the soil impairs water uptake, triggers ionic toxicity, and disrupts plant metabolic pathways, ultimately leading to reduced yield, decreased photosynthetic efficiency, and stunted growth4. Given the accelerating issue of soil salinization driven by anthropogenic activities, inefficient water resource management, and climate change, the development of innovative, sustainable, and environment-friendly strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of this stress has become an undeniable necessity5. Therefore, enhancing crop resilience to salinity stress requires integrated strategies that leverage both conventional knowledge and the current genetic solutions.

Nanotechnology offers a promising and sustainable platform for developing innovative solutions to agricultural challenges. This potential is rooted in the unique properties of engineered nanomaterials (NMs) with structural dimensions of 1–100 nm. At this scale, NMs exhibit a high surface-to-volume ratio, increased reactivity, and unique interfacial interactions, which collectively enable novel mechanisms for mitigating abiotic stress and enhancing plant growth with minimal environmental impact6.

Silicon (Si), the second most abundant element in the Earth’s crust after oxygen, is crucial for the growth and development of many plant species, particularly cereals, including rice. The beneficial effects of Si on the growth, yield, and quality of various agricultural crops are well documented, and Si is now recognized as a key element for plant growth, especially in C4 plant species7. Si enhances plant tolerance to salinity stress through several mechanisms, including regulating sodium (Na) and potassium (K) transport to improve ionic balance, strengthening antioxidant defense systems, promoting osmolyte accumulation, reinforcing cell wall and membrane integrity, enhancing photosynthetic efficiency, modulating the expression of stress-responsive genes, and improving plant water relations. Collectively, these effects lead to improved growth, higher yields, and increased salinity tolerance in various plant species under saline conditions8–10. Silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) are particularly promising for this purpose because of their high biocompatibility, structural stability, and ability to modulate plant and microbial signaling pathways. These properties make them effective tools for enhancing plant–microbe interactions under saline conditions. The primary mechanism by which SiNPs enhance microbial efficiency is by stimulating biofilm formation11.

Beneficial microorganisms, such as plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB), form biofilms that adhere to plant roots, thereby augmenting stress tolerance, facilitating nutrient exchange, and sustaining microbial populations in saline environments12. PGPB are rhizosphere-colonizing microorganisms that enhance plant growth and development under saline stress. Within the rhizosphere, plant roots establish a specialized niche in which microbes perceive plant-derived chemical signals, facilitating their colonization and proliferation in the root system13. The plant growth-promoting activity of PGPB is mediated through multiple mechanisms, including increased nutrient bioavailability, biological nitrogen fixation, phytohormone synthesis, and biocontrol of soil-borne pathogens14. Consequently, PGPB application represents a sustainable agricultural strategy that supports crop productivity while concurrently improving soil health. Research has shown that PGPB promote plant tolerance to abiotic stresses, such as salinity, by improving root architecture, optimizing water-use efficiency, and facilitating nutrient acquisition15.

Among PGPB, gram-positive, spore-forming Actinomycetota with a high genomic G + C content have attracted particular interest because of their ability to produce secondary metabolites and antibiotics. These microorganisms inhabit various plant compartments, including the phyllosphere, endosphere, rhizosphere, and bulk soil16. The rhizosphere hosts a diverse range of Actinomycetota, which are extensively utilized for the production of secondary metabolites. These bacteria exhibit remarkable physiological and metabolic versatility, enabling them to produce extracellular enzymes and synthesize a broad spectrum of secondary metabolites17,18. The beneficial effects of Streptomyces inoculation are consistent with the recognized role of Actinomycetota in enhancing salinity tolerance. Streptomyces isolates improve nutrient solubilization, produce phytohormones, enhance root development18, and stimulate antioxidant defenses, collectively reducing oxidative damage and improving the water status19.

Maize (Zea mays L.) is a leading global cereal crop that is vital for human nutrition. To meet the rising demand, particularly in developing nations, production must be significantly increased. However, as a crop sensitive to salinity, maize suffers substantial yield losses under saline stress, which threatens food security10. Therefore, developing effective strategies to enhance salinity tolerance in maize is crucial for sustainable production.

Although the individual effects of Si and PGPB on plant health have been established, the potential synergistic effect between nano-silicon (nano-Si) and Actinomycetota has not been systematically investigated yet. This is particularly relevant for maize (Zea mays L.), a high-value silicon-accumulating crop that is exceptionally vulnerable to salinity and is a major constraint on global maize productivity. Previous studies rarely examined whether beneficial Streptomyces, together with nano-Si, can shift soil Si pools toward more plant-available forms while simultaneously improving maize performance under salinity stress. We hypothesized that the integration of nano-Si with Streptomyces albogriseolusis a promising strategy for enhancing maize tolerance to salinity stress. Therefore, this study aimed to (I) assess the combinatorial effects of nano-Si and selected Actinomycetota on the growth, physiology, and nutrient uptake in maize (Zea mays L.) and (II) evaluate Si dynamics in rhizospheric soil subjected to salt stress.

Material and methods

Molecular identification of the selected isolate

The Actinomycetota strain, originally isolated from the soil rhizosphere in a previous study20, was selected for its potent plant growth-promoting traits. In brief, the soil samples were collected from the rhizosphere of crop plants including maize, tomato, soybean and sesame during the flowering stage. For the isolation of Actinomycetota, the dilution plate method was used. For the selective isolation of Actinomycetota, pre-treatment methods including air-drying for 72 h and dry-heat treatment at 110 °C for 30 min were applied. The treated soil samples were spread onto humic acid vitamin agar (HVA) (1 g L−1 humic acid, 0.02 g L−1 CaCO3, 0.5 g L−1 NaHPO4, 1.7 g L−1 KCl, 0.05 g L−1 MgSO4·7H2O, 0.01 g L−1 FeSO4·7H2O, 1 mL L−1 B-vitamins, 18 g L−1 agar21. To inhibit fungal growth, 100 mg L−1 nystatin was added to culture medium. The selected isolate was taxonomically identified using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from a pure culture grown in International Streptomyces Project-2 (ISP2) medium using a modified phenol–chloroform extraction protocol22. Briefly, a single colony was lysed using the STE buffer, SDS, and Proteinase K at 56 °C for 1 h. The lysate was purified by successive extractions with phenol–chloroform-isoamyl alcohol and chloroform followed by precipitation with ethanol. The DNA quality was spectrophotometrically verified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the universal primers 27F and 1492R using the following PCR protocol: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 8 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 52 °C for 45 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 1 min. Amplicons were visualized on 1% agarose gel, purified using the Expin Combo Kit (GeneAll, Korea), and sequenced bidirectionally. The resulting reads were assembled using Geneious Prime v2023.2.1, and the consensus sequence was used in a BLASTN search of the NCBI database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to determine the phylogenetic affiliation of the isolate.

In vitro screening of the Streptomyces isolate for plant growth-promoting traits under salt stress

Indolic compound production

The production of indolic compounds was quantified spectrophotometrically using the Salkowski reagent23. Briefly, the isolate was inoculated (108 CFU mL−1) into 50 mL of ISP2 broth supplemented with 2 mg mL−1 L-tryptophan in three replicates and incubated in the dark at 28 ± 2 °C with agitation (180 rpm) for seven days. The culture supernatant was obtained by centrifugation (10000×g for 10 min). Subsequently, 1 mL of the supernatant was reacted with 2 mL of Salkowski reagent, and the absorbance was measured at 530 nm.

Inorganic phosphate solubilization

Phosphate solubilization potential was assessed using the National Botanical Research Institute’s phosphate (NBRIP) growth medium24. NBRIP broth was inoculated with 1% (v/v) of an ISP2 pre-culture in triplicate and incubated at 28 ± 2 °C (180 rpm, 7 days). The culture was centrifuged (10000×g for 10 min), and the soluble phosphorus content in the supernatant was determined colorimetrically at 470 nm using the ammonium molybdate-vanadate method.

Silicate solubilization

The capacity of the isolate to solubilize silicate was evaluated in Bunt and Rovira liquid medium amended with 2.5 g/L magnesium trisilicate as the insoluble source25, with three replicates. After 7 day incubation period under shaking conditions (28 ± 2 °C, 180 rpm), the culture broth was centrifuged. The soluble Si concentration in the supernatant was measured spectrophotometrically at 650 nm using the molybdenum blue method with a standard curve of Si (0–20 mg L−1) for quantification.

Siderophore production was quantified using the Chrome Azurol S (CAS) assay26. Experimental design and treatments

A factorial pot experiment was conducted based on a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications to assess the individual and interactive effects of nano-Si and S. albogriseolus on the morphological traits, physiological performance, nutrient uptake, and soil Si fractions of maize under salinity stress. The treatments consisted of three factors: (a) three levels of salinity stress: non-saline control (1.4 dS m−1), moderate salinity (5 dS m−1), and severe salinity (8 dS m−1); (b) two independent seed inoculation: non-inoculated (B0) and inoculation with S. albogriseolus (B1) and (c) nano-Si application application at four levels: 0 (Si-0), 100 (Si-100), 200 (Si-200), and 400 (Si-400) mg nano-Si per kg of soil. Soil sample was collected from a depth of 0–30 cm in the Hezar Pich area, Golestan Province, Iran (36° 50′ 18′′ N, 54° 23′ 47′′ E). The physical and chemical properties of the soil were characterized prior to the experiment. Soil texture was classified as silty clay loam using the hydrometer method27. Soil pH (7.4) and electrical conductivity (EC) (1.4 dS m−1) were measured in a saturated paste extract28. Organic carbon (0.98%)29, total nitrogen (0.07%)30, available phosphorus (4.3 mg kg−1)31, and available potassium (130.8 mg kg−1)32 were quatified.

To induce salinity stress, the soil was salinized in pots. The quantities of NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 to achieve target EC levels of 5 and 8 dS m−1 were calculated using Excel Solver Tool. To prevent solute leaching and maintain stable salinity profiles, each pot was lined with an impermeable polyethylene membrane and irrigated with the prepared saline solutions. The EC of both the leachate and saturated paste extract were monitored until the desired salinity levels were consistently achieved33. Following the stabilization of salinity treatments, the soil in designated pots was amended with nano-Si. The nano-Si material (Nano-SiO2, Nanosany Company, Ltd., Iran, US Research Nanomaterials source) was characterized as amorphous silica (> 99% purity) with an average primary particle size of 20–30 nm, a bulk density of < 0.10 g cm−3, and a true density of 2.4 g cm−3. Inductively coupled plasma analysis confirmed low metal impurity contents (Ti < 120, Ca < 70, Na < 50, Fe < 20 mg kg−1). This nano-Si was thoroughly homogenized with the soil to achieve final concentrations of 0 (control), 100, 200, and 400 mg kg−1 soil.

Based on the soil test recommendations, phosphorus (P) (as triple superphosphate) and potassium (K) (as potassium chloride) were supplied as basal fertilizers and incorporated into the soil before planting. Nitrogen (N) (as urea) was applied in a split application: half as pre-planting and the remaining five weeks after planting. To supply micronutrients, the plants were irrigated monthly with 100 mL of the modified N-P-K-free Hoagland solution. Seeds of the spring maize hybrid KSC703 were surface-sterilized by immersion in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 30 s, followed by immersion in 2.5% (w/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for 3 min, and finally rinsed eight times with sterile distilled water. For inoculum production, S. albogriesolus was cultivated in International Streptomyces Project 2 (ISP2) liquid medium. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C for 7 days under continuous agitation at 180 rpm in an orbital shaker.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000×g for 5 min after thorough homogenization of culture broth via vortexing. The cell pellets were then washed and resuspended in sterile 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution. Surface-sterilized seeds were inoculated by immersion in a bacterial suspension (108 cfu mL−1) with shaking (150 rpm) for 2 h, and subsequently dried under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow hood for 2 h34. A second inoculation was applied in the fifth week of the growth period together with the irrigation water. Each pot was filled with 8 kg of prepared soil, and the experiment was conducted outdoors to simulate natural seasonal conditions with plants exposed to ambient sunlight, temperature, and humidity. During the experimental period, ambient air temperature ranged from 18.6 to 33.2 °C and relative humidity varied from 45 to 88%. Eight seeds were sown per pot and thinned to two uniform seedlings per pot after germination. To ensure optimal growth condition, soil moisture was maintained at 70% of field capacity through daily irrigation using gravimetric control. On rainy days, a temporary plastic cover was used to shield the pots from rainfall and preserve the moisture regime. After 80 day growth period, plant and soil samples were collected to analysis of growth performance, physiological and biochemical traits, and soil Si fractions. Measurements from two plants within each pot were pooled or averaged and used as a single replicate for statistical analysis.

Assessment of plant growth and photosynthetic pigments

Plant morphological traits

At harvest (80 day after planting), maize plants were carefully uprooted and rinsed with deionized water to remove the adhering soil. Shoots and roots were then separated, and shoot height (from the base to the tip of the longest leaf) and root length were measured using an engineering scale. Plant components were then placed in an oven at 75 °C for 48 h until a constant weight was achieved. The dry weights of the shoots and roots were recorded separately and used for further analysis.

Leaf proline content

Leaf proline content was quantified spectrophotometrically according to the method described by Dong et al.37. Briefly, fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL 3% (w/v) aqueous sulfosalicylic acid. The homogenate was centrifuged (10000×g, 10 min) to remove celluar debries. Subsequently, 2 mL aliquot of the supernatant was combined with 2 mL of acid-ninhydrin reagent and 2 mL of glacial acetic acid in a test tube. The reaction mixture was heated in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 1 h. After cooling on ice, the chromophore was extracted by mixing the solution with 4 mL of toluene. The absorbance of the toluene phase was measured at 520 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Proline concentration was calculated from a standard curve (0–20 µg mL−1) prepared with analytical garde L-proline and expressed as micrograms per gram of fresh weight (µg g−1 FW). All samples were analyzed in triplicate.

Determination of leaf nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and sodium concentrations

The total nitrogen (N) content was quantified using the standard Kjeldahl method . To quantify potassium (K), phosphorus (P), and sodium (Na), dried plant tissue was subjected to a dry digestion technique. The resulting ash was dissolved in 10 mL of a diluted acid solution (prepared by mixing 300 mL of hydrochloric acid and 100 mL of nitric acid diluted to a final volume of 1000 mL with deionized water). The phosphorus concentration in the digestate was determined colorimetrically using the ascorbic acid–molybdenum blue method at 880 nm38. The potassium and sodium concentrations were quantified in the same digestate using flame photometry.

Analysis of silicon content in plant and soil

Leaf silicon content

Leaf silicon content was determined by alkaline digestion followed by the molybdenum blue colorimetric method, as described by Eraslan et al.39 with minor modifications. Briefly, 100 mg of dry, ground leaf tissue was digested with 2 mL of 30% (v/v) H2O2 at 95 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, 4 mL of 50% (w/v) NaOH was added, and the mixture was incubated for 4 h to complete the alkaline digestion. Subsequently, 1 mL of 50 mM NH4F was added to the digest to stabilize the silicon, the volume was adjusted with deionized water, and the solution was filtered. For colorimetric analysis, an aliquot of the filtrate was sequentially mixed with acetic acid, ammonium molybdate, tartaric acid, and a reducing agent (ascorbic acid). The resulting blue complex was allowed to develop for 60 min, and its absorbance was measured at 630 nm. The silicon concentration was quantified using a standard curve prepared from certified SiO2 solutions (0–20 mg L−1).

Sequential extraction of soil silicon fractions

Sequential chemical extraction was employed to fractionate rhizosphere soil silicon into soluble, exchangeable, and amorphous pools, according to the method described by Yang et al.40. All extractions were performed on 2 g soil samples in 50 mL centrifuge tubes with shaking at 180 rpm and separated by centrifugation at 3000×g for 5 min. The soluble silicon fraction (CaCl2–Si) was extracted with 20 mL of 0.01 M CaCl2 for 1 h. After centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. The resulting soil residue was subsequently extracted with 20 mL of 0.5 M acetic acid for 1 h to separate the exchangeable silicon fraction (HAc-Si), followed by immediate filtration of the supernatant. Finally, the amorphous silicon pool (Na2CO3–Si), was extracted by agitating the final soil residue with 40 mL of 0.5 M Na2CO3 for 16 h. The resulting supernatant was recovered via centrifugation followed by filtration. The Si concentration in all the extracts was determined spectrophotometrically at 630 nm using the molybdenum blue method41.

Statistical analysis

All data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The in vitro plant growth promoting attributes of the halotolerant S. albogriseolus isolate were analyzed for statistical significance (P < 0.05) as one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Three-way ANOVA was performed using the SAS software (v. 9.1, SAS Institute) to evaluate the main and interactive effects of salinity, Streptomyces inoculation, and nano-Si application in pot experiment. When ANOVA indicated significant effects, a mean comparison was conducted using Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a significance level of p < 0.05. Relationships among all measured parameters were examined using Pearson’s correlation analysis in the R software (v. 4.5.1). A correlation heatmap was visualized using ggplot2 and pheatmap packages. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the FactiMineR and Factoextra packages to identify major patterns of variation and reduce data dimensionality.

Results

Phylogenetic identification and plant growth-promoting traits of selected Streptomyces under salinity stress

Phylogenetic analysis of the partial 16S rRNA gene sequence identified the isolate as a member of the Actinomycetota phylum. Nucleotide BLAST (BLASTN) analysis demonstrated > 99% sequence similarity to validated reference strains within the genus Streptomyces. Based on the 16S rRNA gene sequencing results, the isolate exhibited the highest nucleotide sequence similarity (99.78%) to S. albogriseolus and was deposited in the NCBI database under the accession number PQ326866. Previous research indicates the strain is halotolerant (up to 8% NaCl) and exhibits multiple plant growth-promoting activities in standard media, such as the production of indole compounds, siderophores, and the solubilization of phosphate and silicate under non-saline conditions20. To determine whether these plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits were retained under osmotic stress, they were quantitatively assessed in S. albogriseolus across a gradient of NaCl concentrations (0–450 mM) (Table 1). The data demonstrated that salinity stress had a significant (P < 0.05) inhibitory effect on all measured traits. Notably, phosphate solubilization exhibited significant stimulation at 100 mM NaCl (125.1 mg L−1) compared to the non-saline control (113.1 mg L−1) before declining sharply at higher concentrations (45.3%). In contrast, indolic compounds production and silicate solubilization progressively decreased with increasing salinity. Indolic compound production remained stable between 0 and 100 mM but showed a significant reduction (19%) at higher osmotic stress. Silicate solubilization capacity decreased significantly with increasing NaCl concentration (p < 0.01), declining by 63.4% from ~ 99 mg L−1 at low salinity (0–100 mM) to 36.3 mg L−1 at 450 mM NaCl. Siderophore production showed significant salinity tolerance, with no statistically significant reduction observed at 150 mM NaCl compared to the non-saline control (p > 0.05). Activity remained substantial at 450 mM NaCl, retaining 76.3% of the control level (p < 0.01) (Table 1).

Plant growth and photosynthetic pigments

The combined application of S. albogriseolus and nano-Si significantly alleviated the detrimental effects of salinity stress on maize growth (Table 2). Inoculation with S. albogriseolus enhanced all measured morphological parameters across all salinity levels (1.4 (control), 5, and 8 dS m−1), with the most substantial effect observed under severe salinity (8 dS m−1). Similarly, increasing the nano-Si concentration from 0 to 400 mg kg−1 improved all measured parameters at each salinity level, with 200 mg kg−1 nano-Si identified as the optimal dose under non-saline conditions. However, the highest amount of growth parameters was observed when the two treatments were combined. This combination was particularly effective in counteracting salt-induced growth suppression. Under sever salinity stress (8 dS m−1), the combined treatment of S. albogriseolus and 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si most effectively mitigated growth inhibition. This treatment combination significantly (P < 0.001) increased shoot length by 51.4% and root length by 82.2% compared to the stressed control (Table 3). Similarly, the combined treatment increased shoot dry weight by 55% relative to the stressed control (Table 3). It also markedly enhanced dry matter accumulation, raising shoot and root dry weight by 55.5% and 78.6%, respectively.

Physiological parameters

Electrolyte leakage (EL), a key indicator of membrane integrity, was significantly influenced by salinity stress and Streptomyces inoculation (P < 0.001). In the non-inoculated control, electrolyte leakage exhibited a pronounced increase in response to salinity, from 59.7% under non-saline conditions to 88.7% under severe salt stress (8 dS m−1) (Fig. 1b). Streptomyces inoculation significantly mitigated membrane damage at all tested salinity levels. Under non-saline conditions, inoculation with S. albogriseolus reduced the EL from 59.7 to 50.9%. This protective effect was most pronounced under moderate salinity (5 dS m−1), where EL was reduced from 76.04 to 63.01%. Even under severe salt stress (8 dS m−1), inoculation with S. albogriseolus had a substantial effect, lowering EL from 88.7% to 74.2%. Nano-Si application also contributed to membrane stabilization, although to a lesser extent than S. albogriseolus inoculation. In non-inoculated plants under severe salinity (8 dS m−1), increasing the nano-Si concentration to 400 mg kg−1 reduced the EL from 88.7% to 78.9%. However, the most effective treatment was combination of S. albogriseolus inoculation with 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si, which resulted in the lowest EL (70.6%) under severe salinity conditions (Fig. 1b).

Salinity stress induced significant reduction (P < 0.001) in leaf relative water content (RWC) (Table 2). In the noo-inoculated controls, RWC decreased from 55% under non-saline conditions to 31.5% under severe salinity (Fig. 1c). Nano-Si application increased RWC under severe salinity stress (8 dS m−1), reaching 38.2% with the application of 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si compared with the untreated stressed control.. Relative water content (RWC) was significantly (P < 0.001)improved by bacterial inoculation (Table 2). Under severe salinity (8 dS m−1), plants inoculated with S. albogriseolus alone retained 41.9% RWC, approximately 10% more than non-inoculated controls. This protective effect was further amplified by the addition of nano-Si, with the combination treatment (400 mg kg−1) yielding the peak RWC value of 46.7% (Fig. 1c).

Oxidative stress marker and antioxidant enzyme activitiy

Inoculation with S. albogriseolus significantly alleviated salinity-induced oxidative stress in maize by modulating key biochemical markers. A three-way ANOVA confirmed that the main effects of S. albogriseolus inoculation, nano-Si application, and salinity stress were highly significant (p < 0.01) (Table 2) for malondialdehyde (MDA) content and the activities of the antioxidant enzymes catalase (CAT) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX). Under severe salinity (8 dS m−1), MDA in non-inoculated controls reached 68.2 nmol g−1 FW, representing an 183.3% increase in MDA (Table 4). In contrast, S. albogriseolus inoculation effectively mitigated this damage, reducing MDA levels by up to 35.5% under the same stress conditions. Plants inoculated with S. albogriseolus exhibited significantly (P < 0.001) higher CAT and APX activities than their non-inoculated counterparts at all salinity levels. This enhanced enzymatic capacity was most pronounced under severe stress (8 dS m−1), where the combined treatment of S. albogriseolus and 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si resulted in the highest CAT activity (213.2 U g−1 FW) (Table 4).

Ionic homeostasis and nutrient acquisition in maize

The individual and interactive effects of salinity, nano-Si, and inoculation with S. albogriseolus on the nutrient composition of the maize leaves are shown in Fig. 2. Salinity stress significantly (P < 0.001) compromises the nutritional status of the plants. In the non-inoculated controls, an increase in salinity resulted in a significant (P < 0.05) reduction in leaf nitrogen (Fig. 2a), phosphorus (Fig. 2b), K (Fig. 2c), and Si (Fig. 2f) concentrations. Concurrently, pronounced sodium accumulation (Fig. 2d) led to substantial reduction in the critical K/Na ratio (Fig. 2e). The application of nano-Si significantly mitigated salinity-induced nutrient imbalance. The application of 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si significantly (P < 0.001) mitigated ion imbalance and improved nutrient status in non-inoculated plants under sever salinity (8 dS m−1). It increased leaf N (by 31.5%), P (by 19.3%), and K (by 9.09%) concentrations while decreasing Na by 12.4% compared to the non-inoculated control (Fig. 2). Additionally, this treatment enhanced leaf Si content under both moderate (5 dS m−1) and severe (8 dS m−1; 36%) and (8 dS m−1; 28.9% ) salinity stress compared to their respective controls. Inoculation with S. albogriseolus alone consistently enhanced the nutritional status of maize across all tested salinity levels. Under severe salinity stress (8 dS m−1), Streptomyces inoculation significantly (P < 0.001)increased leaf N, P, and K concentrations by 26.08%, 12.9%, and 6.3%, respectively, while reducing Na accumulation by 4.8%, compared with the non-inoculated control (Fig. 2a–d). Notably, S. albogriseolus also induced a significant (P < 0.001) increase in leaf Si concentration compared with the control, even under high salinity level (Fig. 2d). Under severe salinity (8 dS m−1), the combined treatment of S. albogriseolus inoculation and 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si significantly (P < 0.001) increased leaf concentrations of N, P, K, and Si by 41.3%, 25.8%, 22.7%, and 28.9%, respectively, while simultaneously reducing leaf Na concentration by 18.9% compared to the control (Fig. 2a–d). The nutrient modulations collectively led to noticeable improvement in the leaf K/Na ratio (Fig. 2e). Although all treatments ameliorated the salinity-induced decline in this ratio, the synergistic effect of S. albogriseolus and the 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si combination under severe salinity stress (8 dS m−1) yielded a K/Na ratio that was significantly higher than that of all other treatments.

Effect of treatments on soil silicon fraction

Salinity significantly (P < 0.001) altered the distribution of soil Si fractions, reducing plant-available soluble Si (Fig. 3a) and exchangeable Si (Fig. 3b). In contrast, the fraction of less available amorphous Si remained stable or increased slightly with increasing salinity (Fig. 3c). The application of nano-Si effectively reversed the salinity-induced depletion of labile Si pools and significantly (P < 0.001) increased soil soluble and exchangeable Si compared to the control, and this increase was observed across all salinity levels. Application of 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si elevated soluble Si to the maximum level in the non-inoculated soil. Under high-salinity stress, the most pronounced effects were observed at higher application rates of 200 and 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si. Concurrently, the amorphous Si fraction decreased, indicating that the applied nano-Si was retained in plant-available forms rather than transformed into unavailable pools. Inoculation with S. albogriseolus significantly (P < 0.001) influenced soil Si dynamics in this study. Streptomyces inoculation consistently enhanced the levels of soluble and exchangeable Si compared with their non-inoculated counterparts at all salinity levels. A pronounced synergistic interaction between streptomyces inoculation and nano-Si is evident. The combined treatment with S. albogriseolus and 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si consistently resulted in the maximum shifts in the soil Si fractions. Under high salinity (8 dS m−1), streptomyces inoculation combined with 400 mg kg−1 nano-Si produced the highest soil soluble Si content (Fig. 3a), whereas streptomyces inoculation with 200 mg/kg nano-Si produced the highest soil exchangeable Si content (Fig. 3b). These levels were substantially greater than those in corresponding non-inoculated treatmentss or treatments with lower nano-Si application levels. Discussion

Maize (Zea mays L.) is a crop that is moderately sensitive to salinity and experiences significant physiological impairments owing to increased concentrations of phytotoxic ions, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and ionic imbalances, which collectively suppress yield and quality34. Salinity stress disrupts fundamental physiological processes such as photosynthesis, respiration, stomatal function, and nutrient uptake, leading to reduced biomass accumulation and crop productivity42,34. One promising strategy for mitigating these effects is the use plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB). PGPB enhance crop performance in saline soils through diverse mechanisms, including the production of phytohormones, siderophores, and antifungal compounds, as well as facilitating nutrient solubilization to improve plant growth and nutrient acquisition43,44. This study demonstrates that a halotolerant S. albogriseolus isolate, capable tolerate NaCl concentrations up to 8%and exhibits multiple plant growth-promoting traits, effectively enhances maize growth under salinity stress. The improvement is mediated by the positive modulation of key morpho-physiological and biochemical attributes. This aligns with previous findings confirming that halotolerant Actinomycetota can sustain their plant growth-promoting activities even under high-salinity conditions34,45.

The S. albogriseolus isolate exhibited multiple plant growth-promoting traits, such as production of indolic compounds, siderophores, and extracellular enzymes, along with the ability to solubilize phosphate and silicate minerals. Although elevated salinity reduced some of these activities, the isolate maintained a significant functional efficacy. One of the principal mechanisms by which beneficial microorganisms promote plant growth is the biosynthesis of phytohormones such as auxins. These hormones play a pivotal role in stimulating root development and enhancing overall plant growth. Beyond direct growth promotion, auxin production also contributes to improved abiotic stress tolerance including resilience against drought and salinity46. Furthermore, plant growth-promoting bacteria mitigate critical nutrient limitations by converting insoluble soil phosphates into bioavailable forms, thereby alleviating phosphorus deficiency 18,47 and solubilizing silicates into plant-accessible monosilicic acid (H4SiO4), which contributes to stress tolerance through deposition in plant tissues48. Additionally, siderophore-producing bacteria facilitate iron acquisition under iron-limiting conditions by secreting organic chelators. This mechanism contributes to biomass accumulation, root development, and phytopathogen suppression, making siderophore production a key plant growth-promoting trait49. The results confirmed that S. albogriseolus maintained functional under high salinity, retaining its ability to produce indolic compounds and siderophores and to solubilize phosphate and silicate under salinity stress up to 450 mM NaCl. This functional resilience under salinity stress is consistent with reports of other Streptomyces strains, such as S. niveoruber and S. lienomycini from the Atacama Desert sustained plant beneficial activities at 200 mM NaCl and improved salt tolerance in lettuce50.

Several studies have highlighted the synergistic potential of the combined application of nano-silicon and plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) on crop quality and yield under salinity stress. This integrated approach has been shown to significantly enhance plant growth and development, with notable improvements in root and shoot length and overall biomass accumulation51,5253. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that salinity stress significantly inhibited maize growth and reduced the root length, shoot length, and dry biomass. Application of nano-Si and S. albogriseolus inoculation effectively mitigated these negative effects. We observed that lower nano-Si concentrations primarily stimulated vegetative growth, whereas combined application of nano-Si and Streptomyces was pivotal for sustaining growth under high-salinity conditions. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of PGPR in promoting plant resilience to salinity stress. At the physiological level, Halobacillus marinus has been shown to protect spinach by upregulating the plant’s own osmolyte production and antioxidant systems under salin conditions54. At the biochemical level, Streptomyces isolates CLV97 and CLV179 exhibit tolerance to 300 mM NaCl and promote plant growth through indolic compounds and siderophore production55. Also, Enterobacter bugandensis R-18 improves plant height and ionic homeostasis under 100 mM NaCl through multiple plant growth-promoting mechanisms, including IAA synthesis and phosphate solubilization activity56. Parallel to microbial benefits, the positive role of silicon in stress mitigation is widely recognized57,58. Silicon can also enhance plant–microbe interactions, particularly with plant growth-promoting microorganisms, thereby improving plant stress resistance and growth15,59. In the present study, the interaction between salinity and silicon was not significant for any of the measured vegetative traits, indicating that silicon consistently enhanced maize growth regardless of salinity level. This suggests that silicon’s protective and growth promoting effects are relatively independent of salinity stress. In contrast, the effects of Streptomyces albogriseolus inoculation were salinity dependent, highlighting that plant–microbe interactions are more responsive under salt stress conditions. Therefore, while silicon provides a stable benefit across varying salinity levels, the growth promoting effect of bacterial inoculation is modulated by salinity, resulting in greater increases in shoot and root growth and biomass under higher salt concentrations.

Salinity stress compromises plant water relations and membrane integrity, as evidenced by a decline in relative water content (RWC) and an increase in electrolyte leakage (EL), which are key indicators of osmotic and oxidative damage This disruption occurs because salinity limits soil water uptake, induces osmotic stress, and damages cellular membranes, thereby increasing permeability60. Consequently, the associated reduction in RWC and increase in EL impairs critical physiological functions, including chlorophyll synthesis and photosynthetic efficiency, ultimately suppressing productivity61. In the present study, nano-Si application effectively ameliorated the reduction in photosynthetic pigments and RWC, whereas Streptomyces albogriseolus inoculation significantly mitigated membrane damage, as indicated by reduced EL. The most pronounced recovery was achieved by the combined application of streptomyces and nano-Si, which concurrently enhanced pigment retention, improved leaf RWC, and minimized EL. Silicon can maintain photosynthetic efficiency and chloroplast integrity under high salinity by protecting key photosynthetic enzymes, such as RubisCO, ATP synthase, and ferredoxin–NADP reductase, ensuring that essential photosynthetic processes remain functional62. Furthermore, Si enhances water uptake and transport, helping to maintain turgor and RWC, which are vital for photosynthesis63. This aligns with reports on peas, where nano-Si boosted RWC and diminished EL under salinity, likely by enhancing water retention64.

Silicon also plays a crucial role in enhancing the antioxidant defence system under salinity stress, primarily through the upregulation of key antioxidant enzymes62. Studies have shown that nano-Si promotes the activity of enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), which are crucial for scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and alleviating oxidative stress65–67. A key consequence of this enhanced enzymatic activity is a reduction in lipid peroxidation, which is a destructive process that compromises membrane integrity. For instance, silicon application in Glycyrrhiza uralensis elevated POD and SOD activities and decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) content, a key marker of oxidative damage68. In the present study, antioxidant enzyme activity increased in response to salinity,however, in control plants, this increase as part of the plant’s defensive strategy, was insufficient to prevent oxidative damage. In contrast, the application of nano-Si significantly enhanced the activities of CAT and reduced MDA accumulation. S. albogriseolus conferred protective effects by mitigating oxidative stress. Notably, the combined treatment with nano-Si and S. albogriseolus demonstrated the highest efficacy, indicating enhancement of the plant’s antioxidant capacity. These findings underscore the complementary action of nano-Si and S. albogriseolus in activating a robust defense system under stress, a phenomenon also observed in barley, where combined treatment with Si nanoparticles and plant growth-promoting bacteria improved antioxidant enzyme performance and physiological parameters under salinity69. Although nano-Si application had a significant effect on CAT activity (P < 0.01), its interactions with S. albogriseolus were not statistically significant for the antioxidant parameters, indicating that the bio-inoculant was the primary driver of the plant’s enzymatic defense system against oxidative stress.

Salinity stress profoundly disrupts plant nutrition by impairing nutrient uptake and inducing ionic imbalance. High salt concentrations induce osmotic stress and ion toxicity, which suppresses the absorption of essential nutrients such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), while simultaneously promoting the accumulation of toxic ions including sodium (Na) and chloride (Cl−)70,71. In the present study, salinity stress significantly (P < 0.001) reduced leaf N, P, and K+ concentrations, and elevated Na+ levels, resulting in stunted plant growth. Both nano-Si and S. albogriseolus inoculations effectively alleviated these adverse effects. Individually, each treatment enhanced the uptake of essential nutrients and reduced Na+ accumulation, and their combined application proved most effective in restoring the critical K/Na ratio. Furthermore, the observed increase in leaf Si content under these treatments confirmed their role in improving Si uptake, which contributes to nutrient homeostasis under saline conditions.

It was also observed that S. albogriseolus exhibits a strong capacity to solubilize silicates through the secretion of organic acid, thereby enhancing the availability of plant-accessible silicon72. Studies have shown that this process not only improves Si accumulation but also promotes the uptake of other nutrients, such as P and K, as observed in the rice seedlings73. Concurrently, nano-Si improves water retention and regulates ion transport, reducing Na uptake and facilitating the solubilization of essential nutrients, such as K and P. Moreover, nano-Si has been shown to reshapethe the rhizosphere microbiome, thereby promoting nutrient mobilization74.

Plant growth-promoting bacteria, including Streptomyces, enhance nutrient acquisition by modifying root architecture and increasing soil nutrient75. This finding aligns with previous studies in which combined Si and PGPR applications increased macronutrient concentrations in mung beans and alfalfa under saline conditions76. These effects are likely due to the role of Si and PGPR in enhancing nutrient uptake through osmotic regulation, increased leaf surface area, root elongation, and improved root exudation under stressful conditions77. Similarly, the co-application of Pseudomonas psychrotolerans and silicon in maize reduced Na uptake by 42.3% while enhancing K absorption, thereby optimizing the K/Na ratio78.

Silicon also enhances nitrogen-use efficiency by upregulating genes involved in nitrogen uptake and transport and by stimulating nitrogen assimilation and remobilization79. This effect is complemented by nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which enrich soil nitrogen pools, reduce dependence on chemical fertilizers, and improve overall soil health and crop productivity14,80. Collectively, these findings illustrate the synergistic potential of nano-Si and Streptomyces inoculation in sustaining nutrient balance and improving plant performance under saline conditions.

These findings are consistent with previous studies on the synergistic benefits of combining plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) and nano-Si fertilization in soil–plant systems. However, the specific mechanisms through which this synergy influences soil Si fractions, particularly soluble (plant-available), exchangeable, and amorphous forms, remain poorly understood. Silicon fertilization generally enriches these soil pools, thereby enhancing the nutrient reservoir for plant uptake81. Although PGPR are known to improve soil fertility and nutrient cycling, their direct effects on Si dynamics require further investigation82. Nevertheless, PGPR-mediated stimulation of microbial activity may facilitate the transformation of silicon into more plant-accessible forms, thereby amplifying the benefits of Si fertilization83.

In this study, salinity stress reduced the concentrations of soluble and exchangeable silicon while increasing the amorphous silicon content. Nano-Si application counteracted this effect by increasing the amount of soluble and exchangeable Si and reducing the amount of amorphous Si. This shift was further enhanced by Streptomyces inoculation, which significantly improved plant access to bioavailable Si. Combined treatment resulted in the most pronounced increase in soil-available Si, indicating a synergistic improvement in Si cycling under saline conditions. These results are consistent with the findings of Valizadeh-rad et al.83, who reported that nano-Si application increased soluble and exchangeable Si in wheat and canola rhizospheres.

Amorphous Si constitutes the dominant fraction in most soils83,84, whereas soluble Si, the primary plant-available form, is susceptible to depletion through plant uptake, microbial sequestration, and adsorption to mineral surfaces85. Soil microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi, actively participate in Si mobilization by releasing it from mineral substrates86. Environmental factors such as pH, ionic strength, and silicon concentration further modulate silicon fractionation87.

Streptomyces inoculation promotes silicate dissolution, likely through the secretion of exopolysaccharides, organic acids, and siderophores. This microbial function may reduce the dependence of silicon-accumulating crops, such as rice and maize, on conventional silicon fertilizers88. The results of the present study demonstrate that the silicate-solubilizing capacity of S. albogriseolus, observed in vitro conditions, remains functional in a saline rhizosphere. This strain actively mobilizes silicon from the native soil pool, as evidenced by the corresponding shifts in soil Si fractions.

The enhanced performance of nano-silicon sources over conventional amendments, such as potassium silicate, is fundamentally linked to their distinctive physicochemical properties. The reduced particle size and consequent high specific surface area enhance dissolution and silicon bioavailability while simultaneously inhibiting its polymerization to recalcitrant amorphous pools. Although Si sources influence solubility and bioavailability89, the total soil Si content is a poor indicator of plant-available Si90. Therefore, combining nano-Si with silicate-solubilizing PGPR offers a promising strategy for optimizing Si bioavailability for sustainable crop production83.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation among treatments, indicating that salinity level was the dominant factor influencing plant physiological and biochemical responses. Growth traits were predominant under low salinity, whereas stress indicators prevailed under severe salinity, reflecting the well-documented negative effects of excess salt on osmotic balance, ion homeostasis, and membrane stability Elevated Na+ accumulation, electrolyte leakage, and MDA content under high-salinity conditions confirmed the induction of salt damage. The association between moderate salinity and available silicon fractions supports the protective role of Si in alleviating salinity stress. Si has been shown to enhance cell wall stability, improve water relations, regulate ion transport, and stimulate antioxidant defense systems, thereby reducing Na+ toxicity and oxidative damage91,92. The positive relationship between nano-Si application and growth traits observed at 200 mg kg−1 suggests that this concentration optimizes Si-mediated stress mitigation without imposing potential toxicity. The higher catalase activity detected at 400 mg kg−1 indicates the activation of defense pathways, which may reflect a threshold beyond which plants invest more in protection than in growth.

The strong positive correlations observed between growth traits, key nutrients (N, P, K), and bioavailable soil Si fractions underscore the integral role of nutrient acquisition, particularly Si availability, in sustaining plant growth and water status under saline conditions93. The pivotal role of ionic balance is further highlighted by the strong positive association between the K/Na ratio and favorable growth and physiological outcomes, reinforcing its established function as a key determinant of salt tolerance.

Conclusions

This study provides compelling evidence that integrated application of nano-Si and halotolerant S. albogriseolus can substantially mitigate the detrimental effects of salinity on maize growth. Salinity stress significantly reduced plant biomass, photosynthetic efficiency, and relative water content while increasing membrane damage and oxidative stress. However, the co-application of nano-Si and S. albogriseolus not only counteracted these effects, but also improved plant growth, nutrient uptake, and antioxidant enzyme activities. Enhanced catalase (CAT) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activities indicated that the combined treatment reinforced the plant’s antioxidant defense system, whereas higher proline content and reduced malondialdehyde levels reflected improved osmotic regulation and protection against oxidative damage. In addition, the synergistic treatment improved nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium uptake, while significantly lowering sodium accumulation, and maintaining a balanced K/Na ratio is an essential determinant of salt tolerance. Moreover, the combined application increased the soluble and exchangeable fractions of Si in the soil, suggesting enhanced Si bioavailability and improved plant–microbe–soil interactions. In summary, the combination of nano-Si and Streptomyces improves maize performance through multiple complementary mechanisms, enhancing physiological balance, nutrient efficiency, and antioxidative stability, thus offering a sustainable strategy for maintaining productivity in salt-affected agroecosystems. These results highlight that the integration of nanotechnology with beneficial rhizosphere Actinomycetota not only enhances plant resilience to salinity, but also supports soil fertility and microbial activity.
