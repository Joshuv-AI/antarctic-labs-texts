Cocaine-induced granulomatosis with polyangiitis: an under-recognised condition
Authors: Gill Charn, Joseph Sturman, Leyla Ozbek, Scott Henderson, Aine Burns, Sally Hamour, Ruth Pepper, Lisha McClelland, Dimitrios Chanouzas, Simon Gane, Alan Salama, Lorraine Harper
Published: 2024

Introduction

Cocaine is the second most commonly abused drug in the UK,1 with 2.6% of the population aged between 16–59 years old using it. It is known to cause significant pathology, including cocaine-induced midline destructive lesions (CIMDL),2 and various vasculitic lesions,3 although renal and pulmonary involvement is rare.4, 5, 6, 7 There remains ongoing debate about the optimal evidence-based diagnosis and management of this patient population, with significant variation in practice across the globe.8 In this retrospective study, we report outcomes from our cohort of 42 patients to contribute additional data enhancing our understanding of this condition.

Material and methods

We performed a retrospective case series analysis of patients presenting with cocaine-induced midline destructive lesions or vasculitis compatible with granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) from two large UK tertiary vasculitis clinics between 2016 and 2021. The data collected included clinical manifestations, ANCA serology both by ELISA and immunofluorescence (IIF), urine toxicology, and nasal tissue histology as well as further management and outcomes at follow-up.

Results and discussion

42 patients with cocaine-induced midline lesions or systemic disease were identified. The median age was 41 years (range 23–66). Current cocaine use was common, with 20/23 of samples provided being positive when routine urine toxicology was performed; nine patients who denied ever using cocaine were identified based on urine toxicology analysis, while 11 who stated they were ex-users still tested positive. There was a high incidence of septal perforation (75%) and oronasal fistula (15%). None of our patients had pulmonary haemorrhage and median creatinine was 76 mmol/l (range 50–220 mmol/l). Two patients had a renal biopsy due to haematuria, with only one patient having evidence of inflammation consistent with IgA vasculitis. This patient was the only patient with acute kidney injury (creatinine 220 umol/l) and had no ENT symptoms. 56% of our patients were PR3-ANCA positive with none testing positive for MPO-ANCA. Symptom remission required cocaine discontinuation even when immunosuppression was administered.

Conclusion

This study represents the largest cohort of patients with cocaine-induced vasculitis in the UK and our findings make a significant contribution to the existing literature. Patients with destructive nasal lesions, especially young patients, should have urine toxicology performed for cocaine before diagnosing GPA and considering immunosuppressive therapy. The ANCA pattern is not specific for cocaine-induced midline destructive lesions. Treatment must be focused on cocaine cessation and conservative management in the first instance as our data suggest immunosuppression without cocaine cessation does not lead to recovery.
