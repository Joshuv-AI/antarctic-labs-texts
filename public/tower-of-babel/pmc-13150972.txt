Splenectomy in β‐thalassemia patients: Practices and risks in a nationwide study
Authors: Audrey Benoit, Justine Pages, Sarah Szepetowski, Imane Agouti, Isabelle Thuret, Enora Le Roux, Catherine Badens, Valentine Brousse, NaThalY Network, Catherine Mathey, Louisa Bey, Marie‐Josée Lucchini, Valérie Li Thio Te, Reda Garidi, Loïc Garcon, Corentin Orvain, Jean‐François Brasme, Claire Reynes, Marie Belloy, Julie Durin, Oula Alhindy, Lara Sabbagh, Borhane Slama, Anthony Sanchez, Alice Moulin, Danielle Belgodere, Lise Benjemia, Pauline Simon, Eric Oziol, Nathalie Aladjidi, Yoann Huguenin, Charlotte Jubert, Edouard Forcade, Pierre Duffau, Abdallah Maakaroun, Florence Lachenal, Charline Pegon, Marie‐Helene Odievre, Charlotte Petitdidier, Sai Atmani

β‐thalassemia is a common hemoglobinopathy characterized by a genetically determined defective production of the β‐globin chain of hemoglobin (Hb), resulting in chronic anemia and ineffective erythropoiesis. 1 Reducing transfusion requirements to better control iron overload is a major goal in patient care. Several approaches have been developed to that end, including drugs to decrease ineffective erythropoiesis and improve anemia, 2 , 3 , 4 and splenectomy to decrease spleen red blood cell trapping. The indication for splenectomy remains, however, challenging to define due to its arguable risk‐to‐benefit ratio: while surgery may improve transfusion requirements, this benefit can be outweighed by an increased risk of infectious and thrombotic complications. This retrospective study aims to further analyze the incidence of spleen‐related complications, namely thrombotic events (TE) and pneumococcal complications, in a large national cohort of patients with β‐thalassemia followed longitudinally in the French Thalassemia Registry (NaThalY) to better characterize the risk associated with splenectomy.

As of December 2022, a total of 834 patients with β‐thalassemia were enrolled in the registry, including 544 (65%) with thalassemia major (TM) and 290 (35%) with thalassemia intermedia (TI) (Supporting Information Methods). Overall, 322 (39%) patients underwent splenectomy with a significantly greater proportion and at a younger age in TM patients. Focusing on the main common known risks of splenectomy, all data entries in the registry of infectious or thrombotic nature were considered and analyzed during the follow‐up period for all patients. The observation period was 20 200 patient/years (PY) with a median follow‐up for all patients of 20.0 years. Further characteristics and general outcome are detailed in Table 1A.

A sharp decrease was evidenced over time in the proportion of splenectomized patients (from 80% in patients born in the 60 s to 30% in those born in the 2000s) along with a decrease in median age at splenectomy (17.9 years vs. 6.5 years) in both thalassemia phenotypes (Figure 1A).

Regarding infectious complications, four cases due to encapsulated bacteria were documented (two pneumococcal meningitis, one pneumococcal pneumonia, and one pneumococcal sepsis), all in splenectomized patients (3 TM, 1 TI), yielding an incidence of 0.04 PY. Median time of onset after splenectomy was 15.4 years (min 5; max 22). Additionally, 27% (n = 13/48) of causes of death reported during the study period were due to infections (with no identified pathogen available), with 92% (n = 12) involving splenectomized patients.

The occurrence of TE was analyzed according to spleen status and phenotype. Distribution is shown in Table 1B. A total of 92 TE was observed in 59 patients, including 55 (93%) who underwent splenectomy, showing a significantly higher rate than in nonsplenectomized patients (P < 0.0001). The overall TE incidence dramatically increased by a 13‐fold following splenectomy (0.79 vs. 0.06 PY; P < 0.0001). Those TE included 27 venous thrombosis (31.4%), 19 pulmonary embolisms (22.1%), 15 cases of pulmonary hypertension (17.4%), 11 portal thrombosis (12.8%), 6 thrombosis associated with a central device (7%), 6 strokes (7%), and 2 arterial thrombosis (2.3%). Multiple TE occurred in 30% (n = 17) of splenectomized patients and included up to 6 episodes in two. Of the four non‐splenectomized patients who experienced TE, three were TI with a first event occurring at a mean age of 67 years.

The total number of TE was significantly higher in TI patients compared to TM, with an incidence of 0.64 PY and 0.31 PY, respectively (P = 0.0008). TI patients also displayed a higher rate of multiple events.

Following splenectomy, the highest overall incidence was evidenced in patients with TI (1.39 PY) compared to TM patients (0.48 PY). Median (Q1; Q3) time between splenectomy and first TE was 18.7 (8.6; 24.1) years, similarly in TM and TI patients (Figure 1B).

At first TE, 22 patients (37%) had comorbidities known to be associated with an increased thrombotic risk, such as heart failure, arrhythmia, diabetes, cancer, or cirrhosis. Other known TE risk factors were identified in 5 (8.5%) patients (factor V Leiden and factor II mutations, protein C and S deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), or oestroprogestative contraception). Only one death was related to a TE: a 30‐year‐old female died from a pulmonary embolism in a context of heart failure and diabetes, 20 years after splenectomy.

Multivariate analysis, including β‐thalassemia phenotype, spleen status, and age, showed that splenectomy was the most important risk factor for TEs, with a risk increased by 20. As expected, TI phenotype came second, followed by age (Table 1C). Transfusion therapy did not appear to be fully protective of TE, with 64% (n = 38) of patients who experienced TE being regularly transfused at the time of first occurrence.

This study, focusing on post‐splenectomy events on a nationwide basis over multiple decades, allows to demonstrate two key findings: a significant decline in the use of splenectomy among patients with β‐thalassemia over the last 20 years; and a high incidence of thrombotic complications following splenectomy, across all thalassemia phenotypes, with the highest incidence, consistent with previous evidence, observed in TI patients. 5 , 6 , 7

Abnormal, prematurely senescent, or adherent red cells circulating in thalassemia patients are normally cleared by specialized macrophages within the spleen. Following splenectomy, these abnormal red cells along with an increased platelet count are found in the peripheral circulation altogether increasing the thrombotic risk. 8 Historically, splenectomy was indicated to improve anemia in TI and reduce transfusion needs in TM, so we can reasonably assume that was the case for the patients included in this cohort. Nevertheless, presumably due to growing awareness of the high incidence of complications, indications for splenectomy in TM patients have decreased over time in France, similar to other countries, with the prompt initiation of iterative transfusions in severely anemic patients, reducing the occurrence of hypersplenism. 9 , 10 In fact, splenectomy should rarely be indicated in well‐transfused patients as regular transfusions suppress ineffective erythropoiesis, extra medullary erythropoiesis, and splenomegaly. In TI patients, who are the most prone to post‐splenectomy TE, a discussion on the benefit/risk ratio of splenectomy versus regular transfusions should be undertaken. Access to medications that alleviate anemia and reduce ineffective erythropoiesis, such as pyruvate kinase activators, activin ligand traps, or HbF inducers, will be of potential benefit in those patients. 11

Expectedly, venous thrombosis was the most frequent event. Portal thrombosis merits special attention as it was mainly found upon systematic screening in otherwise asymptomatic patients, sometimes during a distant follow‐up from splenectomy and found with a higher frequency (3.4%) than in other conditions described in the literature. 12 As it may additionally impact liver function, it appears particularly important to systematically monitor this complication with portal echography screening post‐surgery. Comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease or diabetes, which are known thrombotic risk factors, are also frequent in β‐thalassemia patients due to iron overload. In this study, more than one‐third of patients who experienced TE had comorbidities or identified risk factors, thus highlighting the need for these high‐risk patients to be screened and closely monitored.

Regular transfusions reduce the risk of TE by suppressing ineffective erythropoiesis and diluting abnormal red cells in the circulation. In this study, ten patients classified as TI were regularly transfused at first event, suggesting that the protecting effect of transfusion is offset by the effect of splenectomy in the long term, particularly in TI patients. 5 Overall, prophylactic anti thrombotic treatment seems reasonable to recommend given the high rate of TE and the hypercoagulable state in beta thalassemia patients, particularly in TI patients. 13

Very few studies have previously evaluated the timing of thrombotic complications after splenectomy and to our knowledge, none have done so in both TM and TI population over an extended period. 14 , 15 , 16 One notable feature here is the ability to assess this long‐term risk. With 6734 cumulated years of follow‐up after splenectomy, complications occurred up to 42 years after surgery with no significant difference between TM and TI, underscoring the need for continued follow‐up.

Higher rates of infections have long been reported after splenectomy in general. 17 While the incidence may have been underestimated by the inability to identify the pathogen and missing data, notably regarding infectious causes of death, the low incidence of post‐splenectomy sepsis caused by encapsulated bacteria remains an interesting feature in this study compared to the incidence observed in the context of trauma‐related splenectomy. 17

This study has several limitations. As is often the case, the data extracted from a registry may result in biases and/or be subject to missing information within or outside the collected dataset, such as indication for splenectomy, post‐splenectomy TE prevention, and additional risk factors. Also, the older age of splenectomized patients in this study may have overestimated the association between splenectomy and TE, while conversely, the incidence of TE in nonsplenectomized patients may rise with time. Finally, in this study, as in others, a strong association between variables does not permit us to infer causality.

Altogether, these findings add to the growing body of evidence that splenectomy is associated with a high risk of thrombotic complications in β‐thalassemia patients, particularly in TI patients. They support the need for a rigorous individualized assessment of risk factors and highlight the importance of lifelong prevention and monitoring of TE in these patients. Hopefully, the development of new therapies that alleviate the burden of anemia and improve the quality of life for affected individuals will further reduce the need for splenectomy.
