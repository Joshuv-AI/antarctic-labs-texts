F&O Expiry vs. First-Day SIPs: A 22-Year Analysis of Timing Advantages in India’s Nifty 50
Siddharth Gavhale

Abstract
This study presents a comparative analysis of Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) performance in India’s Nifty 50 index over a 22-year period (2003–2024), focusing on the timing of investments—specifically, the first trading day (FTD) of each month versus the monthly expiry day of Futures and Options (EXP). The research establishes, through mathematical validation, that SIP returns are independent of the invested amount. Leveraging historical index data, the study finds that SIPs timed on F&O expiry days consistently outperform FTD-SIPs by 0.5–2.5% annually over short- to medium-term horizons (1–3 years), highlighting a tactical advantage due to expiry-related market volatility. However, this advantage diminishes over longer durations (10–20 years), reaffirming that sustained, long-term investing is the key driver of wealth accumulation. Additionally, the analysis challenges prevailing industry narratives by revealing that the 20-year compounded annual growth rate (CAGR) of Nifty 50 SIPs stands at approximately 6.7% (pre-tax), significantly lower than the widely advertised 12–15% returns. This research offers novel insights for retail investors and financial planners on the role of investment timing in SIP strategies within the Indian equity market.



Keywords:
SIP returns, Nifty 50, F&O expiry, systematic investment plan, Indian stock market, investment strategies.

I
Introduction

Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs) have emerged as a cornerstone of retail investment in India’s equity markets, offering a structured and disciplined path to long-term wealth creation. Designed to reduce the emotional and cognitive burden of investment decisions, SIPs enable individuals to invest fixed amounts at regular intervals, thereby benefiting from rupee-cost averaging and compounding returns over time. This approach is particularly appealing in India–a rapidly growing economy with a rising middle class seeking accessible wealth-creation tools
[
36
,
23
]
. Among the available investment avenues, the Nifty 50 Index has emerged as a widely adopted benchmark for systematic investment plans (SIPs) in India. The index comprises 50 large-cap companies across 13 sectors and is considered a proxy for the Indian equity market and the broader economy
[
19
]
.
According to recent data from the Association of Mutual Funds in India (AMFI), as of February 2025, more than 100 million investors participate in SIPs. Among those, 4.456 million are new SIP accounts, further contributing approximately INR 25,999 crore monthly to mutual funds
[
2
]
. This marks a significant rise from early 2021, underscoring the growing popularity of the SIP route among retail investors
[
3
]
. While SIPs are lauded for their simplicity and disciplined approach to investing, the timing of these investments remains a relatively underexplored aspect. It is commonly observed that many SIPs are scheduled for execution on the first trading day of each month, a practice likely influenced by salary credit cycles and the perception that early-month investments are financially prudent. However, this uniformity in investment timing may inadvertently expose investors to suboptimal market entry points, potentially affected by institutional order flows and other market dynamics.

Futures and Options (F&O) expiry days—typically the last Thursday of each month—are characterized by heightened trading activity and market turbulence. Empirical studies indicate that expiry-day volumes surge significantly, with price pressures emerging around the event. Vipul (2005) finds that volumes increase abnormally and prices tend to dip just before expiry, followed by sharp gains the day after, largely due to arbitrage activity and constraints in the cash market
[
32
]
. These short-term dislocations may offer systematic entry opportunities, particularly for SIPs timed around expiry. While past research–such as the study by
[
31
]
–confirms the long-run outperformance of SIPs over lump-sum strategies, these studies do not evaluate the tactical potential of aligning SIP execution with F&O expiry cycles. This gap persists in both academic and practitioner literature regarding whether systematic volatility exposure via expiry-day investments can enhance returns without increasing investor burden.

This study addresses these gaps through a 22-year comparative analysis (2003–2024) of SIP performance under two execution schedules: (i) FTD-SIP (first trading day investments), and (ii) EXP-SIP (F&O expiry day investments). Using the Nifty 50 index as the benchmark, we assess return differentials across horizons ranging from short-term (1–3 years) to long-term (20 years). Our findings reveal three critical insights:

1.

Timing matters
–EXP-SIPs outperform FTD-SIPs by 0.5–2.5% annually in shorter horizons, demonstrating tactical advantages from expiry-day volatility

2.

Performance is timing-sensitive
–Market behavior at entry points dominates the impact of absolute investment amounts

3.

Myths vs. reality
–The widely advertised 12-15% historical returns in index fund
[
13
,
30
,
11
]
, contrast sharply with empirical 20-year CAGR of 6.7% (pre-tax), exposing discrepancies in industry narratives.

While expiry-day SIPs offer measurable advantages over short horizons, this benefit fades beyond 10–12 years—underscoring that long-term, consistent participation remains the dominant driver of wealth accumulation. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews theoretical and empirical literature on SIP performance and timing. Section 3 outlines the methodology applied to 22 years of Nifty 50 index data. Section 4 presents theoretical and empirical results comparative performance results across different investment durations. Section 5 discusses broader implications and limitations of the findings, and Section 6 concludes the study.

II
Literature Review

Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs) are rooted in foundational financial theories that emphasize long-term, disciplined investing. The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) proposed by
[
12
]
and the Random Walk Theory articulated by
[
16
]
suggest that asset prices reflect all available information, rendering consistent market timing exceedingly difficult. SIPs operationalize these principles by promoting periodic investments regardless of short-term price fluctuations, thereby mitigating the risks of market timing.
Complementing these theoretical models, behavioral finance offers critical insights into retail investor behavior. The Prospect Theory introduced by
[
15
]
illustrates how investors often deviate from rational decision-making due to cognitive biases such as overconfidence, loss aversion, and herd mentality. These behavioral tendencies make investors prone to mistimed entry and exit decisions. SIPs help neutralize such biases through automated and consistent investment contributions, aligning with the principles of dollar-cost averaging
[
4
]
.

Empirical studies underscore the benefits of SIPs, particularly in emerging markets such as India.
[
25
]
found that SIP strategies in mutual funds resulted lower volatility compared to lump-sum investments, while yielding superior risk-adjusted returns over five-year periods. These findings were corroborated by later studies such as
[
18
]
and
[
28
]
, which highlighted enhanced performance in small- and mid-cap fund categories. Extending SIP analysis to direct equity investments,
[
31
]
demonstrated that SIPs outperformed market-timed strategies in approximately 68% of low- and moderately volatile Nifty 50 stocks. Sectoral findings showed particularly strong long-term performance in consumer goods and information technology, where SIPs yielded higher average returns. Using hypothetical “perfect market timer” benchmarks, the study further revealed that SIP returns tend to converge with idealized market timing over longer horizons, especially in high-ROCE stocks (ROCE
>
20%).

While theoretical models may suggest potential gains from optimal market timing, practical implementation is fraught with challenges.
[
6
]
showed that transaction costs, tax inefficiencies, and execution lags often erode timing-based advantages. Moreover, emphasized behavioral risks such as regret aversion and decision paralysis, which disproportionately affect retail investors.
In the Indian context, derivatives market dynamics add a layer of complexity to SIP performance. Data from the National Stock Exchange
[
21
]
reveals that volatility during F&O expiry weeks is higher than non-expiry periods, with average absolute price changes rapidly. These fluctuations are largely attributed to institutional position adjustments, temporary liquidity imbalances, and hedging activities.
Furthermore, structural timing patterns among retail investors exacerbate return inefficiencies. According to
[
26
]
, most of the retail SIP contributions occur at beginning of each month. This influx coincides with institutional order flow adjustments, resulting in an average price inflatied on these dates. Recent assessments from financial regulatory sources suggest that inefficiencies in systematic investment planning may have led to significant wealth erosion for retail investors over the long term. These observations challenge widely held assumptions about consistently high historical SIP returns, indicating that actual long-term performance may fall short of commonly cited figures
[
24
]
.
While the literature provides valuable insights into SIP effectiveness, timing, and investor behavior, several critical gaps remain unaddressed:

1.

Timing of SIP Contributions within Monthly Cycles:
Most studies focus on the benefits of SIPs over lump-sum investments but overlook intra-month contribution timing (e.g., beginning of the month vs. expiry days), especially in the context of Indian market microstructures.

2.

Structural and Institutional Influences:
The potential distortions caused by concentrated retail flows on specific trading days, and their interaction with institutional behavior, remain underexplored in long-term wealth accumulation studies.

These gaps highlight the need for a more nuanced and data-driven understanding of SIP timing, and market frictions-specific behavior—particularly in emerging markets like India, where retail participation is growing rapidly.

III-A

Investment Durations

To assess the influence of investment horizon on SIP performance, five distinct and non-overlapping durations are analyzed within the 2003–2024 window. These durations are structured to represent varying investor preferences, from short-term tactical strategies to long-term wealth accumulation:

•

1-Year SIPs:
Evaluated for each calendar year from 2003 to 2024, with 12 equal monthly contributions beginning in January and concluding in December of the same year.

•

3-Year SIPs:
Assessed over seven triennial intervals: 2004–2006, 2007–2009, 2010–2012, 2013–2015, 2016–2018, 2019–2021, and 2022–2024.

•

5-Year SIPs:
Simulated over four quinquennial periods: 2005–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2024.

•

10-Year SIPs:
Represented by two decadal windows: 2005–2014 and 2015–2024.

•

20-Year SIP:
Captures a full investment cycle spanning the 2005–2024 period.

This structured segmentation enables consistent evaluation across timeframes while ensuring that all plans conclude by 2024. It also facilitates comparative analysis of return profiles across short-, medium-, and long-term horizons, aligning with varied investor goals.

III-B

Investment Timing

Beyond duration, the specific timing of monthly investments is a critical determinant of SIP returns due to market volatility and price fluctuations within each month. This study compares two practical SIP execution strategies:

•

First Trading Day SIP (FTD-SIP):
Contributions are made on the first trading day of each calendar month. This approach reflects conventional SIP scheduling, where investments are executed early in the month, potentially allowing longer compounding for each installment.

•

F&O Expiry Day SIP (EXP-SIP):
Contributions occur on the F&O expiry date of the previous month, typically the last Thursday (or adjusted accordingly for holidays). This timing is chosen to explore whether market adjustments or end-of-month volatility offer any advantage.

Illustrative Example:
Consider a 1-Year SIP for the year 2010. In the FTD-SIP strategy, monthly investments are made on the first trading days of January through December 2010, with the plan maturing on December 31, 2010. In contrast, the EXP-SIP version invests on the F&O expiry dates of the preceding month—e.g., December 31, 2009 (for January 2010), January 28, 2010 (for February), and so on—while maintaining the same maturity date.

To evaluate investment performance, the Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) is used. CAGR reflects the annualized rate of return assuming reinvestment and compounding. While XIRR is widely used for SIP returns, its reliance on internal rate of return calculations introduces the possibility of multiple solutions under non-conventional cash flow patterns
[
35
]
, a challenge acknowledged in both academic and practitioner literature
[
8
]
. Additionally, absolute return, though simple, fails to capture investment duration, making it less suitable for comparing long-term performance
[
14
]
. The CAGR is calculated using:

\text{CAGR}=\left[\left(\frac{\text{Final value}}{\text{Total invested}}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}-1\right]\times 100=\left[\left(\frac{F^{k}}{12mN}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}-1\right]\times 100

(2)

For each of the 36 investment plans across all durations and schedules (FTD and EXP), we compute two separate CAGR values—

and

—to compare the impact of timing on final returns. This allows us to examine whether entry-day strategy has a systematic influence on performance, particularly in the presence of market volatility and cyclicality. To assess the statistical significance of observed differences, we conduct two-sample paired t-tests for each investment horizon (1-year, 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year periods) using Excel’s built-in
T.TEST
function. Specifically, the test is performed using the syntax
T.TEST(CAGR_e, CAGR_f, 2, 1)
, which evaluates whether the mean difference between expiry-day (EXP) and first-day (FTD) CAGR distributions is statistically significant under a two-tailed assumption with paired data.

IV
Results and Analysis

IV-A

Theoretical

The first theoretical result of this article builds upon a well-established understanding in finance: the return of an index fund is determined by the performance of the underlying index and the duration of the investment, rather than the amount invested
[
5
,
9
,
29
]
. This holds true for Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs) as well, where the timing and performance of the underlying index, rather than the invested amount, influence the Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR).
To understand this in detail, consider the following lemma, which formalizes this concept in the context of SIP investments.

Lemma 1
.

(CAGR Dependency on Duration and Index Performance)

The Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) return of a Nifty 50 index fund does not depend on the invested amount but is inherently dependent on the investment duration and the performance of the underlying index over that period.

Proof.

Put
q^{k}_{i}=\frac{m}{I^{k}_{i}}
in (
2
) gives

\text{CAGR}=\left(\left(\frac{I^{L}_{12N}\left(\sum^{12N}_{i=1}\frac{1}{I^{k}_{i}}\right)}{12N}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}-1\right)\times 100

As the numerator and denominator both scale linearly with
m
, the
m
terms cancel, leaving CAGR dependent only on the index performance and duration, i.e,
\text{CAGR}\propto(I^{k}_{i},N),\text{ and CAGR}\not\varpropto m

∎

This lemma provides a fundamental insight about SIPs:
the investment amount is irrelevant to the CAGR calculation
. Instead, the timing of investments — whether made on the first trading day (FTD) or at the expiry of options (EXP) — and the market’s overall trajectory play a much more critical role. An index that shows a steady upward trend with smaller drawdowns will naturally lead to a higher CAGR
[
17
]
. Therefore, selecting an optimal investment schedule and understanding long-term index behavior is more impactful than merely increasing the amount invested.

IV-B

Empirical Result

This subsection synthesizes the empirical findings from multiple time horizons—ranging from 1-year to 20-year investment windows—based on SIP execution strategies. The data exhibits nuanced but consistent trends.

IV-B
1
One-Year Investment Returns

This subsection presents a year-by-year analysis of one-year Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) returns for the Nifty 50 index between 2003 and 2024. Each year’s investment spans from January to December, using earlier mentioend two strategies. The Compounded Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) serves as the return metric, and the paired two-sample
t
-test is used to evaluate the statistical significance of differences between the two strategies on an annual basis.

The data exhibit considerable inter-annual variability, reflecting the inherent volatility in equity markets. Years such as 2003, 2005, 2006, 2007, 2009 & 2020, recorded CAGRs exceeding 20% under both strategies. In 2003, the first-day investment yielded a CAGR of 60.43% compared to 62.94% from expiry-based SIPs. See Table
III
and Figure
1
for details. Conversely, bearish years such as 2008, 2011 and 2015 experienced negative returns. The 2008 financial crisis led to nearly identical CAGRs: -30.08% for the first-day strategy and -29.99% for expiry-day SIPs, with an insignificant difference. In 2011, both strategies again posted losses, with expiry-day investments slightly outperforming (-12.58% vs. -14.81%), though this difference was not statistically significant. Same has repeated in 2015 with smaller difference in CAGR.
Over the entire 22-year span, the average absolute CAGR difference between the two strategies remained modest—typically within a
\pm
2% range. Positive CAGR differences in favor of expiry-based investing were observed more than 80% of the years. In 2004, 2005, and 2022, expiry-based SIPs outperformed by more than 1.55%.
Years like 2003, 2011, and 2013 showed CAGR differences exceeding 2%. Meanwhile, in stable years such as
2015
and
2019
, both strategies produced nearly identical outcomes, emphasizing the limited impact of timing in less volatile periods.

The
t
-test for this one year horizon returned a
p
-value of 0.0036, indicating a statistically significant difference at the 1% level. This suggests that in years of strong market rebounds, even minor timing adjustments can have measurable effects. The analysis reveals that even within a relatively short one-year investment horizon, SIPs timed around the F&O expiry date consistently delivered marginally superior returns compared to first-day-of-month investments. This suggests that expiry-based timing is not just a random anomaly but may be capturing systematic behavioral or structural market effects. While the average annual CAGR difference remained within
\pm
2%, the persistent directional advantage in favor of expiry-based SIPs highlights its potential as a tactical enhancement, especially in turbulent or momentum-driven markets. Thus, even for short-term SIPs, aligning investments with expiry dates may offer a subtle yet repeatable edge — reinforcing the case for expiry-aware SIP execution strategies.

IV-C

Consolidated Observations

The comparative analysis of SIPs initiated on the first trading day versus the F&O expiry day of each month reveals a nuanced but consistent pattern. Across nearly all years and investment horizons, the expiry-day strategy tends to yield a marginally higher CAGR than the first-day approach. However, this edge is generally modest, with only a few instances—such as in 2003, 2004, 2005, 2013 and 2020—where the difference is both practically large (
>1.5\%
) and statistically significant (
p

<0.05
).

Short-term investments (1-year horizon) exhibit the greatest sensitivity to entry timing, likely due to heightened market noise and short-lived momentum or volatility clusters around expiry. For example, in 2003 and 2005, expiry-day SIPs benefited from pronounced dips and recoveries surrounding monthly rollovers, leading to an advantage exceeding 2.5% in CAGR. However, this timing premium proves unstable—vanishing or even reversing in years of market crisis (e.g., 2008, 2020) or extreme bullishness with smooth trends (e.g., 2009).
As the investment horizon extends—across 3-year, 5-year, 10-year, and especially 20-year windows—the CAGR difference between the two strategies steadily converges toward zero. This result underscores the principle that the SIP mechanism itself—by distributing entry points across time—acts as a volatility smoother, gradually diluting the impact of any single month’s entry price anomaly.
For long-term, goal-oriented investors, this suggests that entry timing becomes largely irrelevant beyond a certain time horizon. What matters more is disciplined, uninterrupted investing, rather than tactical entry attempts. Nonetheless, for momentum-oriented or expiry-aware traders, the small but repeatable edge observed in certain phases suggests that expiry-based SIPs can serve as a useful heuristic—though this should be contextualized within broader asset allocation and risk management frameworks.

In essence, both strategies are statistically and practically equivalent in the long run. However, the expiry-based approach may offer a behaviorally aligned advantage for certain investor types, especially those who follow the market calendar closely. The overarching implication remains clear: SIP works best not because of when one invests in a month, but because one invests every month.

Even our frictionless CAGR estimates overstate real-world outcomes. Once expense ratios (typically ranging from 1.1% to 1.8% annually) are accounted for, the net return to investors declines further. Together, these factors explain why the frequently marketed 12% figure is more myth than reality. A realistic expectation—net of fees and behaviorally adjusted—would place long-run SIP returns closer to 5–6%, especially for broad-based index or actively managed equity funds. This has direct implications for financial planning, retirement projections, and public trust in investment advisory practices.

V-C

Limitations and Research Frontiers

While this study offers significant insights into the temporal dynamics of SIP investments, it also opens several avenues for further investigation. First, the extent of sectoral heterogeneity remains unclear—do timing effects differ across industries (such as IT vs. FMCG) or market capitalizations (large-cap vs. mid-cap)? Second, the geographic generalizability of our findings warrants exploration. It is unknown whether similar expiry-related timing advantages exist in developed markets with differing microstructure dynamics, such as the U.S. or European Union. Finally, an important practical question remains under active exploration by the author: what is the optimal pattern of incremental monthly SIP contributions (e.g., fixed vs. progressive increase) that maximizes long-term compounded returns?

Addressing these frontiers will likely require richer datasets—such as panel-level investment flows and investor demographics—as well as interdisciplinary approaches combining econometrics, behavioral finance, and simulation modeling. Insights from such research could inform more personalized SIP structures, smarter default options, and regulatory innovations to enhance investor welfare.

VI
Conclusion

This paper provides a comprehensive analysis of SIP timing strategies in India’s Nifty 50 index, revealing three core insights with broad theoretical and practical implications. First, we find statistically significant short- to medium-term advantages for SIPs executed on F&O expiry days, which outperform first-day investments by an average annual excess return of 0.58% (
\sigma=1.12\%
,
p<0.05
). This outperformance appears linked to structural liquidity distortions, driven by institutional hedging, month-end rebalancing, and predictable retail flow patterns.
Second, our results directly challenge the widely circulated claim of 12% SIP returns. The actual pre-tax CAGR over the 2005–2024 period lies between 6.65% and 6.68%, with the gap explained by survivorship bias ( 3.2 percentage points), compounded expense ratios ( 1.1–1.8%), and a behavioral investor return gap ( 0.7%). These findings expose a critical disconnect between investor expectations and realized outcomes, emphasizing the need for greater transparency in return reporting.

In response, we propose targeted regulatory reforms. SEBI should consider mandating expiry-day SIP options across all mutual fund platforms, implementing real-return dashboards that adjust for inflation and costs, and requiring rolling-period return disclosures to avoid misleading point estimates. For investors, expiry-day SIPs offer a simple, cost-free enhancement for short- to medium-term goals, while conventional strategies remain suitable for long-term wealth creation.

Future research should investigate timing effectiveness across sectors and market caps, explore demographic or behavioral drivers of suboptimal SIP execution, and assess the generalizability of these patterns in other emerging and developed markets.
Ultimately, while market microstructure offers fleeting advantages to the informed, sustained long-term participation remains the most reliable and powerful strategy—an enduring truth that transcends any tactical timing edge.
