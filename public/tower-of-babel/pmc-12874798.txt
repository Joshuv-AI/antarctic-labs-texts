Parental estimates of children’s weight status: evidence from Serbian children aged 6–9 years
Authors: Szabolcs Halasi, Višnja Đorđić, Lidija Marković, Dragan Cvejić, Predrag Božić, Nebojša Trajković, Sergej M Ostojić
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNumerous studies have found that parents underestimate the overweight and obese weight status of their children, which may lead to future health risks. The purpose of the paper was to examine how parents in Serbia perceive their children’s weight status. A nationally representative sample of 6–9-year-old children (n = 2700) was evaluated as part of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) European Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative (COSI). The children’s body mass index (BMI) was categorized as BMI-for-age Z-scores based on the 2007 WHO-recommended growth references. The family questionnaires, which were filled in by parents or caregivers, contained, among others, questions seeking to identify the adults’ perceptions of their children’s weight status. The weight distribution of the participant children was the following: 2% of the children were in the category of ‘thinness’, 20.6% were in ‘overweight’ and 14,7% were in the category ‘obese’. A total of 83.2% of the parents accurately perceived their child’s healthy weight (normal weight), however, 71% of the parents underestimated the overweight status of their children and 91,2% of the parents failed to acknowledge the obese status of their child according to the WHO definition. Childhood obesity should be continuously monitored; existing health promotion interventions should be more strictly controlled. This study found that parents need more health-related education in the future, with close cooperation of schools and stakeholders in raising healthier generations.Keywords: Children, Obesity, Overweight, Parents’ perception, Serbia

Introduction

Despite advancements in technology and accessibility of resources, physical inactivity has become increasingly prevalent in modern society. This sedentary lifestyle has contributed to a global rise in obesity and overweight individuals, affecting not only adults but also children [5, 11, 17, 57]. Childhood obesity rates show an increasing trend in Serbia [4, 21, 31], while in 2016, 30%, and in 2019, 34.4% of the representative sample was obese or overweight [30]. The rise in obesity prevalence among children is particularly alarming, since it will likely result in a number of adverse health consequences during childhood, but can also be traced into adolescence and adulthood [8, 9, 47]. Having obesity during childhood can also predispose individuals to many other chronic diseases in the long term, such as type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance [23], increased mechanical stress on joints [34], obstructive sleep apnea [22], impaired cardiovascular function [12, 53] and social functioning [13], reduced health-related quality of life [15] and mental health [19]. The root causes of childhood obesity include biological (e.g., genetics and epigenetic variants, intrauterine exposures, early nutrition, and microbiome) and environmental (both physical and social) factors [46].

In this study, authors investigated the most crucial social factor, namely, the parents. At this age (6–9 years) the parents’ model is seen as the most important one, followed by siblings, kindergarten teachers, teachers and peers. This also means that at this age child spend most of their time with their parents, who have a strong influence on the youngsters’ eating and drinking habits [60], and activity levels [25]. More importantly, parents and caregivers also have a considerable impact on the prevention and treatment of childhood obesity [1]. Therefore, active parental involvement in intervention programs plays a vital role in both preventing and treating childhood overweight and obesity [27, 50]. It is crucial for parents to have a precise understanding of their child's weight status, as this awareness greatly influences their willingness to engage in intervention programs [45]. Research indicates that parents who perceive their child's weight as a health concern are notably more inclined to implement lifestyle changes for their child compared to those who do not acknowledge the issue [40]. Yet, the parents’ perception often does not match with the actual weight status. Perceptions are intricately shaped by the dynamic interplay between the perceiver and the perceived individual. This means perceiver's unique experiences, beliefs, and inherent characteristics will affect thoughts, feelings, and attitudes towards the perceived individual. Simultaneously, the perceived person's own attributes also contribute to these perceptions [55].

Research found that parents may not be able to accurately perceive their children’s weight status, they tend to underestimate weight in overweight children [33, 35, 38, 42, 49, 54], which could influence future comorbidities. The literature has highlighted various factors that may impact the precision of parental perception regarding their child's body weight status, including the child's gender and age, as well as the parents' weight status and level of education [7, 10, 37, 39, 42, 43, 52, 59]. Therefore, an American study indicated a correlation between older children age and reduced instances of parental misperception regarding self-reported child weight status [29].

Parental misconceptions about children's weight status highlight the need for targeted interventions. Healthcare providers could offer accurate, evidence-based information through one-on-one counseling or family workshops to raise awareness of obesity risks. Collaborating with schools and community organizations could promote a healthy diet and physical activity. Policymakers could also implement public health campaigns and regulations to raise awareness and encourage healthier lifestyles.

Most research on parental perceptions of children’s weight has been conducted in Western countries, often focusing on mothers [26, 42]. Limited evidence exists from other cultural contexts, including Eastern Europe. Factors such as traditional diets, cultural beliefs, and limited opportunities for physical activity may normalize overweight and obesity in children [2, 4, 44]. Understanding how parents perceive their children’s weight status is therefore critical in Serbia, where economic and environmental constraints on lifestyle behaviors further complicates the issue [3, 51].

Althoug a numerous studies have explored parental perceptions of their children's weight status, a notable gap persists in understanding the factors influencing these perceptions and the potential implications for children's health outcomes. The majority of studies examining parental estimates of children's weight status have been conducted in Western contexts, with limited representation from diverse cultural backgrounds. This gap hinders our ability to generalize findings across different populations and understand the nuances of parental perceptions in varied cultural settings.

There is a more lenient approach toward body weight, with limited focus on the long-term health risks of childhood obesity in Serbia. Traditional diets, characterized by high-calorie foods, and restricted physical activity opportunities may normalize overweight and obesity in children [4]. Economic barriers, especially in rural or disadvantaged areas, further limit access to organized physical activities. Present authors examine how parents in Serbia see the weight status of their children.

Materials and methods

Design and settings

This study used data from the Children Obesity Surveillance Initiative (COSI). COSI is a unique system that has measured trends in overweight and obesity among primary school-aged children since 2007. COSI has been involved in taking standardized height and weight measurements from over 300,000 children across the WHO European Region, providing nationally representative data for participating countries, as well as a large region-wide dataset for analysis of the determinants of childhood overweight and obesity. In 2018–2019, the fifth round of COSI was organized in 36 countries of the WHO European Region, providing national representative data in all countries.

In Serbia, the nationally representative samples were selected through cluster sampling, with the primary school as the primary sampling unit. Schools were selected randomly from a list of public primary schools provided by the Serbian Ministry of Education. Stratification by the region, district, and level of urbanization was applied. The minimal planned sample size consisted of 2400 children per each round, as recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) European Office. The selection process utilized proportional sampling methods based on the nomenclature of territorial units for statistics (NUTS) levels 2 and 3, corresponding to statistical regions, districts, and levels of urbanization. A total of 3,397 children from 57 public elementary schools were initially recruited during the data collection period. Of these, two schools declined participation, leaving 55 schools from all five statistical regions (NUTS level 2) in Serbia and 26 out of 29 districts (NUTS level 3) included in the study. Following data verification, 162 children were excluded due to missing or inaccurate information, and 168 were excluded for being outside the eligible age range of 6 to 9.99 years. The final sample comprised 3,067 children, 47% of whom were girls. Trained field examiners performed standardized anthropometric measurements in participating schools. The research was conducted following ethical principles, all procedures were in accordance with ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. The COSI study was performed under the COSI protocol, cross-sectional design was employed in both rounds, targeting primary school children aged 6.00 to 9.99 [56], conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with procedures approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Sport and Physical Education, University of Novi Sad (46–06-01/2020–1).

Parents, teachers, and school administrators were fully informed about all study procedures. Written consent was obtained from the parents, while on the day of the measurement, assent to participate in the study was also obtained from the child.

Measuring procedures

During the measurement, children were asked to take off their shoes and remove any heavy objects like wallets and mobile phones before measurement. They were mostly measured in everyday light clothes, with bodyweight additionally corrected for an average weight of the clothes worn (gym clothes − 0.15 kg, light clothing − 0.35 kg, heavy clothing − 0.5 kg). Bodyweight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using portable digital scales (Omron BF214, Kyoto, Japan), while height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm by a portable stadiometer (Seca 213, Hamburg, Germany). Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). The WHO AnthroPlus software (Nutritional Survey module) for 2007 WHO growth reference was used to calculate the Z-scores for BMI-for-age [58]. Each child was classified in one of the following categories: underweight, normal weight, overweight (excluding obesity), or obesity.

The family questionnaires were completed by parents or caregivers (parents). The form included questions about parents’ perception of their child’s weight status, as well as other simple indicators of children’s dietary intake, physical activity or inactivity patterns, the family’s socioeconomic characteristics, and the presence of comorbid conditions associated with obesity [56].

Parental perception of their child’s weight status included in the analysis, the distribution of children according to the WHO weight status classification was estimated along with the distribution by parental perception of child’s weight status (percentage values and 95% confidence intervals). Percentages of accurate, overestimating, or underestimating perceptions were calculated for each parent and child pair.

Statistical analysis

Mean and standard deviation (SD) for age, weight, height, and Body Mass Index (BMI) were calculated for the survey respondents aged 6.00 to 9.99 years.

The independent samples t-test was used to examine for differences in anthropometric variables between boys and girls. The distribution of children according to the WHO weight status classification was estimated along with the distribution by parental perception of child’s weight status (percentage values and 95% confidence intervals). Percentages of accurate, overestimating, or underestimating perceptions were estimated for each pair of mother/father and child. In order to compare average rates, Pearson Chi Square test was used. The data was analyzed using the statistical package (SPSS Statistics 21.0, IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA).

When analyzed by gender, the same trend of underestimation was evident, though with slight differences. Among boys, parents were more likely to underestimate their child’s weight status compared to girls, particularly in the overweight and obese categories. In contrast, parents of girls demonstrated somewhat higher accuracy in recognizing normal and a little overweight status. Overestimation rates were comparable between genders across all categories. These findings underscore the need for further research in this area, as they suggest that while misclassification occurred in both groups, parents of boys more frequently underestimated their children’s excessive weight.

Discussion

The authors examined the concordance between parents’ perception and WHO values of BMI among a sample of children aged 6.00–9.99 years in Serbia. Overall, 83.2% of parents, who responded to the survey, accurately perceived their child’s healthy weight in category of normal weighted children, which was lower than the percentage in a representative sample in Europe (86.5%) [38], indicating that Serbian parents should improve in identifying the weight of their child. Specifically, only 27.7% of the parents recognized the overweight and 8.8% the obese status of their child. Although this value is lower than the estimated percentages from one meta-analyses (37.6%) (Rietmeijer-Mentnik et al., 2013), it is still higher than the European results (23,6%) [38]. Moreover, American findings revealed a significantly higher percentage of overweight and obese children recognized by parents, caregivers, and healthcare professionals, with 88% and 32% of children identified, respectively [48].

Parental inability to recognize their children’s excess weight has been pointed as a factor contributing to the increasing rates of childhood obesity [20]. Moreover, parental underestimation may influence overweight children’s self-assessment of their weight, as well as their desire to lose weight and improve their lifestyle [16]. The proportion of parents (11.8%) who underestimated the healthy weight status of their child is lower than the percentages in a meta-analysis (14%) [27] and 12.2% in Europe [38], yet it also warrants attention, because this may lead parents to encourage their child’s consumption of extra food to facilitate weight gain, thereby elevating the child’s risk of future obesity [16].

These findings, which align with previous research, are significant as they indicate that parental misperceptions of children’s weight status vary by gender. Consistent with our results, several studies have found that parents tend to underestimate excess weight in boys more than in girls [7, 28, 38, 42, 43, 52]. This trend may be a reflection of culturally reinforced beliefs that boys are “naturally bigger” or that a larger body size in boys is less concerning than in girls.

Conversely, as shown in our sample, parents of girls demonstrated slightly higher accuracy in recognizing normal and overweight status, a tendency also observed in previous research [37, 60]. These authors found that parents were more inclined to perceive daughters as overweight even when their weight fell within the normal range, whereas sons’ excess weight was more often overlooked.

Moreover, it underscores the significant role of gender in parental perceptions of child weight. The systematic bias towards under-recognition of overweight among boys highlights the urgent need to tailor public health communication to challenge normative beliefs about body size across genders, thereby promoting more accurate parental perceptions and healthier outcomes for children.

Parents serve as crucial role models in shaping children’s health-related behaviors. However, evidence suggests that most parents do not model healthy lifestyle habits [6], underscoring the need for family-based interventions.

Additionally, previous studies have shown that parents often misjudge their children’s abilities in various domains, indicating a general tendency toward biased parental perception [14, 32].

As found in other studies [33, 35, 38, 42, 49, 54], parental perception of their children’s overweight was poor and only a small number of parents were aware of their children’s actual weight status. Overweight children were misjudged more often, compared to the normal weight children. It is an imperative if parents are engaged in health promotion programs. Engaging parents in their adolescents’ empowerment-inspired PA intervention had multiple components: positive effect on not only their adolescents’, but also their own physical activity [24]. An educational and culturally intervention, could encompass various components such as promoting school sports week, or offering health-focused classroom lessons on making positive lifestyle choices, including the importance of physical activity, balanced nutrition, hydration, and sleep. The intervention might also include strategies to encourage active breaks, extracurricular physical activities, and the use of active transportation. Additionally, it could involve replacing unhealthy vending machine options, offering cooking classes, initiating school gardening projects, and utilizing modern tools like fitness apps, pedometers, posters, and digital platforms. All of these efforts would be reinforced through peer involvement, parental engagement, and community support.

Unfortunatelly, a limited number of studies address the role of parents, as well as peers and the media in determining children's body image concerns [41]. Therefore, organizers and educators need to understand the parents’ perspectives for involving it [36]. At the population level, this knowledge can help to identify opportunities for parental engagement to develop childhood obesity prevention programs and policies that are supportive, non-stigmatizing, and effective [38].

Strengths

The improved sampling design, which resulted in a well-balanced sample stratified by region and district (corresponding to NUTS 2 and 3) and level of urbanization, contributed significantly to the study's success. For the first time, a voluntary family record form was applied, ensuring better data collection. Enhanced data protection measures were implemented across all phases of the study. Additionally, there was strong collaboration with school administrators, and field examiners, with experience from the first national COSI round, demonstrated high efficiency and precision in data collection and storage.

Limitations

First, relevance of the current study would have been grater if the information on the prevalence and associated factors had been examined. Second, the weighting was not applied in this study, as the dataset used was derived from a nationally representative sample collected as part of the WHO European COSI. Future studies should apply it to maximize the representativeness and applicability of the findings. Third, results may be related to the wording of the question and variables on the record form. For example, “A little overweight” and “Extremely overweight” may have led some parents to misperceive their child’s weight status due to subjective interpretations and internalized weight bias. Furthermore, parents with overweight children may not be as willing to participate in the study, as overweight and obesity prevalence was lower in this setting compared with other national data [18]. This may have confounded the results. Finally, most of the questionnaires were completed by mothers (89.7%), which is a common observation in studies involving parental reporting. Accordingly, achieving a balanced gender distribution would probably provide more comprehensive insights into results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the study underscores a concerning gap in parental perception of their children's weight status in Serbia. While a significant majority accurately identified their child's healthy weight, a notable proportion failed to recognize overweight or obese status. This suggests a need for targeted interventions to enhance parental awareness and understanding of weight-related health risks in children, aiming to improve overall health outcomes in this age group. Public health interventions aimed at promoting healthy childhood growth and development should consider parents’ knowledge and perceptions, as well as the sociocultural contexts in which children and families live. Continued awareness and implications of childhood obesity also could help to address some of the psychological factors that may contribute to what stakeholders, public health professionals, physicians, and nutrition experts alike have called the fastest growing and most preventable cause of disease and death facing many nations today.
