Serum Interferon-γ Is Associated With Refractory Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children
Authors: Hye Young Han, Jeong-min Oh, Jung-Woo Rhim, Mea-young Chang, Eun-Ae Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) infection is associated with refractory severe clinical course, and steroid therapy has been suggested as an effective treatment for refractory MP pneumonia.MethodsA total of 53 patients with MP pneumonia and 9 patients with viral pneumonia patients were enrolled. Point mutations at residues 2063, 2064, and 2067 in domain V of 23S rRNA gene were analyzed, and serum levels of interferon (IFN)-γ, interleukin (IL)-6, TNF-α, IL-10, and IL-1β were measured before and after steroid treatment.ResultsThere was no significant difference in serum cytokine concentrations between the MP pneumonia group and the viral pneumonia group. The A2063G point mutation was identified in 72% (18/25) of tested MP pneumonia patients. IFN-γ and IL-6 levels showed significant positive correlations with the duration of hospitalization, fever after admission and duration of steroid treatment. The macrolide-resistant MP (MRMP) group tended to have higher IFN-γ levels than the macrolide-sensitive MP group (P = 0.055). Notably, all patients (n = 9) with the highest IFN-γ levels (ranked in descending order) belonged to the MRMP group. Steroid treatment significantly reduced IFN-γ and IL-6 levels, suggesting effective modulation of excessive immune responses. LDH and AST levels were also correlated with IFN-γ and IL-6.ConclusionTherefore, IFN-γ may serve as a valuable biomarker for predicting a refractory clinical course of MP pneumonia. In addition, LDH and AST levels, which showed the closest correlation to IFN-γ and IL-6, may also be proposed as indicators of disease severity.Keywords: Interferon-γ, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Pneumonia, Child

INTRODUCTION

Mycoplasma pneumoniae was first isolated from the sputum of patients with pneumonia by Eaton et al. in 1944.1 M. pneumoniae is a small prokaryotic organism with minimal cellular organelles essential for growth and replication in cells and is not detected using gram staining because it has no cell wall. M. pneumoniae is the most common cause of atypical pneumonia in community-acquired pneumonia.2 M. pneumoniae pneumonia epidemic has been reported every 3–4 years since 1987 in South Korea.3,4,5 Although M. pneumoniae pneumonia usually resolves with minimal treatment, some patients develop progressive severe pneumonia that is unresponsive to antibiotics. Antibiotics, such as macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines, are used for the treatment of M. pneumoniae pneumonia,6,7,8 but macrolides are mainly recommended in children because of their safety. In recent years, the incidence of M. pneumoniae pneumonia has increased in Japan, China, and South Korea, suggesting the presence of drug-resistant strains.9,10,11 Macrolide antibiotics inhibit protein synthesis by binding to domain II or V of the 23S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) of the 50S ribosomal subunit of M. pneumoniae. Genetic mutations in domain V of the 23S rRNA of M. pneumoniae have been reported to cause macrolide resistance by interfering with macrolide binding.12 Substitution of G for A at nucleotide 2063 (A2063G) of domain V or G for A at nucleotide 2064 interferes with macrolide binding.9,13,14 Lee et al.15 reported that the frequency of no response to macrolide treatment was markedly higher in the presence of genetic mutations that cause macrolide resistance than in the absence of mutations and that the use of doxycycline in macrolide-resistant bacteria resulted in substantially faster improvement of clinical course.

However, the general use of second-line antibiotics, such as tetracyclines and quinolones, in children is limited, and not all patients with refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia are carriers of macrolide-resistant genetic variants.16 For these patients, corticosteroids have been used because they can rapidly improve the clinical course and chest radiographic findings.17,18,19,20 The immunopathogenesis of M. pneumoniae pneumonia is unclear; some studies have reported that hyperimmune reactions to M. pneumoniae infection are associated with a refractory severe clinical course, and that steroid therapy is effective in treating refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia owing to the regulation of the excessive immune response of the host.2,17 Cytokines play important roles in lung defense mechanisms and inflammation, and several cytokines activated by M. pneumoniae infection cause excessive inflammation through cell-mediated immunity.21 However, few studies have explored the mechanisms underlying the efficacy of steroid therapy for macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae (MRMP), or the pathogenic mechanisms underlying MRMP pneumonia.22

Accordingly, we analyzed the relationship between the clinical course, macrolide-resistant genes, and serum cytokines during the 2019–2020 outbreak of M. pneumoniae pneumonia in Korea and sought to explore the differences in pathogenic mechanisms according to MRMP status through cytokine profiling.

METHODS

Patients

This study enrolled 70 patients with community-acquired pneumonia admitted to the Department of Pediatrics at Daejeon St. Mary’s Hospital, Catholic University of Korea, between September 2019 and March 2020, who consented to participate in the study. Among them, 8 patients who were not diagnosed with either M. pneumoniae pneumonia or viral pneumonia were excluded, leaving 53 patients with M. pneumoniae pneumonia and 9 patients with viral pneumonia for analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). All enrolled patients underwent multiplex real-time bacterial polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests from nasopharyngeal or throat swabs or sputum. As part of routine clinical care, laboratory investigations including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), C-reactive protein (CRP), and standard hematological parameters, as well as serological assays for M. pneumoniae antibodies, were performed on the day of admission and repeated within 48–72 hours. Serum samples for cytokine profiling were collected at both time points, and residual specimens remaining after routine laboratory testing were aliquoted and stored at –70°C until analysis.

Follow-up cytokine measurements were available for all the 28 patients. Among the 53 patients with M. pneumoniae pneumonia, 43 underwent two consecutive serological antibody tests within 48–72 hours. Viral PCR testing was performed in patients with suspected viral pneumonia.

Serological testing was performed using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Diesse Diagnostica, Senese, Italy) and an indirect microparticle agglutination assay (Serodia-Myco II; Fujirebio, Tokyo, Japan). Patients were categorized as positive (> 1.1 AU/mL), negative (< 0.9 AU/mL), or equivocal (0.9–1.1 AU/mL) for immunoglobulin M (IgM) of M. pneumoniae or as positive (> 18 AU/mL), negative (< 12 AU/mL), or equivocal (12–18 AU/mL) for IgG of M. pneumoniae. For the indirect microparticle agglutination assay, titers ≥ 1:40 were considered positive, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Multiplex bacterial PCR assays targeting M. pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Legionella pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Bordetella pertussis and multiplex virus PCR assays targeting human influenza virus A/B, human parainfluenza virus 1/2/3/4, human rhinovirus A/B/C, human bocavirus 1/2/3/4, human coronavirus 229E/OC43/NL63, respiratory syncytial virus A/B, enterovirus, human metapneumovirus, and adenovirus were performed using a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with nasopharyngeal aspiration or nasal swabs. PCR products were stored for genetic analysis.

Diagnosis of M. pneumoniae pneumonia was established according to the following criteria: first, patients with positive PCR results for M. pneumoniae were diagnosed with M. pneumoniae pneumonia; second, in cases with negative PCR results, the diagnosis was based on serological confirmation, defined as a significant increase in IgM titers between two serial tests, seroconversion from negative or equivocal to positive using indirect microparticle agglutination assay, or seroconversion of IgM or IgG from negative to positive using ELISA; third, in patients with negative PCR results for whom two serial serological tests could not be obtained during hospitalization, the diagnosis was made according to a high IgM titer (≥ 1:640) using the indirect microparticle agglutination assay and a positive ELISA IgM.17,23 Viral pneumonia was defined as cases that exhibited a clinical course compatible with viral pneumonia, those that did not meet the diagnostic criteria for M. pneumoniae pneumonia, or those with a causative virus identified using respiratory viral PCR. Of the 53 children treated for M. pneumoniae pneumonia, 50 received steroids as adjuvant treatment within 48 h of hospitalization. As initial treatment, oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) or intravascular methylprednisolone (1–2 mg/kg/day) was administered to patients with mild or small segmental pneumonia lesions, whereas a higher dose of methylprednisolone (2–4 mg/kg/day) was administered to patients with more severe segmental/lobar lesions or respiratory distress (e.g., severe wheezing or tachypnea at presentation). Patients with persistent high fever and rapid disease progression 48–72 hours after the initial antibiotic therapy were administered an additional dose of methylprednisolone (10–30 mg/kg/day). Pulmonary infiltration was classified as bronchopneumonia or segmental/lobar pneumonia based on chest radiographic findings by two authors (EAY and HYH). Clinical characteristics and laboratory findings were reviewed from the medical records.

PCR and serum cytokine analysis

All specimens containing M. pneumoniae DNA were stored at −70°C. MRMP mutation testing was performed on residual nucleic acid samples obtained from patients who tested positive for M. pneumoniae using multiplex bacterial PCR. Nucleic acids were extracted from samples positive for M. pneumoniae using bacterial PCR with Ribospin™ VRD plus (GeneAll Biotechnology, Seoul, Korea), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR was performed on the extracted DNA using a DNA polymerase master mix (Elpis Biotech, Daejeon, Korea), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primers targeted residues 1998–2018 (Myco23S-F: 5′-TCTCGGCTATAGACTCGGTGA-3′) and 2673–2692 (Myco23S-R: 5′-TAAGAGGTGTCCTCGCTTCG-3′) of domain V of 23S rRNA. The PCR products were analyzed using submarine-type gel electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel (Mupid-exU; Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). An approximately 700-bp fragment was detected under UV light, recorded using an imaging system, and excised. DNA bands were extracted from the gel using a commercial purification kit and sequencing was performed using BigDye® Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing reagents and an ABI PRISM 3730XL Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). DNA sequences were evaluated for point mutations (A) at residues 2063, 2064, and 2067 in domain V of 23S rRNA using GenBank accession number X68422 as the reference sequence.

Serum was collected at admission and at 48–72 hours and was stored at −70°C until cytokine analysis. All enrolled patients underwent cytokine testing at the time of admission. Among the 53 patients with M. pneumoniae pneumonia, 28 underwent additional cytokine measurements 48–72 hours after treatment. Interferon γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), and interleukin (IL)-6, IL-10, and IL-1β (respectively) were evaluated using an ELISA Kit (BD OptEIA™; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (range) for continuous variables, or as the number of cases (percentage) for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Correlations between two continuous variables were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. To further investigate the potential enrichment of MRMP across increasing cytokine levels, the baseline IFN-γ values were categorized into tertiles using equal-percentile binning. Comparisons of categorical distributions between the macrolide-resistant and macrolide-sensitive M. pneumoniae (MRMP and MSMP, respectively) groups were performed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. To further evaluate potential trends, linear-by-linear association tests were conducted across ordered IFN-γ categories, and exploratory analyses comparing the highest tertile with the others were performed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the discriminative performance of IFN-γ with the optimal cutoff determined using Youden’s index. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a nonparametric method based on the Mann-Whitney U-test. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of The Catholic University of Korea, Daejeon St. Mary’s Hospital (IRB No. DC23EASI0032). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants and/or their legal guardians before participating in the study.

RESULTS

Comparison of M. pneumoniae pneumonia and viral pneumonia groups

A total of 62 patients were enrolled: 53 with M. pneumoniae pneumonia and 9 with viral pneumonia. Most patients (n = 41) were diagnosed on the basis of positive M. pneumoniae PCR results. Among the 12 PCR-negative cases, 7 cases were confirmed by IgM or IgG seroconversion, 4 cases showed an increase in IgM titers in patients with an initial IgM-positive result, and 1 case by a test performed only once at the time of admission, showing high particle agglutination titer (1:640) and IgM positivity (Supplementary Table 1). The median patient age was 6 years, and the male-to-female ratio was 1.3:1. The median duration of fever before admission was 5.0 days, and the median length of hospital stay was 5 days. Patients with viral pneumonia were significantly younger than those with M. pneumoniae pneumonia (2 vs. 6 years, P < 0.001). Although the duration of fever before admission did not differ between the groups, the total fever duration was significantly longer in the M. pneumoniae group (P = 0.007). Chest radiographs revealed segmental/lobar pneumonia in 94.3% of patients with M. pneumoniae pneumonia, whereas all patients with viral pneumonia had bronchopneumonia (P < 0.001). Pleural effusion was present in 3 patients (5.6%) with M. pneumoniae pneumonia. Most laboratory findings were similar, but white blood cell fractions differed significantly (P < 0.001). Aspartate transaminase (AST) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels were significantly higher in the viral pneumonia group than in the M. pneumoniae group (P = 0.004 and P = 0.028, respectively). However, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate and CRP level were higher in the M. pneumoniae group than in the viral pneumonia group (P = 0.001 and P = 0.003, respectively). No significant differences were noted in the serum cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-1β) between the groups (Table 1).

Bacterial PCR confirmed M. pneumoniae in 41 patients (77.4%), and macrolide resistance gene analysis was performed in 25 patients (61%, 25/41). H. influenzae was the most commonly co-detected organism (46.3%, 19/41). Corticosteroids were administered within 48 hours in 50 patients (94.3%). Of these, 36 received low-to-moderate doses (1–4 mg/kg/day), 6 received high doses (10–20 mg/kg/day), and 3 with progressive disease received additional levofloxacin and high-dose methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg/day).

Clinical characteristics and laboratory findings in the MRMP group

Most patients (18/25, 72%) were infected with M. pneumoniae with the A2063G mutation (MRMP), whereas the other 7 (28%) were infected with M. pneumoniae with no macrolide resistance-related mutations (MSMP group). No significant differences were observed in clinical characteristics or laboratory findings between the groups. However, a significant difference was noted in the treatment methods (macrolide only vs. macrolide with adjuvant steroid therapy vs. secondary antibiotics with high-dose steroid therapy) between the two groups (P = 0.047). Two of the three patients who received high doses of methylprednisolone and levofloxacin harbored macrolide resistance mutations. No significant differences in IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-1β levels were observed between the MRMP and MSMP groups in serum collected during hospitalization (Table 2).

The MRMP group tended to have higher IFN-γ values than the MSMP group (P = 0.064). When baseline IFN-γ levels were divided into tertiles (T1, 0.00–14.27 pg/mL; T2, 16.05–41.42 pg/mL; T3, 44.28–332.10 pg/mL), the proportion of MRMP cases increased progressively across tertiles (55.6%, 62.5%, and 100.0% for T1, T2, and T3, respectively), but the overall categorical comparison did not show statistical significance. However, a linear-by-linear association test across tertiles demonstrated a significant trend (P = 0.042). For exploratory purposes, IFN-γ values were further dichotomized into T3 vs. T1+T2, which similarly demonstrated a significant linear-by-linear association (P = 0.040), suggesting that higher IFN-γ levels may be associated with an increased proportion of MRMP (Supplementary Table 2).

In addition, 2 patients (patients 2 and 6) who received both levofloxacin and high-dose methylprednisolone adjuvant therapy had high IFN-γ levels and did not respond to the initial therapy (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the clinical characteristics, macrolide resistance gene mutations, and cytokine responses of children hospitalized for M. pneumoniae pneumonia. Among the 53 patients diagnosed with M. pneumoniae pneumonia, 41 were PCR-positive for M. pneumoniae. Although M. pneumoniae produces hydrogen peroxide, which is toxic to other bacteria, H. influenzae possesses catalase, which neutralizes hydrogen peroxide, thereby explaining its coexistence.24 The A2063G mutation in the 23S rRNA gene was detected in 72% (18/25) of PCR-positive cases, representing the predominant macrolide resistance mutation in this study.

A significant difference was observed in treatment approaches, with macrolide plus steroid therapy being more frequently administered to patients with MRMP than to those with MSMP (P = 0.047). However, no major differences were observed in the clinical presentation or hematological parameters. Although the overall cytokine levels did not differ significantly between patients with MRMP and those with MSMP, IFN-γ levels tended to be higher in those with MRMP and were associated with a longer duration of fever after admission and greater steroid requirements. Follow-up measurements revealed significant reductions in IFN-γ and IL-6 levels after steroid therapy, highlighting the immunomodulatory effects of corticosteroids on M. pneumoniae pneumonia.

IFN-γ and IL-6 levels were positively correlated with the duration of fever after hospitalization, length of hospital stay, and duration of steroid therapy and were significantly associated with LDH and AST levels. These findings suggest that IFN-γ and IL-6 contribute to disease severity, and may serve as potential therapeutic targets. Previous studies have reported inconsistent clinical differences between patients with MRMP and MSMP. Some studies observed longer fever duration, hospitalization periods, and greater use of second-line antibiotics in MRMP than in MSMP, whereas others observed no significant clinical or radiological differences between the two groups.8,9,10,25,26 Our findings are consistent with the latter, likely because of the early use of corticosteroids, which reduced cytokine production and mitigate lung inflammation.27 In addition, prior clinical studies have demonstrated that IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α decreased more significantly than IFN-γ after 24–48 hours of dexamethasone administration in community-acquired pneumonia, suggesting variability in cytokine responses depending on the causative pathogens and host immune responses.28 Although previous studies have primarily evaluated cytokine changes in relation to the clinical severity of refractory M. pneumoniae,29,30 our study simultaneously examined macrolide resistance gene mutations and cytokine profiles. This integrated approach is novel, as few studies have directly assessed the interplay between genetic resistance and host immune responses, rather than relying solely on clinical outcomes.

Although the precise mechanism of lung injury in M. pneumoniae infections remains unclear, excessive host immune responses are thought to play a central role in disease pathogenesis by contributing to local and systemic inflammation. During pneumonia, microbial toxins, pathogen-associated molecular patterns, and damage-associated molecules released from injured lung tissue can activate systemic immunity and cause clinical manifestations such as persistent fever. In this study, elevated IFN-γ levels were particularly associated with MRMP and worse clinical outcomes, suggesting that IFN-γ-driven hyperinflammation may contribute to refractory disease and, in severe cases, cytokine storm-like responses. IFN-γ is critical for host defense as it activates macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells, inhibits M. pneumoniae growth, and facilitates pathogen clearance. However, its excessive production can aggravate tissue injury.31 Experimental data indicated that IFN-γ secretion through enhanced NK cell function exerts protective effects against M. pneumoniae infection, highlighting its dual role as a protective and potentially pathogenic cytokine.32 Altogether, these findings suggest that MRMP may increase the risk of refractory clinical courses by inducing excessive IFN-γ responses. Although not all MRMP cases progress to severe disease, elevated IFN-γ levels may serve as a useful biomarker for predicting MRMP and refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia. In such cases, corticosteroid therapy may mitigate the detrimental immune responses by attenuating hyperinflammation.17,18,33,34

In addition to cytokines, surrogate biomarkers, such as AST, ALT, LDH, CRP, and ferritin, have been proposed for predicting refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia.35,36,37,38,39 In our study, IFN-γ and IL-6 levels correlated strongly with LDH and AST levels, reinforcing their utility as practical alternatives in clinical settings where cytokine assays are not readily available. Our previous work suggested that persistently elevated LDH levels, despite steroid treatment, identified patients at risk of refractory disease.17 Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of IFN-γ and LDH as important biomarkers of MRMP and refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia.

This study had certain limitations. First, selection bias may have existed because of the retrospective design of the study. Second, the absence of a healthy control group limited the availability of reference cytokine values for comparison. Third, the sample size was relatively small because the analyses were performed using leftover specimens from routine clinical evaluations. Finally, the mutation analysis was restricted to the most common variants in domain V of the 23S rRNA gene (A2063, A2064, and A2067), which accounted for the majority of MRMP cases. Therefore, rare or emerging mutations outside of these sites may not have been detected.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel insights into the immunopathogenesis of pediatric M. pneumoniae pneumonia through the concurrent evaluation of macrolide resistance gene mutations and cytokine profiles. Elevated IFN-γ was particularly associated with MRMP and refractory clinical features, whereas IFN-γ and IL-6 levels significantly decreased after steroid therapy, suggesting their potential as therapeutic targets. Moreover, LDH may serve as a surrogate biomarker for routine clinical practice. Future large-scale prospective studies with broader cytokine profiling and more comprehensive genetic testing are warranted to validate these findings and refine the treatment strategies for pediatric M. pneumoniae pneumonia.
