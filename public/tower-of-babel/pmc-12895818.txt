Temperature-based spatiotemporal heterogeneity of hand, foot, and mouth disease transmission in China
Authors: Zitong Zheng, Junyu He, Longhao Wang, Feng Shao, Danjie Zhang, Ruidong Qu, Liang Wen, Yong Wang, Yuanyong Xu, Zhaorui Chang, Wenyi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD) remains an important public health issue in China with numerous studies showing significant association between temperature and the disease. Amid growing concerns of the impact of climate change on disease, we specifically explore the relationship between temperature and HFMD in mainland China to investigate and estimate key epidemiological parameters and potential heterogeneity.MethodsWe developed an integrated modeling approach that combined HFMD incidence data from 68 cities across 28 Chinese provinces and municipalities from 2013 to 2019 and temperature data with the Susceptible–Exposed–Infectious–Recovered (SEIR) transmission model and Iterated Filtering (IF2) data assimilation. The approach estimated key epidemiological parameters and quantified the impact of temperature on HFMD transmission in different regions.ResultsHFMD transmission characteristics varied considerably by regions. The basic reproduction number () declined spatially from the southeastern coast to the northwestern inland areas, with a maximum of 3.11 in South China, and minimum of 1.49 in Northeast China. The optimal temperature range for HFMD transmission was between 13.61 °C and 29.48 °C, with statistically significant regional differences in these ranges.ConclusionThe SEIR-IF2 model accurately fit the observed outbreak patterns and estimated key epidemiological parameters. Furthermore, it identified the optimal temperature range and revealed spatial heterogeneity in HFMD transmission in mainland China, providing a scientific basis for developing targeted prevention and control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12431-y.Keywords: HFMD, Temperature, Transmission dynamics model, Spatial heterogeneity

Introduction

Hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) is a common childhood infectious disease caused by enteric viruses, primarily affecting children under the age of five. Although most cases present with mild symptoms that typically resolve on their own within a few days [1], a significant proportion of patients, particularly those with severe neurological complications, may experience long-term sequelae. For instance, about 19.8% of patients with severe HFMD develop neurological sequelae or death [2]. In the Asia-Pacific region, particularly in China, HFMD has emerged as a significant public health concern [3, 4]. Studies have indicated that the average cost per HFMD case ranges from USD 990 to 3,000, imposing a substantial economic burden on both society and families. Among these costs, direct medical expenses (outpatient care, inpatient treatment, and self-medication) account for a significant proportion, with the share reaching as high as 86% [5]. Therefore, studying the epidemiological characteristics of HFMD in China, especially its spatiotemporal heterogeneity, is of significant public health importance.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the epidemic patterns of HFMD exhibit pronounced regional and seasonal characteristics [6]. Incidence rates are generally higher in southern regions than in northern regions, with the primary outbreak peak typically occurring in late spring to early summer [7]. In some areas, a secondary peak may also appear in autumn, which possibly influenced by various climatic factors [8]. Distributed Lag Nonlinear Models (DLNM) and logistic regression models have been employed to identify the associations between HFMD incidence and meteorological variables such as temperature, humidity, precipitation, and wind speed [9, 10]. Among these variables, temperature was regarded as one of the most influential factors on HFMD transmission [11, 12], and some studies have revealed an inverted V-shaped relationship between temperature and HFMD incidence, with cases rising at the optimum threshold and then falling as temperatures become more extreme [13, 14]. While these statistical methods are effective in capturing complex lag effects and nonlinear associations, they have limited capacity to estimate key epidemiological parameters, such as the basic reproduction number (). Consequently, researchers are increasingly integrating climate data with epidemiological models to more precisely characterize the role of environmental factors in disease transmission dynamics [15]. This type of mechanism-driven model has been applied to the prediction and evaluation of different infectious diseases [16–18]. In the modeling studies of HFMD, previous studies have incorporated temperature as a variable or employed data assimilation techniques like the Ensemble Kalman Filter with disease models, but these approaches have struggled to capture secondary epidemic peaks and often exhibit delayed responses to outbreak fluctuations [19, 20]. Therefore, overcoming the limitations of existing methods is crucial for a deeper understanding of the transmission mechanisms of HFMD and for improving the accuracy of predictions.

In contrast, the Iterated Filtering 2 (IF2) algorithm is a parameter estimation method specifically designed for nonlinear state-space models [21]. It not only exhibits enhanced robustness and adaptability in the presence of parameter uncertainty and high-frequency data fluctuations, but also provides a plug-and-play likelihood-based framework that does not require closed-form transition densities, making it particularly suitable for partially observed, non-Gaussian epidemic models [22]. Comprehensive methodological reviews have highlighted these advantages and compared IF2 favorably against alternatives such as Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC), Particle Markov Chain Monte Carlo (PMCMC), and Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF) [22]. Furthermore, this method has been widely applied [22–25]. Accordingly, this study employed surveillance data on HFMD from 68 Chinese cities between 2013 and 2019 to develop a temperature-based SEIR–IF2 modeling framework, systematically identify key region-specific epidemiological parameters (e.g., the basic reproduction number and latent period), and quantify the impact of temperature on transmission dynamics and the spatial heterogeneity of HFMD transmission. The findings provide scientific evidence for the role of temperature in infectious disease epidemics and lay a foundation for the development of more targeted public health control strategies.

Methods

Incidence data

HFMD case data were obtained from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Demographic data including birth rate, death rate, and total population were sourced from the National Bureau of Statistics of China and statistical yearbooks of various prefecture-level cities. Meteorological data for the same period were obtained from the China Meteorological Data Sharing Service System and the meteorological bureaus of various prefecture-level cities. Missing values in the meteorological data were imputed using polynomial interpolation.

To comprehensively investigate the spatial heterogeneity of HFMD transmission and the impact of climatic factors, we selected 68 cities from 28 provinces and municipalities in China. These cities are representative of the 28 provincial-level administrative regions and broadly cover mainland China. Moreover, the selected cities have relatively well-developed medical institutions and stronger economies, which ensures higher data accuracy and reliability. The dataset includes incidence rates and case numbers from 2013 to 2019. The 68 cities were geographically classified into seven regions [26]: Northeast China, Northwest China, North China, Central China, East China, South China, and Southwest China. This regional categorization enables more detailed analysis of spatial variations and supports the applicability and scientific rigor of the study in different geographic areas.

SEIR–IF2 system

The SEIR–IF2 system, which integrates the SEIR model with IF2 [21], was used to estimate unobserved state variables and parameters during the period 2013–2019. IF2, a data assimilation technique, employs an iterated, perturbed Bayes map to refine these estimates based on observed data. Unlike the Ensemble Kalman Filter [20], IF2 does not depend on linear approximations or Gaussian assumptions, making it more suitable for highly nonlinear systems. By incorporating parameter perturbations and an iterative mechanism, IF2 effectively mitigates the particle degeneracy issue that Particle Filtering (PF) [16] commonly faces when estimating state variables and parameters simultaneously. In IF2, small independent random perturbations are applied to parameter particles on an internal scale at each iteration and observation time. For each parameter and particle, the perturbation is Gaussian with its mean equal to the particle’s current internal-scale value and its standard deviation given by the product of a parameter-specific step size and the iteration’s cooling factor. The perturbation magnitude follows a cooling schedule—larger initially to encourage exploration and gradually reduced to achieve convergence. When the effective sample size falls to roughly half of the total particles, resampling is performed.

Each iteration of IF2 involves parameter perturbation and a PF to assimilate HFMD incidence time series. The unobserved state variables and parameters are estimated by maximizing the likelihood function of the model with respect to the observed data. The resulting parameter estimates from one iteration serve as the initial conditions for the next. This iterative process aims to converge on state variables and parameters that maximize the likelihood of reproducing the entire incidence time series. In this study, the SEIR–IF2 system was run for 50 IF2 iterations.

To account for the inherent stochasticity in the model and the variability in disease transmission, we conducted twenty independent simulations for each city. In each simulation, slight perturbations were introduced to key model parameters based on their estimated uncertainty ranges. This approach captures the random variations in disease spread and allows us to quantify the potential impacts of parameter uncertainty on the model’s predictions. The results from these independent simulations were then aggregated using Rubin’s rule [35], which provides a robust method for combining estimates while appropriately accounting for variability across runs. This ensures that the final model estimates are not overly reliant on a single deterministic outcome but rather reflect the full range of possible scenarios, offering a more reliable and comprehensive understanding of the uncertainty in key epidemiological parameters and disease dynamics.

After obtaining the fitted incidence and key parameter estimates for each city, we further aggregated them to the regional level. Specifically, the fitted incidence numbers were summed across all cities within each region to represent the regional disease burden. For key epidemiological parameters, we first pooled all city-level estimates within each region to form a regional sample and then assessed its distributional characteristics, using the median and interquartile range (IQR) to summarize parameters with non-normal distributions, while reporting the mean values when the normality assumption was satisfied.

Initialization of the SEIR-IF2 system

The SEIR-IF2 system was initialized with 10,000 particles, collectively representing the initial state variables and parameters()used in the particle filtering approximation. Among the state variables and parameters, the prior ranges for the susceptible population (), reporting rate (), and mixing exponent () were estimated using a time-series Susceptible–Infectious–Recovered (TSIR) model and the R package tsiR (version 0.4.3), based on incidence, birth rate, and total population data [36]. Due to the inevitable under-reporting in surveillance systems, the reporting rate () in our model serves to bridge the gap between the latent true incidence and the cases recorded by the surveillance system. It is important to note that the reporting rate operates only at the observation level, characterizing the mapping between observed cases and latent cases, without altering the intrinsic transmission dynamics of the SEIR process.

This approach provides a more informed alternative to random sampling from broad parameter spaces for setting initial conditions. The prior ranges for the remaining model state variables and parameters were derived from estimates reported in the literature [30].

Model performance evaluation

To evaluate the model’s performance, four metrics were employed: (1) mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and observed incidence; (2) relative root mean square error (RRMSE) between predicted and observed incidence rates; (3) coefficient of determination () between predicted and observed incidence counts; and (4) coverage rate, defined as the proportion of observed values falling within the 95% prediction interval.

Statistical tests

To examine whether there were regional differences in the epidemiological characteristics of HFMD, and given that the data did not satisfy the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, we employed the Kruskal–Wallis H test [37]. This non-parametric method is used to evaluate whether the median distributions of three or more groups differ significantly. A statistically significant H statistic indicates that at least one group differs from the others.

All procedures were conducted using R (version 4.4.0).

Results

Incidence of HFMD

From 2013 to 2019, the average annual incidence rate of HFMD in China was 158.78 cases per 100,000 population. In most cities in South China, East China, Central China, and Southwest China, the reported incidence rates were higher than the national average, with some cities exceeding 300 cases per 100,000 population annually (Fig. 1). In contrast, most cities in North China, Northeast China, and Northwest China reported lower incidence rates, with cities in Northeast China reporting fewer than 50 cases per 100,000 population annually. Overall, the reported incidence was higher in southern regions than in northern ones.

Epidemic dynamics fitting, forecasting, and model parameter estimation

We applied the SEIR-IF2 model to HFMD incidence data reported by 68 prefecture-level cities in China from 2013 to 2019 to infer the transmission dynamics of the disease. The estimated incidence closely matched the observed data (Fig. 2), accurately capturing both unimodal and bimodal outbreak patterns in different regions. Based on these findings, we assessed the model’s performance during the inference period. The evaluation metrics yielded the following results: MAE was 75.83, RRMSE was 0.47, R² was 0.89, and CR was 93.31%. These findings indicated that the model demonstrated high fitting accuracy and robust stability during the inference period.

Estimating the impact of temperature on HFMD transmission

To investigate the temperature conditions most conducive to HFMD transmission, we analyzed the distribution of temperatures corresponding to model-estimated values above and below the annual average (Fig. 4, Figure S2 and Figure S3). By calculating the 95% confidence interval of temperatures associated with values above the mean, we identified a temperature range favorable for HFMD transmission. Overall, this optimal range was 13.61 to 29.48 °C. The regional 95% confidence intervals were as follows: Northeast (15.41–26.03 °C), North China (15.44–26.99 °C), East China (16.49–29.48 °C), South China (20.97–29.43 °C), Central China (16.32–29.41 °C), Northwest (11.76–28.15 °C), and Southwest (16.04–28.98 °C). In addition, we further examined the seasonal variations in optimal temperature ranges across different regions (Table 2). Based on these estimates, the Kruskal–Wallis H test revealed significant differences in temperature distribution among different regions (Table 1). Further comparisons indicated that South China had significantly higher optimal temperatures than all other regions. East and Central China shared similar temperature profiles, with no significant difference between them, but both were significantly warmer than the Northeast, North China, Northwest, and Southwest regions. No significant differences were observed among these latter four regions.

Discussion

Given its high transmissibility and substantial disease burden, HFMD has emerged as a critical global public health challenge [1]. This study develops an infectious disease dynamics model that integrates temperature data and data assimilation techniques to analyze the incidence of HFMD in 68 prefecture-level cities across seven major regions of mainland China. Specifically, the model introduces a quasi-quadratic function to couple temperature with . increases with temperature, reaching a peak at the optimal temperature, and then declines as the temperature continues to rise. When the temperature falls below the minimum effective threshold or exceeds the maximum effective threshold, remains at a low level, indicating that the virus’s transmission potential is constrained under extreme temperature conditions. This approach not only more accurately captures the epidemic trends in different cities, successfully predicts the secondary outbreak peaks, but also significantly reduces prediction errors [20].

Model inferences revealed substantial regional differences in the optimal temperature for HFMD transmission in mainland China, showing strong spatial consistency with . Generally, southern regions (e.g., South China, Central China, East China) exhibited higher values and correspondingly higher optimal transmission temperatures. In contrast, northern regions (e.g., Northeast China, North China, Northwest China) displayed lower values and lower optimal temperatures. These disparities explained the seasonal variation in HFMD transmission patterns between northern and southern China. In northern regions, where temperatures remain relatively low throughout the year, only the summer months fall within the suitable temperature range for transmission, resulting in a typical single-peak epidemic pattern. In southern regions, however, suitable temperatures are reached as early as late spring or early summer, triggering an initial epidemic peak. During midsummer, temperatures in some areas may exceed the optimal transmission range, leading to a decline in and reduced transmission potential. As temperatures decrease again in the autumn, re-entering the favorable range, a second epidemic peak may occur—producing a characteristic double-peak transmission pattern [38, 39]. Meanwhile, the mixing exponent is generally higher in southern regions, reflecting more frequent interpersonal contact and greater social interaction, which further increases the viral transmission potential [40]. This behavioral factor also contributes to the overall higher incidence of HFMD in southern compared to northern China. Moreover, we found that regional differences closely correspond to the distribution of climate zones, further reinforcing the validity of the observed spatial heterogeneity [41]. Previous studies have indicated systematic differences in the sensitivity of HFMD to meteorological factors in different climate zones, with climate zone classification explaining over 15% of the spatial heterogeneity [42]. The influence of meteorological factors is more pronounced in warm-temperate and subtropical regions [43]. These findings collectively support the significant role of climatic background in shaping the optimal temperature range for HFMD transmission in different regions. It is noteworthy that despite its low latitude, the optimal temperature range for HFMD transmission in Southwest China is similar to that of some northern regions. This phenomenon may be closely related to the complex topography and higher altitudes of Southwest China. These factors contribute to a lower optimal temperature for HFMD transmission, a climatic characteristic consistent with the findings of this study [11].

In addition, we observed that in certain cities was significantly higher than in neighboring areas, particularly in regions such as the Sichuan Basin. This suggests that, beyond climatic factors, the intensity of population mobility, level of socioeconomic development, and the pace of urbanization substantially influence the transmission and prevalence of HFMD. Highly urbanized areas, economically developed cities typically present greater transmission opportunities under favorable climatic conditions [44–47], explaining the observed regional variations in the amplitude of school term-time forcing and the mixing exponent.

Beyond reproducing historical epidemic patterns, our temperature-driven SEIR–IF2 framework also holds potential for predictive applications. By incorporating temperature data, the model can be used to forecast future HFMD epidemic trends, intensity, and regional variation. Although such applications are beyond the scope of the present study, they highlight the broader value of our model for both retrospective assessment and forward-looking public health planning.

Our study has several limitations. For simplification, our SEIR model does not account for age-structured social mixing patterns. In addition, this study considered only temperature as the sole climatic factor. Future work will incorporate additional climatic variables such as humidity and vaccination coverage into the model, and may also explore socioeconomic factors to better capture the multifaceted drivers of HFMD transmission. Moreover, to ensure data quality, we selected 68 cities with relatively strong economic foundations and well-developed healthcare resources, with 2–3 cities chosen from each provincial-level administrative region. While this approach improved the accuracy and completeness of case reporting and guaranteed nationwide coverage, it may also introduce limitations. Specifically, cities not included may experience more pronounced under-reporting, and differences in the number of provinces across regions resulted in unequal numbers of selected cities, which could affect regional representativeness. Nevertheless, we believe this impact is limited, as the inclusion of cities from every province ensured broad geographic coverage, and prioritizing cities with higher-quality surveillance systems reduced reporting errors and enhanced the reliability of the estimates.

Conclusions

In summary, this study elucidates the regional spatiotemporal heterogeneity of HFMD transmission, providing a scientific basis for the formulation of targeted intervention strategies by local governments. Moreover, the quantitative assessment of HFMD’s temperature sensitivity provides theoretical support for incorporating real-time temperature data into warning mechanisms. Future studies could explore the association between temperature and HFMD under climate change, which is particularly relevant for regions where climate effects are pronounced. Ultimately, the model outputs offer quantifiable decision-making support for public health practice, enabling more precise allocation of control resources and contributing to the anticipatory management of HFMD and other climate-sensitive infectious diseases.
