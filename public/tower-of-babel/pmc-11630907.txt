The central role of multimodality cardiac imaging in the assessment and management of cardiac masses
Authors: Ahsan A Khan, Syed Rizwan Ali, Timothy C Tan, Jamal Nasir Khan, Amardeep Ghosh Dastidar
Published: 2024

This editorial refers to ‘Cardiac angiosarcoma – How multimodality imaging and excimer laser assisted intracardiac biopsy led to the correct diagnosis – a case report’, by A. Veliqi et al. https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjcr/ytae433.

Cardiac masses comprise a range of primary and secondary cardiac tumours and tumour-like structures such as thrombi, cysts, and caseating calcification. They demonstrate wide heterogeneity in their appearance, sequelae, prognosis, and management. They can portend significant morbidity and mortality due to haemodynamic, arrhythmic, and embolic potential. Accurate, detailed, and timely mass characterization is critically important in their diagnosis, management, and surveillance. Invasive tissue biopsy can be technically challenging and inherently risks. Contemporary multiparametric, multimodality cardiac imaging provides safe, robust, accurate cardiac mass characterization and carries a central role in their diagnosis, management, and treatment surveillance.

Multimodality cardiac imaging in the assessment of malignant cardiac masses

Veliqi and colleagues presented a case where of multimodality cardiac imaging facilitated timely diagnosis of an invasive, primary cardiac malignancy, which was confirmed as a cardiac angiosarcoma when combined with the novel use of an excimer laser sheath in providing intracardiac biopsy. The malignancy was initially incidentally demonstrated on CT pulmonary angiography as a hypoattenuating right atrial mass with associated pleuropericardial effusion in a 54-year-old female presenting to her local emergency department with dyspnoea, chest pain, and palpitations. The patient underwent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrating right atrial wall invasion but no tricuspid valve mechanical effects. Subsequent multiparametric contrast-enhanced cardiovascular MRI (CMR) was consistent with a malignant right atrial mass, demonstrating an invasive, hypointense, hypoperfused, heterogeneously hyperenhancing tumour with no obstructive effects on caval inflow or the tricuspid valve. Diagnostic work-up was completed by transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE)-guided transvenous excimer laser biopsy for histopathological confirmation. The mass was deemed inoperable due to its large size and associated pericardial infiltration, and the patient was palliated with radiotherapy and chemotherapy with TTE-based treatment response surveillance.

The case demonstrated the central role of multimodality cardiac imaging in the diagnosis and management of cardiac masses. The different cardiac imaging modalities provided complementary anatomical, functional, and morphological information, facilitating comprehensive evaluation of size, location, morphology, and local mechanical and invasive effects of the mass. Echocardiography

Echocardiography is often the initial imaging modality due to widespread availability, real-time imaging capabilities, and lack of radiation. It provides information on location, size, mobility, and haemodynamic effects of cardiac masses.5 Due to its excellent temporal resolution, it can detect highly mobile and small masses (<1 cm) or masses attached to valves, and thus can be useful in identifying cardiac masses at an early stage. Transoesophageal echocardiography offers superior spatial resolution and signal-to-noise compared with TTE due to elimination of lung-related artefact and very close cardiac proximity of the oesophagus. Transoesophageal echocardiography is a uniquely valuable modality for real-time imaging of invasive cardiac procedures, as demonstrated by its guidance of excimer laser biopsy sheath cardiac mass biopsy by Veliqi and colleagues.6

Three-dimensional echocardiography (3D echocardiography) provides incremental value for more accurate assessment of cardiac masses. 3D echo overcomes limitations of geometric assumptions and limited projections (cuts) of 2D echocardiography, as it is based on the acquisition of a 3D volume dataset which is post-processed as volume-rendered 3D images or multiplanar reconstructions to allow cropping, reorientation, and visualization from unlimited projections, facilitating powerful anatomical reconstruction of masses and their size, shape, morphology, and relationships with adjacent cardiac structures.4,7 3D echocardiography also permits production of instantaneous, high-resolution 3D volume-rendered images and powerful real-time guidance of intracardiac procedures (e.g. mass biopsy and visualization).

Contrast echocardiography is increasingly used to evaluate cardiac masses and can improve detection sensitivity in patients with poor acoustic windows.4 It is an emerging approach for assessing the relative perfusion and vascularity of a cardiac mass as shown in the case by Veliqi et al.4 These properties can differ between masses and aid their characterization (e.g. thombi are avascular and non-perfusing, angiosarcomas are poorly-perfusing due to limited vascularity, and haemangiomas are highly vascular and avidly perfusing).8–10

Cardiac CT

Unparalleled spatial resolution (0.5 mm isotropic voxels), visualization of calcification, 3D volume acquisition, and variation in post-contrast CT attenuation and resultant signal intensity (brightness) dependent on tissue composition make multidetector cardiac CT a powerful yet relatively underutilized modality in assessing cardiac masses. Thrombus is typically very low attenuating (∼45 Hounsfield units, ratio of thrombus to ascending aorta attenuation of ≤0.25)11 whereas cardiac myxomas are moderately low attenuating (∼75 Hounsfield units)12 and cardiac tumours are generally higher attenuating (40–100 Hounsfield units).2

Modern CT scanners also allow acquisition of the full cardiac cycle and production of moving images akin to echocardiography and CMR with relatively low radiation and excellent spatial resolution, but relatively lower temporal resolution (CT: 70–140 ms 7–14 frames per second (Hertz), CMR: 15–40 Hertz, echocardiography: up to 90 Hertz). Cardiac CT thus provides valuable information about size, anatomy, location, calcification, morphology, and extracardiac spread of cardiac masses. It also facilitates coronary artery assessment (valuable where surgical excision is considered and allows assessment for coronary artery involvement of vascular masses) and visualization of the lungs and wider thoracic and upper abdominal extracardiac anatomy within the same examination.

Cardiovascular MRI

CMR is the gold-standard imaging modality for cardiac mass assessment. It provides excellent soft-tissue contrast, high spatial and temporal resolution, multiplanar imaging, and unparalleled tissue characterization through multiparametric pre- and post-contrast imaging.13–17 Signal intensity of the mass compared with myocardium (hypointense, isointense, or hyperintense), degree of perfusion (first-pass perfusion, early gadolinium enhancement), and degree and pattern of late gadolinium uptake and hyperenhancement allow robust distinction between the wide range of cardiac masses. Sequences include steady-state free precession cine imaging (SSFP) to assess anatomy, motion, intracardiac and pericardial invasion, and mechanical effects of the mass; T1-weighted imaging (spin echo, mapping) to assess lipid, protein, and fibrotic content; T2-weighted imaging (spin echo, mapping) to assess water content; T2*-weighed imaging to assess iron content; first-pass perfusion to assess vascularity (thrombi and cysts do not perfuse, tumours have varying vascularity and perfusion); and gadolinium update hyperenhancement imaging (early and late).13–17 Typical CMR features of cardiac angiosarcomas are illustrated in Table 2.1 CMR also allows assessment of extracardiac thoracic invasion of masses. Nuclear imaging

Nuclear imaging techniques such as positron emission tomography (PET), most commonly using F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) can assess metabolic activity and cellular proliferation of cardiac masses. Often combined with CT (PET-CT), assessment of anatomy and metabolism can be facilitated.4 These modalities are particularly useful for detecting metastatic cardiac masses and assessing treatment response. The choice of imaging modalities therefore depends on mass factors (location, size, mobility), patient characteristics (body habitus, echocardiographic windows, claustrophobia), and local availability of imaging resources. By using a multimodality cardiac imaging approach, the diagnosis, management, and treatment surveillance of cardiac masses can be optimized and obviate the requirement for invasive cardiac biopsy in the vast majority of cases.

A histopathological diagnosis is required to confirm diagnosis in most cases, commonly in suspected malignant tumours. In the case by Veliqi and colleagues, a conventional bioptome was unable to extract sufficient tissue to provide a histopathological diagnosis, attributed to the inability to breach the fibrous capsule of the tumour. Hence, an excimer laser sheath-assisted biopsy was performed under TOE guidance, exploiting sheath manoeuvrability to direct the bioptome to the tumour, illustrating the added value of combining multimodality cardiac imaging and tissue biopsy in the most challenging cases.
