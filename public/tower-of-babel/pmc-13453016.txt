Bulk and single-cell RNA-seq analyses characterize a DBP-related circadian signature in ischemic stroke
Authors: Wen Song, Yafang Liu, Le Zeng, Dong Cao, Jiachong Han, Jinbei Wen, Zeqi Huang, Liping Miao, Zhaoxia Liao, Chuwen Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCircadian rhythm disruption is associated with ischemic stroke outcomes, yet the roles of circadian genes in stroke pathology remain unclear. This study integrated bulk and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) from patients with stroke and mouse models to characterize circadian gene expression patterns. Using machine learning and consensus clustering, we identified a nine-gene circadian signature that distinguished patients with stroke and stratified them into three subtypes with distinct immune infiltration profiles. Single-cell RNA analyses highlighted microglia as key mediators, with DBP downregulation linked to microglial senescence. Experimental manipulation of DBP in microglial cells and a mouse stroke model demonstrated that DBP restoration reduced senescent phenotypes and infarct volume while improving neurological function. These findings establish circadian gene-based patient stratification and implicate microglial DBP as a modulator of post-stroke injury, offering potential avenues for risk assessment and targeted intervention.Keyword: circadian clock, subtype, DBP, ischemic stroke, machine learning

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke has a rapid onset, a narrow recanalization treatment time window, a high disability rate, mortality rate, and recurrence rate.1 Recently, a clinical cohort study reveals that compared with day-onset ischemic stroke, night-onset stroke worsens 3-month functional outcome and aggravates presenting neurologic severity.2 Therefore, early recognition of stroke, early recanalization treatment, and early recovery of cerebral perfusion can reduce brain tissue damage and improve the prognosis of patients with IS. The understanding of the circadian clock in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke might guide the treatment plan and predict the clinical prognosis.

The circadian rhythm of every human life activity is gradually formed in the process of human evolution. The circadian rhythm of humans is 24.18 h. Under the control of the supraoptic nucleus master clock,3 all physiological functions maintain a 24-h biological rhythm, such as the rhythm of sleep-awakening, the rhythm of 24-h blood pressure, the rhythm of cortisol and melatonin secretion, and the rhythm of blood platelet surface activator.4,5 Diurnal dysfunction can damage the function of multiple organs and then lead to a variety of diseases.6 Recently, predictive models based on circadian genes were reported to have good performance and robustness. For instance, Wang et al. have shown that an 18-circadian-gene model is capable of identifying the prognoses of patients with breast cancer.7 Yue et al. demonstrated that nine circadian genes, involving CSNK1D, FBXL3, PRKAA2, BTRC, DBP, NR1D2, CLOCK, CSNK1E, and RORB, were vital prognostic factors in patients with prostate cancer.8

It is known that several chronic diseases show deterioration of circadian rhythm in their symptoms or disease development. Furthermore, core clock genes regulate the neurovascular unit (NVU)-composing cells after similar pathophysiological steps of ischemia via controlling the cell circadian rhythm.9,10 Particularly, the acute immune response of IS is regulated by hormones, immunocytes, cytokines, and chemokines.11 For instance, the M2-polarized microglia enhances angiogenesis in the ischemic penumbra. The M1 and M2 polarization of microglia are involved in both brain damage and repair.12 In recent years, clinical studies have shown that the occurrence, clinical manifestation, and outcome in the acute stage, the target, and the best time of clinical intervention of stroke are highly related to circadian rhythm.2 Understanding its causes and effects and implementing prevention and intervention of circadian rhythm may improve the level of prevention and treatment of stroke.13 These works indicate that circadian time of onset is a vital factor in considering neuroprotective strategies and reperfusion agents for IS.

In this study, we try to deeply understand the changes and mechanisms of rhythm genes in the process of stroke through the big data analysis of rhythm genes, including bulk and single-cell RNA-seq data analyses, and improve new ideas for better prognosis of stroke.

Result

Integration analysis of two ischemic stroke data and multiple circadian genes were differentially expressed

In order to better investigate the circadian genes in ischemic stroke, we downloaded two published datasets related to ischemic stroke (GSE16561 and GSE58294; the baseline clinical characteristics of the two datasets were shown in Table S3). Interestingly, we could see that most circadian genes were abnormally expressed in these datasets (Figures 1A and 1B), indicating the crucial roles of circadian genes in ischemic stroke. Subsequently, we set out to integrate the two datasets by removing the batch effect. Prior to integration, the samples of the two datasets were distinctly separated. Then, they were integrated and mixed well after correcting the batch effect (Figures 1C and 1D). Then, we used the ssGSEA algorithm to calculate the activity score of circadian genes. Consistently, the circadian clock genes were activated in patients with IS (Figure 1E).

Machine learning algorithms identified a circadian clock gene model for predicting the occurrence of ischemic stroke

For filtering the circadian clock genes, the random forest and support vector machine (SVM)-RFE algorithms were adopted. As shown in Figures 3A and 3B, the top important features were identified by random forest, and the AUC of the predictive model was as high as 0.975 (Figure 3C). Then, we used the SVM-RFE algorithm for feature selection. As a result, we intersected the features identified by random forest and the SVM-RFE algorithm and nine hub feature genes were left, including NFIL3, DBP, SMARCD3, CRY2, NCOA1, NR3C1, RBM4, HDAC3, and PPP1CA (Figure 3D). Then, we estimated the nine-gene model using a calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA) (Figures 3E and 3F). Results show that the nine-gene model performed relatively well. To test the stability of the nine-gene model, we further used the bootstrap algorithm, which could construct and test the model 1000 times. As displayed in Figure 3G, the average AUC remained at a high value, indicating the robustness of the model. Then, the hazard ratio was calculated, and the forest plot was presented for the visualization of the risk potentials among these genes (Figure 3H).

Then, we constructed a nomogram for clinical use (Figure S1A). In the nomogram, results show that PPP1CA, NCOA1, SMARCD3, and NFIL3 might play a much more crucial role than other genes. To investigate the relationship between the circadian gene model and infiltrated immune cells, the CIBERSORFT and MCPcounter pipelines were leveraged to calculate the potential expression levels or proportions of infiltrated immune cells. As shown in Figure S1B, we could see that all nine feature genes were correlated with one or another immune cell whose proportion was calculated by CIBERSORFT. As for the MCPcounter pipeline, results also indicate a similar pattern to CIBERSORFT (Figure S1C). Particularly, we also found that the circadian feature genes were highly correlated with inflammatory genes (Figure S1D), indicating that circadian genes might exert an essential role in triggering the immune system when patients suffer ischemic stroke.

Subtype classification of patients with IS based upon circadian genes

To better understand the roles of circadian genes in ischemic stroke, we sought to perform the consensus strategy to classify the patients with IS only based on patients with IS (Figures 4A and 4B). Of interest, data show that circadian genes could divide patients with IS into three subtypes, prompting us to understand the distinctions among the three clusters of patients with IS (Figure 4C). Thus, the WGCNA pipeline was conducted to figure out the hub genes of the three clusters (Figures 4D–4F). All highly expressed genes (top2000) were stratified into several gene modules. The module-trait relationship analysis shows that module brown was positively and highly correlated with cluster1, module blue was positively and highly correlated with cluster2 and cluster3, and module turquoise was positively and highly correlated with cluster3 (Figure 4G). Then, we utilized the fgsea package to figure out the potential biological functions of the three clusters (Figure 4H). Data show that cluster1 mainly participated in “regulation of peptidase activity” and “negative regulation of proteolysis;” cluster2 mainly took part in “mesenchymal cell apoptotic process” and “regulation of vasculature development;” cluster 3 predominantly played a role in “translational initiation,” “establishment of protein localization to endoplasmic reticulum” and “regulation of DNA damage response signal transduction by p53 class mediator” (Figure 4H).

Characteristics of the three clusters identified by consensus analysis developed upon circadian genes

Subsequently, we aimed to study the characteristics of the three clusters in more depth. Interestingly, the three clusters have significantly different expression patterns of circadian genes (Figure 5A). Moreover, cluster 2 tended to be composed of aged patients (Figure 5B). The IS occurrence probability of each patient was also distinguished among the three clusters (Figure 5C). Then, male patients were more likely to be in cluster 2, while female patients tended to be in cluster3 (Figure 5D). Further, the sample collection time had little difference among these clusters (Figure 5E). As vividly demonstrated in Figure 5F, different circadian genes were highly expressed in different clusters, which might be essential for explaining the different clinical information characteristics and undoubtedly contribute to the subtype classification. Particularly, we also found that the immune infiltration score of several cells was different among the three clusters (Figure 5G). For example, T cells and cytotoxic lymphocytes were highly expressed in cluster 3, while cluster2 was relatively lacking these immune cells. The inflammatory genes were also distinct from each other (Figure 5H). Therefore, the subtype classification based on circadian genes might help to understand the different outcomes of patients with IS.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis (scRNA-seq) reveals the pivotal changes of circadian genes in microglia cells

To further explore the functions of circadian genes, we adopted the single cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data to comprehensively investigate the circadian genes and different cell populations in the middle cerebral artery occlusion mouse model (MCAO mice).14 As a result, we obtained 10 cell types in the sham and MCAO mouse model using the six scRNA-seq samples comprising three sham and three MCAO mice (Figure 6A). Next, we scored the circadian gene signature in each cell, and results show that the circadian gene signature was down-regulated in MCAO mice (Figure 6B). In detail, it was found that microglia cells might have large distinctions between the sham and MCAO group (Figures 6C and 6D). Therefore, microglia cells might be responsible for the expression changes of circadian genes following ischemic stroke.

Subcluster analysis, pseudotime and transcription factor regulatory network analysis of microglia cells between the sham and MCAO group

Given that microglia cells might be capable of affecting the circadian genes, we sought to unravel the mechanism. With subcluster analysis, we re-clustered the microglia cells, and 13 subclusters were gained (Figure S2A). Of interest, data show that cell cluster 0, 1, and 11 tended to exist in the sham group, while cell cluster 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 10 were prone to be in the MCAO group, indicating the different microglia cell states during ischemic stroke (Figure S2A). For the purpose of better observing the changes of circadian genes, we chose cell cluster 0 and cell cluster 6, which typically represented the sham group and MCAO group, respectively, for subsequent analysis. In line with the bulk RNA-seq analysis, the circadian gene signature was down-regulated in cluster 6 in MCAO group (Figures S2C and S2D). Then, the pseudotime analysis was conducted to explore the trajectory changes following ischemic stroke. Vividly, we could observe that microglia cell states gradually changed from the sham group to the MCAO group following pseudotime (Figures S2E–S2G), as well as from cluster 0 to cluster 6 (Figure S2H).

Then, the cellchat analysis was performed to understand the cell-cell communication differences among all cell types, especially circadian-gene-signature high- and low-microglia cells (Figures S3A and S3B). Notably, the circadian-gene-signature low-microglia cells had much higher interactive potential than signature-high microglia cells (Figures S3A–S3C). SCENIC analysis was performed, and the results show that microglia cells in the sham and MCAO groups were regulated by quite different TFs. For instance, Foxp1, Lrf7, Klf4, Ets1 and Fli1 were predominantly activated in sham group while Egr1, Junb, Fos, Elk3 and Jund exerted majorly functions in MCAO group (Figures S3D and S3E). To validate the crucial role of these TFs and circadian genes, we used the bulk RNA-seq again to perform the correlation analysis. As expected, a strong correlation was observed between these TFs and circadian genes (Figure S3F). Taken together, microglia cells might serve as different roles in the development of ischemic stroke.

Dysregulation of circadian rhythm, especially DBP, in microglia cells might cause the occurrence of ischemic stroke

To explore the contribution of a lower circadian gene score in microglia cells from patients with stroke, we further narrowed down the circadian genes to only nine genes, which were leveraged to build a predictive model as mentioned above. At the bulk-seq level, we could see that there are five down-regulated genes while four up-regulated genes in IS patients (Figure 7A). When the circadian gene score was calculated only based on nine genes, we also detected lower score levels in microglia cells in MCAO mice (Figures 7B and 7C). Then, the expression level of nine genes was investigated, and DBP expression might be the largest change (Figures 7D and S4). We further performed differential analyses of the nine genes by repeating the key differential expression in each dataset separately. The nine circadian-related genes showed a concordant direction of change across both cohorts (Figure S5). Results in Figures 7E and 7F indicate that DBP might be related to aging. Roussel et al. also reported that DBP was down-regulated in aging tissues.15

Validation of the crucial role of DBP in the mouse MCAO model

In a mouse model of transient MCAO, TTC staining confirmed the infarct volume in the ischemic brain (Figure 8A). This ischemic injury resulted in a significant reduction in DBP protein expression, as shown by western blotting and immunofluorescence, with DBP loss particularly evident in Iba1-positive microglia (Figures 8B–8F). The numerous Iba-1-labeled cells co-localizing with SA-β-gal activity indicated that reduced DBP expression was accompanied by the accumulation of senescent microglia (Figure 8G). Collectively, these findings suggest that ischemic-reperfusion injury resulted in decreased DBP expression in microglia and triggered microglial senescence.

We next assessed the role of DBP in microglial senescence in vitro. Western blotting and RT-qPCR confirmed efficient DBP knockdown and overexpression (Figures 8H–8K). DBP knockdown induced the senescent phenotype, characterized by an accumulation of γH2AX, elevated levels of p16ˆINK4A and p21, a higher proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells, and upregulated expression of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors (Figures 8L–8O). Conversely, DBP overexpression significantly reversed the aging process in doxorubicin-induced microglial senescence. As shown in Figures 8P–8S, compared to senescent cells, DBP overexpression reduced the accumulation of γH2AX, p16ˆINK4A and p21, decreased the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells, and suppressed the upregulation of SASP factors. Together, these findings indicate that ischemic-reperfusion injury downregulates microglial DBP expression to promote microglial senescence, whereas DBP upregulation mitigates this senescent phenotype, highlighting the protective role of DBP in ischemic-reperfusion brain injury.

Microglia DBP overexpression protects against ischemic brain injury in the mouse MCAO model

To further verify the role of microglial DBP in neurofunctional outcomes after ischemic stroke, we constructed an adeno-associated virus (AAV) targeting microglial DBP and then injected it into the left cortex of mice to induce DBP overexpression in vivo before MCAO surgery (Figure 9A). The transfection efficiency of GFP-tagged AAV showed that GFP signals were predominantly localized to Iba1+ microglia, with minimal colocalization with neurons or astrocytes (Figures 9B and 9C). Additionally, AAV-DBP successfully restored microglial DBP levels in MCAO mice, as demonstrated by immunohistochemical staining and RT-qPCR analysis (Figures 9D and 9E). Specific restoration of microglial DBP significantly decreased cerebral infarct volume in MCAO mice (Figures 9F and 9G). Consistently, AAV-DBP overexpression in MCAO mice exhibited improved neurological functions compared with AAV-con mice after ischemic stroke (Figures 9H and 9I). Furthermore, AAV-DBP administration substantially reduced the proportion of senescent microglia compared to AAV-con administration in MCAO mice (Figure 9J).

Discussion

With the potential for intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhages, ischemic stroke accounts for 87% of strokes.16 Circadian rhythm, also known as the physiological clock, is an endogenous and continuous physiological phenomenon, showing changes in a cycle of about 24 h.4,5 The human body’s response to various pathogens, bacterial endotoxins and exotoxins, and proinflammatory cytokines is controlled by circadian rhythm.17 Specifically, more and more evidence shows that the disruption of the endogenous circadian clock participates in the recovery and angiogenesis of IS.18 Our present work studied the changes in rhythm genes in the occurrence of stroke through bioinformatics analyses, and revealed that the disorder of rhythm genes may be one of the key factors leading to the occurrence and even deterioration of stroke.

To explore the pivotal role of circadian rhythm genes in ischemic stroke, we collected PBMC sequencing data of several patients with stroke and integrated sequencing data using the SVA package.19 Meanwhile, we collected scRNA-seq data and explored the mechanism of the mouse ischemic stroke model (MCAO model). Furthermore, the sub-cluster analysis reveals that circadian genes can divide patients with IS into three subgroups. On the other hand, we have identified nine circadian genes, including NFIL3, DBP, SMARCD3, CRY2, NCOA1, NR3C1, RBM4, HDAC3, and PPP1CA, that can help diagnose ischemic stroke using random forest and support vector machine (SVM), and we also performed internal verification through the Bootstrap algorithm. Finally, we used pseudo-time analysis, cell communication analysis (cellchat), and transcription factor regulation network analysis (SCENIC) to reveal the changing pattern of circadian disorder from normal to stroke and the specific regulation of TFs.

Increasing research is becoming interested in studying the function and treatment possibilities of circadian genes in ischemic stroke.20 For instance, in the cerebral infarction rat model, environmental circadian disruption (A total of 6 weeks, induction using 6-h/week phase advances of the light cycle) can worsen IS.21 In the rodent IS model, Esposito et al. demonstrate that the inactive phase administration of neuroprotective strategies is more effective in rescuing the penumbra zone.22 In 55 patients with IS, Bmal1, the clock gene, was negatively related to the IS score.23 As for the Bmal1−/− mice, Bmal1 can revascularize the IS zone.24 Moreover, SHRSP-specific D-box binding PAR bZIP transcription factor (DBP), a circadian gene highlighted in this study, was also considered a potential therapeutic target for stroke.25 Our GSEA results show that DBP was highly correlated with aging, which was in line with the study from Roussel et al., which shows that DBP was down-regulated in aging tissues.15 Thus, we propose several interconnected mechanisms explaining how DBP of microglia affects stroke outcomes. First, DBP is a core circadian transcription factor that binds to E-box elements in the promoters of multiple senescence-associated genes. Our in vitro and in vivo experiments show that DBP silencing in microglia increases senescence markers (SA-β-gal) and promotes a pro-inflammatory phenotype (elevated IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6). Senescent microglia are known to exacerbate secondary brain injury after ischemia by releasing reactive oxygen species and matrix metalloproteinases, which disrupt the blood-brain barrier and amplify neuronal death. More, circadian genes, including DBP, have been shown to control diurnal variations in microglial synaptic pruning and debris clearance. Impaired DBP function could lead either to excessive phagocytosis of viable neurons or to deficient clearance of apoptotic cells, both of which worsen neurological outcomes. Third, microglial DBP may crosstalk with other NVU cells. Senescent microglia secrete SASP factors that can activate astrocytes and compromise endothelial integrity, thereby extending damage beyond the microglial compartment. Fourth, DBP may directly modulate mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis in microglia, as clock genes have been implicated in mitochondrial quality control. Collectively, these pathways—senescence, inflammation, and microglia-other cells crosstalk—likely act in concert to translate DBP downregulation into worse stroke outcomes. In the present work, we constructed an AAV targeting microglial DBP and then induced DBP overexpression in vivo before MCAO surgery (Figure 9A). Thus, in vivo over-expression of DBP in microglia could reduce brain injury after suffering ischemic stroke.

Circadian rhythm is also an important regulator of the human immune system.26 The circadian rhythm oscillation of the immune medium is consistent with the activity of the immune system, which may enable the host to more effectively predict and deal with threats from harmful microorganisms.27 These oscillations may also help to promote tissue recovery and eliminate potentially harmful cytotoxic substances in the human circulation. The key parameters of the immune system in the blood show a circadian rhythm, including the number of hematopoietic cells, hormone and cytokine levels. Secreted cytokines caused by immune response, involving VEGFs, MMPs, ILs, and Mcp-1, are closely pertinent to angiogenesis during the development of ischemic stroke.28 Moreover, acute cerebral hypoxia in stroke leads to inflammatory cascades, which contribute to the secondary injuries of ischemic penumbra.29 Therefore, we also studied the circadian rhythm and the changes in immune cells in the blood circulation of patients with stroke. Using infiltration analyses, we found that the identified nine genes were closely correlated with immune cells.

The NVU—comprising neurons, astrocytes, microglia, pericytes, endothelial cells, and oligodendrocytes—represents an integrated framework for understanding stroke pathophysiology. Recent reviews have systematically delineated how circadian clocks operate across each of these NVU compartments and regulate key processes such as blood-brain barrier permeability, cerebral blood flow, neuroinflammation, and cell-cell signaling.9,10 In particular, clock genes control diurnal variations in phagocytosis and cytokine release, while astrocytic BMAL1 modulates glutamate homeostasis and inflammatory responses, and endothelial clocks regulate xenobiotic efflux and tight junction integrity.10 Our study identifies DBP as a circadian gene that is consistently downregulated in the peripheral blood of patients with stroke and demonstrates that its silencing promotes microglia senescence and pro-inflammatory activation. These findings align closely with the NVU framework: DBP downregulation may disrupt the coordinated circadian timing between microglia and adjacent NVU cells (e.g., astrocytes and endothelial cells), thereby exacerbating neurovascular dysfunction after ischemia. However, future investigation using cell-type-specific knockout models and multi-cellular co-culture systems is still needed.

Circadian rhythm will be a hot spot for disease intervention and treatment in the future. Although this paper has revealed that circadian genes play an important role in the occurrence and development of stroke, more research is still needed, including targeted therapeutic intervention of the circadian rhythm pathway and pharmacological research of circadian rhythm, so as to further analyze the mechanism and impact of circadian rhythm and implement these research results in clinical practice. The two public datasets (GSE16561 and GSE58294) differ in several baseline clinical characteristics (summarized in Table S3). Because individual-level clinical data were not available for all variables, formal statistical adjustment for these differences was not possible, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Therefore, although the nine-gene signature showed robust performance in internal validation, its clinical utility must be further validated in a prospective, well-matched, multi-center cohort with harmonized baseline data collection. Further microglia-specific DBP knockout mice and single-cell analyses of the peri-infarct region are required to establish causality. Moreover, the diagnostic signature was derived and validated in human blood samples; however, the functional experiments were conducted in murine cells and an acute stroke model. Given the well-known species differences, the translational relevance of the DBP-senescence axis to human stroke requires further investigation using human microglia or patient-derived samples. Future studies should evaluate the nine-gene signature in prospective patient cohorts and examine DBP expression in human postmortem brain tissue or cerebrospinal fluid to bridge the gap between mouse models and human disease.

In short, circadian rhythm disorder can lead to the occurrence of stroke, and the circadian rhythm disorder after stroke will aggravate the adverse outcome after stroke. Only circadian genes could successfully separate patients with IS into different subgroups with distinct characteristics. Circadian rhythm, especially transcription factor DBP, could affect the state of microglia cells, which are responsible for disease development and angiogenesis. Interventions for circadian rhythm disorders may improve stroke prevention and control. Circadian rhythm disorder is becoming a new target for stroke prevention and control.

Limitations of the study

The functional experiments were performed in murine microglial cells and an acute stroke model; species differences limit direct translation to humans, and further verification using human microglia or patient-derived samples is warranted. Moreover, the causal role of DBP in microglial senescence and stroke outcomes needs to be confirmed with microglia-specific knockout models and single-cell analyses of the peri-infarct region. Finally, the bioinformatic analyses are hypothesis-generating and subject to batch effects inherent in public data.

Experimental model and study participant details

Data sources and collections

We collected the peripheral blood of stroke patients for analysis, comprising two peripheral blood datasets, and we also collected datasets of single cell sequencing.14 The first blood samples from dataset GSE16561, which consists of 39 IS patients and 24 controls.30,31,32 For another blood samples used in present work, data were collected from dataset GSE58294, which contains 23 controls and 69 IS patients.33 We downloaded and cleaned the two data and combined the data with removal of batch effect. PCA analysis was conducted to assess the batch effect of the two datasets.

Animals information

We obtained 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (20–25 g) from Charles River (Beijing, China) and housed them under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with food and water ad libitum. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (No. AP20230291).

Method details

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA is a widely used data mining method, especially suitable for studying biological networks based on pairwise correlation between variables.34 At the same time, WGCNA can be used as a feature selection method (for example, as a gene screening approach). WGCNA integrates traditional data exploration technology, and its intuitive network language and analysis framework exceed any standard analysis technology. In this study, WGCNA algorithm is used to obtain modules closely related to the three clusters of IS patients. Soft-thresholding power was chosen to achieve scale-free topology (R2 > 0.85). Module attribution was validated by correlating module eigengenes with stroke status and circadian gene expression.

CIBERSORT analysis

CIBERSORT algorithm requires a “signature matrix” consisting of ordinary RNA sequencing, containing signature genes for each cell type of interest. Once the appropriate knowledge base is created and verified, CIBERSORT can be used to characterize the proportion of cell types in a large number of tissue expression profiles. In this work, CIBERSORT was used to calculate the proportion of cell types in peripheral blood of IS patients and explore the relationship between various cell types and rhythm genes. For this part, we use the IOBR package for CIBERSORT analysis.35

MCPcounter analysis

The MCPcounter method can use transcriptome data to quantify the abundance of 8 immune cells and 2 stromal cells.36 In this work, the ratio of cell types in peripheral blood of stroke patients was calculated by MCPcounter to explore the relationship between various cell types and circadian rhythm genes.

Support vector machine - Recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE)

Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithm is a supervised learning model for analyzing data in classification and regression analysis. SVM-RFE algorithm was used for feature selection. Given a group of training samples, each training sample is marked as belonging to one or the other of the two categories, SVM will create a model that assigns samples to one of the two categories, making it a non-probabilistic binary linear classifier. This machine learning algorithm is used to screen rhythm genes. SVM-RFE algorithm was performed using “e1071” R packages with default parameters (https://CRAN.R-project.org/).

Random forest (RF)

Random forest is an integrated learning method for classification, regression and other tasks. It is operated by constructing a large number of decision trees during training. For classification task, the output of random forest is the class selected by most trees. For regression tasks, return the average value or average prediction of a single tree. In this study, the top 10 genes that are important for judging stroke were obtained through random forest (https://CRAN.R-project.org/).

Correlation analysis

Hub genes tend to play their functions with joint efforts. And pro-inflammatory cytokines contribute to angiogenesis after IS while adverse effects will be developed by excessive pro-inflammation. Therefore, we used the correlation analysis of circadian genes and other signature genes, such as inflammatory genes, inferred TFs and immune cells.

Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Single sample GSEA (ssGSEA) is an extension of gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which calculates the individual enrichment fraction of each pair of samples and gene sets. Each ssGSEA enrichment fraction represents the degree of coordinated up regulation or down regulation of genes in a specific gene set in the sample. In this study, the rhythmic genes collected in reactor are taken as a whole gene set, and the overall score is calculated by ssGSEA algorithm, so as to evaluate the overall change of rhythmic genes during stroke.37 And the fgsea package38 were adopted to better visualize the interested and enriched pathways.

SCENIC (single-cell rEgulatory network inference and clustering)

Single cell RNA-seq data of sham and middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) mouse models were collected from GSE174574 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).14 The SCENIC pipeline could be applied for exploring the transcriptional factors (TFs) of specified cell types.39 In present work, we utilized it to calculate and infer the TFs changes of microglia cells. Only two cell clusters we interested were extracted and put into running the pipeline. We used default parameters but performed an additional AUCell threshold sensitivity test; results were robust to threshold variations.

Cell-cell inter- and inner-communication analysis

To better understand the different and communication strength of various celltypes in IS mice, we also performed the cellchat analysis.40 Using the cellchat package, the communication differences between score-high and -low microglia cells was specifically explored. Ligand-receptor pairs were filtered to those with significant expression in at least 10% of cells in a given cluster; permutation test (n = 1,000) was used for significance.

Trajectory analysis of microglia cells

Cell states often change when normal cells suffer from disease attack. Therefore, the investigation of the dynamic roadmap of interested celltype using healthy controls and experimental groups might be a good approach. In present study, the Monocle2 package was used to reveal the dynamic changes of microglia cells.41

Middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model

Transient focal cerebral ischemia was induced via an intraluminal filament MCAO model, as described previously.42,43 We anesthetized mice with 3% isoflurane (1.5–2% for maintenance) and made a midline neck incision to expose the carotid arteries. We ligated the external carotid artery and introduced a silicone-coated nylon filament (RWD Life Science) through it into the internal carotid artery (∼10 mm) to occlude the MCA origin. After 60 min of occlusion, we withdrew the filament to allow reperfusion. Sham-operated mice underwent the same procedure without filament insertion. Surgeries were performed under a stereomicroscope, and body temperature was maintained at 37°C throughout.

Stereotaxic intracranial injection of adeno-associated virus (AAVs)

An adeno-associated viral vector for DBP overexpression (AAV2/9-Iba1-GFP-DBP-WPRE-polyA, 5✕1012 viral genomes/μL) and negative control vector (AAV2/9-Iba1-GFP-WPRE-polyA, 5✕1012 viral genomes/μL) were generated by Shanghai Genechem (China). And stereotaxic intracranial injection of AAVs was performed as previously described.44,45 Briefly, mice were anesthetized by isoflurane and placed in the stereotactic frame (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). Then, a volume of 1 μL AAV was injected into the ipsilateral cortical ischemic region 21 days before middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO). The target coordinates were 0.5 mm anterior to the bregma, 2.0 mm lateral (left) to the sagittal suture, and 1.0 mm below the skull surface. Using a micro-syringe pump (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China), the injection rate was set at 50 nL/min and remained for 15 min after injection.

TTC staining

At 24 h after MCAO, we euthanized the mice and harvested their brains. We sliced each brain into 2 mm-thick coronal sections and incubated them in 2% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) at 37°C for 30 min (protected from light), then fixed the slices in 4% paraformaldehyde. TTC stains viable tissue red, whereas infarcted tissue remains white. We measured infarct areas using ImageJ (NIH) and calculated infarct volumes by summing area × thickness across all slices.

Neurological behavior tests (mNSS test and Rotarod test)

mNSS test

The modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS) consists of three components: motor function, sensory function, and reflex function, with a maximum total score of 18. Scores of 1–6 indicate mild impairment, 7–12 indicate moderate impairment, and 13–18 indicate severe impairment.

Rotarod test

The motor function in mice was assessed using rotating rod test. The training was conducted preoperatively, and testing was performed 3, 5, 7, and 14 days after MCAO. Mice were placed on an accelerating rotating rod, with the speed increased from 4 rpm to 40 rpm over 3 min, followed by a 2-min maintenance phase. The latency to fall was recorded by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups.

Cell culture and transfection

We maintained BV2 murine microglial cells in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. For transfection experiments, we seeded BV2 cells at 5 × 104 per well in 6-well plates and grew them to ∼70% confluence overnight.

To knock down Dbp, we transfected cells with a small interfering RNA targeting Dbp (D-box binding PAR bZIP protein) or a non-targeting control siRNA (sequences in Table S1) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) in Opti-MEM. We prepared siRNA–lipid complexes according to the manufacturer’s protocol and added them to cells; after 6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM. For overexpression, we transfected a plasmid encoding full-length mouse Dbp cDNA using Lipofectamine 3000. We confirmed knockdown and overexpression efficiencies 48–72 h post-transfection by RT-qPCR and Western blot.

Senescence induction in BV2 cells

We induced cellular senescence in BV2 cells using doxorubicin, following a published protocol.46 BV2 cells were treated with doxorubicin (920 nM in complete medium) for 24 h, then washed twice with PBS and cultured in drug-free medium for an additional 48 h. Control cells received vehicle (medium) for the same durations. At 72 h after initial treatment, the doxorubicin-treated cells displayed senescent characteristics and were used for subsequent assays.

SA-β-gal staining

We assessed senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity (SA-β-gal) using a commercial staining kit (Beyotime, Shanghai). Brain tissue sections and BV2 cells were fixed with the kit’s fixative solution for 15 min (room temperature), rinsed in PBS, and incubated with X-gal staining solution at 37°C overnight (in the dark). Senescent cells develop a blue precipitate (SA-β-gal positive) due to β-galactosidase activity at pH 6.0. After staining, we washed samples with PBS and examined them under a light microscope (Olympus IX73).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For immunohistochemical detection of microglia, we first performed SA-β-gal staining on frozen brain sections as above, then carried out antigen retrieval (10 mM sodium citrate, pH 6.0, ∼95°C, 10 min). Sections were cooled, rinsed in PBS, and blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. We incubated sections overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody against Iba1 (Abcam, ab283319) to label microglia. The next day, sections were washed in PBS and incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at 37°C. We developed the signal with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate for 5 min, yielding a brown precipitate. Sections were rinsed, mounted, and imaged under a light microscope. Co-localization of blue (SA-β-gal) and brown (Iba1) signals in the same cells identified senescent microglia.

Immunofluorescence

Brain samples were embedded in OCT compound and cryosectioned at 10 μm, and BV2 cells were cultured on glass coverslips and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Tissues and cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (20 min) and blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. We incubated samples overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Iba1 (Abcam, ab283319), NeuN (Proteintech, 66836-1-Ig), GFAP (Cell Signaling Technology, 3670) and γH2AX (ImmunoWay, YT1255). After PBS washes, Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Images were captured on an Olympus IX83 fluorescence microscope.

Western Blotting

We lysed mouse brain tissue (ischemic hemisphere) and BV2 cells in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 30 min, 4°C), and protein concentrations were measured by the BCA assay (Beyotime, Shanghai time). We resolved equal amounts of protein (30 μg per sample) by SDS-PAGE and transferred them onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h and then probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies were anti-DBP (Invitrogen PA5-87261), anti-p16ˆINK4A (Cell Signaling Technology, 23200), anti-p21 (Cell Signaling Technology, 64016), and anti-GAPDH (Abcam, ab9485). After washing in TBST, we incubated blots with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Omni) and captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. And all the raw data of the Western Blotting assays were deposited in Figure S6.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

We extracted total RNA from BV2 cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and treated it with DNase I to remove genomic DNA. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Novizan, Shanghai) with oligo(dT) and random primers. We performed quantitative PCR on a Roche LightCycler 96 using SYBR Green detection and gene-specific primers for Dbp and other senescence-related genes, with Gapdh as the internal control. Cycling conditions were 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s, and a melt-curve analysis to confirm specificity. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2ˆ–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.1.0) and GraphPad Prism 8.3. Bioinformatics employed WGCNA, IOBR, MCPcounter, e1071, randomForest, GSVA, fgsea, SCENIC, CellChat, Monocle2, and sva packages. Bulk datasets GSE16561 (39 IS, 24 controls) and GSE58294 (69 IS, 23 controls) were integrated after batch correction; single-cell data comprised 3 sham and 3 MCAO mice (GSE174574). Bootstrap validation used 1,000 iterations. In vivo experiments used n = 6 (TTC), n = 8 (behavior), and n = 5 (IHC/qPCR); in vitro experiments had ≥3 biological replicates with 3 technical replicates each. Two-group comparisons used unpaired two-tailed t-tests; multiple groups used one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc. Differential expression used Limma with Benjamini-Hochberg correction; correlations used Spearman; GSEA used 1,000 permutations; CellChat used permutation tests; SCENIC included threshold sensitivity tests. Data are shown as mean ± SD (bar graphs) or median/IQR (boxplots); behavior data as mean ± SEM. Significance was defined as two-sided p < 0.05, with ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001. Blinding was applied exact n values and test details are provided in figure legends and the result section.
