Atrial mass causing asymptomatic bradycardia: an unusual presentation of cardiac lymphoma
Authors: Jinjin Ni, Yafei Ren, Wenbin Zhang, Ya Li, Takeshi Kitai, Oleg J Zharinov, Deborah Cosmi
Published: 2026

Case description

A 52-year-old male presented with incidentally discovered bradycardia, in the absence of symptoms including dizziness and amaurosis. He had a 9-year history of gout. Physical examination revealed a resting heart rate of 43 b.p.m., with no other positive findings. Thyroid function tests and serum electrolyte were within normal limits. Electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated an atrioventricular junctional escape rhythm with a ventricular rate of 44 b.p.m. (Figure 1A). Holter monitoring showed absence of visible P waves and a sustained atrioventricular junctional escape rhythm with the ventricular rate ranged from 30 to 66 b.p.m. (mean 44 b.p.m.). Echocardiography identified a 56 × 39 mm heterogeneous mass originating from the interatrial septum and right atrial wall, protruding into the right atrium (Figure 1B; Supplementary material online, Video S1). Left ventricular opacification revealed heterogeneous moderate-to-high enhancement within the mass (Figure 1C; Supplementary material online, Video S2). The positron emission tomography showed a mass in the right atrium, along with widespread osteolytic lesions and abnormal uptake of fluorodeoxyglucose in these regions (Figure 1D). Pathological examination of a retroperitoneal mass later confirmed the diagnosis of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (Figure 1E). ECG returned to sinus rhythm after one cycle of pola-R-CHOP chemotherapy. After four cycles, the cardiac tumour size had decreased to 25 × 15 mm.

Multiple-panel image illustrating the following: (A) electrocardiogram showing atrioventricular junctional escape rhythm at approximately 44 b.p.m. (B) echocardiogram revealed a 56 × 39 mm heterogeneous hypoechoic mass in the right atrium near the superior vena cava inlet, adhering to the atrial wall and extending to the interatrial septum, with notable mobility during the cardiac cycle. (C) left ventricular opacification demonstrating a 67 × 52 mm filling defect in the right atrium, corresponding to a mass with heterogeneous moderate-to-high enhancement. (D) Positron emission tomography-computed tomography demonstrated hypermetabolic mass within the right atrium and widespread osteolytic involvement. (E) Pathological examination indicated large atypical neoplastic lymphoid cells (haematoxylin-eosin, magnification ×400).

Bradycardia caused by cardiac tumours is relatively rare, with a few cases reported previously,1–3 and this case similarly showed a return to normal sinus rhythm after treatment of pola-R-CHOP chemotherapy. Cardiac lymphoma has been described as a cause of arrhythmias, with documented frequencies including atrial arrhythmia, atrioventricular block, bundle branch block, and ventricular arrhythmia.4 Although this diagnosis remains uncommon, when unexplained bradycardia occurs, clinicians should consider cardiac tumour in the differential diagnosis. In such cases, cardiac imaging such as echocardiography should be performed.
