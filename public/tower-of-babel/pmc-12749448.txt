Key domains involved in the interaction and assembly of the HCMV terminase complex
Authors: C Gourin, C Lefèvre, T Flores, A Couvreux, S Alain, G Ligat, S Hantz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman cytomegalovirus (HCMV) causes life-threatening infections, particularly in immunocompromised patients. Letermovir targets the viral terminase complex (pUL56, pUL89, pUL51), essential for DNA packaging, but resistance mutations in pUL56 underline the need for new antiviral strategies. Targeting critical interaction domains within the complex, such as the pUL56 peptide 671WMVVKYMGFF680, has emerged as a promising approach. This study identifies two additional pUL56 motifs, 514EVNVRKRAY522 and 757YLLLYRHL764, as key mediators of terminase assembly. These motifs were deleted or mutated in an HCMV-BAC clone, and protein-protein interactions were assessed using NanoBit® PPI/PCI-neo assays in HEK293T cells. Interaction domains were mapped on a 3D model of HCMV terminase and compared with the cryo-EM structure of HSV-1 homologs (PDB 6M5R, 6M5S). Mutations in these motifs strongly impaired viral replication. Functional assays showed that 514EVNVRKRAY522 directly mediates interaction with pUL89, while modeling indicated that 671WMVVKYMGFF680 and 757YLLLYRHL764 stabilize the multimeric ring-shaped complex through contacts with pUL89 and pUL51. Our results suggest that two novel pUL56 regions are essential for terminase assembly and conformation and may represent promising targets for future antiviral development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28915-5.Keywords: Human cytomegalovirus, Terminases, pUL56, pUL89, Therapeutic targetsSubject terms: Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) is a ubiquitous pathogen with significant clinical impact in human health, notably as a leading cause of congenital infections associated with birth defects and severe complications in immunocompromised individuals such as transplant recipients and patients with AIDS1–3. Although still a matter of debate, accumulating observations suggest a possible involvement of HCMV in the pathogenesis and progression of glioblastoma4–6. Furthermore, in July 2025, HCMV was classified as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B) by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)7. Conventional drugs against HCMV target the viral DNA polymerase pUL54 and are frequently associated with adverse effects such as neutropenia and myelotoxicity except for the acyclovir. However, it is not effective in treating CMV infection, and therefore only used for the prevention of congenital HCMV infection. Additionally, prolonged treatment has been identified as a risk factor for the development of resistance mutations8. Therefore, there is a need to develop alternative anti-HCMV therapies targeting novel and virus-specific component of the replication cycle.

One such promising target is the HCMV terminase complex, essential for viral DNA packaging. This complex has no human homolog. It is composed mainly of the pUL56, pUL89 and pUL51 subunits, along with additional accessory proteins9. Letermovir, an inhibitor of the terminase complex, has been approved for prophylaxis in hematopoietic stem cell transplant patients10,11. However, resistance mutations to letermovir have already been reported12,13, calling for continued research into the molecular basis of terminase function and interaction.

Previous studies identified a key interaction between the C-terminal region of pUL56 and pUL89 by coimmunoprecipitation14 and particularly the 671WMVVKYMGFF680 motif, as essential for complex formation15. Following on from these basic works, we highlighted two additional regions of pUL56 514EVNVRKRAY522 and 757YLLLYRHL764 that could also contribute to its interaction with pUL89. Through bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) mutagenesis and NanoBiT protein-protein interaction assays, supported by structure modelling, we provide functional evidence supporting the critical role of these regions either in the integrity of the tripartite terminase complex subunit through interaction between pUL56 and pUL89 and the whole multimeric terminase machinery essential for HCMV replication. These findings enhance our understanding of the molecular interaction within the HCMV terminase complex and identify new peptide regions that could serve as potential targets in the development of new antiviral drugs, peptides or antibodies against HCMV.

Results

Identification of two conserved regions in pUL56 between herpesviruses

Based on sequence alignments, the pUL56 514EVNVRKRAY522 and 757YLLLYRHL764 (pUL56 E514-Y522 and pUL56 Y757-L764 respectively) motifs appear to be highly conserved between betaherpesviruses (Fig. 1). This observation suggests that these two regions should play a major role in the structure or function of pUL56. Moreover, both motifs are located in the C-terminal part of pUL56, which has already been described as responsible for interaction with pUL8915,16. In addition, pUL56 E514-Y522 and Y757-L764 flank the ATPase region17 of pUL56 which is dependent on its interaction with pUL8916,18. Therefore, these two motifs are strong candidates for participating in the interaction between terminase partners.

Deletion and alanine scanning of 514EVNVRKRAY522 and 757YLLLYRHL764 pUL56 motifs affect viral replication in fibroblasts

Using “en passant” mutagenesis, we performed a complete deletion and a partial alanine scanning of pUL56 E514-Y522 and Y757-L764 regions. The alanine-modified residues were located every fourth amino acid, i.e. on one side of a predicted alpha helix. This experiment allowed us to assess the importance of these fragments for viral fitness in human fibroblasts according to foci observations after the transfection of recombinant HCMV-BACs. Eleven days post-transfection, we observed no cytopathic effect or viral foci for HCMV-BAC with pUL56 E514-Y522 or Y757-L764 deletions. At the same time, viral growth was observed for wild-type strain (Fig. 2A). These deletions seem to strongly impair viral replication and cell-to-cell spread. Moreover, the combination of mutations E514A-R518A-Y522A and Y757A-L760A-Y761A-L764A seems to result in an abortive viral cycle (Fig. 2B).

NanoBit protein-protein interaction assays

HEK293T cells were transfected with NanoBiT plasmids C-LgBiT and C-SmBiT containing UL56 or UL56 mutants and 6His-UL89 respectively. Protein-protein interaction was carried out using NanoBiT® PPI system. This system enables to analyze protein-protein interaction according to luminescence emission. Indeed, if the two proteins interact with each other, the two subunits (LgBiT and SmBiT) of the luciferase are also in contact, this led to the formation of functional luciferase. After its addition, the NanoGlo® substrate is then consumed by luciferase and emits a luminescence. Primarily, the first experiment consisted in the validation of the better plasmid pair to have the most efficient luminescent signal. This led us to use the C-LgBiT-pUL56 and C-SmBiT-6His-pUL89 pair as a positive interaction control. Moreover, the addition of pUL51 by transfection of UL51-pCI-neo in HEK293T to the experiment significantly increased luminescence emission (p = 0.005) and thus interaction between pUL56 and pUL89 (Fig. 3A). After confirming our parameters under wild-type conditions, the pUL56 ΔE514-Y522 and ΔY757-L764 mutants were analyzed under the same conditions and a significant decrease in interaction was observed (p = 0.003 and p = 0.001 respectively). pUL56 ΔW671-F680 served as a significant low interaction control for the interaction between pUL56 and pUL89 (p = 0.001)15. Transfection of a single construct or co-transfection of a NanoBiT construct with pCI-neo plasmid did not result in luminescence emission (Fig. 3B).

To confirm that the motifs studied in pUL56 were responsible for interaction with pUL89, we used the same technology using N-SmBiT-6his-UL51 and C-LgBiT-pUL56 constructs without pUL89 (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, the pUL56 mutants ΔW671-F680 and ΔY757-L764 again showed a significantly reduced interaction signal (p = 0.003 and p = 0.003). However, the pUL56 mutant ΔE514-Y522 showed no decrease in luminescence signal compared with wild-type conditions (Fig. 3C). These data suggest that pUL56 514EVNVRKRAY522 seems to be required for the interaction between pUL56 and pUL89. The other two pUL56 regions 671WMVVKYMGFF680 and 757YLLLYRHL764 seem to be responsible for interaction of pUL56 with the other two subunits of the terminase complex. A Western blot analysis was performed to ensure that the constructs had been correctly produced (Fig. 3D). The complete blot is available in Figure S1.

3D interaction model using artificial intelligence

To provide a structural model for our study, we used on-line AlphaFold Server19 based on AlphaFold3 AI to investigate the structure of HCMV terminases. We submitted one copy of each FASTA sequences of the terminase partners pUL56, pUL89 and pUL51 according to biological evidences, we added 2 Mg2+ involved in nuclease and helicase catalytic site, a 20-mer of DNA to mimic substrate during translocation process by the terminase complex, and 2 Zn2+ based on homology with HSV-1 terminase complex structure which was resolved by cryo-EM (PDB ID: 6M5R and 6M5S)20. Among the five models calculated during the calculation job, the rank of them obtained ipTM (interface predicted template modeling) and pTM (predicted template modeling) scores of 0.79 and 0.78, respectively closed to the 0.8 score of good quality models as described in AlphaServer documentation. However, regions already identified as variables and unstructured through herpesviruses are less accurate to be integrated into our analysis (pLDDT score < 50). Indeed, pLDDt map and PAE score of model present low confident predictions for pUL56 (425 to 485 and 778 to 850) and pUL51 (0 to 80) (Figure S1). Best model had a pLDDt score of 76.64, it was the model 0 that we used for all other analysis (Table S1). To leverage this model, we built contact maps between pairwise terminase partners with conserved and variable regions of pUL56 (CR-I to CR-XI, and VR-I and II) and pUL89 (CR-I to CR-XII). Their functional sites described in the bibliography were annotated and highlighted a tight relationship between interactions and evolutionary trace findings. Indeed, this map allowed us to show that many conserved regions are strongly involved in interactions between partners. This was in line with already presented data in the literature16,17,21. This model accounts for interface between pUL56 and pUL89 which is mainly constituted of pUL56 conserved regions IV (that include a zinc-finger), VII and IX on the one hand, and pUL 89 CR II, the nuclease domain and its terminus on the other hand as it was already described17,18,22–25. The theoretical structure also illustrates the chaperon role of pUL51 which acts as a bridge by embracing pUL56 N-terminal half and pUL89 C-terminal moiety (Figure S2).

The model of the HCMV terminase complex, allows to locate the motifs of interest of this study inside the whole complex. The pUL56 E514-Y522 and Y757-L764 regions are part of alpha helices whereas W671-F680 composes a loop (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, nor W671-F680 neither Y757-L764 appear to interact with pUL89 or pUL51 (Fig. 4B and C). On the contrary, E514-Y522 region and pUL89 L605-L620 interact each other (Fig. 4D). To reinforce our model, the same structural analysis was performed on the resolved structure of the HSV-1 terminase complex (PDB ID: 6M5R) and is presented in Figure S2.

Discussion and conclusion

DNA-packaging in orthoherpesviruses is a complex and highly regulated process that relies on multiple protein-protein interactions. In HCMV, the terminase complex (comprising pUL56, pUL89, and pUL51) is essential for cleaving and packaging the viral genome into preformed capsids. In addition to these core subunits, four additional proteins - pUL52, pUL77, pUL93, and pUL104 - have been implicated in the DNA-packaging process, suggesting a coordinated network of interactions required for successful viral replication26–28. Despite the recognized importance of the terminase complex, the precise molecular interactions between subunits remain poorly understood. Previous studies have identified several key regions in pUL56 involved in its interaction with pUL89 such as the conserved W671-F680 motif15. A recent study demonstrated that pUL52 is involved in numerous interactions with pUL56, pUL89 and pUL104 suggesting a role of chaperone protein for terminase subunits29.

Our study further contributes to this understanding by identifying two novel regions of pUL56 -E514-Y522 and Y757-L764 - that seem to be critical for HCMV replication. Using NanoBiT® protein-protein interaction (PPI) technology, we assessed the impact of deleting these motifs on the interaction between pUL56 and pUL89 or pUL51. We found that deletion of either of these regions significantly disrupted pUL56 interaction with pUL89, with additional experiments indicating that the E514-Y522 motif seems to be directly involved in the interaction, while Y757-L764 seems to play a structural role in stabilizing pUL56 conformation. Importantly, our findings confirm the role of pUL51 within the terminase complex, facilitating the interaction between pUL56 and pUL89, in agreement with previously published data30–32. Furthermore, analysis of the amino acid sequence of the E514-Y522 region reveals a RKR motif, largely described as crucial for the formation, localization, and regulation of multiprotein complexes due to their ability to serve as specific interaction motifs and intracellular retention signals33. This provides further support for the hypothesis that the E514-Y522 region may be involved in the interaction between pUL56 and pUL89.

To go a step further in our analysis, we also proposed model of HCMV terminase complex generated by AlphaFold Server relying on cryo-EM structures of homologous HSV-1 terminase assembly (PDB ID: 6M5R and 6M5S)20. This model is able to depict HCMV functional and structural features of pUL56 and pUL89 such as zinc-fingers, ATP/DNA binding sites, helicase and nuclease catalytic sites16–18,23–25,32. This structural investigation of HCMV terminase complex supports our assumptions, indicating that both regions - W671-F680 and Y757-L764 - are crucial for the integrity of the tripartite complex folding along with the assembly of the ring-shaped complex. Indeed, the superimposition of our model with the hexameric form of its HSV-1 terminase homolog allows to hypothesize that these two regions could be involved in the polymerization of the terminase complex to form the canonical ring machinery able to translocate viral DNA. Even if further experiments are needed to verify this hypothesis, our model could nevertheless be a promising starting point to better understand HCMV complex structure, functions and related resistances to antivirals.

These results not only advance our mechanistic understanding of the HCMV terminase complex but also highlight potential new targets for antiviral development. Since these interaction motifs are located in regions distinct from known letermovir resistance mutations, they offer promising alternatives for therapeutic intervention. Moreover, the identification of these motifs opens the possibility of designing inhibitory peptides or small molecules that specifically disrupt essential terminase interactions without affecting human cellular processes, thereby reducing potential toxicity. Thus, further experiments are needed to strengthen our approach.

However, one limitation of our study is that it focuses on isolated interactions between terminase subunits and does not fully account for the broader viral packaging machinery. Proteins such as pUL52, which interacts with all three terminase components and may serve as a structural or functional scaffold, were not included in our experimental setup. Future studies should investigate the interactions between the terminase complex and its auxiliary proteins - pUL52, pUL77, pUL93, and pUL104 - to better delineate the full architecture and functionality of the packaging complex29. Moreover, as NanoBiT® PPI technology is relatively new to the scientific community, it could be interesting in the future to further investigate our results with other approaches such as Co-IP or pull-down assays. However previous studies have already demonstrated that NanoBiT® is a reliable tool for studying protein-protein interactions even with HCMV proteins, which provides confidence in our current findings34,35. Co-IP has several well-recognized limitations, including low sensitivity for transient or weak interactions, nonspecific background binding, and the need for relatively high protein expression levels. By contrast, the NanoBiT® PPI technology has been shown to offer greater sensitivity and specificity, as it enables the detection of direct interactions in living cells under near-physiological conditions. This feature is particularly important for HCMV proteins, which are often expressed at low levels and may form interactions that are transient or spatially restricted. Further confirmatory experiments could, however, help to reinforce these conclusions.

Importantly, our study already provides novel insights into structure–function relationships that may guide future research and molecular developments. Finally, it is imperative to find new treatments that are highly specific to HCMV, with low toxicity and the ability to cross biological barriers. Inhibitory peptides could therefore be an essential means of treating at-risk patients with less toxicity. In this study, we therefore propose two new key areas for specifically targeting the virus. To date, our results could enable the development of new anti-HCMV strategies such as new antiviral molecules or inhibitory peptides, as demonstrated with the leucine-zipper motif of pUL51 or the core nuclear egress complex36,37.

In addition, thanks to AI-based modeling and advances in molecular docking, the identification of existing molecules likely to target these domains should be considered, as this has been performed with letermovir and the pUL56 binding pocket38. Protein-protein interactions could also be used to assess the effect of covalently binding drugs, especially as many drugs available to treat human infections could be tested39–42. Indeed, several molecules have been described as inhibiting HCMV infection by various direct or indirect mechanisms43. Our results make the HCMV terminase complex a target of choice for future antiviral strategies, as previously described9.

In summary, this study identifies two critical motifs in the C-terminal region of pUL56 - E514-Y522 and Y757-L764 - that are essential in the interaction within the terminase complex and for the structural integrity of the full terminase machinery. Collectively, these findings underline the importance of these domains for HCMV replication. Our structure-function approach provides new insights into the role of HCMV terminase interactions. These insights, combined with previous findings15,44, could help guide future researches toward the development of targeted antiviral strategies aimed at disrupting these interactions.

Materials and methods

Cells, bacterial strains and HCMV strains

Cells were cultivated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. MRC-5 human fibroblasts (bioMérieux, France) were harvested in minimal essential medium (MEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) with penicillin and gentamicin. HEK293-T were cultivated in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) with L-glutamine supplemented with 10% FBS and antimicrobials. E. coli strain was used for BAC mutagenesis as previously described45. The Bacterial Artificial Chromosome contains the genome of the HCMV laboratory strain AD169 (HCMV-BAC) and the green fluorescent protein (GFP) gene in the unique short (US) region and was derived from the parental strain pHB5.

Bacterial artificial chromosome mutagenesis

Using “en passant” mutagenesis, we performed a complete deletion and a partial alanine scanning of pUL56 E514-Y522 and Y757-L764 regions using a two-step markerless red recombination system for BAC mutagenesis in E. coli strain GS178345. Single mutations were introduced into an EGFP-expressing HCMV BAC (AD169 backbone), producing several mutants. Used primers are described in supplementary Table S2. SANGER sequencing prior to transfection for each recombinant virus confirmed the presence of the mutation/deletion. We previously showed by whole genome sequencing that the “en passant” mutagenesis does not introduce other mutations that could have a negative impact on viral replication15.

Transient transfection and viral growth analysis

BACs were extracted from bacteria with the NucleoBond™ Xtra Midi system (Macherey-Nagel™, Düren, Germany) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Then, they were transfected into MRC-5 cells (bioMérieux, France) with Transfast™ liposomal reagent (Promega, USA) to reconstitute viruses46. At day 4 and day 11 post-transfection, fluorescent cells and foci were observed. AD169 was used as a control of replication.

NanoBiT® PPI cloning and assays

To assess the influence of mutations on the pUL56/pUL89 interaction, the NanoBiT® interaction system was applied. Prior to the final experiments, each protein was cloned into each NanoBiT® vector to find the most relevant combination of constructs as described in manufacturer’s protocol. Next after setting up the experiment, the wild-type pUL56 and pUL56 mutants were cloned into the pBiT1.1-C [TK/LgBiT] vector (large C-terminal luciferase subunit) and pUL89 was cloned into the pBiT2.1-C [TK/SmBiT] vector (small C-terminal luciferase subunit). Genes were amplified from mutant or wild-type HCMV-BACs with UL56 primers from the pBiT1/2.1-C vector and UL89 from the pBiT1/1.1-C vector (Table S2) which contain the NheI and EcoRI restriction enzyme sites. UL51 was cloned in pBiT2.1-N [TK/SmBiT] vector and in pCI-neo by the same way with primers in Table S3. The PCR was programmed as follows: first denaturation at 98 °C for 5 min followed by 35 cycles of 30s denaturation at 98 °C, 45s hybridization at 58 °C and 20s elongation with a final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products and NanoBiT® plasmids were digested using EcoRI/XbaI. Ligation was performed with T4 ligase (New England Biolabs) at 16 °C overnight in a 3:1 ratio. Ligation products were transformed into electrocompetent MG1656 strains which were harvested on LB agar containing ampicillin (25 µg/µL). Colonies were screened using primers C-LgBiT vector or C-SmBiT vector forward and reverse (Table S3). Constructs were purified using the Xtra-Midi kit (Macherey Nagel) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 2.5 µg of each plasmid was transfected into a 6-well plate containing 5.106 HEK293T cells. Fugene™ 4 K (Promega, USA) was used for transfection in a 4:1 ratio according to manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated for 72 h and then resuspended in 2mL of serum-reduced OptiMEM™ medium (Gibco™, USA). 100µL of cells were plated in three replicates in a 96-well plate and the remainder retained for western blot analysis. NanoGlo® Live cell substrate was then added to the cells and luminescence was read on FLUOstar® Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermofisher Scientific). Samples were prepared with Laemmli sample buffer (Invitrogen) and Dithiothreitol (Thermofisher Scientfic) and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were loaded onto a NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Ref #WBT41220BOX, Invitrogen) and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Biorad). Membrane was blocked with Intercept TBS Blocking Buffer (LI-COR Biosciences) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with 6x-His Tag Monoclonal Antibody (HIS.H8) (1:1000, Catalog #MA1-21315, Thermofisher Scientific) and anti-LgBiT monoclonal antibody diluted in blocking buffer (1:1000, Catalog #N7100, Promega, USA). After washing three times with TBS 1X - Tween 20 0.1%, the membrane was incubated with Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L), HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Ref #W4021, Lot #0000626099) for 1 h at room temperature. Following washes, protein bands were visualized using ECL Select Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Ref #GERPN2235) and imaged using LICOR Odyssey chemiluminescent detection system.

Structure prediction and interaction modelling

Structural investigations of HCMV terminase complex were conducted thanks to AI-based modeling with online AlphaFold server. FASTA sequences of pUL89, pUL56 and pUL51 were submitted to AlphaFold Server19 regarding the bibliography. Subsequentially, jobs were run regarding (1) proteins only, (2) then adding Zn2 + ions based on homology with structure of the apo monomeric terminase complex of HSV (PDB:6M5R)20. Leveraging well known nuclease18 and suggested helicase24 function of pUL89, ATP and 2 Mg2 + ions were also included in the calculation 4. A 20-mer of DNA were finally integrated to analyze protein DNA interaction at the center of the packaging machinery. The addition of this biological evidence improved the accuracy of our model, as shown in Table 1.

Concerning structural visualization, we systematically exclude protein moieties with a pLDDT score less than 50: pUL56 425–485 and 778–850, and pUL51 1–80.

Statistical analysis

To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were done in three independent triplicates at different weeks. Thus, three biological and three technical replicates were done (n = 3 experimental replicates; n = 3 technical replicates). Statistical analysis was done using a student t-test with GraphPad PRISM 8.4.3 to compare mutant conditions to the control group in one-to-one comparison (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001).
