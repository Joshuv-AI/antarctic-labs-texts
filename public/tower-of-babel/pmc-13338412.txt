Systematic review and meta-analysis of stroke blood biomarker data highlights need for more translational research methods
Authors: Christine G. Smothers, Shivam A. Gandhi, Julia H. Chang, Sadia M. Tahir, Jarrett Kelley, Emily Wang, Kareem Fareed, Hannah Mack, Cindy Dong, Madaline Witort, Bethany L. Armentrout, Darynaisha Crawford, Jing Wang, Kara Williams, Zea Bud, Christine Horvat-Davey, Chris Winkelman, Sarah R. Martha, Jon W. Schrock, Agnieszka A. Ardelt, Grant C. O’Connell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke is one of the most frequently mistriaged medical emergencies. As a result, significant efforts have been made to identify blood biomarkers that could aid in stroke recognition. Large numbers of studies have reported candidate assays with seemingly high levels of diagnostic performance; however, practices employed in many of these studies could limit generalizability. Thus, in this systematic review and meta-analysis, we sought to clarify whether any of the assays proposed to date have true potential for clinical use. To do this, we quantified the influence of a wide range of possible translational confounds on the diagnostic performance estimates published for 863 single analyte or multianalyte assays investigated for stroke recognition over the past 30 years. We found that up to 56% of the variance in reported levels of diagnostic performance could be directly attributed to latent study design and reporting factors, rather than true differences in the diagnostic capacity of the assays themselves. After correcting for these factors and further considering analytical measurability in the target use environment, not a single assay displayed evidence of clinical utility. Our results clarify the current state of the push towards a viable blood-based stroke diagnostic, and also provide a contextual framework that can be used to more critically evaluate the broader space of molecular biomarker research.

Introduction

Quick and accurate stroke recognition is essential in reducing the odds of mortality and morbidity1,2. While neuroradiological imaging techniques play a central role in definitively confirming a stroke diagnosis3, these tools are often not available at the initial point of patient contact in the field or emergency department. As a result, clinicians such as paramedics, nurses, and emergency medicine physicians rely on symptom-based stroke recognition instruments to guide critical early triage decisions regarding transport, transfer, and referral4. Unfortunately, the accuracy of these assessments is limited, and anywhere between 10 and 50% of patients experiencing stroke are initially misdiagnosed in the early stages of care5–10, leading to potentially life-threatening delays in treatment. Given these limitations, the discovery of reliable stroke-associated blood biomarkers that could be deployed as part of point-of-care or stat laboratory diagnostics has potential to expedite and improve the accuracy of stroke triage11,12. As a result, stroke recognition has emerged as one of the most active areas of molecular biomarker research over the past several decades. Despite large numbers of studies reporting candidate assays with seemingly high levels of diagnostic performance13–22, none have made their way into clinical use. This discrepancy may be linked to translational limitations in how many of these early-stage biomarker discovery investigations have been designed and carried out.

To have clinical utility, a blood-based stroke diagnostic would have to reliably discriminate the collective mix of cerebrovascular diagnoses present in the suspected stroke population, including hemorrhagic stroke, ischemic stroke, and transient ischemic attack (TIA), from a multitude of non-cerebrovascular pathologies that can produce stroke-like symptoms, often referred to as stroke mimics23,24. Despite this, prior biomarker discovery investigations have often carried out molecular comparisons between stroke groups comprised of a singular cerebrovascular diagnosis and control groups comprised of neurologically normal or healthy individuals. Similarly, there has often been a discrepancy between the timing of blood collection and the target use scenario, as many investigations have performed analyses using specimens collected after treatment or well outside the window where a blood test would be useful. In addition, it is not uncommon to observe missing information in the reporting of these studies regarding potential sources of bias related to blinding, population sampling methods, post-analytical statistical processes, or conflicts of interest that could impact the translational value of the results.

Beyond these study design and reporting factors, many investigations have provided little discussion and seemingly little consideration as to the clinical measurability of the analytes under investigation. Given the target application, said analytes would have to be rapidly quantifiable in the field or emergency department to have utility. Due to their nature, such point-of-care and stat laboratory measures necessitate simplicity in terms of analytical workflow25,26, and often lack the precision and sensitivity of measures made with more labor-intensive and time-consuming techniques available in the research laboratory27–30. For this reason, most molecular diagnostics currently deployed in emergency medicine settings target a small number of analytes that can be measured in minimally processed blood products, and are either relatively abundant or exhibit large physiological alterations in the disease of interest31,32. However, the candidate assays that have been investigated for stroke recognition are often reliant on large numbers of parallel measures, complex specimen processing, or the discrimination of small changes in analyte levels.

Given these translational limitations, it is unclear whether any of the seemingly positive results reported in the literature could yield a viable molecular diagnostic. Thus, we carried out a systematic review and meta-analysis of data aggregated from 30 years of acute stroke blood biomarker investigations in which we sought to clarify whether any of the candidate assays proposed for stroke recognition to date hold real promise for clinical use. To do this, we quantified the influence of a wide range of potentially confounding study design and reporting factors on the diagnostic performance estimates published in the literature, and used the observed relationships to generate a summarized adjusted diagnostic performance estimate for each candidate corrected for said factors. Then, we examined key biochemical and analytical considerations to generate a clinical measurability score for each candidate reflective of how readily it could be deployed within the constraints of the emergency medicine environment. Finally, we used the combined context provided by both the adjusted diagnostic performance estimates and clinical measurability scores to assess each candidate's overall evidence of clinical utility. Our results paint a clearer picture of the current state of the push to develop a blood-based stroke diagnostic. However, they also provide a framework to more critically evaluate the broader space of molecular biomarker research, given that many of the potential confounds we assessed are relevant regardless of target application.

Results

Literature search, data extraction, and unification of diagnostic performance estimates

To aggregate data for our analyses, we performed a series of electronic database and indexing service searches to identify peer-reviewed articles reporting the results of prior human acute stroke blood biomarker investigations. Our query resulted in 26,817 unique potentially relevant bibliometric records, which were subsequently manually screened for inclusion, first via abstract review, and second via full text review when necessary. Articles were ultimately included if they described a study that compared the measures of at least one circulating molecular analyte between subjects with any type of acute stroke diagnosis and subjects with any type of non-stroke diagnosis, pending that diagnostic statistics were reported for said comparison and the summarized time of blood collection was within 72 h of hospital admission. Given the debate as to whether TIA is actually a distinct pathology as opposed to a clinical diagnosis tied to the sensitivity of neuroradiological imaging33, and the arguments that can be made over how such cases should be prioritized for care within the context of a stroke recognition tool, during screening, TIA was considered as either a stroke diagnosis or a non-stroke diagnosis, according to how it was treated by the authors of the original investigation.

Upon screening, 401 total articles published from 1995 to 2025 were deemed to meet the inclusion criteria and were retained for downstream data extraction13–22,34–424 (Fig. 1a). From each article, basic paper-level bibliometric and reporting details such as the presence or absence of conflict of interest disclosures were manually abstracted, along with extensive information regarding all reported diagnostic comparisons. For each unique diagnostic comparison, this information included where available the clinical and demographic composition of the stroke and control groups, the identities and circulating levels of the measured analytes, various methodological considerations such as the timing of blood collection, alongside any associated diagnostic statistics. Collectively, data from 1,590 diagnostic comparisons reporting the discriminatory performance of 863 unique combinations of 1,158 individual analytes were captured, including 680 candidate single analyte assays and 283 candidate multianalyte assays. To facilitate comparison, all originally reported diagnostic statistics, which included a mix of areas under receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, sensitivities and specificities, positive and negative predictive values, positivity rates, and various other metrics, were converted to diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) values425. Given that DOR values can be difficult to interpret and statistically analyze due to their infinite upper limit, all DOR values were further transformed to what we have termed here as symmetric area under curve (sAUC) estimates, which were used in all downstream analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1). This transformation, which is based on the mathematical relationship between the DOR and the area under ROC curve described by Walter and Sinuff426, represents the area under ROC curve associated with a given DOR, assuming the ROC curve is symmetric. This value ranges between 0.5 and 1, and can be interpreted in the same manner as traditional area under ROC curve values. In a subset of 538 diagnostic comparisons that directly reported area under ROC curve values alongside contingency count-based metrics such as sensitivity and specificity, sAUC estimates calculated from contingency metrics were well correlated with actual AUC values (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Unadjusted estimates of diagnostic performance reported in the literature

To determine which candidate assays captured from the literature had the highest reported levels of raw diagnostic performance regardless of study design or reporting considerations, we ranked each according to their summarized sAUC values, which were calculated by simple averaging of the individual sAUC values observed across all available diagnostic comparisons. Given that the symptom-based instruments currently used for stroke recognition can be performed in minimal time at no cost, a molecular diagnostic would have to offer meaningfully higher diagnostic capacity to have clinical value. Thus, to put the resultant summarized sAUC values into better context, we compared them to those calculated from data reported in a recent systematic review by Chaudhary et al.427 that assessed the performance of both the Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Scale (CPSS) and the Recognition of Stroke in the Emergency Room (ROSIER) scale, two of the most commonly used symptom-based stroke recognition tools428,429. Specifically, we summarized the performance of each scale using a subset of the available diagnostic statistics that were associated with their most frequent use scenarios; for the CPSS, evaluation of suspected stroke in the field by paramedics and emergency medical services technicians, and for ROSIER, evaluation of suspected stroke in the emergency department by nurses or emergency physicians.

A digital appendix containing both the individually reported and summarized diagnostic performance estimates for all diagnostic comparisons associated with each candidate assay can be found in Supplementary Data 1, while similar information for the CPSS and ROSIER can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Numerous candidates exhibited high sAUC values, including 18 with perfect values of 1.0. In fact, 80.8% of candidate assays included in the analysis displayed summarized sAUC values that were greater than the summarized sAUC value calculated for the CPSS, and 38.8% displayed summarized sAUC values that were greater than the summarized sAUC value calculated for ROSIER, which tentatively suggested they could offer clinical utility if these estimates accurately reflected the levels of diagnostic performance achievable in a true stroke recognition scenario (Fig. 2a, b).Fig. 2Diagnostic performance estimates reported in the primary literature.a Individual and summarized sAUC values associated with all 863 candidate assays captured in our analysis, directly calculated from the raw diagnostic performance estimates reported in the primary literature without any adjustment, ranked from highest to lowest summarized value. Influence of sample size and composition on diagnostic performance estimates

To identify factors that could bias or inflate the diagnostic performance estimates reported in the literature relative to true clinical use, we broadly assessed the univariate relationships between sAUC value and various methodological and reporting considerations across all captured diagnostic comparisons, regardless of assay, starting with sample size and composition. The combined number of stroke and control subjects varied significantly across diagnostic comparisons, ranging from as few as 6 to as many as 9,793. In terms of diagnoses, 69.9% of diagnostic comparisons reported a control group comprised solely of neurologically normal subjects, whereas only 18.3% reported a more clinically relevant control group including at least a subsample of true stroke mimics (Fig. 4a). 84.2% of diagnostic comparisons reported a stroke group comprised of a singular cerebrovascular diagnosis, most frequently acute ischemic stroke. Conversely, only 13.8% of diagnostic comparisons reported a stroke group that captured both hemorrhagic and ischemic pathology, which is more reflective of the mix of diagnoses clinically present in the suspected stroke population (Fig. 4b). Despite their need to be accounted for clinically, patients diagnosed with TIA were only reported in 15.2% of diagnostic comparisons, whether it be as part of the control group or stroke group. Group composition appeared to clearly affect reported levels of diagnostic performance. For example, on average, comparisons that employed control groups comprised of neurologically normal subjects displayed sAUC values that were 91.0% higher than comparisons that employed control groups comprised of stroke mimics, suggesting that the former are likely to significantly overestimate the levels of diagnostic performance that would be achievable for stroke recognition in clinical use. 34.7% of diagnostic comparisons reported summarized stroke severity information for the stroke group in terms of the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), one of the most widely used instruments for evaluating the presence and severity of stroke symptoms430. The values ranged considerably, from 1.5 to 25, with an average value of 7.7. Furthermore, we observed a significant positive correlation between NIHSS and sAUC (Fig. 4c), generally suggesting that many of the candidate assays studied to date are more readily able to detect stroke in patients with more severe symptoms. However, an acute stroke diagnostic would arguably have the most clinical value in patients with either non-traditional or mild symptoms, which often produce NIHSS scores of 5 or less431, as these cases represent the most frequently mistriaged9. Thus, this association infers that diagnostic performance estimates generated from samples with severe symptomology may not accurately reflect the true levels of diagnostic performance achievable in the clinical population where a blood test is most needed.

In terms of demographic characteristics, across diagnostic comparisons where summary statistics for age were reported, the summarized ages of the stroke groups were significantly higher than the summarized ages of the control groups, and we observed a significant positive correlation between the degree of intergroup age difference and sAUC (Fig. 4d). On average, the summarized proportions of male to female subjects reported for the stroke groups was slightly higher than the control groups, although intergroup sex differences did not appear to meaningfully influence reported levels of diagnostic performance (Fig. 4e). With respect to cardiovascular disease risk factors, the summarized rates of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes that were reported for the stroke groups were all significantly higher than those reported for the control groups; similar to what we observed with age, the degree of intergroup differences in the rates of each of these comorbidities was significantly positively correlated with sAUC (Fig. 4f-h). Collectively, this suggests that for many diagnostic comparisons described in the literature, the reported levels of diagnostic performance were at least partially driven by underlying intergroup differences in demographic factors or the rates of comorbidities, rather than the actual presence or absence of acute cerebrovascular pathology.

Influence of methodological and reporting considerations on diagnostic performance estimates

Beyond composition of the population sample, we assessed further methodological considerations that could bias or inflate the diagnostic performance estimates reported in the literature relative to clinical use, including the timing of blood collection, the number and nature of exclusion criteria, the population sampling method, the use of blinding, the strategy used to generate molecular measures, and the post-analytical processes used to evaluate diagnostic performance.

The summarized blood draw times that were reported across diagnostic comparisons for patients included in the stroke groups ranged from under three hours to as many as three days following symptom onset, and we observed a significant positive association between the blood interval and sAUC (Fig. 5a). A majority of diagnostic comparisons were performed using specimens collected at various timepoints post-hospital admission, or for which the exact timing of blood collection within the context of the care spectrum was unclear. Conversely, only 45.0% of diagnostic comparisons were performed using specimens collected in the prehospital setting or in the emergency department during initial evaluation, which better parallels the timeframe when an acute stroke diagnostic would have clinical utility. In terms of exclusion criteria, some diagnostic comparisons reported no explicit restrictions on the stroke sample, while others reported as many as 18. We observed a strong positive correlation between the number of exclusion criteria and sAUC (Fig. 5c). This relationship, taken with the fact that the suspected stroke population includes a substantial degree of diversity in terms of pathology, demographics, pharmacology, health history, and comorbidities23, suggests that diagnostic performance estimates yielded from highly-excluded homogeneous samples are likely to be overoptimistic relative to use in the target clinical population. Notable in this regard, some of the most frequently observed exclusions were cross sections that are common amongst the suspected stroke population, including patients with a history of prior cerebrovascular events432, prescribed anticoagulants433, blood disorders434, and cardiac conditions435 (Supplementary Fig. 3). As for the strategies used for population sampling, the formal methodology was not reported in the case of 67.5% of diagnostic comparisons. 27.8% of diagnostic comparisons were performed using stroke groups that were recruited consecutively, while the remaining 4.7% were performed using stroke groups that were recruited using a mix of other methods such as random and convenience sampling, which could be argued have greater potential for bias. Diagnostic performance estimates appeared to confirm such bias, as sAUC values from diagnostic comparisons using unreported or non-consecutive recruitment methods were significantly higher (Fig. 5d).

With respect to blinding, there was sufficient information reported to determine that the reference diagnoses were made without knowledge of the molecular measures in only 7.1% of diagnostic comparisons, and similarly, that molecular measures were carried out without knowledge of the reference diagnosis in only 29.7% of diagnostic comparisons. For an overwhelming majority of diagnostic comparisons, there was not adequate information reported to ascertain whether blinding was employed or not. Diagnostic comparisons that were carried out in the absence of blinding or for which blinding information was not reported displayed significantly higher sAUC values, suggesting their associated performance estimates are potentially inflated due to the presence of conscious or unconscious bias (Fig. 5e, f). In regard to analytical paradigms, 85.1% of diagnostic comparisons employed molecular measures that were carried out in batch, whereas 12.1% employed molecular measures that were carried out as independent individual tests, with the former exhibiting significantly higher sAUC values (Fig. 5g). This is unsurprising given the lower degree of analytical variability associated with batch testing; however, it suggests that batch measures made in the research laboratory are likely to produce overoptimistic diagnostic performance estimates relative to what would be observed in the clinical setting where independent tests are a necessity.

As for post-analytical considerations, in 87.7% of diagnostic comparisons, the broader mathematical model or algorithm used for diagnostic discrimination was established a priori. Conversely, in nearly all of the remaining diagnostic comparisons, the mathematical model or algorithm was selected or trained using the observed in-set data. Furthermore, in 49.2% of diagnostic comparisons, the specific diagnostic cutoff used to generate the final reported diagnostic performance estimates was established a priori. However, in nearly all of the remaining diagnostic comparisons, the diagnostic cutoff was selected based on the observed in-set data, most commonly by maximizing the Youden index436,437. sAUC values from diagnostic comparisons where the diagnostic model or diagnostic cut-off was selected based on the observed in-set data were significantly higher than sAUC values from diagnostic comparisons where these parameters were established beforehand (Fig. 5h, i), suggesting that many of the diagnostic performance estimates reported in the literature are likely inflated due to post-analytical issues with overfitting.

Beyond the aforementioned methodological considerations, we also examined the association between conflict of interest reporting and diagnostic performance estimates. For 13.8% of diagnostic comparisons, no conflict of interest information was reported in the associated article. For 17.2% of diagnostic comparisons, the researchers disclosed at least one potential conflict of interest, while for 69.0% of diagnostic comparisons, the researchers reported that there were no potential conflicts of interest to disclose. Diagnostic comparisons for which conflict of interest information was not reported displayed higher sAUC values compared to diagnostic comparisons where conflict of interest information was available (Fig. 5j), raising the possibility that a meaningful number of previously published diagnostic performance estimates have been biased by the presence of undisclosed influences438.

Adjustment of diagnostic performance estimates to account for study design and reporting considerations

Given the associations observed across our univariate analyses, it was apparent that many of the diagnostic statistics reported in the literature likely overestimate the level of diagnostic performance that would be observed in a true stroke recognition scenario. Therefore, it remained unclear which, if any, of the candidate assays that have been studied to date could offer clinically useful levels of discrimination. Thus, we sought to formally model the associations between potentially confounding study design and reporting factors and the published diagnostic performance estimates, and then use said model to generate adjusted diagnostic performance estimates corrected for said factors.

To do this, a stepwise linear regression approach was used in which we initially modeled the sAUC values observed across all available diagnostic comparisons as a function of all potentially confounding study design and reporting factors that exhibited significant associations in our univariate analyses. Any factors that did not maintain the direction of association observed in our univariate analyses in this initial model were then removed, and the remaining factors were used to build a final model. In order to determine which factors had the strongest influence on diagnostic performance estimates, an average over orderings-based variance decomposition approach439 was used to estimate what proportion of the collective modeled variance was attributable to each covariate. In order to assess risk of overfitting, particularly with respect to explained variance, we compared the results associated with the final model to those observed in a parallel five-fold cross validation carried out using the same modeling approach. Although they demonstrated strong correlations with reported levels of diagnostic performance in our univariate analyses, we did not examine stroke severity or the magnitudes of intergroup differences in rates of cardiovascular disease risk factors as potential covariates. Because said information was not available for a considerable number of diagnostic comparisons, modeling these factors would have required a significant amount of imputation that we felt could introduce bias. Furthermore, it could be argued that biomarkers whose levels are altered by antecedent stroke risk factors could still have utility for stroke recognition if differences in said risk factors exist between patients truly experiencing stroke and patients experiencing stroke mimicking conditions440.

12 of the 14 individual factors we interrogated maintained their direction of association in the initial model (Supplementary Table 4) and were included in the final model (Fig. 6a), with a majority of associations remaining statistically significant. Variance in these 12 factors was able to explain 39.9% of the total variance in sAUC values observed across diagnostic comparisons. Furthermore, differences in total number of exclusion criteria, sampling of stroke mimic or TIA diagnoses, post-analytical methods, total sample size, blinding of molecular measures, and timing of blood collection were estimated to be the largest individual drivers of variance in sAUC values. We observed highly similar results and virtually no test-training decay in cross validation, suggesting that these estimates of explained variance are unlikely to be driven by overfitting (Supplementary Fig. 4). The fact that such a large proportion of the variance in sAUC values could be attributed to this relatively small number of latent factors suggests that true differences in diagnostic capacity between the candidate assays that have been studied to date drive a relatively small proportion of the differences between diagnostic performance estimates reported in the literature. To produce adjusted diagnostic performance estimates more reflective of what would likely be observed in a true stroke recognition scenario, the final regression model was used to generate a predicted sAUC value for each diagnostic comparison that we took to signify the level of diagnostic performance that would be expected based on study design and reporting factors alone. We then used the proportion of maximum theoretical diagnostic performance this value represents to generate a corrective scaling factor which was applied to the raw unadjusted sAUC value to yield a final adjusted sAUC value. For example, keeping in mind that sAUC values range from 0.5 to 1.0, for a diagnostic comparison with a predicted sAUC value of 0.6, it was inferred that up to 20% of the maximum theoretical level of diagnostic performance would be expected based solely on observed confounds; thus, if the raw unadjusted sAUC value for this same diagnostic comparison was 0.9, it was accordingly reduced by 20% to yield a final adjusted sAUC value of 0.82. This proportional adjustment strategy yielded a similar but more liberal correction than if we were to have simply used the model residuals directly as adjusted values, given that in the latter more conventional approach441, negative residuals would all be interpreted as adjusted sAUC values of 0.5 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Despite this liberal correction, the resultant adjusted sAUC values were markedly lower than the original unadjusted values, illustrating the degree to which many of the diagnostic performance estimates reported in the literature are likely detached from what would be achievable in true clinical use (Fig. 6b).

To evaluate which candidate assays captured from the literature could offer clinical utility within the context of these adjusted diagnostic performance estimates, we re-ranked each according to their summarized adjusted sAUC values using the same methodology employed in our initial analysis performed with unadjusted metrics. A digital appendix containing the final individual and summarized adjusted diagnostic performance estimates for all diagnostic comparisons associated with each candidate assay can be found in Supplementary Data 2. There was a high degree of discordance between the ranks that we initially observed based on unadjusted sAUC values versus those that we observed based on adjusted sAUC values, with several candidate assays that were highly ranked in our initial analysis becoming some of the most lowly ranked after correction for potential confounds (Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, contrary to our initial analysis performed with unadjusted diagnostic performance estimates, very few candidates displayed values that compared favorably to currently used symptom-based assessments. Not a single candidate displayed a summarized adjusted sAUC value higher than the summarized sAUC value calculated for in-hospital stroke recognition using ROSIER; this infers it is unlikely that any candidate assay captured in our analysis would offer a diagnostic advantage over existing triage tools used in the emergency department if deployed as a standalone test. 79 candidates (9.1%) did display summarized adjusted sAUC values that fell above the summarized sAUC value calculated for prehospital stroke recognition using the CPSS, at least tentatively suggesting they had potential to offer a higher degree of discrimination than existing triage tools used in the field (Fig. 6c).

However, for a majority of these 79 candidates, the entirety of evidence was based on a single investigation (Fig. 6d). Thus, to evaluate the odds that these single-investigation observations would replicate with further study, we examined the relationship between summarized diagnostic performance and the number of associated independent studies across the total pool of candidate assays. We observed a striking funnel shaped association between unadjusted summarized sAUC values and the number of studies, with diagnostic performance tapering towards the mean with increased investigation. This inferred a unique instance of replication decay442,443 that we strongly suspect is driven by the following circumstances: candidate biomarkers with low reported levels of diagnostic performance in initial investigations simply have not been studied further, while conversely, those with high reported levels of diagnostic performance in initial investigations have been studied further, but the results have subsequently failed to replicate. While the magnitude of performance decay for initially high performing candidates was reduced when examining the same relationship in terms of adjusted summarized sAUC values, it was not completely eliminated (Fig. 6e). This suggests that as we suspected, there are other biases that have influenced the diagnostic performance estimates reported in the literature beyond those accounted for in our final model. Perhaps more importantly, it indicates that for the subset of minimally investigated candidate assays that displayed seemingly promising levels of discrimination relative to the CPSS, we would reasonably expect to see an additional drop in diagnostic performance with future replication beyond that already accounted for in our adjusted estimates.

Assessment of clinical measurability

Given that adequate levels of diagnostic performance alone is not enough to infer clinical utility, we also sought to determine how readily the candidate assays that have been studied to date could be analytically deployed for stroke recognition in the field or emergency department. To do so, we evaluated each assay in terms of four distinct criteria relevant to the target application and testing environment, which we respectively scored between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating optimal properties (Fig. 7a). For the first criterion, we assessed preanalytical compatibility with rapid testing; specifically, we examined whether each assay's composite analytes could be measured directly in common blood products such as serum, plasma, or whole blood, or conversely whether their measures necessitated additional specimen processing such as protein fractionation or nucleic acid isolation. For the second criterion, we assessed technical compatibility with clinically available analytical methods; specifically, we examined whether the analytes comprising each assay could be quantified using techniques such as immunoassay, enzymatic assay, or electrochemistry that are currently deployed for stat or point-of-care testing, or conversely whether their quantification required more complicated analytical techniques only available in the research setting. For the third criterion, we simply assessed each assay's total number of composite analytes. For the fourth criterion, we assessed the fold differences in blood levels associated with each assay. In the case of single analyte assays, we averaged the absolute fold differences observed between the stroke and control groups across all available diagnostic comparisons. In the case of multianalyte assays, we first generated a pooled fold difference for each available diagnostic comparison by averaging the absolute fold differences observed across the constitutive analytes, and then averaged the pooled fold differences across diagnostic comparisons. According to this final metric, the collective clinical measurability of the candidate assays captured in our analysis appeared to be relatively low. Only 10.4% of candidates exhibited an overall score of greater than 0.75, indicating optimal properties in at least three of the four domains we evaluated, while only 1.2% exhibited an overall score of 1.0, indicating optimal properties in all four (Fig. 7b). Of the candidates exhibiting the highest overall clinical measurability scores, a majority were single analyte assays comprised of plasma proteins, along with a handful of single analyte assays comprised of plasma metabolites. Generally, assays consisting of RNA or DNA displayed lower scores than those consisting of other types of analytes, as they were penalized due to their need for time-consuming nucleic acid isolation for accurate quantitation; in addition, several were panels consisting of large numbers of composite markers, which would confer a degree of test complexity that would make clinical translation more difficult. This latter barrier associated with high numbers of parallel measures was not isolated to nucleic acid-based assays however, as we did observe some instances of metabolite and protein-based panels comprised of as many as 100 markers. Regardless of analyte type, the fold differences we observed were quite low; 61.4% of assays displayed summarized fold differences of less than 2 that could be difficult to reliably discriminate in a rapid test, while only 5.3% displayed summarized fold differences of over 8. Taken as a whole, these observations suggest that relatively few candidate assays studied to date could be readily quantified in emergency medicine settings using existing molecular diagnostic platforms within the time constraints of stroke recognition, especially at the point-of-care.

Overall evidence of clinical utility

To determine which candidate assays captured in our analysis had the highest overall evidence for potential standalone use in stroke recognition when considering both diagnostic performance and clinical measurability, we assessed each in terms of the intersection between their summarized adjusted sAUC values and clinical measurability scores. According to this set of criteria, we identified 23 candidate assays that we felt demonstrated the highest overall degree of evidence, including 20 consisting of single analytes, and 3 consisting of multianalyte panels (Fig. 8). The remaining panel was comprised of a mix of 30 total analytes, including 4 plasma lipids, 13 plasma metabolites, and 11 plasma proteins that were collectively associated with general risk of cardiovascular disease, the inflammatory response, and several other biological processes less immediately linked to stroke370.

Regarding potential for clinical use, from an analytical perspective, nearly all of the aforementioned assays displayed clinical measurability scores suggesting at least a reasonable likelihood that they could be successfully deployed in the emergency medicine setting; in fact, roughly half already are in some form, overwhelmingly in the clinical laboratory. From the perspective of diagnostic discrimination, a majority of these candidates have been fairly well studied, and their associated summarized adjusted sAUC estimates likely represent a fairly accurate representation of the level of diagnostic performance that would be observed in actual clinical use. Conversely, a small subset have only been studied in isolated investigations, and in these cases, it is probable that a further reduction in diagnostic performance would be observed beyond that already directly inferred by their summarized adjusted sAUC estimates. After accounting for any such potential replication decay, none of these 23 candidates demonstrated estimated levels of diagnostic performance that meaningfully exceeded even the lowest levels of diagnostic performance reported in the literature for in-hospital stroke recognition using ROSIER, nor the summarized levels of diagnostic performance reported in the literature for prehospital stroke recognition using the CPSS. Despite exhibiting the highest levels of evidence in our analysis, given these comparative levels of diagnostic performance relative to current symptom-based stroke recognition tools, it is unlikely there are any clinical situations where these assays would offer utility if deployed as a standalone triage test. It could be argued that because the diagnostic performance estimates associated with these candidates fell above the absolute lowest levels of diagnostic performance reported in the literature for the CPSS, there may be select field scenarios where they could provide improvements in discrimination; however, given that any such improvements would be incremental, it is doubtful they would yield an actual clinical benefit after accounting for the time cost associated with molecular testing.

Alternate search for candidate biomarkers with potential future utility

Given that not a single candidate assay demonstrated adequate evidence to suggest viability for use as a standalone stroke diagnostic in its studied configuration, the secondary question arose as to whether any of the analytes studied to date could have future utility, particularly if combined with yet-to-be investigated analytes as components of panels. Because our primary analysis was focused on evaluating standalone diagnostic utility, the methodology we used had potential limitations in terms of answering this secondary question. Notably, given that we used estimated diagnostic performance in the full suspected stroke population as a key metric, this approach may have failed to highlight instances of individual analytes that could have future value as components of panels because they demonstrate differences in blood levels in specific subsets of stroke pathology. Furthermore, in instances of analytes that have been predominantly investigated in the literature as components of panels already, there is analyte-level information regarding blood measures that could be potentially relevant to this secondary question that would not be captured when assessing the associated panel-level diagnostic performance estimate. Thus, to identify analytes that could have future utility as components of panels, we carried out a secondary analysis to determine which of the 1,158 individual analytes captured in our primary analysis have the highest evidence that their blood levels are altered in stroke pathology.

Specifically, we performed a search in which we first filtered the total pool of analytes to retain only those that had been investigated in multiple independent studies and demonstrated greater than 75% agreement in the direction of fold difference in blood levels reported between stroke patients and controls across all available molecular comparisons, pending at least one molecular comparison was carried out using stroke mimics as the control population, and at least one molecular comparison was carried out using blood collected in the prehospital setting or emergency department. In order to evaluate overall level of evidence, we then ranked each retained analyte according to the geometric mean of its total number of associated individual studies, the average fold difference observed across its associated molecular comparisons, the percent agreement in fold difference across said molecular comparisons, as well as the proportion of said molecular comparisons respectively carried out using stroke mimics and acute blood. This initial search was carried out using all molecular comparisons regardless of stroke group composition, and then subsequently repeated respectively using subsets of molecular comparisons involving stroke groups comprised either solely of hemorrhagic pathology or solely of ischemic pathology.

Discussion

The systematic review and meta-analysis described here provides a comprehensive survey of the evidence surrounding the candidate blood biomarkers that have been previously investigated for stroke recognition. Contrary to the large volume of seemingly promising results reported in the primary literature, our collective findings suggest there is not a single previously proposed assay with evidence to suggest it could offer standalone clinical utility for stroke triage in its studied configuration. Perhaps more importantly, our analyses also identify several critical issues that have limited the translational value of the biomarker discovery investigations that have been carried out in acute stroke to date, which need to be immediately addressed in future studies to ensure a blood-based diagnostic is actually realized. Outside of stroke, the work described here is some of the most comprehensive of its kind performed with respect to molecular biomarkers in general. Given that many of the confounds we highlighted are pervasive beyond stroke biomarker discovery, our results also call into question the value of the larger body of research being carried out surrounding molecular biomarkers as a whole, and imply that many of the same translational limitations need to be more broadly addressed across the field to ensure results with clinical relevance.

In some respects, our conclusions represent a departure from those made in prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses that have been published regarding potential acute stroke blood biomarkers460–470. These prior surveys of the evidence have largely based their conclusions on meta-statistics directly calculated from the raw diagnostic performance estimates reported in the source literature, with no quantitative correction for potential sources of bias or translational confounds in study design460–463,465–467,469,470, nor consideration for analytical feasibility460–470. While they have acknowledged that there are translational limitations underlying the primary investigations that have been performed in the space, nearly all have concluded that there are previously studied candidate biomarkers with a fairly high degree of promise for use in stroke recognition462,463,467,468,470. Furthermore, some have even gone as far as seemingly suggesting that there are previously studied candidate biomarkers that could have immediate clinical utility466,469. However, given the extent to which our results indicate the diagnostic performance estimates reported in the primary literature are inflated due to generalizability issues or the presence of bias, we do not believe there is adequate evidence to support such conclusions, particularly not the latter. While we do feel it is possible some of the most highly evidenced analytes captured in our analysis could have future potential for use in stroke recognition as ancillary components of yet-to-be conceived panels, even our enthusiasm surrounding this particular potential is somewhat muted. In that regard, we should emphasize that the scope of our analysis was limited to evaluating evidence of utility in terms of a true standalone stroke diagnostic that can differentiate the collective pool of acute cerebrovascular diagnoses from stroke mimics. Thus, it is possible that some analytes captured in our analysis may be useful with respect to secondary diagnostic questions such as differentiating large vessel occlusion471, ruling out hemorrhagic pathology66, or confirming etiology472; however, meaningfully evaluating the evidence for these other uses would require dedicated analyses given that they are associated with their own unique population comparisons and translational considerations.

Nonetheless, it is obvious that novel candidate biomarkers need to be identified beyond those that have been previously investigated if a viable blood-based stroke diagnostic is to be developed. In terms of identifying such markers, our results generally confirm that the pathophysiologic processes that have been most frequently targeted for biomarker discovery in the past are associated with detectable molecular alterations. However, we would argue that in many instances, it is likely there are more diagnostically robust markers associated with these processes that have yet to be characterized in stroke. For example, neuron-specific enolase and S100B were among the most frequently investigated potential surrogate indicators of neural tissue damage among those captured in our analysis. These proteins were originally proposed as biomarkers based on the belief that their expression was highly restricted to brain473; however, it is now known that they are enriched as little as 15-fold in brain tissue relative to other tissue types, and there are countless other proteins with a dramatically higher degree of brain enrichment that could potentially offer greater diagnostic specificity236. Furthermore, of these previously targeted pathophysiologic processes, some may offer a higher degree of value for future biomarker discovery than others when considering the target use scenario. For example, in general, analytes associated with cardiac dysfunction are likely to have a low ceiling in terms of overall diagnostic sensitivity for stroke if deployed for use amongst the full suspected stroke population, given that they may only exhibit elevations in a limited subset of patients such as those specifically suffering cardioembolic events474. Conversely, analytes associated with neuronal cell death, tissue ischemia, or peripheral adaptive immune suppression may have potential to offer greater levels of diagnostic sensitivity, as these processes are to varying degrees more broadly associated with both ischemic and hemorrhagic cerebrovascular pathology475–478.

Regardless of biologic strategy, our results reveal the degree to which prior investigations in the space have been hampered by translational confounds, and signal that these limitations need to be addressed in future studies if more robust markers are to be identified. Most notable is the extent of incongruence we observed between the study designs and broader approaches employed in prior investigations and a true stroke recognition scenario. We frequently observed small sample sizes, neurologically normal controls, highly excluded and narrowly defined stroke groups, specimens collected at later stages in pathology, and molecular strategies that would be difficult to deploy in the emergency medicine setting. In fact, only 12 out of 1590 diagnostic comparisons captured in our analysis did not exhibit at least one of these threats to generalizability (Fig. 10a). Our results clearly demonstrate that diagnostic performance estimates generated under such conditions have little value in terms of predicting the levels of diagnostic performance that would be observed in true clinical application. Therefore, to generate findings that have potential to generalize, it is imperative that future studies recruit meaningfully-sized samples that as closely mirror the suspected stroke population as possible, including patients diagnosed with stroke mimicking conditions and the entire spectrum of acute cerebrovascular pathologies. Furthermore, best efforts should be made to sample blood as early as possible relative to onset, ideally prior to treatment during initial evaluation; while this poses challenges in terms of informed consent, these challenges can be overcome through various avenues such as the use of deferred consent protocols or even consent waivers in specific situations479. Beyond these study design choices that clearly have a direct impact on the generalizability of the results, our findings also infer that future stroke biomarker investigations need to take more active steps to eliminate sources of bias. Given the extent to which our analysis suggests that the absence of blinding can inflate diagnostic performance estimates, it is imperative that adequate steps are taken in future studies to ensure both molecular measures and reference diagnoses are generated in an informationally independent manner. However, equally important are issues related to post-analytical methodology. Our results demonstrate that diagnostic performance estimates associated with diagnostic models and diagnostic cutoffs trained or selected based on the observed in-set data are often overoptimistic. In fact, the supposed diagnostic advantages of previously studied multianalyte panels versus previously studied single analyte assays for stroke recognition suggested in prior work469 were completely eliminated in our dataset after accounting for these potential biases (Supplementary Fig. 7). Thus, in order to produce unbiased diagnostic performance estimates, it is imperative that future studies make use of independent training and test cohorts when building candidate diagnostic models in situations involving multianalyte panels. To that end, it is also recommended that predefined diagnostic cutoffs be used wherever possible, as opposed to the in-set maximum Youden cutoff436,437, which is currently common practice. Furthermore, in early-stage investigations where use of the in-set maximum Youden cutoff may be unavoidable, there are currently underutilized steps that should be taken such as ROC smoothing or resampling to reduce bias risk480.

In terms of implementing the aforementioned reforms, it is important to note that historically, it does not appear that the generalizability and bias issues described here have impacted all investigations equally. When examining the translational value of the total body of work captured in our analysis, it is apparent that earlier stage investigations have tended to be carried out using less translationally rigorous methods, and then there has been a tendency to shift towards more translationally rigorous methods in subsequent replication studies (Fig. 10b). This seemingly prevailing paradigm is undoubtedly one of the primary drivers of the decay in reported diagnostic performance that we observed with increased investigation. It suggests that moving forward, true efforts need to be made to address as many of these issues as feasibly possible in the initial stages of investigation in particular, where anecdotally, they are often overlooked due to resource limitations or convenience. This is of particular importance given the increased use of high-throughput omic techniques for biomarker discovery in the space (Supplementary Fig. 8), which we expect will only accelerate. While large-scale omic screens are valuable tools if well implemented, given their inherent ability detect the full range of molecular differences that exist between specimens, the results have the potential to be dominated by false positives in the presence of confounding intergroup differences in donor demographics or clinical features that are not present in the target use population481,482, or process related factors that could introduce bias483. From this perspective, it is concerning that early stage studies carried out using these techniques in particular demonstrated some of the lowest translational value of all investigations captured in our analysis (Fig. 10b). This trend clearly needs to change if the potential advantages484 associated with omic approaches are to be realized.

From the perspective of reporting, the large number of published studies from which we could not retrieve basic information regarding blinding practices, population sampling strategies, sample demographics, the general methodology used to generate reference diagnoses, post-analytical processes, and even financial conflicts of interest was concerning (Supplementary Fig. 9). Our results reaffirm the degree to which this information is needed for readers to be able to fully assess the quality of evidence to make informed conclusions regarding the potential value of the results. These considerations are all covered by items in the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) checklist485 developed by the Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research Network486, which should be a mandatory component of all reports of future investigations. Furthermore, only 3.5% of the diagnostic comparisons captured in our analysis reported a direct contrast between the diagnostic performance estimate yielded from the candidate biomarker or panel under investigation and diagnostic performance estimates yielded from clinically used symptom-based stroke recognition scales assessed in the same population sample. Given the apparent influence of sample composition on diagnostic performance estimates, this under-reported comparison allows for a more valid and meaningful assessment of potential clinical utility as opposed to those frequently made based on the observed magnitude of the diagnostic performance estimate yielded from the candidate biomarker or panel alone57,77,246,260,300,312.

In terms of the broader implications of our findings, it is apparent that the translational issues we identified in our analysis extend beyond stroke. With the push towards precision medicine, the volume of molecular biomarker research has accelerated over the past half century; cursory searches for the keyword ‘biomarker’ in PubMed and Web of Science suggest that up to 85,000 new biomarker investigations are now being published annually (Supplementary Fig. 10). Despite this growing mass of research, the underlying reality is that there are still a comparatively small number of in vitro diagnostics that are approved by regulatory bodies or used in clinical care487–489. This field-wide discrepancy clearly mirrors what has been observed with respect to the search for acute stroke biomarkers, suggesting similar flaws in terms of the translatability of early-stage investigations. Recent reviews of the state of biomarker research in other disease areas suggest that the issues we identified here are indeed reflective of field-wide trends. For example, many have cited similar potential issues with generalizability stemming from incongruences between the population samples and experimental designs being used in studies and real clinical applications490–498. Furthermore, meta-analyses that have assessed STARD adherence in reports of molecular biomarker research in other disease states have highlighted similar issues in terms of reporting potential sources of bias499–504. Our analyses directly quantify the degree to which these seemingly pervasive generalizability and bias issues can influence diagnostic performance estimates, and call into question how meaningful many of the findings that are currently being published in the broader field of molecular biomarker research really are.

In our primary regression analysis, we were able to directly attribute 40% of the variance in the diagnostic performance estimates reported in the literature to a relatively small number of potentially confounding study design and reporting factors. That said, given that our principal goal was to generate correction factors, we chose to use a simple linear modeling-based approach due to its inherent interpretability; however, it is unlikely the relationships we modeled are perfectly linear, and alternate analyses carried out using less interpretable non-parametric modeling approaches such as random forest regression were able to explain up to 56% of variance using the same covariates (Supplementary Fig. 11). Furthermore, a meaningful proportion of the remaining variance we could not directly model was undoubtedly associated with additional confounding factors that cannot be readily abstracted from the literature such as blatant research misconduct505, differences in analyte-specific analytical methods506, heterogeneity in stroke reference assessment507, and residual publication bias508, which was evidenced by the fact that we still observed replication decay after estimate adjustment. Based on these collective considerations, we would conservatively estimate that at least half, if not substantially more, of the variance observed in our dataset was truly driven by latent effects. If these results are indicative of broader field-wide trends as we suspect, it implies that the diagnostic performance estimates that are being published represent a quantification of the underlying study design and methodology moreso than they do a quantitation of the diagnostic potential of the biomarkers themselves. While this sounds pessimistic, it would not necessarily be surprising given what has been previously suggested regarding bias and reproducibility in biomedical research in general509,510. Nonetheless, acknowledging this potential reality is important to spur improved practices that will ultimately ensure the countless avenues through which molecular biomarkers can improve healthcare are realized. To that end, many of the suggestions we have made here to increase the translational value of early stage biomarker research in acute stroke are applicable regardless of disease state.

The work presented here is not without its own limitations. Notably, our data unification approach had potential to introduce technical variance in specific situations where the underlying symmetry assumption associated with the sAUC estimate was violated426. However, one of the advantages of this approach was that it allowed us to capture and cross-compare both contingency metrics and area under ROC values from the source literature, which is not possible using traditional data unification paradigms511; in fact, not using this strategy would have forced us to exclude the evidence associated with 42% of diagnostic comparisons wherein the sole reported diagnostic statistic was an area under ROC curve value. Furthermore, experiments carried out to characterize the sAUC estimate using both simulated and actual data suggested that violation of the symmetry assumption was unlikely to meaningfully affect our overall results. These experiments indicated that much of the discordance observed between sAUC estimates and actual AUC values in our dataset was not driven by the presence of true natural asymmetry, but instead artificial asymmetry resulting from the combined effect of undersampling and misuse of the maximum Youden cutoff to derive the raw diagnostic statistics reported in the primary investigations themselves (Supplementary Fig. 12). Variance associated with this artificial asymmetry was directly accounted for in our final model and ultimately in our final adjusted diagnostic performance estimates by including post-analytical factors as covariates. It is also relevant to disclose that this work was not preregistered via a preregistration database512, which one could reasonably argue increases its own risk for bias. In that regard, one of the biggest target outcomes with research preregistration is to minimize the risk of effect size inflation513; beyond being naturally mitigated by our large sample size509, we made direct efforts to reduce this risk through both our conservative approaches to modeling and diagnostic performance estimate correction.

Taken together, our collective findings clearly indicate that there is not a single previously proposed assay with adequate evidence to suggest it would have true standalone clinical utility for blood-based stroke recognition. While our results do suggest that there may be some previously investigated analytes that could have future utility as components of panels, they would need to be paired with more diagnostically robust yet-to-be identified markers to produce a viable test. More importantly, our analysis also quantifies the substantial degree to which often overlooked generalizability and bias issues have limited the translational value of the early-stage blood biomarker investigations that have been carried out in acute stroke to date, and clearly signal that these issues need to be immediately addressed in future work if it is to be successful. Furthermore, given that many of these same confounds are commonly observed across other ongoing areas of biomarker discovery, our findings also provide key context which can help better assess the value of this larger body of work, and suggest similar reforms are needed more broadly across the field in order to maximize the likelihood of producing results with relevance to clinical application.

Methods

Literature search

The Web of Science core collection (Clarivate Plc) and Medline (National Library of Medicine, National Institutes of Health) were the primary electronic databases used to carry out our literature search. While the Web of Science core collection contains all Medline indexed articles, separate searches of each were carried out because the ability to search Medline directly using mesh terms had potential to yield records not directly identifiable via the search syntax used to query Web of Science. Google Scholar (Google Inc) was ancillarily used to identify any highly cited or highly relevant articles that were not captured in our searches of the aforementioned databases. Searches of each were carried out on 10/16/2018, 1/14/2021, 6/18/2024, and 6/16/2025 using the search strings listed in Supplementary Table 6. All available years of the Web of Science core collection were searched via the Clarivate Web of Science web interface, while all available years of Medline were searched via the PubMed web interface. Google Scholar was searched by individual year from 1990 to 2025 via the Publish or Perish software package (version 8.0, Harzing Research) while excluding citations and patents and limiting results to a maximum of 200 per year. Search result records were collectively downloaded as .ris files for downstream screening.

Abstract and full text screening

Search result records were imported into the Covidence cloud-based systematic review management platform (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd). Upon import, search result records were automatically de-duplicated, and abstracts associated with the remaining unique records were manually screened using the inclusion and exclusion criteria listed in Supplementary Table 7. Each abstract was independently assessed by two reviewers. In cases of discordant decisions, a third reviewer was used to arrive at a final decision. Following abstract screening, Covidence was used to automatically retrieve full texts for the remaining records. In situations where Covidence could not retrieve the full text for a record, the full text was manually retrieved and uploaded. Full texts were then manually screened using the inclusion and exclusion criteria listed in Supplementary Table 8. Similar to abstract screening, each full text was independently assessed by two reviewers. In cases of discordant decisions, a third reviewer was again used to arrive at a final decision.

Data extraction

Data were manually extracted from included full texts at the level of individual diagnostic comparisons using a custom web form built using Google Forms. In situations where target summary statistics for a given experimental group were not reported, but were reported for cross-sections of said group, weighted averages were calculated and recorded. In situations where summarized blood analyte levels were not directly reported in the text, but were graphically depicted in figure form, data were digitally extracted from said figure using the PlotDigitizer software package (version 3.0, PORBITAL).

Given the complexity of the data to be extracted when considering the number of diagnostic comparisons reported in some articles, truly independent double data extraction was not feasible. For each included full text, data were first extracted by a primary reviewer. Each data point was then verified by a secondary reviewer, who used the recorded sample sizes and analytes to initially identify each previously extracted diagnostic comparison. In situations where the secondary reviewer identified a discrepancy between the extracted data and the data reported in the article, could not find the source of the extracted data, or otherwise disagreed with the decision of the primary reviewer, the data in question were flagged for further review. Regular group meetings were held amongst all reviewers, including several with a clinical background in stroke or emergency medicine, in which all flagged data were formally reviewed to reach a final consensus decision.

Quality of reporting scores

During data extraction, each reviewer completed a custom quality of reporting checklist (Supplementary Table 9) for all captured diagnostic comparisons via an integrated set of web form fields. This 10 item checklist was directly based on a subset of the STARD checklist485 items that are most directly centered around facilitating reproducibility and external risk of bias assessment, and was adopted for the specific target application. Standardized decision rules were used to score each item as either 0 or 1 as appropriate, wherein 1 = yes. For subjective items targeted at evaluating reporting of methodology, decisions were largely made based on whether the specific methodological aspect of diagnostic comparison in question could be reasonably replicated based on the reported information. Individual item scores were averaged to generate an overall quality of reporting (QOR) score ranging between 0 and 1 for each diagnostic comparison.

Data cleaning and annotation

Final raw data extracted via Google Forms were downloaded as a .csv file, and cleaned and annotated in Microsoft Excel (version 2510.0, Microsoft Inc). For each numeric variable, simple calculations and/or the conditional formatting tool were used to flag any collected data that were obviously either misrecorded during data extraction or misreported in the primary literature. For example, checks were performed to verify that the recorded counts of demographic features and comorbidities were equal to or smaller than the total sample sizes recorded for their associated groups, summarized group ages were biologically feasible, and the recorded diagnostic statistics fell within their respective theoretical ranges. Similar checks were performed with categorical variables, for example, to verify no typographic errors were present in strings that would ultimately be converted to factors.

The identities of analytes were manually unified and annotated by a reviewer with training in both molecular biology and clinical laboratory science. Each analyte was assigned a final full name, assigned a final abbreviation or short name, and annotated with a permanent accession number from the most relevant biological or chemical database. Proteins were annotated according to their UniProt listings514, miRNAs were annotated according to their miRBase listings515, piRNAs were annotated according to their piRNA Database (piRNAdb) listings516, and DNAs were annotated according to their National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Entrez listings517. lncRNAs, mRNAs, and tRNAS were annotated according to the Entrez listings associated with their corresponding coding genes, or alternatively Ensembl gene listings518 in a small number of instances where an Entrez listing was not available. circRNAs were annotated according to their circBase listings519, or secondarily the Entrez listings associated with their corresponding coding genes. Metabolites were annotated according to their listings in the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB)520, or PubChem521 in a small number of instances where a Human Metabolome Database listing was not available. Clinically measured analytes that were comprised of multiple molecules, or that could not be accurately mapped to molecules listed in any of the aforementioned databases, were annotated according to their most relevant concept listed in the Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes (LOINC) database522. Following unification and annotation, synonym or alias checks were performed in each of the aforementioned databases to verify that each unique final annotation was mapped to a unique analyte.

Final cleaned and annotated data were used to construct a relational database in R (version 4.5.1, R project for Statistical Computing)523 comprised of five inter-referenceable data frames: one containing article level data, one containing diagnostic comparison level data, one containing molecular comparison level data, one containing descriptions of each unique individual analyte, and one describing each unique multi-analyte panel and mapping it to its associated individual analytes. This relational database was used to generate all calculated variables and carry out all downstream analyses.

Unification of diagnostic performance estimates

Cleaned diagnostic performance estimates extracted from the primary literature were unified in R using the custom functions listed in Supplementary Table 10 according to the strategy depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1. For diagnostic comparisons where sensitivities and specificities, positive and negative predictive values, or counts of positive tests were recorded, they were used along with the recorded sample sizes to backcalculate 2 × 2 contingency tables containing the counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. The contingency counts were then used to calculate DOR values according to Equation 1 reported in Glas et al.425 while applying the Haldane-Anscombe correction524 and capping the maximum value at 106. For diagnostic comparisons where only area under ROC curve values were recorded, they were converted to DOR values by iteratively solving for odds ratio in Equation 1 reported in Walter and Sinuff426. The final collection of unified DOR values were transformed to sAUC estimates according to Equation 1 reported in Walter and Sinuff426.

The sensitivity and specificity values presented in the digital appendices were calculated from contingency counts where available. For diagnostic comparisons where contingency counts were not available, what we have termed here as symmetric sensitivity and specificity estimates were presented. Said values represent the sensitivity and specificity for a symmetric ROC curve of a given area at the maximum Youden cutoff436,437. Given that the area under a symmetric ROC curve has a direct mathematical relationship with the DOR426, symmetric sensitivity and specificity estimates were calculated from the final unified DOR values by solving for sensitivity and specificity in Equation 1 reported in Glas et al.425 under the constraint that sensitivity and specificity are equivalent.

Evaluating the drivers and influence of underlying ROC curve asymmetry on the sAUC estimate

To examine the potential effect of true natural asymmetry on the sAUC estimate, we built three ROC curves of similar area but varying symmetry by using random sampling to generate underlying simulated biomarker measures for case and control groups of n = 1,000 each. The first ROC curve was symmetric and produced by generating biomarker measures with equivalent variance in the case and control groups. The second ROC curve was asymmetric via overdispersion and was produced by generating biomarker measures with over twice the variance in the case group versus the control group. The third ROC curve was asymmetric via underdispersion and was produced by generating biomarker measures with less than half the variance in the case group versus the control group. Given the intergroup difference in variance, we took the latter two simulated curves to represent reasonable examples of what would be expected in cases of analytes whose measures naturally produce ROC curves at the high ends of asymmetry. The sensitivity and specificity values for each curve at the maximum Youden cutoff were used to calculate sAUC estimates, which were compared to the actual AUC values (Supplementary Fig. 12).

To examine the potential effect of artificial asymmetry associated with misuse of the maximum Youden cutoff in small samples on the sAUC estimate, we further utilized the simulated analyte measures associated with the symmetric ROC curve described above. Given the large size (total n = 2,000), we took the representative ROC curve to constitute an accurate estimation of the true diagnostic properties of the theoretical analyte. 100 random subsamples of n = 25 each were then taken from the full population sample and used to generate a set of associated subsampled ROC curves. The sensitivity and specificity values for each subsampled curve at maximum Youden cutoff were used to calculate sAUC estimates, which were ultimately compared to the actual AUC of the parent curve associated with the full population sample. This process was further repeated using subsamples of n = 50, n = 100, n = 200, n = 400, and n = 1,000. The relationship between subsample size and the magnitude of difference between sAUC estimate and actual AUC value observed in this simulation was then compared to the parallel relationship observed in our actual dataset with respect to a subset of 538 diagnostic comparisons wherein contingency count-based metrics such as sensitivity and specificity were directly co-reported alongside area under ROC curve (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Across all simulations, biomarker measures were generated or subsampled via the sample() function of base R, and ROC curves were generated using the roc() function of the ‘pRoc’ package (version 1.19.0.1)525.

Linear modeling and generation of adjusted diagnostic performance estimates

All models generated as part of the stepwise linear modeling approach that we used to quantify the relationships between the final unadjusted sAUC estimates and the potentially confounding study design and reporting factors of interest were built using the lm() function of the R base ‘stats’ package. Numerical covariates were log transformed prior to analysis, and categorical covariates were dichotomized where 1 = yes and 0 = no or not reported. Overall model fits were assessed using Pearson’s R2. Partial R2 values for model covariates were generated using the calc.relimp() function of the ‘relaimpo’ package (version 2.2–7)439 via the lmg method. 95% confidence intervals associated with partial R2 values were generated via the percentile bootstrap method526, with 1,000 bootstrap resamples generated using the sample() function base R. Similarly, 5-fold cross validation splits used to assess risk of overfitting were generated using the sample() function and a set seed of 1 to reduce risk of bias. For diagnostic performance estimate adjustment, the predict() function was used to retrieve the predicted sAUC values associated with each diagnostic comparison from the final model. The predicted sAUC values were then used to generate both proportional and residual adjusted sAUC values via custom the functions listed in Supplementary Table 11.

Alternate random forest regression analysis

The same 5-fold cross validation paradigm that was used to evaluate the risk of overfitting associated with our primary linear modeling approach was used to estimate the potential proportion of variance in unadjusted sAUC values that could be attributed to latent study design and reporting factors via random forest regression (Supplementary Fig. 11). Data were pre-processed in the same manner in the primary linear regression analysis, and all models were built using the randomForest() function of the ‘randomForest’ package (version 4.7–1.2)527 using n = 500 trees and mtry = 4. These default hyperparameters were selected a priori with no tuning based on recommendations by Breiman528 to reduce risk of bias. Cross validation splits were again generated using the sample() function of base R and a set seed of 1. For each model, variable importance was assessed using mean decrease Gini coefficient via the importance() function of the ‘randomForest’ package. Overall model fits were assessed using Pearson’s R2.

Clinical measurability scores

Clinical measurability scores were generated for each assay using a combination of manual and computed scoring. The first two component criteria regarding preanalytical and analytical compatibility with emergency medicine testing were manually scored using a custom web form built in Google Forms. With respect to preanalytical compatibility, if the composite analyte(s) comprising the assay could be directly measured in whole blood, serum, or plasma, the assay was assigned a score of 1. If the composite analyte(s) comprising the assay required additional specimen processing such as nucleic acid isolation or protein fractionation to be measured, the assay was assigned a score of 0. In specific instances of multi-analyte assays where a subset of composite analytes fell into the former category and a subset fell into the latter, the assay was assigned a score of 0.5.

With respect to analytical compatibility, a manual search for corresponding test codes in the Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes (LOINC) database522 was first used to determine whether a clinically deployed point-of-care or stat laboratory test exists for the assay; if so, it was automatically assigned a score of 1, if not, the composite analyte type(s) were further considered to guide scoring. If the composite analytes were proteins, the assay was automatically assigned a score of 1, given the large number of analytical platforms that support rapid immunoassay at both the point-of-care and in the clinical lab. If the analyte or panel was comprised of nucleic acids, it was automatically assigned a score of 0, given the lack of existing clinical platforms that can provide accurate DNA or RNA quantification in a short turnaround time; note, while there are currently clinically deployed rapid nucleic acid-based tests that are performed for pathogen screening, these tests exclusively evaluate for the presence or absence of analyte, and none of the nucleic acid-based candidates captured in our analysis would fit this testing paradigm. If an assay did not meet any of the aforementioned prespecified decision rules, it was assigned a score at the best discretion of the reviewer. In specific instances of multi-analyte assays where a subset of composite analytes fell into the former category and a subset fell into the latter, the assay was assigned a score of 0.5.

The first two manually scored criteria were independently evaluated by two reviewers with both molecular biology and clinical laboratory training. In the case of discordant scores, or uncertainty raised by either of the initial reviewers, conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer who is both a PhD-trained molecular biologist and board-certified clinical laboratory technician. These manually generated scores were subsequently appended to the R database containing all other cleaned and annotated data, and basic scripts were used to score the remaining two component criteria regarding analyte number and summarized fold difference, as well as calculate the final overall clinical measurability score.

Comparison of diagnostic performance estimates against symptom-based stroke recognition scales

The summarized sAUC values associated with the ROSIER scale and the CPSS used as comparators were calculated using study-level 2 × 2 contingency table data retrieved from Appendix A of Chaudhry et al.427. The summarized sAUC value reported for ROSIER was calculated using data from a subset of the primary investigations in which evaluation was specifically carried out in the hospital by either a physician or a nurse. The summarized sAUC value reported for the CPSS was calculated using data from a subset of the primary investigations in which evaluation was specifically carried out in the prehospital setting by either an emergency medical technician or a paramedic322,429,529–547.

Statistical analyses

All other statistical analyses not aforedescribed were carried out in R using the base ‘stats’ package. One-way ANOVA or two-sample two-sided t-test were used for statistical comparison of means where appropriate, respectively via the aov() and t.test() functions. In the case of ANOVA, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were carried out using two-sample two-sided t-test via the pairwise.t.test() function. Pearson’s r and it’s associated two-sided t-test were used to evaluate the strength and significance of correlational relationships via the cor.test() function. Pearson’s chi-squared was used for statistical comparison of proportions via the chisq.test() function. In instances of multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method548 via the p.adjust() function. For all statistical testing, the null hypothesis was rejected when p < 0.05. The parameters of all statistical tests performed are outlined in detail within the figure legends.

Data visualization

All plots were generated using either the plot() function of base R or the ‘ggplot2’ package (version 3.5.2)549, saved as vector files, and subsequently stylized using Illustrator (version 24.0.1, Adobe Inc).
