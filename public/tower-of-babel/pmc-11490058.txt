Unraveling Cordia myxa’s anti-malarial potential: integrative insights from network pharmacology, molecular modeling, and machine learning
Authors: Yufei Miao, Wenkang Liu, Sarah Mohammed Saeed Alsallameh, Norah A. Albekairi, Ziyad Tariq Muhseen, Christopher J. Butch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Malaria is a potentially fatal infective illness caused due to parasites that belong to the Plasmodium genus, which are transferred to humans with the help of the stings of affected female Anopheles mosquitoes, and it persists as a serious public wellness problem worldwide. Cordia myxa is a medicinal plant that possesses various medicinal characteristics like antimicrobial, anti-inflammation, antioxidant, and antidiabetic activities, which makes it an important natural resource for the therapy of different maladies in traditional medicine. In this investigation, a certain network pharmacology method has been utilized to identify the potent active components, possible targets as well as signaling pathways present in C. myxa in relation to malaria therapy. The active compounds were submitted to molecular docking approaches to validate their successful activity against the potential targets. The study concluded that three constituents named cosmosiin, stigmastanol, robinetin, and quercetin were highly active and could regulate the expression of Interleukin 6 (IL6) and Cysteine-aspartic acid protease 3 (CASP3), which may act as a potential therapeutic target for malaria treatment. These analyses are validated by molecular dynamics simulation which reflects on the overall structural stability of the intermolecular conformation and interactions. These results can also be witnessed in simulation-based trajectories binding free energies, which concluded the significant role of electrostatic and van der Waals energies in total intermolecular interactions. Finally, we utilized machine learning to predict the anti-malarial activity of C. myxa compounds, comparing them with approved drugs. Using the Chemprop model and MAIP predictions, we assessed ten compounds, revealing their potential as lead anti-malarial agents. This study establishes a groundwork for comprehending the function of the anti-malaria action of C. myxa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10078-9.

Introduction

Malaria is a fatal illness triggered due to the parasite Plasmodium. This disease is spread to individuals through the stings of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes, which typically feed on humans during the night. Malaria poses a considerable public health challenge, particularly in tropical and subtropical areas globally [1]. As per the 2021 World Malaria Report from the World Health Organization (WHO), there has been a concerning rise in the global malaria caseload, with a total of 2.41 million cases recorded in 2020. The report underscores a rise in malaria-related fatalities in the year 2020. This increase serves as a reminder that malaria remains a major global health challenge, and more efforts are needed to combat this deadly disease [2]. This illness can result in intense fever, chills, headaches, and symptoms resembling the flu. Without proper treatment, it has the potential to give rise to complications such as anemia, respiratory distress, and organ failure [3]. Prevention measures include the use of mosquito nets, insect repellents, and antimalarial medication [4].

Cordia myxa, commonly known as the Assyrian Plum or Lasura, represents a medicinal plant that has a history of centuries in the context of traditional medicine systems worldwide. This plant is valued for its various therapeutic properties, including its ability to lower fever, relieve pain, treat respiratory infections, and aid in digestion [5, 6]. The C. myxa comprises bioactive ingredients among which are alkaloids, flavonoids, polyphenols, and saponins that are responsible for its medicinal properties [7]. This plant has been used to treat ailments such as coughs, colds, sore throat, asthma, and gastrointestinal disorders. Additionally, C. myxa has been recorded to contain anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial functions [8]. Due to its numerous medicinal benefits, C. myxa has importance in the field of pharmacology and medicine. Ongoing research is being conducted to further explore the therapeutic potential of this plant, including its potential as a source of novel drug compounds. Overall, C. myxa is a valuable medicinal plant that has contributed significantly to the advancement of traditional and modern medicine.

The domain of drug discovery is constantly evolving, and network pharmacology (NP) has emerged as a promising approach that integrates systematic medicine with information science [9]. By creating a network of protein-compound/disease-gene interactions, NP offers a powerful tool for identifying potential targets and developing new therapeutics [10, 11]. This approach enables researchers to study the complex interactions between multiple components in a network-targeted manner, which can lead to the creation of more safer and efficient drugs [12, 13]. The ability of NP to identify synergistic interactions among compounds in traditional medicines has transformed the framework from a “single-target, single-drug” approach to a broader and more integrative approach that takes into account the entire network of interactions involved in a disease. This has the potential to greatly enhance drug discovery and lead to the creation of more effective therapies for several ailments [14]. Wang et al. employed network pharmacology analysis to reveal the anti-malaria mechanism of Artesunate [15]. Bunga et al. utilized network pharmacology analysis to investigation of the role of Lunasia amara Blanco in the treatment of malaria [16].

The current investigation marks a considerable advancement in the area of drug discovery by employing an extensive network pharmacology (NP) dependent approach to examine the active phytochemicals of C. myxa and their potential therapeutic mechanism against malaria. As far as our knowledge is concerned, this is the first study that combines computational biology with NP for this purpose. By revealing the bioactive constituents in addition to the molecular network process of C. myxa counter to malaria at a systematic and molecular level, this research offers a better understanding of its anti-malaria effect and has the potential to expedite the drug discovery process. Molecular docking analysis also reinforces the putative molecular mechanisms of C. myxa against malaria. The molecular dynamic simulation analysis was also done to study the behavior of active compounds in the real environment. Additionally, the incorporation of machine learning predictions highlights the potential of novel computational approaches in identifying promising anti-malarial compounds from natural sources [17, 18]. Additionally, in vivo and in vitro studies are essential to fully determine the pharmaceutical potential, pharmacokinetics, and biosafety profile of C. myxa, to comprehensively assess its candidacy for use in malarial treatment.

Materials and methods

Database construction and inspection of active constituents of C. myxa

To identify and screen active compounds in C. myxa, a comprehensive search was conducted in various sources, including literature and databases. These sources included IMPPAT [19], PubMed [20], and Google Scholar [21]. The search process involved using the keyword “Cordia myxa” to retrieve relevant information from these databases. All the identified ingredients were subjected to virtual screening based on bioavailability (F) and drug-likeness (DL) measures [22]. These measures are essential in evaluating the absorption, distribution, metabolism as well as excretion (ADME) characteristics of drugable compounds. Merely ingredients with DL ≥ 0.18 and F ≥ 30% were retained [23]. SwissADME [24] and Molsoft L.L.C [25] were employed to calculate the F and DL values of all compounds respectively. Furthermore, chemical details such as the CID number, canonical smiles as well as molecular weight (MW) of filtered chemicals were retrieved through PubChem [26] and ChemSpider [27]. The 2D representation of constituents was generated from ChemDraw Ultra 12.0 [28].

ADMET profiling, which refers to Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity, plays a crucial role in the drug discovery and development process. This analysis provides valuable information about how different compounds behave in the body. By assessing absorption, researchers can estimate the drug’s potential efficacy, while understanding its distribution helps confirm whether it reaches the target site effectively. Metabolic analysis offers insights into the drug’s duration of action and possible interactions, helping identify harmful metabolites. Excretion studies are important for determining proper dosage, and toxicity assessments are vital for ensuring the safety of patients. In this study, the ADMET properties of the selected compounds were predicted using SwissADME [24] and Protox II [29].

Finding and evaluating potential target genes for C. myxa’s active ingredients

Swiss Target Prediction [30] and STITCH [31] online databases were employed to predict the potential targets of the chosen compounds of C. myxa. The identified compounds were submitted to the STITCH database, and the inquiry was confined to “Homo sapiens” to acquire the expected targets. Targets meeting the criteria of a combined score of ≥ 0.7 were exclusively taken into account for further analysis. Moreover, the online Swiss Target Prediction platform was employed for predicting potential targets, utilizing a reverse pharmacophore matching approach with the SMILES number of each constituent. Targets with a possibility ≥ 0.7 were subsequently taken for additional analysis [32, 33].

Forecasting disease-related genes stands as a pivotal initial stage in unraveling the molecular interactions of therapeutic plants in addressing numerous disorders and maladies. To anticipate genes linked to diseases, the GeneCards database [23] was queried using the keyword “Malaria,” delivering succinct genomic details and functional explanations for identified human genes. Redundant genes were excluded from the ultimate gene list to uphold accuracy. Subsequently, the expected target genes of screened C. myxa compounds overlapped with targets associated with malaria, and a Venn representation was made to recognize common targets for subsequent analysis. This allowed the identification of potential molecular targets that could be targeted by C. myxa compounds to treat malaria.

Functional enrichment and pathways analysis

To gain deeper insights into potential molecular targets, the DAVID [24] was employed for functional annotation and enrichment analysis. DAVID analysis was performed on the key targets to anticipate functionalities in three categories: biological process (BP), molecular functionality (MF), and cellular component (CC). DAVID is a widely utilized tool for the functional categorization and enrichment scrutiny of genes and proteins, offering a comprehensive overview of the biological processes involved in gene regulation. In this study, a standard for statistical relevance was established at an adjusted p-value of ≤ 0.05. The top 10 Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment labels and the top 10 KEGG pathways with larger counts were visually represented through a bubble plot. GO enrichment analysis facilitates the functional classification of genes based on their molecular functionalization, biological activity, and cellular components. KEGG pathway analysis provides a pathway-centric view of gene function and facilitates the identification of signaling pathways that are associated with specific biological processes.

Construction of networks

To attain a more profound understanding of the mechanism through which C. myxa treats malaria, network analysis was conducted using Cytoscape 3.9.1 [25]. Cytoscape is an open-access graphical user interface facilitating the import, visualization, and analysis of molecular interaction networks [26]. Within the network, nodes were employed to depict active constituents and target genes, whereas edges denoted interactions between the active elements and their associated target genes. The CytoHubba plugin [27] within Cytoscape was utilized to compute the degree, a topological feature revealing the importance of a compound, target gene, or pathway in the network graph. Target genes showing the highest degree of connection were identified as “key targets” and were subsequently explored for their potential role in the mechanism of action of C. myxa combats malaria.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

Protein-protein interactions (PPI) play a pivotal role in various biological processes due to their high versatility, adaptability, and specificity. In this investigation, the STRING [28] was employed to ascertain the functional relationship among key targets, with a cumulative score exceeding 0.4. The PPI network derived from STRING underwent further analysis using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape, a robust tool for identifying the central regulatory targets of the PPI network and the key targets essential for the overall regulatory network. This approach offers insights into the key biological processes and pathways associated with the therapeutic effects of C. myxa in malaria.

Molecular docking analysis

To validate the key target, a molecular docking approach was applied in this study. The 3D X-ray crystallography configuration of the top 2 candidate targets were retrieved and downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank [29], a global archive containing details on the 3D configurations of proteins and nucleic acids. The structures underwent refinement using UCSF Chimera [30]. The binding cavities of the target proteins were determined using CASTp v.3.0 [31], and target docking was carried out using PyRx software to elucidate the interactions between the central targets and active constituents [32]. The best-docked configurations with the lowest binding energy and root mean square deviation (RMSD) were selected. The main criterion for screening out possible elements and their predicted targets was the score of docking between the compounds and the center target. The model with the highest absolute binding affinity value and lowest RMSD was considered for each docked complex. Additionally, Chimera X [33] and Discovery Studio [34] were employed for visualizing interactions among active compounds and predicted protein/target.

Molecular dynamic simulation analysis

To examine the systems’ configurational constancy, 100 ns of molecular dynamic simulations of the best docked complexes were run. The AMBER22 simulation package was used to carry out the simulations [34]. The simulation process defined by the authors of [35] was pursued to finish the test. To put it briefly, AMBER’s antechamber module [36] was used to pre-process the systems, and the AMBER FF19SB force field [37] was applied to specify the parameters. The systems were dissolved into the OPC water box and then balanced by adding the appropriate counterions. Subsequently, the systems underwent three distinct types of energy minimization: non-heavy atom energy minimization, water box energy minimization, and hydrogen atom energy minimization. Following that, the systems become progressively warmed to 300 K while being kept at that temperature using Langevin dynamics [38]. The hydrogen bonding was subjected to constraints utilizing the SHAKE algorithm [39]. The NPT ensemble was utilized to equilibrate the pressure after the systems were brought to equilibrium for 100 ps [40]. Lastly, using a 2-fs time scale, a 100 ns production run of simulations was started. Several structural evaluations of the simulated trajectories were examined utilizing the AMBER CPPTRAJ module [41]. The trajectories were visually assessed using version 1.9.3 of the Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) application [42]. Each of the aforementioned processes was completed by protocol described by [43].

Machine learning prediction and comparison with approved malaria drugs

The malaria Inhibitor Prediction (MAIP) platform is maintained by the European Bioinformatics Institute for blood-stage malaria inhibition prediction, which is executed by a metamodel that is trained on a collection of malaria data, believed to be the largest assembled dataset [45]. We screened our compounds with MAIP to understand their relative likelihood of antimalarial activity. However, some of the datasets used in MAIP are not accessible to the public, making it unclear whether our compounds are included and outperform than expected. Therefore, we collected publicly available malaria data and trained our own Chemprop model to predict the potential antimalaria activity of compounds [46, 47] (Supplementary Information 1). Chemprop model has been demonstrated powerful and promising in the recent development of new antibiotics discovery for the treatment of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [48, 49]. The methodologies of these two models are different, with the MAIP using a fingerprint-based naïve bayes classifier, while Chemprop uses a graph message passing approach. Thus, we expect the two predictions to be complementary and somewhat orthogonal. The workflow of the current study is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Results

Identification of bioactive molecules and their respective targets

After exploring, refinement, and removing the repetitions, 10 active compounds were selected based on their bioavailability rate (F ≥ 30%) and the potential for oral drug candidacy (DL ≥ 0.18) (Table 1). Bioavailability (F) refers to the extent and speed at which the pharmacologically active substance of a medication is absorbed and reaches the systemic circulation, enhancing its efficacy [50]. Meanwhile, drug-likeness (DL) is used to measure the potential of a compound to turn into an oral medication by evaluating its structure and properties [51].

Swiss Target Prediction and STITCH databases were utilized to examine probable target genes of the 10 active constituents. As a result, 132 total targets were obtained. To further narrow down the search, 11,563 genes associated with malaria were downloaded through the GeneCards database. A Venn diagram was used to recognize overlapping targets of both malaria and active compounds, and 59 putative anti-malaria genes of C. myxa were recognized as key targets (Fig. 2A). Functional annotation and KEGG pathway analysis

To further comprehend the dynamic activity of putative targets of C. myxa against malaria, a GO enrichment and KEGG pathway investigation was performed. The GO analysis displayed that the targets of C. myxa were associated with a variety of biological processes (BP) comprising drug response, response to xenobiotic stimulus, negative regulation of metabolic processes, and more (Fig. 3A). The GO cellular component (CC) analysis revealed that these target genes were distributed in different parts of the cell, including the plasma membrane, extracellular matrix, and endoplasmic reticulum membrane among others (Fig. 3B). The GO molecular function (MF) analysis uncovered that the targets were associated with functions such as ATP binding, protein binding, protein homodimerization activity, and other functions (Fig. 3C). The KEGG pathway analysis revealed various pertinent signaling pathways associated with C. myxa’s anti-malaria action. According to this, most of the genes were implicated in pathways related to oncogenesis, Relaxin signaling pathways, PI3K-Akt signaling, and other pathways (Fig. 3D). Moreover, the KEGG pathway analysis highlighted that interleukin 6 (IL6), caspase-3 (CASP3), prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2), SRC proto-oncogene (SRC), heme oxygenase-1 (1HMOX1), Matrix Metallopeptidase-9 (MMP9), apolipoprotein E (APOE), nitric oxide synthase-3 (NOS3), Epidermal growth factor receptors (EGFR), and Matrix Metallopeptidase-2 (MMP2) were highly significant target genes.

Construction of PPI network

To investigate the prospective targets of C. myxa for malaria therapy, the 59 overlapped genes were analyzed using the STRING database to construct a PPI network. This network provided insights into the interrelationship among multiple targets throughout the progression of the disease, with nodes and their associated interactions indicating these connections. To conduct a more detailed analysis of the PPI network of the intersecting genes, the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape was employed. The results showed that IL6 (76), CASP3 (66), PTGS2 (60), SRC (56), MMP9 (50), HMOX1 (48), APOE (46), NOS3 (44), EGFR (44), and MMP2 (40) had the highest degrees of overlapping (Fig. 2C). This indicates that these genes are highly correlated with each other and may be key targets for the treatment of malaria. Comparison with enrichment analysis results pinpointed IL6 and CASP3 as the primary anti-malaria targets of C. myxa. These genes were chosen for molecular-docking experiments to determine their potential binding affinity with compounds from C. myxa.

Compound-target-pathway network construction

To enhance our understanding of the pathways in which the target is implicated, KEGG analysis was utilized to gather data on the target pathways, which was then imported into Cytoscape 3.9.1. Through this process, several pathways were identified that were top-ranked by KEGG and known to be associated with malaria. Notably, this analysis highlighted the involvement of target proteins in various metabolic pathways. These include pathways in cancer, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, proteoglycans in cancer, Relaxin and TNF signaling pathway, VEGF signaling pathway, MicroRNAs in cancer, lipid, and atherosclerosis, IL-17 and HIF-1 signaling pathway, and AGE-RAGE signaling pathway (Fig. 4). Identifying these specific pathways can provide further insights into the mechanisms underlying malaria and potentially assist in the advancement of more effective treatments for this devastating condition.

Molecular docking

The structured analysis of the PPI network was conducted to identify the top target IL6 and CASP3 for molecular docking. The crystal arrangement of IL6 (PDB ID: 5FUC) and CASP3 (PDB ID: 2DKO) were obtained through the Protein Data Bank (PDB), and structural improvement was carried out utilizing the UCSF Chimera tool. Following that, minimization of energy was executed to ensure that the protein receptors were devoid of clashes and incorrect configurations, and any atypical residues were discarded. The molecular docking process was undertaken to look for a putative target with the potential to reduce the risk of malaria. The analysis revealed strong binding affinities between the constituents and the target protein’s binding pockets, assessed through docking scores and RMSD (Table 4). Protein 5FUC exhibited the highest binding energies with cosmosiin (-7.7 kcal/mol), robinetin (-7.6 kcal/mol), and stigmastanol (-7.4 kcal/mol). The RMSD of cosmosiin, robinetin, and stigmastanol was 3.222 Å, 1.638 Å, and 0.951 Å respectively. Notably, all compounds exhibited robust hydrogen bonding with the conserved LYS B:171 except beta-sitosterol and allantoin (Fig. 5).

Molecular dynamic simulation analysis

Molecular dynamic simulation studies are necessary to verify macromolecules’ dynamic behavior [34]. The radius of gyration (RoG) [52], root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) [35, 53], and root mean square deviation (RMSD) [53] are all included in the simulations analysis. The carbon alpha atom of the complexes served as the reference for these investigations. The studies aimed to assess the persistence and stability of interactions between the ligand and receptor during the simulation. The accurate delivery of the ligand to the IL6 target protein hinges on stable receptor-ligand interactions. Initially, no significant structural alterations were observed, as indicated by the consistent RMSD plots of the systems. The highest RMSD values of the systems reached up to 3 Å, with mean values for IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin calculated at 3.11 Å and 3.31 Å, respectively, and maximum values were 4.32 Å and 4.89 Å, respectively (Fig. 7A). Secondly, RMSF was computed to reveal details about the flexibility of receptor residues in the presence of the ligand molecule (Fig. 7B). Most system residues exhibited average stability (< 5 Å). The RMSF values for IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin showed maximum values of 5.68 Å and 6.44 Å, mean values of 1.57 Å and 1.93 Å, and minimum values of 0.66 Å and 0.77 Å, respectively. Radius of Gyration (RoG) analysis was used to assess system compactness over time. Increased flexibility in certain residues was attributed to internal loop dynamics within the system, but did not affect ligand binding to the receptors. RoG values indicated maximum values of 15.91 Å and 15.64 Å, mean values of 15.52 Å and 15.37 Å, and minimum values of 15.17 Å and 15.11 Å for IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin, respectively (Fig. 7C). At the end of the simulation, RMSD indicated that each system remained compact with no significant changes, except for IL6_Robinetin which exhibited some initial fluctuations that stabilized towards the end of the simulation. The following results were derived from the Beta Factor study: The mean values for IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin were 78.55 Å and 121.48 Å, respectively; the lowest values were 11.68 Å and 15.61 Å, while the maximum values were 851.67 Å and 1092.63 Å (Fig. 7D).

MMPB/GBSA analysis

The chosen docked complexes were subjected to MMPB/GBSA analysis to investigate their binding affinity with the receptor protein. These techniques are generally considered more effective in assessing the interaction between the docked ligand and the receptor protein. It was observed that strong intermolecular interactions were formed, leading to stable complexes, as indicated by the highly negative net binding energies of all docked and control complexes. Among these interactions, the van der Waals force was found to be the strongest, contributing to ligand docking at the designated site and promoting system stability. Specifically, the IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin complexes exhibited net van der Waals energies of -62.48 kcal/mol and − 55.38 kcal/mol, respectively. Furthermore, a consistent electrostatic energy was observed in each docked complex. Among all the computed energies, solvation energy had the least favorable contribution to the net energy, with a negative value. In MM-GBSA, the IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin complexes showed net solvation energies of 10.39 kcal/mol and 8.67 kcal/mol, respectively. Conversely, in MM/PBSA, the net solvation energies of IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin were 9.67 kcal/mol and 8 kcal/mol, respectively. Prediction and comparison with approved malaria drugs through machine learning

To evaluate the potential activities of our 10 screened compounds, we upload them to the MAIP platform obtain MAIP predictions, and predict them with our trained Chemprop model. Combining the predictions of the two models, we calculate the weighted score (Supplementary Information 2). To understand the similarity between our compounds and approved antimalaria drugs. 31 approved antimalaria drugs (Supplementary Information 3) were obtained from DrugBank [49]. To describe a larger drug similar chemical space, physically similar but topologically distinct, drug-dissimilar decoys were generated by DUD-E [54] based on the drugs. RDKit [55] was used to generate the Morgan fingerprint of compounds, then t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [56] and a grid regularization using the Jonker-Volgenant (JV) algorithm were used to show the chemical space (Fig. 9). This process generates a grid of molecules where adjacent entries are highly similar, preserving the strongest relationships while allowing for greater embedding flexibility as similarity decreases.

Discussion

Malaria poses a severe health risk and is instigated by the Plasmodium parasite, with transmission occurring via the stings of Anopheles mosquitoes, primarily the female carriers of the infection [57]. It is widespread in tropical and subtropical areas globally, encompassing regions such as Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The disease can be caused by any of the five species of the parasite, with P. falciparum being the deadliest [58]. Malaria poses a significant public health concern, affecting millions of people annually, with over 400,000 reported deaths each year [59]. The disease spreads when an infected mosquito stings a person, injecting the parasite into the bloodstream. Upon entering the body, the parasites undergo multiplication in the liver and subsequently invade red blood cells, giving rise to the onset of symptoms. Malaria transmission can occur through blood transfusions, needle sharing, or from an infected mother to her child during pregnancy or childbirth [60].

Natural products derived from medicinal plants are acknowledged for their abundant reserves of medicinal agents due to the presence of naturally occurring compounds [17]. Their structural diversity, multi-target action, and low toxicity make natural products and their derivatives a prominent focus in the development of targeted drugs, constituting nearly half of all clinically used pharmaceuticals [61]. This has emerged as a prominent area of research, leveraging high-throughput techniques to screen the pharmacological effectiveness of herbal medicines for drug discovery. The discovery of bioactive compounds with the potential to alleviate the pathophysiology of diverse disorders and diseases is regarded as a substantial breakthrough in modern times [62].

C. myxa, commonly known as “Assyrian Plum” or “Lasura,” is a medicinal plant that has been traditionally used in various regions of the world for its therapeutic properties. The plant is known for its potent anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, and antimicrobial properties. The fruit of C. myxa is a rich source of vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants, which make it an effective natural remedy for several ailments. The plant has been used to treat respiratory infections, asthma, bronchitis, and other respiratory disorders [7]. It is also known to improve digestion, relieve constipation, and alleviate stomach ulcers. C. myxa has been found to possess potent wound-healing properties and has been used as a natural remedy for cuts, wounds, and burns. Furthermore, research studies have revealed that C. myxa possesses anticancer properties and has the potential to reduce the growth of cancer cells. The therapeutic benefits of C. myxa have led to its increasing importance in traditional medicine and have sparked interest in further scientific research to explore its medicinal potential [63]. This research is a landmark study in the preliminary screening of bioactive compounds found in C. myxa and provides a novel therapeutic concept for further investigation of the mechanisms involved in malaria treatment. Our screening results have shown that flavonoids and steroids are the primary bioactive compounds of C. myxa, playing a crucial role in the establishment of malaria by impacting the IL6 and CASP3 genes. Moreover, molecular docking has confirmed our findings, demonstrating stable binding forces between core compounds and key target. Molecular docking analysis revealed that cosmosiin, stigmastanol, and robinetin exhibit strong binding affinity with IL6. On the other hand, cosmosiin, robinetin, and quercetin showed strong binding affinity with CASP3.

IL6 blockade has been shown to reduce the instances of malaria in animal models through various mechanisms. Research indicates that IL6 plays a crucial role in the immune response to malaria infection. By inhibiting IL6, the inflammatory cascade triggered by the parasite is attenuated, leading to a less severe disease course [64]. Additionally, IL6 blockade can modulate the cytokine environment, promoting a balanced immune response that effectively controls the parasitic load. This has been demonstrated in studies using mouse models, where IL6 inhibition resulted in decreased parasitemia and improved survival rates [65].

Inhibiting CASP3 could serve as a novel therapeutic strategy in malaria by preventing the premature apoptosis of infected red blood cells, thereby disrupting the parasite’s replication cycle [66]. Blocking CASP3 in mouse models of malaria has been shown to reduce the parasite burden by preventing the premature destruction of infected red blood cells, thereby limiting parasite replication and spread [67]. Additionally, CASP3 inhibition helps protect vital immune cells from apoptosis, enhancing the host’s immune response and reducing the severity of malaria-related symptoms in these models [68].

The molecular dynamic simulation analysis carried out in this study has provided significant insights into the behavior of the IL6_Cosmosiin and IL6_Robinetin complexes. The analysis of RMSD revealed that both complexes maintained structural stability throughout the simulation period, with only minor fluctuations observed in IL6_Robinetin towards the end of the simulation. This indicates that the interactions between the ligands and receptors were stable and that the ligands successfully interacted with the IL6 target protein. Furthermore, the analysis of RMSF demonstrated that the residues of the receptors exhibited average stability, providing further support for the stability of the ligand-receptor complexes. The analysis of RoG indicated that the systems were compact and possessed a consistent degree of flexibility, which did not impact the binding of the ligand-receptor complexes. The analysis of SASA revealed that the ligands displayed varying degrees of interaction with the solvent molecules, with IL6_Cosmosiin having a higher average SASA value in comparison to IL6_Robinetin. Finally, the analysis of MMPB/GBSA demonstrated that both complexes formed strong intermolecular systems with negative net binding energies, suggesting stable ligand-receptor interactions. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the potential therapeutic applications of C. myxa for the treatment of malaria.

The GO functional analysis of C. myxa’s anti-malaria targets has indicated their involvement in response to the drug, ATP binding, and protein binding activity. These targets play a crucial role in the regulation of the immune response, inflammation, and oxidative stress, all of which are critical factors in the pathogenesis of malaria. KEGG pathway studies have further revealed that these targets are concentrated in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, which is a critical regulator of cell survival and apoptosis, as well as in pathways in cancer, which are implicated in cell proliferation and differentiation [69]. Interestingly, Relaxin signaling pathways have also been identified as potential targets of C. myxa’s anti-malaria properties. Relaxin is recognized for its involvement in the regulation of inflammatory response, tissue remodeling, and cell growth, and therefore represents a prospective therapeutic target for malaria treatment [70]. In conclusion, this research has identified promising targets and pathways for the development of novel anti-malaria therapies using C. myxa. Continued investigation into these targets and pathways holds the potential to advance the development of more effective and safe treatments for this disease.

The integration of machine learning predictions and comparative analysis with approved malaria drugs has provided valuable insights into the potential effectiveness of natural compounds derived from C. myxa in the treatment of malaria. The Chemprop model, which was trained on a diverse dataset consisting of malaria bioactivity data from multiple sources, demonstrated strong performance with an overall test AUC of 0.868590 ± 0.007887. This emphasizes the usefulness of machine learning approaches in predicting the bioactivity of compounds, offering a quick and cost-effective way to identify promising drug candidates. Additionally, by combining MAIP predictions and Chemprop predictions, weighted scores were obtained that comprehensively assessed the bioactivity of the selected compounds [71]. Interestingly, several compounds, such as Cosmosiin and Stigmastanol, displayed high weighted scores, indicating their potential as effective anti-malarial agents. Furthermore, when compared to approved malaria drugs from DrugBank, similarities in bioactivity profiles were observed, suggesting potential similarities in the mechanisms of action between natural compounds and existing pharmaceuticals. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of natural compounds from C. myxa and justify further investigation to understand their specific modes of action and optimize their efficacy in the treatment of malaria.

Although network pharmacology has become a popular approach for identifying potential drug targets, it has its limitations. The basic pharmacological mechanisms for the treatment of malaria using this approach are only achieved through data mining from different databases, which can be subject to discrepancies due to various sources of information and experimental data. To overcome this issue, modern high-throughput chemical identification techniques such as ultra-performance liquid chromatography-electrospray mass spectroscopy can be employed to validate the identified bioactive compounds [72]. While the results of this study on C. myxa are promising, further studies and clinical trials are necessary to validate its medicinal uses and potential as a therapeutic agent for malaria. Therefore, it is important to continue investigating the mechanisms of action of C. myxa, to conduct more extensive clinical trials to evaluate its efficacy and safety. In the end, this research has the potential to pave the way for the development of novel treatment options for individuals affected by malaria.

Conclusion

This study investigates the potential of C. myxa as a source of bioactive compounds for malaria treatment. We identified ten active compounds, predominantly flavonoids and steroids, with promising bioavailability and drug-likeness properties. These compounds have multi-targeted interactions, suggesting a synergistic effect in malaria treatment. Molecular docking simulations further support the strong binding affinity between core compounds and key target proteins. Our study also elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying the efficacy of C. myxa compounds against malaria, highlighting their impact on crucial pathways and target genes associated with immune response and cell survival. Integration of machine learning predictions and comparative analysis with approved malaria drugs validate the bioactivity and structural similarity of C. myxa compounds to existing antimalarial agents, supporting their candidacy for drug development. Although our study has limitations, including the need for experimental validation and clinical trials, its insights lay a solid foundation for the continued exploration of C. myxa as a natural remedy for malaria and offer promising prospects for the development of novel, effective, and safer treatment options for this debilitating disease.
