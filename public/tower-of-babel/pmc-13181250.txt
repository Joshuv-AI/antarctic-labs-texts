Time-Critical Cardiovascular Risk After COVID-19: A Population-Based Analysis Across Variant Eras

McLeod, Christoffel J. van Niekerk, Bryan Taylor, DeLisa Fairweather, Carlos Vergara Sanchez, Matt Utset, Ufuk Vardar, Robert D. McBane, Claire Raphael, Jack Kiernan, Tahir Kafil, Leslie T. ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo define the timing and intensity of cardiovascular risk after severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection across the pre-Delta, Delta, and Omicron eras.Patients and MethodsWe conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study of 162,471 adults with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection from March 1, 2020 to December 31, 2023 using the Rochester Epidemiology Project, a medical records-linkage system covering a 27-county region in Minnesota and Wisconsin. Outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular events, thrombotic events, and dysrhythmias, identified using validated International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes. Cumulative incidence accounted for the competing risk of death, and multivariable Cox regression assessed age- and variant-specific risk.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 1.9 years (interquartile range, 1.2-2.4 years), 4922 individuals experienced major adverse cardiovascular events. Risk was highest within 30 days after infection and declined thereafter (P<.0001). During this early hazard window, adults aged ≥80 years had substantially higher adjusted risk in the pre-Delta and Delta eras compared with Omicron (pre-Delta hazard ratio, 3.89 [95% CI, 3.17-4.77]; Delta hazard ratio, 2.91 [95% CI, 2.28-3.71]). Thrombotic events showed a similar early, age-dependent pattern, peaking during the pre-Delta and Delta eras. Early dysrhythmia rates rose steeply with age and peaked during the Delta era, exceeding 60 events per 1000 person-months among adults aged ≥80 years. Late dysrhythmia incidence was stable across eras, reflecting persistent age-related risk.ConclusionCardiovascular events after corona virus disease 2019 infection clustered within the first 30 days, with variation by age and variant era. This early concentration, most pronounced among older adults during the pre-Delta and Delta eras, is supportive of a clinically relevant hazard window while distinguishing observed patterns from causal inference. These findings support targeted, time-sensitive cardiovascular monitoring in higher-risk populations.

The cardiovascular consequences of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection have emerged as a major global concern. Early in the pandemic, reports described acute cardiovascular complications, including myocardial infarction, stroke, arrhythmias, and thrombotic events in the days to weeks after corona virus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection.1, 2, 3, 4 Histopathologic studies confirmed direct endothelial infection and diffuse endotheliitis, providing a biological basis for early thrombotic and vascular injury.5

Attention soon shifted to the postacute and long-term cardiovascular sequelae of COVID-19. Large population-based studies suggested that risk for cardiovascular disease may remain elevated for months after recovery, raising concern for a chronic “long COVID” cardiovascular syndrome.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Despite these insights, the timing, magnitude, and duration of risk remain incompletely defined. Most prior studies emphasized cumulative long-term outcomes, providing limited insight into the timing and duration of peak cardiovascular risk.

In addition, how cardiovascular risk evolved across viral variant eras, age groups, and shifting patterns of immunity and care delivery is unknown. Defining these temporal and demographic boundaries is essential for guiding surveillance, prevention, resource utilization, and mechanistic investigation.

We therefore used the population-based Rochester Epidemiology Project (REP) to characterize cardiovascular outcomes after SARS-CoV-2 infection across 3 pandemic eras. Our aim was to identify the timing and intensity of cardiovascular risk, evaluate patterns of early event clustering after infection, and assess how this risk varies by age and viral variant.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

We conducted this study at Mayo Clinic using the REP, a population-based research infrastructure encompassing a 27-county region in southern Minnesota and western Wisconsin.11, 12, 13, 14 The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic institutional review board (IRB #24-004421) and determined to be exempt from the requirement for informed consent given the use of deidentified, population-level data. However, patients who declined Minnesota research authorization at 1 or more sites were excluded. The cohort included 162,471 adults (aged ≥18 years) diagnosed with COVID-19 between March 2020 and December 2023, identified through laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection (Supplemental Table 1, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org). During the early pandemic period (spring-summer 2020), diagnostic testing within the REP region was primarily polymerase chain reaction–based and limited to symptomatic or hospitalized individuals. By late 2020, the expansion of regional laboratory capacity through the Mayo Clinic network enabled widespread community and asymptomatic screening. Rapid antigen and home testing became broadly available in 2021, with documentation of self-reported positive results in the electronic health record increasing substantially during the Omicron era. To ensure comprehensive case ascertainment across variant eras, we included individuals identified by any of the following: a positive laboratory test, a documented self-reported home test, or a prescription for Paxlovid temporally consistent with acute infection. This approach provided robust coverage across evolving testing modalities and pandemic phases.

Outcomes

We examined 3 prespecified composite cardiovascular categories:(1)Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE): myocardial infarction, stroke, and all-cause mortality, consistent with prior landmark COVID-19 outcomes studies. Arrhythmic outcomes, including life-threatening arrhythmias (ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation), were analyzed separately within the broader dysrhythmia category to align with prior definitions of MACE and improve interpretability.(2)Thrombotic complications: deep vein thrombosis, superficial vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism.(3)Dysrhythmias: Atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, heart block, atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia, atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and ventricular tachycardia were included. Only new diagnoses occurring after the index SARS-CoV-2 infection were included as events. Individuals with a documented diagnosis of atrial fibrillation or flutter before the index date were excluded from the dysrhythmia end point to restrict analyses to incident post-COVID events.

Diagnoses were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, which were derived from validated definitions used in prior REP and national cohort studies. To assess coding accuracy, we performed a targeted manual chart review of a random sample of cases for each outcome, including dysrhythmia subtypes. For each subtype, 10 cases were reviewed, including 5 incident events to confirm timing and clinical context and 5 with pre-existing diagnoses to confirm accurate diagnostic classification. These reviews demonstrated high concordance between coded diagnoses and clinically adjudicated findings. Events were further categorized by timing: early (≤30 days after infection) and late (>30 days after infection). The validity of outcome ascertainment within the REP has been demonstrated in multiple prior studies.15, 16, 17 Patients who experienced events of each type before infection were excluded from those specific outcome analyses. Patients were censored at the last known clinical follow-up or when moving out of the 27-county region.

Statistical Analysis

Incidence of MACE was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and for nonfatal outcomes, the cumulative incidence was estimated accounting for the competing risk of death. Multivariable cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models estimated associations between the COVID-19 variant era and cardiovascular outcomes with adjustment for covariates. These results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with associated 95% confidence limits. Analyses were stratified by 4 predefined age groups (<40, 40-59, 60-79, and ≥80 years) selected to balance clinical interpretability with statistical power.

These ∼20-year age bands reflect distinct biological and cardiovascular risk profiles:•18-39 years: generally low baseline cardiovascular risk but possible susceptibility to myocarditis or postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome.18, 19, 20, 21•40-59 years: transitional age for atherosclerotic disease, particularly in males, allowing evaluation of excess ischemic risk.22,23•60-79 years: accumulation of comorbidities, potentially vulnerable to COVID-related cardiac remodeling or delayed mortality.24,25•≥80 years: highest expected event burden, with a greater proportion of women due to the delayed onset of cardiovascular disease.26,27

This stratified approach was chosen to enhance the detection of age-specific patterns that might otherwise be obscured in broader groupings. Given the observed differences between rates of events and differences in associations with variant over time, analyses for all outcomes were separated into events within 30 days and beyond 30 days. Outcome analyses beyond 30 days excluded patients who experienced the event within 30 days. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed visually within each time window by plotting of Schoenfeld residuals. All analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute), and statistical significance was defined as P<.01 for all analyses, with this more stringent threshold selected to reduce the likelihood of type I error given multiple comparisons

Results

MACE

Within the first 30 days after infection, MACE risk was highest, with a marked decline thereafter (Figure 1; Supplemental Figure 1 and Supplemental Table 2, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org). Crude 30-day event rates rose sharply with age: among adults ≥80 years of age, rates reached 140 per 1000 person-months in the pre-Delta era and 97 per 1000 person-months during Delta, compared with 36 per 1000 person-months in the Omicron era. Adults aged 60-79 years showed a similar pattern, with rates of 17 per 1000 person-months in pre-Delta and 20 per 1000 person-months in Delta vs 6 per 1000 person-months in Omicron. In the 40-59 age group, rates increased from 1.8 per 1000 person-months in pre-Delta to 3.8 per 1000 person-months during Delta before declining to 1.8 per 1000 person-months in Omicron. In contrast, adults aged <40 years had uniformly low rates across eras (<1 per 1000 person-months).

These crude patterns aligned with adjusted relative risks (Table 1): adults aged ≥80 years had the highest early hazard when compared with Omicron era infections of the same age (pre-Delta HR, 3.89; 95% CI, 3.17-4.77; Delta HR, 2.91; 95% CI, 2.28-3.71). Elevated risks were also observed in adults aged 60-79 years (pre-Delta HR, 3.09; 95% CI, 2.44-3.92; Delta HR, 3.72; 95% CI, 2.90-4.76) and 40-59 years (Delta HR, 2.33; 95% CI, 1.48-3.65). Although there were similar elevations in adults aged <40 years, absolute MACE rates in this age group were low across all eras. When death was examined as a competing risk, differences in risks of myocardial infarction and stroke across eras were attenuated, underscoring the substantial contribution of all-cause mortality to the early MACE burden. Beyond 30 days after infection, MACE risk was substantially lower than during the acute period but continued to rise with age (Figure 1; Supplemental Figure 1 and Supplemental Table 3, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org). Crude rates among adults aged ≥80 years ranged from 119 to 138 events per 1000 person-years across eras, compared with 26-29 per 1000 person-years in those aged 60-79 years and 7-8 per 1000 person-years in adults aged 40-59 years. Adults aged <40 years had the lowest late-period rates, between 1.2 and 1.8 events per 1000 person-years. Adjusted models similarly demonstrated modest era-specific differences. Compared with Omicron era infections, individuals aged ≥80 years had a slightly elevated late risk during the pre-Delta (HR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.07-1.40) and Delta eras (HR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.01-1.37), whereas adults aged 40-79 years showed no significant inter-era variation (Supplemental Table 4, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org). Adults aged <40 years had small relative reductions in late MACE risk during the pre-Delta (HR, 0.67) and Delta (HR, 0.81) eras compared with Omicron. Overall, these data indicate that MACE risk beyond 30 days is low and age-dependent, with major era-related differences largely absent after the acute phase.

Thrombotic Events

A total of 2090 thrombotic events occurred during follow-up. Within 30 days, thrombotic risk varied by variant era and age (Figure 2; Supplemental Table 2). Events were rare in adults aged <40 years, with crude rates ranging from 0.66 to 1.74 per 1000 person-months across eras. In contrast, risk increased progressively with age. Among adults aged 60-79 years, rates reached 11.6 per 1000 person-months in the pre-Delta era and 15.0 per 1000 person-months during Delta, falling to 3.7 per 1000 person-months in the Omicron era. The highest early incidence occurred in adults aged ≥80 years, with rates of 26.5 per 1000 person-months in the pre-Delta era and 21.3 per 1000 person-months during Delta, compared with 7.0 per 1000 person-months in the Omicron era. A significant COVID-19 era × age interaction confirmed this age-dependent gradient in early thrombotic risk (P=.01), whereas no interactions were observed with sex, race, or Hispanic ethnicity. Taken together, these patterns show a substantial early thrombotic hazard in older adults (particularly during the pre-Delta and Delta eras) contrasted with minimal absolute risk in younger adults.

Beyond 30 days after infection, thrombotic event rates were low across all age groups and showed minimal variation across variant eras (Supplemental Figure 2; Supplemental Table 3). Among adults aged ≥80 years, crude thrombotic rates ranged from 17.8 to 24.7 per 1000 person-years across eras, with no significant COVID era × age interaction (P=.14). Adults aged 60-79 years exhibited similarly stable rates (9.4-10.3 per 1000 person-years), and individuals aged 40-59 years had rates between 4.6 and 5.0 per 1000 person-years. Consistent with early findings, thrombotic complications remained rare in those <40 years (1.6-2.1 per 1000 person-years). Overall, late thrombotic risk showed substantial attenuation compared with the early hazard window and converged across variant eras, indicating little sustained excess risk in the postacute period. Adjusted analyses paralleled these patterns. Early thrombotic risk was significantly higher during the pre-Delta and Delta eras in adults aged ≥40 years (HRs ranging from 1.72 to 4.24 vs Omicron; Table 1), whereas those aged <40 years showed more attenuated excess early risk. Beyond 30 days, HRs across eras were not significantly different from 1.0 for all age groups with no significant interactions (Supplemental Table 4), indicating that excess risk did not persist during longer-term follow-up.

Dysrhythmia

Dysrhythmias occurred in 6765 individuals overall. Within the first 30 days after infection, dysrhythmia incidence varied substantially by variant era and age (Supplemental Table 2; Figure 3). Crude early event rates rose with both age and earlier variant eras: among adults aged ≥80 years, rates were 47.3 per 1000 person-months in the pre-Delta era and 68.9 per 1000 person-months during Delta, compared with 22.2 per 1000 person-months in the Omicron era. Adults aged 60-79 years showed a similar gradient (pre-Delta, 20.2; Delta, 21.8; Omicron, 10.5 per 1000 person-months), whereas rates were markedly lower among those aged <40 years (1.9-2.9 per 1000 person-months across eras).

Consistent with these patterns, early dysrhythmia risk was significantly elevated during the Delta era for adults aged ≥40 years, with the highest hazards observed among those aged ≥80 years (HR, 3.08; 95% CI, 2.08-4.56) and 60-79 years (HR, 2.23; 95% CI, 1.75-2.85); adults aged 40-59 years also demonstrated increased early risk during Delta (HR, 2.74; 95% CI, 1.94-3.86) (Table 1). A significant COVID-19 era × age interaction was present in the first 30 days (P=.006), confirming age-specific differences in early susceptibility. No significant interactions were observed by race or Hispanic ethnicity.

Beyond 30 days, dysrhythmia incidence increased progressively with age but showed minimal variation across COVID-19 eras (Supplemental Table 3). Among adults aged ≥80 years, crude late-period rates were 99.9 per 1000 person-years in the pre-Delta era, 111.6 per 1000 person-years during Delta, and 108.5 per 1000 person-years in the Omicron era, indicating persistently high but relatively stable long-term incidence across variants. Adults aged 60-79 years exhibited similar consistency, with rates of 51.2, 51.7, and 54.6 per 1000 person-years across the pre-Delta, Delta, and Omicron eras, respectively. Rates were lower in adults aged 40-59 years (17.2-18.9 per 1000 person-years) and lowest in adults aged <40 years (8.3-9.7 per 1000 person-years).

In adjusted models, no significant COVID-19 era × age interaction was observed (P=.20). HRs for the pre-Delta and Delta eras relative to Omicron were consistently near 1.0 across all age groups (Supplemental Table 4), confirming the absence of meaningful long-term era-specific differences. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that dysrhythmia burden during extended follow-up is driven primarily by age, whereas excess risk attributable to SARS-CoV-2 is confined to the first 30 days after infection.

Dysrhythmias were heterogeneous in composition, with marked variation by subtype and age group. Within 30 days of infection, sinus node dysfunction accounted for the largest proportion of events, particularly among younger and middle-aged adults, whereas atrial fibrillation predominated among older adults. Ventricular arrhythmias (ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation) were comparatively infrequent across all groups. Similar age-dependent patterns were observed beyond 30 days, with overall distributions remaining stable over time. Discussion

In this large, community-based cohort spanning 3 COVID-19 variant eras, we observed a marked clustering of cardiovascular events within the first 30 days after SARS-CoV-2 infection. This early period was characterized by increased detection of MACE, thrombotic events, and dysrhythmias, with risk most pronounced during the pre-Delta and Delta eras among older adults. Beyond 30 days, risk attenuated sharply and converged across variant eras. Although this pattern may in part reflect increased health care contact and diagnostic intensity in the peri-infectious period, the consistency, magnitude, and temporal coherence of early event clustering across outcomes and subgroups support a clinically meaningful, time-limited concentration of cardiovascular events after infection. Accordingly, our findings highlight a time-sensitive period of elevated risk after SARS-CoV-2 infection while distinguishing observed event patterns from definitive causal inference.

Our findings build on and refine prior evidence describing the cardiovascular consequences of SARS-CoV-2 infection. The landmark Veterans Affairs study by Xie et al1 reported elevated 1-year risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, arrhythmias, and thromboembolism, particularly among hospitalized individuals; however, outcome ascertainment relied exclusively on administrative diagnostic codes, and the cohort primarily comprised older male veterans. Analyses from population registries in South Korea and Japan also demonstrated persistent cardiovascular risk extending up to 18 months after infection, although event capture varied across institutions and data systems, introducing heterogeneity in diagnostic ascertainment.7 In contrast, the present study leveraged the REP’s unified medical records infrastructure, which enables comprehensive capture of encounters, diagnoses, procedures, and hospitalizations across all health care systems within a defined population of southern Minnesota and western Wisconsin. Cardiovascular outcomes were identified using validated ICD-10 codes, supplemented by a manual review of a subset of cases to ensure diagnostic accuracy.

Importantly, prior studies emphasized cumulative long-term risk without resolving how cardiovascular outcomes vary across viral variant eras or how risk is distributed over time after infection. By providing temporal granularity and era-specific estimates, our population-based design delineates how the cardiovascular burden of COVID-19 evolved across successive phases of the pandemic and clarifies the timing and context in which risk is greatest. Most notably, our findings highlight 3 defining features of COVID-19-associated cardiovascular risk: its marked temporal concentration within the first 30 days, strong age dependence, and attenuation across successive variant eras.

Dysrhythmias and Mechanistic Insights

The attenuation of risk during the Omicron era likely reflects a combination of reduced viral virulence, increased population immunity through vaccination or prior infection, and improvements in clinical management.4 Nonetheless, despite the sharp concentration of dysrhythmia risk within the first 30 days after infection, a measurable burden persisted over long-term follow-up, particularly among older adults. These observations are consistent with proposed mechanisms, including immune activation, autonomic dysfunction, and myocardial inflammation after SARS-CoV-2 infection.28, 29, 30 Prior imaging and translational studies have demonstrated persistent vascular and myocardial inflammation, which may contribute to electrical instability.31, 32, 33

These mechanisms, though hypothesis-generating in the context of our observational design, are consistent with our finding that sinus node dysfunction was the most common dysrhythmia subtype, comprising 60%-70% of early and late events across variant eras (Table 2). Persistent inflammatory and autonomic disturbances may impair sinus node automaticity, whereas immune-mediated myocardial remodeling and fibrosis may create substrates for both bradyarrhythmias and re-entrant tachyarrhythmias. Together, these pathways offer biologically plausible hypotheses for the sustained dysrhythmia burden observed in our community-based cohort.

Ischemic Risk

Our study also clarifies ischemic risk trajectories. Although early declines in myocardial infarction hospitalizations were initially attributed to care avoidance and health system strain,34,35 accumulating evidence indicates that ischemic risk is highest within the first 30 days after infection, with much smaller increases thereafter.1,7 Consistent with this, myocardial infarction risk in our cohort peaked early and rapidly attenuated, without a discernible subacute window.

Mechanistically, SARS-CoV-2 infection has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and inflammatory pathways that may promote plaque instability.36, 37, 38, 39, 40 These pathways provide biologically plausible explanations for the observed early concentration of ischemic events, although causal inference cannot be established in this study.

Thrombotic Events

Thrombotic events in our cohort were similarly front-loaded. Within the first 30 days, adults aged ≥80 years experienced crude rates of 26.5 per 1000 person-months in the pre-Delta era and 21.3 in the Delta era, over 3-fold higher than during Omicron. In contrast, events were rare in adults aged <40 years. Although reduced mobility during acute illness or early pandemic restrictions may have contributed, the magnitude of early thrombotic risk and its consistency across variant eras suggest an important biological contribution from SARS-CoV-2 itself.

A low but persistent rate of thrombotic events continued beyond 30 days, particularly among older adults. Whether this late risk exceeds background rates is uncertain, but our findings align with recent Singapore data showing modest, sustained increases in vascular events up to 3 years post-infection.41 Mechanistic studies suggest persistent endothelial dysfunction and vascular injury after infection, which may contribute to a prothrombotic milieu, particularly in older adults.42 Together, these observations suggest that SARS-CoV-2 may establish a lingering prothrombotic milieu in older adults, while absolute risk remains very low in younger populations.

MACE

When interpreted alongside pre-pandemic cardiovascular benchmarks, the magnitude and timing of MACE risk observed in our study are striking. Within the first 30 days after infection, adults aged ≥80 years experienced event rates of 140 per 1000 person-months in the pre-Delta era and 97 per 1000 person-months during Delta, far exceeding expected background rates of myocardial infarction, stroke, or all-cause mortality. Adults aged 60-79 years showed similar patterns, with early crude rates of 17-20 per 1000 person-months during the pre-Delta and Delta eras. These sharp, age-dependent gradients translated into 3- to 4-fold higher early HRs in the pre-Delta and Delta eras compared with Omicron, underscoring a substantial, variant-modified acute cardiovascular impact. By contrast, adults aged <40 years demonstrated uniformly low early event rates (<1 per 1000 person-months), consistent with baseline epidemiology.

Although some early MACE likely reflected complications of severe COVID-19 illness (eg, hypoxia, systemic inflammation, or critical-care exposure), the marked clustering of events within the first 30 days highlights a time-sensitive period of increased clinical risk after infection. This early composite signal is likely influenced, in part, by all-cause mortality during acute illness and should not be interpreted as reflecting uniform increases in individual nonfatal cardiovascular events. This distinction is particularly important in older adults, in whom competing risks and frailty substantially influence cardiovascular risk estimation and event interpretation.43 Importantly, these era-specific differences largely disappeared beyond 30 days, when MACE incidence fell to stable, age-dependent background levels and HRs converged across variant eras.

This temporal pattern aligns closely with national excess mortality data. Han et al3 reported 2 major peaks in cardiovascular deaths during March-June 2020 and June-November 2021, corresponding directly to our observed pre-Delta and Delta hazard windows. Taken together, these findings suggest that variant era viral burden and inflammatory severity, rather than prolonged postacute sequelae alone, may have been key drivers of acute cardiovascular morbidity during the early pandemic.

Clinical and Public Health Implications

From a clinical perspective, these findings support targeted cardiovascular monitoring during the first 30 days after SARS-CoV-2 infection, particularly among older adults. Beyond this early period, event rates were low and stable across variant eras, suggesting that extended routine surveillance may offer limited benefit for most patients. These data define a time-limited window of elevated risk that may help guide pragmatic, resource-conscious clinical strategies during future waves or similar infectious outbreaks.

Strengths and Limitations

Finally, our study design offers several strengths. The REP integrates clinical and administrative data from multiple health systems, providing near-complete population capture within a defined geographic region. This linkage allows cross-validation of diagnostic codes against the medical record, reducing misclassification and enhancing accuracy compared with administrative data alone. Manual validation of ICD-10 outcomes in a subset further supports internal validity. As new variants emerge and vaccine strategies evolve, ongoing surveillance will be critical to track cardiovascular outcomes and inform prevention strategies.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, we lacked granular data on illness severity, hospitalization, and vaccination timing, which may influence cardiovascular risk trajectories and vary across pandemic eras. Although the population-based design of the REP captures events that come to clinical attention, differences in health care utilization, testing practices, and thresholds for evaluation over time may have influenced event detection, particularly in the early postinfectious period. In addition, although SARS-CoV-2 infection was primarily identified through laboratory-based testing within the REP system, a small proportion of cases (<10%) may have been identified through home-based testing, which could introduce minor variability in exposure ascertainment; however, the low frequency of such cases makes it unlikely to meaningfully influence the observed patterns. Second, variant classification was based on calendar time rather than viral sequencing, introducing potential misclassification; however, infected populations may have differed across eras with respect to prior immunity, vaccination uptake, clinical management, and hospitalization patterns, all of which may contribute to observed differences independent of variant-specific biology (Supplemental Table 5, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org). Thus, era-based comparisons should be interpreted as reflecting a combination of viral, host, and health care system factors rather than direct measures of variant-specific risk alone. Third, our analysis did not include an uninfected comparator group. The widespread adoption of unreported home testing, particularly during the Omicron era, made it infeasible to reliably identify truly uninfected individuals, and a matched control design would have introduced substantial exposure misclassification. Instead, we leveraged within-cohort temporal variation across variant eras, an approach that minimizes bias and aligns with our primary aim of defining variant-specific cardiovascular risk. Fourth, the study population was predominantly White and Midwestern, which may limit generalizability; however, prior analyses have demonstrated that the demographic and mortality characteristics of Olmsted County are broadly comparable to those of the Upper Midwest and the United States overall.44 Finally, although ascertainment bias may have increased event capture during acute COVID-19, particularly among hospitalized patients, and some mild late events may have gone unreported, the consistent attenuation of clinically detected events beyond 30 days across variant eras supports the conclusion that the observed excess risk is concentrated early after infection.

In summary, this study provides novel, time-sensitive, and variant-specific insights into the cardiovascular consequences of COVID-19. By defining an acute hazard window and identifying vulnerable subgroups, these findings offer a pragmatic framework for targeted surveillance, clinical intervention, and future research into viral-triggered cardiovascular disease.

Conclusion

Cardiovascular risk after SARS-CoV-2 infection was highest within the first 30 days, with variation by variant and age, and the greatest burden among older adults during the pre-Delta and Delta eras. These findings demonstrate a time-dependent clustering of events early after infection and are supportive of a clinically meaningful hazard window. Although increased health care contact and diagnostic intensity in the peri-infectious period may contribute, this early concentration of events supports targeted, time-sensitive monitoring strategies by era and age. These findings may also inform expectations regarding the timing and magnitude of cardiovascular complications in future respiratory pandemics.
