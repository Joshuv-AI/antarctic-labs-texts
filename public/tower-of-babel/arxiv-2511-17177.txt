Orbital rotation of spheroidal Mie particles driven by counter-propagating circularly-polarized beams
E. N. Bulgakov, A. E. Ershov, V. Kimberg, V. S. Gerasimov, D.N. Maksimov

Abstract
We theoretically consider orbital rotation of a spheroidal submicron particle in the field of two counter-propagating circularly polarized Gaussian beams. We derived equations connecting the parameters of the circular orbits centered on the beams axis to the optical force and torque. The equations show that, besides orbital rotation, the spheroidal particle simultaneously rotates around its equatorial axis. We found that two distinct dynamic regimes are possible. The orbital motion can be accompanied by a rapid proper rotation with angular velocity an order of magnitude larger than the angular velocity of the orbital rotation. Alternatively, the orbital and proper rotations can be synchronized. The direction of orbital rotation can either coincide with or be opposite to the direction of rotation of the electric vector. The findings are confirmed by direct numerical simulations. The results can be of use in development of nano-scale gyroscopes as well in shape-selective sorting of submicron particles.

3
Dynamics

We start with writing down the dynamic equations for a spheroidal particle driven by circularly polarized beams.
The components of the Stokes force in the rotating reference frame, see Fig.
1
, can be written as

\begin{split}F^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm{(s)}}}_{x^{\prime}}=-\nu C_{1}v_{x^{\prime}},\\
F^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm{(s)}}}_{y^{\prime}}=-\nu C_{3}v_{y^{\prime}},\end{split}

(2)

where
v_{x^{\prime}}
,
v_{y^{\prime}}
are the Cartesian components of velocity in the rotating reference frame, and
C_{1}
,
C_{3}
are the shape-dependent drag correction factors
31

After applying Eq. (
1
) and Eq. (
2

cylindrical coordinates takes the following form

\displaystyle m(r\ddot{\phi}+2\dot{r}\dot{\phi})=F_{\phi}(r,\psi)-r\dot{\phi}{\nu}\left[C_{1}\sin^{2}(\psi)+C_{3}\cos^{2}(\psi)\right]-\dot{r}\frac{\nu}{2}(C_{1}-C_{3})\sin(2\psi),

\displaystyle m(\ddot{r}-r\dot{\phi}^{2})=F_{\rm{r}}(r,\psi)-r\dot{\phi}\frac{\nu}{2}(C_{1}-C_{3})\sin(2\psi)-\dot{r}{\nu}\left[C_{3}\sin^{2}(\psi)+C_{1}\cos^{2}(\psi)\right],

(3)

where
m
is the mass of the particle. The above equations have to be complemented with the equation describing rotation of the spheroid about the equatorial axis

I_{\scriptscriptstyle{\perp}}\ddot{\alpha}=M_{z}(\psi,r)-\nu\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\perp}}\dot{\alpha},

(4)

where
I_{\scriptscriptstyle{\perp}}
is the corresponding component of the moment of inertia tensor, and
\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\perp}}
is a size- and shape-dependent coefficient that determines the resistance of the medium to rotation
31
.

The condition
\dot{r}=0
corresponds to rotation around the
z
-axis with orbital angular velocity

\Omega=\dot{\phi}
in a circular orbit of radius
r
.
Equation (
3
) is, then, reduced to

\displaystyle 0=F_{\phi}(r,\psi)-r\dot{\phi}{\nu}\left[C_{1}\sin^{2}(\psi)+C_{3}\cos^{2}(\psi)\right],

\displaystyle mr{\Omega}^{2}=-F_{\rm{r}}(r,\psi)+r\dot{\phi}\frac{\nu}{2}(C_{1}-C_{3})\sin(2\psi).

(5)

Assuming
that the proper angular velocity is much greater that orbital angular velocity
\omega\gg\Omega
,
where
\omega=\dot{\alpha}
, one can find the approximate analytic solution of Eq. (
3
) by averaging over a rapidly changing variable
\psi=\omega t-\phi
. After the average Eq. (
3

\displaystyle 0=\bar{F}_{\phi}(r)-r\Omega\frac{\nu}{2}\left(C_{1}+C_{3}\right),

\displaystyle mr{\Omega}^{2}=-\bar{F}_{\rm{r}}(r),

(6)

where
\bar{F}_{\phi}(r),\bar{F}_{\rm{r}}(r)
are the components of the net force averaged over
\psi
. There are two unknowns in Eq. (
3
), namely,
the orbit radius

, and the orbital angular velocity
\Omega
. Equation (
3
) can be rewritten as a single equation below

\frac{\bar{F}^{2}_{\phi}(r)}{\bar{F}_{\rm{r}}(r)}=-\frac{r}{m}\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)^{2}(C_{1}+C_{3})^{2},

(7)

meanwhile the angular velocities are given by

\displaystyle\Omega=\sqrt{\frac{|\bar{F}_{\phi}(r)|}{mr}},\ \omega=\frac{\bar{M}_{z}}{\nu\Gamma_{\perp}},

(8)

where
\bar{M}_{z}
is the average
z
-component of the torque, and the expression for
\omega
is derived from Eq. (
4
). Once the orbit radius is known the angular velocities can be found from Eq. (
8
).
Note that the left hand part of Eq. (
7
) is proportional to the power of the beams
P
whereas the right hand part is independent of

Another possible scenario is the phenomenon of phase synchronization

\omega=\Omega={\rm Const}.

(9)

The phase-synchronization or phase-locking is typically explained by coupling between two oscillatory degrees of freedom with the difference between individual natural frequencies smaller than the sum of coupling strengths

Equation (

4
Conclusion

We have investigated orbital rotation of a spheroidal particle in the field of two counter-propagating circularly polarized Gaussian beams. Similar to the earlier findings for spherical particles
44
;
39
we observe that with increase in intensity the particle departs from the beam axis evolving to a circular orbit limit cycle.
Thus, this work extends the results of Svak
et al

44
, who demonstrated that a spherical dielectric particle exposed to a circularly polarized beam in optical tweezers can depart from axial equilibrium and undergo circular motion around the beam axis, driven by a Hopf bifurcation that produces coherent thermally excited orbits. We advanced this line of research by theoretically analyzing the orbital rotation of spheroidal particles, explicitly accounting for the transfer of spin angular momentum into particle rotation
4
.
As the central result, equations connecting the parameters of the circular orbits to the optical force and torque are derived analytically. The equations show that, besides circular orbital rotation, the spheroidal particle also rotates around one of its equatorial axes which is parallel to the beam axis. We found that two distinct dynamic regimes are possible. For example, with particles
a_{x}=0.250~\mu m
, and
a_{x}=0.400~\mu m
in size the orbital motion is accompanied by a rapid proper rotation with angular velocity an order of magnitude larger than the angular velocity of the orbital rotation. For particles with
a_{x}=0.450~\mu m
the orbital and proper rotations of the particle are synchronised, i.e. occur with the same angular velocity. The direction of orbital rotation can either coincide with or be opposite to the direction of rotation of the electric vector. The regime of rapid proper rotation can be observed at room temperature. However, the synchronized rotation can only be observed at temperatures of several Kelvins. We believe that this low temperature effect is experimentally feasible in a view of of the results on millikelvin cooling of optically trapped microparticles
22
. We speculate that lowering the temperature threshold for synchronized motion constitutes an interesting subject for future research. In our analytic model we neglected longitudinal motion. This leaves us with an unanswered question of whether the reported two-dimensional dynamic regimes are stable with respect to perturbations in
z
and
\beta
. To address this question the stability of the orbits shown in Fig.
3
is confirmed by performing full-fledged three-dimensional modelling in six dimensional configuration space with account of the Brownian force. Interestingly, we found that with a further increase of intensity the orbital motion bifurcates to three-dimensional attractors to be considered in the future studies. We believe that the result presented can be of use in development of nano-scale gyroscopes as well in shape-selective sorting of submicron particles that can rotate in different orbits depending on their size.
