Barriers and facilitators to implementing electronic patient reported outcome screening for mental illness and substance use disorders at HIV clinics in Alabama: a qualitative study
Authors: Ellen Eaton, C Greer McCollum, Kelly W Gagnon, Anna-Julia Enid Kutsch, Kaylee Burgan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople living with HIV face disproportionately high rates of mental health issues (MH) and substance use disorders (SUD), which can contribute to reduced antiretroviral treatment (ART) adherence. When left untreated, these psychosocial comorbidities pose an additional threat to the goals of the Ending HIV Epidemic Initiative, which aims to optimize HIV outcomes in states like Alabama, where the rural burden of HIV is among the highest in the nation. Fortunately, electronic patient reported outcome measures (ePROs) are effective in screening for MH and SUD in a wide variety of care settings, facilitating early referrals to evidence-based interventions. In this study, we use the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to identify barriers and facilitators to the implementation of depression, anxiety, and SUD ePROs in HIV clinics in Alabama, an Ending the HIV Epidemic Priority Jurisdiction.MethodsWe interviewed 15 clinical and non-clinical staff at five Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program (RWHP) funded HIV clinics in Alabama. We queried their experiences with an ePRO intervention starting in 2022. We used a rapid analysis technique to explore themes related to the Characteristics of Individuals, Process, Characteristics of Innovation, and Inner Setting domains of CFIR. Two experienced qualitative researchers conducted the semi-structured interviews and iteratively analyzed the transcripts.ResultsWe identified a variety of barriers and facilitators to implementation of MH and SUD ePROs in HIV clinics that fell under all four included domains. Participants reported significant staff turnover and/or limited staff capacity as primary barriers to consistent implementation. Sites also reported the lack of ongoing training and onboarding at the site level as a barrier that further impeded capacity and understanding. Positively, though, participants also reported that the intervention enhanced patient-provider communication and increased the rate of MH and SUD identification. Having a site champion as the primary point of contact, both internally and externally to the investigation team, was a key facilitator. The champions also increased team motivation and kept the intervention on track. Tablets were key to the intervention, enabling automatic results of ePROs.ConclusionsDespite numerous barriers, such as staff turnover, participants found the intervention to be easy to use, brief, and effective. This success was largely attributed to several facilitating factors, such as the use of tablets and the support of site champions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13596-7.Key Words: Electronic patient-reported outcomes, HIV care delivery, Mental health screening, Substance use disorders, Implementation science, Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research Barriers and facilitators, Rural health services, Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative, Digital health interventions

The study’s qualitative approach, including interviews with clinical and non-clinical staff, captures on-the-ground experiences with ePRO implementation. Key findings—such as the role of site champions, the impact of staff turnover, and the necessity of ongoing training—offer practical guidance for scaling ePROs in similar settings.

4. Contributes to the Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) Initiative which focuses on Alabama and other poor, rural states

By focusing on a priority jurisdiction (Alabama), the study directly supports national efforts to optimize HIV outcomes. It underscores the importance of integrating behavioral health screening into HIV care and provides an evidence-based model that can be adapted in other high-burden areas.

5. Advances Digital Health Solutions for Underserved Populations

The study reinforces the effectiveness of digital screening tools in improving patient-provider communication and increasing MH and SUD identification rates. Given the barriers to traditional care in rural areas, the findings highlight how technology-driven interventions can enhance healthcare access and equity.

Introduction

Across the US, there is an epidemic of mental health issues (MH) and substance use disorders (SUD), with an estimated 48.7 million people aged 12 years or older (17.3%) having a substance use disorder, 59.3 million adults (23.1%) having any mental health disorder, and 21.5 million adults (8.2%) living with both [1]. This epidemic is pronounced among people living with HIV (PLWH). One study found, for instance, that around 36% of PLHW have major depression and 15.8% have generalized anxiety disorder, compared to 6.7% and 2.1% in the general population, respectively [2]. Additionally, an estimated 48% of PLWH have a SUD [3]. These behavioral health disorders are concerning by themselves, but for PLWH, MH and SUD can also lead to reduced antiretroviral treatment (ART) adherence and high viral loads (VL), contributing to excess morbidity and mortality and increasing the likelihood of HIV transmission [4–7].

In 2021, around 1.2 million individuals were living with HIV in the US, with roughly 32,100 new infections that year: a 12% decrease compared to 36,500 in 2017 [8]. Within the US there is a great regional heterogeneity in HIV burden, with Southern states experiencing some of the worst HIV outcomes in the nation [8]. Due to its high rural HIV burden, Alabama is 1 of 7 states included as an Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) priority jurisdiction [9]. Rurality adds barriers to care for many Alabamians living with HIV who have MH and/or SUD, including a lack of providers, transportation difficulties, limited public health infrastructure, and poverty [10–12]. A lack of Medicaid expansion further exacerbates access to care for under- and uninsured persons in Alabama [13, 14].

Electronic Patient Reported Outcome Measures (ePROs) allow patients (using tablets, laptops, and smartphones) to discreetly complete validated questionnaires about their mental health, such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a brief depression check [15]. ePROs allow patients to directly provide information about their own health, instead of having to verbally relay the information to a provider, thus minimizing social desirability bias and misinterpretation [16–18]. A Cochrane Review from 2021, which included ePROs, found that there is moderate certainty that PROs, overall, enhance patient-provider communication, improve quality of life, increase diagnosis and notation, and help control disease [19]. In HIV care, PROs have been used to screen for a wide-variety of health issues, including MH and SUD, and have been found to enhance patient-provider communication and to increase the identification, recording, and treatment of complex behavioral health issues [20].

Despite evidence suggesting ePROs enhance MH and SUD identification and treatment, few clinics have implemented them into routine HIV care, particularly in poor and rural Southern states. To advance the ambitious goals of the EHE initiative, we implemented an ePRO focused intervention: HIV + Service delivery and Telemedicine through Effective PROs (+ STEP) to provide screening and treatment for MH and SUD with the overall goal to improve early detection and intervention for MH and SUD that impede HIV care continuum outcomes. +STEP uses ePROs to deliver standardized depression, generalized anxiety disorder, alcohol use disorder, and SUD screening to PLWH as part of routine clinical care and trains clinicians on evidence-based MH- and SUD-care. +STEP intervention teams at participating sites received on-site, in-person training on the intervention. Participating clinics are geographically dispersed across the state and located in regions with high HIV prevalence [21].

The objective of this study is to understand the barriers and facilitators to implementing ePROs in Ryan White HIV/AIDS Programs (RWHAP)-funded clinics in Alabama [21, 22]. Specifically, we queried participants’ perspectives on the integration of ePROs in HIV clinics.

Procedure

Semi-structured qualitative interviews were conducted by a UAB + STEP research team member, KG, with qualitative interviewing experience via videoconferencing software between July and November 2023. Interviews lasted an average of 25 min and were audio recorded and transcribed prior to analysis, without any identifiers (i.e., name, date of birth) using a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant transcription service, Landmark.

An interview guide based on domains from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) was developed, focusing on Characteristics of Individuals, Process, Characteristics of Innovation, and Inner Setting (Table 2) [23]. CFIR was developed to predict or explain barriers and facilitators to implementation effectiveness, and includes five domains (innovation, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, and implementation process), each of which contain multiple constructs and subconstructs [23].

Analysis

We used rapid qualitative techniques to collect and analyze interview data in an efficient manner to impact implementation of future ePRO initiatives in Alabama. Rapid qualitative analysis is an applied method commonly used in health services research and evaluation to obtain actionable, targeted data on a shorter timeline than traditional qualitative methods while adhering to accepted scientific processes and rigor [24, 25]. Specifically, two team members with qualitative research experience (KG, KB) condensed data from interview transcripts into a template with domains aligned with interview questions. Next, both team members transferred the content into a matrix organized by CFIR domains. One matrix was developed to analyze all participant responses. Each row in the matrix corresponded to a given interview and each column referred to a unique domain or question from the interview guide. Matrix columns were then analyzed by both team members for similarities, differences, and trends in responses. During the analysis of column domains, it was determined that content largely coalesced around two main domains – barriers and facilitators. Discrepancies between analysts were resolved via discussion and consensus.

This was done in accordance with 00000 checklist. See supplemental file.

Characteristics of individuals

Individual identification with organization

Site implementation teams consisted of site leadership and implementing staff who managed the day-to-day of the intervention. Interview participants’ roles included program managers, clinic directors, nurses and nurse practitioners, intake and social work coordinators, case managers, and clinic coordinators. Nine participants were active implementers of + STEP, three were non-implementing senior leadership, two transitioned from an implementer role to leadership, and one reported being “partially involved” in implementation.

Individual stage of change

Overall, participants reported that the intervention required an iterative and adaptive learning process, as significant barriers impacted the ability to consistently and effectively implement + STEP. Participants reported the need to further modify workflows to accommodate the completion of screeners, scoring, and discussion with patients on treatment options, as well as challenges in internal communication on when and to whom to deliver screeners. Despite these challenges, participants reported general comfort with the intervention itself.

Knowledge and beliefs about the intervention

Overall, participants found + STEP to be a useful intervention for identifying MH and SUD in their patient population. Particularly, participants appreciated the electronic screeners and found them valuable in fostering communication on available treatment options with patients. Participants found the process of delivering the intervention to be relatively quick and believed that most patients enjoy completing screeners on tablets rather than on paper or verbally with their provider. Nevertheless, some participants reported patient-specific challenges, such as low literacy or discomfort with technology, as barriers to keeping the process consistently quick and seamless. Lastly, one participant noted liability concerns about the timeliness of reviewing screening scores, specifically questions on suicidality.

Process

Planning

When preparing for the implementation of + STEP, participants reported having little to no site-level planning. Outside of the + STEP Research Team’s in-person onboarding processes (which included developing an implementation packet, REDCap training, and practice REDCap encounters), sites did not take additional steps to prepare for implementation. Some sites reported discussion of workflow changes, however, this was not universal. Lastly, due to staff turnover, many sites had to onboard new staff for + STEP, in which they were onboarded via a “train-the-trainer” model, with older staff members providing an overview of + STEP to them. Participants did not provide further detail on the utility of this model; however, one participant reported feeling “thrown in” to implementation.

Executing

Overall, participants found + STEP to be an advantageous intervention that was useful in identifying MH and SUD in their patient population. Specifically, participants found the results from ePRO screening (validated scores) to be helpful in note documentation. They particularly appreciated these scores being electronic, as it made it easier for upload to clinical notes compared to traditional paper screeners and scoring. Despite seeing the intervention as advantageous, many participants reported ongoing competition with other grants and priorities, particularly in terms of time available to dedicate to the intervention. Several participants also reported inconsistent or interrupted implementation at their respective clinics due to external barriers, most often staff turnover or organizational changes.

Reflecting and evaluating

Overall, participants expressed general interest in continuing + STEP at their site, as they believed it to be useful in starting conversations about MH and SUD with their patients; however, additional resources would be needed to continue implementation. Participants noted the need for continued access to REDCap to maintain + STEP records, continued IT equipment and support (e.g., tablets, tablet carts, and protective cases, etc.), ongoing training and education, and patient incentives to continue + STEP moving forward. Regarding facilitators, participants reported that having a site champion was instrumental in implementing + STEP. Participants also noted that using tablets to facilitate data collection and scoring was a primary benefit of the intervention. Participants viewed the intervention as quick, which made it relatively easy to integrate into existing workflows and believed that scores from ePROs help facilitate communication with patients on treatment options. While participants noted no additional justifications were needed to keep + STEP going, they did report competition for time due to demand from other grants and priorities.

Intervention characteristics

Relative advantage

Participants reported + STEP as being relatively advantageous for furthering clinic goals and missions. Specifically, participants found + STEP to be a useful intervention for identifying MH and SUD in patients and noted that it helped facilitate conversations on treatment options. Participants also noted that having the ePROs delivered independently and electronically through tablets and then uploading those scores into the electronic health record was useful for clinic purposes. While advantageous, participants noted that there is ongoing competition for time, but that this competition was to a lesser extent than other barriers reported.

Inner setting

Leadership engagement

Overall, participants reported site leadership as being appropriately engaged in the implementation of + STEP at their site. For most, leadership was not typically involved in day-to-day + STEP activities but was useful in communicating with UAB, obtaining resources, and troubleshooting implementation challenges as needed. For others, leadership was not involved in + STEP at all but felt that it was appropriate for the context of their clinic. Regardless of the level of leadership engagement, sites reported that leadership changes introduced complications and challenges for the implementation of the intervention.

Networks and communications

Overall, participants noted that communication with UAB’s + STEP Research Team was effective, they knew who their contacts were, and they felt comfortable reaching out with questions or concerns. Conversely, many participants indicated challenges with internal communication, specifically those sites with multiple satellite clinics or locations that are also implementing + STEP. One site noted specific challenges in communicating with front desk staff on which patients should or should not receive the intervention. Participants also expressed a desire to increase and improve communication efforts internally to identify and overcome challenges.

Participant suggestions and recommendations for improvement

Participants provided various suggestions for improvement for other clinics to consider when implementing + STEP at their site. Notably, participants emphasized the need for a site champion. While sites had identified champions prior to the implementation of + STEP, staff turnover and leadership changes led to inconsistencies in who the champion should be and what their role was. Participants also recommended gaining team buy-in across the clinic, noting the need to ensure clear lines of communication prior to implementation. Similarly, participants indicated several workflow adjustments were needed and recommended having a solid workflow in place before implementation roll-out. Lastly, participants recommended having additional training on the intervention to assist with consistent implementation.

Participants reported various barriers encountered throughout the implementation of + STEP for their respective clinics. Most notably, participants reported significant staff turnover and/or limited staff capacity as primary barriers to consistent implementation of + STEP. Similarly, sites reported the lack of training and onboarding at the site level as a barrier that further impeded capacity and understanding of the intervention. One site experienced considerable organizational change in conjunction with staff turnover, which resulted in the need to re-launch the intervention. Similarly, other sites reported the need to pause and relaunch implementation to account for competing demands and priorities. Participants also reported significant challenges with integrating the intervention in their clinic workflow, gaining provider buy-in, and patients following through with referrals and treatment as key barriers to implementation.

Participants also noted several facilitators that assisted in the implementation of + STEP for their site. Notably, participants reported having a site champion as the primary point of contact, both internally and externally, as a key facilitator. Participants noted that site champions ensured team motivation and kept implementation on track. Participants also reported that using tablets for ePROs, particularly the automatic scoring feature, streamlined the intervention. Correspondingly, participants reported that having these screeners, and their corresponding scores, immediately available for the provider helped facilitate meaningful conversations on treatment options with patients. Lastly, while some noted workflow challenges, many participants reported that the intervention was quick and did not add much time to the overall clinic workflow.

Discussion

Through a set of 15 staff interviews, we examined barriers to and facilitators of the implementation of MH and SUD ePROs at HIV clinics in Alabama. Overall, we found that staff members had positive views of the implementation process, reporting that the ePROs are relatively brief and easy-to-use and that the screeners seemed to enhance patient-provider communication. Interviewees reported that site champions where important motivators and enabled communication with the research team. Interviewees did, though, cite some barriers to implementation, including staff turnover, limited staff capacity, lack of site-level training, competing priorities, and intra-agency communication. Of note, this study was conducted during the SARS-CoV-2 public health emergency, which likely contributed to the above reported barriers.

Although few studies exist on the implementation of ePROs in HIV Care, there is a robust literature on the implementation of PROs in healthcare, more broadly, which generally show similar results to our study: a complex mix of barriers and facilitators to implementation that greatly affect intervention effectiveness. A systematic review from 2018, for instance, described the importance of two early phases of PROs implementation: the design of the intervention and the preparation of site staff [26]. Site staff were not extensively involved in the design phase of our intervention beyond agreeing to participate and mapping site-specific workflows (e.g., did not help select the specific ePROs); however, our interviews did reveal some of the preparation barriers sites faced, such as feeling “thrown into” the intervention without much site-level preparation beyond the pre-implementation training that was offered by the UAB team. Some participants also noted how staff turnover also affected how prepared the sites felt to continuously implement the intervention. Importantly, though, site staff discussed how helpful it was to directly engage the UAB team on an ad hoc basis whenever issues arose. Interviewees also noted how helpful it was to have site champions lead planning and implementation, although a relatively high rate of staff turnover across all sites limited the impact of the champions.

Multiple reviews of the implementation of PROs (not just electronic PROs) describe some similar findings to ours. This includes the importance of interventions being simple and easy-to-use, the seamless integration of PROs into existing workflows, choosing relevant and valid measures, having supportive and engaged leadership and site champions, and using technology to facilitate implementation [26–28]. The literature also describes how staff turnover and demanding workloads can act as barriers to successful implementation, as can limited patient literacy levels and the unavailability of PRO scores at clinically meaningful times [26, 27]. Some barriers may be exacerbated for minoritized and marginalized populations (who are overrepresented among PLWH), such as resource-constrained healthcare providers, low literacy rates, and being non-English speaking [29]. Although no systematic reviews exist, to our knowledge, regarding the implementation of PROs specifically within HIV-care settings, a number of individual studies have examined this topic, with most generally agreeing that PROs within HIV-care are feasible and acceptable to both staff and patients and help identify less-observable health issues, like suicidal ideation, reduce social desirability bias, and enhance patient-provider communication [20, 30–33].

Similar to what we found, the investigators of a 2016 study found, when interviewing 27 individuals across 4 HIV clinics in the US, that providers perceive some of the barriers to ePRO implementation to be the disruption of clinic flow and the correct interpretation of results [33]. Thankfully, though, implementation strategies exist to address these barriers, as well as other barriers we identified in our study, such as insufficient training and staff turnover. Engaging staff early in the project development process (e.g., with the selection of ePROs) helps lay the foundation for continued buy-in and ongoing training helps staff stay motivated and allows new staff to get up-to-speed [28, 34]. Involving site staff early in the design process can also help minimize complexity and ensure the intervention interrupts current clinic flow as little as possible, and choosing PROs with relatively easy scoring guidelines may help with correctly interpreting results, as may interpretation aids, like graphs displaying patients’ score changes over time [26, 33, 35]. Developing a robust knowledge-sharing system, such as a Community of Practice, may help intra- and inter-site communication and the identification of solutions to common barriers, like low literacy levels among patient populations [36].

Our study did identify some additional intervention characteristics and contextual factors that may inform a clinic’s decision to continue implementation that have not previously been highlighted in the literature to our knowledge. For instance, one participant noted concerns with liability if ePRO results are not immediately available (e.g., if suicidality is identified but not addressed in the same visit). Additionally, some participants discussed how competing demands, like other grants’ requirements, may limit their clinic’s ability to continue with the intervention long term.

Limitations

Despite being a robust investigation of clinic staff perceptions of the implementation of an ePRO intervention in HIV clinics, our study does contain a few limitations. Firstly, we only recruited from staff at clinics participating in the + STEP intervention, thus our findings may not reflect the opinions of staff at HIV clinics more broadly. Additionally, we achieved a relatively modest participation rate of 56% via our recruitment strategy, thus we can’t be certain the views expressed by those who did not participate are adequately captured by the perspectives of those who did participate. Although not specifically mentioned by participants, this study was implemented during the SARS-CoV-2 public health emergency which likely contributed to participation, staff turnover, and competing demands. We utilized a deductive rapid interviewing and analysis technique, which means that we may have missed emergent themes that could have come through had we used a more inductive approach. The literature demonstrates, though, that rapid qualitative research produces similar results to more traditional, inductive approaches [25]. Even with these limitations we believe our study provides valuable insights in regard to the implementation of ePROs, especially MH and SUD ePROs, in HIV-care setting.

Conclusion

Through qualitative interviews with staff at four HIV clinics in Alabama participating in an ePRO intervention, +STEP, we found that staff, both clinical and administrative, generally found the intervention to be quick and easy-to-use and that it helped with patient-provider communication, as well as with the identification of MH and SUD. They felt that the use of tablets instead of paper questionnaires enhanced implementation, as did site champions, who acted as intermediaries between the investigators and implementing staff. Interviewees did, though, identify some barriers to the successful implementation of + STEP, including, among other things, the limited preparation sites undertook prior to implementation, high staff turnover, at times inadequate buy-in from leadership and implementing staff, the lack of treatment options for MH and SUD, low patient literacy levels, and modified workflows. Although there is limited peer-reviewed literature on the implementation of ePROs in HIV-care setting, these barriers and facilitators, overall, reflect what has previously been reported in the literature for PROs implementation in other care setting. Our study did uncover some previously unreported potential barriers that may impact continued implementation, such as liability concerns and competing demands from other grants.
