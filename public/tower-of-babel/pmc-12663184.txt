Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition analysis of the urban-rural gap in child nutrition in Bangladesh
Authors: Mahbub Hossain, Mohammad Jahangir Alam, Ismat Ara Begum, Huichun Sun, Andrew M McKenzie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChild undernutrition is a leading cause of death among young children worldwide. In Bangladesh, severe undernutrition is prevalent among nearly 25% of the under five children. Additionally, the prevalence of child undernutrition varies significantly between urban and rural areas in the country. This widespread prevalence of undernutrition and regional disparity raises concerns among Bangladeshi policymakers because childhood undernutrition can lead to irreversible consequences. Using the latest data from Bangladesh’s Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), this study examines factors contributing to the urban-rural gap in child nutrition. The Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition results suggest that nearly 78% of the observed difference in weight-for-age Z score (WAZ) between urban and rural children can be attributed to disparities in the factors determining child nutrition (endowment effect) across urban and rural households. The analysis also reveals that disparities among households in socioeconomic and maternal education play a significant role in the observed urban-rural difference in WAZ. In conclusion, this study suggests that the government should emphasize reducing socioeconomic inequalities amongst households to reduce regional disparities in child nutrition in Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26857-6.Keywords: Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition, Urban-rural gap, Child undernutrition, Multiple indicator cluster survey, BangladeshSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Health occupations

Introduction

Child undernutrition is a leading cause of death among young children worldwide, with long-term implications such as reduced working capacity in adulthood and potential economic productivity losses for countries1,2. In Bangladesh, child undernutrition is alarmingly high3. According to the latest report on the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) of the UNICEF and Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS), there is a significant disparity in child nutritional status between urban and rural areas4. For instance, the prevalence of underweight children is approximately 19% (3% are severely underweight and 16% are moderately underweight) in urban areas compared to about 24% (4% are severely underweight and 20% are moderately underweight) in rural areas. Conversely, the share of normal-weight children in urban areas is 79%, whereas the share is 74% in rural areas. In the case of stunting, the urban-rural gap in prevalence is around 2%. Recognizing these disparities, national policy planners are keen not only on reducing overall child undernutrition but also on narrowing the urban-rural gap because Bangladesh is striving to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which require reducing child undernutrition across the country. Since undernutrition tends to be passed down from one generation to the next, there is a high likelihood that the urban-rural gap in child nutrition will persist unless policy-level interventions are implemented to bridge this divide. To address such challenges effectively, policymakers require a comprehensive understanding of the factors contributing to the observed differences in child nutritional status in Bangladesh. Therefore, this research aims to identify the factors associated with the urban-rural gap in child undernutrition using a national-level dataset from Bangladesh. Bridging the urban-rural gap in child nutrition is crucial for achieving Bangladesh’s development goals related to health, nutrition and broader development goals. If closing the urban-rural gap is a goal of policy interventions, policymakers must understand what factors contribute to it and to what extent. Therefore, identifying the factors behind the urban-rural disparity in child nutrition is essential not only for crafting effective policy measures but also for achieving the SDGs. Bridging this gap would lead to overall improvements in child nutrition across the country.

Child undernutrition manifests at the individual level, but its causes can be rooted in different levels5. While several studies have examined the effects of various socioeconomic factors on child nutritional status6–14; only a few studies have analyzed the urban-rural gap in child malnutrition in different countries. For example, Kumar and Kumari15 examined the factors responsible for the rural-urban gap in child malnutrition using national family health survey data from India. Their study found a significant urban-rural gap in child nutrition, attributed to differences in household socioeconomic status and parental education. Importantly, the relevance of socioeconomic variables in explaining the urban-rural gap in child nutrition remains consistent across different distributions of anthropometric indicators, as found by Srinivasan et al.16 in Nepal and Bangladesh. Another study by Sharaf and Rashad17 examined the urban-rural differences in child malnutrition in three Arab countries: Egypt, Jordan, and Yemen. Using demographic and health survey data, they found evidence of varying degrees of child malnutrition between rural and urban areas, with a small gap in Egypt and Jordan, but a considerably larger gap in Yemen. However, in terms of contributing factors to the observed urban-rural gap, there is commonality across the countries. Income inequality between rural and urban areas was identified as a major factor contributing to the observed gap in child nutrition across these countries. In Bangladesh’s context, one study documented urban-rural differences in child nutrition using relatively old data from the demographic and health survey18. This study found that rural children in Bangladesh are 1.3 times more likely to be malnourished compared to their urban counterparts. Children born to mothers with low levels of education and with poor body mass index were more likely to be malnourished in the country. However, this study did not examine the extent to which the urban-rural gap can be explained by the factors that determine a child’s nutritional status. In a cross-country study, Fagbamigbe et al.19 demonstrated that socioeconomic status, source of drinking water, maternal education, and birth weight contribute significantly to the reduction of the urban-rural gap in child nutrition in low and middle-income countries.

Another strand of literature has focused on the poor-nonpoor gap in child malnutrition across different country contexts. For instance, Hasan et al.20 explored socioeconomic inequality in child malnutrition using two rounds of demographic health and survey data from Bangladesh. They found that malnourished children were concentrated among poor households, with a high incidence of malnutrition being observed in households with less educated mothers. Similarly, Pulok et al.21 analyzed how various socioeconomic factors are associated with child malnutrition and how these factors account for the poor-nonpoor gap in child nutrition, using two rounds of demographic and health survey data from Bangladesh. As expected, they found that children from poor households tend to suffer more from malnutrition, and parental education significantly reduced the average level of malnutrition across socioeconomic classes. Kumar and Singh22 decomposed the gap in childhood undernutrition between poor and nonpoor households in the urban context of India, using national family health survey data. They identified differences in the use of healthcare services as one of the most significant factors underlying the poor-nonpoor gap in urban child malnutrition. Bhusal23 examined the poor-nonpoor difference in nutritional status among Nepalese under five children and found that about 47% of the gap can be explained by factors such as maternal education, residency of household, and toilet facilities.

Uthman24 measured and decomposed socioeconomic inequality in child malnutrition in Nigeria and found results similar to those in the South Asian region. For instance, maternal education, place of residency, and access to toilet facilities contributed significantly to the overall socioeconomic inequality in child nutrition. A similar finding was reported by Chaudhan et al.25 in a decomposition-based study conducted in Sierra Leone, highlighting the ongoing reduction in the incidence of stunting and underweight over time. They reported that mothers’ exposure to media and the place of child delivery contributed to the poor-nonpoor gap.

In Bangladesh, differences in child nutritional status have further been researched from the perspective of household ethnicity. For example, Sinharoy et al.26 explored the differences in child nutrition between Bengali children and those from the Chattrogram Hill Tracts (CHT) using national nutrition surveillance data from 2003 to 2006. Consistent with other studies in Bangladesh, they found that parental education had a significant explanatory effect on the differences in nutritional status between Bengali and CHT children. Some recent studies related to child nutrition have also examined the factors contributing to the reduction of stunting and underweight over time in South Asia. Kumar et al.27 focused on Bangladesh, while Nie et al.28 studied India. Both studies underscored the importance of maternal characteristics, including education, nutritional status, and age. Furthermore, they noted that improvements in socioeconomic status have positively impacted child health. In favour of maternal education in improving child nutrition in Bangladesh, Hossain29 provides evidence that education augments mothers’ bargaining power within the household, thereby contributing to better child health outcomes.

It is evident from the above discussion that, despite the importance of understanding the underlying causes of the urban-rural gap in Bangladeshi children’s nutritional status, few studies have attempted to explain it, let alone quantify the relative contributions of various determinants of child nutrition to this gap. In the interest of making overall progress with regard to the under-five children’s nutritional status, it is crucial for policymakers to understand whether or not a similar set of factors determines child nutrition in urban and rural areas of Bangladesh and if there is any difference in the contributing magnitude of such factors. Also, to what extent such factors are responsible for the observed urban-rural gap in child nutrition warrants a rigorous investigation. If such evidence can be generated, policymakers will be better equipped to design appropriate interventions to address the sharp urban-rural gap in child nutrition in Bangladesh. Given the above context, this study has set the following specific objectives: (i) to estimate the effect of various determinants on the nutrition status of young children in Bangladesh; (ii) to identify the factors that contribute to the observed urban-rural disparity in child nutritional status; and (iii) to estimate the relative contribution of these factors to the rural-urban gap in child nutritional status in Bangladesh. The key contribution of this paper is that it generates empirical evidence on the factors that are responsible for the sharp difference in under-five children’s weight-for-age Z score (WAZ) using decomposition analysis.

This paper has five sections. The following section describes the sources of data and the analytical techniques used in this research. The third section presents summary statistics, regression estimates, and decomposition results. Discussions on the empirical findings are provided in section four, and finally, concluding remarks are made in section five.

Methodology

Source of data

This research relies on observational data collected from the Multiple Cluster Indicator Survey (MICS), conducted jointly by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) and UNICEF. The MICS covers a large number of indicators relating to Bangladeshi children’s situation. The latest survey was conducted in 2019, making it the most recent MICS dataset available to date. This comprehensive dataset covers the entire Bangladesh and is representative of both rural and urban households4. The MICS adopted a two-stage stratified cluster sampling approach following the sampling frame of the Bangladesh Census of Population and Housing 2011. The primary sampling unit, i.e., the enumeration area, was chosen in the first stage, and the sample of households for each enumeration area was selected in the second stage. Data were collected using a well-constructed, pre-tested MICS standard questionnaire through face-to-face interviews. The data collection fieldwork took place between 19 January and 1 June 2019. Trained enumerators used a computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) package on digital devices such as Tablets for collecting data. Verbal consent was obtained from respondents before the interviews, and the respondents were informed of their right to refuse to answer questions, either partly or wholly. Given its extensive coverage and representation, the MICS 2019 dataset seems highly suitable for achieving the specific objectives of this research. It is to be noted that the working sample of this study is derived from those MICS surveyed households for which under five children’s nutritional status-related information is available.

Nutritional status-related data collection was completed for 23,099 children in the MICS 2019; however, 1,018 children were excluded due to errors in the WAZ computation, resulting in the working sample falling to 22,081 children. After excluding 6,144 children who were not the household head`s children (e.g., grandchild, nephew, niece or other relative), the working sample further decreased to 15,937 children. Additionally, 1,724 young children (up to 6 months), such as those who were exclusively breastfed and infant and young child complementary feeding (IYCF) had not started were excluded. Furthermore, 56 children were excluded from the working sample on account of the mismatch of the mother’s information. Eventually, the final working sample of this study consisted of 14,157 children: 2,678 children from urban areas and 11,479 from rural areas.

Analytical techniques: Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition analysis

This research encompasses several distinct objectives, each requiring specific analytical tools to achieve them. These tools include a combination of statistical and econometric techniques. Descriptive statistics have been used to document the socioeconomic characteristics of the sample children and their households. For the sake of comparison across regions, a t-test has been performed in the case of the variables/characteristics that are quantitative. Similarly, a chi-square test was performed to compare qualitative characteristics.

Outcome variable

For the remainder of the analysis, the key variable of interest is the child’s nutritional status, measured by an anthropometric indicator, namely the weight-for-age Z score (WAZ), following the World Health Organization (WHO) standard30. It is to be noted that weight-for-age reflects the overall nutritional health of a population31. Mathematically, WAZ can be defined by the following expressions: . A WAZ score equal to or below minus two standard deviations and minus three standard deviations, respectively, means moderate underweight and severe underweight.

In demographic and health research, anthropometric indicators of child nutrition have been analyzed using OLS regression models14,32. In line with such research, this study has adopted OLS regression analysis to explore the determinants of WAZ.

Maternal characteristics, such as the mother’s age, the mother’s educational attainment (pre-primary, primary, secondary, or higher secondary), are also considered. Household-level factors related to children’s nutrient intake are captured by the child’s dietary diversity score, which reflects the variety of foods consumed. A higher dietary score indicates greater dietary variety. For calculating the child’s dietary diversity, 11 food groups were considered: cereals, vitamin A-rich (colored) vegetables, leafy vegetables, beans, roots, meat, organ meat, eggs, fish, fruits, and dairy products. These were selected based on the MICS survey, which asked whether a child consumed these food groups in the past day of the survey. Other household-related factors include the number of dependent children (under 5 years). To denote the socioeconomic status of households, wealth quintile dummies were included. To capture health environment-related factors, we included variables such as whether the household uses safe drinking water, whether a water-sealed toilet is in use, and whether the household has access to an electricity connection. Households without access to safe water are more likely to rely on unsafe water sources for drinking, cooking, and other purposes. Similarly, households without water-sealed toilets are more likely to practice open defecation. Consequently, children in these households face a higher risk of waterborne diseases such as diarrhoea, which is not only a leading cause of child mortality but also a significant contributor to severe weight loss. Therefore, these variables reflect the household and community-level health environment. Access to electricity is an important indicator of household living conditions. Households without electricity are less likely to have access to television and other media of information. Limited media exposure can hinder mothers’ ability to adopt good child care practices, ultimately affecting child nutrition. Finally, to control for regional variability, dummy variables for the largest administrative units in Bangladesh- divisions – were included.

To adopt an appropriate technique for decomposing the observed gap in the mean of a standardized continuous variable, i.e., WAZ, between urban and rural children, previous studies have been consulted. Based on studies15,17 that analysed the urban-rural gap in nutritional status and a more recent study by Khudri et al.34 on the body mass index disparities between urban and rural women of childbearing age in Bangladesh, this study adopts the Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition technique35. According to the Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition technique (see Rahimi & Nazari36; O’Donnell et al.37 for a comprehensive explanation), the urban-rural difference in the mean WAZ can be expressed as

Thus, the mean difference in WAZ between urban and rural children can be expressed in terms of (1) differences in the endowments, (2) differences in the effect of endowments, and (3) interaction between the differences in endowments and the differences in the effects of endowments. The endowment effect, E, represents the portion of the urban-rural gap in WAZ which is explainable, while combined C and CE represent that portion of the urban-rural gap in WAZ which is unexplainable37.

Following the Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition analysis, the next step involves computing the relative contribution of each explanatory variable considered in the regression model. This relative contribution helps to identify which variable makes the largest contribution to the observed gap. The explained gap, unexplained gap, and the contribution of the determinants were obtained using the Stata user-written command by Jann38.

Ethical approval

No experiments were conducted in this study. Instead, the analysis is based on observational secondary data obtained from the MICS, collected with prior informed consent from all respondents. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Maternal education is found to be a statistically significant factor in determining WAZ, a finding consistent across both urban and rural subsamples. Evidently, children of mothers with higher secondary education or more exhibit significantly higher WAZ compared to those children whose mothers have completed only preprimary education. In contrast to illiterate or less educated mothers, more educated mothers are likely to comprehend nutrition-promoting information and are more likely to adopt practices that are conducive for child health. While the statistical significance remains consistent across urban and rural subsamples, the magnitude of the positive effect of mothers having higher secondary education appears to be more pronounced in urban areas, suggesting a differential effect across the regions. This hints that highly educated mothers in the urban areas, as opposed to their rural counterpart, possibly have better opportunities to utilize health and hygiene-related factors. In the rural subsample, there is an additional observation that children of mothers with secondary education have significantly higher WAZ compared to those children whose mothers completed only preprimary education. Furthermore, the coefficient for secondary education is larger in the rural subsample, further pointing to a differential effect of maternal education between urban and rural regions. Unlike urban areas, children’s WAZ may significantly improve if they belong to mothers who have completed more than primary education. This finding of the positive effect of maternal education thus corroborates the cited studies, which highlighted the beneficial aspects of maternal education. Age of the mother appears not to have any significant influence on determining WAZ.

The number of children under five years of age in a household is negatively associated with children’s WAZ in both urban and rural subsamples, indicating that children’s nutritional status deteriorates with an increase in the number of dependent children in a household. This is possible because the extent to which care and attention are required for optimal nutritional growth may become difficult to provide for mothers in households where there are many dependent children. However, the significance of the negative association is found to be more pronounced in rural areas. Child’s dietary diversity did not appear significant in predicting children’s WAZ despite theoretical importance. The insignificance of the child’s dietary diversity may reflect the fact that other factors, for instance, socioeconomic status, might have picked up the effect, assuming diversified diets are associated with socioeconomic status. Alternatively, it may simply mean factors other than dietary diversity are more important in predicting WAZ.

Regarding socioeconomic classes, all socioeconomic dummies are statistically significant in rural areas, indicating that children from any socioeconomic class other than the poorest tend to have significantly better WAZ. In urban areas, however, only the richest dummy is statistically significant. This suggests that children from the richest households in urban regions have significantly better WAZ compared to children from the poorest households. Evidently, poverty is a strong correlate of a child`s nutritional status.

Finally, among the regional dummies, it can be seen that, except for the Barisal and Chattogram dummies in the urban area, all other division dummies are statistically highly significant and positive. This indicates that children in these divisions have significantly better WAZ compared to children of the Sylhet division, which is the base group in this regression analysis.

The F-test statistic for the overall significance of the regression model has also been reported for both urban and rural subsamples, and it is statistically highly significant in both cases. This suggests that the effect of at least one explanatory variable in each subsample is significantly different from zero.

Evidently, the raw difference in the mean WAZ between urban and rural children is 0.32, which is statistically highly significant. Table 3 shows that the endowment effect, reflecting differences in the levels of determinants across the urban and rural subsamples, contributes 0.25 to the overall raw difference in mean WAZ. Additionally, the coefficient effect, representing differences in the effect of determinants between groups, contributes 0.08 to the overall gap in mean WAZ. Together, the endowment and coefficient effects contribute a negligible amount of −0.01 to the gap, which is statistically insignificant as well. Notably, both the endowment and coefficient part of the observed urban-rural gap are statistically highly significant.

As discussed earlier, in this particular context, the endowment effect is considered the explained portion of the gap, while the coefficient effect, along with interaction effects, are categorized as the unexplained gap. Based on this analysis, approximately 78% [(0.2481/0.3181)×100 = 78%] of the observed urban-rural gap in mean WAZ can be attributed to the explainable part. This implies that no less than three-fourth of the urban-rural difference in the children’s nutritional status arises from the variations in nutrition-determining factors between urban and rural areas in the country, highlighting disparities in the distribution of such nutrition-determining factors. Furthermore, there exists a disparity in how these endowments affect urban and rural children, as indicated by the significance of coefficient effects in Table 3. The coefficient effect, combined with interaction effects, accounts for the remaining 22% of the urban-rural gap, which is regarded as an unexplainable part. In other words, this 22% gap in mean WAZ is attributable to the differences in the effect of determinants across urban and rural areas. It is worth noting that the interaction between coefficients and endowments contributes only a very few portion to the observed gap, which is not statistically significant.

Table 4 allows us to observe how much each determinant (x’s) contributes to the overall explained gap, the coefficients gap, and the interaction gap. It is evident that variables which are related to the socioeconomic group, maternal education, number of dependent children, and household access to electricity significantly contribute to the explained part of the observed difference in mean WAZ. Notably, the indicator of the richest class makes the largest contribution to the explained gap. This means that the difference in whether a child belongs to the richest household contributes more than any other factor to widening the nutritional gap across urban and rural regions. To assess the robustness of our results using an alternative indicator of nutritional status, we re-conducted the decomposition analysis using the height-for-age Z score (HAZ). The results are presented in Appendix A1 and Appendix A2. Notably, the results remain consistent, indicating that the factors contributing to the observed urban-rural gap in child nutrition are not dependent on the specific nutritional status indicator used.

Discussion

It is well documented that child undernutrition is more prevalent and severe in rural areas in Bangladesh. Against such a backdrop, this study aims to understand why the urban-rural difference in child undernutrition exists in the country, utilizing national-level survey data.

Based on anthropometric and household-specific data from 14,157 children under the age of five in Bangladesh, this research has documented that maternal education and the socioeconomic status of a child’s household are crucial determinants of child nutrition. In the urban areas, children of the richest households only have significantly better WAZ than the base group, and children of no other socioeconomic group have better WAZ than the base group. This is quite different in rural areas, i.e., compared to the base socioeconomic group, children of any other socioeconomic group have significantly better WAZ. It suggests that socioeconomic inequality is more pronounced in the case of urban households. Mother’s level of education has appeared to have a differential effect on determining WAZ depending on residency. For instance, children of mothers who have secondary education have significantly higher WAZ than children of mothers who have pre-primary education. On the other hand, in urban settings, to have significantly better WAZ, children need to have mothers who completed higher secondary or more.

This research has shown that the difference in mean WAZ between urban and rural children is statistically highly significant. This is consistent with Rahman and Rahman18. Notably, no less than three-fourth of the observed difference in WAZ between urban and rural children can be attributed to differences in the distribution of factors influencing child nutrition across these regions. What is particularly striking is that about 96% of this explained gap originates from the socioeconomic status of the children’s households. It can be seen that being a child in a poor household significantly widens the urban-rural gap in the WAZ score of the sample children, and in contrast, being a child in a rich household significantly reduces the urban-rural gap significantly. Such evidence reflects the fact that undernutrition is closely associated with poverty43.

The finding that differences in socioeconomic status largely contribute to the urban-rural gap in child nutrition is noteworthy for policymakers. Similar finding have been found in different country contexts19. It points out that addressing the socioeconomic gap at the societal level may significantly reduce the difference in nutritional status between urban and rural children. Put another way, about 731 percent of the overall urban-rural gap in children’s WAZ in Bangladesh could be eliminated if socioeconomic differences among households were reduced. There is considerable variation in the proportion of households across different socioeconomic classes in Bangladesh, as shown in Table 1. The policymakers need to take this seriously because if inequality within society persists, then it would hinder the progress of other interventions aiming to improve the child nutrition situation in the country. Therefore, government efforts should emphasize allocating scarce budgets to reduce income inequality and uplift the poorest segments of society.

The other important contributor to the urban-rural gap in WAZ is maternal education, which has been stressed in a good number of past studies in Bangladesh27 and beyond28. Nearly 10% of the explained gap is attributed to differences in the share of mothers who have completed higher secondary education or more. This is in line with the globally recognized understanding that maternal education plays a beneficial role in determining child nutrition44,45. In addition, the decomposition analysis of this study suggests that maternal education also plays a role in reducing the urban-rural gap in child nutrition. Educated mothers are arguably better equipped to provide care for their children compared to non-educated mothers46. Furthermore, it is important to note that the level of maternal education matters, as evident in the regression analysis. This means that mothers with higher education levels are more adept at utilizing other health resources compared to those with lower educational attainment. Recognizing these findings, the government should prioritize continuing programs that promote women’s education up to at least the higher secondary level or more, particularly in rural areas.

The policymaker may note from this study that by utilizing relatively inexpensive policy tools such as reallocating budgets, it may be possible to bring about considerable positive change towards eliminating the urban-rural gap in child nutrition and improving the overall national nutritional scenario in Bangladesh. Reducing the incidence of child undernutrition and narrowing the regional gap in child nutritional status may involve considerable costs if improvements are to be made in the health environment and in transforming the food system into a nutrition-enhanced food system. Although such initiatives may be regarded as ideal; yet, such actions may not be inexpensive and prompt. On the other hand, redistributing resources to pull the poor segments up and thereby eliminate socioeconomic inequality may bring about expected changes in child nutrition. While doing so, especially when the government designs and implements policy actions, it should consider the socioeconomic heterogeneity among rural and urban households. Taking such heterogeneity into account will ensure that support reaches everyone, including even the poorest members of society. Without this consideration, policy interventions may not achieve the desired impact in eliminating regional gaps in child undernutrition.

Maternal education beyond the secondary level seems to have a perceivable effect in narrowing the urban-rural gap in child nutrition. However, there remains a substantial urban-rural variation in the proportion of mothers who have acquired higher secondary education. To reduce this gap in the future, policy measures should aim to prevent female student drop-outs at the higher secondary level, particularly in rural areas. In this context, the strict enforcement of minimum marriage age for girls is essential to curbing early and child marriages, which are widely recognized as key barriers to girls’ completion of secondary education or beyond.

Over the past decades, many initiatives have been undertaken in Bangladesh to raise awareness about nutrition-specific interventions, such as exclusive breastfeeding, timely initiation of complementary feeding, and increasing dietary diversity and frequency. Nonetheless, this study highlights an important fact that nutrition-specific interventions, such as promoting dietary diversity, may not significantly reduce the urban-rural gap in Bangladesh. Instead, this study points to the fact that addressing socioeconomic inequalities would likely be more effective in narrowing this gap. One specific measure policymakers could consider to reduce socioeconomic disparities in rural areas is the creation of increased employment opportunities.

This study has some limitations. For instance, the OLS regression model may not have controlled for all relevant factors due to the unavailability of data in the MICS dataset, which could result in endogeneity and bias. Furthermore, the decomposition analysis does not account for potential endogeneity issues in the OLS estimates.

Conclusion

The purpose of this research has been to empirically explain the difference in the prevalence of urban-rural gaps in Bangladeshi children’s nutritional status and to explore its components. This study has revealed a substantial urban-rural difference in the WAZ of children under five, with about three-fourths of this gap attributed to variations in the determinants of child nutrition between urban and rural regions in Bangladesh. Urban children have been found to exhibit better nutritional status, and the difference between urban and rural children’s nutritional status is influenced significantly by household and maternal characteristics. Importantly, the empirical evidence of this study suggests that much of the observed difference in urban-rural child nutrition is addressable through policy measures in the sector of women’s education and reducing household socioeconomic inequality.

It is anticipated that these findings will inform and guide policies aimed at reducing regional inequality in child health. In the short term, addressing this inequality could contribute to achieving Sustainable Development Goal 2. In the long term, mitigating regional inequalities may foster more equitable growth and development across the country.

Finally, the cross-sectional nature of this study prevents us from making causal claims. Nonetheless, it serves as a valuable reference for researchers focusing on development issues in Bangladesh and other South Asian countries. One potential extension of this study would be to examine whether the extent of the explained portion of the urban-rural gap in child undernutrition has changed over time, as well as to investigate whether there are any shifts in the relative contributions of the various determinants.
