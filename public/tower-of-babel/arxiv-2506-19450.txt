Asymptotic Equilibrium Analysis of the Boston Mechanism
Josué Ortega

Abstract
We analyze the performance of the Boston mechanism under equilibrium play in uniform random markets. We provide two results. First, while the share of students assigned to their first preference is 63% under truthfulness, this fraction becomes vanishingly small in any Nash equilibrium of the preference revelation game induced by the Boston mechanism. Second, we show that there is a Nash equilibrium of the corresponding preference revelation game where the average student is assigned to a highly undesirable school—dramatically worse ranked than the logarithmic rank achieved under truthfulness.



1
Introduction

School choice mechanisms are central to education policy, with the Boston mechanism (also known as Immediate Acceptance, IA) remaining one of the most widely adopted approaches despite concerns about its incentive properties
(
Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez 2003
)
.
A central metric for evaluating these mechanisms—and one often emphasized by policymakers—is the
first preference rate
: the proportion of students assigned to their top-choice school.
For example, England’s Department for Education publishes the first preference rate for primary and secondary schools annually (currently 93% for primary and 83% for secondary).
1
1

1

See
https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/secondary-and-primary-school-applications-and-offers
.

In this paper, we examine IA’s
expected
first preference rate in equilibrium, complementing
Pritchard and
Wilson 2023
’s (
Pritchard and
Wilson 2023
) (henceforth PW) seminal work providing the first asymptotic analysis of IA, albeit only under the assumption of truthful preference revelation. By accounting for strategic behavior in random markets, we identify how incentives reshape PW’s findings.

We find that accounting for strategic behavior changes PW results drastically.
Under truthful preference revelation in random markets with preferences and priorities drawn independently and uniformly at random and with
n
students and schools, PW show that IA’s first preference rate converges to a constant (
1-e^{-1}\approx 0.63
) as the market size grows large. Their result demonstrates IA’s superior first preference rate performance compared to other Pareto-efficient mechanisms. For instance, Rank-Minimizing, Serial Dictatorship and Top Trading Cycles all attain a first preference rate of only 0.5 asymptotically
(
Ortega and
Klein 2023
)
. However, under strategic behavior, this advantage vanishes completely—in any Nash equilibrium of the corresponding preference revelation game, IA’s first preference rate converges to 0 at a rate of
1/\log(n)
, highlighting the mechanism’s extreme vulnerability to strategic manipulation.

Our second finding concerns the expected placement of the average student under IA. Under truthful behavior, PW show that IA’s average placement is better than under the serial dictatorship mechanism. This implies that the average rank is weakly smaller than
\log n
. However, we show that when accounting for strategic behavior, the average placement can also change dramatically: there exists a Nash equilibrium of the preference revelation game associated to the Boston mechanism in which the average rank is in the order of
n/\log n
. To put these expressions in context, with
n=1000
students and schools, the average student is assigned to his 7-th most preferred school under truthful preference revelation, whereas under some Nash equilibrium of the corresponding preference revelation game, the
average
student is assigned to his 145-th most preferred school.

Our findings quantify IA’s vulnerability to strategic behavior, showing significant effects on performance statistics widely used by education policymakers.

2
Model

We study a one-to-one matching problem, which consists of:

1.

A set of students
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\}
,

2.

A set of schools
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
, with each school having capacity for one student only,

3.

Strict students’ preferences over schools,
\succ\coloneqq(\succ_{1},\ldots,\succ_{n})
, and

4.

Strict schools’ priorities over students,
\triangleright\coloneqq(\triangleright_{s_{1}},\ldots,\triangleright_{s_{n}})
.

A
matching problem

P
is a four-tuple
(I,S,\succ,\triangleright)
. We refer to
n
as the size of the problem. An
allocation

x
assigns each student to exactly one school. We denote by
x_{i}
student
i
’s assigned school.

The function
\textup{rk}_{i}(x_{i})
returns an integer between 1 and
n
corresponding to the ranking of
x_{i}
in student
i
’s preference list (with 1 representing the most preferred school). A
mechanism
is a map from matching problems to allocations.

A
mechanism

M
returns an allocation
M(P)
for every matching problem
P
. We mainly focus on the Boston mechanism, which is also known as Immediate Acceptance (IA). It operates as follows:
2
2

2

PW call this algorithm the
adaptive
Boston mechanism, as rejected students do not have to apply to schools with filled seats.

•

In the first round, each student applies to their most preferred school. Each school that receives at least one application immediately and permanently accepts the applicant with the highest priority, removing both itself and the matched student from the market.

•

In each subsequent round
k
, each remaining student applies to their
k
-th preferred school. Each remaining school that receives at least one application immediately and permanently accepts the highest-priority applicant.

•

The process continues until all students are assigned to a school.

We compare the Boston mechanism to student-proposing Deferred Acceptance (DA). DA operates similarly to IA, but schools’ acceptances are temporary: a student may be rejected in later rounds if a higher-priority applicant applies.
We denote by
\mathtt{IA}(P)
and
\mathtt{DA}(P)
the allocation produced by IA and DA for the matching problem
P
, respectively.

2.1
Performance Metrics

We are interested in the
first preference rate

\mathtt{f}(M(P))
, defined as the fraction of students assigned to their most preferred school in the allocation
M(P)
:

\mathtt{f}(M(P))\coloneqq\frac{|\{i\in I:\operatorname{rk}_{i}(x_{i})=1\}|}{n}

(1)

Additionally, we consider the
average rank
, denoted by
\mathtt{r}(M(P))
, which is the average position of the assigned school in students’ preference lists:

\mathtt{r}(M(P))\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in I}\operatorname{rk}_{i}(x_{i})

(2)

2.2
Random Markets

To analyze the expected behavior of a mechanism—rather than its performance on a specific matching problem—we consider a
random matching problem
in which strict preferences and priorities are drawn independently and uniformly at random. While this assumption may be unrealistic for many applications, it enables a tractable analysis and for this reason it has been extensively used in the literature
(
Wilson 1972
;
Knuth 1976
;
Knuth 1996
;
Pittel 1989
;
Pittel 1992
;
Ashlagi
et al. 2017
;
Nikzad 2022
;
Ortega and
Klein 2023
;
Ortega
et al. 2025
;
Ronen
et al. 2025
)
.
Yet, this literature has not studied the Boston mechanism, with the exception of the recent work by PW.

We denote by
\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{IA}_{n})
the expected first preference rate in a random matching problem of size
n
in IA under truthful preference revelation.
We also use
\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{IA}^{*}_{n})
to denote the first preference rate in an
arbitrary
Nash equilibrium of the preference revelation game induced by IA.
Likewise, the notation
\mathtt{r}(\mathtt{IA}_{n})
represents the average rank under truthful behavior.

3
Results

3.1
First Preference Rate

Truthful Behavior

We begin by describing PW’s result quantifying IA’s first preference rate under truthful behavior.

Proposition 1

(
Pritchard and
Wilson 2023
)
.

Under truthful preference revelation, the expected first preference rate of the Boston mechanism approaches
1-e^{-1}\approx 0.63
as the market size grows large:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{IA}_{n})=1-e^{-1}

(3)

Their result is particularly interesting because it shows that the Boston mechanism assigns a significantly higher fraction of students to their first preference compared to other standard efficient mechanisms such as serial dictatorship.

Strategic Behavior

While the Boston mechanism exhibits an impressive first preference rate under truthful preference revelation, its well-known manipulability raises questions about its performance when students behave strategically. We analyze how strategic behavior affects the first preference rate in equilibrium.

Proposition 2
.

In any Nash equilibrium outcome of the corresponding preference revelation game, the expected first preference rate of the Boston mechanism converges to zero as the market size grows large:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{IA}_{n}^{*})=0

(4)

Proof.

The proof follows from three observations. First, as shown by
Ergin and
Sönmez 2006
, the set of Nash equilibrium outcomes of the preference revelation game induced by the Boston mechanism corresponds exactly to the set of stable matchings under true preferences. Therefore, any equilibrium allocation must be stable.

Second, from
Gale and
Shapley 1962
, we know that the student-proposing Deferred Acceptance (DA) outcome is weakly preferred by every student to any other stable allocation. Thus,
\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{IA}_{n}^{*})\leq\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{DA}_{n})
.

Third, in random markets, the average number of applications made by a student, as well as the average number of applications received by a school is
\Theta(\log n)

(
Knuth 1976
;
Pittel 1989
)
.
3
3

3

The
\Theta
notation provides an upper and lower bound on the growth rate of a function. Formally
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))
if there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2},n_{0}
such that
c_{1}\cdot g(n)\leq f(n)\leq c_{2}\cdot g(n)
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
Consequently, the probability that a student is assigned to their first preference is
\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)
.
4
4

4

This result is
“almost deterministic”
because the average number of applications is sharply concentrated around its expected value
(
Motwani and
Raghavan 1995
)
.

Since
\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{IA}_{n}^{*})\leq\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{DA}_{n})
and
\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{DA}_{n})=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)
, it follows that:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{IA}_{n}^{*})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathtt{f}(\mathtt{DA}_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)=0

(5)

∎

This result reveals a stark contrast between truthful and strategic behavior in the Boston mechanism. While truthful reporting leads to around 63% of students receiving their first preference regardless of the market size
n
, strategic behavior causes this percentage to vanish as markets grow large. This difference highlights how the incentives created by the Boston mechanism transform its properties substantially when participants behave strategically.

3.2
Average Rank

Truthful Behavior

Beyond the first preference rate, the average rank is also a valuable performance statistic that allows us to understand how satisfactory is the average student placement.
Pritchard and
Wilson 2023
provide theoretical and simulation results that strongly suggest that under truthful reporting, IA’s average rank is strictly better than Serial Dictatorship’s known
\Theta(\log n)
performance.

Conjecture 1

(
Pritchard and
Wilson 2023
)
.

Under truthful preference revelation, the expected average rank of the Boston mechanism grows logarithmically with the market size:

\mathtt{r}(\mathtt{IA}_{n})=\Theta(\log n)

(6)

Strategic Behavior

We now study how strategic behavior affects the average placement. We will denote by
\mathtt{r}(\mathtt{IA}_{n}^{\dagger})
the average rank under
a specific
Nash equilibrium for every random market, namely the school-proposing DA algorithm. In this set-up, we obtain the following surprising result:

Proposition 3
.

In a specific Nash equilibrium outcome of the corresponding preference revelation game, the expected average rank of the Boston mechanism is:

\mathtt{r}(\mathtt{IA}_{n}^{\dagger})=\Theta\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)

(7)

Proof.

The proof uses similar tools as Proposition
2
. First, for any matching problem, the school-optimal stable matching is implementable as a Nash equilibrium of IA
(
Ergin and
Sönmez 2006
)
. This stable matching leads to an average rank of
\Theta\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)

(
Knuth 1976
;
Pittel 1989
)
. Finally, taking expectation over market realizations yields the result.
∎

We conclude with a brief discussion. First, our equilibrium selection reveals that strategic behavior undermines average rank performance as severely as it does first preference rates. The transition from logarithmic growth to
n/\log n
performance represents a dramatic deterioration in rank-efficiency due to strategic manipulation.
5
5

5

Using an identical argument, one can also show that under any equilibrium selection, the average rank is bounded below by
\log n
(showing no equilibria improves upon truthfulness) and above by
n/\log n
.
Second, the equilibrium selection we imposed is empirically relevant:
Ashlagi
et al. 2017
show that in unbalanced markets with more students than seats, all stable matchings yield average ranks as poor as the school-optimal one, making our computed bound the likely outcome.
