Probing Light-Matter Interactions: Fluorescence Lifetime Manipulation in a Crystalline Colloidal Array
Authors: Haley
W Jones, Yuriy Bandera, Stephen H Foulger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe connection between a structured environment and the decay kinetics of an embedded emitter is explored by copolymerizing a naphthalimide derivative within polystyrene-based nanoparticles. The nanoparticles spontaneously self-assemble into a crystalline colloidal array, resulting in a partial photonic bandgap, or rejection wavelength, in the visible regime. The rejection wavelength of the liquid ordered array can be shifted across the emission spectrum of the nanoparticles by dilution with deionized water, which increases the interparticle spacing of the array. Time-resolved fluorescence of the ordered array at various rejection wavelength conditions is monitored at high- and low-energy electronic transition frequencies across the emission spectrum of the naphthalimide-copolymerized nanoparticles. Careful attention is given to the reference systems that are used to quantify photonic effects, the wavelengths at which decay kinetics are monitored, and the quantum yield of the naphthalimide-derived emitter. Increased and decreased excited-state lifetimes are observed, depending on the position of the rejection wavelength in relation to the emission of the emitter and the monitored wavelength, revealing critical insights in the context of quantum light-matter interactions and opportunities for strategic control over emitter decay pathways.

Introduction

Understanding and controlling quantum light-matter interactions at the nanoscale is fundamental to advancing quantum technologies, such as quantum computing and communication, and a crucial research focus within the ongoing second quantum revolution.1−3 While the influence of a structured environment on the spontaneous emission of light from a quantum emitter is well understood,4,5 the literature presents varying and sometimes inconclusive findings regarding the influence of a structured environment on the excited-state lifetime of an emitter.6−29 A typical approach to modulating the spontaneous emission of an emitter using nanophotonic strategies involves its integration within a photonic crystal. The extensive application of photonic crystals for this objective stems from their intrinsic photonic bandgap, where this bandgap pertains to wavelengths that are prohibited from propagating through the crystal and is often denoted as the rejection wavelength (λrw). The photonic bandgap arises in these structures due to the contrasting refractive indices of the meticulously arranged constituents of the periodic framework.30 When the photonic bandgap frequency coincides with the emission of an embedded emitter, the diminished local density of optical states (LDOS) within the bandgap results in inhibited photon emission at that frequency.31−33 The bandgap-induced suppression of spontaneous emission is often accompanied by the spectral and angular redistribution of energy to higher or lower frequencies, a phenomenon that has been both theoretically and experimentally explored in the literature.16,24,34

This work focuses on the electrostatic-packing of colloidal nanoparticles into a three-dimensional periodic structure known as a crystalline colloidal array (CCA).35,36 At high particle concentrations, repulsive Coulombic interactions induce self-assembly into a minimum energy configuration, which is a face-centered cubic (FCC) structure.37,38 Due to the low refractive index contrast between the building blocks of the periodic arrangement and surrounding medium, CCAs exhibit an incomplete photonic bandgap (i.e., stop-band) that exists in the visible regime and can be approximated using Bragg’s Law (cf. eq 1), where the wavelength of diffracted light from the CCA (λrw) is related to the refractive index of the structure (n), the angle of incident light (θ), and the interplanar spacing of the nanoparticles (dhkl).39−41 CCAs are exploited in this work for the dynamic control over the stop-band position, which can be shifted through the visible spectrum by increasing the interparticle spacing with water dilution. CCAs are commonly employed to control the spontaneous emission of embedded emitters;42−44 however, their excited-state lifetime manipulation is unresolved.

Since the 1990s, various theoretical9,10 and experimental6−8,11−22,26−29 investigations regarding the manipulation of spontaneous emission and lifetime of an emitter by a structured environment have been reported. A pioneering study exploring an electrostatically-packed array of polystyrene nanoparticles surrounded by dye-doped aqueous media reported an inhibited radiative decay rate when the stop-band was coupled to the emission of the dye.6 The increased lifetime was identified through comparisons with a reference system of dye-doped media surrounding a disordered array of nanoparticles. However, conflicting reports argued that the structured environment had no effect on the lifetime and any observed enhancements were attributed to geometric and chemical effects.7,13 After extensive discussion in the literature,8,11,12 the choice of reference system for assessing photonic effects on emitter lifetime was identified to be of paramount importance.19 Highly relevant studies regarding the connection between a structured environment and the lifetime of an embedded dye have continued to be investigated throughout the past decade, where this fundamental light-matter interaction remains unresolved.22,26−29 Recently, enhanced lifetimes were reported in rhodamine B-doped polystyrene nanoparticles sterically packed into an ordered array,22 while suppressed lifetimes were reported in sterically-packed polystyrene nanoparticles with embedded nanodiamonds containing nitrogen-vacancy centers.26

Generally, increased lifetimes have been reported when a photonic bandgap is coupled to the emission of an embedded emitter. However, many uncertainties still arise in the literature ascribed to three critical components: (1) the comparability of the reference system(s) employed to assess the changes in emitter lifetime; (2) the wavelength(s) and bandwidth used to monitor lifetime in relation to the spectral properties of the emitter and the photonic bandgap frequency; and (3) the quantum yield (QY, ϕ) of the embedded emitter. To definitively examine the effect of a photonic bandgap on the lifetime of an emitter, a reference system that accurately reflects variables that may alter the measured lifetime, such as particle density and emitter content, is critical. Additionally, meticulous attention must be given to the wavelength and bandpass width of which the lifetime is observed. Especially when dealing with a partial photonic bandgap, it is crucial to execute lifetime measurements at precisely the λrw, where the emission intensity of the emitter exhibits a local minimum. Moreover, it is imperative to measure the lifetime of the emitter at wavelengths shorter and longer than λrw to identify modulations at the high- and low-energy electronic transition frequencies of the emitter, particularly in the context of energy redistribution. Furthermore, the QY of the emitter requires consideration, where the lifetime of high efficiency emitters is dominated by radiative processes, while that of lower efficiency emitters has contributions from both radiative and nonradiative processes. Notably, emitters typically studied in physical photonic bandgap structures have been those with high QYs, such as rhodamine dyes13 and quantum dots,15,20 while theoretical calculations assume a value of unity or 1.25 If limited nonradiative decay pathways exist, such as in high QY emitters, the measured lifetime is dominated by the suppressed radiative decay rate at the bandgap, yielding the typically observed enhanced lifetime. However, the consequence of a structured environment on the lifetime of an emitter with comparatively available radiative and nonradiative decay modes remains unexplored.25 It is important to note that enhanced intersystem crossing (i.e., a nonradiative mechanism) has been demonstrated in a sterically-packed colloidal crystal with an embedded emitter, where the photonic bandgap effect was suggested to enhance the probability of the emitter to decay via nonradiative pathways.45

Motivated by the highly relevant and unresolved topic, a CCA composed of ca. 136 nm polystyrene-based nanoparticles with a covalently incorporated naphthalimide derivative (NMMA) was fabricated. Covalently incorporating emitter molecules within the nanoparticles allows for precise and dynamic control over the stop-band of the CCA through changes in interparticle spacing, leaving the emitters chemically and geometrically unaltered between measurements. A fluorophore with a lower QY than that of emitters typically investigated was exploited such that nonradiative pathways for fluorophore de-excitation were more appreciable. Steady state and time-correlated fluorescence measurements of the CCA with various stop-band positions were performed, where care was taken such that the reference systems used for all measurements were precisely comparable at each stop-band condition in regard to nanoparticle density and emitter content, and the wavelengths at which the lifetime was monitored were judiciously chosen.

Results and Discussion

The NMMA-copolymerized polystyrene-based nanoparticles investigated in this work were synthesized using a modified emulsion polymerization procedure with the addition of a naphthalimide derivative, 2-(3-oxo-6-piperidino-2-aza-2H-phenalen-2-oyl)ethylmethacrylate (NMMA) (cf. Experimental Section and Figure S1). The monodisperse (hydrodynamic diameter = 136 ± 13 nm) and colloidally stable (ζ potential = −56.4 ± 2.1 mV) nanoparticles spontaneously self-assembled into a crystalline colloidal array (CCA), termed the OA (i.e., ordered array). Studies aimed at evaluating the presence of NMMA dye on the particle surface indicate minimal, if any, following the synthesis (Figure S2). This observation is not unexpected, given the high solubility of the NMMA dye in the styrene monomer. This arrangement effectively inhibits unwanted chemical interactions between the NMMA dye and the surrounding media or particle surfaces.8,13 It is important to note that the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles contained a notably high dye concentration (ca. 9.5 mM) within the nanoparticles. To ascertain that any trends observed in the steady state and time-correlated fluorescence measurements of the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles were maintained in a system with less dye content, NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles with 100 times less NMMA content (ca. 0.095 mM) were synthesized (cf. Experimental Section), and additional steady state and time-correlated fluorescence studies were performed (cf. Supporting Information).

Photophysical Properties of the NMMA Dye and NMMA-Copolymerized Nanoparticles

The incorporation of NMMA in the nanoparticles was confirmed by optical absorption measurements, where the absorption of NMMA in aniline and the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles in aniline exhibit the characteristic absorption peak of naphthalimide with a maximum at 425 nm (Figure S3). Using the estimated average molar absorption coefficient of NMMA in aniline (8852 ± 6% M–1cm–1 at 425 nm) obtained using three concentrations of NMMA in aniline (1 μg mL–1, 2 μg mL–1, and 5 μg mL–1) and the Beer–Lambert law, the number of NMMA molecules per copolymerized nanoparticle was estimated to be 7492 ± 6%. The photoluminescence excitation (PLE) and photoluminescence (PL) spectra of both the free NMMA dye and the NMMA-copolymerized individual and isolated nanoparticles are presented in Figure 1a,b, respectively, where the spontaneous emission of the particles in deionized water is ca. 6 nm red-shifted with respect to that of the free dye in toluene. This slight red-shift is attributed to the copolymerization of NMMA in the polystyrene-based host material and different optical environment compared to that of the free NMMA dye in toluene. A time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) technique was utilized to scan the excited-state lifetime of the free NMMA dye in toluene and NMMA-copolymerized individual and isolated particles in deionized water at various wavelengths across their respective PL emission spectra. The observed lifetime of the free NMMA dye in toluene, shown in Figure 1a (cf. Table S1), exhibited exponential decay behavior and a progressive positive increment with increasing monitored wavelength. It is hypothesized that there are NMMA dye species or intermediates that emit at longer, red-shifted wavelengths, leading to a variation in decay kinetics throughout the emission spectrum.46 Consequently, the average decay time tends to rise with an increase in the monitored emission wavelength. Additionally, the high decay rate and residue shown in Table S1 at shorter wavelengths can be attributed to reabsorption that is possible at shorter wavelengths, where the PLE and PL emission of the NMMA dye are overlapping. A rise in decay time with increasing monitored wavelength was also observed in the lifetime of the individual and isolated NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles presented in Figure 1b (cf. Table S2). Specifically, when collected to a decay profile peak count condition of 10,000 (P10K), the observed average lifetime of the free NMMA dye in toluene varied from a low of 6.7 ns at 450 nm to a high of 8.0 ns at 570 nm (cf. red circles in Figure 1a), while that of the individual and isolated NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles in deionized water varied from a low of 8.9 ns at 450 nm to 10.4 ns at 570 nm. It is important to note that the decay profiles of the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles monitored between 450–470 nm were best fit with a double exponential decay function and two lifetime components (cf. red squares in Figure 1b), where the smaller lifetime component is attributed to the polystyrene-based host material (cf. Tables S11–S18).19 All other particle decay profiles were fit well with a single exponential decay function and one lifetime component (cf. red circles in Figure 1b). The increased lifetime and variation in lifetime across the emission spectrum of the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles compared to that of the free dye are attributed to the immobilization of the NMMA dye in the polystyrene-based host material and decreased degrees of freedom. The reduced molecular motion of the dye results in decreased nonradiative relaxation pathways and an increased probability of radiative decay. The QY of the free NMMA dye (ϕN) in toluene was estimated to be 0.54 by the comparative method with Coumarin 30 in acetonitrile (cf. Supporting Information, Figure S4). A similar method could not be employed to estimate the QY of the isolated NMMA-copolymerized colloidal nanoparticles (ϕP) in water due to significant scattering of the particles in water, which prevented the acquisition of accurate absorption spectra. To approximate the QY of the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles in water, the QY was first estimated for the NMMA-copolymerized particles in aniline using the comparative method with Coumarin 30 in acetonitrile (cf. Figure S4). Due to the close refractive index match between aniline and polystyrene, absorption measurements of the nanoparticles in aniline were possible; however, the poor miscibility of the nanoparticles in aniline resulted in a very low QY estimate of 0.0047 ± 5%, with weak emission observed (cf. Figure S5). Through a comparison of the PL of the nanoparticles in aniline and water, the QY of the nanoparticles in water was approximated to be 0.47 (cf. Supporting Information, Figure S5). Despite the only modest difference between the QY of the NMMA dye in toluene and the nanoparticles in water, it was expected that the QY of the nanoparticles in water would be greater, in line with the longer excited-state lifetimes. This discrepancy can be attributed to the inferential method of approximating the QY of the nanoparticles in water and larger error associated with these measurements compared to that of the comparative method, which is in the range of 5–10%.47

Nanophotonic Manipulation of Photoluminescence

Placing the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles within a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette leads to the spontaneous formation of a CCA aligned such that the {111} planes of its FCC unit cell are parallel to the cuvette wall.38,48 Consequently, the observed reflection maximum, λrw, of the liquid system can be ascribed to the {111} stop-band. Calculations of the bandwidth of a similarly sized polystyrene CCA predict a narrow 6–9 nm range,49 while the bandwidths observed in the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles range from 13–16 nm. The broader bandwidth of the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles compared to theoretical calculations is attributed to distortions and defects in the lattice.49 By measuring the λrw of the CCA normal to the cuvette wall and [111] plane, the Bragg angle (θ) is 90◦ and the interplanar spacing of the [111] plane of the crystal (d111) can be approximated by Bragg’s law (cf. eq 1). Using the estimated d111 spacing, the unit cell parameter (ac) is given by eq 2) while the nearest neighbor distance between colloidal particles (ann) can be predicted by eq 3.49 Contrary to sterically packed CCAs, the ann can be significantly greater than the nanoparticle diameter in electrostatically stabilized CCAs.

The partial photonic bandgap of the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles assembled into an OA could be red-shifted in a ca. 300 nm range through the emission of the NMMA emitter by increasing the interparticle spacing, which was achieved by dilution of the colloid with deionized water. Using the refractive index (nc) for this system49 and the observed λrw, the d111 spacing of the OA increased from 159 to 270 nm and the ann ranged from 59 to 194 nm as the λrw shifted from 436 to 738 nm. Nanophotonic manipulation of the spectral properties of the OA is presented in Figure 1c, where attenuated emission at the λrw (indicated by red arrows in Figure 1c) was observed, indicating the decreased local density of optical states (LDOS) at the stop-band.50 The λrw-induced emission reduction at the stop-band is clearly illustrated in Figure 1d–f, where the emission of the OA at each λrw position is presented in reference to the emission of corresponding disordered arrays (DAs), where the particle density and emitter content of each DA was kept constant in relation to each OA. Introducing a small amount (ca. 3.8 × 10–5 M) of an ionic impurity (sodium chloride, NaCl) to the OA disrupts the long-range order of the nanoparticles38,48 (cf. Figure S6) and generates a corresponding reference DA that reflects the photophysical properties of the OA without assembly into a structured environment, allowing for a precise and direct comparison of emission characteristics with and without a structured environment. It is important to note that the emission of the OA and corresponding DA overlay when the λrw is far red-shifted with respect to the emission of the nanoparticles (cf. Figure S7).

Emission characteristics of the NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles with and without assembly into a structured environment were evaluated when the λrw was positioned at 493, 543, and 583 nm (cf. Figure 1d–i), where a sharp dip in emission intensity was observed at each λrw coincidence. Specifically, the change in emission intensity of each OA in reference to the corresponding DA (ΔPL) integrated over wavelengths spanning the full width at half-maximum (fwhm) of the reflection peaks was −86%, −77%, and −34% when the λrw was at 493, 543, and 583 nm, respectively. When the stop-band was positioned on the blue- or red-edge of the emission maximum of the nanoparticles, the overall emission intensity integrated over the total spectral range exhibited an overall enhancement. Specifically, a +78% and +132% total emission enhancement was measured when the λrw was at 493 and 583 nm, respectively. However, when the stop-band coincided with the emission maximum of the nanoparticles (543 nm), the overall emission intensity exhibited a −4% reduction. The emission of the OA at each λrw position was further evaluated by independently assessing changes on either side of the stop-band. In all cases, increased emission intensity was detected at wavelengths longer than λrw, indicating a redistribution of energy to lower-energy electronic transitions. When the λrw was at 493, 543, and 583 nm, the emission at wavelengths longer than the λrw increased by +109%, + 63%, and +156%, respectively. Positioning the λrw at 583 nm (red-edge) exhibited the only case in which the emission was also enhanced (by 140%) at wavelengths shorter than the λrw. Positioning the λrw at 493 and 543 nm resulted in reduced emission at wavelengths shorter than the λrw, where the emission was reduced by −22% and −21%, respectively. These findings directly exemplify the Purcell effect,31 a phenomenon that elucidates the amplification or diminution of spontaneous emission from an emitter, attributable to alterations in its immediate surroundings. This is illustrated in Figure 1j,k, where photographs of a droplet of the liquid OA under white and ultraviolet (UV) light are presented, respectively. The λrw of the left half of the liquid droplet was at 530 nm, exhibiting an overall suppressed emission, while that of the right half of the liquid droplet was at 592 nm, exhibiting an overall enhanced emission. The OAs of NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles with a λrw at 530 and 592 nm were first fabricated in two separate cuvettes and the λrw of each OA was measured normal to the [111] plane as described previously. The droplet pictured in Figure 1j,k was fabricated by carefully adding aliquots of the two OAs such that they joined to form a droplet but did not exhibit significant mixing.

Nanophotonic Manipulation of Excited-State Lifetime

A time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) technique was utilized to scan the lifetime of the OA at various wavelengths throughout the emission spectrum to investigate the influence of the partial photonic bandgap on the decay kinetics of the NMMA dye’s high- and low-energy electronic transitions from the excited to ground state. As shown in Figure 2, changes in decay kinetics were evaluated as the stop-band was shifted across the emission spectrum of the OA, from 486 to 645 nm. As described previously, the stop-band was shifted across the emission spectrum of the liquid OA by increasing the interparticle spacing of the OA through dilution with DI water. Figure 2a–d presents the spectral changes in OA emission compared to that of the corresponding DA as the λrw (denoted by a red arrow) was red-shifted through the emission spectrum of the nanoparticles. The previously described procedure was employed to fabricate a corresponding reference DA for each λrw condition of the OA such that the nanoparticle density and emitter content remained constant. The red and blue crossbars presented in Figure 2e–h provide an introductory scan, collected to a decay profile peak maximum of 500 counts (P500), of the lifetime of the nanoparticles as the λrw was shifted to 486, 536, 590, and 645 nm. A more comprehensive lifetime scan (cf. red and blue circles presented in Figure 2e-h) was later obtained using a decay profile peak maximum of 10,000 counts (P10K). Lifetimes collected for P500 and P10K presented in Figure 2e–h are an average value over at least three separate acquisitions obtained at different locations in the array, where the maximum and minimum observed lifetimes at each wavelength are also presented. Decay profiles collected to P500 exhibited exponential decay behavior, yielding a single lifetime component (τ1) ascribed to the NMMA dye (cf. Figures S8–S11 and Tables S3–S6). Decay profiles collected to P10K exhibited nonexponential decay behavior and were adequately modeled by assuming two (τ1–2) or three (τ1–3) distinct decay components identified using a multiexponential least-squares fitting procedure and are discussed later (cf. Figures S12–S15 and Tables S7–S10). A fast scan (P500) of decay kinetics was initially collected across the emission of the OA prior to more comprehensive scans (P10K) due to the ca. 20× longer acquisition time of P10K compared to P500. Additionally, all decay profiles monitored at wavelengths outside of the stop-band required a significantly shorter acquisition time than those monitored at the stop-band, which required up to 1 h when collected to P500 and extended up to 12 h when collected to P10K. Further, a 2 nm bandwidth was judiciously employed for all decay profile collections to minimize observations of electronic transitions beyond the intended frequency range, such as those occurring outside of the stop-band when monitoring at the stop-band. It should be noted that the exact maximum of the λrw observed in each initially and comprehensively scanned OA are ca. 0–5 nm shifted from one another due to new samples prepared for each study. Additionally, the observed average lifetimes collected for P500 and P10K demonstrated a progressive positive increment as the monitored wavelength increased for both the OA and DA systems. As discussed previously, this phenomenon was observed in the lifetime of the free NMMA dye and individual and isolated NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles, indicating that this is characteristic of the dye itself rather than an artifact of the photonic environment. In the introductory scan of decay profiles to P500, observed lifetimes of the OA varied from 8.7 ns at 470 nm when the λrw position was 486 nm to a high of 14.1 ns at 575 nm when the λrw position was shifted to 645 nm. In the more comprehensive scan of decay profiles to P10K, observed lifetimes attributed to the NMMA dye varied from 9.8 ns at 475 nm when the λrw position was 486 nm to a high of 12.5 ns at 550 nm when the λrw position was shifted to 642 nm.

Introductory Lifetime Scan

In the initial scan of decay kinetics to P500, when the stop-band was positioned at the blue- or red-edge of the maximum emission of the OA, resulting in an enhanced total emission intensity as shown in Figure 2a,c,d, enhanced lifetimes were detected at the maximum emission. Figure 2i–l presents the changes in OA lifetime compared to that of the corresponding DA collected to P500. When the λrw was at 486 nm (blue-edge), a +2.5% increase in the lifetime was detected at 510 nm. When the λrw was at 590 and 645 nm (red-edge), a +5.8% and a +7.2% increase in lifetime was detected at 530 and 575 nm, respectively. Interestingly, when monitoring the lifetime of the OA at the stop-band, reduced lifetimes were detected at each λrw condition, with the exception of the λrw at 486 nm, which exhibited a slight increase attributed to potential photon reabsorption at shorter wavelengths. Nonetheless, the OA lifetime at the stop-band decreased by −2.5% when the λrw was at 536 nm, −2.1% when the λrw was at 590 nm, and −1.9% when the λrw was at 645 nm. Additionally, reduced lifetimes were detected at wavelengths shorter or longer than the stop-band, depending on the position of the stop-band with respect to the maximum emission of the OA. Wavelengths shorter than the stop-band exhibited a reduced lifetime when the stop-band was at the blue-edge of the maximum emission, while wavelengths longer than the stop-band exhibited a reduced lifetime when the stop-band was at the red-edge of the maximum emission. In contrast to the previously described cases of the stop-band positioned on the blue- or red-edge of the emission of the OA, when the stop-band coincided with the maximum emission of the OA, resulting in a reduced total emission intensity as shown in Figure 2b, reduced lifetimes were detected at the maximum emission. In this case, when λrw was at 536 nm, a −2.0% decrease in lifetime was detected at 536 nm. Additionally, reduced lifetimes were detected at wavelengths shorter and longer than the stop-band.

Comprehensive Lifetime Scan

To ascertain that the trends observed in the introductory scan of decay kinetics are genuinely representative of the system’s behavior, a more exhaustive study of decay profiles collected to P10K was subsequently conducted (cf. red and blue circles in Figure 2e–h). As stated previously, decay traces collected to P10K exhibited nonexponential decay behavior and were modeled by assuming two (τ1–2) or three (τ1–3) decay components. The multiexponential fits of the decay profiles collected to P10K of the OA and corresponding DA when the λrw of the OA was at 486, 536, 585, and 642 nm are shown in Figure 3. The multiexponential characteristics of the emission decay are ascribed to different subensembles of dye molecules, each subject to varying nonradiative decay rates, as well as contributions from the emission of the polystyrene-based host polymer. It is assumed that the extended lifetimes with the largest contribution to the decay fit (τ1), which range from approximately 10 to 13 ns and are in agreement with the introductory scan, are due to the NMMA dye. Conversely, the components with shorter lifetimes, which are less than ca. 6 ns (Figure S16 and Tables S7–S10), are ascribed to the host material (Tables S11–S18) and excluded from further analysis.

The analysis of decay profiles collected to P10K validates the trends initially observed in the introductory scans. When the partial photonic bandgap was coupled to the blue- or red-edge of the emission of the OA, the observed fluorescence lifetime at the maximum emission showed a noticeable enhancement while that at the stop-band exhibited a reduction. As the observed λrw was red-shifted across the emission of the OA, the lifetime detected at the λrw decreased by −4.2% at 486 nm, −1.7% at 585 nm, and −1.4% at 642 nm while the lifetime increased by +0.5% at 520 nm when the λrw was at 486 nm, by +2.8% at 530 nm when the λrw was at 585 nm, and by +2.0% at 550 nm when the λrw was at 642 nm in reference to the corresponding DA systems (cf. Figure 2m,o,p). Additionally, wavelengths shorter than the stop-band exhibited a reduction when positioned on the blue-edge of the OA emission, while wavelengths longer than the stop-band exhibited a reduction when positioned on the red-edge of the OA emission. Conversely, when the stop-band overlapped with the maximum emission of the OA, the observed lifetimes at and on either side of the stop-band exhibited a reduction. Specifically, when the λrw was at 536 nm, the lifetime was reduced at all wavelengths and exhibited the largest decrease of −2.9% at the λrw (cf. Figure 2n).

The observed reduction in lifetime at the partial photonic bandgap, which was detected at every observed λrw position in Figure 2, is somewhat surprising since, according to Fermi’s Golden Rule, the probability per unit time that a system will transition from an initial quantum state to a final state is proportional to the density of states (DOS) of the final state.51 When the emission frequency of the dye coincides with the stop-band, the DOS at the stop-band is reduced, yielding emission suppression at that frequency.31−33 While it has been reported that the attenuated emission at the stop-band modifies the lifetime of an emitter, the lifetime modification is highly dependent on the photophysical nature of the emitter,25 where the observed lifetime, or total decay rate (ΓT), is influenced by contributions from the radiative (kr) and nonradiative (knr) decay processes (cf. eq 4). The DOS decrease at the stop-band results in a diminished probability for radiative transitions, thereby increasing the lifetime of the dye if nonradiative decay channels are limited, such as in historically studied high efficiency emitters. It is hypothesized that the inhibited photon emission at the stop-band decreases the lifetime of a dye if sufficient nonradiative decay pathways exist and are more probable at the stop-band. Thus, even with a suppressed DOS as predicted by the bandgap effect, the total decay rate may still increase if other mechanisms, beyond those directly related to the DOS, are at play, leading to a shorter observed lifetime. The noticeable reduced lifetime at the stop-band in this work suggests that the NMMA dye possesses sufficient nonradiative decay pathways for de-excitation from the excited to ground state. This hypothesis is substantiated by the photophysical observations and QY estimations of the free NMMA dye and diluted NMMA-copolymerized nanoparticles, where the QY is related to the radiative and nonradiative decay rates of the emitter (eq 5). Owing to the increased availability of nonradiative decay pathways, the inhibited photon emission at the stop-band results in the observed decreased lifetime, emanating from the decreased probability of radiative decay and increased probability of nonradiative decay, where the decay rate of the more probable pathway is much shorter than that of the less probable pathway. Conversely, the increased lifetime detected at the maximum emission when the total emission was enhanced is indicative of an increased probability of decay via radiative pathways at the dye’s most favorable transition from the excited to ground state when radiative decay pathways for other transitions, such as those at the stop-band coincidence, are suppressed. In this case, the enhanced lifetime at the maximum emission is attributed to the increased photon emission efficiency exemplified in Figure 1d,f and Figure 2a,c,d. However, when the stop-band overlaps the maximum emission, resulting in suppressed photon emission at the most favorable electronic transition of the dye, the observed lifetime is decreased across the entire emission spectrum. This reduction emanates from the overall suppressed efficiency of the dye to emit a photon upon photon absorption, as exemplified in Figures 1d and 2b. Although the observed variations in lifetime are relatively modest, such modest changes are not surprising given the weak photonic characteristics of the system under study.9 Nonetheless, these results reveal new insights regarding the connection between the lifetime of an emitter and a structured environment and suggest that the excited-state decay pathways of an emitter can be strategically manipulated and controlled by coupling a photonic bandgap to specific positions within the emission of the emitter.

It is important to note that the colloidal array under examination in this work is characterized by a notably higher dye concentration, with each particle containing approximately 7500 dye molecules or a dye concentration of ca. 9.5 mM within the nanoparticles. This concentration is nearly 2 orders of magnitude higher than the concentrations reported in earlier studied CCAs with dye-doped media (0.1 mM)6 and dye-doped nanoparticles (0.5 mM).13 Nonetheless, the observed trends in lifetime when coupled to a partial photonic bandgap were maintained in subsequent studies using NMMA-copolymerized colloidal arrays with 2 orders of magnitude less NMMA content (ca. 0.095 mM) within the particles (cf. Figures S17–S18).

Conclusion

In this work, manipulation of the photoluminescent spectral properties of a quantum emitter embedded within a structured nanophotonic environment, known as a crystalline colloidal array (CCA) was demonstrated. Control over the fluorescence lifetime of an emitter was presented with careful consideration of the reference system used to identify changes in lifetime, the wavelengths at which the lifetime was monitored, and the quantum yield (QY) of the emitter. Specifically, a NMMA dye copolymerized within the polystyrene-based nanoparticle building blocks of a CCA exhibited enhanced and suppressed (up to ±7%) excited-state lifetimes when the stop-band was coupled to the emission of the emitter. Coupling the stop-band of the ordered array to the high- or low-energy electronic transition frequencies (i.e., blue- or red-edges of the maximum emission) of the copolymerized emitter resulted in a reduced lifetime at the stop-band and an enhanced lifetime at or near the most favorable electronic transition frequency (i.e., maximum emission). However, coinciding the stop-band frequency with the maximum emission frequency resulted in a reduced lifetime at the stop-band and across the entire emission spectrum of the emitter. While at first glance contradictory to previous reports, these results reveal new insights regarding the effect of a structured environment on the decay kinetics of an emitter with comparatively available radiative and nonradiative decay pathways. Reports in the literature generally focus on high QY emitters due to the favorable photon absorption to photon emission efficiency. Nonradiative decay pathways for these high efficiency emitters are limited, and the observed decay rate is governed by the radiative decay rate; thus, inhibited photon emission at the stop-band results in the observed increased lifetime. In contrast, the NMMA dye employed in this work has a comparatively lower quantum yield. Owing to the increased availability of nonradiative decay pathways, the inhibited photon emission at the stop-band results in a decreased total lifetime, emanating from the decreased probability of radiative decay and increased probability of nonradiative decay. These results suggest that the lifetime of an emitter can be strategically controlled by its surrounding environment, and the resultant changes in decay kinetics are highly dependent on the photophysical properties of the emitter. The phenomenon reported here offers significant insights into the realm of quantum light-matter interactions. Implications of these results are particularly relevant to fundamental aspects of quantum technologies, which is a critical research focus of the current decade.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Solvents

Styrene (99% Alfa Aesar), propargyl acrylate (96% Alfa Aesar), and divinylbenzene (80% Aldrich) were passed over the alumina basic to remove inhibitors prior to use. All of the other commercial reagents were used without further purification. Deionized (DI) water was obtained from a Nanopure System and exhibited a resistivity of ca. 1018 MΩ·cm. The copolymerizable naphthalimide derivative (NMMA) was synthesized using previously described methods52 and the “clickable” azide-modified naphthalimide derivative (azNap) was synthesized using previously described methods.53

Chemical Characterization

A JEOL ECX-300 spectrometer was used to record 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra. Chemical shifts for protons are reported in parts per million (ppm) downfield from tetramethylsilane and are referenced to residual protium in the NMR solvent (CDCl3: δ 7.26 ppm).

Synthesis

NMMA-Copolymerized Colloidal Nanoparticle Synthesis

Monodisperse NMMA-copolymerized colloidal nanoparticles were prepared using a modified general emulsion polymerization procedure.49,54,55 A standard emulsion apparatus equipped with a 250 mL 4-neck round-bottom flask, condenser, dropping funnel, nitrogen inlet/outlet, and stir bar was used for this synthesis. Initially, 50 mL of DI water was added to the round-bottom flask along with 0.32 mL (2.9 mmol) propargyl acrylate and 3.68 mL of a 0.3 w/w % solution of the naphthalimide derivative, NMMA, in styrene (32.1 mmol styrene, 25.5 μmol NMMA). Next, 11.04 mL of the solution of NMMA in styrene (96.4 mmol styrene, 76.4 μmol NMMA) and 0.96 mL (8.7 mmol) propargyl acrylate were added to the dropping funnel, and the apparatus was purged with nitrogen and stirred for 40 min. After a 40 min purge, 0.18 mL (0.18 mmol) of an aqueous 29 w/w % sodium dodecyl sulfate solution followed by 0.10 mL (0.7 mmol) divinylbenzene was injected into the round-bottom flask and the temperature was raised to 50 °C. Once stabilized at 50 °C for 5 min, a solutions of 0.14 g (0.99 mmol) disodium phosphate in 0.8 mL DI water and 0.14 g (0.82 mmol) potassium persulfate in 1 mL DI water were injected into the round-bottom flask and the temperature was increased to 70 °C. Once stabilized at 70 °C for 20 min, the contents of the dropping funnel were added dropwise at a rate of ca. 10 drops in 20 s. After half of the dropping funnel (6 mL) had been added to the round-bottom flask, 0.24 mL (1.7 mmol) divinylbenzene was injected dropwise to the solution followed by the rest of the dropping funnel contents. The reaction was allowed to run for 2.5 h. After 2.5 h, the reaction was cooled to ca. 37 °C and then gravity filtered through filter paper (P5 Fisherbrand). The resulting particles were placed in dialysis bags (50,000 MWCO) and dialyzed against DI water for 1–2 weeks, with water changed once a day. The dialyzed product was then shaken and stored with an excess of mixed bed ion-exchange resin (Bio-Rad AB-501-X8(D)) in a Nalgene container to remove any trace electrolytes or ions that might still be present after dialysis. The hydrodynamic particle size of the NMMA-copolymerized colloidal nanoparticles after dialysis was 136 ± 13 nm and the zeta potential was −56.4 ± 2.1 mV. The negative surface charge of the nanoparticles after polymerization and dialysis is attributed to some surfactant molecules that remain adsorbed on the particle surfaces and terminating SO4– groups from the anionic potassium persulfate initiator.

Polystyrene-Based Colloidal “Blank” Nanoparticle Synthesis with No NMMA Dye

Monodisperse polystyrene-based colloidal nanoparticles were prepared using a modified version of the general emulsion polymerization procedure described previously. Poly(propargyl acrylate) Colloidal Nanoparticle Synthesis

Poly(propargyl acrylate) colloidal nanoparticles were synthesized using a standard emulsion polymerization procedure described elsewhere.56 The resulting particles were placed in dialysis bags (50,000 MWCO) and dialyzed against DI water for 1–2 weeks, with water changed once a day. The dialyzed product was then shaken and stored with an excess of mixed bed ion-exchange resin (Bio-Rad AB-501-X8(D)) in a Nalgene container. The hydrodynamic particle size of the poly(propargyl acrylate) nanoparticles after dialysis was 113 ± 11 nm, and the zeta potential was −40.4 ± 1.4 mV.

Naphthalimide “Clicked” Poly(propargyl Acrylate) Colloidal Nanoparticle Preparation

Naphthalimide “clicked” poly(propargyl acrylate) colloidal nanoparticles were synthesized using a copper(I)-catalyzed azide/alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) click reaction. Briefly, 0.5 mL of the aqueous dispersion of poly(propargyl acrylate) colloidal nanoparticles were dispersed in 1 mL DI water and 2 mL tetrahydrofuran (THF) in a J-KEM mini reactor tube equipped with a stir bar. Next, 0.5 mL of a 1 mg mL–1 solution of the azide-modified naphthalimide derivative (azNap) in THF was added to the nanoparticle dispersion followed by 0.5 mL of an aqueous solution of copper sulfate (0.68 mg mL–1) and 0.5 mL of an aqueous solution of sodium ascorbate (1.4 mg mL–1). The resultant mixture was placed in a J-KEM mini reactor under nitrogen with stirring for 24 h at 28 °C in the dark. After 24 h, the naphthalimide-clicked nanoparticles were cleaned six times to remove any unattached azNap using THF and DI water in a 1:1 ratio via centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 15 min.

Nanoparticle Characterization

The hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles was measured by using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Coulter N4Plus dynamic light scatter). The zeta potential of the nanoparticles was measured by using a ZetaPlus zeta potential analyzer (Brookhaven Instruments Corp.).

Optical Characterization

A PerkinElmer Lambda 900 UV–vis/NIR spectrophotometer was used to obtain absorption spectra (cf. Figures S3 and S4). A Horiba Jobin-Yvon Fluorolog 3–222 Tau/TCSPC spectrofluorometer was used to collect photoluminescence excitation (PLE) and photoluminescence (PL) spectra as well as excited-state lifetime measurements via the time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) technique. For PLE/PL spectra presented in Figure 1a,b, a 0.5 s integration time, entrance/exit slit widths of 2 nm, and indicated excitation wavelengths were used. For PL spectra presented in Figure 1c, a 0.2 s integration time, entrance/exit slit widths of 2 nm, and an excitation of 405 nm was used. For PL spectra presented in Figure 1d–f, a 0.5 s integration time, entrance/exit slit widths of 2 nm, and an excitation of 405 nm was used. For PL spectra presented in Figures 2a–d and S16a–d, S17, and S18a,b, an integration time of 0.5 s, an exit slit width of 2 nm, and the 370 nm NanoLED light source employed in all fluorescence decay measurements was used as the excitation. For PL enhancement and quenching measurements (cf. Figure S2) utilizing BSA and Cu(II), respectively, PL spectra was collected using a 0.1 s integration time, entrance/exit slit widths of 2 nm, and an excitation of 400 nm. For quantum yield (QY) measurements, PL spectra (cf. Figures S4d and S5) was collected using a 0.2 s integration time, entrance/exit slit widths of 2 nm, and an excitation of 403 nm. For PL spectra presented in Figure S7, a 0.1 s integration time, entrance/exit slit widths of 2 nm, and an excitation of 396 nm was used. All TCSPC lifetime measurements (cf. Figures 1a,b, 2e–h, 3, S8–S15, S16e–k, and S17c,d) utilized a Horiba Jobin-Yvon NanoLED 370 nm pulsed light source operating at a frequency of 1 MHz and utilized a bandpass of 2 nm at the monitored wavelength. Reverse mode was utilized for all TCSPC data collection along with a TAC range of 100 ns, coaxial delay of 50 ns, sync delay of 116 ns, and peak presents of either 500 counts (P500) or 10,000 counts (P10K). For all data collection it was ensured that α < 2%. Horiba Jobin-Yvon DAS6 fluorescence decay analysis software was used to analyze the lifetime measurements and best fits were determined by the lowest χ2 value, which was always at least <2. An Ocean Optics bifurcated fiber optic bundle attached to an Ocean Optics USB2000 fiber coupled spectrometer was used to collect reflectance spectra of the colloid (cf. Figures 1–2 and S6–S7). The input arm of the fiber bundle was attached to a white light source (Ocean Optics LS-1-CAL) and the output arm was attached to the spectrometer. The incident light for all reflectance spectra was normal to the sample surface in the [111] plane of the CCA. A Spectroline B-100 black light lamp (long wave ultraviolet, 365 nm) was utilized as the UV excitation source for optical images (cf. Figure 1j,k). A Canon EOS Rebel T6 camera was used to capture the photographs.
