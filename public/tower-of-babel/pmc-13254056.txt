Thermodynamic-microphysical coupling mechanisms of hydrometeor phase transitions during persistent wind turbine icing
Authors: Jie Tang, Tao Feng, Lei Wang, Li Li, Huayu Zhang, Zhou Jian, Yi Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An extreme winter icing event from 1 to 7 February 2024 in central-eastern China induced sequential hydrometeor phase transitions (freezing fog, freezing drizzle, intense freezing rain, and mixed-phase precipitation) at Hunan’s Gutaishan wind farm. To investigate the underlying physical mechanisms, this study utilized multisource observational data, ERA5 reanalysis, and turbine operational records. The combined influence of the South Branch Trough and the Western Pacific Subtropical High drove a prolonged convergence of southwesterly warm-moist advection with easterly cold air, maintaining a stable temperature inversion. During the freezing-fog stage, this cold-warm-cold inversion structure trapped near-surface moisture, leading to the accumulation of supercooled droplets that ultimately caused a shutdown of 50% of the operational turbines. The freezing-drizzle stage was governed by the warm-rain mechanism. Due to the absence of ice-phase hydrometeors in shallow clouds (cloud-top temperatures > -10 °C), purely liquid drizzle drops fell through the underlying cold layer to become supercooled, triggering a farm-wide shutdown. The intense freezing-rain stage followed the melting mechanism. Abundant ice-phase hydrometeors from deep clouds (cloud-top temperatures < -20 °C) melted completely within a pronounced warm layer (vertical temperature inversion strength of 10.0 °C km− 1) and subsequently became supercooled in the underlying cold layer, accelerating blade icing. Finally, the mixed-phase stage was driven by a melting-refreezing mechanism. Mid-level dry-cold air intrusion weakened the warm layer, causing descending solid hydrometeors to undergo only partial melting. These hydrometeors then formed mixed-phase precipitation (coexisting ice pellets and trace supercooled rain) within the deep cold layer, maintaining the farm-wide shutdown.

Introduction

Wind power is a cornerstone of the global energy transition and a key component of the renewable energy sector. However, extreme icing events pose a critical threat to the operational reliability of wind power systems. By degrading blade aerodynamic performance, such events lead to substantial power losses and unplanned outages, representing a major challenge for the wind energy industry, especially as development increasingly expands into icing-prone regions1–4. This challenge is particularly acute in the mountainous and hilly regions of southern China, where complex low-temperature freezing-rain events frequently occur and pose severe icing hazards to wind turbines. A representative case is the 2020–2021 winter event in Hunan Province, where grid-connected wind farms experienced a curtailed capacity of 5,342 MW due to icing (80.4% of the total installed capacity), resulting in direct economic losses of 136 million CNY5. This highlights the urgent need for effective icing mitigation solutions tailored to such complex icing processes.

Freezing rain is a primary driver of extreme icing events6,7, and research on this phenomenon intensified following the catastrophic 2008 freezing-rain event in southern China8,9. La Niña events can induce persistent Eurasian circulation anomalies, promote the southward intrusion of cold air, while facilitating the northward warm-moist advection from the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea. These processes establish favorable conditions for widespread and persistent freezing rain in southern China10,11. Observational and radiosonde studies have identified two primary microphysical mechanisms of freezing-rain formation: the melting mechanism, in which ice-phase hydrometeors completely melt within an elevated warm layer (> 0 °C) and subsequently become supercooled in a near-surface cold layer, and the warm-rain mechanism, in which the collision-coalescence of liquid cloud droplets generates precipitation that becomes supercooled in the cold layer12–14. These mechanisms exhibit distinct spatial variability across China. The melting mechanism predominates north of 30°N15–17, whereas both mechanisms coexist farther south, with warm-rain processes prevailing in high-altitude regions such as the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau16,18,19. The 2008 extreme event exemplified this contrast: freezing rain in Hunan originated from classical melting processes, whereas in Guizhou, it primarily resulted from the warm-rain mechanism. This contrast highlights the combined influence of vertical thermodynamic structures (e.g., vertical temperature inversion strength and warm-layer depth) and cloud microphysical properties (e.g., cloud-top temperature and cloud-layer thickness) on hydrometeor phase transitions9.

Previous studies have primarily relied on surface observations, soundings, and reanalysis data to examine the synoptic conditions and cloud-precipitation processes associated with freezing events. However, a systematic understanding of the dynamic coupling between thermodynamic structure and microphysical evolution during continuous hydrometeor phase transitions remains limited. Recent advances in remote sensing technologies provide new opportunities to address this gap. Millimeter-wave cloud radars enable real-time observations of cloud vertical structure and hydrometeor evolutions20–22; microwave radiometers allow for minute-scale retrievals of atmospheric temperature and humidity profiles23; and fog droplet spectrometers can capture the concentration spectra and size distributions of near-surface supercooled droplets24. Building on these advances, this study integrated multisource ground-based observations with ERA5 reanalysis and wind turbine operational data to analyze the continuous hydrometeor phase transitions (evolving sequentially from freezing fog to freezing drizzle, intense freezing rain, and mixed-phase precipitation comprised of coexisting ice pellets and trace supercooled rain) observed at the Hunan Gutaishan wind farm in early February 2024. This investigation focused on vertical temperature and humidity profiles, cloud microphysical characteristics, and the underlying physical mechanisms. The results aim to provide a physical basis for improving forecasts of low-temperature icing events, optimizing anti-icing strategies under complex phase-transition conditions, and enhancing the resilience of wind power systems and grid operations during extreme weather.

Instruments and data

Field campaign observations and wind turbine operational records were collected during an extreme ice accretion event from 1 to 7 February 2024 at the Gutaishan wind farm (111.04°E, 27.82°N; 1400.4 m ASL) in Loudi, Hunan Province, China.

The core meteorological instrumentation deployed at the site included a Ka-band millimeter-wave cloud radar, a boundary-layer wind profiler radar, a microwave radiometer, and a fog droplet spectrometer. The Ka-band (35 GHz) cloud radar was operated in a vertically pointing mode to conduct continuous observations of cloud and precipitation structures. Compared with conventional centimeter-wave radars, the Ka-band system exhibits enhanced sensitivity to small cloud particles while maintaining greater penetration capability through dense clouds than lidar systems25,26. It features a vertical resolution of 30 m across 500 range gates (up to 15 km), providing primary detection parameters such as radar reflectivity (Z), Doppler velocity (Vr), velocity spectrum width (Wd), and linear depolarization ratio (LDR). Additionally, the boundary-layer wind profiler radar measured horizontal wind fields and vertical airflow up to 5 km with a 50-m vertical resolution. Thermodynamic profiles were retrieved via the microwave radiometer, which provided vertical profiles of temperature and relative humidity (RH) across 58 layers from the surface to 10 km, with resolutions ranging from 50 m (below 500 m) to 250 m (above 2 km). Near-surface microphysical characteristics, including fog particle number concentrations (Nc), liquid water content (LWC), effective diameter (ED), and median volume diameter (MVD), were measured using the fog droplet spectrometer (2–50 μm range) based on laser forward-scattering principles.

To assess the impact of the hydrometeor phase transitions on wind power generation, turbine operational status, including curtailed capacity and the number of shutdown units, was obtained from the wind farm’s SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) system. Furthermore, the ERA5 reanalysis (0.25°×0.25° spatial and 1-hour temporal resolution) from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) was utilized to investigate the synoptic circulation and the vertical hydrometeor structure of the icing event. Specifically, the vertical profiles of four hydrometeor species were extracted: specific cloud liquid water content (clwc), specific cloud ice water content (ciwc), specific rain water content (crwc), and specific snow water content (cswc). To evaluate the large-scale drivers of the event, daily variations in the polar vortex area (based on 500-hPa geopotential height) and the Western Pacific Subtropical High (WPSH) indices (intensity and ridge position) were also assessed using data from the National Climate Centre of China.

Results

Background circulation analysis

The extreme freezing-rain and snow event over central-eastern China from 1 to 7 February 2024 8,27 was driven by synoptic-scale circulation patterns resulting from the interaction between the Southern Branch Trough and the continuously developing Western Pacific Subtropical High (WPSH) (Fig. 1a, b). This synoptic configuration enhanced the southwesterly warm-moist advection into central-eastern China, where it persistently interacted with southward-intruding cold air propagating along an easterly trajectory, thereby providing the essential thermodynamic background for the severe icing weather. Specifically, during the initial phase (1–4 February), the expansion of the Asian polar vortex (indicating an intensified cold air mass) and the strengthening of the WPSH (reflecting reinforced warm-moist air transport) occurred concurrently (Fig. 2a, c). This interaction established a persistent convergence zone formed by the meeting of cold and warm air masses over the Jianghuai, Jianghan, and Jiangnan regions. Consequently, the surface 0 °C isotherm remained anchored across the southern part of central China, resulting in widespread freezing precipitation (Fig. 3a). During the subsequent phase (5–7 February), the sustained high intensity of the polar vortex, combined with the significant southward retreat of the WPSH ridge (Fig. 2b, c), facilitated a deeper southward penetration of the cold air. This caused the areas affected by the subsequent freezing rain to spatially overlap with the pre-existing frozen zones, leading to a severe accumulation of atmospheric icing (Fig. 3b).

Under these synoptic-scale forcings, the Gutaishan wind farm (50 MW installed capacity) in Hunan Province experienced significant icing-induced operational disruptions. Based on the SCADA operational records (Fig. 4a, b), this extreme icing event is categorized into four distinct stages: (1) On 1 February, temperatures dropped rapidly, falling below 0 °C by nightfall. This was accompanied by freezing fog, which triggered the initial shutdown and standby of several turbines. (2) On 3 February, supercooled drizzle occurred at the surface, exacerbating ice accretion and leading to a farm-wide shutdown. (3) From late 3 February to the early morning of 4 February, the precipitation phase transitioned into intense freezing rain, causing accelerated ice accumulation while the turbines remained offline. (4) Following a daytime precipitation hiatus on 4 February, the precipitation transformed into mixed-phase precipitation (coexisting ice pellets and trace supercooled rain) from the afternoon of 5 February to the early morning of 7 February under sustained low-level cold temperatures, maintaining the farm-wide outage. The icing process terminated as the weather cleared on the afternoon of 7 February. To characterize the complex hydrometeor phase transitions observed across these four stages, the following sections systematically analyze multisource ground-based observations and ERA5 reanalysis data to investigate the thermodynamic and microphysical mechanisms underlying these transitions.

Freezing-fog icing phase from the evening of 1 February to the early morning of 3 February

During this initial stage (the evening of 1 February to the early morning of 3 February), although significant surface precipitation was absent in the study area, the wind turbines experienced icing-induced shutdowns. To investigate the physical mechanisms of this non-precipitation icing, the vertical thermodynamic structure of the atmosphere was first analyzed. Microwave radiometer observations (Fig. 5) revealed persistent cold-air accumulation in the near-surface layer starting from the night of 1 February. This caused the surface temperature to rapidly drop below 0 °C, forming a stable cold layer with a vertical thickness of approximately 300 m. Meanwhile, the wind profiler radar detected a sustained, weak warm and moist southwesterly advection (2–5 m s− 1, Fig. 6) in the lower troposphere, driving the development of a pronounced warm layer (T > 0 °C) between 300 and 2000 m above ground level (AGL; all heights hereafter are AGL). Particularly from 06:00 to 18:00 (UTC + 8) on 2 February, the warm-layer maximum temperature exceeded 5 °C, while a secondary sub-freezing layer (T < 0 °C) reappeared above 2000 m. This classic cold-warm-cold vertical temperature structure indicated high static stability below the temperature inversion top.

Millimeter-wave cloud radar observations (Fig. 7) showed intermittent weak echo signals (-30 to -20 dBZ) in the near-surface layer (< 500 m). To further clarify the phase composition of the hydrometeors generating these weak echoes, ERA5 reanalysis data (Fig. 8) were examined. The results indicated that the atmospheric hydrometeors were almost entirely dominated by liquid cloud droplets, featuring a high specific cloud liquid water content (clwc) concentrated in the near-surface layer and decreasing with altitude. Conversely, the specific contents of other hydrometeor species, such as rain water (crwc), snow (cswc), and cloud ice (ciwc), were near zero throughout the entire atmospheric column. This distribution pattern indicated that neither ice-phase nucleation nor the fallout of precipitation particles occurred during this period. Instead, abundant moisture was trapped in the near-surface layer by the strong inversion, facilitating the formation of freezing fog. Concurrent observations from the fog droplet spectrometer revealed the microphysical characteristics of the droplets during this stage: the droplet number concentration (Nc) averaged 117.65 cm− 3 (peak: 644.96 cm− 3), the liquid water content (LWC) averaged 0.08 g m− 3 (peak: 0.42 g m− 3), the median volume diameter (MVD) averaged 21.17 μm (peak: 48.90 μm), and the effective diameter (ED) averaged 16.02 μm (peak: 37.64 μm).

The persistent accumulation of supercooled fog droplets in the near-surface layer, maintained by the stable temperature inversion, formed dense freezing fog. This fog significantly reduced surface visibility, obscuring the wind turbines from ground-level observation (Fig. 22a). The impact and subsequent freezing of these supercooled droplets on the cold turbine blades directly drove the ice accretion process. Consequently, 20 turbines (80% of the total units) had cumulatively entered a protective shutdown state by the morning of 2 February. As daytime solar radiation increased, the fog dissipated, allowing 18 turbines (72% of the total units) to resume operation. However, radiative cooling after sunset triggered a renewed fog formation process, which subsequently reduced the number of operational turbines to 50% of the total units by the early morning of 3 February (Fig. 4a).

Freezing drizzle icing phase from the morning to the evening of 3 February

Starting from the morning of 3 February, meteorological conditions in the observation area transitioned from the previous intermittent freezing fog to a light precipitation regime. Microwave radiometer profiles (Fig. 9a-c) revealed a characteristic thermodynamic structure consisting of cold-moist, warm-moist, and cold-moist layers within the 0–3 km (AGL) atmospheric layer, with this entire 3-km layer remaining quasi-saturated (RH ≈ 100%). The near-surface cold layer cooled further to -4 °C, accompanied by a retrieved liquid water content peak of approximately 0.15 g m− 3. Overlying this was an exceptionally deep warm layer (spanning 200 to 2000 m), featuring an inversion top at approximately 1 km and a vertical temperature inversion strength of up to 7.5 °C km− 1. Above the warm layer, the temperature in the secondary cold layer (2–3 km) remained above − 10 °C. Wind profiler radar observations (Fig. 10) showed a steady southwesterly warm-moist advection (4–7 m s− 1) maintained below 1 km, providing the necessary conditions to sustain the temperature inversion and moisture supply.

The microphysical evolution of this transition was distinctly captured by the millimeter-wave cloud radar (Fig. 11a). The cloud layer exhibited intermittent development and dissipation, yet the cloud-top height remained persistently below 3 km, corresponding to cloud-top temperatures above − 10 °C. Well-established cloud microphysics theory indicates that these temperatures are generally insufficient to initiate heterogeneous ice-phase nucleation13,14. Cloud radar polarimetric measurements further corroborated this: throughout the vertical cross-section, the linear depolarization ratio (LDR) remained persistently below − 18 dB (Fig. 11b), with a complete absence of the radar bright band typically associated with the melting of ice-phase hydrometeors. Furthermore, the vertical hydrometeor distribution derived from the ERA5 reanalysis (Fig. 12) showed that both the specific cloud ice water content (ciwc) and the specific snow water content (cswc) were zero. While the specific cloud liquid water content (clwc) decreased with altitude, the specific rain water content (crwc) exhibited a slight but distinct increase in the near-surface layer (< 500 m). This is highly consistent with the weak precipitation echo characteristics (-10 to 0 dBZ) detected by the cloud radar near the surface.

These combined thermodynamic and microphysical characteristics indicate a light precipitation process driven by the warm-rain mechanism. Within the cloud, cloud droplets grow into drizzle drops via collision-coalescence, becoming supercooled as they fall through the near-surface cold layer. The subsequent freezing of these supercooled drizzle drops resulted in continuous ice accretion on exposed surfaces, as evidenced by the surrounding vegetation (Fig. 22b). This persistent ice accretion gradually degraded the aerodynamic performance of the turbine blades, ultimately leading to a farm-wide shutdown.

Intense freezing-rain icing phase from the late night of 3 February to the morning of 4 February

From late 3 February to the early morning of 4 February, the vertical thermodynamic structure of the atmosphere exhibited significant changes. The vertical temperature profiles retrieved by the microwave radiometer (Fig. 13a) revealed that the depth of the underlying cold layer expanded to approximately 1 km, with the near-surface temperature remaining well below freezing. Simultaneously, the vertical extent of the warm layer (T > 0 °C) expanded between 1 and 3 km (reaching a warm-layer maximum temperature of 8.5 °C at an altitude of approximately 1.5 km), and the vertical temperature inversion strength intensified to 10.0 °C km− 1. This established a distinct thermal configuration featuring a deep cold layer and a pronounced warm layer. Wind profiler radar observations (Fig. 14) revealed that the southwesterly warm-moist advection below 1.5 km had strengthened to 16 m s− 1. This strong warm advection not only maintained the deep warm layer structure but also provided substantial moisture for the vertical development of the precipitating cloud systems.

Millimeter-wave cloud radar observations (Fig. 15a) showed that the cloud layer exhibited deep vertical development, with the cloud-top height exceeding 6 km and the corresponding cloud-top temperature falling below − 20 °C. These thermodynamic conditions supported the formation of ice-phase hydrometeors in the upper-level clouds. The vertical hydrometeor distribution derived from the ERA5 reanalysis (Fig. 16) suggested the coexistence of specific cloud ice water content (ciwc), snow water content (cswc), and cloud liquid water content (clwc) in the 3.0–6.0 km altitude range. This vertical hydrometeor structure is consistent with a mixed-phase cloud environment in which ice crystals could grow by vapor deposition (Bergeron process) and subsequently aggregate into snow crystals. The concurrent atmospheric profiles retrieved by the microwave radiometer (Fig. 13a-c) presented a thermodynamic environment conducive to the presence of supercooled liquid water at these altitudes. Although the radiometer cannot directly resolve the microphysical compositions of hydrometeors, these thermodynamic retrievals are consistent with the inferred mixed-phase conditions.

The ERA5 profiles showed that as the upper-level ice-phase hydrometeors (ciwc/cswc) descended into the warm layer below 3 km, their specific contents decreased sharply. Correspondingly, the specific rain water content (crwc) began to emerge and increase below 2 km, reflecting the process of ice-phase hydrometeors melting into raindrops. This microphysical transformation was evidenced in the radar observations by a distinct radar bright band in the linear depolarization ratio (LDR) cross-section at an altitude of 1.5 km (Fig. 15b), where the LDR exhibited a pronounced maximum. As the liquid raindrops fell through the deep cold layer below 1 km, they became supercooled before reaching the surface. The distinct presence of crwc in the near-surface layer (Fig. 16), together with an elevated surface liquid water content (0.40 g m− 3) retrieved by the microwave radiometer and enhanced near-surface radar reflectivity (10–15 dBZ), collectively indicated the occurrence of intense freezing rain.

These combined thermodynamic and microphysical characteristics demonstrate that the icing event during this stage was dominated by the classical melting mechanism. Upon impacting exposed surfaces, the supercooled raindrops rapidly froze, forming glaze ice, as directly observed in field photographs (Fig. 22c). This continuous ice accretion increased the aerodynamic and structural loads on the turbine blades, maintaining the wind farm in a state of shutdown.

Mixed-phase icing phase from the afternoon of 5 February to the early morning of 7 February

Following a precipitation hiatus from the morning of 4 February to the morning of 5 February, during which near-surface temperatures persistently remained below 0 °C, a new precipitation episode developed in the observation area starting in the afternoon of 5 February. This episode primarily occurred during two distinct periods: from the afternoon of 5 February to the early morning of 6 February, and from the afternoon of 6 February to the early morning of 7 February. Vertical profiles retrieved by the microwave radiometer (Fig. 17a, b) revealed a distinct reconfiguration of the vertical thermodynamic structure, characterized by a shallow warm layer overlying a deep cold layer. The warm layer (T > 0 °C) was confined to an altitude range of 2–3 km, with its thickness shrinking to approximately 1 km and its warm-layer maximum temperature dropping to around 3.5 °C. Concurrently, the underlying cold layer expanded upward to approximately 2 km, causing near-surface temperatures to decrease to between − 5 °C and − 7 °C, while relative humidity approached saturation (RH ≈ 100%). Wind profiler radar observations (Fig. 18) consistently detected northerly wind components exceeding 5 m s− 1 above 800 m. This dynamic feature indicated the intrusion of mid-level dry and cold air, which directly weakened the thermal intensity of the previously robust warm layer.

Millimeter-wave cloud radar observations (Figs. 19a and 20a) indicated that the vertical development of the precipitating cloud systems was somewhat restricted, with cloud-top heights falling below 5 km. Nevertheless, the cloud-top temperatures remained below − 20 °C, which was still conducive to the generation of ice-phase hydrometeors. The vertical hydrometeor distribution derived from the ERA5 reanalysis (Fig. 21) exhibited features distinctly different from those of the preceding intense freezing-rain stage. As the upper-level snow particles descended through the shallow warm layer at 2–3 km, their specific snow water content (cswc) showed an evident increasing trend. This indicates that as the descending snow particles entered the warm layer, their surfaces began to melt, creating a liquid water film that rendered the hydrometeors highly adhesive and promoted an aggregation growth process.

As the precipitation particles continued descending toward the bottom of the warm layer, the melting process intensified. The ERA5 profiles showed that, downwards from an altitude of 2 km, the cswc began to decrease rapidly. Correspondingly, specific rain water content (crwc) started to emerge. This vertical distribution demonstrates that a portion of the hydrometeors underwent substantial melting at the base of the warm layer. However, owing to the shallow depth and insufficient heat content of the warm layer during this phase, a large number of solid hydrometeors underwent only partial melting. When these partially melted hydrometeors and fully melted raindrops descended into the deep cold layer below 2 km, the fully melted liquid drops became supercooled raindrops. Simultaneously, the partially melted ice-phase hydrometeors refroze in the cold environment, transforming into ice pellets. Ultimately, a mixed microphysical structure characterized by the coexistence of supercooled rain and ice pellets formed in the near-surface layer.

The aforementioned phase-transition mechanisms are supported by multisource remote sensing data. Throughout the vertical cross-section, the linear depolarization ratio (LDR) measured by the millimeter-wave cloud radar predominantly remained above − 16 dB (Figs. 19b and 20b), and the radar bright band associated with the complete melting of ice-phase hydrometeors was notably absent. These elevated LDR values consistently indicated that the precipitation particles did not undergo complete liquefaction. Moreover, the near-surface liquid water content retrieved by the microwave radiometer was maintained at a low range of 0.02–0.03 g m− 3 (Fig. 17c). It is important to acknowledge the inherent uncertainty of microwave radiometers in phase detection, as they often have difficulty differentiating low amounts of liquid water from higher ice water contents during ice pellet occurrences. Nonetheless, combining the radar’s signatures of incomplete melting with the reanalysis-derived hydrometeor profiles strongly suggests that the precipitation reaching the surface was mixed-phase precipitation (coexisting ice pellets and trace supercooled rain). This microphysical state is visually evidenced by the on-site accumulation of spherical ice pellets (Fig. 22d). The continuous collision and adhesion of these mixed hydrometeors onto the blades contributed to persistent icing, thereby maintaining the prolonged shutdown conditions at the wind farm.

Conclusion and discussion

Based on multisource observational data (millimeter-wave cloud radar, microwave radiometer, wind profiler radar, and fog droplet spectrometer) and ERA5 reanalysis data, combined with the operational data of wind turbines, this study systematically investigated the sequential hydrometeor phase transitions and their underlying thermodynamic and microphysical mechanisms during the icing-induced shutdown event at the Gutaishan wind farm in Hunan Province from 1 to 7 February 2024. The main conclusions are as follows::

Based on the sequential hydrometeor phases and the associated icing mechanisms, the icing process at the wind farm was divided into four evolutionary stages: (a) the freezing-fog icing stage; (b) the freezing drizzle icing stage; (c) the intense freezing-rain icing stage; and (d) the mixed-phase icing stage.The freezing-fog icing stage occurred under conditions where surface precipitation was absent. A stable cold-warm-cold vertical temperature structure trapped abundant moisture near the surface. In the absence of ice-phase nucleation, the accumulation and freezing of supercooled fog droplets directly drove the ice accretion process on turbine blades, ultimately reducing the number of operational turbines to 50% of the total units.The freezing-drizzle icing stage was dominated by the warm-rain mechanism. With the shallow development of the cloud system (cloud-top temperatures > -10 °C) precluding ice-phase generation, precipitation was purely liquid. Drizzle drops formed via the collision-coalescence of cloud droplets became supercooled upon traversing the underlying cold layer, leading to the continuous ice accretion that triggered a farm-wide shutdown.The intense freezing-rain stage was governed by the classical melting mechanism. The atmospheric vertical structure evolved into a configuration with a pronounced warm layer overlying a deep cold layer, promoting deep cloud development (cloud-top temperatures < -20 °C) and abundant ice-phase particle generation. Upper-level solid hydrometeors melted completely within the strong warm layer (vertical temperature inversion strength of 10.0 °C km− 1) and subsequently became supercooled in the underlying cold layer. Upon entering the near-surface deep cold layer, these partially melted hydrometeors refroze into ice pellets, while fully melted drops formed trace supercooled rain. This resulted in mixed-phase precipitation (coexisting ice pellets and trace supercooled rain), which contributed to continued turbine icing, thereby prolonging the shutdown of the wind farm.

In the absence of ice-phase nucleation, the accumulation and freezing of supercooled fog droplets directly drove the ice accretion process on turbine blades, ultimately reducing the number of operational turbines to 50% of the total units.

The freezing-drizzle icing stage was dominated by the warm-rain mechanism. With the shallow development of the cloud system (cloud-top temperatures > -10 °C) precluding ice-phase generation, precipitation was purely liquid. Drizzle drops formed via the collision-coalescence of cloud droplets became supercooled upon traversing the underlying cold layer, leading to the continuous ice accretion that triggered a farm-wide shutdown.

The intense freezing-rain stage was governed by the classical melting mechanism. The atmospheric vertical structure evolved into a configuration with a pronounced warm layer overlying a deep cold layer, promoting deep cloud development (cloud-top temperatures < -20 °C) and abundant ice-phase particle generation. Upper-level solid hydrometeors melted completely within the strong warm layer (vertical temperature inversion strength of 10.0 °C km− 1) and subsequently became supercooled in the underlying cold layer. The rapid freezing of these supercooled raindrops formed glaze ice, which increased the aerodynamic and structural loads, keeping the wind farm in a shutdown state.

The mixed-phase icing stage was governed by a melting-refreezing mechanism. The intrusion of mid-level dry and cold air weakened the warm layer, causing the descending solid hydrometeors to undergo only partial melting. Upon entering the near-surface deep cold layer, these partially melted hydrometeors refroze into ice pellets, while fully melted drops formed trace supercooled rain. This resulted in mixed-phase precipitation (coexisting ice pellets and trace supercooled rain), which contributed to continued turbine icing, thereby prolonging the shutdown of the wind farm.

This study systematically reveals the physical mechanisms linking synoptic-scale vertical thermodynamic structures, microphysical hydrometeor evolutions, and surface icing hazards. The findings provide robust physical insights to mitigate the adverse impacts of winter storms on wind farms. However, the current study has not yet conducted a quantitative retrieval of progressive ice accretion thickness. Future research should integrate theoretical physical models with multisource data assimilation to develop three-dimensional numerical simulations of wind turbine icing dynamically constrained by hydrometeor phases. This will facilitate comprehensive engineering strategies for disaster mitigation, including accurate phase identification, quantitative ice load prediction, and optimized de-icing operations.
