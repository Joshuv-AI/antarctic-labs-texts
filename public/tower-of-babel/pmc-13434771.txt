Information overload and pregnancy outcomes in IVF: psychological distress as an explanatory pathway
Authors: Qian Yu, Xia Zhao, Tao Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examined the associations among information overload, psychological distress (fear of progression and perceived stress), and clinical pregnancy outcomes in 194 patients undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF) at a reproductive medicine center in China. Data were collected using validated scales. Information overload, fear of progression, and perceived stress were all reported at moderate to high levels and were positively correlated with each other (r = 0.407–0.465, all P < 0.01). After adjusting for clinical confounders, higher scores on information overload (OR 0.894, 95% CI 0.825–0.969), fear of progression (OR 0.915, 95% CI 0.851–0.984), and perceived stress (OR 0.903, 95% CI 0.835–0.977) were each associated with a lower likelihood of clinical pregnancy (all P < 0.05). These findings suggest that psychological distress may represent a potential explanatory pathway linking information overload to pregnancy outcomes in IVF patients. Routine psychological assessment and targeted interventions to reduce information burden may be beneficial.

Along with the trend of advancing maternal age at childbearing, there has been a steady increase in the incidence of infertility1. The advancement of assisted reproductive technology (ART) represents a critical therapeutic option for infertile couples seeking pregnancy. However, the protracted treatment cycles and complex procedures, coupled with limited success rates, impose substantial physical and psychological burdens on patients2. Existing evidence suggests that psychological states can modulate hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis function via neuroendocrine pathways, thereby influencing subsequent processes of embryo implantation and pregnancy maintenance3. Information overload refers to a state in which the volume of information an individual receives surpasses their cognitive capacity to process it, resulting in impaired decision-making and psychological distress4. Within the digital healthcare landscape, IVF patients are exposed to vast amounts of pre-pregnancy-related information via multiple sources, including online platforms and mobile applications. However, the variable quality and contradictory nature of this information can impede patients’ ability to evaluate it effectively, leaving them in a state of confusion. Studies have shown that information overload is prevalent among chronically ill populations and is significantly associated with patient demographic characteristics5.

The transactional model of stress and coping provides a useful theoretical framework for understanding these relationships. According to this model, stress arises when individuals appraise environmental demands (here, excessive and contradictory information) as exceeding their coping resources. This appraisal process can trigger negative emotional states such as fear of progression and perceived stress, which may in turn affect health outcomes. Applying this framework to the IVF context, information overload can be conceptualized as a cognitive stressor that, depending on the patient’s appraisal and coping capacity, may contribute to psychological distress.

A critical gap remains in the reproductive medicine literature regarding whether information overload is associated with IVF pregnancy outcomes through its relationship with psychological distress. The specific mechanisms through which information overload may relate to reproductive outcomes remain largely underexplored. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between information overload and clinical pregnancy rates among IVF patients and to explore whether psychological distress represents a potential explanatory pathway underlying this relationship.

Based on the theoretical framework and empirical background, the following hypotheses were formulated: (H1) information overload is positively correlated with psychological distress (fear of progression and perceived stress); (H2) higher levels of psychological distress are associated with a lower likelihood of clinical pregnancy; and (H3) psychological distress functions as an explanatory pathway in the relationship between information overload and clinical pregnancy outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center, cross-sectional study enrolled patients who underwent in vitro fertilization (IVF) treatment at the Reproductive Medicine Center of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University, between March 2024 and September 2024. A consecutive sampling approach was used, whereby all patients who met the eligibility criteria during the study period were invited to participate. The cross-sectional design was chosen to provide an initial exploration of multiple associations among the study variables, recognizing that causal inference would require longitudinal follow-up. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (Approval No.2021ZDSYLL207-P01) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and the “Measures for Ethical Review of Biomedical Research Involving Humans” issued by the National Health Commission of China (2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) currently undergoing or preparing to undergo IVF treatment; (2) aged ≥ 23 years; (3) capable of completing the questionnaire independently, with adequate reading and comprehension abilities; and (4) provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in this study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of severe organic or mental illness; and (2) cognitive dysfunction that precluded cooperation with the investigation.

General information questionnaire

Designed by the researchers, this questionnaire collected socio-demographic characteristics (age, occupation, education level, place of residence) and clinical characteristics (type of infertility, duration of infertility, number of miscarriages, BMI, AMH, AFC, number of embryos transferred, embryo quality, embryonic stage, clinical pregnancy outcome).

Information overload scale

Information overload was assessed using the Information Overload Scale, which was originally developed by Obamiro et al.6 and subsequently adapted into Chinese by Ding et al.7. The scale consists of 8 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 8 to 32. Higher scores indicate greater information overload. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the Information Overload Scale was 0.918, indicating good internal consistency reliability in this population of reproductive medicine patients. The Chinese version has demonstrated satisfactory construct validity in previous studies7.

Psychological distress scales

Psychological distress was assessed across two dimensions using validated instruments: fear of progression was measured by the Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form, and perceived stress was measured by the Perceived Stress Scale.The Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF), originally developed by Mehnert et al.8 and subsequently adapted into Chinese by Wu et al.9, was used to assess patients’ fear of disease progression. The scale consists of 12 items across two dimensions: physical health and social/family functioning, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Items are scored on a Likert 5-point scale from “never” (1 point) to “always” (5 points). The total score rangesfrom 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating a higher level of fear of disease progression. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.889, indicating good reliability in reproductive medicine patients. The Chinese version has shown adequate construct validity in patient populations9.Perceived Stress Scale (PSS): Perceived stress was assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale, which was originally developed by Cohen et al. and subsequently revised for the Chinese cultural context by Yang et al.10 to evaluate an individual’s perception of stressful events over the past month. The scale comprises 14 items across two dimensions: tension and loss of control. Seven items (4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 13) are reverse-scored. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often), with total scores ranging from 0 to 56. Higher scores indicate greater perceived stress. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in this study, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.827, indicating satisfactory reliability. The Chinese version has been validated in previous epidemiological studies10.

Survey method

Data were collected using a paper-based questionnaire administered to all participants at the time of enrollment. Following systematic training and standardized assessment, investigators distributed paper-based questionnaires to eligible patients. During the survey, they explained the purpose, completion instructions, and precautions in a clear and concise manner, instructing patientsto answer independently based on their own circumstances. All questionnaires were completed and collected immediately on-site. The data were then double-entered and cross-checked by twoindependent investigators to ensure accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with between-group comparisons conducted using the chi-square (χ2) test. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships between continuous variables. Multivariate analysis was conducted using binary logistic regression, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Pearson correlations among information overload, fear of progression, and perceived stress ranged from 0.407 to 0.465. According to established guidelines11,12, correlations below 0.7 typically do not produce problematic multicollinearity. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values for the three predictors ranged from 1.21 to 1.28, confirming the absence of substantial multicollinearity. Therefore, all three predictors were simultaneously entered into the multivariate logistic regression model.

Control variables (age, BMI, AMH, AFC, number of embryos transferred, embryo quality, and embryonic stage) were selected based on previous literature demonstrating their association with IVF pregnancy outcomes1.

Regarding sample size, based on the recommended minimum of 10 events per predictor variable for logistic regression, and with 10 predictor variables included in the final model and 103 clinical pregnancy events, the current sample (N = 194) meets the minimum requirement, although the number of events per predictor approaches the lower bound.

No large language models were used in the preparation of this manuscript.

The survey revealed that the 194 patients undergoing IVF reported a total score of 18.58 ± 4.41 for information overload, 28.79 ± 4.01 for fear of progression, and 32.13 ± 3.97 for perceived stress. Analysis of individual item scores revealed that the item “I feel at a loss when faced with numerous IVF treatment suggestions” was the most strongly endorsed, while the lowest endorsement was observed for the item “I feel many of the claims about IVF treatment are not very reliable.”

Discussion

This study focused on patients undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF), specifically investigating the key psychological challenges—information overload, fear of progression, and perceived stress—that they encounter throughout the treatment trajectory. The survey data indicated a moderate-to-high level of information overload among participants (mean total score: 18.58 ± 4.41). At the item level, the highest-scoring item was “I feel at a loss when faced with numerous IVF treatment suggestions” (2.58 ± 0.53), whereas the lowest-scoring item was “I feel many of the claims about IVF treatment are not very reliable” (2.11 ± 0.44). These findings suggest that while internet-based medical resources have expanded patients’ access to information, the fragmented and homogenized nature of the current online environment may exacerbate decision-making anxiety when patients are confronted with large volumes of unstructured information13. In addition to information-related challenges, the psychological burden of IVF patients warrants attention. Both fear of progression (28.79 ± 4.01) and perceived stress (32.13 ± 3.97) were reported at moderate-to-high levels. This may be attributed to the unique nature of IVF treatment, which constitutes not only a sustained physiological challenge but also a prolonged test of psychological resilience14. Throughout the cyclical course of IVF treatment, repeated blood draws, ultrasound examinations, medication injections, and oocyte retrieval procedures impose significant physical discomfort on patients. After embryo transfer, the patient’s strong hope for a successful pregnancy coexists with the risk of failure, further increasing their psychological burden15. In particular, among patients with prolonged infertility or a history of recurrent miscarriage, this phenomenon—characterized by a perceived loss of control over treatment and uncertainty regarding outcomes—is even more pronounced. Given the moderate-to-high levels observed across all study variables, this study proposed and examined a conceptual framework describing the associations among information overload, fear of progression, and perceived stress, as informed by the transactional model of stress and coping. The findings aim to inform the development of targeted psychological intervention strategies.

Associations among information overload, perceived stress, and fear of progression in IVF patients

Having observed elevated levels of information overload, perceived stress, and fear of progression among IVF patients, we further identified significant positive pairwise correlations among these three variables (r = 0.407–0.465, all P < 0.01). These findings suggest a significant association between the degree of information load and the emotional state of patients undergoing IVF16. The mechanisms through which information overload relates to negative emotions may be understood through multiple interrelated dimensions.For instance, exposure to complex and contradictory medical information can trap patients in decision-making dilemmas, thereby fostering subjective feelings of helplessness17. This cognitive load is associated with worries about disease progression. Furthermore, patients experiencing chronic information overload may exhibit a diminished sense of perceived controllability over various life domains, thereby elevating their overall stress levels18. Furthermore, fear of progression and perceived stress share a bidirectional and mutually reinforcing relationship. Specifically, worry about treatment progress is associated with higher daily stress experiences, while a persistent state of heightened stress further is associated with greater fear regarding future treatment outcomes19. This study also confirmed a significant positive correlation between fear of progression and perceived stress (r = 0.423, P < 0.01). These findings suggest a bidirectional and mutually reinforcing relationship between information overload and negative emotional states in IVF patients. This finding has important implications for clinical practice: healthcare professionals should move beyond single-dimensional information support toward a dual-integrated care strategy that integrates cognitive regulation with emotional intervention. By streamlining and curating high-quality medical information sources, interventions aimed at reducing patients’ information load may not only directly alleviate cognitive burden but also indirectly improve psychological well-being through reducing the pattern of interdependent relationships linking information overload, fear of progression, and perceived stress.

Factors associated with information overload in patients undergoing IVF

Univariate analysis revealed that patients aged > 35 years, those who were unemployed or had a lower education level, as well as those with secondary infertility, infertility duration > 3 years, or a history of ≥ 2 miscarriages, had significantly higher information overload scores (all P < 0.05). Information overload scores exhibited a gradient pattern across educational levels: patients with middle school education or below had the highest scores, whereas those with a college degree or above had the lowest, a difference that was statistically significant (F = 28.456, P < 0.001). This may be attributed to the stronger information literacy of patients with higher education levels, who are better equipped to access, comprehend, and synthesize medical information, enabling them to critically evaluate online content and identify truly useful resources. In contrast, patients with lower education levels may struggle to understand professional terminology, rendering them more susceptible to frustration and feelings of helplessness20, thus leading to information overload. Given their diminished reproductive window, older patients often experience a heightened sense of time urgency, accompanied by elevated anxiety regarding pregnancy outcomes21. This anxiety may motivate them to engage in more extensive information-seeking behaviors as a means of regaining a sense of control over the treatment process. However, intensive information seeking in the absence of effective screening and integration skills may precipitate confusion and further exacerbate cognitive burden. Patients with secondary infertility or a history of two or more miscarriages have a history of unsuccessful pregnancy outcomes. Consequently, they may harbor both heightened hope and underlying fear as they approach subsequent fertility treatment. This emotional ambivalence renders them eager to seek out any information that might improve success rates, yet ill-equipped to process the resulting influx, thereby heightening their susceptibility to information overload.

Psychological burden as an independent risk factor for clinical pregnancy rate in IVF patients

As shown in Table 4, patients in the non-pregnancy group exhibited significantly higher scores for information overload, perceived stress, and fear of progression than those in the pregnancy group (all P < 0.001), providing preliminary evidence for an association between psychological distress and clinical pregnancy failure. Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 6) revealed that after adjusting for potential confounders including age, BMI, AMH, AFC, number of embryos transferred, embryo quality, and embryonic stage, information overload (OR 0.894, 95% CI 0.825–0.969), fear of progression (OR 0.915, 95% CI 0.851–0.984), and perceived stress (OR 0.903, 95% CI 0.835–0.977) remained significant independent predictors of clinical pregnancy (all P < 0.05). This finding suggests that even in the presence of high-quality embryos, higher psychological burden may be associated with a lower likelihood of clinical pregnancy22. Regarding potential mechanisms, prior research has identified several pathways through which chronic psychological stress may be associated with pregnancy rates: (1) neuroendocrine pathway—stress hormones may negatively affect endometrial receptivity, which in turn has been linked to lower implantation rates in prior studies23; (2) immunomodulatory pathway—psychological stress has been associated with alterations in the Th1/Th2 cytokine balance, which may affect immune activity at the maternal-fetal interface24; and (3) behavioral pathway—patients with severe information overload may exhibit reduced treatment adherence, which has been associated with suboptimal treatment timing. These findings suggest that targeting patients’ psychological state and reducing information overload may represent a novel intervention strategy to improve clinical pregnancy rates in ART. In clinical practice, alongside the pursuit of high-quality embryos, equal emphasis should be placed on cultivating a “high-quality psychological state.”

Recommendations

Based on these findings, we propose a multi-component intervention system encompassing the following strategies:

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the temporal relationships among information overload, fear of progression, perceived stress, and clinical pregnancy; prospective cohort studies are warranted to establish causality. Second, although a consecutive sampling approach was used to recruit participants, this remains a form of convenience sampling conducted at a single center over a fixed time window. As such, it does not carry the generalizability associated with probability‑based sampling methods. Our findings may therefore not be generalizable beyond patients treated at similar tertiary reproductive medicine centers in China. Future multi‑center studies are needed. Third, the assessment of psychological variables relied on patient self-reports, which are susceptible to recall and reporting bias. Future research should incorporate larger, multi-center samples and employ qualitative methods to gain deeper insight into patients’ authentic experiences with information overload. Fourth, with 10 predictor variables and 103 pregnancy events, our sample size (n = 194) meets the minimum recommended 10 events per predictor but remains modest; this may limit the stability of the regression estimates. Future studies with larger samples are warranted to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

This study reveals that patients undergoing IVF exhibit moderate-to-high levels of information overload, perceived stress, and fear of progression, with significant positive correlations observed among all three variables. Patients aged > 35 years, those who were unemployed or had lower education levels, as well as those with secondary infertility, infertility duration > 3 years, or a history of ≥ 2 miscarriages were identified as high-risk groups for information overload. Further analysis revealed that higher psychological burden was independently associated with a lower likelihood of clinical pregnancy, with a magnitude of effect comparable to that of traditional physiological indicators such as embryo quality. These findings underscore the importance of integrating psychological assessment and intervention into routine care protocols for IVF patients, alongside continued attention to physiological indicators such as embryo quality. Cultivating a “high-quality psychological state” may synergistically optimize pregnancy outcomes in IVF patients.
