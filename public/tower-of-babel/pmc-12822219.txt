Assessment of shared decision-making tool for eliciting informed goals of care in the hospitalized elderly (ASKmeGOC): protocol for a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Chris Martin, Kelly Cruise, Monica Aggarwal, Doug Austgarden, Giulio DiDiodato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGoals of care discussions (GOCDs) occur between patients, substitute decision-makers, and healthcare providers to ensure shared decision-making about treatment preferences that are aligned with both patients’ values and goals and healthcare provider recommendations. GOCDs promote patient autonomy and high-value care. This study will examine the real-world effectiveness of using a novel ASKmeGOC e-tool (ASKmeGOC e-tool) to support nursing-facilitated GOCDs with patients and their substitute decision-makers. We hypothesize that these GOCDs will be acceptable to patients and attending physicians, promote more informed decisions about life-sustaining treatment preferences, and result in fewer undocumented CODE STATUS preferences that will result in both reduced rates of intensive care utilization and direct patient hospitalization costs compared to usual GOCDs conducted by physicians.MethodsThe ASKmeGOC trial is designed as a prospective, single-center, stratified, parallel group, allocation concealed, assessor masked, randomized, pragmatic, mixed-method, and comparative effectiveness trial. The primary outcomes include total days of intensive care, ventilator and dialysis utilization during both the index hospitalization, and at 12 months post-admission. The hypothesized sample size is 950 participants per group to demonstrate a reduction in utilization of ≥ 20%. This study will evaluate the real-world effectiveness of a novel tool to facilitate GOCDs by non-physician healthcare providers. Background

Goals of care discussions (GOCDs) refers to the process of shared-decision making that occurs between a healthcare provider (HCP) and a patient and, ideally, their substitute decision-maker [1, 2]. The focus of GOCDs is to elicit care preferences that are informed by and aligned with a patient’s values and goals, while also taking HCP recommendations into consideration [3–6]. GOCDs make known the care preferences of patients and increase the likelihood that patients receive goal-concordant care [7] by promoting and protecting patient autonomy and minimizing unwanted or low-value care [8].

Many hospitalized patients with serious or deteriorating illnesses go on to receive aggressive medical and surgical interventions. Unlike terminal illnesses, it is difficult to predict which patients will benefit from these treatments [9, 10], making decisions about whether to accept (patient), provide (HCP), or withdraw (patient and HCP) life-sustaining therapies (LSTs) in these situations very complex and uncertain leading to tremendous variation in LST utilization [11, 12]. In addition, up to 75% of seriously ill patients will lack decision-making capacity at the time of their serious illness [13], with less than 25% having communicated their treatment preferences with either their substitute decision-maker or HCP. Failure to engage hospitalized patients with or at high risk of developing a serious illness may, therefore, lead to unwanted or low-value care and contribute to unnecessary pain and suffering in patients. This may also cause a negative emotional burden for substitute decision-makers who may doubt whether they had made the right decisions about LSTs [14, 15], thus emphasizing the need for high-quality upstream GOCDs in high-risk populations, prior to deterioration or development of a serious illness. Some have even suggested that this failure to engage high-risk hospitalized patients in GOCDs is tantamount to a medical error of omission that adversely affects patient safety [16]. Even in those patients who have been approached by HCPs about their preferences, the language used in these conversations is critical and can lead to significant misunderstandings between patients and HCPs that may promote goal-discordant care [17–21].

Given the multidimensional and complex nature of GOCDs, it is not surprising that there exist many barriers to effective communication between HCPs and patients [3, 21–34] resulting in many missed opportunities for elicitation of goals of care in seriously ill hospitalized patients [35]. The most commonly reported barriers are lack of knowledge about outcomes and prognoses associated with acute illnesses, lack of knowledge about risks, benefits and outcomes associated with care options and preferences, and communication training and time to commit to a GOCD.

In response, many recent studies and recommendations to promote GOCDs have been published [6, 29, 33, 34, 36–41]. While providing useful guides to promote conversations that contain important components of a comprehensive GOCD, they are limited by the general nature of their content, inability to personalize information about care options, outcomes, and prognoses, and variability in their adoption and implementation in clinical practice. Few of the tools are disease agnostic, therefore limiting their scalability and applicability to a general hospitalized population at high risk of dying from any cause and not necessarily from the terminal progression of a specific chronic illness. For all the guides and tools, there was a lack of consistent patient outcomes evidence to recommend the clinical use of any one intervention compared to another. There was a lack of evidence to support how best to implement these interventions in a systematic way across the healthcare system to ensure a standardized approach to GOCDs, and this resulted in very few tools being adopted and implemented as a part of the routine care of seriously ill hospitalized patients. The difficulty of translating patient’s values and goals into care options and recommendations was frequently encountered with few tools equipped to facilitate this component of GOCDs. Many of the tools were not publicly available, and many neglected to use evidence-based techniques for presenting outcome and prognostic information. Because most tools were paper based, the content could not be easily personalized for individuals’ specific set of clinical circumstances, and they could not be easily updated. While all of the computer-based tools tested in randomized clinical trials demonstrated efficacy to improve at least one patient-centered outcome such as decisional conflict, satisfaction with decisions, or health care outcomes, all were directed to specific chronic disease conditions, and none was generalizable to seriously ill hospitalized patients.

In response, we developed a digital tool (ASKmeGOC e-tool) to promote GOCDs in a general hospital population and tested feasibility of implementation, intervention fidelity, and patient and healthcare provider acceptability [42]. Our pilot study demonstrated acceptable implementation, intervention adherence, and acceptability among stakeholders and suggested there may be an important benefit in ensuring that treatment preferences for LSTs are goal-concordant and documented across the healthcare system. In that pilot, the ASKmeGOC e-tool was used by a physician assistant to deliver a facilitated GOCD (f-GOCD). Our plan is to conduct a randomized proof-of-concept study to verify the findings from the feasibility study. We will randomize patients to f-GOCDs delivered by nurses using the ASKmeGOC e-tool compared to usual care to determine if there is evidence of effectiveness in reducing utilization of LSTs.

Design

The ASKMEGOC trial is designed as a prospective, single-center, stratified, parallel group, allocation concealed, assessor masked, randomized, pragmatic, mixed-method, and comparative effectiveness trial in hospitalized elderly patients 80 years and older.

Sample size

In our pilot study, there was an absolute difference of 38.2% (95% CI 22.1% to 54.3%) fewer patients in the f-GOCD group whose final CODE STATUS included LSTs compared to the control group [42]. We hypothesized that a ≥ 20% reduction in LSTs days is clinically significant. A Poisson regression of the composite outcome of LST utilization for the index hospitalization on a binomial distributed independent variable (f-GOCD vs usual care) using a total sample of 800 observations would achieve power ≥ 80% at the Bonferroni-corrected = 2.5% significance level for a two-sided test to detect a reduction in the composite outcome of LST utilization of ≥ 20% due to the f-GOCD intervention if the baseline composite LST utilization rate was 0.017 LST days per hospital day, and the mean hospital length of stay was 12 days. These assumptions were derived from the Canadian Institute for Health Information (estimated average length of stay in elderly patients ≥ 80 years was 12.27 days (95% CI 11.9, 12.6)) [43], and the average composite outcome of LST utilization rate of 0.017 days per hospital day was derived from our own hospital’s statistics for the 5-year period from 2016 to 2020 (mean = 0.0169 (SD 0.0039)).

This study will be conducted at the Royal Victoria Regional Health Centre in Barrie, ON, Canada. The Royal Victoria Regional Health Care Center is a 388-bed acute care, large community hospital serving a municipality of 150,000 residents located 100 km north of Toronto, ON, in Central Ontario.

f-GOCD intervention

The f-GOCD intervention has been previously described in our pilot study [42]. In brief, a locally developed web-based, ASKmeGOC e-tool will provide a structured approach to conducting f-GOCDs with hospitalized patients at high risk of serious illness. Different from the pilot study, ICU nurses use the ASKmeGOC e-tool to conduct the f-GOCD. Preemptive consent to approach eligible patients for a f-GOCD was obtained from the department chiefs for all attending physicians. Each morning at 9:00 am from Monday to Friday, an ICU study nurse will run a study report that identifies all consecutive patients ≥ 80 years old admitted to a hospital ward in the previous 24 h (or from Friday 09:00 to Monday 09:00 on Monday mornings since there is no coverage on either Saturday or Sunday). The ICU study nurse will then verify eligibility by using the electronic health record and/or contacting the primary care team as required. All eligible patients will undergo Zelen randomization [45] by the research coordinator. The ICU study nurse will approach the patient and/or the substitute decision-maker to obtain verbal consent to conduct a f-GOCD using the ASKmeGOC e-tool. If consent is provided, a scheduled time with both the patient and/or the substitute decision-maker will attempt to be organized for the f-GOCD. Ideally, all f-GOCDs will take place with both of these agents to ensure that the substitute decision-maker is fully aware of the patient’s treatment preferences. The goal is to ensure that capable at-risk patients are making their own informed decisions well in advance of a life-threatening illness. This will not only ensure goal-concordant care in the event of deterioration but will reduce the decision-making burden of substitute decision-makers, as patient wishes have already been expressed. The ASKmeGOC e-tool has four major components that include the following: (i) Prehospital assessment of quality of life and frailty, (ii) prognostic score for expected hospital survival rate given admission diagnosis, (iii) prognostic score for expected hospital survival rate after inhospital cardiopulmonary arrest, and (iv) elicitation of patients’ values and goals and alignment with HCP recommendations. Once completed, a formal review of the patient’s resuscitation treatment preferences will take place, and, if desired, the ICU study nurse will help the patient/substitute decision-maker complete or revise their CODE STATUS. The ICU study nurse will then review the f-GOCD and the CODE STATUS with the study intensivist. The study intensivist will document their opinion (agreement or disagreement) about the patient’s CODE STATUS. Any disagreement will be resolved by the study intensivist discussing the GOCD contents with the attending physician/patient/substitute decision-maker. Following this review, the study intensivist will contact the attending physician and inform them of any changes to the CODE STATUS. The attending physician will be asked to provide their opinion (agreement or disagreement) about the patient’s CODE STATUS. In our pilot study [42], no attending physician disagreed with the patient’s/substitute decision-maker’s preferences elicited through the intervention. The ICU study nurse will document the outcome of the f-GOCD using a standardized reporting template along with updating the patient’s CODE STATUS in the patient electronic medical record (EMR). Both will be available to any healthcare provider with access to the EMR, including the patient’s family physician in the community who will receive a copy of the EMR report of the f-GOCD summary.

Usual care

In the usual care arm (control or declined f-GOCD intervention), attending physicians may choose to conduct GOCDs as deemed necessary using whatever approach they deem appropriate. Attending physicians may choose to complete the CODE STATUS with the patient and/or their substitute decision-maker as deemed necessary using whatever approach they deem appropriate. Attending physicians will not be able to access the ASKmeGOC e-tool to facilitate their GOCDs during the study period nor will they be able to request a f-GOCD from the study nurses to prevent contamination. Patients who do not complete a CODE STATUS are, by default, to receive full resuscitative measures at the Royal Victoria Regional Health Centre.

Data management

Only authorized trial personnel will have password-protected access to the study data. All study data will be stored according to the Personal Health Information Protection Act (PHIPA) [50] in REDCap [51]. All trial personnel who work with this data will have their Tri-Council Policy Statement 2 certification in the ethical conduct for research involving humans. A randomly generated unique study identifier will be assigned to each study participant. This study identifier will be used exclusively in all analytical files. This study identifier will be linked to a unique patient health identifier in a master list that will be used throughout the study duration. This master list will be maintained by the principal investigator in a password-protected Excel file on a dedicated laptop computer in the principal investigator’s private office to ensure file security. This file will be made available to other trial personnel as needed for the sole purpose of conducting the study. All study data will be maintained for 15 years and subsequently permanently destroyed.

The minimum amount of personal health information will be collected to limit the risk of data breaches. All personal identifying information will be removed from REDCap once enrolment and follow-up are complete. Direct and indirect personal health identifiers will not be included in the analytic database.

Participant encounters by trial personnel will take place in private areas only to avoid risk of f-GOCDs being overheard by others not directly involved in the research. Follow-up phone calls (if applicable) will be conducted by trial personnel from private offices to avoid being overheard by anyone who is not directly involved with the research. Voicemail messages will not be left for participants who are not available by phone.

Safety

The principal investigators have extensive experience with the f-GOCD intervention, having created the software, tested it on volunteers, trained healthcare providers (study ICU nurses and physician assistants) on how to incorporate the tool in a structured approach to f-GOCDs, and used it to pilot test its effectiveness in a similar population included in this trial [42]. During the pilot, there were no reported adverse effects among the exposed patients, substitute decision-makers, or their attending physicians. Additional protective measures include trial personnel certified in human subjects’ research ethics and regulations, voluntary participation of patients, and substitute decision-makers through an informed consent process, PHIPA-compliant data collection, storage and use, study monitor, and data monitoring committee providing independent audit and trial oversight.

Statistical analysis plan

LST utilization

We will use zero-inflated count multivariate regression model to assess the primary composite outcome of LST utilization during the index hospitalization and the 12-month period from the time of admission of the index hospitalization. The rationale for this modelling is based on review of the historical ICU utilization data previously mentioned. Sensitivity analysis assessing the potential impact of overdispersion on the model outcomes will be included. Only the final CODE STATUS categories will be forced into the final model, as all other variables are expected to be balanced given the RCT design. The risk period for the index hospitalization will be defined as the hospital length of stay, and the risk period for the 12-month period following admission for the index hospitalization will be defined as the number of days alive (up to a maximum of 365.25 days from the time of admission of the index hospitalization). The main analyses will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis by analyzing participants according to the treatment to which they were assigned and regardless of compliance.

Baseline data and secondary outcomes

For secondary outcomes that report proportions, 2 analyses will be used to compare groups. For secondary outcomes that report scores from questionnaires, the ANOVA or Wilcoxon rank-sum test will be used to compare groups depending on the underlying distribution of scores. We will compare the demographic and baseline values of all variables across the treatment arms using ANOVA and 2 tests for continuous and binary data, respectively, and include those variables in a sensitivity analysis in the final statistical model whose p-value < 0.001 for differences across groups. For descriptive analyses, means and 95% confidence intervals (CI) or medians and interquartile ranges will be used to summarize observations depending on the underlying distribution of data. All categorical data will be reported as frequencies and proportions. All secondary outcome differences are exploratory. During the qualitative study, data collection and analysis will take place concurrently. The transcripts will be analyzed using techniques of qualitative thematic analysis, inspired by a deductive directed approach, since the CFIR constructs is being used as an a priori analysis framework. Transcripts will be coded using NVivo 10 qualitative analysis software. Thematic analysis of data will take place using the constant comparative method. To ensure that there is inter-rater reliability, a member of the team will read a sample of transcripts to confirm the accuracy and reliability of the coding. If the comparison of coding is not within 95%, researchers will work together to build consensus on the coding structure. All interviews will be reviewed and re-coded based on this structure. Discussion between reviewers will be ongoing to ensure the qualitative analysis is reliable and accurate.

Missing data

No imputation procedures will be used to replace missing data. All patients, regardless of missing data, will be included in the final analyses. All outcome data will be collected for all randomized patients to the intervention group regardless if they provide consent for the intervention. The rationale for this is that all outcome data is being collected in patients regardless of their inclusion in the study given this is routinely collected data during their hospitalization. This practice is consistent with pragmatic studies.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were involved in the design of the original version and subsequent modified versions of the ASKmeGOC e-tool [42].

In general, GOCD should include the following four component parts [3, 4, 6, 36, 52]:

There is a single study that attempted to validate the value of these components in promoting goal-concordant care [53]. Prior to their validation, Sinuff et al. had developed a set of quality indicators using a Delphi method to evaluate GOCDs that included these four components [54]. In their final list of 13 quality indicators for GOCDs, component (i) was ranked as the most important among the indicators with a final mean score of 6.75 (standard deviation (SD) 0.44) (maximum importance score of 7 on a Likert scale). Component (iii) was ranked second (mean score 6.63 (SD 0.65)), component (ii) was ranked sixth (mean score 6.29 (SD 0.81)), and component (iv) was ranked ninth (mean score 6.21 (SD 1.02)). In the documentation category, documentation of the GOCDs in the medical record was ranked second overall among the 32 quality indicators they developed to evaluate the entire spectrum of end-of-life communication and decision-making. In their validation study [53, 55], Heyland et al. enrolled 297 hospitalized patients and 209 family members at 12 Canadian teaching hospitals. The medical records and participants were reviewed to determine if any of the quality indicators had been part of a serious illness conversation. The investigators assigned the importance scores derived from the Delphi study to each quality indicator that had been affirmed to be part of a serious illness conversation. They derived an overall quality indicator score by summing the importance scores of all the quality indicators included in the serious illness discussion and then standardized this value on a scale between 0 and 100% by dividing each sum by the raw sum of highest possible values of these items. The quality indicator scores for GOCDs ranged between 5 and 31%, suggesting that the quality of GOCDs occurring in these hospitals with these patients and families was both highly variable and very poor. Even the documentation scores were very low, ranging from 42 to 78%. The correlation between the GOCD quality indicator scores and concordance between stated preferences and documented goals of care was moderate but not statistically significant (r = 0.53, p = 0.08). The correlation between the ACP quality indicator scores and concordance between stated preferences and documented goals of care was even weaker (r = 0.35, p = 0.3), reinforcing the importance of having real-time GOCDs with hospitalized patients [56].

Identifying hospital inpatients at high risk of clinical deterioration is an essential first step in targeting communication about goals of care. Many studies have identified high-risk patients for targeting GOCDs through the use of the “surprise” question (“Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next year?”) [57]. Originally designed to identify cancer patients who might benefit from a palliative care consult, its use has been extended to include non-cancer patients. This systematic review evaluated the performance characteristics of the surprise question in 16 studies involving over 11,621 patients. The investigators found moderate (n = 10) to high (n = 4) risk of overall bias in 14 studies. The discriminative ability of the surprise question for predicting death at 6 to 12 months was modest in cancer patients (area under summary receiver operating curve 0.83 (95% CI 0.79, 0.87)) and poor in non-cancer patients (area under summary receiver operating curve 0.77 (95% CI 0.73, 0.81)). The surprise question had no role in predicting death during an acute hospitalization. Other studies have included patients between the ages of 55 and 79 years of age with ≥ 1 end-stage organ failure [52, 58–60]. While the rationale to support inclusion of this heterogenous group of patients was never provided in these studies, their face validity appears to be consistent with clinical experience and the natural history of their chronic diseases’ illness trajectories. In our pilot study, we targeted all hospitalized inpatients 80 years and older [42]. From 2014 to 2018, elderly patients 80 years and older accounted for 17.9% (95% CI 17.66, 18.14) of all inpatient hospitalizations despite making up only 4.23% (95% CI 4.15, 4.31) of the Canadian population [43]. Compared to elderly inpatients 65 to 79 years old, the age-specific hospital admission rates for elderly inpatients 80 years and older were 2.12-fold greater (95% CI 2.09, 2.14). These patients accounted for 29.37% (95% CI 28.93, 29.81) of all inpatient hospital days, and their age-specific length of stay was 12.27 days (95% CI 11.93, 12.60) or 1.38-fold longer (95% CI 1.37, 1.39) than elderly inpatients 65 to 79 years old. At the Royal Victoria Regional Health Centre, elderly patients 80 years and older accounted for 49.24% (95% CI 48.11, 50.37) of all inpatient deaths from 2015 to 2019 (personal communication, decision support unit, Royal Victoria Regional Health Centre). Among hospitalized elderly patients 75 years and older, this group accounts for the largest proportion of ICU admissions (30.5% of all ICU admissions) [61]. Among hospitalized patients with an intensive care admission, this group also accounts for the largest proportion of deaths following ICU admission (49.1% of all post-ICU deaths). In a multicenter, cluster-randomized clinical trial examining survival outcomes for 3036 elderly (≥ 75 years old) patients with a serious illness admitted to hospitals that had been randomized to either a systematic strategy promoting ICU admission or usual care, there was no difference in survival at 6 months despite higher rates of ICU admission in the ICU strategy hospitals (relative risk = 1.68 (95% CI 1.54, 1.82)) [62]. The elderly patients included in this study had to have preserved functional status as measured by the Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living score ≥ 4 and preserved nutritional status, thus excluding elderly patients with frailty which is a known risk factor for poor ICU outcomes [63, 64]. Regardless of the reasons for hospitalization, the relative risk for death post-hospitalization in the elderly 80 years and older exceeds that compared to population-matched community-dwelling adults and continues to increase with time post-discharge [65].

There is no consensus as to who should be responsible for initiating and navigating GOCDs in seriously ill hospitalized patients [66]. If GOCDs result in treatment decisions about providing or withholding life-sustaining treatments, then physicians would have to be involved to attest and sign life-sustaining treatment forms. Many studies have examined the use of trained, non-physician facilitators to conduct GOCD with patients [67–74]. In general, these studies have demonstrated improvement in goal-concordant hospital care. When asked about their willingness to engage in GOCDs, nurses were less willing to initiate GOCDs (score of 5 on a scale of 7, 7 = “extremely willing”) compared to both residents and physicians [22]. Nurses consistently rated the importance of barriers to GOCDs higher than both residents and physicians, with the most important barriers identified as discordance between patients’ and families’ and HCPs’ expectations for outcomes, expectations for care options, and goals of care. Nurses did not feel particularly supported to engage in GOCDs by their organizations (score of 4 on a scale of 7, 7 = “extremely supported”). When physicians were asked how acceptable it was for non-physician HCPs to engage in GOCDs, their ratings ranged from as low as 3 to 6 (on a scale of 7, 7 = “extremely acceptable”). Physicians found it to be most acceptable for nurse practitioners and nurses to initiate GOCDs and least acceptable for social workers and other non-physician HCPs. However, when it came to making a final decision about care options, physicians were much less accepting of any non-physician HCP being involved (range 2 to 5, 1 = “extremely unacceptable & 7 = “extremely acceptable”). This pattern of acceptability for different components of GOCDs was also mirrored by nurses’ responses with highest acceptability for initiating GOCDs, followed by decreasing acceptability to exchanging information, being a decision coach and making a final decision. However, nurses were equally accepting of nurse practitioners, nurses, social workers, and other non-physician HCPs engaging in any of the components of GOCDs.

There is no consensus about the ideal time to approach hospitalized patients for GOCDs [75]. In general, patients’ preferences about LSTs are known to vary over time and context. This may explain why wishes for goals of care identified in advanced care plans (ACPs) are poorly predictive of receiving goal-concordant care at some later time in the future during an acute illness [76–78]. Many ACPs are completed far in advance of the time of death without evidence of regular review and updating, bringing into question how valid they may be in representing the patient’s current wishes and priorities for care [79]. As a result, regardless of the availability of ACPs, all hospitalized patients at high risk of serious illness should have a GOCD [80]. Ideally, these GOCDs should not occur in unstable patients at imminent risk of death as these conversations are then focused only on eliciting resuscitative preferences [81]. By the time a patient becomes critically ill and requires ICU admission, this may also be too late [82, 83]. High-risk hospitalized patients who are not so distressed from their acute illness and are not ready for hospital discharge most likely represent the ideal targets for GOCDs. An initial approach at 1 to 2 days after admission may represent the period of hospitalization associated with sufficient stabilization to permit fulsome discussions with family and substitute decision-makers. One timely visit should be sufficient in most cases as the value of allowing patients, family, and substitute decision-makers to deliberate about their care preferences may not add value to their final treatment decisions [84] and may be impractical from the perspective of time and clinical workflow.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study received ethics approval by the Royal Victoria Regional Health Centre Research Ethics Board (September 2, 2022; REB Study No. R22-003). For patients randomized to the f-GOCD group, study ICU nurses will obtain verbal consent from competent patients or, otherwise, their substitute decision-makers. A waiver of the requirements for signed informed consent is requested for the f-GOCD intervention arm of the study. This approach is justified in comparative effectiveness trials involving the randomization of patients to one or more standard of care interventions [85] because the trial poses no more than minimal risk, the alteration from written to verbal consent does not materially affect the rights or welfare of the patient, and verbal consent would be the usual approach used to initiate any GOCD with any patient and/or their substitute decision-maker during routine clinical care. In our pilot study, we used verbal consent from patients or their substitute decision-makers without any adverse consequences documented. Using this approach, 90.2% (95% CI 76.9, 97.3) of patients or their substitute decision-makers consented to receiving the f-GOCD intervention (42). In that study, 100% of patients or their substitute decision-makers completed the intervention. In addition, these patients will be approached for written verbal consent, documented by electronic e-signatures via REDCap by the Clinical Research Associate (CRA) after completion of the GOCD intervention and asked to complete the 7-item SDM GOCD tool evaluation questionnaire. For those participants who consent, the CRA may help them complete the survey during the index hospitalization. These surveys will only be completed during the index hospitalization. Only completed surveys will be compared between groups. For all participants in both the control and intervention arms, the CRA will seek verbal consent to complete the Quality of Communications and 4-item SURE questionnaires. These questionnaires will only be available for completion during the index hospitalization. The CRA will approach these patients for verbal consent after their CODE STATUS has been completed and documented in the EMR. The rationale for not requiring written consent in this part of the study is a quality improvement initiative as the results of the two questionnaires will be fed back to the physicians who signed the patient’s CODE STATUS and the organization so that subsequent improvements can be made in how we approach patients and their substitute decision-makers for end-of-life care decisions. Patients or their substitute decision-makers will be told that they may refuse to participate or withdraw their participation after consent at any time without fear of their ongoing care being compromised. Patients or their substitute decision-makers will be informed that any preferences documented in the CODE STATUS can be changed at any time during their hospitalization, and the trial personnel or principal investigators will be available to answer any questions they may have after the f-GOCD intervention has been completed. Trial personnel will be available to review any questions related to a previously completed CODE STATUS and will also be available to assist patients or their substitute decision-makers in revising these at any time during their index hospitalization.
