Electronic gambling engagement and psychosocial outcomes among students at the University of Ibadan, Nigeria: a theory of planned behavior perspective
Authors: Tolani Samuel Fadipe, Chimezie Obinna Odionye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe proliferation of electronic gambling in Nigeria presents a significant public health concern among youths facing economic adversity. This study applied the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to investigate the socio-cognitive drivers and self-reported psychosocial consequences of electronic gambling engagement among students at the University of Ibadan. Electronic gambling is defined here as any gambling activity conducted through electronic devices, including mobile phones, laptops, and computers, encompassing mobile sports betting applications, web-based casino platforms, and online poker.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey design was employed. Data were collected from 403 undergraduate and postgraduate students at the University of Ibadan using stratified random sampling and a structured self-administered questionnaire adapted from validated instruments. Analyses included descriptive statistics, scale reliability assessment, and binary logistic regression.ResultsThe lifetime prevalence of electronic gambling was 49.6%. Positive attitudes toward gambling, subjective norms involving peer approval, and perceived behavioral control were all significant predictors of gambling engagement (p < .001). Economic hardship was the strongest predictor in the model (OR = 3.06). Among participants who reported gambling, 50.9% reported increased stress and anxiety, 48.6% reported financial strain, and 42.6% reported negative academic consequences.Practical implicationsUniversity health and counseling services should integrate validated gambling screening tools into routine student health assessments. Interventions should address both the economic motivations and the cognitive distortions sustaining gambling engagement. Prevention campaigns should target the specific TPB constructs validated in this study. Introduction

The advent of digital technologies has fundamentally transformed the gambling landscape worldwide, shifting participation toward internet-based and mobile platforms including sports betting, poker, and online casino games [1]. In Nigeria, electronic gambling has grown rapidly among youth, fueled by widespread smartphone ownership, the proliferation of mobile betting applications, and aggressive, youth-targeted marketing by gambling operators [2, 3]. Prevalence studies indicate high engagement rates among Nigerian university students, with lifetime participation rates often exceeding 40% to 50% in cross-sectional samples [4–6]. Within higher education institutions, the combination of digital access, residential concentration of young people, and active commercial marketing creates a high-exposure environment for gambling engagement. The University of Ibadan, as Nigeria’s oldest and most prominent federal university situated in Ibadan, Oyo State, represents one of the most significant higher education settings in the Southwest region. Ibadan hosts multiple tertiary institutions, and physical betting shops are prevalent in student residential areas surrounding these campuses, while digital platforms reach students directly through targeted social media advertising [2, 3].

University students represent a particularly vulnerable group due to their transitional life stage, heightened peer influence, and exposure to socioeconomic pressures including limited income, unemployment, and financial dependency [7, 8]. Many students view gambling as a potential supplementary or primary income source amid these financial constraints [9, 10]. However, gambling is associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including psychological distress such as anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation, debt accumulation, and academic disruption [11–14]. Of particular concern is the absence of formal institutional safeguards. Nigerian universities have not developed explicit gambling harm prevention policies, and the National Universities Commission has not issued guidelines specifically addressing gambling behavior within student affairs frameworks [13]. Students are therefore exposed to gambling marketing and normalization without the protective effect of institutional oversight, a gap that is especially consequential where economic vulnerability is high.

Despite the documented scale of gambling engagement, the socio-cognitive and economic determinants of electronic gambling among Nigerian university students remain poorly characterized in the literature. Most existing Nigerian studies are primarily descriptive, do not employ standardized theoretical frameworks to explain the antecedents of engagement, and are limited in their analytical scope [4–6]. The psychosocial consequences of gambling engagement have not been examined within an integrated theoretical framework in the Ibadan university context. This gap is consequential because effective, targeted interventions require an understanding of the modifiable psychological antecedents of gambling behavior, not merely its prevalence rates. The present study addresses this gap by applying the Theory of Planned Behavior as an organizing framework to examine both the socio-cognitive predictors and the self-reported psychosocial consequences of electronic gambling among students at the University of Ibadan.

Literature review

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) has been applied extensively to gambling behavior across different populations and settings. Martin et al. [15] demonstrated that attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control collectively predicted gambling intentions among college students in the United States, with attitudes as the most salient construct. St-Pierre et al. [16] extended this work to adolescent populations, finding that the TPB accounted for significant variance in gambling intentions even when controlling for problem gambling severity. Lee [17] applied an extended TPB model to undergraduate casino gambling, finding that subjective norms and past behavior were particularly influential. Across these studies, perceived behavioral control, particularly as it manifests in the illusion of control, a cognitive distortion in which individuals overestimate their ability to influence chance-determined outcomes, has emerged as a consistent mechanism linking gambling beliefs to behavioral engagement [18, 19].

Within Nigeria, research on gambling in higher education institutions has expanded over the past decade. Ayandele et al. [4] reported that financial motivation and male gender were the strongest predictors of positive attitudes toward sports betting among young adults in Southwest Nigeria. Daniel et al. [5] found that approximately 45% of undergraduates in a Nigerian university sample reported sports betting engagement, with financial need cited as the primary motivating factor. Adebisi et al. [2] documented the rapid normalization of gambling in Nigerian youth culture, attributed to mobile betting platforms and promotional advertising. Aguocha & George [13] provided a national overview noting the absence of a robust regulatory framework and the particular vulnerability of economically constrained young populations. A recent narrative review synthesized global and Nigerian evidence on sports betting among youth, finding that financial consequences including school fee diversion, mental health impairment, and academic failure were prevalent outcomes, and calling for integrated policy responses across Nigerian tertiary institutions [20]. Community-based research in Southwest Nigeria has further documented a 30.5% prevalence of gambling disorder, and identified the absence of culturally appropriate screening instruments as a critical barrier to clinical identification in Nigerian settings [21].

The psychosocial consequences of gambling among students in low- and middle-income countries have been documented in several studies. Ssewanyana and Bitanihirwe [7] identified financial distress, academic underperformance, and psychological morbidity as consistent correlates of problem gambling among young people in Sub-Saharan Africa. Jolly et al. [12] demonstrated elevated risk of suicidal ideation among individuals with gambling disorder comorbid with major depressive disorder. Aguocha et al. [6] found that gambling was associated with delinquency and academic difficulties among students in Nigeria, with early onset predicting more severe engagement. The relationship between economic adversity and gambling engagement is also well-established: Olason et al. [10] found that economic recession increased gambling participation particularly among financially stressed populations, and Akanle and Fageyinbo [8] described football betting among Nigerian youth as deeply intertwined with aspirations of financial mobility. In resource-constrained settings, gambling functions not primarily as leisure but as a perceived income-generation strategy [3, 9]. A systematic review synthesizing evidence from 13 studies across SSA concluded that gambling harm patterns among youth in the region remain poorly evidenced and emphasized the need for social policies alongside effective public health interventions [22]. A narrative synthesis of the historical and contemporary expansion of commercial gambling across the continent identified lack of stable employment as a primary driver of engagement, and documented that advances in mobile technology have created new markets while significantly increasing the accessibility of gambling products [23]. The regulatory context in which this expansion has occurred is largely inadequate: a comparative policy analysis found that while gambling is legally regulated in over 80% of SSA countries, enforcement remains weak and public health protections are insufficient across the continent [24].

Theoretical framework

The Theory of Planned Behavior [25] serves as the guiding theoretical framework for this study, offering a lens to examine the associations between socio-cognitive drivers and electronic gambling engagement. Rather than strictly testing the theory’s causal validity, this study applies the framework descriptively to characterize the motivations of Nigerian university students. Within this context, Attitudes are conceptualized not merely as recreational preferences, but as cognitive evaluations of gambling as a viable financial strategy or investment amid economic constraints. Subjective Norms capture the perceived social pressure arising from the normalization of betting in student hostels and the ubiquity of peer gambling engagement. Perceived Behavioral Control encompasses the ease of access provided by smartphones and the illusion of control, whereby students may overestimate their ability to influence gambling outcomes. This framework also provides a basis for understanding psychosocial outcomes: when gambling is driven by a perceived need for financial control, the subsequent financial losses and academic disruption may manifest as significant stress and anxiety [15–17]. In low-resource settings such as Nigeria, economic hardship may further amplify these constructs by framing gambling as a rational financial strategy rather than a recreational risk [3, 9].

Few Nigerian studies have applied the Theory of Planned Behavior systematically in university gambling contexts [4]. To address this gap, this study was guided by the following specific objectives:

Methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive cross-sectional survey design was adopted for this study. Cross-sectional designs are appropriate for establishing the prevalence of behaviors and examining associations between variables at a single point in time, and are well-suited to studies whose objectives are descriptive and associational rather than experimental or longitudinal [26, 27]. The study was conducted at the University of Ibadan, a premier federal research institution founded in 1948 and one of the oldest universities in Sub-Saharan Africa, located in Ibadan, Oyo State, between March and August 2025. The University of Ibadan was selected for three reasons. First, as Nigeria’s oldest federal university, it enrolls one of the largest and most socioeconomically diverse student populations in the Southwest region, providing a robust sample frame spanning regular undergraduates, distance learning students, and postgraduate students [13]. Second, its Ibadan location in a major commercial city with high gambling operator density is directly relevant to the research questions. Third, the research team had institutional access to facilitate quality data collection.

Participants and sampling procedure

The study population comprised all registered undergraduate and postgraduate students at the University of Ibadan. A multi-stage stratified sampling technique was employed to ensure a representative sample. First, the population was stratified into three distinct academic categories: Regular Undergraduate, Distance Learning Centre (DLC) Undergraduate, and Postgraduate students. These strata were chosen because they represent meaningfully distinct subpopulations with different socioeconomic profiles, employment rates, and patterns of campus engagement that may influence gambling behavior. DLC undergraduates are typically older and more financially independent than regular undergraduates; postgraduate students differ in developmental stage, income sources, and academic pressures. Second, participants were selected from each stratum using proportional allocation based on probability proportional to size (PPS), ensuring that strata with larger student populations contributed proportionally more participants. Third, within each stratum, participants were selected using a systematic random procedure: a sampling frame was constructed from student registration lists obtained from the respective faculty administrative offices, a random starting point was identified, and participants were drawn at fixed intervals. Where a selected participant was unavailable or declined, the next person on the list was approached. The resulting stratum-level sample allocation is presented in Table S1 below.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) being a currently registered student of the University of Ibadan; and (2) providing informed consent to participate. Students were excluded if they were absent during the data collection period or if they returned incomplete questionnaires.

Sample size determination and ethical considerations

The minimum sample size was calculated using Cochran’s [28] formula for large populations: n = Z²pq/e², where Z = 1.96 (95% confidence level), p = .50 (maximum variance assumption), q = 0.50, and e = 0.05 (margin of error), yielding a minimum of 384. While the University of Ibadan’s student population is technically finite, the Cochran formula for large populations is conventionally applied when total population substantially exceeds 10,000, as the finite population correction factor becomes negligible under those conditions [28, 29]. The University of Ibadan’s total registered enrollment substantially exceeds this threshold. Adjusting for a 10% anticipated non-response rate, the final target sample was 403 students. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Ibadan Research Ethics Committee (Reference: UI/REC/25/0185, 12 March 2025). All participants were informed of the study’s purpose, their right to withdraw without consequence, and the confidentiality of their data before providing written informed consent. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Trained research assistants administered the structured questionnaire at various campus locations.

Measures and instrumentation

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from established measures in the gambling literature [15, 16]. The TPB items were adapted from instruments used by Martin et al. [15] and St-Pierre et al. [16], which have demonstrated acceptable reliability and construct validity in comparable student populations. The full questionnaire is available as Supplementary File 1. The instrument was divided into five sections:

Section A: socio-demographics

Multiple-choice and open-ended questions assessed age, gender, level of study (undergraduate/postgraduate), employment status, and monthly income or allowance.

Section B: gambling behavior

Participants responded to multiple-choice items regarding their lifetime history of electronic gambling (Yes/No), frequency of engagement (daily, weekly, monthly, rarely), and preferred game types (sports betting, poker, slot machines, online casino games). Electronic gambling was defined in the questionnaire as gambling activity conducted through any electronic device.

Section C: theory of planned behavior constructs

The three core TPB predictors were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Attitude was measured with 5 items assessing positive evaluations of gambling as a financial strategy (Cronbach’s α = 0.78). Subjective Norms were assessed via 3 items measuring perceived peer pressure and social approval (Cronbach’s α = 0.72). Perceived Behavioral Control was measured using 4 items assessing confidence in gambling ability and perceived ease of engagement (Cronbach’s α = 0.75).

Section D: economic hardship index

A composite variable was derived from items measuring employment status, self-rated income adequacy, and a 3-item financial stress scale.

Section E: psychosocial and academic effects

Participants who reported gambling rated their level of agreement with statements regarding adverse outcomes, such as increased anxiety, financial strain, and negative academic impact, using a 5-point Likert scale.

Validity and reliability

To ensure content validity, an initial draft of the questionnaire was reviewed by subject matter experts in Sociology, Psychology, and Public Health for item clarity and relevance. Items were revised based on expert feedback before piloting. Reliability was established using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients; all subscales exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70 [30].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27). The analysis was conducted in three stages corresponding to the study objectives. First, descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, and means, were used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics, determine the prevalence and patterns of electronic gambling (Objective 1), and describe the distribution of self-reported psychosocial and academic consequences (Objective 3). Second, prior to inferential analysis, the internal consistency of the measurement scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Third, to examine associations between TPB constructs, economic hardship, and gambling engagement (Objective 2), a binary logistic regression was conducted with gambling engagement (Yes/No) as the dependent variable. Binary logistic regression was selected as the appropriate method because the outcome variable was dichotomous; it is the recommended procedure for predicting group membership from continuous or categorical predictors when the dependent variable is binary [30, 31]. Mean scores for Attitude, Subjective Norms, and Perceived Behavioral Control, alongside the Economic Hardship Index, served as independent variables. Model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R². Statistical significance was set at p < .05. It should be noted that the present study did not include potential confounding variables such as internet use intensity or broader socioeconomic status; the implications of this are addressed in the limitations section.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The sample consisted of 403 students with a mean age of 26.4 years (SD = 4.1). The sample was relatively gender-balanced, with slightly more males (51.9%). A majority (66%) were under 30 years old, and the sample was approximately evenly split between undergraduate and postgraduate levels. Notably, 51.9% reported being employed while studying. Predicting gambling engagement

A binary logistic regression was conducted to examine associations between Theory of Planned Behavior constructs, economic hardship, and gambling engagement. The model was statistically significant, χ²(4) = 86.42, p < .001, explaining 32% of the variance in gambling engagement (Nagelkerke R² = 0.32). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated a good model fit (χ²(8) = 7.24, p = .51). All predictors were statistically significant (Table 3). Economic Hardship was the strongest predictor (OR = 3.06), followed by Positive Attitude (OR = 2.34). Subjective Norms (OR = 1.86) and Perceived Behavioral Control (OR = 2.03) also significantly increased the odds of reporting lifetime gambling engagement.

Self-reported effects of gambling

Among the 200 participants who reported gambling, substantial proportions reported negative consequences (Table 4). Over half (50.9%) agreed that gambling increased their stress and anxiety, and 48.6% reported significant financial strain. Additionally, 42.6% reported that gambling negatively affected their academic performance, and 49.8% reported feeling unable to stop or control their gambling. These findings are descriptive and based on single self-report items; they should not be interpreted as diagnostic indicators of gambling disorder.

Discussion

This study applied the Theory of Planned Behavior to examine electronic gambling engagement among students at the University of Ibadan. The findings reveal a complex interplay between economic constraint and socio-cognitive processes. Positive attitudes toward gambling, perceived social approval, and a belief in one’s control over gambling outcomes were all significantly associated with reporting lifetime gambling engagement. This is consistent with prior applications of the TPB to gambling behavior [15, 16, 25] and extends its application to a financially motivated behavioral context in a low-resource African setting.

The most notable result is that Economic Hardship emerged as the strongest predictor of gambling engagement, with an odds ratio of 3.06. This finding reframes the positive attitudes observed among students: the motivation underlying gambling engagement appears to be not merely recreational preference but a financially driven coping attitude. Students appear to evaluate gambling as a viable, if risky, strategy for economic survival amid limited opportunities. This is consistent with deprivation-based accounts of gambling in which individuals in lower socioeconomic positions gamble in the hope of improving their financial situation [2, 10]. High youth unemployment and income insufficiency in Nigeria likely amplify this dynamic.

Subjective norms were also significantly associated with gambling engagement, highlighting the role of peer culture and digital socialization in normalizing betting behaviors [4]. The proliferation of betting shops and mobile applications creates an environment in which gambling is perceived as socially acceptable and widespread among students, a form of pluralistic ignorance that may sustain engagement even among students who might otherwise disengage.

The predictive value of Perceived Behavioral Control warrants particular attention. In gambling contexts, PBC often reflects an illusion of control, a well-documented cognitive distortion in which individuals overestimate their ability to influence chance-determined outcomes [18]. This finding is conceptually important for two reasons. First, it provides a mechanistic link within the TPB framework between gambling beliefs and potentially escalating engagement: the mistaken belief in control may reinforce continued participation despite losses, facilitating a transition from motivated behavior toward habitual engagement [19]. Second, it suggests that prevention programs targeting attitudes and norms alone may be insufficient; interventions must also directly challenge this distortion through psychoeducation about randomness and the mathematical realities of gambling.

Despite being driven by economic improvement motives, participants who gambled reported substantial negative consequences. The high rates of self-reported stress and anxiety (50.9%) and financial strain (48.6%) suggest a situation in which a behavior adopted to alleviate financial difficulty may actively worsen it. This is consistent with literature on psychosocial costs of gambling in African contexts [7, 13].

The finding that 49.8% of participants who gambled reported feeling unable to stop is descriptively notable. This suggests that for a subset of this population, gambling may have escalated beyond motivated engagement toward patterns consistent with early loss of behavioral control. However, given that gambling status was assessed using a single binary lifetime item, this finding cannot be interpreted as evidence of gambling disorder and should be understood as a preliminary, self-reported indicator warranting further investigation with validated problem gambling instruments.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical implications

The findings extend the applicability of the TPB to an African, lower-income university context, providing cross-cultural support that the framework’s core constructs retain explanatory value beyond the Western, high-income populations in which the theory has been most extensively tested [15–17, 25]. The significant association of all three TPB constructs with gambling engagement is consistent with prior gambling applications of the model [16, 17].

The emergence of economic hardship as the strongest predictor suggests that in resource-constrained settings, TPB constructs do not operate in isolation from structural conditions. Attitudes toward gambling in this population appear to be shaped not primarily by recreational preferences but by financial desperation. This points toward the need for an extended TPB model for use in low-resource settings, one that explicitly incorporates economic constraint as a structural variable shaping the formation and weighting of attitudes and perceived behavioral control. Future theoretical work should develop and empirically test such an integrated model.

The self-reported inability to stop gambling among nearly half of participating students raises questions about the boundary between intentional, TPB-mediated behavior and habitual or compulsive engagement. Longitudinal extensions of the TPB incorporating habit strength and loss-of-control constructs would be theoretically productive in this context.

Practical implications

For university health and counseling services, the findings indicate a need to expand provision to include gambling-related harm. Validated brief screening tools, such as the Brief Problem Gambling Screen, should be incorporated into routine student health assessments, particularly for students presenting with financial distress. Counseling interventions should address both the economic motivations and the cognitive distortions, particularly the illusion of control, rather than relying solely on harm-awareness messaging.

For university administrators, the findings underscore the need for formal institutional gambling policies. No Nigerian university currently has a documented gambling prevention policy, and the National Universities Commission has not issued relevant student affairs guidelines [13]. Universities should develop student-facing gambling harm prevention frameworks including restrictions on gambling advertising in and around campus environments, and training of student affairs personnel to identify and refer students experiencing gambling-related difficulties.

For public health policy, the identification of economic hardship as the dominant predictor has direct implications for intervention design. Campaigns that rely on informational messaging alone are unlikely to be effective if they do not address the underlying financial vulnerability driving engagement. The key lesson for policymakers and university administrators is that gambling prevention among students must be embedded in a broader response to youth economic insecurity. Policy must therefore also create structural alternatives, including work-study programs, competitive micro-grants for student entrepreneurship, and accessible financial literacy education, that reduce the appeal of gambling as an income-generation strategy.

Limitations and future research

The primary limitation of this study is the operationalization of gambling engagement. Engagement was assessed using a single binary lifetime item (yes/no), which does not distinguish between incidental, past, regular, or problematic gambling. A person who gambled once and a person who gambles daily are classified identically under this operationalization. Consequently, findings characterize factors associated with any lifetime gambling experience, not with current, frequent, or disordered gambling. Conclusions regarding addictive behavior or clinical loss of control should be treated as preliminary and descriptive indicators, not clinical assessments. Future studies should employ validated, multi-item problem gambling severity instruments, such as the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) or the South Oaks Gambling Screen (SOGS), to permit clinically meaningful distinctions between gambling involvement levels.

Additionally, the study did not include potential confounding variables such as internet use intensity or broader socioeconomic indicators beyond the composite economic hardship index. Given the strong conceptual overlap between electronic gambling and internet-related behaviors, the absence of internet use as a covariate may limit the precision of the regression estimates. Future studies should explicitly measure and control for internet use and other relevant covariates.

The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference. The TPB posits a directional relationship from cognitions to behavior, but these data represent a single snapshot. We cannot determine whether economic hardship and positive gambling attitudes preceded engagement, or whether escalating gambling losses intensified perceived financial strain and rationalized continued participation. Longitudinal studies are needed to delineate these temporal pathways.

Reliance on self-reported measures introduces potential social desirability bias, particularly regarding gambling frequency and associated harms. The measure of academic impact was subjective; future work would benefit from correlating gambling behaviors with objective academic metrics such as grade point averages or course withdrawal records.

Finally, the sample was drawn from a single institution. Students at the University of Ibadan, while demographically diverse, may not fully represent students at private institutions, polytechnics, or universities in other regions. Caution is warranted in generalizing beyond the study setting.

Conclusion

This study found that electronic gambling engagement among students at the University of Ibadan is associated with economic hardship and with TPB constructs of positive attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Among participants who reported gambling, substantial proportions also reported negative psychosocial and academic consequences. The outcome measure used in this study was a binary lifetime gambling item, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. These results nonetheless provide a useful foundation for developing theory-informed, context-sensitive interventions that address both the economic roots and the sustaining cognitive architecture of gambling engagement among university students in Nigeria.
