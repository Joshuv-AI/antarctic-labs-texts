A Clustering Approach for Basket Trials Based on Treatment Response Trajectories
Masahiro Kojima, Keisuke Hanada, Atsuya Sato

Abstract
Heterogeneity in efficacy is sometimes observed across baskets in basket trials. In this study, we propose a model-free clustering framework that groups baskets based on transition probabilities derived from the trajectories of treatment response, rather than relying solely on a single efficacy endpoint such as the objective response rate. The number of clusters is not predetermined but is automatically determined in a data-driven manner based on the similarity structure among baskets. After clustering, baskets within the same cluster are analyzed using a hierarchical Bayesian model. This framework aims to improve the estimation precision of efficacy endpoints and enhance statistical power while maintaining the type I error rate at the nominal level. The performance of the proposed method was evaluated through simulation studies. The results demonstrated that the proposed method can accurately identify cluster structures in heterogeneous settings and, even under such conditions, maintain the type I error rate at the nominal level while improving statistical power.



Keywords: transition probability; basket trial; silhouette method; hierarchical Bayesian model; weighted final-state probability

1
Introduction

In recent years, there has been growing attention on optimizing dose selection and identifying appropriate target populations in early-phase oncology trials, particularly following initiatives such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) Project Optimus. Among the master protocol designs (e.g. umbrella, platform, and basket trials) developed to evaluate multiple tumor types, biomarkers, or molecular subtypes in parallel, basket trials have become increasingly important in recent years. In basket trials, efficacy is typically assessed using the objective response rate (ORR) within each basket, which often involves a relatively small sample size of approximately 20 to 50 patients
[
Hirakawa2018
,
FrontiersBasket2023
]
. Consequently, efficient statistical methods for analyzing data from basket trials are actively being investigated.

A variety of statistical methodologies have been proposed to address the analytical challenges in basket trials, where heterogeneity in treatment effects across tumor types may mask true efficacy signals. Early analyses treated each basket as an independent subtrial
[
Simon2016
,
Redman2017
]
, which limited efficiency and failed to borrow information across biologically related subgroups.
Conversely, fully pooled analyses assumed common treatment effects across all baskets
[
Park2018
,
Berry2019
]
, often overlooking meaningful heterogeneity.
To strike a balance between these extremes, hierarchical Bayesian models have been widely adopted to allow partial exchangeability of treatment effects and adaptive borrowing of information
[
Thall2013
,
Neuenschwander2016
,
Hobbs2018
]
. Further refinements, such as the calibrated hierarchical Bayesian (CHB) model
[
Berry2020
]
and the EXNEX framework
[
Neuenschwander2009
]
, have enabled more flexible control of shrinkage, while robust extensions have been developed to prevent over-borrowing when baskets differ substantially in response
[
Lee2020
,
Psioda2020
]
. More recently, latent subgroup and partition-based models have been proposed to account for unobserved similarities among baskets.
Chu and Yuan
[
Chu2018
]
introduced the Bayesian Latent Subgroup Design (BLAST), which simultaneously models treatment response and longitudinal biomarkers to detect latent clusters of baskets. Zhou and Ji
[
Zhou2021
]
developed the Robust Bayesian Hypothesis Testing (RoBoT) framework, which identifies groups of baskets with similar efficacy profiles. Building on these ideas, Govande and Slate
[
govande2025using
]
proposed the Bayesian Partition Model with Covariates (BPMx), which integrates subject-level covariate information to guide latent cluster membership.
Biomarker-based models offer a promising approach to explaining the sources of heterogeneity; however, in practical basket trials, the relevant and informative biomarkers are often unknown at the time of trial initiation. Furthermore, it is not always clear which covariates are useful for clustering, and including irrelevant variables may obscure the true basket structure. Therefore, developing methods that can identify similarities among baskets using universally collected data without relying on prespecified biomarkers or covariates is of substantial practical importance. Our proposed approach addresses this need by leveraging the trajectories of treatment response, which are typically observed and closely linked to primary efficacy endpoints such as the ORR in basket trials.

In this study, we propose a novel clustering approach for basket trials that newly utilizes the trajectories of treatment response, which are typically observed and related to the primary efficacy endpoints, for clustering. The proposed method incorporates transition probabilities, computes inter-basket dissimilarities using the Manhattan distance, and performs clustering using established algorithms such as the Silhouette method
[
Rousseeuw1987
]
. The number of clusters is not predetermined but is automatically determined in a data-driven manner based on the similarity structure among baskets. After cluster structures are identified, a hierarchical Bayesian model is applied to improve the precision of efficacy estimation by borrowing information within clusters. The performance of the proposed approach is evaluated through a series of simulation studies under various settings.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 introduces the calculation of the weighted final-state probabilities of treatment response, which account for varying numbers of assessment time points across patients, followed by descriptions of the clustering procedure and the hierarchical Bayesian model.
Section 3 presents the simulation design and results.
Section 4 discusses the implications and limitations of the proposed method.

2
Method

In this section, we describe the transition probability matrix of treatment responses and the final-state distribution, which are key components of our proposed method for basket trials. In addition, we introduce a weighting scheme that accounts for variations in the number of response assessments among patients when constructing the final-state distribution. We further explain the clustering procedure based on the final-state distribution and the ORR.

2.1
Transition Probability Matrix and Weighted Final-State Distribution

We consider a basket trial consisting of
J
baskets. Let
n_{j}
denote the number of patients enrolled in the
j
th basket. Each patient
i
in the
j
th basket undergoes disease response evaluation
t_{ji}
times. The target response is categorized into four states: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). An extension that explicitly accounts for not evaluable (NE) cases by augmenting the state space is provided in the Supplementary Material.
First, let the initial state probability in the
j
th basket be as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcccc}&CR&PR&SD&PD\\
{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}_{j0}=&(\pi_{j01},&\pi_{j02},&\pi_{j03},&\pi_{j04}).\end{array}

Let the state space be
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathrm{CR},\mathrm{PR},\mathrm{SD},\mathrm{PD}\}
. For the
i
-th patient in basket
j
, let
\{X_{ji,1},\ldots,X_{ji,t_{ji}}\}
denote the observed sequence of treatment responses, i.e.,
X_{ji,l}\in\mathcal{S}
. The observed initial state probability in
j
th basket,
{\widehat{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}}_{j0}
, is estimated as a proportion of each state through baseline assessments before administration.
Except for the case where
t_{ji}=1
, the one-step transition counts within patient
i
are defined as follows.

\displaystyle N_{ji}^{rs}=\sum^{t_{ji}-1}_{l=1}I(X_{ji,l}=r,X_{ji,l+1}=s)

(3)

where
r,s\in\mathcal{S}
and
I(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function, which takes the value 1 if the condition inside the parentheses is true and 0 otherwise. The sum of the one-step transition counts across all patients in basket
j
is given by the following.

\displaystyle{\hat{\text{\boldmath$N$}}}_{j}^{rs}=\sum^{n_{j}}_{i=1}N_{ji}^{rs}.

(4)

As the transition probability matrix is defined such that each row sums to one, the following sum is considered to obtain the denominator of the transition probabilities.

\displaystyle{\hat{\text{\boldmath$N$}}}_{j}^{r.}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\hat{\text{\boldmath$N$}}}_{j}^{rs}.

(5)

The
(r,s)
element of the one-step transition probability matrix is given by the following:

\displaystyle{\hat{P}}_{j}^{rs}=\frac{{\hat{\text{\boldmath$N$}}}_{j}^{rs}}{{\hat{\text{\boldmath$N$}}}_{j}^{r.}}

(6)

Remark
.

In empirical estimation of transition matrices, it may occasionally happen
that a certain state
r
in basket
j
has no observed outgoing transitions,
that is,
{\hat{\text{\boldmath$N$}}}_{j}^{r\cdot}=0
.
This issue has been noted in the literature on sparse Markov chain estimation
[
deCastro2014
,
Hao2018
]
,
where small sample sizes or irregular observation schedules can lead to zero rows in the estimated transition count matrix. Since such rows cannot be normalized to valid transition probabilities,
we regularize them by replacing the corresponding row of
{\hat{\text{\boldmath$P$}}}_{j}
with a uniform distribution, i.e.,

{\hat{P}}_{j,rs}=1/|\mathcal{S}|,\quad s\in\mathcal{S}.

This can be interpreted as introducing a weakly informative prior for the unobserved transitions, ensuring numerical stability without distorting the Markov structure. As the sample size increases, the probability of
{\hat{\text{\boldmath$N$}}}_{j}^{r\cdot}=0
rapidly diminishes, so that the effect of this regularization on the overall estimation is asymptotically negligible.

In basket trials, the number of assessment time points for treatment response often differs among patients.
To account for this variation, we introduce a weighting scheme based on the observation frequency for the
j
-th basket as follows:

\displaystyle{\hat{w}}_{jt}=\frac{\sum^{n_{j}}_{i=1}I(t_{ji}=t)}{\sum_{u\in{\cal T}}\sum^{n_{j}}_{i=1}I(t_{ji}=u)}.

(7)

Let
{\cal T}
denote the set of the observed numbers of assessment time points. The final-state distribution for basket
j
, taking into account the number of assessment time points, is given as follows:

\displaystyle{\overline{{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}}}_{j}=\sum_{t\in{\cal T}}{\hat{w}}_{jt}\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}}_{j0}{\hat{\text{\boldmath$P$}}}_{j}^{t-1}={\hat{w}}_{j1}\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}}_{j0}+\sum_{t\in({\cal T}/\{1\})}{\hat{w}}_{jt}\hat{{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}}_{j0}{\hat{\text{\boldmath$P$}}}_{j}^{t-1}

(8)

where
{\hat{\text{\boldmath$P$}}}_{j}^{t-1}
denotes the transition probability matrix obtained by taking the
(t-1)
-fold product of the matrix given in equation (
6
), that is, the transition probability matrix after
t-1
transitions.

2.2
Clustering

Let
\theta_{j}
denote a basket-level efficacy parameter for basket
j
(e.g., the ORR, disease control rate, or another prespecified endpoint), and let
\hat{\theta}_{j}
be its estimator from observed data. For clarity, we illustrate the proposed framework using the ORR, which represents the proportion of patients with either CR or PR, as the primary efficacy endpoint.

We perform clustering using the feature vectors
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}=({\overline{{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}}}_{j}^{\top},\hat{\theta}_{j})^{\top}
, where
{\overline{{\text{\boldmath$\pi$}}}}_{j}
is defined by the equation (
8
) and
\hat{\theta}_{j}
represents the ORR for basket
j
. The length of
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}
is 5. We define the dissimilarity between two baskets
j
and
k
by the
Manhattan distance
(also known as the city-block or
L_{1}
distance),

\displaystyle d({\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{j},{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{k})=\sum_{m=1}^{5}|y_{jm}-y_{km}|.

(9)

The distance has been shown to be more robust to outliers and less sensitive to extreme deviations than the Euclidean distance
[
Jain1988
,
kaufman2009finding
,
Everitt2011
]
. All five components of
{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}
are probabilities on
[0,1]
; therefore, no additional scaling is applied prior to computing the Manhattan distance. We determine the number of clusters using the Silhouette method
[
Rousseeuw1987
]
. For each observation
j
, let
C(j)
denote the cluster to which it belongs. The average dissimilarity between
j
and all other observations within the same cluster is defined as

\displaystyle a_{j}=\frac{1}{|C(j)|-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in C(j)\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}d({\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{j},{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{k}),

(10)

and the minimum average dissimilarity between
j
and all points in other clusters
C^{\prime}\neq C(j)
is given by

\displaystyle b_{j}=\min_{C^{\prime}\neq C(j)}\left\{\frac{1}{|C^{\prime}|}\sum_{k\in C^{\prime}}d({\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{j},{\text{\boldmath$y$}}_{k})\right\}.

(11)

The Silhouette value for observation
i
is then defined as

\displaystyle s_{j}=\frac{b_{j}-a_{j}}{\max(a_{j},b_{j})},\qquad-1\leq s_{j}\leq 1.

(12)

The average Silhouette index

\displaystyle\bar{s}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}s_{k}

(13)

quantifies the overall cluster separation. To avoid instability due to extremely small clusters, the number of clusters
u
was first selected by maximizing the average Silhouette index
\bar{s}
over
u\in\{2,\ldots,J-1\}
, subject to a minimum cluster size of 2. The Silhouette index requires well-defined within-cluster dissimilarities, i.e.,
|C(j)|\geq 2
for every cluster so that
a_{j}
is finite. Therefore, the “all-singleton” partition (
u=J
) is excluded from consideration. In cases where some singleton clusters appear, the overall clustering performance is evaluated based on the Silhouette values of non-singleton clusters, while singleton clusters are retained in the clustering result. Following
[
kaufman2009finding
]
, when the resulting maximum average Silhouette index was small (
\bar{s}\leq 0.25
), the clustering structure was regarded as weak or unreliable, and all baskets were treated as belonging to a single cluster (“One cluster” baseline), which corresponds to full pooling. Alternative clustering algorithms were also explored, but they were not adopted in this study; the rationale for this choice is discussed in the Discussion section.
