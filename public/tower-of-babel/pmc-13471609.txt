A mixed-methods evaluation of a narrative medicine course and its impact on empathy development among medical students
Authors: Xindi Shen, Ruwen Bu, Ming Geng, Yajun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNarrative medicine has been increasingly advocated as a means of fostering empathy in medical education; however, empirical evidence integrating both effectiveness and processes underlying empathy development remains limited, particularly in non-Western contexts. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a narrative medicine–based interdisciplinary course on medical students’ empathy and to explore how such an intervention facilitates empathic development.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods design was employed in this single-center pilot study. Third-year undergraduate clinical medicine students from Shanghai University & Health Sciences were randomly assigned to an intervention group (n = 40), which received an 8-week narrative medicine course including close reading, parallel chart writing, and reflective group discussion, or to a control group (n = 40) that followed the standard curriculum. Quantitative data were collected using the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Student Version (JSE-S) before and after the intervention and analyzed using descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to examine short-term changes in self-reported empathy-related attitudes. Qualitative data were obtained through post-intervention semi-structured interviews and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis to provide contextualized insights into students’ perceived learning processes and to help interpret the quantitative findings.ResultsAfter adjusting for baseline scores, the intervention group was associated with higher post-intervention self-reported overall empathy scores and higher scores in the cognitive dimension of empathy than the control group (p < 0.05). No significant between-group difference was observed for the Compassionate Care subscale. Qualitative analysis identified four core themes: (1) a shift from a disease-centered to a person-centered perspective; (2) enhanced narrative-based communication; (3) emotional resonance and reflective processing; and (4) narrative practices as resources for coping and professional growth. Integration of findings suggests that narrative medicine education may support early cognitive and affective processes related to empathy, with behavioral expressions of compassionate care potentially developing over a longer period.ConclusionThis mixed-methods study found that a structured narrative medicine course was associated with short-term changes in medical students’ self-reported empathy-related attitudes, particularly in cognitive and reflective dimensions. By integrating quantitative outcomes with qualitative insights, the findings highlight empathy as a dynamic process and suggest that incorporating narrative medicine into undergraduate medical education may be beneficial, with behavioral outcomes to be evaluated in future longitudinal studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09584-y.

Background

Empathy is a fundamental clinical competency that underpins effective communication, patient trust, and positive health outcomes [1, 2]. Consequently, its cultivation is considered a central goal of contemporary medical education [3, 4]. However, concerns have been raised that the dominance of the biomedical paradigm, with its emphasis on technological and diagnostic reasoning, may contribute to a decline in empathic engagement, resulting in the so-called “dehumanization” of clinical practice [5, 6]. This trend underscores the urgent need for pedagogical innovations that systematically foster humanistic qualities in future physicians [7–9].

Narrative medicine has emerged as a prominent educational intervention to address this gap [10]. Developed by Rita Charon and colleagues [11], it employs tools such as close reading of literature and reflective writing, including parallel charts, to cultivate the skills of attention, representation, and affiliation. In this framework, attention refers to the capacity to notice patients’ stories, emotions, and subtle cues; representation refers to giving form to clinical experience through writing or other expressive practices; and affiliation refers to the relational connection that may emerge through attentive listening and narrative understanding. Parallel charts are reflective writings that accompany but differ from standard medical records: rather than documenting only biomedical facts, they invite learners to reflect on patients’ lived experiences, their own emotional responses, and aspects of encounters not captured in formal documentation. By engaging with patients’ illness narratives and reflective writing practices, learners are thought to develop deeper cognitive and affective capacities for understanding patient experiences [12–14].

Existing research, primarily from Western contexts, offers promising but limited evidence for the efficacy of narrative medicine [15]. Studies often rely on qualitative designs that richly describe learner experiences but lack generalizability, or on simple pre-post quantitative assessments that measure effect without elucidating the mechanisms of change. A notable limitation is the scarcity of studies that integrate these approaches to provide a comprehensive understanding of both learning outcomes and processes [16, 17]. Furthermore, rigorously designed studies evaluating structured narrative medicine curricula in Asian, and specifically Chinese, medical education settings are largely absent.

To address these gaps, this study employed a convergent mixed-methods design to evaluate a novel, interdisciplinary narrative medicine course. We aimed to quantitatively assess its impact on medical students’ empathy using a controlled pre-post design with the validated Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE) [18], while also qualitatively exploring students’ subjective learning experiences and the perceived mechanisms through which the curriculum influenced their perspectives and attitudes. By integrating quantitative and qualitative data, this research could provide robust, multi-faceted evidence of the curriculum's effectiveness and potentially generate insights into the “black box” of how narrative medicine pedagogy facilitates professional and personal development. Findings from such a study could inform the evidence-based design and implementation of narrative medicine in broader medical education reform efforts.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design [19]. This approach involves the simultaneous but separate collection and analysis of quantitative and qualitative data within a single research project, with the explicit purpose of comparing or combining the results to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the research problem [20, 21]. The design was chosen to achieve methodological complementarity and triangulation, addressing the following key rationales:

First, it aligns with the dual objectives of the research. The study sought to answer two interconnected yet distinct questions: (1) whether the narrative medicine course was associated with short-term changes in students’ self-reported empathy-related attitudes (quantitative strand), and (2) how students experienced the course and perceived its influence on their perspectives, communication, and professional reflection (qualitative strand). The quantitative component, using pre-post controlled measurements with the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Student Version (JSE-S), provided comparative evidence on the direction and magnitude of short-term self-reported empathy outcomes. Concurrently, the qualitative component, drawing on interviews and reflective writings, offered contextualized insights into students’ subjective experiences, meaning-making, and perceived learning processes.

Second, the mixed-methods design was intended to facilitate comparison and integration across the two strands. Quantitative outcomes and qualitative findings were planned to be examined together to identify areas of convergence, complementarity, and divergence. Convergent findings could provide complementary perspectives on students' learning experiences and empathy-related outcomes, whereas divergent findings would prompt cautious interpretation and identify questions requiring further investigation.

Finally, this integrated approach addressed some of the limitations of using a single methodological paradigm. A standalone quantitative pre-post assessment can estimate changes in self-reported empathy outcomes, but it cannot explain how students understand or experience such changes. Conversely, a purely qualitative inquiry can provide rich detail about students’ experiences, but it does not offer a comparative estimate of outcome change between groups. The convergent design therefore combined the breadth of quantitative comparison with the depth of qualitative exploration, enabling a more contextually grounded interpretation of the narrative medicine intervention’s short-term educational value.

Participants and setting

Study setting and participant recruitment

This study was conducted at Shanghai University of Medicine and Health Sciences. Participants were third-year undergraduate students enrolled in the clinical medicine program in the 2024 cohort. A total of 80 students were recruited as volunteers via a campus-wide online announcement, which outlined the study’s purpose, procedures, potential benefits and risks, and participants’ rights, including the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. All volunteers provided verbal consent prior to participation in the screening process.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To control for extraneous variables and ensure sample homogeneity, the following criteria were applied. The inclusion criteria were: (1) full-time third-year undergraduate students majoring in clinical medicine; (2) willingness to participate and provide written informed consent; and (3) no prior systematic training or coursework in narrative medicine. Prior exposure to narrative medicine was assessed using a self-reported screening questionnaire administered before randomization. Students were asked whether they had previously participated in any formal coursework, elective modules, workshops, or systematic training related to narrative medicine, medical humanities, reflective writing, parallel chart writing, or empathy-focused educational programs.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) inability to complete the entire course or data collection procedures due to leave of absence, clinical rotations, or other academic arrangements; and (2) a prior academic background or formal training in psychology, literature, communication studies, counselling, medical humanities, sociology, ethics, or other related fields that could provide prior familiarity with narrative-based reflection or empathic communication. These criteria were used to minimize the potential influence of prior knowledge or training on the intervention effects. All 80 volunteers met the eligibility criteria and proceeded to the randomization stage.

Randomization and blinding

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) design was employed to objectively evaluate the effect of the narrative medicine intervention. The 80 eligible participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 40) or the control group (n = 40) using the following procedure.

Randomization and allocation

Each participant was assigned a unique identification number (01–80). A computer-generated random number sequence was used to randomize these identification numbers. The first 40 numbers in the randomized sequence were allocated to the intervention group, and the remaining 40 to the control group. Group allocation was conducted after baseline data collection to prevent allocation-related bias.

Baseline equivalence

Following randomization, independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests were performed to compare demographic characteristics (e.g., age and gender) and baseline empathy scores between the two groups. No statistically significant differences were observed (all p>0.05), indicating successful randomization and baseline comparability.

Blinding

Due to the experiential and curriculum-based nature of the educational intervention, complete blinding of participants to group allocation was not feasible. Participants were informed that they were taking part in a study examining approaches within medical humanities education, but they were not informed of the specific research hypotheses. Course instructors and data collectors could not be blinded because of their direct involvement in delivering the intervention. To reduce potential assessment bias, standardized procedures were used for outcome measurement, and group labels were masked during the initial stages of quantitative data processing and analysis.These procedures were implemented to minimize potential performance and assessment bias.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on a combination of feasibility considerations and effect size estimates reported in previous narrative medicine and empathy-focused educational intervention studies. Prior randomized controlled trials evaluating narrative-based or medical humanities interventions on empathy outcomes have reported small-to-moderate effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.40–0.65) on the Jefferson Scale of Empathy or related measures [22, 23].

Assuming a moderate effect size (f = 0.25, equivalent to Cohen’s d ≈ 0.50) for group differences in post-intervention empathy scores, a total sample size of approximately 64 participants would be sufficient to achieve 80% power at a two-sided α level of 0.05 in an ANCOVA model controlling for baseline scores. Considering the available cohort size, anticipated attrition, and the educational context of a single academic year, we aimed to recruit 80 participants (40 per group), which exceeds the minimum estimated requirement. This sample size is also consistent with prior mixed-methods and pilot randomized educational studies conducted in medical education settings.

Qualitative sampling strategy

For the qualitative component, a purposive sampling strategy (maximum variation sampling) was employed to recruit information-rich cases from the intervention group. After the course and post-test data collection, 12–15 participants were selected for in-depth, semi-structured interviews based on their distribution of post-test JSE-S scores: (1) High scorers: 4–5 participants from the top 30%; (2) Medium scorers: 4–5 participants from the middle 40%; and (3) Low scorers: 4–5 participants from the bottom 30%. This approach ensured the inclusion of students with a wide range of post-course experiences, encompassing positive, neutral, and challenging perspectives. It also provided diverse and in-depth insights into the underlying mechanisms of learning and empathy development until thematic saturation was achieved.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received full approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shanghai University of Medicine and Health Sciences (Approval No: 2025-CFDY-12). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of any study-related activities.

Intervention: the narrative medicine course

Course philosophy and objectives

The 8-week interdisciplinary course was designed based on the core philosophy of integrating scientific rigor with humanistic inquiry. It moved beyond simply adding literary content to medical training, aiming instead to demonstrate the complementary value of objective clinical data and subjective illness experience, as well as clinical reasoning and narrative understanding. The co-teaching model by instructors from humanities and science/medicine backgrounds was intentionally designed to model this integration for students, guiding them to synthesize the nuanced reflection and interpretation of humanities with the logical precision and empirical basis of science. The primary learning objectives were to: (1) enhance students’ narrative competence (attention, representation, affiliation); (2) foster their empathic capacity towards patients; and (3) develop their ability to reflect on their own professional identity and emotional responses in clinical contexts.

Course structure and content

The course consisted of eight weekly 120-minute sessions. The intervention sessions were delivered as an add-on to the existing standard curriculum; therefore, participants in the intervention group attended two additional hours of class per week beyond their regular coursework. The intervention group attended all narrative medicine sessions, while the control group continued with the standard curriculum without any additional structured activities related to narrative medicine. Structure and content of the 8-week narrative medicine course

During the same 8-week period, all third-year clinical medicine students continued their standard curriculum, which included courses such as Physical Diagnosis, Internal Medicine, and Clinical Ethics. These courses were mainly delivered through conventional lectures, teacher-led questioning, and routine case-based instruction. Although some classroom interaction and case discussion occurred in the standard curriculum, these courses did not include structured reflective writing, such as parallel charts, close reading of literary illness narratives, or facilitated small-group discussions focused on students’ emotional responses to patient stories. Thus, the dialogic and reflective format was an integral component of the narrative medicine intervention.

To enhance reproducibility, the course included pre-class preparation, guided in-class activities, and post-class reflective writing. For selected sessions, students completed pre-class readings or writing assignments requiring approximately 30–45 minutes per week. Before the close reading session, students read a short illness narrative excerpt and prepared discussion questions. Before reflective dialogue sessions, students submitted parallel charts in advance, allowing instructors to select voluntary examples for discussion.

Course materials included clinical and literary illness narratives, physician and patient reflective accounts, and narrative excerpts concerning chronic illness, medical uncertainty, suffering, family roles, dignity, and end-of-life care. For example, in the close reading session, students read a selected excerpt from Paul Kalanithi’s When Breath Becomes Air describing the author’s experience of receiving a terminal cancer diagnosis as both a neurosurgeon and a patient. Students were guided to identify phrases conveying loss of control, metaphors related to time and life, and shifts in narrative voice between the perspectives of “doctor” and “patient.” To guide close reading, students were asked questions such as: “What details in the narrative reveal the patient’s fear, uncertainty, or loss of control?” and “How does the narrator’s perspective shift between the roles of doctor, patient, and family member?”

Structured reflective writing was organized around parallel chart writing. In this course, parallel chart writing was implemented as a structured reflective exercise rather than as a clinical documentation task. Students were asked to write alongside, not instead of, the standard medical record, focusing on dimensions of the encounter not usually captured in formal charts, including the patient’s lived experience, possible unspoken concerns, and their own emotional and professional responses. One writing prompt asked students to recall a patient encounter, either real or based on a standardized case, that evoked an unresolved emotion, such as frustration, helplessness, admiration, or sadness. Students were asked to write a parallel chart of no more than 800 words addressing: (1) what the patient said or did that triggered their emotional response; (2) what they imagined the patient might have been feeling but did not explicitly say; (3) how they responded internally and externally; and (4) one question they would like to ask the patient if they could revisit the encounter.

Small-group reflective dialogue sessions were conducted in groups of approximately 6–8 students, each facilitated by one of the two course instructors. These sessions followed a structured process: silent re-reading of a voluntarily selected parallel chart, attentive listening without interruption, facilitated discussion focusing first on “what I heard” and “what I felt”, and finally a closing reflection linking the discussion to clinical communication and professional development.

Pedagogical methods and teaching team

The primary pedagogical methods included guided close reading of clinical and literary illness narratives, structured reflective writing, especially parallel charts, and facilitated small-group dialogue. These activities were designed to create iterative cycles of experience, reflection, and shared meaning-making: students first encountered illness narratives through reading or clinical observation, then represented and examined their responses through writing, and finally discussed these reflections with peers and instructors.

The course was co-designed and co-taught by a two-member interdisciplinary team: a humanities instructor specializing in literature and narrative theory and a science/medicine instructor with a background in clinical medicine and scientific methodology. Their collaborative teaching in most sessions provided a live model of integrative thinking between humanistic interpretation and clinical reasoning.

Control group activity

Participants in the control group did not receive the narrative medicine intervention. Throughout the same 8-week period, they continued to follow the standard medical curriculum with regularly scheduled courses. No additional instructional activities, contact hours, or structured learning experiences related to narrative medicine, empathy training, or reflective practice were provided to this group.

The control condition therefore represented a standard-curriculum-only comparison, with no additional instructional time and no exposure to the structured dialogicand reflective pedagogical formats used in the intervention. This comparison wasselected to permit a preliminary evaluation of the narrative medicine course underroutine educational conditions.

Data collection and analysis

Quantitative data collection

The primary quantitative instrument was the Chinese version of the Jefferson Scale of Empathy for Medical Students (JSE-S), a previously published and validated self-report questionnaire. This 20-item self-report questionnaire uses a 7-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree to 7=strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 20 to 140, where higher scores indicate greater empathic ability. The JSE-S measures three dimensions: Perspective Taking, Compassionate Care, and Standing in the Patient’s Shoes. The Chinese version has demonstrated good reliability(Cronbach’s α> 0.80) and validity in previous studies [24].

The JSE-S was administered to all participants in both the intervention and control groups at two time points: one week before (T0) and one week after (T1) the 8-week course. A trained researcher administered the survey in a group setting using standardized instructions to ensure participants understood the requirements and completed the questionnaire independently and anonymously. All questionnaires were collected immediately upon completion. Data from T0 established baseline equivalence, while data from T1 assessed the immediate post-intervention effect.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews and reflective writings from students in the intervention group. The semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study based on the course objectives and a review of relevant literature, and is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Following completion of the course and post-test data collection (T1), 12–15 participants were purposively sampled from the intervention group using a maximum variation strategy based on their post-test JSE-S scores. One-on-one interviews were conducted in a private setting, with each interview lasting approximately 30–45 minutes. The interviews were conducted by a trained member of the research team who did not have an evaluative role in the course and was not involved in assigning students’ grades. Participants were informed that their responses would be anonymized and would not affect their course grades or academic standing. This arrangement was intended to reduce potential social desirability bias and perceived pressure to provide favorable responses. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and continued until thematic saturation was achieved.

In addition to interviews, all written assignments produced by students in the intervention group were collected as qualitative data, including parallel charts and reflective journals related to literary readings, clinical encounters, and experiences of medical uncertainty. These reflective writings were gathered at regular submission points throughout the course. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the anonymized use of their interview transcripts and written texts for research purposes.

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26.0), with the significance level set at α =0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant demographics and baseline JSE-S scores. Independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests were conducted to examine baseline equivalence between the intervention and control groups with respect to empathy scores and key demographic variables.

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed as the primary inferential method to evaluate the intervention effect. Post-test (T1) JSE-S total scores and subscale scores served as dependent variables, group assignment (intervention vs. control) as the independent variable, and pre-test (T0) scores as covariates.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data from interview transcripts and reflective writings were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis [25]. Analysis followed a six-phase, iterative process. First, researchers immersed themselves in the data to achieve familiarization. Initial codes were then systematically generated to capture meaningful features of the data. These codes were collated into potential themes, which were reviewed in relation to both the coded extracts and the full dataset to ensure coherence and consistency. Themes were subsequently refined, defined, and named to capture their core meanings, culminating in a final analytic narrative supported by illustrative data extracts.

To enhance analytic depth, two researchers independently coded a subset of the data and then discussed their interpretations. In line with Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis, this process was not intended to establish inter-coder reliability or produce statistical agreement [26]. Rather, differences in coding were used as opportunities for reflexive dialogue, examination of alternative interpretations, and refinement of theme development. Final themes were generated through iterative engagement with the full dataset and discussion within the research team.

Data integration

Following separate quantitative and qualitative analyses, findings from the two strands were integrated during the interpretation phase. Integration was conducted using a joint display approach and conceptual mapping to examine convergence, complementarity, and divergence between quantitative outcomes and qualitative themes. This approach was used to avoid a simplistic one-to-one correspondence between individual JSE-S subscales and qualitative themes, and to explore how multiple qualitative themes could inform the interpretation of each empathy-related outcome.

Overview of qualitative data

Semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with 12 students from the experimental group upon completion of the narrative medicine course. Each interview lasted approximately 45–60 minutes and was audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. In addition, reflective writings from all experimental group participants (n=40) were collected and analyzed. Thematic analysis was employed to identify, analyze, and report patterns within the data, yielding four core themes that illustrate how the course influenced students’ perceptions, communication skills, emotional engagement, and professional identity.

Theme 1: shift in perception—from disease to person

Prior to the course, students reported a predominant focus on biomedical indicators and diagnostic clues. Following the intervention, a notable shift occurred toward viewing patients as whole individuals situated within unique life contexts. This re-framing moved attention from “what is the disease” to “who is the person experiencing it.” “During previous clinical observations, my mind was fixed on ‘what is the diagnosis’, and I kept asking about symptoms. Now I instinctively wonder, ‘What does this illness mean for this teacher’s work, for his family life?’ He is no longer just a ‘hypertension case’, but a teacher worried about not being able to grade his students’ homework on time.” (Participant D, reflective writing)

“Writing the parallel chart forced me to describe the patient’s gaze and tone of voice. That pushed me to observe details I usually overlook. I wrote: ‘When mentioning medical expenses, his previously fluent speech noticeably slowed, and he began to avoid eye contact’. That made me realize that behind the disease lies heavy financial pressure and anxiety about losing social roles.” (Participant F, interview)

Theme 2: deepened communication—listening beyond symptoms

Narrative training transformed students’ communication approach from unidirectional information-gathering to bidirectional story-receiving. They learned to create conversational space that allowed patients’ narratives to unfold, enriching clinical understanding.

“I learned to ask, ‘Could you tell me more about what was happening at that time?’ instead of ‘Are there any other symptoms?’ This simple change in questioning had a remarkable effect. One elderly woman with abdominal pain began to share how she lives alone and fears being a burden to her children—a narrative far richer and more revealing than a standard symptom history.” (Participant H, interview)

“In small-group discussions where we shared our parallel charts, I realized each of us heard a different emphasis in the same patient’s story. This taught me that listening is not only about content, but about understanding what the patient is trying to highlight emotionally. I now pay attention to what the patient repeats—that is often their deepest concern.” (Participant J, reflective writing)

Theme 3: emotional resonance—engaging with patient suffering

Students described an enhanced capacity to connect with and comprehend patients’ emotional experiences. The course also provided a legitimate and constructive outlet for processing their own affective responses to illness and dying.

“Reading When Breath Becomes Air, I cried several times. It wasn’t out of pity, but because I could almost touch his sense of reluctance and attachment toward life fading. That resonance has given me, when facing terminally ill patients in the clinic, not just helpless panic, but a quieter courage to be present.” (Participant L, interview)

“Before, when encountering a depressed patient, I instinctively wanted to escape because I didn’t know how to handle the heaviness. The parallel chart became my ‘emotional container’. Writing down that sense of powerlessness and sadness was itself a process of sorting and release. Hearing in the group that others felt similarly made me realize I wasn’t alone—that was tremendously supportive.” (Participant B, reflective writing)

Theme 4: re-conceptualizing professional identity and values

Engagement in narrative practices prompted students to reconsider the boundaries of medicine and the physician’s role, expanding from a technical fixer to a companion, witness, and co-constructor of meaning.

“I used to think the goal of medicine was cure. Now I understand that when cure is impossible, ‘healing’ can still happen—by alleviating suffering, honoring patient preferences, preserving dignity. Medicine’s value lies not only in technical victory, but in never abandoning care for the person, regardless of the outcome.” (Participant M, interview)

“This course helped me recover my original motivation for studying medicine. Among the vast amount of knowledge and technical procedures, I had once lost my way. These patients’ stories and our reflective writing repeatedly pulled me back, reminding me that what I will face in the future is not just organs and tissues, but living people—with joys, sorrows, attachments, and dreams, just like the protagonists in these narratives. This has reshaped my definition of a ‘good doctor’.” (Participant E, reflective writing)

Discussion

This study can be considered a pilot randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate both the effectiveness and underlying mechanisms of a structured narrative medicine curriculum within a Chinese medical education context. By integrating quantitative and qualitative findings, we move beyond a simple confirmation of efficacy to offer a nuanced understanding of how and why narrative pedagogy fosters empathic development. The discussion that follows interprets these core findings, situates them within existing literature, explores their implications, and acknowledges the study’s limitations.

Principal findings and interpretation

Our most salient finding is that the narrative medicine intervention was associated with higher post-intervention self-reported empathy-related attitudes, with marked gains in the cognitive dimensions of empathy—Perspective Taking and Standing in the Patient’s Shoes. This finding is consistent with a growing body of international literature demonstrating the positive impact of narrative-based practices within medical humanities education [27, 28]. Importantly, the qualitative findings help contextualize these self-reported score changes by illustrating a concurrent process of perceptual reframing (Theme 1) and communicative deepening (Theme 2). Together, these convergent findings suggest that the course may have supported changes in how some students described conceptualizing and engaging with patients as persons situated within lived contexts.

A related exploratory descriptive observation concerns the control group, whose mean JSE-S total score decreased slightly from 112.38 at T0 to 110.80 at T1. Because this within-group change was not a prespecified primary outcome and was not for mally evaluated as an independent intervention effect, it should not be interpreted as evidence that the standard curriculum caused a decline in empathy. Nevertheless, this pattern is noteworthy because it is consistent with prior longitudinal studies reporting empathy decline during medical training, particularly around the transition to clinical training [29, 30]. Alternative explanations, including normal score variability, regres sion to the mean, measurement error, and contextual academic pressures during the study period, should also be considered.

Beyond empathy enhancement, the qualitative findings also suggest that the narrative medicine course may have supported processes related to professional identity formation (PIF). PIF involves the internalization of professional values, roles, and norms as trainees gradually develop a sense of “thinking, acting, and feeling like a physician” [31, 32]. Theme 4, “Re-conceptualizing the medical profession”, reflected students’ reconsideration of what it means to be a “good doctor”, with participants describing a shift from a predominantly technical view of medicine toward a more relational and patient-centered understanding. This aligns with PIF literature emphasizing guided reflection, personal narratives, relationships, and communities of practice as key drivers of professional development [33]. Thus, narrative medicine may provide a structured reflective space for students to engage with patients’ perspectives and negotiate their emerging professional identities.

A critical quantitative observation was the non-significant between-group difference in the Compassionate Care subscale (p = .082). This finding should be interpreted cautiously. One possible explanation, informed by empathy theory and the qualitative findings, is that cognitive and affective dimensions of empathy may be more immediately responsive to narrative-based reflection than behavior-oriented expressions of compassionate care. Conceptually, the Compassionate Care subscale of the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Student Version places greater emphasis on the behavioral expression of empathic care in clinical encounters, whereas Perspective Taking and Standing in the Patient’s Shoes primarily capture internal cognitive and attitudinal shifts. However, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating and requires longitudinal and behavioral evidence.

In the present study, the 8-week narrative medicine course was intentionally designed to cultivate narrative attention, reflective capacity, and emotional attunement through close reading, reflective writing, and facilitated dialogue. These pedagogical approaches may be particularly suited to supporting early cognitive and affective processes, as reflected in both the quantitative gains in self-reported cognitive empathy and the qualitative evidence of emotional resonance and reflective processing (Theme 3). However, the relatively short intervention duration and the limited opportunity for sustained real-world clinical practice may have constrained the translation of these internal processes into observable, behaviorally expressed compassionate care as measured by the JSE-S subscale.

Taken together, these findings are consistent with the possibility that narrative medicine education first supports internal cognitive and affective processes related to empathy. Nevertheless, the present study cannot determine whether these short-term self-reported and reflective changes translate into sustained compassionate behaviors or observable empathic communication with patients in clinical settings. Longitudinal studies incorporating behavioral assessments, patient or standardized-patient feedback, and follow-up during clinical placements are needed to examine this possibility [34, 35].

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings in this study provided a more nuanced interpretation of students’ short-term self-reported empathy-related changes and learning experiences. While the quantitative data indicated the magnitude and direction of changes in JSE-S scores, the qualitative findings helped contextualize how students described their experiences of the narrative medicine course.

First, the improvements observed in overall self-reported empathy and in the cognitive subdomains of the JSE-S were interpreted alongside the qualitative themes of perceptual reframing and communicative deepening (Themes 1 and 2). The quantitative results alone suggest short-term improvements in self-reported empathy-related attitudes, but they do not explain how students understood or experienced these changes. The qualitative data illustrate that some participants described moving from a primarily disease-centered focus toward a more person-centered understanding, suggesting convergence between the two strands. Without the qualitative strand, these score changes might be interpreted only as changes in questionnaire responses rather than as part of students’ reported learning experiences.

Second, the integration of methods was particularly useful in interpreting the non-significant quantitative finding for the Compassionate Care subscale. From a purely quantitative perspective, this result indicates that the intervention did not produce a statistically significant between-group difference on this subscale. The qualitative findings, however, provide a possible interpretive context by illustrating students’ reported emotional resonance, reflective processing, and emerging awareness of compassionate responses (Theme 3). This suggests that narrative medicine education may support internal cognitive and affective processes, although the present study cannot determine whether these processes translate into observable compassionate behaviors.

Finally, the mixed-methods design helped make visible aspects of students’ learning experiences that would likely remain less apparent if either method were used in isolation. A solely quantitative approach would have provided limited insight into how students interpreted their learning, while a purely qualitative approach would have lacked a comparative measure of short-term self-reported empathy outcomes. By integrating both strands, this study offers a cautious meta-inference: narrative medicine may be associated with short-term self-reported changes in cognitive empathy and with students’ reported experiences of perspective-taking, emotional reflection, and professional identity reflection. Further longitudinal and behavioral research is needed to determine whether these reported changes are sustained and whether they translate into observable empathic communication or compassionate care in clinical settings.

Implications for medical education

Curriculum integration

Narrative medicine may be considered for integration into undergraduate medical curricula, not merely as an isolated elective but potentially as a longitudinal thread supporting empathy development, reflective capacity, and professional identity formation. Based on the findings of this single-institution pilot study, a dedicated 8-week module combining close reading, reflective writing workshops, and facilitated small-group discussion may represent a feasible and promising model for introducing narrative medicine into undergraduate medical education. However, further validation in larger, multi-center, and longer-term studies is needed before this model can be considered generalizable.

Faculty development

Success hinges on faculty competence. Investing in training for both clinical and humanities educators is essential to build interdisciplinary teaching teams capable of guiding reflective practice.

Evaluation of student learning and program outcomes

Evaluation should distinguish between assessment of student learning and evaluation of program outcomes. For student learning, reflective portfolios, parallel chart writing, and facilitated discussion records may help capture students’ engagement with perspective-taking, emotional reflection, and professional identity development. For program evaluation, validated self-report instruments such as the JSE-S should be complemented by behavioral or externally rated outcomes, including observed communication performance, standardized-patient feedback, patient-rated empathy, faculty ratings, and longitudinal follow-up during clinical placements.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

A key strength of this study is its mixed-methods design, which provides both quantitative evidence of short-term changes in self-reported empathy and qualitative insights into students’ learning experiences. The randomized allocation in the quantitative component strengthened internal validity within the volunteer sample, while the thematic analysis helped contextualize how students experienced the narrative medicine course.

Because participants were recruited as volunteers before randomization, self-selection bias is possible. Students who were more interested in medical humanities, empathy development, or meaningful patient relationships may have been more likely to participate. Although randomization helped balance measured baseline characteristics between the intervention and control groups, it could not eliminate potential selection bias in the initial volunteer sample. This limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of medical students.

Several limitations related to sample size should be acknowledged. Although the study was powered to detect moderate intervention effects on overall empathy and key cognitive subdomains, it may have been underpowered to identify smaller effects, particularly within specific subscales such as Compassionate Care. As with many educational randomized controlled trials conducted within a single institution, sample size was constrained by cohort availability and curricular feasibility. Therefore, the findings should be understood as providing preliminary evidence of short-term associations between the narrative medicine course and self-reported empathy outcomes, rather than definitive estimates of effect magnitude.

Another important limitation is the reliance on self-reported empathy measures and post-intervention qualitative accounts. The JSE-S captures students’ self-reported empathic orientation rather than directly observed empathic behavior in clinical encounters. Similarly, interview and reflective writing data may have been influenced by social desirability or perceived expectations of the course. Thus, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of short-term changes in reported empathy-related attitudes and reflections, rather than as direct evidence of sustained behavioral change with patients.

A further limitation concerns the use of a standard-curriculum control condition. Students in the intervention group received an additional 120 minutes of instruction each week, whereas the control group continued with the standard curriculum without an active contact-time-matched comparison. Therefore, the observed effects cannot be attributed solely to narrative medicine content, as non-specific factors such as additional teacher attention, small-group interaction, and opportunities for dialogic reflection may also have contributed to the observed self-reported and reflective changes.

Taken together, these limitations point to several priorities for future research. Multi-center studies with larger and more diverse samples and longer-term follow-up are needed to examine the sustainability of self-reported empathy outcomes. Methodologically, future studies may consider employing an active control condition matched for contact hours and classroom format, such as a general medical humanities seminar or communication skills discussion course, to more precisely isolate the specific contribution of narrative medicine pedagogy. Beyond self-reported empathy measures, further work should incorporate behavioral or externally rated outcomes, such as observed structured clinical encounters, standardized-patient or patient-rated empathy, faculty ratings, and follow-up during clinical placements. Such work would help clarify whether narrative medicine training is associated with observable changes in clinical communication, patient-centered behavior, clinician well-being, and professional development.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods study found that a structured narrative medicine course was associated with short-term changes in medical students’ self-reported empathy-related attitudes. The integration of quantitative and qualitative data suggests possible interrelated cognitive and affective pathways, including a shift toward person-centered perception and a structured space for emotional resonance and reflection. These processes may support the integration of scientific competence and humanistic care within the developing professional identity, although longitudinal and behavioral studies are needed to confirm the persistence and translation of these effects into observable clinical behaviors.

Based on these conclusions, we recommend the systematic integration of narrative medicine into core undergraduate medical curricula through dedicated, faculty-developed modules centered on reflective writing and dialogic learning. To realize its full potential, such initiatives must be supported by faculty development and assessed through methods that value both measurable growth and reflective depth.

Looking forward, narrative medicine represents more than a curricular add-on; it is a critical pedagogical approach for preparing future physicians to engage with the complexity of human illness. By training students to attend to both the disease and the person, it cultivates the essential capacity for empathic, resilient, and integrated medical practice. Future longitudinal and multi-center research is encouraged to solidify these findings and explore their translation into long-term professional behavior and patient care outcomes.
