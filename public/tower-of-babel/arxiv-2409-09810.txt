Local MALA-within-Gibbs for Bayesian image deblurring with total variation prior Thanks: Submitted to the editors on September 18, 2024.
Rafael Flock, Shuigen Liu, Yiqiu Dong, Xin T. Tong

Abstract
We consider Bayesian inference for image deblurring with total variation (TV) prior. Since the posterior is analytically intractable, we resort to Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. However, since most MCMC methods significantly deteriorate in high dimensions, they are not suitable to handle high resolution imaging problems. In this paper, we show how low-dimensional sampling can still be facilitated by exploiting the sparse conditional structure of the posterior. To this end, we make use of the local structures of the blurring operator and the TV prior by partitioning the image into rectangular blocks and employing a blocked Gibbs sampler with proposals stemming from the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA). We prove that this MALA-within-Gibbs (MLwG) sampling algorithm has dimension-independent block acceptance rates and dimension-independent convergence rate. In order to apply the MALA proposals, we approximate the TV by a smoothed version and show that the introduced approximation error is evenly distributed and dimension-independent. Since the posterior is a Gibbs density, we can use the Hammersley-Clifford Theorem to identify the posterior conditionals which only depend locally on the neighboring blocks. We outline computational strategies to evaluate the conditionals, which are the target densities in the Gibbs updates, locally and in parallel. In two numerical experiments, we validate the dimension-independent properties of the MLwG algorithm and demonstrate its superior performance over MALA.

Introduction

Image acquisition systems usually capture only a blurred version of the “true image”.
In many applications, such as remote sensing, medical imaging, astronomy, and digital photography, the aim is to reconstruct the “true image” from the acquired image
[
3
,
4
]
.
Since the blurring can oftentimes be modeled by a linear convolution operator, image deblurring is a classical linear inverse problem.
In this paper, we follow the typical assumptions that the blurred image is obtained by a linear operator and that it is corrupted by additive Gaussian noise.
Computing a solution is usually not straightforward due to the ill-posedness of the problem, which is due to the ill-conditioning of the forward operator and the noise.
To resolve the ill-posedndess, we often require regularization, and a commonly used regularization technique in image reconstruction is the edge-preserving total variation regularization (TV) introduced in
[
32
]
.

In this paper, we are not only interested in computing a reconstruction of the “true image”, but also in quantifying the uncertainty of the reconstruction.
To this end, we formulate the image deblurring problem with TV regularization as a Bayesian inverse problem.
Bayesian inverse problems can be characterized by the posterior density, which is the product of a likelihood function and a prior density
[
10
]
.
Then, in accordance with the deterministic inverse problem described in the first paragraph, the likelihood function is linear-Gaussian and the prior density is a Gibbs density with a potential function given by TV.
This so-called TV prior was introduced for Bayesian inference in electrical impedance tomography in
[
33
,
14
]
and has also been used in other Bayesian image reconstruction problems, e.g., image deblurring
[
2
]
and geologic structure identification
[
18
]
. In
[
16
]
, it is shown that the TV prior is not edge-preserving when refining the discretization of the unknown image while keeping the discretization of the observed image fixed.
We note that our work is not affected by this result, since we assume that the unknown and the observed image have the same discretization.

The posterior obtained from the linear-Gaussian likelihood and the TV prior is log-concave and can be expressed as a Gibbs density of the form

\pi({\bm{x}})\propto\exp(-l({\bm{x}})-\varphi_{0}({\bm{x}})),

(1)

where the potential
l
is continuously differentiable and gradient-Lipschitz, and the potential
\varphi_{0}
is non-smooth.
Target densities of this structure are common in Bayesian inverse problems with sparsity promoting priors
[
1
]
, which are often used in image reconstruction problems and sparse Bayesian regression, e.g., in the famous Bayesian LASSO
[
27
]
.

One way to perform uncertainty quantification with respect to a posterior is by sampling from it.
The samples can then be used to, e.g., approximate expectations via Monte Carlo estimates or to compute posterior statistics such as mean, standard deviation, or credibility intervals (CI).
Since we cannot sample directly from our posterior density in closed form, we use Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.
However, the high dimensionality of images prohibits the direct application of most MCMC methods, as their convergence slows down considerably with increasing dimension.

In this paper, we show how low-dimensional sampling can nevertheless be facilitated by exploiting the sparse conditional structure of the posterior.
The sparse conditional structure is due to the fact that the full conditional posterior of any image patch of arbitrary size only depends on the neighboring pixels within some radius.
This can be intuitively understood by considering that the convolution as well as TV operate
locally
on the image.
Mathematically, the independence relationships among image patches can be proven by the Hammersley-Clifford Theorem because the considered posterior is a Gibbs density with a sparse neighborhood
[
19
]
.
The Gibbs sampler is an attractive choice for posteriors with sparse conditional structure because the reduced dependencies only require a local evaluation of the conditionals, which is usually cheaper to compute.
Moreover, several updates may be performed in parallel.
To make use of these advantages, we partition the image into square blocks of equal size and employ a blocked Gibbs sampler with local and parallel updates of the image blocks.

Since we can not sample the conditionals in the blocked Gibbs sampler in closed form, we employ MALA-within-Gibbs (MLwG) sampling.
In such a sampling scheme, one generates candidate samples via the well-known Metropolis-adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) proposal.
MALA belongs to the class of Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) methods, which are derived by discretizating a Langevin diffusion equation
[
29
]
.
However, LMC methods require the gradient of the log-target density, and due to the non-smoothness of
\varphi_{0}
in
eq.

1
we can not directly use the MALA proposal in the blocked Gibbs sampler.

Several adaptations of LMC methods to non-smooth target densities have been developed in the literature.
Arguably the most prominent adaptations are the proximal LMC algorithms, which were introduced in
[
28
,
6
]
.
Therein, the target is approximated by its Moreau-Yoshida envelope, which is continuously differentiable.
An overview of proximal LMC methods is given in
[
17
]
.
Another adapted LMC method is perturbed Langevin Monte Carlo (P-LMC), which is based on Gaussian smoothing
[
23
,
5
]
.
While both, proximal LMC and P-LMC, solve the issue of non-smoothness, their performance still deteriorates with increasing dimension.

In this work, instead of adapting the sampling method, we approximate the target density
eq.

1
by replacing
\varphi_{0}
with a smooth approximation
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
:

\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\propto\exp(-l({\bm{x}})-\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})).

(2)

The smoothed potential is such that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\to\varphi_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and allows us to use the MALA proposal in the blocked Gibbs sampler.
For the resulting posterior approximation
eq.

2
, we derive a dimension-independent bound on the Wasserstein-
1
distance between the marginal densities of
\pi
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, which guarantees the accuracy of the smoothing.

It is shown in
[
34
]
that under the assumption of sparse conditional structure, MLwG can have dimension-independent block acceptance rate (at fixed step size) and dimension-independent convergence rate.
In this paper, we present similar guarantees with specific conditions tailored to our target density and taking the approximation error into account.
Finally, by making use of the sparse conditional structure of our posterior, which still holds under the smoothing, we show how an efficient
local & parallel
MLwG algorithm can be implemented.
We test the algorithm in two numerical experiments, where we illustrate the dimension-independent block acceptance rate and the dimension-independent convergence.
Moreover, we compare the MLwG algorithm to MALA and show that MLwG clearly outperforms MALA in terms of sample quality and computational wall-clock time with increasing dimension.

Contributions

We now summarize our main contributions.

•

We develop a Stein’s method based approach to prove that the error between the low dimensional marginals of the target distribution
\pi
in
eq.

1
and the smoothed distribution
\pi_{\varepsilon}
in
eq.

2
is dimension-independent.

•

We show how to implement an efficient local & parallel MLwG sampling algorithm by providing the local target densities and their gradients for the block updates.

•

We illustrate the dimension-independent block acceptance and convergence rates in two numerical experiments, where we also show that MLwG clearly outperforms MALA.

Notation

In this paper, we work with discrete and square images of
n\times n
pixels, but the results can be extended to rectangular images.
We employ a vector notation for the images by stacking them in the usual column-wise fashion.
Furthermore, we partition the images into blocks of size
m\times m
, such that
n/m
is an integer.
We denote the number of pixels in one image by
d=n^{2}
, the number of pixels in one block by
q=m^{2}
, and the number of blocks by
b=(n/m)^{2}
.
We denote pixels by lower case Greek letters and blocks by lower case Roman letters.
E.g., for an image
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
We write
[b]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,b\}
where
b
is some positive integer, and
\setminus i\coloneqq[b]\setminus i
for
i\in[b]
.
We use upper case Greek letters to denote sets of pixel indices.
For example,
{\bm{x}}_{\Theta}
is the parameter block of pixels with indices in
\Theta\subseteq[d]
.
We use the notation
\nabla_{i}:=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}
for the gradient operator with respect to the pixels of block
{\bm{x}}_{i}
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Problem setting

We fist consider the classic image deconvolution problem with TV regularization, e.g.,
[
25
]
and assume that a blurred and noisy image
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is obtained by

{\bm{y}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{true}}+\bm{\epsilon}.

(3)

Here,
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{true}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the “true” image,
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\bm{I}_{d})
, and
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the convolution operator.
In particular, one can construct
{\bm{A}}
via the discrete point spread function (PSF), i.e., the convolution kernel.
We assume that the discrete PSF has radius
r>0
, such that
{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}
convolves each pixel with the surrounding
(2r+1)^{2}
pixels.

Equation

3
constitutes an inverse problem where the goal is to recover a solution that is “close to
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{true}}
” from the data
{\bm{y}}
.
Computing a solution is typically not straightforward due to the ill-posedness of the problem.
That is,
{\bm{A}}
may be badly conditioned and thus highly sensitive to the noise.
For this reason, we employ the edge-preserving TV regularization introduced in
[
32
]
, which is a commonly used regularization technique in image reconstruction.
For discretized images, it reads

{\rm TV}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\sqrt{({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}},

(4)

where

{\bm{D}}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
{\bm{D}}^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

are finite difference matrices for the computation of the horizontal and vertical differences between the pixels.
The subscript
\alpha
denotes the
\alpha
-th row of
{\bm{D}}^{(v)}
and
{\bm{D}}^{(h)}
, such that

{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}
are the differences between pixel
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
and its neighboring pixels in the vertical and horizontal directions, respectively.
The finite difference matrices are defined as

\begin{aligned} {\bm{D}}^{(v)}&=\bm{I}_{n}\otimes{\bm{D}}_{n}\\
{\bm{D}}^{(h)}&={\bm{D}}_{n}\otimes\bm{I}_{n},\end{aligned}\qquad\mathrm{with}\qquad{\bm{D}}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1&&&\\
&-1&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&-1&1\\
&&&&-1\end{bmatrix}_{[n\times n]}.

(5)

Here,
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.
In this paper, without loss of generality, we consider zero boundary conditions for the finite difference matrices.
Other boundary conditions can be used by slight modification of the algorithm and the analysis.
In practice, we compute the convolution and TV in a matrix-free way due to the high dimensionality of the problems.
However, the matrix expressions are more convenient for the derivation of our theoretical results.

We now formulate the Bayesian inverse problem by defining the posterior probability density

\pi({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})\propto\pi({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\pi_{\rm prior}({\bm{x}})

with the likelihood function
{\bm{x}}\mapsto\pi({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
and the prior density
\pi_{\rm prior}({\bm{x}})
.
The likelihood function is determined by the data generating model
eq.

3
and reads

\pi({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(6)

We note that the data
{\bm{y}}
is fixed in this paper.
The prior is constructed based on the discretized TV
eq.

4
and is of the Gibbs-type density

\pi_{\rm prior}({\bm{x}})\propto\exp\left(-\delta{\rm TV}({\bm{x}})\right),

(7)

where
\delta>0
is controlling the strength of the prior.
In this paper, we consider
\delta
to be given.
The prior
eq.

7
is called the TV prior and was introduced in
[
33
,
14
]
.
Consequently, the likelihood function
eq.

6
and the TV prior
eq.

7
give rise to a log-concave composite posterior density of the form

\begin{split}\pi({\bm{x}}):=\pi({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})&\propto\exp\left(-l({\bm{x}})-\varphi_{0}({\bm{x}})\right),\\
\text{with the potentials}\quad l({\bm{x}})&:=\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{0}({\bm{x}}):=\delta{\rm TV}({\bm{x}}).\end{split}

(8)

2.2
MALA-within-Gibbs (MLwG)

A Gibbs sampler in its original form, also called component-wise or sequential Gibbs sampler, updates each component
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
sequentially from the full conditional

\pi({\bm{x}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{x}}_{\setminus\alpha})
,
and a new sample
{\bm{x}}
is obtained after all components are updated.
It can be shown that a sample chain constructed by such an algorithm converges to the target density, see, e.g.,
[
29
]
.
Moreover, the convergence result holds also for
block updates
, i.e.,
blocked Gibbs sampling
, which we employ in this paper.

To this end, we partition the image
{\bm{x}}
into
b
square block images
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
i=1,\dots,b
.
See
Figure

1
for an example with a 4-by-4 block partition.
The blocks
{\bm{x}}_{i}
have equal side lengths
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
n/m
is an integer, and they contain
q=m^{2}
pixels.
Moreover, we require
m>2r
, where
r
is the radius of the discrete PSF in the convolution.

Figure 1:
Example of a partition with 16 blocks.
When updating a block, the neighboring blocks must be fixed.
This is exemplified here for block
6
, where the hatched blocks must be fixed.
A possible parallel updated scheme for MLwG is indicated by the index sets
\mathcal{U}_{l},\,l=1\dots 4
, where for a given
l
, all blocks associated to
\mathcal{U}_{l}
can be updated in parallel.

We now establish some notation for the blocked Gibbs sampler.
We call a complete iteration in which all blocks are updated a
cycle
and denote by
{\bm{x}}^{k,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the state
during
the
k
-th cycle
after
the update of the
i
-th block.
To illustrate this notation, consider the following presentation of block updates:

{\bm{x}}^{0}\rightarrow\underbrace{{\bm{x}}^{1,1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}^{1,2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow{\bm{x}}^{1,b}}_{\text{$1$st cycle}}=:{\bm{x}}^{1}\rightarrow\underbrace{{\bm{x}}^{2,1}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}^{2,2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow{\bm{x}}^{2,b}}_{\text{$2$nd cycle}}=:{\bm{x}}^{2}\rightarrow\cdots

(9)

Notice also that we introduce
{\bm{x}}^{k}
to denote the state
after
the
k
-th cycle.
Other block updating rules like randomized sequences could also be employed, but are not discussed here.

Now consider the state
{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}
of the sample chain
eq.

9
.
To move to state
{\bm{x}}^{k,i}
, we fix
{\bm{x}}^{k,i}_{\setminus i}={\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i}
and sample the remaining block

{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}\sim\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i})
.
If it is not possible to sample
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}\sim\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i})
in closed form, one can use a single Metropolis-Hastings step as follows.
A candidate
{\bm{z}}^{k,i}_{i}
is simulated from a proposal density
q(\cdot|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{i})
and accepted with probability

\alpha^{k,i}=1\wedge\frac{\pi({\bm{z}}_{i}^{k,i}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i})q({\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{i}|{\bm{z}}^{k,i}_{i})}{\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i-1}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i})q({\bm{z}}^{k,i}_{i}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{i})}.

(10)

Note that the proposal density
q(\cdot|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{i})
depends on
{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i}
, but here we omit the dependency for clearness.
In MLwG, the MALA proposal density is used.
For the target density
\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i})
, it reads

q({\bm{z}}_{i}^{k,i}|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\tau}\|{\bm{z}}_{i}^{k,i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}-\tau\nabla_{i}\log\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i})\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(11)

and a candidate is generated as

{\bm{z}}_{i}^{k,i}={\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}+\tau\nabla_{i}\log\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}|{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}_{\setminus i})+\sqrt{2\tau}\bm{\xi}_{i}^{k,i},\quad\bm{\xi}_{i}^{k,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{q}).

(12)

Here,
\tau>0
is the step size or scale parameter.
The choice of the step size is crucial for the performance of MALA, as too large step sizes lead to many rejected samples, and too small step sizes lead to samples which are very close to each other.
In both cases, the correlation between the samples becomes large which is not preferable.
Several adaptive strategies for the tuning of the step size exist, and in our numerical experiments in
Section

6
, we use the method from
[
20
]
which adapts the step size during burn-in.
In the MLwG sampler, the adaptive strategies can be used to tune the step size for each block individually and automatically such that the algorithm requires little parameter tuning in general.

Posterior smoothing with dimension-independent error

Since MLwG requires the gradient of the log posterior density in
eqs.

11
and
12
, we propose to approximate the non-smooth posterior density
\pi
in
eq.

8
by a smooth density
\pi_{\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, we show that the introduced error between
\pi
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is distributed uniformly, leading to a local dimension-independent error on the marginal distribution of any block
{\bm{x}}_{i}
.

The non-smoothness of the posterior
eq.

8
originates from the potential
\varphi_{0}({\bm{x}})={\rm TV}({\bm{x}})
in
eq.

4
.
Hence, we replace
\varphi_{0}
with a smoothed potential
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
for some small
\varepsilon>0
, such that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\to\varphi_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Various smoothing methods exist, but it is crucial to ensure that the introduced error remains small, particularly when
{\bm{x}}
resides in a high-dimensional space. In this paper, we consider the approximation

\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}}):=\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\sqrt{({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+\varepsilon},

(13)

for some small
\varepsilon>0
, see, e.g.,
[
36
]
.
Thus, our smoothed posterior density reads

\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}-\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\sqrt{({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+\varepsilon}\right).

(14)

As we modify
\varphi_{0}
, a function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the distance between
\pi
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
generally depends on the dimension
d
.
For instance, one can show
\text{KL}(\pi|\pi_{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon)
.
Here,
\text{KL}(\pi|\pi_{\varepsilon})
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence of
\pi
from
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, see, e.g.,
[
26
]
.
However, when examining the marginals of
\pi
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
over small blocks
{\bm{x}}_{i}
, we can show that the approximation error is independent of the full dimension and thus dimension-independent.
We comment that such dimension independence is crucial for solving the image deblurring problem.
It ensures that the smoothing error is evenly distributed across the image, rather than concentrating on certain pixels and creating unwanted artifacts in the image.
Ensuring a uniformly distributed error is not a concept unique to image reconstruction, for other applications see
[
7
,
35
,
13
]
.

The key to achieve the desired result lies in the sparse conditional structure. Intuitively, such local structure makes the distribution on small blocks mostly influenced by local modifications to
\varphi_{0}
, and modifications on remote blocks have little impact on it. To rigorously justify the above observations, we introduce the concept of
c
-diagonal block dominance, which is imposed on
{\bm{C}}={\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}
to quantify how locally concentrated the blurring matrix
{\bm{A}}
is.

Definition 1
.

Consider a positive definite matrix
{\bm{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and let
{\bm{C}}_{ij}
denote the
(i,j)
-th sub block of
{\bm{C}}
of size
q
.

{\bm{C}}
is called
c
-diagonal block dominant
for some
c>0
, if there exists a symmetric matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
s.t.

1.

for any
i\in[b]
,
{\bm{C}}_{ii}\succeq M_{ii}I
,

2.

for any
i,j\in[b]
,
i\neq j
,
\left\|{\bm{C}}_{ij}\right\|_{2}\leq M_{ij}
,

3.

for any
i\in[b]
,
\sum_{j\neq i}M_{ij}+c\leq M_{ii}
.

Remark 1
.

[
34
]
introduces a similar blockwise log-concavity condition, which is crucial for the dimension-independent convergence rate therein. The
c
-diagonal block dominance here can be viewed as an
\ell_{1}
version of the blockwise log-concavity condition (which is an
\ell_{2}
condition).

Now we state the main theorem, which shows that the marginal error of
\pi
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is dimension-independent if
{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}
is
c
-diagonal block dominant.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the target distribution
\pi
defined in
eq.

8
and its smooth approximation
\pi_{\varepsilon}
in
eq.

14
. Assume that
{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}
is
c
-diagonal block dominant as in
1
.
Suppose
\frac{\lambda}{\delta}\geq\frac{64m}{c\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
.
Then, there exists a dimension-independent constant
C
such that

\max_{i}W_{1}(\pi_{i},\pi_{\varepsilon,i})\leq C\varepsilon.

(15)

Here,
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{\varepsilon,i}
denote the marginal distributions of
\pi
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
on
{\bm{x}}_{i}
, respectively, and
W_{1}
denotes the Wasserstein-
1
distance

W_{1}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|_{2}\gamma({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\mathrm{d}{\bm{y}},

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes all the couplings of
\mu
and

Since the proofs are similar to those for Proposition 3.3 and Theorem 3.6 in

Proposition 3
.

Suppose

\exists C_{\bm{A}}>0\quad\text{s.t.}~\forall i,j\in[b],~\left\|{\bm{A}}_{ij}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{\bm{A}}.

Here,

\max_{j}\left\|[{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}-{\bm{y}}]_{j}\right\|_{2}

Remark 4

In fact, the lower bound depends on the state of x through the term
\max_{j}\left\|[{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}^{k,i-1}-{\bm{y}}]_{j}\right\|_{2}

However, since the pixels values are bounded in practice, this term is usually also bounded and dimension-independent.

Proposition 5

Suppose
{\bm{A}}
is bounded as in

5
Local & parallel MLwG algorithm

The posterior
eq.

14
can be interpreted as a Gibbs density over an undirected graph.
The nodes are given by the pixels, and the edges are given by the neighborhoods of the pixels.
In particular, a pixel is connected to all its neighbors.
Pixels are neighbors to each other if their associated random variables directly interact with each other in the joint density.
For example, two random variables
Z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
Z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
directly interact in the density
\rho(Z)\propto\exp(-Z_{1}Z_{2})
but not in
\rho(Z)\propto\exp(-(Z_{1}+Z_{2}))
.
A set of pixels in which all pixels are neighbors to each other is called clique.
Furthermore, a maximal clique is a clique to which no pixel can be added such that the set is still a clique
[
19
]
.

In the posterior
eq.

14
, the maximal cliques can be associated to the pixels.
This is due to the convolution, where all pixels within the convolution radius have a direct relationship with each other.
A Gibbs density can be factorized over its maximal cliques, and thus we can write for the posterior
eq.

14

\pi({\bm{x}})\propto\exp{\Big(-\sum_{\alpha\in[d]}V_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})\Big)},

(18)

where the maximal clique potentials
V_{\alpha}
are given by

V_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})=\frac{\lambda}{2}({\bm{y}}_{\alpha}-{\bm{A}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}})^{2}+\delta\sqrt{({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+\varepsilon}.

In fact, each clique potential
V_{\alpha}
only depends on the pixels belonging to the clique, but for now we write
V_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\Theta_{\alpha}\subseteq[d]
be the index set containing
\alpha
and the indices of all pixels within a frame of radius
r
around pixel
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
.
Analogously, let
\Phi_{\alpha}\subseteq[d]
be the index set containing
\alpha
and the indices of all pixels within a frame of radius
2r
around pixel
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
.
Here, the integer
r>0
is the radius of the discrete PSF.
Then, we can express the full conditional of
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
as

{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}\sim\pi({\bm{x}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{x}}_{\setminus\alpha})=\pi({\bm{x}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{\alpha}\setminus\alpha})\propto\exp{\Big(-\sum_{\beta\in\Theta_{\alpha}}V_{\beta}({\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{\alpha}})\Big)}.

(19)

Proof.

See
Section

A.2
.

In
Lemma

7
,
\Theta_{\alpha}
is the index set of all clique potentials that depend on
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
, and
\Phi_{\alpha}
is the index set of all pixels on which the clique potentials
\{V_{\alpha}\,|\,\alpha\in\Theta_{\alpha}\}
depend.

Lemma

7
relies on the well-known Hammersly-Clifford theorem
[
12
]
, and
eq.

19
basically states that pixel
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
and all pixels in
[d]\setminus\Phi_{\alpha}
are conditionally independent given the pixels in
\Phi_{\alpha}\setminus\alpha
.
Or in other words, the full conditional of pixel
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
depends only on its neighborhood given by
\Phi_{\alpha}\setminus\alpha
.
These reduced conditional dependence relationships are generally known as
sparse conditional structure
, and we equivalently say that the posterior density
eq.

14
is
local

[
21
,
34
]
.

Sparse conditional structure is often a prerequisite for an efficient and dimension-independent MLwG sampler.
Indeed,
Lemma

7
allows us to equivalently express the target
\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{\setminus i})
of the blocked MLwG
Algorithm

1
by a density with reduced conditional dependencies.
Concretely, we can write similarly to
eq.

19

{\bm{x}}_{i}\sim\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{\setminus i})=\pi({\bm{x}}_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}\setminus i})\propto\exp{\Big(-\sum_{\beta\in\Theta_{i}}V_{\beta}({\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}})\Big)},

(20)

where
\Theta_{i}
is now the index set of all clique potentials that depend on the pixels in block
i
, and
\Phi_{i}
is the index set of all pixels on which the clique potentials
\{V_{\alpha}\,|\,\alpha\in\Theta_{i}\}
depend.

In the following sections, we outline how
\exp{(-\sum_{\beta\in\Theta_{i}}V_{\beta}({\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}}))}
and its gradient, which is required for the MALA proposal
eq.

12
, can be evaluated efficiently.
To this end, for the
(k,i-1)
-th state of the sample chain, we define

•

the
local block likelihood
:
\exp\left(-l^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i})\right)
and

•

the
local block prior
:
\exp\left(-\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i})\right)

with the potentials
l^{k,i-1}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, such that

{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}\sim\exp{\Big(-\sum_{\beta\in\Theta_{i}}V_{\beta}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i},{\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}\setminus i}^{k,i})\Big)}=\exp\left(-l^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i})-\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i})\right).

(21)

In the potentials
l^{k,i-1}
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}
, the pixels
{\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}\setminus i}
are fixed at the
(k,i-1)
-th state of the sample chain, i.e.,
{\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}\setminus i}^{k,i}={\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}\setminus i}^{k,i-1}
, such that
l^{k,i-1}
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}
are functions in
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}
.
In the following sections, we omit the superscripts
k,i
of
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{k,i}
for readability and outline separately the explicit computations of
l^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i})
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i})
, and their gradients.

5.1
Local block likelihood

To evaluate the convolution in
l^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i})
, we require all pixels in the set
\Phi_{i}
, which contains the pixels in block
i
and all pixels within a frame of radius
2r
around block
i
.
In the following, we denote this extended block by
\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr}\eqqcolon{\bm{x}}_{\Phi_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}^{+rr}}
.
Correspondingly, we let
\bm{x}_{i}^{+r}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}^{+r}}
denote the block
\bm{x}_{i}
extended by all pixels within a frame of radius
r
around block
i
.
Note that the extended blocks
\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr}
and
\bm{x}_{i}^{+r}
have different sizes
q_{i}^{+rr}
and
q_{i}^{+r}

For example,

, depending on whether block

Moreover, we define the component selection matrices

and

, which allow for the following mappings among

\bm{x}_{i}^{+r}
, and
\bm{x}_{i}

{\bm{U}_{i}^{+r}}^{\rm T}\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr}=\bm{x}_{i}^{+r},\qquad{\bm{U}_{i}}^{\rm T}\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr}=\bm{x}_{i}.

We show an illustration of the extended blocks in

Now we can evaluate the potential of the local block likelihood by

l^{k,i-1}(\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{y}_{i}^{+r}-{\bm{U}_{i}^{+r}}^{\rm T}\bm{A}_{i}^{+rr}\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr}\|_{2}^{2},

(22)

where
\bm{A}_{i}^{+rr}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}^{+rr}\times q_{i}^{+rr}}
is the convolution matrix with the correct dimensions for the extended block
\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr}
.
Moreover, we let

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{i}

\displaystyle={\bm{U}_{i}^{+r}}^{\rm T}\bm{A}_{i}^{+rr}\bm{U}_{i}

(23)

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{k,i-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{y}_{i}^{+r}-{\bm{U}_{i}^{+r}}^{\rm T}\bm{A}_{i}^{+rr}\bm{P}_{i}^{+rr}\bm{x}_{i}^{+rr}

where
\bm{P}_{i}^{+rr}=\bm{I}-\bm{U}_{i}{\bm{U}_{i}}^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}^{+rr}\times q_{i}^{+rr}}

onto the fixed pixels of the current update, see the hatched area in

(25)

and the gradient is easily obtained as

\nabla l^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i})=-\lambda\bm{A}_{i}^{\rm T}({\bm{y}}^{k,i-1}-\bm{A}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}).

(26)

5.2
Local block prior

The set of pixels
\Phi_{i}
on which the conditional
eq.

20
depends is determined by the convolution.
To compute the TV in
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}
, we do not require all pixels in
\Phi_{i}
, but only the pixels of block
i
, extended by a frame of one pixel around it.
This is similar to the computation of the local block likelihood with
2r=1
, see
Figure

2
again.
In the following, we denote this extended block by
\bm{x}_{i}^{+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}^{+1}}
.
Note that the extended block
\bm{x}_{i}^{+1}
can have different sizes
q_{i}^{+1}\in\{(m+1)^{2},(m+1)(m+2),(m+2)^{2}\}
, depending on whether it is located in a corner, at an edge or in the interior of the image.
Moreover, we define the component selection matrix
\bm{W}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}^{+1}\times q_{i}}
, which allows for the following mapping between
\bm{x}_{i}
and
\bm{x}_{i}^{+1}
:

{\bm{W}_{i}}^{\rm T}\bm{x}_{i}^{+1}=\bm{x}_{i}.

\bm{D}_{i}^{(l)}={\bm{D}_{i}^{(l)}}^{+1}\bm{W}_{i},\qquad\bm{b}_{(l)}^{k,i-1}={\bm{D}_{i}^{(l)}}^{+1}\bm{P}_{i}^{+1}\bm{x}_{i}^{+1},

where

computes the differences in vertical (
l=v
) or horizontal (
l=h
) direction of the extended block
\bm{x}_{i}^{+1}
.
Moreover, the orthogonal projector
\bm{P}_{i}^{+1}=\bm{I}-\bm{W}_{i}{\bm{W}_{i}}^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}^{+1}\times q_{i}^{+1}}
projects
\bm{x}_{i}^{+1}
onto the pixels, which are fixed during the update.
This is similar to the computation of the local block likelihood, see the hatched area in
Figure

2
again.
Then, we can evaluate the potential of the local block prior by

\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}(\bm{x}_{i})=\delta\sum_{\alpha}\sqrt{[\bm{D}_{i}^{(v)}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{b}_{(v)}^{k,i-1}]_{\alpha}^{2}+[\bm{D}_{i}^{(h)}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{b}_{(h)}^{k,i-1}]_{\alpha}^{2}+\varepsilon},

(27)

and its gradient reads

\begin{split}\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k,i-1}({\bm{x}}_{i})=~&{\bm{D}_{i}^{(v)}}^{\rm T}\Lambda(\bm{x}_{i})^{-1}(\bm{D}_{i}^{(v)}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{b}_{(v)}^{k,i-1})\\

(28)

Remark 8
.

It is straightforward to extend the above computations to other types of difference operators (e.g., rank-deficient difference matrices, higher-order finite differences, etc.) as long as they exhibit the local structure, i.e., they act spatially local on the pixels.

Conclusions

Uncertainty quantification in imaging problems is usually a difficult task due to the high dimensionality of images.
For image deblurring with TV prior, we presented a dimension-independent MLwG sampling algorithm.
By exploiting the sparse conditional structure of the posterior, the proposed algorithm has dimension-independent block acceptance and convergence rates, which have been theoretically proven and numerically validated.
To enable the use of the MALA proposal density, we used a smooth approximation of the TV prior.
We showed that the introduced error is uniformly distributed over the pixels and thus is dimension-independent.
Moreover, through numerical studies, we found that the smoothed posterior converges quickly to the exact posterior and yields feasible results for uncertainty quantification.

Various extensions of the local MALA-within-Gibbs method can be explored for imaging problems.
For instance, the TV prior can be generalized to other difference operator-based priors that are of practical interest.
On the implementation side, the local and parallel sampling nature of the method enables efficient GPU acceleration.
We leave these considerations for future work.

A.1.1
Proof of
theorem

2

Due to the Kantorovich duality, the

Wasserstein-
1
distance of
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{\varepsilon,i}
can be written as

W_{1}(\pi_{i},\pi_{\varepsilon,i})=\sup_{\phi_{0}\in{\rm Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q})}\int\phi_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})\left(\pi_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i})-\pi_{\varepsilon,i}({\bm{x}}_{i})\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{i},

where
{\rm Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
denotes the
1
-Lipschitz function class. For any
\phi_{0}\in{\rm Lip}_{1}
, denote

\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})=\phi_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})-\int\phi_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})\pi_{\varepsilon,i}({\bm{x}}_{i})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{i}=\phi_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})-\int\phi_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

Then,
\phi\in{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})
is the class of
1
-Lipschitz functions that are mean-zero w.r.t.
\pi_{\varepsilon,i}
:

{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i}):=\left\{\phi\in{\rm Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{q}):\int\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\pi_{\varepsilon,i}({\bm{x}}_{i})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{i}=0\right\}.

(30)

Then, by definition,

\displaystyle\int\phi_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})\left(\pi_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i})-\pi_{\varepsilon,i}({\bm{x}}_{i})\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{i}=~

\displaystyle\int\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\left(\pi_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i})-\pi_{\varepsilon,i}({\bm{x}}_{i})\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\int\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\pi_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{i}=\int\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\pi({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

Given
\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})
, consider the Poisson equation for
u({\bm{x}})
:

\Delta u({\bm{x}})+\nabla\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\cdot\nabla u({\bm{x}})=\phi({\bm{x}}_{i}).

(31)

By
lemma

9
, the solution exists and satisfies the gradient estimate

\sum_{j}\left\|\nabla_{j}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2\lambda^{-1}c^{-1}.

Then by integration by parts,

\displaystyle\int\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\pi({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=~

\displaystyle\int\left(-\nabla u({\bm{x}})\cdot\nabla\log\pi({\bm{x}})+\nabla\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\cdot\nabla u({\bm{x}})\right)\pi({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{b}\left\|\nabla_{j}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot\max_{j}\left\|\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{\varepsilon}-\nabla_{j}\log\pi\right\|_{L^{1}(\pi)}.

Combining the above two inequalities, we have

\begin{split}\max_{i}W_{1}(\pi_{i},\pi_{\varepsilon,i})=~&\max_{i}\sup_{\phi\in{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})}\int\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\pi({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\\
\leq~&2\lambda^{-1}c^{-1}\max_{j}\left\|\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{\varepsilon}-\nabla_{j}\log\pi\right\|_{L^{1}(\pi)}.\end{split}

(32)

Note here,
\nabla_{j}\log\pi
is not defined pointwise, but it suffices to require that
\nabla_{j}\log\pi\in L^{1}(\pi)
. It suffices to control the right hand side of
eq.

32
. By definition,

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}\log\pi({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})=\nabla_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{j}\varphi_{0}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\left[\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+\varepsilon\right)^{-1/2}-\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\left(\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}\right)\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}\right)^{\rm T}+\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}\right)\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}\right)^{\rm T}\right)=:\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}}).

Here, we denote
{\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times q}
as the
j
-th block of
{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
and similar for
{\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}
. To control
\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})\right\|_{2}
, notice

\displaystyle\left\|\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}\right)\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}\right)^{\rm T}+\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}\right)\left({\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}\right)^{\rm T}\right\|_{2}\leq|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}|\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}\|_{2}+|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}|\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left(|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}|+|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}|\right)\leq 2\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}\right)^{1/2}=:2\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}

.

Here, we denote
{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}=({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}},{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Notice
{\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}={\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}=0
if
\alpha
does not live in block
j
or a neighbor of block
j
, so that there are at most
(m+2)^{2}
indices
\alpha
s.t.
{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})
is nonzero. Therefore,

\begin{split}\|\nabla_{j}\log\pi({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{j}\log&\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\|_{L^{1}(\pi)}\leq\delta\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\sum_{\alpha:{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})\neq 0}\left\|{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})\right\|_{2}\\
\leq~&2\delta(m+2)^{2}\max_{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left[1-\frac{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon}}\right].\end{split}

(33)

Denote the function
I_{\alpha}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left[1-\frac{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+t^{2}}}\right]
. Then
I_{\alpha}(0)=0
and

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left[\frac{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}t}{\left(\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+t^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left[\frac{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+t^{2}}{2\left(\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+t^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left[\frac{1}{2\left(\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+t^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}.

By
lemma

10
, there exists a dimension-independent constant
C_{\pi}
s.t.

\max_{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}\leq C_{\pi}.

This implies that
I_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\leq C_{\pi}/2\Rightarrow I_{\alpha}(t)\leq C_{\pi}t/2
. So that

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left[1-\frac{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{\pi}\varepsilon^{1/2}.

Substituting this into
eq.

33
, we have

\left\|\nabla_{j}\log\pi({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\right\|_{L^{1}(\pi)}\leq C_{\pi}\delta(m+2)^{2}\varepsilon^{1/2}.

Finally, substituting this into
eq.

32
, we have

\max_{i}W_{1}(\pi_{i},\pi_{\varepsilon,i})\leq 2\lambda^{-1}c^{-1}\cdot C_{\pi}\delta(m+2)^{2}\varepsilon^{1/2}.

Notice we take
\frac{\lambda}{\delta}\geq\frac{64}{c\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
and
b>1
. The conclusion follows easily from

2\lambda^{-1}c^{-1}\cdot C_{\pi}\delta(m+2)^{2}\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq C_{\pi}\cdot\frac{(m+2)^{2}}{32}\cdot\varepsilon=:C\varepsilon

for some dimension-independent constant
C
.

Lemma 9
.

Under the assumptions in
theorem

2
, the solution
u({\bm{x}})
to the Poisson equation
eq.

31
exists, unique up to a constant, and satisfies the gradient estimate

\sum_{j}\left\|\nabla_{j}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2\lambda^{-1}c^{-1}.

(34)

Proof.

Denote the operator

\mathcal{L}u({\bm{x}}):=\Delta u({\bm{x}})+\nabla\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\cdot\nabla u({\bm{x}}).

For simplicity, we still denote
\mathcal{L}{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}})
when
{\bm{v}}
is a vector-valued function

\mathcal{L}{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}):=(\mathcal{L}{\bm{v}}_{1}({\bm{x}}),\dots\mathcal{L}{\bm{v}}_{b}({\bm{x}}))^{\rm T}.

It suffices to prove
eq.

34
for
\phi\in C^{1}\cap{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})
. Note this space is dense in
{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})
, so for general
\phi\in{\rm Lip}_{1}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})
, we can take a sequence of
\phi_{k}\in C^{1}\cap{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})
that converges to
\phi
.
eq.

34
holds uniformly for
\phi_{k}
, so that passing the limit shows that it holds for any
\phi\in{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})
. Now fix any
\phi\in C^{1}\cap{\rm Lip}_{1}^{0}(\pi_{\varepsilon,i})
. It is straightforward to verify by standard elliptic theory that the solution exists (up to a constant) and
u\in C^{3}
. Taking the gradient w.r.t.
{\bm{x}}_{j}
in
eq.

31
, we obtain

\nabla_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})=\mathcal{L}(\nabla_{j}u)({\bm{x}})+\sum_{k}\nabla_{jk}^{2}\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{k}u({\bm{x}}).

(35)

Recall by definition
eq.

14
,
\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\propto\exp(-l({\bm{x}})-\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}}))
. Direct computation shows

\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})=-\nabla^{2}l({\bm{x}})-\nabla^{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})=-\lambda{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}-\nabla^{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}}),

where the
(i,j)
-th subblock of
\nabla^{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})
is given by

\begin{split}&\nabla_{ij}^{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})=\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+\varepsilon\right)^{-3/2}\\
&\cdot\Big[\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,i}^{(v)}-({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,i}^{(h)}\right)^{\rm T}\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}-({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}\right)\\
&\quad+\varepsilon\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha,i}^{(v)})^{\rm T}{\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha,i}^{(h)})^{\rm T}{\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}\right)\Big].\end{split}

(36)

For simplicity, denote
H({\bm{x}})=\nabla^{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})
. Multiplying
eq.

35
from left by
-\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}
,

\begin{split}-\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}\nabla_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})+&\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}\mathcal{L}(\nabla_{j}u)({\bm{x}})\\
=~&\sum_{k}\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}(\lambda{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}+H({\bm{x}}))_{jk}\nabla_{k}u({\bm{x}}).\end{split}

(37)

Since
{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}
is
c
-diagonal block dominant with matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
, and
H_{jj}
is positive definite, since
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is convex, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}(\lambda{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}+H({\bm{x}}))_{jk}\nabla_{k}u({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}(\lambda{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}+H({\bm{x}}))_{jj}\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})+\sum_{k\neq j}\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}(\lambda{\bm{A}}^{\rm T}{\bm{A}}+H({\bm{x}}))_{jk}\nabla_{k}u({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle\lambda M_{jj}\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda\sum_{k\neq j}(M_{jk}+\|H_{jk}({\bm{x}})\|_{2})\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\|\nabla_{k}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}.

Next we control
\|H_{jk}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
. Note
{\bm{D}}^{(v)}_{\alpha,i}=0
if
\alpha
does not live in block
i
or a vertical neighbor of block
i
. Similarly for
{\bm{D}}^{(h)}_{\alpha,i}
. Therefore,
H_{ij}({\bm{x}})=0
if
i
and
j
are not neighbors. For vertical neighbors
i,j
, there are exactly
2m
boundary indices
\alpha
s.t.
\left({\bm{D}}^{(v)}_{\alpha,i}\right)^{\rm T}{\bm{D}}^{(v)}_{\alpha,j}\neq 0
, and
\left\|\left({\bm{D}}^{(v)}_{\alpha,i}\right)^{\rm T}{\bm{D}}^{(v)}_{\alpha,j}\right\|_{2}\leq 2
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\|\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,i}^{(v)}-({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,i}^{(h)}\right)^{\rm T}\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(v)}-({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}){\bm{D}}_{\alpha,j}^{(h)}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{2}|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}}|+\sqrt{2}|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}}|\right)^{2}\leq 4\left(({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}})^{2}+({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})^{2}\right)

.

Therefore, recall

36

(38)

A similar result holds when

are horizontal neighbors. Also notice

, where we denote

if
j\neq k
are neighboring blocks. Now substitute the above inequalities into
eq.

37
. We have

\begin{split}&-\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}\nabla_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})+\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}\mathcal{L}(\nabla_{j}u)({\bm{x}})\\
\geq~&\lambda M_{jj}\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}-\sum_{k\neq j}(\lambda M_{jk}+8m\delta\varepsilon^{-1/2}{\bf{1}}_{j\sim k})\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\|\nabla_{k}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}.\end{split}

(39)

Consider
{\bm{x}}
where
\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
reaches its maximum, i.e.
\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}=\left\|\nabla_{j}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
. The first order optimality condition reads

0=\nabla\left(\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\nabla\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\cdot\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}}),

and the second order optimality condition reads

0\geq\Delta\left(\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}\right)=2\|\nabla\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{\rm F}^{2}+2(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}}))^{\rm T}\Delta\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}}).

Thus,
(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}}))^{\rm T}\Delta\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\leq 0
, since
\|\nabla\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{\rm F}^{2}\geq 0
. Under these conditions,

(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}}))^{\rm T}\mathcal{L}(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}}))=(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}}))^{\rm T}\Delta\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})+\nabla\log\pi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})\cdot\nabla\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\cdot\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\leq 0.

Hence, at the maximum point
eq.

39
reads

\displaystyle\lambda M_{jj}\|\nabla_{j}u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}-

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq j}(\lambda M_{jk}+8m\delta\varepsilon^{-1/2}{\bf{1}}_{j\sim k})\left\|\nabla_{j}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla_{k}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}\nabla_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})+\left(\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\right)^{\rm T}\mathcal{L}(\nabla_{j}u)({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{j}u({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\|\nabla_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\|_{2}+0\leq\delta_{ij}\|\nabla_{j}u\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Here, we use
\|\nabla_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{i})\|_{2}\leq\delta_{ij}
, since
\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz and is only a function of
{\bm{x}}_{i}
. So if
\|\nabla_{j}u\|_{L^{\infty}}>0
, it holds that

\delta_{ij}\geq\lambda M_{jj}\|\nabla_{j}u\|_{L^{\infty}}-\sum_{k\neq j}(\lambda M_{jk}+8m\delta\varepsilon^{-1/2}{\bf{1}}_{j\sim k})\|\nabla_{k}u\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Taking summation over
j\in\mathcal{J}:=\{j\in[b]:\|\nabla_{j}u\|_{L^{\infty}}>0\}
gives

1\geq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\delta_{ij}\geq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\|\nabla_{j}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\left[\lambda M_{jj}-\sum_{k\neq j}(\lambda M_{kj}+8m\delta\varepsilon^{-1/2}{\bf{1}}_{j\sim k})\right].

(40)

Notice
\#\{k:j\sim k\}\leq 4
, and we take
\frac{\lambda}{\delta}\geq\frac{64m}{c\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
, so that

\begin{split}&\lambda M_{jj}-\sum_{k\neq j}(\lambda M_{kj}+8m\delta\varepsilon^{-1/2}{\bf{1}}_{j\sim k})\\
\geq~&\lambda\left(M_{jj}-\sum_{k\neq j}M_{kj}\right)-32m\delta\varepsilon^{-1/2}\geq\lambda c-32m\delta\varepsilon^{-1/2}\geq\frac{\lambda c}{2}.\end{split}

(41)

Together with (
40
), we obtain (
34
).

Lemma 10
.

There exists a dimension-independent constant
C_{\pi}
s.t.

\max_{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}\leq C_{\pi}.

Proof.

Fix any
\alpha\in[d]
. For simplicity, denote

{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}=({\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}{\bm{x}},{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}{\bm{x}})=({\bm{x}}_{\alpha}-{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(v)},{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}-{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}).

Now introduce the change of variable
{\bm{z}}=T{\bm{x}}
for some linear map determined via

{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}={\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}-{\bm{x}}_{\alpha},\quad{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}={\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}-{\bm{x}}_{\alpha},

and
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}={\bm{x}}_{\alpha-}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-2}
for the other coordinates. Accordingly,
\pi({\bm{x}})
is transformed into another distribution
\mu({\bm{z}})=\pi(T^{-1}{\bm{z}})
(note
\det T=1
). Also note that
T^{-1}
admits explicit form, i.e.

{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}={\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}+{\bm{z}}_{\alpha},\quad{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}={\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}+{\bm{z}}_{\alpha},\quad{\bm{x}}_{\alpha-}={\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}.

Denote
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}=({\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(v)},{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(h)})
for convenience. Consider the factorization

\mu({\bm{z}})=\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+},{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})=\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}),

where
\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})
denotes the marginal of
\mu
on
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}
. Notice

\log\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}T^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}(T^{-1}{\bm{z}})\right\|_{2}-\log\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})+\text{const}.

Fix
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}
for the moment. Notice
\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}T^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
is
L
-smooth for some dimension-independent
L>0
, since only a dimension-independent number of coordinates of
{\bm{A}}T^{-1}{\bm{z}}
depend on
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}
. Therefore, fix any
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}
, so that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}T^{-1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+},{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}T^{-1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0},{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\right\|_{2}^{2}-{\bm{v}}^{0}\cdot({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}

,

where
{\bm{v}}^{0}
is the gradient w.r.t.
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}
of
\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{A}}T^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
at
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}
. Notice also

\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}(T^{-1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+},{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}))\right\|_{2}\leq\delta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}(T^{-1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0},{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}))\right\|_{2}+8\delta\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}\right\|_{2},

since changing
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}
or
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}
affects
4
finite difference terms in the summation. Combining the above controls, when
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}\in B_{1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}):=\{{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}:\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}\right\|_{2}\leq 1\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\log\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})-

\displaystyle\log\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})+{\bm{v}}^{0}\cdot({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{2}\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}-8\delta\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}\right\|_{2}\geq-\frac{L}{2}-8\delta.

Therefore,

\displaystyle 1=~

\displaystyle\int\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}\geq\int_{B_{1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0})}\exp\left(\log\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\exp\left(-\frac{L}{2}-8\delta\right)\int_{B_{1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0})}\exp\left(-{\bm{v}}^{0}\cdot({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}-{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0})\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\exp\left(-\frac{L}{2}-8\delta\right)|B_{1}|,

where we use the Jensen’s inequality and the symmetry of
B_{1}({\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}^{0})
. Therefore,

\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\leq|B_{1}|^{-1}\exp\left(\frac{L}{2}+8\delta\right).

This holds for arbitrary
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}^{0}
, so that the marginal distribution of
\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+})
satisfies

\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+})=\int\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha-})\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha-}\leq|B_{1}|^{-1}\exp\left(\frac{L}{2}+8\delta\right)=:C^{\prime}.

Note
C^{\prime}
is dimension-independent. Finally, notice

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\pi}\left\|{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}=~

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\mu({\bm{z}}_{\alpha+})}{\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}\right\|_{2}}\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}\leq 1+\int_{\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}\right\|_{2}\leq 1}\frac{C^{\prime}}{\left\|{\bm{z}}_{\alpha+}\right\|_{2}}\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(v)}\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{(h)}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle 1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{C^{\prime}}{r}\cdot r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\hat{\alpha}=1+2\pi C^{\prime}=:C_{\pi}.

Thus,
C_{\pi}
is dimension-independent. This completes the proof.
