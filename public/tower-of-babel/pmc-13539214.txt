HPV Vaccine Uptake, Hesitancy and Information Sources Among Young Adults: A Cross‐Sectional Study of Korea and the US
Authors: Soojung Jo, Dongjuan Xu, Hwanseok Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDespite the high effectiveness of HPV vaccination, uptake among young adults remains suboptimal. Healthcare policies, vaccine hesitancy, and sources of vaccine information may contribute to differences in vaccination behaviors across countries. To examine cross‐national and sex‐specific differences in HPV vaccine uptake and hesitancy among undergraduates in the US and Korea, and investigate the role of information sources.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study targeted undergraduate students in the US and Korea aged between 18 and 25. Data was collected from August 29, 2023, to February 20, 2024 using an online survey through social media exclusive to college students (Korea) and a university listserv (US). A total of 224 survey responses were analyzed after removing missing data (n = 43). HPV vaccine hesitancy, uptake, and frequency of information source use were assessed.ResultsHPV vaccine uptake was substantially higher in the US than in Korea (74.0% vs. 33.1%), with a significant sex gap observed only in Korea. Although no significant cross‐national differences in HPV vaccine hesitancy were observed, sex‐specific patterns emerged within each country (OR = 5.3; 95% CI = 1.1–25.3; p = 0.035). In the US, females reported slightly higher hesitancy than males, whereas in Korea, males reported greater hesitancy than females. In multivariable logistic regression, frequent use of social media was associated with higher odds of HPV vaccine hesitancy (OR = 3.3, 95% CI = 1.0–10.4, p = 0.043), while frequent use of professional organizations as an information source was associated with lower odds of hesitancy (OR = 0.4, 95% CI = 0.2–0.9, p = 0.024).ConclusionsTailored public health strategies that address social media's potential role in shaping HPV vaccine hesitancy, while expanded policy support in Korea could improve uptake and reduce hesitancy.Keywords: health promotion, papillomavirus vaccines, social media, vaccination hesitancy, young

Background

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most prevalent sexually transmitted infection (STI) in the United States (US), with nearly 13 million new cases each year and an estimated population of 42 million infected [1]. Although there is no specific data on STI prevalence in the Republic of Korea (Korea), a study found approximately 34.2% of females aged 18‐79 were infected with HPV between 2006 and 2011 [2]. HPV can cause various cancers, but about 93% of cervical cancers can be prevented through HPV vaccination [3]. The HPV vaccine is routinely recommended for adolescents aged 11 to 12 and can be given from age 9 and is available through age 26 [4]. However, uptake among young adults remains low: in 2019 only 47.0% of US adults aged 18–26 had received at least one dose, compared to 76.0% of adolescents aged 13–17 in 2022 [5, 6]. This gap underscores suboptimal vaccination in young adults.

Healthcare system and policies significantly influence HPV vaccine uptake. In the US, most insurance plans cover the HPV vaccine for both females and males, and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) offers financial support through the Vaccines for Children (VFC) program for those who are uninsured or underinsured [7]. In contrast, Korea's National Immunization Program (NIP) has offered free vaccination to female adolescents aged 12–17 since 2016, while only low‐income women aged 18–26 remain eligible thereafter [8, 9]. Although all Koreans are covered by national health insurance, non‐low‐income female young adults and males must pay out‐of‐pocket, creating financial barriers to vaccination.

Vaccine hesitancy, defined as reluctance, delay, or refusal to vaccinate [10], is a well‐established determinant of HPV vaccine uptake [11]. Yet, research has primarily examined parental perspectives, with limited focus on young adults, who remain eligible for vaccination and are beginning to make independent healthcare decisions. This gap is critical, as young adulthood is a developmental stage in which long‐term health behaviors are established and preventive care is often underutilized [12].

Sources of vaccine information also influence knowledge and uptake [13]. While US studies consistently identify healthcare providers (HCPs) as the main source [14, 15], Korean government data suggest that Korean females primarily rely on social media [16]. Given the high prevalence of social media use among young adults [17], it is critical to examine how online platforms influence HPV vaccination attitudes and behaviors. Social media facilitates sharing of sensitive health concerns due to its relative anonymity [18] and has been associated with greater HPV awareness [19]. At the same time, these platforms disseminate substantial vaccine‐skeptical content [20, 21]. These mixed characteristics highlight the importance of examining the extent to which young adults use different sources of HPV vaccine information, and whether usage patterns vary across countries. Although previous studies have identified different HPV vaccine information sources in the US and Korea, direct cross‐national comparisons among young adults are limited. Consequently, it remains unclear whether differences in healthcare systems and information sources are associated with differences in HPV vaccine hesitancy. In addition, previous studies have reported sex differences in HPV vaccine uptake and vaccine perceptions [22, 23]. However, little is known about whether these differences persist across countries with distinct healthcare systems and vaccination policies.

This study aims to compare HPV vaccine uptake, vaccine hesitancy, and vaccine information sources among undergraduate students aged 18‐25 in the US and Korea, while identifying the sex‐specific patterns. In addition, we examined the associations between vaccine information sources and HPV vaccine hesitancy. In our analysis (in the multivariable logistic regression), HPV vaccine hesitancy was selected as the outcome variable rather than vaccine uptake, as vaccination status among young adults may often reflect parental decision‐making during their adolescence rather than their own information‐seeking behaviors. Furthermore, considering the potential differences between college students and non‐college young adults, and the use of convenience sampling, this study focused on undergraduate students rather than the broader young adult population.

Methods

This cross‐sectional study is part of a broader study to measure undergraduate students’ attitudes and behavior toward the HPV vaccine and information use for vaccines.

Data Collection

To be included in this study, participants needed to be undergraduate students, aged between 18 and 25 years, residing in either the US or Korea at the time of the survey. Data collection in the US was conducted through an undergraduate student listserv managed by the data analytics division at the authors’ affiliated university. In Korea, data was collected using the nationwide college student social media platform, “Everytime” (https://everytime.kr), which requires enrollment verification, with a university‐issued email address and/or student ID confirmation. All data were collected and managed using online data capture tool, REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture). The data collection took place from August 29, 2023, to February 20, 2024.

After excluding responses failing to meet inclusion criteria, a total of 267 responses from both countries were used for the analysis (US: 106, Korea: 161). To treat missing data, we employed listwise deletion, resulting in a final sample of 224 responses (US: 100, Korea: 124).

Ethics Statement

This study was exempted by Purdue University's institutional Review Board (no.: IRB‐2023‐432). All participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and those who provided consent voluntarily completed the survey.

Measurements

We collected demographic data, information use frequency per source, and HPV vaccine hesitancy and uptake. Information use frequency was assessed by asking, “When you searched for information about general vaccines (not COVID‐19 vaccine) in the last 12 months, how frequently did you use each resource listed below?” using a 5‐point Likert scale: never, rarely, sometimes, often, and always. For data analysis, we grouped them into three categories: never/rarely, sometimes, and often/always. The information sources used in this study included (1) social media, (2) Internet, (3) doctors or Healthcare providers, (4) professional organizations, (5) newspapers, (6) family, and (7) friend/co‐worker. To reduce ambiguity, examples were provided for selected information sources. For example, the CDC and WHO were listed under professional organizations, while Facebook and Instagram were listed under social media.

HPV vaccine hesitancy was measured by asking, “How hesitant about the HPV vaccine would you consider yourself to be?” using a 4‐point Likert scale: not at all hesitant to very hesitant. This item was adapted from a vaccine hesitancy measure originally developed for assessing parental vaccine hesitancy [24]. For the analysis, we grouped them into two categories: Not at all hesitant/not that hesitant, and somewhat hesitant/very hesitant. HPV vaccine uptake was assessed by asking, “Have you ever received a dose of the HPV vaccine?” The answer options were yes, no, and I don't know. For the analysis, we grouped the responses into two categories by combining ‘no’ and ‘I don't know’ due to the low response rate of ‘I don't know’ (n = 10, 10% in the US and n = 12, 9.7% in Korea). Demographic characteristics included sex at birth, year in college, major (health‐related or not), and country.

Analysis

We first conducted chi‐square tests to compare differences in each variable by country. We additionally performed chi‐square tests to compare sex differences within each country in HPV vaccine hesitancy and vaccine uptake. For the multivariable analysis, a logistic regression was conducted to investigate the relationships between vaccine information sources and HPV vaccine hesitancy. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. To investigate whether cross‐national differences in HPV vaccine hesitancy varied by sex, an interaction term between sex at birth and country was included in the logistic regression model. Adjusted predicted probabilities of HPV vaccine hesitancy were estimated from the fitted model and plotted to facilitate interpretation of the interaction effect. Since age was highly correlated with year in college (Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, ρ = 0.72) and had more missing data, age was excluded from the model. All data analysis were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas). Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p < 0.05.

Demographic Characteristics

Demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the respondents was 21.0 (± 1.8). Over half of the respondents were female (58.9%) and Korean students (55.4%). The distribution of students by academic year was as follows: first year (17.0%), second year (18.3%), third year (25.9%), fourth year (35.3%), and fifth year (3.6%). Students majoring in health‐related fields comprised 27.7% of the respondents. Regarding academic major, a greater proportion of US respondents were enrolled in non‐health‐related fields compared to Korean respondents (p = 0.001). Less than one‐third (23.2%) were hesitant about the HPV vaccine, and more than half (51.3%) reported having received the HPV vaccine.

Sex‐Specific Cross‐National Differences in HPV Vaccine Uptake and Hesitancy

Just over half of respondents (51.3%, Table 1) reported having received the HPV vaccine. Uptake was substantially higher in the US, where nearly three‐quarters (74.0%) were vaccinated, compared with only one‐third (33.1%) in Korea (p < 0.001). Sex‐specific differences were evident in Korea but not in the US (Table 2). In the US, uptake did not differ significantly by sex (females: 75.9%; males: 71.4%; p = 0.618). In contrast, in Korea, uptake was substantially higher among females than males (50.0% vs. 8.0%; p < 0.001).

Less than one‐quarter of participants (23.2%) expressed HPV vaccine hesitancy. Although no significant cross‐national differences in hesitancy were observed (US: 22.0%; Korea: 24.2%), sex‐specific patterns emerged within each country. In the US, females reported slightly higher hesitancy than males (24.1% vs. 19.1%), whereas in Korea, males reported greater hesitancy than females (32.0% vs. 18.9%).

Sources of Vaccine Information

Among all respondents, the most frequently used source to search for vaccine information was the Internet, with 60.7% reporting that they often or always used it. This was followed by professional organizations (44.6%), doctors or HCPs (39.3%), and newspapers (34.8%). Family was cited as an information source by 32.1% of respondents, while friends/co‐workers were reported by 22.3%. Social media was the least frequently used source, with 21.0% reporting often or always using it. Table 1 provides a detailed distribution of respondents’ reported source of vaccine information.

Marked differences were found in the frequency of using various information sources for vaccine‐related content. Korean respondents reported more frequent use of social media (33.1%, p < 0.001), the Internet (75.8%, p < 0.001), newspapers (60.5%, p < 0.001), and friends/co‐workers (34.7%, p < 0.001), whereas US respondents more often reported low or no use of these sources. In contrast, US respondents more frequently used professional organizations for vaccine information (55.0%), while Korean respondents more often reported infrequent use of this source (p = 0.010). No statistically significant cross‐national differences were found in the use of doctors/HCPs or family as sources of vaccine information.

Associations Between Sources of Vaccine Information and HPV Vaccine Hesitancy

Logistic regression results on HPV vaccine hesitancy are presented in Table 3. Relative to those who rarely or never used social media to search for vaccine information, undergraduates who used social media sometimes and often/always were more likely to experience HPV vaccine hesitancy (sometimes: OR = 1.2, 95% CI = 0.4–4.0, p = 0.760; often/always: OR = 3.3, 95% CI = 1.0–10.4, p = 0.043). However, only the students who often or always used social media showed statistically significant results. Besides social media, we also found that undergraduates who received vaccine information from professional organizations were less likely to be hesitant about receiving the HPV vaccine (sometimes: OR = 0.2, 95% CI = 0.0–0.6, p = 0.009; often/always: OR = 0.4, 95% CI = 0.2–0.9, p = 0.024). However, no significant associations were found between other information sources and HPV vaccine hesitancy.

Discussion

This study found notable cross‐national and sex‐specific patterns in HPV vaccination behavior and attitudes. We also found that HPV vaccine hesitancy differed depending on the sources from which respondents obtained vaccine information. Overall, HPV vaccine uptake was higher in the US than in Korea, and a significant sex difference in uptake was evident only in Korea, where females were far more likely to be vaccinated than males. The observed interaction between sex and country suggests that HPV vaccine hesitancy is particularly higher among Korean males, confirming combined influence of national context and sex in shaping attitudes toward the HPV vaccine. These findings may suggest that both structural factors (e.g., vaccination policies and financial coverage) and social factors (e.g., sex) contribute to cross‐national differences in HPV vaccine attitudes.

This discrepancy may reflect different national vaccination policies and culture related to the HPV vaccine. Since 2016, Korea's NIP has provided free HPV vaccination only for 12‐year‐old girls [8], whereas in the US, insurance generally covers all individuals within the recommended age range [7], supporting higher completion rates [25]. Moreover HPV vaccination in Korea is promoted through schools, public health centers, pediatricians, and gynecologists, as the concept of primary care providers is less common [26]. These structural and cultural features align with prior evidence on sex‐specific patterns. In the US, females are more likely to be aware of the HPV vaccine and to receive recommendations from HCPs [27]. Similarly, studies in Korea show that females tend to have higher vaccination intention than males [28]. Historically, the vaccine's framing as a “cervical cancer vaccine” has reinforced the perception that HPV vaccination is primarily for females [29], contributing to lower awareness and uptake among males. In Korea, the lack of public funding for male vaccination may further reinforce hesitancy. Thus, this study confirms the sex disparities in HPV vaccination and emphasize the need for public health strategies that are tailored to local contexts.

This study revealed the main sources of vaccine information among undergraduate students in the US and Korea. In Korea, despite the ability to directly access specialists without a primary care provider same‐day [30], only 37.9% reported using a doctor or HCPs and 36.3% used professional organizations for vaccine information. In contrast, US students most frequently used doctors or HCPs, followed by professional organizations. Notably, Korean students were far more likely to use the Internet (75.8%) compared with US students (42.0%). Social media use for vaccine information was low in both countries, but 83.0% of US students reported never or rarely using social media compared with 48.4% of Korean students. One possible explanation is that HPV vaccination is typically discussed during preventive care visits rather than in routine encounters across all medical specialties. As a result, Korean students who do not regularly seek preventive healthcare may have fewer opportunities to obtain HPV vaccine information and may instead rely on online sources. In contrast, US students may be more accustomed to obtaining preventive health information from their primary care provider. These differences may reflect variations in healthcare utilization patterns, information‐seeking behaviors, and cultural norms across countries. Given that healthcare provider recommendation is one of the strongest predictors of HPV vaccine uptake [31], increasing access to provider‐based vaccine information may help improve vaccination among young adults.

The significant relationship between social media use and HPV vaccine hesitancy, and the opposite pattern with professional organization use, could indicate that addressing social media influence may be crucial in young adult population. A recent scoping review examining social media and HPV‐related outcomes among adult population reported inconsistent findings [19]. For example, one study found no association between social media use and HPV awareness or knowledge [32], while another showed that general social media use was not associated with parental HPV vaccine hesitancy but engagement with anti‐vaccine content was strongly related [11]. Comparing with the literature, the results of this study may suggest that young adults may hold distinct perspectives, and frequent social media use could influence vaccine attitudes. Social media may therefore serve as a powerful tool for targeting young adults, and interventions such as tailored messages or social influencers have been highlighted as effective [33]. Future research should examine how social and regional contexts shape attitudes and develop targeted social media campaigns to reduce hesitancy.

The mechanism underlying the association between social media use and vaccine hesitancy is not fully understood, but the type of content appears critical. Negative HPV vaccine information is prevalent on platforms such as Instagram, often presented in story‐based formats that enhance persuasion and reinforce skepticism [34]. This could explain why frequent users showed greater hesitancy, as screen time with such content may shape negative attitudes. Individuals who distrust professional organizations or HCPs may also rely on social media, where misinformation is common and often negative [35], further reinforcing hesitancy. Nevertheless, the present study did not assess the specific content to which participants were exposed on social media. Therefore, it was not possible to determine whether the observed association reflected exposure to pro‐vaccine, neutral, or anti‐vaccine information. These findings should be interpreted with caution because information sources were assessed for vaccines in general (excluding COVID‐19) rather than HPV vaccination specifically. The survey was designed to capture patterns of vaccine information‐seeking behaviors over the previous 12 months, which may reflect the broader information environment influencing HPV vaccine attitudes. HPV vaccine information is often sought only when individuals are actively considering vaccination or after receiving a recommendation from an HCP.

By contrast, while using doctors or HCPs was not significantly associated with hesitancy, the association with professional organization use is noteworthy. One possible explanation is that trust in healthcare professionals and healthcare institutions may differ across populations. Previous research has shown that trust in doctors, nurses, hospitals, and clinics is lower in Korea than in the US [36], which may reduce the influence of provider‐based recommendations. Information from professional organizations may be perceived as more authoritative and evidence‐based, contributing to lower vaccine hesitancy. Moreover, professional organizations are more accessible than HCPs and may allow students to seek information independently. This autonomy in decision‐making may reduce hesitancy, consistent with findings that opposition to mandatory vaccination often reflects a desire for personal choice [37]. Public health strategies could leverage this dynamic by promoting professional organizations as a trusted, accessible source of vaccine information.

This study assessed information sources for vaccines in general rather than HPV vaccination specifically. These measures may still capture routine vaccine information‐seeking behaviors among young adults. Individuals are often sought only when individuals are actively considering vaccination or receive a recommendation from a HCP. Therefore, general vaccine information‐seeking patterns may better reflect the broader information environment influencing HPV vaccine attitudes. In addition, the 12‐month reference period was intended to capture usual information‐seeking behaviors rather than one‐time HPV‐specific searches. Nevertheless, future studies should examine HPV‐specific information sources directly.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, survey questions on information sources did not specifically address HPV but rather general vaccines (excluding COVID‐19), which may not fully capture HPV‐specific information‐seeking behavior. However, these items were intended to capture vaccine information‐seeking behaviors patterns. Nonetheless, such patterns may still reflect broader attitudes toward HPV vaccination. Second, the sample was limited to undergraduate students, reducing generalizability to the wider young adult population. Third, the instrument used in this study was not formally validated. To our knowledge, no validated measure currently exists to assess HPV vaccine attitudes among young adult populations in this context. Future research should prioritize the development and validation of such measures to improve the accuracy and consistency of findings. Finally, the cross‐sectional design prevents causal inference between social media use and HPV vaccine hesitancy. Future experimental studies are needed to clarify these relationships.

Conclusions

This study suggests that vaccine information sources may be associated with HPV vaccine hesitancy among undergraduate students in the US and Korea. Reliance on professional organizations was associated with lower hesitancy, whereas social media use was associated with higher hesitancy. The findings also suggest potential sex‐ and country‐specific differences in HPV vaccination attitudes and behaviors. The results of this study emphasize the need for policy support to address vaccine hesitancy and improve uptake, particularly among Korean students.
