The Dirichlet heat trace for domains with curved corners
Shi-Zhuo Looi, David Sher

Abstract
. We study the short-time asymptotics of the Dirichlet heat trace on planar curvilinear polygons. For such domains we show that the coefficient of t 1 / 2 t^{1/2} in the expansion splits into a boundary integral of κ 2 \kappa^{2} and a sum of local corner contributions, one for each vertex. Each curved corner contribution depends only on the interior angle α \alpha and on the limiting curvatures κ ± \kappa_{\pm} on the adjacent sides. Using a conformal model and a parametrix construction on the sector heat space, we express this contribution in the form c 1 / 2 ​ ( α ) ​ r 0 ​ ( α , κ + , κ − ) c_{1/2}(\alpha)\,r_{0}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-}) , where c 1 / 2 ​ ( α ) c_{1/2}(\alpha) is given by a Hadamard finite part of an explicit trace over the exact sector. For right-angled corners we compute c 1 / 2 ​ ( π / 2 ) = 1 / ( 16 ​ π ) c_{1/2}(\pi/2)=1/(16\sqrt{\pi}) and obtain a closed formula for the t 1 / 2 t^{1/2} coefficient. As an application we extend a previous result in the literature by showing that any admissible curvilinear polygon that is Dirichlet isospectral to a polygon must itself be a polygon with straight sides.



1.
Introduction

A classical problem in spectral geometry is to determine what geometric information is encoded in the spectrum of the Laplacian on a Riemannian manifold
[
8
]
. This is frequently approached using heat trace methods. Specifically, one examines the short-time asymptotic expansion of the trace of the heat kernel. This trace is spectrally determined, and therefore the coefficients in the asymptotic expansion are
spectral invariants
– geometric quantities determined by the spectrum. In 1967, McKean and Singer computed the first three heat invariants for a smoothly bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
7
]
. Using these, they showed that the area, perimeter, and Euler characteristic of such a domain are all spectral invariants. It is natural to ask whether one can deduce similar information for a less smooth domain, and indeed this has been studied in depth
[
2
,
11
,
10
]
.

We now explain the setting of this paper. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a curvilinear polygon, with piecewise smooth boundary, smooth except for a finite number
n
of corners
P_{j}
of interior angles
\alpha_{j}>0
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. Let
\kappa
be the inward-pointing curvature of the boundary, and let
\gamma(s)
be an arc-length parametrization of the boundary, oriented counterclockwise. For each
j
, let
\kappa_{j,\pm}
be the limits of
\kappa(\gamma(s))
as
s
approaches
\gamma^{-1}(P_{j})
from above and below respectively.

It is well known that the trace of the Dirichlet heat kernel on
\Omega
,
H^{D}_{\Omega}(t)
, has an asymptotic expansion as
t\to 0
, beginning with a
t^{-1}
term. The
t^{-1}
,
t^{-1/2}
, and
t^{0}
terms of this expansion have been computed, first in the exact polygonal setting in unpublished work of Ray, as cited by
[
2
]
and
[
12
]
, and then extended to the general curvilinear setting
[
10
]
. Our interest here is in the next term, which we expect at order
t^{1/2}
. In
[
4
]
, based on results in the literature such as
[
1
]
, the authors give the coefficient of this
t^{1/2}
term in the “straight corners” case, where a neighborhood of each corner
P_{j}
is isometric to an exact sector. Without this assumption, the curvature of the sides may interact with the angle at the corner. As we show, this interaction produces a term at order
t^{1/2}
.

Theorem 1
.

With notation as above, the Dirichlet heat trace
H^{D}_{\Omega}(t)
has an expansion as
t\to 0
given by

(1)

H^{D}_{\Omega}(t)=\frac{|\Omega|}{4\pi t}-\frac{|\partial\Omega|}{8\sqrt{\pi t}}+\frac{1}{12\pi}\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\,ds+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\pi^{2}-\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2\alpha_{j}}\Big)\\
+\sqrt{t}\Big(\frac{1}{256\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa^{2}\,ds+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha_{j},\kappa_{j,+},\kappa_{j,-})\Big)+O(t\log t),

where
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}
is a function only of the angle
\alpha
and the limit curvatures
\kappa_{\pm}
.

We can say substantially more about
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})
whenever
\alpha\neq\pi
. Define

(2)

r_{0}=r_{0}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})=\frac{1}{2}\csc\alpha\sqrt{\kappa_{+}^{2}+\kappa_{-}^{2}-2\kappa_{+}\kappa_{-}\cos\alpha}.

Theorem 2
.

For
\alpha\neq\pi
, the function
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})
has the form

(3)

\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})=c_{1/2}(\alpha)r_{0}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})

where
c_{1/2}(\alpha)
is a function depending only on
\alpha
, whose general expression is given by a Hadamard renormalized trace on an exact sector:

(4)

\displaystyle c_{1/2}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\,\operatorname*{fp}\iint_{(0,\infty)\times(0,\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sigma)^{-1}\sigma^{-1/2}\iint_{(0,\infty)\times(0,\alpha)}

\displaystyle a\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt{1-\sigma}},\theta,\frac{\sqrt{\sigma}\,\rho}{\sqrt{1-\sigma}},\phi\right)\,b\left(\rho,\phi,\frac{R}{\sqrt{\sigma}},\theta\right)\,\,\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi\,\,R\,dR\,d\theta.

Here
a
and
b
are functions related to the exact heat kernel on a sector of angle
\alpha
, explicitly given by (
31
) and (
46
), and
\operatorname*{fp}
denotes the Hadamard finite part at
R=\infty
of the improper integral in
R
.

Moreover,
c_{1/2}(\alpha)
has the special value

(5)

c_{1/2}(\frac{\pi}{2})=\frac{1}{16\sqrt{\pi}}.

Remark
.

A few comments:

•

The
O(t\log t)
error in (
1
) is not necessarily optimal, and in fact we expect that it should be
O(t)
.

•

Each of
a
and

is a function only of
\alpha
and the variables of integration, see (

), (

). Thus (

.

•

For inverse spectral results, it is crucial that (

. It would be of significant interest to find out for which

•

1.1.
Inverse spectral applications

In
[
4
]
, the authors prove that any curvilinear polygon with straight corners is in fact isospectral to a polygon. They require straight corners because of a lack of a formula in the curved corner case. We can strengthen their result.

Definition
.

A curvilinear polygon
\Omega
is
admissible
if, for each
j
, either
c_{1/2}(\alpha_{j})>0
or
\Omega
is straight near
P_{j}
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that an admissible curvilinear polygon
\Omega
is Dirichlet isospectral to a polygon. Then
\Omega
is a polygon.

Proof.

For a polygon, the
t^{1/2}
term in the heat trace vanishes. So it must vanish for
\Omega
. But under our assumptions, by Theorems
1
and
2
, the
t^{1/2}
coefficient in
H^{D}_{\Omega}(t)
is given by

(6)

\frac{1}{256\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa^{2}\,ds+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{1/2}(\alpha_{j})\sqrt{\kappa_{+,j}^{2}+\kappa_{-,j}^{2}}.

Since this coefficient is non-negative and only zero for an exact polygon,
\Omega
is an exact polygon.
∎

Remark
.

Observe from (
5
) that any polygon where all non-straight corners are right angles is admissible.

Plan of the paper

In section

we prove Theorem
1
. This is done by examining the geometric microlocal description of the heat kernel given in
[

]

\mathcal{C}_{1/2}\sqrt{t}

. To take advantage of this uniformity, in section

we introduce a simple conformal model for a curvilinear corner and compute the contribution
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}\sqrt{t}
in that model. This computation proceeds via a parametrix construction as in

3
) and (
4
).
Finally, in section

) by computing a sufficient number of terms in the Dirichlet heat trace expansion for a semicircle and comparing with (

2.
The corner contribution

Our approach to understanding the heat kernel on
\Omega
is based on the methods of geometric microlocal analysis
[
9
,
10
]
. The strategy is to first resolve the singularities of the heat kernel at
t=0
by constructing a “double heat space,”
\Omega_{h}^{2}
, via a sequence of geometric blow-ups. The heat kernel lifts under these blow-ups to a well-behaved (“polyhomogeneous conormal”) function on the manifold with corners
\Omega_{h}^{2}

[
10
]
. Roughly speaking, polyhomogeneous conormal functions are those whose asymptotic expansions at the boundary hypersurfaces are compatible and jointly polyhomogeneous at higher-codimension corners; the precise definition may be found in
[
9
]
.

The boundary of
\Omega_{h}^{2}
consists of boundary hypersurfaces corresponding to distinct asymptotic regimes. Among these hypersurfaces are:

•

The interior diagonal (td), modeling heat flow far from the boundary.

•

The side faces (sf), modeling heat flow near a smooth edge.

•

The front faces (ff), modeling heat flow at a corner.

The method of proof in
[
10
]
, which is important for our work here, is to use geometric microlocal analysis to perform a parametrix construction. Specifically, one constructs a parametrix
K(t,z,z^{\prime})
, an approximate solution to the heat equation, by specifying its leading order behavior at each of the three boundary hypersurfaces td, sf, and ff. This should be done in such a way so that when the heat operator is applied to the parametrix
K(t,z,z^{\prime})
, the result
R(t,z,z^{\prime})
is lower order at td, sf, and ff. In order to arrange this, we choose leading order behavior that solves the relevant model problems for the heat equation at each boundary hypersurface. We also must ensure that the specified behaviors at td, sf, and ff are consistent with each other at the intersections of the boundary hypersurfaces, so that there actually exists a polyhomogeneous conormal function on
\Omega_{h}^{2}
with all of the correct behavior. Once we have a good enough parametrix, we can recover the true heat kernel via the Volterra series

H^{D}_{\Omega}=K-K\star R+K\star R\star R-\cdots.

To analyze this Volterra series, we use a composition formula for polyhomogeneous conormal functions on
\Omega_{h}^{2}
, which in our setting is
[
10
, Theorem 3.16]
.

To be specific about what we mean when we talk about orders: set
T=\sqrt{t}
. We measure orders at each of td, sf, and ff in powers of
T
; a function which has leading order behavior
T^{-2}
has order
-2
. “Lower order” means larger powers of
T
. Throughout, “vanishes to infinite
order at td (or sf, or ff)” means the expansion has no terms of any finite
T
-order there.

The model cone heat kernel has order
-2
at td, sf, and ff. We write

\operatorname{ord}_{\mathrm{ff}}(\cdot)
for this
T
-order.
The key result of
[
10
, Theorem 3.16]
for our setting is that if
B
vanishes to infinite order at td, then

\operatorname{ord}_{\mathrm{ff}}(A\star B)=\operatorname{ord}_{\mathrm{ff}}(A)+\operatorname{ord}_{\mathrm{ff}}(B)+4
.

As a first use of these techniques, we show that the corner contribution
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})
indeed depends only on
\alpha
and
\kappa_{\pm}
. Suppose that there exist two domains
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
, each with one corner, with the same
\alpha
and the same
\kappa_{\pm}
. By
[
10
]
, the heat kernels
H^{D}_{\Omega}
and
H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
are polyhomogeneous conormal on
\Omega_{h}^{2}
and
(\Omega^{\prime})_{h}^{2}
respectively. Since
\alpha
is the same, the faces ff of these respective heat spaces are geometrically identical. We now state a lemma which informally says that: under these conditions,
H^{D}_{\Omega}-H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
has leading order at worst
t^{0}
at ff.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
be domains with a single corner having the same opening angle

\alpha
and the same one‑sided boundary curvatures
\kappa_{\pm}
at that corner.
Then
H^{D}_{\Omega}-H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
has order
\leq T^{0}
(at worst
T^{0}=t^{0}
) at ff.
Equivalently, their ff coefficients of orders
T^{-2}=t^{-1}
and
T^{-1}=t^{-1/2}
agree.

Proof.

We use
H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
to build a parametrix
K
for
H^{D}_{\Omega}
. We will build this parametrix
K
for
H^{D}_{\Omega}
by prescribing its leading behavior at the three faces. Specifically:

•

At td, we specify that
K
should have the usual interior expansion, which in this setting is simply the Euclidean heat kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which has order
-2
with no lower order terms.

•

At sf, we specify that
K
should have the same expansion as the Dirichlet heat kernel on a smooth domain with boundary whose boundary coincides with the appropriate smooth part of
\partial\Omega
. Such a heat kernel is known to have a polyhomogeneous conormal expansion by
[
6
]
, and that expansion is consistent with the Euclidean heat kernel at td. The first term, at order
-2
, is universal. The second term, at order
-1
, follows from the boundary calculus construction in
[
6
]
; by a standard scaling argument, this coefficient is a universal multiple of the curvature
\kappa(z^{\prime})
.

•

At ff, we specify that
K
should have the same
t^{-1}
(order
-2
) and
t^{-1/2}
(order
-1
) terms as
H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
. These are consistent with the interior expansion at td, since they are consistent for
H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
.

We must check that the ff specifications are consistent with the sf ones. But this is the point of having
\kappa_{\pm}
equal. Since the heat kernel
H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
is polyhomogeneous conormal, our ff specifications are consistent with the Euclidean behavior at td and also with the first two terms at sf on
(\Omega^{\prime})_{h}^{2}
. But since
\kappa_{\pm}
are identical between
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
, these terms on
(\Omega^{\prime})_{h}^{2}
have the same restrictions to sf
\cap
ff as the first two terms at sf on
\Omega_{h}^{2}
. Thus our ff and sf specifications are consistent.

This consistency means that there exists a parametrix
K(t,z,z^{\prime})
with the specified expansions at td, sf, and ff. This parametrix solves the model problems to infinite order at td and sf and to two orders at ff. So
R(t,z,z^{\prime})=(\partial_{t}-\Delta_{\Omega})K(t,z,z^{\prime})
has leading order
-2
, as opposed to
-4
, at ff, and leading order
\infty
at td and sf. Using the composition formula of
[
10
]
, we conclude that for each
n\geq 1
,
K\star(R^{\star n})
has leading order at worst
t^{0}
at ff.

\operatorname{ord}_{\mathrm{ff}}(K\star R^{\star n})\geq(-2)+n(-2)+4n=2n-2\geq 0\quad(n\geq 1).

Thus

H^{D}_{\Omega}-K=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n}K\star R^{\star n}

has order at worst
0
at ff, and since the same is true for
H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}-K
, so
H^{D}_{\Omega}-H^{D}_{\Omega^{\prime}}=O(T^{0})
at ff. The Lemma is proven. ∎

We can now prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

As in
[
10
]
, the heat trace is obtained by restricting
H^{D}_{\Omega}
to the spatial diagonal and then integrating over
z\in\Omega
. The technical tool used is Melrose’s pushforward theorem. We will not repeat all the details here, but the upshot is that each face td, sf, and ff gives a separate contribution to the expansion (
1
). A priori, the expansions can interact to give logarithmic terms, but for the same reasons as in
[
10
, p. 49]
, keeping in mind that we now have two orders at ff rather than one, there are no logarithmic terms until at least
O(T^{2}\log T)=O(t\log t)
(and potentially much later, if at all). The contribution from td is

\frac{|\Omega|}{4\pi t}+O(t^{\infty}).

The contributions from sf are

-\frac{|\partial\Omega|}{8\sqrt{\pi t}}+\frac{1}{12\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\,ds+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{256\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa^{2}\,ds+O(t).

And the contributions from ff, since the first is already known and the second depends only on
\alpha
and
\kappa_{\pm}
, are

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\pi^{2}-\alpha_{j}^{2}}{24\pi\alpha_{j}}+\sqrt{t}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha_{j},\kappa_{j,+},\kappa_{j,-})+O(t),

for some unknown function
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})
. Summing the three faces gives Theorem
1
.
∎

Note that we will perform a similar parametrix construction, in detail, in the next section, when we build a conformal model to find an expression for
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})
.

3.
The conformal model and the construction

The goal of this section is to find an expression for the contribution
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}
. Since it depends only on
\alpha
and on
\kappa_{\pm}
, we can work with a specific domain with those values. We take advantage of a conformal transformation to build such a domain, and then we construct its heat kernel.

3.1.
The conformal model domain

We define a conformal mapping from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}
. Let
w
be the complex coordinate on the target space and
z
be the complex coordinate on the source space. The Euclidean metric on the target
\mathbb{C}
is
g_{Euc}=dw\,d\bar{w}
.
Fix
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and let
\Phi=\Phi_{z_{0}}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be the holomorphic map given by

w=\Phi(z)=z+z_{0}z^{2}.

Near the origin, it is a small perturbation of the identity map.

To compute the pullback metric
\Phi^{*}(g_{Euc})
, we use the standard fact that for a holomorphic map
\Phi
,
dw=\Phi^{\prime}(z)dz
and
d\bar{w}=\overline{\Phi^{\prime}(z)}d\bar{z}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}(g_{Euc})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{*}(dw\,d\bar{w})

\displaystyle=(\Phi^{\prime}(z)dz)(\overline{\Phi^{\prime}(z)}d\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(z)\overline{\Phi^{\prime}(z)}dz\,d\bar{z}

\displaystyle=|\Phi^{\prime}(z)|^{2}dz\,d\bar{z}

Thus, the pullback metric is:

\Phi^{*}(g_{Euc})=|1+2z_{0}z|^{2}dz\,d\bar{z}.

Let
F(z)=|1+2z_{0}z|^{2}
be the conformal factor derived above. An immediate calculation yields

(7)

F(z)=1+4\Re(z_{0}z)+4|z_{0}|^{2}|z|^{2}.

If we write
z=x+iy
and
z_{0}=u_{0}+iv_{0}
, we obtain

(8)

F(x+iy)=1+4(u_{0}x-v_{0}y)+4(u_{0}^{2}+v_{0}^{2})(x^{2}+y^{2}).

On the other hand, if we write
z=re^{i\theta}
and
z_{0}=r_{0}e^{i\theta_{0}}
(this
r_{0}
matches (
2
); see (
12
), below), we obtain

(9)

F(re^{i\theta})=1+4r_{0}r\cos(\theta+\theta_{0})+4r_{0}^{2}r^{2}.

We see that
F
, however it is written, is smooth, though of course not holomorphic, from
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
. It is also smooth down to zero in polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
.

For later use, we also record the gradient of
F
at the origin:

(10)

\nabla F(0,0)=\langle 4u_{0},-4v_{0}\rangle=4r_{0}\langle\cos\theta_{0},-\sin\theta_{0}\rangle,

where the latter expression is in polar coordinates but still measuring
x
and
y
-derivatives,

We now revert to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\Omega_{0}
be the flat sector
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid 0<\arg(z)<\alpha\}
. The map
\Phi
takes
\Omega_{0}
to a curvilinear domain
\Omega
with the same corner angle
\alpha
.
The signed curvatures of the two boundary edges of
\Omega
at the corner are given by:

•

\kappa_{1}
(on the image of the
\theta=0
ray):
2\Im(z_{0})

•

\kappa_{2}
(on the image of the
\theta=\alpha
ray):
-2\Im(z_{0}e^{i\alpha})

Here the orientation is chosen so that the signed curvatures measure curvature in the inward-pointing direction, so that a convex domain will have
\kappa_{1}\geq 0
and
\kappa_{2}\geq 0
. A domain with symmetry about
\arg(z)=\alpha/2
will have
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}
.

By choosing the complex parameter
z_{0}
, we can match any desired curvatures
\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-}
, as long as
\alpha\neq\pi
. Solve the following linear system for
z_{0}=u_{0}+iv_{0}
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{+}

\displaystyle=2v_{0}

\displaystyle\kappa_{-}

\displaystyle=-2\Im((u_{0}+iv_{0})(\cos\alpha_{0}+i\sin\alpha_{0}))=-2(u_{0}\sin\alpha_{0}+v_{0}\cos\alpha_{0})

This yields
z_{0}=u_{0}+iv_{0}
, where

(11)

\begin{cases}u_{0}=&-\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{+}\cot\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{-}\csc\alpha;\\
v_{0}=&\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{+}.\end{cases}

In terms of the curvatures, we observe that

(12)

r_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\csc\alpha\sqrt{\kappa_{+}^{2}-2\kappa_{+}\kappa_{-}\cos\alpha+\kappa_{-}^{2}};\quad\theta_{0}=\textrm{arg}(u_{0}+iv_{0}).

The pullback by
\Phi
of the Euclidean metric on
\Omega
is

F\cdot g_{Eucl,\Omega_{0}},

where we now interpret
F
as a function with domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
rather than
\mathbb{C}
.
By the formula for the Laplacian in two dimensions under a conformal change of metric
g=Fg_{0}
, the new Laplacian is
\Delta_{g}=F^{-1}\Delta_{g_{0}}
. Therefore, the pullback Laplacian is

\Phi^{*}(\Delta_{\Omega})=F^{-1}\Delta_{\Omega_{0}}.

We want to understand the heat kernel for the operator
\Delta_{\Omega}
on the curved domain
\Omega
. By pulling back, this is equivalent to studying the heat kernel for the operator
F^{-1}\Delta_{\Omega_{0}}
on
\Omega_{0}
. The corresponding heat equation is
\partial_{t}H=F^{-1}\Delta_{\Omega_{0}}H
. We define our heat operator on the model space
\Omega_{0}
as:

\mathcal{L}=\partial_{t}-F^{-1}\Delta_{\Omega_{0}}.

We now construct the heat kernel for
\mathcal{L}
. We use the techniques of
[
10
]
, working at td, then at sf and ff. This corresponds to building a parametrix for the heat kernel, first at the interior diagonal, and then at the diagonal both near the boundary and near a corner. Once we have a sufficiently accurate parametrix, we iterate away the error via a Volterra series to produce the true heat kernel.

3.2.
The heat kernel at td

It is proved in
[
10
]
that the heat kernel for
\mathcal{L}
, being the pullback of the heat kernel for the Laplacian on the curvilinear polygon
\Omega
, is polyhomogeneous conormal on the double space of
[
10
]
. We also know that its full expansion at td is the same as that for the heat kernel of the operator
\Phi^{*}(\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{2}})=F^{-1}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
. This expansion may be written in terms of the (interior) boundary defining function
T=\sqrt{t}
as

T^{-2}a_{-2}+T^{-1}a_{-1}+T^{0}a_{0}+\ldots,

where
a_{-2}
,
a_{-1}
,
a_{0}
, etc. are smooth functions on the face td.
We refer to
a_{k}
as the td-coefficients of the heat kernel; in particular,
a_{0}
denotes the coefficient of order
T^{0}
at td, whose restriction along

\mathrm{td}\cap\mathrm{ff}
will determine
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}(\alpha,\kappa_{+},\kappa_{-})
.

From
[
10
]
, these functions are smooth down to ff as well. And
T
vanishes to first order at ff as well as at td. Thus the term
T^{k}a_{k}
has order
k
at ff.
1
1

1

This is the reason that the heat expansion does not pick up logarithmic terms from the pushforward theorem coming from the intersection td
\cap
ff. When we convert to densities, the

term has order
k+2
at ff and since this is not

, there are no log terms. Similar considerations rule out logarithmic terms from the other intersections.
Therefore, the only terms which are relevant to the leading and subleading terms at ff are
T^{-2}a_{-2}+T^{-1}a_{-1}
.

We now use the work of Grieser
[
6
]
to compute

and
a_{-1}
. We use his notation throughout this subsection: the coordinates are
(t,x,y)
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an interior coordinate on the first factor and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
on the second. The initial parametrix for the heat kernel is

3.3.
A parametrix for the heat kernel

We take our heat operator

and apply it to a parametrix

where

is the Dirichlet heat kernel for an exact sector of angle

Now examine

\mathcal{L}
to leading order at ff; we say that it “solves the model problem” to one order at ff. It also solves the model problem to two orders at td. And since

agrees to first order at sf with the initial parametrix

also solves the model problem to one order at sf.

Now define:

R^{(1)}:=\mathcal{L}(H^{(1)}).

If

were not correctly chosen, we would expect

to be two orders worse than

at each of td, ff, and sf, which means order

at each. However,

solves the model problem to two orders at td and one order at ff, and also one order at sf,

has leading orders given by
-2
at td,
-3
at ff, and
-3
at sf.

In order to use the composition theorem of
[

given by solving away the full expansion at td. This equals

plus a term which is order

then

at ff.
H^{(2)}-H^{(1)}
also has order
-2
at ff. Moreover,
R^{(2)}
vanishes to infinite order at td and it has leading order
-3
at both ff and sf.

By Duhamel’s principle and our usual Neumann series sum, the true heat kernel for our conformal metric is

H=H^{(2)}-H^{(2)}*R^{(2)}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}H^{(2)}*(R^{(2)})^{k}.

We are interested in finding its term of order

the terms with

(28)

\displaystyle b(R,\theta,R^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}D^{2}S\Big)\,a(R,\theta,R^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\,4R\cos(\theta+\theta_{0})\,\Delta_{R,\theta}a(R,\theta,R^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{R,\theta}\big(D^{2}S\cdot a(R,\theta,R^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\big)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(D^{2}S)\,\Delta_{R,\theta}a(R,\theta,R^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}).

Either (
44
) or (
46
) may be used.

3.4.1.
The little trace and the convolution

To find the heat trace, we first restrict our kernel to the diagonal to find the so-called “little trace”. By (
40
), the restriction to the diagonal of the
T^{-1}
coefficient of
GH_{cone}
is zero, and therefore the first term in (
28
) does not contribute to the
T^{-1}
term of interest. The only contribution comes from the second term in (
28
), and that contribution is the convolution of the leading orders of the two factors. This convolution is

-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}(t-s)^{-1}a(\frac{r}{\sqrt{t-s}},\theta,\frac{r^{\prime}}{\sqrt{t-s}},\theta^{\prime})s^{-3/2}b(\frac{r^{\prime}}{\sqrt{s}},\theta^{\prime},\frac{r^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{s}},\theta^{\prime\prime})\,r^{\prime}\,d\theta^{\prime}\,dr^{\prime}\,ds.

Switching to front face variables yields that the term of interest,
H_{\text{sub}}
, is, where
\cong
denotes having the same heat trace coefficient at order
T^{-1}
,

(47)

H_{\text{sub}}\cong-T^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}(1-\sigma)^{-1}\sigma^{-3/2}\\
\times a\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt{1-\sigma}},\theta,\frac{R^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\sigma}},\theta^{\prime}\right)b\left(\frac{R^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\sigma}},\theta^{\prime\prime}\frac{R^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{\sigma}},\theta^{\prime\prime}\right)\,R^{\prime}\,d\theta^{\prime}\,dR^{\prime}\,d\sigma.

Setting
(R^{\prime\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})=(R,\theta)
gives the little trace. If you also make the change of variables
\tilde{R}^{\prime}=R^{\prime}/\sqrt{\sigma}
, then
R^{\prime}dR^{\prime}=\sigma\tilde{R}^{\prime}d\tilde{R}^{\prime}
. Then

(48)

(\textrm{tr }H_{\text{sub}})(R,\theta)=-T^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}(1-\sigma)^{-1}\sigma^{-1/2}\\
\times a\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt{1-\sigma}},\theta,\frac{\sqrt{\sigma}\tilde{R}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\sigma}},\theta^{\prime}\right)b\left(\tilde{R}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\frac{R}{\sqrt{\sigma}},\theta\right)\,\tilde{R}^{\prime}\,d\theta^{\prime}\,d\tilde{R}^{\prime}\,d\sigma.

To find the contribution to the heat trace, we first restrict this kernel to the diagonal by setting
(R^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})=(R,\theta)
, then we integrate with respect to
r\,dr\,d\theta=T^{2}R\,dR\,d\theta
. By Melrose’s pushforward theorem (see
[
9
]
; see also
[
5
, Thm 3.10]
for a precise statement), the trace contribution is given by a
renormalized
integral. Writing down this renormalized integral yields (
4
).

4.
The case
\alpha=\pi/2

Our goal here is to compute through the
t^{1/2}
term in the short-time expansion of the heat trace of the Laplacian on a domain
\Omega
which is a half-disk of radius 1, with Dirichlet boundary conditions. This heat trace is denoted

(49)

\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{-t\Delta_{\Omega}}.

The spectrum of the Dirichlet Laplacian on
\Omega
consists of
\{j_{m,k}^{2}\}_{m,k\in\mathbb{N}}
, each with multiplicity one. This may be compared to the spectrum of the Dirichlet Laplacian on a disk
D
, which consists of all of these eigenvalues but with multiplicity two, as well as the set
\{j_{0,k}^{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, each with multiplicity one. As a result, if we let

(50)

H(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-tj_{0,k}^{2}},

then

(51)

\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{-t\Delta_{\Omega}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{-t\Delta_{D}}-\frac{1}{2}H(t).

We will take advantage of this.

The heat trace
\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{-t\Delta_{D}}
is well understood and has a full polyhomogeneous asymptotic expansion in half-integer powers as
t\to 0
. The beginning of this expansion is, from
[
4
]
,

(52)

\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{-t\Delta_{D}}=\frac{1}{4}t^{-1}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}t^{-1/2}+\frac{1}{6}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{128}t^{1/2}+O(t).

For
H(t)
, we use the asymptotics of Bessel function zeroes due to McMahon
[
3
, (10.21.19)]
:

(53)

j_{0,k}=(k-\frac{1}{4})\pi+\frac{1}{8(k-\frac{1}{4})\pi}+O(k^{-3}),

which imply

(54)

j_{0,k}^{2}=(k-\frac{1}{4})^{2}\pi^{2}+\frac{1}{4}+O(k^{-2}).

Based on this we define the function

(55)

\tilde{H}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp[-t((k-\frac{1}{4})^{2}\pi^{2}+\frac{1}{4})]

and estimate its difference with
H(t)
.

Lemma 3
.

With all notation as above, as
t\to 0
,

(56)

H(t)-\tilde{H}(t)=O(t).

This is helpful because we also have the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

As
t\to 0
,

(57)

\tilde{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}t^{-1/2}+c-\frac{1}{8\sqrt{\pi}}t^{1/2}+O(t).

Here
c
is a constant which is irrelevant to our purposes.

From these two Lemmas and (
51
) we immediately deduce this proposition.

Proposition 2
.

As
t\to 0
,

(58)

\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{-t\Delta_{\Omega}}=\frac{1}{8}t^{-1}-(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{8}+\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}})t^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{2}c+(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{256}+\frac{1}{16\sqrt{\pi}})t^{1/2}+O(t).

Remark
.

We could reverse engineer
c
from the known expansion from
[
10
]
but there is no need.

We are now in a position to state what the contribution of each corner of the half-disk to the
t^{1/2}
term is. The
t^{1/2}
term above is the sum of a curvature integral over the boundary and a pair of identical corner contributions. From
[
4
]
we see that the
\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{256}
is precisely the curvature integral. Dividing the remainder in half shows

Theorem 3
.

The contribution to the
t^{1/2}
coefficient in the heat trace of a half-disk of radius 1 from each of its two corners is

(59)

\frac{1}{32\sqrt{\pi}}t^{1/2}.

It remains only to prove the Lemmas.

4.1.
Proofs of the Lemmas

First we prove Lemma
3
.

Proof.

Rearrangement of the various expressions gives

(60)

H(t)-\tilde{H}(t)=e^{-t/4}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-t(k-\frac{1}{4})^{2}\pi^{2}}(1-e^{-t\cdot O(k^{-2})}).

Here
O(k^{-2})
is shorthand for
j_{0,k}^{2}-(k-\frac{1}{4})^{2}\pi^{2}-\frac{1}{4}
, which is bounded in absolute value by
Ck^{-2}
for some universal constant
C
. Since
k\geq 1
and
t\leq 1
is all that is relevant, we have that for
t\leq 1
,
|1-e^{-t\cdot O(k^{-2})}|\leq\tilde{C}tk^{-2}
for a universal constant
\tilde{C}
. This implies

(61)

|H(t)-\tilde{H}(t)|\leq\tilde{C}t\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-t(k-\frac{1}{4})^{2}\pi^{2}}k^{-2}.

Estimating each exponential from above by 1 yields that the right-hand side is
O(t)
as desired.
∎

Now we prove Lemma
4
.

Proof.

First write

(62)

\tilde{H}(t)=e^{-t/4}\hat{H}(t),

where

(63)

\hat{H}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp[-t(k-\frac{1}{4})^{2}\pi^{2}].

We know from [NRS] that
\mathrm{Tr}\ e^{-t\Delta_{\Omega}}
has a full polyhomogeneous asymptotic expansion, and therefore by (
51
), so does
H(t)
. By Lemma
3
,
\tilde{H}(t)
has at least a polyhomogeneous expansion up to
O(t)
, and therefore so does
\hat{H}(t)
. The upshot of this is that one may define a zeta function

(64)

\hat{\zeta}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{H}(t)t^{s-1}\,dt,

and that this zeta function, initially defined for
\Re(s)>>0
, has a meromorphic continuation at least to the half-plane
\Re(s)>-1
. Moreover, by the usual argument (agreement for
\Re(s)>>0
),

(65)

\hat{\zeta}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}((k-\frac{1}{4})\pi)^{-2s}.

But now we recognize the zeta function
\hat{\zeta}(s)
as a form of a Hurwitz zeta function. Specifically,

(66)

\hat{\zeta}(s)=\pi^{-2s}\zeta(2s,\frac{3}{4}),

where
\zeta(s,a)
is the Hurwitz zeta function. The function
\zeta(s,a)
has a single, simple pole at
s=1
, with residue 1
[
3
, 25.11(i)]
. So
\hat{\zeta}(s)
has a single, simple pole at
s=1/2
, and its residue there is
\frac{1}{2\pi}
. Thus the function

(67)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{H}(t)t^{s-1}\,dt=\Gamma(s)\hat{\zeta}(s)

has poles at
s=1/2
and also at each non-positive integer; moreover its residue at
s=1/2
is
\Gamma(1/2)/(2\pi)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}
.

We can now reverse-engineer the expansion of
\hat{H}(t)
as
t\to 0
, which is morally taking an inverse Mellin transform. We already know such a partial expansion exists up to order
O(t)
, and now we know that its coefficients are at orders
t^{-1/2}
,
t^{0}
, and higher integer powers of
t
which are absorbed in
O(t)
. The coefficient of
t^{-1/2}
is precisely the residue of
\Gamma(s)\hat{\zeta}(s)
at
1/2
, which is
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}
. So, for some constant
c
which is not relevant for our purposes,

(68)

\hat{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}t^{-1/2}+c+O(t).

Multiplying by
e^{-t/4}\sim 1-\frac{t}{4}+O(t^{2})
,

(69)

\tilde{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}t^{-1/2}+c-\frac{1}{8\sqrt{\pi}}t^{1/2}+O(t),

completing the proof.
∎
