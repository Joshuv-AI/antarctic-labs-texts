Closing paths to cycles in symmetric graphs
Martin Milanič, Đorđe Mitrović

Abstract
It was shown by Beisegel, Chudnovsky, Gurvich, Milanič, and Servatius in 2022 that every induced 2 2 -edge path in a vertex-transitive graph closes to an induced cycle. Similar results were obtained for 3-edge paths closing to cycles in edge-transitive graphs, where the cycle can be assumed to be induced if the path is induced. Motivated by these results, we consider the following problem: For a given class of graphs, determine all integers ℓ ≥ 0 \ell\geq 0 such that for every graph in the class, every path of length at most ℓ \ell closes to a cycle. We also consider the variant of the problem for induced paths closing to induced cycles. We completely solve these problems for the classes of (finite) vertex-transitive graphs, edge-transitive graphs, and edge-transitive graphs that are not stars. For all but one case of a negative answer, we provide infinite families of connected counterexamples.

Introduction

All graphs considered in this paper are finite, simple, and undirected.
An automorphism of a graph is a permutation of its vertices mapping edges to edges and non-edges to non-edges.
The relationship between existence of symmetry (i.e., a nontrivial automorphism) in a graph and its structural properties is one of the main objects of study in algebraic graph theory (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
).
The general principle is that the existence of symmetry in a graph restricts its structural properties; a trivial example of this is that vertex-transitive graphs are regular, that is, all vertices have the same degree.

These structural restrictions also have to do with the existence and relations between subgraphs of the graph in question.
In this article, the subgraphs we will be particularly interested in are paths and cycles.
There is a number of results on these particular substructures in symmetric graphs already available in the literature.
For example, in
[

Moreover, if equality is attained, the graph must be bipartite and have diameter

Preliminaries

A graph
G
is an ordered pair
(V,E)
, where
V=V(G)
is the vertex-set of

and

is the edge-set of

Given a graph

\{x,y\}\in E(G)
.
Vertices

such that

of

in

.
The number of neighbours of

is the

of

, denoted by

.
The

of a graph

is defined as

.
A graph
G
is said to be

if all the vertices have the same degree.
If this common degree is
d
, the graph is said be
d
-regular
.
We denote the
n
-vertex complete graph by
K_{n}
.
A
graph class
is a collection of graphs closed under isomorphism.

A graph
G
is said to be
bipartite
if it has a
bipartition
, that is, a partition of

into two sets of pairwise nonadjacent vertices. A
complete bipartite graph
is a bipartite graph with a bipartition
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}
such that every two vertices
u\in V_{1}
and
v\in V_{2}
are adjacent (note that there are no other edges).
For two nonnegative integers

and

, we denote by

the complete bipartite graph with a bipartition
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}
such that
|V_{1}|=m
and

.
For any

, the complete bipartite graph

is said to be a

(or simply a

).

A

is a sequence of pairwise distinct vertices

such that
\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}\in E

. If

contains

A

(x_{0},\ldots,x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell}=x_{0})
with
\ell\geq 3
such that

x_{i}\neq x_{j}
for

and

\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}\in E
for
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
.
Such a cycle

is also referred to as an
\ell
-cycle
or a

\ell
.
It will often be convenient to identify a path in a graph

with the subgraph formed by the vertices and the edges of the path, and the same for cycles.
If this subgraph is an induced subgraph of
G
,
we say that the path, respectively cycle, is

.
We say that a path

in a graph

closes to a cycle

if there exists a cycle

in

such that

is a subgraph of

.
Similarly, we say that an induced path

Given an integer

, a graph

By

Observation 2.1

Let

Then

We next recall basic concepts related to symmetries of graphs.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
A permutation of vertices
\phi\colon V\rightarrow V
is called an

of
G
if for all distinct vertices

if and only if
\{\phi(x),\phi(y)\}\in E
.
The set of all automorphisms of

.
We refer to

of

. A graph

is said to be

.

Observation 2.2
.

Graph automorphisms preserve vertex degrees. In particular, vertex-transitive graphs are regular.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
\phi\in\Aut(G)
an automorphism of
G
.
The automorphism

maps vertices to vertices, and it naturally induces a mapping from edges to edges,

It is not difficult to verify that the induced mapping is a permutation of

A graph

is said to be

if for every two edges

there exists an automorphism
\phi\in\Aut(G)
such that
\phi(e)=f
. The

of a graph

, denoted

, is a graph with vertex set

with two distinct edges

.

Observation 2.3
.

For

, permutation of

induced by

is an automorphism of

. In particular, the line graph of an edge-transitive graph is vertex-transitive.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with minimum degree at least
2
.
Then, every path in
G
of length at most
\kappa(G)
closes to a cycle.

Proof.

Let
P=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell-1},v_{\ell})
be an
\ell
-edge path in

with

. If

, then

is already a cycle, so we can assume

. As
\kappa(G)>\ell-1\geq 1
, the graph

obtained by deleting the

from

Hence, there exists a path
P^{*}=(v_{0}=w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}=v_{\ell})
in

connecting
v_{0}
and
v_{\ell}
.
Note that
P^{*}
is also a path in

. Furthermore,

and

share no edges and the only vertices they have in common are

and

.
Since

and

, we infer that

and

form a cycle

of length

By construction, this cycle contains

Since

, every

Hence, by the first part of the proof, every

-edge path in

∎

Observation 2.5
.

A useful strategy for constructing examples of paths that are not contained in any cycle is the following.
Choose a vertex

.
Suppose that it is possible to construct a path

in some order, possibly with extra vertices in between, and terminates at a non-neighbour of

Then

E(K_{n}^{\diamond})=\bigcup_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\{\{v_{i},v_{ij}\},\{v_{ij},v_{j}\},\{v_{j},v_{ji}\},\{v_{ji},v_{i}\}\}.

Note that for

As we have seen in

It turns out that this trivial obstruction is the only one, and in this section, we resolve
1

of edge-transitive graphs that are not stars.
We first make a few easy observations.

Proposition 4.3

Let

at least
2

Then, every path in

closes to a cycle.

Proof.

2.3

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G
be a connected edge-transitive graph with minimum degree at most
1
.
Then,
G
is a star graph
K_{1,n}
for some

Proof.

Suppose first that
\delta(G)=0
.
Then,
G
has an isolated vertex.
Since
G
is connected, it follows that
G
is isomorphic to
K_{1}=K_{1,0}
.

Suppose now that
\delta(G)=1
.
Then, there exists a vertex
v\in V(G)
with degree
1
. In particular,
v
has a unique neighbour in
G
, call it
u
.
If
G
contains no vertices other than
v
and

, then
G
is isomorphic to
K_{2}=K_{1,1}
.

Otherwise, let
w\in V(G)
be an arbitrary vertex of
G
distinct from
v
and
u
. As
G
is connected, it contains a path
P=(w=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=u)
from
w
to
u
.
Since
G
is also edge-transitive, there exists an automorphism
\phi
of
G
such that
\phi(\{v,u\})=\{w,x_{1}\}
.
As automorphisms preserve the degree of a vertex, it is clear that
\phi(v)\neq x_{1}
, since
x_{1}
is of degree at least
2
.
Hence,
\phi(v)=w
. In particular, the degree of
w
is equal to the degree of
v
, which is
1
.
Therefore, we conclude that every vertex of
G
distinct from
u
has degree
1
.
As
G
is connected, it follows that
G
is isomorphic to the star graph
K_{1,n}
(with
u
as its center), where
n\coloneqq|V(G)\setminus\{u\}|=|V(G)|-1
.
∎

Proof.

5
Induced paths closing to induced cycles in vertex-transitive graphs

From now on, we focus on
2
, a variant of
1
where both the paths and the cycles under consideration need to be induced subgraphs of the given graph.
In this section, we resolve
2
for the class of vertex-transitive graphs.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
G
be a connected vertex-transitive graph with
|V(G)|\geq 3
.
Then, every induced path in
G
of length at most
2
closes to an induced cycle.

Proof.

By
3.2
, it follows that every induced
0
-edge path (i.e., vertex), as well as every induced
1
-edge path (i.e., edge) closes on a cycle.
As the shortest cycle among all cycles in
G
that pass through a given vertex or a given edge is necessarily induced, the conclusion follows for induced paths of length at most
1
. The fact that the same holds for induced
2
-edge paths is precisely the statement of
1.2
.
∎

It turns out that the bound on the length of induced path in
5.1
is best possible.
In order to prove this, we first recall the following result from the literature.

Lemma 5.2

(
[
6
, Lemma 13]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
L(G)
be its line graph.
Then, the following statements hold.

(a)

Let
P
be a path of length
\ell\geq 1
in
G
and let
P^{\prime}
be the sequence of edges of
P
along the path. Then
P^{\prime}
is an induced path of length
\ell-1
in
L(G)
.

(b)

Let
C^{\prime}
be an induced cycle of length at least four in
L(G)
. Then, the sequence of vertices of
C^{\prime}
along the cycle yields a sequence of edges of
G
that forms a cycle
C
in
G
.

We can now prove the following.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\ell\geq 3
be an integer and let
G
be a connected edge-transitive graph containing a path
P
of length
\ell
that does not close to a cycle.
Let
L(G)
be the line graph of
G
and let
P^{\prime}
be the sequence of edges of
P
along the path.
Then
L(G)
is a vertex-transitive graph and
P^{\prime}
is an induced path of length
\ell-1
in
L(G)
that does not close to an induced cycle.

Proof.

We have already remarked in
2.3
that, if
G
is edge-transitive, then
L(G)
is vertex-transitive.
That
P^{\prime}
is an induced
(\ell-1)
-edge path in

follows from
5.2
(
a
). Assume that
P^{\prime}
lies on an induced cycle
C^{\prime}
in
L(G)
. As
P^{\prime}
is of length
\ell-1\geq 2
and induced in

, it follows that
C^{\prime}
is of length at least four.
By
5.2
(
b
), the sequence of vertices of
C^{\prime}
along the cycle yields a sequence of edges of
G
forming a cycle
C
, which contains
P
(since
C^{\prime}
contains
P^{\prime}
).
This is a contradiction, as it was assumed that
P
does not close to a cycle.
Hence,
P^{\prime}
does not close to an induced cycle in

.
∎

6.1
Excluding star graphs

As discussed previously, star graphs pose an obstruction to extending
1.3
to shorter induced paths in edge-transitive graphs. As it turns out, quite similarly to
1.6
, this trivial obstruction is the only one.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
G
be a connected edge-transitive graph that is not a star.
Then every induced path of length at most
3
in
G
closes to an induced cycle.

Proof.

Since
G
is assumed not to be a star, it follows by
4.4
that the minimum degree
\delta(G)
of
G
is at least
2
.
In particular, every induced
0
-edge path (i.e., vertex) closes to an induced
1
-edge path (i.e., edge).
By
1.6
, every
1
-edge path closes to a cycle.
A cycle of minimum length through a given edge must be induced, establishing the claim for (induced) paths of length
0
or
1
.

We now prove the claim for induced
2
-edge paths. Let
P=(x,y,z)
be an induced
2
-edge path in
G
. Note that since
P
is induced, it follows that
x
and
z
are nonadjacent.

If
G
is vertex-transitive, the conclusion follows by
1.2
.
Hence, we can assume that
G
is not vertex-transitive.
2.1
now tells us that
G
is bipartite.
Since the minimum degree of
G
is at least
2
, the vertex
x
has a neighbour
x^{\prime}\in V(G)\setminus V(P)
.
Note that
x^{\prime}
is not adjacent to
y
, as in this case
(x^{\prime},x,y,x^{\prime})
would be a
3
-cycle in
G
, a contradiction with
G
being bipartite.
If
x^{\prime}
is adjacent to
z
,
(x^{\prime},x,y,z,x^{\prime})
is an induced
4
-cycle containing
P
, and we are done.

The final case to consider is when
x^{\prime}
is not adjacent to
z
.
It follows that the
3
-edge path
P^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},x,y,z)
containing
P
is an induced path in
G
.
By
1.3
, the path
P^{\prime}
closes to an induced cycle.
This induced cycle also contains
P
.

Finally, the fact that this claim holds for
3
-induced paths is precisely the statement of
1.3
.
∎
