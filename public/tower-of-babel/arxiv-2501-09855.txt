So long Kolmogorov: the forward and backward turbulence cascades in a supernovae-driven, multiphase interstellar medium Facilities: This research was undertaken with the assistance of resources from the National Computational Infrastructure (NCI Australia), an NCRIS enabled capability supported by the Australian Government.
James R. Beattie †,⋆, Anne Noer Kolborg ⋆, Enrico Ramirez-Ruiz, Christoph Federrath

Abstract
The interstellar medium (ISM) of disk galaxies is turbulent, and yet the fundamental nature of ISM turbulence, the energy cascade, is not understood in detail. In this study, we use high-resolution simulations of a hydrodynamical, gravitationally stratified, supernova (SNe)-driven, multiphase ISM to probe the nature of a galactic turbulence cascade. Through the use of velocity flux transfer functions split into interactions between compressible 𝒖 c \bm{u}_{c} and incompressible 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} modes, we show that there exists a large-to-small-scale cascade in both 𝒖 c \bm{u}_{c} and 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} when mediated by an additional 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} mode. But the 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} cascade is highly non-local. Moreover, there is a 𝒖 c \bm{u}_{c} mediated component of the 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} cascade that proceeds in the opposite direction – an inverse cascade from small-to-large scales. The cascade feeds flux into scales well beyond the scale height, energizing the winds and fueling the direct cascades. Both the strongly non-local and the inverse 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} cascades happen on scales that have a power law 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} energy spectrum, highlighting how degenerate the spectrum is to the true underlying physical processes. We directly show that the inverse cascade comes from 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} modes interacting with expanding SNe remnants (SNRs) and that 𝒖 s \bm{u}_{s} modes are generated to leading order via baroclinic, highly corrugated cooling layers between warm ( T ≲ 10 4 ​ K ) (T\lesssim 10^{4}\,\rm{K}) and hot ( T ≫ 10 4 ​ K ) (T\gg 10^{4}\,\rm{K}) gas in these SNRs. Finally, we outline a complete phenomenology for SNe-driven turbulence in a galactic disk, estimate a 10 − 16 ​ G 10^{-16}\,\rm{G} Biermann field generated from SNR cooling layers, and highlight the strong deviations that SNe-driven turbulence has from the conventional Kolmogorov model.

II.1.3
Supernova driving

Energy, mass and momentum are injected into the medium via core collapse SNe detonations. The injection is modeled using the sub-grid model in
Martizzi et al. [112]
. The model takes into account ambient
\rho
and
Z
in the local medium where a SNe detonates and partitions the total energy,
E_{\rm SNe}=10^{51}\,\rm{erg}
into (radial) momentum,
\bm{p}_{\rm SNe}
and thermal energy,
E_{\rm th,SNe}
. Each SNe ejects
M_{\rm ej}=$6.8\text{\,}{\rm M_{\odot}}$
new material, including
Z_{i}
, into the ISM, and as the shock wave expands through the medium it continuously sweeps material
M_{\rm swept}
.
M_{\rm ej}
,
\bm{p}_{\rm SNe}
and
E_{\rm th,SNe}
are deposited over a region of size
\ell_{\rm inj}=2\operatorname{d}\!{x}
, fixed for all
N_{\rm grid}
. This means that the SNe are detonated on progressively smaller physical scales as we go to higher
N_{\rm grid}
. This is, of course, a degree of freedom, in that we could have fixed
\ell_{\rm inj}
to a physical scale, but in reality the SNe will be detonated on scales well below any of the resolved scales (
\operatorname{d}\!{x}\sim 1\;\rm{pc}
at the highest resolutions), so this is indeed well justified, but does mean our energy injection scale shifts as we go to higher
N_{\rm grid}
. We perform convergence studies on the spectra in
Appendix F
to ensure that nothing peculiar happens to the turbulence between the different
N_{\rm grid}
. The volumetric rate of SNe,
\dot{n}_{\rm SNe}
is given,

\dot{n}_{\rm SNe}=\frac{\dot{\Sigma}_{*}}{2z_{\rm eff}100\,{\rm M_{\odot}}},

(9)

where
\dot{\Sigma}_{*}\propto\Sigma_{\rm gas}^{1.4}
is the surface density of star formation
[
82
,
83
]
. This results in one SNe occurring for every
100\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
stars formed.

The positions of a SNe is chosen at random within a fixed volume. Parallel to the gravitational potential, the distribution function or SNe positions is,

\displaystyle p(z)=\left\{\begin{matrix}1/(2z_{\rm eff}),&|z|\leq z_{\rm eff},\\
0,&|z|>z_{\rm eff},\\
\end{matrix}\right.

(10)

i.e.,the SNe have an equal probability of happening anywhere within
z\pm z_{\rm eff}
of the disk midplane and zero probability outside this region. In the perpendicular direction (i.e.,in the disk plane) the SNe positions follow a uniform distribution. This is a very simple prescription, but we intend to explore other schemes in future works.

II.1.4
Heating and cooling

We employ a heating and cooling model to capture the multiphase nature of the ISM
[
115
,
157
]
. The model is based on the microphysical heating and cooling prescriptions studied in
Theuns et al. [150]
and
Sutherland & Dopita [147]

\Lambda
has both cooling
\mathcal{C}
and heating
\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\Lambda(n_{\rm H},Z)=\mathcal{H}(n_{\rm H})-\mathcal{C}(n_{\rm H},Z).

(11)

The model solves a time-dependent chemical network consisting of HI, HII, HeI, HeII, HeIII and free electrons. The solution to this network forms the basis for computing
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{H}
is due solely to photoheating, which is the excess energy in free electrons that have been ejected from HI, HeI and HeII atoms,

\mathcal{H}=\left(n_{\rm HI}\epsilon_{\gamma\rm HI}+n_{\rm HeI}\epsilon_{\gamma\rm HeI}+n_{\rm HeII}\epsilon_{\gamma\rm HeII}\right)/n_{\rm H}^{2},

(12)

where the photo-heating rates,
\epsilon_{\gamma}
, are those reported in Table B4 of
Theuns et al. [150]
and depend on the rate of photoionization by the background power-law UV spectrum,
J(\nu)
(see Equation B11 of
Theuns et al. 150
). The total cooling rate,
\mathcal{C}
, is given by

\mathcal{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{10}c_{i}(T,n_{\rm H})+c_{\rm metal}(T,n_{\rm H},Z),

(13)

where we sum over each of five physical processes: collisional ionization (HI, HeI and HeII); recombination (HII, HeII, HeIII); dielectronic recombination (HeII); collisional excitation (HI and HeII); and, Bremsstrahlung (HII, HeII and HeIII). We use the rates from Appendix B1 of
Theuns et al. [150]
. However, our cooling model neglects the inverse Compton cooling term, which is included in the original
Theuns et al. [150]
model. Furthermore, we supplement the
Theuns et al. [150]
model with cooling due to metal line emission,
c_{\rm metal}
, following the model from
Sutherland & Dopita [147]
.
Z
is sourced by the injection of metals from SNe, as we indicated in
Equation 7
. As discussed in
Karpov et al. [80]
, SNe injection will not create a solar abundance pattern, and in reality, one needs many additional metal production channels, which we do not take into account in our model.

II.2
Characteristic scales,

dimensionless numbers and stationarity

In this section, we describe the characteristic velocities, length scales, and time scales of the simulation. We first calculate the (instantaneous) global average turbulent Mach number,

\mathcal{M}=\left\langle{\left(\frac{u}{c_{s}}\right)^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2},

(14)

given by the ratio between the dispersion of the local nonthermal, turbulent (rest-frame) velocities
\left\langle{u^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2}
and thermal velocity dispersion,
\left\langle{c_{s}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2}
. Furthermore, it is useful to consider the measured scale height of the gaseous disk (rather than
z_{\rm eff}
, which is the analytical steady state, not including SNe contributions)
\ell_{0}
, such that
\rho(z)\propto\exp\left(-|z|/\ell_{0}\right)
. With our cooling function, as well as the SNe detonation and turbulence, it makes the analytical equilibrium profile and
\ell_{0}
challenging to derive; hence, we resort to numerical means for computing this scale. We directly fit an exponential model to the steady state data and measure
\ell_{0}
, and then average it over time. From
\left\langle u^{2}\right\rangle^{1/2}_{\V}
and
\ell_{0}
we define the characteristic time scale of the simulation, the turbulent turnover time at
\ell_{0}
,

t_{0}=\frac{\ell_{0}}{\langle u^{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}^{1/2}}.

(15)

We will use
\mathcal{M}
,
\ell_{0}
and
t_{0}
regularly throughout the study. This is, in many ways, analogous to the non-dimensionalizations used regularly in turbulence box simulations
[
15
, e.g.,]
. We do, however, note that the
\ell_{0}
we compute above is not the outer scale of the turbulence, which we compute independently below in
Section IV
, directly from the velocity spectrum.

Because we are using a second-order spatial reconstruction method, we can directly estimate the hydrodynamical Reynolds number utilizing numerical dissipation relations in
Shivakumar & Federrath [144]
. For our
N_{\rm grid}
we get
{\rm{Re}}\sim 10^{4}
on the outer scale, which maps to the
{\rm{Re}}
of the transonic, volume-filling WIM
(T\sim 10^{4}\,\rm{K})
in our simulations. At these
{\rm{Re}}
, we do resolve the HIM
({\rm{Re}}\sim 10^{2})
, which is relatively viscous because of the high temperatures,
T\sim 10^{6}\;\rm{K}
, and low electron densities (
n_{e}\sim 10^{-3}
), leading from the fact that
{{\rm{Re}}}\propto n_{e}T^{-5/2}

[
51
]
. However, for the WIM and WNM, which are the volume-filling phases in our simulation, this is a few orders of magnitude away from a realistic
{\rm{Re}}
, with
{\rm{Re}}\sim 10^{7}
. However, at
{\rm{Re}}\sim 10^{4}
we will still have a turbulent warm phase that ought to have at least some of the cascade resolved (indeed we see this is the case in
Section V
), at least when we compare to turbulent box simulations with the same order spatial reconstruction method
[
43
, e.g.,]
. Based on the required
{\rm{Re}}
, to get a completely resolved WIM we would need to go to grid resolutions of
N_{\rm grid}\gtrsim 10,\!000
(as in
Federrath et al. 48
and
Beattie et al. 11
) and above, which would be an exciting direction for future work.

The early time in the simulation is characterized by relaxation from the initial conditions – as the gas cools it collapses in
\bm{\nabla}\phi
; simultaneously, feedback from SNe turns on injecting mass, momentum and energy into the disk
[
88
,
89
]
. These injections drive turbulence in the ISM and contribute to pressure support of the gas (via
\bm{\nabla}u^{2}
) in
\bm{\nabla}\phi
. Eventually the disk settles into a statistically steady-state where

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}+P\mathbb{I}\right)\right\rangle_{\V}=

\displaystyle-\left\langle\rho\bm{\nabla}\phi\right\rangle_{\V}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\dot{n}_{\rm SNe}\bm{p}_{\rm SNe}(Z,n_{H})\right\rangle_{\V},

(16)

in which a stationary turbulent cascade forms, e.g.,
\varepsilon_{\rm injection}=\varepsilon_{\rm dissipation}

[
107
]
, where
\varepsilon_{\rm injection}
is the flux from the SNe injection, and
\varepsilon_{\rm dissipation}
is the dissipated flux, which is both a function of
Equation 12
and the numerical dissipation from the discretisation, which we discuss in detail in
Section IV
and
Section V
.

derived from observations of the volume-filling, warm-ionized medium in the ISM
[

,
51
,

,

]
. Compared to Fourier driven turbulent boxes
[
43
,
16

,

has many more intermittent events, associated with sudden intense detonations of SNe
[
88
]
. However, the intensity of these fluctuations decreases as we go from
N_{\rm grid}=256
to
N_{\rm grid}=1,\!024
, most likely due to the reduction of the volume-filling factor for the initial SNe seeds. In
Section V.5
, we will later explore the effect (the time variability, not

) that this has on the
k
space statistics, including the velocity flux in the velocity cascade. Using our exponential profile model, in this state we find

III
Velocity structure

& Helmholtz Decomposition

Critical to our analysis of the turbulent cascade is the nature of the velocity modes. We decompose
\bm{u}
into compressible
\bm{u}_{c}
(
|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}_{c}|=0
) and incompressible
\bm{u}_{s}
(
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}_{s}=0
) modes, using a Helmholtz decomposition of the field, such that,

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bm{u}_{c}+\bm{u}_{s},\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{u}_{c}\cdot\bm{u}_{s}=0.

(17)

We do this in Fourier space, where

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{c}(\bm{\ell})=\int\operatorname{d}\!{\bm{k}}\;\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{k})}{k^{2}}\bm{k}\exp\left\{2\pi i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\ell}\right\},

(18)

is the inverse Fourier transform of the Fourier transformed velocity
\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{k})
projected along the wave vector
\bm{k}=2\pi/\bm{\ell}
, where
\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{k})
is defined,

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{N^{3}_{\rm grid}}\int\operatorname{d}\!{\bm{\ell}}\;\bm{u}(\bm{x})\exp\left\{-2\pi i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\ell}\right\}.

(19)

and
Equation 17
is used to calculate
\bm{u}_{s}
. In
Figure 4
we plot the mean normalized gas density,
\rho/\left\langle{\rho}\right\rangle_{\V}
(top row), and the rms normalized magnitudes,
u_{s}/\left\langle{u_{s}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2}
(middle row) and
u_{c}/\left\langle{u_{c}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2}
(bottom row), components of velocity. In the left-hand column we show these fields at a time shortly after the beginning of the simulation,
t/t_{0}=4
, where the simulation is yet to form a time-stationary state (i.e.,
Section II.2
). During this time it is straightforward to pick out the regions influenced by individual SNR, which are spheres of large density gradients around low-density interiors. It is also clear to see that the interiors of SNRs are closely associated with
\bm{u}_{s}
modes, while the SNe shock fronts are associated with
\bm{u}_{c}
modes. In the middle and right-hand column we show two-dimensional slices in the steady state (see
Figure 3
),
t/t_{0}=30
and
t/t_{0}=37
, respectively. The density has collapsed in the
\bm{\nabla}\phi
direction, leading to a steeper gradient parallel to
\bm{\nabla}\phi
, resulting in the disk being supported by both thermal,
\bm{\nabla}P
, and turbulent,
\bm{\nabla}u^{2}
, pressure gradients. The effects of multiple, interacting SNRs become apparent for these
t/t_{0}
, and turbulent velocity modes have developed over the entire simulation domain (see the middle row). Having gained a qualitative understanding of the velocity structures in our simulated ISM we now turn our attention to quantitative methods to dissect the energy spectrum and
k
space velocity flux transfer, beginning with the velocity power spectrum.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{u}(\bm{k})=|\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{k})\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})|,

(20)

where
\square^{\dagger}
denotes the complex conjugate. Due to the acceleration from
\bm{\nabla}\phi
, there is a global anisotropy in the velocity field which we must account for in the analysis of the spectra. Therefore we integrate the spectra into cylindrical wave numbers, which are defined as follows. Following the geometry of the galaxy disk we define two length scales,
\ell_{\parallel}
and
\ell_{\perp}
. The first, parallel to
\bm{\nabla}\phi

\ell_{\parallel}^{-1}\sim k_{\parallel}=|k_{z}|

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{u}(k_{\parallel},k_{\perp})=\int\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega_{k_{\perp}}}\;\mathcal{P}_{u}(\bm{k})\,2\pi k_{\perp},

(21)

where
\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega_{k_{\perp}}}

\mathcal{P}_{u}(k_{j})=\int\operatorname{d}\!{k_{i}}\;\mathcal{P}_{u}(k_{i},k_{j}),

(22)

where all of our integrals conserve the total power in the field such that

\displaystyle\left\langle{u^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}=\int\operatorname{d}\!{k}\;\mathcal{P}_{u}(k),

(23)

\frac{\ell_{\mathrm{cor},i}}{L}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{d}\!{k_{i}}\;(kL/2\pi)^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{u}(k_{i})}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{d}\!{k_{i}}\;\mathcal{P}_{u}(k_{i})}.

In fact, it seems like the compressible modes, in general, are undergoing much more local cascades, which is completely at odds with the conventional wisdom for supersonic turbulence.

We leave the magnetized case for a future endeavor, but what we learn from our study is there are still a lot of unknowns to be discovered and made more precise, even in the hydrodynamical case.

VI
What fuels the incompressible cascades in supernova-driven turbulence?

In
Figure 8
we showed that
\bm{u}_{c}
interactions can transport energy from small scales to large scales, which can then be parasitized upon by low
k
modes through other flux transfers. Regardless of how efficient this process is, we need some way of turning
\bm{u}_{c}
modes generated by SNe into
\bm{u}_{s}
modes to fuel the incompressible cascades. As we derived in
Section V
, the interaction that creates the
\bm{u}_{s}\rightarrow\bm{u}_{s}
flux transfer is from
\bm{u}\cdot\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}
. We can expand this into two further terms,
\bm{u}\cdot\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}=(1/2)\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}u^{2}-\bm{u}\cdot(\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega})
, where
\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}
is the Lamb vector. Hence, by exploring
\bm{\omega}
we are able to probe at least part of the turbulence generated via
\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}
. The simplest way of determining
\bm{\omega}
sources is by directly taking the curl of
Equation 4
. It is,

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bm{\omega}}}{\operatorname{d}\!{t}}=

\displaystyle\overbrace{-\bm{\omega}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})}^{\text{compression}}+\underbrace{\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}}_{\text{stretching}}+\overbrace{\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P}^{\text{baroclinicity}},

(38)

where
\operatorname{d}\!{}/\operatorname{d}\!{t}=\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}
is the Lagrangian derivative,
\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\nabla}\phi=0
by definition, and similarly, by definition, for the point-source supernova term in
Equation 4
.

The first term
\bm{\omega}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})
is the vortex compression term, which we associate with the post-shock regions from the supernova detonations. The third term
(1/\rho^{2})\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P
is the baroclinic term, which is a battery term for
\bm{\omega}
, generated between misaligned pressure
\bm{\nabla}P
and gas density
\bm{\nabla}\rho
gradients. Misalignment may occur through interacting supernova shells, where the
\bm{\nabla}P
from one shell is not aligned with the
\bm{\nabla}\rho
from a neighboring shell, or even in the simpler case, where there is any obliqueness within a single expanding compressible wave (e.g., a corrugated shock wave or an expanding cooling layer), which is always the case. In fact, any kind of phase mixing may also excite the baroclinic term. This is similar to the Biermann battery effect (only the proportionality constant is different;
McKee et al. 117
) for generating astrophysical magnetic fields in collisionless cosmic shocks
[
20
]
. Finally, for an incompressible fluid,
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=0
, the second term
\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}
is the vortex stretching term, which we associate with vorticity structures interacting with a background shear flow. However, our fluid is highly compressible, and hence we must decompose
\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}
into components to extract only the (volume-preserving) stretching terms to make this equivalent to the incompressible vortex stretching operator
[
137
]
. Performing the same
\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}
decomposition as in
Beattie et al. [12]
, the vorticity equation becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bm{\omega}}}{\operatorname{d}\!{t}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\bm{\omega}}{3}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})+\bm{\omega}\cdot(\mathbb{S}+\mathbb{A})+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P,

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}+[\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}]^{T}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{I}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u},

(40)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}-[\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}]^{T}\right),

(41)

where
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{A}
are the rate of strain and rate of rotation tensors, respectively, and
[\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}]^{T}
indicates the transpose. It is straightforward to show that
\mathbb{A}
only changes the unit vector of
\bm{\omega}
, since the enstrophy equation (for
\omega^{2}
) gives rise to a term
\bm{\omega}\otimes\bm{\omega}:\mathbb{A}
, which is identically zero because
\bm{\omega}\otimes\bm{\omega}
is a symmetric tensor and
\mathbb{A}
is antisymmetric, completely analogous to the turbulent dynamo process for a magnetic field embedded in a turbulent medium
[
12
]
. Under this decomposition, the vortex compression term is modified to
-2\bm{\omega}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})/3
, and the stretching term is simply
\sim\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbb{S}
, no longer contaminated with compressions, and maintaining the vortex stretching definition as in the
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=0
regime.

VII
Summary, conclusions and limitations

In this study, we perform a detailed analysis of the multiphase, hydrodynamical turbulence born from detonations of supernovae in a stratified disk undergoing cooling and heating through a time-dependent chemical network and the adiabatic expansions of the gas. In particular, we focus a lot of our efforts on the transfer of velocity flux through the nonlinear advection term
u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{j}u_{i}
in the momentum equation, which defines both the compressible and incompressible mode turbulence cascades. We do this using velocity flux transfer functions, which allow us to compute all three mode interactions in
u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{j}u_{i}
, split into incompressible
\bm{u}_{s}
and compressible
\bm{u}_{c}
modes. In this summary, we will focus on synthesizing the physical picture that has emerged from the analysis of our simulation for how supernova-driven turbulence works in a disk of a galaxy. This will have missing physics, such as rotation, magnetized turbulence and dynamo, and cold plasma phases, but our main goal was always to gather a simple physical picture of supernova-driven turbulence, which we hope can help understand the nature of the turbulence in our own Galaxy, at least in the volume-filling warm phases.

VII.1
A supernova-driven turbulence phenomenology

Let us put together everything that we have learnt in this study into a coherent narrative for the nature of SNe-driven turbulence. SNe-driven turbulence injects compressible modes,
\bm{u}_{c}
, on the smallest scales in the system, i.e.,it is compressibly-driven turbulence. As the SNe detonate they heat the gas to
T\gg 10^{4}\,\rm{K}
and as they expand into the WIM (
T\sim 10^{4}\,\rm{K}
) they cool adiabatically, creating corrugated cooling layers at
\ell\approx R_{\rm cool}
, the cooling radius of the SNe. Due to the corrugation,
|\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P|\gg 1
, which intensely generates incompressible, vortical modes,
\bm{u}_{s}
, across a broad spectrum of
k
modes determined by the fractal nature of the layer (see
Figure 14
). These modes energize the scales below the gaseous scale height,
\ell_{0}
, peaking at
\approx R_{\rm cool}
, such that almost 90% of the energy is in
\bm{u}_{s}
on these scales. On scales beyond
R_{\rm cool}
, the expanding SNRs facilitate an inverse cascade via the three-mode interaction,
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{s}
, that brings
\bm{u}_{s}
modes up to scales well beyond
\ell_{0}
, e.g., into the galactic winds and throughout the disk. At the same time, the post-shock regions have relatively local, direct cascades,
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{s}}\bm{u}_{s}
, that transport velocity flux down to smaller scales. On the large scales, the
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{s}}\bm{u}_{s}
cascade is highly non-local, with
\bm{u}_{s}
modes more or less coupling across all scales, meaning energy from
\bm{u}_{s}
is deposited directly from the large scales (
1\,\rm{kpc}
) to the small scales (
10\,\rm{pc}
), without undergoing a
Kolmogorov [91]
-style cascade. The compressible modes from the expanding SNRs interact with one another through the three-mode interaction
\bm{u}_{c}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{c}
, which gives rise to a relatively non-local direct transfer of energy from large to small scales, perhaps resembling the non-local
Burgers [26]
-type turbulence. However, there is a
\bm{u}_{c}
cascade that is very local across all scales through the
\bm{u}_{c}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{s}}\bm{u}_{c}
interaction, perhaps scattering the
\bm{u}_{c}
modes down the
\bm{u}_{s}
-dominated galactic winds and post-shocked regions. By this time, fully developed turbulent spectra have been established in the medium, albeit with significant time variability associated with the strong SNe detonation events. The spectra are
\mathcal{P}_{u_{c}}(k)\sim k^{-2}
for the compressible modes and
\mathcal{P}_{u_{s}}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
for the incompressible. Over the range of scales for which these power laws hold, the
\bm{u}_{c}
modes are undergoing both local
\bm{u}_{c}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{s}}\bm{u}_{c}
and non-local
\bm{u}_{c}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{c}
cascades, whereas the
\bm{u}_{s}
modes are undergoing
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{s}
inverse and
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{s}}\bm{u}_{s}
non-local cascades. Hence, neither of the incompressible cascades in SNe-driven turbulence are consistent with the
[
91
]
phenomenology of turbulence.

Finally, it is worth highlighting that in the phenomenology we have described, whether it be by bringing energy from the small-to-large-scales via an inverse cascade, or generating vorticity via the baroclinic term, the compressible modes play a critical and vital role in a SNe-driven medium, and are not passive in any of the processes, which has been the conventional wisdom of the magnetohydrodynamic ISM community
[
108
]
. Indeed, our results suggest that the multiphase ISM turbulence ecosystem relies critically on both the incompressible and compressible modes playing different but very important roles in creating and maintaining the turbulent fluctuations and cascades.

We now itemize some of the key results from this study:

•

We drive a stratified ISM into stationarity with SNe detonations happening at the grid scale, resulting in
\mathcal{M}=1.75\pm 0.05
(
Figure 3
). The SNe and our time-dependent cooling network give rise to a multiphase ISM, with a stable phase at
T\approx 10^{4}\,\rm{K}
(
Figure 2
). Regardless, the gas is able to cool to
\lesssim 10^{2}\,\rm{K}
through the adiabatic expansion of the disk, as shown with the blue adiabat in
Figure 2
. Based on the velocity dispersion,
\left\langle{u^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2}=28\pm 6\,\rm{km\,s^{-1}}
, and gaseous scale-height,
\ell_{0}=85\pm 6\,\rm{pc}
, we calculate a turbulent turnover time on
\ell_{0}
as
t_{0}=2.9\,\rm{Myr}
.

•

We decompose the velocity field into incompressible
\bm{u}_{s}
and compressible
\bm{u}_{c}
modes using the Helmholtz decomposition, and with this directly calculate cylindrically (
\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}
, with respect to the gravitational potential gradient,
\bm{\nabla}\phi
) summed energy spectra
\mathcal{P}_{u}(k)
for each type of mode (
Figure 5
). We find
\mathcal{P}_{u_{s}}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
and
\mathcal{P}_{u_{c}}(k)\sim k^{-2}
, regardless of the direction, indicating that the turbulence is rather isotropic. By computing the ratio between the spectra we find that the turbulence is everywhere dominated by
\bm{u}_{s}
modes, and particularly on modes
k>\ell^{-1}_{0}
, i.e.,on scales below the scale-height, peaking at the estimates SNe cooling radius
R_{\rm cool}
. On these scales,
\bm{u}_{s}
is more dominant than what even local turbulent boxes driven completely with solenoidal modes can achieve. We show that the correlation scales of the turbulence are
\sim 4\ell_{0}
, indicating that the turbulence is correlated well into the galactic winds.

•

Using spectral transfer functions for the
\bm{u}_{c}
and
\bm{u}_{s}
kinetic flux we investigate four different cascades in two different directions generated from the
u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{j}u_{i}
nonlinearity (
Equation 29
-
Equation 32
), shown via shell-to-shell transfers in

. Similar to the power spectrum, there is not a significant difference between the two directions. The
\bm{u}_{c}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{s}}\bm{u}_{c}
is a highly-local, direct cascade,
\bm{u}_{c}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{c}
is a more non-local, direct cascade, potentially due to a mechanism like
Burgers [26]
-type turbulence.
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{s}}\bm{u}_{s}
is a highly non-local, direct cascade, specifically at scales where
\mathcal{P}_{u_{s}}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
and
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{s}
is a mixture of inverse cascade on the scales, where
\mathcal{P}_{u_{s}}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
and a direct cascade at smaller scales. The cascades are roughly split at the SNe cooling radius. The cascade varies significantly in total velocity flux, length and even direction when sampled across multiple realizations in the steady state (
Figure 10
).

•

By visualizing the velocity flux everywhere in space (
Figure 9
) we reveal that there are simultaneous inverse and direct cascades happening everywhere in the
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{s}
transfer of velocity flux. Specifically, we identify strong sources of inverse cascades as expanding SNRs that advect
\bm{u}_{s}
modes to lower
k
, larger
\ell

\bm{u}_{c}
modes in the winds, beyond the gaseous scale height of the disk.

•

We further show that the dominant source of incompressible modes in SNe-driven turbulence is from baroclinicity in fractal cooling layers in the SNRs (
Figure 11
and
Figure 12
). This is the corrugated layer between the hot,
T\gtrsim 10^{4}\,\rm{K}
SNR interior and warm
T\sim 10^{4}\,\rm{K}
surrounding ISM, shown very clearly in
Figure 13
. We hypothesize that the cooling layer generates the
\bm{u}_{s}
modes that are then taken to the large scales through the
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{s}
inverse cascade mechanism, which contributes to feeding
\bm{u}_{s}
modes into the winds and also the direct cascades. We use the measured
\omega/t_{0}^{-1}
and WIM plasma parameters to directly estimate the Biermann field that the cooling layer would generate (
Equation 43
), which is
B\sim 10^{-16}\,\rm{G}
– strong seed magnetic field that could contribute to magnetizing the galaxy and surrounding medium when coupled to other processes, like the turbulent dynamo.

VII.2
Limitations of the numerical model

In
Section II
and in the current section we discuss the limitations of the simulations presented in this study.

Background shear profile

Firstly, we want to stress that our models are simplistic descriptions of galactic SNe-driven turbulence, with the aim of understanding the nature of SNe-driven turbulence in a stratified disk. Unlike our Galaxy, the disk in our model does not have a velocity and hence shear profile
[
74
]
. However, this means we are more easily able to probe vorticity generation directly from the SNe, which is desirable for our study.

Stable cold phase gas

Further, our cooling function,
\Lambda
, is mostly truncated at
T\approx 10^{4}\,\rm{K}
, as we show in
Figure 1
. This means we do not form cold gas through
\Lambda
(e.g., through far-infrared fine-structure lines, low-
J
molecular transitions, or dust). Instead, the
T\lesssim 10^{4}\,\rm{K}
gas, which is the bulk of the gas in the disk, settling to
T\approx 4\times 10^{3}\,\rm{K}
, creates a WNM phase analogue through macroscopic processes (e.g., adiabatic expansion) rather than through
\Lambda
. This mechanism significantly contributes to the formation of colder gas in an unstable WNM, which is very strongly influenced by the nature of the turbulence itself (e.g., in volume-filling factor), as
Hu [72]
shows in their Figure 2, and as
Connor et al. [36]
shows by modeling the sound speed spectrum.

In a medium with an even colder molecular (
T\approx 10\,\rm{K}
) phase, the cold gas will condense, even in the absence of self-gravity
[
53
]
. However, as
Fielding et al. [53]
shows, the turbulent kinetic energy spectrum does not significantly differ in a simulation in which this phase is formed via cooling, versus an isothermal approximation (their Figure 4, top panels), with the turbulence organizing itself in
k
-space in a universal manner. The cold clumps that form from condensing ISM plasmas (or gases) are typically of order 0.1-10
\rm{pc}

[
129
]
, which, in our simulations, are at the numerical diffusion limit for our uniform grids or less. Even though it is unlikely that the cold phase gas will change the spectral slopes, if the galaxy models are able to produce a molecular cold phase, there will be more phase interfaces to create
\bm{\nabla}P\times\bm{\nabla}\rho
, which promotes incompressible turbulence generation, as we showed in
Section VI
. This means the
\bm{\omega}
generation (and hence the Biermann field) are only lower limits of what one may expect from phase mixing in SNRs alone. With extremely high-resolution simulations of single SNe,
Guo et al. [63]
showed that the internal structure of a SNR can host many phases (indeed, many pioneering ideas about the multiphase ISM were developed specifically in terms of SNe forming all phases of the ISM;
McKee & Ostriker 115
). Rigorous analysis on local simulations such as the ones in
Guo et al. [63]
may reveal in detail how a fully resolved bi- or tri-stable ISM (locally within an SNR) generated by SNe can be batteries for both vorticity and magnetism. The expectation is that stronger batteries than those in our study will be present.

Self-gravity, star formation and SNe prescription

Along with the omission of the cold phase, our simulations do not include self-gravity, which would be particularly important for the cold phase component and being able to self-consistently for star-cluster particles to be used as SNe detonation coordinates, as modeled in other simulations
[
133
, e.g.,]
. Our SNe prescription, first used in
Martizzi et al. [113]
, is elementary in this sense. We uniformly seed SNe through roughly a gaseous scale height in the disk. This is in the aim of performing simple, controlled numerical experiments to understand the overall flow of velocity flux, without the need for performing a full star formation and feedback simulation. We present a more detailed study on the effect of the SNe seeding and galactic model (parameterized by both the static potential
Equation 8
, and SNe detonation rate
\dot{n}_{\rm SNe}
) in a forthcoming study (Connor, Beattie, et. al, in prep.).

Magnetic fields and cosmic rays

A significant omission to the numerical model is the lack of magnetic fields and cosmic rays, two important components of the pressure budget of a star-forming galaxy
[
134
]
. Both magnetic fields and cosmic rays directly change the nature of the turbulence. By comparing
Federrath et al. 47
with
Beattie et al. 11
, we see that the subsonic spectrum changes from
k^{-5/3}
to
k^{-3/2}
for the velocity with the addition of a magnetic field, meaning that the magnetic field allows for the sequestering of velocity modes on trans-to-sub-sonic scales more efficiently with a magnetic field.
Sampson et al. [135]
showed that CRs can be constantly reaccelerated through parasitic interactions with the
\bm{u}_{c}
modes, decaying the
\bm{u}_{c}
spectrum on all scales.
Bustard & Oh [30]
showed a similar effect for the
\sqrt{\rho}\bm{u}
spectrum. Furthermore, cosmic rays can facilitate large-scale pressure gradients that constantly accelerate winds and plasma out of the Galaxy
[
24
,
134
]
, which will facilitate stronger anisotropies between the perpendicular and parallel transfer functions in
Figure 8
.

Furthermore, even in the absence of a strong magnetic field, a dynamo, both small-scale
[
25
,
44
,
96
,
13
,
95
,
55
]
, and large-scale (e.g., stochastic
\alpha
, even in the absence of large-scale rotation;
Rincon 132
) may be excited. The small-scale dynamo will be saturated almost immediately to
E_{\rm kin}\approx E_{\rm mag}
, given the large Reynolds number of the warm and cold ISM and the kinematic growth rates scaling like
\gamma\approx\rm{Re}^{1/2}

[
137
]
. This will make the turbulent magnetic field dynamically important, independent of its initial seed field
[
13
]
. Our simulations neglect these effects, which will change the nature of the energy flux transfers by magnetizing the turbulent fluctuations
[
62
,
11
]
.

Kinetic plasma effects in SNRs

Finally, we should highlight that SNe shocks, the type that drives the turbulence in our simulations, are known to be collisionless, in that the shock thickness becomes of the order of the ion skin depth,
d_{i}

[
131
, e.g.,]
. This gives rise to a number of effects that are not captured by a fluid model, like the Weibel instability
[
160
,
161
]
, or the details of the shock heating on the underlying plasma species
[
57
]
. The Weibel instability can efficiently seed a magnetic field at
d_{i}\sim 10^{5}-10^{8}\,\rm{cm}
in the ISM
[
51
]
, quickly establishing a field that becomes comparable to the thermal energy on
d_{i}

[
76
]
. Further simulations, utilizing a generalized Ohm’s law that includes
-\bm{\nabla}P_{e}/n_{e}
, the electron pressure and density, will be required to understand how strong the true Biermann field (not just estimated, as in
Section VI
) is compared to estimates of the Weibel fields in the SNRs.

\Pi(k)

Appendix D
Antisymmetric property of Helmholtz decomposed transfer functions

In general, transfer functions should have the antisymmetric property,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(Q,K)=-\mathcal{T}(K,Q),

(D1)

and in this section we shall explore this property for the Helmholtz decomposed transfers, focusing, as we have done for the entire study, just on the
\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}:\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}
nonlinearity. Consider the classical turbulence cascade term that we use to explore the cascade in
Section V
,

\displaystyle-\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u^{K}_{i}u_{j}\partial_{j}u^{Q}_{i}=\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;\partial_{j}(u^{K}_{i}u_{j}u^{Q}_{i})+\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u^{K}_{i}u^{Q}_{i}\partial_{j}u_{j}+\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u_{j}u^{Q}_{i}\partial_{j}u^{K}_{i},

(D2)

from the product rule, written in tensorial form. Using the divergence theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;\partial_{j}(u^{K}_{i}u_{j}u^{Q}_{i})=\oiint_{\partial\mathcal{V}}u^{K}_{i}u_{j}u^{Q}_{i}\operatorname{d}\!{\partial\mathcal{V}_{j}}=0,

(D3)

if the domain is periodic. In our case, we have only doubly-periodic boundaries, so this surface integral will not be exactly zero. However, based on the antisymmetry that is preserved in the incompressible mediated transfer functions (see
Figure 8
), seemingly this has a very small effect. Then,

\displaystyle-\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u^{K}_{i}u_{j}\partial_{j}u^{Q}_{i}=\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u^{K}_{i}u^{Q}_{i}\partial_{j}u_{j}+\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u_{j}u^{Q}_{i}\partial_{j}u^{K}_{i}.

(D4)

Now consider the compressible
u_{i}^{c}
and incompressible
u_{i}^{s}
mode decomposition that we perform throughout
Section V
. Without loss of generality, if we consider only the mediating mode, for the compressible mode we have

\displaystyle-\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u^{K}_{i}u_{j}^{c}\partial_{j}u^{Q}_{i}=\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u^{K}_{i}u^{Q}_{i}\partial_{j}u_{j}^{c}+\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u_{j}^{c}u^{Q}_{i}\partial_{j}u^{K}_{i},

(D5)

and for incompressible,

\displaystyle-\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u^{K}_{i}u_{j}^{s}\partial_{j}u^{Q}_{i}=\int_{\V}\operatorname{d}\!{\V}\;u_{j}^{s}u^{Q}_{i}\partial_{j}u^{K}_{i}.

(D6)

This means that transfer functions mediated by
u_{i}^{s}
are antisymmetric with themselves, as is expected from regular incompressible transfer function theory
[
3
]
, but transfer functions mediated by
u_{i}^{c}
are not. From
Equation D5
, they are instead antisymmetric with the total velocity transfer function,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{c}(Q,K)=-\int\operatorname{d}^{3}\!{\bm{\ell}}\;\left[\bm{u}^{K}\otimes\left(\bm{u}_{c}:\bm{\nabla}\otimes\bm{u}^{Q}+\bm{u}^{Q}:(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}_{c})\mathbb{I}\right)\right],

(D7)

which we do not investigate in this study, due to the fact that the antisymmetric component of a transfer function we are already computing does not contain any further information. However, this does explain why all of the
\mathcal{T}^{c}(Q,K)
transfer functions are not antisymmetric with themselves, whereas the
\mathcal{T}^{s}(Q,K)
transfers are.

Antisymmetric transfers are at the heart of cascade transfers. Hence, we perform one final step to the
\mathcal{T}^{c}(Q,K)
transfer functions to ensure antisymmetry, where we redefine then such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{c}(Q,K)\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{T}^{c}(Q,K)+\mathcal{T}^{c}(K,Q)\right),

(D8)

isolating only the antisymmetric transfers in
\mathcal{T}^{c}(Q,K)
.

Appendix E
Kinetic helicity

The kinetic helicity,
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kin}=\left\langle{\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}}\right\rangle_{\V}
, describes the volume-averaged asymmetry in the left versus right eigenmodes of
\bm{u}_{s}
in the fluid
[
2
,
130
, clockwise orientated
\bm{\omega}_{+}
versus anticlockwise orientated
\bm{\omega}_{-}
; ]
. If
|\left\langle{\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}}\right\rangle_{\V}|\gtrsim 0
then we expect there to be an excess of left or right modes, which, when interacting,
\bm{\omega}_{\pm}\rightarrow\bm{\omega}_{\pm}
, have, on average, a flux from small to large scales,
\varepsilon<0
. Hence,
\bm{\omega}_{+}\rightarrow\bm{\omega}_{+}
or
\bm{\omega}_{-}\rightarrow\bm{\omega}_{-}
velocity mode interactions can give rise to an inverse cascade, as demonstrated utilizing
\bm{\omega}_{\pm}
transfer functions in three-dimensional hydrodynamic turbulence driven with net kinetic helicity
[
2
,
130
]
. In our study, we show that the
\bm{u}_{s}\rightarrow\bm{u}_{s}
mediated by
\bm{u}_{c}
interaction can also provide a mechanism for inverse transfer. Therefore, a natural question is the nature of the handedness of
\bm{u}_{s}\rightarrow\bm{u}_{s}
transfers during this interaction. We leave the details of this for a future study, where we intend to track both eigenmodes, but we provide
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kin}
as a function of
t/t_{0}
for the
512^{3}
simulation to at least understand if the supernova-driven turbulence becomes net helical (note that
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kin}
is no longer an invariant in compressible hydrodynamics, so small helicity fluctuations could indeed grow).

We show the kinetic helicity in
Figure 20
, where for all time the supernova-driven turbulence is completely non-helical (in volume average)
|\left\langle{\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}}\right\rangle_{\V}|\approx 0
, at least to single precision. We also show a two-dimensional slice of the point-wise kinetic helicity
\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}
normalized by the rms,
Figure 21
, with the same annotations as in
Figure 13
. This is early in the evolution of the simulation, just to easily visualize what is happening in and around the SNRs, but no conclusions change in the steady state. What we observe are strong
\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}
fluctuations, localized around the SNRs. The signed fluctuations become comparable to the size of the typical size of the SNRs, significantly larger than the background fluctuations in the disk. Furthermore, they are bright, indicating that the fluctuations contain strong alignment between
\bm{u}
and
\bm{\omega}
. This suggests, like
\bm{u}_{s}\xrightarrow{\bm{u}_{c}}\bm{u}_{s}
interactions, the
\bm{u}_{c}
modes generated in the SNRs take strong
\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}
fluctuations to large scales. As previously discussed in
Käpylä et al. [79]
, this is important for large-scale, stochastic
\alpha
dynamos
[
132
]
. So even though
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kin}\approx 0
, the fluctuations in
\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}
may still be important for both the ISM dynamo and the handed interactions in the turbulence.
