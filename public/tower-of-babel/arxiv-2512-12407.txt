The generic canonical form for ⋆ congruence of matrices
Fernando De Terán, Froilán M. Dopico

Abstract
First, we prove that the set of n × n n\times n complex matrices is the closure of a certain open subset whose elements have a very specific canonical form under congruence, which is uniquely determined up to the values of some parameters, but which has a slightly different expression depending on whether n n is even or odd. As a consequence, the canonical form under congruence of the elements of this subset can be considered the generic canonical form under congruence of complex n × n n\times n matrices. Second, we prove that the set of n × n n\times n complex matrices is the union of the closures of certain ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor n/2\rfloor+1 open subsets and that, for each of these subsets, its elements have a very specific canonical form under ∗ congruence, which is uniquely determined up to the values of some parameters. As a consequence, the ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor n/2\rfloor+1 canonical forms under ∗ congruence of the elements of each of these subsets can be considered the generic canonical forms under ∗ congruence of complex n × n n\times n matrices. So, there is only one generic canonical form under congruence whereas the number of generic canonical forms under ∗ congruence is ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor n/2\rfloor+1 instead, which reveals a strong dichotomy between the relations of congruence and ∗ congruence with respect to generic structures. In other words, we determine in this paper the generic matrix representations of n × n n\times n bilinear and sesquilinear forms in ℂ n × ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n} .

Introduction

Two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are said to be
congruent
(respectively,
∗
congruent
) if there is an invertible matrix
P\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
P^{\top}AP=B
(resp.,
P^{*}AP=B
), where
M^{\top}
(resp.,
M^{*}
) denotes the transpose (resp., conjugate transpose) of
M
. For simplicity, we will use the notation
\star
to denote both the transpose (
\top
) and the conjugate transpose (
*
). Both congruence and
∗
congruence are equivalence relations on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and this allows us to classify the set of
n\times n
complex matrices into the corresponding equivalence classes. Each equivalence class is the
orbit
under
⋆
congruence of a certain matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, namely:

{\mathcal{O}^{\star}}(A):=\left\{P^{\star}AP:\ P\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\ \mbox{invertible}\right\}.

(1)

The search for a set of “canonical” representatives of the equivalence classes has led to the “canonical forms for congruence and
∗
congruence” (CFC and
∗
CFC, respectively) introduced in
[
17
, Th. 1.1]
(later reformulated by means of tridiagonal matrices in Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 in
[
13
]
). Following the notation above, we will use
⋆
CFC to denote both the CFC and the
∗
CFC.

The question that motivates the present work is the following: which is the most-likely
⋆
CFC? In order to answer this question we first need to clarify what we mean by “most likely”. The approach we follow here uses the notion of
⋆
congruence bundle
, which is revised in Section
2
and further studied in Section
3
. Such a bundle is a union of orbits with a common feature. To be more precise, the
⋆
CFC is a direct sum of blocks of three different types (see Theorem
2.1
). For
\star=*
, two of these three types of blocks depend on some complex values, namely:

•

unit complex numbers,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, with
|\alpha|=1
(Type I blocks), and

•

complex numbers,
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, with
|\mu|>1
(Type II blocks),

and, for
\star=\top
instead, one of the three types of blocks depends on:

•

complex numbers,
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, with
0\neq\mu\neq(-1)^{k+1}
(Type II blocks with size
2k\times 2k
).

A
⋆
congruence bundle is the set of matrices with the same
⋆
CFC up to the values of the complex numbers
\alpha
’s and
\mu
’s mentioned above (see Definitions
2.3
and
2.4
), which in addition must satisfy some constraints that guarantee that all the orbits in the same bundle have the same dimension. This definition mimics, though it is more complicated, the one of bundles under similarity of
n\times n
complex matrices, namely, a bundle under similarity is the set of matrices with the same Jordan canonical form (JCF), up to the values of the eigenvalues, provided that the sets of distinct eigenvalues and the sizes of the corresponding Jordan blocks are the same (see
[
1
, §5.3]
). It is well known that the “most likely” JCF of complex
n\times n
matrices is
{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, with
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for
i\neq j
, namely, most
n\times n
complex matrices have
n
distinct complex eigenvalues. From a topological point of view, this means, first, that, for every matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, there is a sequence of matrices
\{A_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
A_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which converges to
A
, and such that the JCF of
A_{k}
is
{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\lambda_{n}^{(k)})
, with
\lambda_{i}^{(k)}\neq\lambda_{j}^{(k)}
for
i\neq j
, and, second, that the set of matrices whose JCF is of the type
{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, with
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for
i\neq j
, is an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. In terms of bundles, this means that the bundle under similarity of
{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, with
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for
i\neq j
, is
dense and open
in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, where the closure and the openness in this paper are considered in the standard topology of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. Accordingly, we say that
{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, with
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for
i\neq j
, is the
generic
JCF in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.

For
⋆
congruence we follow an approach like the one described in the previous paragraph for the similarity relation, though some important differences show up due to the existence of three types of different blocks in the
⋆
CFC, in contrast with the unique type of blocks existing in the JCF. Based on this approach, we will prove that
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is the closure of only one
⊤
congruence bundle (
⊤
congruence bundles are named just congruence bundles for simplicity), which is an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. This bundle is then termed the “generic” congruence bundle and, accordingly, the CFC of the matrices within this bundle is the “generic” CFC of
n\times n
complex matrices. This result is stated in parts (a) of the Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
. Thus, the situation for congruence is like the one for similarity in the sense that there is only one generic CFC. This scenario is in stark contrast with the one for
∗
congruence, since we prove in parts (b) of Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
that
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is the union of the closures of
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1

∗
congruence bundles, and that each of these bundles is an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. These
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
bundles are termed the “generic”
∗
congruence bundles and the
∗
CFCs of the matrices within each of these bundles are the “generic”
∗
CFCs of
n\times n
complex matrices.

In order to get more insight on the striking difference between the number of generic CFC and of generic
∗
CFCs, we explore the connection between an
n\times n
complex matrix
A
and the associated
\star
-palindromic pencil
A+\lambda A^{\star}

[
18
]
. In particular, the number of generic
⋆
CFCs is related to the possible number of (distinct)

Type I blocks in the generic
⋆
CFCs of
n\times n
complex matrices. These (distinct) Type I blocks correspond to different unit eigenvalues, i.e., eigenvalues of modulus
1
, of the pencil
A+\lambda A^{\star}
. When
\star=\top
and
n
is even, there are no, generically, unit eigenvalues in a
\top
-palindromic pencil

, and when
n
is odd there is just one (the eigenvalue
-1
, which is always an eigenvalue of a regular
\top
-palindromic pencil with odd size because

is skew-symmetric and
\det(A-A^{\top})=0
). However, when
\star=*
, a generic
n\times n

*
-palindromic pencil can have
0,2,\ldots,n
unit eigenvalues, which are different to each other (when
n
is even), or
1,3,\ldots,n
unit eigenvalues, which are different to each other (when
n
is odd).

Another approach to obtain the generic canonical structures of matrices or matrix pencils, different from the topological one followed in this paper, is by means of the codimension of the orbits or bundles. More precisely, the generic canonical structures are those for which the corresponding bundles have codimension
0
within the whole space of matrices or matrix pencils. This has been the approach followed in
[
8
, Cor. 7.1]
for the
Kronecker Canonical Form
of rectangular matrix pencils, and in
[
6
]
and
[
5
]
for, respectively, the CFC and the
∗
CFC of
n\times n
complex matrices. The result presented in Theorem
4.1
-(a) for the CFC is in accordance with
[
6
, Th. 4]
, as expected (namely, the same unique generic CFC is obtained in both cases, which has slightly different expressions for
n
even or odd). However, we want to highlight that Theorem 5.3 in
[
5
]
is incomplete since it states that there is only one generic
∗
CFC, which, again, has slightly different expressions for
n
even or odd. The generic
∗
CFC in
[
5
, Theorem 5.3]
is only one of the
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1

∗
CFCs defined in Theorem
4.1
-(b), more precisely that corresponding to
\ell=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. The reason of this disagreement is not that the codimension approach fails in this case, but that
[
5
, Theorem 5.3]
did not identify correctly all the existing
∗
congruence bundles that have codimension
0
(see Remark
4.2
).

In terms of bilinear and sesquilinear forms, Theorem
4.1
provides the matrix representation of a generic bilinear or sesquilinear form. In particular, there is just one generic
n\times n
bilinear form, whereas there are
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
generic
n\times n
sesquilinear forms. Further insights on the geometry and topology of the spaces of bilinear and sesquilinear forms, in terms of the changes of the canonical forms of the matrix representations, can be found in
[
10
,
11
,
2
]
.

In the last section of the paper, we perform several numerical experiments with

to confirm the genericity of the
⋆
CFC identified in Theorem
4.1
. In these experiments, we have computed the eigenvalues of a large number of
n\times n

\top
-palindromic and
*
-palindromic pencils (for
n=24
and
25
) generated with random matrices, and we have counted the number of unit eigenvalues of each pencil. Then, we have counted the number of pencils having exactly
m
unit eigenvalues, for each

. Since, as we mentioned before, the distinct unit eigenvalues of a palindromic pencil correspond to distinct

.

In several of the proofs of this paper, we will make use of the connection between the

). We have already mentioned that

are congruent if and only if the pencils
A+\lambda A^{\top}
and
B+\lambda B^{\top}
are strictly equivalent. Therefore, it is not surprising that there is a bijection between the blocks of the CFC of
A
and the blocks of the KCF of
A+\lambda A^{\top}
. This bijection can be found, for instance, in
[
3
, Th. 4]
, which will be referred to in the proofs of several of the results of this paper. In contrast, there is no a bijection between the blocks of the
∗
CFC of
A
and the blocks of the KCF of
A+\lambda A^{*}
. More precisely, the
∗
CFC of
A
determines the KCF of
A+\lambda A^{*}
, but the KCF of
A+\lambda A^{*}
only determines the
∗
CFC of
A
up to multiplication of any Type I block by
-1
. The detailed relation between such
∗
CFC and KCF can be found in
[
7
, Th. 2.13-(b)]
, which will be also referred to in the proofs of several of the results of this paper.

The next lemma allows us to determine if a matrix
A
is in the closure of the
⋆
congruence bundle of another matrix just by studying whether the
⋆
CFC of
A
belongs to that closure or not.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. Then
A\in{\overline{\mathcal{B}^{\star}}}(B)
if and only if
P^{\star}AP\in{\overline{\mathcal{B}^{\star}}}(B)
, for every invertible matrix
P
.

Proof.

Assume that
A\in{\overline{\mathcal{B}^{\star}}}(B)
. Then, there is a sequence of matrices
\{B_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
which converges to
A
and with
B_{k}\in{\mathcal{B}^{\star}}(B)
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the sequence
\{P^{\star}B_{k}P\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
P^{\star}AP
and
P^{\star}B_{k}P\in{\mathcal{B}^{\star}}(B)
. Therefore,
P^{*}AP\in{\overline{\mathcal{B}^{\star}}}(B)
. The other implication is trivial, taking
P=I_{n}
.
∎

1+\frac{1}{(n/2-\ell)k}&0\end{bmatrix}}^{n/2-\ell}\right)P^{\top},\;\quad\mbox{if $n$ is even},\\[28.45274pt]
P\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\ell}H_{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{i})\oplus\overbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1+\frac{1}{k}&0\end{bmatrix}\oplus\cdots\oplus\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1+\frac{1}{(\lfloor n/2\rfloor-\ell)k}&0\end{bmatrix}}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-\ell}\oplus 1\right)P^{\top},\;\quad\mbox{if $n$ is odd}.\end{array}\right.

For all

, where
k_{0}
is sufficiently large,

belongs to
{\mathcal{B}^{c}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n/2}H_{2}(\mu_{i})\right)
, if

, if

belongs to
{\overline{\mathcal{B}^{c}}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n/2}H_{2}(\mu_{i})\right)

is even, or to
{\overline{\mathcal{B}^{c}}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}H_{2}(\mu_{i})\oplus 1\right)
, if

is odd, which proves (

) and (

5
Openness of the generic bundles in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

As announced in the introduction, this section is devoted to prove that the bundles introduced in Theorem
4.1
are open subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. Thus, combining Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
, we get that the unique congruence bundle introduced in (
22
) (with slightly different expressions for
n
even or odd) is an open and dense subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and, so, it can be properly termed as a
generic
subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, according to the standard definition in topology. Analogously, from Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
, we get that the union,
\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
, of the
∗
congruence bundles defined in (
23
) is an open and dense subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and, so, generic. In Theorem
5.1
, we will use the Frobenius norm of a matrix
[
15
, p. 321]
, denoted by
\|A\|_{F}
, to be specific, but any other norm can be used.

Theorem 5.1
.

(a)

The set
\mathcal{G}
defined in (
22
) is an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. That is, for any
A\in\mathcal{G}
, there exists a number
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A):=\{S\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\,:\,\|A-S\|_{F}<\varepsilon\}\subset\mathcal{G}.

(b)

The subset
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
defined in (
23
) is an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
for any
\ell=0,1,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. That is, for any
A\in\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
, there exists a number
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A):=\{S\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\,:\,\|A-S\|_{F}<\varepsilon\}\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ell}.

Proof.

We prove first in detail part (b), which is longer. Then, we sketch the proof of part (a).

Proof of
(b). The proof has two parts. In the first part, we prove that if
A\in\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
and
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, then

\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)\subset\bigcup_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathcal{G}_{k}
. With this result at hand, in the second part we prove that
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. If
A\in\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
, then
A
is invertible, the pencil
A+\lambda A^{*}
is regular and, from
[
7
, Theorem 2.13-(b)]
, this pencil has
n
distinct eigenvalues, all different from zero and infinite, such that
\ell
of them have modulus larger than
1
,
\ell
of them have modulus smaller than
1
, and
n-2\ell
have modulus equal to
1
. Thus, for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, if
S\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)
, then
S
is invertible, the pencil
S+\lambda S^{*}
is regular, and it has
n
distinct eigenvalues, all different from zero and infinite, by the continuity of the eigenvalues of regular pencils (Th. 2.1 in
[
21
, Ch. VI]
). Combining this with the relationship between the KCF of
S+\lambda S^{*}
and the
∗
CFC of
S

[
7
, Theorem 2.13-(b)]
, one gets that
S\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
, for some
k=0,1,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. So,
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)\subset\bigcup_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathcal{G}_{k}
for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

For the second part of the proof, that is, to prove that there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
, we proceed by contradiction and assume that
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)\not\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. This and the result above imply that
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(A)\cap\bigcup_{k=0,k\neq\ell}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathcal{G}_{k}\neq\emptyset
for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. Therefore,
A\in\overline{\bigcup_{k=0,k\neq\ell}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathcal{G}_{k}}=\bigcup_{k=0,k\neq\ell}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\overline{\mathcal{G}_{k}}
, which is in contradiction with Theorem
4.1
-(b2) because
A\in\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
.

Proof of
(a). It is completely analogous to the first part of the proof of (b), with the only difference that
[
3
, Th. 4]
has to be used to relate the CFC of
A
with the KCF of the pencil
A+\lambda A^{\top}
. Note that a second part of the proof is not needed in this case because there is only one generic congruence bundle
\mathcal{G}
. We omit the details for brevity.
∎

6
Numerical experiments

We have used several times in this paper the fact that, for any matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, there is a bijection between the CFC of
A
and the KCF of the
\top
-palindromic pencil
A+\lambda A^{\top}
, which can be found in
[
3
, Th. 4]
, and a very close connection between the
∗
CFC of
A
and the KCF of the
*
-palindromic pencil

[
7

A
belongs to some of the generic

n-2\ell
eigenvalues of modulus

of the modulus seems very particular. At first glance, one might think that eigenvalues of modulus

should not appear very often in
*
-palindromic pencils or, if they do, they should appear in a very small number. The main purpose of this section is to show through some numerical experiments that eigenvalues of modulus
