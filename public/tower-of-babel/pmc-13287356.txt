Long-term risk of second primary malignancy in young women with breast cancer receiving postoperative radiotherapy
Authors: Jingna Wu, Bo Gao, Yunuo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTherapy for young women with breast cancer (YWBC) in early stage includes surgery, radiotherapy, and endocrinotherapy. Radiation exposure, including from postoperative radiotherapy (PORT), is known to increase the risk of subsequent malignancies, particularly in younger individuals with longer life expectancy and greater tissue susceptibility. However, the long-term risk of second primary malignancy (SPM) associated with PORT specifically in YWBC remains poorly characterized.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study using sing data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-8 registries (1975–2018), we identified women aged 18–39 years diagnosed with localized or regional stage breast cancer. Background

Breast cancer ranks the leading cancer-related cause of disease burden, affecting one in twenty globally and as many as one in eight in high-income countries [1]. Female breast cancer acted as the most commonly diagnosed cancer, with an estimated 2.26 million new cases and 0.68 million deaths each year [2]. With the advancement of diagnostic technology and the application of multidisciplinary therapy including surgery, radiotherapy, and endocrinotherapy, the mortality of female breast cancer has improved [3]. However, the clinical outcome of young women with breast cancer (YWBC, defined as age 18–39 years at diagnosis) is poor, due to its high aggression and mutations for hereditary cancer genes [3, 4]. Among all the therapy approaches, radiotherapy is considered suitable for breast cancer in both early and locally advanced stage, as well as metastatic cases [5]. Evidence has shown that radiotherapy can control breast cancer and improve patient outcomes [6]. However, recent study showed that postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) of malignancies was associated with increasing risk of second primary malignancy (SPM), especially for young individuals with cancer [7]. These data emphasize the necessity of exploring the correlation between PORT of YWBC and the risk of SPMs, which may provide more guideline for improving outcomes and follow-up plans.

SPM is one of the main causes of long-term death of cancer patients [8]. Young people with cancer are more likely to experience secondary effects after PORT due to their higher tissue susceptibility and longer life expectancy [9]. A cross-sectional study showed that radiotherapy was linked to increased risk of arm lymphedema in breast cancer [10]. Moreover, external beam radiotherapy increased the risk of breast fibrosis and retraction [11]. Due to the require of long follow-up time required and the large number of cases, no study has been performed to explore the correlation between PORT and the risk of SPM among YWBC in early stage. Thus, the current study is the first investigation to explore the correlation between PORT and SPM risk among YWBC in early stage.

The current study performed a comprehensive investigation about the correlation between PORT and SPM incidence among YWBC in early stage based on the data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Our result may provide more guideline for effective interventions for the prevention of SPM and the treatment and follow-up plans of YWBC.

Methods

Data sources

We conducted a retrospective population-based cohort study using data from the SEER database. From the SEER database, we identified women diagnosed with a first primary breast cancer between 1975 and 2018 from the SEER-8 registries (San Francisco, Connecticut, Detroit, Hawaii, Iowa, New Mexico, Seattle, and Utah), which collectively cover approximately 9% of the U.S. population. We classified YWBC in localized or regional stage using the SEER Combined summary stage and Historic Stage A variables. Those cases received PORT or without radiation record would be selected in our study. The inclusion criteria also included: (1) YWBC with an accurate and completed followed up data, (2) YWBC age from 18–40(consistent with the National Cancer Institute’s definition of adolescents and young adults with cancer). Exclusive criteria: (1) YWBC received intraoperative radiation or radiation prior to surgery, (2) YWBC had distant metastasis at diagnosis. We also excluded cases age < 18 years due to the rarity of breast cancer in this population and distinct etiologic considerations. According to the National Cancer Institute’s definition, adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with cancer are those diagnosed between ages 15 and 39 years. In our study, we included women age 18–39 years, consistent with this definition and with previous studies of YWBC [3]. In our study, sensitivity analyses were performed in YWBC in those younger than age 40 years as diagnosis.

Definition of exposure and outcome

PORT was defined as receipt of radiotherapy after 1988 or “other radiation” or “radiation NOS”, as opposed to external beam radiotherapy before 1988. Patients whose radiation treatment was recorded as beam radiation before 1988 or “other radiation” or “radiation NOS” after 1988 were excluded.

The outcome of interest was SPMs, including second primary hematologic malignancies (SPHMs) and second primary solid malignancies (SPSMs). Considering the radiotherapy area, only SPSMs in the chest and neck area were included in our study. Considering the minimal latency period for radiation induced carcinogenesis, SPHMs and SPSMs would be recorded since 2 and 5 years after the diagnosis of breast cancer, respectively [12]. The endpoint for follow-up was the diagnosis of SPM, death or last follow-up time, whichever happened the earliest.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis used Fine-Gray competing risk regression to estimate adjusted subdistribution hazard ratios (aHRs) for SPM associated with PORT, accounting for death as a competing event. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated and compared between PORT groups. As a secondary analysis, multivariable logistic regression was performed to estimate odds ratios (ORs), with results interpreted cautiously given the limitations of this approach for time-to-event data. All regression models were adjusted a priori for: age at diagnosis (continuous), race (White, Black, Other), marital status (married, other), median household income (<$75,000, ≥$75,000, unknown), diagnostic year (1975–1999, 2000–2018), tumor grade (well/moderately differentiated, poorly/undifferentiated, unknown), clinical stage (localized, regional), estrogen receptor (ER) status (negative, positive, unknown), and progesterone receptor (PR) status (negative, positive, unknown). Variables were included in multivariable models regardless of statistical significance in univariable analyses to minimize residual confounding. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of the PORT-SPM association across these strata using multivariable Fine-Gray models.

During the follow-up period, a total of 1591 and 1445 YWBC have developed SPMs in PORT group and no PORT group, ranking for 10.1% and 7.7% of all YWBC, respectively (Table 1).

PORT was associated with higher risks of SPMs in YWBC

As shown in Fig. 1, the relative risk of all SPMs in PORT group was higher than the no PORT group in YWBC (RR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.47–1.69). As the different latency periods of SPHMs and SPSMs, we then compared the relative risks of SPHMs and SPSMs between PORT group versus no PORT group, respectively. YWBC received PORT were substantially correlated with elevated risks of SPSMs (RR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.49–1.71). However, YWBC who received PORT showed a negligible risk of SPHMs (RR = 1.18; 95% CI: 0.88–1.58). Specifically, YWBC received PORT showed significant correlations with elevated risk of second primary breast cancer (RR = 1.99; 95% CI:1.82–2.17), second primary lung cancer (RR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.39–2.18) and second primary thyroid cancer (RR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.04–1.77) (Fig. 1). However, there is no significant correlation between PORT and the risk of any subtype of SPHMs, including myeloma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and acute lymphocytic leukemia et al. (Fig. 1).

In multivariable Fine-Gray competing risk analysis adjusting for age, race, marital status, income, diagnostic year, grade, stage, ER status, and PR status, women who received PORT had significantly higher risk of all SPMs (adjusted HR (aHR) = 1.52) and SPSMs (aHR = 1.54, p < 0.001) compared to those who did not receive PORT (Fig. 2A and B). However, no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of all SPSMs was obtained between PORT group and no PORT group (Fig. 2C, p = 0.141). The results also suggested a higher cumulative incidence of second primary breast cancer (aHR = 1.85, p < 0.001), second primary lung cancer (aHR = 1.79, p < 0.001) and second primary thyroid cancer (HR = 1.54, p = 0.014) in the PORT group (Fig. 2D and F).

To minimize immortal time bias, we conducted two sensitivity analyses: (1) a landmark analysis excluding women who developed SPM or died within 6 months of diagnosis, and (2) a time-dependent Cox regression model. Both yielded results consistent with the primary findings (landmark analysis aHR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.39–1.61; time-dependent aHR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.38–1.59).

Compared with YWBC in White race, YWBC in Black race (aHR = 1.41) and other races (aHR = 1.23) had a higher risk of developing SPMs (Fig. 3). The result also demonstrated an elevated risk of developing SPM among YWBC patients who were not married (aHR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.19–1.36), diagnosed among 2000–2018 (aHR = 3.21; 95% CI: 2.85–3.61), ER negative (aHR = 2.43; 95% CI: 2.24–2.63), and PR negative (aHR = 2.28; 95% CI: 2.11–2.48) (Fig. 3). In planned exploratory analyses examining other factors associated with SPM risk, we found that the risk of SPM was higher in YWBC received PORT in different subgroups based on the category of age, race, median household income, marital status, diagnostic year, tumor grade, clinical stage, ER status, and PR status (Fig. 3).

Discussion

PORT plays a crucial role in the management of breast cancer, achieving tumor control and patient life extension [13, 14]. With the advancements of technology, the side effects of PORT have minimized, However, PORT may be still possible to increase the risk of developing SPMs, especially for adolescent and young individuals with cancer [9]. The current study firstly comprehensively explored the correlation between PORT and the risk of developing SPMs in YWBC in early stage using a large population cohort from SEER cohort. The data demonstrated that PORT was associated with increasing risks of SPSMs, including breast cancer, lung cancer and thyroid cancer. The risk of PORT-related SPSMs (breast cancer, lung cancer and thyroid cancer) increased with longer follow-up period, suggesting the need for long-term surveillance of these patients. The association between PORT and SPM risk varies by cancer type. In head and neck cancer, Zhu et al. found that PORT was associated with higher long-term risk of SPMs [7]. Another study by Hauptmann et al. [15] enrolling 23,982 testicular cancer patients from 1947 to 1991 suggested that radiotherapy was associated with increased risk of second primary pancreatic cancer (RR = 2.9) [15]. In contrast, Wang et al. found no increase in SPMs among rectal cancer patients receiving radiotherapy [16]. Focusing on breast cancer, Kaufman et al. reported that PORT was associated with a significant risk of second primary lung cancer [17]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Grantzau and Overgaard showed that radiotherapy for breast cancer is associated with excess risk of second non-breast cancers [18]. Our findings extend this literature by specifically quantifying PORT-associated SPM risk in young women with breast cancer, a population with longer life expectancy and potentially greater susceptibility to radiation-induced carcinogenesis.

The therapy approaches for YWBC in early stage included surgery, radiotherapy or other treatments [19]. Adjuvant radiotherapy is traditionally recommended for breast cancer with N1-3 disease but not routinely for cT1-2cN0 disease [20]. By focusing on early-stage YWBC, our investigation helps comprehensively map the potential long-term risks if PORT is utilized outside of standard guidelines. Because SPM probability is low, studies require large populations and long follow-up periods. Our enrollment of 34,580 cases from 1975 to 2018 met these requirements, providing reliable conclusions.

Our study also found that YWBC who were Black race, not married, estrogen receptor negative and progesterone receptor negative status had a higher risk of developing SPMs. Previous study also suggested that breast cancer patients in Black race correlated with higher risk of a second primary contralateral breast cancer [21]. Another study also found that progesterone receptor negative indicated a higher risk of second breast cancers during the first 5 years after diagnosis [22]. Estrogen receptor negative also suggested a higher risk of developing second primary lung cancer [23].

The cohort study design was selected because it allows for estimation of cumulative incidence over time, assessment of temporal relationships between PORT exposure and SPM development, and calculation of both relative and absolute risk measures, which are essential for understanding the clinical impact of PORT-related SPM risk.

Our study offers several novel contributions to the literature. First, to our knowledge, this is the largest study specifically examining PORT-associated SPM risk in young women with breast cancer (age 18–39 years), a population with distinct biology, treatment considerations, and longer life expectancy. Second, we provide quantitative estimates of both relative (HRs) and absolute (EARs) risks, which are essential for clinical decision-making and patient counseling. Third, we developed and validated a prediction nomogram incorporating easily available clinicopathological factors to estimate individual SPM risk, which may facilitate risk-stratified surveillance strategies. Fourth, our use of competing risk methodology accounts for death as a competing event, providing more accurate cumulative incidence estimates than traditional Kaplan-Meier methods. These findings suggest that while PORT remains an important component of breast cancer treatment, the long-term risk of SPM warrants consideration in treatment planning and follow-up care for young women.

Our exploratory analyses suggested that Black race, unmarried status, ER-negative, and PR-negative status were associated with higher SPM risk. While these findings are consistent with previous studies, they should be interpreted cautiously as they were not the primary focus of our study and may be subject to residual confounding. These analyses were planned as exploratory hypothesis-generating investigations, and the associations observed warrant confirmation in dedicated studies.

Several limitations exist. Selection bias is inherent in retrospective databases; unmeasured factors (e.g., comorbidities, patient preference) may affect PORT receipt and SPM risk. While we addressed immortal time bias, residual bias may persist. External validity may be limited to the US population, and we lacked detailed data on radiotherapy fields, doses, techniques, family history, genetic predispositions (e.g., BRCA), and lifestyle factors like smoking. Changes in radiotherapy techniques over the study period may have influenced SPM risk, though we adjusted for diagnostic year. Finally, some subgroup analyses may have been underpowered.

Conclusion

PORT for YWBC was correlated with elevated risks of SPMs. The risk of PORT-related SPMs (breast cancer, lung cancer and thyroid cancer) increased with longer follow-up period, suggesting the need for long-term surveillance of these patients.
