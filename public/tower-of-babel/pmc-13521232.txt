Isolation and identification of host-associated lactic acid bacteria with probiotic potential from Red Junglefowl (Gallus gallus) and preliminary in vivo evaluation in broiler chickens
Authors: Mueena Jahan, Md. Morshedur Rahman, Mst. Umme Habiba, Kazi Md. Al-Noman, Md Robiul Karim, Sm Mostafizur Rahaman Sumon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gastrointestinal tract of host-associated birds may provide avian-adapted probiotic candidates for poultry production. This study isolated and characterized lactic acid bacteria (LAB) from Red Junglefowl (RJF; Gallus gallus), screened representative strains for probiotic-related in vitro traits, and performed a preliminary in vivo evaluation in broiler chickens. Thirty-nine presumptive LAB were recovered from the crop, small intestine, large intestine, and cecum of RJF birds and were assigned by near full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing to Limosilactobacillus reuteri (28/39, 71.8%), Ligilactobacillus salivarius (8/39, 20.5%), and Weissella paramesenteroides (3/39, 7.7%), represented 14 sequence types. Representative strains were assessed for auto-aggregation, surface hydrophobicity, antagonistic activity against Escherichia coli and Salmonella sp., acid and bile tolerance, hemolysis, DNase activity, and phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility. Marked strain-to-strain variation was observed, but L. reuteri-1, L. salivarius-3, and W. paramesenteroides-3 showed the most balanced overall profiles and were selected for a multi-strain formulation. In a 35-d broiler trial, 108 Arbor Acres chicks were divided into three groups: a PBS control group, an RJF-derived candidate probiotic mixture (RJF-Mix) group, and a commercial probiotic product (CPP) group, with 6 pens per treatment and 6 birds per pen. All chicks had almost similar initial body weights, and there was no significant difference among treatments in total feed intake. However, final body weight was 2485.5 ± 37.3 g/bird in the RJF-Mix group and 2513.7 ± 23.4 g/bird in the CPP group, compared with 2288.6 ± 27.8 g/bird in the control group (P < 0.0001). The RJF-Mix (1.377 ± 0.006) and CPP group (1.364 ± 0.010) showed lower final cumulative feed conversion ratio as compared to the control group (1.517 ± 0.014; P < 0.0001). To our knowledge, this is the first study to recover host-associated LAB from the wild ancestor of the domestic chicken, RJF, and to advance selected isolates from a transparent, criterion-based in vitro prioritization to preliminary in vivo broiler validation against a CFU-matched commercial probiotic comparator. In conclusion, these findings indicate that RJF might be a promising natural resource of host-associated LAB with potential for poultry probiotic development; however, the three selected isolates are presented as promising probiotic candidates rather than fully validated probiotics, and further genomic, safety, and larger-scale validation are required.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is increasingly recognized as a barrier to sustainable poultry production, and practical strategies capable of promoting bird growth and intestinal health whilst reducing the use of antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) or routine antimicrobials are required. The reason why this issue is crucial lies in the fact that intestinal disorders, inefficient nutrient utilization and gut-associated foodborne pathogens can eventually impair bird performance and pose a threat to food safety within poultry production. Consequently, there has been considerable interest in using probiotics and other direct-fed microbials as potential practical alternatives to routine antimicrobial administration. These beneficial microorganisms exert positive effects in poultry by ensuring better gut microbial balance, enhancing intestinal barrier and immune mechanisms, increasing nutrient utilization, and reducing the ability of harmful pathogens to establish in the gut. However, probiotic effects are not always consistent. Their efficacy depends on several factors, including strain identity, host adaptation, microbial viability, dose, formulation, timing of administration, and farm or experimental conditions (Dong et al., 2023; Halder et al., 2024; Jha et al., 2020; Naeem and Bourassa, 2025; Wickramasuriya et al., 2024).

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are among the most significant and extensively researched probiotic microorganisms for poultry. Lactobacilli and other related LAB isolated from chicken have been described with relevant characteristics such as acid tolerance, bile tolerance, adhesion-related properties, antimicrobial activity against chicken-associated pathogens, evidence of intestinal colonization after oral administration and beneficial effects in broiler models (Blajman et al., 2015; Kobierecka et al., 2017; Messaoudi et al., 2011; Musikasang et al., 2009; Sirisopapong et al., 2023). Major taxonomic revisions had to be done on the genus Lactobacillus, with bacteria historically reported as Lactobacillus reuteri and Lactobacillus salivarius now being placed in Limosilactobacillus reuteri, and Ligilactobacillus salivarius, respectively (Zheng et al., 2020). These bacteria are important members of the avian gut ecosystem and are often included when searching for potential poultry probiotics.

The Red Junglefowl (RJF; Gallus gallus), the principal wild ancestor of domestic chickens, is an attractive but underexplored source of host-associated bacteria. RJF possess ecological and phenotypic features distinct from intensively selected commercial broilers, and genomic evidence supports their close ancestral relationship with modern domestic chickens (Desta, 2019; Wang et al., 2020). Because host-associated probiotic candidates may be better suited to survival and function in the target host, the gastrointestinal tract of RJF may contain avian-adapted LAB with practical relevance for poultry production. This idea is especially relevant in Bangladesh and similar poultry-producing regions, where locally sourced probiotic candidates could reduce reliance on imported products and where published local work has focused mainly on LAB from domestic poultry rather than RJF (Kabir et al., 2016).

In addition to established lactobacilli, Weissella spp. are increasingly recognized as LAB with probiotic and biotechnological potential. Some Weissella strains show antimicrobial activity, stress tolerance, exopolysaccharide production, and other functional traits, including in recent probiogenomic studies of Weissella paramesenteroides (Ma et al., 2025; Rocha et al., 2025). At the same time, genus-level promise does not establish strain-level safety. Candidate strains intended for feed or probiotic use should therefore be evaluated through stepwise functional and safety screening, followed by genome- and MIC-based confirmation where appropriate (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012, 2018).

Despite a large body of work that has screened LAB from domestic and indigenous chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus), the gut community of their wild ancestor, the RJF, has remained an essentially untapped reservoir for poultry probiotic discovery, and this gap defines the novelty of the present work. First, the study deliberately targets the ancestral host rather than the intensively selected domestic lines that dominate the existing literature, on the rationale that LAB co-evolved with this host may be particularly well matched to the poultry gut. Second, to our knowledge it is the first study to integrate, within a single design, host-associated LAB isolation from RJF, transparent multi-trait candidate prioritization, defined multi-strain formulation, and preliminary in vivo broiler validation against a CFU-matched commercial probiotic comparator. It is also the first such report from the Bangladesh context, where earlier local efforts have characterized LAB only from domestic poultry. These features distinguish the present strains and workflow from the numerous chicken-derived LAB studies already in the literature.

The present study was designed to move from source discovery to preliminary biological validation. We isolated LAB from the crop, small intestine, large intestine, and cecum of RJF, identified representative sequence types by near full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing, evaluated probiotic-related in vitro traits, and prioritized the most balanced candidates. A selected multi-strain preparation was then evaluated in broiler chickens under controlled climatic-chamber conditions against a commercial probiotic comparator and a PBS control. We hypothesized that RJF would harbor host-associated LAB with functional traits relevant to poultry probiotic development and that the selected mixture would show encouraging growth-performance responses under controlled rearing conditions.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study was conducted in two phases. First, LAB were isolated from RJF gastrointestinal samples, identified by near full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and screened for probiotic-related in vitro traits. Second, the highest-priority candidates were combined as a multi-strain preparation and evaluated in a preliminary broiler trial against a commercial probiotic comparator and a PBS control.

Red Junglefowl collection and gastrointestinal sampling

Three RJF were collected from southeastern Bangladesh: two were collected from Shonaichhari mouza, Betbunia union, Rangamati district, and one was collected from Baishari union, Naikhongchhari upazila, Bandarban district. Since RJF are rarely available for research use and their collection is constrained by ethical, logistical, and conservation-related considerations, the source-discovery phase was limited to three birds. Birds were identified phenotypically using external characteristics described for RJF and transported to the laboratory. After collection, birds were humanely euthanized according to the approved animal-use protocol and dissected for aseptic gastrointestinal sampling (Desta, 2019). To maximize bacterial recovery from this limited material, four gastrointestinal regions (crop, small intestine, large intestine, and cecum) were sampled separately from each bird. Thus, this phase aimed to explore and identify promising bacterial strains from RJF, rather than describe the entire gut microbiota profile of RJF population at large.

Preparation of gastrointestinal homogenates and isolation of presumptive LAB

Each segment of the gastrointestinal tract underwent a brief wash with 70% ethanol to decrease surface contamination, followed by two sterile distilled water rinses. Then, tissue samples were homogenized aseptically in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) at a ratio of 1:10 according to the general method used for isolation of chicken gastrointestinal LAB (Musikasang et al., 2009). The homogenates were serially diluted ten-fold, and appropriate dilutions were spread onto de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar supplemented with 0.02% bromocresol purple. Plates were incubated anaerobically at 41°C for 48 h. Colonies with clear halos were purified by repeated streaking and screened by Gram staining and catalase testing. Gram-positive, catalase-negative isolates were retained as presumptive LAB and stored at -80°C in MRS broth containing 25% glycerol.

16S rRNA gene sequencing, phylogenetic analysis, and sequence divergence

Genomic DNA was extracted from purified isolates using a commercial bacterial genomic DNA isolation kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Near full-length 16S rRNA genes were amplified using universal primers 27F (5′‐AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG‐3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′), and purified amplicons were sequenced in both directions. Consensus sequences were compared with GenBank records using BLAST. Multiple sequence alignment and downstream sequence analyses were performed in MEGA12 using ClustalW-aligned and manually curated sequences (Kumar et al., 2024). For phylogenetic analysis, the dataset included 14 representative sequence types from the current study, together with 12 selected reference sequences representing Limosilactobacillus reuteri (n = 5), Ligilactobacillus. salivarius (n = 3), and Weissella paramesenteroides (n = 4). Lactiplantibacillus plantarum JCM 1149 (NR_117813.1) was used as the outgroup. A Neighbor-Joining tree was constructed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Variable sites and number-of-differences matrices were computed in MEGA12. The p-distance heat map was generated using Heatmapper2 after omitting the outgroup to improve within-ingroup visualization (Kernick et al., 2025).

Selection of representative isolates and general culture conditions

The 39 isolates were grouped into 14 representative sequence types. One representative from each sequence type was screened for auto-aggregation, surface hydrophobicity, antagonistic activity, acid tolerance, bile tolerance, DNase activity, hemolytic behavior, and phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility. Frozen stocks were reactivated by two successive subcultures in MRS broth under anaerobic conditions at 41°C for 24 h before each assay.

Auto-aggregation and cell-surface hydrophobicity

Auto-aggregation was measured after 24 h anaerobic culture using a standard sedimentation-based approach applied in LAB probiotic screening (Sirisopapong et al., 2023). Cultures were vortexed, allowed to stand for 4 h, and absorbance of the upper phase was measured at 660 nm at 0 and 4 h. Auto-aggregation was calculated as AAg (%) = [(A0 - At)/A0] × 100. Cell-surface hydrophobicity was determined by the xylene partition method after washing bacterial cells and adjusting the suspension to approximately 0.5 at 660 nm. Hydrophobicity was calculated as SHb (%) = [(A0 - A1)/A0] × 100. Values were described as high (>70%), medium (20-70%), or low (<20%) for comparative screening.

Antagonistic activity against indicator pathogens

Antagonistic activity was evaluated by agar well diffusion against E. coli and Salmonella sp. isolated from broiler intestine, following the general logic of LAB supernatant-based inhibition assays (Bian et al., 2010; Halder et al., 2017). Indicator cultures were adjusted to approximately McFarland 0.5 and swabbed on Mueller-Hinton agar. Cultured broth, cell-free supernatant, and resuspended cell fractions of each candidate isolate were loaded into 6-mm wells (80 µL/well). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h, and inhibition zones were measured in millimeters. Zone-diameter classes used in the heatmap were descriptive and literature-derived, not clinical breakpoints.

Acid tolerance, bile tolerance, DNase activity, and hemolysis

Acid tolerance was assessed in MRS broth adjusted to pH 2.5, 5.0, and 7.5, and bile tolerance was assessed in MRS containing 0.3% Ox gall, with modifications from commonly used probiotic-screening protocols (Sirisopapong et al., 2023; Todorov et al., 2012). After inoculation and anaerobic incubation at 41°C for 24 h, absorbance was measured at 600 nm. Growth inhibition (%) was calculated relative to control broth as [(Acontrol - Atest)/Acontrol] × 100. Growth inhibition <20%, 20-70%, and >70% was interpreted as high, medium, and low tolerance, respectively. DNase activity was assessed on DNase agar, and hemolysis was evaluated on 5% sheep blood agar after anaerobic incubation. Only DNase-negative and gamma-hemolytic isolates were considered eligible for further prioritization.

Phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility and candidate prioritization

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed by the disc diffusion method using antibiotics commonly used in the Bangladesh poultry sector, namely amoxicillin, neomycin, doxycycline, gentamicin, and ciprofloxacin. Inhibition zones were classified for screening as sensitive (≥21 mm), intermediate (16-20 mm), or resistant (≤15 mm), following the descriptive scheme used in the original screening design. Antibiogram results were summarized as phenotypic caution tiers but were not used alone to exclude isolates, because safety assessment of bacterial feed candidates requires MIC- and genome-based evaluation (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012, 2018). To reduce subjectivity, candidate priority was assigned using a predefined, criterion-based scheme rather than an overall subjective impression. Each isolate first had to pass two mandatory safety gates, namely a DNase-negative phenotype and a gamma (non)-hemolytic phenotype; any isolate failing either gate was excluded from Priority I irrespective of its other traits. Among isolates passing both gates, Priority I required the simultaneous fulfilment of four functional criteria: high auto-aggregation (>70%), high surface hydrophobicity (>70%), at least medium acid tolerance under the most stringent challenge (pH 2.5), and high bile tolerance in 0.3% Ox gall, together with measurable antagonistic activity against at least one indicator pathogen. Isolates that showed strong adhesion-related traits but did not meet the combined acid-and-bile tolerance threshold were designated Priority II (reserve candidates), and all remaining isolates were designated Priority III. The antibiotic caution tier was recorded as a flag for downstream genome- and MIC-based confirmation but was not applied as a stand-alone exclusion criterion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012, 2018). Applying this scheme to the 14 representative isolates, only three strains met every Priority I criterion. Strains classified as Priority I showed the most balanced functional and safety profile. Therefore, three Priority I strains were selected for the preparation of a multi-strain candidate probiotic mixture and subsequent preliminary in vivo validation in broiler chickens.

Preliminary in vivo validation in broiler chickens

Preparation of multi-strain candidate probiotic mixture and commercial comparator. Three representative Priority I strains, Limosilactobacillus reuteri-1 (GenBank accession no. MK572785), Ligilactobacillus salivarius-3 (MK572795), and Weissella paramesenteroides-3 (MK572798) were selected and reactivated anaerobically. The overnight cultures were harvested, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended, and viable count-adjusted to be 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL for each strain suspension. Equal volumes of the three standardized suspensions were then combined (1:1:1) to prepare the RJF-derived candidate mixture (RJF-mix), giving a final total concentration of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, with each strain contributing approximately 3.33 × 10⁶ CFU/mL. The RJF-mix was prepared freshly before each administration. A commercial probiotic product (CPP), PoultryStar Sol (Renata PLC), containing Enterococcus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., Pediococcus spp., and Lactobacillus spp., with a labeled concentration of 5 × 10¹² CFU/kg, was used as the comparator. The recommended dose from the manufacturer was 2 g per 1,000 birds (0.002 g/bird), which equivalent to an estimated delivered dose of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/bird (5 × 10⁹ CFU/g × 0.002 g). For each administration, the required amount of CPP powder was suspended in sterile PBS to provide 1 mL per bird at 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, thereby matching the RJF-mix on a CFU basis.

Birds, housing and environmental management. A total of 108 one-day-old Arbor Acres broiler chicks of mixed sex were obtained from a local hatchery and routinely vaccinated against Newcastle disease and infectious bursal disease. Birds were reared in a vertical three-tier cage system (two cages per tier; each cage 0.7 m × 0.8 m × 0.4 m) placed within an environmentally controlled climatic chamber (3.0 m × 3.0 m × 3.0 m) equipped with heating, cooling, humidification, dehumidification, and automated fresh-air exchange. Temperature and relative humidity (RH) were adjusted according to age-specific Arbor Acres management recommendations (Aviagen, 2025). Temperature, humidity and carbon-di-oxide (CO₂) was monitored using an SCD30 sensor, and ventilation was activated when CO₂ reached 1,000 ppm. Temperature-Humidity Index (THI) was calculated according to the equation of Tao and Xin (2003) when chamber thermal comfort was summarized.

Diets and feeding. Birds received a commercial starter diet (d 1-15) and finisher diet (d 16-35), and feed and water were offered ad libitum throughout the study.

Experimental design and probiotic administration. Chicks were randomly assigned to three treatments (36 birds/treatment): Ctrl (PBS only), RJF-mix, and CPP. Each treatment consisted of 6 replicate pens with 6 birds per replicate. On d 7, 14, 21, and 28, birds in the RJF-mix and CPP groups received 1 mL/bird of the assigned preparation by oral administration, while Ctrl birds received 1 mL PBS. The trial ended on d 35.

Climatic-chamber management and performance recording. Environmental monitoring was used mainly to confirm that the birds were kept under stable housing conditions, rather than as a primary biological outcome. Body weight was recorded at scheduled time points, feed intake was measured at the pen level, and body weight gain, feed intake, feed conversion ratio, and mortality were calculated. Climatic-chamber records were then summarized to verify that temperature, RH, THI, and CO₂ remained close to the intended management targets. Any short-term deviations from these targets were considered expected and were mainly associated with automatic ventilation cycles and routine entry into the chamber for bird management.

Statistical analysis

Data from in vitro assays were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from independent replicate measurements. For continuous in vitro variables, differences among representative isolates were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test when model assumptions were met. Normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance were assessed before analysis. When assumptions were not satisfied, an appropriate non-parametric alternative was used. Antagonistic-activity and antibiogram heat maps were presented primarily as descriptive visual summaries. For the broiler trial, pen was considered the experimental unit for feed intake, body weight gain, feed conversion ratio (FCR), and endpoint treatment comparisons. Body-weight data collected over time were analyzed using a mixed-effects model with treatment, age, and treatment × age as fixed effects and pen as a random effect. Endpoint variables, including final body weight, total body weight gain, total feed intake, and final cumulative FCR, were analyzed at the pen level using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons. Mortality was summarized descriptively because no mortality occurred during the trial. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

The distribution of taxa across gastrointestinal segments was not uniform. L. reuteri was recovered from all sampled regions and was most common in the large intestine and crop, whereas L. salivarius was frequently represented in the cecum. W. paramesenteroides was recovered from the small intestine and cecum. This distribution supported the decision to retain representative sequence types rather than selecting isolates by abundance alone.

Variable-site analysis revealed clear differences between species, but very little variation within each species group. When the outgroup was included, 364 of the 1,586 aligned positions were variable. After removing the outgroup, 333 of 1,586 sites remained variable. Considering only the 14 isolates from the current study, 238 of 1,586 positions were variable.

In contrast, variation within individual species groups was much lower. Among the L. reuteri sequence types (n = 8), only 5 of 1,586 sites were variable. Similarly, the L. salivarius sequence types (n = 3) showed variation at only 3 sites, and the W. paramesenteroides sequence types (n = 3) showed variation at only 5 sites. Overall, these results indicate that most sequence variation occurs between species rather than within species. This pattern was further supported by the number-of-differences analysis which exhibited substantial species-level separation but low genetic divergence within isolates of the same species.

In vitro probiotic-related traits

The adhesion-related and gastrointestinal tolerance profiles of the 14 representative isolates were differed substantially (Table 2). The 14 representative isolates differed substantially in adhesion-related and gastrointestinal tolerance traits (Table 2). Seven isolates showed high auto-aggregation, whereas hydrophobicity also varied widely across taxa. Candidate prioritization

Based on the integrated in vitro profile, and applying the predefined criterion-based prioritization scheme described in the Materials and Methods, only L. reuteri-1, L. salivarius-3, and W. paramesenteroides-3 were classified as Priority I candidates. These isolates combined favorable adhesion-related traits, the strongest acid/bile tolerance balance, measurable antagonistic activity, DNase-negative and non-hemolytic behavior, and interpretable antibiotic-screening profiles. L. reuteri-7 was retained as a Priority II reserve candidate.

Discussion

The present study supports the hypothesis that RJF can be a relevant biological source of host-associated LAB for poultry probiotic discovery. The isolation of 39 presumptive LAB from only three RJF birds, which were subsequently clustered into 14 representative sequence types based on sequence variation, suggests that the gastrointestinal tract of RJF harbors considerable cultivable LAB diversity, even within a small exploratory study (Table 1). The predominance of L. reuteri and recovery of L. salivarius are supported by previous studies that have shown that chicken intestinal LAB frequently include lactobacilli with probiotic-relevant properties (Blajman et al., 2015; Kobierecka et al., 2017; Messaoudi et al., 2011; Musikasang et al., 2009; Sirisopapong et al., 2023). Moreover, the identification of W. paramesenteroides is of interest because Weissella has gained emerging attention as a promising probiotic and potential biotechnological application species, although strain-level safety confirmation remains essential (Ma et al., 2025; Rocha et al., 2025).

The present study is characterized by the fact that it makes use of RJF as the source host. It has been suggested that the genomic characteristics between RJF and domestic chickens indicate its ancestral role in chicken domestication (Wang et al., 2020). Junglefowl also exhibits complex adaptive phenotypic and ecological traits that are distinct from intensively selected commercial lines (Desta, 2019). Although this does not prove that their gut microbiota contains superior probiotics, it offers a reasonable biological basis for evaluating the gastrointestinal tract as a source of avian-adapted LAB. This is also relevant in Bangladesh, where earlier work has characterized LAB from domestic poultry sources whereas RJF have not yet been adequately assessed as a potential reservoir of beneficial microbes (Kabir et al., 2016). The phylogenetic and sequence-divergence analyses strengthen the taxonomical aspects of the study. Strains from the current study grouped with their respective reference lineages and formed clearly delineated clusters away from the outgroup, while the p-distance heat map provided a clear visualization of ingroup relatedness (Fig. 1). The results of the variable-site and number-of-differences analysis provided a quantitative characterization of sequence variation. As 16S rRNA is highly conserved, these analyses should be viewed as species-level confirmation and sequence-type differentiation rather than as a substitute for whole-genome strain resolution.

The novelty of this study is best framed in relation to the existing literature. Numerous reports have screened LAB with probiotic potential from domestic and indigenous chickens, and species such as L. reuteri and L. salivarius are recovered repeatedly from poultry. The present work is therefore distinguished less by the species names recovered than by the source host and the integrated discovery-to-validation pathway. By targeting RJF, the wild progenitor of the domestic chicken, the study deliberately explores a reservoir of avian-adapted LAB that has been largely overlooked in the chicken probiotic literature, including in Bangladesh, where earlier screening has focused on domestic poultry (Kabir et al., 2016). In addition, the recovery of W. paramesenteroides alongside the dominant lactobacilli adds to the still-limited body of work on host-associated Weissella from poultry (Ma et al., 2025; Rocha et al., 2025). Beyond the source, the study links a transparent, criterion-based candidate-prioritization scheme to a defined multi-strain RJF formulation that was then benchmarked in vivo against a CFU-matched commercial probiotic under controlled climatic-chamber conditions, an end-to-end design that is uncommon among single-study reports. Taken together, these elements differentiate the present strains and approach from the many chicken-derived LAB studies already published, while the strains themselves are deliberately positioned as promising candidates pending genome-based confirmation and comprehensive safety assessment.

The in vitro screening results shown in Table 2 demonstrate why candidate selection should not rely on a single assay. Indirect measures of adhesion-related attributes, such as auto-aggregation and surface hydrophobicity, indicate possible host-interaction potential; antimicrobial activity suggests pathogen-inhibitory potential; and acid/bile assays mimic important stress conditions encountered in the gastrointestinal tract (Sirisopapong et al., 2023; Todorov et al., 2012). The challenge at pH 2.5 represents a stringent upper-gastrointestinal acid stress, whereas growth at pH 5.0 and 7.5 reflects tolerance under milder pH conditions relevant to much of the intestinal environment. This interpretation is consistent with the dynamic pH conditions and microbial succession patterns described for the broiler gastrointestinal tract (Ranjitkar et al., 2016).

Antagonistic activity was demonstrated principally in the cultured broth and cell-free supernatant fractions. This indicates that there were substances released outside the bacterial cells which may have contributed to the inhibition of E. coli and Salmonella sp. Such evidence is biologically plausible because LAB are known to inhibit pathogenic by excreting organic acids, bacteriocin-like substances and secreted antimicrobial compounds (Bian et al., 2010; Halder et al., 2017). The difference in inhibitory activity against E. coli and Salmonella sp. also shows that LAB antagonism is both strain-dependent and target-dependent. Therefore, the antimicrobial heat map (Fig. 2) should be interpreted as a comparative screening tool, not as direct evidence that these isolates can control pathogens under in vivo conditions.

The probiotic candidate selection strategy employed in this study is more robust than choosing strains based only on their abundance. The selected three strains, L. reuteri-1, L. salivarius-3, and W. paramesenteroides-3, showed the most balanced overall probiotic profile. These strains performed well across several important traits, including adhesion-related properties, acid and bile tolerance, antagonistic activity, DNase-negative and gamma-hemolytic behavior, and antibiotic caution tier. This integrated approach is consistent with poultry probiotic screening studies, where several in vitro characteristics are considered together to narrow down promising candidates before testing them in animals (Blajman et al., 2015; Kobierecka et al., 2017; Sirisopapong et al., 2023).

Safety screening of the tested strains should be interpreted with caution. Favorable baseline safety characteristics are also suggested by the DNase-negative and gamma-hemolytic results. Nevertheless, these results are not sufficient to ensure total safety of the target candidate probiotics. Likewise, disc-diffusion antibiogram results are valuable for comparing phenotypic antibiotic responses among strains but cannot be used to provide proven antimicrobial-resistance characterization. Current feed-additive safety guidelines emphasize the need for MIC-based antimicrobial susceptibility testing and evaluation of the genetic basis of resistance (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012, 2018). Therefore, the antibiogram heat map (Fig. 3) should be used only as a prioritization aid. Before practical application, the selected strains should be examined further by whole-genome sequencing and MIC-based antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Until such genome-based characterization, including screening for virulence factors and transferable antimicrobial-resistance determinants, has been completed, the present isolates should be regarded strictly as probiotic candidates and not as validated probiotics suitable for commercial development.

The preliminary broiler trial provides an important link between in vitro screening and animal-level performance. The RJF-Mix improved final body weight and feed conversion ratio compared with the PBS control and showed performance close to that of the CPP group. This result agrees with recent poultry probiotic studies showing that probiotic supplementation can support growth performance, feed efficiency, gut microbial balance, and immune function (Dong et al., 2023; Halder et al., 2024; Jha et al., 2020; Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). Interestingly, the RJF-Mix did not increase total feed intake, suggesting that the improved performance was likely related to better feed utilization rather than simply greater feed consumption.

The use of a climatic chamber is a strength of this study because it improved experimental control. By regulating temperature, RH, ventilation, and CO₂ concentration, the chamber likely reduced environmental variation and allowed the biological effect of the candidate probiotic mixture to be observed more clearly. Management according to Arbor Acres recommendations and monitoring of the thermal environment also strengthened the consistency of the in vivo validation (Aviagen, 2022, 2025; Tao and Xin, 2003). However, controlled chamber conditions are different from open-sided or commercial poultry houses, where birds are exposed to greater environmental fluctuation. For this reason, the broiler trial should be presented as preliminary validation under controlled conditions, not as final proof of field-level efficacy. Larger trials under semi-commercial or commercial production conditions are still needed.

Several limitations of the present study should also be acknowledged. First, the source-discovery phase was limited to three RJF because live birds were rarely available for research use and their collection was constrained by ethical, logistical, and conservation-related considerations. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as defining the full LAB diversity, prevalence, or population-level gut microbiota structure of RJF. Rather, the study should be viewed as an exploratory strain-discovery effort. To make the best use of the limited source material, multiple gastrointestinal regions were sampled separately, which allowed recovery of 39 presumptive LAB isolates and their classification into 14 representative sequence types. Second, near full-length 16S rRNA sequencing supported species-level identification and sequence-type grouping, but it cannot fully resolve strain-level functional potential or safety. Third, the in vitro assays used here are useful for comparative screening but do not prove intestinal colonization, mechanism of action, or field efficacy. Reproducibility and future validation are supported by the detailed isolation, identification, screening, and broiler-validation workflow; the GenBank accession numbers of representative strains; and preservation of the selected strains for further verification. The selected strains should therefore undergo whole-genome sequencing, MIC-based antimicrobial susceptibility testing, virulence/resistance gene screening, and larger broiler trials under commercial or semi-commercial conditions before practical application. Collectively, the small number of source birds (n = 3), the use of 16S rRNA gene sequencing rather than whole-genome data for identification, and the absence of a genome-based safety assessment limit the strength of the probiotic claims that can be made at this stage. For this reason, the conclusions are presented as exploratory and the isolates as probiotic candidates that require genomic confirmation, comprehensive safety evaluation, and larger-scale validation before any commercial consideration.

Conclusion

This exploratory study suggests that the gastrointestinal samples of RJF yielded a panel of host-associated LAB dominated by L. reuteri, L. salivarius, and W. paramesenteroides. Among 14 representative sequence types, L. reuteri-1, L. salivarius-3, and W. paramesenteroides-3 showed the most balanced probiotic-related screening profiles and were selected for multi-strain formulation. Preliminary broiler validation indicated that the selected RJF-Mix improved growth performance and feed efficiency relative to the PBS control and performed comparably to the commercial probiotic product under controlled climatic-chamber conditions. These results highlight the RJF, the wild ancestor of the domestic chicken, as a novel and still underexplored source of poultry-relevant probiotic candidates, while emphasizing the need for whole-genome characterization, MIC-based safety assessment, and larger field-scale validation before practical application.
