Is there any association between latent Toxoplasma gondii seropositivity and hypothyroidism? A case-control registry-based study
Authors: Zahra Hosseininejad, Ozra Akha, Eisa Nazar, Ali Sharifpour, Mahboobeh Mosavimehr, Mahdi Fakhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypothyroidism is a relatively common endocrine disorder in human societies. Toxoplasmosis is also one of the most prevalent parasitic infectious diseases in mammalian hosts, causing opportunistic infections in healthy individuals and complications in immunocompromised patients. This infection is associated with several autoimmune diseases, including autoimmune thyroid disease. This case-control study aims to investigate latent T. gondii infection in patients with hypothyroidism who were registered with the Iranian National Registry Center for Toxoplasmosis (INRCT).MethodsWe conducted a case-control study involving 118 patients diagnosed with hypothyroidism and 100 subjects without hypothyroidism, serving as the control group. The Toxoplasma status was confirmed through the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for anti-Toxoplasma IgG and IgM antibodies. Levels of T3, T4, and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) were measured using chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA).ResultsAnti-Toxoplasma IgG was detected in 69.5% of patients with hypothyroidism (82/118) and in 35.0% of the control group (35/100) (P < 0.05). However, all participants were negative for anti-Toxoplasma IgM. Based on the results from the multiple logistic regression (LR) model, and after adjusting for confounding factors, we found that the likelihood of hypothyroidism in subjects with Toxoplasma infection is 2.4 times greater than that of seronegative individuals.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate a significant association between latent T. gondii infection (IgG seropositivity) and hypothyroidism, supporting a potential role for chronic toxoplasmosis in thyroid dysfunction. These findings contribute to the understanding of potential links between chronic infections and endocrine dysfunction. However, the clinical utility of routine T. gondii screening in hypothyroid patients remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Hypothyroidism, Seropositivity, Toxoplasma gondii, Iran

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) is an intracellular parasitic protozoan [1] that, unlike many parasites, has a high prevalence due to its broad host range and various transmission routes in its life cycle [2]. This parasite is responsible for deaths and complications all over the world. T. gondii is transmitted through water and food contaminated with sporulated oocysts of the parasite, as well as raw or undercooked meat and animal products containing cysts [3]. Another way of contracting the parasite is vertical transmission, which occurs due to the transfer of the parasite from the mother to the fetus during pregnancy [4].

T. gondii attacks all organs and glands of the body, including the thyroid gland. This parasite is also associated with autoimmune diseases, such as autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) [5].

The activation and proliferation of macrophages and TH2 cells, as well as the production of interferon by natural killer cells, is done by cytokines, ultimately leading to the control of T. gondii infection in the host’s body [6]. On the other hand, in some autoimmune diseases such as AITD, systemic lupus erythematosus, and rheumatoid arthritis, an increase in the level of cytokines is seen [7].

The interplay between the immune system and thyroid hormones (THs) is currently well established, although the exact mechanism is not yet fully understood. These trigger an immune response against microbial or sterile irritants, these hormones induce migration, proliferation, antibody production, and release of cytokines in leukocytes. At the same time, the pathophysiological changes in the immune system, such as infections and inflammation, affect the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis, the direct result of which is the effect on the mechanism of thyroid hormone activity [8].

There is little information about Toxoplasma infection in the hypothyroidism, but in a study where cases of disseminated toxoplasmosis were autopsied, researchers observed thyroid gland involvement [3]. In addition, thyroid dysfunction has been reported in mouse toxoplasmosis [9]. It is interesting to note that increased susceptibility to infectious diseases has been seen in hypothyroidism [10, 11]. Overall, while T. gondii infection may contribute to thyroid dysfunction in certain populations, such as pregnant women or autoimmune thyroid diseases, the evidence remains inconsistent and suggests a complex interplay between infection, autoimmunity, and thyroid health.

The epidemiology of toxoplasmosis in northern Iran demonstrates a particularly high disease burden, as evidenced by a recent meta-analysis encompassing 2000–2023 data. This comprehensive study revealed a striking regional seroprevalence of 61%, significantly exceeding both the national Iranian average and global estimates [12]. This study aimed to investigate the association between Toxoplasma gondii seropositivity and hypothyroidism among patients who were subjected to the Iranian National Registry Center for Toxoplasmosis (INRCT) at the Imam Khomeini Teaching Hospital, Sari, northern Iran.

Patients and methods

Study design and sample collection

The Research Ethical Review Committee from Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences approved this study (IR.MAZUMS.REC.1399.824). This study was carried out following the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This case-control study included the assessment of 118 patients suffering from hypothyroidism and 100 healthy subjects without hypothyroidism who registered at INRCT as a matched control group. The controls were matched to the cases based on age and gender to minimize confounding factors. The control samples were collected from the same geographic area and healthcare settings as the cases to ensure comparability.

The patients with hypothyroidism attended the endocrinology clinic in Sari City, northern Iran, between 2022 and 2023; the corresponding age range was 7–85 years. The demographic variables, including age, gender, residency, marital status, living place, and educational level, was collected from each participant using a special informative questionnaire.

A total of 5 mL of venous blood was obtained through venipuncture using a sterile disposable syringe. The blood was drawn in a plain tube without anticoagulant, labeled appropriately, and allowed to clot at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum. Each serum samples were transferred into a sterile 2 mL labeled microtube, and stored at -20 °C until further use.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients with confirmed hypothyroidism, identified through a combination of positive clinical symptoms and confirmed hormonal changes, were included in this study. All participants with a history of other infectious diseases, particularly those taking antibiotics or with immune deficiencies, and those who unwillingness subjects to participate in the study were excluded from this study.

Detection of anti-T. gondii IgG and IgM antibodies

Detection of anti-T. gondii IgG antibodies were performed using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit: “Toxo IgG” (PishtazTeb, Iran). Negative and positive controls provided by the manufacturer were included in each run, and the test procedure was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Anti-Toxoplasma IgG antibody levels were interpreted according to the manufacturer’s specifications: samples with values > 11 IU/mL were considered positive, 9–11 IU/mL as borderline, and < 9 IU/mL as negative (with kit-reported sensitivity and specificity approaching 100%). For IgM antibodies, values < 0.9 were classified as negative, 0.9–1.1 as borderline, and > 1.1 as positive.

Detection of T3, T4, and TSH hormones by CLIA

The chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) with Siemens diagnostic kit (Germany) and VIDAS instrument was employed to analyze TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), T3, and T4 to diagnose thyroid dysfunction. The test procedure was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the control provided by the manufacturer was included in each run.

The assay’s analytical sensitivity was 0.01 µIU/mL, with intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) of 2.1–3.5% and 3.0-4.8%, respectively, ensuring high precision. The reference ranges used were 0.4–4.0 µIU/mL for TSH, as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. Similarly, T3 and T4 levels were quantified, with sensitivity limits of 0.20 ng/dL and 0.40 µg/dL, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Group differences between cases and controls were evaluated using appropriate statistical tests based on variable types. For categorical variables (including demographic characteristics and seropositivity rates), we used chi-square (χ²) test when expected frequencies exceeded 5 in all cells; otherwise, Fisher’s exact (Freeman-Halton) test was applied. Continuous variables (such as hormone levels) were analyzed using independent t-tests for normally distributed data or Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data, with normality assessed via Shapiro-Wilk test. To examine associations between independent and dependent variables, we employed odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals and the multiple logistic regression (LR) model (adjusted for confounders like age and gender) for categorical outcomes (e.g., Toxoplasma infection status). For continuous outcomes (e.g., TSH levels), Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated, and linear regression models were applied where appropriate. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Our study of 218 participants (56.4% female, 43.6% male) revealed significant demographic and serological associations with hypothyroidism. The data showed an overall T. gondii IgG seroprevalence of 53.7%, with markedly higher rates in hypothyroid cases (69.5%) versus controls (35.0%, p < 0.05). Moreover, anti-Toxoplasma IgM was not detected in any of the participants. Demographic analysis (Table 1) identified key group differences: hypothyroid participants were predominantly older (50–59 years: 27.1% vs. 1% in controls, p < 0.001), female (79.7% vs. 29%, p < 0.001), and married (94.9% vs. 50%, p < 0.001). Controls had higher education levels (84% diploma + vs. 56.8%, p < 0.001), while residential distribution showed no significance (p = 0.20).

Male gender showed a protective effect (AOR = 0.10, p < 0.001), while married status strongly associated with hypothyroidism (AOR = 12.14, p < 0.001). The model’s excellent predictive accuracy was confirmed by an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (AUC (area under the curve) = 0.903), indicating robust discrimination between groups. These findings suggest T. gondii infection may represent a modifiable risk factor for hypothyroidism, particularly in women and older married individuals, though the cross-sectional design warrants cautious interpretation of causality.

Discussion

The past two decades have witnessed an increase in the prevalence of thyroid disease worldwide [13]. However, the magnitude of the problem is not fully understood, nor is its relationship with other health conditions well established [14]. Although the Toxoplasma parasite has been found in the human thyroid gland, it is not well understood whether thyroid dysfunction is related to Toxoplasma infection [3]. In this research, by designing a case-control study, we sought to investigate the seroprevalence of Toxoplasma and its correlation with demographic and risk factors in hypothyroidism patients subjected to INRCT at Imam Khomeini Teaching Hospital in Sari located in the north of Iran.

Generally, in the present study, the seroprevalence of Toxoplasma in hypothyroid patients was significantly higher than in the control group (69.50% versus 35%). In a study conducted in Iraq by Murad et al. on pregnant women, there was a significant difference between toxoplasmosis seropositive and seronegative women in terms of hypothyroidism (59% vs. 43%) [4]. Also, in a study by Shapira et al. investigating the relationship between autoimmune diseases and toxoplasmosis, a statistically significant association was observed between AITD and Toxoplasma infection (p < 0.0001) [15]. However, a study conducted in 2019 by Alvarado et al. in Mexico did not report a significant relationship between Toxoplasma infection and thyroid dysfunction [3].

The pro-inflammatory cytokines released during an infectious disease can enhance negative feedback on thyroid hormones, resulting in reduced TSH secretion from the pituitary gland, potentially leading to hypothyroidism [16]. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 increase CRP protein secretion from the liver, which has been associated with hypothyroidism [17]. Moreover, thyroid dysfunction, predominantly hypothyroidism, has been reported in patients with malignant tumors and hepatitis treated with gamma interferon [18].

Given that the host immune response to Toxoplasma infection triggers the release of cytokines, such as interleukin-6 and interferon-gamma [19], and the relationship above between cytokines and hypothyroidism, the results of our research on a significant increase in hypothyroidism in people with a history of toxoplasmosis can be justified.

Haddad et al. reported significantly elevated serum IFN-γ concentrations and expression of the IFN-γ gene in Toxoplasma-seropositive patients with autoimmune thyroid disorders compared to seronegative patients [20].

Another way to explore the relationship between thyroid dysfunction and toxoplasmosis is whether hypothyroidism makes individuals more susceptible to toxoplasmosis. Hypothyroidism has been linked to a decrease in lymphocyte proliferation [21] and reduced levels of cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ [22], it can be concluded that the dysfunction of the immune system in hypothyroid individuals, makes them susceptible to toxoplasmosis. This could cause the higher incidence of toxoplasmosis observed in our study’s hypothyroid population compared to the healthy population.

Studies on the prevalence of T. gondii infection among individuals with hypothyroidism or thyroid dysfunction show varied results. Some research indicates that the seroprevalence of T. gondii antibodies in patients with thyroid dysfunction is comparable to or even lower than that in control groups without thyroid disease, with reported rates around 6.3% to 14.6% in thyroid patients versus 9.1% to 18.2% in controls [3, 4]. However, other studies, particularly those focusing on pregnant women or AITD, have found a higher prevalence of T. gondii infection associated with thyroid hormone changes and autoimmune markers, suggesting a possible link between latent toxoplasmosis and thyroid autoimmunity or dysfunction [5, 23]. For example, in pregnant women, seropositivity for T. gondii has been correlated with increased rates of hypothyroidism and autoimmune thyroid antibodies, potentially due to immune cross-reactivity [5]. Despite these associations, some studies report no statistically significant difference in T. gondii seroprevalence between hypothyroid or hyperthyroid patients and healthy controls, highlighting the need for further research to clarify the role of T. gondii in thyroid disorders [24]. As a whole, while T. gondii infection may play a role in thyroid dysfunction in specific populations-such as pregnant women the existing evidence is inconsistent and points to a complex interaction between the infection, immune responses, and thyroid function.

A critical consideration in interpreting our results is the potential for confounding by age. The seroprevalence of T. gondii IgG is known to increase with age due to cumulative exposure over a lifetime [25–27]. Similarly, the incidence of hypothyroidism also rises significantly in older populations. It is therefore reasonable that the association we observed is partly driven by this shared relationship with age, rather than a direct pathophysiological link. Our multiple logistic regression model adjusted for age as a categorical variable, and the association remained significant, suggesting an independent effect. However, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. This pattern is seen in studies linking latent toxoplasmosis to other chronic conditions of aging, such as certain neurodegenerative diseases or cardiovascular risk factors, where disentangling causation from co-occurrence remains a challenge [28, 29]. Future studies with a longitudinal design and a more granular age-matching strategy would be essential to clarify whether T. gondii infection is a true risk factor or an age-associated epiphenomenon in the development of hypothyroidism.

Limitations

Several important limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. The primary constraint was our sample size, which restricted our ability to conduct more granular analyses of subgroups within the study population. This limitation necessitates cautious interpretation of the results, particularly regarding potential associations between variables. Additionally, our cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences, meaning we cannot determine temporal relationships between the observed variables. Future research with larger, more diverse samples and longitudinal designs would help address these limitations and provide more definitive conclusions.

Conclusion

Based on our serological analysis, we observed a significant prevalence of anti-T. gondii IgG antibodies among hypothyroidism patients, suggesting potential associations between latent toxoplasmosis and thyroid dysfunction. The absence of IgM-positive cases in our registry- based study indicates that recent T. gondii infections were not detected in this population. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence examining the relationship between chronic parasitic infections and endocrine disorders. However, the cross-sectional nature of our study design precludes causal interpretations. To move from association to causation, future research should prioritize longitudinal cohorts that measure T. gondii serostatus prior to the onset of thyroid dysfunction. Furthermore, mechanistic studies are needed to investigate whether specific T. gondii strains, host genetic factors in immune response (e.g., HLA haplotypes), or direct parasitic effects on thyroid tissue mediate this association. Combining serological profiling with assessments of thyroid autoimmunity (e.g., TPO antibodies) and IgG avidity testing could also help distinguish between a trigger for autoimmunity and a contributor to non-autoimmune thyroid failure.
