“Thinking traces” in large reasoning models: Cognitive cost or performative scaffolding?
Authors: Yueqing Hu
Published: 2026

De Varda et al. (1) report a robust alignment between large reasoning models’ chain-of-thought (CoT) length and human reaction times across several reasoning domains, and interpret this as evidence that the “cost of thinking” is similar in models and humans. The empirical pattern is striking: Trace length tracks item difficulty within tasks and remains predictive even when problem length is accounted for. However, correlation alone does not establish that intermediate token generation indexes the causal computations that produce correct answers, nor that token count reflects the same bottlenecks that govern human reaction time.

A key inferential gap is that human reaction time aggregates perceptual encoding, working-memory maintenance, strategy selection, and motor execution, whereas model “effort” is operationalized as the number of generated intermediate tokens. A shared monotonic relationship with item difficulty could therefore arise even if the limiting factors differ. In particular, token length may reflect a learned performative scaffolding (how much the model “talks through” a problem) rather than incremental internal computation.

One plausible alternative is that intermediate tokens function as performative scaffolding: sequences that condition subsequent generations without faithfully representing the computations yielding the final answer. Stechly et al. (2) show that “reasonless” intermediate tokens can improve success on planning and reasoning tasks, including cases where intermediate content is semantically invalid or transferable across items, implying that trace strings can be nonfaithful even when useful.

Related analyses further suggest that trace length is sensitive to prompting and decoding choices. Palod et al. (3) argue that the correlation between CoT length and problem complexity can be brittle, consistent with length reflecting policy artifacts rather than an invariant effort variable. This motivates a robustness check for (1): Does the length–reaction time alignment persist under controlled perturbations that systematically change trace length while holding accuracy approximately constant (e.g., alternative prompts, decoding parameters, or explicit length constraints), and does the inferred mapping remain stable when length is manipulated independently of correctness?

Posttraining may further amplify this confound. Samineni et al. (4) note that trajectory-level credit assignment in reinforcement learning (RL)-style posttraining can indirectly favor longer intermediate sequences under some objectives. A useful diagnostic would be to report whether trace length correlates with reward/advantage proxies during posttraining, and whether adding or strengthening length costs alters both accuracy and the length–reaction time relationship.

Finally, trace usefulness for models does not guarantee cognitive interpretability for humans. Bhambri et al. (5) find that DeepSeek-R1 traces yield strong downstream model gains yet are judged least interpretable relative to alternative trace types, consistent with traces optimizing for model-internal utility rather than human-readable logical structure.

We therefore encourage caution in interpreting token count as a cost of thinking. Token length may be a valuable behavioral marker of difficulty, but mechanistic claims about shared cost would benefit from complementary causal and faithfulness-focused checks (inference-time truncation/padding with accuracy monitoring; faithfulness metrics such as step validity and answer consistency; and sensitivity analyses across prompts/decoding and training reward). These additions would help distinguish shared computational constraints from trace-generation policies shaped by training and decoding.
