Universal L 2 L_{2} -approximation using median lattice algorithms
Zexin Pan, Takashi Goda, Peter Kritzer

Abstract
We study the problem of multivariate L 2 L_{2} -approximation of functions in a weighted Korobov space using a median lattice-based algorithm recently proposed by the authors. In the original work, the algorithm requires knowledge of the smoothness and weights of the Korobov space to construct the hyperbolic cross index set, where each coefficient is estimated via the median of approximations obtained from randomly shifted, randomly chosen rank-1 lattice rules. In this paper, we introduce a universal median lattice-based algorithm , which eliminates the need for any prior information on smoothness and weights. Although the tractability property of the algorithm slightly deteriorates, we prove that, for individual functions in the Korobov space with arbitrary smoothness and (downward-closed) weights, it achieves an L 2 L_{2} -approximation error arbitrarily close to the optimal rate with respect to the number of function evaluations.

multivariate
L_{2}
-approximation, median algorithm,
rank-1 lattice rule, weighted Korobov space, universality

Introduction

This paper deals with the classical problem of approximating functions in the
L_{2}
-norm, defined on a so-called
weighted Korobov space
, which consists of
d
-variate, one-periodic functions with sufficiently fast decaying Fourier coefficients. Korobov spaces are reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, and it is known that their elements can be represented pointwise by absolutely convergent Fourier series, i.e., we are dealing with functions of the form

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}),

where, as usual,

\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{h}}):=\int_{[0,1)^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\exp({-2\pi\mathtt{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

is the
\boldsymbol{h}
-th Fourier coefficient of
f
for
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
\mathbb{Z}
denotes the set of integers.

Korobov spaces are
of particular interest in the context of
quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) rules
, and have been studied frequently in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
21
]
and the references therein. Indeed, we shall also use QMC methods in the present paper; these methods are equal-weight quadrature rules that are frequently used for the numerical integration of multivariate functions. To be more precise, QMC rules have the form

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}g(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\approx\int_{[0,1]^{d}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

for a suitably defined
g
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}
are carefully selected sampling points.

In the context of

L_{2}
-approximation, QMC rules are typically employed for approximately computing suitably selected Fourier coefficients of the function to be approximated, or in splines. Previous work analyzing such methods include
[
1
,
3
,
5
,
18
,
19
,
22
,
27
]
and many others. The approach considered here uses
rank-1 lattice point sets
as the QMC samples, and in particular studies the use of so-called
median rules
. Median rules have first been dealt with for numerical integration and are based on the following principle. When working with QMC integration rules, one often introduces a suitable randomization thereof, and then repeats the integration algorithm for independent realizations of the randomized rule. For median rules, one then takes the median of these independent instances as an approximation to an integral. A considerable advantage of median rules over usual QMC rules is that they are frequently independent of the parameters (as, e.g., the smoothness) of particular function spaces, and therefore offer a high degree of
universality
. Median rules for numerical integration have been presented, e.g., in
[
11
,
12
,
23
]
. In the recent paper
[
22
]
, the authors have successfully used median lattice algorithms for
L_{2}
-approximation in weighted Korobov spaces. However, a drawback of the results in that paper is the partial loss of the universality of the median approach, due to the definition of the algorithm in
[
22
]
. The goal of the present paper is to provide an alternative algorithm that regains this universality. As we shall see below, our algorithm can be shown to converge at a rate that is arbitrarily close to optimal. By “arbitrarily close to optimal” we mean that if we consider the number
M
of function evaluations of a given function
f
required by our algorithm to compute an approximation to
f
, the algorithm converges with a rate of
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha+\varepsilon})
for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
, where
\alpha
is the
smoothness parameter
of the Korobov space under consideration (see below for the precise definition of Korobov spaces).

As shown in
[
18
]
and related works, using a single lattice rule with
M
nodes to approximate the Fourier coefficients yields a convergence rate of the worst-case (i.e., considering the supremum over all functions in the unit ball of the space)
L_{2}
-approximation error (see
Section

2
) arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha/2})
. This result is in a certain sense best possible, because, in a slightly more general setting,
[
2
]
proves that the rate
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha/2})
cannot be improved with such an approach for any
d\geq 2
. However, as shown in
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
, this convergence rate can be improved in a probabilistic sense by employing so-called
multiple lattices
. Moreover, in the recent paper
[
3
]
by Cai, Goda, and Kazashi, the authors introduced a randomized lattice-based algorithm for
L_{2}
-approximation and proved that one can achieve a convergence rate arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha(2\alpha+1)/(4\alpha+1)+\varepsilon})
of the worst-case root-mean-squared
L_{2}
-approximation error, which is better than the rate
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha/2})
. The same paper proves that this randomized algorithm cannot achieve a better rate than
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha/2-1/2})
. In
[
22
]
, we used median lattice rules based on

R
randomized lattice point sets with
N
points each, i.e., our algorithm required
M=RN
function values of a function
f
to be approximated. For such algorithms, we could show that we achieve
a root-mean-squared error with a convergence order arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha})
, and this result is best possible for Korobov spaces. However, as pointed out above, the drawback of the result in
[
22
]
is that the algorithm is not universal with respect to the parameters of the Korobov space, which we try to overcome in the present paper. (Note that there exist other approaches by various authors that yield a convergence rate arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{O}(M^{-\alpha})
, but these are not based on lattice rules or QMC methods; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
10
,
15
,
26
]
.)

Moreover, we will show that, under suitable assumptions, this convergence can take place with only a mild dependence on the dimension of the problem. We will achieve this by making use of the concept of weighted function spaces in the sense of Sloan and Woźniakowski, see
[
25
]
. To this end, let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}
be a collection of
weights
. Here and in the following, we write
\{1{:}V\}
to denote the set
\{1,\ldots,V\}
for a positive integer
V
. The basic idea of weights is to assign a positive number to every possible group of variables
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}
, which describes how much influence the
respective groups of variables have in the problem. Large values of
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}
indicate higher influence, low values of
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}
indicate less influence. These weights are incorporated in the inner product and norm of the weighted Korobov space. It is known that under certain summability conditions on the weights the curse of dimensionality can be weakened or even vanquished completely. This corresponds to the idea that, while the approximation problem may nominally depend on a large number of variables, their influence on the problem diminishes fast, such that only rather few of them contribute significantly to the problem.

Section
3

-error bound (Theorem

), along with a discussion of its tractability properties.
In Section
4

‣

), establishing probabilistic bounds that, when combined, yield the main result.
We conclude this paper with a discussion in Section
5
.

2
Preliminaries and notation

In what follows, we denote the set of integers by
\mathbb{Z}
and the set of positive integers by
\mathbb{N}
. As usual, we denote the exponential function by
\exp
, and write
\exp(1)=e
for short. Bold symbols are used to denote vectors, whose lengths are usually clear from the context. We denote the imaginary unit by
\mathtt{i}:=\sqrt{-1}
, and further notation will be introduced as necessary.

2.1
The weighted Korobov space

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}
be a collection of positive real numbers, which we refer to as the weights in what follows. As mentioned in the introduction, the weight
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}
models the influence of the group of variables
x_{j}
with indices
j\in\mathfrak{u}
.
Throughout this paper, we assume
0<\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}\leq 1
; this assumption could be relaxed without changing the essence of our results, at the cost of additional technical notation.
We further assume that the weights satisfy
\gamma_{\mathfrak{v}}\leq\gamma_{\mathfrak{w}}
whenever
\mathfrak{w}\subseteq\mathfrak{v}
, a property we refer to as being
downward-closed
.

For a collection of weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}
and a real number
\alpha>1/2
, we define, for
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h}):=\gamma_{\supp(\boldsymbol{h})}^{-1}\prod_{j\in\supp(\boldsymbol{h})}\left|h_{j}\right|^{2\alpha},

where
\supp(\boldsymbol{h})=\{j\in\{1{:}d\}\,\colon\,h_{j}\neq 0\}
.

We now define the weighted Korobov space
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, which is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space consisting of one-periodic functions on
[0,1)^{d}
, where periodicity is understood with respect to each variable. The norm in
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is defined in terms of the Fourier coefficients by

\|f\|_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{2}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{h}})\right|^{2}r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h}).

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is a subspace of
L_{2}([0,1)^{d})
, and that all elements of the Korobov space with
\alpha>1/2
can be represented pointwise by their Fourier series. Moreover, it is known that if
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, the univariate Korobov (periodic Sobolev) space that serves as the building block of
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
consists precisely of one-periodic functions on
[0,1)
whose derivatives up to order
\alpha-1
are absolutely continuous and whose
\alpha
-th derivative belongs to
L_{2}([0,1))
. For this reason,
\alpha
is called the smoothness parameter. In the classical product-weight setting (i.e., when
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}=\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\gamma_{j}
), the
d
-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is the
d
-fold tensor product of these univariate spaces. For general weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, the space is defined by the Fourier-weighted norm above; see
[
7
]
for further details.

2.2
L_{2}
-approximation

We will study
L_{2}
-approximation of functions
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. More formally, this means that we study the approximation of the
embedding
operator

S:\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\to L_{2}([0,1)^{d})
,
S(f)=f
. For a given deterministic approximation algorithm
A:\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\to L_{2}([0,1)^{d})
, a common error measure considered in the literature is the worst-case error,

\mathrm{err}(\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}},L_{2},A):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\\
\left\|f\right\|_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|f-A(f)\|_{L_{2}},

i.e., one considers the worst performance of
A
across the unit ball of
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

In this paper, we will deal with
randomized algorithms
instead of deterministic algorithms. When we speak of a randomized approximation algorithm, we refer to a pair consisting of a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
and a family of mappings
A=(A^{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
, where each
A^{\omega}
is a deterministic approximation algorithm for fixed
\omega\in\Omega
.
In the randomized setting, a common error measure is the worst-case root-mean-squared error, also referred to as the
randomized error
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}^{\mathrm{ran}}(\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}},L_{2},(A^{\omega})):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\\
\left\|f\right\|_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[\|f-A^{\omega}(f)\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

Alternatively, one can consider the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximation framework, see for instance
[
17
]
, which asks for which pairs
(\epsilon,\delta)
the algorithm satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\omega}\left[\|f-A^{\omega}(f)\|_{L_{2}}>\epsilon\right]\leq\delta\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all $f$ with $\left\|f\right\|_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq 1$.}

(1)

Our main result (see Corollary
3.8
below) is that we can reach a probabilistic error bound of order
M^{-\alpha}
when using
M
function evaluations with high probability, by using a median algorithm (see Section
3
for the precise definition), where the median is taken over
R
randomized instances of QMC rules. Here,
M
is of the form
RN
, where
N
is the number of integration nodes used in each single QMC rule. The essential progress in comparison to our previous result in
[
22
]
is that the algorithm will not be depending on the parameters
\alpha
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
of
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, and thus can be considered as much more universal.

2.3
Rank-1 lattice rules

Let us now outline which type of QMC rules we use in our median algorithm, which are rank-1 lattice rules. For simplicity, let us assume that
N
is a prime in the following (assuming that
N
is prime guarantees that all one-dimensional projections of a lattice point set are evenly distributed, which is an advantage. If we would allow composite
N
, we presumably could derive similar results, but would need more technical notation). Let
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
be a
vector with each component in
\{1{:}(N-1)\}
. We can then define a
lattice point set
\mathcal{P}_{N}
with points
\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}
as follows. For
j\in\{1{:}d\}
and
k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, the
j
-th component of
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
is given by

x_{k}^{(j)}:=\left\{\frac{kz_{j}}{N}\right\},

where
\{y\}=y-\lfloor y\rfloor
denotes the fractional part of a real number
y
. By repeating the above procedure for all
j\in\{1{:}d\}
and
k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, we obtain the full lattice point set
\mathcal{P}_{N}
. A QMC rule using
\mathcal{P}_{N}
is called a (rank-1) lattice rule.

Note that, for fixed
N
and
d
, a lattice rule is fully characterized by the choice of the
generating vector

\boldsymbol{z}
. Not all choices of
\boldsymbol{z}
yield lattice rules that have sufficient quality to be usefully employed in integration or approximation algorithms. However, there are fast construction algorithms available that return good generating vectors for given
N
,
d
,
\alpha
, and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. We refer to the books
[
8
,
24
]
for overviews of the theory of lattice rules, and in particular to
[
8
, Chapters 3 and 4]
for constructions of good lattice rules. In the present paper, however, we will make a random choice of the generating vectors
\boldsymbol{z}
to obtain the lattice point sets used. Therefore, we need not be concerned with construction algorithms here, which is a general computational advantage of median rules.

It is sometimes useful, and we shall also do so here, to introduce an additional random element when applying lattice rules. This is commonly achieved by a
random shift
,
\Delta\in[0,1)^{d}
. Given a lattice point set
\mathcal{P}_{N}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}\}
and drawing
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
from a uniform distribution over
[0,1)^{d}
, the corresponding
randomly shifted lattice rule
applied to a function
g
is then given by

Q_{N,d,\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(g):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}g\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\}\right),

i.e., all points of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
are shifted modulo one by the same
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
.
If we would like to emphasize the role of the generating vector
\boldsymbol{z}
of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
, we also write
Q_{N,d,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
in the following.

In the subsequent section, we will give our median algorithm based on lattice rules and analyze the corresponding approximation error.

As outlined in the introduction, we now turn to the definition of the
median lattice-based algorithm
, which will be used for
L_{2}
-approximation in the weighted Korobov space
\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. Throughout this section, we assume downward-closed weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, that is,
0<\gamma_{\mathfrak{v}}\leq\gamma_{\mathfrak{w}}\leq 1
whenever
\mathfrak{w}\subseteq\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}
, and a smoothness parameter
\alpha>1/2
. The parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\alpha
are assumed to be fixed with these properties but otherwise arbitrary.

, which provides a probabilistic error bound for the algorithm. The proof of this result relies on the error decomposition given in (

3.1
The universal median lattice-based algorithm

For a real number
L\geq 0
, we define

\mathcal{A}_{d}(L):=\left\{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\prod_{j\in\supp(\boldsymbol{h})}\left|h_{j}\right|<L\right\},

and

\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L):=\left\{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h})<L^{2\alpha}\right\}=\left\{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:r_{1,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{1/(2\alpha)}}(\boldsymbol{h})<L\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{1/(2\alpha)}:=(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}^{1/(2\alpha)})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}
.
Note that
\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{d}(L)
, since
\sup_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}\leq 1
and
2\alpha>1
.

For an odd positive integer
R
, we define the median of
R
complex numbers
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{R}
by

\operatorname*{median}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{1{:}R\}\end{subarray}}(Z_{r}):=\operatorname*{median}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{1{:}R\}\end{subarray}}\Re(Z_{r})+\mathtt{i}\,\cdot\,\operatorname*{median}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{1{:}R\}\end{subarray}}\Im(Z_{r}),

where
\Re(Z_{r})
and
\Im(Z_{r})
denote the real and imaginary parts of
Z_{r}
, respectively.

In the following, we also assume that
N
is an odd prime number. Although this requirement is not necessary for all results presented below, it is convenient to make this assumption from the outset, as some statements do rely on it.

Algorithm 3.1
.

For given
R,d\in\mathbb{N}
and an odd prime
N
, perform the following steps:

1.

For

r=1,\dots,R
, do:

(a)

Randomly draw
\boldsymbol{z}_{r}
from the uniform distribution over
\{1{:}(N-1)\}^{d}
.

(b)

Randomly draw
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}
from the uniform distribution over
[0,1)^{d}
.

(c)

For
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, define

\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}f\left(\left\{\frac{k\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}{N}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}\right\}\right)\exp\left(-2\pi\mathtt{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\left(\frac{k\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}{N}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}\right)\right).

2.

Order
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
such that the medians of

|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}
over
r\in\{1{:}R\}

are in descending order, i.e.,

\operatorname*{median}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{1{:}R\}\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{1})|^{2}\geq\operatorname*{median}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{1{:}R\}\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{2})|^{2}\geq\cdots

and set
K_{N}=\{\boldsymbol{h}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{h}_{N}\}
.

3.

Define the median estimator

\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(\boldsymbol{h}):=\operatorname*{median}_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{1{:}R\}\end{subarray}}\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h}),

and the corresponding approximation

(A^{\mathrm{rand}}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(f))(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in K_{N}}\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(\boldsymbol{h})\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}\,\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}),

where
\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}
denote the collections
of
\boldsymbol{z}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}
,
r=1,\dots,R
, respectively.

Remark 3.2
.

The key difference from the algorithm proposed in
[
22
]
is that the present method does not require prior specification of the smoothness parameter
\alpha
or the weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
By considering the enlarged hyperbolic cross index set
\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
and sorting the estimated Fourier coefficients in descending order, the algorithm automatically adapts to the underlying smoothness and weights of the function.

Remark 3.3
.

The total number of function evaluations is
M=RN
.
Since we compute
\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})
for all
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
, an additional computational effort of order
O(|\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)|)
is required.
It is known that
|\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)|\asymp N(\log N)^{d-1}
(see, for instance,
[
6
, Chapter 2]
), so that the associated computational cost can become significant for large
d
.
Such an exponential dependence of the work on the dimension
d
does not occur in the previous method studied in
[
22
]
.
This highlights a trade-off between achieving universality and controlling the computational budget.

3.2
Error analysis: the main result

In this subsection, we first establish a few basic lemmas and then state the main result of this paper, Theorem
3.8
, which provides a probabilistic bound on the
L_{2}
-approximation error. This result is based on the error decomposition given in (

We first estimate the cardinality of

\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)
. The following result was shown in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}

be arbitrary positive weights. Then, for
\alpha>1/2
and
\lambda>1/(2\alpha)
,

\left|\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)\right|\leq T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\,L^{2\alpha\lambda},

where

T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}):=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:d\}}\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}^{\lambda}\,(2\zeta(2\alpha\lambda))^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|},

and
\zeta(q):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-q}
denotes the Riemann zeta function.

Next, for an odd prime
N
, we define

\displaystyle N_{*}:=\sup\left\{L\geq 0:|\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)|\leq\frac{N-1}{16}\right\}.

(2)

Since
|\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(0)|=0
, the above set is nonempty and thus
N_{*}>0
.
By definition, we then have

|\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(N_{*})|\leq\frac{N-1}{16}.

(3)

Note that
N_{*}<N/2
because there are already
N
integers between
-N/2
and
N/2
.
We also have the following lower bound on
N_{*}
:

Lemma 3.5
.

For an odd prime
N
,
\alpha>1/2
,
\lambda>1/(2\alpha)
, and positive weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}

N_{*}\geq\left(\frac{N-1}{16\,T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}\right)^{1/(2\alpha\lambda)}.

Proof.

Let
L>N_{*}
. By Lemma
3.4
and the definition of
N_{*}
, we have

\frac{N-1}{16}<|\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)|\leq T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\,L^{2\alpha\lambda}.

Taking the limit
L\downarrow N_{*}
then yields the desired bound.
∎

Intuitively,
\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(N_{*})
contains all the large Fourier coefficients that we want our algorithm to estimate and include in
K_{N}
.
The next lemma provides a bound on the error arising from leaving out frequencies that are not in
\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(N_{*})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For
L>0
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{L^{2\alpha}}.

Proof.

We have

\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\frac{r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h})}{L^{2\alpha}}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{L^{2\alpha}}.\qed

In the following, we write

N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}:=\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\ell_{j}\equiv 0{\;(\mathrm{mod}\;N)}\text{ for all }j\in\{1{:}d\}\}.

The next result is an immediate corollary of Lemma
3.6
.

Corollary 3.7
.

For an odd prime
N
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N_{*}^{2\alpha}}.

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
implies
\boldsymbol{h}\in(-N/2,N/2)^{d}
for an odd prime
N
, the shifted lattices
\boldsymbol{h}+N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are mutually disjoint for different
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
. Moreover, for any
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
and
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
, we have
\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\ell}\notin\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
. Hence, by Lemma
3.6
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(N/2)}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{(N/2)^{2\alpha}}.

The result then follows from the fact that
N_{*}<N/2
.
∎

To derive a probabilistic error bound for the algorithm
A^{\mathrm{rand}}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}
, we introduce some
additional notation and an auxiliary set of indices. For the set

generated by our algorithm, define

f_{K_{N}}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in K_{N}}\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}).

Moreover, consider an alternative approximation to
f
using an oracle with access
to all Fourier coefficients of

. For
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
, define

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}.

(4)

Ordering the elements
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)

K^{\prime}_{N}=\{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}_{N}\}

The main idea of the remaining proof is to use the triangle inequality to obtain an upper bound on the squared error:

\displaystyle\|f-A^{\mathrm{rand}}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(f)\|^{2}_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\|f_{K_{N}}-A^{\mathrm{rand}}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(f)\|_{L_{2}}+\|f-f_{K_{N}}\|_{L_{2}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f_{K_{N}}-A^{\mathrm{rand}}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(f)\|^{2}_{L_{2}}+2\|f-f_{K_{N}}\|^{2}_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in K_{N}}\left|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})\right|^{2}+2\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus K_{N}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in K_{N}}\left|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})\right|^{2}+2\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus K^{\prime}_{N}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}+2\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2},

(
\clubsuit
)

where we have used the orthonormality of the Fourier basis in the equality.

We will analyze the three summands in the last line separately in the next section.
The corresponding probabilistic bounds are established in Theorems

,
4.6
, and
4.14

By combining these results, we arrive at the following main theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

, and let

R

\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\leq\varepsilon

\varepsilon_{2}

\|f-A^{\mathrm{rand}}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(f)\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{32\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(2N-1)}{N^{2\alpha}_{*}(N-1)}\left(\frac{991N-255}{120(2N-1)}+\max\!\left(960,\sqrt{240N^{4\alpha}_{*}\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}}\right)\right),

where
N_{*}
and

With the

for

Remark 3.9

Given a failure probability tolerance

.

3.3
Tractability analysis

The probabilistic bound for the
L_{2}
-approximation error established in Theorem
3.8
still depends on the quantities
N_{*}
and
\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}
, and thus does not yet yield an explicit error bound that is independent of these. In this subsection, we analyze these quantities under suitable assumptions and derive bounds that either depend only polynomially on the dimension
d
or are independent of
d
. This, in turn, allows us to investigate the tractability properties of the universal median lattice-based algorithm.

First, let us consider the quantity
\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}
defined in (
17
).
By Lemma
3.4
, for any
\lambda\in(1/(2\alpha),1)
we obtain

\int_{N^{\ast}}^{\infty}\frac{\left|\mathcal{A}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(L)\right|}{L^{4\alpha+1}}\,\mathrm{d}L\leq T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\int_{N^{\ast}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{L^{4\alpha+1-2\alpha\lambda}}\,\mathrm{d}L=\frac{T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{2\alpha(2-\lambda)}\,\frac{1}{N_{*}^{4\alpha-2\alpha\lambda}}.

This implies that

\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}\leq\frac{32}{2-\lambda}\,\frac{T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{(N-1)N_{*}^{4\alpha-2\alpha\lambda}}+\frac{16}{N^{4\alpha}}\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\left(2^{1+4\alpha}\zeta(4\alpha)\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}.

Moreover, since
N_{*}<N/2
always holds, we further obtain

N^{4\alpha}_{*}\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}\leq\frac{32}{2-\lambda}\,\frac{N^{2\alpha\lambda}T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{2^{2\alpha\lambda}(N-1)}+\frac{16}{2^{4\alpha}}\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\left(2^{1+4\alpha}\zeta(4\alpha)\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}.

Combining the above estimate with Lemma
3.5
and Theorem
3.8
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 3.10
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, if the odd prime
N
and the odd natural number
R
satisfy condition (
5
), then with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\|f-A^{\mathrm{rand}}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(f)\|^{2}_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\frac{C_{1}}{N^{2\alpha}_{*}}\left(1+C_{2}\sqrt{N^{4\alpha}_{*}\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\frac{C_{1,\alpha,\lambda}(T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}))^{1/\lambda}}{N^{1/\lambda}}\left(1+C_{2,\alpha,\lambda}\sqrt{N^{2\alpha\lambda-1}T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\left(2^{1+4\alpha}\zeta(4\alpha)\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}}\right),

(6)

for any
\lambda\in(1/(2\alpha),1)
, where
C_{1},C_{2},C_{1,\alpha,\lambda},C_{2,\alpha,\lambda}
are constants independent of
N
,
R
, and
d
, but possibly depending on
\alpha
and
\lambda
, as indicated by their subscripts.

From Corollary
3.10
, we can draw several important conclusions regarding the median lattice-based algorithm. First, by choosing
\lambda
arbitrarily close to
1/(2\alpha)
, we can achieve, for any given
f
, a convergence rate for the
L_{2}
-approximation error that is arbitrarily close to the optimal rate
N^{-\alpha}
, with high probability. Since our algorithm does not require any prior knowledge of the function’s smoothness or the weight parameters, this near-optimal convergence rate applies universally for any smoothness
\alpha>1/2
and any downward-closed weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

Next, for
q\geq 0
, define

B_{\lambda,q}:=\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\frac{1}{d^{q}}\,T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\widetilde{B}_{q}:=\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\frac{1}{d^{q}}\,\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\left(2^{1+4\alpha}\zeta(4\alpha)\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}\right).

If, for some
\lambda\in(1/(2\alpha),1)
and some
q>0
, we have
B_{\lambda,q}<\infty
and
\widetilde{B}_{q}<\infty
, then the error bound in (
6
) depends at most polynomially on
d
, and an error of at most
\varepsilon>0
can be achieved with
N
depending at most polynomially on
d
and
\varepsilon
, with high probability. Moreover, the number
R
of independent rank-1 lattices required for this result depends only logarithmically on
N
and linearly on
d
by (
5
). This situation is referred to as
polynomial tractability
in the context of
Information-Based Complexity
(see, e.g.,
[
21
]
).

In the case of
product weights
, where
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}=\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\gamma_{j}
with
\gamma_{j}\leq 1
, we have

T_{\alpha,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:d\}}\prod_{j\in u}\left(2\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}\zeta(2\alpha\lambda)\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+2\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}\zeta(2\alpha\lambda)\right).

Assume that

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}:=\limsup_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}<\infty,

(7)

for some
\lambda\in(1/(2\alpha),1)
.
This assumption implies that, for any fixed
\delta>0
, there exists
d_{\delta}
such that

\frac{1}{\log d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}<A_{\lambda}+\delta,\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all $d\geq d_{\delta}$.}

Applying the elementary inequality
\exp(x)\geq x+1
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{q}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+2\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}\zeta(2\alpha\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{q}}\exp\left(2\zeta(2\alpha\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{q}}\exp\left(2(A_{\lambda}+\delta)\zeta(2\alpha\lambda)\log d)\right)=d^{2(A_{\lambda}+\delta)\zeta(2\alpha\lambda)-q}.

Hence,
B_{\lambda,q}<\infty
for any
q>2A_{\lambda}\zeta(2\alpha\lambda)
. Similarly, if (
7
) holds with
\lambda=2
, then
\widetilde{B}_{q}<\infty
for any
q>2A_{2}\zeta(4\alpha)
.

If
B_{\lambda,q}<\infty
and
\widetilde{B}_{q}<\infty
even hold for
q=0
, then the error bound becomes independent of
d
. In the case of product weights, these conditions simplify to

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}<\infty\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}^{2}<\infty,

respectively.
Unfortunately, the required number
R
still depends linearly on
d
(cf. (
5
)), which prevents us from claiming
strong polynomial tractability
for our approximation algorithm. This limitation appears to be a consequence of the universality of the algorithm. Nevertheless, the linear dependence on
d
may be acceptable for applications with moderate dimension. If strong tractability is desired, one could instead use the algorithm from
[
22
]
, which achieves the same near-optimal convergence order but does not maintain universality.

First, we show that, for most random choices of

Lemma 4.1

For any

\displaystyle P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}:=\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\boldsymbol{z}_{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\ell}\equiv 0{\;(\mathrm{mod}\;N)}\}.

For any

\Pr(P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq\emptyset)\leq\frac{1}{16}.

By (

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}|

Lemma 4.2

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}>\delta(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq\emptyset)+\Pr\left(\{|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}>\delta(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\}\cap\{P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\emptyset\}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr(P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq\emptyset)+\Pr\left(\delta(\boldsymbol{h})^{-2}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\boldsymbol{1}\{P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\emptyset\}>1\right)

\int_{[0,1)^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\boldsymbol{1}\{P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\emptyset\}\right]

The next lemma illustrates how the median trick reduces the failure probability. It comes from

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

\Pr\left(\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}(X_{r})\notin I\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\,(4p(1-p))^{R/2}.

The following result is a direct consequence of Lemmas

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
R

\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)

\Pr\left(\left|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{\{1{:}R\}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\{1{:}R\}}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})\right|^{2}>2\,\delta(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\right)\leq 2^{-R/2},

Proof.

By Lemma

, for each

\Pr\left(|\Re\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})-\Re\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}>\delta(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{8}.

Applying Lemma
4.3
, it follows that

A similar bound holds for the imaginary part. The claim then follows by applying a union bound.

Theorem 4.5

\displaystyle=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathfrak{u}}\in(N\cdot\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{h}+(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathfrak{u}},\boldsymbol{0}_{-\mathfrak{u}}))}\left|\boldsymbol{h}+(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathfrak{u}},\boldsymbol{0}_{-\mathfrak{u}})\right|^{-4\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathfrak{u}}\in(N\cdot\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}(h_{j}+\ell_{j})^{-4\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\left(2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}((k-1)N+N/2)^{-4\alpha}\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\left(\frac{2^{1+4\alpha}}{N^{4\alpha}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(2k-1)^{-4\alpha}\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N^{4\alpha}}\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1{:}d\}}\gamma^{2}_{\mathfrak{u}}\left(2^{1+4\alpha}\zeta(4\alpha)\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|},

where we have used the assumption that
\gamma_{\mathfrak{v}}\leq\gamma_{\mathfrak{w}}

whenever
\mathfrak{w}\subseteq\mathfrak{v}
. Combining the above expectation bound with Markov’s inequality, and recalling that
\Pr(r_{\min}(\boldsymbol{h})<N^{2\alpha}_{*})\leq 1/16
, we obtain the desired probability estimate.
∎

Now we are ready to show that, with high probability, every
\boldsymbol{h}\notin K^{\prime}_{N}
has a small value of

\operatorname{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}\lvert\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})\rvert^{2}
,
while every
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}
with a large value of
\lvert\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})\rvert^{2}

also attains a correspondingly large

\operatorname{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}\lvert\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})\rvert^{2}
.
These two facts together imply that, except with small probability,
the ordering of frequencies induced by the medians in Step 2 of Algorithm
3.1

correctly separates the significant Fourier coefficients (which should belong to
K_{N}
)
from the insignificant ones (which should lie outside
K_{N}
);
this is the key ingredient for controlling the third summand in (

♣

‣
3.2
).

Lemma 4.10
.

For
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, let

\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{h})=4\left(\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N_{*}^{2\alpha}(N-1)}+F(\boldsymbol{h})\right)^{1/2},

where
F(\boldsymbol{h})
is defined in (
4
). Then

\Pr\left(\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}\left|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})\right|^{2}>\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}2^{-R/2}.

Proof.

By replacing (
11
) with (
13
), the same argument as described in the proof of Lemma
4.2
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\boldsymbol{1}\{P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\emptyset\}\right]\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N_{*}^{2\alpha}(N-1)}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}=\frac{\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}{16}.

Hence, using Lemma
4.1
together with Markov’s inequality (as in (
4.1
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}>\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq\emptyset)+\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\boldsymbol{1}\{P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\emptyset\}\right]\leq\frac{1}{8}.

Finally, applying Lemma
4.3
to the
R
independent replicates gives the claimed probability bound.
∎

The next lemma provides an upper bound on
F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}_{N})
, the smallest value of
F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})
among
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\subset\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
, in terms of the norm
\|f\|_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
,

F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}_{N})\leq\frac{16}{15}\,\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N_{*}^{2\alpha}(N-1)}.

Proof.

First observe that for any
\boldsymbol{h}\in(-N/2,N/2)^{d}
,

r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h})=\min_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}}r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\ell}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{h}})|^{2}r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h})\geq\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)}F(\boldsymbol{h})r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h})\geq F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}_{N})\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}}r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}).

(19)

Since
|K^{\prime}_{N}|=N
, while at most
(N-1)/16
indices
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
satisfy

r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})<N_{*}^{2\alpha}
(by (
3
)), we obtain

\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}}r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})\geq N_{*}^{2\alpha}\left(N-\frac{N-1}{16}\right)\geq\frac{15}{16}N_{*}^{2\alpha}(N-1).

Inserting this estimate into (
19
) yields the desired inequality.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

For
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, define

\delta^{\prime\prime}=4\left(\frac{31}{15}\,\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N_{*}^{2\alpha}(N-1)}\right)^{1/2}.

Then for all
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)\setminus K^{\prime}_{N}
,

\Pr\left(\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}\left|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})\right|^{2}>\delta^{\prime\prime 2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\,2^{-R/2}.

Proof.

If
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)\setminus K^{\prime}_{N}
, then by construction

F(\boldsymbol{h})\leq F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}_{N})\leq\frac{16}{15}\,\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N_{*}^{2\alpha}(N-1)},

where the second inequality follows from Lemma
4.11
.
Substituting this bound into the definition of
\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{h})
in Lemma
4.2
gives
\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\leq\delta^{\prime\prime 2}
. The claimed probability estimate then follows immediately from Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

For
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, if

|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\geq\bar{S}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\max\left(960N^{-2\alpha}_{*},\sqrt{240\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}}\right),

(20)

where both
\bar{S}(\boldsymbol{h})
and
\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}
are as defined in Lemma
4.9
, then

\Pr\left(\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\frac{F(\boldsymbol{h})}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{R/2}.

Proof.

From (
13
), we have

\int_{[0,1)^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in P_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r}}^{\perp}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}\geq F(\boldsymbol{h})\geq|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2},

which provides a lower bound on the expectation of
|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}
when
\boldsymbol{z}_{r}
is fixed and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}
follows a uniform distribution over
[0,1)^{d}
.
Let
\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{h})
denote the corresponding variance as in Lemma
4.8
.
Then, Cantelli’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1)^{d}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\frac{F(\boldsymbol{h})}{2}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[0,1)^{d}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\int_{[0,1)^{d}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}-\frac{F(\boldsymbol{h})}{2}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}{(F(\boldsymbol{h})/2)^{2}+\sigma(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1+|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{4}/(4\sigma(\boldsymbol{h})^{2})}.

(21)

Under the “good lattice” event, i.e., when
r_{\min}(\boldsymbol{h})>N^{2\alpha}_{*}
,
r_{\mathrm{sum}}(\boldsymbol{h})<\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}
, and
S(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}<\bar{S}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}
, Lemma
4.8
gives

\frac{\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{h})}{|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{4}}\leq 8N^{-2\alpha}_{*}\frac{\bar{S}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}{|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}}+2\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}\frac{\bar{S}^{4}(\boldsymbol{h})}{|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{4}}.

By condition (
20
), the right-hand side is at most
1/60
, so the right-most side of (
4.3
) is bounded by
1/16
. Hence, by Lemma
4.9
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\frac{F(\boldsymbol{h})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\{r_{\min}(\boldsymbol{h})\leq N^{2\alpha}_{*}\}\cup\{r_{\mathrm{sum}}(\boldsymbol{h})\geq\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}\}\cup\{S(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\geq\bar{S}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\Pr(\{|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq F(\boldsymbol{h})/2\}\cap\{r_{\min}(\boldsymbol{h})>N^{2\alpha}_{*}\}\cap\{r_{\mathrm{sum}}(\boldsymbol{h})<\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}\}\cap\{S(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}<\bar{S}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}.

The conclusion then follows from Lemma
4.3
.
∎

Finally, we are now at the stage of proving a probabilistic upper bound on the third summand in the error decomposition (

♣

‣
3.2
).
The following theorem formalizes this bound.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let odd prime
N
and odd natural number
R
be sufficiently large such that

\varepsilon_{2}:=\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)|}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{R/2}+\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{R/2}<1.

(22)

Then, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{2}
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})|^{2}\leq\frac{16\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N_{*}^{2\alpha}}\left(\frac{62N}{15(N-1)}+\frac{2N-1}{N-1}\max\left(960,\sqrt{240N^{4\alpha}_{*}\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}}\right)\right).

Proof.

Applying Corollary
4.12
to all
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)\setminus K^{\prime}_{N}
and Lemma
4.13
to all
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}
satisfying (
20
), a union bound argument shows that, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{2}
, we have:

•

For all
\boldsymbol{h}\notin K^{\prime}_{N}
,

\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\delta^{\prime\prime 2}.

•

For all
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}
satisfying (
20
),

\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})|^{2}>\frac{F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})}{2}.

Now, for any
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}
, either

|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})|^{2}<\bar{S}(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}\max\left(960N^{-2\alpha}_{*},\sqrt{240\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}}\right)

or, if this is not the case, we argue as follows: for any
\boldsymbol{h}\in K_{N}\setminus K^{\prime}_{N}
, by the definition of
K_{N}
,

\frac{F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})}{2}<\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})|^{2}\leq\operatorname*{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}\leq\delta^{\prime\prime 2},

which, together with the definition of
F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})
, implies

|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})|^{2}\leq F(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})\leq 2\delta^{\prime\prime 2}.

Combining the upper bounds in the two cases, we obtain

\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})|^{2}\leq|K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}|\cdot 2\delta^{\prime\prime 2}+\max\left(960N^{-2\alpha}_{*},\sqrt{240\bar{r}_{\mathrm{sum}}}\right)\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}}\bar{S}^{2}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}).

Finally, using
|K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}|\leq N
and

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}}\bar{S}^{2}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 16\left(|K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}|\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N-1}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{N}\setminus K_{N}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in N\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}|\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\ell})|^{2}r_{2\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\ell})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 16\left(\frac{N\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{N-1}+\|f\|^{2}_{d,\alpha,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right),

the claim follows.
∎

5
Discussion

In this work, we have extended the median lattice-based algorithm from
[
22
]
to the setting where no prior knowledge of the smoothness
\alpha
and the weight parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
of the Korobov space is available. A major limitation of our method, as pointed out in Remark
3.3
, lies in Step 2 of Algorithm
3.1
, where we need to order
\mathrm{median}_{r\in\{1{:}R\}}|\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})|^{2}
for
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
and select the
N
largest frequencies
K_{N}=\{\boldsymbol{h}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{h}_{N}\}
.
A naive implementation requires computing
\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})
for all
r\in\{1{:}R\}
and for every
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
, whose cardinality grows exponentially with
d
. It is therefore natural to ask whether
K_{N}
, or an adequate substitute, can be found in computation time polynomial in
d
. For example, a mild assumption on
\alpha
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
may suffice to identify a polynomial-size subset
\mathcal{A}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{d}(N/2)
that contains
K_{N}
. In the extreme case where
\alpha
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
are fully known,
K_{N}
can be replaced by the set
\mathcal{A}_{d}(N_{*})
defined in
[
22
]
while still achieving a near
O(M^{-\alpha})
convergence rate. Another possible approach is to estimate
\alpha
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
empirically by computing
\widehat{f}_{N,\boldsymbol{z}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}}(\boldsymbol{h})
over a small test set for
r\in\{1{:}R\}
. We leave this question for future research.
