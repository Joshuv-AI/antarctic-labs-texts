Metabolomic profiling of sweat VOCs for occupational stress surveillance in firefighters: a GC-MS pilot study
Authors: Teerada Somphot, Thanaphol Sirithaweesuk, Luxsana Dubas, Nuttanee Tungkijanansin, Chavit Tunvirachaisakul, Michael Maes, Patthrarawalai Sirinara, Chadin Kulsing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith limited access to mental health specialists for diagnosis, current stress tracking methods rely on questionnaire results. These self-report questionnaires often obtain untruthful answers, especially in beneficial situations such as career hiring or promotion. This study aimed to evaluate an alternative method for stress screening based on analysis of sweat volatile organic compounds.MethodsSweat samples of 44 firefighters were collected using headspace-solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME). Sweat VOCs were analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). The compounds were identified by comparing the experimental retention indices (I) and MS spectra with the database from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library.ResultsThe obtained data were correlated with the standardized questionnaire-based Thai version of the perceived stress scores (T-PSS-10) of the volunteers. The significant peaks were then selected based on the individual accuracy and t-test. Six possible volatile features for high stress (PSS score ≥ 32) samples were revealed. Their potential sources could involve human and microbiome metabolism/catabolism. By using partial least square discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), these markers could be combined into a single feature. The feature value thresholds were then varied for construction of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. With the optimum threshold, the combined marker offered the accuracy, sensitivity, selectivity and area under curve (AUC) of 84%, 87%, 81% and 91%, respectively.ConclusionsThis pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of using sweat VOC profiling via HS-SPME GC-MS as a rapid and non-invasive method for occupational stress screening. The technique provides objective biochemical markers that could enhance the reliability of conventional questionnaire-based assessments in high-risk occupational groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12995-025-00486-1.Keywords: Sweat, Firefighters, Psychological stress, Headspace solid phase microextraction, Volatile organic compounds, Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry

Introduction

Stress is very common and can be found regardless of gender, age, or occupation. It is associated with many factors, including feeling unsafe, handling difficult situations, struggling to adapt to new environment, enduring illness or excessive workload. Stress affects various physiological systems, such as the autonomic nervous system, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and immune system [1]. The relationship between stress and immunity can be bidirectional. Stress is associated with several immune responses. The immune cell products resulting from stress potentially affect the brain, which leads to more serious mental health conditions, such as chronic stress and depression damaging physical and mental health [2]. Diagnosis of stress relies on very limited mental health specialists worldwide, the numbers varied from 0 to 48 per 100,000 population depending on countries [3]. Currently, stress tracking methods are questionnaire-based, such as the Thai version of the perceived stress scores (T-PSS-10), the Perceived Stress Questionnaire (PSQ), and the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21). Among these, the Thai version of the PSS-10 (T-PSS-10) has been validated for assessing perceived stress levels in the Thai population and was used in this study. These self-report methods often obtain untruthful answers, especially in beneficial situations such as career applications. The untruthful answers could lead to incorrect results of stress level assessment. An alternative and more objective approach for stress level assessment, such as obtaining chemical-based information could be helpful in order to confirm the questionnaire results.

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) have been reported as markers for screening of various diseases, including infectious, metabolic, and genetic disorders [4]. A study in 1971 applied gas-liquid partition chromatography for quantitative analysis of VOCs in urine vapor and exhaled breath [5]. This analysis is non-invasive and enables real-time monitoring of biomarkers for metabolic disorders and other diseases. This analysis advances personalized medicine through improved biomarker detections and disease management strategies. Later reports include analysis of potential volatile biomarkers in breath responding to asthma treatment [6], and monitoring airway inflammation in exhaled breath [7]. Skin secretion is another source of VOCs samples which offers simplicity of the collection process with effective cost of operation [8], safety with negligible virus level [9] and stability of the volatile chemical profiles within a certain period of time (a few hours). Characteristic odor active compounds in sweat could be products from the apocrine glands [10, 11]. Substances released from sebaceous glands, such as triglycerides, could be hydrolysed by lipases produced from cutaneous anaerobic bacteria, resulting in long-chain fatty acids. These compounds could be further converted into saturated and unsaturated fatty acids, smaller volatile aldehydes and alcohols by aerobic bacteria [12, 13]. Emotional responses involve the production of specific neural substrates. After complex multistep processes, the substrates differently affect peripheral physiological systems [14, 15]. As a result, different sweat VOCs were secreted and some of these VOCs could be used as stress biomarkers. Thus, a sweat-based analysis could be an alternative approach for stress level assessment.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is an analytical method that combines GC and MS to identify volatile or semi-volatile substances in samples [16, 17]. This technique has been widely used to identify VOCs by comparing experimental retention indices (I) and MS spectra with the database of the standard compounds documented in the libraries. After compound identification, the data are aligned and statistical analysis can be further applied to classify the samples. The identified compounds can be proposed as volatile markers for various diseases. Quantification can also be analyzed by construction of calibration curves. However, some VOCs concentrations may not be necessarily correlated with severity of the illness [18]. The increased benzoic acid content in sweat was reported along with decreased xylene and 3-carene contents in sweat after experiencing stress [19, 20]. These marker compounds are not expected to be produced by human metabolism. Alternatively, microbiomes, such as Bacteroides, Enterobacteriaceae, Veillonella, Streptococcus and Clostridium could produce benzoic acid [21].

GC-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) was applied to analyze sweat samples of a population under stress and anxiety induced by the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST). This technique and GC-MS were used to propose the marker compounds, which included pentadecane, 4,6-dimethyl-dodecane, dodecanal, 1-dodecanol, hexadecane and tetradecanal [22]. GC-IMS was also applied to propose the highly volatile compound markers in sweat of nurses with high stress [23]. Comprehensive two-dimensional GC hyphenated with quadrupole time-of-flight MS [24] was applied and showed that emotional sweating, triggered by stress, could be involved in the release of VOCs of seven acids at the armpits. By using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, the researchers found a significant linear relationship between physiological stress markers, e.g., nose mean temperature and mean heart rate variability (HRV), and the concentration of dodecanoic acid in sweat. Dodecanoic acid in sweat explained the majority of the variance in temperature and heart rate variability and was proposed as a potential biomarker. This compound could be related to the sympathetic nervous system responding to stress. Another study [25] revealed that medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs) such as decanoic acid can reduce oxidative stress levels in two different neuroblastoma cell lines which could be associated with the hepatic metabolism of MCFAs. This could result in the production of ketones as energy sources to compensate for glucose consumption in the brain. Several previous studies reported the compound markers for situational stress, it is still a challenge to investigate the markers for cumulative stress.

Previous research has also explored stress markers in high-risk occupational groups such as nurses [23], police officers [26], and paramedics [27]. These studies highlight that first responders frequently experience elevated and cumulative stress levels, often reflected in altered biochemical or physiological profiles. Moreover, geographic and cultural variations may influence both stress perception and biomarker expression, as occupational structures and working cultures differ across regions [3]. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of investigating firefighters as a distinct first responder group in Thailand, who face unique occupational demands and cumulative stress exposure.

This study aimed to obtain volatile compound profiles in sweat samples collected from 44 professional firefighters in Bangkok, Thailand and investigate their relationships with perceived stress scores obtained from the questionnaire. In addition, this study aimed to identify possible volatile markers for stress screening and propose an alternative method for stress screening based on analysis of sweat VOCs. Sample collection is critical for VOCs identification as demonstrated in the study of Liu et al. [28] The samples were herein collected onto cotton rods which have been previously investigated as effective material for sweat-based disease screening. The volatile compounds were then extracted using headspace solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME) before separation and detection of their volatile organic components using GC-MS [29, 30]. Data alignment and statistical analysis were then performed to propose possible volatile markers for stress screening. These markers were then tentatively identified by comparison with library data of MS spectra and GC retention indices with the discussion of their possible origins. The screening capability was then investigated.

Methods

Investigated population and questionnaire

The study included 44 male Thai firefighters, all classified at the operational level, who volunteered from 15 fire stations listed in the Bangkok Fire and Rescue Department Registry under the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration (BMA). Exclusion criteria included an annual physical ability test score of < 70%, current use of stress or depression medications, diabetes, showering immediately before sweat collection, or sweat samples with total peak areas < 100,000 counts in GC-MS analysis. All participants completed the standardized Thai version of the Perceived Stress Scale (T-PSS-10; score range 0–40) [31]. Participants with < 70% physical ability scores were excluded to reduce potential confounding from underlying health conditions or reduced fitness, which may independently influence sweat composition or stress responses. This approach ensured a relatively homogeneous baseline health status, allowing clearer assessment of the association between stress levels and sweat VOC profiles. This self-rated questionnaire contained 10 items involving different degrees of perceived stress in life events. Each item score ranged from 0 to 4. The T-PSS-10 internal consistency was evaluated with the Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 and a content validity of 0.82 toward measurement of the perceived stress [31]. A score of ≤ 10 was identified as non-stress, while a score of 32–40 was classified as high stress. Participants with PSS scores ≥ 32 were categorized as the high-stress group, and those with scores < 32 as the control group (participants with low and medium stress levels). Based on the questionnaire, 21 participants were assigned to the control group and 23 to the high-stress group. These PSS cut-off values are based on the validated Thai version of the PSS-10 [31]. Sweat samples were collected within 15 min of completing the questionnaire, ensuring temporal alignment between psychological and biochemical assessments. Firefighters were selected as the study population because their stress tends to accumulate gradually across work shifts under routine occupational demands. Sampling was performed under baseline conditions, defined as periods without immediate firefighting interventions, vigorous physical exercise, or acute psychological stressors. While high PSS scores may reflect both occupational and personal stressors, the homogeneous occupational context suggests that work-related factors were a major contributor. The sampling period for each participant was therefore limited to routine station duty, without emergency responses, in order to capture cumulative rather than acute stress.

Collection of sweat samples and headspace solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME)

Sweat samples were collected under the supervision of trained staff. Volunteers placed two autoclaved cotton rods (0.8 × 2.5 cm) under their left and right armpits for 15 min. All sweat samples were collected under baseline, pre-shift conditions between 1:00 p.m. and 4:00 p.m. to minimize acute effects of recent firefighting activity. Sweat collection and questionnaire completion were performed on the same day, with the T-PSS-10 administered once during this period. Future studies may investigate alternative sampling times, such as pre- versus post-shift, to determine whether specific work periods provide greater reliability for stress biomarker detection. Each sample consisted of two rods inserted into a 20-mL screw-thread headspace glass vial, sealed with an aluminum cap and PTFE/silicone septa (National Scientific, Rockwood, TN, USA). Samples were stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Prior to analysis, vials were equilibrated at room temperature (22–23 °C) for at least 10 min and then immersed in a water bath at 80 °C for five minutes. Volatile compounds were extracted by HS-SPME at 80 °C for 50 min using a 50/30 µm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber and holder (Sigma-Aldrich, Bellefonte, PA, USA). Extracted analytes were subsequently desorbed in the GC injection port at 220 °C for five minutes. To validate the PLS-DA scoring approach, additional sweat samples from a researcher (non-firefighter participant) were collected on six different days under varying emotional conditions. Three samples were obtained during non-stress periods and three during stressful periods.

GC-MS analysis

The GC-MS Agilent Technologies model 7890B was used to obtain VOC profiles in human sweat. GC analysis utilized a HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) with the oven temperature gradient from 40 to 260 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min, accompanied by a helium flow rate of 0.8 mL/min over a total runtime of 55 min. The VOCs were separated and subsequently detected using mass spectrometry in scan mode, with a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) range of 30–300 amu, at an ion source temperature of 230 °C. The analysis time (excluding sample logistics and storage) for each sample, encompassing sample collection, extraction, and GC-MS runtime was approximately 125 min. Peak identification was achieved by comparing the experimental retention indices (I) and MS spectra with those from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library. The criteria for identification included a match score above 650 (out of 1000) and a retention index difference within ± 20 units compared with the literature values of the same or a similar stationary phase. All results were further processed using Microsoft Excel. The I values of the peaks in a sample were calculated by comparing with those of n-alkane peaks under identical experimental conditions, following the van den Dool and Kratz method [32]. The n-alkanes (C8-C20) obtained from Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO were utilized as a standard reference.

Data analysis

The study utilized Agilent MassHunter software for data presentation, peak integration, and identification. Data alignment, t-tests, and calculations for sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were conducted using Microsoft Excel. The accuracy rate was calculated as the proportion of true positives and true negatives among all evaluated samples, using varied cutoff thresholds of peak areas to construct the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Potential marker peaks were chosen under the optimal threshold conditions and provided the greatest accurate values (substantially separating the positive and negative samples based on their peak area data). The significant features were then selected based on the individual accuracy and t-test with p-values of < 0.05. Partial least squares discrimination (PLS-DA) was conducted using Chemoface in order to combine all the selected marker peaks into a single feature to improve screening performance.

Results and discussion

Human sweat contains volatile compounds that reflect a variety of metabolic processes. These volatiles may originate directly from apocrine gland secretions or arise through interactions between skin microorganisms and non-volatile metabolites from eccrine or sebaceous glands [11, 33]. Individuals experiencing long-term stress are expected to exhibit sweat VOC profiles distinct from those of non-stressed controls. Firefighters, who frequently confront emergencies and accidents, are at particularly high risk of work-related stress and were therefore selected as the study group. A total of 44 participants were enrolled, all Thai male firefighters classified at the operational level. The mean (± SD) age was 45 (± 12) years and the mean (± SD) BMI was 25 (± 7) kg/m². Demographic characteristics, including age, BMI, symptoms, and medication use, did not significantly differ between groups or influence stress levels, as shown in Table 1. There was a statistically significant difference in work years between the two groups, with firefighters in the high-stress group having significantly longer work years than those in the control group (p = 0.01). The participants were classified into two groups: control (n = 21) and high stress (n = 23) groups, based on their PSS obtained from the questionnaire (Supplementary Table 1).

GC-MS analysis and possible volatile compound markers

The volatile compounds were collected and investigated by HS-SPMEGC-MS. Six possible volatile compound markers for stress screening were identified by statistical analysis. The screening performance was evaluated based on the generation of the ROC curve. GC-MS analysis demonstrated the differences in volatile compound profiles between the high stress and control groups as shown in Fig. 1. There were 430 detected peaks, according to the criteria of MS match scores of > 650 and ±20 retention index units compared with the library. The identifiable peak data are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Only the compounds related to human and microbiome metabolism/catabolism, which were reported in literature or documented in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) or microbial volatile organic compounds (mVOC) databases, were reported. Significant features were selected based on comparison between the high stress group and control group. Their positions in the contour plots were indicated by the dashed regions (Fig. 1).

Examples of GC-MS Chromatogram of the sweat samples: (A-C) 1st − 3rd control and (D-F) 4th- 6th high stress samples

The marker peak profiles across all samples were further analyzed using the Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA). The volatile compound peak profiles of all participants were transformed into dots in the Score plot (Fig. 2). This analysis demonstrated the difference between the high stress and control groups. This result suggested the potential of sweat-based analysis for stress screening via Headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS). Four volunteers (control subject number 3 and high-stress subjects 27, 37, and 39) reported using roll-on products (Fig. 2). Their positions in the score plot remained consistent with their respective groups, with the exception of subject 39. However, given the small number of such cases, no firm conclusions can be drawn regarding the impact of roll-on use on stress screening. It should also be noted that PLS-DA is a supervised technique, and the clustering observed in Fig. 2 may partly reflect latent variable weighting and potential overfitting. While the findings suggest promising discriminatory potential, they should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory results. Future studies with larger sample sizes and additional validation approaches, such as cross-validation and permutation testing, are warranted to confirm the robustness of these observations.

Their related biological mechanisms based on either human or microbial pathways can be proposed as follows: 2-methylheptadecane could be present inside human bodies with the related biological mechanisms, such as oxidative and beta-oxidation reactions, facilitated by a network of enzymes [34–38] or microbiota influencing gut health [38]. This compound could also be generated by microbiomes, such as B. megaterium and P. putida [21]. Ethyl pentadecanoate could be a human metabolite involving pathways, such as hydrolysis by carboxylesterases involving pentadecanoic acid and ethanol metabolisms [39–41]. Alternatively, this compound could be associated with human gut microbiomes through enzymatic processes, which may include hydrolysis of esters, subsequently releasing pentadecanoic acid and ethanol [42]. Heptadecanol could be related to pathways of a long-chain fatty alcohol inside human bodies through several enzymatic processes [35]. This compound could also be associated with the microbiomes, such as H. lacustris, H. rubiginosum and P. impudicus, which could be observed in Thailand’s environment, such as soil and water [43]. 2,6,10,15-tetramethyl-heptadecane could be observed with the microbiomes, such as S. Aureus and B. Subtilis [21]. It is possible that similar markers occur in the general Thai population, but likely at lower concentrations and requiring further investigation.

Stress screening performance

Optimal threshold of the marker features was calculated by varying several cutoff threshold values to effectively discriminate stress positive and negative sweat samples. True positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), false negative (FN), sensitivity ratio = TP/(TP + FN) and specificity ratio = TN/(TN + FP) of the PLS-DA scores were calculated. The ROC curve was generated by plotting the TP rate (sensitivity, y-axis) and the FP rate (1-specificity, x-axis) of all possible threshold values of the PLS-DA scores. The ROC curve represents the stress screening performance at each threshold (Fig. 3A). The corresponding PLS-DA scores of sweat samples are plotted in Fig. 3B. The dot in A indicates the optimal stress screening performance, the solid horizontal line in B indicates the optimal cutoff threshold of the score. Control samples and high stress samples were expected to show higher scores and lower scores, respectively, than the cutoff threshold (horizontal line).

(A) ROC curve with the maximum sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of the potential marker peaks of the axillary sweat samples (downregulation). (B) PLS-DA score distributions of different sweat samples

In addition, this study included four volunteers utilizing deodorant products. The analysis showed the true results for the three samples: number 3 (control), 27 (high stress) and 37 (high stress) as shown in Fig. 3B. Only one high stress sample (number 39) was predicted with the false negative result. At the optimum threshold, the measurement of the PLS-DA scores by this approach offered 84% accuracy with the sensitivity of 87% and specificity of 81%. The reliability and validity of the study were assessed at both internal and external levels. To internally validate the PLS-DA scoring approach, sweat samples were collected from six additional firefighter participants beyond the original cohort. Three with high stress scores (PSS ≥ 32) and three with control scores (PSS < 32) were evaluated, yielding mean PLS-DA scores of 1.00 ± 0.06 and 0.77 ± 0.20, respectively. To externally validate the method, sweat samples were also collected from a non-firefighter participant (research staff) on six different days under varying emotional states. Three samples were obtained during non-stress periods and three during stressful periods, with mean PLS-DA scores of 0.95 ± 0.26 and 0.48 ± 0.10, respectively. The consistent separation between stress and non-stress conditions in both analyses supports the robustness of the approach and its potential applicability beyond the firefighter cohort. Nevertheless, external validity remains limited, as the main study included only Thai male firefighters from a single occupational group. Larger, more diverse cohorts and the inclusion of independent non-firefighter control groups will be required to further strengthen generalizability. Additional limitations include the modest sample size, single pre-shift sampling window, and the absence of stratification across low, moderate, and high PSS categories.

Moreover, stress-related biomarkers have also been reported in other high-risk occupational groups, including nurses [23], police officers [26], and paramedics [27]. These studies collectively suggest that the observed differences are likely related to stress levels themselves rather than being unique to the occupational context of firefighting. Nevertheless, inclusion of matched non-firefighter controls in larger future cohorts will be important to further disentangle occupation-specific from universal stress markers.

It should be noted that although this pilot study categorized participants into two groups (PSS < 32 and ≥ 32), future research with larger cohorts could benefit from stratification into low, moderate, and high PSS categories. Such an approach may provide more refined stress classification and offer actionable thresholds for occupational health policy makers, enabling clearer recommendations for stress management and preventive strategies in firefighting populations.

Conclusions

GC-MS was successfully applied for analysis of armpit sweat samples from 44 firefighters. HS-SPME-GC-MS revealed six volatile features that were associated with differences in stress levels as determined by the questionnaire. These compounds may originate from specific microorganisms as well as from human metabolic or catabolic processes. The findings suggest that sweat analysis could serve as an approach for stress screening. The method is non-invasive and can be completed within two hours from sample collection to instrumental analysis. Data analysis using PLS-DA scores achieved an accuracy of up to 84% for identifying individuals with high stress.
