Predictors of low-level viremia in chronic hepatitis B and the efficacy of pegylated interferon-alpha: a real-world study
Authors: Wenyuan Zhang, Jia Chen, Wenjin Sun, Nana Xie, Fangbing Tian, Wencong Zhang, Qiurong Ruan, Jianxin Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow-level viremia (LLV) is closely associated with the prognosis of patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB), it can increase the incidence of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Our research aims to explore how to identify LLV early and implement effective therapeutic intervention.MethodsThis retrospective study included 720 patients who were either treatment-naive or had not received treatment within the past 5 years. After 48 weeks follow-up, they were categorized into either the complete virological response (CVR) or LLV. Independent risk factors associated with LLV were screened by LASSO regression analysis and a prediction model incorporating these factors was developed. Furthermore, among patients who developed LLV, we compared the therapeutic efficacy of nucleos(t)ide analogue (NAs) monotherapy and pegylated interferon alpha (IFNα)-based combination therapy.ResultsFirstly, four independent risk variables were found to be connected to the incidence of LLV, including age, baseline HBV DNA level, baseline HBsAg level, and baseline HBeAg status. The model showed AUCs of 0.861 (training) and 0.799 (validation), with good calibration. Secondly, between IFNα-based combination therapy and NAs monotherapy group, HBV DNA clearance rates showed no difference at week 24 but significant divergence at week 48 (p = 0.684 and p = 0.001). HBsAg decline rates (in patients with baseline HBsAg ≥ 1500 IU/mL) differed significantly at both 24/48 weeks (p < 0.001). Throughout the 48-week follow-up, the cumulative rates of HBV DNA clearance (p < 0.001), HBsAg loss (p < 0.001), HBeAg loss (p = 0.008), and HBeAg seroconversion (p = 0.001) were all significantly higher in the combination therapy group, though the cumulative HBsAg seroconversion rate was not (p = 0.535). Furthermore, stratified analysis confirmed the superior efficacy of IFNα-based combination therapy across key subgroups. It outperformed NAs monotherapy both in patients with HBsAg ≥ 1500 IU/mL (24w: p = 0.031; 48w: p = 0.002) and in those with HBV DNA < 500 IU/mL (24w: p = 0.01; 48w: p < 0.001).ConclusionAge, baseline HBV DNA level, HBsAg level, and HBeAg status were independently associated with the prevalence of LLV independently. Compared to NAs monotherapy, IFNα-based combination therapy in LLV patients demonstrated significantly higher rates of CVR, HBsAg decline, HBsAg loss/seroconversion, and HBeAg loss.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02981-7.

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is a sustained inflammatory liver disease resulting from chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection [1]. Until 2019, according to epidemiological data of WHO, an estimated 3.8% of the global population remains chronically infected with HBV, with nearly 820,000 deaths caused by HBV-related diseases [2, 3]. Untreated CHB patients develop cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in around 15–40% of cases [4].

By preventing pregenomic RNA (pgRNA) from being reverse-transcribed into HBV DNA, nucleotide analogues (NAs) could significantly reduce hepatic inflammation, fibrosis development, and the likelihood of cirrhosis and HCC, therefore improving long-term clinical outcomes [5]. With the widespread implementation of NAs therapy, the annual incidence of new HBV infections has shown a sustained decline. However, 20–40% of patients exhibit persistent low-level viremia (LLV) [6–8], defined as HBV DNA levels of 20–2000 IU/mL, after 48 weeks of NAs treatment [9]. Compared to patients with complete virological response (CVR), patients with LLV demonstrate a higher likelihood of liver fibrosis progression and are recognized as a significant risk factor for HCC following antiviral therapy [10, 11]. A study by Kim et al. showed that, with a median follow-up of 4.5 years, compared with CVR group, the LLV group hold a higher risk of HCC among cirrhotic patients [6].

The occurrence of LLV is directly linked to persistent replication and stable maintenance of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) in hepatocytes, coupled with inadequate host immune clearance capacity [12]. This is problematic because NAs do not directly target cccDNA reservoirs or fully restore host antiviral immune responses against HBV [13]. Therefore, simply switching between NAs regimens theoretically provides limited therapeutic efficacy for LLV patients, highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies with complementary mechanisms of action.

Pegylated interferon alpha-2b (peg-IFNα) represents such a strategy, as its dual mechanisms immunomodulatory and antiviral properties directly counter the limitations of NAs in LLV [14]. It not only suppresses cccDNA transcription but also promotes its degradation and clearance [15–17], while simultaneously exerting immunomodulatory functions to enhance host immune control [18]. Given this mechanistic rationale, we hypothesized that an IFNα-based therapy would outperform NAs monotherapy in managing LLV. Indeed, peg-IFNα has shown superior clinical outcomes, significantly enhancing rates of HBV DNA loss [19, 20] and HBsAg seroconversion [21]. However, the optimal application of IFNα-based strategies for LLV remains exploratory, which this study aims to address.

Studies have identified multiple factors influencing the occurrence of LLV, including high ALT level [22], high HBsAg quantification [23], HBeAg positivity [24, 25], and cirrhosis [24]. However, no comprehensive system currently integrates these independent risk factors into a simplified scoring model for direct evaluation of baseline parameters in primary care patients. Additionally, the optimal treatment regimen for LLV is still in the exploratory stage. Therefore, our study aims to investigate influencing factors of LLV development and establish a clinically applicable predictive model to create a tool for early identification of LLV risk. Then, given the clinical dilemma LLV presents, we evaluated the differential therapeutic efficacy between NAs monotherapy and IFNα-based combination regimens in LLV management, thus exploring optimal treatment strategies.

Methods

Patients

1,129 CHB patients from Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (2018–2023), were enrolled in this real-world retrospective study, meeting all inclusion criteria: (a) age 18–65 years; (b) serum HBsAg positivity > 6 months or documented clinical history; (c) serum HBV DNA positivity; (d) NAs-naïve or had not received any NAs within the preceding 5 years. A total of 409 patients were excluded based on exclusion criteria: (a) prior peg-IFNα therapy; (b) treatment failure, discontinuation, or incomplete data; (c) decompensated cirrhosis or HCC; (d) coinfection with other hepatitis viruses or HIV. Ultimately, 720 patients were included.

The Tongji Hospital Ethics Committee accepted the study (TJ-IRB-20220929), which met with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because of the retrospective design, written informed consent was waived.

Study design

Baseline data were collected for all enrolled patients, including age at initiation of NAs antiviral therapy, sex, previous antiviral treatment, type of NAs, HBV DNA, HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bilirubin (TB), direct bilirubin (DB), indirect bilirubin (IB), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total cholesterol (TC), and liver ultrasound findings.

Patients were stratified into CVR (HBV DNA < 20 IU/mL) and LLV (20 ≤ HBV DNA < 2000 IU/mL) groups based on HBV DNA clearance status at 48 weeks. The LLV cohort was further divided into two subgroups by different treatments: NAs monotherapy group (switching to other NAs) and IFNα-based therapy group (NAs combine with peg-IFNα−2b). Parameters collected before treatment included age, sex, HBV DNA, HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, and TC. The NAs monotherapy group was evaluated every 24 weeks (HBV DNA, HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe), while the IFNα-based therapy group was evaluated every 12 weeks (HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe). Due to the retrospective design and differing standard clinical practices for each regimen, the timing of HBV DNA assessments varied between the treatment groups. For the IFNα-based therapy group, the assessment was conducted after completion of the finite peg-IFNα course. For the comparative analysis, data collected at the end of either the 24 week or 48 week treatment course were selected.

Laboratory measurements

The COBAS TaqMan HBV test (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) was used to measure the levels of serum HBV DNA, using a 20 IU/mL lower limit of quantification. HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, and Anti-HBe were measured via the Elecsys HBsAg II Quant II assay, Elecsys Anti-HBs II assay, Elecsys HBeAg, and Elecsys Anti-HBe (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), respectively. Serological thresholds were defined according to WHO guidelines: HBsAg negativity was set at < 0.05 IU/mL, and anti-HBs positivity at ≥ 10 IU/L. Biochemical parameters were analyzed using standardized enzymatic assays at department of laboratory.

Clinical outcome

Primary outcomes of this study comprised two components: (1) HBV DNA undetectability (defined as < 20 IU/mL) at 48 weeks of NAs antiviral therapy in all patients; (2) HBV DNA clearance in LLV patients under different therapeutic regimens, with a minimum follow-up of 24 weeks (extending to 48 weeks for patients continuing therapy).

Secondary outcomes included the proportion of LLV patients achieving HBsAg levels < 1500 IU/mL, HBsAg loss, HBsAg seroconversion (HBsAg loss along with anti-HBs positive), and HBeAg loss, HBeAg seroconversion (HBeAg loss along with anti-HBs positive).

Statistical analysis

Variable selection was conducted using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression on all candidate variables, with the optimal penalty parameter (λ) selected via tenfold cross-validation. Predictors with non-zero coefficients at this optimal λ were retained and subsequently integrated into a multivariable logistic regression model to build the final predictive nomogram. Decision curve analysis (DCA), calibration plots, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to thoroughly assess the model’s performance. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables were presented as proportions. Between-group differences in continuous parameters were analyzed using the t-test Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical parameters were compared via Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Cumulative incidence across groups were evaluated through Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing.

The statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2, IBM SPSS software, version 26.0, and Graph Pad Prism version 8.0.2, with two-tailed tests at a significance level of 0.05.

This nomogram was subsequently transformed into a simplified scoring system, with optimal cutoff values for individual variables determined by maximizing the Youden Index (sTable 1). The allocation of points was proportional to the regression coefficients (β) of the predictors, thereby reflecting their relative weights in the model. The scoring system was constructed by assigning the maximum score of 10 points to the variable category possessing the largest absolute β-coefficient, whereby points for all remaining categories were proportionally scaled. This methodology ensures an accurate representation of the relative contribution of each predictor to the model outcome. Points were assigned to each variable as follows: age (3 points), baseline HBV DNA level (5 points), baseline HBsAg level (3 points for 1000–3000 IU/mL; 10 points for > 3000 IU/mL), and baseline HBeAg status (3 points) (Table 3). Using the Youden index, the optimal cutoff threshold was determined to be 10 points. We evaluated this prediction model using ROC curves, calibration curves, and DCA. The discriminatory ability of the nomogram was rigorously evaluated using ROC analysis. The model demonstrated excellent performance, with area under the curves (AUCs) of 0.861 (95% CI: 0.827–0.895) in the training set and 0.799 (95% CI: 0.758–0.841) in the validation set, indicating excellent discriminative power and good generalizability (Fig. 2A-B). Calibration curves showed close alignment with the 45-degree ideal line across most predicted probabilities, reflecting high reliability and accuracy in predicted versus observed outcomes (Fig. 2C-D). Patients were stratified into high HBV DNA (≥ 500 IU/mL) and low HBV DNA (< 500 IU/mL) groups based on baseline levels. Analysis of HBV DNA clearance rates revealed that IFNα-based therapy group demonstrated significantly superior efficacy compared to NAs monotherapy in the low HBV DNA group at both week 24 (p = 0.01) and week 48 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3C). Similarly, we stratified patients into two group by pretreatment HBsAg levels: a ≥ 1500 IU/ml group and a < 1500 IU/ml group. Comparative analysis showed that IFNα-based therapy group achieved significantly higher HBV DNA clearance rates than NAs monotherapy in the high HBsAg group at week 24 (p = 0.031) and week 48 (p = 0.002) (Fig. 3D).

Discussion

Even though the use of antiviral medications has reduced the incidence of HBV-related illnesses and consequences, some individuals continued to display LLV after antiviral therapy. In this study, the LLV incidence rate was 37.1%, which is concordant with findings from others research [26, 27].

The mechanisms underlying the development of LLV remain incompletely understood and are likely influenced by multiple factors. Previous studies suggest potential associations with the cccDNA reservoir [28, 29], host immune factors [30], NAs resistance and limitations [31], as well as patient adherence [32]. However, specific baseline clinical characteristics and virological parameters may serve as predictive factors for LLV occurrence. Kim et al. identified baseline HBV DNA load as an important risk factor for LLV [33]. In the cohort studied by Li et al. [32], the patients with baseline HBV DNA ≥ 6.0 log₁₀ IU/mL was significantly higher in the LLV group (63.8%) compared to the CVR group (45.1%). Our analysis showed that the development of LLV was independently correlated with a baseline HBV DNA level ≥ 5.81 log₁₀ IU/mL (OR = 2.927, 95% CI: 1.627–5.266, p < 0.001), which is consistent with these findings.

Representing HBV subviral particles, HBsAg serves as a surrogate marker of viral transcriptional activity [34]. According to earlier research, a larger chance of reaching CVR is linked to lower baseline HBsAg levels [35]. Consistent with this, Li et al. demonstrated significantly higher baseline HBsAg levels in patients developing LLV compared to those achieving CVR, identifying HBsAg ≥ 9000 IU/mL as an individual risk factor for LLV [24]. Our findings extend these observations, revealing that baseline HBsAg thresholds (≥ 3000 IU/mL) are linked to a substantially increased risk of LLV development, reinforcing the predictive value of quantitative HBsAg assessment for LLV (OR = 9.311, 95% CI: 4.273–20.293, p < 0.001).

HBeAg is a key serological marker reflecting active HBV replication and heightened infectivity when positive. Chen et al. identified HBeAg positivity as an essential risk factor for LLV development in CHB patients receiving long-term ETV therapy [23]. This association has been corroborated by subsequent independent studies [25]. In our cohort, LASSO regression analysis similarly selected HBeAg positivity as a relevant predictor of LLV risk. Consequently, despite exhibiting only borderline statistical significance (OR = 1.747, 95% CI: 0.938–3.252, p = 0.076) in the final multivariable model, it was retained due to its strong prior evidence from the literature and established clinical importance for predicting disease activity.

Furthermore, in our model, age < 41 years was identified as an independent risk factor for LLV (OR = 0.546, 95% CI: 0.329–0.907, p = 0.018), which consistently confirmed in prior literature [27]. The underlying mechanisms for this paradoxical association remain incompletely characterized but may be interpreted in the context of the natural history of CHB. Younger adults are more likely to be in the immune-active phase of chronic infection, characterized by a robust but incomplete immune response against hepatocytes infected with HBV. This state can sustain active viral replication and hepatic inflammation without achieving full virological control, potentially predisposing them to the persistent, low-level viral replication observed in LLV [36].

Based on the independently associated risk factors identified above, we developed a predictive model for LLV development. Notably, significant univariate associations emerged between LLV development and several clinical parameters: prior NAs exposure, anti-HBe status, and levels of ALT, AST, and GGT, among others. Although these factors were not incorporated into the final multivariable predictive model due to lack of independent significance, their observed associations with LLV merit further investigation in prospective cohorts. The conversion of the nomogram into a simplified scoring system was aimed at enhancing clinical practicality by facilitating rapid risk stratification and enabling timely assessment of LLV progression likelihood.

Consequently, for patients identified as high-risk or who develop LLV, the subsequent focus of our study shifts to informing optimal therapeutic decisions. Currently, standardized treatment for LLV remain undefined. The European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) suggests maintaining the current antiviral regimen if HBV DNA < 69 IU/mL and switching to other NAs or use two NAs concurrently with 69–2000 IU/mL [37]. However, if detectable HBV DNA remains after 48 weeks of NAs therapy, the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Chronic Hepatitis B advise changing the treatment plan, either by switching to another NAs or initiating combination antiviral therapy [38]. A retrospective study demonstrated significantly higher CVR rates with TDF add-on therapy compared to TDF switch therapy in patients exhibiting HBV DNA > 100 IU/mL after 48 weeks of ETV treatment. Furthermore, Li et al. reported that among 211 LLV patients after 48 weeks of ETV therapy, those switched to TAF achieved a significantly higher CVR rate (62.7%) than those continuing ETV (9.3%) in 24 weeks [39]. A study has demonstrated that switching to tenofovir amibufenamide (TMF) enables most CHB patients with LLV during ETV or TAF therapy to achieve CVR [40]. Moreover, dose escalation of ETV has also shown potential in reducing HBV DNA levels in some studies [41].

Compared with NAs, Peg-IFNα could enhance the removal of HBsAg and efficiently inhibit the replication of HBV DNA, thereby significantly reducing the viral load in vivo and aiding in the achievement of clinical cure [42]. Thus, we aim to evaluate the efficacy of combination therapy incorporating IFNα for patients with persistent LLV.

In this real-world clinical study, we observed no significant difference in HBV DNA clearance rates at week 24 between the two groups. However, by week 48, the IFNα-based therapy group achieved significantly higher rates of CVR compared to the NAs monotherapy group. These findings converge with a recently published study demonstrating enhanced efficacy of IFNα in suppressing viral replication with prolonged treatment duration [43]. Our study further demonstrated that the IFNα-based therapy group achieved a significantly higher cumulative HBV DNA clearance rate at week 48 compared to the NAs monotherapy group.

It is well established across multiple studies that IFNα therapy significantly reduces HBsAg levels. Wang et al. documented that IFNα combined with NAs achieves higher rates of HBsAg loss in young, HBeAg-positive children and adolescents with chronic HBV infection and normal ALT levels [44]. A meta-analysis from Zhang et al. confirmed that sequential IFNα combination therapy significantly enhances HBsAg loss and seroconversion rates compared with NAs monotherapy in CHB patients receiving long-term NA treatment [45]. Consistently, our study demonstrated significantly greater HBsAg decline in the IFNα-based therapy group versus the NA monotherapy group at both 24 weeks and 48 weeks. Moreover, the IFNα-based regimen achieved significantly higher rates of cumulative HBsAg loss and HBeAg seroconversion at week 48. In contrast to NAs monotherapy, IFNα-based therapy produced noticeably greater serological response rates, according to a recent study, including HBeAg loss (p < 0.001) [46]. Similarly, our study also discovered that IFNα can promote HBeAg loss but does not influence its serological conversion.

In addition, our study found that in patients with LLV who had pretreatment HBV DNA < 500 IU/mL or HBsAg ≥ 1500 IU/mL, with IFNα-based therapy was more likely to achieve CVR than NAs monotherapy. This is possibly because a high baseline HBsAg level (≥ 1500 IU/mL) indicates a large intrahepatic cccDNA reservoir and profound immune tolerance [47]. NAs monotherapy is limited in such cases, while IFNα can epigenetically silence cccDNA and rebuild antiviral immunity, enhance the body’s ability to clear the virus [48]. However, we speculate that from a virological perspective, HBV DNA < 500 IU/mL means that viral replication has been partially inhibited. In this setting of extremely low viral load, IFNα therapy can better activate the patient’s immune system and enhance the body’s ability to clear the virus.

Our study has several limitations. First of all, as a retrospective investigation, the factors influencing LLV require validation in prospective cohort studies. Secondly, the schedule for HBV DNA assessment was not uniform between the treatment groups due to the retrospective design and standard clinical practices. Specifically, the NAs monotherapy group was assessed at routine intervals, while for the IFNα-based therapy group, HBV DNA was typically measured at a single time point after the completion of the finite treatment course. Thirdly, the majority of the LLV population in this study was HBeAg-positive, potentially introducing bias into the subsequent therapeutic outcomes. Finally, while all LLV patients received at least 24 weeks of therapy, the number of individuals who underwent extended IFNα-based therapy to 48 weeks was relatively small. Of the 117 patients who completed the 48-week follow-up, only 38 were in the IFNα-based therapy group, compared to 79 in the NAs monotherapy group. Our findings indicate that IFNα-based therapy extending beyond 24 weeks is more conducive to achieving complete virological suppression and HBsAg loss; this observation needs further investigation and validation in subsequent prospective trials.

Our study identified age < 41 years, high HBV DNA level, high HBsAg level, and HBeAg positivity as independent high-risk factors linked to LLV. Furthermore, IFNα-based therapy exhibited superior efficacy over NAs monotherapy in achieving virological suppression against LLV and promoting HBsAg and HBeAg loss.
