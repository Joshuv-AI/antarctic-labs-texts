Triple Organ Transplantation in the United States After the 2018 Heart Allocation Policy Implementation
Authors: Amit Alam, Yuki Low, Jamil Francis, David Golombeck, Randal Goldberg, Alex Reyentovich, Sommer Gentry, Allan Massie, Nader Moazami, Jason N. Katz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Triple organ transplantation (TOT) may represent the sole definitive therapeutic option for some patients with end-stage heart failure accompanied by irreversible dysfunction of additional organs, including the lungs, liver, and kidneys.1 In October 2018, the United States heart allocation system underwent a major revision, replacing the previous three-tiered system with a six-tiered model prioritizing the sickest candidates with temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) and promoting broader geographic sharing. Comprehensive national data examining TOT and related outcomes before and after the 2018 policy change remain scarce.

The data used in this study have been provided by United Network for Organ Sharing as contractor for Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN). The OPTN data include data about donors, waitlisted, candidates, and transplant recipients, submitted by members of the OPTN. This study of deidentified data is considered exempt by the Institutional Review Board of NYU Langone Health. Recipients were classified by TOT combinations, including heart-liver-lung (HR-LI-LU), heart-kidney-pancreas (HR-KI-PA), heart-kidney-liver (HR-KI-LI), and heart-kidney-lung (HR-KI-LU).

Using the OPTN database between the years 1989 and 2025, we plotted the total number of heart-related TOTs each year. We used a univariable Poisson regression model to evaluate whether the annual number of TOTs increased beginning in 2019 following a major change to U.S. heart allocation policy. A binary indicator variable for the post-2018 period was included to assess for a change in transplant volume, and this reflects the association for this pre-2018 vs post-2018 indicator rather than a continuous time effect or an interaction term. We also produced Kaplan-Meier curves of patient mortality overall and by TOT category. From these curves, we extracted 1-year and 3-year mortality estimates for each group.

There were 99 TOTs performed between 1989 and 2025 (Figure 1A). The annual number of procedures averaged 1.6 from 1989 to 2018 and increased to 7.8 from 2019 to 2025 (P < 0.001). The median (IQR) age at transplant was 47.0 (37.0-55.0), 30.3% of recipients were females and 9.1% of recipients were Hispanic/Latino. The most common types of TOTs were HR-KI-LI at 63.7%, followed by HR-KI-LU at 16.2%. Overall, 1-year mortality was 17.5% increasing to 27.2% at 3 years. Among the different TOT combinations, HR-LI-LU experienced the poorest long-term survival (3-year mortality 66.0%), whereas HR-KI-LU had the most favorable outcomes (12.5% for 1- and 3-year mortality). HR-KI-PA and HR-KI-LI recipients had intermediate 3-year mortality rates (22.2% and 23.6%, respectively) (Figure 1B). A log-rank test was performed to compare cumulative mortality across the four transplant group strata, testing the null hypothesis that the survival rate was the same in all groups; the result did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06).

We believe that we are the first to report that since the 2018 heart allocation policy change: 1) there has been a significant rise in the rates of TOT; 2) the most common type of TOT was combined HR-KI-LI; and 3) the overall mortality among TOT was 17.5% 1 year after transplantation and 27. 2% at 3 years.

The 2018 heart allocation policy prioritized patients requiring tMCS and, as a result, tMCS use increased significantly thereafter (from 7.4% to 22.4%, P < 0.001).1 This shift toward greater utilization of tMCS has enabled more sustained hemodynamic stabilization in critically ill patients, allowing additional time for end-organ assessment and perhaps more coordinated multiorgan allocation.

Favorable 1-year outcomes for dual organ transplantation, particularly heart-kidney with survival rates of ∼90%, provide a foundation for extrapolating to triple organ use.2 Prior studies have demonstrated that TOT achieves comparable midterm and long-term survival to dual organs,3 suggesting that in carefully selected patients the addition of a third organ may not significantly compromise outcomes and may justify expansion of TOT under the current allocation system.

The increasing use of TOT also raises important ethical and allocation considerations, as the survival benefit achieved in a single recipient must be weighed against the opportunity cost of organs that might otherwise benefit multiple transplant candidates. Although emerging data suggest acceptable outcomes in carefully selected candidates, the balance between individual patient benefit and broader organ stewardship remains uncertain. Additional longitudinal data are needed to better define the long-term survival and organ-utilization implications of TOT.

The mortality patterns observed in multiorgan transplantation likely reflect a balance between early peri-operative risk and later stabilization. Early risk is driven by hemodynamic instability, right ventricular dysfunction, and ischemia-reperfusion injury, with kidney allografts particularly susceptible given their dependence on cardiac output.4 This is reflected in higher rates of early kidney allograft loss following simultaneous heart-kidney transplantation compared with contralateral isolated kidney transplantation (14.7% vs 4.5%); a finding thought to be largely driven by hemodynamic compromise rather than rejection.4 However, these early risks are concentrated within the first year, after which outcomes appear to stabilize, likely reflecting both functional restoration of multiple organ systems and immunologic advantages conferred by multiorgan transplantation, including enhanced immunological tolerance with reduced rates of rejection and vasculopathy.4 Our study found that patients with HR-KI and another organ (HR-KI-LU, HR-KI-PA, and HR-KI-LI) had acceptable 1- and 3- year mortality, whereas HR-LI-LU experienced the poorest long-term survival, underscoring the need for caution when considering this transplant combination given the use of three scarce donor organs. Future studies are needed to identify the risk factors associated with survival, as well as to better understand the impact of center effects and the role of institutional expertise in the peri-operative and postoperative management of these complex patients.

The next reiteration of heart allocation (ie continuous distribution [CD]) is expected to be implemented by 2027.5 The intent of CD is to rank waiting list candidates on points for various factors simultaneously—such as medical urgency, patient access, and candidate biology among other attributes—promoting a more equitable approach to matching heart donors and candidates.5 This would eliminate the current 2018 six-tiered heart allocation system and convert it into a continuous rating scale. Some factors currently not included in the allocation, such as waiting time on durable LVADs, blood type, and sensitization will be incorporated.5 Under the current expected CD model, need for multiorgan transplantation and post-transplant expected survival will not be included in the composite allocation score until more longitudinal data are available. What impact CD will have on TOT rates and outcomes remains to be determined.

The granularity of the available data set limited our ability to characterize demographic data of recipients and pretransplant mechanical circulatory support in detail. Device coding did not distinguish between specific temporary ventricular assist device brands or models (such as Impella), so we could not determine how many recipients required these devices at the time of transplant. In addition, the rarity of TOT limited subgroup analyses by transplant era and organ combination. Despite not reaching statistical significance for survival likely due to the small number of HR-LI-LU recipients, the poor outcomes observed warrant caution when considering this transplant combination.

TOT expanded substantially in the United States following the 2018 heart allocation policy change shift toward greater utilization of tMCS. The most common type of TOT following this was HR-KI-LI transplantation. Further studies are required on these individuals to improve long-term outcomes.
