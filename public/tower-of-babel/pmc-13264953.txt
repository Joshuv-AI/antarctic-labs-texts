Targeting ATF4 overcomes dual resistance to chemo-immunotherapy in gastric cancer by disrupting the DNA damage response
Authors: Huili Ye, Wentao Zhang, Hongtai Cao, Zhiyi Peng, Tong Xu, Zhenhua Xia, Long Li, Na He, Yali Zhou, Junjie Qin, Kejun Ma, Wen Ren, Haobo Han, Weiwen Cai, Long Qin, Zuoyi Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe efficacy of chemo-immunotherapy in gastric cancer (GC) is limited by heterogeneous resistance and the lack of predictive biomarkers. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying concurrent intrinsic chemoresistance and extrinsic immunosuppression remain poorly understood.MethodsWe performed integrative analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing and bulk transcriptomic datasets from GC cohorts to identify key regulators of chemo-immunotherapy resistance. The functional role of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) was validated in GC cell lines, patient-derived organoids (PDOs), and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Mechanistic investigations employed chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, RNA-seq, and multiplex immunofluorescence to elucidate the ATF4-HSPA9-DNA damage response (DDR) axis. Clinical relevance was assessed in GC patients treated with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) monotherapy and those receiving combination 5-FU plus anti-PD-1 therapy.ResultsWe identify integrated stress-response transcription factor ATF4 as a key regulator of dual resistance. Mechanistically, ATF4 transcriptionally upregulates HSPA9 to activate the DDR, which promotes both intrinsic chemoresistance and extrinsic immunosuppression. ATF4 overexpression reduces 5-FU sensitivity and induces CD8+ T cell exhaustion in PDOs and PDX models. Clinically, low ATF4 expression correlates with improved survival in GC patients receiving chemo-immunotherapy.ConclusionsThese findings establish the ATF4-HSPA9 axis as a dual-function therapeutic target and predictive biomarker in GC chemo-immunotherapy. This axis bridges DNA damage repair-mediated chemoresistance and T cell exhaustion, providing a strategy to overcome resistance to 5-FU and PD-1 blockade.Keywords: Gastric Cancer, Immunosuppression, Immunotherapy, T cell, Biomarker

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a leading global health burden, ranking as the fifth most common malignancy yet exhibiting a 5-year survival rate below 30%,1 2 due largely to the dual obstacles of chemoresistance and immune evasion.3 4 Although 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-based chemotherapy continues to form the backbone of systemic treatment for GC,3 5 its effectiveness is consistently undermined by innate and acquired resistance mechanisms.6,8 Meanwhile, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) against PD-1/PD-L1 have transformed oncology by revitalizing antitumor immunity, but their standalone utility in GC is modest, with objective response rates hovering around 20%.9,11 This therapeutic impasse highlights the urgent need to develop synergistic strategies and decode the mechanistic crosstalk between chemoresistance and immune evasion, thereby opening new avenues to overcome clinical challenges in GC.

The DNA damage response (DDR) pathway serves as a central regulator in GC progression, concurrently mediating 5-FU resistance7 12 13 and fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).14 15 On one hand, incorporation of 5-FU metabolites into nucleic acids induces DNA breaks and triggers γH2AX-focused repair complexes that activate multiple DNA repair pathways.7 12 13 On the other hand, DDR signaling transmitted via the ATM–ATR/CHK1 axis drives PD-L1 upregulation and CD8+ T cell exclusion, thereby promoting immune evasion.14,16 Although these fundamental mechanisms have been characterized, the key molecular players and precise networks coordinating this chemo-immune interplay remain poorly delineated. Notably, the mitochondrial HSP70 member HSPA9 (Mortalin) is a critical stress-adaptive modulator implicated in cancer cell survival, metabolism, and immune escape.17,19 It drives chemoresistance and immune evasion by sustaining mitochondrial integrity and modulating stress signaling.18 19 Thus, HSPA9 emerges as a promising functional link between DDR activation and these dual resistance mechanisms. Consequently, elucidating its precise role is a strategic imperative for discovering new therapeutic targets in GC chemoimmunotherapy.

Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) is a stress-responsive bZIP transcription factor that binds cAMP-responsive elements (CRE)20 and is activated by hypoxia,21 ER stress,22 nutrient deprivation,23 24 and oxidative stress.25 Beyond its canonical stress-adaptation role, we previously showed that ATF4 promotes 5-FU resistance via PICH interaction and Cyclin A1 transactivation.26 Importantly, the role of ATF4 extends beyond autonomous cancer cell survival to tumor-immune interactions. Recent evidence indicates that tumor-intrinsic and T cell-derived ATF4 can both foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment, facilitating immune escape.27,29 However, precise mechanistic insights into how ATF4 coordinates these dual roles to shape chemoimmunotherapy efficacy remain elusive. In this study, we unveiled the heterogeneity of 5-FU-resistant cells and dentified ATF4 as a key regulator of chemoresistance and immune evasion in GC. Specifically, ATF4 transcriptionally activates HSPA9 to enhance ATM-dependent DNA repair, promoting both 5-FU resistance and immune evasion. Clinical relevance of ATF4 showed significant correlation with poor survival in patients receiving 5-FU combined with anti-PD-1 therapy. Our findings provide novel mechanistic insights into ATF4-driven chemoimmunotherapy resistance, thereby establishing ATF4 as a promising therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens

From tissue microarrays (TMAs), 91 matched pairs of gastric tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues were selected to evaluate the diagnostic value of ATF4 and PD-L1 for sensitivity to 5-FU chemotherapy (see online supplemental table S1). These tissues were derived from patients who had undergone radical resection followed by 5-FU-based adjuvant chemotherapy. The inclusion criteria for the 91 GC samples were: (1) diagnosis of advanced GC and (2) treatment with fluorouracil, tegafur, or capecitabine. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior neoadjuvant chemotherapy, preoperative radiotherapy, or second-line or later-line therapy; (2) prior chemotherapy with paclitaxel or docetaxel; (3) postoperative pathological stage I or IV according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer classification; (4) prior targeted therapy or immunotherapy; and (5) death due to causes unrelated to tumor recurrence. Histopathological features and diagnosis for each case were reviewed and confirmed by experienced pathologists. Additionally, six pairs of fresh gastric tumor tissues were collected for further analysis. mRNA and protein expression levels of ATF4 in these tissues were assessed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and immunoblotting, respectively.

Data acquisition and processing

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data analyzed in this study were obtained from the publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository under accession number GSE183904. This dataset comprises a comprehensive atlas of GC, including 31 primary gastric tumor samples with paired adjacent normal tissues across clinical stages I–IV. The R package Seurat was employed for analyzing scRNA sequencing data. Quality control measures were implemented, with cells having fewer than 200 or more than 4000 expressed genes, over 15 000 total UMIs, over 15% mitochondrial genes, over 1% hemoglobin genes, and over 50% ribosomal genes being excluded, leading to the retention of cells for subsequent analysis. The LogNormalize method ensured data normalization, while batch effects were removed using the R package Harmony. FindVariableFeatures function was used to identify the top 2000 variable genes. Principal component analysis, t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were used for dimensionality reduction, and followed by cell clustering using FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Cell annotation was facilitated using the R package SingleR.30

Identification of 5-FU-resistant clusters

The R package Scissor is a novel approach that utilizes the phenotypes, such as disease stage, tumor metastasis, treatment response, and survival outcomes, collected from bulk assays to identify the most highly phenotype-associated cell subpopulations from single-cell data. In our study, we employed three data sources as inputs for the Scissor algorithm: (1) A single-cell expression matrix of GC. (2) A bulk RNA-seq expression matrix derived from GC patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) who received 5-FU-based chemotherapy. (3) The corresponding overall survival information for these TCGA patients. Using these inputs, the Scissor algorithm was applied to selectively identify 5-FU-resistant epithelial cells and revealed a notable concentration of 5-FU-resistant cells in specific clusters. By collecting DNA damage repair-related genes from the Gene Ontology (GO) database, we quantified DNA damage repair scores (DDRs) for each tumor cell using the R package AUCell. Tumor cells with higher DDRs were considered more likely to be 5-FU-resistant.30

Screening of CD8+ T cell response genes in 5-FU-resistant epithelial cells

To screen for upregulated CD8+ T cell-related genes in 5-FU-resistant epithelial cells, we first focused on CD274 (encoding PD-L1). Differential expression analysis between drug-resistant and drug-sensitive epithelial cells was performed using the FindMarkers function (Seurat V.4.3, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Genes with |log2FC|>0.25 and p<0.05 were considered significantly upregulated in the resistant group. Genes showing zero expression across all epithelial cells with non-zero CD274 expression were excluded, retaining 68 genes. Pearson correlation coefficients (R) with CD274 were then computed across all epithelial cells; genes with R>0.5 were selected as the primary CD274-associated set, yielding seven genes. To assess whether these seven genes also correlate with other immune-related genes implicated in drug resistance and immune evasion (CD276, CD47, IDO1, LGALS9), pairwise Pearson correlations were calculated. Descriptive statistics (mean R and range) were reported, and correlations were visualized using heatmaps.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis based on HSPA9

Based on the GEO dataset GSE146996 (comprising 65 GC samples), samples were first divided into high-expression and low-expression groups according to the median expression level of HSPA9. Subsequently, differential expression analysis was performed using the limma R package (vV.3.58.1), with the screening criteria set as a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 and |log2FC|>1. The obtained differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were further subjected to functional enrichment analysis via the Metascape platform, covering GO biological processes, KEGG, and Reactome pathways. The criteria for identifying significantly enriched terms were p<0.01, a gene count ≥3, and an enrichment factor >1.5. Finally, the analysis results were visualized using the ggplot2 R package.

Cell culture

Human GC cell lines AGS and MKN-45 were obtained from the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). HEK293T cells were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection. All cell lines were routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination and authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; #C11995065, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; #10099141C, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Animal experiments

Female NOD-Prkdcscidil2rgeml/Smoc (M-NSG) mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China; #NM-NSG-001) and randomly allocated into four experimental groups (n=10 per group). Within each group, five mice were assigned to imaging studies and five mice to survival analysis.

To assess the impact of ATF4 on the therapeutic efficacy of 5-FU combined with anti-PD-1, we established two independent xenograft models: a cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model using MKN-45/Luc GC cells, and a third-generation patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model. For the CDX model, mice were inoculated subcutaneously in the flank with 1×10⁶ SgCtrl or SgATF4 cells resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel (#354234, BD Biosciences). When tumor volumes reached 90–100 mm³, mice were immunologically reconstituted by tail vein injection of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). 1 week post-PBMC injection, immune reconstitution was confirmed by flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood using a panel of fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human antibodies (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8) together with an anti-mouse CD45 antibody to exclude murine leucocytes, with successful engraftment defined as ≥5% human CD45+ cells among total CD45+ lymphocytes. Treatment with 5-FU (20 mg/kg;i.p., intraperitoneally) plus anti-PD-1 antibody (10 mg/kg, i.p.) was then administered twice weekly for 6 weeks. All mice were sacrificed 43 days after treatment initiation. For the PDX model, fresh tumor tissues from two GC patients were minced into fragments (~10 mm³) and subcutaneously engrafted into 6-week-old female M-NSG mice. Following serial in vivo passages, mice bearing tumors with an average volume of 80–90 mm³ were reconstituted with PBMCs via tail vein injection. 1 week post-PBMC injection, immune reconstitution was confirmed by flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood using a panel of fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human antibodies (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8) together with an anti-mouse CD45 antibody to exclude murine leucocytes, with successful engraftment defined as ≥5% human CD45+ cells among total CD45+ lymphocytes. PDX mice were subsequently stratified into ATF4High and ATF4Low groups based on immunohistochemical analysis of ATF4 expression in the parental tumor tissues. Mice then received either vehicle or combination therapy (5-FU, 20 mg/kg, i.p.; anti-PD-1, 10 mg/kg, i.p.) twice weekly for 6 weeks. Tumor dimensions were measured twice per week using digital calipers. At the experimental endpoint, all animals were euthanized, and tumors were collected for subsequent analysis.

Human GC organoids (patient-derived organoids)

Human GC organoids (patient-derived organoids, PDOs) were established from freshly resected tumor tissues of two GC patients. Tissue specimens were washed with DMEM/F12 medium (#11320033, Gibco) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine and 10% FBS, minced into approximately 10 mm³ fragments, and digested enzymatically with 0.1 mg/mL collagenase IV (#C4-28-100MG, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 1–2 hours. The digested mixture was filtered through a 100 µm cell strainer. Cell clusters were pelleted by centrifugation at 350 × g for 5 min, resuspended in 50% Matrigel/organoid Cultrex UltiMatrix RGF BME (#BME001-05, Bio-Techne), and plated as 50 µL drops in 24-well plates. Following matrix polymerization, organoids were maintained in gastric organoid growth medium (#K2179-GC, bioGenous).

Lentiviral production and infection

Stable AGS and MKN-45 cell lines were established by lentiviral transduction with commercial constructs for ATF4 knockout (#HZL1064, Huazhi Tiancheng Biotechnology) or HSPA9 overexpression (#HZP0109, Huazhi Tiancheng Biotechnology). Transduction was carried out with 8 µg/mL polybrene, followed by selection with 2 µg/mL puromycin.

Cell viability and IC50 determination

Drug sensitivity was evaluated in both conventional 2D cultures and 3D PDO models. For 2D assay, cells (5×10³/well) were seeded in 96-well plates, adhered overnight, and treated with a concentration gradient of 5-FU (#HY-90006, MedChemExpress) for 48 hours. Viability was quantified using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution (#G3580, Promega). For 3D (PDO) assay, approximately 100 organoids per well were suspended in BME, plated in 96-well plates, and treated with 5-FU for 5 days. Viability was measured with the CellTiter-Glo 3D Assay Kit (#G9683, Promega). Dose–response data from both assays were fitted using GraphPad Prism 8.3 to calculate IC50 values.

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine staining assay

Cells and organoids were seeded in 96-well plates and pulsed with 10 µM 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) for 16–24 hours. Incorporated EdU was detected using the BeyoClick EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (#C0078S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33 342. Samples were imaged using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Image analysis and EdU-positive cell quantification were performed using ZEN Blue software (Carl Zeiss, Germany) or ImageJ.

CD8+ T cell isolation by negative selection

Cryopreserved human PBMCs (#CZPB100M, Yayu Biological Technology) were thawed in a 37 °C water bath, washed twice with pre-warmed complete medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin), and resuspended at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. For negative selection, 100 µL of PBMC suspension (1 × 10⁷ cells) was transferred to a sterile tube, followed by the addition of 10 µL Biotin-Antibody Mix (Beaver Biomedical Engineering, China). The mixture was gently vortexed and incubated at 4°C for 10 min. Subsequently, 30 µL of prewashed BeaverBeads Streptavidin magnetic beads (Beaver Biomedical Engineering, China) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 4°C for an additional 10 min. Following incubation, 2.5 mL of sorting buffer was added, and the cell suspension was gently pipetted five times to ensure thorough mixing. The tube was then placed on a magnetic separation stand for 5 min to immobilize magnetically labeled non-CD3+ cells. The supernatant containing untouched CD3+ T cells was carefully transferred to a new sterile tube, followed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min. The resulting CD3+ T cell pellet was resuspended in complete medium. The isolated CD3+ T cells were subsequently used for activation.

CD8+ T cell activation using CD3/CD28 magnetic beads

Freshly isolated CD3+ T cells were resuspended in complete medium at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Human CD3/CD28 T Cell Activation Magnetic Beads (#422603, BioLegend) were thoroughly vortexed for 3–5 min, washed twice with complete medium or PBS using a magnetic stand, and resuspended in complete medium. Beads were added to the T cell suspension at a bead-to-cell ratio of 1:1. The mixture was incubated with gentle rotation for 30 min at room temperature to allow bead binding to CD3+ T cells. The bead-cell complexes were then seeded into culture flasks or 24-well plates at a density of 0.5–1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. 24 hours postactivation, recombinant human IL-2 was added at a final concentration of 100 U/mL (approximately 10 ng/mL) to support sustained T cell proliferation and viability. For long-term expansion, cultures were maintained at a density of 0.5–2 × 10⁶ cells/mL, with half-medium replacement containing fresh IL-2 every 2–3 days.

For bead removal, an aliquot of the cell suspension was transferred to a sterile centrifuge tube and placed on a magnetic stand for at least 1 min. The supernatant containing bead-free activated T cells was carefully transferred to a new tube, and the process was repeated once to ensure complete bead removal. Following centrifugation, the bead-free activated CD8+ T cells were resuspended in fresh complete medium and processed for functional analysis.

Co-culture of tumor models with CD8+ T cells

For co-culture studies, target cells were prepared in two formats. Monolayer cultures of AGS and MKN-45 GC cells were established by seeding in 96- or 24-well plates followed by overnight adhesion. Alternatively, PDOs were gently dissociated into small clusters (~50–100 cells) using TrypLE Express (#12604039, Gibco), reseeded, and allowed to re-establish for 30–60 min. Both target cell preparations were then co-cultured with activated human CD8+ T cells across a gradient of effector-to-target ratios (1:1, 2:1, 4:1, 8:1, 16:1) for durations ranging from 8 to 72 hours. These co-cultures were subsequently analyzed for target cell viability/cytotoxicity and CD8+ T cell functional phenotypes.

Cytotoxicity assays

Cytotoxicity against target cells or organoids was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (#J2381, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, target cells were seeded at 1×10⁵ cells/mL (100 µL per well) in a 96-well plate. For co-culture conditions, 100 µL of effector cell suspension or test substance was added. Control wells received 100 µL of culture medium (spontaneous release) or 0.2% Triton X-100 (maximum release). After 8–48 hours of incubation at 37°C with 5% CO₂, plates were centrifuged at 2500×rpm for 5 min. A 50 µL aliquot of supernatant from each well was transferred to a fresh 96-well plate, mixed with 50 µL of CytoTox 96 Reagent, and incubated for 30 min at room temperature protected from light. The reaction was stopped with 50 µL of Stop Solution, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm. Cytotoxicity was calculated as: % Cytotoxicity = [(Experimental Release – Spontaneous Release) / (Maximum Release – Spontaneous Release)] × 100. All conditions were performed in triplicate.

ELISA

The concentrations of human cytokines in supernatant were quantified using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, IFN-γ and GZMB levels in supernatants from CD8+ T cell co-culture assays with GC cells or organoids were measured using the Human IFN-γ ELISA Kit (#SEKH-0046, Solarbio) and Human Granzyme B ELISA Kit (#SEKH-0193, Solarbio), respectively. Briefly, 100 µL of sample or standard was added per well and incubated at 37°C for 1–2 hours with gentle shaking. Following sequential incubations with biotinylated detection antibody and enzyme conjugate, the plate was developed with chromogenic substrate; the reaction was then stopped, and absorbance was read at 450 nm within 10 min on a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (#C1383S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected and washed twice with cold PBS. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 1×Annexin V binding buffer at a density of approximately 1×10⁶ cells/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of the cell suspension was incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL of propidium iodide (PI) staining solution for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Following incubation, 400 µL of binding buffer was added to each sample, and analysis was performed immediately using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Flow cytometry

For single-cell preparation, harvested tumors were minced and digested with collagenase IV. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 70 µm strainer and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in 45% Percoll and centrifuged at 350×g for 7 min. After erythrocyte lysis, cells were resuspended in PBS with 2% FBS. For surface marker staining, cells were incubated for 30 min at 4°C in the dark with the following anti-human antibodies: FITC anti-human CD3 (#317306, Biolegend, RRID:AB_571907), PE anti-human CD4 (#300508, Biolegend, RRID:AB_314075), APC anti-human CD8 (#344722, Biolegend, RRID:AB_2075388), APC-Cy7 anti-human CD45 (#368516, Biolegend, RRID:AB_2566376), APC anti-human PD-1 (#329908, Biolegend, RRID:AB_940475), PE/Cyanine7 anti-human TIM-3 (#345014, Biolegend, RRID:AB_2561720), and PE anti-human LAG-3 (#369306, Biolegend, RRID:AB_2629592). For intracellular staining, cells and organoids were permeabilized with Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (#00-5523-00, eBioscience) according to manufacturer’s instructions, and stained with IFNγ (#502531, Biolegend, RRID:AB_10900083) and Granzyme B (#372203, Biolegend, RRID:AB_2687027) for 30 min at 4°C. Flow cytometry was performed on a FACS LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences, USA). Data were analyzed with FlowJo software V.10.0.7.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded TMA sections as well as whole-mount tissue or organoid samples were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval. Sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ATF4 (#DF6008, Affinity Biosciences, 1:200, RRID:AB_2833025), P53 (#AF0879, Affinity Biosciences, 1:200, RRID:AB_2827700), Ki67 (#AF0198, Affinity Biosciences, 1:200, RRID:AB_2834152), or PD-L1 (#ab205921, Abcam, 1:100, RRID:AB_2687878). After washing, bound antibodies were detected using an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). Slides were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Whole-slide digital images were captured using a KF-PRO-120 slide scanner (Konfoong, Zhejiang, China). Protein expression was evaluated independently by two board-certified pathologists blinded to sample identity, who scored both staining intensity (0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong) and the percentage of positively stained tumor cells within malignant glandular areas (0%–100%). An H-score was calculated for each marker as the product of the intensity score and the percentage of positive cells.

H&E staining

H&E staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections from PDOs and tumor TMAs. Slides were deparaffinized in xylene (three changes, 5 min each) and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series. After rinsing in distilled water, sections were stained with Harris hematoxylin (3 min), briefly differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, and blued in 0.2% ammonia water. Following a wash in running tap water, slides were counterstained with 1% eosin Y (1 min). Stained sections were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series (70% to 100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with resinous medium. Histological images were captured at 20×magnification using a bright-field slide scanner (KF-PRO-120, Konfoong).

Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from collected cells and tissues with TRIzol reagent (#15596026CN, Invitrogen) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (#RR036Q, Takara) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. qRT-PCR was carried out on a LightCycler instrument (Roche, Indianapolis, USA) with SYBR Green dye (#RR420A, Takara) and gene-specific primers at a final concentration of 10 µM each. All primer sequences are provided in online supplemental table S2.

Immunoblotting

Cells or tissues were lysed with RIPA buffer and centrifuged at 12 000×g for 10 min. Proteins were separated using SDS‒PAGE. These samples were transferred to membranes and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against HSPA9 (#14887-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2120458), ATF4 (#10835-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2058600), PD-L1 (#ab205921, Abcam, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2687878), P53 (#ab32049, Abcam, 1:1000, RRID:AB_776982), P-P53 (#28960-1-AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2918223), γH2AX (#83307-2-RR, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_3670974), H2AX (#68888-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_3670974), STAT3 (#A1192, ABclonal, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2861642), P-STAT3 (#AP0070, ABclonal, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2771569), P-ATM-S1981 (#AP1030, ABclonal, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2863913), ATM (#27156-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2880780), BAX (#ER0907, HUABIO, 1:1000, RRID:AB_3069018), BCL2 (#ab182858, Abcam, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2715467), Caspase-3 (#ab32351, Abcam, 1:1000, RRID:AB_725946), β-actin (#Ab8227, Abcam, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2305186), GAPDH (#Ab181602, Abcam, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2630358), Lamin B1 (#12987-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2136290). Secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP were used to capture the primary antibodies, which were then visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence using a miniature chemiluminescent fluorescence imaging analysis system (MiniChemi 610 Plus; Beijing Saizhi Venture Technology, Beijing, China).

Immunoprecipitation assay

Cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer with protease inhibitors. After centrifugation (12 000×g, 10 min, 4°C), the supernatant was collected. BeaverBeads Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (20–30 μL, #22301, Beaver) were washed and incubated with 5 µg of primary antibody (anti-HSPA9: #14887-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2120458; anti-ATM: #27156-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2880780) in 200 µL binding buffer for 2 hours at 4°C with rotation. The antibody-coupled beads were washed three times with binding buffer, then mixed with 500 µL cleared lysate (0.7 mg total protein) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Beads were collected magnetically, washed three times with washing buffer, and eluted with 25–50 μL 1×SDS loading buffer (100°C, 5 min). Eluates were analyzed by Immunoblotting.

In vivo ubiquitination assay

AGS cells co-expressing HA-ubiquitin together with either Flag-HSPA9 or wild-type (WT) HSPA9 were lysed in lysis buffer, then incubated with Flag-gel beads at 4°C for 2 hours. After washing, elution, neutralization, and boiling, the samples were analyzed by Immunoblotting.

Protein half-life assay (CHX Chase Assay)

AGS cells expressing either Flag-HSPA9 or wild-type (WT) HSPA9 were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured overnight to reach 70%–80% confluence. To block de novo protein synthesis, cycloheximide (CHX, #66-81-9, MedChemExpress) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 10 µM. Cells were harvested at indicated time points (0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 hours) after CHX treatment. At each time point, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. Lysates were briefly sonicated and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit. Equal amounts of protein (30–50 µg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subjected to immunoblotting using an anti-ATM antibody or an anti-HSPA9 antibody. Tubulin was used as a loading control. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ, and the remaining protein fraction at each time point was normalized to the 0 hours time point. The half-life was calculated by fitting the decay curve to a one-phase exponential decay model using GraphPad Prism V.8.3.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation

WT and 5-FU (100 µM)-treated AGS cells were harvested at the indicated time points (0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 hours). At each time point, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in ice-cold Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent A (200 µL per 20 µL of cell pellet, equivalent to ~2 × 10⁶ cells) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. The suspension was vortexed vigorously for 5 s, incubated on ice for 10–15 min, and then mixed with Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent B (11 µL per 200 µL of Reagent A). After a 5 s vortex and 1 min incubation on ice, the lysate was centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant (cytoplasmic fraction) was collected and stored at –80°C. The pellet was washed once with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in ice-cold Nuclear Extraction Reagent (50 µL) containing 1 mM PMSF. The suspension was intermittently vortexed (15–20 pulses), incubated on ice for 30 min with occasional mixing, and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant (nuclear fraction) was stored at –80°C. The detailed procedure was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (#P0027, Beyotime Biotechnology).

Time-lapse analysis

For time-lapse analysis, SgCtrl, SgATF4, and SgATF4 and/or HSPA9 overexpressing cells or organoids were cultured in glass-bottom dishes. After 6, 8, or 24 hours, the cells or PDOs were monitored and captured using a time-lapse microscope (Operetta CLS, PerkinElmer, Waltham, Mass, USA).

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy analysis

Cells cultured on 35 mm glass-bottomed Microwell Dishes (MatTek Corporation, Ashland, USA), PDO, and tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and subjected to permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100. Next, the cells were covered by 3% bovine serum for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies against CD8 (#AF5126, Affinity Biosciences, 1:100, RRID:AB_2837612) and γH2AX (#AB00-053, Thermo Fisher, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2891851) overnight at 4°C, washed thrice in PBS, and further incubated with the appropriate fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies and DAPI. Confocal fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss LSM laser microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). CD8 and γH2AX positivity was quantified using ImageJ V.1.53c software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Cells were co-transfected with the WT (#HZPP0109, Huazhi Tiancheng Biotechnology) or mutant (#HZPP0109T, Huazhi Tiancheng Biotechnology) HSPA9 promoter–pGL3.2–Luci vector. After treatment, the cells were processed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (#RG027, Beyotime Biotechnology), and luminescence was measured using an EnVision luminometer (PerkinElmer, Waltham, Mass, USA). Firefly luminescence was normalized to Renilla luminescence.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed following previously established procedures.26 Briefly, crosslinked chromatin was sonicated to an average fragment size of 200–500 bp. Protein-DNA complexes were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C using anti-ATF4 antibodies (#11815, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:50, RRID:AB_2616025) or normal IgG (#30000-0-AP, Proteintech, 1:50, RRID:AB_2819035) as a control. Following elution and reverse crosslinking, DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR with HSPA9 primers. For ChIP sequencing (ChIP-seq), immunoprecipitated DNA fragments were prepared into sequencing libraries using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V3 (Vazyme). Sequencing was performed on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech) with 150 bp paired-end reads. Reads were aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using Bwa (V.0.7.15), and peak calling was conducted with MACS2 (V.2.1.1.20160309) using default parameters (bandwidth=300 bp; model fold=5, 50; p=0.05). All primers used are listed in online supplemental table S2.

Comet assay

The comet assay was performed under alkaline conditions using a Comet Assay kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (#C2041S, Beyotime Biotechnology). Cells were harvested, washed in ice-cold PBS, and resuspended at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. A three-layer agarose gel was prepared on slides: a base layer of 1% normal-melting-point agarose, followed by a cell-containing layer (0.7% low-melting-point agarose mixed with cell suspension), solidified at 4°C. Slides were lysed in chilled buffer containing 10% DMSO (2 hours, 4°C), equilibrated in alkaline electrophoresis buffer (pH >13, 30 min, dark), and electrophoresed at 25 V for 30 min at 4°C. After neutralization, DNA was stained with PI (20 µg/mL). Images were acquired by fluorescence microscopy, and tail DNA percentage and olive tail moment were quantified from at least 50 comets per condition using CometScore or equivalent software. Untreated cells served as negative controls.

Quantification and statistical analysis

SPSS (V.24.0; IBM) and GraphPad Prism V.8.3 (GraphPad, USA) were used for the statistical analyses and graphing, respectively. An unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups, and one-way ANOVA analysis was performed for multiple groups, followed by the post hoc LSD method (homogeneity of variance) or Dunnett’s T3 method (heterogeneity of variance). Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test were used for survival studies. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the efficiency of ATF4 as a biomarker. Data are presented as the mean±SD. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Each experiment was repeated at least three times.

Identification of 5-FU-resistant cell clusters reveals ATF4 as a key driver

To investigate the relationship between 5-FU resistance and the TME in GC, we performed quality control and batch effect removal on the single-cell data, followed by dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell annotation (figure 1A). A total of 31 cell clusters were acquired and annotated into 13 cell types, encompassing CD8+ T cells, plasma cells, macrophage cells, T_reg cells, NK_cells, epithelial cells, B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblast cells, mast cells, pericyte cells, dendritic cells, and pit cells (figure 1A,B). Specifically, within our defined GC epithelial cells, the expression levels of the marker genes PGC, TFF2, and ELF3 were significantly upregulated (figure 1C). To characterize the divergent properties of 5-FU-resistant and 5-FU-sensitive cell clusters, we employed the Scissor algorithm to integrate bulk phenotypic data with single-cell transcriptomic data, thereby linking individual clusters to their underlying phenotypic-level functions. Specifically, integrating GC single-cell data from the GEO database (GSE183904) with bulk RNA-seq data for gastric adenocarcinoma (STAD) from the TCGA database enabled the Scissor algorithm to delineate three discrete cell clusters: a 5-FU-resistant cluster, a 5-FU-sensitive cluster, and an undefined cluster (figure 1D,E). Furthermore, we identified and analyzed the top five upregulated DEGs between the 5-FU-resistant cell cluster and the 5-FU-sensitive cell cluster (figure 1F). Subsequently, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis of DNA-related processes among the DEGs distinguishing clusters 5-FU sensitive and 5-FU resistant. This analysis identified five significantly enriched pathways: (1) regulation of viral RNA replication via dsDNA intermediate; (2) stress-induced regulation of DNA-templated transcription; (3) DDR; (4) core-promoter DNA binding; and (5) positive regulation of DNA methylation-dependent heterochromatin formation (figure 1G). Considering the central role of DDR in 5-FU resistance, the cellular DDR mechanisms were under investigation. By collecting DNA damage repair-related genes, we quantified DNA damage repair scores for each tumor cell cluster using the R package AUCell, revealing that 5-FU resistant cell clusters exhibited the highest DDRs (figure 1H). Furthermore, we performed a comparative analysis of cell populations in clusters 5-sensitive and 5-resistant. Our results revealed a significant enrichment of CD8+ T cells in cluster 5-sensitive, whereas cluster 5-resistant exhibited a notable predominance of epithelial cells (figure 1I). To elucidate the molecular links between 5-FU-resistant epithelial cells and CD8+ T cells in the TME, we analyzed the correlation between differentially up-regulated genes in 5-FU-resistant clusters and CD8+ T cell response genes within epithelial cells, and calculated their correlation coefficients. This analysis identified seven critical oncogenes that were highly correlated with CD8+ T cell function (figure 1J). Further correlation analysis with the survival of 5-FU-treated patients from KM-Plot database enabled us to narrow down our focus to ATF4 as the key candidate gene for subsequent investigation (figure 1J; see online supplemental figure S1A–G). In addition, we performed quantitative expression analysis of ATF4 in epithelial cells from clusters 5-FU sensitive and 5-FU resistant. ATF4 was significantly upregulated in cluster 5-FU resistant epithelial cells compared with cluster 5-FU sensitive (figure 1K).

Elevated ATF4 correlates with poor clinical responses to 5-FU and ICB therapy

To investigate the diagnostic and therapeutic value of ATF4, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of TMAs containing paired gastric cancerous and adjacent normal tissues from 91 GC patients who received 5-FU-based chemotherapy (see online supplemental table S1).26 We found that ATF4 was highly expressed in GC samples compared with that in adjacent normal tissues (figure 2A,B). In addition, qRT-PCR and immunoblotting analyses further confirmed a higher ATF4 expression in GC than in the adjacent normal tissues (figure 2C,D). Subsequently, we evaluated the correlation of ATF4 with 5-FU-based chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Patients who survived for more than 5 years after surgery were defined as “sensitive” whereas those who died from disease recurrence were defined as “resistant”. Compared with the “sensitive” group, the “resistant” group displayed markedly elevated expression of both ATF4 and PD-L1 (figure 2E,F). A positive correlation between ATF4 and PD-L1 expression was further identified via Spearman correlation analysis (figure 2G; see online supplemental figure S1H). ROC analysis showed that ATF4 had a high diagnostic value for 5-FU chemosensitivity in GC, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.82 (sensitivity: 95.45%, specificity: 67.86%; see online supplemental figure S1I). We divided the patients into ATF4 lower (H-score <155) and higher (H-score ≥155) groups according to the optimal cut-off value of the ROC analysis based on previous reports,26 and found that high ATF4 protein levels were significantly correlated with worse prognosis in patients who received 5-FU-based chemotherapy (HR=4.288; figure 2H). To further investigate the correlation between ATF4 expression and therapeutic efficacy, IHC was performed on tumor specimens from two GC patients treated with a combinatorial regimen of 5-FU and anti-PD-1. Notably, elevated ATF4 expression (IHC score ≥155) in patient A correlated with diminished clinical benefit, manifesting as new metastatic lesions on CT imaging and a concurrent rise in serum tumor markers during follow-up. Conversely, patient B with low ATF4 expression showed a favorable prognosis (figure 2I). In addition, the multivariate analyses revealed that ATF4 expression was an independent prognostic factor for GC patients received 5-FU-based chemotherapy (see online supplemental figure S1J). These results suggest that ATF4 is a potential predictor of response to 5-FU plus anti-PD-1 therapy in GC.

ATF4 plays pleiotropic roles in 5-FU chemoresistance and CD8+ T cell exhaustion

To elucidate whether ATF4 is a critical determinant of 5-FU chemosensitivity in GC, we established stable cell lines with ATF4 knockout in AGS and MKN-45 GC cell lines, which are known for their relatively higher ATF4 expression levels (see online supplemental figure S1K). We then assessed the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) in response to 5-FU using proliferation assays in SgATF4 and SgCtrl AGS and MKN-45 cells. Intriguingly, our results revealed that ATF4 deficiency significantly sensitized GC cells to 5-FU, which was further confirmed by the EdU staining assay (figure 3A–D). Moreover, we generated organoids derived from two GC patients and successfully achieved ATF4 knockout in these organoids (see online supplemental figure S2A,B). Organoid viability assays demonstrated that ATF4 knockout consistently enhanced the sensitivity of these organoids to 5-FU, as evidenced by reduced IC50 values and decreased EdU incorporation (figure 3E–H). This finding consistently confirmed ATF4’s critical role in modulating 5-FU response in both cellular and organoid models.

Next, we co-cultured CD8+ T cells with SgATF4 cell lines or organoids to determine if these models modulated T cell-mediated immune responses through direct cellular interactions (figure 3I). Co-culture assays revealed that SgATF4 models significantly increased CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity (figure 3J; see online supplemental figure S2C,D), evidenced by an increase in LDH release and a reduction in viable cell populations, with maximal inhibition achieved at a 1:16 effector:target ratio in a dose-dependent manner (figure 3K,L; see online supplemental figure S2E–H). Furthermore, ELISA further revealed that SgATF4 enhanced IFN-γ production by 2–7 fold along with a 2.5–20 fold rise in Granzyme B (GZMB) activity in the co-culture system, compared with SgCtrl (figure 3M,N; see online supplemental figure S2I,J).

ATF4 transcriptionally upregulates HSPA9 expression and is associated with DNA damage and repair pathway

To unravel the underlying mechanisms of ATF4 in 5-FU resistance and immune suppression, we performed ChIP and ChIP sequencing (ChIP-seq) assays of ATF4 in AGS cells, which identified 21 433 high-confidence peaks of promoters (p<0.05). Remarkably, as shown in the heatmap and ChIP-seq density plot, ATF4 peaks were highly enriched near the TSS (transcription start site), and 4.62% of peaks were enriched in gene promoter regions (figure 4A–C). KEGG and GO analyses consistently demonstrated that ATF4-bound promoters exhibited the highest enrichment in pathways associated with DNA replication (figure 4D,E), strongly suggesting its pivotal involvement in the DDR pathways. Then, the top transcriptional targets related to DDR were identified by ranking genes according to a fold-change (FC) score. Strikingly, HSPA9 emerged as a poorly understood top candidate, with an exceptional fold change (FC) of 82.45 (figure 4F).

To assess whether ATF4 directly regulates HSPA9 transcription, we analyzed the normalized ChIP-seq data using the Integrative Genomics Viewer, which revealed a significant enrichment of ATF4, with its signal substantially above the input background (figure 4G). De novo motif analysis using HOMER identified the top three DNA elements, with the higher-scoring consensus sequence (TGTCATCA, p=1e-31) matching the ATF4 peak summit at −385 bp (figure 4H). Then, ChIP-qPCR of ATF4 immunoprecipitation showed strong ATF4 enrichment at the HSPA9 promoter (figure 4I). Next, we performed dual-luciferase assays using wild-type (WT) and ATF4-binding site-mutated (Mut) HSPA9 promoters. ATF4 overexpression (ATF4OE) strongly activated the WT but not the Mut promoter (figure 4J), demonstrating ATF4-specific transcriptional activation. Consistently, ATF4 knockout (SgATF4) in AGS cells markedly reduced both HSPA9 mRNA and protein levels (figure 4K). Together, these findings establish ATF4 as a direct transcriptional regulator of HSPA9.

ATF4/HSPA9 axis inhibits chemo-immunotherapy by regulating DNA damage and repair

To elucidate the functional relationship between ATF4 and HSPA9 in DNA damage and repair regulation, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing HSPA9 in ATF4-deficient cells and organoids (see online supplemental figure S3A,B). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that HSPA9 reconstitution significantly attenuated γH2AX foci formation in ATF4 knockout cells, restoring DNA repair capacity to wild-type levels (figure 5A). This rescue effect was further validated through comet assay, demonstrating reduced tail moment in HSPA9-overexpressing cells compared with controls (figure 5B, see online supplemental figure S3C). Apoptosis assays further revealed that HSPA9 overexpression significantly attenuated SgATF4-induced apoptosis (figure 5C, see online supplemental figure S3D). Additionally, HSPA9 overexpression in SgATF4 organoids significantly restored DNA repair capacity (figure 5D; see online supplemental figure S3E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HSPA9 is an indispensable component of the ATF4-mediated DDR in GC cells.

We next evaluated the impact of HSPA9 on ATF4-mediated 5-FU resistance. HSPA9 overexpression in SgATF4 cells or organoids significantly restored 5-FU chemoresistance that had been sensitized by ATF4 knockout, as demonstrated by cell viability assays in both cellular and organoid models (figure 5E,F; see online supplemental figure S3F,G). We then assessed the role of HSPA9 in ATF4-mediated regulation of CD8+ T cells using a co-culture system with GC cells/organoids. Specifically, reconstitution of HSPA9 led to a concomitant reduction in LDH release and an increase in the population of viable target cells, as observed in both GC cell lines and PDOs (see online supplemental figures S3H and S4A–C). Correspondingly, cytokine analysis showed that HSPA9 effectively reversed the elevated secretion of IFN-γ and GZMB induced by ATF4 deficiency (figure 5G; see online supplemental figure S4D,E). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that HSPA9 reconstitution markedly promoted CD8+ T cell exhaustion, indicated by upregulation of the immune checkpoint molecules LAG-3 and TIM-3 (figure 5H; see online supplemental figure S4F,G). Therefore, our data reveal that HSPA9 acts as a central regulator in ATF4-mediated processes, orchestrating both 5-FU chemoresistance and immune evasion.

ATM is a key effector of the ATF4/HSPA9 axis-mediated DNA damage repair

To clarify the role of HSPA9 in the DDR pathway, we analyzed GC transcriptomes by comparing tumors with high versus low HSPA9 expression. Notably, ATM, a central DDR kinase, was significantly enriched in the HSPA9-high group. Pathway analysis further confirmed pronounced enrichment of core DDR processes, including DNA repair and damage pathways (figure 6A,B; see online supplemental figure S4H). Furthermore, immunoblotting analysis revealed that restoring HSPA9 expression abrogated the upregulation of multiple proteins mediated by sgATF4, including pro-apoptotic factors, a key DDR protein, and specific immune-related molecules (figure 6C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ATM may serve as a critical effector of the ATF4/HSPA9 axis for DDR in GC cells. To elucidate the interaction between HSPA9 and ATM, co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed in AGS cells. The results showed that endogenous ATM interacted with HSPA9, but not with control IgG (figure 6D), indicating a physical association between the two proteins. Next, we investigated whether this association modulates ATM function under chemotherapeutic stress. Immunofluorescence and immunoblotting assays revealed that 5-FU treatment promoted nuclear translocation of HSPA9 in AGS cells compared with untreated controls (figure 6E,F). Concurrently, 5-FU treatment led to increased ATM phosphorylation at Ser1981 (figure 6G), indicating ATM activation. Furthermore, overexpression of HSPA9 significantly prolonged the half-life of ATM protein (figure 6H) and reduced its ubiquitination levels (figure 6I), suggesting that HSPA9 stabilizes ATM by suppressing ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation. Collectively, these results indicate that HSPA9 interacts with ATM, facilitates its activation and stability under chemotherapy-induced stress,potentially by promoting HSPA9 nuclear accumulation.

HSPA9 overexpression in SgATF4 cells reduced γH2AX foci formation and shortened comet tail moments, indicating attenuated DNA damage, and concurrently conferred chemoresistance. Notably, ATM inhibition by KU-55933 reversed these effects, leading to increased DNA damage and restored chemosensitivity (figure 6J–L; see online supplemental figure S4I,J). Thus, ATM is required for HSPA9-mediated DNA repair and chemoresistance. We next examined the role of this axis in immune modulation. T cell-mediated tumor cell killing assays revealed that HSPA9 overexpression impaired T cell cytotoxicity (figure 6M), as evidenced by reduced IFN-γ and granzyme B production, whereas ATM inhibition restored T cell effector function (figure 6M see online supplemental figure S4K,L). Furthermore, flow cytometry was performed on CD8+ T cells from co-culture assays. HSPA9 overexpression increased PD-1/TIM-3/LAG-3 CD8+ T cells, indicative of T cell exhaustion, an effect markedly reversed by KU-55933 (figure 6N; see online supplemental figure S4M,N). Moreover, immunoblotting confirmed that HSPA9 overexpression in SgATF4 cells upregulated PD-L1 expression and downregulated P53 expression, effects similarly reversed by ATM inhibition (figure 6O; see online supplemental figure S4O). Collectively, these results further demonstrate that ATM is indispensable for HSPA9-mediated DNA repair and chemo-immunotherapy resistance.

ATF4 impairs CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and compromises the therapeutic synergy of 5-FU with PD-1 blockade in CDX/PDX models

To investigate the role of ATF4 in driving 5-FU resistance and immune evasion in GC, we established immune-reconstituted MKN-45 CDX models in NSG mice (figure 7A; see online supplemental figure S5A). While combination therapy with 5-FU and PD-1 blockade only modestly delayed tumor growth in control (SgCtrl) tumors, it elicited profound and sustained suppression in ATF4-knockout (SgATF4) tumors (figure 7B,C). Notably, the significant extension of overall survival conferred by combination therapy was strictly dependent on ATF4 ablation (figure 7D). To further validate these findings, we generated immune-reconstituted PDX models from GC tissues (figure 7E; see online supplemental figure S5B), stratified into ATF4-high (ATF4High) and ATF4-low (ATF4Low) groups by IHC (see online supplemental figure S5C). Compared with the ATF4High group, ATF4Low PDX tumors exhibited significantly stronger growth suppression and markedly prolonged survival in response to 5-FU plus anti-PD-1 (figure 7F–H), demonstrating that elevated ATF4 expression impairs the therapeutic sensitivity of GC to this combination therapy.

Next, flow cytometric analysis of the TME in both CDX and PDX models consistently revealed that ATF4 depletion (or lower expression) under combination therapy markedly enhanced the expression of the effector cytokines IFN-γ and GZMB, while concurrently reducing the levels of key CD8+ T cell exhaustion markers LAG-3, TIM-3, and PD-1 (figure 7I; see online supplemental figure S5D). Moreover, H&E and IHC further corroborated these immunological changes, demonstrating that ATF4 deficiency potentiated the efficacy of the 5-FU plus anti-PD-1 regimen. Specifically, SgATF4 CDX tumors and ATF4Low PDX tumors exhibited significantly diminished tumor proliferation, elevated apoptosis and DNA damage, and attenuated CD8+ T cell exhaustion compared with their respective controls (figure 7J; see online supplemental figure S5E). Collectively, these findings indicate that ATF4 may serve as a central modulator of treatment response in GC, with its reduced expression potentially enhancing tumor sensitivity to combined 5-FU and anti-PD-1 therapy. By reprogramming the TME, reinvigorating cytotoxic CD8+ T cell activity, and attenuating key pathways of drug resistance and immune evasion, ATF4 could represent a pivotal determinant influencing the efficacy of chemo-immunotherapy in GC.

Discussion

The integration of 5-FU-based chemotherapy with ICIs represents a paradigm shift in GC therapy, yet its clinical efficacy remains constrained by chemoresistance and immunosuppressive TMEs.31 32 Here, we address this critical gap by identifying the integrated stress-responsive transcription factor ATF4 as a central mechanistic node that actively undermines the therapeutic potential of chemo-immunotherapy in 5-FU-resistant patients. Elevated ATF4 expression under resistance conditions drives a dual-pathway resistance mechanism: intrinsically, through transcriptional upregulation of HSPA9 to enhance DNA damage repair and promote tumor cell survival; extrinsically, by remodeling the TME to foster immunosuppressive factor secretion and induce CD8+ T cell exhaustion.

ATF4, a master stress-responsive transcription factor, has emerged as a pivotal player in tumor chemoresistance.26 28 Our integrated analysis of scRNA sequencing data and clinical survival data from bulk transcriptomics in GC revealed that ATF4 exhibits pronounced upregulation in 5-FU-resistant GC cell clusters, correlating strongly with poor patient prognosis. This observation was corroborated with our prior findings on its role in chemoresistance.26 Previously, we demonstrated that ATF4 drives 5-FU resistance by binding to PICH to regulate Cyclin A1 transcription.26 In the present study, ATF4-targeted ChIP-seq identified HSPA9 as the most significantly enriched target gene, which is closely linked to the DDR pathway. Together, these results support a multi-faceted model of ATF4-mediated 5-FU resistance, wherein ATF4 not only cooperates with PICH as a co-factor to drive chemoresistance but may also independently promote therapeutic resistance by directly transactivating HSPA9 to enhance DNA repair capacity.

Interestingly, emerging evidence highlights a distinct yet critical role for ATF4 in shaping CD8+ T cell functionality within the TME.28 29 Under taurine-deficient conditions, ATF4 acts as a central transcriptional regulator that connects endoplasmic reticulum stress in CD8+ T cells to their functional exhaustion, directly upregulating key immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, CTLA-4, TIGIT, and TIM-3.28 Moreover, hypoxia-induced ATF4 in CD8+ T cells has been reported to compromise their proliferative capacity, survival, cytokine production, and differentiation from precursor-exhausted (TPex) to terminally exhausted (Ttex) subsets, thereby attenuating antitumor immunity.29 Our results are consistent with this notion, demonstrating that high ATF4 expression in GC cells fosters cytokine secretion and facilitates CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Importantly, however, our study shifts the emphasis to tumor cell-intrinsic ATF4 as a mediator of immunosuppression, pointing to a cell type-specific mode of action. Moreover, recent studies have further elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying immune evasion in GC. circRHBDD1, which is highly expressed in GC and linked to poor prognosis, upregulates PD-L1 and suppresses CD8+ T cell infiltration by binding IGF2BP2 and blocking its TRIM25-mediated ubiquitination, thereby stabilizing PD-L1 mRNA via m6A modification.33 Hypoxia-targeted TH-302 nanoparticles alleviate tumor hypoxia, reduce HIF-1α and PD-L1, enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration, and increase intratumoral TNF-α, IFN-γ, and granzyme B, thereby improving anti-PD-1 efficacy.34 Together, these findings support the therapeutic rationale for combining ATF4 targeting with ICB. However, the mechanistic crosstalk between ATF4 in tumor versus immune cells remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

While HSPA9 functions as a central structural-signaling center at mitochondria-associated ER membranes and a core component of the ER quality control machinery,35 36 our findings reveal a novel and critical role for this protein, demonstrating that ATF4-driven HSPA9 overexpression directly enhances ATM-dependent DNA repair capacity, as evidenced by a reduction in DNA double-strand breaks, and a significant decrease in γH2AX foci formation. Collectively, these findings lead us to propose a model wherein chronic damage pressure from agents like 5-FU and intrinsic tumor stress, under conditions of high ATF4 expression, drives sustained transcriptional upregulation of HSPA9. We hypothesize that HSPA9 subsequently maintains elevated levels of active ATM kinase potentially by promoting its phosphorylation37 or shielding it from autophagic or ubiquitin-mediated degradation,38 39 thereby amplifying the DNA repair fidelity of ATF4-high tumor cells and ultimately driving resistance to chemoimmunotherapy. The ATM kinase critically mediates both chemoresistance and immune evasion through distinct pathways: it inactivates the tumor suppressor P53 to enhance cell survival,40 while activating STAT3 to drive PD-L1-dependent T-cell exhaustion.41 Our study delineates the ATF4-HSPA9 axis as the master upstream regulator that converges on and amplifies these dual ATM functions. We demonstrate that ATF4-driven HSPA9 upregulation potentiates the DDR, intrinsically fostering 5-FU resistance via enhanced repair of genotoxic lesions. Extrinsically, HSPA9-mediated DDR activation remodels the TME through coordinated immunosuppressive signaling, culminating in CD8+ T-cell dysfunction. Thus, HSPA9 emerges as a pivotal molecular integrator, functionally linking DDR amplification to a coupled resistance phenotype. This integrated ATF4-HSPA9-DDR axis transcends conventional models that treat chemoresistance and immune evasion as separate processes. Unlike prior studies focusing on isolated mechanisms,12 16 our work reveals that therapeutic stress triggers this unified adaptive program, which concurrently undermines chemotherapy cytotoxicity and immunotherapy efficacy by simultaneously enhancing tumor cell fitness and suppressing anti-tumor immunity. This finding provides a novel conceptual framework for understanding and targeting concurrent resistance in GC.

Growing evidence indicates that heterogeneous therapeutic responses to chemo-immunotherapy are driven by widespread therapeutic resistance and the lack of precise predictive biomarkers.42,44 Our study suggests ATF4 may represent a promising predictive biomarker for 5-FU-based chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. We observed elevated ATF4 expression in 5-FU-resistant patients with unfavorable prognosis, and its expression positively correlated with PD-L1 levels. Furthermore, in patients receiving combined 5-FU and PD-1 blockade therapy, those with low ATF4 expression demonstrated significantly improved clinical outcomes and prolonged survival. These findings might establish ATF4 as a potential stratification marker and also reveal its functional role as a therapeutic target, offering a strategic approach to enhance the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy strategies. In the future, independent patient cohorts and randomized clinical trials are required to further demonstrate the predictive value of ATF4 before its clinical application.

In summary, our study redefines the functional role of ATF4 in GC by demonstrating its capacity to orchestrate both chemoresistance and immune evasion through transcriptional activation of HSPA9 and subsequent engagement of the DDR pathway (figure 8). These findings provide a compelling rationale for developing therapies targeting ATF4-dependent signaling, with promising translational implications for overcoming resistance to combined chemo-immunotherapy in GC.

ATF4 confers dual resistance to 5-FU chemotherapy and PD-1 blockade in GC. In GC with high ATF4 expression, ATF4 transcriptionally upregulates HSPA9, which facilitates ATM-mediated repair of DNA damage. This enhanced repair capacity attenuates P53-dependent apoptosis, promoting tumor cell survival. Concurrently, ATF4 activates STAT3, leading to increased PD-L1 expression on tumor cells. Elevated PD-L1 suppresses CD8+ T-cell function, as evidenced by reduced IFN-γ and GZMB production, and induces upregulation of co-inhibitory receptors including LAG-3 and TIM-3. Collectively, these ATF4-driven mechanisms confer resistance to both 5-FU chemotherapy and anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.
