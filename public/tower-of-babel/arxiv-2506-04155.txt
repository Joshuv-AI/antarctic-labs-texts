Signatures of the Fermi surface reconstruction of a doped Mott insulator in a slab geometry
Gregorio Staffieri, Michele Fabrizio

Abstract
We investigate a hole-doped Mott insulator in a slab geometry using the dynamical cluster approximation. We show that the enhancement of the correlation strength at the surface results in the remarkable evolution of the layer-projected Fermi surface, which exhibits hole-like pockets in the superficial layers, but gradually evolves into a single electron-like surface in the innermost layers. We further analyze the behavior of the Friedel oscillations induced by the surface and identify distinct signatures of the Fermi surface reconstruction as function of hole-doping. In addition, we introduce a computationally tractable quantity that diagnoses the same Fermi surface variation by the concurrent breakdown of Luttinger’s theorem. Both the latter quantity and the Friedel oscillations serve as reliable indicators of the change in Fermi surface topology, without the need for any periodization in momentum space.



Introduction

One of the many captivating features of high-T
c
copper-oxide superconductors is the evidence of
a Fermi surface reconstruction upon hole doping
Taillefer 2009
;
Sebastian and Proust 2015
;
Proust and Taillefer 2019
. In the overdoped regime, the Fermi surface is electron-like, large
Vignolle et al. 2008
and aligns with Luttinger’s theorem
Luttinger 1960
. Conversely, in the opposite underdoped regime the Fermi surface consists of Fermi pockets
Norman et al. 1998
;
LeBoeuf et al. 2007
;
Doiron-Leyraud et al. 2007
;
Meng et al. 2009
;
Hartstein et al. 2020
;
Kunisada et al. 2020
that appear to violate
Luttinger’s theorem and coexist with a pseudogap at the antinodal points
Timusk and Statt 1999
;
Damascelli et al. 2003
;
Sobota et al. 2021
. In angle-resolved photoemission, these pockets resembles Fermi arcs
Norman et al. 1998
;
Meng et al. 2009
, which is likely due to the interference with a nearby Luttinger surface
Yang et al. 2006
;
Rice et al. 2011
;
Robinson et al. 2019
;
Fabrizio 2022
,
the location in momentum space of the zeros of the Green’s function at zero frequency
Dzyaloshinskii 2003
, as opposed to the location of poles that defines the Fermi surface.
Indeed, the existence of a Luttinger surface in the underdoped regime of cuprates explains both
the presence of a pseudogap in the spectrum and the violation of Luttinger’s theorem

Heath and Bedell 2020
;
Skolimowski and Fabrizio 2022
.

Evidence of a similar doping-driven Fermi surface reconstruction has been also discovered in the simple single-band Hubbard model by means of various numerical techniques, ranging from variational
Tocchio et al. 2012
and diagrammatic
Rossi et al. 2024
Monte Carlo methods, to cluster extensions of dynamical mean-field theory
Stanescu and Kotliar 2006
;
Maier et al. 2005
;
Stanescu et al. 2006
;
Sakai et al. 2009
;
Sakai et al. 2010
;
Sordi et al. 2010
;
Gull et al. 2010
;
Yamaji and Imada 2011
;
Gull et al. 2013
;
Scheurer et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Bragança et al. 2018
;
Singh et al. 2022
.

In this study, we explore the intriguing effects of such Fermi surface reconstruction on doping a slab of a model Mott insulator. We investigate this phenomenon using the dynamical cluster approximation
Maier et al. 2005
. The slab geometry presents several intriguing avenues that merit exploration. In particular, in Sec.
I
we study
the doped Mott insulating slab with open boundary conditions. We demonstrate that the effective enhancement of correlation strength at the surface, which is responsible for the phenomenon known as the surface dead layer effect
Borghi et al. 2009
;
Tagliente et al. 2025
, results in a non-uniform distribution of doping that decreases as we approach the surface. Consequently, a layer-dependent Fermi surface reconstruction occurs that could be experimentally investigated in a similar manner to the dead layer effect
Rodolakis et al. 2009
.
In Section
II
, we study the Friedel oscillations that arise from the open geometry of the slab. We show that these oscillations can serve as an additional tool to discern the changes in the size and shape of the Fermi surface as the doping level is increased.
Finally, Section
III
is devoted to Conclusions.

We consider a slab composed of
N
layers, each of which is described by a two-dimensional (2D) Hubbard model with nearest neighbor hopping
t
. Nearest neighbor layers are in turn coupled to each other by an interlayer hopping
t_{\perp}
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. The system has periodic boundary conditions along
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
, while open boundary conditions are imposed along
\mathbf{x}
. The Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-t\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\,\sum_{\langle\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R^{\prime}}\rangle\sigma}\,\big(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{R^{\prime}}\ell\sigma}+H.c.\big)

(1)

\displaystyle-\mu\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\,\sum_{\mathbf{R}\sigma}\,n_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}+U\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\,\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\,n_{\mathbf{R}\ell\uparrow}\,n_{\mathbf{R}\ell\downarrow}

\displaystyle-t_{\perp}\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}\,\sum_{\mathbf{R}\sigma}\,\big(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{R}\ell+1\sigma}+H.c.\big)\,.

The operators
c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}
and
c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}
annihilate and create, respectively, one electron on site
\mathbf{R}
of
layer
\ell=1...N
with spin
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
, while
n_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}=c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{R}\ell\sigma}
. The other parameters are
U
, the on-site Hubbard repulsion, and
\mu
, the chemical potential.

We study the Hamiltonian (
1
) by means of dynamical cluster approximation (DCA)
Maier et al. 2005
. Within DCA, the Brillouin zone is partitioned into patches and the self-energy assumed constant within each patch, resulting in a piecewise function of momentum. We use the quantum impurity solver implemented within the TRIQS library
Parcollet et al. 2015
, which employs a continuous time quantum Monte Carlo (CTQMC) evaluation of the partition function expanded in powers of the hybridization
Seth et al. 2016
.
Hereafter, we consider a partition of the Brillouin zone in four patches centered at the high symmetry points,
\mathbf{\Gamma}=(0,0)
,
\mathbf{X}=(\pi,0)
,
\mathbf{Y}=(0,\pi)
and
\mathbf{M}=(\pi,\pi)
, with the same tiling of Ref.
Hettler et al. 2000
. This choice is the simplest one to access the antinodal points
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, which are important to study the Fermi surface reconstruction
Bragança et al. 2018
.

Layered systems, such as slabs, interfaces and heterostructures, have been extensively investigated by DMFT
Helmes et al. 2008
;
Ishida and Liebsch 2009
;
Potthoff and Nolting 1999
, where the self-energy is assumed to be local in each layer but layer dependent,
\Sigma_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(i\omega_{n})=\Sigma_{\ell}(i\omega_{n})\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
, where
\omega_{n}
are Matsubara frequencies. We improve this description by including the coarse-grained momentum effects of DCA, thus a self-energy matrix with components

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{P})=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\,\Sigma_{\ell}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{P})\,,

where
\mathbf{P}
is one of the four patches.
Within this approximation, the lattice Green function
\hat{G}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})
is a matrix in layer indices
Helmes et al. 2008
whose inverse satisfies

\hat{G}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})^{-1}=i\omega_{n}+\mu-{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k})-\hat{\Sigma}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{P_{\mathbf{k}}})+\hat{t}_{\perp}\,,

(2)

where
\hat{t}_{\perp}
is the matrix with elements
t_{\perp}
just between nearest neighbor layers,

\mathbf{k}
the momentum in the
y
-
z
plane,
{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k})=-2t(\cos k_{y}+\cos k_{z})
, and
\mathbf{P_{\mathbf{k}}}
the patch reference momentum that contains
\mathbf{k}
. The local layer-dependent Green function is obtained averaging the diagonal elements of
\hat{G}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})
over each patch

\displaystyle G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{P},\ell)=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;N_{\mathbf{P}}\;}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{P}}\,G_{\ell\ell}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\,,

(3)

with
N_{\mathbf{P}}
the number of
\mathbf{k}
-points in the patch
\mathbf{P}
,
and provides, through Dyson’s equation, the Weiss field of the effective four-site impurity model.
The latter is in turn used to compute the layer-
\ell
partition function, thus closing the self-consistent loop. For simplicity, in what follows we take
t_{\perp}=t
the unit of energy.

As we already mentioned, weakly doping with holes the paramagnetic Mott insulating state of the 2D
Hubbard model leads to a pseudogap phase characterized by a coexistence of a Luttinger surface and hole-like Fermi pockets
Stanescu and Kotliar 2006
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Bragança et al. 2018
. Upon further doping, the Fermi surface seems to undergo a Lifshitz transition and to turn into the expected electron-like one. Concurrently, the pseudogap disappears and conventional Fermi liquid properties are recovered
Wu et al. 2018
;
Bragança et al. 2018
. In a slab geometry, correlation effects are enhanced at the surface
Borghi et al. 2009
;
Tagliente et al. 2025
,
which envisages the possibility of a layer dependent Fermi surface topology.

We address this issue by studying a 5-layer doped Mott insulating slab for different values of
\Delta\mu=\mu-U/2<0
, which is the deviation of the chemical potential from the particle-hole symmetric point,
\Delta\mu=0
. Throughout this work, we take
U=8
and temperature
T=1/40
, parameters for which we have verified that the Lifshitz transition coincides with the destruction of the pseudogap. In the isolated, single-layer Hubbard model, this transition is conventionally characterized through the sign of the renormalized dispersion
r(\mathbf{k})={\epsilon}(\mathbf{k})-\mu+\text{Re}\,\Sigma(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{k})
, where
\omega_{0}=\pi T
is the first fermionic Matsubara frequency, computed at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{X}

Šimkovic et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Bragança et al. 2018
. In a slab, since nearest neighbors layers are coupled to each other, the nature of the layer-resolved Fermi surface may be characterized through the real part of the layer-dependent Green’s function at
\mathbf{X}
,
\text{Re}\,G(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{X},\ell)
. When
\text{Re}\,G(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{X},\ell)
is positive(negative), the layer Fermi surface is hole(electron)-like. In Fig.
2
, left panel, we plot this quantity for different values of
\Delta\mu
. We observe that
\text{Re}\,G(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{X},\ell)
strongly depends on the distance from the surface. In particular, the transition to an electron-like Fermi surface occurs at first in the central layer when
\Delta\mu\lesssim-1.1
, while the outer layers, which feel an enhanced correlation
strength, still display the hole-like Fermi surface of a weakly doped Mott insulator. Decreasing
\Delta\mu
, we observe that the transition spreads to the superficial layers until it reaches the surfaces,
\ell=1
and
N
, at
\Delta\mu\simeq-2
. In Fig.
2
, right panel, we plot the layer dependent electron density for the same values of
\Delta\mu
as in the left panel. We note that, at equal
\Delta\mu
, the outer layers are less doped that the inner ones, revealing once again the dead-layer effect.
In the figure, we also show the density
n^{*}_{\ell}
at which the Fermi surface reconstruction occurs in layer
\ell
. As expected,
n^{*}_{\ell}
is significantly smaller at the surface layers, as if they
were characterized by a larger
U

Bragança et al. 2018
;
Šimkovic et al. 2024
.
For completeness, we also define a layer-dependent renormalized dispersion in the patch
\mathbf{X}
through

r(\mathbf{X},\ell)={\epsilon}(\mathbf{X})-\mu+\text{Re}\,\Sigma_{\ell}(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{X})\,,

(4)

which we plot in Fig.
3
. We expect that
r(\mathbf{X},\ell)>0
corresponds to an electron-like Fermi surface, while

r(\mathbf{X},\ell)<0
to a hole-like one. Indeed, the behavior of
r(\mathbf{X},\ell)
in
Fig.
3
and that of
\text{Re}\,G(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{X},\ell)
in Fig.
2

are perfectly consistent with each other.

I.1
Another marker of the Fermi surface reconstruction

The hole-like Fermi surface at weak doping evidences a violation of Luttinger’s theorem
Luttinger 1960
. The total number of electrons
N(T)
at fixed
\mu
and as function of temperature
T
can be calculated through

\displaystyle N(T)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\,T\sum_{n}\,\text{e}^{i\omega_{n}0^{+}}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\ln G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}\;}

(5)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\,T\sum_{n}\,G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\Sigma(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i\omega_{n}\;}

\displaystyle=N_{L}(T)+I_{L}(T)\,.

At
T=0
, the number of electrons is an integer. Also
N_{L}(0)
becomes an integer and corresponds
to twice, because of spin, the number of the
\mathbf{k}
-points in the regions enclosed by the surfaces at which the Green’s function at zero frequency
G(0,\mathbf{k})
changes sign. We emphasize that such sign change occurs at the Fermi surface, where
G(0,\mathbf{k})
has a pole, as well as at the Luttinger surface
Dzyaloshinskii 2003
, where
G(0,\mathbf{k})
vanishes. In other words, both surfaces should contribute to the electron counting if Luttinger’s theorem, which states that
I_{L}(0)=0
, were literally taken. However, it has been demonstrated
Heath and Bedell 2020
that Luttinger’s theorem is violated in presence of a
Luttinger surface. In such case, therefore,
I_{L}(0)
must be not only finite but also quantized in integer values. We recall that Luttinger’s demonstration of his theorem invokes the invariance of
the Luttinger-Ward functional with respect to a global shift of the Green’s function frequencies,
which implies that, e.g.,

I^{\Delta}_{L}(T)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,T\sum_{n}\,G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\;\Delta\Sigma(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=0\,,

(6)

where

\displaystyle\Delta\Sigma(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=\frac{\displaystyle\;\Sigma(i\omega_{n}+i\Omega,\mathbf{k})-\Sigma(i\omega_{n}-i\Omega,\mathbf{k})\;}{\displaystyle\;2i\Omega\;}\;,

with
\Omega=2\pi T
, is the discrete derivative. It is tempting to argue that, in the limit
T\to 0
,

\Delta\Sigma(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\to\partial_{i\omega}\,\Sigma(i\omega,\mathbf{k})
, thus that (
6
)
implies
I_{L}(0)=I^{\Delta}_{L}(0)=0
. However, this conclusion is generally not true. The reason is
that the function that is summed in
I^{\Delta}_{L}(T)
of (
6
) has different
analytic properties at any finite
T
than the function in
I_{L}(T)
of (
5
), recalling that the imaginary parts of the Green’s function and self-energy may have a discontinuity crossing zero Matsubara frequency. In the supplementary material of Ref.
Fabrizio 2022
it was demonstrated that at leading order of an expansion in powers of
T

\displaystyle I_{L}(T)-I^{\Delta}_{L}(T)=I_{L}(T)

(7)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\text{Re}\,G(i\pi T,\mathbf{k})\,\text{Im}\,\Sigma(i\pi T,\mathbf{k})+O\big(T^{2}\big)\,,

where the first term on the right hand side is linear in
T
both in absence and in presence of
a Luttinger surface.

In Ref.
Skolimowski and Fabrizio 2022
it was further shown that
I_{L}(T)
is a boundary term, actually the
value divided by
\pi
of the imaginary part of a function
evaluated at the first Matsubara frequency
\omega_{0}=\pi T
. Since
I_{L}(T\to 0)
is an integer,
it was also conjectured
Skolimowski and Fabrizio 2022
that such imaginary part is the phase accumulated by a complex function
\Phi(i\omega_{n})=\Phi(-i\omega_{n})^{*}
from 0 to
\infty
, and thus that

I_{L}(T)=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\,\tan^{-1}\frac{\displaystyle\;\text{Im}\,\Phi(i\omega_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;\text{Re}\,\Phi(i\omega_{n})\;}_{\big|^{\infty}_{\omega_{0}}}\;,

(8)

with
\text{Im}\,\Phi(i\omega_{n})\sim\omega_{n}
at small
\omega_{n}
, consistently with (
7
).

Figure 4
:
Layer dependence of
S(T)
in (
9
) for different values of
\Delta\mu
.

The proposed expression (
8
) shows that, despite the expansion in
T
suggests that

I_{L}(T\to 0)\to 0
, in reality the series converges to a finite integer
value if
\text{Re}\,\Phi(i\omega_{0}\to 0)<0
.
If we assume that (8) is correct, thus that (
7
) is actually the leading term of the expansion in powers of
T
of
\text{arg}\big(\Phi(i\pi T)\big)
, we may diagnose Luttinger’s theorem breakdown simply by the sign change of

S(T)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\text{Re}\,G(i\pi T,\mathbf{k})\,\text{Im}\,\Sigma(i\pi T,\mathbf{k})\,.

(9)

In Fig.
4
we plot the layer dependence of

for the same values of
\Delta\mu
of

. We observe that the sign change of

reproduces well
the Lifshitz transition that we inferred from the figures

3

, but also demonstrate that
S(T)
in (
9
), a quantity that can be calculated by DCA
without any periodization, can be employed as an effective marker of the Luttinger’s theorem breakdown
due to the emergence of a Luttinger surface.

II
Friedel oscillations

The Friedel oscillations (FOs) of the electron density or of the density of states occur in metals due to the presence of defects
Friedel 1952
;
Friedel 1958
. Specifically, for a defect with dimension
d_{*}<d
, with
d
the system dimension, and an isotropic Fermi surface, the FOs decay asymptotically as a power law in the distance
r
from the defect
Kittel 1963
,

\displaystyle\text{FOs}(r)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\displaystyle\;\cos(2k_{F}r+\delta)\;}{\displaystyle\;r^{d-d_{*}}\;}\;,

\displaystyle k_{F}\,r

\displaystyle\gg 1\,,

(10)

where
k_{F}
is the Fermi momentum and
\delta
a phase shift. In our slab geometry, the FOs are caused by the surfaces, thus
d_{*}=2
and
d=3
. Since the periodicity depends on
k_{F}
, the FOs are very sensitive to the shape and topology of the Fermi surface
Hosur 2012
. In this Section, we investigate the signatures of the doping-driven Fermi surface reconstruction on the FOs caused by the open geometry.

II.1
Friedel oscillations in a non-interacting slab

In order to have a reference case, we start by considering our slab in absence of interaction.
In this case, the electron wavefunction is the product of an in-plane Bloch wave
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{R})
and out-of-plane one,

\displaystyle\phi_{k_{x}}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}\;}\;\sin k_{x}\ell\,,

\displaystyle k_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;\pi n_{x}\;}{\displaystyle\;N+1\;}\;,

(11)

where both
n_{x}
and
\ell
run from 1 to
N
.
The Green’s function in the diagonal basis simply reads

G_{0}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k},k_{x})=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\;i\omega_{n}+\mu-{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k})+2t\cos k_{x}\;\;}\;,

(12)

thus the layer-dependent local Green’s function

G_{0}(i\omega_{n},\ell)=\sum_{k_{x}}\,\phi_{k_{x}}^{2}(\ell)\,\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;V\;}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k},k_{x})\,,

(13)

with

the number of sites within each layer.
We here concentrate on the FOs in the local spectral function at the chemical potential,
\mathcal{N}(\ell)
, which can be approximated by the imaginary part of the local Green’s function at the first Matsubara frequency,
\mathcal{N}(\ell)\simeq-\text{Im}\,G(i\omega_{0},\ell)/\pi
. We choose this quantity because the FOs in the density are severely suppressed by correlations, and thus harder to disentangle from the QMC noise. In the non-interacting slab,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{0}(\ell)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\,\text{Im}\,G_{0}(i\omega_{0},\ell)=\sum_{k_{x}}\,\phi_{k_{x}}^{2}(\ell)\,I_{0}(k_{x})\,,

(14)

\displaystyle I_{0}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi V\;}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\text{Im}\,G_{0}(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{k},k_{x})\,.

Equation (
14
) allows quantifying through
I_{0}(k_{x})
the contributions to

\mathcal{N}_{0}(\ell)
of the different
k_{x}
-dependent Fermi surface sheets,
\mathbf{k}_{F}(k_{x})
, defined through

{\epsilon}\big(\mathbf{k}_{F}(k_{x})\big)=\mu+2t\,\cos k_{x}\,.

(15)

In Fig.
5
we plot these sheets in a slab with
N=5
, for both the half-filled and hole-doped cases.

II.2
Friedel oscillations in the interacting slab

In the interacting slab,

is no longer a good quantum number and the analysis of the FOs
is not as simple as in the absence of interaction.
In Fig.

II.3
Friedel oscillations in a slab with homogeneous self-energy

The Friedel oscillations are evidently sensitive to the size and shape of the Fermi surface. Therefore, they should bear signatures of the Lifshitz transition we earlier discussed, specifically of the fact that at low doping the Fermi surface is composed of hole-like Fermi pockets and turns electron-like
above a threshold doping. For that, we could perform the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{N}(\ell)
, in analogy to the non-interacting case. However, it is numerically not affordable to study a slab with a number of layer larger than 5, which is not enough to perform a meaningful Fourier analysis. Moreover, the coexistence of Fermi surfaces of different topologies makes the process of identifying the fingerprints of a specific Fermi surface character significantly harder. To overcome these issues, we study a simplified system where the self-energy is assumed not to depend on the layer index. A similar approach, known as homogeneous self-energy approximation (HSEA)
Chatterjee et al. 2019
has been used to study the FOs caused by an impurity in a 2D Hubbard model for large system size. This approximation
is expected to reproduce the leading asymptotic behavior of the FOs far away from the defect. In our specific example, we implement HSEA by considering the DCA self-energy of the central layer (
\ell=3
) of the 5-layer slab, computed through the fully self-consistent algorithm described in Section
I
, and use it as a homogeneous self-energy for all layers of a slab with
N\gg 5
at the same chemical potential as the original
N=5
slab. In this way, all layers share the same Fermi surface topology. This method allows us to treat slabs with a large number of layers and thus study with a better resolution the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{N}(\ell)
. Moreover, since the self-energy is the same for the all layers,
k_{x}
is again a good quantum number, which allows straightly generalizing (
14
) replacing
I_{0}(k_{x})
with

\displaystyle I(k_{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;4\pi\;}\,\text{Im}\sum_{\mathbf{P}}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;N_{\mathbf{P}}\;}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{P}}

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\;i\omega_{0}+\mu-{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k})+2t\cos(k_{x})-\Sigma(i\omega_{0},\mathbf{P})\;\;}\;.

In the left panel of Fig.

at the surface is larger than in the bulk, similarly to what happens in the non-interacting case. This qualitative difference is not observed if one computes
\mathcal{N}(\ell)
using a homogeneous single-site DMFT self-energy, in which case

is always larger at the surface. This suggests that the observed difference is in fact related to the change in Fermi surface topology, which single-site DMFT cannot capture.

Finally, we investigate how the period of the FOs and

In the scenario proposed by Stanescu and Kotliar

In other words, the hole-like Fermi pockets appear around the maxima of the renormalized dispersion

r(\mathbf{k})

in Fig.

III
Conclusions

We have demonstrated that the surface dead layer effect
Borghi et al. 2009
can lead to a remarkable phenomenon: the reconstruction of the Fermi surface as a function of the distance from the surface in a hole-doped Mott insulator. This effect occurs because the more correlated surface layers are less doped, and, as evidenced by several numerical studies in single layers, they host hole-like Fermi pockets that violate Luttinger’s theorem. In contrast, the doping increases in the less correlated inner layers, which display the expected electron-like Fermi surface. We have also introduced a novel quantity,
S(T)
in (
9
), which serves to directly diagnose the breakdown of Luttinger’s theorem.

Furthermore, we have investigated how the different topology of the Fermi surface at weak and large hole-doping affects the Friedel oscillations in a slab geometry. When the doping is weak, the Friedel oscillations are shown to be consistent with the existence of hole-like Fermi pockets. Conversely, at higher doping, the Friedel oscillations bear the hallmarks of an electron-like Fermi surface adiabatically connected to the non-interacting one.

Both the Friedel oscillations and
S(T)
in (
9
) are therefore effective tools to
characterize the Fermi surface topology, without the need of any periodization scheme that may introduce biases when extracting the Fermi surface from the Green’s function.

In conclusion, all our results highlight how a slab geometry can be efficiently employed to infer additional information beyond calculations using periodic boundary conditions.
