School-level variation in physical fitness outcomes among children and adolescents
Authors: Dario Novak, Arunas Emeljanovas, Rockli Kim, Soohyeon Ko, S V Subramanian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical fitness is one of the key indicators of overall health in youth and plays an important role in decreasing the risk of a number of chronic conditions. There has been limited research on the relationship between school-level factors and young people’s physical fitness, with no studies examining school-level variation in physical fitness outcomes for children and adolescents in Croatian schools. The primary aim of this article is to examine school-level variation in a number of health and performance/skill-related physical fitness outcomes. Specifically, we tested the following hypotheses: H1: When variation in physical fitness outcomes is partitioned across schools, counties, and regions, the largest contextual variation will be attributable to the school level; H2: School-level variation in physical fitness outcomes will remain substantial even after adjusting for individual-level covariates such as sex and class, and H3: School-level variation in physical fitness outcomes will be consistently high across both elementary and secondary schools. A total of 15,106 children and adolescents from elementary and secondary schools in Croatia participated in this study. They were tested in the following areas: body size, coordination, speed, agility, flexibility, strength, power and cardio. The multilevel analysis revealed that the largest contextual variation in physical fitness outcomes is attributable to the school level, underscoring the critical role that schools play in shaping students’ physical fitness. Even after adjusting for individual-level covariates such as sex and class (grade), substantial variation between schools remained. Finally, we observed that school-level variation in physical fitness outcomes was consistently high across both elementary and secondary schools. The findings suggest that there is a need for comprehensive, multi-tiered interventions designed to guarantee that every student can access high-quality physical education and ample opportunities for physical activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29086-z.Keywords: Health behavior, Children, Adolescence, Physical fitness, Multilevel modellingSubject terms: Health care, Risk factors

Introduction

Physical activity and health are essential components of overall well-being. When people engage in regular physical activity, they improve their aerobic fitness, neuromuscular fitness, boost immunity, and enhance psychological well-being (World Health Organization, 2018 [34]). Physical fitness is one of the key indicators of overall health in youth and plays a substantial role in decreasing the risk of a number of chronic conditions, like cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, musculoskeletal disorders diabetes16; Guthold et al.,2020 [13]). Physical fitness is a multi-dimensional construct that encompasses both health-related and performance/skill-related components. Health-related fitness includes cardiovascular endurance, muscular strength, flexibility, and body composition, all of which are important indicators of overall health and predictors of future chronic disease risk in children and adolescents (Ortega, et al., 2023). Performance-related fitness involves components such as agility, balance, coordination, and speed, which are crucial for motor development and engagement in physical activities and sports during youth (Ortega, et al., 2023). Recently studies have shown a strong link between aerobic and neuromuscular fitness in youths with general mortality and cancer related mortality later in life7,15; Ballin, Nordström, Nordström2,. Fitness consists of various components, however aerobic fitness along with muscular strength and power have demonstrated the strongest and most consistent links to health. As a result, these are regarded as the most essential elements of health-related physical fitness22. Other physical fitness components include mobility or flexibility, and body composition or anthropometry22.

For children and adolescents, schools serve a vital role in supporting physical fitness, especially through the regular physical education classes23. Essentially, schools can greatly influence students’ health and health related behaviours in multiple ways. The influence of schools extends beyond academic learning and includes shaping physical, mental, and social well-being3,4. For instance, schools can influence health and health behaviours through various means, such as health and safety protocols, the establishment of school policies, peer interactions, extracurricular activities, teacher support, and fostering a sense of school community. Additionally, they can shape students’ individual traits, skills, and knowledge that contribute to their overall health and health related behaviours9,10,29; Novak, et al., 2024 [20]). By providing education, resources, and a supportive environment, schools can promote lifelong health and wellness habits. Creating a healthy school environment benefits not only the individual students but also the larger community, as healthy behaviors often extend beyond school walls into home and society.

School-level variation in physical fitness refers to differences in fitness outcomes attributable to characteristics and environments unique to individual schools, beyond the influence of regional or individual factors. These may include the quality and frequency of physical education classes, availability of extracurricular sports and fitness programs, access to facilities, and overall school culture promoting physical activity19,27,32. Recent studies have demonstrated that schools offering more frequent school-based interventions and a wider range of organized sports opportunities tend to have students with higher levels of physical fitness27,32. Similarly, participation in extracurricular sports clubs has been linked to improved performance-related fitness components19. Although our study did not directly measure these factors, recognizing their potential influence helps contextualize the observed school-level variation and highlights important targets for future research and intervention. In the past decade, there has been growing interest in researching school-based interventions aimed at improving health. These studies have primarily focused on enhancing youth health by addressing various aspects of the school environment, such as students’ social capital, school facilities, and physical education policies (Fletcher, Bonell, & Hargreaves12,. A key element of these interventions is the belief that some of the variation in health outcomes can be attributed to the school environment and level14. Research involving datasets where individuals are grouped within units, such as schools, enables the partitioning of variance in health outcomes across both individual and school levels using multi-level modelling14,25. This approach allows researchers to quantify the variation due to differences between schools and compare it to the variation between students. In essence, between-school variance estimate provides a more accurate measure of contextual effect or how differences between schools affect health outcomes after adjusting for individual demographic factors14. For example, a study analyzing school-level variation in different health outcomes, found that 4–40% of total variance in smoking can be attributed to school environment. The school-level variation in measures of problem behaviour and well-being was generally less pronounced compared to the variation observed in substance use28. Another study examined the variation between schools in physical activity, aerobic fitness, and participation in organized sports. While most of the variation in these variables was found within schools and individual classes, the study highlighted that school class, in particular, plays a significant role in influencing physical activity both during and outside of school hours17.

Taken together, school-level variation in health and various physical fitness outcomes likely reflects the combined effects of contextual factors in regard to the school environment, its resources, curricula, and socio-economic context. Understanding school-level variation in various physical fitness outcomes is crucial because it helps determine the extent to which interventions targeting school-level factors can impact overall youth health, such as a school’s ability to promote health and well-being among children and adolescents. Additionally, this data is essential for designing effective interventions within schools. For instance, estimating school-level variation is necessary to calculate the statistical power needed for school-based cluster randomized trials. However, there has been limited research on the relationship between school-level factors and young people’s health-related physical fitness, with no studies examining school-level variation in physical fitness outcomes for children and adolescents in Croatian schools.

In Croatia, physical education is a mandatory part of the school curriculum across all grades and is centrally regulated by the national education authority. However, despite standardized guidelines, implementation can vary widely between schools due to differences in available facilities, teaching quality, and extracurricular support for physical activity. Unlike in some countries where school-level differences in physical fitness have been extensively studied and monitored (e.g., through national fitness surveillance programs in countries like Slovenia), limited research has examined how such variations manifest within the Croatian context. Understanding school-level variation in Croatia is therefore essential for identifying gaps in equity and ensuring that national policies are effectively translated into consistent practice across all schools. Therefore, the primary aim of this article is to examine school-level variation in a number of health and performance/skill-related physical fitness outcomes. This will allow a robust analysis of school-level variation across childhood and adolescence in large Croatian sample. Specifically, we tested the following hypotheses:

H1: When variation in physical fitness outcomes is partitioned across schools, counties, and regions, the largest contextual variation will be attributable to the school level.

H2: School-level variation in physical fitness outcomes will remain substantial even after adjusting.

for individual-level covariates such as sex and class.

H3: School-level variation in physical fitness outcomes will be consistently high across both elementary and secondary schools.

Methods

Subjects

This research was a component of the Croatian Fitness (CROFIT) project, a nationwide study aimed at creating updated normative data for field-based health and performance/skill-related physical fitness tests for children and adolescents in primary and secondary schools, aged 11 to 18. The selection process was both random and stratified by county. Croatia is divided into 21 counties, and within each county, schools were randomly selected for inclusion in the study. In each school, every teacher measured a minimum of 100 students, with one class representing each age group, consisting of 25 students. The protocol was standardised for both primary schools (ages 11–14) and secondary schools (ages 15–18) across each county. Every school had an equal chance of participating in the study and schools were randomly selected from the sampling frame using a computer-generated randomization procedure. Once the project was finished, the final sample size for which all fitness field-based tests were completed was 15,106. Thus number of children and adolescents was drawn from multiple schools across various regions and counties, encompassing both primary (elementary) and secondary schools. To investigate school-level variation in physical fitness outcomes, participants were categorized based on key demographic and contextual factors. This included gender, school level (primary vs. secondary), and geographic location (region and county). While data on school-specific characteristics such as weekly frequency of physical education classes, extracurricular sports participation, and organized sports or fitness programs were not directly collected in this study, these factors are acknowledged as important contributors to school-level variation in physical fitness. Prior to the study, written informed consent was obtained from participants and their parents/guardians. All procedures were conducted anonymously and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to varying missing values across outcomes, the sample size differs for each outcome. Detailed information about the sample size for each outcome is provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Testing protocol

The physical fitness tests were administered during specific periods in October, February, and April. Prior to the study, each physical education teacher was provided with a standardised instruction protocol. Teachers recorded the data in an Excel spreadsheet, following a predetermined order (sex, age, height, weight, and all fitness tests).

The testing protocol involved two measurement days in each month: (1) in October, they completed coordination and agility tests, (2) in February they completed anthropometric measurements and mobility and flexibility tests, and 2) in April they completed strength and power as well as aerobic tests. Each student had enough time to rest between the tests (approximately 15 min).

Anthropometric measurements

Height was measured using a calibrated anthropometer (Alumet, Warsaw, Poland), from the Basis point to the Vertex point, with an accuracy of 1 mm. Body weight and body fat percentage were assessed using a TANITA electronic scale (Tanita Corporation of America Inc., Arlington Heights, IL, USA). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the participants weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters (BMI = kg/m²).

Performance/skill-related fitness components

To assess the level of performance/skill related fitness components, the following field tests were performed: (1) polygon backwards (whole body coordination), (2) polygon with 180-degree turn (whole body coordination), (3) ball rolling with non-dominant arm (speed and coordination), (4) sprint 20 m (sprint strength of lower extremities), (5) lateral agility, (6) transversal agility, and (7) shuttle and run. A brief explanation of each test is presented below:

Polygon backwards

The subject is placed in a four-legged position (resting on the front part of the foot and on the palms), with their back turned to the obstacles. The front part of the foot is placed directly in front of the starting line, and the direction of movement is looking between the legs. At the start signal, the subject moves as fast as possible backwards on all fours along the entire length of the 10-meter space. Crosses the 1 st obstacle, which is 3 m away from the starting line, weaves through the 2nd obstacle, which is 6 m away from the starting line, and crosses the finish line. Passes and pull-throughs are performed so that the legs pass first, followed by the other parts of the body. The task is completed when the subject, after completing the previously described tasks, crosses the finish line with both hands/last hand. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in s) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.93).

Polygon with 180-degree turn

The subject is placed in a four-legged position (resting on the feet and palms), facing the direction of movement. The palms are placed directly in front of the starting line. At the start signal, the subject moves forward on all fours as fast as they can to the 1 st line which is 3 m away from the starting line. When he crosses the 1 st line with either hand, he turns 180 degrees on all fours, continues on all fours backwards, pulls through the obstacle which is placed at the 6th meter away from the starting line, and crosses the finish line which is placed 9 m away from the starting line. The subject performs the pull-through so that the legs pass first, followed by the other parts of the body. The task is completed when the subject, after completing the previously described tasks, crosses the finish line with both hands/last hand. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in s) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.93).

Ball rolling with non-dominant arm

The subject stands in a diagonal position next to the starting line facing the direction of movement. The ball is in front of the subject on their non-dominant side. At the start signal, the subject, as fast as they can, rolls the ball on the ground with their non-dominant hand, changing the direction of the roll between and around the cones. The movement is performed 4 lengths in a row (1 length indicates the distance between the 1 st and 3rd cone and each cone is 3 m away from each other. The task is completed when the subject, after completing the 4th length, crosses the finish line by rolling the ball. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in s) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.93).

Sprint 20 m Each participant began by standing still at the starting point. Upon receiving the signal, they were required to sprint as quickly as possible for 20 m, with the task ending once they crossed the finish line. The final time, measured in seconds (s), was the average of three attempts (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.99).

Lateral agility

The subject stands inside the lines, next to one of the lines, sideways in relation to the direction of movement, in a shoulder-width stance. At the start signal, the subject moves sideways as fast as they can from one line to the other. The movement is performed 6 lengths in a row (1 length indicates the distance between two lines which are 4 m away). They change the direction of movement by stopping at the moment when they step on the line with his outside foot or crosses it. After the 6th length, he crosses the line without stopping. The task is completed when the subject, after performing the 6th length, stands on the line with the outside leg or crosses it without stopping. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in s) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.96).

Transversal agility

The subject stands in a high start position, in front of one of the lines (starting line) and next to the rack, facing the direction of movement. At the start signal, the subject moves as fast as they can, going around the racks, following the imaginary line of the placed number 8, running around the rack without touching it with their hands and bending down each time under the stretched elastic band. The movement is performed 4 lengths in a row (1 length indicates the distance between two racks which are 4 m away). The task is completed when the subject, after performing the 4th length, crosses the imaginary plane of the target line with their chest. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in s) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.95).

Shuttle and run

The subject stands on the outside of the start-finish line in a high starting position, facing the direction of movement. At the start signal, the subject runs as fast as they can across a space of 9 m, takes one sponge from the ground, carries it back, and places it on the ground behind the start-finish line. They repeat the same task with another sponge. The task is completed when the subject places the second sponge behind the start-finish line. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in s) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90).

Health-related fitness components

To assess the level of health-related fitness components, the following field tests were performed: (1) sit and reach (hip and spine mobility, hamstrings flexibility), (2) sit and reach short (hip and spine mobility, hamstrings flexibility), (3) stand and reach (hip and spine mobility, hamstrings flexibility), (4) standing broad jump (lower body strength), (5) medicine-ball throw (upper body strength), (6) sit-ups (core strength endurance), (7) sit-ups/short (core strength endurance), (8) squats (lower body strength endurance), (9) 600 m running (aerobic fitness test for girls in elementary schools), (10) 800 m running (aerobic fitness test for boys in elementary schools and aerobic fitness test for girls in secondary schools), and 11) 1000 m running (aerobic fitness test for boys in secondary schools). A brief explanation of each test is presented below:

Sit and reach

The subject sits cross-legged on the ground, with their back against the wall, with the legs extended exactly along the lines. they are leaning against the wall with the back of their head, shoulder blades and the sacral part of the spine, after which they stretch’s out their arms in this position and places the palm of their right hand on the back of their left hand. In this position, the palms are placed so that the middle fingers are covered, and the subject, without leaving the initial position next to the wall, lowers their arms fully extended to the ground in front of them. Exactly at that point, the point where the tip of the middle finger touches the ground, the examiner sets the zero value of the centimeter tape. The subject performs the maximum forward bend by slowly lowering, without jerking. The task is completed when the subject touches the maximum personal value on the centimeter tape with his fingertips and holds the position until the result is read. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in cm) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.98).

Sit and reach (short)

The subject sits cross-legged on the ground, legs spread two-foot lengths apart. In this position, the subject reaches forward with outstretched arms and places the palm of the right hand on the back of the left (the middle fingers are covered). The examiner places a centimeter tape between the legs so that the 40th centimeter is exactly on the imaginary line connecting the heels. After two slight forward bends, the subject slowly descends into the largest possible forward bend. The task is completed when the subject reaches and holds the maximum personal value on the centimeter tape with his fingertips for one second so that the result can be read smoothly. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in cm) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.99).

Stand and reach

The subject stands on the bench cross-legged in a joint position. Their legs are fully extended, and the tips of their toes are placed at the edge of the bench. The subject stretches out their arms and places the palm of their right hand on the back of their left hand, so that the middle fingers are covered. The subject performs the maximum forward bend by slowly lowering. The task is completed when the subject touches the maximum personal value on the centimeter tape with their fingertips and holds the position until the result is read. The average out of three attempts was taken as the final score (expressed in cm) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.94).

Standing broad jump

Each participant performed distance jumps from a standing position. They were instructed to bend their knees with their arms extended in front of them, parallel to the ground, then swing both arms, push off forcefully, and jump forward as far as possible, aiming to land with their feet together and maintain balance. The distance was measured in centimetres from the starting line to the heel of the nearest foot, with the average of the three attempts used as the final score, which was recorded in centimetres (cm) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.99).

Medicine-ball throw

Each participant threw a 1 kg medicine ball as far as possible from a lying position. The throw was executed using a flexion-extension movement, with the participant’s back remaining in contact with the ground. The distance was measured from the starting point (the participant’s shoulders) to where the medicine ball landed on the floor. The final score, expressed in decimetres (dm), was the average of four attempts (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.98).

Sit-ups in one minute

Trunk strength was evaluated by counting the maximum number of sit-ups completed in one minute. The participants sat on the floor with their backs straight, hands held together behind their necks, and knees bent at 90°, with their heels and feet flat on the mat. They then lay back, with their shoulders touching the mat, and returned to the sitting position, reaching their elbows forward to touch their knees while keeping their hands clasped behind their necks throughout the movement. The score was the total number of correctly performed sit-ups within one minute (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.99).

Sit-ups in one minute (short)

Trunk strength was measured by the maximum number of sit-ups completed in one minute. The participants sat on the floor with their backs straight, hands resting on their thighs, and knees bent at 90°, with their heels and feet flat on the mat. From this position, they lay back so that their shoulders touched the mat, with their palms placed on the upper thighs. Starting from this position, they had to lift their upper body forward until their palms reached their knees, which were bent at 90°, and then return to the lying position. The total number of correctly performed sit-ups within one minute was recorded as the score (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.99).

Squats in one minute

The participant stood with their legs shoulder-width apart, heels at the edge of the mat, and hands relaxed in a natural position. During the test, they lowered themselves until the tips of both hands touched the ground, then rose back up with both legs fully extended. The score taken was the total number of correctly performed squats completed in one minute (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.99).

600 m, 800 m, 1000 m running.

Each subject stood still in a standing position at the starting point. When the signal was given, the subject had to run as fast as possible for either 600, 800–1000 m. The task was completed after crossing the finishing line. After the run, the result was taken as the final score (expressed in s).

Statistical analyses

We employed multilevel modeling to quantify the variation in physical fitness outcomes across regions, counties, schools, and individuals.

At the regional and county levels, geographic location was used as a grouping factor to account for broader contextual influences. The school level captured differences attributable to individual school environments, such as physical education curriculum implementation or resources, which were not directly measured but inferred through variation between schools.

Results

The final analytical sample consisted of participants with at least one recorded physical fitness outcome. Among 9,199 elementary school students, the sample was composed of 49.04% boys and 50.96% girls. Similarly, the secondary school sample included 5,907 students, with 45.86% boys and 54.14% girls. Detailed information about the mean and standard deviation for each physical fitness outcome is provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

The multilevel analysis revealed substantial school-level variation in physical fitness outcomes across both elementary and secondary school students. VPCs demonstrated that individual-level differences accounted for the majority of the total variance, while school-level variation consistently emerged as a significant factor. In elementary schools, school-level variation for anthropometric measurements was relatively small (1–4.07%) (Table 1). However, for other physical fitness outcomes, school-level variation was notably higher, ranging from 5.08% to 51.14%. For example, school-level variation was highest for the 600 m run (51.14%) and 800 m run (44.29%), and lowest for weight (1.74%) and BMI (1.00%). In secondary schools, school-level variation, including anthropometric measurements, was considerably larger, ranging from 6.35% to 63.80% (Table 2). Specifically, transversal agility (63.80%) showed the largest variation across schools, while weight (6.35%) showed the smallest variation. Adjusting for sex and class resulted in notable changes in the PCV across levels, as summarized in Tables 3 and 4. In elementary schools, PCV showed that the inclusion of sex and class as covariates explained a significant proportion of school-level variance for anthropometric outcomes such as Weight (71.21%) and Percentage of Body Fat (41.44%). However, many other physical fitness outcomes showed limited explanatory power of these covariates, with some even exhibiting negative PCV values, such as Sprint 20 m and Ball Rolling. In secondary schools, adjusting for sex and class similarly explained most of the school-level variance for anthropometric outcomes and was more effective than in elementary schools for other physical fitness outcomes. For example, Standing Broad Jump (67.00%) and Medicine-ball Throw (84.39%) showed substantial reductions in school-level variance. Nevertheless, several outcomes still retained unexplained variance, showing relatively small or even negative PCV values, highlighting the limited explanatory power of these covariates for certain school-level differences. In some models, the proportional change in variance (PCV) was negative. This indicates that the inclusion of certain covariates increased the unexplained between-school variance rather than reducing it. Although counterintuitive, negative PCVs are a recognized feature in multilevel modeling and can occur when added variables explain more within-group variability than between-group variability or introduce additional heterogeneity at the group level. This does not represent a modeling error but rather reflects the complex structure of the data and the relationships among variables.

Discussion

This article aimed to explore the differences between schools in various health and performance/skill-related physical fitness outcomes. Consistent with H1, our findings indicate that the largest contextual variation in physical fitness outcomes is attributable to the school level, underscoring the critical role that schools play in shaping students’ physical fitness. This highlights schools as important targets for interventions designed to reduce disparities and promote physical health. In line with H2, even after adjusting for individual-level covariates such as sex and class (grade), substantial variation between schools remained. This suggests that factors inherent to the school environment - such as PE curriculum quality, facilities, or extracurricular opportunities - may strongly influence physical fitness beyond individual characteristics. Finally, supporting H3, we observed that school-level variation in physical fitness outcomes was consistently high across both elementary and secondary schools. This consistency across educational stages suggests that disparities in physical fitness are present throughout the school-age spectrum.

This significant variation may be attributed to the absence of nationwide policies and interventions addressing health issues and risk behaviours, which could otherwise help improve fitness outcomes and reduce the disparities between schools.

Studies show that physical fitness outcomes, such as aerobic fitness, strength, flexibility, and body composition, vary significantly across different schools14,17. This variation can be attributed to several factors, including the school’s location, resources (e.g., sports equipment, gym facilities), and the quality of physical education (PE) programs.

For example, urban schools may have different physical fitness outcomes compared to rural schools, influenced by access to sports facilities, outdoor activities, and overall community engagement in physical activity (Tian et al., 2023 [31]). Research from Canada found that schools in more affluent areas had better physical fitness outcomes compared to those in lower-income neighborhoods (Wong, Tung, Chan, 2022). Some studies also found that the presence of dedicated PE teachers, more structured activity programs, and stronger family and community support were associated with higher levels of fitness and physical activity among students (Novak, Suzuki, Kawachi21,; Michael et al.18,. A study in the U.S. found that physical fitness levels varied widely across schools, with wealthier schools showing significantly higher levels of fitness (Wolfe, Lee, Laurson33,. These differences could be attributed to factors such as greater access to quality PE programs and extracurricular activities, as well as better nutrition in higher-income areas (Tan, Cai, Bodovski30,.

Schools with PE teachers who are well-trained and dedicated tend to have higher fitness outcomes among students. Similarly, schools that integrate physical activity throughout the school day (not just in PE classes) may see better results. The curriculum and structure of PE programs play a significant role in shaping students’ physical fitness outcomes. Research has shown that schools with mandatory, high-quality PE programs see better fitness levels in students24.

Schools with better sports facilities (e.g., gyms, tracks, fields) often have higher levels of physical activity and fitness outcomes among students. A well-maintained gymnasium, outdoor fields, and other sports equipment encourage participation in physical education classes and extracurricular activities (Darling, Caldwell, & Smith8,; Eime et al.11,. In schools with fewer resources, students may not have access to quality PE programs, sports teams, or extracurricular physical activities. This can negatively affect their overall fitness levels and experience5. In Australia, a study examining physical fitness in different school systems showed that schools that provided more opportunities for active play during recess and lunch periods saw better fitness outcomes, especially for younger students26.

Many schools implement regular fitness assessments to monitor student fitness levels and provide individualized feedback. These tests can motivate students to improve their fitness. However, Croatia never had a mandatory fitness assessments policy until 2024 when for the first time this assessment is becoming obligatory at the national level (Burusic, Baic, Leko6,. In some schools, the focus on academics may overshadow physical education, resulting in limited opportunities for students to participate in physical activity. Research suggests that schools with a stronger focus on holistic development (including physical fitness) have better outcomes (Acar1,.

The present manuscript is not without its limitations. First, this is cross-sectional study, and it would be better to access the physical fitness outcomes in youth from a larger longitudinal study that give better insights into the individual growth and development changes. Second, biological age was not measured within this research. This could possibly diminish the variability between individuals of the same chronological age, especially during adolescence. Third, there is a possible measurement error given that data were collected by physical education teachers rather than researchers; fourth, there is potential influence of the COVID-19 context in 2020 on students’ fitness levels and fifth, data on school-specific characteristics such as weekly frequency of physical education classes, extracurricular sports participation, and organized sports or fitness programs were not directly collected in this study. Future research incorporating these variables would provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying differences in physical fitness across schools. Conversely, our study offers several notable strengths and contributions. First, it utilizes a unique dataset comprising various health and performance/skill-related physical fitness outcomes collected directly from real-world physical education settings. Second, the study features a large sample size of over 16,000 children and adolescents. Lastly, it aims to enhance the understanding and interpretation of physical fitness testing by examining school-level variance, with the goal of improving the quantity, quality, and accessibility of youth physical fitness data, specifically in Croatia.

Conclusion

This study is among the first to examine school-level variation in multiple health and performance/skill-related physical fitness outcomes in a large population-based sample from Croatia. Consistent with H1, our findings indicate that the largest contextual variation in physical fitness outcomes is attributable to the school level, underscoring the critical role that schools play in shaping students’ physical fitness. In line with H2, even after adjusting for individual-level covariates such as sex and class (grade), substantial variation between schools remained. Finally, supporting H3, we observed that school-level variation in physical fitness outcomes was consistently high across both elementary and secondary schools. The observed differences in physical fitness across schools are likely influenced by a complex interplay of factors, which may include the quality of physical education curricula, availability of resources, socioeconomic contexts, and broader aspects of school culture. While these factors were not directly measured in this study, they represent plausible explanations for the variation observed and warrant further investigation. Addressing such variations may require comprehensive, multi-level strategies that aim to provide equitable access to high-quality physical education and physical activity opportunities for all students, regardless of socioeconomic background or school resources. Interventions designed with consideration of individual, school, and community influences could help foster inclusive environments that support the physical well-being of children and adolescents.

The findings reported here can assist health and education professionals - such as physical education teachers - in assessing physical fitness, monitoring individual progress, and evaluating intervention outcomes at the school level. Future research should explore the specific school and contextual factors contributing to these variations to better inform targeted efforts.
