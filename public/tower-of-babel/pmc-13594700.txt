Multidisciplinary management of rare scrotal hemangioma in adolescents: A case report
Authors: Farhan Haidar Fazlur Rahman, Indri Aulia, William Makdinata, Fakhri Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHemangiomas are benign vascular tumors characterized by rapid endothelial cell proliferation. A 16-year-old male presented with scrotal swelling sized 25x25 × 15 cm with arterio-venous malformation in the scrotal region based on the contrast computed tomography angiography examination. Treatment management consisted of embolization carried out by thoracic and vascular surgeon, surgery of the scrotal hemangioma performed by urologist, and the closure of the wound using locoregional flap completed by the plastic surgeon. Scrotal hemangioma cases require enhanced clinical awareness, accurate diagnostic imaging, and precise surgical management to prevent further complications and achieve optimal outcomes.

Introduction

Hemangiomas are frequently encountered in pediatric populations, characterized by rapid endothelial cell proliferation followed by gradual involution, and are usually benign. As one of the most common vascular malformations of childhood, it has a reported incidence as 1–3% of full-term neonates and 10%–12% of children above the age of 12 months.1 Hemangiomas usually are found in the region of head, neck, trunk, or extremities. However, the incidence of hemangiomas rarely occurs in the scrotal area.2 Hemangiomas localized at the scrotum are rare with less than 50 cases published. Hemangioma is a known complication which occurs during its rapid growth phase and especially in anatomic areas where additional stimulating factors exist.

Given the rarity of scrotal hemangioma cases, clinical suspicion and awareness among healthcare providers remain crucial for timely recognition, appropriate diagnostic workup, and management. As such, this study aims to present a rare case of scrotal hemangioma in adolescents.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old male patient came with complaints of swelling and pain in the right and left scrotum since 2019. In July 2023, the swelling in the scrotal area started to get worse. An ultrasound examination of the scrotum was performed with the result of hydrocele and thus was planned for hydrocelectomy. Intraoperatively, there was massive bleeding in the scrotum, so the surgery procedure was terminated at that time. After the operation, a contrast computed tomography (CT) angiography examination was performed with the results of arterio-venous malformation (AVM) in the scrotal region (Fig. 1). The patient then referred to our hospital. There were no previous symptoms of disturbance in the urinary tract. In our center, we observed from clinical examination that the patient had a scrotal swelling sized 25x25 × 15 cm, with ulcer sized 10 × 10 cm, no visible discharge or blood on the scrotal area. Tenderness was positive, with unclear margin, along with hyperthermia in scrotal area (Fig. 2).

Result of lower extremity CT angiography with contrast (a). Coronal view, (b). Axial view.

Clinical appearance of scrotal hemangioma when the patient was admitted to the hospital (a). Frontal view, (b). Lateral view.

Prior to surgery, a multidisciplinary meeting between urology, thoracic and vascular surgery, and plastic surgery division was conducted to decide the step-by-step management to solve the case. We decided to do embolization first, which was then performed by the thoracic and vascular surgeon six days prior to the surgery. They used double 6 mm coil vortexes in the feeding vessel from the left internal iliac artery and a covered stent in the right deep femoral artery as embolization. Then we proceeded with surgical excision of the scrotal hemangioma, followed by closure of the wound using locoregional internal pudendal artery perforator (IPAP) flap conducted by the plastic surgeon.

Surgical intervention commenced under general anesthesia. A circumferential incision was made around the lesion, extending from the pubic region bilaterally towards the inguinal areas and perineum, encompassing the suspected AVM region. The incision was deepened through cutaneous, subcutaneous, and fascial layers. The mass was meticulously dissected from surrounding healthy tissues with careful hemostatic control, preserving both spermatic cords and testes bilaterally. Total intraoperative blood loss was approximately 500 mL (mL). Following the excision, the resulting defect was successfully managed using a locoregional flap by plastic surgeons.

Postoperative care included vigilant monitoring of clinical status and vital signs, antibiotic prophylaxis with Ceftriaxone, analgesia management using Ketorolac and Paracetamol, and routine assessments of blood parameters and wound status. The patient was discharged three days after the surgery procedure.

At two-weeks postoperative follow-up, the patient demonstrated favorable recovery, with good wound healing and no signs of surgical site infection, flap necrosis, or other complications (Fig. 3). Histopathological evaluation, including immunohistochemistry, confirmed the diagnosis of a benign AVM, with no evidence of malignancy or atypical vascular proliferation.

Clinical condition during two-weeks post operative follow-up, (a). Left lateral view, (b). Right lateral view.

At one-year postoperative follow-up, the wound was healed completely (Fig. 4). Both testicles were not clearly visible in the neoscrotum, although both were palpable. There were no signs of infection, inflammation or other complications. The patient was content with the cosmesis, and could perform normal daily activity.

Discussion

Hemangiomas are one of the most common vascular lesions in childhood. Scrotal hemangiomas are rare vascular tumors, with most reported cases occurring in infancy or early childhood. Their presentation in adolescents, as seen in this case, is uncommon and poses unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Hemangiomas are typically classified into superficial, deep, or mixed types. Deep scrotal hemangiomas, like in our patient, may remain unnoticed for years and are often misdiagnosed as more common conditions such as hydrocele or inguinal hernia.1,2 In such a case, detailed physical examination should be performed to differentiate scrotal hemangiomas with other conditions such as hydrocele, using a transillumination test.

A major concern with deep hemangiomas in the scrotal region is their tendency for rapid enlargement, ulceration, bleeding, and secondary infection. The scrotal region's abundant vasculature and delicate tissue structures amplify these risks. In our case, intraoperative findings of a large, ulcerated lesion with massive bleeding are consistent with the known behavior of high-flow AVMs.3

Diagnostic delays are common due to the rarity and nonspecific clinical features of these lesions. While ultrasonography is often the first-line imaging modality, it may be insufficient in detecting complex vascular lesions. Contrast CT angiography or magnetic resonance (MR) angiography is superior in delineating the lesion's extent and identifying feeding and draining vessels, thereby facilitating surgical planning.4,5 This approach was crucial in our patient to confirm AVM and guide subsequent embolization and excision, where the diagnosis was only confirmed after contrast CT angiography performed.

Multidisciplinary management is essential in cases of scrotal AVMs. In our patient, collaboration between urology, cardiothoracic surgery, and plastic surgery ensured both clearance through wide excision and functional reconstruction using a locoregional flap. In several cases, embolization performed prior to surgery significantly reduced intraoperative bleeding, especially for high-flow lesions.6 Nevertheless, surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for hemangiomas. Wide local excision aims to remove the entire lesion with a margin of healthy tissue while preserving vital structures such as the testes, spermatic cords, and urethra to avoid recurrence and achieve both therapeutic and diagnostic goals, as histological confirmation is essential.1 In our case, there was intraoperative bleeding that occurred due to some factors. One of them was permanent embolizing agents that were not used in the embolization procedure in our case.7 Instead double 6 mm coil vortexes were used for the embolization. Post-excision defect closure and scrotal reconstruction are often necessary, especially in cases involving extensive excision and skin loss. Locoregional flaps provide reliable coverage with good vascularity and cosmetic outcomes. The decision to involve a plastic surgery team, as we did, aligns with practices described by Stahl et al.8 who emphasize the reconstructive challenges posed by large vascular lesions of the scrotum.

A particularly important long-term consideration in scrotal hemangiomas is their impact on fertility. One of the primary mechanisms is increased intrascrotal temperature. Hemangiomas, particularly high-flow AVMs, contribute to local hyperthermia due to their abnormal vascular architecture and blood pooling. The elevated scrotal temperature caused by these lesions may result in testicular atrophy, impaired Sertoli and Leydig cell function, and disrupted blood-testis barrier integrity. Another mechanism involves vascular congestion and impaired venous outflow, which can lead to interstitial edema and hypoxia of testicular tissue. Furthermore, ulcerated or thrombosed hemangiomas may incite local inflammation, increasing the release of cytokines and reactive oxygen species that may have cytotoxic effects on testicular germ cells. These phenomena caused by vascular malformations may adversely affect spermatogenesis and testicular function, possibly leading to azoospermia or infertility.8,9 Although both testes were preserved without signs of ischemia or infection in our patient, close monitoring and fertility assessment in follow-up are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, scrotal hemangiomas, although rare, necessitate thorough clinical awareness, detailed physical examination, precise diagnostic imaging, and meticulous surgical management to mitigate complications and achieve optimal outcomes. This case underscores the importance of recognizing this condition in adolescents, as well as the significance of multidisciplinary management in complicated hemangioma cases.
