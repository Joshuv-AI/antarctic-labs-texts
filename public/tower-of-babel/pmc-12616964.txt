Cerebral small vessel disease score associated with brain hypoperfusion predicts cognitive decline: a longitudinal study
Authors: Xiaoqian Zhang, Sirui Liu, Bo Hou, Xiaoyuan Fan, Hui You, Mingli Li, Yicheng Zhu, Chao Ma, Zhentao Zuo, Feng Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the association between cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) score and cerebral blood flow (CBF) at baseline in cognitively intact older adults and to explore whether the total CSVD burden score serves as an imaging marker that can predict cognitive impairment in aging populations.MethodsMR images acquired from 509 participants with normal cognition were analyzed to evaluate the association between the total CSVD burden score and CBF. Structural scans, pseudo-continuous arterial spin labelling (pCASL) for CBF quantification, and 3D T1-weighted sequences were obtained. CSVD burden scores were rated using a validated 5-point scale by assessing white matter hyperintensity, lacunes, perivascular spaces, and microbleeds. Participants underwent structured cognitive assessments via telephone at a mean follow-up of 7.6 ± 0.1 years post-baseline. Differences in CBF between the CSVD burden groups were compared using univariate linear models, and logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate the risk of longitudinal cognitive impairment. The predictive model was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsSevere CSVD scores (> 2) were significantly associated with decreased CBF in many cortical regions (Padj< 0.05). Participants with higher CSVD scores were more susceptible to longitudinal cognitive decline (OR 2.995, 95% CI = [1.540, 5.825]; P = 0.001, adjusted for age and sex). The CSVD score model effectively predicted cognitive impairment (AUC = 0.808, P < 0.001), with an optimal cut-off value of grade > 2 (specificity = 88.9%).ConclusionSevere CSVD score, which is associated with cortical hypoperfusion, was found to be an effective imaging marker for predicting longitudinal cognitive decline. Thus, CSVD scoring may be considered a clinically accessible tool for risk stratification and individualized health monitoring in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-01967-9.

Background

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a common age-related small vascular disease of the brain that often exhibits an insidious onset and is partially identified only by imaging examination [1]. Common neuropathological features of CSVD derived from MRI include recent small subcortical infarcts, white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes, perivascular spaces, cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), and brain atrophy. Considering the temporal coherence and combined effects of these features, an overall measurement called “total CSVD burden score”, which includes evaluations of WMH, perivascular spaces, CMB, and lacunes, may better reflect the combined effect of neurological changes in CSVD [1, 2]. However, the clinical significance of imaging markers associated with total CSVD burden, which are also observed in the cognitively normal older population, has not yet been elucidated.

Numerous neuroimaging studies have reported that patients undergo global changes in brain structure and metabolism. The total CSVD burden score is associated with changes to local clustering and nodal efficiency of the brain network [3], blood–brain barrier permeability [4], and global and regional cerebral blood flow (CBF) [5]. Chronic brain hypoperfusion can lead to age-related cognitive and neurological degeneration [6]. Moreover, regional and tissue-specific atrophy of the grey matter is associated with clinical symptoms [7, 8]. Several recent studies have indicated that total CSVD burden scores are negatively correlated with cognitive function [2, 9–11]. However, most of these studies were limited by their cross-sectional designs [2, 9, 11, 12] or patient enrolment strategies [9–11, 13]. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether total CSVD scores, along with baseline brain alterations, are associated with cognitive decline in the general normal-aging population. Few investigations have linked CSVD burden to quantitative perfusion metrics, such as arterial spin labelling -CBF, a potential gap hindering pathophysiological insight.

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) refers to the self-perceived decline in normal objective cognitive function [14]. SCD is considered both a preclinical stage of the Alzheimer’s disease continuum and an independent predictor of dementia conversion [15]. The prevalence of SCD is increasing given the rapid aging of the global population. Here, we assumed SCD to be an outcome of long-term cognitive impairment when retrospectively analysing baseline CSVD characteristics and exploring their potential associations.

The primary purpose of this study was to investigate the correlation between total CSVD burden score and alterations in grey matter CBF in a large sample with normal cognition. Additionally, we evaluated whether the total CSVD score could serve as an effective imaging marker to independently predict longitudinal cognitive function decline. This prospective cohort study followed the Marti–Bonmati criteria (level 2b).

Methods and materials

Participants

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH, JS-2653 and K2102). All participants provided written informed consent. The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were enrolled in two cohort studies (A and B). Cohort A was a community-based study primarily focused on middle-aged and older individuals from Beijing, China. Cohort B was a joint large-sample human body donation project between PUMCH and the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences [16] that remains ongoing. Some of our current data have been used for basal ganglia perivascular space research [17]. This present study was an observational ambidirectional cohort study.

Figure 1 shows the enrolment flow diagram and exclusion criteria of the study participants. A total of 1,054 participants who underwent head MRI were included in the baseline cross-sectional analysis (301 in cohort A and 753 in cohort B). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) younger than 50 years; (b) Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) score < 27 indicating cognitive impairment [18] or unavailable MMSE score; (c) self-reported cognitive or memory loss; (d) stenosis (≥ 50%) or infarction of major intracranial or extra-cranial artery; (e) diagnosis of genetic/metabolic related CSVD or other leuko-encephalopathies (e.g. demyelinating disease); (f) a history of intracranial trauma or surgery; (g) a history of stroke, brain tumour, or neuropsychiatric disorder of drug abuse or dependence in the past or present. Following these criteria, 327 participants were excluded. The remaining 727 participants (n = 252 for cohort A and n = 475 for cohort B) were included in the imaging analysis. Participants with insufficient image quality for CSVD score assessment (n = 40), unavailable pseudo-continuous arterial spin-labelling (pCASL) data for post-processing (n = 178), and/or incomplete clinical data (n = 163) were also excluded.

A total of 509 participants were filtered and included in the correlation analysis of total CSVD burden and grey matter CBF; 346 participants were screened for the investigation of the relationship between CSVD scores and vascular risk factors.

Longitudinal follow-up was conducted exclusively in Cohort A (community-based cohort). Of the 252 Cohort A participants included in the baseline imaging analysis, 148 were enrolled in the follow-up study based on their consent for follow-up and available contact information. During the mean follow-up period of 7.6 years, 62 participants were lost to follow-up, five declined further participation, and three died. Thus, 78 participants completed the telephonic cognitive assessment (78/148, 52.7%).

MRI protocols

All MR examinations of the two cohorts were conducted using a 3.0 Tesla MRI scanner (GE Discovery MR750 for cohort A, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA; GE MR750W for cohort B, GE Healthcare Systems, Chicago, IL, USA). The MRI scanner was equipped with an 8-channel phased array head coil. Participants from both cohorts underwent standard MRI protocols, including structural and perfusion sequencing. The MRI protocols for cohort A included a sagittal 3D T1-weighted imaging (WI) sequence [inversion time (TI) = 400 ms, time of repetition (TR) = 6.9 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.6 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2, slice thickness = 1.0 mm; matrix size = 256 × 256], an axial T2WI sequence (TR = 7912 ms, TE = 92 ms, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, and slice thickness = 4 mm), a fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence (TR = 12,000 ms, TE = 120 ms, TI = 2,000 ms, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, and slice thickness = 4 mm), a susceptibility WI (SWI) sequence (TR = 47 ms, TE = 28 ms, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, and slice thickness = 2 mm), a diffusion WI (DWI) sequence (TR = 4,400 ms, TE = 67 ms, slice thickness = 4 mm, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, b-values = 0 and 1000 s/mm², diffusion directions = 3), and an axial pCASL sequence (TR = 4,886 ms, TE = 10.5 ms, post-labelling delay time = 2,025 ms, labelling duration = 1,450 ms, clarified number of arms = 6, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, slice thickness = 4 mm, effective resolution = 3.75 × 3.75 × 4 mm³). The MRI protocols for cohort B were as follows: a sagittal 3D T1 sequence (TI = 450 ms, TR = 7.4 ms, TE = 3.2 ms, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2; slice thickness = 1.1 mm; matrix size = 256 × 256), an axial T2 sequence (TR = 3324 ms, TE = 81 ms, slice thickness = 4 mm, and FOV = 256 × 256 mm2), a FLAIR sequence (TR = 8000 ms, TE = 147 ms, TI = 2200 ms, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, slice thickness = 4 mm), a susceptibility WI sequence (TR = 51 ms, TE = 26 ms, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, slice thickness = 1 mm), a diffusion WI sequence (TR = 4400 ms, TE = 67 ms, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, slice thickness = 4 mm, b-values = 0 and 1000 s/mm², diffusion directions = 3), and an axial pCASL sequence (TR = 4,874 ms, TE = 10.7 ms, time of post-labelling delay = 2,025 ms, labelling duration = 1,450 ms, clarified number of arms = 6, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, slice thickness = 4 mm, effective resolution = 3.75 × 3.75 × 4 mm³). Both cohort studies used similar MRI sequences and parameters for quantification, which was expected to greatly limit potential confounding differences that may arise from the use of different MRI scanners [19].

Grey matter CBF data acquisition

CBF maps were calculated from 3D-pCASL images using the Function Tool (AW 4.5 Workstation, GE Healthcare) [20, 21]. The imaging data were pre-processed using SPM12 implemented in the Matlab environment (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) [17]. First, 3D T1WI structural images were collected for anatomical co-registration of CBF. Subsequently, the co-registered 3D T1 images were segmented into grey matter, WM, and cerebrospinal fluid probability maps, followed by normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute space. Subsequently, spatial smoothing was applied using an isotropic Gaussian smoothing kernel with a full width at half maximum of 6 mm. A threshold of 0.5 was set for the resultant smoothed probabilistic grey matter map to create a binary grey matter mask. Finally, regional grey matter CBF values were extracted using the Hammersmith n30r83 atlas. We excluded all infratentorial structures and supratentorial structures without grey matter (e.g., all ventricles, brain stem and corpus callosum).​​ ​​Specific 58 grey matter brain regions (including cortical and subcortical structures) were selected for subsequent statistical analyses [22, 23].​.

Scoring of CSVD total burden

All four structural MRI markers of CSVD were determined in accordance with the consensus guidelines and scored based on the STRIVE-2 criteria [1]. A detailed evaluation of the total CSVD burden score is shown in Fig. 2 and Table S1. First, deep WMH (DWMH) and periventricular WMH (PWMH) were graded separately according to the Fazekas scale [24]. According to the stratification scale, participants with PWMH = 3 or DWMH ≥ 2 or both were considered to possess a WMH-1 score; otherwise, they were regarded as WMH-0. The score of perivascular spaces (< 3 mm in diameter) was counted as perivascular spaces − 1 if there were more than 11 visible perivascular spaces or category ≥ 2 in unilateral basal ganglia, and the more severe side was considered when bilateral asymmetry was observed [25]. One point was awarded for the presence of one or more lacunes and one or more CMBs. The total CSVD burden score was calculated by aggregating the scores of the four markers for each patient [1], and the total CSVD burden score ranged from 0 to 4. All participants were assigned to mild (CSVD score ≤ 2) or severe (score >2) burden groups.

Imaging analysis was independently performed by two neuroradiologists (X.Z. and S.L., with five and six years of experience, respectively) who were blinded to all clinical data. Discrepancies in the CSVD scoring quantification were first resolved through a consensus discussion. In case of persistent disagreements, a senior neuroradiologist (H.Y., with over 15 years of experience) provided the final adjudication.

Assessment of long-term cognitive impairment

A structured survey evaluated cognitive impairment using the Subjective Cognitive Decline Questionnaire 9 (SCD-Q9) [26], telephone MMSE (t-MMSE), and clinical history. Trained interviewers, blinded to both baseline imaging and clinical data, administered standardized cognitive tests via structured telephone interviews.

The total t-MMSE score ranges from 0 to 26 [27]. Studies have confirmed that the t-MMSE and MMSE scales are strongly correlated and can be used as alternative tools to screen for overall cognitive function [27, 28]. The questionnaires used for the SCD are listed in Table S2. SCD required the following [29]: (a) “yes” to the first question, “Do you have a problem in memory?”; (b) total score >5 on the SCD-9 questionnaire; and (c) objective cognitive examination score within the normal range. For participants screening positive for potential mild cognitive impairment or poor performance on the questionnaire (t-MMSE ≤ 21 [30] or SCD-Q9 score >5), secondary clinical evaluations were conducted, and a specialist neurological review was performed by board-certified neurologists blinded to baseline imaging data. Clinically diagnosed dementia, mild cognitive impairment, or SCD were defined as the primary outcomes of long-term cognitive impairment.

Statistical analysis

SPSS statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics, v 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and MedClac Version 20.0.8 were used for the statistical analysis. The weighted κ value was calculated for the inter-observer agreement of the CSVD total burden score. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, following comparison with the chi-squared test. Continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples t-test if they were homogeneous in variance; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used, and the results were presented as median (Q25, Q75).

To compare differences in grey matter CBF between the two groups, sex, age, scanner manufacturer, and regional grey matter volume were entered as covariates in the univariate model matrix. The adjustment for multiple testing for CBF was conducted using the Benjamini and Hochberg false discovery rate correction method [31], and Padj values < 0.05 were considered significant. Logistic regression for binary categorical variables was performed to assess risk factors for longitudinal cognitive impairment. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Predictive performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Specificity and sensitivity were obtained from ROC analysis.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Baseline demographic characteristics and imaging features are listed in Table 1. A total of 509 participants (114 with severe CSVD burden and 395 with mild CSVD burden) were included in the grey matter CBF analysis: 208 (40.9%) male participants and 301 (59.1%) female participants. Among them, 143 (28.1%) scored 0, 156 (30.6%) scored 1, 96 (18.9%) scored 2, 75 (14.7%) scored 3 and 39 (7.7%) scored 4. Subjects in the higher CSVD burden group were highly likely to have more WMHs (90.4% vs. 16.5%, χ2 = 218.468, P < 0.001), more perivascular spaces (96.5% vs. 40.3%, χ2 = 112.284, P < 0.001), lacunes (86.8% vs. 13.7%, χ2 = 225.299, P < 0.001), and CMBs (60.5% vs. 17.7%, χ2 = 81.656, P < 0.001).

A total of 346 participants (85 with severe CSVD burden and 261 with mild CSVD burden), including 141 male participants (40.8%) and 205 female participants (59.2%), were enrolled in the cardiovascular factor analysis. Table 2 shows a comparison of cardiovascular risk factors between the two groups of participants stratified by CSVD burden severity. Severe CSVD burden scores were significantly associated with male sex (χ2 = 8.338, P = 0.004) and age (t = 6.079, P < 0.001). Participants in the higher CSVD burden group were highly likely to have hypertension (χ2 = 6.520, P = 0.011), higher systolic blood pressure (t = 3.936, P < 0.001), higher diastolic blood pressure (t = 3.215, P = 0.001), and higher total cholesterol (t = 2.323, P = 0.021). The inter-observer agreements on the presence of the CSVD scores were excellent and good with κ values of 0.867 (95% CI 0.836–0.899).

Association between total CSVD burden and longitudinal cognitive decline

Follow-up telephone questionnaires were completed by 78 participants (52.7% of eligible Cohort A participants; n = 148). Among them, 15 (19.2%) participants experienced long-term cognitive decline: 3 cases of dementia (3.8%), 1 case of mild cognitive impairment (1.3%), and 11 cases of SCD (14.1%). The mean follow-up age was 74.30 ± 6.96 years, and the average follow-up duration was 7.56 ± 0.06 years. The odds of longitudinal cognitive decline increased as the total CSVD burden score increased (OR = 2.995, 95% CI = [1.540, 5.825], P = 0.001, adjusted for age and sex).

In line with the ROC analysis, the CSVD total burden model offered good predictive ability for long-term cognitive impairment (AUC = 0.808, 95% CI 0.703–0.888, P < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 4. According to the ROC analysis (Table 4), the optimal cut-off value of this predictive model was > 2 at a sensitivity of 60% (95% CI, 32.3%–83.7%) and specificity of 88.9% (95% CI 78.4%–95.4%).

Discussion

In cognitively intact older adults, we found a significant association between the total burden score of CSVD and widespread cortical hypoperfusion in the grey matter CBF at baseline. Total CSVD score is a convenient and effective imaging marker of long-term cognitive decline. Our findings build on prior evidence [32, 33] demonstrating that a higher CSVD burden may serve as an early indicator of neurodegeneration prior to overt cognitive impairment.

Widespread decreased cortical perfusion was observed in the severe CSVD burden group compared to that in the mild CSVD group. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that whole-brain alteration occurs due to severe CSVD burden. The observed cortical hypoperfusion in severe CSVD aligns with existing evidence linking small vessel pathology to impaired cerebral haemodynamics [5]. While previous studies have primarily focused on patient populations [13], our findings in cognitively intact older individuals highlight the insidious progression of CSVD-related perfusion deficits prior to the onset of clinical symptoms. Some results pertaining to deep nuclei have been contradictory [5, 34]. The preserved CBF in subcortical structures (e.g. basal ganglia), despite severe CSVD burden, may reflect differential vascular vulnerability between cortical and deep perforating arterioles. Disruption of the blood–brain barrier and endothelial–pericyte axis as well as impaired neurovascular coupling may synergistically drive CBF reduction [35]. Extravasation of intravascular materials causes hyaline degeneration of the small vessel wall and toxic neuronal damage, which contributes to nerve fibre and myelin disruption, astrocyte proliferation, microglial activation, and neuroinflammation [36]. This cascade of events partially accounts for lacunes, WMH, and CMB. Loss of small arterial wall tight junctions and elevated permeability obstruct the CSF reflux, allowing for the observation of enlarged perivascular spaces. In addition, the grey matter of the cerebral cortex is more sensitive to ischemic injury than the WM [37] and is rich in neurons, making it particularly important to study the total CSVD burden on grey matter CBF in older adults. Brain hypoperfusion is linked to neuronal degeneration and impaired perception [38]. Although not a cognitive impairment condition, severe CSVD burden puts the patient at risk owing to an underlying pathological vascular disorder.

The total CSVD burden scorepredicts longitudinal cognitive decline in older adults, as demonstrated in previous studies, such as that by Hamilton et al. [39]. Our findings corroborate and extend this key relationship significantly. We demonstrated that a simplified, visually assessed total CSVD burden score, readily obtainable from routine clinical MRI, offers good predictive performance (AUC = 0.808) with a specific, clinically actionable cut-off value (>2, specificity = 88.9%). Thus, total CSVD burden score may be considered a highly practical and accessible imaging marker that is particularly suitable for implementation in diverse clinical settings, including primary care and risk stratification in aging populations. The ease of scoring makes it feasible to incorporate it into annual health monitoring programs. SCD, a premorbid state of dementia identified in our study, also affects the emotional state and quality of life of older individuals. This underscores the importance of early identification of individuals with severe CSVD burden, as they may benefit from targeted lifestyle modifications or pharmacological interventions to mitigate future cognitive decline. CSVD is associated with multiple cognitive declines, which were initially recognized in previous cross-sectional studies [2]; however, prior studies have mainly concentrated on the role of individual imaging markers in CSVD. The emergence of total CSVD burden remedies this deficiency, which is an innovative aspect of the present study. In addition, this study demonstrates that elevated CSVD burden reflects altered intracerebral perfusion status at baseline and that the independent use of CSVD scores can be an alternative to the use of multiple markers at baseline to predict long-range cognitive decline, which has significant implications for individual health management and disease prognosis. The total CSVD burden score is accessible, reliable, and stable as long as standardized definitions are adopted. In contrast, the presence of higher CSVD burden scores may be of warning significance when regional CBF post-processing is cumbersome and clinically relevant sequences are not performed. Our findings advocate a three-tiered screening protocol: (1) Older adults with vascular risk factors: annual CSVD burden scoring; (2) CSVD score ≥ 3: quantitative pCASL-CBF mapping; (3) Hypoperfusion subgroups: implement advanced prevention such as by using cilostazol [40].

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size involved in the telephone follow-up was small, probably because of the long follow-up period and high rate of missed visits. The attrition rate observed over 7.6 years may introduce bias; however, attrition analysis showed no baseline differences between groups. Second, cognitive assessments via telephone, while cost-effective for long-term follow-ups, lack the sensitivity of in-person neuropsychological batteries for detecting early subclinical decline. Finally, the visual CSVD scoring system is an observer-dependent mission and a semi-quantitative evaluation tool that does not stratify the anatomical location and size of lacunes and CMB. Future multicentre studies involving automated lesion quantification and detailed cognitive profiling are warranted.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that in cognitively intact older individuals, severe CSVD total burden is associated with widespread cortical hypoperfusion. This study also revealed the predictive value of CSVD total burden scores for predicting long-term cognitive decline in older individuals, which has important implications for managing individual health and stratifying dementia risk in the aging population.
