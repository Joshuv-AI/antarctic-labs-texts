Investigate the relationship between self-directed learning and emotional, spiritual, and moral intelligences among nursing students
Authors: Farzaneh Maghaminejad, Nahid Dehghan-Nayeri, Zahra Delshad, Mahdi Shafiee Sabet, Mitra Rahimzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-directed learning is a crucial aspect of nursing education. Intelligence, as a factor that can be linked to self-directed learning and enhance students’ capacity for independent learning, is of significant interest. Consequently, this study aims to investigate the relationship between emotional intelligence, spiritual intelligence, and moral intelligence with self-directed learning among nursing students.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted on 300 nursing students during 2024. The data-gathering instrument consisted of five parts. The first part consisted of questions on demographic information and the rest parts were standard questionnaires included: Self-Directed Learning, emotional, spiritual and moral intelligence. Data were analyzed using the SPSS software. For statistical analysis independent t-test, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression were used.ResultParticipants were mostly female (74%), student (77.67%), single (87.33%). There were significant positive correlations between self-directed learning and emotional (r = 0.63, p < 0.05), spiritual (r = 0.43, p < 0.05), and moral intelligence (r = 0.62, p < 0.05). Emotional, spiritual, and moral intelligence together accounted for 52.1% of the variance in the self-directed learning.ConclusionEmotional, spiritual, and moral intelligence have been shown to be predictor of success in self-directed learning. Focusing on enhancing these factors in academic training can lead to improved self-directed learning.Keywords: Self-directed learning, Emotional intelligence, Spiritual intelligence, Moral intelligence, Nursing students

Background

Self-directed learning (SDL) is a crucial skill in higher education that plays a significant role in the professional development and empowerment of students, particularly in health sciences professions like nursing [1]. Research has consistently shown that self-directed learning is the most effective method for nurses to acquire knowledge and skills in clinical settings [2]. Recent research conducted in Iran has revealed that a mere 40% of nursing students possess the necessary level of self-directed learning skills [3].

In dynamic and challenging clinical environments, it is crucial for nurses to constantly update their knowledge and skills [4]. Success in self-directed learning is influenced by various factors, including cognitive, psychological, and ethical components [5]. These components, such as emotional intelligence (EI), spiritual intelligence (SI), and moral intelligence (MI), play a crucial role in the learning process [6, 7].

Emotional intelligence is the ability to understand, manage, and use emotions in social interactions. It can be a powerful tool in motivating students to learn independently and effectively manage academic challenges [8]. Zhu states that, emotional intelligence, which encompasses the ability to regulate one’s emotions and create positive feelings, is crucial for self-directed learning, and students with higher emotional intelligence are more self-directed, leading to greater success in their academic growth [9]. However, in the recent study of emotional intelligence among Iranian nurses, it was found that 45.5% nurses scored below the overall average [10].

On the other hand, spiritual intelligence, which encompasses an individual’s ability to engage in deep contemplation, find purpose, and exhibit resilience in challenging circumstances, can aid nursing students in sustaining their intrinsic motivation during stressful clinical situation [11]. Samuel’s study concluded that emotional and spiritual intelligence play a significant role in students’ self-leadership. This not only fosters a positive learning environment, but also enhances their performance [12]. However, research conducted in Iran in 2020 reveals that 45% of nurses have below average levels of spiritual intelligence [13].

Moral intelligence, which refers to the adherence to ethical principles and social responsibility, has the potential to greatly impact students’ dedication to lifelong learning and ethical clinical decision-making [14]. Nuraini proposes that moral intelligence, which encompasses qualities such as honesty, compassion, forgiveness, and responsibility, plays a significant role in students’ academic success [15]. However, Approximately 40% of nursing students experience significant doubts when confronted with ethical dilemmas [16].

Despite the importance of these variables, there has been limited research examining the simultaneous relationship between emotional intelligence, spiritual intelligence, and moral intelligence and self-directed learning in nursing students. Nursing students possess a unique sensitivity towards human interactions and carry significant moral responsibilities, particularly when faced with critical decision-making situations. Additionally, most of the existing research has been conducted in Western countries, which may not accurately reflect the cultural differences in components such as spiritual intelligence and moral intelligence. Feng et al. (2019) and Kwon (2008) argue that researchers from diverse sociocultural backgrounds may utilize similar theoretical foundations and discuss similar concepts, but may interpret them differently based on their own beliefs regarding faith, religiosity, and/or existential concerns when defining spiritual intelligence [17, 18]. As a result, the generalizability of foreign findings to Iranian society is limited. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the relationship between self-directed learning and emotional intelligence, spiritual intelligence, and moral intelligence in nursing students.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional design to investigate the relationship between self-directed learning and emotional, spiritual, and moral intelligences among nursing students. study was conducted from September 2023 to November 2024.

Study settings

This study was conducted in the Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) program at the two University of medical science and Azad universities in Kashan, Iran.

Participants

Sampling is performed using a convenience sampling method. To determine the appropriate sample size, Mousavi’s article suggests a correlation of r = 0.232 between emotional intelligence and Moral’s performance. This estimation takes into account a first type error of α = 0.05 (95% confidence level) and a second type error of β = 0.15 (test power of 85%). Based on this relationship, the study will include at least 200 nursing students [10]. The inclusion criteria were, providing informed consent, being enrolled in a bachelor’s program in nursing, and not having any diagnosed psychological disorders as determined by a university counselor. We excluded participants who did not complete the questionnaires in full. To account for potential attrition, we distributed a total of 350 questionnaires, out of which 300 were used for analysis.

In this study, the researchers obtained an official letter of introduction from the Research Vice-Chancellor of Tehran University of Medical Sciences before visiting the medical science and Azad universities in Kashan, Iran. Data was collected by visiting the selected universities on all days of the week and conducting sampling. The objectives of the research were explained to the participants, and written consent was obtained before distributing the questionnaires. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their information and were asked to answer all questions.

Measure

Data was collected using a questionnaire divided into five sections: demographic information (age, gender, occupation, marital status, and academic year), self-directed learning, emotional intelligence, spiritual intelligence, and moral intelligence. Self-Directed Learning Questionnaire: Developed by Fisher et al. (2001), this self-report measure comprised 40 items related to self-management, self-control, and self-motivation, using a 5-point Likert scale. The questionnaire demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.83) and construct validity [19]. Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire: Developed by Bradburry and Greaves (2004), this questionnaire measures self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship management. It consists of 28 items using a 6-point Likert scale. Reliability was established through Cronbach’s alpha (0.88) [20]. Spiritual Intelligence Questionnaire: Developed by King (2008), this questionnaire contains 24 items and four subscales: critical existential thinking, personal meaning production, transcendent awareness, and expanded states of consciousness. Reliability was estimated using Cronbach’s alpha (0.88) [21].

Moral Intelligence Questionnaire: Developed by Lennick and Kiel, this questionnaire consists of 40 items using a 5-point Likert scale to assess moral competence. Reliability was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha (0.93) [22].

Data was analyzed using SPSS version 22. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression analysis were employed to analyze the data.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS version 22. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. No missing value was existed. Frequency and percent were presented for all variables and frequency tables were used. Also, the Pearson correlation was employed to measure associations between self-direction learning and emotional, spiritual, and moral intelligences. We conducted an independent t-test to analyze the correlation between the self-directed learning score and various independent variables, including gender, marital status, academic semester, and employment status. In addition, multiple regression analysis was performed to predict the self-direction learning in emotional, spiritual, and moral intelligences.

Ethical consideration

After obtaining approval from the ethics committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences with the code of IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1401.172, a letter of introduction was obtained from the university’s research vice chancellor. The project was found to be in accordance with ethical principles and national norms and standards for conducting medical research in Iran. Before distributing the questionnaires, the objectives of the study were explained to the participants and written informed consent was obtained from them. Separate written consent was obtained for each individual interview. Participants were assured that their information would remain confidential and they would also have the option to receive research results if desired.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between emotional, spiritual, and moral intelligences and self-directed learning. The results of this study indicate that there is a strong positive correlation between all three types of intelligence (emotional, spiritual, and moral) and self-directed learning. However, the impact of each type of intelligence on explaining the variation in self-directed learning differs.

This study revealed that emotional intelligence is the most significant predictor of self-directed learning. The obtained results were in line with studies carried out by Koç, Lakshmi and Lyngdoh [23, 24]. However, Rahimi’s study did not find a significant relationship between emotional intelligence and academic success [25]. Emotional intelligence can help individuals manage their stress levels when faced with challenging tasks, reduce anxiety when learning new material, and overcome frustration when encountering obstacles [26]. According to studies, students with higher emotional intelligence tend to perform better in independent settings [27, 28]. According to the results, it appears that training in emotional intelligence skills, such as conflict resolution and stress management, can assist students in improving their self-directed learning abilities.

Based on the findings, it can be concluded that moral intelligence is closely related to self-directed learning. According to the study by Arabshahi (2024), moral intelligence has been found to enhance an individual’s dedication to learning goals by promoting a sense of responsibility and academic conscience [29]. Recent research (Warsah et al., 2024) has demonstrated a positive correlation between moral intelligence and self-efficacy in students. This, in turn, has been found to enhance the quality of self-directed learning [30]. There is currently no study that specifically examines the relationship between moral intelligence and self-directed learning. However, based on the findings of our study and those of other researchers, we propose that incorporating ethical considerations, such as scientific integrity, justice, and responsibility, into educational programs can enhance self-directed learning.

In our study, we found that spiritual intelligence had a lesser impact on self-directed learning compared to other types of intelligence. According to Amram (2007), spiritual intelligence helps individuals make sense of learning challenges and develop resilience in the face of failure [31]. However, its effects may be more noticeable in the long term and in complex situations, rather than in everyday learning. Seifi et al.‘s (2022) study revealed that spiritual intelligence has a stronger influence on academic life satisfaction compared to direct learning performance [32]. This suggests that engaging in spiritually-oriented thinking activities, such as reflecting on learning goals, can be advantageous for students in higher grades.

One notable finding of this study was that employed students in lower academic semesters had lower scores in self-directed learning. Ayadurai’s 2018 study provides evidence to support this conclusion [33]. Self-directed learning theory, as proposed by Knowles in 1975, emphasizes that self-directed learning is a skill that can be acquired and improved through experience [34]. According to a study conducted by Sadeghi et al. in 2024, senior students scored higher on a self-directed learning questionnaire [35], these results suggest that students in higher semesters are more familiar with metacognitive strategies, such as planning and self-assessment, due to their greater experience.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that educational methods should focus on developing emotional, spiritual, and moral intelligence in addition to cognitive skills. By encouraging self-directed learning, educators can empower students to take charge of their own learning, improve their emotional and spiritual health, and foster strong moral principles. To support working students, it is important for us to provide a variety of flexible learning plans, such as online courses, evening classes, or self-paced modules. Additionally, we must offer time management advice to help students balance their work and studies. By designing workshops that focus on learning strategies, we can help lower-semester students bridge the skills gap and mitigate any potential challenges they may face. Therefore, it is essential to prioritize these initiatives in order to ensure the success of our students.

Limitations

The use of self-report questionnaires in our research study may introduce bias. For instance, students may be inclined to provide socially desirable responses when it comes to measuring spiritual intelligence and moral intelligence. Utilizing a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods can be an effective approach in gaining a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms of impact.
