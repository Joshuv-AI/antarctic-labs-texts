Tsallis Entropy derived from the Chaitin-Kolmogorov Informational Entropy
Airton Deppman

Abstract
We provide a rigorous first-principle derivation of the non-additive Tsallis’ entropy by employing the Chaitin-Kolmogorov algorithmic information theory. By applying non-local restrictive rules on the string formation (grammar), we show that the algorithmic cost follows a power-law of the string length, instead of the linear behaviour obtained in the classical theory. As a result, the Tsallis entropy governs the increase of information. We explore the result showing, through Landauer’s limit, that the heat dissipation in systems with long-range correlations is diminished. The Ω q \Omega_{q} number, which remains incompressible, now offers the possibility of a continuous increase of complexity, measured by the parameter q q . We show the consistency of the results by a numerical simulation, and discuss Zipf’s law in light of the new findings.



I
Introduction

This work provides the first derivation of the Tsallis entropy
21
grounded on the algorithmic information theory. By employing Chaitin’s approach of informational complexity
7
, we show that imposing restrictive grammar rules (grammar) on strings generated by the universal Turing machine yields the non-additive entropy proposed by Tsallis, providing a first-principle foundation for this entropic form.

Among the few subtle concepts of physics that defy our deepest understanding, entropy appears as a special case, as it extends far beyond the borders of physics, arising in information theory, biological systems, environmental sciences, economics, sociology and complexity.

The origins of the concept of entropy are usually associated with the fundamental works by Clausius
8
, becoming a central quantity in the works by Boltzmann and Gibbs, who laid the foundation of statistical mechanics
11
. Later, Shannon provided a critical step, showing that the concept appears in communication technology
18
. An extension for quantum systems was provided by von Newmann
23
. Mathematically, the Boltzmann-Gibbs (BG) entropy is written as

S=-k\sum_{i}p_{i}\ln p_{i}\,,

(1)

where
k
is the Boltzmann constant. For the equiprobable case (
p_{i}=p~\forall i,~\textrm{$p$ constant}
), it reduces to the well-known Boltzmann formula
S=k\ln W
, where
W=1/p
. An axiomatic development of the BG statistics was developed by Khinchin
14
. A cornerstone work was provided independently by Kolmogorov
15
and Chaitin
5
, showing that the informational entropy is a measure of the irreducible complexity.

A generalisation of the BG entropy was proposed by Tsallis, who defined the
S_{q}
entropy by

S_{q}=k\sum_{i}p_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{q-1}-1}{1-q}\,,

(2)

where
q
is called the entropic index. A prominent feature of this generalisation is the non-additivity of entropy, expressed by the composability rule:

\frac{S(AB)}{k}=\frac{S(A)}{k}+\frac{S(B)}{k}+(1-q)\frac{S(A)}{k}\frac{S(B)}{k}\,.

(3)

For
q\neq 1
, this is a non-additive relation. For
q=1
, the entropy
S_{q}
reduces to the BG entropy and the composability becomes additive, completely recovering the entropy which grounds BG statistics as a special case. A special algebra, the
q
-deformed algebra, and the respective differential calculus
4
, was developed to properly deal with the non-additivity of Tsallis statistics.

Tsallis entropy is the only that is simultaneously trace-form and composable
22
. It can be obtained by generalising one of the Khinchin axioms, namely, the additivity
1
;
12
. An alternative derivation emerges from the Superstatistics
3
framework, in which fluctuations of an intensive parameter deform the probability space. When the fluctuations follow the Euler’s Gamma Function the Tsallis distribution is obtained.

An independent way to the Tsallis distribution arises from the thermofractal structure
10
. Thermofractal is a system whose thermodynamical functions exhibit aa fractal behaviour within a hierarchical, self-similar structure that can possibly extend infinitely. This hierarchy induces a fractal structure in the momentum space, endowing the system with an intrinsic fractal structure that persists even if the system lives in Euclidean configuration spaces. Recent works have established rigorous mathematical connections between fractal derivatives and
q
-deformed derivatives, thereby providing a solid theoretical foundation for the intimate relationship between fractal structures and Tsallis statistics.

Despite the success of the nonextensive statistics, a microscopic derivation of the Tsallis entropy has long been missing. An early attempt to use information theory to derive the Tsallis statistics was presented in Ref.
24
, but it does not follow the standard framework id the algorithmic information theory. The present work closes this gap by providing a derivation of the
S_{q}
entropic formula based on informational principles, in a path that aligns with the approach developed by Chaitin
6
;
5
and Kolmogorov
15

II
The algorithmic approach to entropy

In 1948, Claude Shannon established the foundations of Communication Theory by defining entropy as a measure of uncertainty. His key innovation was to link the BG statistical mechanics to the transmission of symbols. In the 1960s, Andrey Kolmogorov
15
and Gregory Chaitin
5
independently reversed this scenario. They recognised that the "randomness" of a sequence is related to the way it is generated, but depends on the extent to which it can be compressed. This novel approach promoted important advancements in the understanding of the concept of entropy, providing an entropic formula that recovers the one originally proposed by Boltzmann.

Chaitin defined the complexity
C(S)
of a string
S
of length
L
as the size of the shortest program
p
that computes
S
on a Universal Turing Machine
U
.
The present work follows Chaitin’s approach. Let
{\cal M}=M^{L}
be the total number of possible sequences. An algorithm that writes an arbitrary sequence of
L
symbols can be formed by a table of
M^{N}
words of
N
symbols, and a pointer to the words in the table. Any sequence of
L
symbols will be composed of
L/N
such words.

The size of this pointer is the number of words that can be formed,
c=M^{N}
, where
c
is the size of the algorithm, apart from some constants and terms
O(1/N)
. We have the coupled equations

\begin{cases}nN=L\\
C(N)=nM^{N}+O(1)\end{cases}\Rightarrow C(L)=LM^{N}/N\,.

(4)

Taking the logarithm of both sides, we get

\ln C=\ln L-\ln N+N\ln M\,.

(5)

The central goal of Chaitin’s work was to show the conditions that minimise
C(L)
(the computational cost) for a fixed
L
. In the continuous limit, this optimisation problem is considerably simplified, as the optimal length can be found by extremising cost function underthe constraint of fixed
L
. This procedure yields

-\delta\ln N+\delta N\ln M=0
, therefore,

N^{-1}=\ln M\,.

(6)

Substituting the optimised result in Eq. (
5
), we get

C(N)=(\ln M)LM^{N}\rightarrow\ln C=\ln L+\ln(\ln M)+N\ln M\,.

(7)

The last term in the above equation is
1
so, for sufficiently large
L,M
, we get

C=e(\ln M)L+O(1)\,,

(8)

where
e
is the Napier’s number. The entropy is defined as proportional to the cost, so

H=(\ln M)L\,,

(9)

which increases proportionally to the string length
L
, showing it is extensive. It is easy to see that, if you have two strings
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, the string formed by the combination of the two strings has entropy that is the sum of the individual entropies, so this entropy is also additive
1

1

Chaitin calls
H
the complexity of the string
S
in the alphabet
M
.
.

The entropy can be expressed in terms of the program size
|p|=n+O(1)\approx n
. For the optimal program, we get
H=n
, so the complexity is fully determined by the optimised program size. The algorithmic information theory uncovers a fundamental aspect: the entropy associated to the string
L
has no relation with the source, but depends only on the string size. Moreover, a change of the alphabet used to generate the strings, i.e., the number of possible symbols used to form the string, rescales the entropy by a multiplicative constant. Thus, the entropy is a measure of the intrinsic complexity of the string, and can be identified to the size of the shortest program able to generate it.

The relation between
n
and
H(L)
enables further developments. The quantity
n
represents the size of the optimal program, i.e., the minimum number of symbols a programm must contain in order to generate strings of length
L
. If binary symbols
(0,1)
are used, there are
2^{n}
possible programs.

III
Introducing complexity

Chaitin’s original problem consisted in optimising the code for a random string of
L
symbols with an alphabet with
M
symbols, without imposing any constraint on the possible sequences. In contrast, as in any formal language, the presence of rules restrict the possibilities for allowed strings, effectively reducing the number of possibilities of well-formed strings. In what follows, we investigate how these restrictions modify Chaitin’s original results

The introduction of rules in the composition of strings reduces the space of possible strings. The number of sequences of
N
letters is reduced, and the algorithmic cost to identify a sequence turns out to be

C=\nu(n)M^{N}+O(1)\Rightarrow\ln C=\ln\nu+N\ln M\,,

(10)

where
\nu(n)
is a function that summarises all the effects of a particular grammar (rules on string formation) on the algorithm optimisation. We will assume that the grammar cannot destroy the alphabet (e.g., by forbidden strings with a subset of letters).

Following the Chaitin method, as exposed in the previous section, we extremise
\ln C
, yielding

\Big[\frac{1}{\nu}\frac{\partial\nu}{\partial N}+\ln M\Big]\delta N=0\,.

(11)

Since
n=L/N
, the derivative
d\nu/dN=-(L/N^{2})\nu^{\prime}
, where
\nu^{\prime}=d\nu/dn
. Thus, the optimised algorithmmust satisfy

\frac{L}{N^{2}}\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{\nu}=\ln M\,.

(12)

The restriction on the possible strings may effectively introduce voids in the set of
N
-symbol sequences available to the program if they result in a non-Markovian sequence of decisions
2
. With a number of rules that is finite and small compared with
N
, the density of voids is roughly independent of
N
, the
\nu(n)
is scale-free and can be written as a power-law:
\nu(n)=n^{\alpha}
. Then, we get

N=\frac{\alpha}{\ln M}\,.

(13)

The assumption of a power-law behaviour for the number of strings that satisfy an important class of grammar constraints is confirmed by numerical simulations and by empirical observations, as discussed below. This corresponds to a fractal distribution of voids in the space of sequences, in a way that resembles the Cantor set. We observe that with the introduction of restrictive rules, the number of words hardwired in the program increases. This is a manifestation of the grammar, as some of the words are forbidden and cannot be included. It means that, for the same program, we can use words with a larger number
N
of symbols.

The algorithm entropy becomes

C(L)\propto H(L)=\Big[\frac{(\ln M)}{\alpha}\Big]^{\alpha}L^{\alpha}\,,

(14)

which is a non-additive entropy. The non-additivity results, in the context of algorithmic information, from the reduction of the valid strings in the grammar. As the restriction affects words of almost any size (assuming
L
sufficiently large), the power-law behaviour reflects the scale invariance of the grammar constraints on the space of valid strings.

Contrary to Chaitin’s result, where the entropy density
H(L)/L
was constant, here it depends on
L
. This behaviour uncovers the underlying mechanism of the grammar rules: it reduces the overall entropy observed in completely aleatory strings, reducing the chaotic nature of unconstrained sequences of letters. The rate of entropy increase with
L
is

\frac{dH}{dL}=\ln MH^{q}\,,

(15)

where
q=(\alpha-1)/\alpha
, therefore,
q=1-1/\alpha
.

The total entropy for a sequence of letters up to
W
can be easily calculated. In fact, the equation above is separable, yielding

\frac{dH}{H^{q}}=\ln MdL\,.

(16)

integrating both sides up to
L=W
, results in

\int_{1}^{W}\frac{dH}{H^{q}}=\int_{0}^{L}(\ln M)dL^{\prime}\,,

(17)

leading us to the Tsallis entropy

S_{q}(W)=\frac{W^{1-q}-1}{1-q}=(\ln M)L\,.

(18)

This is the Tsallis entropy for equiprobable distributions of microstates. Observe that the Tsallis entropy recovers the entropic extensivity, with
S_{q}\propto L
. We note that also in the non-additive case, the size of the optimal program is
\nu(L)=[(\ln M~L)/\alpha]^{\alpha}
.

Now we can derive the non-equiprobable Tsallis entropy. Programs with size
|p|=n
can calculate any string with size up to
L
. We can infer the probability of obtaining a particular string
L_{k}
with size
L_{k}<L
by randomly selecting a program with size
n
among all possible programs with the same size. the probability for getting precisely the code that will generate
L_{k}
is
p_{k}(L)=1/W
.

The entropy for the particular string
L_{k}
results

S_{q}(p_{k})=\frac{p_{k}^{q-1}-1}{1-q}\,.

(19)

The expectation value of the complexity calculated over all possible strings is

S_{q}[p]=\sum_{i}p_{i}\frac{p_{k}^{q-1}-1}{1-q}=\frac{\sum_{i}p_{i}^{q-1}-\sum_{i}p_{i}}{1-q}=\frac{\sum_{i}p_{i}^{q-1}-1}{1-q}\,,

(20)

which is the Tsallis entropy. The distribution of probabilities that extremise the Tsallis entropy is the
q
-exponential function

p(x)=e_{q}(x)=A\Big[1+(1-q)x\Big]^{\frac{1}{1-q}}\,.

(21)

This probability distribution is defined for
x>-1((q-1)
, otherwise
p_{i}=0
. If
p(x)
is a decreasing
q
-exponential, this yields
17
,

p(x)=A[1+(q-1)\beta x]^{-1/(q-1)}\,,

(22)

where
A
is a normalisation factor and
x
represents the different possible outcomes.

We note that
q<0
if
\alpha<1
, which is an uncommon but possible value in the nonextensive statistics
9
. A discussion on the possible values for these parameters will be presented below.

IV
Linguistic foundations of the power law distribution

A central aspect of the emergence of Tsallis entropy is the power-law scaling of the number of strings of size
N
, given by
\nu(n)=n^{\alpha}
, with
\alpha=1/(q-1)
. We will analyse the reasonableness of this assumption by examining how grammar rules in natural languages, and by linking the assumed power law to empirical observations in linguistic studies, such as Zipf’s law
25
and Heaps’ law
13
. In this context, we consider that the
N
-sequences hardwired in the program form the vocabulary that is used to produce the text. In addition, we will not restrict to a fixed length, so the vocabulary is formed by a combination of words with slightly different lengths, which is an indication that, in human communication, compressibility is not the most relevant aspect.

Heaps’ law is the empirical observation that the vocabulary of a text increases as a power law of the text length, i.e.,
V\propto L^{\beta}
. We can translate this law in terms of the algorithmic information quantities by noting that
V
corresponds to the number of words hardwired in the program,
\nu(n)\propto L^{\alpha}
, so
\nu
is the size of the vocabulary and
\beta=\alpha
. Thus, Heaps’ law means that the algorithmic cost
C=\nu(L)M^{N}
is reduced because the vocabulary of the text does not use all possible sequences of
N
-symbols words. Empirical studies show that
0.4<\beta<0.6
, determining the range of
\alpha
for natural languages, indicating that
-1.5<q<-0.67
. Negative values for
q
emerge as a consequence of more efficient text coding: longer texts are produced with a reduced vocabulary by increasing the frequency of some words at the expense of others.

The word probability distribution must follow an increasing
q
-exponential function of the rank
r
, so

f(r)=C[1+(\bar{q}-1)r]^{\frac{-1}{\bar{q}-1}}\,.

(23)

,
From here, considering
r\ll 1/((1-\bar{q})
, we get

f(r)\approx\frac{1}{r^{1/(1-\bar{q})}}\,.

(24)

This is known as Zipf’s law of word distribution, which says that the frequency of words follows a power law function of their rank, i.e.,
f(r)=1/r^{s}
. The Zipf exponent is
s=1/(1-\bar{q})
.

The entropy rate measures the increase in complexity as one additional sequence is added to the list of hardwired words.
In natural languages, the measure of text complexity is indicated by word frequency, which is known to follow Zipf’s law, given by
f(r)=C/r^{s}
, where
r
denotes rank of the word frequency. The best known form of Zipf’s law is the word frequency form; however, there is a lesser known versions of the law based on the word length, which is called brevity law. The brevity law is a direct result of the Tsallis entropy, with
s=1/(1-\bar{q})
.

The analysis of natural-language text based on a corpus of selected authors reveals a tension between Zipf’s and Heaps’ laws. The laws’ exponents are expected to be related as
\beta=1/s
; however, this relation is not corroborated by the empirical data. The use of Tsallis statistics opens a way for relaxing the tension, because there is a narrow window for getting sensible results with
\beta\approx 0.8
, giving
q\approx 2.25
, therefore
\bar{q}=-0,25
and
s\approx 0.8
. These values are closer to the observed exponents, although they still present a clear disagreement.

The study of natural language characteristics is relevant, in the present work, to show that the power-law
\nu(n)=n^{\alpha}
is a common result of the constraints of grammar. This aspect is clearly demonstrated by Heaps’ law and by Zipf’s law. A comprehensive analysis of the specific values for the exponents – which are representative of deeper challenges for the understanding of the complexity of grammar in information theory – remains for future work. Now, we provide a numerical simulation to show the emergence of power laws by imposing simple rules on the formation of valid strings.

VI
Applications

In this section, we briefly investigate possible applications of the results obtained in the present work. The applications of Tsallis statistics expand to several fields; however, the link with informational entropy opens new possibilities of developments. Here we delineate two such applications: Landauer’s limit and Chaitin’s number.

An immediate consequence of the nonextensive entropy is the modification of the Landauer limit
16
. In the classical regime (
q=1
), the erasure of one bit requires the dissipation of
Q=kT\ln 2
. In the non-additive case, the erasure process dissipates
Q_{q}
calculated as

Q_{q}\geq kT|\Delta S_{q}|=kT\Big(\frac{1-2^{1-q}}{q-1}\Big)\,,

(33)

which can be written as
Q_{q}\geq T\ln_{q}(2).
For
q>1
, the results show that the dissipated energy in the non-additive case is smaller than in the classical case.

The algorithmic information approach reinterprets the Turing Theorem for the Halting Problem in terms of a halting probability: the probability that a program
p
, constructed by a sequence of random bits and running in a Universal Turing Machine, will halt. This formulation is mathematically expressed by the Omega number

\Omega=\sum_{p\in{\cal H}}2^{-|p|}

(34)

where
{\cal H}
is the set of halting programs,
|p|
is the program length in bits. Chaitin demonstrated that this number is incompressible, and represents the absolute limit of what is knowable in an unconstrained string space. The fundamental cause for the incompressibility measured by
\Omega
is the fact that the number of possible strings grows with
2^{L}
.

When the rules of grammar are introduced, reducing the possibility of meaningful strings, the number of possible strings grows at a slower pace. From Eq. (
18
, we find that

W(L)=\Big[1+(q-1)(\ln M)L\Big]^{1/(q-1)}\,.

(35)

The rate of possible strings changes from an exponential regime to a power-law regime. The number
\Omega_{q}
results

\Omega_{q}=\sum_{p\in{\cal H}}\Big[1+(q-1)(\ln M)L\Big]^{1/(q-1)}\,,

(36)

which recover the number
\Omega
for
q=1
. Chaitin proved that
\Omega
is incompressible: to calculate its
n
first digits, it is needed a program with length
|p|=n
. The same argument applies to
\Omega_{q}
, this is also an incompressible number.

In this context, we must conclude that if
\Omega\neq\Omega_{q}
, then the grammar rules are restricting some of the halting programs. In the context of the Turing Theorem, it means that some of the programs that could produce a result are not present in the set of allowed programs according to the adopted grammar. Nevertheless, if the allowed programs are still sufficiently complex, it is still impossible to decide the halting of programs in general. However, the generalised Chaitin number,
\Omega_{q}
, opens the possibility of studying the continuous change of undecidability as the system becomes less complex.

VII
Conclusions

We have used the algorithmic information theory methods to demonstrate that, when restrictive rules are imposed on the string formation (grammar), the resulting entropy is the Tsallis non-additive entropy, instead of the classical Boltzmann entropy. Therefore, we provide the first derivation of the non-additive entropy entirely grounded on first-principles, in a bottom-up approach.

By employing the Chaitin method and discussing the effects of restrictive rules on the program cost, we discuss that for a finite and sufficiently small number of rules, the allowed sequences of symbols form a fractal set, where the hierarchical levels are determined by the program size. The geometrical modification of the string space shifts the optimal algorithmic cost and modifies the linear dependence on the string size. The optimisation of the algorithm cost leads to the Tsallis statistics.

The observed discrepancy between the derived and empirical exponents reflects a known tension in quantitative linguistics. Rather than a limitation of the current derivation, the application of Tsallis statistics provides a mathematical mechanism to relax this tension, offering a more nuanced description of how hierarchical grammar constraints influence information density.

Based on this finding, we investigated a generalised Landauer’s limit, showing that the heat dissipation in such systems is diminished. Furthermore, we investigate Chaitin’s incompressibility theorem, based on the
\Omega
-number under the light of the generalised entropy.

Our derivation moves beyond mathematical generalisation. It shows that Tsallis entropy is the unique effective description for the informational cost of generating strings in a universe with syntactic rules. It is not an arbitrary choice but the inevitable outcome of constrained computation.
