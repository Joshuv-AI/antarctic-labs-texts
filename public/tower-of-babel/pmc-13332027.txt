Genomic profiling and therapeutic targets of Thai melanoma revealed by next-generation sequencing
Authors: Kornyok Kamdee, Manop Pithukpakorn, Panitta Sitthinamsuwan, Teerapat Paringkarn, Janista Thumrongtharadol, Vuthinun Achariyapota, Sitthichoke Taweepraditpol, Manasmon Chairatchaneeboon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic alterations represent key therapeutic targets in melanoma, but genomic profiles differ across ethnic groups and remain poorly defined in Thai patients. We characterized the somatic landscape of 33 melanoma samples (13 cutaneous, 13 acral, 7 mucosal) using the 501-gene OCA-Plus panel with next-generation sequencing, integrating clinical and genomic features. The somatic landscape revealed BRAF as the most frequently mutated gene, primarily involving the V600E variant, followed by KIT. Both BRAF and KIT emerged as significant drivers and exhibited mutual exclusivity, with BRAF mutations clustering specifically in cutaneous melanoma. Given the limited cohort size and heterogeneity, cross-ethnic comparisons of major mutational drivers revealed no statistically significant differences, necessitating further large-scale validation. Mutations in the RAS-MAPK and NOTCH oncogenic signaling pathways were the most frequent, and co-occurring alterations in MYC and Cell Cycle pathways were observed. Putative clinically actionable variants—primarily in BRAF, KIT, and NRAS—were present in 48.5% of patients, occurring most frequently in cutaneous melanoma. Notably, patients harboring KIT mutations showed a trend toward shorter disease-free survival, suggesting a potential prognostic role that warrants further investigation. These findings provide initial genomic understanding of Thai melanoma and highlight candidate actionable mutations for future precision oncology research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51731-4.Keywords: Genomic landscape, Melanoma, Next-generation sequencing, Somatic mutationSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

Although melanoma incidence varies globally—highest in Australia, North America, and Europe, and lowest in Africa and Asia—, mortality rates are relatively uniform across regions1. Genetic alterations that drive melanoma initiation and progression2 serve as therapeutic targets and are associated with improved clinical outcomes3–6. Characterizing the genomic landscape and integrating it with clinical data may inform precision oncology and improve patient outcomes.

A key research challenge is the difficulty of personalizing cancer therapy, given the complexity of tumor genomic profiles and the need to align treatments with clinical guidelines. Furthermore, melanoma subtypes display distinct clinical outcomes7,8 and are driven by unique genetic alterations9. However, existing genomic data are largely derived from Western melanoma cohorts, with limited Asian representation. Mutation frequencies of cancer-related genes vary by ethnicity. For the melanoma driver genes, the prevalence of BRAF mutations ranges from 25 to 28% in Asians to 66% in Caucasians10–12, while KIT mutations range from 6% in Caucasians to 8–10% in Asians11,13,14. These differences suggest that current genomic datasets may not reflect the Thai melanoma population, and assumptions of shared biology or treatment strategies could be misleading.

Comprehensive genomic profiling of Thai melanoma remains lacking, raising questions about whether commonly mutated genes in other populations show similar patterns or whether additional mutations are prevalent.

This study aimed to characterize the genomic profiles of melanoma across melanoma subtypes in Thai patients using formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues and the 501-gene Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus (OCA-Plus; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Results

Clinicopathologic and molecular features of melanoma subtypes

Thai patients with melanoma in this study had a mean age at diagnosis of over 60 years in the overall cohort and across all subtypes (cutaneous, acral, and mucosal melanoma). All patients with mucosal melanoma were female and presented with ulceration, the rate of which was significantly higher than in acral and cutaneous melanoma (P = 0.007 and P = 0.002, respectively). In contrast, no significant differences were observed among melanoma subtypes with respect to age, stage, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, metastasis, or clinical status (Table 1). Comprehensive genomic profiling of Thai melanoma

We performed targeted sequencing of 33 FFPE melanoma tissues from Thai patients using the 501-gene OCA-Plus panel to comprehensively characterize the somatic landscape. The panel enables detection of single nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions (indels), and copy number variations (CNVs) across more than 1.4 Mb of coding sequence. It is primarily designed to target the full coding DNA sequence (CDS) of 227 genes, while also including recurrent hotspots for additional genes, such as TERT. Samples were sequenced to a mean depth of 3,053×. From 3,444 total variants, we identified 228 somatic non-synonymous variants (205 SNVs and 23 indels) spanning 148 genes, with a median of four mutations per sample (Supplementary data 2). The median tumor mutational burden (TMB) was 3.84 mutations/Mb (Fig. 1f and Supplementary data 6). We compared TMB across the melanoma subtypes identified in our cohort; as shown in Fig. 2e, the median TMB was significantly lower in the acral subtype compared to the cutaneous subtype (P < 0.03; 3.8 vs. 9.48 mutations/Mb).

The somatic landscape (SNVs/indels) showed that BRAF was the most frequently mutated gene in our melanoma cohort, followed by KIT, TET2, CREBBP, AMER1, ATM, CIC, HLA-A, HNF1A, and KMT2A (Fig. 1a), while TERT mutations were identified in 3% of this cohort. Among these, variant allele frequency (VAF) analysis showed that KIT, HLA-A, TET2, and BRAF had mean allele frequencies of 30–50% (Supplementary Fig. 1). Specifically, BRAF and KIT emerged as driver genes in Thai melanoma based on positional clustering analysis using oncodriveCLUS algorithm15,16 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Hotspot mutations with frequencies exceeding 3% within this cohort were mapped, with BRAF V600E as the predominant variant (Supplementary Fig. 3). In 33 Thai melanoma FFPE samples, we identified novel somatic variants that were not documented in public databases (Supplementary Tables 11 and Supplementary Data 4).

Protein domain mapping was performed for five frequently mutated genes to assess the roles of these somatic mutations in cancer development and progression. In this study, all identified BRAF mutations are clustered within the kinase domain, with the V600E missense variant being the most prevalent (Fig. 1b). Specifically, 71.4% (10 of 14) of the cases harbored V600E/K mutations, while 28.6% (4 of 14) exhibited non-V600 BRAF mutations, including G469A, N581I, T599dup, and G593_D594del. KIT mutations were exclusively missense, mainly L576P and K642E, followed by V559A, and Y578C (Fig. 1b). The L576P, V559A, and Y578C impact the catalytic domain, while K642E affects the kinase domain. Regarding AMER1 (also known as WTX or FAM123B), only the R497 mutation was identified, which is located within the kinase-binding region. Notably, no mutations were detected in the functional domains of CREBBP or TET2 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Analysis of the somatic landscape (SNVs/indels) by melanoma subtypes revealed that the most frequently mutated genes were BRAF, TET2, and NOTCH1 in cutaneous melanoma; KIT, BRAF, and GATA3 in acral melanoma; and SF3B1, HLA-A, and ERBB2 in mucosal melanoma (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 5). To investigate subtype-specific patterns in melanoma genetics, pairwise analysis of mutation frequencies across melanoma subtypes was conducted. Tumors with BRAF mutations were enriched in cutaneous melanoma (OR = 6.30, P = 0.02, FDR = 0.17) (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 2), whereas the mutation rates of other genes did not differ significantly across melanoma subtypes.

Gene associations were assessed using co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity analyses. CSMD3, AMER1, and KMT2A exhibited the highest frequency of co-occurring events. Significant pairs included CSMD3 and AMER1 (OR = Inf, P = 0.006), CSMD3 and KMT2A (OR = Inf, P = 0.006), AMER1 and KMT2A (OR = 38.87, P = 0.02). Mutual exclusivity was observed between the melanoma driver genes KIT and BRAF (OR = 0, P = 0.03) (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 5).

Following exclusion of samples that failed CNV quality control, 31 melanoma tumor samples were analyzed (Supplementary data 3). Of these, 23 (74.2%) showed copy number alterations, with AMER1, MCL1, PCBP1, CDK4, and CYP2C9 being the most frequently affected genes (Fig. 2a). The most common mutated CNV genes, classified by melanoma subtypes, were identified: AMER1 and ATRX deletions in cutaneous melanoma, PCBP1 and CDK4 amplification in acral melanoma, and ARID1B and JAK2 deletions in mucosal melanoma (Fig. 2c upper and Supplementary Fig. 6). However, pairwise analysis of copy number variation frequencies across melanoma subtypes revealed no distinct CNV patterns (Supplementary Table 3).

Oncogenic signaling pathways in Thai melanoma

We examined recurrent alterations in major oncogenic signaling pathways in cancer, including Cell Cycle, HIPPO, MYC, NOTCH, NRF2, PI3K, RTK-RAS-MAPK, TGF-β, TP53, and β-catenin/WNT17. Using a previously established analytical approach18, we identified the most common alterations in RAS-MAPK, NOTCH, and WNT oncogenic signaling pathways in Thai melanoma (Fig. 2b). Among these, RAS-MAPK and NOTCH were the most frequent across all melanoma subtypes (Fig. 2c lower and Supplementary Fig. 7). Nevertheless, no subtype-specific patterns of key oncogenic pathway alterations were observed across melanoma subtypes (Supplementary Table 4). We examined significant co-occurring and mutually exclusive alterations across oncogenic pathways. Co-occurring alterations were observed in the MYC and Cell Cycle pathways (OR = 22.10, P = 0.03), whereas mutually exclusive patterns were not observed (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 6).

Potentially clinically actionable variants

Personalizing cancer therapy is challenging due to the complexity of tumor genomic profiles and the need to align recommended drugs with clinical guidelines.Clinically actionable variants in Thai melanoma were classified according to ASCO–AMP–CAP guidelines (Tier IA to IIC)19 and matched to therapies approved by the FDA, EMA, NCCN, and ESMO. Overall, 72.7% (24/33) of Thai melanoma carried at least one clinically actionable genomic variant. Of these, 48.5% were classified as Tier IA (approved for melanoma), with BRAF (V600E/K), KIT (K642E, L576P, V559A), and NRAS (Q61R) as druggable targets, while 24.2% classified as Tier IIC (approved for non-melanoma indications), with BCOR (K1364*, R342*), BRAF (N581I, G469A), NF1 (E725*), NRAS (G12D), PTEN (deletion), and SF3B1 (R625H/G) (Fig. 3a and c, and Supplementary data 5). We further explored the potentially actionable variants across melanoma subtypes. Specifically, cutaneous melanoma exhibited the highest rate of Tier IA alterations (approved for melanoma) at 61.54%, followed by acral melanoma (46.15%) and mucosal melanoma (28.57%) (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Tables 7, and Supplementary Fig. 8).

Clinical impact of genomic profiles

We investigated the potential clinical impact of the somatic profiles by evaluating their association with disease-free survival (DFS). KIT mutations were associated with shorter DFS compared with those without KIT mutations in this small cohort (P = 0.006; HR = 5.37, 95% CI: 1.418–20.399) (Fig. 4). Further validation in larger cohorts is required to determine the independent prognostic value of these alterations. However, BRAF mutations showed no association with DFS (supplementary Fig. 9).

Comparative analysis of genomic mutations in Thai melanoma versus other ethnicities

We explored the differences in mutational frequencies among our Thai melanoma cohort, Caucasian cohorts and Asian cohorts. We compared mutational frequencies of eight melanoma driver genes (BRAF, KIT, SF3B1, CDKN2A, NF1, NRAS, PTEN, and DDX3X)—previously identified as putative melanoma drivers14,20 and also detected in Thai melanoma—with those in Caucasian cohorts (TCGA20, Caucasian cohort21(consisting mainly of patients from Australia, the U.S., Sweden, and Switzerland), Australian14, and US-UK22) and Asian cohorts (Chinese11, Japanese23, Taiwanese24, and Korean25, stratified by melanoma subtypes to assess ethnic-specific patterns. No significant differences in mutational frequencies were found among our Thai melanoma cohort, Caucasian patients, and Asian patients (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables 8–10). However, several trends were noted: cutaneous melanoma showed higher NF1 frequencies than Japanese (P = 0.06); acral melanoma exhibited higher BRAF than Taiwanese (P = 0.07), higher KIT than Chinese (P = 0.07) and Caucasian cohorts (P = 0.06), and lower NRAS than Chinese (P = 0.07) and Taiwanese cohorts (P = 0.06).

Discussion

Melanoma is a genetically heterogeneous disease in which genetic alterations drive cancer initiation and progression2,26. Identifying and understanding these mutations may reveal potential therapeutic targets to advance precision cancer therapy. However, the genomic profile of melanoma in Thai patients has not yet been defined. In this study, we comprehensively characterized the somatic mutational landscape of melanoma in a Thai cohort.

In the clinicopathological analysis, the high ulceration rate of mucosal melanoma may be attributed to its hidden location, resulting in delayed diagnosis, as well as its inherently aggressive biological behavior27. This aggressive phenotype is likely driven by the rich blood and lymphatic supply surrounding the tumor cells28.

Regarding the somatic landscape, BRAF has been established as the most frequent driver mutation in both primary and metastatic melanoma11,29. Additionally, KIT mutations are also prevalent within Asian cohorts11,30. Consistent with these findings, somatic landscape analysis identified BRAF as the most frequently mutated gene within the coding regions analyzed in this panel, followed by KIT. Notably, TERT promoter mutations are a hallmark of melanoma, with reported frequencies as high as 68%-71%31,32, often exceeding those of BRAF. However, the 501-gene OCA-Plus panel utilized in this study is primarily designed to target the full coding DNA sequence (CDS) of 227 genes; while it includes recurrent hotspots for additional genes, it does not provide comprehensive CDS or promoter region coverage for the TERT gene. In this study, we identified TERT mutations in only 3% of the cohort, which is consistent with the 4% frequency reported in a recent Chinese melanoma cohort using targeted sequencing11. Consequently, a comprehensive analysis of the TERT promoter and the non-coding landscape in Thai patients is warranted to definitively validate TERT mutation rates in this population. BRAF (a serine/threonine kinase) and KIT (a receptor tyrosine kinase) mediate MAPK pathway signal transduction, playing critical roles in cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation33. Mutations in these genes are primary mediators of MAPK pathway hyperactivation, thereby driving melanoma progression9,33–35. Driver mutations occur in the early stages of oncogenesis and provide growth advantages to the cancer cells. Our positional clustering analysis15,16 confirmed BRAF and KIT as primary drivers in the Thai melanoma cohort, aligning with established literature14,20,30. We also identified several melanoma driver genes previously reported in other studies14,20, including the oncogenes SF3B1 and NRAS, as well as the tumor suppressor genes CDKN2A, NF1, PTEN, and DDX3X. With a mean allele frequency of approximately 40–50%, BRAF, KIT, HLA-A, and TET2 are likely clonal and may be present at early stages of tumor formation. HLA class I molecules (HLA-A, -B, and -C) play a critical role in presenting tumor antigens to CD8 + T cells, enabling tumor cells killing36. HLA-A was also identified as a commonly mutated gene in mucosal melanoma37. Reflecting a trend seen across malignancies, melanoma harbors higher HLA mutation rates than other cancers, except for head and neck cancer38. These alterations drive immune evasion and potentially reduce the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI)38,39. However, further research is required to identify the specific HLA-A alleles involved. TET2 is known to promote the expression of genes in the MHC Class I pathway, including HLA-A, by modulating the epigenetic landscape. Consequently, loss of functional TET2—whether through somatic mutation or downregulation— reduces tumor antigen presentation, allowing melanoma cells to evade T-cell-mediated immune surveillance40,41. Our finding of frequent early-stage HLA-A and TET2 mutations suggests that survival in Thai melanoma may be associated with immune evasion, highlighting a potential need for combination treatment strategies to overcome resistance.

Furthermore, the high frequency of AMER1 deletions (a WNT pathway regulator also known as WTX or FAM123B) and MCL1 amplifications (anti-apoptotic) observed in our study suggests that WNT signaling dysregulation and apoptotic evasion may represent more prominent drivers in Thai melanoma. MCL1 amplifications have been reported in more than 10% of multiple malignancies, including breast, lung, and melanoma42. These copy number alterations are likely to drive cancer initiation and progression43,44.

Consistent with previous reports14,20,21,45, the high frequency of RAS-MAPK pathway alterations—across the overall cohort and each melanoma subtype—indicates that these alterations represent primary oncogenic drivers in Thai melanoma. Consequently, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting the RAS-MAPK axis, including BRAF and MEK inhibitors, as precision treatment strategies for Thai patients with melanoma.

We mapped the distribution of somatic mutations across functional domains to evaluate their impact on melanoma development and progression. BRAF and KIT mutations were found to cluster within kinase domain, consistent with previous reports34,37. BRAF (V600E/K and non-V600 mutations G469A, N581I, and T599dup) and KIT (L576P, K642E, V559A, and Y578C) mutations have been reported as gain-of-function mutations that enhance kinase activity and activate downstream MAPK pathway, thereby driving melanoma progression9,33–35,46. Both non-V600E/K and non-V600 variants have demonstrated high sensitivity to dual BRAF and MEK inhibition6,47. However, BRAF G593_D594del remain less well characterized. AMER1 is a tumor suppressor gene involved in regulating cell growth via the WNT signaling pathway. We identified the R497* mutation, located within the kinase-binding region; this variant results in a loss-of-function and is commonly associated with the development of colorectal cancer (CRC)44. However, its association with melanoma development or progression has not yet been reported.

Melanoma subtypes display distinct clinical outcomes7,8 and are driven by unique genetic alterations9. BRAF mutations are frequently observed in cutaneous melanoma subtype, particularly superficial spreading melanoma20,48. Consistent with this, we found BRAF mutations to be highly enriched in our cutaneous melanoma cases, suggesting that they may be the primary molecular mechanism driving cutaneous melanoma in the Thai population. This may guide oncologists toward highly effective therapies, especially BRAF inhibitors in combination with MEK inhibitors4,6. In contrast, acral and mucosal melanoma typically harbor different mutations, such as KIT, NRAS, or NF1, or copy number variations14,34. However, we did not find any mutations associated with acral or mucosal melanoma; this may be attributed to ethnicity or small sample size.

Mutations found to co-occur in driver genes suggests synergistic interactions that may inform combination therapeutic targets, whereas mutually exclusive mutations imply potential synthetic lethality49. In our cohort, CSMD3, AMER1, and KMT2A showed the highest incidence of co-occurring events. These genes are well-established drivers of cancer development and progression; KMT2A (an epigenetic regulator) acts as a potent oncogenic driver when rearranged50, whereas CSMD3 (involved in cell surface maintenance) and AMER1 (a WNT pathway regulator) generally function as tumor suppressors that lose their activity when mutated. Co-occurring alterations in the MYC and Cell Cycle pathways were observed in our cohort, underscoring their cooperative role in melanoma progression in this population. These data suggest that such co-occurring mutations may drive melanoma progression in Thai patients. Given that this gene combination has not been previously described, further functional studies are required to validate the impact of these variants.

BRAF mutations are typically mutually exclusive with KIT, NF1, and NRAS11. Consistent with previous reports, we observed a mutually exclusive relationship between BRAF and KIT mutations in Thai melanoma. Because these mutations activate the same downstream MAPK pathway, this mutual exclusivity underscores the redundant nature of these drivers in promoting oncogenesis. Clinically, these findings support personalized treatment strategies for Thai melanoma patients through informed therapeutic selection: patients harboring BRAF mutations are candidates for combined BRAF and MEK inhibition (e.g., dabrafenib and trametinib), whereas those harboring KIT mutations may benefit from tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib or nilotinib. Identifying these specific drivers is therefore essential, as the presence of one typically precludes the need for targeting the other, ensuring a more focused and effective precision medicine approach.

TMB, a marker for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) response (threshold ≥ 10 mut/Mb), correlates with UV exposure in cutaneous melanoma51,52. Our Thai melanoma cohort exhibited a lower median TMB than Caucasian cohorts51. This is likely due to the fact that our cohort was composed primarily of acral and mucosal melanoma—subtypes known to be driven more by structural variations than by single-nucleotide variants. While the TMB varied significantly by subtype (with the acral subtype being lower than the cutaneous subtype, P < 0.03) (Fig. 2e), the frequency of C > T transitions—an indicator of UV-induced DNA damage—remained statistically comparable across all subtypes (P = 0.204) (Supplementary Table 12). This suggests that the lower overall TMB in our cohort compared to Western populations is likely attributable to the predominance of acral and mucosal subtypes rather than a fundamental difference in UV exposure within the cutaneous cases. Consequently, Thai melanoma may derive less clinical benefit from ICI therapy than Caucasian cohorts. Despite the implementation of validated protocols and a rigorous bioinformatic pipeline for tumor-only sequencing, rare germline contamination may remain; TMB interpretation should therefore be carefully considered. Nevertheless, with rigorous bioinformatic filtering, TMB calculated from FFPE tumor-only sequencing has been shown to provide a high correlation with matched tumor-normal sequencing53,54, making it a robust tool for guiding ICI therapy55,56. Huang et al. recently demonstrated that robust filtering in tumor-only assays maintains clinical validity and shows a consistent association with ICI outcomes across diverse ancestries, including Asian populations57. Furthermore, our methodology aligns with FDA-approved platforms such as the FoundationOne CDx assay, which utilizes similar tumor-only protocols to guide therapeutic decisions (TMB ≥ 10 mut/Mb)55,56,58. Crucially, the fact that our median TMB remained significantly below the 10 mut/Mb threshold—rather than being over-inflated—provides further empirical evidence that our filtering process effectively prevented the upward bias typically associated with germline contamination. This suggests that our results remain biologically meaningful and are not driven by unfiltered germline noise.

Analysis of actionable targets in Thai melanoma revealed that 72.7% of patients carried at least one variant, comparable to the 75.0% reported in Chinese cohorts59, but higher than the 56% observed in Western cohorts60. Notably, 48.5% of our cohort harbored Tier I melanoma-specific mutations (primarily in BRAF, KIT, and NRAS). This frequency is similar to that reported in Chinese cohorts (47%) despite differences in database annotation59, but is higher than the rate observed in Western cohorts (approximately 25–28%)60,61. This may be attributed to the limited representation of acral and mucosal subtypes in Western cohorts, which are known to harbor a high frequency of actionable KIT mutations14,34,62. The frequency of BRAF V600 actionable variants in our Thai cohort (30.3%) appears to be higher than that reported in Japanese populations (24%; 22% of skin and 2% of mucosal melanomas)45. However, these observations must be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and the tumor-only sequencing approach, which may influence the precision of variant frequency estimates. These preliminary findings suggest that Thai melanoma patients may have a high prevalence of targetable mutations. They also underscore the potential value of implementing multi-gene testing panels in Thailand to enable broader access to targeted therapies for a large proportion of patients.

KIT mutations are well recognized drivers of melanoma progression33,34. We further explored the clinical impact of our genomic profiling and found that KIT mutations were associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS) compared with wild-type cases in this small cohort (P = 0.006). This is consistent with previous reports in other malignancies, such as acute myeloid leukemia63,64, and suggests that KIT mutation status may serve as a prognostic marker in the Thai population. These results indicate that early identification of these high-risk patients may help inform clinical management, as such patients may require closer surveillance or the consideration of KIT-targeted therapies, such as imatinib or nilotinib, to mitigate the risk of rapid disease progression. However, these results remain hypothesis-generating due to the small cohort size. Moreover, the heterogeneity in key clinicopathologic variables—including subtype, stage, ulceration, and metastatic status—may also influence prognosis. Given the limited sample size of this study, further validation in larger cohorts is required to determine the independent prognostic value of KIT alterations in Thai melanoma.

Interestingly, our analysis revealed that the mutational frequencies of key melanoma driver genes in our Thai cohort were comparable to those reported in Caucasian14,20–22 and other Asian populations11,23–25. Our results align with a large-scale comparison of 18 solid tumor types, which found that the mutation frequencies of the top 50 cancer genes in Chinese patients are generally comparable to those in U.S. populations65. Recent meta-analyses suggest that no global relationship exists between the presence of BRAF mutations and the geographic or ethnic origin of the population48. This indicated that, despite environmental and ethnic variations, the primary oncogenic drivers of melanoma appear to be comparable across diverse populations when adjusted for histological subtype. However, these cross-study comparisons were limited by heterogeneity in cohort composition, including variations in sequencing platforms (e.g., panel-based vs. whole-exome sequencing) and variant-calling pipelines, both of which can influence mutation frequency estimates. Consequently, these results should be interpreted with caution and do not necessarily imply biological equivalence between Thai and other populations. Given the limited sample size, our findings suggest that while major oncogenic drivers appear to be consistent, further large-scale studies are required to validate these preliminary comparative results.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small due to the rarity of melanoma in this population, and the clinical follow-up period was limited. Second, the use of tumor-only sequencing without matched normal DNA. While this approach is common in clinical oncology when germline samples were unavailable, it presents challenges in definitively distinguishing somatic mutations from rare germline variants. To address this, we implemented a stringent, multi-tiered bioinformatic pipeline that integrated population databases with specific Variant Allele Frequency (VAF) thresholds. Variants were further cross-referenced with COSMIC to prioritize somatic drivers. Despite these rigorous measures, the potential for residual germline noise remains. Furthermore, a third limitation involves the technical scope of the OCA-Plus panel; while it targets protein-coding sequences, it does not provide CDS or promoter region coverage for the TERT gene. Given that TERT promoter mutations are highly prevalent in melanoma31,32, our findings primarily characterize the landscape of protein-coding drivers and may underestimate the total mutational burden within non-coding regulatory regions. Future prospective studies utilizing matched tumor-normal sequencing and whole-genome approaches in larger Thai cohorts are warranted to confirm these preliminary findings and fully elucidate the non-coding landscape of Thai melanoma.

Conclusion

Our study provides a comprehensive genomic profile of melanoma in Thai patients, revealing commonly mutated genes, potential drivers, and putative clinically actionable variants. While our findings suggest that certain alterations, such as KIT mutations, may have prognostic implications, these results remain hypothesis-generating due to the small cohort size. Nevertheless, these findings contribute to the understanding of melanoma genetics in the Thai population, highlighting potential therapeutic targets that warrant further investigation. These preliminary observations underscore the importance of developing customized genetic strategies to eventually advance precision oncology in Thailand.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort details

The cohort comprised Thai patients aged ≥ 18 years who were diagnosed with melanoma at Siriraj Hospital between January 2009 and December 2019 (n = 40). Forty FFPE specimens from primary diagnostic biopsies or resections, obtained prior to neoadjuvant therapy and confirmed by pathologists, were sequenced using the 501-gene OCA-Plus panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After quality filtering, 33 tumors were retained for downstream analysis.

Clinicopathology and classification of melanoma subtypes

Clinicopathological data, including sex, age at diagnosis, tumor site, TNM stage, and patient outcomes, were obtained from Siriraj Hospital records. Melanomas were categorized into three subtypes according to disease site11,14: (1) cutaneous melanoma, arising in sun-exposed regions and encompassing superficial spreading (SSM), nodular (NM), and lentigo maligna melanoma (LMM); (2) acral melanoma, localized to the palms and soles; and (3) mucosal melanoma, originating in mucous membranes and occurring in UV-independent sites such as the anal verge, clitoris, and vulva.

Somatic sequencing, variant calling, and filtering

Genomic DNA was extracted and purified from FFPE tissues using reagents from PerkinElmer Chemagen Technologie GmbH (Baesweiler, Germany) and repaired with NEBNext FFPE DNA Repair Mix. Between 20 and 200 ng of DNA was used to construct sequencing libraries with the Oncomine™ Comprehensive Assay Plus (OCA-Plus) kit, including Uracil DNA Glycosylase (UDG) treatment to correct FFPE-induced deamination. Libraries were sequenced on an Ion S5 XL system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using Ion Torrent NGS technology.

Raw reads were aligned to the GRCh37 reference genome using Torrent Suit software v5.16.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples failing manufacturer-recommended OCA-Plus quality control thresholds were excluded. Somatic variant calling and annotation of SNVs, indels, and CNVs were performed using Ion Reporter software v5.16.0.2 with the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus - w2.1 - DNA - Single Sample workflow (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Tumor samples were sequenced in the absence of matched normal DNA from blood samples. To address this limitation and distinguish somatic mutations from potential germline variants or artifacts with the highest possible accuracy, we implemented a rigorous, multi-tiered bioinformatic filtering pipeline. Potential germline variants were initially filtered out using the default Oncomine Extended v5.16 filter chain of the Ion Reporter software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), including MAF ≥ 10−6, 5000 exomes global MAF ≥ 10−6, ExAC GAF ≥ 10−6, and UCSC common SNPs. To mitigate FFPE-induced artifacts, samples with a deamination score > 60 or C: G > T:A mutation proportion > 25% were excluded due to likely FFPE-induced false positives66. VCF files were annotated and converted to MAF using vcf2maf (Cyriac Kandoth. mskcc/vcf2maf: vcf2maf v1.6.19. [2020]. doi:10.5281/zenodo.593251) and VEP.

Remaining variants were further filtered using the following criteria: (1) variants with a Variant Allele Frequency (VAF) < 5% or a read depth < 200× were removed; (2) variants with a population allele frequency ≥ 1% in gnomAD or the 1000 Genomes Project were excluded. Variants confirmed as somatic drivers in COSMIC were retained; (3) known germline polymorphisms documented in dbSNP were removed; and (4) variants predicted to be functionally neutral by SIFT or PolyPhen were excluded. Additionally, indels located in homopolymer stretches ≥ 4, confirmed via IGV67, or overlapping with simple repeats/microsatellite tracts were removed. Only non-synonymous variants affecting exons or splice sites were retained. Finally, all retained variants were manually reviewed to minimize false positives.

Somatic CNVs were identified using Ion Reporter™ with default parameters. Only samples passing the Median Absolute Pairwise Difference (MAPD) QC metric (< 0.5) were included, resulting in 31 samples for analysis. CNVs calling defined amplifications as copy number (CN) ≥ 6 with 95% CI > 4 and deletions as CN ≤ 1 with 95% CI < 1, according to manufacturer-recommended somatic confidence filters (Oncomine™ Comprehensive Assay Plus User Guide, Revision C.0). Only CNVs classified as functional gains or losses via the Oncomine panel (OncomineGeneClass) were retained. TMB was defined as the ratio of total somatic variants to the total genomic target region (Mb), calculated using Ion Reporter™ TMB Algorithm V4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Identification of clinically actionable variants

Actionable variants were identified using the commercial Oncomine Knowledgebase Reporter (OKR) software v5.9.2, data version 2025.04(004) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Variants were classified according to evidence-level tiers IA–IIC following the AMP/ASCO/CAP joint consensus guidelines for somatic variant interpretation in cancer, with associated drug approval status reported for FDA, EMA, NCCN, and ESMO19. Copy number (CN) amplifications and deletions were defined as ≥ 6 and ≤ 1, respectively. CN amplifications were not identified as actionable variants in this study.

Genomic and statistical analysis

Analyses and visualizations were performed in R (v4.3.1) using the maftools package16, and statistical analyses were conducted with R and SPSS (v25). Clinicopathological differences among melanoma subtypes were analyzed using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. Gene alteration frequencies between Thai and other cohorts were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Specific maftools functions employed included lollipopPlot for somatic position distribution at the protein level, plot and somaticInteractions for mutually exclusive and co-occurring events, oncodrive for cancer driver gene identification via positional clustering based on algorithm oncodriveCLUS15, and clinicalEnrichment for subtype-enrichment analysis. Disease-free survival (DFS), defined as the interval from the date of surgery to either disease relapse or the last follow-up, was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Differences between groups were compared via the log-rank test, and a Cox proportional hazards model was employed to determine the Hazard Ratio (HR) and 95% Confidence Interval (CI). Survival curves were visualized using the ggsurvplot function within the survminer package in R. Significance was defined as two-tailed P < 0.05, with FDR < 0.25 for somatic interaction analyses.
