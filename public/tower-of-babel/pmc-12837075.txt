Patterns of prospection bias predict mental health among Chinese college students: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Lei Xia, Xianglin Bai, Zhengzhi Feng, Jinglu Yan, Zhuoya Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health among college students represents a significant and growing public health concern. Negative bias in prospection is closely related to depression and anxiety. Prospection bias (PB) encompasses increased negativity, reduced positivity and overgeneralization, which exhibit intricate co-occurrence patterns and exert a complex influence on mental health. However, the presence of distinct patterns of PB and their impact on mental health remain unknown.MethodsWe recruited 1,030 Chinese college students to complete assessments of PB, depression, anxiety, stress and resilience. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to identify distinct PB profiles. Linear regression was then applied to examine their effects on mental health outcomes.ResultsThe results suggested six profiles: (1) high levels of increased negativity and overgeneralization but a low level of reduced positivity (contradictory overgeneralizers), (2) low PB, (3) moderate low PB, (4) a high level of increased negativity but low levels of reduced positivity and overgeneralization (simple contradictory), (5) high PB, and (6) moderate high PB. Regression analyses demonstrated that high prospection bias predicted more severe stress, depressive and anxious symptoms, as well as lower resilience. Additionally, the results implied that handling increased negativity and reduced positivity of prospection might be potential ways to improve mental health.ConclusionsThese findings may facilitate the early detection of mental health issues among college students and contribute to the refinement of future interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07732-0.Keywords: Prospection, Depression, Mental health, Latent profile analysis

Background

Mental health issues among college students are a growing public health concern. Studies have shown that the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress is significantly greater among college students than among the general population [1, 2]. Moreover, mental health issues among college students are associated with poor academic performance, dysfunctional relationships, poor quality of life and a greater incidence of physical and psychiatric disorders over the long term [3]. Therefore, it is important to improve the early detection and intervention of mental health issues in this population.

Prospection, the mental simulation of future events, plays a key role in emotion regulation, motivation and decision-making [4, 5]. As the episodic simulation model posits, prospection is constructed through flexible combination of past elements [6]. Prospection bias (PB) is characterized by the propensity to envision future events in a negatively skewed and distorted manner, which may be driven by biased cognitive schemas [7]. Based on previous research, PB may include increased negativity (excessive negative future events and enhanced accompanying negative emotion), reduced positivity (lack of positive future events and diminishing anticipatory pleasure) and overgeneralization (the inclination to generate categorical information instead of a specific event during prospection; Yang et al., [8]. Examples of these PB were listed in supplementary materials. PB can have significant detrimental effects on mental health [4]. On one hand, PB may influence resilience, a crucial protective factor consistently identified in research on the mental health of college students [9]. In the context of the episodic simulation model, individuals draw upon their successful past experiences to construct rehearsals of future scenarios, which serve as a source of resilience for coping with future challenges [10, 11]. However, in the presence of PB, the retrieval of successful experiences may be inhibited, and the rehearsal of specific coping strategies could be impeded by the negativity and overgeneralization of future simulations. On the other hand, PB has been closely linked to stress, depression and anxiety [12, 13]. Stress can trigger biased schemas that provide automatic negative templates for future simulations; and in return, PB may exacerbate depressive and anxious symptoms, leading to a cycle of increased stress [7]. Previous research suggested that PB was associated with stress-related cortisol [12]. Moreover, persistent PB has been identified in individuals experiencing mood disorders [13]. According to Beck’s cognitive triad [14] and the “prospection in depression” model [7], PB is considered not only a core symptom but also a causal factor that drives and maintains depression. In addition, the looming vulnerability model of anxiety argues that anxiety arises from the dynamic prospection of future threats [15]. Furthermore, changing PB could be a way to promote depression recovery and resilience [7].

The three PB factors, namely increased negativity, reduced positivity and overgeneralization, are highly correlated but distinct factors. Increased negativity and reduced positivity are supported by previous factor analysis studies [8, 16], which may have different interactions with mental health. Longitudinal research has indicated that reduced positivity for the future, rather than increased negativity, predicts depression onset and suicide [17]. Moreover, prior studies suggest that PB may exhibit differential correlations with depression and anxiety [13]. The most robust finding is that depression is associated with reduced positivity, and anxiety links to increased negativity [13]. Nonetheless, additional research is required to resolve persisting debates, such as whether depression is also related to increased negativity [18, 19] and anxiety to reduced positivity [18, 20]. Overgeneralization is another prospection impairment consistently found in both clinical and subclinical settings [21, 22]. Meta-analyses have shown a robust association between depression and overgeneralization of prospection [21]. These factors exhibit intricate co-occurrence patterns; for instance, individuals across different clinical groups frequently present with varying manifestations of PB [13, 22]. Furthermore, the interplay of these factors may exert a multifaceted impact on mental health, as each factor has the potential to lead to distinct adverse health outcomes [17, 23]. Consequently, it is more insightful to evaluate these three factors collectively rather than assessing each factor of PB in isolation.

Prospection has usually been assessed by tasks such as the future thinking task (assessing the amount of positive/negative future events generated in a limited time; MacLeod et al., [24] and the adapted autobiographical interview (measuring specificity, details, vividness, emotional experience etc.; Addis & Schacter [25]. These tasks typically adopt standardized materials and procedures, and each specific prospection index can be quantified. Despite the merits of these tasks, most of them do not include all three factors of PB, and the administration and scoring procedure is usually time-consuming and complex [26]. Therefore, it is difficult to efficiently capture the three factors of PB simultaneously in large samples. It remains unclear how different patterns of PB present and what their relationships with mental health are. Recently, the Negative Bias in Prospection Scale (NBPS, Yang et al., [8] was developed to assess the three PB factors, which could be an efficient complementary tool. The current study aimed to investigate interactions of the three PB factors and their relationships with mental health in a large sample using the NBPS.

At present, most of the previous findings were obtained using a variable-centered approach. Given that the sample studied was heterogeneous, the variables might not be correlated with each other in the same way for all individuals, which could be one of the reasons for the contradictory results of previous studies. Latent profile analysis (LPA) can be used to divide populations into different subgroups with distinct sets of characteristics [27]. As a complement to previous variable-centered studies, the LPA approach can be applied to uncover different patterns of PB, allowing the prediction of mental health outcomes based on specific patterns of PB. Recently, the LPA was used to identify optimism-pessimism profiles and examine their ability to predict emotional outcomes [28]. Optimism and pessimism reflect general attitudes toward the future. Here, we aimed to investigate negative bias in generating specific future events, which may reveal more information for the refinement of relevant interventions.

The current study applied the LPA approach in a sample of college students to identify subgroups of individuals based on a combination of different levels of the three factors of PB (increased negativity, reduced positivity and overgeneralization). Furthermore, we assessed the impact of these profiles on depression, anxiety, stress and resilience.

Methods

Participants

Undergraduate students were recruited from several universities. A total of 1162 participants completed the online self-report questionnaires. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the score on either of the lie detection items was incorrect (the response was Yes to the item “I’ve never had a cold, even a mild one” or the item “I have never told a lie, even a small one”); or (2) the completion time did not fall within the 2.5th percentile to 97.5th percentile. After applying these criteria, 1,030 participants were retained for analysis. The respondent-to-item ratio was 18.73, which was significantly greater than the conventional threshold of 10:1 [29, 30]. Among them, 37.9% of the participants were male (n = 390), and the average age of the whole sample was 19.45 years (SD = 1.45). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants at the beginning.

Measures

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ)

Depressive symptoms were measured by the PHQ-9 scale, which was developed by Kroenke and Spitzer (2002). The PHQ-9 comprises nine items scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0 represents not at all, 3 represents nearly every day). The adapted Chinese version shows good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.80 in current research.

Generalized anxiety disorder questionnaire-7 (GAD)

The GAD-7 aims to assess individuals’ anxiety symptoms (Spitzer et al., 2006). It includes seven items, scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). This scale has been demonstrated to have good reliability in the present research, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.89.

Perceived stress scale-14 (PSS-14)

The PSS-14 has been widely used to measure stress levels (Cohen et al., 1983; Mimura and Griffiths, 2004). It comprises 14 items and is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0: Never; 4: Very often), with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. At present research, this scale shows good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.80.

10-item Connor-Davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC-10)

The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale was developed to evaluate resilience [31], specifically the ability to cope with adversity. The CD-RISC-10 was extracted from the original CD-RISC [32]. This scale comprises 10 items and is scored from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (true nearly all the time), ranging from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater ability to cope with adversity. The CD-RISC-10 has good validity and reliability in college students and depressive patients [33]. The Cronbach’s alpha of this scale in the current research is 0.92.

Negative bias in prospection scale

Negative bias in prospection was evaluated by the fourteen-item NBPS scale [8], which contains three factors: increased negativity, reduced positivity and overgeneralization. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very false for me) to 5 (very true for me). This scale shows good reliability and validity, with CFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.07, and Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91 of present research.

Data analysis

To address the missing values in the present study, the point estimates were imputed based on the maximum likelihood estimates of the means and covariances [34]. The expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm was applied under the assumptions that the data followed a multivariate normal distribution and that the missingness was at random. While simulations have demonstrated that the EM algorithm is relatively robust to violations of the multivariate normality assumption, we still assessed the skewness and kurtosis of the score distribution. Given that the skewness and kurtosis values ranged from − 2 to 2, this indicated that the data distribution could be considered approximately normal [35].

To assess whether students can be characterized through a combination of different levels of the three factors of PB (increased negativity, reduced positivity and overgeneralization), the LPA was adopted, which is an approach taking heterogeneity issues into consideration. Three factors of PB (increased negativity, reduced positivity and overgeneralization) were entered into the LPA model, and to determine the best solution for the identified profiles, the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator was used, and the classifications of two to eight profiles were explored. The number of initial stage random starts was set at 200, with the 50 best solutions retained for the final stage of the optimizations. To achieve the best solution for the LPA, several criteria were used: Akaike’s information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the sample-size adjusted BIC (a-BIC), the adjusted Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (aLMR) [36], the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and the entropy. Moreover, the sample size of the smallest group was also taken into consideration, which suggests that the smallest group should contain at least 2% of the sample [37]. Models with lower AICs, BICs and a-BICs were considered to better fit the data. For aLMR and BLRT, when the comparison between models with K profiles and K-1 profiles yielded a p value greater than 0.05, the model with K-1 profiles should be selected as the final model. Apart from the aLMR and BLRT, entropy was also chosen as a criterion to evaluate the quality of profile membership classification, relating to the accuracy of the model to distribute students into a specific profile. The entropy value ranges from 0 to 1, with a value closer to 1 indicating better classification [38]. When the value of entropy is equal or greater than 0.8, the accuracy of profile membership classification could exceed 90%. However, we should note that no single statistic has been demonstrated to be preferable across every condition [39], and profile interpretability and uniqueness are also taken into account when deciding the final model [40].

After determining the best solution for the identified profiles, we examined whether profile membership could predict mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, stress and resilience. Linear regression analyses with class-varying residual variances were used to test these relationships [28]. Profile membership served as the predictor, and all analyses were conducted in Mplus 7 and SPSS 24.

Results

Common method bias

All data were collected via self-report, raising the possibility of common method bias. We addressed this issue in two ways. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted via exploratory factor analysis. A single factor accounted for only 26.24% of the total variance, well below the 40% threshold [41]. Second, we used Harman’s factor analysis again, but this time we extracted factors based on their eigenvalues, instead of fixing them to one factor. The items yielded nine factors, explaining 59.101% of the total variance. Among these factors, the first extracted factor could account for 26.24% of the variance. This is far less than the 50% standard put forward by Hair et al. [42].

This group, termed “simple contradictory”, comprises 6.990% of the sample. Profile 5 included students with the highest level of PB, representing 7.282% of the sample. Discussion

This study investigated profiles of prospection bias (PB) and their associations with mental health. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to do so using a person-centered approach. We identified six subgroups: (1) contradictory overgeneralizers: those with high levels of increased negativity and overgeneralization but a low level of reduced positivity (profile 1: 2.718%); (2) those with low PB (profile 2: 8.155%); (3) those with moderate low PB (profile 3: 35.340%); (4) simple contradictory: those with a high level of increased negativity but low levels of reduced positivity and overgeneralization (profile 4: 6.99%); (5) those with high PB (profile 5: 7.282%); and (6) those with moderate high PB (profile 6: 39.515%). The three factors of PB share similar response patterns in profiles 2, 3, 5 and 6, reflecting that these biases may often co-occur in these college students. Profile 1 and profile 4 exhibited relatively high levels of increased negativity but low levels of reduced positivity, reflecting that there may be a few college students who showed increased negativity during prospection but preserved a relatively intact ability to generate and preexperience positive future events.

We assessed the differences in mental health scores between PB profiles. We found that in general, individuals with higher levels of PB had more severe depression, anxiety and stress symptoms (profiles 5 > 6 > 3 > 2) and a lower level of resilience (profiles 5 < 6 < 3 < 2). This is in line with previous studies suggesting that depressed and anxious people manifest PB [13]. Previous research also indicated that PB could be a significant predictor of depression [17]. Though further investigation of PB patterns in other representative samples (e.g., large community samples, clinical samples) are needed, our findings are consistent with the prospection in depression model [7] and the looming vulnerability model of anxiety [15], highlighting the significant associations between prospection bias and mental health.

It is worth noting that profile 1 (contradictory overgeneralizers) and profile 4 (simple contradictory) had levels of depression, anxiety and stress similar to those of profile 6 (moderate high PB), and a level of resilience similar to that of profile 3 (moderate low PB). Given that profiles 1 and 4 showed high levels of increased negativity, similar to profile 6, this may suggest that increased negativity of prospection could be more closely related to stress and depressive and anxious symptoms than other PB factors. On the other hand, considering that profiles 1 and 4 exhibited relatively low levels of reduced positivity compared with profile 2 (low PB), this finding may also indicate that the ability to experience pleasant future events could have a close relationship with resilience and be a protective factor of mental health. This finding is consistent with prior research demonstrating that negative expectations about the future, anxiety and negative affect were loaded on a common negative factor, while positive expectations and positive affect were loaded on the other positive factor [16]. In the context of emerging prospection intervention, previous studies have focused on the effects of the anticipatory pleasure training [43] and the specificity training [44] on alleviating depression symptoms. Our results highlighted the importance of addressing increased negativity in future interventions. Additionally, our findings implied that enhancing positivity in prospection might help to cope with adversity and be a potential way to boost resilience in college students.

The regression analyses suggested that college students with low (Profile 2) or moderate low (Profile 3) PB showed negative associations with stress and depressive and anxious symptoms and presented a positive link to resilience, while the reverse pattern was found for college students with high PB (Profile 5). In addition, our results showed that a moderately high level of PB (profile 6) was associated with increased depression, stress and decreased resilience but did not significantly increase anxiety. These findings indicated that high PB could be a crucial risk factor for poor mental health. Our findings have several practical implications for improving the mental health of college students. Firstly, Profile 5 merits particular attention for early detection and intervention, as this subgroup exhibits the highest levels of PB and the poorest mental health outcomes. Secondly, preventive strategies may be necessary to improve mental health in individuals with moderately high PB (profile 6, 39.515%), given that chronic stress has been demonstrated to be a causal factor in the development of mental disorders [45]. Lastly, the ability to generate positive future events may serve as an effective target for the maintenance of mental health. We found that profile 1 (contradictory overgeneralizers) and profile 4 (simple contradictory) were not significantly related to any of the outcome variables. These two profiles are similar to the “symptomatic but content group” in the dual factor model of mental health [46, 47], presenting both risk and protective factors.

There are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this study employed a cross-sectional design. Future longitudinal research is necessary to investigate how PB profiles change over time and how these changes relate to mental health outcomes. Second, all the measures were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias in responses and inflation bias in data analysis. Furthermore, self-report scales may not accurately capture participants’ precise stress levels [48]. Future research should incorporate both subjective and objective assessments to provide a more comprehensive examination of PB and mental health. Third, our study exhibited participation bias, with a lower number of male students participating. Future research should aim for a more balanced gender ratio to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the causal relationship between PB and mental health issues remains unclear. Further investigation is needed to elucidate how PB contributes to the development of psychopathology. Fourth, we should notice that the group of contradictory overgeneralizers (profile 1) occupied 2.718% of the whole sample, falling below the commonly accepted minimum of 5% for stable class estimation [49]. Considering this, further replication is needed to improve the rationale of retaining this subgroup.

In conclusion, the LPA identified six distinct subgroups based on three PB factors (increased negativity, reduced positivity and overgeneralization) in college students. Moreover, these PB profiles were significantly associated with mental health outcomes. Our findings suggest that higher levels of prospection bias are associated with more severe stress, depressive and anxious symptoms and lower resilience. In addition, our results suggest that addressing increased negativity and reduced positivity of prospection could represent promising strategies for improving these symptoms and enhancing resilience. These findings may facilitate the early detection of mental health issues in college students and contribute to the refinement of future interventions.
