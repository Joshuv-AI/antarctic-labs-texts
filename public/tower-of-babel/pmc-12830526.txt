Progress in the antitumor molecular mechanisms of Isorhamnetin
Authors: Anyang Liu, Xunxing Hao, Yong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and the limitations of conventional chemotherapy—such as severe side effects and drug resistance—underscore the urgent need for novel anti-cancer agents with high efficacy and low toxicity. In this context, natural products derived from traditional medicinal sources have attracted increasing attention as promising alternatives. Isorhamnetin, a naturally occurring flavonoid found in plants like Ginkgo biloba and sea buckthorn, has emerged as a compound of particular interest due to its broad spectrum of pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, vascular-protective, and notably, anti-tumor activities. Recent studies have shown that Isorhamnetin can inhibit tumor cell proliferation and suppress the progression of various cancers, such as breast, pancreatic, gastric, and liver cancers. Its anti-tumor effects are mediated through multiple mechanisms, including induction of apoptosis, inhibition of proliferation, suppression of invasion and migration, and enhancement of chemosensitivity. Moreover, Isorhamnetin exerts its therapeutic effects by modulating diverse signaling pathways. Despite its potential, a comprehensive understanding of its molecular mechanisms remains incomplete, and systematic reviews are still lacking. This review aims to summarize the current evidence on the anti-cancer mechanisms of Isorhamnetin, providing a scientific basis for its clinical application and the development of effective, low-toxicity anti-cancer therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03265-w.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, an estimated 19.3 million new cancer cases and nearly 10 million cancer-related deaths were reported globally in 2020. These numbers are projected to rise to approximately 28.4 million new cases by 2040 [1]. Despite continuous advances in cancer treatment, chemotherapy and radiotherapy remain the cornerstone of conventional therapies. However, their clinical efficacy is frequently compromised by severe side effects, poor selectivity, and the development of drug resistance. These limitations not only impair patients’ quality of life but also hinder long-term therapeutic outcomes, particularly in those with advanced or metastatic disease [2, 3]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying tumor progression and identifying highly effective, low-toxicity anticancer agents are of critical importance for improving cancer management.

In recent years, traditional medicine has gained increasing recognition for its anti-cancer potential, largely due to its multi-target effects and relatively low toxicity [4]. A considerable number of modern anti-cancer drugs—such as paclitaxel derived from Taxus chinensis—originate from natural products and traditional medicinal herbs [5, 6]. These agents exert comprehensive anti-tumor effects by modulating oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, epigenetic mechanisms, the tumor microenvironment, and cancer stem cell activity [5, 7]. Among the growing array of plant-derived compounds used in cancer therapy, flavonoids—a class of natural polyphenolic compounds—stand out, with approximately 4,000 species identified to date. Isorhamnetin (Iso), a naturally occurring small-molecule flavonoid, is one such compound. Its chemical name is 3,5,7-trihydroxy-2-(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)benzo[b]pyran-4-one, and its molecular formula is C₁₆H₁₂O₇ [8]. The chemical structure of Iso is presented in Fig. 1. Iso, a naturally occurring methylated metabolite of quercetin, has attracted growing attention owing to its diverse pharmacological properties, including vascular protection, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antitumor activitie [9]. Compared with its parent compound quercetin and other quercetin derivatives, Iso exhibits superior pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles, characterized by greater metabolic stability, enhanced membrane permeability, and improved bioavailability, all of which contribute to its therapeutic potential [10, 11]. Notably, Iso has demonstrated stronger anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects than related metabolites such as tamarixetin and quercetin-3-glucuronide in various tumor models [12, 13]. These distinctive features highlight Iso as a particularly promising candidate for anticancer drug development and provide a solid rationale for the focused discussion in this review.

A growing body of evidence suggests that Iso exerts its antitumor effects by modulating key cellular processes, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and cell cycle arrest, as well as by enhancing the efficacy of conventional chemotherapeutic agents [14–18]. However, despite the increasing number of preclinical studies, a comprehensive review systematically summarizing the molecular mechanisms underlying Iso’s anticancer actions remains lacking. To address this gap, the present article offers a critical and up-to-date overview of the anticancer activities of Iso, with the aim of supporting its further development as a safe and effective natural product-derived therapeutic agent.

Antitumor molecular mechanisms of ISO

Inhibition of tumor cell proliferation

As one of the fundamental cellular functions that constitute life, proliferation is a precisely ordered process under strict control [19]. Cancer is a proliferative disease marked by an imbalance between oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Cancer cells can secrete growth factors that bind to their respective receptors to promote proliferation [20]. Therefore, inhibiting tumor cell proliferation remains a standard clinical strategy for cancer treatment.

Zhai et al. [18] demonstrated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments that Iso suppresses the proliferation of gallbladder cancer (GBC) cells, thereby impeding disease progression. They treated GBC cell lines (GBC-SD and NOZ) with various concentrations of Iso in vitro and found that Iso inhibited proliferation in a time- and dose-dependent manner. In vivo, using a mouse xenograft model and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, they confirmed that Iso inhibits proliferation and metastasis of GBC cells, induces apoptosis by inactivating the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathway, and arrests the cell cycle at the gap 2/mitosis (G2/M) phase. Similarly, Li et al. [12] reported that Iso dose-dependently inhibits the proliferation of AGS-1 and HGC-2 gastric cancer cells. The mechanism was found to involve mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis.

Numerous natural flavonoid compounds have been reported to exert anti-tumor effects by inducing cell cycle arrest, a key regulatory mechanism in various tumor types [21–23]. Wang et al. [15] discovered that Iso induces S-phase arrest in pancreatic cancer PANC-1 cells by inhibiting the Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway activity, thereby significantly suppressing the growth of PANC-1 cells. The mechanism may be due to Iso increasing the expression of cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (Cdk2) protein in PANC-1 cells, thereby promoting the initiation of the S-phase, while decreasing the intracellular expression of cyclin A, which ultimately inhibits the progression of the S-phase and arrests the cells in the S-phase. During this process, the phosphorylation levels of downstream cascade proteins mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) in the Ras/MAPK signaling pathway were significantly reduced. In contrast, the expression level of Ras protein remained unchanged, indicating that Iso may achieve S-phase arrest in PANC-1 cells by regulating the Ras/MAPK signaling pathway. This suggests that Iso may become a new natural therapeutic agent for treating pancreatic cancer.

Esophageal cancer is among the six most common cancers globally. According to the World Health Organization, approximately half of the cases occur in China, with a five-year overall survival rate of less than 20% [24, 25]. Despite advancements in radiotherapy and chemotherapy, prognosis remains poor, and adverse effects are substantial, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic options. Ma et al. [26] investigated the in vitro anti-tumor effects of Iso on human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) Eca-109 cells. Their findings revealed that Iso significantly reduced Eca-109 cell viability and dose-dependently inhibited their proliferation, thereby impeding esophageal cancer progression.

The anti-tumor mechanisms of Iso have also been linked to the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma/phosphatase and tensin homolog/protein kinase B (PPARγ/PTEN/AKT) pathway. Zhang et al. [27] reported that Iso inhibits bladder cancer progression by activating this pathway and downregulating carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9), a downstream target. Specifically, Iso binds to and upregulates PPARγ, inhibits PTEN activity, reduces AKT phosphorylation, and suppresses CA9 expression, thereby inducing G1 phase arrest and inhibiting tumor cell proliferation. These findings support the potential application of Iso as a therapeutic agent for bladder cancer. Moreover, extensive studies have confirmed that quercetin—the parent compound of Iso—possesses strong anti-proliferative activity, which may partly explain the in vitro anticancer effects of Iso and its metabolites [28]. Iso and Isorhamnetin-3-glucuronide (I3G), both water-soluble quercetin derivatives, have been shown to inhibit the proliferation of MCF-7 breast cancer cells. The primary mechanism involves cell cycle arrest at the S phase, accompanied by a reduction in the proportions of cells in the G0/G1 and G2/M phases [29].

The phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K-AKT-mTOR) signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating cell proliferation, glucose metabolism, and survival [30, 31], and is frequently dysregulated in tumorigenesis. It is thus considered a key therapeutic target in cancer [32, 33]. Li et al. [34] found that Iso markedly inhibits the proliferation of colorectal cancer (CRC) cells—including HT-29, HCT116, and SW480—by inducing G2/M phase arrest and suppressing the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway. These findings suggest that Iso may act as a selective PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway inhibitor and a promising therapeutic candidate for CRC. In addition, Iso has demonstrated anti-tumor activity in liver cancer. Choi et al. [35] showed that Iso induces G2/M phase arrest and promotes apoptosis in Hep3B human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells. This effect was associated with downregulation of cyclin A and cyclin B1, along with upregulation of p21, indicating a role in cell cycle regulation.

Induction of tumor cell apoptosis

Apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death, plays a pivotal role in tumor development and progression [36]. A growing body of evidence indicates that Iso exerts potent antitumor effects by inducing both cell cycle arrest and apoptosis across various cancer cell lines, while exhibiting minimal toxicity toward normal cells [37, 38].

Apoptotic signaling is generally divided into two main pathways: the extrinsic (death receptor-mediated) pathway and the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway [39, 40]. PARP (poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase), a well-known substrate of caspases, serves as a hallmark of apoptosis once cleaved. Iso has been shown to activate caspase-8 and caspase-9—key initiator caspases in the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways, respectively—subsequently leading to the activation of the effector caspase-3 and the degradation of PARP [35].

In hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cells, Iso increases intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which in turn induces G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis [41–43]. The ROS-mediated activation of both extrinsic and intrinsic apoptotic pathways suggests a dual mechanism of action. Indeed, ROS generation has been widely recognized as a key strategy of many anti-cancer agents that induce apoptosis by disrupting redox balance or triggering endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [44, 45]. In breast cancer, Yang et al. [46] demonstrated that Iso significantly elevated ROS accumulation and induced extensive DNA damage in doxorubicin-resistant MDA-MB-231/DOX and MCF7/ADR cells, both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, Iso activated the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling pathway and inhibited mTOR/ p70 ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p70S6K) activity, which may have further enhanced ROS-mediated apoptotic responses. These results suggest that Iso promotes apoptosis in drug-resistant breast cancer cells via ROS-mediated DNA damage and AMPK activation.

Endometrial cancer (EC) is a prevalent female cancer that has attracted increasing attention. It is the third most common malignant tumor leading to female death, following ovarian cancer and cervical cancer [47, 48]. Iso has also shown promise in EC, a major gynecological malignancy. Ye et al. [49] reported that Iso activated both the mitochondrial and death receptor pathways in Ishikawa cells in vivo, while concurrently inducing ER stress and upregulating key markers of the unfolded protein response (UPR). These findings suggest that Iso exerts anti-endometrial cancer effects by triggering apoptosis through multiple mechanisms, including ER stress induction and simultaneous activation of both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways.

In gastric cancer (GC), mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis has been highlighted as a key mechanism. Li et al. [14] reported that treatment of AGS-1 and HGC-27 cells with Iso led to the upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax) and cytochrome c, increased levels of cleaved caspase-3 and PARP, and a concomitant downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2). These molecular alterations collectively indicate that Iso induces apoptosis in GC cells via the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway.

Similar mitochondrial-dependent mechanisms were also observed in melanoma. Duan et al. [50] found that Iso significantly upregulated Bax and caspase-3, while downregulating Bcl-2, thereby increasing the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio in B16F10 cells. An increased Bax/Bcl-2 ratio promotes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, thereby activating caspase-dependent apoptosis—a desired effect in cancer therapy aiming to eliminate malignant cells [51]. Iso also inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and reduced nuclear translocation of Nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), suggesting that its pro-apoptotic effects are also mediated through suppression of survival pathways.

Moreover, Iso demonstrates unique efficacy under hypoxic conditions, which are common in the tumor microenvironment and often promote resistance to therapy [52]. In a study on MKN-45 GC cells, Li et al. [53] found that Iso significantly reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and induced apoptosis under hypoxia. This effect was accompanied by the upregulation of mitochondrial apoptotic markers—including cytochrome c, caspase-9, and caspase-3—and by the inhibition of PI3K-AKT-mTOR-mediated adaptive autophagy. These findings underscore Iso’s potential to overcome hypoxia-induced resistance and promote cancer cell death even in stress-adaptive tumor niches. Inhibition of tumor cell invasion and migration

Cancer cell metastasis remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality and involves a complex, multistep process comprising detachment from the primary tumor, invasion of surrounding tissues, intravasation into the bloodstream, and colonization at distant sites [54, 55]. Among the most critical factors contributing to poor prognosis and high mortality is the capacity of tumor cells to invade and metastasize. Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is recognized as a pivotal process in tumor progression, wherein epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal properties, thereby enhancing their motility, invasiveness, and resistance to apoptosis [56]. EMT is commonly associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes and is closely linked to metastasis and recurrence. However, the functional significance of EMT is highly context-dependent, varying across tumor types, stages, and microenvironmental conditions. Thus, targeting EMT may offer therapeutic benefits in certain cancer subtypes or disease stages, but such strategies should be pursued with caution and specificity.

Luo et al. [57] investigated the anti-metastatic effects of Iso in A549 human lung cancer cells and demonstrated that Iso significantly inhibited cell adhesion, invasion, and migration in a concentration-dependent manner. These inhibitory effects were associated with reduced activity and expression of matrix metalloproteinases-2 (MMP-2) and MMP-9, which are key enzymes involved in extracellular matrix degradation. Additionally, Iso downregulated mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, vimentin, and Snail) while upregulating the epithelial marker E-cadherin, indicating a reversal of EMT-like changes. Mechanistically, Iso suppressed the Akt/ERK1/2 signaling pathway, which is known to regulate EMT and metastatic progression. By targeting this pathway, Iso effectively attenuated EMT-associated molecular events, thereby reducing the metastatic potential of A549 cells. These findings suggest that Iso exerts anti-metastatic effects by modulating EMT-related signaling pathways, although its efficacy may differ depending on tumor type and context, warranting further investigation.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, play a central role in promoting cancer cell invasion by degrading the extracellular matrix (ECM) [58]. Elevated expression of these proteases has been observed in numerous malignancies and is often correlated with poor clinical outcomes and reduced patient survival [59]. In breast cancer, Li et al. [60] examined the anti-invasive properties of Iso in MDA-MB-231 cells and found that Iso significantly inhibited both the activity and expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9. Further mechanistic analyses revealed that Iso suppressed the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and STAT3, suggesting that its anti-metastatic effects may be mediated through inhibition of the p38/STAT3 signaling axis. The results showed that Iso inhibits the phosphorylation of p38 protein and STAT3. Similar findings were observed in gallbladder cancer cells, where Iso treatment led to a dose-dependent decrease in migration- and invasion-associated proteins, including MMP-2, MMP-9, and N-cadherin, while markedly increasing E-cadherin expression [18]. These changes indicate that Iso effectively inhibits in vitro metastasis of gallbladder cancer cells. Furthermore, in endometrial cancer models, Iso has been shown to downregulate MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression both in vitro and in vivo, thereby inhibiting tumor cell dissemination [49]. Collectively, these results highlight the potential of Iso as a natural therapeutic agent for suppressing cancer metastasis.

Despite significant advances in treatment modalities, GC remains the fourth most common cancer globally and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [61, 62]. Cancer metastasis, the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumor to distant sites where they form secondary tumors, is considered one of the major causes of cancer-related deaths [63–65]. A research team utilized the Becton Dickinson (BD) Biocoat Tumor Invasion System to investigate the effects of Iso on the invasion of GC cells [37]. It was demonstrated for the first time that Iso can inhibit the migration and invasion of GC cells. These effects could be reversed in the presence of a PPAR-γ inhibitor, indicating that the anti-migration and anti-invasion effects of Iso may be mediated through the activation of the Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) pathway. Cai et al. [66] reported that Iso suppressed the overexpression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 and inhibited migration and invasion in a concentration-dependent manner. Iso also downregulated p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-mTOR, indicating that Iso may act as a selective inhibitor of the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway. These findings support the potential of Iso as a therapeutic candidate for the treatment of androgen-independent prostate cancer. Enhancing the sensitivity of chemotherapy drugs

As cancer patients undergo multiple cycles of chemotherapy, the development of drug resistance becomes increasingly common. Once tumor cells acquire resistance, the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents is markedly reduced, often leading to therapeutic failure. Therefore, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of drug resistance and identifying effective strategies to overcome it are critical components of modern cancer treatment. Although chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of cancer therapy, resistance allows malignant cells to evade cytotoxic effects, diminishing clinical outcomes—particularly in advanced-stage disease.

Cisplatin and carboplatin are among the most commonly used platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents; however, their clinical utility has been increasingly challenged by the emergence of drug resistance [67]. To improve treatment outcomes and prognoses in lung cancer patients, especially those with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), there is an urgent need to develop novel anti-tumor agents. Zhang et al. [68] demonstrated that Iso, either alone or in combination with cisplatin or carboplatin, exhibits significant anti-tumor activity against A549 lung cancer cells. Their results showed that co-treatment with Iso and either cisplatin or carboplatin produced a more potent inhibitory effect on cancer cell proliferation and induced apoptosis more effectively than single-agent therapies. Specifically, the combination treatments led to G2/M phase cell cycle arrest, activation of the caspase-dependent apoptotic pathway, and reduced cell viability, suggesting that Iso enhances the sensitivity of A549 cells to both cisplatin and carboplatin. These findings indicate that Iso, when used in combination with platinum-based drugs, could be a promising strategy for NSCLC chemotherapy.

In addition to platinum compounds, capecitabine is a widely used chemotherapeutic agent, particularly for treating advanced breast, rectal, and gastric cancers that are resistant to taxane- and anthracycline-based regimens. Its anti-tumor activity is believed to involve modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [69]. Studies have shown that Iso can inhibit the expression of various NF-κB-regulated genes in GC cell lines SNU-5 and SNU-16, and induce PARP cleavage, thereby enhancing the pro-apoptotic effects of capecitabine. In vivo experiments further revealed that the combination of Iso and capecitabine significantly suppressed the growth of GC xenografts compared to capecitabine monotherapy. Moreover, expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 and NF-κB-associated proteins such as p65, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2) and MMP9 was markedly downregulated in the combination treatment group [70]. These findings suggest that Iso enhances the anti-tumor activity of capecitabine in GC by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Radiotherapy is another widely used modality in cancer treatment, with more than 50% of patients receiving it at some point during their clinical course. Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 1.38 million deaths annually [71, 72]. NSCLC comprises nearly 80% of lung cancer cases, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) being the most prevalent subtypes [73, 74]. Despite its widespread use, radiotherapy alone offers a poor five-year survival rate of only 5–10% in lung cancer patients [75] A major challenge contributing to poor outcomes is the intrinsic or acquired radioresistance of cancer cells [76]. Recent studies have indicated that Iso may serve as a potential radiosensitizer. In vitro analyses revealed that Iso significantly reduced the survival of lung cancer cells following radiation exposure, suggesting a radiosensitizing effect [77]. Mechanistically, this enhancement was associated with increased expression of interleukin-13 (IL-13), suppression of NF-κB activation, and amplified DNA damage. These results suggest that Iso may improve the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy in NSCLC by modulating pro-inflammatory signaling and enhancing radiation-induced cytotoxicity.

Autophagic tumor cell death induction

In addition to apoptosis, autophagy is recognized as another critical form of programmed cell death. Upon activation, microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) undergoes cleavage and lipidation to generate LC3-II, a key autophagosome marker that facilitates the formation of autophagic vesicles and promotes cellular degradation processes. Thus, the induction of autophagy has emerged as an important mechanism by which anticancer agents exert cytotoxic effects [78]. In lung cancer A549 cells, treatment with Iso significantly increased LC3-II expression in a dose-dependent manner. The expression of Beclin-1, another essential regulator of autophagy initiation, also showed a similar upregulation trend, indicating that Iso may promote autophagy-mediated cell death in lung cancer cells [79].

A growing body of evidence suggests that hypoxia—a common feature of the tumor microenvironment—can induce cytoprotective autophagy in cancer cells [80, 81]. Autophagy serves as an intrinsic survival mechanism by removing damaged organelles and misfolded proteins, which accumulate under hypoxic stress. These cellular components are degraded in lysosomes into metabolic intermediates such as amino acids and fatty acids, which can then be recycled to maintain energy balance and intracellular homeostasis [82, 83]. Importantly, inhibiting autophagy has been shown to suppress GC cell proliferation [84]. Under hypoxic conditions, enhanced autophagy helps tumor cells adapt to metabolic stress. However, when autophagy is blocked, damaged organelles and metabolic byproducts accumulate, tipping the balance toward cell death [85, 86]. This suggests that targeting autophagy under hypoxic stress may represent a viable strategy to impede tumor progression [87].

Li et al. [53] reported that Iso can inhibit autophagy in MKN-45 GC cells under hypoxic conditions. Their results showed that Iso treatment reduced the expression levels of LC3B and Beclin-1 proteins in MKN-45 cells. Similarly, in MKN-28 (a human gastric carcinoma cell line) and AGS (adenocarcinoma gastric cell line) cells exposed to hypoxia, Iso also decreased LC3B levels. They observed a significant increase in autophagic activity and autophagosome formation in MKN-45 cells under hypoxia, and Iso enhanced apoptosis by suppressing this autophagic response. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is a well-established regulator of autophagy and was found to be activated in MKN-45 cells under hypoxic conditions. Notably, 3-methyladenine (3-MA), an autophagy inhibitor, exerts its effects by targeting PI3K, indicating that PI3K plays a key role in autophagy regulation during hypoxia [88].

In summary, Iso promotes apoptosis in MKN-45 GC cells under hypoxic conditions by inhibiting PI3K/AKT/mTOR-mediated adaptive autophagy. These findings suggest that the regulation of autophagy via PI3K inhibition is a critical mechanism underlying Iso’s anti-tumor effects, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic agent for GC.

Others

Previous studies have demonstrated a strong link between chronic inflammation and tumorigenesis, with certain inflammatory responses actively contributing to cancer initiation and progression [89]. As a result, targeting inflammation and its associated molecular mediators has emerged as a promising strategy for cancer prevention and treatment. Chronic inflammatory diseases such as ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease have been associated with an increased risk of CRC, underscoring the carcinogenic potential of sustained inflammation [90–92]. Saud et al. [93]. reported that mice treated with Iso showed faster recovery from colitis compared to the model group. The Iso-treated group exhibited significantly lower rates of microadenoma formation, colorectal hyperplasia, squamous metaplasia, and overall tumor incidence, suggesting that Iso may exert tumor-preventive effects via its anti-inflammatory properties. COX-2 is a key mediator of inflammatory responses, and its product, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), exhibits certain carcinogenic effects [94]. Kim et al. [95] found that treatment of human squamous cell carcinoma A431 cells with 10 µmol/L of Iso nearly completely suppressed COX-2 expression. Further investigations revealed that Iso could inhibit COX-2 promoter activity, thereby preventing epidermal growth factor (EGF)-induced carcinogenesis in JB6 mouse epidermal cells.

Nonmelanoma skin cancer (NMSC) is the most prevalent form of cancer in the United States, affecting approximately 1.3 million individuals annually [96]. The associated healthcare burden is substantial, with an estimated economic cost of $13 billion per year [97]. Environmental changes, ozone depletion, pollution, and dietary habits have all contributed to the rising incidence of NMSC [98]. Therefore, identifying effective chemopreventive agents is essential for reducing both the disease burden and associated healthcare costs [99]. MEK1 and PI3K have emerged as attractive molecular targets for therapeutic intervention in both proliferative and inflammatory diseases. Studies have shown that Iso exerts anti-skin cancer effects by directly binding to Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1 (MEK1) in an ATP-noncompetitive manner and to PI3K in an ATP-competitive manner, thereby inhibiting their enzymatic activities [95]. Discussion

According to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were approximately 19.3 million new cancer cases globally in 2020, with China alone accounting for 4.57 million cases (23.7% of the global total). The global number of cancer-related deaths reached nearly 10 million, with China reporting approximately 3 million deaths (30% of the total) [1, 100]. As such, China ranks first worldwide in both new cancer incidences and cancer mortality, highlighting the increasing burden of malignancies. Current treatment strategies—including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies—remain the mainstay of clinical cancer management. However, challenges such as treatment-related toxicity, drug resistance, and reduced quality of life necessitate the development of safer and more effective therapeutic alternatives. In recent years, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has played an increasingly important role in comprehensive cancer treatment. Owing to its multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway characteristics, TCM—including monomers, herbal pairs, and compound formulas—has demonstrated unique advantages in modulating tumor progression and has become a significant focus in oncology research.

Flavonoids, a class of natural compounds with a benzopyranone skeleton, possess diverse pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and anticancer effects [101, 102]. Several flavonoid compounds have shown considerable potential in tumor suppression across various malignancies [103, 104]. Iso, a flavonol derived from plants such as Hippophae rhamnoides and Ginkgo biloba, exhibits broad-spectrum antitumor activity. It functions by regulating multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt/mTOR, Ras/MAPK, and Wnt/β-catenin. Iso has been reported to induce cell cycle arrest, promote apoptosis, and suppress tumor invasion and metastasis. Furthermore, Iso may act as a radiosensitizer and chemosensitizer, potentially enhancing the efficacy of conventional therapies.

In addition, emerging evidence suggests that Iso may exert immunomodulatory effects, such as regulating cytokine expression, suppressing chronic inflammation, and potentially influencing immune checkpoint pathways. These properties indicate its promise not only as a cytotoxic agent but also as an immunotherapeutic adjuvant, capable of modulating the tumor immune microenvironment and enhancing antitumor immunity.

However, despite promising preclinical evidence, current research on Iso remains limited in several aspects. First, most studies have been conducted in vitro or in vivo using animal models. While these studies provide valuable mechanistic insights, their clinical relevance is uncertain due to interspecies differences and the lack of human pharmacokinetic data. The translation of these findings to human patients must therefore be approached with caution. Clearly defining the limitations of in vitro or animal-based data and evaluating Iso in clinically relevant models is necessary for its advancement in drug development pipelines.

Secondly, although Iso exhibits multifaceted antitumor effects, the precise molecular targets and regulatory networks remain to be fully elucidated. Future research should focus on high-throughput screening and omics-based approaches (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics) to identify specific molecular targets and biomarkers of response. In particular, its immunoregulatory role in reshaping the tumor microenvironment, enhancing T cell infiltration, or modulating antigen presentation remains largely unexplored and represents a crucial direction for future investigation.

From a translational perspective, addressing key issues in drug development is essential. These include pharmacokinetics (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion), bioavailability, drug stability, and toxicity profiles. The poor water solubility and low oral bioavailability of many flavonoids, including Iso, may hinder their clinical application. Therefore, further studies should explore structural modification, nanoformulation, or combination strategies to enhance Iso’s drug-like properties.

Moreover, the development of Iso-based therapeutics should consider combination regimens. Given the complexity of tumor biology, monotherapy may not yield optimal outcomes. Iso could be evaluated in combination with standard chemotherapeutics or immunotherapies to assess potential synergistic effects and resistance-reducing capacities.

Clinical translation also demands well-designed preclinical models and eventually, human studies. To bridge the gap from bench to bedside, future directions should include: Standardized pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies in animal models. Evaluation of Iso in patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) or organoids that better recapitulate human tumors. Phase I clinical trials to determine safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy in cancer patients. Biomarker-driven stratification strategies to identify patients most likely to benefit from Iso treatment.

In conclusion, the rising interest in natural products, especially TCM monomers, offers a promising avenue for novel anticancer drug development. Iso, with its multi-target antitumor potential, represents a compelling candidate. Nevertheless, comprehensive studies addressing its mechanisms, pharmacological properties, and clinical efficacy are essential. With continued research and technological advancements, Iso may eventually be translated into a safe and effective therapeutic agent for cancer patients.
