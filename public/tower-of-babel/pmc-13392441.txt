Tailoring polymers of intrinsic microporosity as photoredox catalysts for continuous-flow reaction–separation processes
Authors: Martin Gede, Gergo Ignacz, Catherine S. P. De Castro, Busra Dereli, Mariia Ferree, Frederic Laquai, Luigi Cavallo, Gyorgy Szekely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of sustainable, metal-free photocatalysts that combine homogeneous reactivity with effective recyclability remains a key challenge in photoredox catalysis. Here, we report a series of rationally designed polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs) tailored for visible light-driven photoredox reactions. Using a multidisciplinary approach by integrating in silico predictions, photophysical and materials characterization, and a series of benchmarking studies, we establish these PIMs as highly effective photocatalysts with broad applicability across diverse photoredox reactions, including Minisci reactions, aryl amination, trifluoromethylation, radical sulfonylation, and dual Ni-catalyzed C–N, C–C, and C–S couplings. We further evaluate multiple recycling strategies, including precipitation, heterogeneous coating, and homogeneous recovery by nanofiltration. The PIM catalysts exhibit near-complete retention in nanofiltration, enabling the development of a continuous-flow reaction–separation system with in-line catalyst recovery and real-time process monitoring. This work establishes a platform for tunable, scalable, and recyclable photocatalysis and provides a blueprint for sustainable continuous-flow fine chemical manufacturing.

Introduction

Visible–light induced photoredox chemistry has recently seen great progress, leading to advancements in fine-chemical synthesis, in particular for natural products1–3. Catalysts comprising polypyridyl complexes of ruthenium and iridium dominate photochemical reactions due to their long excited-state lifetime, wide and adjustable redox windows, strong absorption in the visible-light region and stability in solution4. Despite these advantages, the scarcity and toxicity of transition metals render their use expensive and unsustainable5, which impedes their adoption in industrial-scale manufacturing, where catalyst cost and recovery are crucial6,7.

Extensive research efforts have been directed towards the exploration of metal-free photoredox organocatalysts, which in numerous instances demonstrated performance comparable to their transition metal-based counterparts8,9. However, their synthesis typically involves multiple reaction–separation steps, which result in high cost. Therefore, their reuse via catalyst recycling is highly desirable. The use of heterogeneous catalysts can generally simplify their separation and reaction workup processes10–13, however reduced light transmission often leads to reduced reaction efficiency and throughput14. Reaction efficiency can be considerably improved by employing microflow conditions due to the high surface area-to-volume ratio, which increases the irradiated surface area and mixing15. Furthermore, flow chemistry also offers improved control over the reaction parameters, allowing photoredox reactions to be reliably conducted at multikilogram scale for producing pharmaceutically relevant compounds16–18. Owing to these advantages, the development of recyclable homogeneous catalysts is of utmost importance.

Membrane-based technologies, such as organic solvent nanofiltration (OSN), offer a sustainable solution for homogeneous catalyst recovery in continuous-flow processes19. OSN is a pressure-driven separation technology that can distinguish solutes in the range of 100–2000 Da20 based on the physicochemical properties of the solutes. OSN can operate at ambient temperature, allowing the separation of heat-sensitive molecules without degradation, thereby making it an ideal choice for the fine-chemical and pharmaceutical industries21. Continuous catalyst recovery via OSN has been demonstrated in metathesis reactions22 and Heck coupling reactions23. A recent study demonstrated the batch-to-batch recovery of various photoredox catalysts, including ruthenium and iridium complexes, using dead-end filtration cells24. Noël et al. also achieved in-line recovery of tetrabutylammonium decatungstate photocatalyst using nanofiltration membranes25.

Photoredox catalysis is advantageous for late-stage functionalization of pharmaceuticals and natural products owing to its mild reaction conditions and excellent functional group tolerance26. However, separation efficiency depends on size and other physicochemical differences27, rendering the nanofiltration design challenging as pharmaceuticals and common photoredox catalysts are closely similar. To improve separation efficiency, enlargement of the catalyst is needed to increase the differences in physicochemical parameters between the catalysts and the rest of the reaction mixture. One possible approach is to form a macromolecular complex by attaching a ruthenium complex to a second-generation polyamidoamine dendrimer28; although this approach improves separation efficiency, it requires additional complex synthetic steps and reduces catalytic activity because of dendrimer degradation28.

An effective strategy to increase the molecular weight of a catalyst while preserving its activity is to chemically link photocatalytic units to form polymers29, where each unit serves as an independent catalytic site. Unlike previous studies that aimed to develop heterogeneous organic polymers12,30, this study aimed to develop photocatalytically active polymers that retain the solubility characteristics of the parent compounds to enable their use as homogeneous catalysts. Although increased molecular weight typically leads to reduced solubility, the rational selection of linker groups, such as porosity-promoting spirocyclic moieties, can improve the solubility of the polymer31. Contorted units are key elements of polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs). PIMs are characterized as rigid, linear, ladder-type polymers with low conformational flexibility, resulting in restricted interchain interactions32. The archetype of PIMs, PIM-1, is synthesized via the step-growth polymerization of spirocyclic bis-catechol (A1) and tetrafluoroteraphthalonitrile (TFTPN)33, which results in the formation of a push–pull chromophore system (Fig. 1b) commonly found in many cyanoarene-based photocatalysts2,34,35. To evaluate the catalytic performance of the PIM-based photocatalysts, we selected the Minisci alkylation as a pharmaceutically valuable reaction system37. Using redox-active esters as radical precursors requires photocatalysts with sufficiently high excited state oxidation potentials, while the strongly acidic conditions provide an additional test for catalyst stability38. Minisci reactions have also become major targets for continuous-flow photochemistry due to their relevance to pharmaceutical manufacturing39. Furthermore, Minisci alkylations are widely used for the late-stage functionalization of complex active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs)40. However, as mentioned earlier, the separation of complex molecules from standard, non-enlarged photocatalysts are challenging when using OSN41,42, therefore, we chose this transformation to leverage on the macromolecular nature of our catalysts.

In this work, we address the critical challenge of developing metal-free, recyclable, homogeneous photocatalysts suitable for continuous-flow processes with in-line catalyst recovery. We envisioned that polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs) could serve as an effective catalytic platform for this purpose. By combining rational monomer selection with control over the degree of polymerization, we sought to fine-tune both the electronic properties relevant for photoredox catalysis and the physicochemical characteristics required for membrane-based separations. Our multidisciplinary approach, as illustrated in Fig. 1a, integrates polymer chemistry, catalyst design, photophysical characterization, and process engineering to bridge the gap between molecular-level catalyst development and sustainable process implementation.

Results

Catalyst design, synthesis and properties

To develop redox-active polymers suitable for visible-light photoredox catalysis, we designed and synthesized a series of polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs) based on structurally diverse chromophore scaffolds. Structural diversity was introduced through the targeted synthesis of various monomers. While a range of SNAr-active monomers has been reported for PIMs, modifications to the bis-catechol units have primarily focused on structural variations to enhance material properties, rather than altering their electronic characteristics. In particular, the spirocyclic bis-catechol A1 (Fig. 1c) has not been previously modified, likely due to its high sensitivity, which limits the accessible chemical space and the diversity of polymer structures that can be obtained. To overcome this limitation, we developed a halogenation protocol using N-halosuccinimide reagents followed by a reduction step, affording the halogenated monomers A2 and A3 (Fig. 1c). These were polymerized to produce the halogenated polymers with the use of B1 monomer, resulting in PIM-Cl and PIM-Br polymers. Attempts to further diversify the A1 monomer structure through iodination, fluorination, and nitration were unsuccessful, likely due to steric hindrance and the oxidative sensitivity of the bis-catechol moiety, which also prevented the formation of aminoaryl derivatives (see Supplementary Section S19 for details). To further expand the catalyst structural variety, we employed a range of commercially available and custom-synthesized SNAr-active monomers (Fig. 1d). These included tetrafluoroterephthalonitrile (B1), tetrafluoropyridine (B2), alkyl-sulfone (B3), tetraoxidethianthrene (B4), phenazine-based tetrahalogenated monomers (B5), and an ABA-type spirocyclic monomer (B6). Their polymerization led to the formation of PIM-1, PIM-Py5k, PIM-Sulfon5k, TOTPIM5k, PIM-75k, and PIM-Cop5k oligomers (Fig. 1e).

As we aimed to synthesize macromolecular photocatalysts, we sought to investigate how increasing molecular weight influences the catalytic properties. Therefore, PIM-1 was synthesized with different molecular weights incorporating 5–20 repeating units, resulting in oligomers of PIM-12.5k, PIM-15k, PIM-110k, and the high-molecular-weight (HMW) PIM-1HMW polymer. Moreover, the corresponding mono-unit model compounds derived from the protected catechols A4, A5, and A6 were also synthesized to serve as experimental benchmarks against the oligomers. By varying the degree of polymerization and synthesizing different model compounds, we obtained a total of 20 different photocatalysts, allowing us to assess crucial aspects of polymer-based photoredox catalyst design and establish meaningful structure–property relationships.

The solubility of the PIM-1 series with different degrees of polymerization followed the expected trend: lower degree of polymerization resulted in better solubility, while increasing the molecular weight reduced the solubility (Fig. 1e, bar chart). For instance, PIM-1 oligomers and PIM-Py5k were readily soluble in N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc), tetrahydrofuran (THF), and chloroform; however, the tetrahalogenated catalysts exhibited considerably lower solubilities attributed to strong halogen–halogen interactions43. This solubility issue was mitigated via the copolymerization of the brominated A3 monomer with a spirocycle-incorporated monomer B6, resulting in the perfectly alternating copolymers PIM-Cop5k and PIM-CopHMW (Fig. 1e), which comprised two bromine substituents on each repeating unit. PIM-Sulfon5k and PIM-75k were moderately soluble in DMAc, while TOTPIM5k exhibited limited solubility in the same solvent. To assess the necessity of the contorted spirocyclic moiety, we synthesized the MBDTPN5k oligomer (Fig. 1e), comprising a simple methylene unit linker, and also prepared DCB, a structural analog of PIM-1 without its bulky aliphatic substituents. MBDTPN5k and DCB exhibited virtually no solubility in typical organic solvents, highlighting the necessity for the presence of spirocyclic- or similar porosity-promoting groups.

To complement experimental efforts and explore redox potential trends, we computationally evaluated the redox potentials and E0–0 transition energies of possible PIM polymer structures, using a model chromophore scaffold shown in Fig. 2a. By changing the respective R1 and R2 substituents as well as the electron-withdrawing acceptor units EWG1 and EWG2, we calculated the energetics of 16 different chromophores to identify the range of attainable redox potentials (SI Tables 1–3). Time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations revealed that excited-state oxidation potentials ranged from − 0.36 to − 2.48 V and excited-state reduction potentials ranged from + 1.07 to + 2.55 V. To test the accuracy of the TD-DFT prediction and its applicability to polymer structures, we determined the energy of the first singlet excited state, ground state-to-excited singlet state transition wavelength, and singlet–triplet energy gap (∆ES-T) from monomer to tetramer structures (Supplementary Fig. S2). The negligible differences in the predicted energies (∆ES-T = 47.0–48.0 kcal mol−1) and wavelengths (457.93–458.69 nm) indicated that the electronic structure remained consistent with increasing polymer size (SI Table 4). Molecular orbital analysis also confirmed a similar electronic structure with varying polymer size, wherein charge transfer occurred from the donor benzodioxin units to the nitrile acceptor (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S2). Notably, the computational insights guided our synthetic efforts. For instance, predictions of strong visible-light absorption and high reducing power encouraged the synthesis of A3 bis-sulfone containing polymers (SI Table 1). Conversely, polymer structures such as PIM-Br-Sulfon showed limited predicted absorption in the visible range; therefore, they were not synthesized. To experimentally validate the computationally predicted redox potentials, we measured cyclic voltammograms of eight representative oligomers (PIM-15k, PIM-Cl5k, PIM-Br5k, PIM-Py5k, PIM-Sulfon5k, PIM-75k, PIM-Cop5k, and TOTPIM5k). Due to limited solubility, PIM-Br5k and PIM-75k allowed only partial scans, while TOTPIM5k could not be measured due to solubility issues. The experimentally determined ground- and excited-state redox potentials are summarized in Fig. 2a, while complete datasets and voltammograms are provided in Supplementary Section S5. Overall, the experimentally measured potentials agreed well with the TD-DFT predictions; however, for a few structures, deviations of 0.4–0.6 V were observed, variations that fall well within the typical uncertainty of TD-DFT-based redox calculations. Using PIM-1 as the parent scaffold, introduction of chlorine substituents into the aromatic core increased excited-state oxidation potential by 0.19 V while leaving excited-state reduction potential unchanged. This widening of the redox window can be attributed to the dichotomous electronic character of chlorine and is analogous to the behavior observed for halogen-substituted isophthalonitrile acceptors in donor–acceptor photocatalysts reported earlier35. In contrast, bromination increased only the excited-state reduction potential, yielding a more strongly oxidizing photocatalyst without significantly modifying the excited-state oxidation potential. This is consistent with the stronger inductive effect of bromine and its reduced π-donor contribution compared to fluorine or chlorine43. Among the functionalized PIM derivatives, PIM-Sulfon5k, PIM-Py5k, and PIM-Cop5k exhibited similar excited-state oxidation potentials, with values ranging from − 1.44 to − 1.37 V. The phenazine-containing PIM-75k showed the weakest reducing capability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{{ox}}^{*}=\,-1.04{V}$$\end{document}Eox*=−1.04V). The spectroscopically-determined photophysical properties of the polymers were in good agreement with the TD-DFT calculations of the absorption and optical gap values (Supplementary Section S3). The obtained polymers exhibited absorption in the visible-light region (405–438 nm; Fig. 2c) with the exception of TOTPIM5k (322 nm). Increasing molecular weight exerted a negligible effect on the observed absorption and fluorescence emission wavelengths (SI Table 5). The introduction of halogens induced a hypsochromic (blue) shift in absorption and emission spectra, contributing to optical gap values larger than those of PIM-1 by 0.1 eV (SI Table 5). Time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy results showed that the fluorescence decay lifetimes of the PIM catalysts were in the range of 5.5 to 10.3 ns, with PIM-75k displaying the shortest lifetime at 2.75 ns. These lifetimes are sufficiently long to overcome the diffusion limitation in bimolecular reactions4. Further investigation of the PIM-15k oligomer by photoinduced absorption spectroscopy revealed no detectable triplet or long-lived species, and the oxygen-insensitive delayed fluorescence indicates that the photocatalyst most likely involves a singlet excited state instead of triplet (further discussion can be found in Supplementary Section S4.2). Overall, the photophysical characteristics of the polymers were deemed well-suited for application in visible-light-driven photoredox catalysis.

The molecular weights of the synthesized oligomers and polymers were estimated using ¹H NMR spectroscopy, in which the detection of distinct end-group nuclei was possible (Supplementary Fig. S65). In cases where end groups were not observable due to substitution or potential oxidation, MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry was employed as a complementary tool (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Section S23). The experimentally determined degrees of polymerization were in close agreement with the expected values. For high-molecular-weight samples, gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was used, and HMW polymers were obtained in the range of 23–59 kDa (Supplementary Section S22). Due to the reduced solubility of PIM-Cl and PIM-Br polymers, we also performed BET surface area analysis (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Section S21) to assess how halogenation affects intrinsic microporosity through the introduction of ortho substituents. The halogenated polymers retained a microporous structure, with surface areas of 500–600 m² g–1, slightly lower than that of the unsubstituted PIM-1 scaffold (730 m2 g– 1), but still within the microporous range as the pore size distribution analysis indicated (Supplementary Section S21 and Supplementary Fig. S32). To evaluate the thermal stability of the halogenated PIMs, we conducted thermogravimetric analysis coupled with infrared spectroscopy (TG-IR). All materials showed high thermal stability with decomposition onset above 450 °C (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Section S20). TG-IR indicated release of hydrochloric and hydrobromic acid as part of decomposition products, consistent with their structure. These results confirm the robustness of the polymer backbone and support the safe handling of the catalysts under conventional polymer processing conditions.

Catalytic performance evaluation

The catalytic performance was assessed using the Minisci reaction due to its valuable C–H functionalization of heteroarenes38 under flow and batch conditions (Fig. 3). Previously, the Minisci reaction has been investigated using different photoredox catalysts such as ruthenium44 and iridium45 complexes as well as 4CzIPN46 and carbon nitride47; therefore, this reaction serves as a valuable benchmark to compare PIM compounds with commercial catalysts. For the isoquinoline cyclohexylation model reaction, we used the N-acyloxyphthalimide-based redox-active ester 2a (Fig. 3a) as the radical source. Using the PIM-15k oligomer as the photocatalyst, the desired product 3a was obtained in 80% isolated yield (Fig. 3a, entry 1). Further control experiments indicated that catalyst concentration can be decreased to 1 mol.% (entry 2–4). The necessity of the photocatalyst, trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), and light irradiation for product formation was confirmed (entries 5–7), while the addition of the radical scavenger TEMPO completely prevented product formation (entry 8). The presence of oxygen or water had a negligible effect on the reaction yield (entry 9–10), which is crucial for process development and scale-up. We used a Box-Behnken design-of-experiment approach to show that the molar ratio of the TFA and redox-active ester 2a greatly influences the conversion, while the temperature dependence was negligible (Supplementary Section S8). Solvent screening revealed that DMAc provides optimal yield because of its ideal solubilization power for the oligomers; other polar aprotic solvents (DMF and THF) and greener alternatives (PolarClean and 2-MeTHF) were also effective (Fig. 3b). In addition, we performed a separate photostability test to compare the stability of the PIM-Cl5k oligomer with its corresponding mono-unit model compound under identical conditions and catalyst concentration. Moreover, the increased reduction potential of tetrahalogenated oligomers may promote solvent oxidation, in particular for DMAc, resulting in radical accumulation and reduced photostability. These findings emphasize the importance of fine-tuning redox potentials for long-term operation and selecting suitable solvents to maximize catalyst lifetime and turnover numbers in continuous photoredox processes.

Mechanistic investigations and synthetic applicability

To better understand the enhanced performance of the halogenated PIM catalysts, we investigated the Minisci reaction mechanism by performing DFT calculations and Stern–Volmer fluorescence quenching analysis. Although many factors, such as solubility, absorption, quantum yield, and excited-state lifetimes can influence catalytic performance4, it is essential to identify whether the reaction proceeds via an oxidative (OPC) or reductive photocatalytic cycle (RPC). The mechanism can be divided into three main parts: (i) the main photocatalytic cycle (OPC and RPC, Fig. 4a), (ii) the isoquinoline cycle (Fig. 4b) b), and (iii) the proposed off-cycle (Fig. 4c). Both OPC and RPC begin with photoexcitation of the ground-state catalyst [PIM-1] to its triplet excited state 3[PIM-1] via intersystem crossing. In the RPC, 3[PIM-1] is reduced by isoquinoline radical A (SET1), generating the product and the radical anion 2[PIM-1]•−. The cycle closes with electron transfer from 2[PIM-1]•− to redox-active ester 2a (SET2) regenerating [PIM-1] and forming the cyclohexyl radical B, phthalimide anion C, and CO₂. Alternatively, in the OPC, 3[PIM-1] directly reduces 2a (SET3), followed by oxidation of isoquinoline radical A (SET4) to regenerate the ground-state catalyst. While both pathways are thermodynamically feasible, DFT calculations, as computed based on the Marcus–Hush theory (Supplementary Section S11), indicate that the RPC is more favorable, with lower activation barriers for SET1 and SET2 (9.1 and 10.2 kcal mol–1, respectively). Cartesian coordinates of the optimized structures are provided in the Source Data file. This supports the experimentally observed improved performance of catalysts with higher excited-state reduction potential. After establishing the generality of the RAE scope, we next explored the functionalization of a range of electron-deficient heterocycles, including quinoline (5a, 61%), quinoxaline (5b, 41%), dichloropyridazine (5c, 53%), and benzothiazole (5d, 55%), as well as several pharmaceutically relevant APIs (Fig. 5b). The antiarrhythmic agent quinidine was successfully functionalized at the quinoline heterocycle core (5e; 83%), while the antifungal agent voriconazole was selectively derivatized on its pyrimidine core (5f; 42%). Adenosine was successfully functionalized in the absence of any protecting groups (5g; 28%), while the anticancer agent gefitinib also provided a good yield (5h; 58%). In several heterocycle cases (5b, 5c, 5d, 5f), incomplete conversion was observed with the complete consumption of the RAE 2a. However, spiking the reaction mixture with additional 2a did not improve the yield, suggesting that the reactions plateau at this stage, which has been previously reported as a limitation for Minisci reactions37. Overall, these results demonstrate that PIM oligomers serve as robust and versatile photocatalysts for late-stage, regioselective C–H functionalization of pharmaceutically relevant scaffolds under mild conditions.

To demonstrate the versatility and broad applicability of our PIM catalysts beyond the Minisci reaction, we investigated their performance across eight additional photoredox transformations. We selected three representative oligomers based on their distinct redox potentials: strongly reducing PIM-15k, strongly oxidizing PIM-Py5k, and PIM-Br5k with more balanced redox properties (Fig. 2b). These PIM oligomers proved effective across diverse reactions, including trifluoromethylation, radical sulfonylation, oxidative arene bromination, and dual nickel-catalyzed cross-couplings for C–N, C–C, and C–S bond formation (Fig. 6). In the nickel metallaphotoredox-catalyzed C–N coupling previously reported by the MacMillan group52, PIM-Py5k provided the aryl amine compound 8 in excellent yield (88%), comparable to the originally used iridium transition-metal complex (Fig. 6a). Trifluoromethylation of caffeine53 (9) was successfully accomplished in 67% yield (Fig. 6b), with PIM-Br5k providing superior results due to its higher reduction potential, facilitating effective reductive quenching of the Langlois reagent. Similarly, PIM-Br5k enabled C–S cross-coupling by generating thiyl radicals54 via reductive quenching with a comparable yield (78%) to Ir-based catalysts (Fig. 6c). Inspired by a previous report55, we explored the possibility of a competing Minisci-type reaction through the generation of α-amidoalkyl radicals from the DMAc solvent using PIM-Br5k catalyst. Under mild conditions and without the addition of peroxydes, the amidoalkylated isoquinoline derivative 16 was obtained in 91% yield (Fig. 6d). In addition, PIM-Br5k resulted in 35% yields in C(sp²)–C(sp³) coupling reactions using benzyl trifluoroborate salt (15) as radical precursor, following Molander’s protocol56 (Fig. 6e). Employing redox-active esters as radical sources, we successfully performed C–H amination of arenes by generating nitrogen-centered radicals57 (Fig. 6f). Here, PIM-15k and PIM-Py5k performed well due to their favorable excited-state oxidation potential, effectively reducing redox-active ester 19. Similarly, we performed radical sulfonylation58 using redox-active ester 2h, affording alkyl sulfon compound 12 in 57% yield (Fig. 6g), where PIM-Br5k provided the highest yield, most likely due to its efficient quenching with TFA. Finally, we successfully carried out the oxidative photocatalytic bromination of anisole, achieving moderate yields, highlighting the possibility of PIMs as alternatives to potent oxidizing Mes-Acr-Me59 or 3CzClIP35 photocatalysts. (Fig. 6h).

In the aforementioned reactions, the original catalysts were replaced by our PIM catalysts without prior reaction optimization, highlighting the versatility and ease of applicability of polymer-type photocalysts. These results demonstrate the applicability of PIMs as strong alternatives to transition-metal complexes. Moreover, the versatility of polymer synthesis through the strategic selection of monomers facilitates a toolbox-like approach to construct photocatalysts specifically tailored to the redox potentials required for targeted chemical transformations. By selecting monomers rationally and subsequently using the most suitable polymer catalyst, reaction performance can be considerably enhanced, thereby positively influencing yield and product throughput by increasing TON and turnover frequency values. This approach is not limited to PIM-based polymers but can also be applied to any polymer-based catalyst design.

Leveraging the polymer nature of PIMs and their robust, flexible film-forming capabilities33, we explored their application as heterogeneous photocatalysts. While the PIM-1HMW catalyst performed adequately as a stirred slurry in batch (SI Table 14), our objective was to adapt the polymer for use in a continuous-flow system. To achieve this, the photoactive polymer was coated onto glass beads, which were then conveniently packed into transparent FEP tubing as depicted in Fig. 7b. The coating was performed via rotary-assisted evaporation using a powder-drying flask to promote uniform deposition, and successful coating was confirmed by SEM imaging (Fig. 7b SEM image, Supplementary Section S14). Reactions were carried out in non-solvents for PIMs, such as ethanol and acetonitrile. However, over extended use, the polymer coating gradually dissolved from the glass bead surfaces. Despite the solvent classification as a non-solvent, UV/Vis analysis revealed a low but detectable solubility, with an absorbance of 0.06 at 438 nm, confirming catalyst leaching. Without covalent anchoring, long-term flow operation remains limited due to progressive catalyst loss.

To surpass the limitation of batch-to-batch recovery techniques, nanofiltration was used as a primary tool to enable the complete recovery of the catalysts in solution. The schematics of rejection measurements and permeance tests are shown in Fig. 7c. Rejection (Eq. 1) and selectivity (Eqs. 2,3) values were employed to evaluate the membrane performance across different solutes (Fig. 7e, SI Table 16). The PIM catalysts (PIM-15k, PIM-Br5k, PIM-Cl5k and PIM-Cop5k) achieved near-complete rejection, surpassing commercial catalysts and reaction components. Since PIM-Cop5k exhibits near-complete rejection, its rejection selectivity is more than twentyfold higher than that of Ru and Ir photoredox complexes, enabling more efficient catalyst–solute separations (Fig. 7e, inset). In contrast, commercial catalysts show considerable leaching into the permeate, leading to high operational costs and reduced purity of the products. The disparity in rejection rates suggests that PIM-based catalysts can be efficiently recovered in a single-stage process, while commercial catalysts may necessitate multistage cascade nanofiltration processes. This distinction can substantially reduce the capital expenditures, operational expenses, and system footprint, particularly given that PIMs can be synthesized at a relatively low cost (Supplementary Section S17). Consequently, the use of PIM-based photocatalysts can enable process intensification in continuous nanofiltration recycling systems.

The in-house manufactured crosslinked P84 polymer membrane demonstrated stability over 180 days of testing (Fig. 7f) with negligible changes in permeance and solute rejection values. The membrane maintained its stability in the polar aprotic solvent DMAc, which is considered a harsh environment for polymer membranes. A consistent permeance (Eq. 4) of 1.55–1.71 L m−2 h−1 bar−1 was maintained and rejection for 2a compound over a span of 180 days, including 22 separate solute rejection experiments. As long-term membrane stability rarely reported in the literature, this rejection experiment highlights the robustness of this crosslinked membrane.

Integration of PIMs into continuous-flow separation processes

Integration of homogeneous photocatalysts into continuous-flow reaction–separation systems remains a major challenge in sustainable chemical manufacturing, particularly when catalyst recovery and long-term operation are required. To demonstrate the practical utility of our PIM-based catalysts in a process-relevant environment, we established a continuous-flow photoredox reaction platform coupled with in-line catalyst recovery via organic solvent nanofiltration (OSN). For this purpose, PIM-Cop5k was selected as the model catalyst for continuous-flow–nanofiltration experiments due to its excellent solubility (Fig. 1e), catalytic activity (Fig. 3e), and high rejection rate (Fig. 7e).

The continuous-flow reactor was coupled with a single-stage nanofiltration unit, as shown in Fig. 8a, enabling the continuous withdrawal of the product through the permeate stream while directly recycling the retentate stream — containing the catalyst — back to the beginning of the reaction. By employing this ‘closed-loop’ system from the catalyst’s perspective, new reagents can be fed into the system while continuously collecting the product from the permeate stream. We identified the catalyst concentration as a critical process parameter to ensure high conversion and to monitor the separation performance within the membrane unit. Therefore, in-line catalyst monitoring in the retentate stream was implemented as a process analytical technology tool (Fig. 8a). Over a 48-hour continuous experiment, the pressure was gradually increased from 1.8 to 2.5 bar to maintain a constant permeate flowrate of 0.7 mL min⁻¹ (Supplementary Fig. S23), compensating for concentration polarization effects. A stage cut of 0.7 (Eq. 5) was selected based on the solubility limit of the PIM-Cop5k catalyst: turbidity was observed above ~ 16 mM concentration, while the presence of other reactants can further reduce solubility. Operating the nanofiltration unit at a 0.7 stage cut can keep the concentration of the catalyst in the retentate at 8.3 mM, which is well below its solubility threshold, while still enabling efficient product recovery. During operation, the reaction conversion gradually declined to 80% after 20 h (Fig. 8b), accompanied by a decrease in catalyst concentration from 5 mol% to 2.5%, as measured by in-line UV/vis spectroscopy. This catalyst loss is attributed to either photocatalyst degradation or its deposition on the membrane surface. To investigate these phenomena, a separate long-term membrane stability test was conducted under similar conditions (Supplementary Section S16), including the presence of reactive intermediates and strong acidic media. The results show that the crosslinked P84 membrane continues to retain the PIM-Cop5k catalyst with high rejection (Supplementary Fig. S22), however a substantial amount of catalyst deposition can occur on the membrane surface. This indicates that implementing higher shear conditions, such as by adding a gear pump into the nanofiltration loop or applying membrane surface modifications, would be beneficial to minimize deposition and prevent catalyst loss from the retentate stream. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first report of a large-scale Minisci reaction incorporating in-line catalyst recycling techniques.

Inspired by the partial solubility of the high-molecular-weight polymer in ethanol, we explored replacing DMAc with ethanol as a more sustainable solvent for the Minisci reaction. To enhance catalyst solubility, we selected PIM-12.5k, an oligomer with the lowest degree of polymerization, achieving a saturated solution with a concentration of 0.01 mM (0.046 mg mL–1) in ethanol. Notably, this low concentration was sufficient to deliver a high yield in the initial reaction run. However, the limited solubility of the redox-active ester and phthalimide in ethanol prevented the use of in-line recovery techniques at this stage, as precipitation would likely cause membrane fouling and clogging in the flow system. As an alternative, diafiltration was employed to selectively remove the product and organic solutes into the permeate while retaining the PIM-12.5k catalyst in the retentate. Reaction progress was monitored using benchtop NMR to precisely determine the endpoint and avoid unnecessary over-irradiation, as presented in Fig. 8c. The Minisci model reaction proceeded efficiently at 0.09 mol% catalyst loading, reaching full conversion within 60 min (Fig. 8f). The concentration and decomposition of the redox-active ester could be also monitored in real-time, allowing reagent replenishment if needed (Fig. 8e, f). Due to spectral overlap with ethanol and other aliphatic species, however, product quantification by NMR was not feasible. Diafiltration was carried out by continuously feeding ethanol into the system while collecting the permeate.

To track membrane performance and potential fouling, the permeate was collected into a vial on an analytical balance connected to a computer, enabling real-time flux monitoring. The permeate flux (Eq. 6) remained stable over 8 h (as shown in Fig. 8d), indicating robust membrane performance. After six diafiltration volumes, approximately 75% of the product was removed from the feed solution (Fig. 8d). The retained catalyst was then reused in subsequent reaction cycles. In later cycles, reaction times were extended to over 16 h (Fig. 8g), likely due to gradual catalyst loss through photodegradation or catalyst adsorption on the membrane surface. Nevertheless, a turnover number (TON) of 3093 was achieved, demonstrating the viability of this strategy. Importantly, the use of ethanol as a solvent further contributes to the overall sustainability of the process.

These nanofiltration findings underscore the recyclability of the PIM oligomers and pave the way for the development of more efficient polymer-based photocatalysts without transition metals. The high rejection rates and permeance enable process intensification in fine-chemical manufacturing. The integration of nanofiltration-based catalyst recovery with continuous-flow photoredox reactors and separation modules allows for considerable process intensification compared to the traditional batch or semi-continuous systems. Moreover, we anticipate a rise in the industrial applications of nanofiltration coupled with reaction processes for catalyst recovery, marking a great advancement in the field. Our methodology contributes to sustainable manufacturing from several perspectives: (i) development of transition-metal–free organocatalyst, (ii) employment of green solvents, (iii) use of photochemistry under mild conditions, and (iv) energy-efficient membrane separation. The sustainable aspects of this work are exemplified by the fact that 19 out of the 24 principles of Green Chemistry60 and Green Engineering61 have been addressed (Supplementary Fig. S16).

Discussion

We have systematically explored and demonstrated polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs) as a versatile and tunable platform for metal-free photoredox catalysis, enabling their integration into continuous-flow reaction–separation processes with in-line catalyst recovery. We demonstrated that PIM-based photocatalysts can be confidently applied across a wide range of photoredox transformations, including C–H functionalizations, late-stage modification of complex drug molecules, dual/Ni-catalyzed C–N, C–C, and C–S couplings, radical sulfonylation, oxidative bromination, and trifluoromethylation. Through the strategic selection of monomers, a broad range of redox potentials can be accessed, highlighting the versatility of polymer-based photocatalyst synthesis. Importantly, the introduction of halogenated PIMs expanded the accessible redox window, enabling the development of more potent oxidizing catalysts. Furthermore, by precisely adjusting the degree of polymerization, we tailored the solubility and physicochemical properties of the catalysts, enabling their optimization for specific applications in homogeneous or heterogeneous catalysis. Critically, we demonstrated that PIM-based catalysts can be synthesized with molecular weights that allow complete rejection in organic solvent nanofiltration (OSN), facilitating highly efficient catalyst recovery in continuous-flow systems. This tailored design enabled the integration of PIM oligomers into in-line catalyst recycling system, achieving high turnover numbers, paving the way for sustainable process intensification in flow photochemistry.

For the broader scientific community, our findings offer a practical and accessible approach to design robust, inexpensive, and recyclable organic photocatalysts. By adjusting the degree of polymerization, catalysts can be readily adapted for use in both homogeneous flow reactions and heterogeneous conditions. These results highlight the potential of PIM-based photocatalysts for sustainable fine-chemical manufacturing, where their high rejection rates, tunable redox properties, and compatibility with green solvents enable process intensification. The integration of nanofiltration-based catalyst recovery with continuous-flow photoredox reactors represents an important advancement in reaction engineering and catalyst design, offering a blueprint for the broader adoption of catalyst recovery technologies in industrial photochemistry.

Computational details

Full-geometry optimizations were performed at the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) hybrid PBE0 functional62 in Gaussian 0963. Numerical integrations were performed with an ultrafine grid. For geometry optimizations, the Def2-SVP64 basis set was used for all atoms. Optimizations were performed in the gas phase, while Geometry optimizations of the photocatalyst (PC) and isoquinoline cycles were carried out, including solvation effects and Becke–Johnson D3 dispersion corrections. The natures of all stationary points were determined by the calculation of the analytical vibrational frequencies. These were also used to compute the molecular partition functions (298 K, 1 atm) with the conventional particle-in-a-box, rigid-rotator, quantum-mechanical harmonic oscillator approximation, except that all vibrational frequencies below 50 cm−1 were replaced with values of 50 cm−1 (the quasi-harmonic-oscillator approximation). Single-point energy calculations were performed at M0665 level of theory using def2-TZVPP64 and SMD66 solvation model with solvent parameters of DMAc (ε = 37.8). The ΔG value was obtained by augmenting the electronic energies at M06(SMD-DMAc)/Def2-TZVPP with the respective free energy corrections at the PBE0/Def2-SVP level. TD-DFT calculations were performed at the at M06(SMD-DMAc)/Def2-TZVPP level of theory. Redox potentials were calculated with respect to a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) in DMF (ε = 36.71), which has a dielectric constant similar to that of DMAc (ε = 37.78) used in the experiments. Accordingly, the absolute potential of SCE in DMAc was taken as 4.38 V67. The free energy barrier of the single electron transfer process was estimated based on the Marcus–Hush theory using the protocol as described in our previous work68.

General procedure for the synthesis of oligomers/polymers

A high-temperature69 or low-temperature70 polymerization procedure was followed according to reported protocols with slight modifications.

For the high-temperature method, a two-necked Schlenk flask was equipped with a magnetic stir bar, a gas inlet valve, and a Dean–Stark apparatus. The system was evacuated and nitrogen backfilled thrice. Under constant nitrogen flow, the flask was charged with the corresponding bis-catechol and SNAr active monomers in accordance with the stoichiometric ratio according to the Carothers equation to achieve controlled molecular weight polymerizations. The targeted degree of polymerization, in most cases, was set to 10, which corresponds to a monomer ratio of 0.8181. The SNAr active monomer served in all cases as the limiting reagent. Anhydrous DMAc and toluene were added to the mixture in a volume ratio of 4:3. The solution was heated to 135 °C followed by the addition of finely ground K2CO3 (5.0 eq.) in one portion. The temperature was then raised to 155 °C in an elevated nitrogen flow to facilitate the removal of water. Toluene was added to the mixture as necessary to compensate for the evaporative loss. After vigorously stirring for 15 min, the reaction mixture was poured into water, and hydrochloric acid was added to the mixture to achieve pH 2. The precipitated solids were filtered by vacuum filtration, washed with additional water, and dried in a vacuum for 12 h at 120 °C. Further purification of the polymers was achieved by dissolution in THF solvent followed by reprecipitation into methanol.

For the low-temperature polymerization method, a two-necked flask was equipped with a magnetic stir bar and a gas inlet valve. The system was evacuated and nitrogen backfilled thrice; then, under constant nitrogen flow, it was charged with the corresponding monomers and finely ground K2CO3 (2.1 eq.). Anhydrous DMF was added to the mixture to achieve a solid content of 5 wt.%. The flask was then placed in a preheated oil bath at 70 °C and stirred for 72 h. The solution was then cooled to room temperature and poured into water; subsequently, the solid precipitates were collected, washed with additional water, and dried in vacuum for 12 h at 120 °C. The polymers were further purified by dissolution in THF solvent followed by reprecipitation into methanol.

Photocatalyst comparison study under flow and batch conditions

To compare the photocatalytic performance of each oligomer with different properties and different degrees of polymerization with commercially available photocatalysts, we adopted a rigorous testing method to ensure consistent reaction conditions across all catalysts. To avoid plateauing conversions and ensure comparable results, a residence time of 5 min and a reaction time of 120 min reaction time were selected for flow conditions in batch experiments. Each catalyst and reagent were prepared in anhydrous DMAc, and reaction mixtures were made by combining the solutions to afford a 50 mM concentration of isoquinoline (1), with 10 mol% photocatalyst, 1.5 eq. redox-active ester 2a, and 2.0 eq. TFA. The combined reaction mixture was sparged with argon for 5 min. The solution was then divided into four portions for use in batch and flow experiments, both of which were run in duplicates. The molar mass of the polymer catalysts was determined according to the mass of the repeating unit (Supplementary Fig. S1).

In the case of batch reactions, 1 mL of the stock solution was taken in a 10 mm glass vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar and closed with a septum. The vial was placed on a preheated heating block at 40 °C, and the solution was stirred at 150 rpm in all cases. The vials were irradiated for 2 h with blue light using a Kessil light source from a distance of 75 mm; the sample for HPLC–MS was taken immediately thereafter.

For flow reactions, a Vapourtec flow reactor with a blue LED light source was used. The reactor comprised FEP tubing (outer diameter 1/16″, IDEX Health & Science, USA) with a reactor volume of 10 mL and a 1 mL injection loop. The reaction mixture (750 µL) was injected into the injection loop by separating, leaving approximately 200 µL nitrogen before and after the reaction aliquot. After the injection, the DMAc solvent was used to push the reaction mixture through the reactor by utilizing a syringe pump with a flowrate of 2 mL min–1, resulting in a residence time of 5 min. To ensure reproducibility and sampling from the steady-state reaction volume, the first 200 µL of the reaction mixture was discarded, and only the central segment of the reaction mixture was sampled for HPLC–MS measurements. After each sample collection, the sample was immediately analyzed by using external calibration, as mentioned before.

To assess the potential cross-contamination of the reactor tubing with Ru or Ir, we analyzed a reaction mixture containing the PIM-Br5k catalyst by inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectrometry (ICP–OES). The results confirmed the absence of Ru and Ir contaminants (Supplementary Section S9).

Catalyst recycling via non-solvent induced precipitation

To explore the potential recyclability of the oligomer catalyst through nonsolvent-induced precipitation, a four-step catalyst recycling process was implemented: the process comprised the Minisci model reaction, workup of the reaction mixture, catalyst separation, and catalyst drying. Initially, a 46.5 mL 100 mM solution of the reaction mixture was prepared in anhydrous DMAc; the mixture was composed of 1.0 eq. isoquinoline, 1.25 eq. of the redox-active ester (2a), 2 eq. of TFA, and 5 mol% of PIM-Cop5k catalyst. The reaction mixture was sparged with argon for 5 min and pumped through a Vapourtec flow reactor equipped with blue LED lights and 1/16″ FEP tubing. The residence time was 20 min, and the reactor was maintained at 40 °C. The reaction mixture was collected, and an HPLC sample was taken to determine the conversion and yield. The mixture was then poured into a dilute solution of NaHCO3 to remove DMAc solvent and TFA; the organic products were then extracted with heptane. Since the yellow catalyst precipitated near the interface in the aqueous phase, the medium was filtered through a fritted glass filter by vacuum filtration. The oligomer was then washed consecutively with distilled water, heptane, and methanol; this series of washing was performed thrice. The collected solid was then dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h before the next experimental run. Because of the loss of catalyst mass in the filtration step, the reaction volume was subsequently lowered to maintain the same reaction concentrations.

Continuous Minisci reaction with in-situ catalyst recycling

To assess the feasibility of in-situ catalyst recycling in the Minisci model reaction, a photoreactor setup combined with an OSN unit was used. The configuration comprised a reagent feed tank, reaction feed tank, and buffer tank, all maintained under a nitrogen atmosphere. The photoreactor was a Vapourtec UV-150 flow photoreactor equipped with three V-3 peristaltic pumps and blue LED lights. The reactor contained 1/16″ diameter fluorinated ethylene–propylene (FEP) tubing, and its internal volume was 15 mL. Residence time was 15 min and the reactor was maintained at 40 °C. The outlet of the reactor was connected to the buffer tank, which was directed into the membrane cell. The membrane cell had an internal volume of 71 mL and was equipped with a magnetic stir bar (150 rpm), and the heating plate was set to 30 °C to mitigate membrane fouling and reduce the concentration polarization effect. The membrane was a crosslinked P84 polyimide membrane as described previously. The retentate was connected to a Zaiput BPR-10 (Zaiput, USA) variable back pressure regulator; pressure adjustments were made by the direct connection with the pressure regulator connected with a nitrogen gas cylinder (AirLiquid, Saudi Arabia). The retentate stream was passed through a flow-through cuvette that monitored the catalyst concentration in the stream. For this purpose, an AvaSpec-ULS2048XL-EVO spectrometer (Avantes Technologies, The Netherlands) was used with an AvaLight-DHc light source using 400 µm fiber optic cables. Concentration calibration of the catalyst was taken at 462 nm at concentrations of 1.25, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 12.5 mM in DMAc. The spectrometer was connected to a computer and enabled continuous monitoring of the catalyst concentration in the retentate stream via absorbance measurements at regular intervals of 60 s. The retentate stream was directed back to the reaction feed tank, where it was mixed with fresh reagent solution from the reagent feed tank. Simultaneously, the permeate stream was collected in a separate bottle.

The reaction conversion and relative yield, as well as the relative concentration of the permeate stream, were determined by HPLC–MS by taking samples from the buffer tank and permeate stream by applying external calibration. The flux was determined by measuring the amount of permeate collected within a given time.

Initially, the system was filled with a solution of the catalyst in anhydrous 2.5 mM DMAc, contributing to a 5 mol.% concentration relative to the isoquinoline starting material. The reaction feed tank and buffer tank contained 5 mL each of this solution; thus, the total system volume was 0.1 L. This total volume comprised the volume of the membrane cell (71 mL), 15 mL reactor volume, 5 mL for each reagent and the buffer tank, and an additional 4 mL for tubing connections.

To initiate the process, the HPLC pump started transferring the solution from the buffer tank to the membrane cell. Once the retentate began flowing into the reaction feed tank, pump II was started. This pump delivered the reaction mixture through the photoreactor into the buffer tank with a flowrate of 1 mL min–1. Simultaneously, pump I was also initiated, and it delivered the solution from the reagent feed to the reactor feed tank. The flow rate of pump I (0.5–0.75 mL min–1) was adjusted in accordance with the amount of permeate required to maintain the complete reaction volume of the system. Pump II provided a flowrate volume identical to that of the HPLC pump connected to the membrane cell to maintain consistent levels in each tank.

Upon reaching the end of the reaction, the system was in a closed-loop setup to ensure complete conversion of the starting material. After reaching the desired conversion value, the system was washed with pure DMAc solution to eliminate and collect the organic solutes from the system into the permeate stream. The total TON was calculated by measuring the concentration of the product in the collected permeate and dividing it by the initial molar quantity of the catalyst within the entire reactor–membrane cell system volume (0.25 mmol).

Continuous extraction of the permeate stream

A continuous extraction experiment was conducted to extract the product from the permeate stream by removing the DMAc solvent, TFA, and phthalimide from the mixture, using a NaOH solution (0.2 M) as a quencher and heptane as an extractant. The collected permeate was pumped into a quad mixer using a PFA tubing (1/16″ diameter) with a peristaltic pump. This permeate was mixed with the quenching solution and heptane. The combined mixture was directed into a tubular static mixer (Vapourtec, 20 mL internal volume with 3.2 mm inner diameter), followed by a Zaiput SEP-10 liquid–liquid phase separator unit equipped with an OB-2000 membrane. The flowrates of the permeate stream, sodium hydroxide solution, and heptane were 0.5, 1.5, and 2 mL min–1, respectively, resulting in a combined flowrate of 4 mL min–1.

Flow photoreaction–diafiltration setup

A reaction mixture was prepared containing isoquinoline (1, 8.0 mmol), redox-active ester 2a (1.25 eq., 10.0 mmol), TFA (2.0 eq., 16 mmol), and PIM-12.5k catalyst (0.09 mol%) in ethanol (0.1 M). The mixture was transferred into a glass vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar, sealed with a septum, and sonicated for 10 min, followed by sparging with nitrogen for an additional 10 min. A Vapourtec UV-150 photoreactor equipped with blue LED light (450 nm) and FEP tubing (with 5 mL reactor volume) was first flushed with ethanol and subsequently purged with nitrogen. The outlet of the photoreactor was connected to a benchtop NMR instrument (Magritek Spinsolve 60, Germany) equipped with a glass flow-through cell for inline reaction monitoring. The reaction mixture was introduced into the system using a Vapourtec V-3 peristaltic pump at a flowrate of 800 µL min–1. Once the system was fully filled and operated in a closed-loop configuration, the NMR acquisition sequence was initiated simultaneously with the light irradiation. Conversion of isoquinoline was monitored continuously until complete consumption of the starting material was observed. At this point, the reaction was stopped, and the entire reaction mixture was collected into the glass vial. The system was subsequently washed with 16 mL of pure ethanol, and this wash was combined with the collected reaction mixture. The crude reaction mixture was then subjected to diafiltration.

The diafiltration system consisted of an HPLC pump (Knauer S100, Germany) connected to a custom-built membrane cell (Voratus Inc, Canada) equipped with a Duramem 500 nanofiltration membrane (effective membrane area: 6.56 cm2) and an adjustable back-pressure regulator (Swagelok, United States). The retentate outlet of the membrane cell was directed back into the feed vial, providing a closed-loop recirculation, while the permeate stream was collected into a separate vial placed on an analytical balance connected to a PC for continuous permeate flow monitoring. This setup enabled real-time adjustment of solvent addition without requiring manual permeate quantification. Pure ethanol was introduced into the feed vial using an SF-10 pump (Vapourtec, UK) at a variable flowrate adjusted to match the permeate flow reading, thereby maintaining a constant feed volume throughout the diafiltration process. The HPLC pump was operated at a flowrate of 5 mL min–1 and the pressure was set to 30 bar. After approximately eight diafiltration volumes, the collected permeate was evaporated under reduced pressure, and the crude product was purified according to the general workup procedure described previously. Recovery of the purified retentate containing the polymer photocatalyst was achieved by stopping the HPLC pump and switching the inlet line to pure ethanol. An additional 20 mL of ethanol was circulated through the system and collected into the feed vial. The obtained solution was concentrated under reduced pressure, charged with stoichiometric quantities of reagents and the mixture was re-dissolved with 8 mL of ethanol to initiate the next reaction-diafiltration cycle under identical conditions.
