Patterns of Health Care Spending in Prostate Cancer
Authors: Jiaye Shen, Aaron J. Katz, Yahui Xie, Ronald C. Chen, Mutlay Sayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow did spending for prostate cancer–related health care change from 2012 to 2021, and which components of care accounted for these changes?FindingsIn this population-level economic evaluation using Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results–Medicare data, prostate cancer–related expenditures increased from $504 million in 2012 to $1.9 billion in 2021. Systemic therapy became the dominant contributor to spending, increasing from $142 million of total expenditures in 2012 (28%) to $1.4 billion of total expenditures in 2021 (74%), driven largely by second-generation androgen receptor–targeted therapies.MeaningProstate cancer spending has shifted substantially toward systemic therapy, highlighting the growing importance of treatment duration and advanced disease management in discussions of health care sustainability.

Introduction

Prostate cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignant diseases in the US1 and a growing contributor to national health care expenditures.2,3 Advances in screening, imaging, and treatment have improved outcomes across the disease spectrum but have also increased the complexity and cost of care. Understanding how health care spending for prostate cancer has evolved over time, and which components are driving these changes, is critical to informing both clinical decision-making and health policy.

Historically, prostate cancer care costs have been dominated by local therapies, including surgery and radiotherapy, and diagnostic and surveillance services.3,4 Prior studies have examined several aspects of spending for prostate cancer–related health care, including the excess costs associated with progression to metastatic disease,5 costs and utilization of definitive local treatment modalities,6 and changing costs of active surveillance compared with radiotherapy.7 During the past decade, however, the therapeutic landscape has shifted with the adoption of systemic agents, particularly second-generation androgen receptor–targeted therapies, used in metastatic disease8,9,10 and now being evaluated in randomized clinical trials in earlier disease states.11,12,13 Although existing studies have provided important insights into specific disease states, specific phases of care, payer populations, and individual treatment modalities, less is known about how the overall composition of prostate cancer–related spending has changed over time across major domains of care. As treatment paradigms have evolved, it remains unclear how they have reshaped prostate cancer spending.

To address this gap, we analyzed prostate cancer–related health care expenditures using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)–Medicare linked database. Our objective was to characterize spending trends and examine how the composition of prostate cancer care has changed.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective, population-level economic evaluation of prostate cancer treatment from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2021, using the SEER-Medicare linked database. We followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) reporting guideline. The study was deemed exempt from review and informed consent by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Kansas because it did not involve human participants.

Data Accrual

We integrated data from the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review database for inpatient stays, the Outpatient file for outpatient services, the National Claims History for physician and supplier services, and the Part D Event file for prescription drug costs. We restricted the cohort to patients with prostate adenocarcinoma based on SEER histology codes. Only claims with a primary diagnosis of prostate cancer were included. Expenditure analyses were restricted to services paid under traditional fee for service (FFS). We identified relevant expenditures by applying a coding hierarchy, including International Classification of Diseases diagnostic and procedure codes, Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), and Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS), to isolate specific treatment, diagnostic, and monitoring services (eTable in Supplement 1). To ensure yearly comparability, all costs were adjusted to 2017 US dollars.

Expenditures were analyzed using a 2-tiered approach to identify the drivers of health care spending. First, we categorized annual costs into 5 major modalities: radiation, surgery, cryotherapy and high-intensity focused ultrasonography, systemic therapy, and diagnostic and monitoring services. Second, to further investigate the growth in pharmaceutical spending, we divided systemic therapy into first-generation androgen deprivation therapies (ADTs), second-generation ADTs, and other therapies.

Systemic therapy was identified using National Drug Codes in the Part D Event file and HCPCS or CPT codes in the Outpatient and Carrier National Claims History files. First-generation ADT included conventional therapy identified either through Part D drug claims or through outpatient and/or physician claims for injectable hormonal agents, gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists or antagonists, and surgical castration. Specifically, first-generation ADT included goserelin, histrelin, leuprolide, triptorelin, degarelix, relugolix, first-generation antiandrogens, and orchiectomy. Injectable or procedure-based ADT was captured using HCPCS and/or CPT codes for gonadotropin-releasing hormone agents and orchiectomy, while oral agents were captured from Part D claims using National Drug Codes. Second-generation ADT included abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide, identified from Part D claims using National Drug Codes.

To provide clinical and demographic context for the claims-based expenditure analyses, we constructed a descriptive patient-level cohort of beneficiaries who were enrolled in traditional FFS Medicare during the month of prostate cancer diagnosis. Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and by diagnosis period, including age, race obtained from the SEER database (categorized as Black, White, and other [including American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Pacific Islander, and unknown race] or unknown), marital status, dual-eligibility status, residence, geographic region, clinical T stage, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level at diagnosis, Gleason score, metastatic status at diagnosis, and duration of observable follow-up while alive and enrolled in FFS Medicare. Race data were collected to describe the demographic composition of the study population and to assess whether the racial distribution of beneficiaries differed across diagnosis periods. This descriptive cohort differs from the denominator used for the aggregate expenditure analyses because the sample was defined at the patient level at the time of diagnosis (Table), whereas the expenditure analyses were defined at the calendar-year claims level. Annual expenditure estimates included prostate cancer–related FFS Medicare claims observed within each calendar year, regardless of whether the beneficiary was diagnosed in that year or in a prior year. Because Medicare enrollment status may change over time, patients enrolled in FFS Medicare at diagnosis may later transition to Medicare Advantage and stop contributing observable FFS claims, while patients not enrolled in FFS Medicare at diagnosis may later enter FFS Medicare and contribute claims to the annual expenditure estimates. Therefore, the Table is intended to provide descriptive clinical context and should not be interpreted as the denominator for the annual aggregate expenditure analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed in January 2026. Patient characteristics were summarized using means and SDs for age, medians and IQRs for duration of observable follow-up, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Differences in categorical characteristics across diagnosis periods were assessed using χ2 tests. Annual expenditures were summarized descriptively as aggregate spending and as the percentage attributable to each treatment category. All costs were inflation adjusted to 2017 US dollars using service-specific price indices from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.14 No inferential testing was performed for temporal expenditure trends. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide, version 8.3, 64-bit (SAS Institute Inc). Two-sided P < .05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

In the descriptive cohort enrolled in FFS Medicare at diagnosis, 1 231 567 beneficiaries with prostate cancer were identified, including 756 951 diagnosed in 2012 to 2014, 205 433 in 2015 to 2017, and 269 183 in 2018 to 2021. The mean (SD) age at diagnosis (73.2 [7.7] years overall) decreased modestly over time, from 73.7 (7.6) years in 2012 to 2014 to 72.3 (7.7) years in 2018 to 2021. The median observable follow-up while patients were alive and enrolled in FFS Medicare was 88 (IQR, 38-150) months overall and was shorter in more recent diagnosis periods, as expected. In terms of race, 123 922 patients (10.1%) were Black, 1 063 885 (86.4%) were White, and 43 760 (3.6%) were of other or unknown race. Most beneficiaries resided in metropolitan areas. Clinical information was incomplete for a substantial proportion of patients, including metastatic status at diagnosis, which was unknown for 573 787 patients (46.6%) overall. Among all patients in the descriptive cohort, documented metastatic disease at diagnosis was uncommon and remained relatively stable over time, increasing from 29 952 of 756 951 (4.0%) in 2012 to 2014 to 13 897 of 269 183 (5.2%) in 2018 to 2021.

Analysis of aggregate health care spending from 2012 to 2021 demonstrated a substantial shift in the cost structure of prostate cancer care. Total expenditures increased from $504 million in 2012 to $796 million in 2016 and $1.9 billion in 2021; however, this growth was driven primarily by compositional changes rather than uniform expansion across treatment modalities. The proportional contribution of traditional curative interventions declined over time (Figure 1). Radiotherapy accounted for $227 million (45.0%) in 2012, $180 million (22.6%) in 2016, and $228 million (12.0%) in 2021, reflecting relatively stable absolute expenditures but a declining share of total spending. Similarly, surgery accounted for $53 million (10.5%) in 2012, $52 million (6.5%) in 2016, and $67 million (3.5%) in 2021. In contrast, systemic therapy emerged as the dominant cost driver, increasing from $142 million (28.1%) in 2012 to $468 million (58.8%) in 2016 and $1.4 billion (74.0%) in 2021. Diagnostic and monitoring services accounted for $100 million (19.8%) in 2012, $108 million (13.6%) in 2016, and $219 million (11.4%) in 2021. Spending on cryotherapy and high-intensity focused ultrasonography remained minimal throughout the study period.

We further disaggregated systemic therapy expenditures into first-generation ADTs, second-generation ADTs, and other systemic agents (Figure 2). Spending on first-generation ADTs increased modestly from $57 million in 2012 to $72 million in 2016 and $105 million in 2021. In contrast, expenditure for second-generation ADTs rose from $46 million in 2012 to $335 million in 2016 and $1.2 billion in 2021. By 2016, second-generation ADTs accounted for 71.7% of systemic therapy spending and 42.1% of total aggregate prostate cancer–related expenditures. By 2021, second-generation ADTs accounted for 86.6% of systemic therapy spending and 64.0% of total aggregate prostate cancer–related expenditures.

Discussion

In this economic analysis, we observed substantial growth in prostate cancer–related health care expenditures during the past decade and a pronounced shift in the composition of spending across treatment domains. While total expenditures increased over time, this growth was largely attributable to rising spending on systemic therapy rather than uniform increases across all modalities. In particular, expenditures associated with second-generation ADTs increased markedly and emerged as the dominant component of prostate cancer–related spending by 2021. In contrast, spending on traditional curative-intent interventions, including surgery and radiotherapy, remained relatively stable in absolute terms and declined in proportional contribution. Together, these findings demonstrate a reorganization of prostate cancer–related health care spending.

These changes in spending composition likely reflect evolving treatment paradigms, particularly in advanced disease where management has increasingly relied on systemic therapies administered over prolonged periods rather than finite curative-intent local treatments.15 Second-generation ADTs are now central to the treatment of metastatic disease and account for a substantial proportion of systemic therapy use.16 As a result, metastatic disease care may disproportionately shape overall health care expenditures despite representing a minority of prostate cancer diagnoses. Use of these agents in earlier disease states could further increase their impact on spending.

Longer survival among patients with advanced prostate cancer may also contribute to the observed increase in systemic therapy spending. As treatment outcomes improve, patients may remain alive and receive systemic therapy for longer periods, increasing cumulative expenditures even if the proportion of patients presenting with metastatic disease does not change substantially. In the descriptive patient-level cohort, documented metastatic disease at diagnosis was uncommon and relatively stable over time, increasing from 4.0% in 2012 to 2014 to 5.2% in 2018 to 2021; however, metastatic status was unknown for a substantial proportion of patients. Therefore, these data provide clinical context but do not allow us to determine whether improved survival, longer treatment duration, delayed progression from localized to advanced disease, or changes in stage distribution directly explained the observed spending trends.

The increase in systemic therapy spending should also be interpreted in the context of improvements in outcomes for patients with advanced prostate cancer during the same period. During the study period, randomized clinical trials demonstrated that treatment intensification with docetaxel and androgen receptor pathway inhibitors improved clinically meaningful outcomes, including overall survival and progression-related end points, in metastatic prostate cancer.9,10,17,18,19 Thus, the observed increase in systemic therapy spending likely reflects, at least in part, adoption of therapies associated with meaningful patient benefit. However, the current analysis was not designed to estimate the incremental value or cost-effectiveness of these therapies because aggregate expenditure data were not linked to patient-level survival, metastasis-free survival, quality-adjusted survival, or treatment-specific outcomes.

These findings also have important implications for early detection and curative-intent strategies in prostate cancer. Definitive management of localized disease is typically delivered over a finite period, whereas advanced disease often involves prolonged or continuous systemic therapy with cumulative resource utilization.16 Although we did not assess screening practices or stage at diagnosis, the observed differences in treatment duration and care intensity suggest that earlier diagnosis and successful definitive intervention could meaningfully affect long-term health care utilization, particularly by reducing reliance on prolonged systemic therapy.

These findings also help place ongoing discussions about the cost of prostate cancer care into a broader perspective. Much of the existing literature has focused on the costs of individual treatments or technologies considered in isolation.20,21 However, our results suggest that treatment use over time may matter more for overall health care spending than the cost of individual treatments alone. In particular, changes in treatment duration, sequencing, and intensity across disease states may influence population-level health care expenditures more than 1-time, modality-specific costs. Although radiotherapy expenditures increased modestly in absolute terms, the aggregate claims-based design did not allow us to determine whether this change was driven by modality, fractionation, reimbursement, or radiotherapy use across localized, salvage, metastatic, or palliative settings. Understanding prostate cancer–related spending therefore requires considering how care is delivered over disease course.

Second-generation ADTs are typically administered continuously until progression, toxic effects, or discontinuation, unlike most curative-intent local therapies, which have fixed durations.22 As these agents are increasingly used across a broader range of disease states, their prolonged duration of use represents an important factor in understanding patterns of prostate cancer–related health care spending.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, although more recent data would provide additional insight into evolving spending patterns, SEER-Medicare data linked through 2024 were not available for this analysis; therefore, the study reflects the most recent complete SEER-Medicare data available for this project, covering expenditures through 2021. Our analysis was limited to FFS Medicare beneficiaries and may not apply to younger patients or those enrolled in different plans. We were unable to link health care expenditures to specific disease states, treatment lines, or clinical outcomes, limiting conclusions about the drivers of spending. In addition, because the expenditure analyses were based on annual claims rather than complete longitudinal patient-level cost trajectories, the descriptive cohort in the Table could not be used to perform reliable stage-stratified spending analyses or to compare expenditure changes between early- and late-stage disease. Interpretation was further limited by incomplete metastatic status at diagnosis in the descriptive cohort. Similarly, although geographic region was added to the Table for descriptive context, the aggregate claims-based design did not support reliable region-stratified expenditure comparisons. Claims-based data lacks detailed clinical information, such as treatment response, which may influence patterns of care.

Conclusions

In this economic evaluation, spending on prostate cancer–related health care increased substantially during the past decade, with spending shifting markedly across treatment domains. Systemic therapy has emerged as an increasingly prominent component of overall spending, reflecting broader changes in prostate cancer management. As treatment paradigms continue to evolve, understanding care delivery across the disease course will be important for informing future clinical, research, and policy discussions related to the sustainability of prostate cancer care.
