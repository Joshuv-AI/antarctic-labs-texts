Removal of Methylene Blue dye from aqueous solution using Fenton-modified submerged aquatic plant Schoenoplectus species (Bulrush)
Authors: Nozipho Nompumelelo Mzinyane
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhen dumping coloured effluent into the ecosystem, methylene blue dye contributes significantly to environmental health problems. In this investigation, Schoenoplectus species, commonly known as Bulrush, underwent modification using a Fenton reagent to enhance its effectiveness in adsorbing methylene blue (MB) from effluent. The Fenton-treated Bulrush was analysed using SEM, FTIR, BET, and TGA techniques, while various operational parameters were systematically explored to optimise the adsorbent. The FTIR results revealed an increase in the intensity of chemical compounds (–OH, C=C, and C–O, etc.). The FTIR data showed an increase in the intensity of chemical compounds (-OH, C=C, and C-O, among others) and aromatic structures on the exterior of the adsorbent, which aided in MB removal. The results exhibited conformity with the Langmuir model, with an R2 value of 0.9904 with an optimal adsorption capacity of 41.23 mg/g achieved for the Fenton-treated Bulrush. Kinetic analysis indicated adherence to the pseudo-second-order model (R2 = 1), with chemical bonding adsorption primarily governing the adsorption rate. Optimal adsorption conditions were determined to be 26 °C, pH 8, a reaction time of 60 min, a dosing amount of 2 g, and a preliminary concentration of 100 mg/L. Enhanced pH and increased dosing of Fenton-treated Bulrush led to improved adsorption capacity. Crystal structural characteristics and surface functional groupings of Fenton-treated Bulrush emerged as crucial factors influencing adsorption, describing the three main processes that control MB absorption by the adsorbent: physical adsorption, electrostatic attraction, and π-π interaction. These processes demonstrated the economical, efficient, and environmentally friendly attributes of Fenton-treated Bulrush, which shows promise for eliminating dye contaminants from aquatic environments.Keywords: Adsorption, Methylene blue, Aquatic plant, Fenton reagent, Isotherms, Kinetics

Introduction

The manufacture and utilisation of artificial dyes are experiencing significant growth due to increasing need, particularly in the textile and apparel industries. Several dye compounds are produced in massive quantities worldwide, exceeding several thousand tons annually [1]. There are over 10,000 commercially accessible dyes, contributing to an estimated annual production of over 7 million tonnes [2]. About 15 % of dyestuffs are lost during production and processing. The release of dyes from textile industries poses a significant environmental issue, mainly due to the toxicity of some of these substances [3].

Consequently, discharging coloured wastewater into the ecosystem significantly contributes to eutrophication, aesthetic pollution, and disturbances to aquatic life, leading to severe health and environmental risks [4]. The environmental dyes' thermal and photostabilities cause sunlight to be absorbed and reflected, slowing photosynthesis. As a result, the natural food chain's flow is disrupted when plants at the bottom of lakes, rivers, streams, and ponds cannot absorb enough light [[5], [6]].

Dye molecules display considerable structural diversity and can be categorised in two ways: (1) according to their chemical compositions or uses, particularly for fibres, and (2) based on their solubility in water. Water dissolves basic dyes and carries a positive charge in solution, making them electrostatically attracted to negatively charged substrates. Catalytic dyes can have their positive charge localised on an ammonium group or dispersed over the dye cation's structure [3]. One such dye is Methylene Blue (MB) (Fig. 1), an aromatic heterocyclic base with a molar mass of 319.85 g/mol. MB is a widely recognised cationic thiazine dye with the chemical formula C₁₆H₁₈N₃ClS and a maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) of 665 nm. Its excellent water solubility allows for forming a firm aqueous solution at ambient temperature. Thus, Methylene Blue (MB) is used in various fields, including chemistry, biology, medical science, and dyeing industries [7,8]. It is extensively used in industrial applications and often found in coloured effluents. The complex structure of MB and its chemical stability result in prolonged degradation. Prolonged exposure to methylene blue (MB) dye can result in negative consequences such as nausea, vomiting, anaemia, and high blood pressure [9].

Methylene blue's three-dimensional molecular structure [10].

Therefore, to prevent environmental damage, these pollutants' inevitable effects must be treated before being released into the environment [4]. Membrane filtration, ozonation, advanced oxidation, coagulation/flocculation, adsorption and biosorption, and liquid-liquid extraction are just a few of the many physical, chemical, and biological treatment methods invented. Adsorption has been acknowledged as an economical, straightforward, and successful method for eliminating colours from wastewater. Due to its significant adsorption capability, activated carbon has been tried with various adsorbents. However, the operating cost of activated carbon adsorption is excessive and challenging to regenerate.

However, because they are widely accessible, natural adsorbents such as rice hull ash, sugarcane bagasse, sawdust, pine needle, eucalyptus bark, prawn shell activated carbon, and powdered mango seed kernels stand out as the finest substitutes [5,7,9,11,12]. These materials are considered sustainable because they are derived from renewable agricultural waste biomass [11]. As demonstrated by Keskinkan et al. [13], there is increasing interest in employing aquatic plants for wastewater treatment; heavy metals were extracted from effluent using an immersed floating plant (Myriophyllum spicatum). The growing interest in aquatic plants is driven by their crucial role in cleaning water in rivers through a process known as phytoremediation. This process involves the extraction of heavy metals and presents an opportunity to explore their effectiveness in removing dyes from water streams. These aquatic plants, such as Schoenoplectus species, thrive in sunlit marshes, open areas, and along the shores of lakes and ponds, adapting well to water depths of up to 1 m. Moreover, their resilience to fire makes them well-suited for phytoremediation projects aimed at purifying contaminated water or soil. This is due to their adequate capacity to absorb and accumulate heavy metals and other pollutants [14].

This investigation aims to determine whether employing bulrush plants is feasible as a foundation for developing a cost-effective, specially-made adsorbent to remove methylene blue dye from water solutions. Pre-treating the bulrush powder and using Fenton oxidation to oxidise the organic components in carbonaceous materials were necessary to accomplish this purpose. First, ferrous (Fe2+) ions and hydrogen peroxide molecules react to produce ferric (Fe3+) ions and primary hydroxyl radicals. A secondary cellulose radical is then created when the original hydroxyl radicals remove a hydrogen atom from cellulose [15,16].

The use of modified Bulrush to remove methylene blue in wastewater has not been previously reported, highlighting the novelty of this work. Therefore, this research aims to investigate the feasibility of using Bulrush treated with Fenton reagent to extract methylene blue dye from wastewater. Mass, concentration, temperature, and pH are the experimental characteristics that will be changed during the investigation.

Materials and methods

Reagents

All the chemicals were of analytical reagent grade, and no further purification was necessary. Ultra-pure water from a Siemens Labostar water filter (Germany) was used to create aqueous solutions. The Bulrush plants were gathered in Alberton, Gauteng province, South Africa, from Kliprivier. Bulrush stems were meticulously cleaned and dried in an electric thermostatic oven at 80 ֯C for 48 h. Subsequently, the Bulrush was grounded into a fine particle and sieved. Regarding the analysis, powder particles with a diameter between 45 and 90 mm were utilised.

Characterisation of materials

The alterations in functional groups resulting from the chemical derivatisation of Bulrush biomass were determined using a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectra 400 spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) within the 650 to 4000 cm−1 range. The PerkinElmer STA 6000, a simultaneous thermal analyser that can perform both DTA and TGA measurements simultaneously, was used to assess sample weight reduction as a function of temperature. A Carl Zeiss Sigma (Germany) scanning electron microscope (SEM) fitted with a tungsten filament light source was used to analyse the surface morphology and composition of untreated and enhanced Bulrush samples at different magnifications. An EDX analyser was connected to a microscope, and each sample was coated with gold before analysis. Nitrogen adsorption at 77 K was used to measure the surface area, pore volume, and pore size using a Micromeritics Tristar 3000 analyser (Australia) connected to a VacPrep 061 degassing machine.

Preparation of the adsorbent

Fenton treatment

An advanced oxidation process (AOP) known as the Fenton reagent produces potent oxidising agents called hydroxyl radicals (•OH), which can remove organic contaminants, lignin, and polyphenols. This Fenton reagent treatment has proven successful in treating other biomasses, such as pine cone powder [17], Macadamia nutshell powder [18], and coffee pulping [19].

The optimised technique of Ofomaja et al. [20] was used to prepare treated Bulrush. Using this method, 20 g of Bulrush powder was added to a 500 ml volumetric flask that contained 250 ml of various ratios of Fenton's reagent mixture Fe2+/H2O2 (Fe2+ ranging from 50 to 5000 mg/dm3 and H2O2 fixed at 100,000 mg/dm3). An optimum Fe2⁺/H2O2 ratio of 1:50 was chosen to maximise plant component leaching while minimising bulk density, oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), and H⁺ concentration. The solution was swirled at 200 rpm at ambient temperature. The modified Bulrush powder was filtered, cleaned, and dried at 90 ֯C.

Batch adsorption studies of MB dye

Two grams of methylene blue dye were dissolved in 1 L of purified water to create a stock solution that contained 100 mg/L of the dye. A typical 100 mg/L solution was used to assess the effects of agitation duration at 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120-min intervals. After that, 2 g of the material was measured and placed in 100 ml sample bottles with caps. The sample vials were filled with 100 ml of methylene blue dye solution and shaken at 200 rpm to equilibrate it. The materials were then filtered after being centrifuged for 5 min at 6000 rpm. The resulting filtrate was utilised for UV–Vis examination of methylene blue dye.

Mass (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 g), concentration (25, 35, 50, 75, and 100 mg/L), and temperature (26, 35, 45, and 50 ˯C) were all evaluated using a similar experimental protocol, which included 2 h of swirling at 200 rpm. 100 mL of the methylene blue solution was added to each of the five distinct containers to assess the impact of pH. After measuring each solution's starting pH, HCl (0.1 M) or NaOH (0.1 M) were used to regulate the pH to 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5.5, 6.5, 7.5, 8.5, and 10. The amount of adsorbed methylene blue dye was measured using the adsorption capacity (qe) given in mg/g. Equations (1), (2), where (Co) and (Ce) represent the initial and final concentrations of pollutants in the solution (mg/L), respectively, were used to compute the removal percentage (% R). Milliliters (ml) were used to measure the solution's volume (v), and grams (g) were used to measure the adsorbent's mass (m).

Regeneration studies

Regeneration studies were conducted in 100 ml capped sample bottles. The solution containing adsorbents with adsorbed methylene blue (MB) was filtered, washed, dried, and sequentially regenerated using HCl (0.1 M) NaOH (0.1 M), and water (H2O), with shaking at 25 °C for 4 h during the regeneration process. Subsequently, the regenerated absorbents were reintroduced into the flask for adsorption, and this adsorption–regeneration cycle was repeated three times. These cycles yielded data for calculating the methylene blue (MB) removal rate.

Results and discussion

Adsorbent characterisation

Analysis of Fourier Transform Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was conducted better to understand the present functional groups within the lignocellulose matrix. Two samples, untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush, were selected for analysis before adsorption (Fig. 2(a)) and after adsorption (Fig. 2(b)).

FTIR spectra of untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush (a) before and (b) after adsorption.

Fig. 2(a) displays the FTIR spectrum of untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush before adsorption. Fig. 2(a) reveals that the untreated Bulrush exhibits multiple functional groups on its surface, characteristic of lignocellulosic materials. Prominent peaks indicative of cellulose were observed at 3333.7 and 2916 cm−1 connected to the stretching vibration of –O–H groups, indicating the hydrogen bond stretching vibration of α-cellulose, along with the C–H stretching vibration originating from CH and CH2 of the cellulose component [17].

Peaks at approximately 1726 and 1628 cm−1 signify the presence of the C=O bond within carboxylic acid, involving intermolecular hydrogen bonding and the vibration of C=C in the aromatic skeletal mode of lignin. Meanwhile, the peak at 1599 cm−1 represents aromatic C=C stretching vibrations of aromatic rings of lignin [23]. The 1030 to 515 cm−1 peaks can be attributed to stretching –C–C– and –CN, respectively [[24], [25], [26]].

When subjected to Fenton's reagent, the Bulrush exhibited minimal changes in its FTIR spectrum, suggesting that the treatment did not substantially change the Bulrush's general structure. However, it did bring about modifications in specific functional groups. Notably, there was an increase in intensity and a shift in the peak associated with the stretching vibration of cellulose, changing from 3334 to 3339 cm−1. The decrease in α-cellulose was attributed to extracting plant components, such as resin acids, during Fenton's oxidation [27]. A reduction in intensity and a shift in peak related to C–H stretching vibration from 2916 to 2912 cm−1 were observed. This reduction indicates that the bonds between cellulose and lignin have been broken, resin acids have been destroyed by oxidation, and alkyl waxes have been oxidised and extracted from the Bulrush surface [17]. In the cleavage process, carboxylic acid and aldehyde groups form, which explains the shift of carbonyl (C=O) peak intensity from 1726 to 1731 cm−1. Additionally, there was an increase in the intensity of the peak characteristic of C=C from 1599 to 1628 cm−1 in the aromatic skeletal mode of lignin.

Fig. 2(b) displays the FTIR spectrum of untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush after adsorption. While similar peaks are noticeable in this spectrum, a comparison with Fig. 2(a) reveals a reduction in peak size. This decrease could be attributed to methylene blue dye, potentially absorbed by the material's pores [26]. Wang et al. [28] identified the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups as the major functional groups responsible for methylene blue (MB) adsorption. When the N+ atom in the MB molecule binds to the oxygen atom in the cellulose molecule, several electrostatic interactions occur, particularly in acidic and basic environments [3]. Furthermore, MB adsorbs at 1603 cm−1 for the N+ cation and 1424 cm−1 for the -CH3 symmetric bent, according to Can-Terzi et al. [26].

Proposed mechanism

Fenton oxidation was employed to pretreatment Bulrush powder, targeting the oxidation of organic constituents within carbonaceous materials. This process involves the formation of hydroxyl radicals, which is assisted by the reaction between hydrogen peroxide molecules and ferrous (Fe2+) ions, creating ferric (Fe3+) ions and primary hydroxyl radicals. Subsequently, these primary hydroxyl radicals extract hydrogen atoms from cellulose, yielding secondary cellulose radicals [16,29].

Scheme 2 illustrates the adsorption mechanism of methylene blue dye onto Fenton-modified Bulrush, showcasing physical adsorption. As depicted in the SEM image in Fig. 6, visible deposits on the surface of the Fenton-treated Bulrush indicate the adsorption process. This implies that the adsorbent eliminated MB via pore filling and surface adsorption, which led to the development of deposits. Furthermore, infrared data in Fig. 2 verified the presence of -OH and C=O functional groups in Fenton-treated Bulrush; this would facilitate the complexation of the Bulrush's surface-active groups with positively charged MB dye molecules. Moreover, the adsorbent's surface functional groups (–OH, –COOH, etc.) undergo deprotonation, enhancing electrostatic attraction to MB. The image obtained from EDX analysis (Fig. 5, Fig. 6) revealed the traces of Si–O–Si groups. Wang et al. [28] found that the presence of Si-O-Si in alkaline solution enhanced MB adsorption, indicating an n-π interaction between the aromatic structure of MB and Si-O-Si on the adsorbent. The adsorption mechanism in the reaction system may be aided by the hydrogen bonds formed between free H+ on the Fenton-treated Bulrush surface and the primarily non-electrostatic behaviour of the oxygen and nitrogen in MB.

Thermal analysis

Fig. 3 displays the thermal stability of both (a) Untreated Bulrush and (b) Fenton-treated Bulrush before adsorption. In this study, the materials were heated under controlled conditions in a nitrogen environment between 25 and 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C per minute. The TGA thermogram for both untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush revealed a degradation occurring in three stages (Fig. 3(a)). The first stage exhibited a mass change of approximately 5%–10 %, occurring around 100 °C, typically attributed to the evaporation of surface-adsorbed water [30]. After moisture loss below 100 °C, the untreated Bulrush underwent thermal disintegration in three significant steps, while the Fenton-treated Bulrush showed a two-step degradation process. During the rapid stage, starting around 200 °C–344 °C, hemicellulose degradation initiates, leading to noticeable shoulders observed in both untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush (Fig. 3(a)). These shoulders signify the decomposition of hemicellulose [16].

(a)TGA of Untreated Bulrush and Fenton treated Bulrush (b) DTA of untreated Bulrush and Fenton treated Bulrush before adsorption.

The ultimate peak, on the other hand, denotes the unpredictability breaking of the cellulose's glycosidic bond [16]. Despite the thermal stability of hemicellulose resulting from its chemically random and amorphous structure, which makes it easily hydrolysable, as evidenced by a higher mass change of approximately 56 % in untreated Bulrush and 58 % in Fenton-treated Bulrush between 195 and 348 °C. During the primary degradation of cellulose, at around 319 °C for untreated Bulrush and 318 °C for Fenton-treated Bulrush, a distinct peak is observed, corresponding to the maximum rate of decomposition (Fig. 3(b)). In contrast, cellulose molecules, composed of extended polymers of glucose units, exhibit heightened thermal stability due to crystalline regions within the biomaterial [31]. Following the carbohydrate decomposition, the final section signals the onset of lignin decomposition. Lignin exhibits excellent thermal stability and decomposes more gradually, usually between 250 and 500 °C, which leads to a relatively small reduction in weight [31,32]. This results in a mass change of approximately 16 % for untreated Bulrush and 29 % for Fenton-treated Bulrush. Findings imply that the Fenton-treated Bulrush is more thermally stable than untreated Bulrush, as evidenced by both the residue percentage and the weight change, as well as the fact that it begins to break down at higher temperatures and retains more weight at those temperatures. However, the residual weight observed during the third stage for the untreated Bulrush could be attributed to char or other by-products resulting from breakdown reactions [31].

Additionally, as represented in Fig. 3, Fig. 4 of the DTA (Differential Thermal Analysis), the peaks representing the highest degradation temperatures of untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush at 319 °C, and 317 °C were observed for both materials respectively, this suggests that the treated materials demonstrated increased thermal stability. This enhanced stability could be attributed to the Fenton treatment's capability to eliminate organic compounds more effectively from the carbonised material. These findings signify that Fenton treatment enhanced the thermal endurance of Fenton-treated Bulrush [33].

In contrast, the significant mass loss represented the decomposition of organic matter such as hemicellulose. Untreated Bulrush may occur at a higher temperature than Fenton-treated Bulrush due to the presence of untreated fibres. The final mass loss could be due to carbonisation or complete decomposition.

SEM and EDX analysis

Fig. 5, Fig. 6 show the surface morphology of untreated Bulrush and Fenton reagent Bulrush before and after adsorption, as analysed and compared utilising SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy). In Fig. 5 (a), it is evident that untreated Bulrush exhibits a non-uniform shape with an uneven surface, likely due to the presence of pectin and fatty substances such as suberin and wax. The Fenton chemical treatment smooths the surface of the Bulrush particles, exposing many microscopic pores. The emergence of these tiny pores is likely a result of the breakdown and removal of the waxy materials that initially covered the surface of the Bulrush particles. The EDX analysis also verified that the Fenton treatment led to improvement, as evidenced by increased oxygen content. After adsorption (Fig. 5(a) shows untreated Bulrush and (b) shows Fenton-treated Bulrush), MB dye particles filled gaps, and the surface of the adsorbent became saturated and covered with dye molecules.

Fig. 6(c) depicts methylene blue (MB) adhesion on the uppermost layer of untreated Bulrush, a more distinct accumulation of the blue dye on the material's surface. This suggests that untreated Bulrush has a moderate capacity to adsorb the dye, although the adsorption sites on its surface are not as efficient as those of the treated samples. Fig. 6(b) shows Fenton-treated Bulrush after MB adsorption, likely revealing a more even and thorough dye distribution across the surface. This indicates that the Fenton treatment enhances the adsorption capacity of the Bulrush, likely due to increased surface area, porosity, or the introduction of functional groups that increase the binding effectiveness of methylene blue.

Furthermore, the Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) results can corroborate the observations from the methylene blue adsorption images. For the untreated Bulrush (Fig. 6(c)), the EDX data might show limited or lower levels of nitrogen (N) or sulphur (S), which are associated with the methylene blue dye. This would suggest that the dye would adsorb less on the untreated surface.

In contrast, for the Fenton-treated Bulrush (Fig. 6(b)), the EDX would likely show higher concentrations of nitrogen or sulphur on the surface, confirming that more methylene blue was adsorbed. The Fenton process introduces hydroxyl radicals, which increase the surface functional groups and improve the adsorption capacity, and the EDX results should reflect these changes in surface chemistry.

BET analysis

The isotherms of both biomasses can be broadly categorised as Type IV and V, accompanied by the H1 loop, following the IUPAC classification, as shown in Fig. 7(a) and (b) before and after adsorption.

Nitrogen adsorption isotherm of untreated Bulrush and Fenton treated Bulrush (a) before and (b) after adsorption.

This hysteresis type indicates the presence of well-defined pores. N2 adsorption on these materials was detected only at an extremely low relative pressure (P/P°), suggesting a prevailing mesoporous nature with multilayer adsorption, followed by capillary condensation. A slight deviation was observed at P/P◦ higher than 0.95 [34]. At higher pressures, the adsorbed amount increases sharply, primarily due to capillary condensation occurring in mesopores. Furthermore, the untreated Bulrush has a large surface area (1,3418 m2/g) and the number of pores (4914 × 10⁻³ cm³/g) (Table 1). However, the Fenton-treated Bulrush shows a higher surface area (2347 m2/g) and pore volume (6.52 × 10⁻³ cm³/g), suggesting an increase in the mesoporous structure compared to the untreated sample. Similar findings were observed in Kupeta's research [35]. This might be due to oxidation, which caused extractives (such as resin acids and waxes) to leak and some linkages within the material matrix to break.

Additionally, Table 1 indicates that untreated Bulrush, after methylene blue dye adsorption, demonstrates an increased surface area (5945 m2/g) compared to its untreated state, suggesting an enhancement of the porous structure. This increase in the surface area after the adsorption process could imply that adsorption exposed or activated pores by unclogging the pore tunnels that were previously closed or blocked, leading to the observed increase in surface area during BET analysis. However, the Bulrush treated with Fenton exhibits a significantly reduced surface area (0.849 m2/g) and pore volume (4.14 × 10⁻³ cm³/g). This change may be attributed to the clogging of the pore tunnel by reaction by-products, reducing the accessible surface area. This change leads to a Type V isotherm with an H1 hysteresis loop, likely resulting from the saturation of pores on the Fenton-treated Bulrush with MB dye during the adsorption reaction. Due to this saturation, there are fewer mesopores and micropores, which increases the average pore size. The variations in surface area, pore volume, pore size, and the observed isotherm types and hysteresis loops provide useful information about the structural changes caused by Fenton treatment on Bulrush. The data suggests a notable impact on the porous characteristics of the material because of the treatment [36,37].

Adsorption studies

Effect of time on MB dye adsorption

The efficiency of Bulrush without treatment and Bulrush treated with Fenton in adsorbing methylene blue dye was evaluated at various time intervals ranging from 30 to 120 min. Fig. 8 illustrates the influence of agitation duration and preliminary concentration (100 mg/L) on the adsorption of MB dye onto untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush at a temperature of 26 °C. The dye adsorption process proceeded quickly between 30 and 60 min, with the MB dye's adsorption capacity reaching about 40 mg/g for the Fenton-treated adsorbent and 30 mg/g for the untreated Bulrush. However, beyond 60 min, the adsorption rate began to decelerate as the adsorbent surface saturated with MB, ultimately reaching equilibrium after 60 min. This occurrence was due to the abundance of adsorption sites, pores, and functional groups on the adsorbents' surfaces, which resulted in swift adsorption. Nevertheless, in the case of the Fenton-treated Bulrush, the decline in adsorption capacity noted after 90 min could be accounted for by the availability of sites with the progression of contact time [37,38]. The agitation time for both adsorbents was 60 min to achieve the maximum adsorption capacity of MB.

Effects of agitation time on MB removal from aqueous solution (initial concentration: 100 mg/L, volume: 50 ml, and temp.: 26 °C).

Effect of mass on MB dye adsorption

The effect of mass on both adsorbents (untreated and Fenton-treated Bulrush) was investigated, as illustrated in Fig. 9. According to the data, both materials demonstrated the highest adsorption capacity of approximately 48 mg/g when the mass of Fenton-treated Bulrush was 2.0 g. This is well boosted by increasing the number of active sites on the surface of adsorbents, with the Fenton-treated Bulrush showing superior results. Subsequent increases in the absorbent quantity resulted in a plateau, suggesting that both materials were reaching equilibrium. Further increase of Fenton-treated Bulrush quantity became futile, as all active sites appeared saturated with methylene blue dye, leaving no additional active sites available [30]. 2.0 g/L was the ideal dosage for both adsorbents to maximise MB's adsorption ability.

Effect of dye solution pH

The ionic species of the adsorbent and adsorbate are affected by the pH of the solution. A comprehensive investigation into the pH of the solution was conducted within the pH between of 2–10, and Fig. 10 displays the results. According to Oyewo et al. [39], there are two types of methylene blue (MB): zwitterion species, which is found in alkaline environments, and the positive-charged form, which is common in acidic environments. This observation is consistent with the morphology of Bulrush, which contains a variety of functional groups such as amino, hydroxyl, and carbonyl groups (as seen in FTIR (Fig. 2 and Scheme 1, Scheme 2), and the pH of the solution affects these groups [40]. Fig. 10 represents the results from pH control studies on the MB solution, revealing a gradual increase in adsorption capacity for dye removal for both untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush in the alkaline region. This could be attributed to the methylene blue zwitterion species, typically existing within this range [39]. This implies that the dominant charges on the Bulrush are positive at acidic pH levels. Consequently, the primary mechanism driving methylene blue (MB) adsorption with adsorbents is associated with electrostatic attraction, as indicated by Yagub et al. [40] and Ofomaja et al. [5]. A pH of 8 was used for both adsorbents to optimise MB's adsorption capability.

Effect of temperature on MB dye adsorption

Experiments were carried out at temperatures of 26, 35, 45, 50, 60 °C and pH = 10 to examine the impact of temperature on the adsorption of methylene blue (MB) dye by both untreated and treated Bulrush, as illustrated in Fig. 11. As noted, the equilibrium adsorption capacity of methylene blue onto both untreated and Fenton-treated Bulrush was initially higher at 26 °C. However, increasing the temperature decreased the adsorption capacity of both adsorbents. This indicates that the adsorption of methylene blue and both untreated and Fenton-treated Bulrush was exothermic since a temperature increase led to a decrease in surface activity. Consequently, the lower temperature favours biosorption [40,41].

The results showed that the equilibrium adsorption capacity of untreated Bulrush was 18 mg/g at 100 mg/L when methylene blue dye concentrations ranged from 25 to 100 mg/L, while Fenton-treated Bulrush had a capacity of 21 mg/g. More interaction between adsorbent sites and methylene blue is the reason for the rise in total methylene blue accumulation with an elevated starting concentration [42]. Therefore, an optimisation concentration of 100 mg/L was selected.

Adsorption isotherms

This research utilised two well-known adsorption isotherm models, the Langmuir and Freundlich equations, to determine the equilibrium adsorption isotherms, as shown in Table 2. This is essential for measuring the adsorption capacity of methylene blue by the untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush under investigation. The quantity of dye absorbed in untreated Bulrush per mass unit was 0,5814 mg/g; for Fenton-treated Bulrush, it was 0,6874 mg/g. The Fenton-treated Bulrush exhibited a slightly higher qm, indicating better adsorption capacity. The Langmuir constant (kL), connected to the adsorption energy, showed similar values: 41.234 L/mg for untreated Bulrush and 41.233 for Fenton-treated Bulrush. This suggests similar adsorption energies for both biomasses. The dimensionless quantity, known as the separation factor (RL), can be used to define the Langmuir model (Equation (4)). The Irreversible (RL = 0), favourable (0 < RL < 1), linear (RL = 1), and unfavourable (RL > 1) isotherm types are indicated by RL values [43]. However, in this study, both cases suggest favourable adsorption, with Fenton-treated Bulrush being more favourable. According to Langmuir's adsorption theory, monolayer adsorption occurs on a uniform surface where the adsorbed dye molecules do not interact [44]. The R2 value indicates how well the experimental data fits the Langmuir isotherm model, where values close to 1 suggest a good fit, and in this case, both values indicate a suitable for the Langmuir model (Table 2).

Adsorption kinetics

The pseudo-first-order model does not fit the experimental data for untreated Bulrush well, according to Table 3's results (low R2 value of 0.1789). However, it fits much better for Fenton-treated Bulrush (high R2 value of 0.9257). In a related study, Derakhshan et al. [46] reported similar findings when investigating methylene blue dye adsorption using modified pumice stone; their results deviated from the pseudo-first-order model. The rate constant (k1) is significantly higher for Fenton-treated Bulrush. In contrast, the pseudo-second-order fit well for untreated and Fenton-treated Bulrush, with R2 values equal to 1. The rate constant (k2) is higher for untreated Bulrush, indicating a faster reaction without the Fenton treatment. These results suggest that the Fenton treatment enhances the adsorption capacity and reaction rate, particularly under pseudo-first-order kinetics. The pseudo-second-order model suggests a good fit for both cases, but the rate constant differs between untreated and Fenton-treated Bulrush. This implies that the surface adsorption process, particularly chemisorption, is the rate-limiting step in the pseudo-second-order model. Through physical and chemical properties interactions between the two phases, methylene blue dye is removed from a solution through chemisorption [47].

Reusability test

The desorption test is a crucial component of the adsorption method, as it indicates the potential renewal of the adsorbent and the feasibility of its utilisation. It serves as a significant parameter confirming interactions between adsorbate and adsorbent.

The regeneration experiments for untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush were carried out through three consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles, employing three eluting agents: 0.1 M HCl, 0.1 M NaOH, and H2O. The results, shown in Fig. 13(a) and (b), indicate a high adsorption capacity for MB in all three solvents after the first cycle. NaOH emerged as the most effective solvent for untreated and Fenton-treated Bulrush throughout the cycles. A notable decline in HCl adsorption capacity was noted in the second and third cycles, especially for the untreated Bulrush, while a minor drop in H2O was noted in the Fenton-treated Bulrush. The retention of active sites during the initial desorption test might cause this decline [48]. However, the adsorption capacity of MB with NaOH remained significant even after three cycles, demonstrating its feasibility. Consequently, the regeneration of untreated Bulrush and Fenton-treated Bulrush appeared successful, mainly using NaOH as the eluent.

Conclusion

In this investigation, the TGA results indicated that despite Fenton treatment reducing stability, as evidenced by changes in weight and residue percentages, the residual weight in the untreated third stage of Bulrush may be attributed to decomposition by-products, such as char. Additionally, SEM analysis revealed that after treatment with the Fenton reagent, the surface of Bulrush became smoother, exhibiting tiny pores that signify the efficient removal of waxy substances. This transformation results in an enhanced absorption surface of the Fenton-treated Bulrush, contributing to improved performance. Fenton treatment exhibited promising adsorption capabilities of 41.23 mg/g in Bulrush, showcasing enhanced methylene blue removal compared to untreated Bulrush. The critical operational factors for optimal sorption included a dye solution concentration of 100 mg/L, sorbent dosage of 2 g, a contact time of 60 min, and a temperature of 303 K. Methylene blue dye removal was observed to be pH-dependent, with maximum efficiency achieved at pH 8. The Langmuir isotherm model (R2 = 0,9904) better fitted the equilibrium data than the Freundlich model (R2 = 0,9813), confirming the heterogeneous nature of sorption involving physicochemical interactions. A pseudo-second-order kinetics model was used to describe the sorption process, which showed a high correlation coefficient. Reusability experiments showed that the adsorbed MB maintained good adsorption capability in all three solvents after the first cycle. However, NaOH proved to be the most effective solvent throughout the cycles.
