Evaluation of nutritive value of heat pretreated and enzyme pre-digested whole stillage for broiler chickens
Authors: Hossein Rajaei-Sharifabadi, Ahmad Reza Seradj, Saman Lashkari, Tofuko Awori Woyengo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of heat pretreatment (140°C, 70 psi for 15 minutes) and exogenous enzyme predigestion (1% multienzyme complex in acetate buffer, 55°C for 12 hours) on the nutritional value of whole stillage (WS) for broiler chickens. The experimental diets consisted of four corn starch-based diets, each containing 25% of dried distillers grains with solubles (DDGS), untreated (WS-C), heat treated (WS-H) or enzyme treated (WS-E) whole stillage; and a nitrogen-free diet. Male Ross 308 broiler chicks were fed the experimental diets from day 14 to 21 of age. Chemical analysis revealed that WS had higher dietary fiber concentration than DDGS. Heat pretreatment increased acid detergent fiber content and reduced gross energy, and decreased insoluble non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) and hence total NSP. In contrast, enzymatic predigestion increased total NSP by elevating the soluble fraction. The apparent ileal digestibility (AID) and standardized ileal digestibility (SID) of most amino acids were lower in DDGS compared to untreated WS (P < 0.001). Heat pretreatment decreased amino acid AID and SID in WS (P < 0.001), whereas enzymatic predigestion did not affect AID and SID of amino acids. Nitrogen retention was higher in enzymatic predigested WS compared to heat pretreated WS, although no significant differences were observed in dry matter retention, gross energy retention, or nitrogen-corrected apparent metabolizable energy values among treatments. In conclusion, enzymatic predigestion represents a superior processing strategy compared to heat pretreatment for enhancing the nutritional value of WS in broiler diets, primarily through preservation of amino acid quality and improvement of nitrogen retention efficiency.

Introduction

The increased production of corn-based ethanol in North America has intensified competition between the ethanol and livestock feed industries for corn resources. This heightened demand has sparked considerable interest in enhancing the nutritional value of corn ethanol co-products for livestock feed. Whole stillage (WS) is the nutrient-rich slurry remaining after ethanol distillation, comprising the unfermented residues of the grain substrate including fiber, protein, fat, and ash suspended in the stillage liquid. Following distillation, WS undergoes centrifugation to separate the solid fraction (wet distillers grains; WDG) from the liquid fraction (thin stillage), which is subsequently concentrated by evaporation into condensed distillers solubles (CDS). The WDG and CDS fractions are then combined and dried to produce distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS), the most commercially prevalent ethanol co-product used in animal feeding (Ganesan et al., 2006). The contains greater amounts of amino acids, fat, and minerals compared to corn (Spiehs et al., 2002), with a gross energy level nearly equivalent to that of corn. However, both DDGS is characterized by high levels of non-starch polysaccharides (NSP), which have been shown to reduce nutrient utilization in monogastric animals (Stein and Shurson, 2009).

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various strategies to improve the nutrient digestibility and utilization of DDGS in monogastric animals. One widely investigated approach involves supplementing DDGS-based diets with NSP-degrading enzymes, either individually or in combinations. Despite extensive research in both pigs and poultry, these supplemental enzymes have demonstrated limited efficacy in enhancing DDGS digestibility. This limitation is primarily attributed to the resistance of most NSP in DDGS to enzymatic hydrolysis (Jha et al., 2015; Yáñez et al., 2011). Moreover, the acidic environment in the proventriculus may compromise the stability of dietary enzymes, potentially reducing their long-term effectiveness. The relatively brief retention time of feed in the stomach and small intestine further constrains the efficacy of these enzymes by limiting their interaction period with substrates. Additionally, conventional post-manufacture processing techniques such as grinding, pelleting, and extrusion have not yielded significant improvements in mitigating the anti-nutritional effects of DDGS for pigs and poultry (de Vries et al., 2013).

Alternative strategies for enhancing the nutritional value of ethanol co-products have focused on the pretreatment of whole stillage prior to further processing. These approaches include physical (heat) and chemical (acid or alkali) pretreatment of enzymatic predigestion methods (Chatzifragkou et al., 2015; Jerez-Bogota et al., 2021). The rationale behind targeting WS is that the fiber and other components in DDGS form complexes that are challenging to digest (de Vries et al., 2012), whereas WS is anticipated to be more responsive to pretreatment interventions. Moreover, these processes could potentially be integrated into existing ethanol production facilities, thereby minimizing the additional costs associated with producing pretreated or predigested DDGS. This integration is feasible because the pretreated or pre-digested WS can be processed through the existing steps to produce DDGS.

The application of heat pretreatment to lignocellulosic materials, such as those found in WS, has been shown to break down resistant fiber structures, thus increasing the accessibility of the biomass for subsequent enzymatic degradation (Amin et al., 2017). This enhanced accessibility could potentially lead to improved nutrient utilization in monogastric animals, addressing some of the limitations associated with conventional DDGS. In a porcine in vitro study, Zangaro et al. (2018) reported increased digestibility of WS following predigestion with fiber-degrading enzymes or pretreatment with heat, either alone or in combination with diluted acid or alkali. However, there is a notable lack of information regarding the nutritive value of heat pretreated or enzymatically predigested WS specifically for poultry. It was hypothesized that heat pretreatment of WS delignify and solubilize some of the NSP in it, thereby increasing the susceptibility of the NSP for fermentation and increasing the availability of the otherwise NSP-encapsulated nutrients for digestion and absorption in the poultry. It was also was hypothesized that enzymatic predigestion of WS degrades the NSP into short fragments and simple sugars, increasing the susceptibility of the NSP for fermentation and increasing the availability of the otherwise NSP-encapsulated nutrients for digestion and absorption in the poultry. The present study mainly aimed to investigate the effects of heat pretreatment and enzymatic predigestion of WS on the standardized ileal digestibility (SID) of amino acids (AA) and the nitrogen-corrected apparent metabolizable energy (AMEn) value for the same feedstuff in broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

The procedures involving animals in this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Sout Dakota State University (#19-033E).

Experimental ingredients and diets

The corn DDGS and corn WS were supplied by the POET Research Center (Scotland, SD, USA). The WS was pretreated at the POET Research Center (Scotland, SD, USA) at 140°C and 70 psi for 15 min. Untreated and heat pretreated WS were shipped in undried state to Prairie Aquatech (Brookings, SD, USA) where half of the untreated WS was subjected to predigestion with multi-enzyme for 12 h at 55°C under constant agitation. Untreated WS, heat pretreated WS and predigested WS were dried under mild temperature (∼93°C) for ∼15 min in a fluid bed dryer at Prairie Aquatech (Brookings, SD, USA). The multi-enzyme was obtained from BIOCAT (Troy, VA, USA) and supplied xylanase, celullase, α-galactosidase at 200,000, 20,000 and 400,000 U of per kilogram of WS, respectively. Following drying untreated WS, heat pretreated WS and predigested WS were ground in a hammer mill to pass through a 2.8 mm screen before their inclusion in diets.

Five experimental diets were fed. The diets included 4 cornstarch-based diets with either DDGS, untreated WS (WS-C), heat pretreated WS (WS-H) and enzymatically predigested WS (WS-E) as sole source of protein; and nitrogen-free diet (Table 1). Animals and experimental design

A total of 240 one-day-old male broiler chicks of Ross 308 were used in this study. The chicks were housed in electrically heated Petersime battery brooders (Petersime Incubator Co., Gettysburg, OH, USA), with approximately 14 birds per cage (34 cm wide, 102 cm long, and 24 cm high). Room temperature was maintained according to the programs recommended for the Ross 308 strain. Continuous lighting (24 h daily) was provided throughout the experiment. From day 1 to day 14, chicks were fed a drug-free commercial starter diet containing 3,050 kcal/kg metabolizable energy (ME), 22% crude protein (CP), 1.00% calcium (Ca), and 0.45% available phosphorus (P). On day 14, the birds were redistributed into 40 cages, with 6 birds per cage. The experiment followed a completely randomized design with the five experimental diets and 8 replicates (cages) per diet. The experimental diets fed from day 14 to day 21 of age. Fresh water and feed were provided ad libitum throughout the experiment. Excreta samples were collected from each cage on days 19 and 20 of age. These samples were immediately stored at −20°C for further analyses. On day 21, all birds were euthanized via cervical dislocation. Ileal digesta samples were collected by gently squeezing the contents from the ileum. These samples were also stored at −20°C for later analysis.

Sample preparation and analyses

Excreta and ileal digesta samples were pooled by cage to create representative samples. The pooled excreta were oven-dried at 60°C for 96 hours, while the pooled ileal digesta samples were freeze-dried. Following the drying process, all samples, including the dried excreta, ileal digesta, test feedstuffs, and diet samples, were finely ground using a laboratory mill equipped with a 0.75 mm screen. All samples were subjected to analysis for dry matter (DM) and CP using methods 930.15 and 984.13A-D, respectively as descripted by (AOAC, 2006), while gross energy (GE) was determined using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (model 1261, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL) with benzoic as the standard. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and ether extract (EE) were also measured according to AOAC (2006). Amino acids content was determined using the method 982.30 E of AOAC (2006) at the University of Missouri Experiment Station laboratories (Colombia, MO). Titanium dioxide in diet, ileal digesta, and excreta samples was determined by spectrophotometry method (model Spectra MAX 190, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA) at 408 nm after ashing at 525°C for 10 h (Myers et al., 2004). Test feedstuffs were analyzed for the total NSP, insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) and soluble dietary fiber (SDF) by gas-liquid chromatography (component neutral sugars) and by colorimetry (uronic acids) as described by Knudsen (1997). Klason lignin was determined gravimetrically as described by Knudsen (1997). Dietary fiber was calculated as the sum of total NSP and Klason lignin.

Calculations and statistical analysis

Apparent retention of nitrogen and energy, and apparent ileal digestibility (AID) of AA were calculated using the indicator method according to the following equation (Stein et al., 2007):

Apparent digestibility or retention of nutrient or energy, % =[1−(NfNd×MdMf)]×100 where: Nf = nutrient or energy concentration in excreta or ileal digesta (% of DM); Nd = nutrient or energy concentration in the diet (% of DM); Md = titanium concentration in diet (% of DM); Mf = titanium concentration in excreta or ileal digesta (% of DM).

SID of AA was determined by correcting AID values for basal endogenous losses using the following equation:SID,%=AID+[IAAendoAAdiet×100]where IAAend represents the basal endogenous loss of an AA in grams per kilogram of DM intake and AAdiet indicates the AA concentrations (g/kg) in diet DM.

The direct method was employed to determine N retention for the test feedstuffs, while energy retention for the test feedstuffs was calculated using the difference method with N-free diet as basal diet:DA=DD−(DB×SB)SAwhere: DA = retention of energy in an assay feedstuff (WS-C, WS-H, WS-E, or DDGS); DD = retention of energy (%) in an assay diet (corn starch WS-C, corn starch WS-H, corn starch WS-E, and corn starch DDGS); DB = retention of energy (%) in the N-free diet; SB = contribution level of energy from energy-yielding ingredients in N-free diet to the assay diet; and SA = contribution level of energy from test feedstuffs to their respective diets.

The AME values for test feedstuffs were determined by multiplying GE by apparent retention of GE. The AMEn for test feedstuffs were calculated by subtracting nitrogen correction (8.22 × g N retained per g test feedstuff) from AME values according to Hill et al. (1960).

Data underwent analysis of variance using the MIXED procedure of SAS (version 9.3) for a completely randomized design with cage as the experimental unit. Prior to ANOVA, data were evaluated for normality using the UNIVARIATE procedure of SAS and variance homogeneity using residual plots. Five outliers were identified and removed across treatments. Treatment means were compared using the PDIFF option with Tukey-Kramer adjustment when significant effects were detected (P < 0.05).

Ileal amino acid digestibility

Table 4, Table 5 present the AID and SID of amino acids, respectively. DDGS showed lower AID values for amino acids compared to untreated WS (P < 0.001). Heat pretreatment significantly decreased the AID of all amino acids in WS (P < 0.001); however, the magnitude of the reduction differed among individual amino acids. Lysine exhibited the greatest decrease in AID (54.5 vs. 33.8%), whereas tryptophan was comparatively less affected by heat pretreatment (74.7 vs. 69.7%). In contrast, no differences were observed between WS-C and WS-E for the AID of any amino acid. The SID values of amino acids were also lower (P < 0.001) in DDGS compared to WS-C, with the exception of tryptophan. No significant differences were detected among WS-C, WS-H, and WS-E for SID of lysine, methionine, tryptophan, and glycine. However, heat pretreatment reduced SID of other amino acids compared to WS-C. Discussion

The present study was conducted to evaluate the effects of heat pretreatment and exogenous enzyme predigestion on the nutritional value of WS for broiler chickens. Both processing methodologies were designed to modify the fibrous matrix and enhance nutrient bioavailability in WS, an ethanol co-product characterized by substantial concentrations of NSP that typically limits nutrient utilization in monogastric species.

Chemical analysis revealed that WS had greater dietary fiber content than DDGS. The TDF and IDF values of WS in the current study exceeded those previously reported by Jerez-Bogota et al. (2021), although SDF and the TDF to IDF ratio were comparable. The greater dietary fiber content observed in untreated WS compared with DDGS may be explained by differences in processing. During ethanol production, whole stillage retains the intact fiber matrix of the grain alongside protein, oil, and yeast cells. In contrast, DDGS is produced by combining wet distillers' grains with condensed distillers' solubles - a syrup derived from evaporated thin stillage - before drying (Ganesan et al., 2006). The incorporation of condensed distillers' solubles, which is rich in soluble compounds such as sugars and soluble proteins but low in structural fiber, likely dilutes the fiber fraction in DDGS relative to WS (Ganesan et al., 2006).

The gross energy of WS-H decreased by approximately 11% compared to WS-C (4,637 vs. 5,215 kcal/kg), which can be attributed to several mechanisms associated with heat treatment. The most likely primary contributor is the thermal degradation and volatilization of organic matter. Heat treatment promotes the breakdown of labile organic compounds, including simple sugars, short-chain fatty acids, and fermentable carbohydrates, which represent among the highest energy-density constituents of stillage. Since gross energy is fundamentally a function of organic matter composition and content, any net reduction in organic matter directly translates to a lower calorific value. Additionally, the evaporative loss of volatile fatty acids and other volatile organic compounds during thermal processing represents a further pathway through which energy-rich constituents may be physically removed from the material. Collectively, these findings suggest that the reduction in gross energy of WS-H reflects a composite outcome of organic matter degradation, hemicellulose solubilization, and volatile compound loss during heat treatment. However, as volatile compounds, Maillard reaction products, and fermentable carbohydrate fractions were not directly measured in the present study, therefore, their relative contributions to the observed GE reduction cannot be quantitatively partitioned based on the present dataset. Further studies incorporating direct measurements of these components are needed to quantify their individual contributions and to better elucidate the mechanisms underlying gross energy losses during thermal processing. These losses also resulted in a greater concentration of the remaining nutrients on a dry matter basis, which likely explains the higher CP concentration observed in WS-H compared with WS-C.

The observed reduction in NSP content following thermal processing is consistent with previous investigations demonstrating NSP degradation in fibrous feed ingredients subjected to heat application (de Vries et al., 2013). Jerez-Bogota et al. (2021) reported a linear decrease in TDF, IDF, and SDF of WS with increasing temperature of heat pretreatment. In contrast, Chang and Morris (1990) observed that autoclaving reduced SDF content but not IDF content of corn, concluding that the soluble fiber fraction exhibits greater susceptibility to thermal treatments than the insoluble fraction. The results of the present study contrast with these earlier findings, as SDF remained unchanged in heat treated WS while IDF decreased. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by considering the molecular weight distribution of fiber fractions following thermal processing. As observed in the present study, the cellulose content in WS increased from 10.5% to 14.3% following heat treatment, accompanied by a concurrent decrease in hemicellulose content. This pattern most likely reflects the selective thermal degradation of hemicellulose rather than an absolute increase in cellulose concentration. Hemicellulose, particularly arabinoxylans, is structurally less thermally stable than cellulose because of its branched, amorphous structure and lower degree of polymerization. Hydrothermal treatment has been shown to disrupt NSP structures, promoting the solubilization and partial hydrolysis of hemicellulosic fractions, whereas the highly ordered crystalline structure of cellulose provides greater resistance to thermal degradation. Therefore, the observed increase in cellulose content should be interpreted as a relative enrichment effect; as hemicellulose is selectively degraded and lost, cellulose accounts for a larger proportion of the remaining dry matter. Wang et al. (2018) observed reduced IDF and increased SDF without significant change in TDF in defatted corn hulls subjected to hydrothermal treatment, implying conversion of IDF into SDF. However, the interpretation of fiber modifications must account for methodological considerations in fiber analysis. The SDF fraction of corn fiber can be classified into low-molecular-weight and high-molecular-weight components (McCleary, 2019; Wang et al., 2018). Elevated processing temperatures can induce secondary decomposition of SDF into lower molecular weight fractions (McCleary, 2019) that may be underestimated by the method employed in the current study (Ramulu and Rao, 1997). Therefore, it can be postulated that heat pretreatment disrupted both IDF and SDF structures, with SDF degrading to low molecular weight oligosaccharides not recovered by the analytical method, while a substantial proportion of hemicellulose converted to higher molecular weight SDF fractions, resulting in no net change in measured SDF concentration. Although hydrothermal treatments primarily induce depolymerization of NSP to oligosaccharides (Chatzifragkou et al., 2015), formation of monosaccharides may also occur, with this effect exacerbated at elevated pretreatment temperatures (Samala et al., 2012). In marked contrast to heat pretreatment, enzymatic predigestion increased the soluble NSP fraction and decreased the IDF to TDF ratio compared to untreated WS. The efficacy of enzymatic predigestion in elevating soluble NSP concentrations aligns with extensive research demonstrating that exogenous carbohydrases can modify fiber architecture and enhance nutrient accessibility (Bedford and Cowieson, 2012). Collectively, these results indicate that heat pretreatment and enzymatic predigestion disrupt NSP in WS through fundamentally different mechanisms, with thermal processing degrading both IDF and SDF to low molecular weight products, whereas enzymatic hydrolysis converts IDF to high molecular weight SDF fractions.

The results of the present study indicated a reduction in AA content of WS following heat treatment. This reduction is likely attributable to the thermal degradation of free amino acids and/or the occurrence of Maillard reaction. Elevated temperatures can directly reduce recoverable AA concentrations. In addition, Maillard reactions are accelerated under high-temperature conditions and promote the condensation of reducing sugars with free amino acids. This condensation leads to the formation of heat-stable Maillard reaction products that are resistant to hydrolysis and therefore reduce measurable amino acid recovery during analysis. In contrast, higher amino acid concentrations were observed in WS-E compared to WS-C, which can be explained by several complementary mechanisms associated with enzymatic pretreatment. First, the exogenous enzyme preparations applied to WS likely contributed directly to the elevated amino acid pool, as commercial enzyme cocktails are proteinaceous in nature and themselves represent a source of amino acids upon hydrolysis. Second, enzymatic pretreatment facilitates the release of amino acids and peptides that are structurally associated with the fiber matrix. In cereal-based materials, a substantial proportion of protein is known to be physically entrapped within or covalently linked to cell wall polysaccharides, rendering it inaccessible under standard analytical conditions. The hydrolysis of NSP structures by carbohydrases disrupts this protein-fiber complex, liberating previously bound proteins, peptides, and free amino acids into the measurable fraction. Third, the enzymatic breakdown of the fiber matrix may also expose protein fractions that were previously shielded from extraction, effectively increasing the apparent amino acid content without necessarily increasing total nitrogen content.

The amino acid digestibility assessment revealed that DDGS showed lower AID and SID for the majority of amino acids compared to untreated WS, which can be attributed to the intensive processing protocols employed in DDGS production, particularly the drying stage (Pahm et al., 2008). Excessive thermal input during drying is known to compromise amino acid bioavailability in DDGS through Maillard reactions, with lysine demonstrating particular vulnerability (Fastinger and Mahan, 2006). The adverse effects of excessive heat on amino acid bioavailability have been extensively reported in numerous feed ingredients including soybean meal (González-Vega et al., 2011), fish protein isolate (Opstvedt et al., 1984), and canola meal (Almeida et al., 2014).

Similarly, the results of the present study demonstrate that heat pretreatment adversely affected AA digestibility of WS, despite the theoretical potential of thermal fiber modification to improve nutrient utilization in poultry (Kiarie et al., 2014). However, the effects of heat pretreatment on amino acid digestibility were not uniform across individual amino acids. Lysine exhibited the greatest reduction in AID, whereas tryptophan was comparatively less affected. The greater susceptibility of lysine is due to the fact that lysine has an extra amino (ε-amino group) that is reactive, which readily participates in Maillard reactions with reducing sugars during heat processing. These reactions reduce lysine availability by forming nutritionally unavailable complexes that are resistant to enzymatic digestion (Fastinger and Mahan, 2006). In contrast, amino acids such as tryptophan are less susceptible to these heat-induced reactions, resulting in smaller reductions in digestibility. Therefore, the decline in amino acid digestibility following heat pretreatment appears to depend not only on structural changes in the fiber matrix but also on the inherent chemical reactivity of individual amino acids during thermal processing. It is acknowledged that the heat pretreatment applied to WS in the present study (140°C) exceeded the temperatures typically employed during DDGS drying (80–110°C), representing a more severe thermal insult. This discrepancy highlights an inherent trade-off in thermal pretreatment strategies; while moderate heat can beneficially disrupt fiber structures and potentially improve nutrient accessibility, more intensive thermal conditions increasingly risk accelerating Maillard reactions and direct thermal degradation of amino acids, ultimately compromising digestibility. The severity of Maillard reaction damage is influenced by multiple factors including temperature, moisture content, pH, and exposure duration (Almeida et al., 2013). For instance, research on heated soybean meal has shown that autoclaving at 125°C for 15 minutes decreased the SID of lysine and aspartic acid, while autoclaving for 30 minutes reduced the digestibility of all amino acids relative to unheated samples (González-Vega et al., 2011). These findings underscore the importance of carefully controlling thermal processing parameters to balance the benefits of heat pretreatment against the risk of nutrient damage, and highlight the need for further studies to identify optimal heat pretreatment conditions for large-scale processing of WS.

Substantial enhancements in amino acid digestibility through enzyme supplementation in corn-based diets have been demonstrated in numerous studies (Cowieson and Ravindran, 2008a, 2008b). In the present study, enzymatic predigestion did not affect digestibility coefficients comparable to those of untreated WS. The contrasting results between the present study and those reporting improvements in amino acid digestibility through enzyme supplementation may be partially attributed to protein pre-digestibility under gastric conditions where feed proteins are initially exposed to low pH and pepsin activity. Zangaro et al. (2018) suggested that the efficacy of enzyme treatment on in vitro dry matter digestibility of WS improved with acid pretreatment. Nevertheless, additional research is warranted to evaluate optimal enzyme blend composition, concentration, and treatment conditions for WS to maximize improvements in nutrient availability.

The similar AMEn values across treatments indicate that despite modifications in fiber composition and amino acid availability, the overall energy utilization remained consistent. In marked contrast to energy utilization, the improvements in nutrient availability attributable to enzymatic predigestion relative to heat pretreatment were clearly manifested in nitrogen retention values. WS-E exhibited significantly superior nitrogen retention performance compared to WS-H, indicating that enzymatic hydrolysis facilitates protein accessibility through depolymerization of fiber-protein complexes (Adeola and Cowieson, 2011). The enhanced nitrogen retention corroborates previous research demonstrating improved protein utilization through exogenous enzyme supplementation in high-fiber dietary formulations (Choct et al., 2007). The superior nitrogen retention observed in WS-E compared to heat treated samples represents one of the most biologically and economically significant findings of this investigation. Nitrogen retention reflects the efficiency which dietary nitrogen is converted into tissue protein, and improvements in this parameter translate directly to enhanced protein utilization efficiency and reduced nitrogen excretion into the environment. The combination of unchanged amino acid digestibility and enhanced nitrogen retention due to enzymatic predigestion indicates improved efficiency of amino acid utilization for protein synthesis. These findings collectively suggest that the primary benefits of enzyme treatment are associated with protein metabolism rather than energy availability, consistent with observations reported by Adeola and Cowieson (2011) in enzyme supplementation studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that processing method significantly influences the nutritional characteristics of WS for broiler chickens. Heat pretreatment, while effective in reducing total NSP content, adversely affected amino acid profiles and digestibility, likely due to Maillard reaction-induced damage during thermal processing. In contrast, exogenous enzyme predigestion successfully increased the soluble NSP fraction through conversion of insoluble fiber to soluble NSP and maintained amino acid concentrations and digestibility coefficients comparable to untreated WS, while numerically improving nitrogen retention. Despite modifications in chemical composition across treatments, energy utilization remained consistent as evidenced by similar AMEn values, indicating that the primary benefits of enzymatic predigestion are associated with protein metabolism rather than energy availability. The findings indicate that enzymatic pretreatment represents a superior processing strategy compared to thermal treatment for enhancing the nutritional value of WS in broiler diets. From a practical standpoint, the economic feasibility of enzymatic predigestion, including enzyme costs and the energy required to maintain a 12-h incubation at 55°C, must be weighed against the associated nutritional benefits before large-scale industrial adoption can be recommended, and further cost-benefit analyses at commercial scale are warranted. Future research should focus on optimizing enzyme cocktail composition, treatment protocols, and economic feasibility to facilitate commercial adoption of this promising processing technology.
