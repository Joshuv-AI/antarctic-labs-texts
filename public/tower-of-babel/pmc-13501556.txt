Understanding social exclusion among midlife and older family caregivers of Individuals with age related cognitive declines: results from photo-based participatory study
Authors: Anna Urbaniak, Katarzyna Szczerbińska, Jolanta Perek-Białas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamily caregivers of adults with cognitive impairment, including dementia, often face complex challenges. However, social exclusion among caregivers aged 50 years and older particularly within the distinctive Central and Eastern European context, remains critically underexplored. This study uses Poland, a country where strong family care norms coexist with limited formal support, as a crucial case study to examine how social exclusion manifests in the caregiving experiences of older family caregivers.MethodsUsing photovoice, a novel participatory action research method for this context, the study engaged six family caregivers aged 50 and above from a single city in Poland. Participants photographed aspects of their caregiving experiences and took part in reflective group discussions. Data from these discussions were analyzed thematically to identify key domains of social exclusion. No statistical tests were applied due to the qualitative and exploratory nature of the study.ResultsThematic analysis revealed four interconnected domains of social exclusion: (1) Relational exclusion—including the erosion of social networks and experiences of stigma; (2) Institutional exclusion—such as difficulties accessing care services, transportation, and public infrastructure; (3) Material exclusion—highlighting financial strain and unmet resource needs; and (4) Paradoxical caregiving roles—reflecting the simultaneous marginalization and moral elevation of caregivers. These findings illustrate the layered and dynamic nature of exclusion in the caregiving trajectory.ConclusionsA key contribution of this work is the methodological approach. Despite the study’s small and localized sample, it demonstrates the pioneering value of photovoice in capturing the context-specific dimensions of social inequality in caregiving acting as a critical tool to amplify caregiver voices. By centering older caregivers in Poland, the study offers novel insights into how familial care responsibilities intersect with systemic gaps in formal support. Despite limitations in generalizability, these context-specific findings demonstrate the impact of Polish systemic gaps and can enrich cross-national discussions on caregiver vulnerability and resilience, emphasizing the need for targeted social protection policies and inclusive service design.

Background

Age-related cognitive impairments, including dementia and its associated behavioral disorders, present complex health challenges that affect individuals diagnosed, as well as their families and caregivers [43]. With global population aging, dementia prevalence is projected to increase, heightening demands for informal care and amplifying social inequalities [42]. In Poland, official data on informal caregivers are not collected,however, estimates suggest approximately 389,000 people lived with Alzheimer's and related conditions in 2019, a number expected to rise [25].

Dementia encompasses a range of neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and Lewy body dementia, all characterized by progressive cognitive decline that disrupts daily functioning [34, 42]. Family caregivers, who deliver the majority of support often without formal training, confront substantial emotional, physical, and social burdens [5, 6, 38]. Globally, around 11 million family caregivers provided unpaid care for individuals with dementia in the United States in 2022 alone [2], underscoring the scale of this issue. However, caregiving is a complex trajectory that often encompasses positive outcomes, such as deepening bonds and a sense of purpose, alongside inherent challenges. Despite their critical role, these caregivers frequently experience stress, isolation, and social exclusion, which can undermine their health and well-being [24, 36].

Social exclusion in later life—defined as the systematic denial of rights, opportunities, and resources—exacerbates caregiving challenges, manifesting as emotional isolation, limited social support, and barriers to community access [40]. For family caregivers of individuals with dementia, this exclusion can involve withdrawal from social networks, stigmatization, and difficulties balancing personal and caregiving demands [8, 9, 11, 24]. However, research has predominantly focused on later-stage experiences, leaving a gap in understanding initial dynamics and their social contexts [7, 18, 27, 30]. Understanding exclusion early in the caregiving trajectory is vital, as this phase often dictates caregivers' long-term coping strategies and ability to access necessary supports before severe burnout occurs [1, 32].

This study addresses this gap by examining the experiences of midlife and older family caregivers of individuals with age-related cognitive impairments in Poland, with a specific focus on social exclusion in caregiving trajectories. Employing photovoice photovoice—a participatory visual methodology [31] in that national context that constitutes a main strength of this research and enables caregivers to visually document their realities [10, 17]—we aim to amplify their voices and provide uniquely nuanced, visual insights into understudied aspects of caregiving. By doing so, the research contributes to knowledge on social determinants of health, highlighting the need for targeted support in resource-constrained settings.

Method

Study design and rationale

This paper is based on a participatory photovoice study, which forms a key qualitative component of a broader, mixed-methods project investigating caregiver stress and social exclusion (SIWSON). The photovoice methodology was chosen for its foundation in critical pedagogy and community-based participatory research [10, 39],. This approach empowers participants to be co-researchers, using photography and narrative to generate knowledge about their lived realities. This is particularly valuable for illuminating the subjective and often invisible experiences of social exclusion. Unlike traditional methods, photovoice allows caregivers to visually map the systemic barriers (institutional, material) they face, grounding the abstract concept of exclusion in tangible evidence (e.g., inaccessible buildings, austerity of meals).This study prioritized depth and richness of data over breadth, making a small, purposive sample appropriate for the exploratory nature of the research.

Participants and recruitment

A purposive sample of six family caregivers providing care for a relative with dementia in Kraków a city in south Poland was recruited. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 50 years old and to have been providing care for a minimum of two months. Recruitment channels were diverse and included web advertisements, direct outreach to local caregiver support centers, a local radio advertisement, and informal caregiver groups. This multi-channel approach helped reach a range of potential participants. Data collection: the photovoice process

The photovoice process involved three stages: a preparatory workshop, a photography phase, and a participatory discussion workshop.

Introductory workshop

A preliminary workshop was held to introduce participants to the photovoice methodology and the study’s focus on social exclusion. As part of a member checking exercise [3], researchers presented preliminary themes from the project's semi-structured interviews to participants for their validation and feedback, fostering an environment of trust and collaboration. Participants also received a brief training session from a professional photographer on how to capture images that would convey a narrative.

Photography phase

Over a four-week period, participants were instructed to take up to 15 photographs representing their caregiving experiences, including moments of joy, challenge, and social exclusion. They were then asked to select a maximum of five key photographs that they felt best represented their journey and to write short captions for each, explaining its significance and the emotions it evoked. This narrative component was crucial for contextualizing the visual data and guiding subsequent discussions.

Participatory workshop and photo-elicitation

The final workshop served as the primary data collection event. Participants gathered to discuss their selected photographs in a group setting facilitated by the research team. The discussion followed a semi-structured, photo-elicitation format, where participants were invited to present their images and share related stories. Open-ended questions guided the conversation, such as: "What does this photograph mean to you in the context of your caregiving experience?" and "How does this image reflect your feelings of social inclusion or exclusion?" The workshop was audio-recorded with participant consent, providing rich qualitative data for analysis.

Data analysis and reflexivity

Audio recordings from the participatory workshop and accompanying photo narratives were transcribed verbatim. The data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis [4]. The research team engaged in a six-phase process of data analysis: (1) familiarization with the data through repeated reading of transcripts and photo captions; (2) generation of initial codes from the text and images; (3) grouping codes into broader sub-themes; (4) reviewing and refining these themes in relation to the entire dataset; (5) defining and naming the final themes; and (6) writing up the findings. Throughout the analysis, photos were treated as a form of primary data, with the captions and discussion serving as critical context.

As part of the participatory approach [39], the research team engaged in critical reflexivity throughout the study. This involved critically reflecting on our own positionality and assumptions as researchers to minimize potential power imbalances and biases in data interpretation. This ongoing process of self-reflection ensured that the analysis was grounded in the participants' perspectives and experiences.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Jagiellonian University (1072.6120.261.2021). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality was maintained by using pseudonyms and securely storing all data. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Recognizing the sensitive nature of the topic, researchers were trained to handle strong emotions, and participants were provided with contact information for local emotional support resources. Consent was also obtained from any individuals identifiable in the photographs to protect their privacy and autonomy.

Results

The photovoice component of the study involving six midlife and older (50 +) caregivers provided rich qualitative insights into the multifaceted experiences of caregiving. Through their photographs and narratives, participants acted as co-creators in this research, identifying and articulating four primary domains of social exclusion that they face in their caregiving trajectories. These domains are: (1) relational exclusion (eroded social networks, stigmatizing interactions), (2) institutional exclusion (barriers to care services, transport, and public amenities), (3) material exclusion (financial precarity, unmet resource needs), and (4) paradoxical caregiving roles (simultaneous marginalization and moral valorization of care labor). Each theme is discussed below, accompanied by analyses of the images shared by participants.

The results are organized around three primary themes: exclusion from social relations, exclusion from financial and material resources, and exclusion from services, amenities, and mobility. Each theme is discussed in detail below, accompanied by a photo analysis of the most impactful images shared by participants. Additionally, the caregivers shared both positive and negative experiences associated with their caregiving roles, highlighting the critical need for knowledge sharing and peer support.

Exclusion from social relations

A core theme that emerged from caregivers' pictures and narratives was relational exclusion, reflected in feelings of isolation and disconnection from their social networks. Many participants described a gradual withdrawal from social activities, as caregiving responsibilities intensified. For instance, one caregiver noted, “I used to go out with friends, but now I hardly see anyone. It’s like my life has shrunk to just the four walls of my house” (P2). This sentiment was echoed by several participants, who illustrated how caregiving duties often overshadowed their previous social engagements.

The caregivers also expressed feelings of stigma and misunderstanding from their peers. Some reported that friends and family members often did not comprehend the demands of caregiving, leading to feelings of frustration and loneliness. One caregiver articulated this experience by stating, “My brother [who lives abroad and doesn’t provide care to their mother] thinks he understands, but he doesn’t. He says, ‘I can’t believe you are unable to take care of mum,’ but he doesn’t see the constant worry and the exhaustion”(P4). This lack of understanding contributed to a sense of social exclusion, as caregivers felt unable to share their burdens with those, who don’t have caregiving experience.

Moreover, the caregivers highlighted the emotional toll of social exclusion, which often manifested as depression and anxiety. The narratives revealed a cycle where social isolation exacerbated mental health challenges, making it even more difficult for caregivers to engage with others. As one participant poignantly expressed, “I feel like I’m in a dark tunnel, and I can’t find my way out. The loneliness is suffocating” (P3).

Family meeting

One of the photograph depicts a multigenerational family gathering, with several members—especially children—smiling directly at the camera. In contrast, the care recipient positioned in the middle of the picture appears confused, her gaze directed away from the lens. According to the participant, the care recipient has significant hearing difficulties and struggled to follow the conversation during the event. She also expressed dissatisfaction with the soup being served, which she felt was too cold. The participant recalled, “It was supposed to be a joyful occasion, but she couldn’t hear anything and kept complaining about the soup. It made me realize how difficult it is for her to be part of the family.” The image visually conveys this tension: while the scene outwardly presents as celebratory, the care recipient’s facial expression and body language contrast with the visible enjoyment of others. The photograph demonstrates a two-fold relational exclusion: (1) The sensory and cognitive isolation of the care recipient, who, despite physical presence, is unable to fully participate or experience joy due to their condition (as evidenced by their confusion and complaints about the soup); and (2) The emotional exclusion experienced by the caregiver (who stated: "It made me realize how difficult it is for her to be part of the family"), whose awareness of this failed inclusion deepens their own sense of burden and isolation in their role. The visual tension, therefore, serves as a metaphor for the caregiver's enduring struggle to reconcile attempts at inclusion with the reality of cognitive decline.

Exclusion from financial and material resources

The second core domain of exclusion identified by participants, material exclusion, highlighted caregivers' struggles with limited financial and material resources, directly arising from their sustained caregiving responsibilities. Participants in the photovoice sessions frequently described economic hardships, such as reduced income, out-of-pocket medical expenses, and costs for home modifications, which exacerbated their vulnerability as social determinants of caregiving. Caregivers expressed concerns about accessing ‘opieka wytchnieniowa,’ a municipally administered respite-care programme funded through the Fundusz Solidarnościowy. Although subsidised, eligibility requirements and limited local availability often result in long waiting lists or hidden out-of-pocket costs, which restrict access to vital supports and perpetuate inequalities in the welfare system [23]. In Poland, family-based caregiving remains the primary mode of care yet is rarely formally recognised, and public services such as respite care or income support are limited [14, 44].Many participants felt overwhelmed by the financial burden, which often led to feelings of helplessness. A caregiver remarked, “Even if I wanted to get a professional help I worry about how I’m going to pay for it. It’s a constant source of stress”(P1). This financial insecurity further compounded the caregivers’ feelings of exclusion, as they struggled to provide adequate care while managing limited resources.

The caregivers reported a lack of access to financial assistance programs and support services, with many expressing unawareness of available resources or describing the application processes as daunting and complex. One participant stated, "I’ve tried to find help, but it feels like a maze. It’s so frustrating to navigate the system when you’re already overwhelmed" (P6).

Exclusion from services, amenities, and mobility

The third domain of social exclusion identified by caregivers pertained to services, amenities, and mobility. Caregivers reported difficulties accessing essential services, such as healthcare, transportation, and community resources. Mobility challenges were particularly common, with one caregiver stating, "I can’t take my mother out anymore because she can’t walk downstairs and there is no elevator in the building. She is stuck at home most of the time" (P5). Caregivers also expressed frustration with service inadequacies, including long wait times for appointments, limited availability of specialized care, and a lack of tailored services for families affected by dementia, as noted by another: "When I finally get an appointment, it feels like a race against time. I wish there were more resources available for us" (P2). Furthermore, caregivers highlighted barriers to community amenities, such as support groups and recreational activities, due to transportation and scheduling issues, with one participant commenting, "It would be great if there were more activities for us, but I can’t get there. We need places that are easy to access and welcoming" (P1).

Positive and negative experiences of caregiving

While the caregivers articulated numerous challenges associated with their caregiving roles, they also shared positive experiences that emerged from their caregiving trajectories. Many caregivers spoke of the deepening bond they developed with their family members, emphasizing the importance of love and connection in their caregiving experiences. One caregiver reflected, “Despite the challenges, I feel closer to my mother than ever. We share moments that are precious, even in the midst of difficulty”(P3). This perspective highlights the potential for caregiving to foster meaningful relationships, despite the inherent struggles.

Additionally, caregivers reported feelings of fulfillment and purpose derived from their roles. Several participants expressed a sense of pride in providing care and being there for their significant others during challenging times. One caregiver remarked, “I know I’m making a difference in her life. That gives me strength, even on the hardest days”(P6). This sense of purpose can serve as a protective factor against the negative emotional impacts of caregiving.

However, the caregivers also acknowledged the toll that caregiving can take on their physical and mental health. Many reported experiencing burnout, fatigue, and feelings of being overwhelmed. One caregiver openly shared, “There are days when I just want to scream. It’s exhausting, and I feel like I’m losing myself in the process”(P4). This experience underscores the complexity of caregiving, where moments of joy coexist with significant challenges.

Need for knowledge sharing and peer support

The caregivers’ narratives consistently highlighted the need for knowledge sharing and peer support as essential components of their caregiving experience. Many participants expressed a desire for more information on caregiving strategies, available resources, and coping mechanisms. One caregiver stated, “I wish I could talk to someone who understands what I’m going through. I need to learn from others who have been in my shoes”(P5). This desire for connection and shared knowledge emphasizes the importance of creating platforms for caregivers to exchange experiences and insights.

Peer support emerged as a critical theme, with caregivers expressing the value of connecting with others facing similar challenges. Participants highlighted the potential for support groups to provide emotional relief, practical advice, and a sense of community. One caregiver remarked, “Just knowing that others go through similar things makes a huge difference. We can share tips and help each other out”(P6). This sense of solidarity can mitigate feelings of isolation and empower caregivers to navigate their journeys more effectively.

In conclusion, the findings from this participatory study illuminate the multifaceted experiences of family caregivers of individuals living with dementia. The identified domains of social exclusion—exclusion from social relations, financial and material resources, and services, amenities, and mobility—underscore the systemic barriers that caregivers face. While caregivers shared both positive and negative experiences, the overarching need for knowledge sharing and peer support emerged as a critical theme.

Discussion

Grounded exclusively in the Polish context, this photovoice study provides critical insights into the lived experiences of family caregivers supporting relatives with cognitive impairment, revealing how social exclusion manifests across relational, material, and institutional domains. These findings, though context-specific, offer qualitative insights that may inform international audiences by demonstrating how specific national care systems interact with universal caregiving challenges. By employing a participatory visual methodology, this research captures the nuanced, lived experiences of caregivers in ways that complement and extend existing quantitative and qualitative literature on caregiving burdens.

The narratives and photographs provided by participants reveal consistent patterns of social exclusion that significantly impact caregivers' emotional well-being, social connections, and practical caregiving capacities [18]. Our findings identify three key domains of exclusion—relational, material, and institutional—that create systemic barriers affecting both caregiver welfare and care quality. These results align with existing international literature on caregiver exclusion (Brodaty et al., 2009; [15, 33], while offering novel insights into how these exclusionary processes manifest within Poland's family-oriented welfare system. By focusing on midlife and older caregivers in Poland, our study highlights how national cultural expectations—where adult children are socially expected to provide in‑home care for aging relatives—interact with formal support structures [14]. These expectations create specific pressures and exclusions that may differ from global caregiving norms, illustrating how cultural context shapes the experience of caregiving and access to support [41, 44].

Exclusion from social relations

One of the most profound themes emerging from the caregivers’ narratives is the sense of isolation and exclusion from social relations. Many participants articulated feelings of loneliness and disconnection from friends and family, which can be exacerbated by the demands of caregiving. The visual data from this study provides compelling evidence of how caregiving responsibilities systematically erode social connections. The photographs taken by participants serve as a powerful visual metaphor for the relational challenges caregivers face. The composition of one particularly striking image—with the care recipient physically present but relationally distant from the family group—visually demonstrates what participants described as the "shrinking" of their social worlds. This relational distance is experienced by the caregiver as a form of exclusion, stemming from the emotional toll of witnessing the care recipient's inability to connect, even during moments of intended companionship. This finding aligns with previous studies that have documented the social isolation experienced by caregivers, particularly those caring for individuals with dementia [21]. The caregivers in this study described a shift in their social networks, where friendships and quality of family relations often waned and social invitations diminished as their caregiving responsibilities intensified.

The photographs taken by participants also captured moments of solitude, emphasizing the emotional toll of caregiving. This visual representation serves as a powerful reminder of the importance of social connections for caregivers’ mental health and overall well-being [24]. The need for social support is critical, as isolation can lead to increased stress and depression, further complicating the caregiving experience [37]. Thus, fostering social connections and creating support networks for caregivers should be a priority for healthcare providers and community organizations. This visual evidence suggests that interventions to address social isolation must consider not just the quantity of social contact, but its quality and reciprocal nature.

Financial and material resources

Financial strain emerged as another significant domain of social exclusion for caregivers. Many participants expressed concerns about the economic burden associated with caregiving, including costs related to medical care, respite services, and other necessities. This finding is consistent with research indicating that caregivers often face financial hardships due to the demands of caregiving, which can lead to increased stress and anxiety [28]. The intersection of caregiving and financial insecurity can create a cycle, where the inability to access necessary resources further exacerbates feelings of inadequacy and helplessness [12].

The visual analysis reveals several important dimensions of material exclusion. The photographs show how financial constraints shape not just what caregivers can provide, but how they provide it. The simple presentation of food (Photo 2) reflects both economic limitations and the practical realities of preparing meals for someone with cognitive impairment. The photographs of food items and their placement in the home environment visually document the unseen work of budgeting and resource management that caregivers perform daily. The photographs of home environments show how financial constraints shape the physical spaces of caregiving—from the types of food available to the home modifications (or lack thereof) that caregivers can afford.

The visual data particularly highlights the gap between policy provisions and practical accessibility. Photographs of medication bottles, receipts, and home environments show how even when support programs exist (like respite care/opieka wytchnieniowa), bureaucratic and informational barriers prevent caregivers from utilizing them. This visual evidence suggests that material exclusion operates not just through lack of resources, but through systemic barriers that make existing resources difficult to access.

Policymakers and service providers must recognize the economic challenges faced by caregivers and advocate for policies that alleviate financial burdens, such as subsidies for respite care and transportation services. Although this study was carried out in a single country and its findings may not be directly applicable to other settings, the widespread lack of comprehensive information on how to support caregivers remains a significant concern across various contexts [35].

Services, amenities, and mobility

The caregivers also reported challenges related to access to services, amenities, and mobility. Many expressed frustration with navigating healthcare systems and accessing appropriate services for their loved ones, which often felt overwhelming and disjointed. The visual data extends previous work on service barriers [16] by showing how these barriers manifest in the daily environments of caregiving. The photographs of waiting rooms, transportation challenges, and home modifications provide concrete evidence of the institutional factors that create what one participant called the "maze" of caregiving support systems. This was also revealed in time of COVID-19 pandemic when the health care systems were not prepared and not effective [20]. The lack of coordinated services can lead to increased caregiver burden and decreased quality of care for individuals living with dementia [29].

Furthermore, mobility issues emerged as a significant concern, particularly for caregivers who relied on public transportation or lacked access to reliable transportation options. This limitation not only affects caregivers’ ability to attend appointments and access resources but also restricts their opportunities for social engagement and respite. Addressing these mobility challenges is crucial for improving caregivers’ quality of life and enhancing their capacity to provide care. However, this limitation might be result of lack of information as other research indicates that transport possibilities in location, where study was conducted, are well evaluated [26] and there are programs dedicated for older persons and their caregivers (e.g. [Transport for Older Person 70 +]“Meleks dla Seniora 70 + ”).

An important contribution of the visual analysis is showing how institutional exclusion operates through (1) the built environment (lack of elevators, inaccessible buildings); (2) service system design (complex application processes, fragmented services) and (3) information systems (lack of clear guidance about available supports).

The window photograph (Photo 3) particularly illustrates how these factors combine to create what might be called "the architecture of caregiving constraints"—the physical and systemic structures that shape caregivers' daily experiences.

Knowledge sharing and peer support

Amidst the challenges of caregiving, participants also shared positive experiences that highlighted the importance of knowledge sharing and peer support. Many caregivers expressed a desire to connect with others in similar situations, recognizing the value of shared experiences and collective wisdom. This finding underscores the need for structured support groups and peer-led initiatives that facilitate knowledge exchange and foster a sense of community among caregivers. Studies suggest that informal support networks, comprising family, friends, and peers with shared experiences, can be more effective in reducing caregiver burden and enhancing positive outcomes compared to formal, policy-driven support systems [22]. However, it's essential to recognize that the effectiveness of support systems can vary based on individual circumstances, and integrating both informal and formal supports may provide the most comprehensive assistance to caregivers.

The role of technology in enhancing peer support cannot be overlooked. Digital platforms and social media can serve as valuable tools for connecting caregivers, providing them with a space to share experiences, seek advice, and access resources. As the caregiving landscape continues to evolve, leveraging technology to create virtual support networks may offer a viable solution to combat social isolation and enhance caregivers’ resilience [13].

Limitations

This study offers valuable insights into the experiences of family caregivers of older individuals living with memory loss, yet several limitations must be acknowledged. The small sample of six participants, recruited through purposive sampling from caregiver networks and community organizations, limits generalizability. This size was considered appropriate for an exploratory photovoice study emphasizing depth and meaning rather than representativeness. The gender imbalance (five women, one man) reflects broader caregiving patterns in Poland but restricts understanding of men’s perspectives. The domains of exclusion identified—particularly relational isolation and material strain—may be amplified for female caregivers due to entrenched social norms and structural gender inequalities in labor market participation and pension accrual. Future research employing larger samples should conduct comparative analysis to determine how the specific systemic barriers identified here impact male and female caregivers differently within the Polish welfare context.

The photovoice method, while empowering, depends on participants’ comfort with photography and may exclude those less confident with visual expression. Privacy concerns regarding photo consent further constrained image selection. Group discussions may have introduced social desirability bias, and self-reported data remain subject to individual interpretation.

Data collection occurred after the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, when lingering service disruptions and social isolation may have intensified caregivers’ sense of exclusion. Despite these constraints, the study’s value lies in its use of photovoice to illuminate the lived experiences of midlife and older caregivers across different stages of the caregiving journey.

Future research directions

While this study has provided valuable insights into the experiences of family caregivers further research is needed to explore the long-term implications of caregiving and the evolving nature of social exclusion over time. Longitudinal studies that track caregivers’ experiences and outcomes can provide a deeper understanding of the dynamic challenges they face and the factors that contribute to resilience.

Additionally, future research should investigate the effectiveness of various interventions aimed at reducing social exclusion and enhancing support for caregivers. Exploring the impact of peer support programs, educational initiatives, and technology-driven solutions can inform best practices and guide the development of targeted resources for caregivers.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings from this participatory study have significant implications for policy and practice. First and foremost, there is a pressing need for effective policies that adequately address the key barriers faced by caregivers, like lack of regulations to support them, lack of information how to deal with challenges. This includes advocating for increased funding for not only national or regional but local specific caregivers support programs (in this case for those who deal with memory loss, dementia), enhancing access to respite care, and improving the availability of information regarding financial and non-financial resources. Research has shown that comprehensive support services can mitigate the negative effects of caregiving on mental health [19], highlighting the importance of targeted interventions.

Healthcare providers – at various levels—must also play a proactive role in supporting caregivers. Training programs that equip healthcare professionals with the skills to recognize and address the needs of caregivers can lead to more comprehensive care models. By fostering an environment that encourages open communication and collaboration between caregivers and healthcare providers, we can enhance the caregiving experience and improve outcomes for both caregivers and individuals living with age related cognitive declines.

Conclusion

This study provides visual insight into how social exclusion operates within specific national contexts while suggesting patterns that may hold broader, cross-national relevance. The photographs and narratives illustrate how abstract concepts of exclusion and care take material and spatial form in everyday life. Through the use of visual methodologies, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of caregiving by:
