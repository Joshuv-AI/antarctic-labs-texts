Multi-target antidiabetic therapy with voglibose, ubiquinone, and tempol: synergistic effects on liver and skeletal muscle in experimental type 2 diabetes
Authors: Öznur Tufan Akarslan, Dudu Erkoç Kaya, Muhammed Bahaeddin Dörtbudak, Büşra Kilinç, İbrahim Büyüktaşkapulu, Fatma Göktürk, Muhammed Demircioğlu, Ayşe Er, Burak Dik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) is a progressive metabolic disorder marked by insulin resistance and chronic hyperglycemia. Although voglibose, an alpha-glucosidase inhibitor, is used in T2DM management, its efficacy is limited, particularly when used alone. This study aimed to investigate whether combining voglibose with ubiquinone and tempol—agents known for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and insulin-sensitizing properties—could enhance therapeutic outcomes.MethodsA total of 62 male Wistar Albino rats were allocated into eight experimental groups, including healthy, diabetic, and various treatment combinations. Glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory markers, oxidative stress parameters, and the expression levels of GLUT4, IRS1, PIK3R1 genes, as well as histopathological alterations were systematically evaluated for over a 7-week experimental period.ResultsThe combinations of voglibose + ubiquinone and voglibose + tempol + ubiquinone significantly enhanced insulin secretion, decreased blood glucose and HbA1c levels, and mitigated inflammatory and degenerative tissue alterations, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to voglibose alone and, in some parameters, even to the voglibose + glibenclamide combination. These combinations also restored GLUT4 and PIK3R1 gene expression in muscle tissue.ConclusionThe findings support that supplementing voglibose therapy with ubiquinone and tempol enhances its antidiabetic potential and may serve as a safer and more effective strategy, particularly for patients with advanced or treatment-resistant T2DM. Further clinical research is warranted to confirm translational applicability.Keywords: Voglibose, Ubiquinone, Tempol, Insulin resistance

Background

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent metabolic disease characterized by insulin resistance and resulting hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin secretion, activity, or both [1, 2]. As one of the fastest-growing chronic diseases worldwide, T2DM represents a major global health issue [3]. According to the IDF Diabetes Atlas 11th Edition (2025), approximately 58fvascul9 million adults worldwide are living with diabetes, a number projected to rise to 853 million by 2050. Given that diabetes-related deaths currently stand around 3.4 million per year and global healthcare expenditures exceed 1 trillion USD, this disease poses a substantial future economic and health burden [4].

T2DM is clinically defined by insulin resistance, which arises from weakened biological responses in insulin-target tissues such as the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue, leading to excessive glucose accumulation [5]. The liver plays a central role in glycemic control by regulating glucose production, storage, and lipogenesis. However, under insulin-resistant conditions, these processes become dysregulated, leading to excessive hepatic glucose output and contributing significantly to the characteristic hyperglycemia of T2DM [6–8]. Furthermore, skeletal muscle represents the primary site of insulin-dependent glucose uptake, and muscle insulin resistance diverts carbohydrate flux toward the liver, promoting lipid accumulation and increasing the risk of hepatic steatosis. Together, these organs constitute the principal targets of insulin resistance, underscoring their importance in the pathophysiology of T2DM [8]. At the molecular level, hyperglycemia fuels the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by the mitochondrial electron transport chain, causing a decline in antioxidant enzyme levels [9, 10]. The high reactivity of ROS causes DNA and protein modifications and lipid peroxidation, contributing to the development of insulin resistance [9, 10]. This state of excessive glucose metabolism and oxidative stress activates inflammatory pathways, which is a key factor in T2DM-related inflammation. Elevated inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-1β cause functional impairment in various tissues, exacerbating the disease [5, 11].

Pharmacological management of T2DM typically begins with monotherapy using oral antihyperglycemic agents, including α-glucosidases inhibitors, sulfonylureas, biguanides, and others [12]. If a single agent proves insufficient, combination (dual) therapy is initiated. Sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin secretion, are commonly selected as the second-line option [12].

As an α-glucosidases inhibitor, voglibose improves postprandial hyperglycemia and prevents impaired glucose tolerance in T2DM patients. This medication delays glucose absorption by inhibiting intestinal α-glucosidases, thereby lowering postprandial plasma glucose levels [13].

The pharmacokinetic characteristics of voglibose reduce the risk of hypoglycemia and allow its use as monotherapy or in combination with other antidiabetic agents, even in patients with renal impairment or those undergoing hemodialysis [13]. While voglibose is effective in early-stage disease or patients with mild hyperglycemia, its antihyperglycemic effect is relatively modest compared to other antidiabetic agents, which may limit efficacy in obese or advanced T2DM patients [13, 14]. Literature suggests that combination therapy with voglibose enhances glycemic control, particularly when single-agent therapy is insufficient. For instance, the combination of voglibose with sulfonylureas, such as glibenclamide, has been reported to provide superior glycemic control in patients who do not achieve adequate results with monotherapy [14]. However, clinicians must monitor for the increased risk of hypoglycemia inherent to sulfonylurea use [14].

Recent studies indicate that natural compounds with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities may offer synergistic benefits, including enhancement of insulin sensitivity and preservation of pancreatic β-cell function [15–17]. Nitroxides, such as tempol, are potent antioxidants shown to reduce oxidative stress, blood glucose, and triglyceride levels, while enhancing insulin sensitivity [18, 19]. Similarly, Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), a lipid-soluble antioxidant, has demonstrated antihypertensive and anti-inflammatory effects [20–23]. In diabetic mice, CoQ10 administration was found to reduce plasma glucose and HOMA-IR levels [16, 24]. Despite extensive research on T2DM management, current pharmacological treatments are often insufficient to reverse disease progression. Moreover, the development of drug tolerance and various adverse effects compromise the efficacy of existing agents. Crucially, there is a lack of studies evaluating the synergistic effects of voglibose—a drug with modest efficacy, especially in obese patients—with pleiotropic agents like ubiquinone and tempol [13, 25].

This study aimed to investigate the antidiabetic effects of voglibose in combination with tempol and ubiquinone, with a particular focus on the mechanisms involving insulin signaling, glucose uptake, and inflammatory responses in the liver and skeletal muscle. This study hypothesizes that combining voglibose with ubiquinone and/or tempol demonstrate superior antidiabetic effects compared to voglibose alone or the conventional voglibose + glibenclamide combination.

Methods

Animal material

This study involving Wistar Albino rats was approved by the Ethics Committee of Selcuk University Chair of Experimental Medicine Research and Application Center (Ethical approval number 2024-28). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. In addition, all procedures conformed to the EU Directive 2010/63/EU and institutional guidelines.

A total of 62 male Wistar Albino rats, aged 8–12 weeks and weighing approximately 250 g, were used as animal material in this study. Wistar rats were procured from the Selcuk University Experimental Medicine Research and Application Center. Prior to inclusion in the experiment, the health status of all rats was assessed to ensure suitability for the study. Animals were provided with food and water ad libitum. The composition of the diet was as follows: dry matter: 89%, crude protein: 21%, cellulose: max. 5%, ash: max. 10%, calcium: 1–2%, phosphorus: 0.5–1%, NaCl: 0.5%, and metabolizable energy (ME): 2850 kcal/kg. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Selcuk University Experimental Medicine Research and Application Center (Approval number: 2024-28).

Induction of experimental type 2 diabetes mellitus

In healthy animals with statistically similar lipid profiles (p > 0.05), an experimental T2DM model was induced based on the method proposed by K Srinivasan, B Viswanad, L Asrat, C Kaul and P Ramarao [26] and [5]. The rats were fed a high-fat diet (Table 1) for two weeks, in which 58% of the metabolizable energy was derived from animal fat. After the dietary induction phase, a low dose of streptozotocin (STZ, 35 mg/kg, SC) was administered. Rats with a postprandial blood glucose level of ≥ 250 mg/dL were considered diabetic on 5 days following STZ injection [5].

Group [Diabetic Control, (n = 8)]: Rats with experimentally induced T2DM (confirmed by measuring blood glucose levels) received 0.9% saline solution via gastric gavage for 7 weeks.

Group [Diabetic Control, (n = 8)]: Rats with experimentally induced T2DM (confirmed by measuring blood glucose levels) received 0.9% saline solution via gastric gavage for 7 weeks.

Group [Diabetic + Sham, (n = 8)]: Rats with experimentally induced T2DM received 0.9% saline and corn oil via gastric gavage for 7 weeks.

Group [Diabetic + Sham, (n = 8)]: Rats with experimentally induced T2DM received 0.9% saline and corn oil via gastric gavage for 7 weeks.

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose, (n = 8)]: Diabetic + Voglibose (n = 8): Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day, dissolved in 0.9% saline) via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [15].

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose + Ubiquinone, (n = 8)]: Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day, in saline) and ubiquinone (20 mg/kg/day, dissolved in corn oil) via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [15, 27].

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose + Ubiquinone, (n = 8)]: Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day, in saline) and ubiquinone (20 mg/kg/day, dissolved in corn oil) via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [15, 27].

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose + Tempol, (n = 8)]: Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day) and tempol (30 mg/kg/day), both dissolved in 0.9% saline, via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [15, 28].

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose + Tempol, (n = 8)]: Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day) and tempol (30 mg/kg/day), both dissolved in 0.9% saline, via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [15, 28].

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone, (n = 8)]: Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day) and tempol (30 mg/kg/day) in 0.9% saline and ubiquinone (20 mg/kg/day) in corn oil via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [15, 27, 28]

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose + Glibenclamide, (n = 8)]: Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day) and glibenclamide (1 mg/kg/day), both dissolved in 0.9% saline, via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [29].

Group [Diabetic + Voglibose + Glibenclamide, (n = 8)]: Diabetic rats received voglibose (10 mg/kg/day) and glibenclamide (1 mg/kg/day), both dissolved in 0.9% saline, via gastric gavage for 7 weeks [29].

Sample collection and biochemical analysis

At the end of the 7-week treatment period, all animals were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of thiopental sodium (40 mg/kg; Pental Sodium, I.E. Ulugay, Turkey). Blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture into EDTA-containing and gel tubes. Following blood collection, the animals were euthanized by decapitation, and gastrocnemius muscle and liver tissues were excised under sterile conditions, placed in DNA/RNA-free microcentrifuge tubes, and immediately stored in liquid nitrogen.

Blood samples collected in EDTA tubes were used for the analysis of HbA1c using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Trinity Biotech, Premier Hb9210, Ireland). Serum samples were obtained by centrifuging blood collected in gel tubes at 4000 rpm. The separated serum was aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Prior to measurement, samples were brought to room temperature.

Biochemical parameters such as ALT, AST, glucose, BUN, creatinine, LDL, HDL, cholesterol, triglycerides, etc., were measured using an autoanalyzer (BT-3000 Plus, Biotecnica Instruments, Rome, Italy). The levels of insulin (Rat Insulin ELISA Kit, Catalogue no: E0707Ra, BT Lab, China), glucagon (Rat Glucagon ELISA Kit, Catalogue no: E1066Ra, BT Lab, China), leptin (Rat Leptin ELISA Kit, Catalogue no: E0561Ra, BT Lab, China), glutathione (Rat Glutathione ELISA Kit, Catalogue no: EA0113Ra, BT Lab, China), catalase (Rat Catalase ELISA Kit, Catalogue no: E0869Ra, BT Lab, China), TNF-α (Rat Tumor Necrosis Factor α ELISA Kit, Catalogue no: E0764Ra, BT Lab, China), and IL-1β (Rat Interleukin 1β ELISA Kit, Catalogue no: E0119Ra, BT Lab, China) were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols, on an ELISA reader (Bio-Tek Instruments Inc., MWGt Lambda Scan 200).

Real-time PCR analyses

The mRNA expression levels of genes IRS1, PIK3R1, and GLUT4 involved in insulin signaling pathways in muscle tissue were quantified using real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). The frozen gastrocnemius muscle and liver tissues were powdered in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was isolated using a commercial RNA extraction kit (RF-1 total RNA isolation kit, Vivantis) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of the isolated RNA were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using a two-step RT-PCR cDNA synthesis kit (Onescript Plus, ABM), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The synthesized cDNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until use.

Gene-specific primers for the target genes (IRS1, PIK3R1, GLUT4) and the reference gene GAPDH were either designed using primer design software or selected from previously validated sequences in the literature, ensuring specificity to the rat genome. Primers were commercially synthesized. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (BlasTaq™ 2X qPCR Master Mix, ABM). The reaction (mixture 12.5 µL SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (2X), 0.25 µL forward primer (50 µM), 0.25 µL reverse primer (50 µM), 2 µL cDNA, 10 µL nuclease-free water) were carried out in 96-well plates using a LightCycler® 96 real-time PCR system (Roche). The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and a final cooling step at 40 °C for 30 s.

Gene expression levels were analyzed based on Ct (cycle threshold) values Expression data were normalized to GAPDH as the housekeeping gene, and relative changes in expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method [30].

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

The gastrocnemius muscle and liver tissues collected post-necropsy were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Following fixation, samples were washed under running tap water and subjected to a routine tissue processing protocol including dehydration, clearing, and paraffin infiltration. The processed tissues were embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections of 4 μm thickness were cut from each paraffin block using a rotary microtome (Leica RM 2125, Germany) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological evaluation.

Inflammation and oxidative stress in tissues were assessed through the expression of TNF-α and HSP-27, respectively. Immunohistochemical staining was performed as described in a previous study [31]. For immunostaining, primary antibodies against HSP-27 (Cat. no: sc-1048, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) and TNF-α (Cat. no: sc-1348, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) were used. Chromogen development was performed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), and Mayer’s hematoxylin was used for counterstaining.

Slides were examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX 53, Japan) by two blinded researchers. For each sample, six different microscopic fields at x20 magnification were evaluated. Lesions were scored semi-quantitatively based on severity as follows: absent (−), mild (+), moderate (++), and severe (+++).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS version 25.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality and homogeneity of the data were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data showing normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn-Bonferroni post hoc test was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The effects of various treatment strategies on glucose and HbA1c levels in diabetic rats were evaluated (Fig. 1). The diabetic control group exhibited significantly higher blood glucose level compared to the healthy control group (p < 0.05). However, Voglibose + Ubiquinone, Voglibose + Glibenclamide, and Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone treatments statistically significant decreased glucose level (p < 0.05).

Regarding HbA1c levels, the diabetic control group showed significantly elevated value in comparison to the healthy control (p < 0.05). In contrast, a significant reduction in HbA1c levels was detected in the Voglibose + Ubiquinone, Voglibose + Glibenclamide, and Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone treatment groups relative to the diabetic group (p < 0.05).

The effects of voglibose and its combination therapies on insulin and glucagon levels are presented in Fig. 2. Insulin levels were significantly decreased in the diabetic control group compared to healthy controls (p < 0.05). However, treatment with Voglibose, Voglibose + Ubiquinone, Voglibose + Glibenclamide, and Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone effectively prevented this decline, resulting in significantly higher insulin levels than those observed in the diabetic control group (p < 0.05).

Glucagon level was significantly elevated in the diabetic control group relative to healthy controls (p < 0.05). Notably, treatment with Voglibose + Tempol and Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone led to a statistically significant reduction in glucagon levels compared to the diabetic group (p < 0.05).

The effects of voglibose and its combination therapies on the expression of IRS1, GLUT4, and PIK3R1 genes in skeletal muscle tissue are presented in Fig. 3. In the diabetic control group, IRS1 gene expression was reduced by approximately 1.5-fold compared to the healthy control group. However, this reduction was significantly mitigated in the Voglibose + Ubiquinone and Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone treatment groups, with IRS1 expression levels approaching a threefold increase compared to the diabetic control group. Similarly, GLUT4 expression was found to be decreased by approximately 1.7-fold in the diabetic control group relative to the healthy control group. Notably, treatments with Voglibose + Ubiquinone, Voglibose + Glibenclamide, and Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone effectively prevented this downregulation, restoring GLUT4 expression to near-normal level. PIK3R1 gene expression was reduced by approximately 1.3-fold in the diabetic control group relative to the healthy control group. This decline was fully reversed in the Voglibose + Ubiquinone, and Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone groups, indicating a complete normalization of PIK3R1 expression following these combination treatments.

The effects of different treatment combinations on serum biochemical parameters in type 2 diabetic rats are presented in Table 2. A statistically significant increase in AST levels was observed in the diabetic control group compared to the healthy control group (p < 0.05). This elevation was significantly reduced in all treatment groups (p < 0.05). Liver tissues in the diabetes and sham groups exhibited marked pathological alterations, including widespread hydropic and fatty degeneration of hepatocytes, particularly in the central lobules. Coagulative necrosis, sinusoidal dilatation, vascular hyperemia, intercellular hemorrhage, and intense mononuclear leukocyte infiltration were prominent in these groups. Similarly, muscle tissues displayed eosinophilic myocytes, hyaline degeneration, necrosis, disrupted myofibrillar striation, vascular congestion, hemorrhage, and substantial intermuscular leukocyte infiltration (Figs. 4 and 5).

In the Voglibose (V) group, degenerative and necrotic changes were present in both liver and muscle tissues but were less severe compared to the diabetes and sham groups. The Voglibose + Tempol (V + T) group showed milder lesions, with reduced hepatocyte and myocyte degeneration, less hemorrhage, and moderate perivascular leukocyte infiltration. In the Voglibose + Ubiquinone (V + U) and Voglibose + Glibenclamide (V + G) groups, further reduction in tissue damage was observed, with improvements in vascular and parenchymal integrity. The most notable protective effect was seen in the Voglibose + Tempol + Ubiquinone (V + T + U) group, where hepatic and muscular tissues demonstrated near-normal histological architecture, with minimal inflammatory cell infiltration and no evidence of necrosis or hemorrhage. Control group tissues maintained a completely normal histological structure. (Fig. 6 and 7).

Discussion

Voglibose, an alpha-glucosidase inhibitor, is commonly used in combination with various classes of glucose-lowering agents for more effective management of T2DM, and studies have demonstrated that such combinations improve glycemic control [13, 14]. While voglibose can be combined with a range of antidiabetic medications, it is important to note that certain combinations may require careful monitoring. It should be emphasized that combinations with insulin or sulfonylurea drugs (such as glibenclamide) carry a heightened risk of adverse effects, particularly hypoglycemia [13], which necessitates careful monitoring despite potential improvements in insulin-mediated glucose tolerance [32].

Antioxidant-based therapies have shown mixed results in diabetic models. In diabetic rats, 12 weeks of tempol treatment did not significantly affect blood glucose levels or insulin concentrations; however, it mitigated diabetes-associated renal damage due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [33]. In contrast, tempol has demonstrated antidiabetic effects in Zucker diabetic rats by attenuating oxidative stress, improving insulin sensitivity, and restoring renal dopamine D1 receptor signaling and function [19]. Similarly, in T2DM rats, a 28-day treatment with ubiquinone reduced fasting glucose levels and HbA1c percentages without altering insulin concentrations [16]. In Goto-Kakizaki (GK) rats, CoQ10 treatment decreased HbA1c by reducing lipid peroxidation and enhancing glutathione peroxidase activity [34]. In clinical studies, 12-week administration of ubiquinol improved antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT and GPx), decreased HbA1c levels, and maintained HDL-cholesterol in T2DM patients [35]. Moreover, ubiquinone therapy has been reported to improve lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial respiration in muscle tissue, thereby ameliorating insulin resistance through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [36]. Collectively, these studies highlight the multifaceted role of antioxidants such as tempol and ubiquinone in modulating glucose homeostasis and tissue integrity in diabetes.

In addition to metabolic outcomes, ubiquinone has also been reported to influence signaling pathways related to glucose and lipid metabolism. In a diabetes model induced by a high-fat diet and STZ, CoQ10 treatment improved glucose and lipid profiles, insulin receptor and PI3K signaling, adiponectin metabolism, glucose transporters, and visfatin activity [27]. Notably, glimepiride, a sulfonylurea, was also found to influence these pathways beyond its classic insulin secretagogue function. Furthermore, CoQ10 has been shown to attenuate mitochondrial oxidative stress, inhibit inflammatory mediators such as NF-κB, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, and promote GLUT4 translocation in skeletal muscle [27]. These mechanisms collectively contribute to reducing insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, while suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis and inflammation, leading to improvements in both hepatic and systemic metabolic regulation.

Analysis of the current research findings suggests that tempol treatment alone was insufficient to enhance the antidiabetic efficacy of voglibose. However, combinations of voglibose and ubiquinone were found to be as effective as glibenclamide in reducing blood glucose and HbA1c levels, possibly by increasing insulin levels and reducing glucagon secretion. These combinations may also have modulated the insulin signaling pathway by restoring IRS1, GLUT4, and PIK3R1 expression in muscle tissue and elevating serum GSH concentrations, thereby enhancing cellular glucose uptake. Additionally, reductions in TNF-α and leptin levels may have contributed to improved insulin sensitivity. The triple combination of voglibose, tempol, and ubiquinone appeared to potentiate their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, providing greater regulation of oxidative stress defenses, inflammatory responses, and insulin signaling pathways. Such complementary effects may have protected β-cells against oxidative damage, improved insulin secretion, reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis, and suppressed glucagon secretion, ultimately leading to further reductions in blood glucose and HbA1c. These interpretations align with previously reported synergistic benefits of antioxidant–antidiabetic drug combinations in diabetic models [5, 9, 15, 16, 34].

In non-obese individuals with T2DM, leptin levels are often normal or reduced due to limited adipose tissue [37]. Experimental evidence indicates that leptin improves insulin sensitivity and reduces fasting plasma glucose levels in diabetic rats by lowering glucagon secretion and endoplasmic reticulum stress, independent of energy intake [38]. In hyperleptinemic rats, tempol has been shown to attenuate leptin-induced oxidative stress [39]. Similarly, CoQ10 prevents lipid peroxidation and preserves mitochondrial function [27]. Previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding the effects of glibenclamide on leptin levels, with some showing increases and others decreases [40]. In the present study, voglibose may have suppressed lipolysis by stabilizing blood glucose fluctuations, thereby preserving adipose tissue and elevating leptin concentrations. Conversely, voglibose combined with glibenclamide may have promoted lipolysis through sustained insulin secretion, reducing leptin levels. The triple combination of voglibose, ubiquinone, and tempol may have indirectly modulated leptin by improving insulin sensitivity, enhancing lipid metabolism, and possibly stimulating brown adipose tissue activity. These hypotheses are consistent with the reported interactions between leptin, oxidative stress, and antidiabetic therapy [37–40].

The pathophysiological background of oxidative stress and inflammation provides additional support for our findings. Elevated glucose and free fatty acids in T2DM increase ROS production, which activates NF-κB, p38 MAPK, JNK, and ERK pathways, triggering inflammatory cascades and impairing insulin signaling [41]. These processes lead to inflammatory and necrotic changes particularly in muscle and liver tissues [16, 42], as well as hypoxia, apoptosis, and degenerative lesions in pancreatic, renal, and hepatic tissues [43, 44]. Consistent with this, our study demonstrated vascular, degenerative, and inflammatory changes in liver and muscle tissue of diabetic rats, associated with increased TNF-α and HSP expression. Treatment with voglibose + tempol + ubiquinone significantly prevented degenerative necrotic lesions in liver tissue, although the effect was less pronounced in muscle tissue, possibly due to differences in vascularization and metabolic activity. The inhibition of diabetes-induced TNF-HSP cascade which plays a critical role in regulating inflammation, cell death, and stress responses by the combination therapies likely contributed to their protective effects on tissue integrity. Moreover, the triple combination exerted more potent anti-inflammatory and vascular protective effects compared to voglibose + glibenclamide, suggesting a synergistic mechanism. While ubiquinone or tempol alone were insufficient to fully prevent inflammatory and vascular changes, their complementary actions in combination therapy appear to account for the observed improvements. The expression of insulin resistance markers (IRS1, GLUT4, and PIK3R1) in muscle tissue further supports the therapeutic potential of this strategy.

The safety profile of combination therapies targeting multiple pathophysiological pathways in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is an important consideration for translational relevance [45, 46]. Ubiquinone has been extensively studied as an antioxidant and insulin-sensitizing agent, with experimental studies demonstrating that dosages up to 20 mg/kg in diabetic rodent models do not induce significant adverse effects; moreover, at higher doses such as 100 mg/kg, it has been reported to exert therapeutic effects against hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity [27, 47, 48]. Similarly, tempol, a nitroxide antioxidant, has been shown to reduce oxidative stress, improve insulin sensitivity, and ameliorate lipid profiles in diabetic and non-diabetic rats at dosages up to 100 mg/kg, without evidence of systemic toxicity or behavioral abnormalities [28, 49]. In our current study, the combination of voglibose with ubiquinone and/or tempol was well tolerated over a 7-week experimental period, with no observed mortality, clinical, and biochemicalsigns of adverse effects. These findings are consistent with prior preclinical reports, suggesting that the combined use of these agents may represent a safe and effective strategy to enhance antidiabetic efficacy while minimizing potential toxicity. Although detailed renal function parameters (e.g., creatinine clearance, histopathology) were not assessed, the observed reduction in serum urea levels suggests a potential renal benefit; however, this interpretation is limited by the scope of the analyses performed and warrants confirmation in future studies.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Although serum urea levels and other biochemical parameters were assessed, comprehensive renal function analysis, including creatinine clearance and histopathology, would have provided additional insight, particularly considering the potential renal effects of the administered compounds. The study investigated only single dosages of ubiquinone and tempol, and dose-response relationships were not explored, which may be relevant for optimizing therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, although ubiquinone has demonstrated promising antidiabetic and antioxidant effects, its efficacy may be influenced by bioavailability limitations, such as variable gastrointestinal absorption and first-pass metabolism. Finally, while mechanistic interpretations were inferred from gene expression and biochemical analyses, additional experiments would be required to fully confirm the underlying pathways. Despite these constraints, the study provides valuable preclinical evidence supporting the combined use of voglibose with ubiquinone and tempol as a potential strategy to enhance antidiabetic efficacy.

Conclusion

Ubiquinone and tempol combination together with voglibose therapy has produced encouraging outcomes, increasing the therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of T2DM. The combination may regulate glucose metabolism by reducing insulin resistance and alleviate the inflammatory processes associated with T2DM. Notably, the reduction of inflammatory markers in muscle and liver tissues and the observed vascular findings may suggest a potential protective effect against vascular degeneration. However, this interpretation remains speculative and requires further confirmation in future studies. Although the voglibose and glibenclamide combination used in the present study may show strong antidiabetic effects in clinical settings, it should be noted that this therapy carries a risk of hypoglycemia and may cause side effects with prolonged use. These results highlight the potential beneficial effects of the ubiquinone and tempol combination; however, more comprehensive studies are required to clarify whether this approach could serve as an alternative or complementary option to voglibose + glibenclamide therapy.

Larger-scale research is needed to apply the findings of this study to clinical contexts. Although the current data suggest that ubiquinone and tempol could be considered a supportive strategy in diabetes treatment, further research is needed to assess their long-term reliability and efficacy. In this regard, testing various dosages and combinations with the backing of scientific studies could potentially provide better therapeutic alternatives.
