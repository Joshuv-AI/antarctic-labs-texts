Cell Death Induced by Homoisoflavonoid Brazilin and Its Semisynthetic Derivatives on MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 Breast Cancer Cell Lines
Authors: Miriam Zuñiga-Eulogio, Michael Quinteros, Alberto Hernández-Moreno, Tadeo Hernández-Moreno, Tapan Sharma, Monserrat Olea-Flores, Mario Ordóñez, Carla Coste-Sanchez, Teresita Padilla-Benavides, Napoleón Navarro-Tito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flavonoids are naturally occurring polyphenolic compounds that have been extensively explored as scaffolds for drug development due to their diverse biological activities. Brazilin, a homoisoflavonoid with reported antitumoral properties, does not fully meet pharmaceutical criteria, and chemical modification of natural compounds is often required to enhance bioactivity and efficacy. Here, we evaluated the proapoptotic activity of brazilin and its semisynthesized methoxylated (OMe)3 and acetylated (OAc)3 derivatives in triple-negative MDA-MB-231, luminal A MCF7 breast cancer cell lines and nontumorigenic MCF10A cells. We assessed cell viability, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial integrity, and analyzed apoptotic features using confocal microscopy, Western blot, and RT-qPCR. In addition, RNA sequencing was performed to characterize transcriptomic changes in MDA-MB-231 cells following treatment with unmodified brazilin or its derivatives. Brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 significantly reduced cell viability and proliferation in MDA-MB-231 cells, whereas MCF7 cells exhibited a more subtle response. In MDA-MB-231 cells, treatment with brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 induced mild apoptosis-associated features, including chromatin condensation, γH2AX accumulation, and PARP cleavage. These effects were accompanied by a modest increase in oxidative stress and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that in MDA-MB-231 cells brazilin treatment regulates genes associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress and RNA metabolism, among other categories. Only high doses or long exposure to brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 affected the nontumorigenic MCF10A cells. Notably, the methylated compound exhibited minimal biological activity, as it did not significantly affect cell viability across the tested cell lines and induced no substantial transcriptional changes. Collectively, our results suggest that brazilin and its acetylated derivative induce mitochondrial stress and cell death in triple-negative breast cancer cells, potentially involving endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways.

Introduction

In cancer therapy, around 57% of currently used chemotherapeutics have been inspired or derived from natural compounds. Therefore, we continue looking for new leads for drug development in secondary metabolites of fruits and plants. , Among the natural compounds, flavonoids have emerged as a diverse group of bioactive metabolites. Flavonoids are diet-abundant polyphenolic compounds, classified according to their complexity and number of saturations to the core structure. Among these, homoisoflavonoids (3-benzyl-chroman-4-ones) are a subclass of flavonoids, subclassified into scillascillin-, caesalpin-, sappanin-, protosappanin and brazilin-type. , These compounds are known for their anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antidiabetic, antioxidant and antitumoral bioactivities. ,, The antitumoral activity of several homoisoflavonoids has been studied in models of lung cancer, − ovarian cancer, melanoma, nonsmall cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, leukemia, liver cancer and breast cancer. ,−

Brazilin ((6aS,11bR)-7,11b-dihydro-6H-indeno­[2,1-c]­chromene-3,6a,9,10-tetrol; CAS 474-07-7) is a homoisoflavonoid isolated fromCaesalpinia sappan andHaematoxylum brasiletto from Southeast Asia and Pacific coast area, respectively. ,, In traditional ethnopharmacology, numerous plant parts of C. sappan and H. brasiletto species are frequently used in infusions to address health issues such as fever, inflammation, dysentery, diabetes, pneumonia, anemia, and hypertension. Additionally, brazilin extracts are blended into balms to alleviate swelling, rheumatism, and wounds, among other therapeutic uses. Furthermore, purified brazilin inhibits proliferation and cell migration and promotes apoptosis of in vitro models of colon cancer, lung cancer, bladder cancer, cervical cancer, glioblastoma, and osteosarcoma. −

Apoptosis evasion from tumor cells is related to initiation, cancer progression, and chemoresistance. , Apoptosis is driven by the death receptor-dependent extrinsic or the mitochondrial-dependent intrinsic pathways; however, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress can also lead to cell death. The receptor extrinsic pathway begins with the binding of ligands (TRAIL, TNF, FasL) to the respective death receptors (DR4/5, TNFR, and Fas). Intracellular adaptor FADD recruits pro-caspase 8/10 to form the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), which activates the effector caspases 3/7. In the mitochondrial intrinsic pathway, the antiapoptotic BCL-2 and BCL-XL are outnumbered by proapoptotic BAX and BAK proteins, which promote mitochondrial outer membrane permeability (MOMP) to release cytochrome C (Cyt C); Cyt C binds Apaf-1 and pro-caspase 9 to form the apoptosome, then the effector caspases 3/7 are activated. Apoptosis is a finely regulated process that involves the participation of phagocytic cells, which guarantees the integrity of adjacent cells and tissues. Thus, understanding the subjacent mechanisms that drive cellular death resistance in cancer cells and developing specific and effective therapy against these remarkable targets. ,

In cancer therapy, the approaches targeting apoptosis have been mainly focused on inhibiting the antiapoptotic members of the BCL-2 (B-Cell Lymphoma 2) family and IAPs (inhibitor of apoptosis proteins); mimetics of SMAC (second mitochondrial activator of caspases) proteins; use of death receptor agonists; and enhancement of p53 activity by inhibiting MDM2 (Mouse Double Minute 2). A less common strategy to induce apoptosis consists of promoting the activation of the ATF4-CHOP-DR5 axis, which further enhances the extrinsic apoptosis pathway. − Moreover, ATF4 (Activating Transcription Factor 4) activation is also involved in endoplasmic reticulum stress (ER stress) response-mediated apoptosis; the unfolded protein response (UPR) sensor PERK, located in the ER membrane, activates eIF2α, which promotes the translation of ATF4. ATF4 induces the expression of antioxidative genes and proapoptotic genes such as BIM and DR5. ER stress-dependent apoptosis also involves the activation of alternative proteases like calpains and caspase 4, activating the JNK stress-response pathway and the Ca2+ release-induced loss of mitochondrial integrity.

Our group recently isolated and purified the homoisoflavonoid brazilin from the heartwood of H. brasiletto and then synthesized two semisynthetic derivatives by the addition of three methyl or acetyl groups to 3′, 9′, and 10′ OH groups of brazilin: brazilin-(OMe)3 and brazilin-(OAc)3, respectively. Here, we assessed their proapoptotic activity in the triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) MDA-MB-231, luminal A (LA) breast cancer MCF7 cells, and the nontumorigenic breast epithelial MCF10A cell line. We analyzed cell viability using Trypan blue exclusion and MTT (3-(4,5-di methyl thiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assays. Confocal microscopy, Western blot, and RT-qPCR analyses evaluated apoptotic features, and DHE, MitoSox, and TMRE standard assays assessed oxidative stress and mitochondrial integrity. RNA-seq analyses were performed in MDA-MB-231 cells to understand transcriptional changes derived from brazilin and derivatives treatments. Our study shows that both brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 reduce cell viability and proliferation across the tested cell lines, including MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells. MCF10A cells also exhibited reduced viability, although this response was typically observed at higher concentrations or with prolonged exposure, particularly after 48 h. In contrast, the methylated compound showed limited activity in these cellular models, with no significant impact on cell viability and no marked global transcriptional changes.

The responses of MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells to brazilin treatment revealed evidence of DNA damage and PARP cleavage that did not strictly correlate with the expression levels of BAX/BAK or BCL-2/BCL-XL, suggesting that multiple pathways may contribute to the observed phenotype. Brazilin-(OAc)3 elicited a qualitatively similar, though generally less pronounced, response. Transcriptomic analysis further indicated the involvement of stress-related pathways, including induction of the ER stress-associated genes such as ATF3, while oxidative stress and alterations in mitochondrial integrity appeared to contribute, particularly at higher concentrations. A measurable, albeit more modest, induction of DNA damage and oxidative stress was also observed in MCF10A cells following treatment with brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3. However, these effects were primarily associated with high-dose conditions or prolonged exposure, consistent with the overall dose- and time-dependent nature of the response across all cell lines. Under these conditions, the acetylated derivative tended to exhibit slightly reduced cytotoxicity in nontumorigenic cells, although this difference was not uniformly observed.

In the context of breast cancer cell lines, our findings support a model in which brazilin-induced cell death may involve converging stress-response pathways, including ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, rather than a single dominant mechanism. As such, these effects are not restricted to a specific cell line and should be interpreted as context-dependent and dose-dependent phenomena. Accordingly, mechanistic interpretations are best considered as proposed contributors to the observed phenotype. Further studies, including chronic exposure models and additional biological systems, will be necessary to more precisely define these pathways, but fall beyond the scope of the present study.

Results and Discussion

Brazilin and Its Acetylated Derivative Selectively Reduce Viability and Proliferation of TNBC and LA Breast Cancer Cells

Cell viability is a primary parameter for assessing the biological activity of natural products, reflecting both cytostatic and cytotoxic effects. Homoisoflavonoids, including brazilin, have been reported to modulate proliferation across multiple cancer cell models, although their effects may vary depending on cellular context and can include phytoestrogenic activity in hormone-responsive cells. To evaluate the activity of brazilin and two semisynthetic derivatives, brazilin-(OAc)3 and brazilin-(OMe)3, we analyzed their effects in MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative), MCF7 (luminal A), and MCF10A (nontumorigenic) cells.

Brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 exhibited clear time-dependent antiproliferative effects in both breast cancer cell lines (Figure ), with IC50 values in the low-to-mid micromolar range across the evaluated time points (Table , Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). At 48 h, brazilin showed slightly lower IC50 values (34.23 μM in MDA-MB-231 and 33.24 μM in MCF7) compared to brazilin-(OAc)3 (40.49 μM and 49.18 μM, respectively). At 72 h, brazilin maintained similar activity (30.91 μM and 33.67 μM), while brazilin-(OAc)3 exhibited IC50 values of 41.07 μM and 37.19 μM. By 96 h, both compounds remained active, with brazilin showing IC50 values of 30.96 μM (MDA-MB-231) and 31.36 μM (MCF7), and brazilin-(OAc)3 showing 36.15 μM and 33.78 μM, respectively (Supplementary Figure 1). Overall, both compounds displayed comparable antiproliferative activity, with modest differences that were not consistently maintained across time points.

MTT-based viability assays corroborated the trends observed in cell counting and proliferation data for the three cell lines tested here (Supplementary Figure 1n). These complementary approaches assessing the effect of brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 pointed to a deleterious effect on cell viability, while indicating limited activity for brazilin-(OMe)3. The data further suggest that nontumorigenic cells are also responsive to both compounds under prolonged exposure, and do not support a strictly cancer cell-specific effect.

Because brazilin-(OMe)3 produced weak cytotoxic responses and the dose–response curves in breast cancer cells did not reach the inhibitory range required for reliable IC50 estimation, we instead calculated IC10 values, defined as the concentration required to reduce viability to 90% of the control. While useful for identifying the onset of inhibitory activity, IC10 values should be interpreted with caution, as they are derived from the lower portion of the dose–response curve and may be sensitive to small variations in experimental data, particularly when a clear sigmoidal response is not achieved. Accordingly, IC10 values provide an approximate indicator of early inhibitory effects rather than a robust measure of compound potency.

At 72 h, IC10 values for brazilin-(OMe)3 were 29.68 μM (MDA-MB-231), 17.42 μM (MCF7), and 14.72 μM (MCF10A). At 96 h, these values decreased to 18.89 μM, 14.47 μM, and 10.80 μM, respectively. These data indicate a time-dependent increase in sensitivity across all cell lines, including MCF10A, and further support the conclusion that the effects of this derivative are not restricted to cancer cells. Overall, brazilin-(OMe)3 exhibited modest inhibitory activity, with measurable effects primarily at later time points, consistent with its limited cytotoxic profile (Figure ).

Morphological analysis revealed cell rounding and reduced cell density in MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells treated with brazilin or brazilin-(OAc)3, whereas MCF10A cells and those treated with Brazilin-(OMe)3 largely retained normal morphology (Figure ; Supplementary Figures 2 and 3). Based on these results, we selected 20 μM as the working concentration for subsequent experiments with brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3, as this dose produced measurable cellular effects while maintaining sufficient viability for downstream analyses. Brazilin-(OMe)3 was included at the same concentration for comparison. At lower concentrations (2.5–5 μM), brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 primarily induced growth-retarding (cytostatic) effects with minimal loss of viability. In contrast, treatment with 40 μM resulted in a marked reduction in viability in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells, highlighting a threshold-like response at higher concentrations (Figure ).Together, these results point to the importance of hydroxyl substitution patterns for biological activity, as acetylation may preserve or enhance the cytotoxicity of polyphenols, whereas methylation abolishes it. , It is also plausible that the effect observed with brazilin-(OMe)3 in MCF7 cells at low concentrations may reflect phytoestrogenic signaling mediated by estrogen receptor α (ERα), consistent with previous reports describing differential flavonoid responses in hormone-responsive breast cancer models. , Differences between luminal breast cancer models may also contribute to this response, as MCF7 cells exhibit distinct hormonal signaling profiles despite belonging to the same subtype as T47D cells.

The selectivity index (SI) was calculated to evaluate the preferential cytotoxicity of the compounds toward cancer cells relative to nontumorigenic breast epithelial cells (MCF10A), using the ratio SI = IC50 (nontumorigenic cells)/IC50 (cancer cells). Brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 showed SI values below 1 in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells after 96 h treatment, indicating limited selectivity under these conditions (Table ). Specifically, brazilin exhibited SI values of 0.72 in MDA-MB-231 and 0.71 in MCF7, while brazilin-(OAc)3 showed SI values of 0.68 and 0.73, respectively. These results suggest comparable sensitivity between cancerous and nontumorigenic cells at the tested time point. In contrast, brazilin-(OMe)3 was not included in the SI analysis, as its weak cytotoxic activity and poorly defined dose–response curves prevented reliable IC50 determination.

Brazilin and Its Derivatives Induce Apoptotic Features in Breast Cancer Cells

To investigate whether reduced viability resulted from apoptosis, MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and MCF10A cells were treated with 20 μM of each compound for 48 h. Steady state gene expression of apoptosis-related genes showed small increases in the expression of proapoptotic genes (Figure a–d) when treated with brazilin, and in lesser extent with brazilin-(OAc)3. Conversely, the methylated form of the compound seems to repress these genes (Figure a–d). Analysis of the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio indicated a modest shift toward pro-apoptotic signaling in MDA-MB-231 cells treated with brazilin (∼1.5), consistent with the moderate PARP cleavage observed under these conditions (Figure e–g). In contrast, brazilin-(OMe)3 also induced PARP cleavage and DNA damage markers in MDA-MB-231 cells, despite minimal changes in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio (Figures e–g and a,b). These observations suggest that the methylated derivative may induce cellular stress or damage through mechanisms that are not fully captured by canonical apoptotic markers, and that may not directly correlate with mitochondrial apoptotic signaling or reductions in cell viability.

In MCF7 cells, PARP cleavage was limited following brazilin treatment, and only a slight increase in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio was observed in response to brazilin-(OMe)3 (Figure h–j). Nevertheless, γH2AX staining revealed detectable DNA damage following 48 h treatment with brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 (Figure c,d), in the absence of clear nuclear fragmentation. Interpretation of these findings is complicated by the relatively high basal levels of γH2AX foci observed in untreated MCF7 cells, which may reflect replication stress or intrinsic chromosomal instability associated with this highly proliferative cell line. − Additionally, the functional deficiency of caspase-3 in MCF7 cells may contribute to an increased threshold for classical apoptotic execution, allowing accumulation of DNA damage without immediate progression to late apoptotic features. Under these conditions, cellular outcomes may depend on dose and exposure duration, with more pronounced effects observed at higher concentrations or extended treatment times, as reflected in the viability assays (Figure ).

In MCF10A cells, a modest increase in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio was observed in response to all compounds; however, PARP cleavage did not show a significant increase (Figure k–m). Despite this, DNA damage was detectable following treatment with 20 μM brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 (Figure e,f), which was later associated with reduced cell viability at higher concentrations and longer exposure times (Figure , Supplementary Figure 1). These findings indicate that cellular responses are not strictly dependent on canonical Bax/Bak-mediated apoptotic signaling, and may involve multiple, potentially overlapping stress pathways. However, given the modest magnitude and variability of these effects, such mechanisms should be considered proposed contributors rather than definitive pathways. , Importantly, the response observed in MCF10A cells highlights that nontumorigenic cells are also affected under certain conditions, particularly at higher doses or prolonged exposure. This observation warrants careful interpretation and highlights the need for further studies to better define dose- and time-dependent effects, as well as to evaluate whether differential sensitivity can be achieved under alternative experimental conditions.

Selective Mitochondrial Stress and Transcriptional Responses Underlie TNBC Cytotoxicity

Homoisoflavonoids can act as antioxidants or pro-oxidants depending on cellular context. Brazilin exhibited robust radical-scavenging activity in the DPPH assay (20 μM scavenging 29.7 ± 5.4%), whereas derivatives displayed minimal antioxidant capacity. We estimated the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) to scavenge DPPH radicals at >500 μM for brazilin-(OMe)3, and >1 mM for brazilin-(OAc)3 (Figure a; Table ). Despite this, both brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 induced mild cytoplasmic reactive oxygen species (ROS) in MDA-MB-231 and MCF10A cells (Figure b). To further assess whether these compounds induce mitochondrial oxidative stress, we measured mitochondrial superoxide production using MitoSox Red, a fluorogenic dye that selectively accumulates in mitochondria. Upon oxidation by mitochondrial superoxide (O2 •–), the probe emits a red, fluorescent signal proportional to mitochondrial ROS levels in live cells (Figure c–e). We next evaluated whether brazilin or its derivatives affected mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using the TMRE assay (Figure f–h). Quantification of fluorescence intensity in MDA-MB-231 cells, brazilin and its derivatives increased cytoplasmic and/or mitochondrial oxidative stress (Figure b,c). However, only brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 produced a dose-dependent loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Figure f). In MCF7 cells, oxidative stress was not significantly increased, yet higher concentrations of brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 caused a marked reduction in mitochondrial integrity, consistent with the decrease in cell viability previously observed (Figure ). In contrast, the nontumorigenic MCF10A cells showed only a slight increase in oxidative stress that did not translate into mitochondrial alterations at that time point (Figure b–h). Together, these findings suggest that at low-moderate micromolar concentrations, MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cells are potentially prone to oxidative stress and mitochondrial vulnerability, possibly exacerbated by their elevated basal ROS levels.

RNA-seq analysis revealed transcriptomic changes consistent with cellular stress responses and apoptosis-related signaling (Figures and ; Supplementary Table 4). Brazilin treatment resulted in 2885 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including downregulation of pathways associated with cilium organization and ER stress response, and upregulation of genes involved in rRNA/ncRNA processing and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (Figure a; Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). In contrast, brazilin-(OAc)3 modulated a smaller set of 193 genes, including induction of apoptosis-associated genes such as GCLM and downregulation of antiapoptotic humanin-encoding genes (MTRNR2L1/2/8) (Figure b; Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). The methoxylated derivative produced minimal transcriptional changes, consistent with its limited phenotypic and cytotoxic effects at a 20 μM concentration.

Taken together, these data indicate that brazilin and its acetylated derivative potentially trigger transcriptional programs associated with cellular stress and apoptosis, although the magnitude and breadth of these responses differ between compounds. Consistent with our functional assays, the results suggest that mitochondrial perturbation, oxidative stress, and stress-responsive transcriptional pathways may contribute to the cytotoxicity of brazilin in TNBC cells, rather than indicating activation of a single defined pathway. Notably, the larger transcriptional impact observed with brazilin compared with brazilin-(OAc)3 suggests that these derivatives may engage partially overlapping but not identical stress responses, particularly at higher concentrations. In contrast, methylation of brazilin largely abolished these transcriptional and phenotypic effects, highlighting the importance of hydroxyl substitution patterns for bioactivity.

Taken together, these findings indicate that brazilin and its acetylated derivative may exert antiproliferative effects in TNBC cells through the activation of multiple cellular stress responses, including mitochondrial perturbation, oxidative stress, and transcriptional programs associated with stress signaling pathways. While the magnitude of the transcriptomic response differed between compounds, both derivatives ultimately converged on reduced viability and cell death phenotypes in MDA-MB-231 cells. Importantly, methylation of brazilin largely abolished these molecular and phenotypic effects, highlighting the importance of hydroxyl substitution patterns for biological activity. These results support brazilin and brazilin-(OAc)3 as bioactive homoisoflavonoids with potential antitumoral activity in TNBC, while also suggesting that their cytotoxic effects may arise from combined stress mechanisms rather than a single dominant pathway, particularly at higher concentrations. Future studies should evaluate these compounds across a broader panel of TNBC and breast cancer models, as well as in more physiologically relevant systems such as 3D cultures and in vivo models. In addition, further work will be required to clarify the real involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress signaling, and other cellular stress pathways to the observed cytotoxic effects and to determine whether chemical diversification strategies, such as acetylation, can improve potency and selectivity.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Antibodies

3-[4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT; M2128), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH; D9132) and dihydroethidium (DHE; 37291) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, MO). MitoSox (M36008) red mitochondrial superoxide indicator was obtained from Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA), the tetramethyl-rhodamine methyl ester (TMRE)-Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (ab113852) from Abcam (Cambridge, MA). Primary antibodies anti-BAX (A0207), anti-BCL-2 (A19693), anti-cleaved-PARP (A19612), anti-GAPDH (A19056), and anti-phospho-Histone H2AX S139 (γH2AX; AP0099) were purchased from Abclonal (Woburn, MA). Secondary antibodies anti-rabbit HRP (31460) and Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-rabbit (A21070) were from Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific. Vectashield Plus Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (H-2000) from Vector Laboratories (Newark, CA).

Brazilin and Semisynthetic Derivatives

Brazilin ((6aS,11bR)-7,11b-dihydro-6H-indeno­[2,1-c]­chromene-3,6a,9,10-tetrol; CAS 474-07-7) isolation and purification from the duramen of H. brasiletto, as well as the semisynthesis workflow to obtain the derivatives brazilin-(OMe)3 ((6aS,11bR)-3,9,10-trimethoxy-7,11b-dihydroindeno­[2,1-c]­chromen-6a­(6H)-ol) and brazilin-(OAc)3 ((6aS,11bR)-6a-hydroxy-6,6a,7,11b-tetrahydroindeno­[2,1-e]­chromene-3,9,10-triyl triacetate) were previously described. Dr. Jorge Bello-Martínez (Universidad Autónoma de Guerrero) provided us with the initial aliquot of brazilin isolated and purified from H. brasiletto. Brazilin-(OMe)3 and brazilin-(OAc)3 were synthesized in the Design and Synthesis of Bioactive Compounds Laboratory, under the direction of Dr. Mario Ordóñez (Universidad Autónoma del Estado de Morelos). The chemical structures of brazilin, brazilin-(OMe)3, and brazilin-(OAc)3, as described before, are shown in Supplemental Figure 4.

Cell Culture and Treatments

Mammary gland-derived cell lines MCF10A (CRL-10317), MDA-MB-231 (CRM-HTB-26), and MCF7 (HTB-22) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). Breast cancer MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Culture Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Sigma-Aldrich) with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin G/streptomycin (GIBCO). The non-tumorigenic mammary epithelial MCF10A cell line was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20 ng/mL recombinant EGF 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone, 10 μg/mL insulin, 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cultures were maintained in a humified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. For experimental purposes, cells were rinsed with sterile PBS before any treatment. Different concentrations (2.5 μM, 5 μM, 10 μM, 20 μM, 25 μM, 30 μM, 35 μM, 40 μM, and 80 μM) of brazilin and derivatives brazilin-(OMe)3 and brazilin-(OAc)3 were administrated for 48, 72, or 96 h. Staurosporine (50 nM) was used as positive control of apoptosis, and DMSO was used as a treatment-solvent control. Treatment was finished by removing the media.

Cell Proliferation Assays

MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and MCF10A 1 × 104 cells/cm2 were seeded in 24 well plates along with different concentrations (2.5–40 μM) of brazilin and its derivatives. Cells were collected at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after seeding with treatments. Cells were trypsinized and stained with 0.4% Trypan blue, and the number of live cells and cell viability was measured using a Spectrum Cellometer (Nexcelom Biosciences, Lawrence, MA). Data was processed with FCS Express 7 software (De Novo Software, Dotmatics).

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was measured by 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. , MDA-MB-231, MCF7, or MCF10A cells were harvested and counted, 2 × 104 cells were seeded in a 96-well microtiter plate, then treatments with brazilin and derivatives were performed as described above. After 24 and 48 h of treatment, the media was removed, then 0.5 mg/mL MTT was added to each well and incubated protected from light at 37 °C for 4 h. Formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO and absorbances were measured at 570 nm in a Multiskan GO microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The percentage of cell viability was calculated as (absorbance of samples/absorbance of control) ×100.

Dose–response curves for brazilin and derivatives were generated from cell viability measurements at 48 h, 72 and 96 h; curves were fitted using nonlinear regression four-parameter logistic model (4PL) or a three-parameter logistic model (3PL) with a fixed slope (Hill Slope constrained to −1.0) to the IC50 and IC10 values, respectively. In cases were the dose–response curves did not reach the inhibitory range required for reliable IC50 estimation, we instead calculated the IC10.

Brightfield and Confocal Microscopy

Brightfield images of MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and MCF10A cells were obtained at each point of the cell proliferation assays, using a NIKON ECLIPSE Ts2 microscope. For confocal microscopy, MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and MCF10A cells were seeded in coverslips and treated with 20 μM brazilin and derivatives for 48 h, then fixed with 10% formalin-PBS and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. The primary antibody anti-phospho-γH2AX S139 (γH2AX; 1:250 dilution) was incubated overnight in 3% FBS-PBS solution, and the secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 633 anti-rabbit (1:250 dilution) was incubated for 2 h protected from light. Coverslips were mounted using VECTASHIELD with DAPI, imaged with a Leica SP8 Confocal Microscope, and analyzed with the Leica Application Suite X (Leica Microsystems Inc. Buffalo Grove, IL). All images were processed and quantified using ImageJ 1.44p software (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Western Blot

Cells treated with brazilin and the derivatives were solubilized in ice-cold HEPES-Triton X100 lysis buffer supplemented with Pierce protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (A32961, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell lysates were quantified with the Bradford (5000205, Bio-Rad) method, and 25 μg of total protein was resolved by 8, 10 or 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Sigma). The membranes were incubated in a Blocking Buffer (37515, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at RT to block non-specific binding. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies for cleaved-PARP, BAX, BCL-2 (1:2000), and GAPDH (1:5000 dilution). The next day, the membranes were washed with TBS/0.1% Tween20 and incubated with a secondary HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (1:5000 dilution) for 2 h at RT. Proteins were detected using Tanon high-sig ECL western blotting substrate (180–5001, Abclonal) in an automated photodocumenter UVP ChemStudio (Analytic-Jena). Images were processed using ImageJ 1.44p software (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Antioxidant Activity Assays

The antioxidant property of compounds was evaluated by the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenger activity assay. Briefly, 50 μL of brazilin or derivatives (2.5–80 μM) were allowed to react with 150 μL of 0.3 mM DPPH-ethanol fresh solution for 30 min at RT protected from light. Ultrapure water and ethanol were used for the blank control. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm in a Multiskan GO microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The percentage of DPPH radical scavenger activity was calculated as [(absorbance of control – absorbance of samples)/(absorbance of control)] × 100. Results are expressed as half maximal effective concentration (EC50); values refer to the concentration (μM) required for 50% of the antioxidant activity.

Cytoplasmic and Mitochondrial Oxidative Stress Evaluation

Dihydroethidium (DHE) and MitoSOX assays (Sigma-Aldrich) were performed to evaluate cytosolic and mitochondrial superoxide production, respectively. Briefly, 1 × 104 cells/cm2 were seeded in a 24-well plate and then treated for 48 h with 20 μM and 40 μM of brazilin and derivatives. After treatments, cells were incubated and protected from light at 37 °C with 10 μM DHE or 5 μM MitoSOX for 30 and 15 min, respectively. Then, the cells were trypsinized, and the fluorescence was measured using the Spectrum Cellometer (Nexcelom Biosciences) at 370/420 nm (DHE) or 510/580 nm (MitoSOX) of excitation/emission, as previously described. Data was analyzed with FCS Express 7 (De Novo Software).

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential

Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were determined by the tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE)-mitochondrial membrane potential assay (Abcam, Cambridge, MA). Briefly, 1 × 104 cells/cm2 were seeded and grown in a 24-well plate and then treated for 48 h with 20 μM and 40 μM of brazilin. After treatments, cells were incubated with 200 nM TMRE for 15 min at 37 °C protected from light. FCCP served as a depolarization control and was used as a control for low mitochondrial membrane potential. Cells were trypsinized, and the fluorescence was measured using a Spectrum Cellometer (Nexcelom Biosciences) at 502/595 of excitation/emission, as previously described. , Data were analyzed with FCS Express 7 (De Novo Software).

RNA Sequencing and Data Analysis

Samples for sequencing were obtained from MDA-MB-231 cells treated for 48 h with 20 μM of unmodified brazilin or derivatives; the RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer protocol. Samples were purified by chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation, aliquoted for sequencing and RT-qPCR analyses, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use. Two independent biological replicates were submitted for quality-control analysis, library construction, and sequencing to BGI Genomics (Supplemental Tables 4 and 5). Libraries were sequenced using the BGISEQ-500 platform, and reads were filtered to remove adaptor-polluted, low-quality, and high content of unknown base (N) reads. About 98% of the raw reads were identified as clean reads (∼65 M). The resulting reads were mapped onto the reference human genome (hg38) using HISAT. Transcripts were reconstructed using StringTie, and novel transcripts were identified using Cufflinks and combined and mapped to the hg38 reference transcriptome using Bowtie2Langmead. , Gene expression levels were calculated using RSEM. DEseq2 and PoissonDis algorithms were used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEG). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp) was performed on DEGs to cluster genes into function-based categories.

RT-qPCR Gene Expression Analysis

Total RNA was purified from three independent biological experiments of MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and MCF10A cells treated for 48 h with 20 μM of unmodified brazilin or the derivatives. RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 μg of RNA as a template and the HiFi Script RT MasterMix (CWBio, Beijing, CN). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with fast SYBR green master mix (CWBio) on the AriaMX (Agilent Technologies) using the primers listed in Supplemental Table S8, the delta threshold cycle value (ΔC T) was calculated for each gene and showed as the difference between the C T value of the gene of interest and the C T value of the GAPDH reference gene.

Statistical Analysis

Statistics were performed using ANOVA multiple comparisons with Bonferroni post hoc tests using GraphPad Prism 9.0.2 (La Jolla, CA). A statistical probability of *p < 0.05, **p < 0.001, ***p < 0.001 was considered significant. Three or four independent biological experiments were used for analyses as indicated in the figure legends.
