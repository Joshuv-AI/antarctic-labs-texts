Relationship between macronutrients and energy intake and liver serum transaminase levels in elderly athletes and non-athletes: findings from the Neyshabur longitudinal study on aging
Authors: Hossein Taghizadeh Bilondi, Seyyed Mostafa Arabi, Fatemeh Mohammadzadeh, Seyed Reza Mirhafez, Alireza Ostadrahimi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAssessing liver health and its determinants in the elderly is crucial. Lifestyle factors, including nutrition and exercise, may influence liver function. This study aimed to investigate the association between macronutrients and energy intake with serum levels of aminotransferases in elderly Iranian athletes and non-athletes.MethodsA cross-sectional study involving 811 elderly participants (369 athletes, 442 non-athletes) from the Neyshabur Longitudinal Study on Aging (NeLSA) was conducted. Dietary intake was assessed using a food frequency questionnaire. Serum Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were measured. Regression analyses were employed to evaluate the associations between macronutrient intake and liver enzymes, adjusting for potential confounding variables.ResultsIn elderly athletes, higher calorie, protein, and carbohydrate intake were significantly associated with elevated ALT levels (p < 0.01 for all). Additionally, higher carbohydrate and calorie intake were linked to increased AST levels in athletes (p < 0.05 for both). For elderly non-athletes, only higher protein intake was significantly associated with increased ALT levels (p < 0.05), while no nutritional factors were associated with changes in AST levels.ConclusionsThis study revealed that higher calorie, protein, and carbohydrate intake were associated with elevated ALT and AST levels in elderly individuals, particularly athletes. For athletes, all three nutrients were linked to elevated ALT, while only carbohydrates and calories impacted AST. For non-athletes, only protein affected ALT. These findings suggest that tailored nutritional strategies may be necessary to preserve liver health in active aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05445-9.Keywords: Elderly, Alanine aminotransferase, Aspartate aminotransferase, Macronutrients, Athletes

Introduction

The liver’s critical functions in metabolism, detoxification, red blood cell control, glucose regulation, and enzyme production make it essential to evaluate liver health in the elderly [1, 2]. Elevated serum levels of ALT and AST, enzymes secreted by the liver, often indicate liver damage. These elevated levels are commonly associated with conditions such as obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and metabolic syndrome [3–6]. According to the report of the Iranian Ministry of Health, NAFLD is responsible for 1% of mortality in individuals older than 15 years [7].

The aging process is accompanied by physiological changes that increase the susceptibility of older adults to liver dysfunction [8]. These changes include reduced liver volume and blood flow [9], altered enzyme activity, impaired regenerative capacity, and increased drug toxicity sensitivity [10]. Moreover, the prevalence of polypharmacy in this population can further compromise hepatic function [8, 10] and adversely affect exercise performance [11]. Therefore, preserving liver health is crucial for the elderly due to its indispensable roles in metabolism, drug detoxification, and maintaining optimal enzyme levels to prevent inflammation, cellular damage, and exercise performance detriments [8] such as glycogen depletion and reduced anaerobic threshold [12, 13].

Lifestyle factors like nutrition and exercise influence liver health [14]. Regular physical activity serves as a controllable intervention that may positively impact hepatic function and lipid metabolism in the elderly [15]. On the other hand, older athletes encounter physiological and dietary challenges stemming from age and exercise that could impair performance or nutrient absorption [14, 16]. These challenges include changing gut function with age, which can affect nutrient absorption and utilization; altered nutrient requirements due to the aging process and increased physical activity; and anabolic resistance, where the body’s response to muscle-building stimuli is diminished [16]. Notably, these age-related changes can impact energy balance maintenance, including the risk of low energy availability. This could rapidly lead to a loss of muscle mass, strength, and function, along with a transient depression of immune function, ultimately compromising exercise performance [17].

Previous research has shown that protein-energy malnutrition in children is associated with elevated ALT and AST levels, which decrease upon recovery [18, 19]. In a study conducted by Jalili et al. (2022), no significant association was found between BMI and liver enzyme levels after adjusting for caloric and macronutrient intake. These findings suggest that dietary patterns, rather than overall weight, may be primary determinants of dysregulation of serum liver enzyme levels [4]. Ryan et al. (2007) demonstrated that a reduced-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet led to a greater reduction in serum ALT levels compared to a low-fat diet in obese individuals [20]. However, contrary to this, Rodríguez-Hernández et al. (2011) did not find statistically significant differences in liver enzyme levels between low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets in obese women, indicating a more complex relationship between dietary patterns and liver function [21].

Despite conflicting findings from previous studies, research on the relationship between macronutrient and energy intake with serum levels of liver enzymes ALT and AST in elderly athletes and non-athletes remains crucial. This study could provide valuable insights into improving liver function and exercise performance in the elderly. By addressing knowledge gaps, it can contribute to the development of nutritional strategies for maintaining liver health and optimizing physical fitness in elderly athletes and the broader athletic population. The aim of this study is to investigate the relationship between macronutrient and energy intake and liver serum transaminase levels in elderly athletes and non-athletes.

Methods

This cross-sectional study is part of the NeLSA, conducted from 2016 to 2018 as part of the national PERSIAN initiative [22]. It represents the first comprehensive longitudinal study focusing on individuals aged 50 years and older in Iran. For the present analysis, we specifically focused on participants aged 60 years and above, aiming to evaluate various aspects of aging and monitor changes in health and well-being using a broad range of data collection methods. Further information on the NeLSA can be found in a recent publication by Aminisani et al. [23].

Measurements

Demographic

Demographic and medical variables included age (in years), sex, smoking (at least 100 cigarettes in the lifetime/ever used hookah or pipe), alcohol consumption (ever used), opium use (ever used), and underlying diseases. Underlying diseases included gastrointestinal diseases, metabolic diseases, infectious diseases, psychological diseases, skeletal diseases, cardiovascular diseases, respiratory diseases, osteoarthritis, and cancer.

Physical activity

The Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) was used to estimate the level of physical activity. The PASE has been validated in previous studies conducted in Iran [24, 25]. This brief and specific instrument is designed to estimate physical activity levels in older adults over a one-week period. The scale records the frequency and time spent on various activities, including leisure activities (such as walking outside the home, light sports or recreational activities like doubles tennis, jogging, swimming, cycling, singles tennis, aerobic dance, bowling, golf with a cart, shuffleboard, and fishing from a boat or pier), work-related activities (including both paid and volunteer work), and household activities (such as light housework, yard work, and caregiving).

Using the data from the PASE, participants were divided into two distinct groups based on their leisure activities: recreational athletes and non-athletes. The distinction was made by considering the amount of time spent on leisure activities per week. Recreational athletes were defined as individuals who participated in more than 4 h of leisure activities every week, while non-athletes were those who engaged in less than 4 h of such activities weekly [26].

Dietary assessment

Nutrient intake, such as calories, protein, carbohydrates, and fat, was evaluated using a Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) specifically designed for the PERSIAN study. This method, which is simple, cost-effective, and time-saving, is suitable for epidemiological studies. The FFQ contains 57 food items categorized into nine groups, carefully selected by expert dietitians from two previously validated FFQs in Iran. The goal was to develop a detailed yet concise questionnaire for evaluating dietary intake [27, 28]. This condensed version was developed to provide a comprehensive yet efficient tool for dietary assessment, considering the multiple questionnaires participants were required to complete.

Trained interviewers administered the FFQ consistently at the cohort center. Participants were asked to recall their dietary habits over the past year, reporting consumption frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly) and typical portion sizes for each food item. To enhance accuracy in portion size estimation, visual aids such as standard measuring tools and photographic food albums were utilized during the interview process.

To quantify daily intake, reported frequencies were converted to a daily basis and multiplied by the standard portion size weight for each item. This calculation yielded a daily consumption amount in grams for each food item.

Due to Iran’s Food Composition Table having limited data on nutrient content, the United States Department of Agriculture Food Composition Table was used to analyze foods and beverages for energy and macronutrient content. The FFQ used in the present study is adapted from the valid FFQ of the PERSIAN cohort study. The validity and reliability of the FFQ used in the present study are being evaluated and the results will be reported in a separate manuscript (in preparation).

Blood sampling and biochemical analysis

Venous blood specimens were collected from participants after an overnight fast of 8–12 h, between the hours of 7:00 AM and 9:00 AM. The blood tubes were centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute for 15 min to help serum separation. The separated serum samples were aliquoted into 1.5 mL tubes. The liver enzymes ALT and AST were measured in the serum using enzymatic reactions on a chemistry analyzer (BT1500, Italy) with reagent kits from Pars Azmun [29]. In this study, ALT and AST levels greater than 40 units per liter (U/L) were considered abnormally high or elevated [30].

Anthropometric assessments

The anthropometric measurements were conducted by a team of trained research personnel. Height was measured using a stadiometer with a precision of 0.1 cm, while weight was recorded with a Seca scale with a precision of 0.1 kg, with subjects wearing minimal clothing and without shoes or hats. The body mass index was calculated by dividing an individual’s weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters squared (kg/m²). To measure waist circumference (WC), the participants stood upright while a non-elastic tape was wrapped around the narrowest point between the lower rib and the iliac crest, over light clothing. The waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was derived by dividing the waist circumference by the hip circumference, with values less than 0.85 considered acceptable for women and less than 0.90 for men [31].

Psychological measurements

The anxiety level of participants was measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety (HADS-A). The HADS is a self-report scale consisting of two subscales: one for depression (HADS-D) and one for anxiety (HADS-A). It has a total of fourteen questions, with seven items for each subscale, where participants rate their feelings over the past week on a 4-point Likert scale from zero to three. The anxiety subscale includes questions one, three, five, seven, nine, eleven, and thirteen. Total scores are calculated by summing the scores within each subscale. The validity and reliability of the HADS were confirmed in previous studies [32, 33].

The depression level of the participants was determined using the CES-D10. The CES-D scale is a self-report inventory consisting of 20 items that can be completed in fewer than 5 minutes. It was developed by the National Institute of Mental Health as part of a study to evaluate depression in adult communities [34]. A shorter 10-item version was later proposed to address issues faced by elderly respondents [35]. Research has shown that the psychometric properties of the 10-item CES-D are comparable to the original version. Several studies have supported the clinical utility and strong psychometric properties of the abbreviated CES-D 10 [36, 37]. The 10-item version includes three items related to depression, four items for somatic complaints, two items for well-being, and one item for irritability. Each item requires a simple ‘yes/no’ response to minimize respondent burden, with scores ranging from zero to three. Hence, the total score for each questionnaire will fall between zero and thirty [38]. A score of ten or greater indicates clinically significant depressive symptoms, while a score of less than ten is considered as no depression [39]. The reliability, validity, and factor structure of the CES-D have been confirmed among elderly individuals in Iran [38].

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a comprehensive tool developed to assess sleep patterns and differentiate between individuals experiencing sleep difficulties and those with healthy sleep habits. This instrument evaluates seven key components of sleep: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is evaluated on a four-point scale (from 0 to 3), with the total PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores suggest poorer sleep quality, with a score exceeding 5 generally interpreted as indicative of significant sleep disturbances. The PSQI has undergone rigorous validation studies within the Iranian context, confirming its reliability and validity for use in this population [40, 41].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The normality of the distributions was verified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For normally distributed continuous variables, the results were reported as mean (standard deviation (SD)). For non-normal continuous variables, the results were presented as median (inter-quartile range). Categorical variables were displayed as frequency (percentage). Logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between abnormal levels of AST and ALT and nutritional intakes. To account for potential confounding variables, covariates with a p-value below 0.25 in the simple logistic regression models were included in the multiple logistic regression models. Variables with a p-value below 0.05 in the multiple logistic regression models were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The basic characteristics of the studied population are presented in Table 1. This cross-sectional study included 811 elderly Iranian participants aged 60 years and above, consisting of 369 (45.5%) athletes and 442 (54.5%) non-athletes. The study population had an almost equal distribution of males (49.6%) and females (50.4%). The majority were aged 60–69 years (63.4%). Most were single/divorced/widowed (83.8%) and had 1–8 years of education (48.6%). Regarding lifestyle factors, 90% reported not smoking, while 12.6% consumed alcohol, and 22.9% reported substance addiction (with opium or hookah/pipe usage). The prevalence of medical conditions was high, with 92.2% having at least one condition, most commonly cardiovascular disease (56.6%), metabolic disease (34.5%), and gastrointestinal disease (26.9%).

In terms of anthropometrics, the majority of participants were either overweight (39.1%) or obese (25.5%) based on their body mass index. The median calorie intake was 2,203 kcal, with athletes consuming higher calories (2,330 kcal) compared to non-athletes (2,056 kcal) (p < 0.001). The median protein (p < 0.001) and carbohydrate intake (p = 0.004), as well as fat intake (p < 0.001), were also higher in athletes. For liver enzyme levels, the median ALT was 15.3 U/L (p = 0.029) and AST was 18.0 U/L (p = 0.045), with similar values between athletes and non-athletes. Other characteristics such as depression, anxiety, sleep quality, and medication/supplement use are also described in the Table 1.

Relationship between energy and macronutrient intake with liver enzymes

The study revealed notable differences between elderly athletes and non-athletes in the associations of macronutrient intake with serum liver enzyme levels.

Energy and macronutrient intake with ALT in elderly athletes and non-athletes

The individual models for the athletes, non-athletes, and the entire sample indicated that higher calorie intake is associated with increased ALT levels. Notably, this association seems to be stronger among athletes compared to non-athletes. In the fully adjusted models (Model 2; Table 2), it was observed that for athletes, each 1-unit increase in calorie intake was linked to a 0.0020 (95% CI: 0.0006, 0.0035; p = 0.005) increase in ALT levels, while among non-athletes this association was weaker and not statistically significant (β = 0.0009, 95% CI: -0.0003, 0.0021; p = 0.140). Furthermore, in the fully adjusted model for all participants (Model 5; Table 2), the positive relationship between calorie intake and ALT levels remained consistent and statistically significant (β = 0.0015, 95% CI: 0.0006, 0.0025; p = 0.001).

In Model 2 for non-athletes, the regression coefficient for fat intake was 0.0258 (95% CI: -0.0181, 0.0699; p = 0.249), indicating a non-significant relationship. When looking at the total study population in the fully adjusted Model 5, fat intake was not significantly associated with ALT levels (β = 0.0203, 95% CI: -0.0098, 0.0505; p = 0.185).

Energy and macronutrient intake with AST in elderly athletes and non-athletes

The individual models for the athletes, non-athletes, and the entire sample indicated that higher calorie intake is associated with increased AST levels, although the association was weaker compared to ALT. Additionally, in the fully adjusted model for all participants (Model 5; Table 3), the positive relationship between calorie intake and AST levels remained statistically significant (β = 0.0006, 95% CI: 0.0000, 0.0012; p = 0.028).

Discussion

The findings of this study revealed significant positive associations between higher calorie, protein, and carbohydrate intake and elevated serum ALT levels in both elderly athlete and non-athlete groups. Additionally, increased calorie and carbohydrate consumption was linked to higher AST levels specifically among elderly athletes.

In alignment with our findings, a cross-sectional study conducted on residents of the 13th district of Tehran demonstrated that higher energy intake was associated with increased serum ALT levels in adults, which is a predictor of NAFLD [42]. Kwon et al.‘s study further supports our results, revealing that although the high-carbohydrate group (≥ 70% carbohydrate composition) consumed fewer total calories than the high-fat diet group (≥ 30% fat composition), the prevalence of elevated aminotransferase levels was higher in the high-carbohydrate group. This suggests that the percentage of calories derived from carbohydrates has a more substantial impact on aminotransferase levels than total calorie intake, acting as an independent risk factor [43].

Additionally, Ryan et al. divided participants into two groups following low-calorie diets containing either 60% carbohydrates/25% fat or 40% carbohydrates/45% fat (15% protein) for 16 weeks. Notably, serum ALT levels in the group assigned to the 40% carbohydrate diet were reduced by twofold compared to the 60% carbohydrate diet group. This difference remained statistically significant after adjusting for baseline ALT, sex, age, and weight loss. Furthermore, the changes in ALT levels correlated with improvements in insulin sensitivity and daylong insulin concentrations. These findings suggest that in populations at high risk of NAFLD, a low-calorie, relatively lower-carbohydrate diet can reduce serum ALT concentrations to a greater extent than a high-carbohydrate, low-fat diet, despite equal weight loss [20].

Higher carbohydrate intake, particularly from simple sugars like fructose, contributes to elevated aminotransferase levels and NAFLD development [44]. The primary mechanism involves increased de novo lipogenesis within the liver, as excess dietary carbohydrates provide substrates that drive hepatic fat synthesis [45]. As well, high carbohydrate diets have been associated with insulin resistance, which inhibits fatty acid oxidation, leading to accumulated liver fat [46].

Regarding protein intake, the study by Iwamoto et al. found a positive correlation between protein intake and serum ALT activity, but a negative correlation between the carbohydrate energy ratio of the diet and serum ALT activity [47]. A parallel randomized clinical trial study also showed a decrease in ALT and AST after administration of a high protein (soy) diet to NAFLD subjects [48].

The impact of protein on liver enzymes may be related to the composition of amino acids. Animal-based proteins, rich in methionine, homocysteine, and cysteine, undergo transsulfuration in the liver, producing sulfate and potentially contributing to liver dysfunction. In contrast, plant-based proteins containing high levels of glutamate and glycine may have a protective effect by alkalinizing the microenvironment [49].

Additionally, the mobilization of liver fat appears to be independently increased by protein intake, potentially mediated by branched-chain amino acids like leucine and isoleucine [50, 51]. These branched-chain amino acids can bind and activate the E3 ubiquitin ligase Ubr1, leading to the degradation of Plin2, a protein that stabilizes lipid droplets, thereby preventing hepatic steatosis [52].

The lack of a significant association between protein intake and AST levels in the current study contrasts with the potential mechanisms described in the literature, which suggest that certain protein sources or amino acids may have a more pronounced impact on liver enzymes. The differences in the results may be due to variations in the protein sources or amino acid composition of the diets examined, or other factors that influence the relationship between protein intake and liver enzymes.

Remarkably, the impact of increased macronutrient consumption, particularly calories and carbohydrates, on liver health exhibited a greater magnitude among elderly individuals engaged in regular physical activity or athletic pursuits [53, 54]. The differential impact of macronutrient intake on liver enzyme profiles in elderly individuals, particularly AST levels, suggests a potential role for exercise and physical activity in modulating these associations. Potential explanations include differences in energy expenditure, substrate use, and alterations in gut microbiome profiles between the two groups [55–57].

To elaborate, athletes generally have higher energy expenditure and different substrate utilization, potentially affecting how macronutrients impact liver function. Exercise significantly modulates gut microbiota composition, with athletes showing higher gut microbiota diversity and prevalence of beneficial bacteria [58, 59], which can influence metabolic health, including insulin sensitivity and inflammation [60, 61]. The interaction between diet and exercise is crucial, with protein consumption and dietary patterns influencing microbial diversity and function [58, 59]. In addition, the gut microbiome’s composition could influence protein absorption and body composition, potentially affecting physical fitness and sports performance [62].

While our findings provide valuable insights, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes the setting of causality between dietary intake and liver enzyme levels. Potential recall bias and measurement errors in the self-reported food frequency questionnaire may lead to under- or over-reporting of certain foods. Selection bias may be present if study participants differ systematically from the general elderly population in terms of health status or dietary habits. Moreover, the generalizability of our findings may be limited due to the specific characteristics of the study population and cultural dietary patterns. To address these limitations, longitudinal studies and interventional trials are warranted to investigate the long-term impacts of macronutrient intake on liver health in elderly athletes and non-athletes, considering the complex interactions between diet, exercise, gut microbiome, and liver function. Future research should differentiate between plant-based and animal-based proteins, simple and complex carbohydrates, and various fat sources (e.g., saturated, unsaturated, omega-3, omega-6). This approach would provide a more comprehensive understanding of macronutrient impacts on liver health, gut microbiota, and metabolism in elderly athletes and non-athletes, enabling more targeted nutritional recommendations. Additionally, incorporating a more diverse study population and considering regional dietary variations could enhance the generalizability of results.

Conclusions

This study found that elevated nutritional intake, particularly of calories, protein, and carbohydrates, was associated with increased levels of the liver enzymes ALT and AST in elderly individuals. These associations were more pronounced among elderly athletes compared to non-athletes. Specifically, higher calorie, protein, and carbohydrate intake were all significantly linked to elevated ALT levels in elderly athletes, while only carbohydrate intake was associated with increased AST. For non-athletes, higher protein intake was the only nutritional factor significantly associated with increased ALT levels.

The findings suggest that nutritional status may differentially influence liver enzyme levels in elderly athletes versus non-athletes, which could have implications for dietary recommendations aimed at preserving liver health in aging populations with varying physical activity levels. This observation highlights the need for tailored nutritional strategies for this active aging group to support optimal liver function and exercise performance.

Striking a balance between meeting the increased energy demands of physical activity and maintaining a diet that supports liver health is crucial. Managing calorie intake by ensuring adequate intake to support physical activity and energy needs, while avoiding excessive calorie consumption, is essential. Balancing macronutrients, including carbohydrates, protein, and healthy fats, is important for meeting individual requirements and supporting optimal liver function. Since individual dietary needs may vary based on factors such as age, health status, exercise intensity, and body composition, consulting with healthcare professionals, sports nutritionists, and practitioners can provide tailored dietary recommendations for specific circumstances. By following these dietary recommendations and seeking personalized guidance from healthcare professionals, elderly individuals can optimize their nutritional intake, support liver health, and enhance their overall well-being.
