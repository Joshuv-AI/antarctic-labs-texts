The impact of the United States most favored nation policies on prices of branded medicines and research and development
Authors: David Epstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe Executive Order (EO) reintroducing the Most Favored Nation (MFN) policy sets out measures to lower prices of branded medicines in the United States (US) and increase them elsewhere.MethodsTo understand the impact of the policy on prices and on pharmaceutical innovation, this paper reviews empirical literature on historic price trends in the US and other high-income countries, the impact of international reference pricing (IRP), and the impact of regulation and market conditions on Research and Development (R&D).Results49 studies were included. List prices are greater in the US than other high-income countries, and the difference in net prices has widened in the last two decades. The IRP literature indicates that for some drugs, MFN might be able to reduce prices in the US and increase them in reference countries, particularly for drugs where Medicare and Medicaid have a high global market share. In other cases, firms may strategically delay or cancel launch in other countries to avoid price comparisons. It is likely that MFN will provoke a reduction in global pharma revenues and aggregate investment in R&D.ConclusionA few countries are increasing prices as part of trade deals with the US. If countries do not wish to increase prices, then they should expect, and prepare for, slower pharma innovation. From a public health perspective, it is not just aggregate R&D that matters, but whether R&D investment is directed to projects which are likely to promote the greatest health gain.

Introduction

United States (US) policy makers have been arguing for decades that prices of drugs are too high in the US and too low in other countries. For example, Mark McClellan, Commissioner of the FDA, argued in 2003 that other high-income countries are free riding on US research and development (R&D) by not paying their “fair share” of the sunk costs [1]. Many other high-income countries, especially those that provide social insurance or national health systems with near-universal coverage, set up centralized mechanisms such as value-based pricing (VBP) or international reference pricing (IRP) to support price regulation or negotiation for new medicines.

VBP attempts to relate the price of a medicine to an assessment of its additional therapeutic benefit, often taking account of cost-effectiveness evidence and other factors such as availability of alternatives and severity of the condition. Formally, VBP regulates relative prices (the maximum additional cost allowed for a new therapy, above existing practice). VBP does not aim to limit overall pharmaceutical spending. Health systems that apply VBP principles set the amount they are willing to pay for a unit of health benefit.

IRP is a system for regulating prices in the home country based on prices in a group of referenced countries [2]. Designs differ in the choice of reference countries, therapeutic comparators, adjustment for price levels or currency conversion, how the reference price is calculated, sources of data and so on. IRP makes no assessment of the therapeutic value of the drug. Essentially, a health system that applies IRP is adopting other countries assessment of value as its benchmark [2]. While public healthcare systems in other countries were applying price regulation, legislators in the US barred Medicare from negotiating prices for a large category of medicines [3]. Medicare is the second-largest US health insurance program (after Medicaid), with 65 million enrollees. Other US insurers, including public programs such as Medicaid, could negotiate, but Medicaid only compared prices against those obtained by other domestic insurers, not internationally. While this does not demonstrate causality, prices in the US subsequently increased much more rapidly than other countries.

To address high US prices, the first Trump administration proposed the Most Favored Nation MFN Model in 2020, but this early version faced legal challenges and was withdrawn in 2022 by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). The Biden Administration addressed the problem in the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022, which allowed Medicare to negotiate on some drugs. The CMS have announced discounts on the list price of 10 branded drugs coming into effect in 2026 [3], though part of these savings will be offset by the loss of previously available rebates [4] (Supplementary Table S1).

The second Trump administration reintroduced the MFN in 2025 by executive order (EO) [5]. MFN implements IRP of branded, patented drugs for Medicare and Medicaid. CMS must set and communicate MFN price targets to pharmaceutical manufacturers [5]. If voluntary compliance fails, the EO threatens sanctions including allowing parallel imports from cheaper countries, forbid exports of certain medicines from the US, antitrust litigation, tariffs, and using the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to deny or revoke drug approvals to enforce MFN goals.

To date, three CMS programs have been announced, though at the time of writing are not fully implemented. “GENEROUS” is a voluntary scheme that builds on the existing framework for rebates from manufacturers to Medicaid. Up to now, rebates have ensured that net prices to Medicaid are no greater than prices available to other US insurers (“most-favored customer” rule) [6], [7]. “GENEROUS” extends this comparison to prices in 8 high-income reference countries, calculating the benchmark as the second-lowest net price, adjusted for gross domestic product at purchasing power parity [8]. “GLOBE” implements mandatory IRP for Medicare physician administered drugs in an outpatient setting (Part B), and “GUARD” implements mandatory IRP for self-administered Medicare prescription drugs (Part D). GLOBE and GUARD reference prices across 19 OECD countries, using either list prices (with various adjustments for rebates) or manufacturer reported weighted average net prices.

The MFN EO also mandates other measures, particularly discounted sales direct to consumer (that may reduce prices for some patients).

Previous published commentaries on MFN [8], [9], [10] take as fact that prices are greater in the US, often based on the RAND studies of list prices [11], [12]. List prices are published but only apply to patients who pay out-of-pocket; net prices take account of rebates or discounts, but are usually confidential. No previous paper has conducted a comprehensive, systematic review of empirical studies. Previous reviews of price comparisons have focused on a specific group of drugs [13], included a small number of papers [12], or did not focus on comparisons with the US [14]. There are narrative reviews [2], [15] and theoretical papers [16], [17], of the impact of IRP on prices and R&D, alongside one systematic review of the findings of empirical studies [18]. This paper updates, extends and synthesises these reviews and uses this evidence to inform analysis of MFN.

Methods

To provide insight on the potential impact of MFN on prices and R&D, this paper reviews the empirical literature concerning three interlinked questions.

Research question 1 (price comparisons): Comparison of the prices of branded, patented pharmaceuticals in the US with the prices in other high-income countries.

Research question 2 (Influence of IRP on prices): Empirical evidence regarding whether IRP of branded drugs in the US would be effective in reducing those prices, and the effect on prices in referenced countries.

Research question 3 (Influence of IRP, prices or profitability on R&D): Empirical studies on how spending on pharmaceutical R&D is influenced by the prices of branded drugs in high income countries, the profitability of pharmaceutical firms, or international reference pricing.

PubMed and EconLit bibliographic databases were manually searched. Elicit artificial intelligence (AI) was used to complement the manual searches. Grey literature were sought from newspaper articles, social media and institutional websites of CMS and Congressional Budget Office. No AI generated text was used in this paper. Search terms and further details of methods are given in Supplementary Material.

Papers were reviewed for their methodological quality. Some items used by tools to assess risk of bias of observational studies in human subjects [19] (such as sample size calculations and loss to follow up) are not relevant to these economic questions [14]. There are specific methodological challenges when comparing prices across countries or over time [12]. The drugs available or commonly used may differ, so calculation of a price index in each country should be weighted by resource use. Prices may need to be adjusted for inflation. Currencies may be valued at current-year market exchange rates, average market rates over several years, or purchasing power parity. For studies that estimate the impact of profits or market expectations on future innovation, methodological challenges include handling endogeneity (larger and more profitable markets generate incentives for innovation, but better products will have larger markets), controlling for supply side factors such as scientific advances, and the 10–20 year lags between research and market entry [20].

Studies varied greatly in their methodological characteristics, namely, the sample of drugs and comparison countries, whether rebates and discounts were considered, adjustments for currency conversion and price levels, and whether prices were weighted for volume consumed. Estimates of mean prices unweighted by volume of consumption may be distorted by high prices for treatments for small populations.

Cross section studies of list prices show that prices were greater in the US (Fig. 1 and Table 1). For example, RAND [11] found list prices overall in the US in 2022 were 278% of other OECD countries, and 504% for the top 60 branded prescription drugs. However, the cost burden in the US is mainly attributable to the minority of patients who consume branded originator drugs (Supplementary Table S2). 90% by volume of drugs prescribed in the US are unbranded generics, a market penetration which is far greater than other G7.

The RAND study estimated comprehensive, methodologically robust indices of list prices in 2022. However, list prices are not informative about the cost of drugs to insurers. Average discounts reported in company filings range from 9% to 74% of the wholesale acquisition cost [26]. Unfortunately, all but one of the studies estimating net prices use data from 2013 or earlier (Table 1, Table 2).

Studies from 2005 showed US net prices of biologic drugs [30] were in the middle of 9 European countries (Supplementary Table S4 and Fig. 2), and net prices of single-source drugs in the US were closer to those in other countries than multi-source drugs [29]. Net prices of the highest selling branded patent drugs in 2005 were broadly similar in the US to other countries, but by 2010 prices were substantially greater in the US [36]. A study from 1981 to 1992 found that launch prices tended to be greater in the US than 6 other high-income countries, but the difference was not statistically significant [48]. A study from 1998 to 2003 showed US prices were greater than those in heavily regulated countries, but similar to Germany and other lightly regulated countries [44]. Prices were similar in 2006 in the US and Australia for new drugs that offered significant clinical benefit [33].

Later studies show that the difference in net prices of branded, patented drugs between the US and other countries widened in the last two decades. Prices including tax and pharmacy fees for 19 branded in-patent drugs were 17% greater in the US than G5 in 2004, increasing to 127% greater in 2007 [36]. Net prices for 80 physician-administered branded drugs available in 2004 were 29% greater in the US than Germany before 2011, but by 2018 the price difference for those same drugs had widened by another 28.9% [43]. The authors attribute this to the introduction in 2011 of the AMNOG law, which introduced price regulation by tying prices of new drugs to assessment of added clinical benefit one year after launch. Net prices of the highest selling patented branded drugs in 2010 in the US tended to be greater than 7 high income comparator countries [28]. Prices of primary care drugs in 2015 were 152% higher in the US than comparator countries [31]. The price of sofosbuvir for hepatitis C was greater in the US in 2015 than 30 other countries when currency was converted at market exchange rates, but there was more variation between countries when PPP were used [34]. Mean net launch prices increased from 2011 to 2022 by 19.7% per year in the US, compared with 6.0% per year in Germany and 3.2% per year in Switzerland, though this study only compared two years, and did not weight prices by volume of use [22].

Factors influencing prices in the US and other countries

The reasons identified in the literature for the rapid increase in prices in the US are multiple and complex.

First, Medicare has been forbidden to negotiate prices since 2003 [3]. Proponents of the 2003 bill argued that government “interference” in price negotiation, by leveraging its massive bargaining power, would stifle innovation in the sector. It was hoped that competition among private intermediaries to supply Medicare would moderate prices.

Other US health plans, including government sponsored ones like Veterans Affairs (VA) and Medicaid, could negotiate discounts. The VA negotiates discounts with manufacturers averaging 40% - 50% of the list price, with considerable variation [26].

In the absence of price regulation and arbitrage (parallel trade), basic economic theory predicts that a profit-maximising monopolist that produces a product for sale in multiple countries will set the profit markup in each country equal to the reciprocal of the price-elasticity of demand in that country (Lerner rule). That is, prices are higher in the US because demand is more inelastic. This arises because the US is wealthier, and the healthcare system is fragmented, allowing manufacturers the opportunity to price discriminate. Private insurers compete for corporate clients (employers). To attract top employers, insurers must offer an attractive benefits package that includes new technologies quickly after regulator approval. Medicare is required cover six protected classes of drugs, which limits its ability to refuse coverage based on price.

The empirical evidence seems to corroborate the economic theory that prices are greater where price -elasticity is low. Patented innovator drugs with few substitutes have little need to voluntarily offer price discounts to encourage take-up. Several studies find that originator drug does not reduce its net price in the US after the entry of a within-class competitor [13], [39], [40], [46], [49]. Hernandez found that discounts can offset rising list prices, but only after there are several exchangeable brands or biosimilar / generic competition [42].

Social health insurers in other high-income countries rely on price regulation (VBP or IRP or both) rather than competition to control prices of on-patent medicines [16]. VBP aims to align the additional cost of new treatments (over existing therapies) to added therapeutic benefit, though this is better interpreted as an intention, rather than a rule. Salas-Vega et al. find overall survival gain and quality of life benefits were weakly correlated with cancer drug costs in France and the United Kingdom, but not at all in the US or Australia [27]. Other US studies find weak or no correlation between prices and added therapeutic benefit [50] even after controlling for other factors that might influence payers' willingness-to-pay, such as orphan population, presenting RCT evidence, or the drug having accelerated approval [27], [51]. Prices in other high-income countries may be closer to US prices for recently launched, innovator medicines and highly specialized, high cost drugs, such as for treating very rare diseases [23], [25].

Second, the administration of pharmaceutical plans is often delegated by insurers to intermediaries or pharmacy benefit managers (PBM) and it is not necessarily the case that discounts negotiated by PBM filter through to lower prices for the government or patients [52], [53]. The 3 largest PBM manage nearly 80% of prescriptions in the US. Some authors maintain that intermediaries, rather than manufacturers, capture the bulk of excess profits by exploiting the opacity of the industry [52], [54]. Schulman estimates than net revenues for a sample of 13 major pharma manufacturers over 2011–2016 grew by 2.7% annually whereas rebates and other payments increased by 15% annually. However, other authors argue that PBMs can leverage economies of scale and reduce transaction costs by providing specialist management and administration services [55].

Third, another line of argument is that prices are high in the US because US insurers and consumers are willing to pay premium prices to obtain greater, and faster, availability of innovative drugs. In a sample of 287 new drugs in 2022, 74% were available in the US market, compared with 52% in Germany [56], and the timing of launch has been, on average, about one year earlier in the US than other major OECD markets [56]. “Availability” in this context refers to whether the medicine is on sale in the country, but not whether patients without insurance can afford to buy it or afford the coinsurance.

Fourth, prices of branded drugs in the US market follow a different and more volatile dynamic than regulated European markets, typically a year-on-year increase in price post launch with a sharp fall after patent expiry (Table 2) [57]. Different price dynamics complicate any comparison of prices between the US and other countries.

Dubois (2022) estimated own and cross price elasticities of branded patent drugs, competitors and generics using a structural equation model from revenues and sales volumes of hospital drugs in the US and Canada from 2002 to 2013 [58]. They also estimated the bargaining power of regulators (vis-à-vis manufacturers) for different classes of drugs in each country, based on market share and estimates of marginal costs. With these data, they model outcomes of IRP using game theory. If a monopolist firm can perfectly price discriminate, it will set a price in each country that reflects its willingness to pay (Lerner rule). All countries whose willingness to pay is above marginal cost can be supplied. IRP obliges the firm to charge the same price in the US as the referenced country. Now the firm must choose the single price that maximises global profit. If it lowers the price in the US to match the foreign price, without raising the price in the referenced country, it will lose substantial US revenue. If the firm raises the price above the willingness to pay of the referenced country, the referenced country might delist the drug, and the firm would lose all revenue from the referenced country. However, if the referenced country is small, the loss to the firm from not increasing the price in the referenced country may be greater than the loss from increasing the price. These payoffs are known both to the firm and the ex-US payer, and so IRP strengthens the bargaining power of the firm when negotiating with the referenced country. The small country knows that the firm cannot offer it discounts without triggering larger revenue loss in the US. Overall, the model predicts that IRP would result in small price decreases in the US, but large price increases in referenced countries, for the products where the firm has strong bargaining leverage.

This finding is largely confirmed by empirical results from other papers. IPR induces firms to keep prices high in referenced countries [60]. Within the US, Medicaid's Most-Favored-Customer Procurement Rules works in firms' favour by making it easier to extract high prices from commercial US customers [7]. The effect is stronger when Medicaid has a greater market share [6]. Because the Medicaid “GENEROUS” program extends price-comparison to the international stage, a similar effect might be seen in referenced countries.

IRP can induce firms to strategically delay launch in reference countries. Because reference countries may themselves reference other countries, this can lead firms to delay launch elsewhere, especially where the market is small or prices are usually low [62], [64]. Medicaid's Most-Favored-Customer rules increased firms' incentive to introduce new versions with different dosage amounts or route types (e.g., capsule, liquid, tablet) that would have unrestricted base prices [6].

Naïve estimates [8], [65] of the savings that MFN might achieve for US consumers can be exaggerated, if they do not account for mitigating strategies by firms, such as increase in prices in referenced countries, launch delays, and reformulations [2], [11].

Effect of market conditions and price regulation on global R&D

11 papers (Table 4) empirically estimated the effect of market conditions on R&D [20], [66], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73], [74], [75]. Acemoglu (2004), using panel data from 1996 to 1998, found a 1% increase in the size of a potential market (instrumented by exogenous trends in aging) leads to a 4–6% increase in the total number of new molecular entities entering the U.S. market. Vernon (2005) using financial filings by the top 14 pharma firms from 1994 to 1997, found current profitability and lagged cash flow were significantly associated with firm R&D intensity, and estimated that if price regulation in the U.S reduced profit margins to EU levels, pharmaceutical R&D investment would decrease by between 23% to 33%. Giacotto (2005), using financial filings by 48 pharmaceutical firms between 1952 and 2001, estimated that a 10% price rise leads to a 6% R&D intensity increase. Golec (2006), Kyle (2007), Golec (2010) and Eger (2014) found U.S. pharmaceutical firms are more profitable and invest more in R&D than EU firms, which these authors attribute to exposure to price regulation in EU countries. Koenig (2011) found pharmaceutical firms were more likely to finance new direct investments in countries with fewer price controls. However, the effect is restricted to non-manufacturing investments, especially those related to sales, marketing and administrative functions. Blume-Kohout (2012) found the expansion in 2006 of the coverage for Medicare Part D was associated with significant increases in preclinical testing and clinical trials for those drug classes with large Medicare market share (such as Alzheimer's disease), but did not affect R&D in drug classes with small Medicare market share (such as oral contraceptives). Dravnove (2014) found most of this effect was concentrated among products addressing diseases that already had multiple treatments. Shaikh (2020) used firm-level data (2000 to 2017) for the world's top ten pharmaceutical firms. Their study confirms that price regulation negatively affects R&D investment, but firm-specific factors are more influential, such as the preference for financing investment by retained earnings, new equity or debt. Garthwaite (2021) exploited data from the 2007–2010 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey. They find no evidence that greater anticipated demand from Medicaid expansions in 2010 led to greater R&D activity in therapeutic areas with high Medicaid market share. Unlike Medicare, prices of drugs for Medicaid have been strictly regulated for many years [6], [7]. This suggests that actions by lower-reimbursing insurers do not impact innovation incentives. Discussion

The first aim of this review was to compare prices of branded patented drugs in the US against other countries. The included papers are heterogenous, in terms of the drugs included, time horizons, countries compared, weighting, inflation, exchange rates, data on discounts, and use of statistical methods. The US itself is a fragmented and changing landscape with multiple insurers and intermediaries. Hence results are narrative and tentative.

Net prices were not substantially different in the US from many other high-income countries 20 to 30 years ago, but the difference in both list and net prices of branded drugs between the US and other countries widened in the last two decades (Fig. 2 and Table 2).

The second aim was to examine how MFN might influence prices in the US and elsewhere. The goals of MFN are to reduce prices of branded drugs in the US and increase them in other high- income countries. While legal and other challenges may be expected, there is empirical evidence from the IRP literature that MFN can achieve both these outcomes, particularly for drugs where Medicare and Medicaid have a high global market share (Table 3) [58].

Announcements to date have been agreements between manufacturers and Medicaid and Medicare, and discounts on direct-to-consumer sales [8]. Like the predecessor IRA model (Supplementary Table S1), these agreements cover a limited number of drugs, many of which obtained market authorization a decade or more ago (“existing drugs” MFN). While the IRA only compared Medicare prices to other US payers, MFN extends comparisons to other countries. The US government is planning MFN to target prices at launch in commercial, Medicare, Medicaid and cash markets (“prospective” MFN) [76]. Such a measure would represent a far-reaching change in US pharmaceutical policy, as for the first time firms will negotiate prices with the US federal government before launch (as is common in European markets). It is conceivable (as occurs in European markets) that protracted negotiation could lead to delays in US launch, or, if agreement cannot be reached, that the firm might choose not to launch in the US. Evidently, at the time of launch, there will be little price data from other countries to benchmark. US officials suggest that the agreements will also regulate price increases post-launch [8]. Medicaid already has mechanisms for doing this [7].

Prices of drugs do not just reflect a country's aggregate willingness to pay for healthcare, but also determine relative priorities, in terms of the conditions and outcomes that should be endowed with more resources than others. IRP imports reference countries' assessments, rather than the values and goals of US citizens. VBP in reference countries also sets prices of drugs that reflect the cost structure of other health services (hospital, primary care etc), which may be cheaper outside the US. Some US commentators have suggested that launch prices should be informed by comparative value assessments [77], but this seems unlikely to be implemented in the MFN framework.

In principle, the MFN target is based on the drug's net price, using financial data disclosed by the manufacturer [8]. Given the opacity of pricing of branded pharmaceuticals in all countries, it may be difficult to verify that MFN is working. Some firms (and countries) may make gestures on list prices to win favour with the US administration, without any meaningful change in net prices [78].

The third aim was to examine the impact of MFN and firms' revenue expectations on R&D. The overall impact of MFN on firms' global revenues is difficult to predict. The aim of MFN is to reduce prices in the US and increase them in other countries. Even if this outcome is achieved, given the size of the US market (Supplementary Table S2), it seems unlikely that price increases in other countries could compensate the fall in revenue in the US. Much depends on the reaction of firms and payers. If firms delay or cancel launch elsewhere to avoid price comparisons, then there would be no impact on US revenues, while ex-US revenues will be lower. Reductions in prices of branded drugs in the US may increase consumption, perhaps substituting brands for generics.

The MFN EO threatens tariffs against firms and countries that do not cooperate with MFN goals. Tariffs (if used) will reduce revenues of exporters to the US and may increase prices for consumers. As a protectionist measure, tariffs may blunt competition and provide cover for US domestic manufacturers to also increase prices.

The literature on the relation between market conditions and R&D is difficult to interpret (Table 4). Global revenues of the 16 Big Pharma have been increasing by 6% per year in real terms during 2001–2020 [79]. Global R&D spending has increased 39% in real terms since 2010 [80]. It might be tempting to infer that one causes the other, that is, the growth in R&D is being driven by growth in profitability and past cashflows. Some empirical studies corroborate this result [68], [71]. However, this finding implies that capital markets are inefficient. If capital markets were efficient, over the long run, markets (through venture capital, biotech startups etc) would direct investment at future profitable areas [6], [7], [20], [72]. Past revenues, incumbency, retained earnings, or sunk costs would only be influential on future R&D to the extent that they might be indicative of future earnings; a firm with large retained earnings but an unproductive R&D pipeline would be under pressure to return the cash to shareholders, or be a target for take- over [81], [82].

On aggregate, the private pharmaceutical industry globally spent $129 billion on R&D in 2021, $92.2 billion of this in the US. In 2019, across all pharmaceutical firms worldwide, 30% of gross value added was spent on R&D, while the R&D intensity of US companies is around 45% [79]. Several authors attribute this difference in R&D spending between US and EU firms to price regulation in EU countries [61], [66], [67], [74]. As before, this argument is problematic. First, it assumes (rather than proves empirically) that net prices are in fact lower in the EU. Second, “price regulation” is undefined by these studies, and might bundle together health technology assessment, international reference pricing, therapeutic reference pricing, overall profit regulation, or mandatory price cuts [70]. Each of these policies operates according to a different mechanism and may affect patented drugs differently from generic or off-patent. Third, price regulation in any one country might affect the global profit expectations of the industry, and hence global R&D investment. Expectations about future global returns to drug R&D have been largely supported by prices available in the US, where 63% of global revenues are earned (Supplementary Table 2). But the location of R&D assets will typically be only loosely related to where any final product will be sold [70]. Research produces knowledge, which the firm uses to develop a physical innovative product to sell in any location. R&D location decisions will then be influenced more by supply-side variables such as a skilled workforce, infrastructure, network opportunities, or tax credits. Government policies can directly influence each of these, through education, infrastructure construction, basic science and fiscal policy. Since 2010, government spending on R&D in health has increased by 45%. Governments in OECD countries in 2021 spent $69 billion on direct R&D in health (excluding tax credits, funding for higher education and publicly owned enterprises), $45 billion in the US [80].

Firm R&D investment appears concentrated in countries where pharmaceutical prices are greater, or price regulation is lighter, but this does not mean that one causes the other. First, higher income countries tend to invest more in education, infrastructure and basic science, and often reimburse higher prices. Higher prices are thereby a confounding variable, being correlated with other, possibly unobserved, supply-side variables that make the country an attractive R&D location. Second, firms may perceive sudden price regulation as a signal that the government has turned “anti-business”, questioning the reliability of the government to maintain fiscal and other advantages. Third, price regulation in one country can have spillover effects on prices in other countries that reference that country by IRP. Hence firms may wish to “punish” countries that regulate prices as a deterrent for other countries not to follow the same path [70]. Fourth, some R&D is country-specific, such as reformulating a product to match local preferences or avoid price comparisons [6]. Such product development offers little or no clinical benefit and might be considered akin to marketing. Investment in local administration functions such as marketing, sales and distribution have been shown to be sensitive to local prices [70].

If global R&D investment retrenches this may translate into fewer new drugs [67], albeit that the long lags mean that it will take many years to materialize into tangible reductions on the drug pipeline and approvals. Moreover, if firms' investment is sensitive to local price regulation, as claimed by several studies [61], [66], [67], [74] then MFN price reductions in the US and price increases elsewhere will make the US a less attractive location for R&D. If MFN is successful, then the US price will be the second-lowest among reference countries, that is, the price will be greater than the US in at least 6 other high-income countries. This trend will be amplified if the US administration continues weakening financial and moral support for basic science. The EO also threatens to use the FDA in ways that would undermine its scientific credentials, though this may need approval of Congress.

The decision by the UK to increase the cost-effectiveness threshold indicates that for some countries, health policy has become intertwined with industrial policy. Other countries are considering “pull” measures that favour national manufacturers, for example, through advantagous pricing agreements or favourable regulatory approval arrangements, such as the Pharmaceutical Industry Strategy 2024–2028 in Spain, protected prices for national manufacturers of strategic medicines in France, and confidential rebates for companies that have significant investments in Germany [83]. Value-based pricing (in its classic form) is based on the normative idea that the main objective of health expenditure is to promote health gain in the population, regardless of where those technologies were developed or manufactured. Up to now, local multinational companies have not obtained higher prices than foreign multinationals, even in relatively highly regulated countries [44]. A change in this principle would imply that a part of the health service expenditure would be used to support the national manufacturing and R&D base, even if this means less health will be gained from a given budget.

R&D provides spillovers and positive externalities, but also carries an opportunity cost. Pharma R&D consumes resources (financial, human and natural) that could have been employed in other activities (such as other kinds of health care, or R&D in other socially beneficial activities such as renewable energies, etc). It matters not only the aggregate level of R&D, but also in which projects are chosen to be funded [84].

R&D effort appears to be broadly subject to diminishing returns to scale. In the 1980s R&D activity was directed at diseases with high volume of sales. The FDA approved between 80 and 100 new drugs or drug-indications per year since the 1980s [85]. However, global R&D expenditure has been increasing in real terms, so that the overall rate of new approvals per USD billion R&D spend (in 2008 US dollars) decreased from 15 per USD billion in 1985 to 2 approvals per USD billion in 2010. When the reported 5 year lag from R&D spend to approval is considered, the mean R&D spend per approval among the 16 Big Pharma companies (2001−2020) was USD 6.16 billion, and only 9 of the 16 have a positive R&D productivity, that is, are successful not only in launching new drugs but also in commercializing them [79]. The low potential for further breakthoughs and the increasing cost of large clinical trials [81] means recent R&D is increasingly narrowly concentrated in biologic drugs [86]; in oncology, diabetes and autoimmune areas; and in rare, severe diseases [87]. If R&D returns to scale are diminishing, a reduction in aggregate finance for pharma R&D will require the industry to refocus capital and effort on projects which present the greatest returns.

Conclusions

The inability of market forces to restrain prices of branded medicines [13] means that it is entirely understandable that the current US administration wishes to promote some form of price regulation of existing medicines and new launches.

A major theme of the MFN EO is that US revenues are supporting the global profitability of the industry and R&D investment, and the EO provides economic and political levers to place pressure on other countries to increase prices. This outcome may be achievable in situations where the firm has strong bargaining weight in price negotiations with other countries. In other situations, firms may choose to delay or cancel launch ex-US to avoid price comparisons. Overall, the most likely scenario is that MFN will lead to an aggregate decline in global pharma revenues and diminish the attractiveness of investment in R&D.

Some countries are increasing prices as part of trade deals with the US, but most will not voluntarily do this. If countries do not wish to increase prices, then they should expect, and prepare for, slower pharma innovation. But, from a public health perspective, it is not just aggregate R&D that matters, but whether R&D investment is directed to projects which are likely to promote the greatest health gain.

In aggregate, the type of innovation delivered by the industry has been shown to broadly follow long-term market signals about where future demand will be strongest [6], [7], [20], [72]. VBP offers a mechanism for selectively financing only innovations that provide more health gain than would be displaced by its cost. But at a granular level, the correlation between price and incremental therapeutic benefit is weak [27]. This may be because VBP is haphazard and uncoordinated.

Joint Clinical Assessment may present an opportunity to assess health benefit in a more reliable and coordinated way and for European member states to align relative prices accordingly. Furthermore, because IRP imports reference country values into domestic prices, then reference-country VBP price signals may be amplified by being de-facto adopted by MFN into the US market. This might mean that, although aggregate pharma innovation may slow, the innovations that come to market may better contribute to public health goals. Developers will bring forward products that meet those criteria (and abandon R&D effort in those which bring low incremental health benefit) [88]. Governments can also influence the volume and direction of pharma investment by channelling public funds to R&D push incentives and simplifying and streamlining regulatory approval. Addressing supply-side gaps by providing skilled workforce, infrastructure, fiscal incentives and basic science could also encourage domestic R&D investment and manufacturing, and increase economic security [89].
