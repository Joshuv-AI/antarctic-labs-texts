Multifunctional effects of Zizyphus mauritiana leaf extract on oral pathogens and wound healing: an integrative in vitro and in vivo study
Authors: Wiwiek Poedjiastoeti, Rachendra Pratama, Irvan Septrian Syah Putra Rasad, Indrayadi Gunardi, Jonathan Agung Suryowinahyu, Felicia Klarissa Tanzil, Armelia Sari Widyarman, Chaminda Jayampath Seneviratne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-extraction infections and delayed oral wound healing remain clinical challenges due to bacterial biofilms, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Natural plant-derived compounds offer promising alternatives to conventional antimicrobials. Zizyphus mauritiana (daun bidara), widely used in traditional Indonesian medicine, has not been comprehensively evaluated for its potential role in oral infection control and wound repair. This study investigated the antibacterial, antibiofilm, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities of ethanolic extract of Z. mauritiana (ZM) leaves using both in vitro and in vivo models. The antibacterial activity of ZM extract was tested against Prevotella intermedia, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Treponema denticola using the microdilution method to determine minimum inhibitory (MIC) and bactericidal (MBC) concentrations. Antibiofilm activity was assessed using a 96-well plate biofilm assay and confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Antioxidant activity was evaluated using the DPPH assay to determine the IC₅₀ value. For in vivo evaluation, gels containing 50% and 100% ZM extract were applied to tooth extraction sockets in Sprague–Dawley rats (n = 24). On day 3, wounds were analyzed for tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) levels by ELISA and for histological inflammation using immunohistochemistry (IHC). ZM extract exhibited strong antibacterial activity (MIC = 125–500 mg/mL; MBC = 250–500 mg/mL) and significantly inhibited biofilm formation (p < 0.05). The extract showed moderate antioxidant activity (IC₅₀ = 0.13703 mg/mL). In vivo, the 50% ZM gel markedly reduced TNF-α levels (118.7 pg/mL) and produced sparse IHC staining, indicating reduced inflammation and improved healing. Ethanolic extract of Zizyphus mauritiana leaves demonstrates multifunctional bioactivities and holds promise as a natural therapeutic agent for managing oral infections and enhancing post-extraction wound healing.Keywords: Antibacterial Agents, Antibiofilm, Antioxidant, Zizyphus mauritiana

Introduction

Oral mucosal wounds commonly arise from conditions such as gingivitis, aphthous stomatitis, and dental extractions, leading to temporary disruption of mucosal integrity and increased susceptibility to microbial invasion (Tirtania et al. 2024). Following tooth extraction, the exposed alveolar socket provides a nutrient-rich environment that facilitates bacterial colonization and biofilm formation, potentially impairing normal wound healing (De Angelis et al. 2024; Kurniawan et al. 2025; Aguilar-Duran et al. 2019; Periasamy et al. 2012). Several oral microorganisms are frequently associated with post-extraction complications, including Prevotella intermedia, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Treponema denticola, which play important roles in infection, inflammation, and delayed tissue repair (Riba-Terés et al. 2021; Zhu et al. 2018; Astuti et al. 2025). In particular, T. denticola has been implicated in fibrinolytic activity associated with alveolar osteitis, while synergistic interactions between P. intermedia and F. nucleatum enhance anaerobic survival, biofilm persistence, and sustained inflammatory responses (Khalil 2023; Könönen et al. 2022).

Current management of post-extraction infections often relies on systemic or topical antibiotics; however, inappropriate or prolonged antibiotic use has contributed to the global escalation of antimicrobial resistance, reducing therapeutic effectiveness and raising safety concerns (Contaldo et al. 2023; Fathima et al. 2022). Moreover, antibiotics alone do not adequately address oxidative stress and inflammatory dysregulation, both of which are critical determinants of impaired wound healing in the oral cavity. These limitations highlight the need for alternative or adjunctive therapies that provide antimicrobial efficacy while simultaneously modulating inflammation and promoting tissue regeneration.

Herbal medicines have gained increasing attention as potential solutions to these challenges due to their multitarget pharmacological activities, relative safety, and lower propensity for resistance development. Plant-derived bioactive compounds, particularly polyphenols and flavonoids, have demonstrated antibacterial, antibiofilm, antioxidant, and wound-healing properties relevant to oral tissue repair (Ariesanti et al. 2023; Vychaktami et al. 2022). Such compounds can interfere with bacterial adhesion, quorum sensing, and biofilm maturation, while also mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory signalling pathways that delay healing.

Among medicinal plants, Zizyphus mauritiana (ZM) is abundant in Indonesia and has been traditionally used for various therapeutic purposes. Phytochemical analyses have shown that ZM leaves contain alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, tannins, and phenolic compounds with documented antibacterial and antioxidant activities (Nurrahma 2022; Rasad et al. 2023; Youl et al. 2019). Flavonoids and phenolics exert antibacterial effects through disruption of bacterial membranes, inhibition of nucleic acid synthesis, enzyme inactivation, and interference with quorum sensing and biofilm formation (Daglia 2012; Cushnie and Lamb 2014; Koo et al. 2017). Tannins and saponins further enhance antibiofilm activity by increasing membrane permeability and destabilizing the extracellular polymeric substance matrix (Flemming and Wuertz 2019; Flemming et al. 2023). In addition, ZM-derived flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol exhibit anti-inflammatory properties by suppressing nuclear factor-κB activation and reducing tumor necrosis factor-α expression, while their antioxidant activity limits excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) that can impair fibroblast function and collagen synthesis (Ramar et al. 2022).

Based on these considerations, the present study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of ZM leaf ethanolic extract against P. intermedia, F. nucleatum, S. sanguinis, and T. denticola; to assess its antioxidant potential; and to investigate its anti-inflammatory effects in a gel formulation as a potential natural therapeutic agent for supporting post-extraction oral wound healing.

Methods

The study employed both in vitro and in vivo experimental models. Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Dentistry Universitas Trisakti (829/S1/KEPK/FKG/7/2024). The in vivo research was conducted at Laboratory Animal Management Unit (Unit Pengelola Hewan Laboratorium – UPHL), School of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical Science – SVMBS, Institut Pertanian Bogor (IPB).

Flowchart of study

Preparation of Zizyphus mauritiana leaf extract

Zizyphus mauritiana leaves weighing 4 kg were taken from the Indonesian Medicinal and Aromatic Crops Research Institute (IMACRI) also known as Balittro, the leaves had been dried and identified by the National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN). The leaf extraction was performed using the maceration technique. In total, 4 kg of fresh leaves were cleaned, air-dried, and ground into powder yielding 2 kg of simplicia. The powder was soaked in 96% ethanol (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at a ratio of 1:5 for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the mixture was filtered using Whatman filter paper (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, US), and the filtrate was evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Buchi R-215, Flawil, Switzerland) to obtain a concentrated extract.

Bacterial culture and growth conditions

Bacterial reference strains were obtained from MiCORE Laboratory, Faculty of Dentistry, Universitas Trisakti. Reference strains of F. nucleatum ATCC 25586, S. sanguinis ATCC 10556, and T. denticola ATCC 35405 were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion Broth (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK). P. intermedia ATCC 25611 was cultured in enhanced Tryptic Soy Broth (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK), supplemented with 0.5% yeast extract (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK), 5 mg/L of hemin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 400 mg/L of L-cysteine (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 10 mg/L of vitamin K1 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The cultures were homogenized using a vortex mixer (Dlab, Beijing, China) and incubated in an anaerobic jar at 37° C for 24 h. The turbidity of each bacterial suspensions was adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) using a microplate reader (SAFAS MP96; Monaco) at a wavelength of 600 nm prior to experimental use.

Antibacterial assay

The antibacterial activity of ZM ethanolic extract was evaluated using the microdilution method in 96-well plates, to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC). The objective of this assay was to determine the inhibitory and bactericidal effects of ZM ethanolic extract against selected oral bacteria by establishing the MIC and the MBC as the primary outcome measures. Nine experimental groups were prepared as follows: a negative control group using distilled water (Water One OneMed, Surabaya, Indonesia), two positive control groups amoxicillin 100 mg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri) and 0.2% chlorhexidine (Minosep, Depok, Indonesia), and six serial ZM extract concentrations (1,000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, and 31.25 mg/mL). Each experiment was performed in quadruplicate and mean bacterial colony counts were used to establish MIC and MBC values.

For each bacterial strain, 500 µL of standardized suspension was mixed with 500 µL of the test solution in sterile microcentrifuge tubes. The mixture was then homogenized using a vortex mixer and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. For the microdilution procedure, a 1,000-fold dilution was prepared in the 96-well plates, where 180 µL of distilled water was added to each well and mixed with 20 µL of the bacterial test solution mixture. Subsequently, 10 µL of each of the test solutions were streaked onto petri dishes containing Brain Heart Infusion Agar (BHI-A) (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK) using an inoculating loop. This procedure was repeated four times, and the plates were incubated at 37° C for 24 h. Bacterial growth was observed visually, and the number of colonies on each petri dish was counted. Bacterial growth was assessed as the outcome measure by visual inspection and quantitative colony-forming unit (CFU) counting. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of the extract that resulted in a noticeable reduction in the number of bacterial colonies before a completely clear plate was observed. The MBC was determined as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial colony growth was observed on the BHI-A plates.

Therapeutic biofilm assay

The antibiofilm efficacy of the ZM leaf ethanolic extract was evaluated using a therapeutic biofilm model in 96-well culture plates (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China), deploying the crystal violet staining method. This assay aimed to evaluate the ability of ZM leaf ethanolic extract to eradicate preformed mature bacterial biofilms, with biofilm biomass reduction as the primary outcome measure. First, a biofilm was established by adding 200 µL of the bacterial suspension to each well, followed by incubation at 37° C for 48 h for biofilm maturation. After incubation, planktonic cells, along with excess medium, were discarded. The composition of the test groups (n = 9) were the same as that in the antibacterial assay. Each test was conducted in quintuple. To each well, 200 µL containing ZM leaf ethanolic extract were added, followed by incubation again for 24 h at 37° C. The next day, the wells were emptied, and the remaining biofilm was fixated using heat fixation, then stained by 0.5% crystal violet (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The stained wells were left at room temperature (± 25 °C) for 15 min. All the wells were washed afterwards three times using distilled water and air dried for 5 min. Post-test, the dried stained biofilm was resuspended using absolute ethanol (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and the optical density (OD) was then quantified at 490 nm using a spectrophotometer (SAFAS MP96, Monaco) (Dudek-Wicher et al 2022). Biofilm biomass was quantitatively assessed based on OD values, with lower absorbance indicating greater biofilm eradication. The results from five separate wells per group were averaged. Biofilm eradication was quantitatively defined as the percentage reduction in biofilm biomass relative to the untreated biofilm control (negative control). qPCR confirmation

Biofilm that has been treated with each ZM concentrations were collected unto new sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and subjected to DNA extraction via heat-shock method. Firstly, the cells were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended using 120 µL nuclease-free water. The resuspended bacteria were chilled at 4 °C for 15 min, followed by heat exposure using thermal heat block at 80 °C for another 15 min. After heat exposure, the suspension was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Following the centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred into a new sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube as total genomic DNA, then quantified using Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts).

Post DNA quantification, the extracted DNA was mixed with 5 × HOT FIREPol® EvaGreen® qPCR Master Mix (Solis Biodyne, Tartu, Estonia, #LOT 08242740.4) as per the manufacturer’s instruction. After Ct cycle data was acquired, we calibrated the values against the standard curve made from serially diluted known CFU counts of the same target bacteria using species-specific primers for each bacterium, as listed in Table 1, to acquire the estimated viable bacteria in log CFU/mL unit.

Antioxidant assay

To evaluate antioxidant activity, the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay was used to measure absorbance values. The objective of this assay was to evaluate the free radical scavenging capacity of ZM leaf ethanolic extract, with antioxidant activity quantified by percentage inhibition and IC₅₀ value determination. The DPPH solution was reacted with the test solutions, and the resulting absorbance values of each test solution indicated its ability to scavenge DPPH free radicals. For each extract concentration, 1 mL of the test solution was drawn using a micropipette and mixed with 2 mL of methanol and 1 mL of DPPH solution. The mixture was then incubated in a dark room for 30 min. Absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 490 nm. A blank solution was prepared by mixing 3 mL of methanol with 1 mL of DPPH solution (concentration: 0.5 mg/mL). Preparation of ZM gel

ZM leaves were macerated in 70% ethanol for 72 h, filtered, and evaporated to concentrate using rotary evaporator, as mentioned previously. The extract was then mixed with 2% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) to form 50% and 100% gels concentrate of ZM leaves. Quality control tests included pH (6.8–7.2), viscosity, sterility, and homogeneity. The gel was applied topically in sufficient quantity (approximately 20–50 µL) to completely fill the extraction socket once daily. This amount is substantially lower than doses associated with systemic toxicity. Different ethanol concentrations were intentionally used for extraction and formulation. Absolute ethanol (96%) was employed for crude extract preparation to maximize phytochemical yield, whereas 70% ethanol was used in the gel formulation to improve solubility of hydrophilic constituents and ensure better compatibility with topical application.

Animal model and grouping

Twenty-four healthy male Sprague–Dawley rats (age: 8–10 weeks, weight: 200–250 g) were used. Animals were housed under standardized laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. The animals were randomly assigned to four groups (each group consist of six rats) using a computer-generated allocation sequence. The assessor of the outcome of the immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis were blinded to the treatment groups.

Tooth extraction and gel application

Under anesthesia induced by ketamine-xylazine, left mandibular first molars were atraumatically extracted (Fig. 2). Topical applications of the respective gels (0.1 mL) were applied once daily on the wound site. The rats were sacrificed on day 3 by ketamine overdose. To ensure consistency, a single calibrated investigator performed all the extractions using a standardized atraumatic technique under magnification. The concentrations of Zizyphus mauritiana extract used in the gel formulation (50% and 100%) were selected based on preliminary in vitro findings demonstrating significant antibacterial and antibiofilm activity at higher extract concentrations, as well as the need to evaluate dose-dependent anti-inflammatory effects in vivo. This approach allowed assessment of the extract’s multifunctional therapeutic potential, including both antimicrobial efficacy and modulation of inflammatory response during wound healing.

TNF-α quantification

Socket tissues were homogenized and analyzed using a Bioassay Technology Laboratory TNF-α enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Cat: BT-AL032, Shanghai Korain Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Statistical analysis

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess statistical differences among the groups. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to identify significant pairwise differences between the groups. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the ethanolic extract of ZM leaves possess notable antibacterial and antioxidant properties, supporting its potential as a multifunctional therapeutic agent for oral health applications. The observed biological effects are likely attributable to the synergistic actions of several bioactive compounds, such as flavonoids, tannins, and saponins, which are known for antimicrobial and free radical-scavenging effects (Kaczmarek 2020; Shamsudin et al 2022; Tagousop et al. 2018). Moreover, previous studies have reported the hemostatic effect of ZM (Rasad et al. 2023), suggesting further relevance in oral wound management.

The antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of Zizyphus mauritiana (ZM) leaf extract against Prevotella intermedia, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Treponema denticola can be attributed to the multimodal actions of its bioactive constituents, including flavonoids, phenolics, tannins, saponins, and alkaloids. In P. intermedia, a Gram-negative anaerobe associated with periodontal inflammation, polyphenols and flavonoids disrupt outer membrane integrity and inhibit lipopolysaccharide-associated virulence and proteolytic activity, thereby impairing bacterial growth and reducing inflammatory potential (Daglia 2012; Hashim et al. 2025; Shen et al. 2019). For F. nucleatum, a key bridging organism in polymicrobial oral biofilms, tannins and flavonoids interfere with adhesins such as FadA and co-aggregation mechanisms, leading to destabilization of biofilm architecture and reduced interspecies interactions (Koo et al. 2017; Brennan and Garrett 2019). In S. sanguinis, an early Gram-positive colonizer, flavonoids and saponins primarily exert antibacterial effects through cell wall and cytoplasmic membrane disruption, inhibition of glucosyltransferase activity, and suppression of extracellular polysaccharide synthesis, resulting in diminished biofilm formation and metabolic activity (Cushnie and Lamb 2014; Karygianni et al. 2015). Meanwhile, T. denticola, a motile spirochete implicated in advanced periodontal disease, is particularly susceptible to phytochemicals that disrupt outer sheath stability, flagellar-mediated motility, and dentilisin protease activity, key determinants of tissue invasion and immune evasion; antioxidant polyphenols may further attenuate T. denticola-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling (Dashper et al. 2011). Collectively, these complementary, bacteria-specific mechanisms support the potential of ZM leaf extract as a multitarget agent against polymicrobial oral biofilms.

ZM extract exhibited concentration-dependent antibacterial effects against key oral pathogens, with F. nucleatum showing the highest susceptibility (MIC = 125–250 mg/mL) whereas for P. intermedia, S. sanguinis, and T. denticola, the MIC ranged between 250 and 500 mg/mL. The MBC, defined as the lowest concentration capable of killing bacterial growth, was determined based on visual observation of colonies on petri dishes, with a value of 250 mg/mL for F. nucleatum and 500 mg/mL for P. intermedia, S. sanguinis, and T. denticola. The variation in susceptibility may be attributed to structural differences in bacterial cell walls, metabolic profiles, or resistance mechanisms, particularly among gram-negative anaerobes, such as P. intermedia and T. denticola (Zhang and Cheng 2022). These results support the potential of ZM as a promising natural antibacterial agent, particularly for periodontal pathogens that exhibit varying levels of sensitivity towards antibacterial properties of ZM leaf ethanolic extract.

The antibacterial effect of ZM leaf ethanolic extract may be attributed to the presence of diverse phytochemicals including alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, tannins, and phenols. The antioxidant properties of ZM leaf ethanolic extract are primarily derived from flavonoids, tannins, and phenols. As antibacterial agents, alkaloids inhibit bacterial nucleic acid and protein synthesis and disrupt cell wall synthesis, damaging the bacterial cell membrane and interfering with bacterial metabolism (Yan et al. 2021). Flavonoids exhibit antibacterial effects by suppressing nucleic acid synthesis, cytoplasmic membrane function, and energy metabolism and antioxidant effects by improving bone health and vascular function (Shamsudin et al. 2022; Ullah et al. 2020). Saponins exert antibacterial effects by binding to cholesterol in the bacterial cell membrane, forming saponin–cholesterol complexes that ultimately cause cell lysis and disrupt membrane permeability (Khan et al. 2018). Tannins, as antibacterial agents, penetrate bacterial cell walls and interfere with cellular metabolism, and lead to cell destruction. Phenolic compounds contribute to antibacterial activity by disrupting protein–cell wall interactions, altering cytoplasmic function and membrane permeability, and inhibiting bacterial energy metabolism and nucleic acid synthesis. As antioxidants, they scavenge free radicals through hydroxyl groups and chelate iron and metal ions that can trigger oxidation processes (Lobiuc.

Antioxidant activity of the ZM extract was evaluated using a DPPH assay to determine the IC₅₀ value. ZM leaf ethanolic extract demonstrated antioxidant potential, with an IC₅₀ value of 0.137033 mg/mL (moderate antioxidant capacity). Compared to Zizyphus oxyphylla, the IC₅₀ value 13.07 ± 0.27 mg/ml followed by n-butanol fraction 29.79 ± 1.30 mg/mL (Ahmad et al. 2017). ZM leaf ethanolic extract showed significantly stronger antioxidant activity than both the crude extract and n-butanol fraction of Z. oxyphylla. Although the extract showed only moderate antioxidant activity, the consistency of the dose–response trend supports its potential for further development as a natural antioxidant source. The steady increase in radical scavenging activity suggests that ZM leaf ethanolic extract may reduce oxidative stress, particularly when applied in adequate concentrations.

The present study demonstrates that ZM leaf ethanolic extract exhibits significant antibiofilm activity against key periodontal pathogens, with concentrations ranging from 250 − 500 mg/mL achieving > 80% biofilm eradication. The extract showed particularly potent activity against F. nucleatum at 250 mg/mL, whereas 500 mg/mL was required for comparable efficacy against P. intermedia, S. sanguinis, and T. denticola (Fig. 6). These findings align with those of previous research demonstrating the antibiofilm potential of plant extracts against oral pathogens (Dib et al. 2021).

The differential susceptibility observed among the tested bacterial species to ZM leaf ethanolic extract is consistent with that of reports in the literature of susceptibility of periodontopathic bacteria, such as P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and P. intermedia to herbal components. The superior activity of ZM leaf ethanolic extract against F. nucleatum is particularly significant, given its role as a bridging organism in periodontal biofilm formation. However, based on molecular approach using qPCR (Fig. 6), T. denticola appeared to be more susceptible instead of P. intermedia and F. nucleatum, although both bacterial numbers are considerably reduced. This difference likely reflects the sensitivity difference between culture approach and molecular approach. Using culture approach can only detect viable microorganisms while molecular approach detects both dead and live cells (Boutaga et al. 2005). The discrepancies in susceptibility patterns between crystal violet (CV) staining and qPCR arise from methodological differences: CV quantifies total biofilm biomass (including live/dead cells and extracellular matrix), while qPCR (calibrated to log CFU/mL equivalents) estimates bacterial load more specifically, though it may include DNA from non-viable cells. These complementary assays provide a comprehensive view of antibiofilm effects (Jeong et al. 2025). Considering prior evidence that plant extracts effectively inhibit oral pathogens, particularly anaerobic bacteria, and biofilm formation (Dib et al. 2021), the present findings further support the potential of ZM leaf ethanolic extract as a natural antibiofilm agent for periodontal disease management.

The antibiofilm effects demonstrated in this study may further be explained by the ability of ZM-derived bioactive compounds to modulate bacterial stress adaptation and biofilm resilience. Antioxidant polyphenols influence intracellular redox homeostasis, which is increasingly recognized as a regulator of biofilm-associated gene expression, including genes involved in matrix production, energy metabolism, and persistence phenotypes. Disruption of redox balance interferes with bacterial signaling systems that promote biofilm tolerance and recovery following antimicrobial exposure. In addition, flavonoids have been reported to inhibit enzymatic activities required for biofilm maturation and maintenance, rendering established biofilms more susceptible to eradication. These redox-modulatory and enzyme-targeting effects provide a mechanistic basis for the antibiofilm outcomes observed in this study beyond direct antibacterial activity (Koo et al. 2017; Flemming et al. 2016).

The concentrations of ZM leaf ethanolic extract used in the antibacterial and antibiofilm assays (125–1,000 mg/mL) are relatively high compared with conventional small-molecule antibiotics. This phenomenon is frequently encountered in studies of natural products and plant extracts, which often contain complex mixtures of phytochemicals with individual constituents present at low abundance, resulting in higher nominal concentrations being necessary to exert significant antimicrobial effects in vitro (Hickl et al. 2024; Bartels et al. 2025). Furthermore, oral biofilms are intrinsically more tolerant to antimicrobial agents than planktonic cells due to their dense extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, altered metabolic states, and spatial gradients of nutrients and oxygen, which collectively impede antimicrobial penetration and activity (Liu et al. 2024). Because of these biofilm-specific resistance mechanisms, in vitro antibiofilm assays, especially against mature biofilms, often necessitate higher extract concentrations than classical MIC determinations.

Clinically, it is important to recognize that the concentrations evaluated in this study do not directly represent systemic therapeutic doses but rather simulate topical or localized applications where high local concentrations can be achieved, such as in mouth rinses, gels, or intracanal medicaments in dental practice (Hickl et al. 2024). Topical oral formulations commonly incorporate high antimicrobial loads to overcome biofilm tolerance and the complex dynamics of the oral environment.

This study demonstrated that ZM leaf ethanolic extract exhibits dose-dependent anti-inflammatory effects, as evidenced by significant reductions in TNF-α expression (Figs. 8 and 9, Table 3). The 50% ZM extract gel (D1) showed the most pronounced anti-inflammatory activity, with TNF-α levels of 118.70 ± 3.98 pg/mL and a mean area of 7.02 ± 1.02 AU, which was superior to that of both the 100% extract (146.20 ± 5.87 pg/ml) and commercial hyaluronic acid control (137.30 ± 2.91 pg/mL). This paradoxical dose response suggests that optimal therapeutic concentrations may exist below maximum extract concentrations. The anti-inflammatory efficacy of plant extracts against TNF-α has been well documented, with natural products from terrestrial plants serving as promising TNF-α antagonists due to their multifunctional regulatory properties (Dib et al 2021). Furthermore, plant derivatives have become promising therapeutic agents because of their antioxidant and chemical characteristics and anti-inflammatory features. The superior performance of the 50% ZM extract compared to that of hyaluronic acid is noteworthy, as hyaluronic acid promotes wound healing through anti-inflammatory effects and regulation of inflammatory responses (Bonatto et al. 2024). These findings suggest that ZM leaf ethanolic extract contains bioactive compounds with potent TNF-α inhibitory properties, warranting further investigation for the management of inflammatory conditions.

Antioxidant activity provides a unifying mechanism underlying the antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing effects of ZM leaf extract. Polyphenols and flavonoids scavenge excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during infection and inflammation, thereby destabilizing redox-sensitive bacterial membranes and enzymes and enhancing antibacterial efficacy (Daglia 2012; Koo et al. 2017). Excessive production of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is closely linked to oxidative stress, as elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) act as secondary messengers that activate redox-sensitive transcription factors, particularly nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB). Under oxidative conditions, ROS promote phosphorylation and degradation of IκB, allowing NF-κB to translocate into the nucleus and upregulate TNF-α gene expression. Antioxidant polyphenols and flavonoids mitigate this process by scavenging ROS and restoring intracellular redox balance, thereby inhibiting NF-κB activation and subsequent TNF-α overexpression (Li et al. 2016). During wound healing, antioxidants protect fibroblasts and keratinocytes from oxidative damage, promote collagen synthesis, angiogenesis, and re-epithelialization, and create a microenvironment conducive to effective oral tissue regeneration (Zulkefli et al. 2023).

Zizyphus mauritiana leaf extract appears to enhance post-extraction wound healing through a coordinated, multi-layered biological mechanism that simultaneously targets the principal drivers of delayed socket repair, namely microbial colonization, oxidative stress, and dysregulated inflammatory signaling. Following tooth extraction, the socket represents a transient open wound characterized by exposed alveolar bone, disrupted vasculature, and a nutrient-rich environment conducive to rapid colonization by anaerobic polymicrobial communities. Periodontopathogens such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, and Treponema denticola release virulence factors and endotoxins, particularly lipopolysaccharide, which activate Toll-like receptors on resident immune cells and initiate downstream NF-κB signaling. This cascade induces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, thereby amplifying leukocyte recruitment, vascular permeability, and proteolytic tissue degradation. Although these responses are essential for early host defense, excessive or prolonged activation disrupts the orderly transition from inflammation to proliferation, resulting in delayed epithelialization, impaired angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix breakdown (Guo and Dipietro 2010; Rodrigues et al. 2019).

The pronounced antibacterial and antibiofilm activities observed for Z. mauritiana extract likely attenuate this primary inflammatory trigger by reducing microbial load and virulence factor exposure. Phytochemicals such as flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and phenolic compounds can disrupt bacterial membranes, inhibit essential metabolic pathways, and destabilize biofilm architecture, which otherwise confers resistance to host immunity and antimicrobial agents. Biofilm eradication is particularly relevant in oral wounds, where structured microbial communities sustain chronic cytokine production and prevent resolution of inflammation. By decreasing pathogen-associated molecular pattern signaling, the extract may reduce Toll-like receptor activation and downstream NF-κB–mediated cytokine release at its source (Lobiuc et al. 2023).

In parallel, the extract exhibits substantial antioxidant activity attributable to its polyphenolic composition. Reactive oxygen species are abundantly generated during the inflammatory phase by activated neutrophils and macrophages and function both as antimicrobial agents and mediators of tissue injury. Excessive ROS induces lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and oxidation of extracellular matrix proteins, while simultaneously acting as secondary messengers that further activate NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase pathways. This establishes a self-perpetuating cycle of oxidative stress and cytokine production that impairs fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. By scavenging free radicals and chelating pro-oxidant metal ions, Z. mauritiana extract likely interrupts this amplification loop, preserving cellular viability and matrix integrity within the wound environment (Dunnill et al. 2017; Qi et al. 2022).

A central finding of the present study is the significant reduction in TNF-α expression following topical application of the extract, particularly at the intermediate concentration. TNF-α occupies a pivotal regulatory role in wound biology: at physiological levels it promotes leukocyte recruitment and early angiogenic signaling, whereas sustained overexpression inhibits fibroblast proliferation, suppresses collagen deposition, enhances osteoclastogenesis via the RANK/RANKL pathway, and stimulates matrix metalloproteinases that degrade extracellular matrix components. In extraction sockets, persistent TNF-α activity has been associated with delayed soft-tissue closure and increased alveolar bone resorption. The observed downregulation of TNF-α therefore suggests that Z. mauritiana extract does not abolish inflammation but modulates it toward a level compatible with tissue repair. This immunomodulatory effect may involve inhibition of NF-κB nuclear translocation, suppression of cyclooxygenase-2 and inducible nitric oxide synthase expression, and promotion of macrophage polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype, mechanisms widely reported for plant-derived polyphenols (Eming et al. 2014).

The superior anti-inflammatory effect observed with the 50% formulation compared with the 100% gel indicates a non-linear dose–response relationship, a phenomenon commonly observed with phytotherapeutic agents. Excessively concentrated extracts may exhibit reduced tissue diffusion, altered physicochemical interactions within the wound milieu, or mild cytotoxicity that counteracts beneficial effects. Conversely, intermediate concentrations may optimize bioavailability while preserving cellular compatibility, thereby achieving more effective modulation of the local microenvironment. Such hormetic responses are well documented for plant-derived compounds and underscore the importance of dose optimization in translational applications (Calabrese and Mattson 2017).

Collectively, the antibacterial, antibiofilm, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties of Z. mauritiana extract converge to facilitate resolution of the inflammatory phase and promote progression to the proliferative stage of healing. This transition is characterized by fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, granulation tissue formation, neovascularization, and epithelial migration, ultimately restoring structural and functional tissue integrity. Importantly, the extract appears to act not as a single-target anti-inflammatory agent but as a multifunctional regulator that re-establishes biological homeostasis within the extraction socket. Such multitarget activity is particularly advantageous in complex oral wounds, where microbial, oxidative, and immunological factors interact dynamically. The mechanism proposed in this study, illustrated in Fig. 10, supports the potential of Z. mauritiana leaf extract as a promising natural therapeutic strategy for enhancing post-extraction healing while reducing dependence on conventional antibiotics and synthetic anti-inflammatory agents (Rodrigues et al. 2019; Eming et al. 2014).

Proposed mechanism of Zizyphus mauritiana leaf extract in post-extraction wound healing. The extract reduces microbial burden, attenuates oxidative stress, modulates inflammatory cytokine signaling (particularly TNF-α), and promotes the transition from the inflammatory to proliferative phase, resulting in accelerated tissue repair

The present study has several limitations. The broad concentration intervals (125–1000 mg/mL) tested may not have been optimal for identifying precise inhibitory and bactericidal ranges. Furthermore, future studies should focus on fractionating the extract of ZM to isolate bioactive compounds responsible for its antibacterial and antioxidant activities. In vivo studies are recommended to confirm the biological efficacy and safety of the extract under physiological conditions. Cytotoxicity testing and formulation development are essential to explore the clinical potential of ZM leaf ethanolic extract for topical or systemic applications in dentistry. Finally, comparative studies with standard commercial agents could help assess its competitiveness as a natural therapeutic agent.

Conclusions

The 96% ethanolic extract of ZM leaves demonstrated strong antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against P. intermedia, F. nucleatum, S. sanguinis, and T. denticola, with F. nucleatum showing the highest susceptibility. The extract exhibited moderate antioxidant activity, with an IC₅₀ value of 0.137033 mg/mL, with a clear dose–response relationship. In vivo-the ZM based-gel showed significant anti-inflammatory properties. Collectively, these findings highlight the multifunctional bioactivities of ZM leaf ethanolic extract and its promise as a natural therapeutic candidate for managing oral infections and prompting post-extraction wound healing. Further studies focusing on active compound isolation, formulation optimization and clinical validation are warranted to advance its application in dental care.
