Sampling Finite Unit Norm Tight Frames Using Symplectic Geometry
Mason Faldet, Clayton Shonkwiler

Abstract
Unit-norm tight frames in finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces (FUNTFs) are fundamental in signal processing, offering optimal robustness to noise and measurement loss. In this paper we introduce the Eigenlift algorithm for sampling random FUNTFs. Our approach exploits the symplectic geometry of the FUNTF space, which we characterize as a symplectic reduction of frame space by a symmetry group. We then define a Hamiltonian torus action on this reduced space whose momentum map induces a fiber bundle structure. The algorithm proceeds by sampling a point from the base space, which is a convex polytope, lifting it deterministically to a point on the corresponding fiber, then acting on this point by a random element of the torus to obtain a random FUNTF. We implement the method in Python and validate it in low-dimensional settings where it is computationally feasible to sample the base polytope via rejection sampling.



1
Introduction

A
frame
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a sequence of vectors
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset\mathcal{H}
such that for every
v\in\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle a\|v\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i\in I}|\langle v,f_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq b\|v\|^{2}

for some
0<a\leq b
called the
frame bounds
. When
a=b
the frame is called
tight
. A finite collection
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a frame if and only if
\operatorname{span}\{f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
; that is, finite frames are equivalent to spanning sets.

Our main focus is on finite tight frames in which each all of the frame vectors are unit vectors; that is,
\|f_{i}\|=1
for all
i=1,\dots,N
. These are exactly the
finite unit-norm tight frames
(FUNTFs). FUNTFs have proved useful in a variety of contexts because they provide robust representations of signals with optimal resilience to noise and random erasures of measurements
[
10
,
19
,
24
]
.

In this paper, we introduce the
Eigenlift
algorithm for sampling FUNTFs uniformly from a natural probability measure on the space of FUNTFs. This algorithm leverages the toric symplectic structure on the space of FUNTFs described in
[
29
]
. This structure involves a specific toric action on the frame space and a many-to-one map from the frame space to a convex polytope (the
eigenstep polytope
) that is invariant under the torus action. Heuristically, the process consists of sampling a point from Lebesgue measure on the eigenstep polytope, identifying a frame in the fiber over that point, and applying a random element of the torus to the resulting frame.

We have implemented our algorithm in Python and used this implementation to conduct small-scale experiments. A key challenge lies in efficiently sampling from the eigenstep polytope; in principle this can be done using a standard Markov chain, but in practice convergence seems to be quite slow in high dimensions. For our proof of concept, we sampled the eigenstep polytope by rejection sampling from a bounding box; unfortunately, the rejection probability goes rapidly to 1 as the dimension of the space of FUNTFs increases, so this approach is only practical in low dimensions.

, which includes some discussion and future directions.

2.1
Concepts From Frame Theory

Let
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}

\mathbb{C}^{d}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}|&\cdots&|f_{N}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\}\\
\subset\{1,\dots,N\}\end{subarray}}\left|\det\begin{bmatrix}f_{i_{1}}|&\cdots&|f_{i_{d}}\end{bmatrix}\right|^{2},

For any

(i)

The

\displaystyle v\mapsto(\langle v,f_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle v,f_{N}\rangle).

(ii)

The

\displaystyle(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}f_{i}.

(iii)

The

\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}

v\mapsto FF^{*}v

We can re-interpret tightness in terms of the frame operator: a frame

a
-tight

F
is
equal norm
and if

F

unit norm
. Lastly, we say
F
is
full spark
if any size
d
subset
\{f_{i_{1}},\dots,f_{i_{d}}\}
of
F
is a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

2.2
Concepts From Symplectic Geometry

In this work, we equip the space of FUNTFs with a symplectic structure, which is fundamental to our algorithm. Our method centers on inverting the momentum map of a Hamiltonian toric action on the symplectic manifold of FUNTFs. Below is a brief introduction to relevant concepts from symplectic geometry; see
[
27
]
for a comprehensive introduction to symplectic geometry.

A
symplectic manifold

(M,\omega)
is a smooth (real) manifold
M
endowed with a nondegenerate closed
2
-form
\omega
, called a
symplectic form
. For
p\in M
and
X,Y\in T_{p}M
, we use
\omega_{p}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}
to denote the evaluation of
\omega
on the tangent vectors
X
and
Y
.

Nondegeneracy means that at each point
p\in M
, the bilinear form
\omega_{p}
is nondegenerate; that is, if
\omega_{p}(X,Y)=0
for every
Y\in T_{p}M
, then
X=0
. One consequence of nondegeneracy is that that
M
must be even-dimensional.

The requirement that
\omega
be closed means that
d\omega=0
, where
d
is the exterior derivative on forms.

Example 2.4
.

The prototypical example of a symplectic manifold is
n
-dimensional complex space
\mathbb{C}^{n}\approx\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with the
standard symplectic form

\omega
. Using coordinates
(x_{1}+y_{1}\sqrt{-1},\dots,x_{n}+y_{n}\sqrt{-1})
with
x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega
is defined as

\displaystyle\omega=dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}+\cdots+dx_{n}\wedge dy_{n}.

For
p\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
Z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n}),W=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\approx T_{p}\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\omega
can be written in complex coordinates as

\displaystyle\omega_{p}(Z,W)=-\operatorname{Im}(\overline{w}_{1}z_{1}+\cdots+\overline{w}_{n}z_{n})=-\operatorname{Im}(W^{*}Z)

where
\operatorname{Im}
denotes the imaginary part of a complex number.

We can extend the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to a symplectic form on the manifold
\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
of
d\times N
complex matrices by simply composing the standard symplectic form with the reshaping operator
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{C}^{dN}
. In particular, for
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
and
X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}\approx T_{F}\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}

\displaystyle\omega_{F}(X,Y)=-\operatorname{Im}(\operatorname{tr}(Y^{*}X)).

Recall,
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
so that
\omega
restricts to
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
which makes the space of
d\times N
frames a symplectic manifold.

As in so many areas of geometry, we are often particularly interested in symplectic manifolds with symmetries; that is, those symplectic manifolds admitting a smooth action of a Lie group which preserves the symplectic form in an appropriate sense. In this setting, the natural actions are
Hamiltonian actions
, which we now describe. To build intuition, we start by considering a smooth action
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\operatorname{Diff}(M)
of the Lie group
\mathbb{R}
on a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
. We use the notation
\psi_{t}
to indicate the diffeomorphism
\psi(t)\in\operatorname{Diff}(M)
. The action induces a vector field
X_{\psi}
on
M
which is given at a point
p\in M
by

\displaystyle(X_{\psi})_{p}=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\psi_{t}(p).

If the vector field
X_{\psi}
is related to the differential of a smooth function
H:M\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\omega(X_{\psi},\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\hskip 1.0pt)=dH,

(6)

then
X_{\psi}
is called a
Hamiltonian vector field
and
\psi
a
Hamiltonian
\mathbb{R}
-action.
The relation (
6
) leads to many desirable consequences. For example, the flow of
X_{\psi}
—which, by definition, is
\psi
itself—preserves the symplectic form and, the Lie derivative of
H
with respect to
X_{\psi}
is zero. Together, these imply that
H
is constant on orbits of
\psi
.

Now we consider the general case of a smooth action
\psi:G\to\operatorname{Diff}(M,\omega)
where
G
is any Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold. We say
\psi
is a
Hamiltonian
G
-action
if there exists a map
\Phi:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
(analogous to the function
H
in the
G=\mathbb{R}
case above) such that for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, the function
\Phi^{\xi}:M\to\mathbb{R}
defined
\Phi^{\xi}(p):=\Phi(p)(\xi)
and the vector field
X^{\xi}
defined by

\displaystyle X^{\xi}_{p}=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(\exp t\xi)\cdot p

are such that
\omega(X^{\xi},\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\hskip 1.0pt)=d\Phi^{\xi}
, where
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
is the exponential map. Moreover, we require
\Phi
to be equivariant with respect to the
G
-action on
M
and the coadjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, meaning

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ad}^{*}_{g}(\Phi(p))=\Phi(g\cdot p).

(7)

We call
\Phi
the
momentum map
of the Hamiltonian
G
-action. In essence, a
G
-action
\psi
is Hamiltonian if the restriction of
\psi
to any 1-parameter subgroup results in a Hamiltonian
\mathbb{R}
-action on
(M,\omega)
. This becomes clear upon noticing that the quantifier “for all
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
” above parameterizes all 1-parameter subgroups of
G
. The momentum map provides the associated Hamiltonian function
\Phi^{\xi}
and vector field
X^{\xi}
. The importance of equivariance will become apparent below.

Momentum maps of Hamiltonian actions are the means by which we obtain coordinates on the space of FUNTFs that are integral to our sampling scheme. Moreover, momentum maps give rise to symplectic quotients, as first shown by Meyer
[
28
]
and Marsden and Weinstein
[
26
]
:

Theorem 2.5

(Marsden–Weinstein–Meyer Theorem for Regular Values)
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold with a Hamiltonian
G
-action, let
\Phi:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a momentum map for the action, and let
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a regular value of
\Phi
such that
G
acts freely on the level set
\Phi^{-1}(\chi)
. Then the manifold

\displaystyle M\sslash_{\chi}G:=\Phi^{-1}(\chi)/G,

called the
symplectic reduction
or
symplectic quotient
over
\chi
, admits a symplectic structure
\omega_{\text{red}}
which is uniquely characterized by the equation

\displaystyle q^{*}\omega_{\text{red}}=\iota^{*}\omega,

where
q:\Phi^{-1}(\chi)\to M\sslash_{\chi}G
is the quotient map and
\iota:\Phi^{-1}(\chi)\to M
is the inclusion map.

Notice that, by the equivariance of the momentum map (
7
) and the fact that
G
acts on
\Phi^{-1}(\chi)
, it follows that
\chi
is necessarily a fixed point in the coadjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Specifically, for every
g\in G
we have
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi^{-1}(\chi)\subset M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g\cdot\Phi^{-1}(\chi)\subset\Phi^{-1}(\chi)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{\chi\}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ad}^{*}_{g}(\{\chi\})=\{\chi\}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g\cdot(\hskip 2.0pt)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{*}}

This diagram also illustrates the converse: if
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is a fixed point of the coadjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
then the level set
\Phi^{-1}(\chi)
is
G
-invariant.

More generally, the equivariance of
\Phi
implies that
G
acts on the preimage of any coadjoint orbit,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\chi}:=\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}:\xi=\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{*}\chi\text{ for some }g\in G\}.

In the case that
\chi
is a regular value of
\Phi
that is not a fixed point of the coadjoint action,

2.5
generalizes to a quotient over the coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
.

Theorem 2.6

(Marsden–Weinstein–Meyer Theorem for Coadjoint Orbits)
.

Let
M,\omega,G,
and
\Phi
be as above and
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a regular value. If the action of
G
on
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{\chi})
is free, then the manifold

\displaystyle M\sslash_{\mathcal{O}_{\chi}}G:=\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{\chi})/G,

admits a symplectic structure
\omega_{\text{red}}
which is uniquely characterized by the equation

\displaystyle q^{*}\omega_{\text{red}}=\iota^{*}\omega,

where
q:\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{\chi})\to M\sslash_{\mathcal{O}_{\chi}}G
is the projection map and
\iota:\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{\chi})\to M
is the inclusion map.

We state one final useful result on symplectic reduction due to Sjamaar and Lerman
[
31
]
, which concerns the symplectic quotient over a singular value
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
of the momentum map. In this more general case, the quotient isn’t a manifold, but it still has a natural reduced symplectic structure. In particular, they showed that the symplectic quotient over
\chi
is a symplectic stratified space, which is a disjoint union of symplectic manifolds that fit together nicely. Remarkably, if the momentum map is proper, then the symplectic stratified space has a unique open, connected, and dense piece called a
stratum
which is a manifold.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold with a Hamiltonian
G
-action, let
\Phi:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
be a momentum map for the action and let
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. The symplectic reduction

\displaystyle M\sslash_{\mathcal{O}_{\chi}}G:=\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{\chi})/G

is a symplectic stratified space with symplectic structure
\omega_{\text{red}}
characterized by
q^{*}\omega_{\text{red}}=\iota^{*}\omega
. If
\Phi
is proper (for example, if
G
is compact), then the reduction has a unique open stratum which is a manifold and is connected and dense.

3
Symplectic Structure On FUNTFs

In
Example

2.4
we noted that the space
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
of frames consisting of
N
vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a symplectic manifold. Its symplectic structure is obtained by pulling back the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
via the inclusion map
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d,N}
. Let
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})\subset\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
denote the set of unit norm tight frames in
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
. This notation aligns with that of
[
29
]
, where the subscript
\boldsymbol{N/d}
specifies that frames in this space have frame operator spectrum
(N/d,\dots,N/d)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the
\boldsymbol{1}
in parentheses indicates that each frame vector has squared norm equal to 1. To obtain a natural symplectic structure on FUNTFs, we pass to the quotient of
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})
by a certain group of symmetries.

We derive the desired symplectic structure in two steps. First, we construct the space of
N/d
-tight frames within
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
as a symplectic reduction. Next, we obtain the space of unit norm tight frames as the symplectic reduction of the
N/d
-tight frames from step 1. From this point onward, we denote the space of
N/d
-tight frames by
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
.

3.1
Symplectic Structure on
N/d
-Tight Frames

We will show that
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
is the preimage of a regular value in
\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}
under the momentum map of the Hamiltonian
U(d)
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
given by left multiplication. It then follows from
Theorem

2.5
that
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
is a symplectic quotient and thus inherits a natural symplectic structure.

We begin by identifying
\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}
with the space of
d\times d
Hermitian matrices,
\mathcal{H}(d)
, via the isomorphism
\alpha:\mathcal{H}(d)\to\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}
which maps a matrix
\xi\in\mathcal{H}(d)
to the linear functional

\displaystyle\alpha_{\xi}:\mathfrak{u}(d)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

(8)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\eta^{*}\xi).

Notice that
\alpha
is simply the map
\mathfrak{u}(d)\to\mathcal{H}(d)
given by
\eta\mapsto-\sqrt{-1}/2\eta
followed by an application of the Riesz representation theorem with respect to the standard Frobenius inner product on
\mathfrak{u}(d)
. Next, we define an action of
U(d)
on
\mathcal{H}(d)
, referred to as the adjoint action, given by conjugation:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ad}:U(d)\times\mathcal{H}(d)

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}(d)

\displaystyle(A,\xi)

\displaystyle\mapsto A\xi A^{*}.

The action
\operatorname{Ad}
and the isomorphism
\alpha
are compatible in the following sense:

Lemma 3.1
.

The isomorphism
\alpha
is equivariant with respect to adjoint action on
\mathcal{H}(d)
and the coadjoint action on
\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}
, i.e. for each

the following commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}(d)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}(d)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ad}_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ad}_{A}^{*}}

(9)

It follows that, under

is identified with the collection of Hermitian matrices with a fixed spectrum.

Proof.

Let
A\in U(d)
and
\xi\in\mathcal{H}(d)
. We readily check that the diagram (
9
) commutes:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\operatorname{Ad}_{A}(\xi)}(\eta)=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\eta^{*}A\xi A^{*})=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\operatorname{tr}((A^{*}\eta A)^{*}\xi)=\alpha_{\xi}(A^{*}\eta A)=\operatorname{Ad}_{A}^{*}\alpha_{\xi}(\eta)

for any
\eta\in\mathfrak{u}(d)
.

To see the second statement holds, let

. Since
\xi
is Hermitian, it has a purely real spectrum
\lambda
and is unitarily diagonalizable. Hence, there exists an
A\in U(d)
for which

\displaystyle\xi=A(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda))A^{*}=\operatorname{Ad}_{A}(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)).

Denote the adjoint orbit of
\xi
in
\mathcal{H}(d)
by
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}
. Notice that
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}
is equal to the orbit of
\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)
in
\mathcal{H}(d)
, which we denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
, and consists of all
d\times d
Hermitian matrices whose spectrum is
\lambda
. Then on the level of sets, equivariance gives

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{O}_{\lambda})=\alpha(\mathcal{O}_{\xi})=\{\alpha_{\operatorname{Ad}_{A}\xi}:A\in U(d)\}=\{\operatorname{Ad}^{*}_{A}\alpha_{\xi}:A\in U(d)\}=\mathcal{O}_{\alpha_{\xi}},

where

denotes the coadjoint orbit of
\alpha_{\xi}
in
\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}
. Since
\alpha
is an isomorphism, all coadjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}
take the form
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha_{\xi}}
.
∎

With the identification

, we can now express the momentum map for the left multiplication action of

on

more cleanly. Notably,

is a regular value of the momentum map, and its preimage is precisely

Theorem 3.2
.

The action of
U(d)
on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
by left multiplication is Hamiltonian with momentum map

\displaystyle\Phi_{U(d)}:\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}(d)\approx\mathfrak{u}(d)^{*}

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mapsto-FF^{*}

The regular values of

are the negative definite matrices in
\mathcal{H}(d)
. It follows that the space
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
is the symplectic quotient

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}\sslash_{-N/d\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d}}U(d).

In particular, it has a natural symplectic structure.

A direct computation, as demonstrated in
[
29
]
, shows that this action is Hamiltonian, with momentum map
\Phi_{U(d)}
whose regular values are the negative definite matrices in
\mathcal{H}(d)
. Notice that
\Phi_{U(d)}
maps a frame to the negative of its frame operator, thus the preimage of the regular value

. Additionally,

acts on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
: for any

and

, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{U(d)}(AF)=-AFF^{*}A^{*}=-A(-N/d\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d})A^{*}=-N/d\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d}.

Finally, the action on the fiber is free, as

implies

.
Thus, by

, the quotient
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
is a symplectic manifold consisting of unitary equivalence classes of
N/d
-tight frames.

It will be useful to identify the symplectic manifold
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
with a subset in
\mathcal{H}(N)
that corresponds to a coadjoint orbit in
\mathfrak{u}(N)^{*}
. Specifically, let

denote the set of all

Hermitian matrices whose first
d
eigenvalues (in non-increasing order) are

, with the remaining

eigenvalues all equal to
0
. By

, the isomorphism
\alpha
defined in (

) identifies

with a coadjoint orbit

for some
\beta\in\mathfrak{u}(N)^{*}
. Like all coadjoint orbits,
\mathcal{O}_{\beta}
carries a natural symplectic structure, which pulls back along

to a symplectic structure on
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
.

Elements of the tangent space to
\mathcal{O}_{\beta}
at

are of the form
\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\beta
for
\xi\in\mathfrak{u}(N)
where

is the coadjoint representation of
\mathfrak{u}(N)
on
\mathfrak{u}(N)^{*}
which is obtained as the derivative of the coadjoint action
\operatorname{Ad}^{*}
of

on
\mathfrak{u}(N)^{*}
. The natural symplectic form on

, called the

, is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{\beta}^{\text{KKS}}(\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi}\beta,\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{\xi^{\prime}}\beta)=\beta([\xi,\xi^{\prime}]),

where

is the Lie bracket on

.

Recall from the proof of

that
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
is the orbit of

under the proper and free adjoint action of

on
\mathcal{H}(N)
, hence
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
is a properly embedded submanifold of
\mathcal{H}(N)
. Consequently, the restriction of the linear isomorphism

to

is a diffeomorphism from

to

, and thus

is a symplectic form on

.

In
[

]
, Shonkwiler and Needham proved that the space
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
, equipped with the reduced symplectic structure, is symplectomorphic to the submanifold

with the symplectic form
\alpha^{*}\omega^{\text{KKS}}
via the mapping

\displaystyle[F]

\displaystyle\mapsto F^{*}F

(10)

which sends a unitary equivalence class to the Gram matrix of any of its representatives.

3.2
Second Symplectic Reduction

The
N
-torus
U(1)^{N}
can be identified with the standard maximal torus in
U(N)
, which is the subgroup

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}=\big\{\operatorname{diag}\big(e^{\sqrt{-1}\theta_{1}},\dots,e^{\sqrt{-1}\theta_{N}}\big):\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)\big\}.

Under this identification,
U(1)^{N}\approx\mathbb{T}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
by right multiplication. We will see momentarily that this action preserves the spectra of frame operators, thus it restricts to an action on the submanifold
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
.

Define the
U(1)^{N}
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
by
D\cdot F:=FD^{*}=F\overline{D}
where
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
and
D\in\mathbb{T}
. Right multiplication by the Hermitian adjoint is chosen to ensure that this action aligns with the action of the full unitary group
U(N)
on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
. In particular, if
A_{1},A_{2}\in U(N)
and
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
, then

\displaystyle A_{1}\cdot(A_{2}\cdot F)=A_{1}\cdot FA_{2}^{*}=FA_{2}^{*}A_{1}^{*}=F(A_{1}A_{2})^{*}=(A_{1}A_{2})\cdot F

showing that
A\cdot F:=FA^{*}
indeed defines a
U(N)
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
. Without the Hermitian adjoint in the
U(1)^{N}
-action definition, an extension to a full
U(N)
-action would not be possible, as generic matrices in
U(N)
do not commute.

A straightforward computation shows that this action preserves the spectrum of a frame operator. For
D\in\mathbb{T}
and
F\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
, the frame operator of
D\cdot F=FD^{*}
is

\displaystyle(FD^{*})(FD^{*})^{*}=FD^{*}DF^{*}=FF^{*}

which matches the frame operator of
F
. Thus, the action preserves the frame operator spectrum, and in particular,
U(1)^{N}
acts on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
.

By the associativity of matrix multiplication, the left action of
U(d)
on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
commutes with the right action of
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
. This implies that the
\mathbb{T}
-action on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
descends naturally to a well-defined action on the quotient
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
, given by
D\cdot[F]:=[FD^{*}]
. However, the descended action on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
is not effective. In fact, the isotropy group at any unitary equivalence class
[f]
contains the 1-parameter subgroup
\{e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{N}\}_{\theta}\subset\mathbb{T}
. To see this, let
e^{i\theta}\in U(1)
and
F\in\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
. Then

\displaystyle e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{N}\cdot[F]=e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{N}\cdot[e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{d}F]=[e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{d}Fe^{-i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{N}]=[F],

showing that
e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{N}
fixes the class
[F]
. Although the descended action is Hamiltonian, the fact that the isotropy subgroup at each point contains a 1-parameter subgroup implies that every point is a singular point of the momentum map. We prefer an effective action so that the momentum map of the
\mathbb{T}
-action has regular values and a full-dimensional image. This choice will help avoid complications later on.

To get an effective action and avoid the aforementioned complications, we modify the descended torus action on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
. For this, we recall the identification of
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
with the adjoint orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}\subset\mathcal{H}(N)\approx\mathfrak{u}(N)^{*}
as defined in (
10
). Under this identification, the descended
\mathbb{T}
-action on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
corresponds to the action on
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
given by

\displaystyle D\cdot(F^{*}F)=DF^{*}FD^{*},

(11)

where
D\in\mathbb{T}
and
F^{*}F\in{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
. Notice that (
11
) is just the adjoint action of
U(N)
on the orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
, restricted to
\mathbb{T}\leq U(N)
. To obtain an effective action on
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
, we restrict the
\mathbb{T}
-action to the quotient of
\mathbb{T}
by the center of the group,
Z(U(N))=\{e^{i\theta}\mathbb{I}_{N}\}_{\theta}\approx U(1)
. Specifically, we define

\displaystyle G:=\mathbb{T}/Z(U(N))\approx U(1)^{N}/U(1)\approx U(1)^{N-1}.

which we identify with the subgroup of
\mathbb{T}
consisting of matrices whose
(N,N)
-entry is 1. Since
G
doesn’t contain the 1-parameter subgroup of scalar matrices which act trivially, the toric action of
G
on
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
given by (

) is effective.

Theorem 3.3
.

The

-action on

is Hamiltonian with momentum map

\displaystyle\Phi_{G}:{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\approx\mathfrak{g}^{*}

\displaystyle F^{*}F\mapsto

\displaystyle(f_{1}^{*}f_{1},\dots,f_{N-1}^{*}f_{N-1}).

The proof of this theorem relies on standard results concerning the adjoint action of
U(N)
on the adjoint orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}
, which can be found in
[
29
]
. Alternatively, one can directly demonstrate

is the momentum map by noticing that

is the composition
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{O}}_{N/d}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}(N)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{u}(N)^{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{g}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

where

is the inclusion map,
\alpha
is the isomorphism defined in equation (

),

is the projection induced by

, and

is the identification given by:

\displaystyle\bigg(\operatorname{diag}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N-1},0)\in\mathfrak{g}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\xi_{k}\eta_{k}\bigg)\mapsto(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N-1}).

It is clear that the preimage

, where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
, consists of the unitary equivalence classes of unit norm tight frames

. This is the frame space we were hoping to obtain. However, in order to equip this space with a natural symplectic structure, we must pass to the symplectic quotient. It turns out that
\mathbf{1}
is not always a regular value of
\Phi_{G}
, and therefore the symplectic quotient of
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
over

is, in general, a symplectic stratified space (

).

Theorem 3.4

(Needham and Shonkwiler
[
29
, Proposition 2.16]
)
.

For
N>d
the (non-empty) space

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\mathbf{1})/(U(d)\times G):=\big(\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)\big)\sslash_{\mathbf{1}}G

is a symplectic stratified space. Alternatively,
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\mathbf{1})/(U(d)\times G)
can be viewed as the symplectic quotient by the product group

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}\sslash_{(-N/d\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d},\mathbf{1})}(U(d)\times G).

It was shown in
[
14
]
that
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})
is a manifold if and only if
d
and
N
are relatively prime. Thus, while
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
is always a symplectic stratified space, it is a symplectic manifold only when
\text{gcd}(d,N)=1
. In the next section, we will see that for the purpose of sampling FUNTFs, the fact that
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
is a symplectic stratified space but not a manifold is not problematic. We have achieved our first goal of obtaining a symplectic structure on (a quotient of) the space of FUNTFs.

Before proceeding to the next section, we briefly pause to compute the dimension of the quotient
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
. To that end, we assume that
\mathbf{1}
is a regular value of
\Phi_{G}
so that
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
is a manifold. We begin with the first reduction. The space
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
is the preimage of the regular value
-N/d\mathbb{I}_{d}
under the momentum map
\Phi_{U(d)}
, therefore the regular value theorem implies
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}
has codimension
\text{dim }\mathcal{H}(d)=d^{2}
in
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
. Thus,
\text{dim }\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}=2dN-d^{2}
. Moreover, by the quotient manifold theorem

\displaystyle\text{dim }\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)=\text{dim }\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}-\text{dim }U(d)=2dN-d^{2}-d^{2}=2(dN-d^{2}).

Now, onto the second reduction. The space
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/U(d)
is the preimage of the regular value
\mathbf{1}
of the momentum map
\Phi_{G}
, so the codimension of
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/U(d)
in
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}/U(d)
is
\text{dim }\mathfrak{g}=N-1
. Hence,

\displaystyle\text{dim }\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/U(d)=2(dN-d^{2})-(N-1)=2(dN-d^{2})-N+1.

Once more, by the quotient manifold theorem

\displaystyle\text{dim }\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)=

\displaystyle\text{dim }\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/U(d)-\text{dim }G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(dN-d^{2})-N+1-(N-1)=2(d-1)(N-d-1).

We will refer back to this dimension in the following section.

4
Toric Geometry of FUNTFs

We can now refine the statement of our goal, which is to sample FUNTFs. More precisely, the space
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})
inherits a Riemannian metric from the standard Euclidean structure on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
, and the associated Riemannian volume measure agrees with the Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})
inherits as a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
. We would like to sample from this measure, but this measure is not so easy to get a handle on.

In turn, the quotient
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
(or at least the open dense stratum from
Theorem

2.7
) has the Riemannian quotient metric with associated Riemannian volume measure. Again, this measure is not so easy to understand using only Riemannian geometry, but the Riemannian volume measure will turn out to agree with the symplectic volume, which is accessible using our symplectic tools.

In particular, we first derive an algorithm for randomly sampling equivalence classes of FUNTFs
[F]\in\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
. Afterwards, we can obtain a random FUNTF by simply acting on a representative of the sampled equivalence class by a random element in
U(d)\times G
.

As noted beneath
Theorem

3.4
, the quotient
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
is a symplectic manifold if and only if
N
and
d
are relatively prime. When
\text{gcd}(N,d)\neq 1
, however, the quotient is a symplectic stratified space. By
Theorem

2.7
, it then contains a unique open, connected, and dense manifold, which we denote by
S
. The complement of
S
in
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
has positive codimension, and hence has measure zero with respect to the Riemannian volume measure. Consequently, a random sample from
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
will almost surely lie in
S
, so it suffices to find an algorithm for sampling the induced volume measure on
S
. Therefore, moving forward, we assume that
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
is a symplectic manifold, with the implicit understanding that in the case where
\text{gcd}(N,d)\neq 1
, this refers to the unique open, connected, and dense manifold of full measure within
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
.

Any symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
of dimension
2n
is equipped with a natural measure
m_{\omega}
, known as the
symplectic
or
Louisville
measure. To construct
m_{\omega}
, note that the non-degeneracy of
\omega
ensures the maximal wedge product
\omega^{\wedge n}
is nowhere vanishing, and thus defines a volume form on
M
. For a Borel set
U\subset M
, the measure is given by
m_{\omega}(U):=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{U}\omega^{\wedge n}.
In the case of
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
, we have the following useful fact: since
\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
is Kähler, the symplectic reduction

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}\sslash_{(-N/d\cdot\mathbb{I}_{d},\mathbf{1})}(U(d)\times G)=\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G),

is also Kähler. Moreover, the quotient Riemannian volume measure on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})\to\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
coincides with the symplectic measure
[
23
]
.

The key to our algorithm is the fact that
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
admits a half-dimensional Hamiltonian torus action. In particular, the following two powerful theorems apply.

Theorem 4.1

(Atiyah
[
2
]
and Guillemin–Sternberg
[
20
]
)
.

Suppose
(M,\omega)
is a (relatively) compact symplectic manifold of dimension
2n
that admits a Hamiltonian action of a torus
U(1)^{n}
. Let
\Phi:M\to(\mathfrak{u}(1)^{n})^{*}\approx\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the momentum map of the torus action. Then

•

For any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\Phi^{-1}(v)
is either empty or connected.

•

(The closure of) the image of
M
is the convex hull of the images of the fixed point of the torus action.

A
2n
-dimensional symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
is called a
toric symplectic manifold
if it admits a Hamiltonian action by the torus
U(1)^{n}
. By
Theorem

4.1
, (the closure of) the image
\Phi(M)
is a convex polytope
P
, known as the
moment polytope
of the torus action.

We now describe a process for generating random points on toric symplectic manifolds which are also Kähler, which we call
toric Kähler manifolds
. This process will later be applied to the specific manifold
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
.

Let
M
be a
2n
-dimensional toric symplectic manifold that admits a Hamiltonian toric action
\varphi:U(1)^{n}\to\operatorname{Diff}(M)
, with momentum map
\Phi:M\to(\mathfrak{u}(1)^{n})^{*}\approx\mathbb{R}^{n}
and moment polytope
P
. Define
\widetilde{M}
as the open, dense, full measure submanifold consisting of regular points of
\Phi
. Note that
\Phi(\widetilde{M})
has full measure in
P
. The restriction,
\Phi|_{\widetilde{M}}:\widetilde{M}\to\Phi(\widetilde{M})
, forms a
U(1)^{n}
-fiber bundle
[
12
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

We will call a map
\alpha:\Phi(\widetilde{M})\to\widetilde{M}
a
rough section
of
\Phi
if
\Phi\circ\alpha
is the identity map on
\Phi(\widetilde{M})
. Note that there is no assumption that
\alpha
is smooth or even continuous; hence the modifier “rough.”

Suppose there exists a rough section
\alpha:\Phi(\widetilde{M})\to\widetilde{M}
. Let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure on
P
, and let
\nu
denote the standard product measure on
U(1)^{n}
. Consider the following sampling scheme to generate points on
\widetilde{M}
.

Algorithm 1

Toric Kähler sampling

x\sim\text{Uniform}(\Phi(\widetilde{M}),\lambda)

y\leftarrow\alpha(x)\in\Phi^{-1}(x)

\theta\sim\text{Uniform}(U(1)^{n},\nu)

p\leftarrow\varphi(\theta,y)\in\widetilde{M}

return

p

To summarize, we begin by choosing a random point
x
in the image of
\widetilde{M}
under
\Phi
. Using
\alpha
, we determine a specific
y
in the fiber of
\Phi
above
x
; this process can be viewed as selecting a random fiber. Next, we choose a random point
p
on this fiber by applying a randomly selected torus element
\theta
to
y
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Points generated by
Algorithm

1
are sampled from the symplectic measure
m_{\omega}
on
M
.

While this theorem essentially follows from the Duistermaat–Heckman theorem
[
13
]
(see
[
12
, Chapter 30]
or
[
9
, Theorem 1]
), we are not aware of an explicit proof in the literature, so we provide one here.

Proof.

We begin by equipping a fiber of the momentum map
\Phi
with coordinates. For any
x\in\Phi(\widetilde{M})
, define
M_{x}:=\Phi^{-1}(x)
. Notice that
\Phi^{-1}(x)\approx U(1)^{n}
through the mapping

\displaystyle\psi_{x}:=\varphi(\,\cdot\,,\alpha(x)):U(1)^{n}\to M_{x},\hskip 15.0pt\theta\mapsto\varphi(\theta,\alpha(x)),

where
\varphi
is the torus action. Under this identification, the standard angle coordinates
(\theta^{1},\dots,\theta^{n})
in
U(1)^{n}
map to coordinates
(\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{1},\dots,\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{n})
on
M_{x}
given by
\widetilde{\theta}^{i}_{x}=\theta^{i}\circ\psi_{x}^{-1}
.

Next, we derive the Riemannian volume form
dM_{x}
in terms of the coordinates
(\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{i})
. For
i=1,\dots,n
let
Y_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{M})
be the infinitesimal vector field generated by
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\big|_{x}\in T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Phi^{i}=x^{i}\circ\Phi
, where
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
are the standard coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By definition,
Y_{i}
is the Hamiltonian vector field of the circle action
\varphi^{i}
and thus
Y_{i}|_{p}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{i}}|_{p}
. For any
Z\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
,

\displaystyle d\Phi^{i}(Z)=\omega(Y_{i},Z)=g(Y_{i},JZ)=g(-JY_{i},Z)

where
g
is the Riemannian metric and
J
is the complex structure on
M
.

On the other hand, by definition of the Riemannian gradient we know
d\Phi^{i}(Z)=g(\nabla\Phi^{i},Z)
. Thus, for every
Z\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
,
g(-JY_{i},Z)=g(\nabla\Phi^{i},Z)
. By the non-degeneracy of
g
, we have
-JY_{i}=\nabla\Phi^{i}
. This equality allows us to make the following observation:

\displaystyle|g|=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\det\bigg[g\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{j}}\bigg)\bigg]_{i,j}}=\sqrt{\det[g(Y_{i},Y_{j})]_{i,j}}=\sqrt{\det[g(-JY_{i},-JY_{j})]_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\det[g(\nabla\Phi^{i},\nabla\Phi^{j})]_{i,j}}=\sqrt{\det(D\Phi)(D\Phi)^{T}}=NJ\Phi,

where
NJ\Phi
is the normal Jacobian of
\Phi
. Thus,

\displaystyle dM_{x}=NJ\Phi\,d\tilde{\theta}_{x}^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\tilde{\theta}_{x}^{n}.

(12)

Now, we describe the measure from which
Algorithm

1
samples. For a Borel set
U\subset M
, define
{U_{x}:=U\cap M_{x}}
. Consider the measure
\mu_{x}
on the fiber
M_{x}
, defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{x}(U_{x}):=\int_{\theta\in T^{n}}\varphi(\theta,\,\cdot\,)_{*}\delta_{\alpha(x)}(U_{x})d\nu(\theta),

where
\varphi(\theta,\,\cdot\,):M_{x}\to M_{x}
is the map
p\mapsto\varphi(\theta,p)
and
\delta_{\alpha(x)}
is the Dirac measure on
M_{x}
centered at
\alpha(x)
. Notice that the measure
\mu_{x}
can be sampled by applying a random torus element
\theta\sim\text{Uniform}(U(1)^{n},\nu)
to
\alpha(x)
. It follows that
Algorithm

1
generates samples from the measure
\mu
on
\widetilde{M}
defined as

\displaystyle\mu(U):=\int_{x\in\Phi(U)}\mu_{x}(U_{x})d\lambda(x).

Thus, our goal is to show that
m_{\omega}=\mu
.

To better understand the measure
\mu
from which
Algorithm

1
samples, observe that

\displaystyle\varphi(\theta,\,\cdot\,)_{*}\delta_{\alpha(x)}(U_{x})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1&\alpha(x)\in\varphi(\theta,\,\cdot\,)^{-1}(U_{x})\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1&\varphi(\theta,\alpha(x))\in U_{x}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This is equivalent to the characteristic function
\chi_{\psi_{x}^{-1}(U_{x})}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mu_{x}(U_{x})=\int_{\theta\in U(1)^{n}}\chi_{\psi_{x}^{-1}(U_{x})}(\theta)d\nu(\theta)=(\psi_{x})_{*}\nu(U_{x}).

Since the standard product measure
\nu
coincides with the Riemannian volume form
dU(1)^{n}=d\theta^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\theta^{n}
on
U(1)^{n}
, we can use a change of coordinates to express this as:

\displaystyle(\psi_{x})_{*}\nu(U_{x})=

\displaystyle\int_{p\in M_{x}}\chi_{U_{x}}(p)d(\psi_{x})_{*}\nu(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\theta\in\psi_{x}^{-1}(U_{x})}|\det D\psi_{x}(\theta)|dU(1)^{n},

where
|\det D\psi_{x}(\theta)|
is the determinant of the Jacobian of
\psi_{x}
evaluated at
\theta
with respect to the the angle coordinates
(\theta^{i})
on
U(1)^{n}
and the coordinates
(\widetilde{\theta}^{i}_{x})
on
M_{x}
. Since
\widetilde{\theta}^{i}_{x}=\theta^{i}\circ\psi_{x}^{-1}
, it follows that
D\psi_{x}(\theta)=\text{Id}_{n}
.

Using this simplification in combination with the fact that Lebesgue measure on
\Phi(\widetilde{M})
coincides with its Riemannian volume form, we can write
\mu
as

\displaystyle\mu(U)=\int_{x\in\Phi(U)}\bigg(\int_{\theta\in\psi_{x}^{-1}(U_{x})}1dU(1)^{n}\bigg)d\Phi(\widetilde{M}).

(13)

Finally, we turn our attention to the symplectic measure
m_{\omega}
and show it agrees with (
13
). Because
\widetilde{M}
is Kähler,
\widetilde{M}
is also a Riemannian manifold, and its Riemannian volume form coincides with the symplectic measure. Applying the smooth coarea formula and recalling that
dM_{x}=NJ\Phi\,d\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{n}
from (
12
), we have

m_{\omega}(U)=\int_{M}\chi_{U}d\widetilde{M}=\int_{\Phi(U)}\bigg(\int_{U_{x}}\frac{1}{NJ\Phi}dM_{x}\bigg)d\Phi(\widetilde{M})=\int_{\Phi(U)}\bigg(\int_{U_{x}}d\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\widetilde{\theta}_{x}^{n}\bigg)d\Phi(\widetilde{M})\\
=\int_{\Phi(U)}\bigg(\int_{\psi_{x}^{-1}(U_{x})}d\theta^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\theta^{n}\bigg)d\Phi(\widetilde{M})=\int_{x\in\Phi(U)}\bigg(\int_{\theta\in\psi_{x}^{-1}(U_{x})}dU(1)^{n}\bigg)d\Phi(\widetilde{M})=\mu(U)

as desired.

∎

4.1
Circle Actions

We define a Hamiltonian torus action on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
as a product of individual circle actions, which we now describe. First, we define the
U(1)
-actions on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
and then descend these actions to the quotient
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
.

Let
F=[f_{1}\,|\,\cdots\,|\,f_{N}]\in\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
, and let
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Recall that the
k^{th}
partial frame operator of
F
is defined as
S_{k}:=f_{1}f_{1}^{*}+\dots+f_{k}f_{k}^{*}
. The matrix
S_{k}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(d)
and has rank at most
\text{min}\{k,d\}
. In particular,
S_{k}
is unitarily diagonalizable, meaning there exist
U_{k}=[u_{k,1}\,|\,\cdots\,|\,u_{k,d}]\in U(d)
and
\mu_{k,1}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{k,d}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
S_{k}=U_{k}^{*}\operatorname{diag}(\mu_{k,1},\dots,\mu_{k,d})U_{k}
, where
u_{k,j}
is the eigenvector of
S_{k}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu_{k,j}
. As introduced in
Section

2.1
, the values
\mu_{k,j}
are called the
eigensteps
of the frame
F
. Now, fix
j\in\{1,\dots,\min\{k,d\}\}
and assume that
\mu_{k,j}
is an isolated eigenvalue of
S_{k}
. This assumption implies that the eigenspace of
\mu_{k,j}
is
\text{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{u_{k,j}\}
, so there is only one orthonormal basis for this eigenspace, up to the choice of scalar factor. Since
u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}(d)
, it follows that
\sqrt{-1}u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*}\in\mathfrak{u}(d)
and hence
\{\exp(t\sqrt{-1}u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*})\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is the 1-parameter subgroup of
U(d)
generated by
\sqrt{-1}u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*}
. We define the
U(1)
-action as follows:

\displaystyle\varphi_{k,j}:U(1)\times\mathcal{F}^{d,N}\to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{d,N}

(14)

\displaystyle(t,F)\mapsto

\displaystyle\bigg[\text{exp}(t\sqrt{-1}u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*})\big[f_{1}\,|\,\cdots\,|\,f_{k}\big]\,|\,f_{k+1}\,|\,\cdots\,|\,f_{N}\bigg],

(15)

so that
\varphi_{k,j}
multiplies the first
k
frame vectors of
F
by
\exp(t\sqrt{-1}u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*})
while leaving the remaining
N-k
frame vectors fixed. Note that
u_{k,j}
depends on
F
.

Remark 4.4
.

The purpose of this remark is to clarify why
\varphi_{k,j}
is a
U(1)
-action. At first glance,
\varphi_{k,j}
may appear define an action by
\mathbb{R}
, since we could evaluate (
15
) for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. However,
\varphi_{k,j}(t,F)=\varphi_{k,j}(t+2\pi n,F)
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
; that is,
\varphi_{k,j}
is
2\pi
-periodic. To see this, note that
u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}(d)
is a rank-1 projector, so
(u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*})^{2}=u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*}
. This allows us to simplify the Taylor series expansion of the matrix exponential as follows:

\displaystyle\text{exp}(t\sqrt{-1}u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{d}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(t\sqrt{-1})^{m}(u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*})^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{d}+u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(t\sqrt{-1})^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{d}+u_{k,j}u_{k,j}^{*}(e^{t\sqrt{-1}}-1),

which shows the action is indeed
2\pi
-periodic. Thus, taking values
t\in[0,2\pi)
captures all of
\varphi_{k,j}
, and identifying
[0,2\pi)
with
U(1)
via the mapping
t\mapsto e^{t\sqrt{-1}}
confirms that
\varphi_{k,j}
is a genuine
U(1)
-action on
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
.

Remark 4.5
.

Technically, the action
\varphi_{k,j}
is only defined on the open dense subset of
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
consisting of frames for which the

partial frame operator has its

eigenvalue isolated when the spectrum is arranged in nonincreasing order. Specifically, if
F

is an eigenvalue of

Theorem 4.6

(Needham and Shonkwiler

\varphi_{k,j}
descends to a well-defined action
\widetilde{\varphi}_{k,j}
on the open, dense, full measure subset
q(\mathcal{U}_{k,j})\subset\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
given by
\widetilde{\varphi}_{k,j}(t,[F])=[\varphi_{k,j}(t,F)]
.

When
(k,j)\in\mathcal{I}
, the momentum map
\Phi_{k,j}
descends to a well-defined map on
q(\mathcal{U}_{k,j})\subset\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
. To see this, take an
F\in\mathcal{U}_{k,j}
and let
AFD
be an element in the
U(d)\times G
orbit of
F
, where
A\in U(d)
and
D\in G
. The
k^{th}
partial frame operators of
F
and
AFD
are given by

\displaystyle S_{k}(F)=f_{1}f_{1}^{*}+\cdots+f_{k}f_{k}^{*}

and

\displaystyle S_{k}(AFD)=Af_{1}D(Af_{1}D)^{*}+\cdots+Af_{k}D(Af_{k}D)^{*}=Af_{1}f_{1}^{*}A^{*}+\cdots+Af_{k}f_{k}^{*}A^{*}.

Notice that
S_{k}(AFD)
is simply
S_{k}(F)
conjugated by the unitary matrix
A
, so
S_{k}(F)
and
S_{k}(AFD)
have identical spectra. This implies that
\Phi_{k,j}
is
(U(d)\times G)
-invariant, so the map

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{k,j}:q(\mathcal{U}_{k,j})\to\mathbb{R}\approx\mathfrak{u}(1)^{*},\hskip 15.0pt[F]\mapsto\Phi_{k,j}(F)

is well-defined. Furthermore,
\widetilde{\Phi}_{k,j}
serves as momentum map for the descended action, making
\widetilde{\varphi}_{k,j}
a Hamiltonian action.

Theorem 4.7
.

For each
(k,j)\in\mathcal{I}
, the
U(1)
-action
\varphi_{k,j}
descends to a well defined circle action
\widetilde{\varphi}_{k,j}
on the open and dense full measure submanifold
q(\mathcal{U}_{k,j})
of
\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
. Moreover, the action is Hamiltonian and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{k,j}
is the momentum map.

Proof.

This follows from a more general fact about Hamiltonian actions. If the product Lie group
H_{1}\times H_{2}
acts on a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
in a Hamiltonian way with momentum map
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):M\to\mathfrak{h}_{1}^{*}\times\mathfrak{h}_{2}^{*}
, then
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
individually act on
M
and the two actions commute. Moreover, for any coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathfrak{h}_{1}^{*}
, the
H_{2}
action is
H_{1}
-invariant on
M\sslash_{\mathcal{O}}H_{1}
so the
H_{2}
-action descends to a well defined action on the quotient
M\sslash_{\mathcal{O}}H_{1}
. The descended action is Hamiltonian with momentum map
\widetilde{\mu}_{2}
characterized by
q^{*}\widetilde{\mu}_{2}=\iota^{*}\mu_{2}
where
q:\mu_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{O})\to M\sslash_{\mathcal{O}}H_{1}
is the quotient map and
\iota:\mu_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{O})\to M
is the inclusion map. Then setting
M=\mathcal{F}^{d,N}
,
H_{1}=U(d)\times G
, and
H_{2}=U(1)
gives the desired result.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

The torus
U(1)^{d_{T}}
of dimension
d_{T}:=(d-1)(N-d-1)
acts on the open, dense, and full measure submanifold
q(\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{I}})\subset\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
as the product of the circle actions
\varphi=\bigtimes_{(k,j)\in\mathcal{I}}{\widetilde{\varphi}_{k,j}}
. The action is Hamiltonian and
\Phi:\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{I}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}}\approx(\mathfrak{u}(1)^{d_{T}})^{*}
given by
[F]\mapsto(\mu_{k,j}(F))_{(k,j)\in\mathcal{I}}
is the momentum map. Since

\displaystyle d_{T}=\frac{1}{2}\text{ dim }\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)=\frac{1}{2}\text{ dim }\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{I}},

\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{I}}
is a toric symplectic manifold.

Proof.

As shown in
[
29
]
, for each
(k,j),(l,m)\in\mathcal{I}
the circle actions
\widetilde{\varphi}_{kj}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{kj}
commute; i.e.,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}_{kj}(t,\widetilde{\varphi}_{lm}(s,[F]))=\widetilde{\varphi}_{lm}(s,\widetilde{\varphi}_{kj}(t,[F])).

Additionally, it is easy to see that the cardinality of
\mathcal{I}
is
d_{T}
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{P}_{d,N}:=\Phi(\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{I}})

\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d,N}:=\text{cl }\mathcal{P}_{d,N}

Notice, given an
[F]\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{I}}

5
Implementation

As a proof of concept, we implemented this algorithm in Python. The implementation supports any values of
d
and
N
, provided that
N>d+1
. However, for large
d
and
N
, our simple implementation becomes intractable due to computational complexity. It is well known in the literature that uniformly sampling a convex polytope is a challenging problem, and our implementation reflects this difficulty.

The computational complexity of
Algorithm

2
is primarily dictated by the first step: sampling
\mu
from
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d,N}
. We attempted to use the standard hit-and-run algorithm
[
5
,
4
,
1
,
32
]
, which is a Markov chain that converges to Lebesgue measure on any convex polytope. However, it performed poorly, exhibiting extremely slow convergence on our polytope
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d,N}
.

For the purpose of this paper, our goal is to demonstrate a proof of concept in low dimensions. Given this, a reasonable approach is to use rejection sampling within the bounding box of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d,N}
. However, for the algorithm to be practical with larger values of
d
and
N
, a new sampling paradigm must be developed for generating random independent eigensteps. Note that the Top Kill algorithm
[
18
]
samples eigensteps from a measure on
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d,N}
which is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, but not from Lebesgues masure itself.

In our proof-of-concept experiment, we sampled
10^{6}
uniformly random unitary classes of FUNTFs from
\mathcal{F}_{N}/(U(d)\times G)
using Algorithm
2
. We then recorded the coherence, defined for a unit norm frame
F
as
\text{Coh}(F):=\max_{i\neq j}|\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle|
, of the representative in a histogram. The results are shown in
Fig.

4
.

Figure 4:
Distribution of coherences of representatives of random unitary classes FUNTF’s generated using Algorithm
2
.

We note that these coherence values lie within the known bounds listed in
[
25
]
and the distributions for all
d=2
are consistent with those shown in
[
30
]
. Moreover, in the cases where
d=3,N=5
and
d=4,N=7
, the maximum coherence is not 1, but rather
\frac{N-d}{d}
. This observation led us to the following proposition. While this fact may be familiar to experts, we have not encountered it in the literature.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
F\in\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})
, the coherence of
F
is bounded above by
\min\{1,(N-d)/d\}
.

Proof.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}^{d,N}_{\boldsymbol{N/d}}(\boldsymbol{1})/(U(d)\times G)
. If
N\geq 2d
, then
\min\{(N-d)/d,1\}=1
. Since each frame vector of
F
has squared norm equal to
1
, this provides the trivial bound on
\text{Coh}(F)
. Moreover, this bound is sharp: when
N\geq 2d
, it is possible to fill in the eigenstep array from Figure
1
starting with
\mu_{1,1}=1
,
\mu_{2,1}=2
, so there is a frame having these eigensteps; for example, when
N=2d
we can simply concatenate two copies of the same orthonormal basis. This choice of eigensteps corresponds to the situation when the first two frame vectors lie in the same line, and hence
\text{Coh}(F)=1
.

It remains to consider the case
N<2d
, when
\min\{(N-d)/d,1\}=(N-d)/d
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\max_{i\neq j}|\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle|
is attained by the first two frame vectors
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
i.e.,
\text{Coh}(F)=|\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle|
. Recall that the eigensteps
\mu_{2,1}
and
\mu_{2,2}
are defined to be the eigenvalues of
S_{2}=f_{1}f_{1}^{*}+f_{2}f_{2}^{*}
. These are also the eigenvalues of the upper left
2\times 2
minor of the Gram matrix
F^{*}F
, given by

\displaystyle[F^{*}F]_{M}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle\\
\langle f_{2},f_{1}\rangle&1\end{bmatrix}.

The characteristic polynomial of
[F^{*}F]_{M}
is

\displaystyle\chi_{[F^{*}F]_{M}}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-2\lambda+1-|\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle|^{2}.

Solving for its roots, the larger eigenvalue is

\displaystyle\mu_{2,1}=1+|\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle|.

From our work done in Figure
1
, we know that
\mu_{2,1}\leq\mu_{N,1}=N/d
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle 1+|\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle|\leq\frac{N}{d}.

After rearranging, we arrive at the desired bound

\displaystyle\text{Coh}(F)=|\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle|\leq\frac{N-d}{d}.

∎

For
d=3

N=5

; then, we compute the coherence at each grid point; and finally, we present the results as a heat map. The heat maps corresponding to two regular values are shown in
Fig.

6
Discussion

In order to efficiently sample higher-dimensional FUNTF spaces, we need a better approach to sampling the eigenstep polytope than either rejection sampling (which seems to scale exponentially) or hit-and-run (which seems to converge too slowly to be practical). When
d=2
there is a volume-preserving affine transformation
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{2,N}\to[-1,1]^{d_{T}}

[
7
]
; since
\frac{\operatorname{vol}\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{2,N}}{\operatorname{vol}[-1,1]^{d_{T}}}=O(N^{-3/2})
, rejection sampling becomes feasible even for very large
N
, and this gives a sampling algorithm for length-
N
FUNTFs in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with expected runtime
\Theta(N^{5/2})

[
30
]
.
1
1

1

In fact, by more recent work
[
8
]
, this can be speeded up to
\Theta(N^{2})
.
Does such an affine tranformation exist for larger
d
? And what is the (asymptotic) volume of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d,N}
?

Given some such way of speeding up the eigenstep sampling part of
Eigenlift
, it would then be interesting to perform numerical experiments on the distributions of various statistics of interest on FUNTFs. For example, what can be said about the distribution of coherence? The family of distributions observed in
Fig.

4
seems hard to characterize without more data.

Haikin, Zamir, and Gavish
[

]
have observed that for many classes of FUNTFs, the spectra of random submatrices follow a MANOVA distribution (see also
[
17
,
15
]
). This, in turn, implies that these frames have better properties for applications like compressed sensing than random Gaussian frames. It seems quite reasonable to guess that the same is true for FUNTFs as a whole,

and this would be an interesting conjecture to test experimentally.

Finally, there is nothing particularly special about FUNTFs in our development of
Eigenlift
. Analogous algorithms should exist for complex frames with
any
prescribed spectrum of the frame operator and
any
prescribed frame vector norms. The quotient of such frame spaces by
U(d)\times G
