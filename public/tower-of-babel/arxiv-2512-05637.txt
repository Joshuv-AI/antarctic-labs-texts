Geometric control of boundary-catalytic branching processes
Denis S. Grebenkov, Yilin Ye

Abstract
Boundary-catalytic branching processes describe a broad class of natural phenomena where the population of diffusing particles grows due to their spontaneous binary branching (e.g., division, fission or splitting) on a catalytic boundary located in a complex environment. We investigate the possibility of geometric control of the population growth by compensating the proliferation of particles due to branching events by their absorptions in the bulk or on boundary absorbing regions. We identify an appropriate Steklov spectral problem to obtain the phase diagram of this out-of-equilibrium stochastic process. The principal eigenvalue determines the critical line that separates an exponential growth of the population from its extinction. In other words, we establish a powerful tool for calculating the optimal absorption rate that equilibrates the opposite effects of branching and absorption events and thus results in steady-state behavior of this diffusion-reaction system. Moreover, we show the existence of a critical catalytic rate above which no compensation is possible, so that the population cannot be controlled and keeps growing exponentially. The proposed framework opens promising perspectives for better understanding, modeling and control of various boundary-catalytic branching processes, with applications in physics and life sciences.

process based on the boundary local time, a spectral representation of
the population size and its long-time asymptotic behavior, matched
asymptotic expansions, and optimization schemes for choosing the

In this study, we aim at answering how the geometric configuration of
absorbing and catalytic regions controls the long-time behavior of the
population size

The diffusive dynamics is governed by the Laplace operator and its
spectral properties. When the domain

is bounded, the
spectrum is discrete, i.e., there are infinitely many eigenpairs

\{\lambda,u\}
, satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=\lambda u~~\textrm{in}~\Omega,\qquad\partial_{n}u-q_{\mathrm{c}}u=0~~\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},

(2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u+q_{\mathrm{a}}u

\displaystyle=0~~\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}},\qquad\partial_{n}u=0~~\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}.

(2b)

The eigenvalues are enumerated by the index

\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\nearrow+\infty

N(t|\bm{x})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-Dt\lambda_{k}}\,u_{k}(\bm{x})\int\limits_{\Omega}u_{k}.

(3)

It is therefore clear that the long-time behavior of

controlled by the principal (smallest) eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}

contrast to the survival probability, which always decays

\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
and the destroying effect of absorption events on

\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}

may exponentially decay

; (ii) for given

and

, to find the optimal
geometric configuration of absorption regions to achieve the desired

\lambda_{0}
. In the following, we will mainly focus on the first
aspect for the target value
\lambda_{0}=0
ensuring the steady-state

population size reaches a finite steady-state value
N(\infty|\bm{x})=1+(R/|\bm{x}|)/(1/(q_{\mathrm{c}}R)-1)
; (iii) in the critical setting
q_{\mathrm{c}}=1/R
, the population size exhibits an unlimited power-law growth:

N(t|\bm{x})\propto\sqrt{Dt}/|\bm{x}|
, as
t\to\infty
. Moreover, the
critical value
1/R
can be identified again with the principal
eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
of the Steklov problem (
9
) in the
exterior of a ball, for which
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}=\emptyset
.

While the above picture was based on the explicit solution for the
exterior of a ball, it remains valid for general exterior domains. In
fact, let
\mu_{0}
be the principal Steklov eigenvalue in the exterior

\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\overline{\Omega_{0}}
of a bounded domain

\Omega_{0}
with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
(see

Auchmuty14
;
Arendt15
for its rigorous formulation).
The Laplace operator in
\Omega
with Robin boundary condition on

\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
has (at least) one strictly negative eigenvalue, if and
only if
q_{\mathrm{c}}>q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}=\mu_{0}

Bundrock25
. As a
consequence, even though the spectral expansion (
3
)
is not applicable because the Laplace operator has the essential
spectrum
[0,+\infty)
, the presence of the isolated eigenvalue

\lambda_{0}<0
ensures an exponential growth of the population size
with the rate
1/(D|\lambda_{0}|)
(see

Freitas15
;
Krejcirik18
;
Krejcirik20
for further discussions of
the related spectral properties). In contrast, the analysis of the
long-time asymptotic behavior of the population size in the case
q_{c}\leq\mu_{0}
remains an open problem.

Throughout this Letter, we assumed that the particles could be killed
exclusively on the absorbing region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
. In many
applications, the bulk medium itself can be aggressive and lead to
eventual destructions of the diffusing particles with some rate
\nu>0

Yuste13
;
Meerson15
;
Grebenkov17
. This mechanism, which can
also account for a finite lifetime of particles and their spontaneous
death in the bulk, can be easily incorporated by adding the reactive
term
-\nu N(t|\bm{x})
to the right-hand side of the diffusion equation
(
1
). In this case, the steady-state regime would
correspond to
\lambda_{0}=-\nu/D
instead of
\lambda_{0}=0
, and the
above analysis can be adapted to this more general situation.

In summary, we formulated and solved the fundamental problem of
geometric control of the population size of BCB processes. In fact,
we identified the generalized Steklov problem as the proper
mathematical tool to determine the optimal absorption rate

\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},\lambda_{0})
that ensures the desired behavior of
the population size at long times. We showed that the absorption
events can compensate the branching events to reach a nontrivial
steady-state limit only if the catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}
does not exceed
the critical value
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
. The latter depends on the
geometric configuration of both catalytic and absorbing regions. In
the typical case of small regions, the asymptotic formula for

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
was derived. These theoretical advances open
promising ways for engineering optimal configurations of absorption
regions to put the population size under control in various
diffusion-reaction systems. Moreover, many other aspects of BCB
processes, such as their first-passage time statistics, eventual
instabilities due to nonlinear nature of branching events, the rate of
relaxation to the steady-state solution (controlled by the next
eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
), role of fluctuations in this critical regime,
and extreme value statistics, remain unknown and present open
challenging problems for statistical physics and related disciplines.

SM.I
Low-rate regime

In this Section, we provide some details on the perturbation approach
in the low-rate regime when both rates
q_{\mathrm{a}}
and
q_{\mathrm{c}}
are
small. In this setting, the Laplace operator with mixed boundary
conditions (
2
) can be seen as a perturbation of
the Laplace operator in the same bounded domain with Neumann boundary
condition on the whole boundary
\partial\Omega
. As a consequence, one can
search the principal eigenpair
\{\lambda_{0},u_{0}\}
of the spectral
problem (
2
) as

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{(0)}+\epsilon\lambda^{(1)}+\epsilon^{2}\lambda^{(2)}+\cdots,

(S11a)

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=u^{(0)}+\epsilon u^{(1)}+\epsilon^{2}u^{(2)}+\cdots,

(S11b)

where
\epsilon
is a small parameter, and superscripts are used to
indicate the correction orders in powers of
\epsilon
. The principal
eigenvalue of the “unperturbed” operator is zero,
\lambda^{(0)}=0
, whereas the associated eigenfunction is constant:
u^{(0)}=const
. Substituting the above expansions into
Eqs. (
2
), setting
q_{\mathrm{a}}=a\epsilon
and

q_{\mathrm{c}}=c\epsilon
, and collecting the terms of the same order, we
get in the first order:

\displaystyle-\Delta u^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{(1)}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,\quad\partial_{n}u^{(1)}=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}},

(S12a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u^{(1)}

\displaystyle=c\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},\quad\partial_{n}u^{(1)}=-a\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}.

(S12b)

Integrating the above Poisson equation over
\Omega
and applying the
Green’s formula, one has

-|\Omega|\lambda^{(1)}=\int\limits_{\Omega}\Delta u^{(1)}=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{n}u^{(1)}=c|\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}|-a|\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}|.

Multiplication of this relation by
\epsilon
yields
Eq. (
4
), in the first order in
\epsilon
.

In the next order, we have

\displaystyle-\Delta u^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{(2)}+\lambda^{(1)}u^{(1)}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,\quad\partial_{n}u^{(2)}=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}},

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u^{(2)}

\displaystyle=cu^{(1)}\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},\quad\partial_{n}u^{(2)}=-au^{(1)}\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}.

Integration of the Poisson equation over
\Omega
yields

-|\Omega|\lambda^{(2)}-\lambda^{(1)}\int\limits_{\Omega}u^{(1)}=\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}c\,u^{(1)}-\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}}c\,u^{(1)}.

(S13)

At the same time, multiplying Eq. (
S12a
) by
u^{(1)}
and
integrating over
\Omega
, we deduce with the help of the Green’s
formula and boundary conditions:

-\lambda^{(1)}\int\limits_{\Omega}u^{(1)}=-\int\limits_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{(1)}|^{2}+\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}c\,u^{(1)}-\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}}a\,u^{(1)}.

(S14)

Subtracting these equations, we obtain

\lambda^{(2)}=-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int\limits_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{(1)}|^{2}<0.

(S15)

We conclude that the second-order correction
\lambda^{(2)}
to the
principal eigenvalue is negative. As a consequence, even if we fix

q_{\mathrm{a}}
according to Eq. (
5
) to ensure that

\lambda^{(1)}=0
, the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
would be
small in amplitude but strictly negative, implying an exponential
growth of the population size.

SM.II
Solution for the interval

The spectral properties of the Laplace operator on the interval

(0,L)
are well known (see, e.g.,

SM_Carslaw
;
SM_Thambynayagam
;
SM_Grebenkov13
). Searching eigenvalues and
eigenfunctions in the form

u(x)=a\cos(\alpha x)+b\sin(\alpha x),\qquad\lambda=\alpha^{2},

(S16)

one relates the unknown coefficients
a
,
b
and
\alpha
by
rewriting Robin boundary conditions in Eqs. (
2
)
as

\displaystyle-b\alpha-q_{\mathrm{c}}a

\displaystyle=0\quad(\textrm{at}~x=0),

\displaystyle\alpha(-a\sin(\alpha L)+b\cos(\alpha L))

\displaystyle+q_{\mathrm{a}}(a\cos(\alpha L)+b\sin(\alpha L))

\displaystyle=0\quad(\textrm{at}~x=L).

Combining these relations yields

(\alpha^{2}+q_{\mathrm{a}}q_{\mathrm{c}})\frac{\tan(\alpha L)}{\alpha}=q_{\mathrm{a}}-q_{\mathrm{c}},

(S17)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
6
) from the main text.

In the case
q_{\mathrm{a}}=q_{\mathrm{c}}=q
, we get
(\alpha^{2}+q^{2})\tan(\alpha L)/\alpha=0
so that
\alpha_{k}L=\pi k
, with
k=1,2,\cdots
, i.e., we retrieve the eigenvalues for an interval with
reflecting endpoints. In turn, the principal eigenvalue is given by

\lambda_{0}=\alpha_{0}^{2}=-q^{2}<0
.

SM.III
Critical catalytic rate

Let us consider the confining domain
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}~:~R<|\bm{x}|<L\}
between two concentric spheres of radii
R
and
L
(
d\geq 2
). The inner sphere is the catalytic region with
q_{\mathrm{c}}\geq 0
,
whereas the outer sphere is the absorbing region with
q_{\mathrm{a}}\geq 0
.
For a given catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}
, we aim at finding the optimal
absorption rate
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)
so that the absorption events
compensate the branching events and thus give the steady-state
population. Setting
\lambda=0
into Eq. (
2
),
one can search the associated eigenfunction in the radial form as

u(\bm{x})=A+Br^{2-d}
for
d\geq 3
, or as
u(\bm{x})=A+B\ln r

for
d=2
, where
r=|\bm{x}|
, with unknown constants
A
and
B
.
The Robin boundary conditions in Eqs. (
2
) read as

\displaystyle-\partial_{r}u-q_{\mathrm{c}}u

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{at}~r=R,

(S18a)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}u+q_{\mathrm{a}}u

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{at}~r=L,

(S18b)

allowing us to deduce the relation between
q_{\mathrm{a}}
and
q_{\mathrm{c}}
.

In the planar case (
d=2
), Eqs. (
S18
) read as

\displaystyle\frac{B}{q_{\mathrm{c}}R}+A+B\ln R

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\frac{B}{q_{\mathrm{a}}L}+A+B\ln L

\displaystyle=0,

so that the nontrivial solution is possible if and only if

-\frac{1}{q_{\mathrm{c}}R}+\frac{1}{q_{\mathrm{a}}L}+\ln(L/R)=0,

from which

q_{\mathrm{a}}=\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)=q_{\mathrm{c}}\frac{R/L}{1-q_{\mathrm{c}}R\ln(L/R)}\,.

(S19)

We get thus Eq. (
7
) from the main text, with

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
given by Eq. (
8
).

When
d\geq 3
, Eqs. (
S18
) read as

\displaystyle\frac{(d-2)B}{q_{\mathrm{c}}R^{d-1}}-\biggl(A+\frac{B}{R^{d-2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-\frac{(d-2)B}{q_{\mathrm{a}}L^{d-1}}+\biggl(A+\frac{B}{L^{d-2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=0,

so that the nontrivial solution is possible if and only if

\frac{d-2}{q_{\mathrm{c}}R^{d-1}}-\frac{d-2}{q_{\mathrm{a}}L^{d-1}}+\frac{1}{L^{d-2}}-\frac{1}{R^{d-2}}=0,

from which

q_{\mathrm{a}}=\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)=q_{\mathrm{c}}\frac{(R/L)^{d-1}}{1-q_{\mathrm{c}}R(1-(R/L)^{d-2})/(d-2)}\,.

(S20)

We retrieve again Eq. (
7
) from the main text.

SM.IV
Asymptotic behavior near critical regime

In this Section, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of the optimal
absorption rate
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)
as the catalytic rate

q_{\mathrm{c}}
approaches its critical value
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
. We recall
that
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)
corresponds to the solution of
Eq. (
2
) with
\lambda=0
and can be determined
as
-\sigma_{0}
, where
\sigma_{0}
is the principal eigenvalue of the
following Steklov problem

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(S21a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u

\displaystyle=\sigma u\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}},

(S21b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u

\displaystyle=q_{\mathrm{c}}u\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},\qquad\partial_{n}u=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}.

(S21c)

We multiply Eq. (
S21a
) by the eigenfunction
v

satisfying Eqs. (
9
), multiply Eq. (
9a
)
by
u
, subtract them, integrate over
\Omega
, use the Green’s
formula, and apply the boundary conditions to get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega}\bigl(u\Delta v-v\Delta u\bigr)=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}\bigl(u\partial_{n}v-v\partial_{n}u\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}}\frac{\partial_{n}u}{\sigma}\partial_{n}v+(\mu-q_{\mathrm{c}})\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}uv.

Applying this identity to the principal eigenpair
\{\sigma_{0},u_{0}\}

of the spectral problem (
S21
) and the principal
eigenpair
\{\mu_{0},v_{0}\}
of the spectral problem (
9
),
we identify
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)
with
-\sigma_{0}
and
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
with
\mu_{0}
. In this case, the above identity reads

\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)=\frac{1}{q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}-q_{\mathrm{c}}}\frac{\int\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}}(\partial_{n}u_{0})(\partial_{n}v_{0})}{\int\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}u_{0}v_{0}}\,.

(S22)

This relation is exact and valid for any
0<q_{\mathrm{c}}<q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
. As the eigenfunction
u_{0}
depends on
q_{\mathrm{c}}
, this
representation remains quite formal. However, as the limit
q_{\mathrm{c}}\to q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
corresponds to
q_{\mathrm{a}}\to\infty
(and thus

\sigma_{0}\to-\infty
), the PDE (
S21
) becomes closer
and closer to the PDE (
9
), so that
u_{0}
approaches

v_{0}
. As a consequence, the ratio of two integrals in
Eq. (
S22
) approaches a finite limit so that

\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)\approx\frac{1}{q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}-q_{\mathrm{c}}}\frac{\int\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}}(\partial_{n}v_{0})^{2}}{\int\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}v_{0}^{2}}\qquad(q_{\mathrm{c}}\to q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}).

(S23)

We therefore deduced Eq. (
10
) from the main text.

In a similar way, we can estimate the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}

when
q_{\mathrm{c}}
is close to
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
(and
q_{\mathrm{a}}=\infty
).
Multiplying Eq. (
2a
) by
v_{0}
, multiplying
Eq. (
9a
) by
u
, subtracting them, integrating over

\Omega
, using the Green’s formula and boundary conditions, we get

\displaystyle\lambda\int\limits_{\Omega}u\,v_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega}(u\,\Delta v_{0}-v_{0}\,\Delta u)=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}(u\,\partial_{n}v_{0}-v_{0}\,\partial_{n}u)

\displaystyle=(q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}-q_{\mathrm{c}})\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}u\,v_{0},

where
\mu_{0}
was again identified with
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
. This
identity is valid for any eigenpair
\{\lambda,u\}
of the Laplace
operator. When
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
is the principal eigenvalue, the
associated eigenfunction
u_{0}
should be close to
v_{0}
in the limit

q_{\mathrm{c}}\to q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
, so that

\lambda_{0}\approx(q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}-q_{\mathrm{c}})\frac{\int\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}v_{0}^{2}}{\int\nolimits_{\Omega}v_{0}^{2}}\,.

(S24)

This first-order approximation can be seen as a linear response of the
system to a small perturbation of
q_{\mathrm{c}}
around its critical value

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
. In particular, if
q_{\mathrm{c}}>q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
,
then
\lambda_{0}<0
, and the population size grows exponentially.

SM.V
Small-region asymptotic results

In this Section, we employ recent asymptotic results to estimate the
critical catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
in the case when both
catalytic and absorbing regions are small. We recall that

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
is given by the principal eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
of
the Steklov problem (
9
).
The asymptotic analysis of this spectral problem in two dimensions was
undertaken in
SM_Grebenkov25
, which was focused on the case when
the smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
is connected, i.e., both regions

\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
are
reactive patches
on the
otherwise reflecting boundary. More precisely,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
was
assumed to be a single connected arc-like subset, centered around

\bm{x}_{1}\in\partial\Omega
and of length
2\epsilon_{1}
, whereas
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
was
the union of
N-1
arc-like subsets, each being connected, centered
around
\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\Omega
and of length
2\epsilon_{j}
,
j=2,\cdots,N
.
All lengths
\epsilon_{j}
are small as compared to the diameter
2L
of
the domain,
\varepsilon_{j}=\epsilon_{j}/L\ll 1
, whereas the centers
\bm{x}_{i}

and
\bm{x}_{j}
are well separated from each other:
|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}|\sim L

for all
i\neq j
.

Under these assumptions, the asymptotic behavior of the principal
eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
of the spectral problem (
9
) was
obtained as

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}=\mu_{0}\simeq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}\bigl[C\ln(L/\epsilon_{1})+C_{0}\bigr]}\,,

(S25)

where
C_{0}=\frac{2}{\pi}\bigl(\frac{3}{2}-\ln 2\bigr)\approx 0.5137

is a numerical constant, whereas
C
depends on the lengths of all
patches, their locations on the boundary
\partial\Omega
, and the shape of the
confining domain in a sophisticated way:

C=\frac{2}{\pi}\biggl({\bf{e}}_{1}^{\dagger}{\bf{M}}_{0}^{-1}({\bf{e}}_{1}-\bm{\nu}{\bf{e}}/\bar{\nu})\biggr)^{-1}.

(S26)

Here
{\bf{M}}_{0}={\bf{I}}+\bigl({\bf{I}}-\bm{\nu}{\bf{e}}{\bf{e}}^{\dagger}/\bar{\nu}\bigr)\bm{\nu}{\bf{G}}
,
{\bf{I}}
is the identity matrix,
\nu_{1}=-1/\ln(\varepsilon_{1})
,
\nu_{j}=-1/\ln(\varepsilon_{j}/2)
for
j=2,3,\ldots,N
,
\bar{\nu}=\nu_{1}+\ldots+\nu_{N}
, and we used the following matrix notations:

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\nu_{1}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\nu_{2}&\ldots&0\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
0&0&\ldots&\nu_{N}\\
\end{array}\right),~{\bf{e}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\ldots\\
1\\
\end{array}\right),~{\bf{e}}_{k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\\
\ldots\\
0\\
\end{array}\right),

where
1
stands on the
k
-th row of the vector
{\bf{e}}_{k}
. The matrix

{\bf{G}}
has the elements

{\bf{G}}_{i,j}=\pi G(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})\quad(i\neq j),\qquad{\bf{G}}_{i,i}=\pi R(\bm{x}_{i}),

(S27)

where
G(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is the surface Neumann Green’s function satisfying
for any
\bm{y}\in\partial\Omega
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{x}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,\qquad\int\limits_{\Omega}G(\bm{x},\bm{y})d\bm{x}=0,

(S28a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}G

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega\backslash\{\bm{y}\},

(S28b)

\displaystyle G(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}{\sim}-\frac{1}{\pi}\ln|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|+R(\bm{y})+o(1),

(S28c)

where
|\Omega|
is the volume of
\Omega
, and
R(\bm{y})
is the regular
part of
G(\bm{x},\bm{y})
(i.e., its constant value once the singular
logarithmic term is subtracted).

To illustrate this asymptotic result, we take
\Omega
to the unit
disk (
L=1
), for which

G(\bm{x},\bm{y})=-\frac{1}{\pi}\ln|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|+\frac{|\bm{x}|^{2}}{4\pi}-\frac{1}{8\pi},\quad R(\bm{y})=\frac{1}{8\pi}\,.

(S29)

If the absorbing region consists of a single patch,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}^{1}
, the above matrices of size
2\times 2
can be computed
explicitly, yielding
SM_Grebenkov25

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}\approx\frac{\pi}{2\epsilon_{1}}\biggl(-\ln(\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2})+\frac{3}{2}+2\ln|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}|\biggr)^{-1}.

(S30)

Expectedly, when the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
is reduced (i.e.,

\epsilon_{1}\to 0
), the critical catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}

increases, so that it is easier to compensate the effect of branching
events. In contrast, if the absorbing region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
is reduced
(i.e.,
\epsilon_{2}\to 0
), less particles can be trapped, and

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
decreases. Similarly, the critical catalytic rate
also decreases when the distance
|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}|
between two regions
increases. However, both effects are weak due to the logarithmic
nature of the terms
\ln(1/\epsilon_{2})
and
\ln|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}|
.

As discussed in
SM_Grebenkov25
, the above asymptotic results can
also be generalized to the case when
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
and/or

\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}^{i}
are located in the bulk of
\Omega
. In fact, one has
to set
\nu_{j}=-1/\ln(d_{j})
, where
d_{1}
is the logarithmic capacity
of the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
and
d_{j}
is the logarithmic
capacity of the absorbing subset
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}^{j}
(
j=2,3,\cdots,N
); in addition, Eq. (
S27
) is replaced by

{\bf{G}}_{i,j}=2\pi G(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})\quad(i\neq j),\quad{\bf{G}}_{i,i}=2\pi R(\bm{x}_{i}),

(S31)

where
G(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is the bulk Neumann Green’s function (or
pseudo-Green’s function) satisfying for any
\bm{y}\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{x}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega\backslash\{\bm{y}\},

(S32a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}G

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega,\quad\int\limits_{\Omega}G(\bm{x},\bm{y})d\bm{x}=0,

(S32b)

\displaystyle G(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{x}\to\bm{y}}{\sim}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|+R(\bm{y})+o(1),

(S32c)

and
R(\bm{y})
is its regular part. Finally, the constant
C_{0}
has to
be recomputed from the asymptotic behavior of the reactive capacitance

{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)
(see Appendix C in
SM_Grebenkov25
for
details).

While a systematic analysis of this problem is beyond the scope of
this Letter, we provide an extension of Eq. (
S25
) to
the particular case when the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
is the
circle of radius
\epsilon_{1}
, located at point
\bm{x}_{1}\in\Omega
,
whereas the absorbing region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
is the union of
N-1

circles of radii
\epsilon_{j}
, located at points
\bm{x}_{j}\in\Omega
.
As previously, we assume that all
N
circles are small (
\epsilon_{j}\ll L
) and well-separated from each other and from the reflecting
outer boundary
\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}
of
\Omega
(i.e.,
|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}|\sim L

and
|\bm{x}_{i}-\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}|\sim L
), where
2L
is the diameter of the
domain
\Omega
. In this case,
d_{j}=\epsilon_{j}
,
{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)=1/\mu
so that Eq. (5.13) from
SM_Grebenkov25
becomes

\frac{1}{\mu_{0}\epsilon_{1}}\approx\frac{1}{\nu_{1}}\biggl({\bf{e}}_{1}^{\dagger}{\bf{M}}_{0}^{-1}({\bf{e}}_{1}-\bm{\nu}{\bf{e}}/\bar{\nu})\biggr).

(S33)

As a consequence, Eq. (
S25
) remains valid, with

C=\biggl({\bf{e}}_{1}^{\dagger}{\bf{M}}_{0}^{-1}({\bf{e}}_{1}-\bm{\nu}{\bf{e}}/\bar{\nu})\biggr)^{-1},\quad C_{0}=0.

(S34)

In the case when
\Omega
is the unit disk (
L=1
), the bulk Neumann
Green’s function was given in
SM_Kolokolnikov05
:

\displaystyle G(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(-\ln|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|-\ln\biggl|\bm{x}|\bm{y}|-\bm{y}/|\bm{y}|\biggr|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(|\bm{x}|^{2}+|\bm{y}|^{2})-\frac{3}{4}\biggr),

(S35a)

\displaystyle R(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(-\ln(1-|\bm{y}|^{2})+|\bm{y}|^{2}-\frac{3}{4}\biggr).

(S35b)

For instance, in the case of two circular regions (one catalytic and
one absorbing), we get (see Eq. (6.11) from
SM_Grebenkov25
)

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}\simeq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}\bigl[-\ln(\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2})+\delta G(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})\bigr]}\,,

(S36)

where

\displaystyle\delta G(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})=2\pi[R(\bm{x}_{1})+R(\bm{x}_{2})-2G(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})]

\displaystyle=\ln\biggl(\frac{|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}|^{2}\,\bigl|\bm{x}_{1}|\bm{x}_{2}|-\bm{x}_{2}/|\bm{x}_{2}|\bigr|^{2}}{(1-|\bm{x}_{1}|^{2})(1-|\bm{x}_{2}|^{2})}\biggr).

(S37)

To illustrate the utility of these asymptotic results, we estimate the
critical catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
from
Eqs. (
S25
,
S34
) for two configurations
shown in Fig.
2
(a,b). Substituting the centers
\bm{x}_{j}

of ten circles into Eqs. (
S31
,
S35
),
we first evaluate the matrix
{\bf{G}}
. Setting
\epsilon_{1}=R=0.1
and

\epsilon_{j}=R_{\mathrm{a}}=0.05
(
j=2,\cdots,10
) for the first
configuration, we compute the matrices
\bm{\nu}
and
{\bf{M}}_{0}
, from which
we get the coefficient
C
from Eq. (
S34
). Its
substitution into Eq. (
S25
) yields
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}\approx 6.57
, which is remarkably close to the numerical value

q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}\approx 0.61
obtained by solving the spectral
problem (
9
) by a FEM (see
SM.VI
). Even though
the circles are not too small, the relative error of the asymptotic
value is below
1\%
. For the second configuration with even larger
circles (
R_{\mathrm{a}}=0.1
), the asymptotic value is
7.31
, which
differs from the benchmark value
7.62
of the FEM by
4\%
. A
further analysis of critical catalytic rates in complex environments
with multiple small absorbing regions presents an interesting
perspective. An extension of the asymptotic tools to the
three-dimensional case is also possible.

SM.VI
Finite-elements method

In this Section, we briefly describe the numerical technique that we
used to prepare Fig.
2
. We focus on the steady-state
regime and consider two related problems: (i) computation of the
optimal absorption rate
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{a}}(q_{\mathrm{c}},0)
by solving the
Steklov problem (
S21
); (ii) computation of the
critical catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}
by solving the Steklov
problem (
9
).

Both Steklov problems were solved by using a home-built implementation
of a finite-element method developed in
SM_Chaigneau24
. All
computations are done in Matlab PDEtool, which was also used to
triangulate the computational domain
\Omega
. The maximal meshsize

h_{\rm max}
controls the accuracy of the computation, which was
validated on a circular annulus, for which the exact solutions
(
7
,
8
) are available.

SM.VII
Solution for the exterior of a ball

It is instructive to consider the BCB process in the exterior of a
ball of radius
R
:
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}~:~|\bm{x}|>R\}
, with the
catalytic region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
on its spherical boundary (here

\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}=\emptyset
). If the sphere was partially
absorbing (i.e., if
q_{\mathrm{c}}
was negative), the solution of
Eq. (
1
) would be the survival probability of a particle
in the presence of a partially reactive sphere. In the context of
chemical physics, this solution was first discussed in

SM_Collins49
:

\displaystyle N(t|\bm{x})=1-\frac{R/r}{1-1/(q_{\mathrm{c}}R)}e^{-(r-R)^{2}/(4Dt)}\times

(S38)

\displaystyle\biggl\{\mathrm{erfcx}\biggl(\frac{r-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\biggr)-\mathrm{erfcx}\biggl(\frac{r-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}+\sqrt{Dt}(-q_{\mathrm{c}}+1/R)\biggr)\biggr\},

where
r=|\bm{x}|
, and
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)=e^{z^{2}}\mathrm{erfc}(z)
is the scaled
complementary error function (note that Eq. (29) from

SM_Collins49
contains a misprint: the sign in front of the last
term should be negative, as in Eq. (
S38
)). The
expression (
S38
) remains valid for positive
q_{\mathrm{c}}

that we consider here. To inspect its long-time limit, we use that

\mathrm{erfcx}(z)\approx 1
as
z\to 0
,
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)\approx 1/(z\sqrt{\pi})

as
z\to\infty
, and
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)\simeq 2e^{z^{2}}
as
z\to-\infty
.
Using
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}=1/R
from Eq. (
8
), we
distinguish three regimes.

(i) When
q_{\mathrm{c}}<1/R
, the population size
N(t|\bm{x})
approaches the
steady-state limit

N(\infty|\bm{x})=1-\frac{R/r}{1-1/(q_{\mathrm{c}}R)}\,.

(S39)

As
q_{\mathrm{c}}
is positive, the branching events lead to the population
size above
1
. Moreover, as
q_{\mathrm{c}}\to 1/R
, the limiting value

N(\infty|\bm{x})
diverges, thus indicating that the branching events
become more and more proliferative.

(ii) When
q_{\mathrm{c}}>1/R
, the last term in Eq. (
S38
)
leads to the exponential growth:

N(t|\bm{x})\simeq\frac{2R/r}{1-1/(q_{\mathrm{c}}R)}\,e^{Dt(q_{\mathrm{c}}-1/R)^{2}}\quad(t\to\infty),

(S40)

in agreement with our general analysis; in particular, the isolated
eigenvalue of the Laplace operator is
\lambda_{0}=-(q_{\mathrm{c}}-1/R)^{2}
.

(iii) Finally, at the critical catalytic rate,
q_{\mathrm{c}}=1/R
,
one can evaluate the limit
q_{\mathrm{c}}\to 1/R
in Eq. (
S38
) to get

\displaystyle N(t|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,r}e^{-(r-R)^{2}/(4Dt)}

\displaystyle-(1-R/r)\mathrm{erfc}\biggl(\frac{r-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\biggr).

(S41)

In the long-time limit, one has
N(t|\bm{x})\simeq 2\sqrt{Dt}/(r\sqrt{\pi})
in the leading order.

SM.VIII
Insights from spectral expansions

Additional insights onto the population size can be gained from
spectral expansions that were obtained in

SM_Grebenkov19
;
SM_Grebenkov20
in the case of a single absorbing
region with a given reactivity. While an extension of the
encounter-based approach to multiple targets was discussed in

SM_Grebenkov20b
, no spectral expansion is available for this more
general case, which includes, in particular, our setting with both
catalytic and absorbing regions. In this Section, we propose a
partial solution to this problem, which consists in fixing the
absorption rate
q_{\mathrm{a}}
and treating the catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}
as a
spectral parameter.

Let us consider the Laplace transform of the population size:

\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,N(t|\bm{x}),

(S42)

which, according to Eq. (
1
), should satisfy the mixed
boundary value problem:

\displaystyle(p-D\Delta)\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=1\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(S43a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=q_{\mathrm{c}}\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},

(S43b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})+q_{\mathrm{a}}\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}},

(S43c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}.

(S43d)

One can search the solution of Eqs. (
S43
) as a linear
combination of two functions:

\tilde{N}(p|\bm{x})=\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})+\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x}),

(S44)

where
\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})
is the solution of the PDE with the source
term in the bulk:

\displaystyle(p-D\Delta)\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=1\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(S45a)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},

(S45b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})+q_{\mathrm{a}}\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}},

(S45c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}.

(S45d)

This solution can be interpreted as the Laplace transform of the
survival probability
S_{0}(t|\bm{x})
for a particle diffusing in
\Omega

in the presence of the perfectly absorbing region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
and
partially absorbing region
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}
. In other words, this
classical quantity does not involve catalytic branching events. In
turn, the second function in Eq. (
S44
) is the solution of
the PDE with the source term on the boundary:

\displaystyle(p-D\Delta)\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(S46a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})-q_{\mathrm{c}}\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{n}\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},

(S46b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})+q_{\mathrm{a}}\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}},

(S46c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}.

(S46d)

To solve this PDE, we introduce the generalized Steklov problem:

\displaystyle(p/D-\Delta)V^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(S47a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}

\displaystyle=\mu^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}V^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}},

(S47b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}+q_{\mathrm{a}}V^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}},

(S47c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}},

(S47d)

with the spectral parameter
\mu^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
instead of
q_{\mathrm{c}}
. In
analogy to the conventional Steklov problem (corresponding to
p=0
,

\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}=\partial\Omega
and thus
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}=\emptyset
), this spectral problem has a discrete spectrum, i.e., a
countable set of eigenvalues
\{\mu_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}\}
, enumerated by the
index
k=0,1,2,\cdots
in an increasing order,
\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}\leq\mu_{1}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}\leq\cdots\nearrow+\infty
; in turn, the restrictions
of the associated eigenfunctions
\{V_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}\}
onto
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}

form a complete orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}})

SM_Levitin
.
The last property allows one to expand any function from

L^{2}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}})
on this basis and thus to get the following
spectral expansion of
\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})
:

\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{V_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}(\bm{x})}{\mu_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}-q_{\mathrm{c}}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}d\bm{y}\,V_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}(\bm{y})(-\partial_{n}\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{y})).

(S48)

Since both
\tilde{S}_{0}(p|\bm{x})
and the eigenpairs
\{\mu_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})},V_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}\}
are in general hard to access, this is a very formal
representation of the population size
N(t|\bm{x})
in the Laplace domain.

At the same time, this formal representation brings some spectral
insights onto the asymptotic behavior of the population size. In
fact, setting
p/D=-\lambda
reduces Eqs. (
S47
) to the
eigenvalue problem (
2
) for the Laplace operator.
This reflects the duality between the Robin and Steklov spectral
problems
SM_Levitin
. In particular, the Laplacian eigenvalues

\lambda_{k}
satisfying (
2
) can be searched as
real solutions of equations
\mu_{j}^{(-D\lambda,q_{\mathrm{a}})}=q_{\mathrm{c}}
with

j=0,1,2,\ldots
. This is also consistent with the Laplace transform
inversion of the spectral expansion (
S46
) that requires
finding the poles
p_{k}=-D\lambda_{k}
of
\tilde{U}(p|\bm{x})
that
corresponds to zeros of
\mu_{k}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}-q_{\mathrm{c}}
(note that the
principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
is related to the principal
eigenvalue
\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
).

To illustrate this point, let us consider again the spherical shell

\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}~:~R<|\bm{x}|<L\}
between two concentric
spheres of radii
R
and
L
, associated with the catalytic and
absorbing regions, respectively (here
\Gamma_{\mathrm{r}}=\emptyset
). The
rotational symmetry of this domain allows for the separation of
variables to solve Eq. (
S47
). In particular, the
eigenfunction
V_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
associated to the principal eigenvalue

\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
can be searched in the form (up to a suitable
normalization):

\displaystyle V_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}(r)=\biggl[\alpha i^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha L)+q_{\mathrm{a}}i_{0}(\alpha L)\biggr]k_{0}(\alpha r)

\displaystyle\qquad-\biggl[\alpha k^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha L)+q_{\mathrm{a}}k_{0}(\alpha L)\biggr]i_{0}(\alpha r)\quad(R\leq r\leq L),

which respects Eqs. (
S47a
) and (
S47c
). Here

\alpha=\sqrt{p/D}
,
i_{0}(z)=\sinh(z)/z
,
k_{0}(z)=e^{-z}/z
, and
prime denotes the derivative with respect to the argument. In turn,
the boundary condition (
S47b
) determines the eigenvalue as

\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}=\frac{(-\partial_{r}V_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})})_{r=R}}{V_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}(R)}\,.

(S49)

Figure
S3
illustrates the behavior of
\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
as a
function of
p
for three values of
q_{\mathrm{a}}
. Expectedly, all three
curves monotonously increase with
p
, and they cannot cross each
other. Moreover,
\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
diverges to
-\infty
as
p

approaches to some critical value
p_{q_{\mathrm{a}}}
, which depends on
q_{\mathrm{a}}
.
Even though
\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
is well defined for
p<p_{q_{\mathrm{a}}}
, we
restrict our discussion to
p>p_{q_{\mathrm{a}}}
. The principal Laplacian
eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
can be obtained as
-p_{0}/D
, where
p_{0}
is the
unique solution of the equation
\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}=q_{\mathrm{c}}
on

(p_{q_{\mathrm{a}}},\infty)
. Depending on
q_{\mathrm{c}}
and
q_{\mathrm{a}}
, one may get
p_{0}<0
,
p_{0}=0
or
p_{0}>0
. If we are interested in the steady-state
solution with
\lambda_{0}=0
(and thus
p_{0}=0
), the eigenvalue

\mu_{0}^{(0,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
determines the catalytic rate
q_{\mathrm{c}}
, associated to
the imposed absorption rate
q_{\mathrm{a}}
. Alternatively, one can formally
invert the function
\mu_{0}^{(0,q_{\mathrm{a}})}
to determine
q_{\mathrm{a}}
from a
given
q_{\mathrm{c}}
.
From this figure, it is also clear that if
q_{\mathrm{c}}
lies above

\mu_{0}^{(0,\infty)}
(shown by a red filled circle), only a positive
solution
p_{0}
of
\mu_{0}^{(p,q_{\mathrm{a}})}=q_{\mathrm{c}}
exists, yielding

\lambda_{0}<0
and thus an exponential growth of the population. In
other words,
\mu_{0}^{(0,\infty)}
determines the critical catalytic
rate:
q_{\mathrm{c}}^{\rm crit}=\mu_{0}^{(0,\infty)}
. Moreover, setting
p=0
and
q_{\mathrm{a}}\to\infty
, one reduces Eqs. (
S47
) to the
eigenvalue problem (
9
) so that
\mu_{0}^{(0,\infty)}
can
be identified with
\mu_{0}
discussed in the main text.
