Systematic review and meta-analysis of disease clustering in multimorbidity: a study protocol
Authors: Jennifer Ferris, Lean K Fiedeldey, Boah Kim, Felicity Clemens, Mike A Irvine, Sogol Haji Hosseini, Kate Smolina, Andrew Wister
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMultimorbidity is defined as the presence of two or more chronic diseases. Co-occurring diseases can have synergistic negative effects, and are associated with significant impacts on individual health outcomes and healthcare systems. However, the specific effects of diseases in combination will vary between different diseases. Identifying which diseases are most likely to co-occur in multimorbidity is an important step towards population health assessment and development of policies to prevent and manage multimorbidity more effectively and efficiently. The goal of this project is to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies of disease clustering in multimorbidity, in order to identify multimorbid disease clusters and test their stability.Methods and analysisWe will review data from studies of multimorbidity that have used data clustering methodologies to reveal patterns of disease co-occurrence. We propose a network-based meta-analytic approach to perform meta-clustering on a select list of chronic diseases that are identified as priorities for multimorbidity research. We will assess the stability of obtained disease clusters across the research literature to date, in order to evaluate the strength of evidence for specific disease patterns in multimorbidity.Ethics and disseminationThis study does not require ethics approval as the work is based on published research studies. The study findings will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and disseminated through conference presentations and meetings with knowledge users in health systems and public health spheres.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42023411249.Keywords: epidemiology, public health, aging, statistics & research methods, systematic review

Introduction

Multimorbidity is the co-occurrence of chronic diseases, defined as the presence of two or more chronic conditions in a single individual.1 2 Multimorbidity is one of the most complex and pressing challenges for modern healthcare systems. It is estimated that more than 50% of the global population above the age of 60 have multimorbidity.3 These numbers are projected to rise,4 due to the global increase in non-communicable disease prevalence and ageing population demographics in many countries.5 6 Multimorbidity increases the complexity of patient care7 and associated healthcare costs.8 Individuals with multimorbidity also have worse outcomes on a variety of health indicators, such as increased mortality,9 increased risk of adverse drug events,10 11 more frequent hospitalisations,12 13 accelerated functional decline14 and reduced quality of life.15 16 Yet, chronic disease surveillance systems and clinical guidelines traditionally take a single-disease focus, making it difficult to account for the deleterious interactive effect of co-occurring diseases on health.11 17 In order to improve clinical multimorbidity management and inform public health planning and policy, we need to develop novel strategies to measure multimorbidity that can accommodate a multiple-disease lens.

Multimorbidity is inherently complex because different disease combinations will have different effects on health outcomes and care. An individual with depression and cancer has a different profile of health risks, needs and outcomes than an individual with diabetes and cardiovascular disease. However, the most common method to measure multimorbidity is to count the number of co-occurring conditions,18 which gives the same multimorbidity ‘score’ to each of these two individuals. For these reasons, an identified research priority is to establish which specific diseases are most likely to co-occur (or cluster) in multimorbidity, in order to evaluate their interactive and temporal effects on health.1 In parallel, the recent uptake of statistical techniques for unsupervised data clustering has advanced technical capacity to perform such complex clustering analyses.19 There is a rapidly growing body of literature attempting to partition multimorbid diseases into groups based on patterns of disease occurrence in large population-based samples.20 21 A critical assessment and synthesis of research findings is needed to evaluate the overall progress of disease cluster identification in multimorbidity research.

This review will provide an overview and analysis of the current state of research on disease clustering in multimorbidity. There have been previous systematic reviews evaluating multimorbid disease clustering literature,22 23 but none to date have performed a quantitative meta-analysis of disease cluster composition. Additionally, these previous reviews did not include studies using network science approaches to identifying disease patterns. Network science methods are increasingly applied to multimorbidity research24–27 and can be extended to evaluating the genetic and environmental determinants of multimorbidity28–30 as well as temporal disease trajectories.31–34 The present systematic review and meta-analysis will address these research gaps by surveying emerging data clustering methodologies in the multimorbidity literature. We will restrict study inclusion to multimorbidity research that has used administrative data sources with standardised disease code definitions, in order to facilitate the harmonisation of disease definitions across research studies and to maximise the generalisability of our research findings to public health surveillance efforts. We will also perform a novel meta-analysis of disease clusters in multimorbidity by analysing cluster membership of a list of the most common chronic diseases included in multimorbidity research18 23 35 and chronic disease surveillance in western countries.1 36–38 Restricting our meta-analysis to this list of chronic conditions will allow us to harmonise data across studies for cluster stability assessments and meta-clustering analyses of multimorbid diseases. Our study will identify the most common disease patterns that have emerged in multimorbidity research and directly assess their stability. This review will provide a critical synthesis of existing data and analytic recommendations to inform future research on disease pattern identification in multimorbidity.

Review objective

The objective of this review is to identify disease clusters that have appeared in multimorbidity research of the general adult population and to perform a meta-analysis testing the stability (reliability) of disease clusters.

Hypothesis

We hypothesise that multimorbid disease clusters will emerge in our meta-analytic data clustering analysis and that disease clusters will vary in their stability. Certain disease clusters with strong existing epidemiological and physiological evidence for their co-occurrence (such as diabetes, hypertension and heart disease40) will show high stability across the multimorbidity clustering literature, indicating strong evidence for their non-random association in multimorbidity. Other clusters will show lower stability and therefore a lower strength of evidence for their non-random association in multimorbidity.

Methods and analysis

This systematic review will be conducted and reported according to Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.41

The software Covidence will be used for study screening and selection. All search results will be imported into Covidence, and duplicate records will be identified and removed. Study title and abstracts will be screened against inclusion criteria. Full-text studies will then be imported into Covidence and screened for inclusion; we will record reasons for study exclusion at the full-text stage. All screening steps will be completed by two independent reviewers, and disagreements will be resolved by a third reviewer.

Information sources

We will search the following databases: MEDLINE and Embase. The databases will be searched without any restrictions on publication date, from inception to the search date (search performed: 24 March 2023). We will restrict our database search to published articles in peer-reviewed journals. The grey literature (conference proceeding, theses and preprints) will be identified based on consultation with content experts in the field of multimorbidity research. We will hand-search reference lists of included studies, and consult with content experts, to identify any additional relevant studies to screen for potential inclusion. Prior to manuscript submission, the search will be rerun to identify any newly published studies to be screened for inclusion.

Search strategy

The search strategy is designed to capture two themes: multimorbidity and data-driven clustering methodologies. The two themes are combined with the Boolean operator ‘AND’ to identify the relevant literature.

Comorbidity is a closely related concept to multimorbidity. We follow current recommendations for terminology of the terms ‘multimorbidity’ and ‘comorbidity’1 42: the term ‘multimorbidity’ refers to the coexistence of multiple conditions of any kind; the term ‘comorbidity’ refers to the existence of additional diseases in reference to a focal disease of interest. The term comorbidity has sometimes been used interchangeably with multimorbidity. In piloting our search strategy, we found that including terms for comorbidity dramatically reduced the specificity of our search results. Therefore, a final step in our search strategy combines keywords for comorbidity and patterns or clusters to capture only the most relevant comorbidity search results. This search strategy was developed by the study authors with a consultation with a research librarian.

Inclusion criteria

Study characteristics

We will include observational studies (cross-sectional, case–control, cohort) that used data-driven methodology to classify diseases into multimorbidity clusters. There will be no restrictions on articles by publication date or language of publication. For included articles not in English, we will translate documents with consultation with colleagues who speak the language in question, with online translation software, or by contacting the publishing authors for clarification when needed.

Participants

We will include studies that examine the general population in which groups or subgroups of participants are comprised of adults aged 18 years and older. Studies will be excluded if the study population is exclusively paediatric (<18 years of age) or is non-human animals.

Exposure

We will include studies that consider chronic disease multimorbidity; that is, studies of patterns of co-occurring diseases without any single disease focus. We will exclude studies with samples restricted to participants with a principal disease of focus (ie, comorbidity studies; eg, a study population including only adults with heart disease). Additionally, we will exclude studies restricted to population health subgroups with specific health contexts that are unrelated to multimorbidity (eg, study populations that are exclusively combat veterans, pregnant people, individuals in motor vehicle collisions, unhoused people, etc).

Study methodology

We will include studies that characterised disease status with standardised coding systems (such as International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes) and exclude studies that characterised disease status through other means (such as self-report).

We will include studies that used unsupervised data-driven methods to assign diseases to groups based on disease co-occurrence patterns. A variety of statistical methods could meet this inclusion criterion. We anticipate that the majority of included methods will fall into two broad categories: (1) Factor-based approaches, in which the goal of the analysis is to identify latent structure in disease co-occurrence data, and then assign diseases to groups based on their contributions to latent structure. Examples include but are not limited to: exploratory/confirmatory factor analysis, principal/independent components analysis and latent class analysis. (2) Cluster-based approaches, in which the goal of the analysis is to use a metric of distance to group diseases by maximising between-group distance and minimising within-group distance. Examples include but are not limited to: hierarchical cluster analysis, and C-means or K- means clustering. Network-based approaches can fall in either category depending on the algorithm applied to detect community structure in the disease network.

We will exclude studies that use non-data-driven approaches to assign diseases to multimorbidity categories. Examples include but are not limited to: counts of the number of co-occurring diseases, a priori assignment of diseases to groups, analysing disease combinations without clustering or using index measures such as the Charlson or Elixhauser Comorbidity Index.

We will include studies where diseases were clustered based on patterns of disease co-occurrence, meaning the distance metric applied in the clustering algorithm was some measure of disease prevalence or co-occurrence across the study population. We will exclude studies that clustered diseases based on measures other than disease co-occurrence or that incorporated non-disease measures in clustering algorithms. Examples include: grouping diseases based on their relationship to an outcome (such as clustering individuals by socioeconomic status, then reporting the common diseases in each cluster) and clustering diseases and non-disease entities together (such as medications).

Studies need to include a minimum of 12 diseases into clustering analyses, as recommended by Fortin et al.43

Data extraction

Data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment of included studies will be performed on a subset of included studies by two independent reviewers, with disagreements being resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. If agreement between the two raters on the study subsets is 80% or higher, then data extraction for the remaining studies will be performed by a single reviewer.44 In cases of missing data, the study team will contact the corresponding author of the study. The data extraction form will follow Conducting Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Observational Studies of Etiology recommendations,45 with additional data categories related to our research question.

Data extracted will include: bibliographic information, study design, exposure (multimorbidity) definitions, data source and dates, study population, disease definitions, number of diseases included in analysis, disease clustering methodology, distance metric applied, criteria for cluster membership, methods to analyse temporal relationships (if any), subgroup stratifications (if any), details about obtained clusters (total number of clusters and disease composition of each cluster).

Risk-of-bias assessment of each included study will be conducted with the ROBINS-E tool.39 A custom risk-of-bias assessment category was added to the ROBINS-E tool to assess bias specific to the statistical methodologies applied in the clustering literature. This risk assessment category was developed by the research team and evaluates whether authors conducted any quantitative analyses of the internal validity of their clustering solutions, and whether clustering results were validated using multiple parameters, algorithms or independent datasets.

Our review will present a narrative synthesis and critique of statistical methodologies applied in multimorbidity research. Strengths and limitations of the most commonly used statistical approaches for application in multimorbidity will be discussed, including considerations such as: whether the clustering unit is the individual or the disease, whether diseases can have membership in multiple groups (hard vs fuzzy clustering), whether the algorithm forces cluster membership, the ability to quantify strengths of relationships between and within clusters, whether nested hierarchy is considered and whether temporality is incorporated in clustering. Furthermore, we will present an overview of methodological quality applied in the literature to date and recommendations to improve the rigour of this field moving forward.

Data synthesis

There is no agreed consensus for which diseases should be included in multimorbidity analyses, and a significant source of heterogeneity in the literature comes from the number and selection of diseases to include in multimorbidity analyses.23 43 46 47 Therefore, to synthesise data across studies, we have defined a list of chronic diseases a priori for meta-data extraction. The scope of this meta-analysis will be limited to the diseases on our disease list.

The selection of diseases for our list was informed by comparing six chronic disease lists included in public health surveillance and previous multimorbidity literature. For public health surveillance chronic disease lists, we compared three chronic disease lists included in the Canadian Chronic Disease Surveillance System (CCDSS),36 the US Office of the Assistant Secretary of Health Multiple Chronic Conditions framework37 and the UK Quality and Outcomes Framework.38 To identify diseases that have frequently been used in previous multimorbidity research, we compared three lists of the top 20 chronic diseases measured in previous systematic reviews of multimorbidity,18 23 35 including the list of 20 recommended chronic diseases for multimorbidity research from Ho et al18.18

Our initial disease list of 20 diseases is presented in box 1. There was large agreement between our list sources of priority chronic conditions. Our final list included nine conditions that were included in all six list sources (asthma; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; dementia; diabetes mellitus (type 1 or type 2); heart failure; hypertension; ischaemic heart disease; osteoporosis and stroke), three conditions that were included across all lists but at different levels of subcategorisation (arthritis: split into rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis; mood and anxiety disorders) and seven diseases that were included in two or more lists (multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, gout, chronic kidney disease, Parkinson’s, schizophrenia and cancer). We additionally included HIV/AIDS due to its identified importance for multimorbidity surveillance.1 48 This disease list is subject to revision depending on data availability. Our goal is to provide the most granular differentiation of disease categories possible, based on the disease classifications employed in included studies. If data allows, diseases may be expanded into more granular subcategories. Conversely, if data is limited, diseases may be collapsed into higher categories. Any changes to this disease list in our final analysis will be transparently justified and reported.

Meta-analysis

It is challenging to evaluate the performance of unsupervised clustering methods, because there is no ‘gold-standard’ cluster output to compare results against. One approach to assess clustering performance is to determine whether the output consistently assigns elements of a set to the same clusters, given perturbations in the data source or changes in the clustering methodology applied.49 Thus, the goal of our meta-analysis is to assess the stability of clustering results for major chronic diseases across published literature on multimorbidity. Data synthesis will be achieved by cross-referencing diseases cluster results against our selected disease list (box 1). Our disease list represents elements in a set and our meta-analytic approach will compare the partitioning of these set elements into clusters across previous research studies. Provided our included studies are at an acceptably low risk of bias, then this data pooling approach will create qualitatively similar categories of data for analysis that will be robust to anticipated methodological heterogeneity between studies.45

Meta-analysis of multimorbidity disease clusters will be performed across two phases mentioned below.

Constructing a meta-analytic disease network and extracting disease clusters

To test specific disease cluster composition, we will create a matrix for disease cluster membership. The cells of the matrix will represent the number of times each pair of diseases appeared in the same cluster across every included study, as a proportion of the total number of times each disease was included across each study to account for discrepancies in the frequency of disease inclusion across studies. This matrix will be used to compute an undirected weighted network, where each disease is represented by a node and the co-occurrence between disease pairs across the literature is represented by the weighted links between nodes.

We will then apply the link community detection algorithm developed by Ahn et al50 on the disease network to extract meta-analytic multimorbid disease clusters. The link community detection algorithm identifies community structure in a network by clustering the unique links between nodes based on the proportion of neighbours shared between links.50 The Ahn algorithm has been extended to weighted networks using the Tanimoto coefficient.51 Weighted link similarity between link eik and link ejk is defined as:

The Tanimoto coefficient is an extension of the Jaccard index, where ai is a vector of the link weights between node i and all other nodes directly connected to both nodes i and j.51 Link similarity is the dot product of ai and aj divided by the squared norms of ai and aj minus the dot product.51 Next, a hierarchical clustering algorithm (HCA) with Ward’s minimum variance is applied to group links by similarity. In the HCA, each link is a leaf in the dendrogram and branches are link communities.50 To obtain disease clusters, the dendrogram is cut at the height that maximises the density of the community partition.50 The Ahn link community detection algorithm is an ideal choice for our research question, as it allows for multiple community membership for each disease node, does not force classification of each link into a cluster and maintains good performance across networks of varying size and density.50

Assessing disease cluster stability

Once disease clusters are derived from the network analysis, we assess the quality and stability of the overall disease network and each obtained disease cluster.

To test the overall quality of the meta-analytic network, we will follow the approach outlined by Arriata for clustering assessments in weighted networks.52 Briefly, we will compare the community structure of our network against a null network model generated by random subsampling of our network nodes and link weights.52 If true community structure exists in the data, then community structure scores should be higher in the real network when compared with null networks generated randomly without any expectation of community structure. In the context of multimorbidity, this analysis will capture if there is good evidence for overall clustering structure between diseases in previous multimorbidity research.

Certain disease clusters may be more reliable than others, as both meaningful and spurious clusters can emerge from the output of any clustering method. To test cluster-wise stability for our obtained meta-analytic disease clusters, we will follow the analytic approach outlined by Hennig53 and extended to network analyses by Tian.54 We will test the stability of obtained meta-clusters by calculating the average Jaccard coefficient for each cluster. The Jaccard coefficient measures the proportion of shared members between two sets. We will measure the disease nodes shared between two disease cluster sets, where C0 is a cluster obtained in our meta-analytic network, and Cb is a cluster in our comparative sample that shares node k.

Our analytic approach will compare our meta-analytic disease clusters against the disease clusters obtained in each of the included research studies (for a measure of the stability of disease clusters in the multimorbidity research literature) and will compare meta-clusters against null network models with random resampling.53 54 A Jaccard coefficient below 0.5 indicates a cluster has low validity or stability, whereas a Jaccard coefficient of 0.75 or higher has high validity or stability.55

Depending on data availability, we may perform additional meta-clustering and stability estimates on clusters stratified by population characteristics (age groups, sex and high-income vs low-middle income country).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis will provide a synthesis of the current state of disease cluster discovery in multimorbidity research. Our synthesis will also generate analytic recommendations for future research. We anticipate that our study will identify disease combinations with strong evidence of clustering across the general adult population. Constraining our analysis to diseases defined by standardised coding systems means this evidence will be internationally relevant for population-level disease surveillance efforts. The findings of this study will be informative for public health and healthcare system professionals, policy-makers and researchers in efforts to minimise the health impacts of chronic disease multimorbidity.
