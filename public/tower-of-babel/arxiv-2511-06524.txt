Input–Output Data-Driven Stabilization of Continuous-Time Linear MIMO Systems
Haihui Gao, Alessandro Bosso, Lei Wang, David Saussié, Bowen Yi

Abstract
In this paper, we address the problem of data-driven stabilization of continuous-time multi-input multi-output (MIMO) linear time-invariant systems using the input–output data collected from an experiment. Building on recent results for data-driven output-feedback control based on non-minimal realizations, we propose an approach that can be applied to a broad class of continuous-time MIMO systems without requiring a uniform observability index. The key idea is to show that Kreisselmeier’s adaptive filter can be interpreted as an observer of a stabilizable non-minimal realization of the plant. Then, by postprocessing the input–output data with such a filter, we derive a linear matrix inequality that yields the feedback gain of a dynamic output-feedback stabilizer.



I
Introduction

Obtaining controllers of dynamical systems directly from trajectory data has a long history, ranging from adaptive control
[
20
]
to modern data-driven approaches
[
25
]
.
In particular, a major recent trend in data-driven control has been to design controllers directly from experimental datasets via linear matrix inequalities (LMIs).
This end-to-end paradigm avoids any intermediate modeling step and may be effective even when the data are not informative enough to perform system identification.

In the
discrete-time
context, pioneering works on data-driven LMIs are
[
8
]
and
[
24
]
, which mainly focus on the state-feedback stabilization of linear time-invariant (LTI) systems.
While the former derives LMIs by developing state-space results inspired by Willems et al.’s fundamental lemma
[
28
]
, the latter introduces the framework of data informativity to provide necessary and sufficient conditions for data-driven analysis and control.
Later, alternative LMIs particularly suited for noisy datasets have been derived from quadratic matrix inequalities
[
23
]
.
The data-driven LMI paradigm has also been applied to time-varying systems
[
16
]
, nonlinear systems
[
8
,
23
]
, and output-feedback control, using either non-minimal realizations involving shifted input–output data
[
8
,
1
,
13
]
or the behavioral framework
[
26
]
.

In contrast, direct data-driven control for
continuous-time
systems remains less developed.
Recent progress includes continuous-time versions of the fundamental lemma
[
15
,
21
]

and the study of the effect of sampling on informativity
[
9
]
.
Unlike the discrete-time case, continuous-time methods must handle derivative signals, which are usually unavailable in practice.
Most state-feedback results derive LMIs assuming that state derivatives are measurable
[
8
,
9
,
11
]
.
To remove this need,
[
19
]
proposes orthogonal polynomial bases, while
[
17
]
explores more general sampling functionals.
On the other hand, data-driven control methods that rely purely on input–output data remain rare.
Notable exceptions include
[
7
,
6
]
, which design output-feedback controllers for LTI systems using non-minimal realizations that can be reconstructed by linear filters of the input–output signals.
While these results address single-input single-output (SISO) and multi-input multi-output (MIMO) systems with a uniform observability index, respectively, finding an approach for general continuous-time MIMO LTI systems remains an open problem.
This paper aims to close this gap with the following contributions:

C1

We propose a method to derive an output-feedback stabilizer directly from an input–output trajectory of MIMO LTI systems without restrictions on the observability indices.
We obtain this result by showing that Kreisselmeier’s adaptive filter
[
12
]
acts as an observer of a canonical non-minimal realization of the plant
[
6
]
.
The resulting output-feedback controller combines the filter with a stabilizing feedback law depending on the filter states.

C2

To compute the gains of the feedback law, we postprocess the input–output data with the filter and construct an LMI with samples of the input, output, and filter trajectories.
Since the necessary rank conditions ensuring feasibility of the LMIs in
[
7
,
6
]
do not hold in general due to the filter structure, we formulate the LMI applying a suitable decomposition of the data that exploits the stabilizability of the non-minimal realization, along with sufficient excitation of the controllable states.

II
Problem Statement

Consider a continuous-time LTI MIMO system of the form

\displaystyle\dot{x}=Ax+Bu,\quad y=Cx

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state,

is the control input, and

is the measured output, while

,

, and

are constant unknown matrices of appropriate dimensions that satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The pair
(A,B)
is controllable and the pair
(C,A)
is observable.

We aim at solving the following input–output data-driven stabilization problem.

Problem 1
.

Suppose that the input–output dataset

{\cal E}_{\tt D}:=\{u(t),y(t)\}_{[0,t_{\tt D}]},

(2)

has been acquired from the plant (
1
) during an experiment of duration
t_{\tt D}>0
, under some excitation condition of the input
u
and from an unknown initial condition
x(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Moreover, we suppose that
u(t)
and
x(t)
are piecewise continuous and absolutely continuous over
[0,t_{\tt D}]
, respectively.

Without any prior information on
A
,
B
, and
C
except for the state dimension
n
, use (
2
) to design a linear dynamic output-feedback controller of the form

\dot{x}_{\textup{c}}=A_{\textup{c}}x_{\textup{c}}+B_{\textup{c}}y,\quad u=C_{\textup{c}}x_{\textup{c}}+D_{\textup{c}}y,

(3)

having state
x_{\textup{c}}
and matrices
A_{\textup{c}},B_{\textup{c}},C_{\textup{c}}
and

, such that the origin
(x,x_{\textup{c}})=0
of the interconnection of (
1
) and (

) is globally exponentially stable (GES).

III
Proposed Approach

III-A

Kreisselmeier’s Adaptive Filter

Our approach is inspired by and built on Kreisselmeier’s adaptive filter
[
12
]
, which is given by the following dynamical system:

\dot{M}=FM+\begin{bmatrix}y^{\top}\otimes I_{n}&u^{\top}\otimes I_{n}\end{bmatrix},

(4)

where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\mu}
is the filter state and
F\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a Hurwitz matrix, with
\mu\coloneqq(p+m)n
.
In
[
12
]
, it is shown that the filtered signal
M(t)
satisfies an algebraic relation with the solution
x(t)
of system (
1
), for any initial condition of (
1
) and (
4
).

Below, we provide a version of this result tailored to our case.

Lemma 1
.

Consider systems (
1
), (
4
) and let Assumption
1
hold.
Also, let
F
in (
4
) be a Hurwitz matrix with distinct eigenvalues.
Then, there exist a non-singular matrix
T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a column vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\mu}
such that, for all initial conditions
x(0)=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
M(0)=M_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\mu}
,

Tx(t)=M(t)\theta+e^{Ft}(Tx_{0}-M_{0}\theta).

(5)

Proof.

Since the pair
(C,A)
is observable due to Assumption
1
, there exists a matrix
L
such that the spectra of
A-LC
and
F
coincide.
Then, the fact that
F
has distinct eigenvalues implies that
A-LC
and
F
are similar, i.e., there exists a non-singular matrix
T
such that
F=T(A-LC)T^{-1}
.
Using the change of coordinates
\bar{x}=Tx
, system (
1
) with initial condition
x_{0}
becomes

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{x}}=\bar{A}\bar{x}+\bar{B}u,\qquad y=\bar{C}\bar{x}

(6)

with
\bar{A}=TAT^{-1},\bar{B}=TB
,
\bar{C}=CT^{-1}
, and initial condition
\bar{x}_{0}=Tx_{0}
.
System (
6
) can be equivalently written as

\dot{\bar{x}}=F\bar{x}+TLy+TBu.

(7)

The main results in
[
12
]
follow immediately.
The solution
\bar{x}(t)=Tx(t)
of (
7
) reads as

\displaystyle Tx(t)

\displaystyle=e^{Ft}\bar{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\!e^{F(t-s)}[TLy(s)+TBu(s)]\text{d}s

\displaystyle=e^{Ft}\bar{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\!e^{F(t-s)}\!\begin{bmatrix}y^{\top}(s)\otimes I_{n}&u^{\top}(s)\otimes I_{n}\end{bmatrix}\!\text{d}s\theta

\displaystyle=e^{Ft}Tx_{0}+\left(M(t)-e^{Ft}M_{0}\right)\theta,

where in the second line we exploited the identities
TLy=(y^{\top}\otimes I_{n})\mbox{vec}(TL)
,
TBu=(u^{\top}\otimes I_{n})\mbox{vec}(TB)
and defined:

\theta\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{vec}(TL)\\
\mbox{vec}(TB)\end{bmatrix},

while in the third line we used the solution
M(t)
of (
4
).
Rearranging the terms, we obtain (
5
).

\triangleleft

Remark 1
.

While vectorizing the matrix variable
M
reveals that the filter dynamics of
\mathrm{vec}(M)
resemble those in
[
6
, Eq. (85)]
, the filter (
4
) does not exploit any structural property of MIMO systems.
As a consequence, unlike the design of
[
6
]
, our approach does not require knowledge of the observability indices of the pair
(C,A)
.
The trade-off, however, is the introduction of excessive coordinates in the filter.
This is not a major concern for offline data processing.

Remark 2
.

The filter (
4
) does not rely on specific values of
A,B
, and
C
, but only on their dimensions for the given plant (
1
).
Even in such a case, we have an algebraic relation (
5
) between the unknown state
x
and the available filtered variable
M
, where the vector
\theta
is unknown.
Indeed, the use of similar filters to process input–output signals has a longstanding history in adaptive control
[
18
]
.

III-B

Filter-Based Output Feedback Stabilization

Our approach is motivated by the indirect output-feedback approach
[
3
]
.
In the indirect approach, we first design an observer that achieves an exponentially convergent estimate to the system state, uniformly in the “exogenous” signals
(u,y)
.
Then, we find a “state-feedback” controller for the observer.
Roughly speaking, we aim at the following property:

\lim_{t\to\infty}M(t)=0\quad\mbox{(exp.)},

which then implies from (
5
) that
x(t)\to 0
exponentially as
t\to\infty
.

From Lemma
1
, we observe that the set

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\{(x,M)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times\mu}:Tx=M\theta\}

(8)

is invariant and attractive. As a consequence, it makes sense to design a feedback law that depends only on
M
, as in classical controller design based on Luenberger observers.
However, it is important to remember that the system does not start in general on
\mathcal{A}
because
M(0)
would require unavailable prior information.
Thus, we need to account for the perturbation of the
transverse coordinate

\beta:=Tx-M\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(9)

To facilitate the data-driven controller design, we provide the following corollary of Lemma
1
, which highlights the dynamics of the transverse coordinate
\beta
.

Corollary 1
.

The transverse coordinate
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined in (
9
), satisfies the dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}=F\beta.

(10)

whose origin
\beta=0
is globally exponentially stable.

Proof.

Rearranging terms in (
5
), we immediately obtain (
10
), where
F
is Hurwitz by design.

\triangleleft

Remark 3
.

Transverse coordinates have been widely explored in various fields, especially in nonlinear control, such as path following of mechanical systems
[
10
]
, orbital stabilization
[
29
]
, transverse stability analysis
[
2
]
, and immersion and invariance control
[
5
]
.

IV
Canonical Non-Minimal Realization for Data-Driven Control

We now show that the filter (
4
) can be interpreted as an exponentially convergent observer of a
canonical non-minimal realization
of the plant (
1
), of which we recall the definition.

Definition 1
(
[
6
]
).

Under Assumption
1
, a non-minimal realization of system (
1
) of the form

\dot{\xi}=A_{\xi}\xi+B_{\xi}u,\quad y=C_{\xi}\xi,

with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
and
n_{\xi}>n
, is said to be

if there exist a Hurwitz matrix
F_{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}\times n_{\xi}}
and a matrix
L_{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}\times p}
such that
A_{\xi}=F_{\xi}+L_{\xi}C_{\xi}
.
In particular,
x=\Pi\xi
, where
\Pi
is the solution to the equations

\displaystyle\Pi(F_{\xi}+L_{\xi}C_{\xi})

\displaystyle=A\Pi,\quad\Pi B_{\xi}=B

(11)

\displaystyle C_{\xi}

\displaystyle=C\Pi.

To highlight that the dynamics of
(x,M)
restricted onto the set
\mathcal{A}
is a canonical non-minimal realization of (
1
), we first provide the vectorization of the filter dynamics (
4
).

Lemma 2
.

Let
\hat{\xi}:=\mathrm{vec}(M)
.
Then, it holds that

\dot{\hat{\xi}}=F_{\xi}\hat{\xi}+B_{\xi}u+L_{\xi}y

(12)

where

\displaystyle F_{\xi}

\displaystyle\coloneqq I_{\mu}\otimes F

(13)

\displaystyle B_{\xi}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0_{\,pn^{2}\times m}\\
I_{m}\otimes\mathrm{vec}(I_{n})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle L_{\xi}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}\otimes\mathrm{vec}(I_{n})\\
0_{mn^{2}\times p}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

The filter (
4
) can be written as

\dot{M}=FM+\begin{bmatrix}0_{n\times pn}&u^{\top}\otimes I_{n}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}y^{\top}\otimes I_{n}&0_{n\times mn}\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

We now perform vectorization of the terms in the right-hand side of (

For the first term, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}(FM)

\displaystyle=(I_{\mu}\otimes F)\mbox{vec}(M)=F_{\xi}\mbox{vec}(M)

For the second term in (
14
), it holds that

\mbox{vec}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0_{\,n\times pn}&u^{\top}\otimes I_{n}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\mbox{vec}\left(\left(\begin{bmatrix}0_{p\times m}\\
I_{m}\end{bmatrix}u\right)^{\top}\otimes I_{n}\right).

For convenience, let

and use the relation
\mbox{vec}\bigl(\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\top}\otimes I_{n}\bigr)=\mathrm{e}_{i}\otimes\operatorname{vec}I_{n},

where
\mathrm{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+m}
denotes the
i
-th component of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{p+m}
, and
a_{i}
denotes the
i
-th entry of

. We can rewrite (
15
) as

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}(a^{\top}\otimes I_{n})=

\displaystyle\mbox{vec}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p+m}a_{i}\bigl(\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\top}\otimes I_{n}\bigr)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p+m}\mbox{vec}(\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\top}\otimes I_{n})a_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p+m}\bigl(\mathrm{e}_{i}\otimes\mbox{vec}(I_{n})\bigr)a_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(I_{p+m}\otimes\operatorname{vec}(I_{n})\bigr)a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(I_{p+m}\otimes\operatorname{vec}(I_{n})\bigr)\begin{bmatrix}0_{p\times m}\\
I_{m}\end{bmatrix}u.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0_{\,pn^{2}\times m}\\
I_{m}\otimes\mathrm{vec}(I_{n})\end{bmatrix}u=B_{\xi}u.

Using similar arguments, the third term in (
14
) becomes

\mbox{vec}\left(\begin{bmatrix}y^{\top}\otimes I_{n}&0_{n\times mn}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}\otimes\operatorname{vec}(I_{n})\\
0_{mn^{2}\times p}.\end{bmatrix}y=L_{\xi}y.

Combining the above computations, the vectorization of the dynamics (
4
) is given by

\mbox{vec}(\dot{M})=F_{\xi}\mbox{vec}(M)+B_{\xi}u+L_{\xi}y,

(16)

which is exactly (
12
).

\triangleleft

We are ready to show the structure of the canonical non-minimal realization of system (
1
) as per Definition
1
.

Lemma 3
.

Under Assumption
1
, the system

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}

\displaystyle=A_{\xi}\xi+B_{\xi}u

(17)

\displaystyle y_{\xi}

\displaystyle=C_{\xi}\xi

with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
,
n_{\xi}\coloneqq n^{2}(p+m)
, and

\displaystyle A_{\xi}\coloneqq F_{\xi}+L_{\xi}C_{\xi},\quad C_{\xi}\coloneqq C(\theta^{\top}\otimes T^{-1})

is a canonical non-minimal realization of the plant (

). Moreover, the pair

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, the set
{\cal A}
defined in (

) is an invariant subspace.
Hence, it holds that

\left.\begin{aligned} Tx(0)&=M(0)\theta\\
\hat{\xi}(0)&=\mbox{vec}(M(0))\end{aligned}\right\}~\implies~x(t)=\Pi\hat{\xi}(t),\;\forall t\geq 0

(19)

for any

, where

\Pi\coloneqq\theta^{\top}\otimes T^{-1}.

(20)

From the definition of

in (

(21)

Furthermore, on the set

, we can compute the derivative of the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle Ax+Bu=\Pi(F_{\xi}\hat{\xi}+B_{\xi}u+L_{\xi}y)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle A\Pi\hat{\xi}+Bu=\Pi(F_{\xi}\hat{\xi}+B_{\xi}u+L_{\xi}C\Pi\hat{\xi})

\displaystyle A\Pi\hat{\xi}+Bu=\Pi A_{\xi}\hat{\xi}+\Pi B_{\xi}u.

Note that (

) holds for all

.
In fact, since

Finally,

Hurwitz.

.

Data-driven State Decomposition for Stabilizable Systems

Given the dynamics (
30
) and the batches (
33
), one may be tempted to directly apply the LMIs for data-driven stabilization by state feedback of
[
8
]
to find a stabilizing gain
K_{e}
.
In this context, it is known that a sufficient condition for the feasibility of the LMIs would be

\mbox{rank}\;\begin{bmatrix}U\\
Z\end{bmatrix}=m+n+n_{\xi}.

(34)

In our scenario, in general, (
34
) cannot be guaranteed, regardless of the input
u(t)
injected in the system.
In fact, since
(A_{e},B_{e})
is stabilizable but not controllable and due to the high number of states introduced by the filter (
4
), it is possible that the uncontrollable modes contained in
z(t)
are linearly dependent, so that the following matrix

\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t_{\tt D}}z(s)z(s)^{\top}\text{d}s,

(35)

loses rank.
In turn,
\mbox{rank}\;\mathcal{Z}<n+n_{\xi}
implies
\mbox{rank}\;Z<n+n_{\xi}
, regardless of the sampling choice, causing the LMIs in
[
8
]
to become unfeasible because a necessary rank condition fails, see
[
7
, Proof of Thm. 1]
.

To circumvent this issue, in this work, we propose an approach that handles the scenario
\mbox{rank}\;\mathcal{Z}<n+n_{\xi}
by directly exploiting the stabilizability of the pair
(A_{e},B_{e})
.
Notably, similar ideas are under investigation also in the discrete-time context
[
22
]
.

In particular, our key idea is to require that
\mathcal{Z}
loses rank only in directions corresponding to some uncontrollable modes of
z(t)
, and can be formalized as follows:

\mbox{Im}\;\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mbox{Im}\;\mathcal{Z},

(36)

where
\mathcal{R}\coloneqq[B_{e}\;\;A_{e}B_{e}\;\;\cdots\;\;A_{e}^{n_{z}-1}B_{e}]
is the reachability matrix of the pair
(A_{e},B_{e})
.
Note that condition (
36
) is weaker than
\mathcal{Z}\succ 0
in the scenario where
\mbox{rank}\;\mathcal{R}<n+n_{\xi}
.
Since
\mathcal{Z}
can be computed from the available signal
z(t)
, it makes sense to exploit (
36
) to search for a change of coordinates
T_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}\times n_{z}}
, by which we divide
z
as follows:

\begin{bmatrix}z_{a}\\
z_{b}\end{bmatrix}=T_{z}z,

(37)

where we aim to include all controllable modes in
z_{a}

The partition

VII
Concluding Remarks

We proposed an approach for data-driven stabilization of general continuous-time LTI MIMO systems without any restriction on the observability indices.
We showed that Kreisselmeier’s adaptive filter acts as an observer of a canonical non-minimal realization of the plant, allowing to design an output-feedback controller comprising the filter dynamics and a linear feedback law depending on the filter states.
Due the filter structure, we compute the gains of the feedback law with a data-based LMI formulation where, exploiting the stabilizability of the non-minimal realization, it is not necessary to have full-rank data.
Future work will aim at reducing the dimension of the controller dynamics by either considering smaller filter structures or investigating model reduction to simplify the controller implementation.
Furthermore, we will investigate how to remove the assumption of prior knowledge of the state dimension
n
.
