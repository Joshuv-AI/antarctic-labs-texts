Development of a colloidal gold immunochromatographic test strip for detecting the smooth Brucella
Authors: Qi Wu, Xiaohan Guo, Qianhan Huang, Yujia Xie, Liping Guo, Xiangdong Yang, Mingjun Sun, Dehui Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrucellosis, caused by Gram-negative Brucella, spreads in human and animal populations through contact with infected animals and products. Developing a rapid and sensitive detection technology for pathogen is crucial to reduce the risk of this disease transmitting between animal populations and to humans. We produced a monoclonal antibody LPS-6B5, which shows high affinity to LPS and limited cross-reactivity with other bacteria. Based on LPS-6B5, a colloidal gold immunochromatographic assay (GICA) was developed which demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity in detecting cultured B. melitensis, B. abortus and B. suis. The Gold Immunochromatographic Assay (GICA) strips exhibited the most sensitive detection limits, with a value of 7.8125 × 105 CFU/mL for Brucella melitensis, surpassing the sensitivity levels observed for Brucella abortus and Brucella suis. It is also suitable for clinical and field samples, providing a cost-effective and user-friendly alternative to traditional methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76026-4.Keywords: LPS, Smooth Brucella, Monoclonal antibody, Rapid test, Colloidal gold immunochromatographic assay (GICA)Subject terms: Immunology, Microbiology, Bacteria

Introduction

Brucellosis, a zoonotic infection caused by the Gram-negative Brucella (1), is primarily transmitted through direct contact with infected animals, their byproducts, or contaminants such as undercooked meat, unpasteurized milk, and infected secretions(2). This disease presents as a chronic and debilitating condition, featuring systemic symptoms like fever, headache, and chills, along with potential local complications like arthritis, endocarditis, and meningitis. The clinical presentation of brucellosis often lacks specificity, making it one of the most commonly overlooked bacterial diseases worldwide(3). Previous research has confirmed its widespread occurrence in over 170 regions and countries, with approximately 5 million new cases annually, presenting significant public health challenges(4). In China, brucellosis has been identified among animals in all provinces, with higher positivity rates in northern regions compared to southern areas(5). Recent years have seen increased trade activities, facilitating the movement of livestock products and potentially accelerating the spread of the disease, notably from north to south(6). Concurrently, human brucellosis is emerging as a significant public health issue in China, demanding urgent attention and intervention(7, 8).

There are 12 species of Brucella found globally, with the most prevalent in China being B. abortus, B. melitensis, B. suis, and B. canis. The outermost layer of B. abortus, B. melitensis and B. suis is rich in lipopolysaccharides (LPS), serving as the primary pathogenic factor and antigenic epitope for smooth Brucella(9). This LPS is widely used as an immunological diagnostic antigen(10). Conversely, B. canis lacks the O-chain of the LPS antigen, making its diagnosis fundamentally distinct from that of other animals(9). Currently, bovine and sheep breeds contribute significantly to China’s brucellosis cases, with the disease showing a broad geographical distribution. As a result, research focused on detecting smooth Brucella has received considerable attention. The A19 vaccine strain’s antigenic epitope is prevalent in Chinese strains of B. abortus, B. melitensis, and B. suis(11, 12), making it a suitable choice for preparing the LPS antigen.

Immunological methods commonly used to diagnose smooth Brucella include the Rose Bengal Test (RBT), tube agglutination test (SAT), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), fluorescence polarization test (FPA), and immunochromatographic technique (ICA) (13). Although these methods are widely utilized and can rapidly detect positive cases in animals, they are limited in identifying pathogenic factors and risk points, which become apparent only after infection in both animals and humans. This inherent delay underscores a drawback of immunological testing (14). In contrast, pathogenetic testing plays a pivotal role in interrupting the transmission of brucellosis to humans and animals by pinpointing the causative agent and source of infection. However, the options for pathogenic testing of Brucella are significantly fewer than those available for immunological tests. Currently, pathogen isolation and quantitative real-time fluorescent PCR (qPCR) are the primary methods employed. While pathogen isolation is accurate, it is time-consuming and labor-intensive, presenting significant infection risks to laboratory staff (15). As a result, it must be conducted in a biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facility, significantly limiting its feasibility. Although qPCR is sensitive, it requires strict laboratory conditions and sophisticated equipment, rendering rapid testing impractical. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a diagnostic technique for brucellosis that is simple, fast, and suitable for field use, addressing the gaps in current testing methods. The colloidal gold immunochromatographic assay (GICA) emerges as a promising solution. Developed in the early 1980s, this technique is easy to perform, yielding results that are stable against variations in temperature and time. Its portability and capacity for large-scale field testing make it particularly valuable in emergency situations (16). Consequently, this study aims to expand the application of GICA, assessing its effectiveness and reliability compared to the current standard tests for diagnosing brucellosis.

The timely detection of infected animals and their products is crucial for controlling and mitigating Brucella transmission due to the associated risks with brucellosis. This study seeks to create an LPS monoclonal antibody and a rapid, sensitive colloidal gold immunochromatographic strip technology to detect Brucella-infected animals and products. These endeavors play a vital role in reducing the risk of transmission between animals and humans, thereby assisting in the successful management of brucellosis.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

All procedures involving animals or animal samples adhered to ethical guidelines and received approval from the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (ethical approval no.: 201801W005) and ARRIVE guidelines. The study complies with local and national guidelines and regulations.

Animals and bacteria

A total of four female specific-pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c mice, aged 7 weeks and weighing 18 ± 2.0 g, were obtained from Beijing Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The experimental materials consisted of the following: Brucella LPS, polyclonal antibody (LPS-PAb), the B. abortus A19 vaccine strain, B. abortus strain DT21, B. melitensis strain XJ-18, B. suis vaccine strain 2, B. canis strain, as well as Salmonella (ATCC 13311), Escherichia coli O157:H7 (ATCC 35350), Listeria monocytogenes (LM) (ATCC 19111), Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae (E. rhusiopathiae ) (ATCC 19414), and Ochrobactrum anthropic(O. anthropic) (ATCC 49188), all provided by the China Animal Health and Epidemiology Center.

Preparation of monoclonal antibodies using Brucella LPS

Preparation of LPS antigen

The lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from the B. abortus A19 vaccine strain was isolated using the phenol-water extraction method(17). The strain was first cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) medium, then underwent centrifugation to collect the organisms. Subsequently, the organisms were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and heated to 66 °C. Concurrently, a 90% phenol aqueous solution was heated to the same temperature. After that, the two components were combined and centrifuged at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant, which contained the LPS, was left at room temperature for 24 h. The phenol phase (bottom layer) was recovered, filtered, and then added to a solution of 1% saturated sodium acetate in methanol at three times the volume of the LPS. This mixture was allowed to precipitate at 4 °C for 2 h. The precipitate was collected, underwent another round of centrifugation, and the supernatant was retained. The remaining precipitate was dissolved in sterilized purified water, subjected to centrifugation, and the supernatant was collected. This supernatant was combined with the previously retained supernatant, filtered, and treated with trichloroacetic acid. The resulting mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was subjected to dialysis, lyophilization, weighing, and made ready for use. The purity of the LPS was evaluated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by silver staining.

Immunization of BALB/c mice

Sixty micrograms of LPS was combined with an equal amount of Freund’s complete adjuvant (Sigma, Germany) and administered to four female SPF BALB/c mice (designated as numbers 1, 2, 3, and 4) via subcutaneous multiple injections for the initial immunization. Two weeks later, 30 micrograms of LPS was mixed with an equal amount of Freund’s incomplete adjuvant (Sigma, Germany) for booster immunization. Following five booster immunizations conducted every two weeks, serum samples were collected for potency testing. Subsequently, the mice were immunized with 50 micrograms of LPS via intraperitoneal injection.

The mice were housed in cages with a 12-hour light-dark cycle and given unrestricted access to water and food for a week before the experiment. No anesthesia was used during the study. At the end of the experiments, the mice were euthanized using CO₂ asphyxiation.

Cell Fusion assays and monoclonal screening

Splenocytes obtained from immunized mice underwent fusion with SP2/0 cells and were then cultured in HAT selection medium (Sigma, Germany) at 37 °C with the aid of PEG1450 (Sigma, Germany). The fusion process was terminated by the addition of serum-free IMDM (Sigma, Germany), followed by centrifugation. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in neonatal bovine serum along with prepared thymocytes, combined with semi-solid medium, thoroughly mixed, poured into petri dishes, and placed in an incubator for culturing. Ninety-three fused cells per plate were selected from ten plates and cultured in a ninety-six-well microtiter plate (Corning, USA) that had been pre-coated with thymocytes (100 µL/well). Monoclonal cell supernatants were removed from the microtiter plate, replaced with IMDM medium containing HT (Sigma, Germany) and 20% neonatal bovine serum (200µL/well) for the initial screening. Subsequently, 2 µg/mL LPS was added to the plates, and culture supernatants from wells displaying hybridoma cell growth were gathered for the second screening using indirect ELISA. SP2/0 culture supernatant was utilized as the negative control, while PBS served as the blank control, and positive serum, diluted 1000 times with PBS, acted as the positive control. Absorbance values were measured at dual wavelengths (450 nm, 630 nm) for the ninety-six-well microtiter plates. Samples with OD450 values exceeding 2.1 times the negative serum value were considered positive.

Mass preparation of monoclonal antibodies

A large quantity of monoclonal antibodies was generated using the ascites method. Two weeks before inoculating hybridoma cells, mice were given an intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 mL of liquid paraffin. Hybridoma cells were suspended at a concentration of 2 × 106/mL in serum-free medium, with each mouse receiving a 0.5 mL intraperitoneal injection. Collection of ascites began approximately 8 days after injection and was stored at -20 °C. The monoclonal antibodies in the ascites were purified using a commercial Protein G column (Sigma, Germany). The purity of the monoclonal antibodies was confirmed through SDS-PAGE, and the antibody concentration was determined using the BCA method. Subsequently, the antibodies were diluted to a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL, and their potency and subtype were determined using indirect ELISA.

Determination of Equilibrium dissociation constant (kd) of LPS-mAb (indirect ELISA)

B. abortus (20 µg/mL) and B. melitensis (20 µg/mL) antigens were suspended in coating buffer (0.01 M PBS, pH 7.4). The antigens were twofold diluted at a ratio of 1:400, and 100 µL of the diluted solution was added to each well of a 96-well microtiter plate (Corning, USA). Subsequently, the plate underwent an overnight incubation at 4 °C to facilitate the coating process. Following three washes with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20), the plate was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h at 37 °C and subsequently washed again with PBST three times. LPS monoclonal antibodies were then added at a 1:200 dilution, with PBS serving as the negative control. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After another three washes with PBST, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibodies (Thermo, USA) were applied at a 1:5000 dilution for 1 h at 37 °C. Following a final wash, 100 µL of TMB substrate solution was added to each well, and the reaction allowed to proceed for 15 min. Subsequently, 50 µL of stop solution (2 M H2SO4) was added to each well to terminate the reaction. Optical density values were measured at 450 nm (OD450) using an ELISA plate reader (BioTek), with the OD450 values utilized as indicators of the color reaction. The color reaction results were represented as the ratio of OD450 values from the test wells to OD450 values from the negative control wells (S/N). A criterion of S/N ≥ 2.1 was applied to determine positivity. All experiments were repeated 3 times.

Identification of LPS-mAb cross-reactivity

The cross-reactivity of the monoclonal antibody was assessed using the indirect ELISA method outlined above. Inactivated whole organisms of E. coli O157:H7, O. anthropi, LM, and Salmonella, were utilized as antigens (concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL in PBS), coated onto 96-well microtiter plates (Corning, USA). The LPS monoclonal antibody served as the primary antibody in this assay. Following the indirect ELISA protocol, the OD450 value of each well was measured post-reaction. This allowed for the evaluation of cross-reactivity exhibited by the monoclonal antibody with the aforementioned bacteria. All experiments were repeated 3 times.

Preparation of colloidal gold immunochromatographic assay (GICA) strips

Preparation of gold-labeled LPS-mAb

The Brucella GICA strip utilizes the double antibody sandwich method, employing immunochromatography principles, as depicted in Fig. 1. Firstly, gold-labeled LPS-mAb was prepared based on the optimum conditions. In brief, 100 mL of colloidal gold solution is mixed with 12 µg of LPS-mAb, allowed to stand for 30 min, followed by the addition of 1% BSA solution and left to block for 20 min. Then, centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, discard the supernatant, resuspend the precipitate in 10 mL of antibody dilution buffer (containing 1% BSA and 5% sucrose). The diluted gold-labeled LPS-mAb solution is evenly spread on a glass fiber membrane, dried at 37 °C for 2 h, and kept for later use.

Basic structure and detection principle of Brucella LPS LFIA, the figure was created by Microsoft PowerPoint 2016 MSO (version 2408 Build 16.0.17928.20114).

Determination of optimal LPS-PAb

LPS-PAb was used to make testing line (T-line). Two concentrations of LPS-PAb and two types blocking solution were assessed, leading to four experimental groups: (1) 1.5 mg/mL T-line LPS-PAb with 5% Casein solution, (2) 1.5 mg/mL T-line LPS-PAb with 10% PEG20000 solution, (3) 2.6 mg/mL T-line LPS-PAb with 5% Casein solution, and (4) 2.6 mg/mL T-line LPS-PAb with 10% PEG20000 solution. B. abortus (20 µg/mL) was used to monitor color development in each experimental group, while LM and Salmonella (concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL) were used as negative control. Additionally, a blank control was integrated into the experimental setup. Subsequent to the experiments, a comparative analysis was conducted based on the color development outcomes. By integrating these results with the analysis of the experimental data, an optimal antibody concentration and blocking solution matching scheme was devised for Brucella detection.

Assembly of GICA strips

The nitrocellulose (NC) membrane’s testing line (T-line) is immobilized with LPS-pAb, while rabbit anti-mouse IgG (Thermo, USA) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL is immobilized on the quality control line (C-line) of the same membrane. Sequentially place the water absorption pad, nitrocellulose membrane, and gold labeled LPS-mAb pad on the PVC base plate, cut them into 3 mm wide test strips using an automatic cutting machine (Autokun), then put them in a sealed bag with desiccant, and store them at 4 °C in the dark.

Determination of LOD of the GICA strips

GICA strips were assessed using inactivated Brucella abortus strain 19, Brucella suis S2, and Brucella melitensis, provided by Chinese Center for Animal Hygiene and Epidemiology. These bacteria have been quantified using bacterial plate counting to determine colony-forming units (CFU) in biosafety level III laboratory. The bacteria were first diluted to a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL in PBS and then subjected to a two-fold dilution method. Subsequently, 10 µL of each Brucella suspension was pipetted into the sample adding hole on the strips, followed by adding 100 µL of PBS solution. After a 30-minute incubation period, the strips were visually examined for results. The intensity of the color at the T-line provides information about the quantity of the target antigen present in the sample. A test was considered valid if the control line was detectable.

Determination of the cross-reactivity of the GICA strips

To assess cross-reactivity, several bacterial strains, including Listeria monocytogenes, Escheric coli O157:H7, B. canis, Ochrobactru anthropi, Salmonella, and Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, were utilized. These bacteria were cultured and diluted in PBS and quantified using bacterial plate counting to determine colony-forming units (CFU). Subsequently, the bacterial suspensions were uniformly diluted to a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL and then heat-inactivated in a water bath at 80 °C for 2 h. During testing, 10 µL of the inactivated bacterial solution was applied to GICA strips. The results were observed after allowing the test strips to incubate for 30 min. All experiments above were repeated 3 times.

The potency of four monoclonal antibodies against LPS by iELISA

Following the established procedures, we screened four hybridoma cell strains—LPS-2G4, LPS-3C4, LPS-6B5, and LPS-9G6. Monoclonal antibodies derived from these cells were obtained through the ascites production method, and subsequent purification yielded highly pure monoclonal antibodies, as evidenced by 12% SDS-PAGE electrophoresis (see Fig. 3). The electrophoresis results showed distinct bands at around 50 kD and 26 kD, corresponding to the heavy and light chains of the monoclonal antibodies, respectively. The potency of these antibodies was evaluated using iELISA with LPS-coated enzyme labeling plates, which demonstrated their high potency. Particularly, monoclonal antibodies from LPS-2G4 and LPS-3C4 cell lines exhibited noticeable color reactions even when diluted 1,280,000-fold, while those from LPS-6B5 and LPS-9G6 maintained specificity at dilutions up to 2,560,000-fold. The results indicate that B. melitensis can be accurately identified at an antigen dilution of 1:25,600 and an LPS monoclonal antibody dilution of 1:12,800. Likewise, accurate recognition of B. abortus is attained at an antigen dilution of 1:6,400 with an LPS monoclonal antibody dilution of 1:6,400. These findings highlight the high detection limit of the LPS monoclonal antibody for both B. melitensis and B. abortus, meeting the experimental criteria.

Cross-reactivity of LPS-mAb

The iELISA conducted with various bacteria susceptible to cross-reactivity with smooth Brucella antigens revealed Signal-to-Noise (S/N) values below 2.1 for all bacteria, except for human O. anthropi, at an antigen dilution of 1:800 (see Table 2). This observation indicates minimal cross-reactivity of the LPS monoclonal antibody with these bacteria, implying negligible ELISA cross-reactivity. However, significant ELISA cross-reactivity was observed with human O. anthropi only at LPS monoclonal antibody dilutions of 1:400 and higher, and antigen dilutions of 1:3,200 and higher. This finding suggests a certain level of cross-reactivity of the LPS monoclonal antibody with human O. anthropi. Therefore, higher antigen and monoclonal antibody dilutions are required to reduce the impact of cross-reactivity.

Optimal LPS-PAb concentration and blocking solution

The strips exhibited distinct positive and negative coloration patterns upon application of LPS-PAb at a concentration of 2.6 mg/mL and sealing with a 5% Casein solution. However, when the membrane was coated with LPS-PAb at 2.6 mg/mL, using 10% PEG20000 as a sealing agent resulted in weak coloration for Brucella positives, rendering it ineffective. Similarly, employing 10% PEG20000 as a sealant with the membrane coated at 1.5 mg/mL led to faint positive coloration for B. melitensis and B. suis, thus deemed inadequate. Additionally, utilizing 5% Casein as a sealant with the membrane coated at 1.5 mg/mL produced negligible positive coloration for B. melitensis and B. suis strains, and was consequently disregarded. Optimal T-line antibody (LPS-PAb) labeling concentration was determined to be 2.6 mg/mL through paired testing, with 5% Alkali-soluble Casein identified as the most effective sealing solution. The antibody concentration on the membrane and choice of sealing solution are critical factors influencing both the intensity of positive coloration and the prevention of false negatives in the results.

Determination of LOD of the GICA strips for Brucella

The GICA strips underwent evaluation to assess its effectiveness in detecting varying concentrations of Brucella derived from pure cultures. The strips demonstrated the lowest detection limits at 7.8125 × 105 CFU/mL of B. melitensis, 3.125 × 106 CFU/mL of B. abortus, and 1.5625 × 106 CFU/mL of B. suis, respectively. As the bacterial concentrations increased, the color intensity at the T-line position of the strips intensified progressively. These results indicate that the LOD of this strip for B. melitensis was higher compared to that for B. abortus and B. suis (see Fig. 4). However, according to our laboratory’s results, qPCR can detect Brucella after more than 10 copies (see Raw data), the LOD of this strip appears to be inferior.

Cross-reactivity assessment of GICA strips

During the evaluation of cross-reactivity, all strips designed for Brucella abortus, Brucella melitensis, and Brucella suis showed distinct coloration at both the Test (T) and Control (C) lines. In contrast, test strips for Brucella canis and other bacterial strains exhibited prominent coloration only at the C-line. These results suggest that this GICA strips utilized in this study specifically identified smooth Brucella strains, effectively differentiating them from rough Brucella strains and other frequently encountered organisms (see Fig. 5 and Figure S2-S5).

Discussion

The LPS located on the surface of smooth Brucella strains acts as a crucial virulence factor and antigenic component(18). In response to Brucella infection, the host mounts a significant humoral immune response, with antibodies directed against LPS being notably prominent and persistent. Importantly, LPS of smooth Brucella strains exhibits distinct differences from the typical LPS found in other Gram-negative bacteria(19). This unique feature enables the effective utilization of monoclonal antibodies that specifically target it for the detection of Brucella pathogens or antibodies. Given the location of LPS in the outermost layer of bacteria, the development of monoclonal antibodies against LPS antigens becomes paramount to ensure the high specificity of colloidal gold immunochromatographic assays. This specificity is crucial for the accurate and reliable detection of Brucella infections or antibodies.

In this study, a monoclonal antibody against Brucella LPS was initially prepared and optimized for increased potency and a low Kd using iELISA. Despite showing limited cross-reactivity at higher antigen dilutions, additional dilution steps might be required for O. anthropi to reduce cross-reactivity. Importantly, the LPS antigens of O. anthropi and smooth Brucella share common epitopes, potentially leading to cross-reactivity in their respective detection assays. To reduce this cross-reactivity, it is necessary not only to decrease the dilution concentration but also to consider other measures. As a result, a novel immunochromatographic test strip based on the developed LPS monoclonal antibody was subsequently designed.

The Brucella GICA strip was developed based on the double antibody sandwich principle employing LPS-mAb and LPS-PAb. The results demonstrate that the Brucella LPS GICA strip showed a low LOD. While the LOD was comparatively higher than that of other GICA strip test kits for Brucella antigen detection (107 CFU/mL in pure cultures)(20), and it is even more impossible to compare with qPCR (21), it is still significant. Zainab et al. (22) conducted a study on the diagnostic accuracy of Lateral Flow Immunochromatographic Test (LFIT) nanogold diagnostic kits for brucellosis detection in sheep. Their research involved testing 2-fold serial dilutions of B. melitensis positive standard antiserum for sensitivity to LFIT, which was not detectable at a 64-fold dilution of the serum. This highlights the relatively higher LOD of the developed LFIA paper test, capable of detecting Brucella antigens at much lower bacterial concentrations.

One critical aspect of a diagnostic tool in the context of Brucella detection is its susceptibility to cross-reactivity. Smooth Brucella strains are generally deemed more virulent compared to rough Brucella strains due to their non-endotoxic LPS coating, which enables them to evade the host’s innate immune response(1, 23). Specifically, the LPS of smooth Brucella contains an O antigenic side chain absent in rough Brucella(24, 25). These differences in bacterial cell wall morphology manifest in variances in serologic assays. As a result, serologic assays targeting smooth Brucella species are typically able to differentiate them from rough Brucella like B. canis(24). The GICA strip developed in our study shows excellent specificity in detecting smooth Brucella, ensuring no interference from rough Brucella during the process. Moreover, previous findings by Corbel in 1985 revealed cross-reactivity of Brucella with other microorganisms that share similar antigenic determinants, leading to false positive results. Nielsen et al. (26) highlighted that the chemical structure resemblance of the O-polysaccharides between smooth Brucella and certain microorganisms like E. coli O157: H7, Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella, and Pseudomonas maltophilia is the primary cause of cross-reactivity. Our assay exhibited zero cross-reactivity when testing pure cultures of E. coli O157: H7, Salmonella, LM, O. anthropi, E. rhusiopathiae, and B. canis at a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL. Therefore, the Brucella GICA developed in this study effectively discerns smooth Brucella from other bacteria with minimal cross-reactivity, establishing it as a reliable tool for detecting and diagnosing smooth Brucella species.

Limitations of the study

The test strips demonstrate increased sensitivity to B. melitensis. While the Brucella GICA strip developed in this study shows potential as a substitute for current smooth Brucella detection methods, it is not without limitations. For example, the detection limit is far inferior to PCR, and validation on a wide range of clinical samples (such as milk, beef, vaginal secretions, etc.) is crucial due to possible contaminants such as placental tissues, blood components, and other bacteria that could affect the LOD and cross-reactivity of the test outcomes.
