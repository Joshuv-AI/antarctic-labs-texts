Introduction

Heart failure is a highly prevalent condition of great medical, social, and economic importance (Savarese et al., 2022). With a high mortality rate (~ 168 per 100,000 people in the U.S), exceeding that of the combination of the most common cancers (lung, prostate, breast, and pancreatic affecting ~ 141 per 100,000 people in the U.S.) based on mortality data from the Centers for Disease Control and National Institutes of Health/National Cancer Institute, respectively, the burden of heart failure on healthcare expenditures in the U.S. alone is estimated to be around $69.8 billion by 2030 (Askoxylakis et al., 2010; Heidenreich et al., 2013; Jones et al., 2019). As such, a comprehensive understanding of heart failure patho-mechanisms are crucial for developing strategies for accurate diagnosis and adequate treatment.

During heart failure, the heart undergoes pathological remodeling, including processes such as cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and myocardial fibrosis (MF) (Azevedo et al., 2016). The latter has been shown to impact both the onset and clinical outcomes of heart failure by facilitating adverse conditions, such as malignant arrhythmias, systolic and diastolic dysfunction, and ventricular aneurysms (Azevedo et al., 2016). Common causes of MF include hypertension and coronary artery disease, whereas less common etiologies include cardiac sarcoidosis and cardiac amyloidosis (Azevedo et al., 2016; Markatis et al., 2020; Schelbert, 2019). These rarer causes are often difficult to distinguish clinically, since they may present with similar phenotypes during diagnostic procedures (i.e. angiography, chest X-ray, or ECG) (Muchtar et al., 2017). Because these different etiologies require distinct therapeutic strategies, accurate identification of MF according to its underlying cause is critical for patient prognostication and the development of precise diagnostic tools (Muchtar et al., 2017).

While, in principle, clinical diagnosis of MF is possible, all currently available methods face limitations in routine practice. The gold standard, histopathological analysis of myocardial biopsies, is rarely performed due to its invasive nature and the risk of false-negative results due to sampling error (López et al., 2015). Gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography/computerized tomography (PET/CT), and single-photon emission computerized tomography (SPECT) are expensive with limited sensitivity for certain patterns such as diffuse MF, or are not yet fully validated (Karamitsos et al., 2020; Ambale Venkatesh et al., 2014). Echocardiography, the most commonly used diagnostic method, has low sensitivity for quantifying MF (Karamitsos et al., 2020).

The development and progression of MF are accompanied by alterations in cardiac metabolism, both locally in the heart and systemically in the blood. Accurately measuring MF metabolomics can quantify disease metabolic status and provide additional clinical parameters that may prove to be critical for accurate disease diagnosis. Here, using the demonstrated capability of high-resolution magic angle spinning (HRMAS) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy for measuring blood specimens in µL scales (Kobus et al., 2026; Schult et al., 2021), we retrospectively analyzed serum samples obtained from patients with various MF cardiac etiologies. HRMAS NMR of µL scale blood sera provides a non-destructive and minimally invasive avenue to discover biochemical changes with limited retrospective specimens from patients with known clinical outcomes. Using this technique, disease specific metabolites can be detected and quantified to improve our understanding of MF arising from various origins, which other diagnostic techniques are not able to identify. Metabolomic markers thus discovered may inform the development of assays for future routine clinical use.

Methods

Patient population

Patient selection involved surveying the biorepository of MGB Biobank, which at the time of this study contained blood sera from about 380,000 patients collected since 2010, and cross-checking these samples for specific keywords within MGB Biobank’s patient databases, which in turn were linked with the patients’ medical records on Epic. Those keywords were: 'magnetic resonance imaging' (MRI), ‘MRI’, ‘biopsy’, ‘Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography’ (SPECT), ‘SPECT’, Positron Emission Tomography’ (PET), and ‘PET’. If the patient records mentioned any of these terms, the patients were selected for further detailed review. This meant that the patients’ records in MGB Biobank were accessed and further screened for whether the records mentioned the heart, otherwise those patients were excluded from further screening. This way, we identified 927 potential candidate subjects for this study. For the final selection, the available reports (MRI, PET, SPECT, and histopathological examinations) were reviewed individually on Epic for each potential subject. This meant reading each report and identifying whether they included a definitive confirmation of the diagnosis of myocardial fibrosis. Reports with ambiguous wording or insufficiently reported methodology, e.g., cardiac MRIs (cMRIs) where it was unclear whether late gadolinium enhancement was tested for, were excluded. If follow-up reports indicated remission before the date of sample collection, then those patients were also excluded. In the case that there were no follow-up reports, persistence of the disease was assumed. Additionally, it was ensured that the blood samples were collected after the corresponding diagnosis had been confirmed. After this detailed review of the clinical records, we were able to recruit three patient groups, each with matched controls: (1) MF due to common causes including hypertension and coronary artery disease (MF; n = 13), (2) transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (CA; n = 18), and (3) cardiac sarcoidosis (CS; n = 12). Diagnoses were confirmed according to established clinical criteria: for the MF group, through histopathological evaluation of myocardium samples and cardiac MRI if available; for the CA group, through Tc99m-pyrophosphate SPECT and/or cardiac MRI; and for the CS group, through cardiac MRI, Fluorodeoxyglucose-(FDG)-PET/CT and/or biopsy. For the matched controls, cMRI reports were reviewed for clear absence of late gadolinium enhancement in the heart and/or definitive exclusion of the diagnosis of myocardial fibrosis by the reporting physician. Again, in the case of ambiguous wording and/or insufficiently reported methodology, the report, and hence the patient, was excluded from this study. Heart transplant patients were also excluded from this study. Out of ~ 380,000 patients from the Biobank, 488 patients met these criteria. Myocardial biopsies were obtained primarily from the apex of the right ventricle and less frequently from the left ventricle or during myomectomy. Since myocardial biopsies are not routinely performed in clinical practice, it was required only for inclusion in the MF group, whereas other established diagnostic tools (cardiac MRI, FDG-PET/CT, and Tc99m-pyrophosphate SPECT) were considered sufficient for the CA and CS group.

Supplemental Table S1 summarizes the patient demographics for each group. For the MF and CS groups, successful matching between cases and controls allowed for paired statistical analyses. However, for the CA group, due to limited numbers of available control samples, only unmatched control cases were used. Age-matching was suboptimal, since subjects from the patient groups were on average older than the control subjects. Consequently, some patient case samples were excluded from the matched analyses.

Whole blood, obtained as part of a dedicated research draw by the Biobank, was primarily sampled from the peripheral vein, although other sites could have been used. Approximately 0.5 mL aliquots of serum were processed within four hours of collection using a centrifuge, and stored in ultralow freezers at − 80 °C.

The following matching parameters were noted for each patient: age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; according to the formula created by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration – CKD-EPI) (Inker et al., 2021), and the Model-of-End-Stage-Liver-Disease-Sodium Score (MELD-Na) (Kim et al., 2007). The latter two have been included as matching parameters since chronic renal and hepatic diseases have been shown to be associated with fibrosis and thus may be confounders when investigating myocardial fibrosis (Inker et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2007). Sex and race are reported as originally stated by the participating patients at the time of blood draw. Case-control matching was performed according to best fit of the aforementioned matching parameters. As a system for quality control, 20 random serum samples were pooled and analyzed intermittently along with the tested samples.

HRMAS NMR

All HRMAS NMR measurements were conducted on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 600 MHz spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin, Billerica, MA). Before the HRMAS NMR measurements, the samples from − 80 °C freezer were thawed on ice for one hour. All the following sample preparation steps were carried out on ice, when possible. Each sample was vortexed for 15 s. Next, 10 µL of a serum sample were loaded with 2.5 µL of D2O (spectrometer field lock) into a 4 mm rotor, using a 12 µL Kel-F insert. HRMAS proton (1H) NMR data were collected at 4 °C with a spinning rate of 3600 Hz and a rotor synchronized Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) method. The order of sample preparation and data collection was random. The other spectral conditions included: 5 s recycle time, 100 CPMG π pulses with a total mixing time of 55.56 ms, 32 K data points with a total acquisition time of 1.36 s, and a spectral width of 20 ppm. As a methodological control, after every 6th analyzed sample, an experiment protocol was run on the pooled control sample to ensure system stability. As the collected human serum samples are precious and limited in volume (10 µL), HRMAS was able to better resolve the metabolite resonances, and narrow lineshape from components with reduced molecular mobility (e.g., lipid droplets). Given the limited sample volume, HRMAS NMR was deemed to be more advantageous than traditional solution state NMR.

Data analysis

Spectra analysis was conducted off-line with Bruker Topspin 3.6.2 (Bruker BioSpin, Billerica, MA). The spectral processing procedure included: 0.5 Hz line-broadening, one-time zero-fill to 64 K data points, Fourier transformation, automatic and manual phasing, baseline correction, chemical shift calibration according to the up-field peak of lactate doublets at 1.32 ppm, and resonance peak curve-fitting through complete deconvolutions. Within the analyzed 0.50–4.50 ppm spectral region (401 chemical shift data points), the total spectral intensity was used to normalize the measured and deconvoluted spectral peak intensities. Using a threshold of 80% of each ppm data point having non-zero values, our spectral analyses produced 61 spectral regions of interest (ROI). We then analyzed these spectral ROIs, rather than consider them as individual metabolites, as multiple metabolites may contribute to a ROI, and vice versa, one metabolite may be present in different ROIs.

Following ROI analyses, using the complete serum metabolite list collected in Human Metabolome Database [HMDB, https://hmdb.ca] and a laboratory developed program for assessment of potential serum metabolites in experimentally derived ROIs, termed ROIAL-NMR (Chen et al., 2025), we evaluated the potential contributing metabolites for these 61 ROIs, according to their significance levels and increase/decrease trends when comparing disease groups with controls, as shown in Supplemental Table S2, and among different disease etiologies. With these potential contributing metabolites, we further discussed their potential metabolic implications when appropriate. Spectral processing takes ~ 10–15 min depending on user experience. Detailed breakdown of the time required for spectral processing and ROIAL-NMR are stated in the original ROIAL-NMR study (Chen et al., 2025).

Using JMP Pro (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), we conducted hierarchical clustering analyses of ROIs to distinguish diseased individuals from their matched controls. Statistical analyses on these identified ROIs included univariate analyses according to the Wilcoxon/Kruskal–Wallis (KWW) test, as well as unsupervised multivariate principal component analyses (PCA). Following PCA, individual principal components were analyzed using the same protocol as above outlined for the ROIs. P-values before and after false discovery rate (FDR) corrections are presented and indicated where appropriate. Paired Wilcoxon tests were performed for two group comparisons, and unpaired tests were performed for the comparison of more than two groups.

Identification of potential serum metabolites

The identified 61 spectral ROIs, along with their corresponding significance statuses (described as univariate, multivariate, and FDR corrected results for differentiating tested groups), were used as inputs for a laboratory-developed Python program (ROIAL-NMR) (Chen et al., 2025). At present, ROIAL-NMR utilizes NMR information from HMDBto identify and categorize potential metabolites. Briefly, the program first identifies all possible NMR-observable metabolites for each ROI based on chemical shift values, according to HMDB’s characterizations of serum metabolites. It then filters these possible metabolites according to the “match ratio” of each potential metabolite throughout all the ROIs, with only metabolites having ≥ 50% of their spectral regions (SRRMs: spectral region resonance multiplicity including the multiplicity/splitting due to J-coupling interactions) present in the determined ROIs kept for further categorization. ROIAL-NMR then filters the metabolites according to their physiological concentrations, as listed on HMDB (with possible upper and lower concentrations) for the specific biofluid type (e.g., CSF, serum, urine, etc.). ROIAL-NMR categorizes the metabolites according to 3 concentration groups: (1) > 500 µM, (2) 5–500 µM, and (3) not quantified. Any metabolites outside the physiological concentrations listed on HMDB will be excluded and listed as ‘not quantified’ for further investigation. ROIAL-NMR references metabolite concentrations listed in the HMDB for each biofluid type. We use the highest and lowest reported values as their potential concentration limits. ROIAL-NMR will exclude any metabolites present below 5 µM, as this is below the lower detection limits of NMR. As HMDB still lists metabolites that are not quantified, we include them in category 3-“not quantified” to avoid missing out on potential metabolites present in the sample. Furthermore, all potential metabolites were required to present the same “trend” for all observable spectral regions, namely, increased, decreased, or no difference in spectral intensity as compared to a separate tested group. Finally, the output metabolites were sorted by their significant statuses, according to Wilcoxon tests or FDR corrections. Please note that ROIAL-NMR annotates all potential metabolites that meet the match criteria as stated above. This list serves a comprehensive list of metabolites that are defined with the literature and database used (HMDB used here). Their presence in the sample is not absolute, but a starting point for further confirmation with literature sources. This is also subject to updates within the metabolite database itself.

Discussion

The results presented here demonstrated that: (1) human blood sera metabolomics can be measured from 10 µL serum samples with HRMAS NMR spectroscopy; (2) detectable serum metabolomics and their changes can differentiate between healthy and disease groups and among disease etiologies; and (3) the identified metabolites may reveal metabolic pathways and markers linked to diseased conditions. Currently, there is little clinical knowledge regarding metabolites that may be associated with myocardial fibrosis of various origins.

Metabolomics distinguishing heart failure: reduced vs. preserved ejection fractions, branched chain amino acids, and the Warburg effect

The metabolomic data on myocardial fibrosis is still growing. In previous studies, it has been found that heart failure (HF) both with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) are associated with abnormal energy metabolism and increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), affected fatty acid beta-oxidation, and impaired mitochondrial function (Noordali et al., 2018). In HFpEF, which is more common in CS and CA, higher levels of hydroxyproline and symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) have been observed compared to HFrEF, reflecting increased fibrosis, inflammation, and myocardial structural changes (Hage et al., 2020). Similarly, we observed increased levels of hydroxyproline in CS (not significant) and CA (significant after FDR corr.).

Growing bodies of evidence reveal that the Warburg effect and abnormalities in branched chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism may contribute to HF (Murashige et al., 2022). In particular, leucine has been proposed as a risk markers for various cardiometabolic features, in particularly HFrEF (McGarrah & White, 2023; Sun et al., 2016). Elevated plasma BCAAs in blood plasma may arise from Warburg-driven metabolic shifts, which favor aerobic glycolysis over mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in cancer cells (Nava & Madrigal Perez, 2022). Upregulated BCAAs fuel protein synthesis, which may be reflected in reduced glutamate levels (observed in this study) due to increased glutamate consumption (or decreased glutamate synthesis), and activate the rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, contributing to aberrant cell proliferation (Kaspy et al., 2023) In a mouse model of cardiac sarcoidosis, chronic mTOR complex 1 activation in myeloid cells initiated progressive cardiac granuloma accumulation, increased fibrosis, impaired cardiac function, and decreased plakoglobin expression (Bueno-Beti et al., 2023).

We observed increased leucine and lactic acid (significant after FDR corr.), which is in line with the Warburg and BCAA hypothesis. We also observed decreased 3-hydroxyisovaleric acid (3-HIVA, significant after FDR corr.), a downstream catabolic product of leucine produced from isovaleryl-CoA, in all patient groups, contradicting previous reports of increased 3-HIVA in HF (Murashige et al., 2022).

Metabolic changes in myocardial fibrosis

Among the potential metabolites characterized in Fig. 6, medium- and long-chain fatty acids such as caprylic acid (C8:0) and palmitic acid (PalA) have been linked to MF through disrupted myocardial fatty acid homeostasis. Deletion of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), which reduces β-oxidation of medium and long chain fatty acids, has been linked to MF (Watanabe et al., 2000). On the contrary, PPARα activation has led to reduced MF in pressure-overloaded rat hearts (Ogata et al., 2002). In the cultured rat cardiomyocyte H9C2, external administration of palmitic acid led to increased expression of pro-fibrotic genes (Lin et al., 2022). In our experiments, serum levels of caprylic acid and palmitic acid were decreased (significant after FDR) across all disease groups, potentially due to sequestration in fibrotic myocardium, or alternatively from reduced PPARα activity, leading to increased uptake of those fatty acids into other tissues, such as liver and skeletal muscle that contain PPARα to compensate (Fruchart et al., 1999).

Moreover, some metabolites altered in the MF group, including trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), may originate from the gut microbiome and be linked to the Gut-Heart-Axis (Xu et al., 2023), linking the gut microbiome and the intestinal metabolomic profile to HF. TMAO is typically elevated HF patients and is associated with MF (Organ et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2023). In our study, serum levels of TMAO were decreased across all disease groups relative to the control (significant after FDR corr. in CS and CA, significant before FDR corr. in MF). This may be a compensatory response to ongoing MF. In fact, a myocardial infarction rat model showed reduced MF following TMAO depletion (Yang et al., 2019).

Metabolic changes in cardiac sarcoidosis and amyloidosis

There is limited information regarding metabolomic changes associated with CS. NMR spectroscopy of saliva samples from (pulmonary) sarcoidosis patients identified increased lactate, acetate, and N-butyrate, and decreased levels of butyrate and methanol (Duchemann et al., 2016). P-coumaroylagmatine and palmitoylcarnitine have also been reported as potential biomarkers of fibrosis in (pulmonary) sarcoidosis (Mirsaeidi et al., 2016). These findings corroborate our observations, in terms of increased lactate (significant after FDR corr.) and the decreased 3-hydroxybutyric acid levels (not significant), though the mentioned study refers to pulmonary rather than cardiac sarcoidosis. Additionally, multiple metabolites containing uric acid were elevated in the disease groups, though uric acid itself was not significantly altered. Zhu et al., observed decreased uric acid in pulmonary sarcoidosis which related to high prognostic value (Zhu et al., 2025). Hyperuricemia is also seen in vitamin D deficiency (Isnuwardana et al., 2020), which has also been associated with sarcoidosis, particularly among African Americans (Gianella et al., 2020). These findings suggest that uric acid metabolism may help distinguish between different sarcoidosis manifestations and warrant further investigation. Thus, the increased uric acid metabolites observed here may reflect interactions between vitamin D, sarcoidosis and uric acid metabolism.

Metabolomic data on CA is also very sparse. Cardiovascular MR imaging and spectroscopy show a reduction in the triglyceride (TAG)-to-water ratio in CA patients relative to controls, whereas creatinine-to-water ratios remained unchanged (Gastl et al., 2019), making TAG a promising biomarker for detecting CA (Gupta et al., 2021). Although TAG reductions have been reported in transthyretin amyloidosis without specific cardiac involvement (Luigetti et al., 2022), our study also detected metabolomic changes in fatty acids, such as PalA, that may reflect reduced serum TAG in CA patients.

Between group metabolomic variations in energy and inflammatory metabolism

Simultaneous analysis of all three myocardial fibrotic diseases enabled multigroup comparisons, revealing key differences in spectral ROIs corresponding to energy metabolism compounds, including pyruvic acid, alanine, lactic acid, and acetyl-carnitine. As previously mentioned, alterations in fatty acid oxidation and Warburg effects were evident, highlighting the potential influence of the Randle cycle in aberrant cardiac metabolism, where glucose and fatty acids compete for oxidation in muscle and adipose tissue (Birkenfeld et al., 2019). While the healthy heart primarily relies on fatty acid oxidation (relying on glycolysis for only 5% of its ATP stores (Birkenfeld et al., 2019), the failing heart shows disrupted energy metabolism, where fatty acid oxidation is reduced and glucose metabolism is preferred (Birkenfeld et al., 2019). Moreover, metabolic patterns can vary with HF severity, with glucose oxidation increasing in the hypertrophied heart, but declining in advanced HF, where reliance on anaerobic glycolysis dominates (Li et al., 2021). Impaired glucose oxidation is associated with cardiac dysfunction, electrical remodeling, and mitochondrial dysfunction (Piao et al., 2010).

We report significantly decreased serum levels of several fatty acids, including palmitic, caprylic and nervonic acids (each significant after FDR corr.). In the CS group, fructose-6-phosphate (not significant) and lactate (significant after FDR corr.) were increased. Interestingly, pyruvate was significantly decreased in MF and CS but increased in CA, suggesting a distinct metabolic profile in CA. Elevated myocardial oxygen consumption previously reported in CA patients (Clemmensen et al., 2018), may reflect a compensatory upregulation of oxidative glucose metabolism, whereas MF and CS may rely more on anaerobic glycolysis. Further research is required to clarify these mechanisms.

The ROI 3.40–3.37 ppm (corresponding to uric acid metabolites mentioned above) was particularly relevant for differentiating CA from CS, two conditions that are clinically difficult to distinguish, as both result in HFpEF and produce nonspecific findings when using common imaging techniques (Muchtar et al., 2017).

Overall, beyond linking metabolomic profiles of HF to pro-fibrotic changes, Hage et al. argue that metabolomic profiling has prognostic value (Hage et al., 2020). In HFrEF, metabolomic profiling may even outperform common current predictors like brain-natriuretic peptide (Cheng et al., 2015; Lanfear et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018). Additionally, HFpEF-related metabolomic changes can be correlated with clinical parameters, including diastolic dysfunction (Fatima et al., 2019). Within this study we identified distinct metabolic profiles characterizing key cardiac fibrosis etiologies. These findings can inform clinicians of specific pathway disruptions and aid in the discovery of etiology-specific biomarkers. Furthermore, this study shows that HRMAS NMR spectroscopy could provide a reliable method to detect and differentiate the various disease etiologies which can improve prognostic assessment, clarify clinical correlations, and reveal new therapeutic targets.

Limitations and prospectives

Although the MGB BioBank contains blood specimens from over 380,000 patients, only a small number could be recruited for this study. This limited sample size prevented age-based matching in the CA group, as well as differentiation of the investigated diseases from other conditions, including non-cardiac diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis, chronic liver disease, or chronic kidney disease, which could confound the results (Hage et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2007). Additionally, due to the stochastic nature of myocardial biopsy sampling and the fact that not all patients received imaging, overlap between the disease groups cannot be excluded with certainty.

Patient selection in the MF group was more heterogenous than in the other groups. It has been described that MF due to arterial hypertension results in different pathophysiological changes than MF due to ischemic causes, like coronary artery disease (Bishop, 1999; Weber, 2000). Therefore, subgroup analysis would be advisable, but not achievable with our limited case numbers. These limitations may be overcome with future multi-center studies.

Additionally, our study provides only a snapshot of metabolomic profiles, not dynamic changes. It would be valuable to observe how these metabolomic profiles evolve over time, and whether metabolomic alterations precede (para)clinical changes.

Furthermore, the reported potential metabolites should only be viewed as NMR-derived metabolic possibilities based on current knowledge and reports in literature, such as HMDB or any other NMR metabolite database, and can therefore only serve as starting points for further evaluations into metabolic realities. Multidimensional NMR experiments (e.g., 2D HSQC, COSY) offer increased resolution to confidently detect and annotate metabolites that may be overlapping in 1D 1H NMR, used here. Due to the limited sample volume and experimental time constraints, we were not able to obtain 2D spectra with reasonable signal-to-noise. As such, ROIAL-NMR was used to annotate all potential metabolites that may resonate within the determined ROIs with the corresponding SRRM. The metabolites here should not be considered as certain existences in the samples tested, but rather as a comprehensive untargeted list of compounds that may potentially be in the samples. Further confirmation with literature is necessary. Other analytical techniques such as mass spectrometry or liquid/gas chromatography may be used to verify these results. Finally, it may be of interest to compare our proposed metabolomic profiles with histopathological findings to probe potential associations between the severity of myocardial fibrosis and the metabolomic changes detected.

Conclusion/summary

Myocardial fibrosis is a relevant contributor to the development of heart failure, which remains a leading cause of morbidity and death in Western countries and is consequently of high medical, social, and economical significance. Often, cardiac pathologies may present themselves in a similar manner despite the originating cause. As such, to implement effective treatment strategies, it is important to accurately diagnose the disease etiology. Metabolomics offers an avenue to non-invasively identify and monitor disease specific biomarkers. Here, we report our HRMAS NMR-based metabolomic analyses on blood sera of patients with myocardial fibrosis of various origins (cardiac sarcoidosis and amyloidosis), which allowed for differentiations between diseased and healthy individuals as well as among the different etiopathologies. We provide evidence supporting aberrant metabolic states in the fibrotic heart, including but not limited to fatty acid, glucose, and amino acid oxidation. Exploring the influence of pre- and post-diagnosis time intervals, cardiovascular risk factors, and potential correlations with histopathological examinations of myocardial tissues in future studies would be beneficial for advancing a more comprehensive understanding of myocardial metabolomics.
