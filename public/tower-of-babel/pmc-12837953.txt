The value of translational therapy in the treatment of advanced hepatoid adenocarcinoma of stomach
Authors: Liqiao Chen, Jieli Xu, Jialin Li, Ke Ji, Ji Zhang, Xiaojiang Wu, Kai Zhou, Jiatian Tang, Yan Wu, Anqiang Wang, Zhaode Bu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvanced hepatoid adenocarcinoma of stomach still lacks effective and standardized treatment options. This study aims to explore the therapeutic value of translational therapy in advanced hepatoid adenocarcinoma of stomach.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 37 patients with advanced hepatoid adenocarcinoma of stomach who successfully underwent translational therapy followed by surgical intervention between October 2009 and October 2024. Through survival analysis and Cox regression, we identified the clinicopathological factors that significantly impact patient prognosis.ResultsThe median survival time of 37 patients was 39.0 months. 70.3% of patients achieved R0 resection, with an average OS 93.8 months and a 5-year survival rate of 64.4%. And 10.8% of patients achieved pathological complete remission, with an average OS of 65.7 months and a 5-year survival rate of 100%. Additionally, radical surgery, pM stage, cytology, and surgery type were identified as independent risk factors influencing the prognosis of patients.ConclusionTranslational therapy is a promising strategy for patients with initially unresectable advanced hepatoid adenocarcinoma of stomach. Achieving R0 resection and attaining pathological complete remission are crucial milestones in improving patient prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15442-9.

Introduction

Gastric cancer, currently the fifth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, remains one of the most prevalent and deadly forms of cancer worldwide. The incidence and mortality rates of gastric cancer exhibit significant regional variations, with particularly high rates observed in East Asia [1]. Meanwhile, gastric cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease, exhibiting extensive clinical, pathological, and molecular differences both within the tumor and among different patients [2]. There is also significant heterogeneity between various pathological subtypes of gastric cancer. For instance, hepatoid adenocarcinoma of stomach (HAS), a subtype with a relatively low incidence but extremely high malignancy, is characterized by distinct histological features and unique biological behaviors. HAS is highly invasive and is prone to hepatic metastasis in clinical practice. Due to its histological similarities to hepatocellular carcinoma, serum or immunohistochemical tests often reveal elevated or positive alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels [3, 4]. In addition, from a molecular perspective, HAS is prone to frequent genetic mutations, such as those in TP53, SPTA1, and ZFHX4, as well as high-frequency copy number amplifications of genes like CCNE1. These genetic alterations are closely associated with clinical pathological features, including poor differentiation and increased lymphovascular invasion [5]. Due to the aforementioned characteristics, patients with HAS generally have a poor prognosis. By the time of diagnosis, HAS is often at an advanced stage, leaving little opportunity for surgical intervention. Furthermore, conventional medical treatments tend to show limited effectiveness [6]. Therefore, improving the prognosis for patients with HAS has become an urgent issue, requiring the exploration of more effective treatment strategies and novel therapeutic approaches.

Translational therapy, as a promising approach in advanced gastric cancer, has the potential to improve patient prognosis. This strategy involves integrating novel therapeutic drugs and technologies into clinical practice, aiming to enhance the effectiveness of existing treatments and develop new models which are better suited to the molecular characteristics of gastric cancer. By focusing on targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and personalized medicine, translational therapy seeks to address the complex heterogeneity of gastric cancer and provide more tailored and effective treatment options for patients, particularly in advanced stages where conventional therapies often fall short [7]. One of the key areas of translational therapy is targeted therapy. For example, trastuzumab, which targets HER2, has been shown to significantly improve the prognosis of patients with HER2-positive advanced gastric cancer when used in combination with chemotherapy [8]. Similarly, ramucirumab, which targets VEGFR2, has demonstrated good efficacy in second-line treatment for advanced gastric cancer, whether used alone or in combination with chemotherapy [9]. In addition, immunotherapy is a crucial component of translational therapy. Patients with advanced gastric cancer who exhibit MSI-H or PD-L1 expression generally respond well to immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab [10, 11]. Translational therapy integrates the synergistic effects of different treatment modalities, such as combining targeted therapy with chemotherapy or immunotherapy, significantly improving resistance and survival outcomes in advanced-stage patients [12, 13]. At the same time, utilizing biomarkers to guide treatment decisions and monitor therapeutic responses is key to translational therapy, which can accurately provide individualized diagnosis and treatment for patients [14].

Consequently, a retrospective analysis was conducted on a cohort of 311 patients diagnosed with HAS at Peking university cancer hospital between 2009 and 2024. Among these, 37 patients underwent translational therapy followed by surgical intervention. This subset of patients was specifically analyzed to assess the value of translational therapy in the management of advanced HAS. This study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first retrospective study related to translational therapy in HAS.

Methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Pathologically confirmed diagnosis of HAS; (2) Diagnosed as unresectable locally advanced or potentially resectable metastatic HAS (potentially resectable metastatic HAS includes: Liver metastases, limited to one to three lesions under 4cm or confined to one lobe, without major vessel or bile duct involvement, are resectable according to hepatobiliary surgeons. The liver can tolerate surgery if the residual volume is at least 30%−40%; liver metastases exceeding the number and size described above, but still technically resectable based on surgical evaluation; Limited para-aortic lymph node (16a2/b1) metastasis; Free cancer cells were positive in the peritoneal cavity; Ovarian metastasis); (3) Age between 18 and 75 years (at the time of informed consent), regardless of gender; (4) It is anticipated that R0 resection will be achievable; (5) ECOG score was 0–1; (6) Estimated survival time was months or more; (7) The patients exhibited optimal organ function, enabling them to tolerate chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and surgical interventions effectively. The main exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Metastatic Gastric Cancer; (2) Simultaneous primary neoplasms in different organs or dual neoplastic occurrences; (3) Patients died during the perioperative period.

Disease management

In this study, all patients underwent endoscopic examination and pathological biopsy to confirm the diagnosis of HAS. Preoperative tumor location, tumor size, as well as lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis, were evaluated through computed tomography (CT). Tumor markers were also tested to assist in assessing the overall condition of the patients. Before surgery, a personalized translational therapy plan was discussed by a multidisciplinary expert team with the patient’s informed consent. After completing the translational therapy, the treatment effectiveness was reassessed, and the multidisciplinary expert team would discuss whether surgery was feasible and develop the surgical plan accordingly. Postoperatively, the decision for adjuvant therapy was based on the patient’s overall condition and pathological results. Regular postoperative follow-up was conducted, continuing until the patient’s death or October 2024.

Definition and collection of clinicopathological information

In this study, the diagnosis of HAS for all patients was based on the definition provided in the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of digestive system tumors. Specifically, the diagnosis required the observation of large polygonal eosinophilic neoplastic cells (hepatic differentiation areas) in pathological slides under a microscope [15]. All clinicopathological information of the patients in this study was independently collected and verified by two physicians. They made every effort to collect complete clinical and pathological data. However, due to factors such as the long time span and the preservation of electronic medical records, some patient information may be incomplete. The overall survival (OS) was defined as the period from the initiation of treatment to termination of follow-up or death, and it served as the primary endpoint.

Statistical analysis

The Chi-square test was utilized to analyze the correlation among two variables. The Cox proportional hazards model was employed for both univariate and multivariate analyses. The Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were used to assess the impact of variables on overall survival (OS). P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed by IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient enrollment and basic data

Between October 2009 and October 2024, a cohort of 311 patients diagnosed with HAS and who underwent surgical treatment at Peking University Cancer Hospital were collected. Of these, 37 patients satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria for translational therapy (Figure. 1). Consequently, the clinicopathological data of these 37 patients were incorporated into this study for retrospective analysis. The 40 clinicopathological variables of the 37 patients were shown in Table 1. Among them, 26 patients (70.3%) completed translational therapy and achieved R0 resection. Seven patients (18.9%) achieved major pathological response (MPR), and four patients (10.8%) achieved pathological complete response (PCR). Among the 31 patients who developed lymph node metastasis, 14 patients (37.8%) were in stage N1, 4 patients (10.8%) were in stage N2, and 13 patients (35.1%) were in stage N3. Additionally, 15 patients (40.5%) had liver metastasis. Furthermore, 32 patients (86.5%) had elevated serum AFP levels or were positive for AFP on immunohistochemistry. Among these, 25 patients (71.4%) had solely elevated serum AFP levels. Clinicopathological factors associated with radical surgery, MPR and PCR in HAS patients

Subsequently, we performed a chi-square test to analyze the clinicopathological factors associated with the radical surgery, MPR and PCR (Table 2). The results showed that the clinicopathological factors associated with radical surgery were family history of malignancy (P = 0.011) and cytology (P = 0.026). The clinicopathological factors associated with PCR were tumor regression grade (TRG, P < 0.001), MPR (P < 0.001) and pT stage (P < 0.001). The clinicopathological factors associated with MPR were TRG (P<0.001), PCR (P<0.001), pT stage (P<0.001), tumor size (P = 0.012), and lymphovascular invasion (P = 0.006). Risk factors affecting the prognosis of HAS patients

Survival analysis revealed that the borrmann classification (P = 0.008), lauren classification (P = 0.003), lymphovascular invasion (P = 0.041), and pT stage (P = 0.044) were significantly associated with OS in HAS patients after translational therapy, and could serve as predictive indicators for OS in these patients (Figure. 2 A-D). Although no statistically significant differences were observed in terms of radical surgery (P = 0.056), pM stage (P = 0.059), cytology (P = 0.052), and surgery type (P = 0.082), patients who achieved R0 resection, had no distant metastasis, exhibited negative cytology, or did not undergo total gastrectomy still showed an impressive trend in overall survival (Fig. 3A-D). To further explore the independent risk factors which have an influence on the prognosis of HAS patients after translational therapy, we performed both univariate and multivariate cox regression analyses. After considering clinical and statistical differences, 11 clinicopathological factors were included in the analysis. The results showed that radical surgery (P = 0.043), pM stage (P = 0.003), cytology (P = 0.026), and surgery type (P = 0.007) were independent risk factors influencing the prognosis of HAS patients (Table 3). The most important finding was that patients who achieved R0 resection had 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates of 100%, 72.5%, and 64.4%, respectively. Meanwhile, patients who achieved pathological complete response had a 5-year survival rate of 100% (Figure.4 A). Discussion

HAS is a rare and highly aggressive subtype of gastric cancer, with histological features resembling hepatocellular carcinoma. Due to its high metastatic potential and malignant biological behavior, the prognosis for HAS patients is generally poor [17, 18]. For patients with initially unresectable gastric cancer, translational therapy followed by surgical resection has become a promising treatment approach. A study on the combination chemotherapy regimen of docetaxel, cisplatin, and S-1 demonstrated a high translational rate, with a significant number of patients achieving R0 resection, resulting in a notable improvement in OS [19] Similarly, another study investigating the combination of apatinib, paclitaxel, and S-1 found high translational rates and R0 resection rates, bringing significant survival benefits to initially unresectable gastric cancer patients [20]. Moreover, the role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in improving tumor resectability has been supported by clinical trials, showing that preoperative chemotherapy can downstage locally advanced gastric adenocarcinoma and facilitate surgical resection. These findings underscore the importance of achieving R0 resection and PCR in improving survival outcomes for patients with advanced gastric cancer, including those with hepatoid adenocarcinoma [21].

Other conversion therapy strategies, such as chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy, also show substantial potential in reducing tumor burden and increasing the likelihood of R0 resection. These evolving treatment approaches hold promise for improving the prognosis of patients with advanced gastric cancer. A case report on HAS demonstrated that preoperative treatment with SOX and sintilimab significantly reduced tumor size and led to a substantial decrease in serum AFP levels. Following this treatment, surgery was performed, successfully achieving R0 resection without evidence of lymph node metastasis [22]. These results are similar to our findings, where the average OS for patients who achieved R0 resection was 93.8 months, with a 5-year survival rate of 64.4% compared to only 24.0 months and 30.8 months for patients who underwent R1 and R2 resections, respectively. The median survival time of patients with R1 resection and R2 resection was 24.0 months and 35.0 months, respectively. Notably, in our study, patients who achieved PCR remaine 100% alive at the end of the follow-up period, with the longest OS reaching 97.0 months. This remarkable result highlights the potential of achieving PCR in significantly improving survival outcomes, further supporting the value of conversion therapy and effective treatment regimens in managing advanced gastric cancer, including hepatoid adenocarcinoma. Such findings suggest that early and aggressive therapeutic strategies can potentially offer long-term survival benefits for patients with initially unresectable tumors.

Additionally, this study has several limitations. First, there are inherent biases associated with retrospective studies: as a single-center, retrospective analysis, data collection may be subject to selection bias and information bias. Second, the relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power of some analyses. Finally, due to the extended time span of the study, there is considerable heterogeneity in the translational therapy regimens, making it difficult to determine the optimal translational treatment strategy.

In conclusion, translational therapy for advanced HAS, especially through the combination of various treatment strategies, shows significant promise. Achieving PCR or R0 resection is a key objective, offering substantial survival benefits for patients. At the same time, this highlights the necessity for further prospective studies with large sample sizes and multicenter cohorts to refine and optimize these treatment strategies, ensuring more effective outcomes for patients with this challenging cancer.
