Integrated Functional Characterization of Hemileia vastatrix Effector Candidates Reveals Coordinated Immune Suppression, Sequential Deployment and Compartment‐Specific Targeting
Authors: Thiago Maia, Gustavo Marin‐Ramirez, Maicon N. Araujo, Larissa G. Zanardo, Sérgio H. Brommonschenkel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCoffee leaf rust, caused by the obligate biotrophic fungus Hemileia vastatrix, remains the most destructive disease of coffee worldwide. Although genomic and transcriptomic studies have identified a large number of candidate effectors, experimental evidence supporting their biological roles during infection remains limited. Here, we integrated functional assays, temporal expression profiling during coffee infection and subcellular localization analyses to investigate the biological properties of 44 H. vastatrix effector candidates (HvECs). Using the Pseudomonas fluorescens EtHAn effector delivery system in Nicotiana benthamiana, 15 HvECs consistently suppressed pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI), indicating that immune suppression is a widespread property among the H. vastatrix effector repertoire, as assessed in this heterologous system. Five HvECs also attenuated AvrB‐triggered effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), and three suppressed both PTI and ETI, suggesting that a subset of HvECs targets conserved regulatory nodes shared by these interconnected immune pathways. Temporal expression profiling revealed sequential deployment of HvECs throughout infection, with distinct subsets predominating during pre‐biotrophic development, host penetration or biotrophic colonization, consistent with stage‐specific functions during fungal pathogenesis. Subcellular localization analyses further showed that HvECs preferentially accumulated in the nucleus or chloroplasts, compartments known as central hubs of plant immune regulation. This study provides the most comprehensive functional characterization of H. vastatrix effector candidates to date, establishes a biologically informed framework for prioritizing candidates for future identification of avirulence determinants recognized by SH resistance genes, and advances our understanding of how the coffee rust fungus orchestrates immune suppression across time and cellular space during pathogenesis.

Introduction

Coffee leaf rust (CLR), caused by the obligate biotrophic fungus Hemileia vastatrix, remains the most destructive disease of coffee worldwide and continues to threaten the durability of genetic resistance despite more than a century of research. Although resistant cultivars carrying SH resistance genes represent the most economical and sustainable strategy for disease management, the recurrent emergence of physiological races capable of overcoming most resistance sources highlights the remarkable adaptive potential of the pathogen and underscores the need to better understand the molecular mechanisms governing pathogenicity, host colonization and immune recognition (Cabral et al. 2009; Talhinhas et al. 2017; Sera et al. 2022; Silva et al. 2022; Brenes et al. 2025). As an obligate biotroph, H. vastatrix establishes prolonged interactions with living host cells through specialized haustoria, requiring continuous modulation of host physiology throughout a latent period that may extend for nearly one month (Maia, Badel, Fernandes, et al. 2017; Silva et al. 2002). This intimate association strongly suggests that successful colonization depends on the coordinated deployment of effector proteins that suppress host immunity while modulating cellular processes required to establish and maintain long‐term biotrophy.

Effector proteins are widely recognized as central molecular components of the molecular machinery that enables pathogenesis in filamentous plant pathogens (Li et al. 2024). Although they manipulate a wide range of host cellular processes, suppression of plant immunity represents one of their most fundamental and best‐characterized functions. Increasing evidence further indicates that effector activity is intimately associated with both their temporal deployment during infection and their localization within specific host cellular compartments, where they interact with distinct immune regulatory pathways (Petre et al. 2014; Rocafort et al. 2020; Ngou, Ding, and Jones 2022). Recent advances in comparative genomics, structural biology and artificial intelligence have dramatically expanded the discovery of candidate effectors (Jones and Raffaele 2025), shifting the field from candidate identification towards functional characterization. At the same time, subcellular localization has emerged as an important predictor of effector function because individual cellular compartments harbour specialized signalling networks involved in immune regulation. Likewise, temporal expression patterns provide an additional functional dimension, reflecting the dynamic deployment of effector repertoires during successive stages of host colonization. Because intracellular nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat (NLR) receptors perceive effector activities directly or indirectly through their effects on host targets (Ngou, Ding, and Jones 2022), identifying effectors that suppress immunity, are expressed at biologically relevant stages of infection, and localize to strategic host compartments provides a rational strategy for prioritizing candidates involved in virulence and SH‐mediated resistance.

Although genomic and transcriptomic studies have identified multiple H. vastatrix candidate effectors expressed during coffee infection, particularly during the biotrophic phase (Fernandez et al. 2012; Talhinhas et al. 2014; Porto et al. 2019; Castro et al. 2022), experimental evidence supporting their biological functions remains extremely limited. To date, HvEC‐016 is the only effector experimentally demonstrated that may function as an avirulence determinant recognized by coffee genotypes carrying the SH1 resistance gene (Maia, Badel, Marin‐Ramirez, et al. 2017). Consequently, the immune‐suppressive activities, temporal deployment, intracellular localization and contribution to pathogenesis of the vast majority of predicted H. vastatrix effectors remain unknown. This disparity reflects a broader transition in effector biology, in which advances in candidate discovery have substantially outpaced experimental validation, making integrated functional characterization one of the principal challenges for understanding the molecular basis of pathogenesis and host adaptation.

Progress in addressing this knowledge gap for CLR has been constrained by major experimental limitations. The obligate biotrophic nature of H. vastatrix precludes stable genetic transformation and reverse‐genetic analyses, while coffee still lacks robust, high‐throughput transient expression systems suitable for large‐scale functional studies, despite recent advances in Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation of detached leaves (Vargas‐Guevara et al. 2018). Consequently, heterologous experimental systems remain indispensable for investigating H. vastatrix effectors. Among these, the Pseudomonas fluorescens EtHAn effector delivery system provides a unique advantage because it retains a functional Type III secretion system while lacking endogenous bacterial effectors, allowing fungal effectors to be delivered into plant cells with minimal background interference (Thomas et al. 2009; Maia et al. 2022). Combined with the experimental versatility of Nicotiana benthamiana, whose conserved immune signalling pathways provide an effective platform for dissecting effector activities, this system enables high‐throughput functional analyses of immune suppression while simultaneously facilitating studies of intracellular targeting.

We hypothesized that H. vastatrix deploys functionally distinct effector candidates in a coordinated manner throughout infection and that integrating immune‐suppressive activity with temporal expression and subcellular localization would reveal how these effectors contribute to fungal pathogenesis while enabling the biologically informed prioritization of candidate effectors involved in SH‐mediated immune recognition. To test this hypothesis, we functionally characterized a collection of previously predicted H. vastatrix effector candidates using the P. fluorescens EtHAn effector delivery system, evaluated their ability to suppress pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), determined their expression profiles during coffee infection and investigated the subcellular localization of selected proteins. Collectively, these complementary analyses provide the first comprehensive functional characterization of a diverse repertoire of H. vastatrix effector candidates, revealing how immune suppression, temporal deployment and compartment‐specific targeting are coordinated throughout fungal pathogenesis. Our study also establishes a biologically informed basis for prioritizing candidate effectors for future studies aimed at identifying avirulence determinants recognized by SH resistance proteins.

Results

Functional Screening Identifies HvECs That Suppress PTI in a Heterologous N. benthamiana System

To identify H. vastatrix effector candidates (HvECs) capable of suppressing PTI, 44 candidates were individually delivered into N. benthamiana leaf cells using the P. fluorescens EtHAn (Pf EtHAn) effector delivery (EDV) system. In this assay, infiltration with the nonpathogenic Pf EtHAn strain activates PTI, which restricts the growth of the subsequently infiltrated pathogenic bacterium Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pto) DC3000, preventing its population from reaching the level required to deliver sufficient Type III effectors (T3Es) and thereby preventing the hypersensitive response (HR) (Figure 1a). Restoration of HR following HvEC delivery therefore indicates suppression of PTI (Crabill et al. 2010; Nguyen et al. 2010; Badel et al. 2013; Maia et al. 2022).

Among the 44 HvECs screened, 15 consistently suppressed PTI (HvEC_002, HvEC_004A, HvEC_005, HvEC_006, HvEC_016, HvEC_021, HvEC_028, HvEC_036, HvEC_040, HvEC_053, HvEC_056, HvEC_064, HvEC_071, HvEC_081 and HvEC_101), as evidenced by HR development at the intersection between the bacterial infiltration zones in ≥ 50% of infiltration sites, the threshold used to classify PTI suppressors (Figure 1b; Table S1). Twelve candidates exhibited strong suppressive activity, inducing HR in more than 83% of the tested infiltrated leaves, whereas three candidates displayed intermediate suppression frequencies ranging from 62.5% (HvEC_056) to 75% (HvEC_005 and HvEC_040) (Figure 1b). For each of these 15 HvECs, macroscopic tissue collapse consistent with HR was visible within the overlapping region of N. benthamiana leaves infiltrated with Pf EtHAn expressing the corresponding HvEC and subsequently with Pto DC3000, and this cell death was confirmed by trypan blue staining of the same tissue (Figure 1c). Candidates exhibiting suppression frequencies below 50% were considered non‐suppressor in this assay. Overall, approximately one‐third (34%) of the HvECs evaluated functioned as PTI suppressors, suggesting that immune suppression is a widespread property among candidate effectors of the predicted H. vastatrix repertoire, as assessed in this heterologous Pf EtHAn/N. benthamiana system.

To determine whether PTI suppression resulted in enhanced pathogen colonization, the 15 PTI‐suppressing HvECs were further evaluated for their ability to promote multiplication of Pto DC3000 in N. benthamiana. Leaves were first infiltrated with Pf EtHAn expressing individual HvECs and subsequently challenged with Pto DC3000. All 15 HvECs that suppressed PTI also promoted bacterial proliferation within the overlapping infiltration zones (Figure 2). The effect became evident at 24 h after Pto DC3000 inoculation and was most pronounced at 48 h, when bacterial populations exceeded those of the empty vector control by more than three orders of magnitude (Figure 2). By contrast, no detectable bacterial multiplication occurred in the negative control, indicating that PTI elicited by Pf EtHAn effectively restricted pathogen growth in the absence of HvEC‐mediated immune suppression.

Together, these results demonstrate that a substantial proportion of the H. vastatrix effector repertoire can effectively suppress PTI when delivered via the Pf EtHAn system, with a clear biological consequence of enhanced Pto DC3000 proliferation.

A Subset of HvECs Suppresses ETI

To determine whether H. vastatrix effectors also interfere with ETI, 20 HvECs, including the 15 PTI suppressors identified above and five additional candidates, were evaluated using an AvrB‐induced hypersensitive response (HR) assay in N. benthamiana (Maia et al. 2022). Leaves were co‐infiltrated with Pf EtHAn expressing AvrB and Pf EtHAn delivering individual HvECs at a 1:1 ratio, and HR development was evaluated 48 h after infiltration. As expected, Pf EtHAn expressing AvrB elicited a robust HR, whereas Pf EtHAn carrying the empty pEDV6 vector did not induce HR, confirming the specificity of the assay (Figure S1).

Five HvECs (HvEC_006, HvEC_032, HvEC_043, HvEC_053 and HvEC_081) consistently attenuated or suppressed AvrB‐triggered HR in more than 50% of the tested leaves (Figure 3a; Table S2). HvEC_043 exhibited the strongest activity, suppressing HR in more than 70% of the infiltrated leaves, whereas HvEC_032 and HvEC_081 displayed intermediate suppression frequencies of 66.6% and 62.5%, respectively (Figure 3a). Notably, three of these candidates (HvEC_006, HvEC_053 and HvEC_081) also suppressed PTI (Figures 1 and 2), indicating that a subset of HvECs interferes with both major layers of plant innate immunity.

To quantitatively validate these observations, ion leakage associated with HR‐associated membrane disruption was monitored between 0 and 28 h after infiltration. From 16 h onward, conductance values for AvrB co‐expressed with each of the five HvECs remained consistently below the standard error range of the AvrB + empty vector control (Figure 3b), indicating a reduction in electrolyte leakage associated with each of these effectors and confirming that they attenuate ETI‐associated cell death.

Together, these results identify five HvECs capable of suppressing ETI and reveal three multifunctional effectors that suppress both PTI and ETI, suggesting that they may target conserved components shared between the two immune pathways.

The HvECs displayed distinct temporal expression patterns that could be grouped according to the stage at which transcript accumulation reached its maximum (Figure 4). Four genes (HvEC_036, HvEC_040, HvEC_028 and HvEC_032) were most highly expressed during the pre‐biotrophic phase. HvEC_036 and HvEC_040 reached peak transcript abundance in dormant urediniospores, whereas HvEC_028 and HvEC_032 peaked during urediniospore germination (Figure 4a,b). Although predominantly associated with early development, these genes remained expressed during the initial stages of host colonization, with HvEC_032 maintaining detectable transcript levels throughout the biotrophic phase.

A second group of HvECs exhibited maximum expression after host penetration. HvEC_004A and HvEC_005 were strongly induced at 24 h post‐inoculation (hpi), coinciding with fungal penetration and the establishment of biotrophy (Figure 4c). HvEC_004A remained highly expressed throughout the subsequent infection stages, whereas HvEC_005 exhibited a pronounced but transient induction during penetration.

In contrast, HvEC_002 accumulated predominantly during the later stages of colonization, reaching its highest transcript levels at 9 and 15 days post‐inoculation (dpi) (Figure 4d), suggesting a role in maintaining the biotrophic interaction.

Overall, the temporal expression profiles indicate that HvECs are deployed sequentially throughout infection, from urediniospore germination to late biotrophic colonization, supporting the hypothesis that distinct subsets of effectors contribute to successive stages of host manipulation during infection.

HvECs Target Distinct Plant Subcellular Compartments

To gain insight into their potential modes of action, the subcellular localization of 11 HvEC‐GFP fusion proteins was examined by transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves.

Nine HvECs accumulated in defined cellular compartments (Figures 5 and 6). Six proteins localized predominantly to the nucleus. HvRTP_01B‐GFP, HvEC_004A‐GFP and HvEC_040‐GFP accumulated mainly in the nucleus while retaining weak cytoplasmic fluorescence, indicating nucleocytoplasmic localization (Figure 5a). In contrast, HvEC_006‐GFP, HvEC_016‐GFP and HvEC_036‐GFP were concentrated in the nucleoplasm, with no obvious accumulation in the nucleolar region (Figure 5b). Nuclear localization was confirmed by co‐localization with the Arabidopsis thaliana nucleoplasmic marker AtWWP1‐mCherry (Silva et al. 2015); as this marker labels the nucleoplasm rather than the nucleolus specifically, definitive confirmation of nucleolar exclusion would require co‐localization with a dedicated nucleolar marker such as fibrillarin (Zheng et al. 2016).

Conversely, HvEC_028‐GFP and HvEC_101‐GFP displayed diffuse fluorescence without obvious compartment specificity (Figure S2), whereas free GFP was distributed uniformly throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure S3).

Discussion

Suppression of host immunity is one of the best‐characterized activities of filamentous pathogen effectors and a common prerequisite for successful colonization of living host tissues (Rocafort et al. 2020; Ngou, Ding, and Jones 2022; Li et al. 2024). Accordingly, effector biology has evolved from identifying candidate secreted proteins towards understanding how entire repertoires manipulate interconnected immune networks throughout infection (Li et al. 2024; Petre et al. 2014; Henningsen et al. 2025). Consistent with this perspective, 15 of the 44 HvECs examined consistently suppressed PTI when delivered into N. benthamiana using the P. fluorescens EtHAn Type III secretion system. Their ability to restore tissue viability following Pf EtHAn infiltration while simultaneously promoting increased proliferation of Pto DC3000 demonstrates that these proteins actively interfere with conserved immune processes rather than simply delaying host cell death. Similar results have been reported for effectors from diverse filamentous pathogens using the same experimental platform (Badel et al. 2013; Cheng et al. 2017; Ramachandran et al. 2017; Qi et al. 2019; Maia et al. 2022), reinforcing the utility of this heterologous system for functional characterization when studies in the native host remain experimentally inaccessible.

Approximately one‐third of the HvECs reproducibly suppressed PTI, indicating that immune suppression is a common property of the H. vastatrix secretome rather than a feature restricted to a few specialized proteins. Comparable proportions have been reported in other rust fungi and biotrophic pathogens, where only subsets of predicted secreted proteins exhibit measurable immune‐suppressive activity despite widespread transcription during infection (Petre et al. 2014; Rocafort et al. 2020). This apparent functional specialization likely reflects the evolutionary diversification of effector repertoires, in which individual proteins fulfill complementary roles during successive stages of infection. The relatively high frequency of PTI suppressors identified here therefore supports the view that successful colonization depends on multiple effectors targeting distinct components of the coffee immune system, not on a single effector.

Current models of plant immunity recognize PTI and ETI as highly interconnected signalling networks rather than independent defence layers (Yuan et al. 2021; Ngou, Ding, and Jones 2022). Activation of intracellular NLR receptors amplifies PTI outputs, whereas PTI provides essential components required for full ETI activation, creating extensive molecular crosstalk between these immune pathways. Consequently, effectors capable of attenuating both PTI and ETI are expected to interfere with conserved regulatory nodes positioned upstream of immune network divergence. In agreement with this model, five HvECs attenuated AvrB‐triggered ETI, three of which (HvEC_006, HvEC_053 and HvEC_081) also suppressed PTI. This dual suppressive capacity does not appear to reflect unrelated activities but instead suggests that these HvECs target central regulators shared by both immune pathways.

Although the molecular targets of HvECs remain unknown, studies in bacterial and fungal pathogens provide plausible mechanistic hypotheses. Diverse effectors converge on conserved immune hubs, including receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases, RIN4‐associated complexes, MAP kinase cascades, vesicle trafficking machinery and ubiquitin‐mediated protein turnover, thereby simultaneously attenuating PTI‐ and ETI‐associated responses (Feng et al. 2012; Xu et al. 2017; Redditt et al. 2019; Ngou, Ding, and Jones 2022). Likewise, fungal effectors frequently manipulate central regulatory nodes rather than pathway‐specific components, enabling relatively few proteins to exert broad effects on plant immunity (Rocafort et al. 2020). Whether HvEC_006, HvEC_053 and HvEC_081 converge on similar host targets remains to be determined, but their dual suppressive activity identifies them as particularly strong candidates for future studies aimed at elucidating their molecular functions and interacting host proteins.

Although these experiments were performed in a heterologous system, several lines of evidence support their biological relevance. Core immune signalling pathways are remarkably conserved across dicot species, and homologues of both RIN4 (GenBank accession XP_027114570.2) and RIPK (GenBank accession XP_071908951.1) were identified in Coffea arabica , including strong conservation of their kinase domains. It should be noted, however, that sequence conservation of RIN4 and RIPK does not by itself demonstrate that the AvrB‐RPM1‐RIN4 signalling module operates identically in coffee; functional validation of this pathway in C. arabica remains an important direction for future work. Similar conservation has been demonstrated for several PTI‐ and ETI‐associated signalling components across angiosperms (Ngou, Heal, et al. 2022), explaining why N. benthamiana has become an effective platform for functional analyses of effectors from phylogenetically diverse pathogens. While individual molecular interactions will ultimately require validation in coffee, the convergence of immune‐suppression assays with conserved immune signalling, temporal expression and subcellular localization provide strong evidence that the HvECs identified here represent biologically relevant components of the H. vastatrix infection repertoire.

The prolonged biotrophic lifestyle of H. vastatrix provides an important biological context for interpreting these findings. Following penetration through stomata, the fungus establishes haustoria and maintains intimate interactions with living host cells for nearly 4 weeks before sporulation begins (Silva et al. 1999; Maia, Badel, Fernandes, et al. 2017). Sustaining compatibility with living host tissues over such an extended period is unlikely to depend on the constitutive activity of a few individual effectors. Instead, these results support the view that H. vastatrix relies on successive cohorts of functionally specialized effectors that collectively maintain immune suppression throughout infection, thereby promoting fungal pathogenesis and long‐term biotrophic colonization.

This functional specialization is further supported by the temporal regulation of HvEC expression during infection. Increasing evidence from filamentous pathogens indicates that the biological significance of an effector may depend not only on its molecular activity but also on the stage of infection at which it is expressed and delivered (Rocafort et al. 2020; Petre et al. 2014). Integrating temporal expression with functional characterization therefore provides biological context that cannot be inferred from transcriptomic or immune‐suppression analyses alone.

The temporal expression profiles obtained here, together with those previously reported by Maia, Badel, Marin‐Ramirez, et al. (2017), indicate that H. vastatrix deploys successive waves of effectors throughout infection. Seven candidate effector genes (HvEC_004A, HvEC_005, HvEC_054, HvEC_059, HvEC_071, HvEC_084 and HvEC_125) reached peak transcript accumulation at 24 hpi, coinciding with fungal penetration and the earliest stages of host colonization. During this period, germ tubes differentiate substomatal vesicles, primary hyphae and haustorial mother cells before establishing the first haustoria within subsidiary and mesophyll cells (Silva et al. 1999). These developmental transitions expose the pathogen to intense immune surveillance, making early suppression of host defences particularly important for successful establishment. The coordinated induction of these HvECs therefore strongly suggests that their encoded proteins contribute to overcoming the first immune barriers encountered immediately after penetration.

Interestingly, four PTI‐suppressing effectors (HvEC_028, HvEC_032, HvEC_036 and HvEC_040) exhibited maximum transcript accumulation in dormant or germinated urediniospores, prior to appressorium formation and stomatal penetration. These candidates were originally identified from the HEVA‐Sanger cDNA library generated from germinated urediniospores (Maia, Badel, Marin‐Ramirez, et al. 2017), but their biological significance remained unresolved. Their functional characterization demonstrates that these proteins actively suppress plant immunity. This finding is consistent with, and extends, growing evidence that molecules relevant to the plant–rust fungi interactions can act at the spore or germling stage, well before host penetration or haustorium formation. In the barley– Puccinia graminis f. sp. tritici system, host recognition of urediniospore‐associated proteins occurs within minutes of spore contact, and virulence‐associated proteins have been isolated directly from ungerminated spores, both acting long before haustorium formation (Nirmala et al. 2010, 2011). Similarly, a virulence‐contributing effector protein was recently identified in germinated urediniospores of Puccinia triticina (Ozketen et al. 2026). Our results corroborate this emerging pre‐penetration paradigm in H. vastatrix and suggest that immune modulation may begin as early as spore adhesion to the host leaf surface, prior to stomatal penetration and haustorium formation. Consequently, studies focused exclusively on in planta transcript accumulation are likely to underestimate the contribution of pre‐penetration effectors to fungal pathogenesis.

Temporal coordination of effector deployment has emerged as a common feature of filamentous pathogens. In rust fungi, transcriptomic studies consistently associate distinct subsets of secreted proteins with successive developmental stages, including spore germination, penetration, haustorium differentiation and maintenance of biotrophy (Petre et al. 2014; Rocafort et al. 2020). Similar patterns have been described in both biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens, where successive waves of effectors facilitate transitions between infection stages rather than functioning as constitutively expressed proteins (Kleemann et al. 2012; Lorrain et al. 2019). Our results extend this concept by demonstrating that temporally regulated HvECs include proteins with experimentally validated immune‐suppressive activity, providing functional evidence that these transcriptional programmes reflect biologically meaningful transitions during H. vastatrix pathogenesis rather than simple developmental changes.

The unusually long latent period of coffee leaf rust further emphasizes the importance of this regulatory strategy. Unlike rapidly necrotizing pathogens, H. vastatrix must maintain host cell viability for several weeks while continuously acquiring nutrients through haustoria. As infection progresses, the physiological status of the host and the intensity of immune responses inevitably change, making it unlikely that a single constitutively expressed set of effectors would remain equally effective throughout colonization. Sequential deployment of functionally specialized effectors therefore provides a flexible strategy by which the fungus can continuously adjust host manipulation while maintaining compatibility with living tissues. Such temporal regulation may also reduce selective pressure acting on individual effectors, thereby contributing to the long‐term maintenance of compatible interactions despite the continuous evolution of host immune systems.

Temporal expression alone, however, does not fully explain effector function. Successful host manipulation also depends on the intracellular destination of these proteins after delivery into host cells. Increasing evidence indicates that pathogen effectors preferentially target intracellular compartments functioning as regulatory hubs rather than being randomly distributed throughout the host cell (Petre et al. 2016; Figueroa et al. 2021). In this context, defining the subcellular destinations of HvECs provides mechanistic insight into the host cellular processes targeted during infection.

Six HvEC–GFP fusion proteins accumulated predominantly within the nucleoplasm, with no obvious nucleolar accumulation, tentatively suggesting that these effectors may target processes associated with transcriptional regulation rather than ribosome biogenesis; confirming this would require co‐localization with a dedicated nucleolar marker, as only a nucleoplasmic marker was used here. The nucleus has emerged as one of the principal destinations of filamentous pathogen effectors because it integrates transcriptional reprogramming, chromatin remodelling, RNA processing, hormone signalling and NLR‐mediated immune responses (Petre et al. 2016; Rocafort et al. 2020). Numerous rust fungal effectors exploit these regulatory processes. For example, the Melampsora larici‐populina effector Mlp124478 functions as a DNA‐binding protein that suppresses defence‐associated transcription (Ahmed et al. 2018), whereas the Phakopsora pachyrhizi effector PpEC23 interacts with the soybean transcription factor GmSP12I to attenuate both host and non‐host immunity (Qi et al. 2016). Likewise, members of the Rust Transferred Protein (RTP) family accumulate within host nuclei following translocation from fungal haustoria (Kemen et al. 2005; Qi et al. 2019). The predominance of nucleoplasmic localization observed here therefore suggests that H. vastatrix similarly manipulates nuclear regulatory networks, reinforcing the importance of transcriptional reprogramming during rust fungal pathogenesis.

Three HvECs localized specifically to chloroplasts, extending previous observations that plastids are major targets of pathogen effectors across bacteria, fungi, oomycetes and viruses (Nomura et al. 2012; Sowden et al. 2018). Beyond photosynthesis, chloroplasts coordinate multiple defence‐associated processes, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, calcium signalling, salicylic acid biosynthesis, jasmonate metabolism and retrograde signalling to the nucleus, making them central regulators of plant immunity. These subcellular localization patterns, both nuclear and chloroplastic, were obtained under constitutive CaMV 35S‐driven overexpression in N. benthamiana, a non‐host species for H. vastatrix used here solely as a transient expression system, and may not fully recapitulate native HvEC expression levels, host‐specific interactions or subcellular dynamics during infection of coffee, the natural host.

Evidence from other rust fungi further highlights the importance of chloroplast‐targeted effectors during infection. The M. larici‐populina effector CTP1 belongs to a conserved family of plastid‐targeted proteins within the Melampsoraceae (Petre et al. 2016). In P. striiformis f. sp. tritici, Pst_12806 suppresses chloroplast‐associated immunity through interaction with TaISP, a component of the cytochrome b6f complex (Xu et al. 2019), whereas Pst11215 targets host mitochondria to interfere with immune activation (Pan et al. 2025). More recently, Pst_TTP1 was shown to inhibit chloroplast import of the thioredoxin TaTrx, thereby suppressing ROS accumulation, delaying senescence and promoting prolonged biotrophy (Li et al. 2025). Collectively, these studies indicate that rust fungi preferentially manipulate intracellular organelles that integrate immune signalling rather than isolated defence components. The chloroplast localization of HvEC_021, HvEC_053 and HvEC_056 therefore identifies plastid‐associated immune regulation as a likely target of H. vastatrix during coffee infection.

An important implication of this work is its contribution to identifying candidate avirulence determinants involved in SH‐mediated resistance. Although HvEC‐016 remains the only experimentally validated avirulence effector that may be recognized by an SH resistance protein (Maia, Badel, Marin‐Ramirez, et al. 2017), the present study identifies several HvECs that combine biological characteristics expected for proteins involved in host immune recognition. Effectors that suppress plant immunity, are expressed at biologically appropriate stages of infection and accumulate within immune regulatory compartments constitute particularly strong candidates for future studies aimed at elucidating the molecular basis of SH‐mediated resistance.

Functional validation of these candidates will require complementary approaches, including interaction studies with coffee proteins, transient expression assays in coffee tissues, structural prediction, protein–protein interaction analyses and reverse‐genetic strategies. Until stable transformation or reverse‐genetic tools become available for H. vastatrix, transient delivery of candidate HvECs using the Pf EtHAn system provides a practical platform for accelerating Avr gene discovery. Combined with genetic cosegregation analyses in coffee populations segregating for specific SH resistance genes, this experimental platform would enable direct evaluation of candidate effectors for SH‐specific immune recognition.

When considered together, the functional, temporal and localization datasets reveal a coherent pattern of effector deployment during infection. Immune‐suppressive activity identifies proteins capable of modulating host defences, temporal expression defines the developmental stages at which these activities are required, and subcellular localization indicates the host compartments where they are most likely to operate. Rather than acting as independent proteins with isolated functions, HvECs appear to constitute a coordinated repertoire deployed sequentially throughout infection to manipulate complementary components of the host immune system. This integrated view moves beyond descriptive cataloguing of candidate effectors and provides a biologically grounded model for understanding how H. vastatrix sustains prolonged biotrophic colonization, while establishing a foundation for elucidating the molecular basis of SH‐mediated resistance and ultimately supporting the development of more durable coffee rust resistant cultivars.

Experimental Procedures

Cloning of Hemileia vastatrix Effector Candidates

Candidate effector sequences (HvECs) lacking the predicted N‐terminal signal peptide were amplified by PCR from cDNA prepared from coffee ( C. arabica ‘Catuaí Vermelho IAC‐44’) leaves infected with H. vastatrix race II, the most widespread physiological race of the pathogen (Talhinhas et al. 2017), or from germinated urediniospores as previously described (Maia, Badel, Marin‐Ramirez, et al. 2017). HvEC sequences had been previously predicted and deposited in GenBank under accession numbers KX670432–KX670525. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

PCR products were initially cloned into the Gateway entry vector pENTR/D‐TOPO (Invitrogen) and subsequently recombined into the pEDV6 destination vector (Fabro et al. 2011) using Gateway LR Clonase II (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer's instructions, to generate constructs for delivery through the P. fluorescens EtHAn Type III secretion system. For subcellular localization assays, HvEC sequences lacking both the signal peptide encoding region and the stop codon were amplified using gene‐specific primers and recombined into the binary vector pK7FWG2 (Karimi et al. 2002), generating C‐terminal GFP fusion constructs under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. The integrity and reading frame of all pEDV6::HvEC and pK7FWG2::HvEC‐GFP constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Bacterial Strains and Growth Conditions

Escherichia coli DH5α was used for routine plasmid construction. Triparental mating was performed to mobilize pEDV6::HvEC constructs into P. fluorescens EtHAn using E. coli HB101 carrying pRK2013 as the helper strain. The empty pEDV6 vector was maintained in E. coli DB3.1. EtHAn transformants were selected on Luria Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with chloramphenicol (30 μg mL−1) and gentamicin (25 μg mL−1).

For transient expression assays, Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (pMP90) carrying pK7FWG2::HvEC‐GFP constructs was grown on LB medium supplemented with rifampicin (100 μg mL−1), gentamicin (25 μg mL−1) and spectinomycin (125 μg mL−1).

PTI Suppression Assay

PTI suppression assays were performed essentially as described by Badel et al. (2013) with minor modifications. Suspensions of P. fluorescens EtHAn carrying pEDV6::HvEC constructs or the empty pEDV6 vector were adjusted to 2 × 107 CFU mL−1 in 10 mM MgCl2 and infiltrated into fully expanded leaves of 4‐ to 5‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants using a needle‐free syringe. Seven hours later, an overlapping region was infiltrated with Pto DC3000 at 3 × 106 CFU mL−1 to trigger HR.

HR development was evaluated macroscopically 3 days after infiltration. HvECs were classified as PTI suppressors when they reproducibly restored tissue viability within the overlapping infiltration zone compared with the empty vector control. PTI suppression was quantified as the percentage of infiltration sites exhibiting induction of HR relative to the total number of infiltrated sites. Three independent biological experiments were performed, each comprising eight biological replicates, resulting in a total of 24 biological replicates (n = 24; Table S1).

Cell death was further confirmed by trypan blue staining. Leaf tissues collected 3 days after infiltration were immersed in trypan blue–lactophenol solution, briefly heated to boiling, incubated for 2 h at room temperature and subsequently cleared in chloral hydrate solution until complete removal of chlorophyll. Stained tissues were examined using bright‐field microscopy.

Quantification of Pto DC3000 Proliferation

To validate PTI suppression independently of HR scoring, bacterial growth assays were performed following the protocol of Badel et al. (2013) with minor modifications. Leaves were first infiltrated with P. fluorescens EtHAn carrying either the empty pEDV6 vector or individual pEDV6::HvEC constructs at 5 × 106 CFU mL−1. Seven hours later, overlapping regions were infiltrated with Pto DC3000 at 1 × 105 CFU mL−1.

Leaf discs (1 cm diameter) were collected immediately after inoculation (0 h) and at 48, 72 and 96 h post‐infiltration from three independent plants. Samples were homogenized in 10 mM MgCl2, serially diluted and plated onto King's B medium supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics. Bacterial populations were quantified by enumeration of colony‐forming units (CFU).

ETI Suppression Assay

Suppression of ETI was evaluated using the AvrB/RPM1 recognition system as previously described (Innes et al. 1993; Kessens et al. 2014). Equal volumes of P. fluorescens EtHAn carrying pVSP6::AvrB and Pf EtHAn carrying either pEDV6::HvEC constructs or the empty pEDV6 vector were mixed to obtain a final bacterial concentration of 2 × 107 CFU mlL−1 and co‐infiltrated into fully expanded N. benthamiana leaves.

HvECs were classified as ETI suppressors when they consistently suppressed or substantially attenuated AvrB‐induced HR in more than 50% of infiltrated sites across independent experiments. ETI suppression was quantified as the percentage of infiltration sites exhibiting complete or partial suppression of HR relative to the total number of infiltration sites. HR development was evaluated visually and confirmed by trypan blue staining. Three independent experiments with eight biological replicates each were performed (n = 24; Table S2).

Electrolyte Leakage Assay

Electrolyte leakage was used as an independent quantitative measure of ETI‐associated cell death following Block et al. (2014) with minor modifications. Five leaf discs (8 mm diameter) from each treatment were transferred to 5 mL of sterile deionized water and incubated for 30 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. The incubation solution was subsequently replaced with fresh deionized water, and conductivity was measured at regular intervals from 0 to 28 h after infiltration using a conductivity meter (mCA‐150; MS Tecnopon Instrumentação Científica). Conductivity values (μS cm−1) were used as an indirect estimate of membrane damage associated with ETI‐induced cell death. Treatment effects were assessed descriptively based on the separation between mean conductivity values and their standard errors across the time course.

RT‐qPCR Analysis of HvEC Expression During Coffee Infection

Temporal expression profiles of selected HvEC genes were determined by RT‐qPCR using RNA samples previously obtained from the compatible interaction between H. vastatrix race II and C. arabica ‘Catuaí Vermelho IAC‐44’, as described by Maia, Badel, Fernandes, et al. (2017). Samples comprised dormant urediniospores, germinated urediniospores and infected leaf tissues collected at 12, 24, 48 and 72 h, and 9 and 15 days post‐inoculation, thereby encompassing the major developmental transitions of the fungal infection cycle.

Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and quality assessment were performed as previously described (Maia, Badel, Marin‐Ramirez, et al. 2017). Gene‐specific primers used for RT‐qPCR are listed in Table S4. Amplification reactions were carried out using a 7500 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Green chemistry. Each reaction contained cDNA equivalent to 20 ng of total RNA and was performed in technical duplicate using three independent biological replicates. Cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min followed by 40 amplification cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. Amplification specificity was verified by dissociation curve analysis (60°C–95°C), and primer amplification efficiencies, determined from standard curves, ranged from 92% to 100%, satisfying the requirements for comparative expression analyses.

Relative transcript abundance was calculated using the 2−ΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Expression levels of each HvEC gene were normalized against the geometric mean of three previously validated H. vastatrix reference genes: glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (HvGAPDH), cytochrome c oxidase subunit III (HvCytIII) and β‐tubulin (Hvβ‐tub) (Fernandez et al. 2012). RT‐qPCR analyses were performed using three biological replicates. For each biological replicate, qPCRs were carried out in duplicate (technical replicates), and the C t values of the two technical replicates were averaged before calculating relative transcript abundance.

Subcellular Localization of HvECs

Subcellular localization of HvEC proteins was investigated by transient expression of C‐terminal GFP fusion proteins in N. benthamiana leaf epidermal cells using A. tumefaciens ‐mediated transformation. Overnight cultures of A. tumefaciens GV3101 (pMP90) carrying pK7FWG2::HvEC‐GFP constructs were harvested by centrifugation (5000 g, 5 min), washed and resuspended in infiltration buffer containing 10 mM MgCl2 and 150 μM acetosyringone.

To determine intracellular localization, HvEC‐GFP constructs were co‐infiltrated (1:1 ratio) with well‐established organelle markers fused to mCherry. Nuclear localization was assessed using AtWWP1‐mCherry (Silva et al. 2015), whereas plastid localization was evaluated using the tobacco RuBisCO small subunit fused to mCherry (Dabney‐Smith et al. 1999). Final bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 4 × 107 CFU mL−1 before infiltration into fully expanded leaves of 3‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants.

Fluorescence signals were examined 48 h after agroinfiltration using a Zeiss LSM510 META laser‐scanning confocal microscope. GFP fluorescence was excited at 488 nm using an argon laser and detected at 500–550 nm. mCherry fluorescence was excited with a helium‐neon laser at 543 nm and detected between 560 and 615 nm. Images were processed using the Zeiss LSM software, and subcellular localization was assigned only when GFP fluorescence consistently colocalized with the corresponding organelle marker in independent biological replicates.
