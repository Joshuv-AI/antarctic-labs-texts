Neutrophil depletion dampens inflammation and enhances CD8+ T cell effect on tumor control but undermines anti-PD-1 therapy
Authors: Maike Zheng, He Chang, Shuqin Li, Chaohong Wang, Sibo Long, Yongqiang Wang, Tongmei Zhang, Guirong Wang, Yu Wang, Yan Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with poor survival outcomes. Despite the widespread use of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade therapy, more than 70% of patients fail to benefit and might even develop adverse inflammatory responses. Clinical observations have shown that patients with high neutrophil levels experience worse outcomes following PD-1 blockade. However, the underlying mechanism remains elusive. This study aimed to investigate the impact of neutrophils on anti-PD-1 therapy in lung cancer.MethodsClinical cohort analysis revealed that peripheral neutrophil levels correlated with survival and metastasis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients treated with PD-1 blockade. In this study, we established a subcutaneous Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) mouse model, and depleted neutrophils by anti-Ly6G antibody with or without PD-1 blockade. Dynamic changes of peripheral immune cells (CD4+T cells, CD8+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and myeloid-derived cells) in mice were examined by Flow cytometry during treatment, and cytokine levels were measured using Luminex multiplex assays.ResultsClinically, patients with high neutrophil counts exhibited significantly shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), and were more prone to distant organ metastases. In the mouse model, neutrophil depletion enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumor tissues and suppressed neutrophil-mediated interleukin (IL)-5, IL-6 and IL-17A inflammation, resulting in the inhibition of tumor growth. However, the combination of neutrophil depletion with PD-1 blockade paradoxically restored inflammatory cytokine production and increased myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSCs) infiltration, thereby compromising tumor suppression.ConclusionsOur observation shows that high neutrophil levels in cancer patients are associated with poorer prognosis and increased risk of distant metastasis. Depletion of neutrophils reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment, wherein excessive inflammatory cytokines drive resistance to PD-1 antibody therapy.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Neutrophil depletion alone attenuates tumor-associated inflammation, enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration, and achieves effective tumor suppression. However, in programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-resistant tumors, the combination of neutrophil depletion with PD-1 blockade paradoxically reinstates inflammatory responses and facilitates tumor progression.

What is known and what is new?

• Natural killer cells and T cells are recognized as the core mediators of antitumor immunity, and PD-1 blockade combined with various therapies is generally considered to enhance tumor control.

• Neutrophils may contribute to tumor progression by mediating interleukin (IL)-5, IL-6, and IL-17A-driven inflammatory responses that suppress CD8+ T cell activity and promote tumor development. Importantly, in PD-1-resistant tumors, PD-1 blockade may paradoxically promote the recruitment of tumor-associated neutrophils and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby undermining therapeutic efficacy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In advanced lung cancer, upfront chemotherapy combined with PD-1 blockade may not be appropriate, and patients unresponsive to anti-PD-1 therapy should avoid further exposure, as it may aggravate immunosuppression. Chemotherapy often causes neutropenia, and clinicians usually administer granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor for recovery; however, we recommend using a reduced dose (e.g., 75% of the standard) to control symptoms such as nausea and vomiting without fully restoring neutrophil counts.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide (1,2). The advent and extending use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), have revolutionized the treatment of lung cancer and significantly improved patient survival outcomes (3-5). However, despite the widespread application of immune checkpoint blockade therapy, more than 70% of patients failed to respond to PD-1 inhibition (6,7), and a group of patients may even experience adverse inflammatory responses (8-11). These observations underscore the critical need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying resistance to PD-1 therapy and to identify the immunological basis of such resistance.

In general, T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages have been regarded as the major players in tumor immunity. However, recent advances in single-cell sequencing and multi-omics analysis have revealed that neutrophils also contribute significantly to immune and inflammatory responses within the tumor microenvironment (TME) (12-15). Due to their short lifespan, neutrophils have historically received limited attention in cancer research. However, emerging studies have identified neutrophils as key mediators in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis (16-21). In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), for instance, it has been demonstrated that an immunosuppressive TME is largely shaped by the accumulation of neutrophils in the tumor periphery (22-26). Whether a similar mechanism exists in lung cancer remains unclear.

In our previous clinical cohort study of patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), we found that high peripheral neutrophil levels were associated with poorer outcomes following immune combination therapy (27). Specifically, patients with elevated neutrophils exhibited shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). To investigate the involvement of neutrophils in tumor progression and the underlying mechanisms, we established a subcutaneous Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) model using C57BL/6 mice, depleted neutrophils by anti-Ly6G antibodies, and evaluated the effect of neutrophil depletion alone and in combination with PD-1 blockade on tumor progression and immune response. Unexpectedly, we observed that in the context of a PD-1-resistant LLC model, neutrophil depletion alone significantly inhibited tumor growth by abolishing neutrophil-mediated inflammation. However, this suppressive effect was paradoxically lost when combined with PD-1 antibody treatment. Thus, we propose that PD-1 therapy should be carefully combined with other treatments with a complete understanding of tumor-specific immune contexts. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1323/rc).

Methods

Clinical immunotherapy cohort

The immunotherapy cohort was derived from data published in our previous study (27). This cohort included 144 patients with advanced NSCLC treated at Beijing Chest Hospital, Capital Medical University, who received PD-1 or PD-L1 antibody immunotherapy between January of 2018 and December of 2023. The median OS was 30.0 months, and the median PFS was 10.7 months. Among them, 46 patients achieved a best response of complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), while 98 patients had stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD). Based on the optimal neutrophil cutoff value established in the original study, patients were stratified into two groups: a high-neutrophil group (>5×109/L) and a low-neutrophil group (≤5×109/L).

Mice and cell lines

Male C57BL/6 inbred mice (6-8 weeks old, weighing 18–20 g) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, ambient temperature maintained at 22±1 ℃, and relative humidity of 60–70%. Mice were provided with standard laboratory diet ad libitum. Mouse were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Treatment and measurement orders were alternated to avoid systematic bias. Cage positions were rotated weekly. Animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. AW52405202-2-01) granted by the Animal Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University. The animal facility maintained the institutional animal care license (SYXK [Beijing] 2024-0067), and all experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Lewis lung cancer cells, a lung adenocarcinoma cell line derived from C57BL/6 mice, were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The tumor cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) in CO2 culture chamber (37 ℃, 5% CO2). After the growth with 70–80% confluence, the cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin and split for passage or for transplantation.

Establishment of subcutaneous lung adenocarcinoma xenograft model with neutrophil depletion and anti-PD-1 antibody treatment strategy

Thirty-five 9-week old C57BL/6 mice were randomized into five groups with seven each, which included: (I) blank control group: non-tumor-bearing mice injected with 100 µL PBS via i.p. route; (III) tumor control group: tumor-bearing mice treated with 100 µL PBS via i.p. injection; (III) neutrophil depletion group: tumor-bearing mice treated with 30 µg of anti-Ly6G antibody (Clone 1A8, Absolute Antibody, Oxford, UK) via i.p. injection on days 7, 9 and 11, followed by treatment with 50 µg of anti-Ly6G on days 13, 16 and 18 post tumor cell transplantation; (IV) immunotherapy group: tumor-bearing mice treated with 200 µg of anti-PD-1 antibody (Clone J43, BioXCell, Lebanon, NH, USA) via i.p. injection on days 10, 13, and 16 post tumor cell transplantation; (V) combination group: tumor-bearing mice treated with both anti-PD-1 (as above) and anti-Ly6G antibodies at the same dose as above described.

Tumor induction in mice: twenty-eight mice were inoculated subcutaneously with 0.1 mL of 3×106/mL LLC cells. Seven days post tumor cell inoculation, mice in different groups were treated with anti-Ly6G antibody and/or anti-PD-1 antibody, or BPS as controls accordingly as above described. All mice were euthanized on day 19 post treatment for tumor gravimetric analysis.

Tumor monitoring

Tumor volume measurement: Subcutaneous tumor growth was monitored every other days by measuring the maximum length (a) and minimum width (b) with calipers. Tumor volume (V) was calculated as V = (ab2)/2.

Endpoint criteria: mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation if tumor volume exceeded 2,000 mm3 (per animal welfare guidelines). On day 19, tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed.

Sample collection

Blood collection and processing

Peripheral blood samples were collected on days 9, 12, 15 and 19 by infraorbital sinus under anesthesia using EDTA-coated capillaries and EDTA tubes (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Complete blood count (CBC): an automated hematology analyzer (K-4500, Sysmex, Japan) was used for blood cell account and analysis per the manufacture’s instruction.

Tissue collection

Tumor and spleen: mice were sacrificed according to the approved protocol of Animal Ethics and Welfare, and tissues were collected on day 19 post antibody treatment. Tissues were washed in cold PBS (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA), cells were prepared for flow cytometry analysis, or protein was extracted for Luminex assays.

Preparation of single-cell suspension of from tumor or spleen

Tumors were minced and digested in 500 µL collagenase D (Roche, Shanghai, China; 1 mg/mL in HBSS (BioTopped, Beijing, China) with DNase I (20 U/mL, Sigma, Louis, MO, USA) and 3 mM CaCl2 for 60 min at 37 ℃ with vortexing every 10 min. Digested tissue was filtered (70-µm mesh), and cells were purified via density gradient centrifugation (40%/70% layers, 1,260 ×g, 30 min) using Percoll (BioTopped, Beijing, China). To prepare splenocytes, spleens were collected as above described, homogenized through a 70-µm mesh, treated with RBC lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and washed in PBS buffer.

Examination of immune cells by flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were incubated with anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD8-PE-Cy5, anti-Ly-6G-APC, anti-F4/80-APC, anti-CD11b-PE (all from BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) or corresponding isotype IgG as controls in the dark for 45 min at 4 ℃. After washing with cold PBS twice, stained cells were analyzed with a BD FACSCalibur™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data acquisition was performed using CellQuest Pro software (BD Biosciences), and subsequent analysis was conducted with FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA). Appropriate isotype controls and fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls were included for gating strategy validation.

Luminex multiplex assay

Frozen tumor tissues (5 mg/sample) were homogenized in 0.5 mL PBS containing Complete Mini protease inhibitor cocktail (11836153001, Roche) using a tissue homogenizer for 30 sec. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 ℃ to remove insoluble debris. The supernatant was collected and protein concentration was determined using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The levels of Th1/Th2/Th9/Th17/Th22 cytokines in tumor tissues were measured using the ProcartaPlex™ Mouse Th1/Th2/Th9/Th17/Th22/Treg Cytokine Panel (17-plex, EPX170-26087-901, Thermo Fisher) per the manufacturer’s instruction. The Luminex 200 system (Thermo Fisher) was used for data acquisition, and results were analyzed using xPONENT 4.2 software. The levels of multiple cytokines in the plasma were also determined by ProcartaPlex™ Mouse Th1/Th2/Th9/Th17/Th22/Treg Cytokine Panel as above described.

Statistical analysis

Clinical patient characteristics were compared using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, Graphpad prism 10.0 for Kaplan-Meier method was used to calculate PFS and OS curves, and log-rank test to evaluate differences, and treatment response patterns were visualized using Sankey diagrams. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for multi-group analyses. In cases where the assumptions of normality or equal variance were not met, non-parametric alternatives (such as the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test) were applied. Survival outcomes were evaluated by Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank testing. Data are presented as mean ± SD, with statistical significance threshold set at P<0.05 for all tests.

Blinding

Group allocation was conducted by M.Z. and Sibo Long, who were responsible for designing the experiment and assigning mice into appropriate treatment groups. During the conduct of the experiment, including antibody administration and tumor monitoring, M.Z. and C.W. were aware of the group allocations to ensure correct implementation of the treatment protocols. Outcome assessment, including tumor measurement, flow cytometry, and blood analysis, was performed by H.C. and Shuqin Li, who were blinded to the group identities to reduce measurement bias. Data analysis was conducted independently by Y.W. and Shuqin Li, with Shuqin Li remaining blinded to group allocation until the completion of the primary analysis.

Results

High levels of neutrophil correlate with inferior immunotherapy response in NSCLC patients

Analysis of our team’s prior NSCLC immunotherapy cohort revealed that among 144 PD-1 inhibitor-treated patients, patients with higher peripheral neutrophil counts (>5×109/L) showed significantly worse outcomes than those of patients with lower counts (≤5×109/L). In particular, the OS of patients with a larger number of neutrophils had shorter medians [19.8 vs. 38.0 months; hazard ratio (HR) =2.093, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.312–3.337, P=0.002] and lower PFS (9.1 vs. 20.5 months; HR =2.642, 95% CI: 1.534–4.552, P=0.006) (Figure 2A,2B).

Peripheral blood neutrophil counts correlate with the efficacy of immunotherapy and distant metastasis in NSCLC patients

Therapeutic efficacy was assessed by the best objective response software using RECIST criteria (version 1.1), with complete/partial response (CR/PR) indicating tumor shrinkage or stable/progressive disease (SD/PD) as treatment failure. Among 144 patients, 46 (31.9%) achieved CR/PR while 98 (68.1%) with SD/PD. Although neutrophil counts were numerically lower in responders (4.16±1.53 ×109/L) versus non-responders (4.65±1.97 ×109/L), this difference was not statistically significant Figure 3A) (P=0.13).

Distant metastases (including bone, liver, adrenal, and brain metastases) were closely associated with elevated neutrophil counts. Our data show that patients with metastatic spread had higher neutrophil counts compared to those without metastases (Figure 3B) (P=0.004), suggesting that neutrophils may facilitate metastatic progression in NSCLC. Consistently, a Sankey diagram visually demonstrates the relationships between neutrophil counts, treatment response, and metastatic patterns (Figure 3C), highlighting the potential prognostic value of neutrophil monitoring.

Neutrophil depletion enhances tumor control but attenuates PD-1 blockade efficacy in LLC

To validate the clinical observation that neutrophils affect PD-1 antibody therapy, we inoculated mice with 3×106 (cells/L) LLC cells in 100 µL suspension by s.c. injection and examined the tumor growth at different time points post tumor cell transplant (Figure 4A). As shown in Figure 4B, around 7 days post tumor cell transplant, a palpable tumor mass could be detected, and tumor volume increased progressively over time. Furthermore, at the end of the experiment, the mean tumor volumes of the anti-Ly6G group were significantly decreased compared to those of controls (684.7±263.2, n=7, vs. 1,833.0±614.3 mm3, n=7, P<0.001) (Figure 4B,4C). Similarly, the tumor volume in the anti-Ly6G and anti-PD-1 combined group was markedly decreased compared to controls (942.4±275.0, n=7 vs. 1,833.0±614.3 mm3, n=7, P<0.01). However, the tumor volume in anti-Ly6G-treated mice with or without anti-PD-1 antibody showed no significant difference (684.7±263.2, n=7, vs. 942.4±275.0 mm3, n=7, P>0.05).

Interestingly, the tumor weight in the PD-1 antibody-treated group did not change compared to controls (P>0.05). In contrast, tumor weight in neutrophil-depleted mice significantly decreased relative to controls (P<0.001). Similarly, the tumor weight in the anti-Ly6G and anti-PD-1 combination group significantly decreased compared to controls (P<0.05) (Figure 4D). However, the combination of anti-Ly6G antibody-mediated neutrophil depletion and PD-1 blockade did not provide any additional benefit over neutrophil depletion alone in suppressing tumor growth, suggesting that some factors in PD-1 blockade counteracted the efficacy of neutrophil depletion.

Neutrophil depletion enhances peripheral CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and monocytes in immunotherapy

To assess the impact of neutrophil depletion and PD-1 blockade on peripheral immune cells during treatment, we collected blood samples from the retro-orbital sinus of mice 9, 12, 15, and 19 days post antibody treatment. A portion of each sample was used for white blood count (WBC) using an automated hematology analyzer to quantify immune cell populations at different time points post treatment, while the remaining sample was analyzed by flow cytometry to detect CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. As shown in Figure 5A,5B, the total number of white blood cells and lymphocytes in the blood of each group were similar post treatment. However, the proportion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in peripheral lymphocytes markedly increased in mice 19 days post neutrophil depletion (P<0.05) (Figure 5C-5E), suggesting that neutrophils may modulate the immune microenvironment to suppress T cells and their removal relieves this suppression. Interestingly, despite PD-1 antibody treatment being expected to increase T cell levels, we did not observe a significant rise in T cells in immunotherapy group. In the tumor-bearing control group, neutrophil levels progressively increased with tumor growth, likely due to tumor-induced inflammation (Figure 5F), however the anti-Ly6G antibody effectively depleted neutrophils when used alone, however, its neutrophil-clearing effect was attenuated when combined with PD-1 blockade. Notably, we found that the number of monocytes in blood markedly increased upon neutrophil depletion by anti-Ly6G antibody alone, suggesting that there may be a compensatory response that was triggered by neutrophil loss (Figure 5G). It seems that the number of eosinophils and basophils in the blood of mice were unaffected by the tansplanted LLC cells or by the treatment with anti-Ly6G or anti-PD-1 antibody (Figure 5H,5I).

Proportion of immune cells in tumor-bearing mice treated with anti-Ly6G and anti-PD-1 antibodies

The percentages of immune cells in the peripheral blood of tumor-transplanted mice treated with or without anti-Ly6G and anti-PD-1 antibodies were measured using a CBC analyzer, the clinical laboratory uses distinct methods to measure immune cell counts and their percentages in CBCs. As shown in Figure 6A-6C, injection of mice with anti-Ly6G antibody effectively depleted the neutrophils in vivo. However, despite increasing the dosage from 30 to 50 µg in later stages, the neutrophil depletion efficiency declined compared to that of the initial stage of treatment. In particular, the combined treatment of mice with anti-Ly6G and anti-PD-1 antibodies abolished the neutrophil depletion (Figure 6D). Throughout the treatment, the proportion of peripheral blood CD4+ and CD8+ T cell proportions progressively increased following neutrophil depletion (Figure 6E-6G), while the combination group exhibited the highest CD4+ and CD8+ T cell percentages, exceeding those in anti-Ly6G antibody treated group. These data indicate that depletion of neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice affect the proportion of immune cells, which might have some clinical relevance to immunotherapy.

Neutrophil depletion combined with or without PD-1 blockade affects splenic immune cells

On day 19 post anti-Ly6G antibody treatment, mice were euthanized and splenic cell suspensions were prepared for flow cytometry analysis. Immune cell populations including CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were examined (Figure 7A). Notably, neutrophil-depleted mice exhibited reduced proportions of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the spleen, which contrasted with the increased frequencies observed in peripheral blood. This discrepancy suggests active mobilization of splenic T cells to peripheral circulation and tumor sites for antitumor responses (Figure 7B,7C). In addition, CD11b+ myeloid cells and F4/80+ activated macrophages were assessed (Figure 7D). Despite T cell reduction, neutrophil-depleted mice showed significant expansion of CD11b+ myeloid cells in the spleen (Figure 7E), indicating compensatory myelopoiesis following neutrophil ablation.

Interestingly, neither neutrophil depletion nor PD-1 blockade alone increased activated macrophage populations, whereas combined treatment markedly enhanced the proportion of activated macrophages (Figure 7F), suggesting that neutrophil depletion or PD-1 blockade alone may be insufficient to overcome the immunosuppressive status or provide adequate signals for macrophage activation. Furthermore, the highest elevated splenic immune cell populations was observed in the combination therapy group (Figure 7G), suggesting that the inflammatory immune response was more severely induced by combined antibody treatment.

Neutrophil depletion enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration in the TME

To investigate the impact of neutrophil depletion and anti-PD-1 therapy on tumor-infiltrating immune cells, cell suspensions were prepared from the dissociated tumor tissues of mice 19 days post antibody treatment, and analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 8A,8B neutrophil depletion did not affect CD4+ T cells but promoted CD8+ T cell accumulation in the tumor tissues compared to that of controls (P<0.01). However, neither anti-PD-1 antibody treatment alone nor combined antibody treatment affected CD8+ T cell infiltration, suggesting that anti-PD-1 antibody treatment inhibited CD8+ T cell infiltration. Notably, although anti-Ly6G monotherapy effectively depleted tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), this effect was attenuated when combined with anti-PD-1 (Figure 8C). Furthermore, flow cytometry was used to examine CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD11b+Ly6G+ neutrophils (Figure 8D). The results showed that CD11b+ myeloid cells increased in number across all antibody treatment groups, suggesting that opsonin-mediated phagocytosis by macrophages via Fc receptor may contribute to the antitumor response (Figure 8E). In the anti-Ly6G group, TANs were almost completely eliminated, whereas their numbers partially rebounded when anti-PD-1 antibody was combined with neutrophil depletion (Figure 8F). Stacked bar plots of immune cell composition further demonstrated that tumors from the anti-Ly6G monotherapy group contained the highest proportion of CD8+ T cells, while tumors from the anti-PD-1 monotherapy group exhibited the highest overall immune cell infiltration (Figure 8G).

Neutrophil depletion reduces tumor inflammation, but PD-1 blockade restores pro-tumor cytokine milieu

To investigate the mechanism underlying the diminished anti-tumor effect upon neutrophil depletion combined with PD-1 blockade, we performed Luminex multiplex cytokine assays to examined cytokines in tumor tissues and plasma (Figure 9A,9B). Our results show that neutrophil depletion reduced multiple proinflammatory cytokines in the TME, including IL-5, IL-6, IL-17A (Figure 9C-9E), although this reduction was not statistically different from that of controls. Notably, neutrophil depletion also led to elevated granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) levels in both the TME and peripheral blood, which may reflect a compensatory response to the loss of granulocytes (Figure 9F). However, upon neutrophil depletion combined with anti-PD-1 antibody treatment, some anti-tumor cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-9 and IL-27 (28-30), were increased in the tumor tissue, particularly those linked to CD8+ T cell activity (Figure 9G-9I). Furthermore, the systemic cytokine levels in peripheral blood were markedly increased following combined neutrophil depletion and PD-1 blockade.

Based on our earlier flow cytometry data, we hypothesize that PD-1 blockade may compensate for neutrophil depletion by recruiting or reactivating a subset of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which in turn secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby restoring the tumor-promoting inflammatory milieu. In tumors resistant to PD-1 inhibition, the pro-tumorigenic effects of TANs-driven inflammation may outweigh the limited anti-tumor benefits conferred by PD-1 blockage of activated T cells and their cytotoxic cytokines. Based on these results, we constructed a schematic diagram to delineate the potential regulatory mechanism underlying neutrophil depletion with or without PD-1 blockage (Figure 9J).

Taken together, we found that that high neutrophil levels in cancer patients are associated with poorer prognosis and increased distant metastasis. Neutrophil depletion inhibited tumor progression via promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration and reducing IL-5, IL-6 and IL-17A contents in tumor tissues. Furthermore, anti-tumor cytokines including IFN-γ, IL-9 and IL-27 induced by PD-1 blockade may be insufficient to counteract the effect of pro-tumor inflammatory cytokines and immunosuppressive cells recruited as a result of therapy, highlighting the complex and context-dependent role of neutrophils in modulating immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Discussion

Although PD-1 blockade has improved the OS of patients with lung cancer, more than 70% of patients still fail to benefit from this therapy (6,7,31-35). The underlying mechanisms of inflammatory responses in some patients remain elusive. It is intriguing to enhance antitumor efficacy of PD-1 blockade by combining anti-PD-1 antibody with other therapeutic agents or regimens. Recent studies focusing on neutrophils in cancer patients have shown that increased intratumoral neutrophil infiltration is associated with reduced clinical benefit from immunotherapy in NSCLC, renal cell carcinoma, and melanoma (12,36-38). In this study, first we found that patients with high neutrophil counts exhibited significantly shorter PFS and OS, and were more prone to distant organ metastases. Second, we successfully established mouse model of tumor transplantation with anti-Ly6G-mediated neutrophil depletion, and found that neutrophil depletion in mouse enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumor tissues and suppresses neutrophil-mediated IL-5, IL-6 and IL-17A inflammation, resulting in the inhibition of tumor growth. Third, combination of neutrophil depletion with PD-1 blockade restored inflammatory cytokine production and increased MDSCs infiltration, thereby compromising tumor suppression. These data suggest that the roles of neutrophils in tumor progression and modulating immunotherapeutic efficacy are complex and context-dependent.

In our clinical observations, NSCLC patients with high neutrophil levels during anti-PD-1 antibody treatment exhibited shorter OS and PFS, along with a higher likelihood of distant organ metastasis. The pro-metastatic role of neutrophils in promoting distant organ dissemination in cancer patients has been confirmed by multiple studies (18-21). LLC is a cell line previously reported to be poorly responsive to PD-1 antibody therapy (39). Our data show that neutrophil depletion alone exhibited superior tumor suppression compared to PD-1 blockade, suggesting that the loss of neutrophil-mediated inflammation within the TME may primarily account for the observed tumor regression. Notably, tumor tissues from the neutrophil-depleted group exhibited a marked reduction in various cytokines, with IL-5, IL-6, and IL-17A showing the most significant decreases. It is well established that IL-6, is a key mediator of inflammatory responses, promotes both tumorigenesis and tumor-associated inflammation (40-43), IL-5 involved in tumor-related inflammation, with elevated IL-5 levels prior to therapy being associated with poorer prognosis in cancer patients (44,45), and IL-17, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is particularly recognized for its tumor-promoting role in colorectal cancer (46,47). In this study, depletion of neutrophils led to the most prominent reductions in these three pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that neutrophils modulated cytokine production. In contrast, tumors from mice treated with PD-1 antibody showed upregulation of IFN-γ, IL-9 and IL-27. It is widely established that IFN-γactivates multiple immune cell populations, including T cells, NK cells, B cells, and macrophages, promoting their differentiation into tumoricidal effectors (48). IFN-γinduces tumor cell apoptosis through the JAK-STAT1-caspase signaling cascade (49,50). However, clinical translation has proven challenging, with IFN-γ monotherapy demonstrating limited therapeutic efficacy in cancer patients (51). Furthermore, IFN-γ-mediated upregulation of PD-L1 represents a resistance mechanism to PD-1 checkpoint blockade therapy (52-54). Both of IL-9 and IL-27 are closely associated with CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against tumors (28-30). It is possible that PD-1 blockade promotes the production of these cytokines, thereby enhancing and activating the antitumor functions of CD8+ T cells. We speculate that a delicate cytokine equilibrium exists in the TME. Although PD-1 blockade enhances IFN-γ, IL-9, and IL-27 dependent tumoricidal activity, this antitumor response is insufficient to counterbalance the concomitant induction of other pro-inflammatory factors, thereby contributing to the observed resistance to combination PD-1 blockade therapy.

Our data show that neutrophil depletion led to a significant increase in CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood and TME, and anti-Ly6G treatment effectively eliminated TANs. Interestingly, after PD-1 antibody treatment, partial restoration of neutrophil levels was observed in both peripheral blood and the TME. This may be attributed to PD-1 blockade on other immune cells leading to the accumulation of MDSCs, which suppress T cell responses and foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment (55). We propose that the observed increase in CD8+ T cells following neutrophil depletion may result from the depleted of neutrophil-derived immunosuppressive factors, thereby relieving suppression and enhancing T cell activity. It is noteworthy that macrophage populations markedly increased in the spleens of mice treated with the combination of anti-Ly6G and PD-1 antibody, possibly reflecting the infiltration of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly M2-type TAMs known to support tumor progression. Alternatively, neutrophil depletion may have altered the oxygenation and pH within the TME, creating conditions that counteract the effects of PD-1 blockade. These findings highlight that PD-1 inhibitors may not be universally effective when combined with all therapeutic strategies.

Neutrophils contain abundant cytoplasmic granules and secrete various matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), including MMP-9, which degrades extracellular matrix components, disrupts tissue barriers, and promotes tumor angiogenesis, thereby facilitating tumor progression and metastasis (56). Neutrophils can also activate NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing DNA damage and secreting neutrophil elastase (NE), all of which contribute to tumor development (57-61). Furthermore, neutrophil-derived arginase-1 has been shown to suppress T cell activity (62,63). The tumor-suppressive effects observed following neutrophil depletion in our model may be attributable to the removal of these pro-tumorigenic mechanisms.

Previous studies have explored the therapeutic effects of neutrophil depletion in combination with immune checkpoint blockade in tumor-bearing mice. For example, in a HCC mouse model, combined treatment with anti-Ly6G and immune checkpoint inhibitors significantly enhanced tumor suppression (64). Consistently, our data show that neutrophil depletion led to increased CD8+ T cell infiltration. However, in contrast to our results, the antitumor effect in their model was augmented by PD-1 blockade, whereas in our study, the combination of neutrophil depletion and PD-1 antibody failed to suppress tumor growth. This discrepancy may be attributed to fundamental differences between the tumor models: HCC versus lung adenocarcinoma. The LLC model used in our study is a spontaneously derived murine lung carcinoma model characterized by a relatively low mutational burden and limited neoantigen presentation, making it poorly responsive to PD-1 blockade and less capable of initiating a de novo T cell response (65). In tumors that are intrinsically sensitive to immune checkpoint blockade, depletion of neutrophil-mediated inflammation may synergize with checkpoint inhibition to enhance antitumor effects. However, in PD-1-resistant tumors, such as LLC, the inflammatory milieu appears to be a key determinant of tumor progression, and its modulation can have complex and context-dependent outcomes. Furthermore, hypoxia is more pronounced in HCC than that in lung cancer, and it has been shown that hypoxic conditions upregulate PD-1 expression on T cells, thereby rendering them more responsive to PD-1 blockade (66).

Of note, there are several limitations to our study. First, we did not employ humanized mouse models, which limits the translational relevance of our findings with respect to human immune cell dynamics. Second, we did not assess the role of NK cells, which may also contribute to antitumor immunity. Third, we used a subcutaneous tumor implantation model, which does not fully recapitulate the spontaneous development and microenvironmental characteristics of primary lung cancer. Future studies should employ genetically engineered or spontaneous lung cancer models to better mimic clinical scenarios. Moreover, if the focus is on mechanistically dissecting the interaction between neutrophils and PD-1 antibody treatment without considering clinical relevance, PD-1 sensitive tumor cell lines may be more appropriate. In addition, it would also be valuable to explore experimental approaches that stimulate neutrophil expansion, rather than depletion alone, in order to more comprehensively delineate the dual roles of neutrophils in tumor initiation and progression.

From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of stratifying patients based on their responsiveness to PD-1 blockade. In patients who are non-responsive to PD-1 blockade, the addition of such agents may not only be ineffective but could exacerbate immunosuppressive mechanisms, including increased infiltration of MDSCs and inflammatory cytokines. In chemotherapy-treated patients, neutropenia is a common side effect, and clinical guidelines often recommend administration of GM-CSF to restore neutrophil counts. However, excessive restoration may be counterproductive; a reduced dosage (e.g., 75% of the standard recommended dose) may suffice to alleviate symptoms such as nausea and vomiting while avoiding excessive neutrophil-mediated inflammation. In patients unresponsive to PD-1 therapy, targeting inflammatory pathways such as the use of IL-6 inhibitors may be a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate tumor-promoting inflammation and improve clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In PD-1-resistant tumors, neutrophil depletion alone may offer a therapeutic benefit by reducing immunosuppressive cytokine production and attenuating tumor-associated inflammation. Neutrophils modulate the expression of immunosuppressive cytokines that can inhibit CD8+ T cell proliferation, thus neutrophil depletion may relieve this inhibition and restore T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. However, combining neutrophil depletion with PD-1 blockade may paradoxically promote immune suppression through increased recruitment of MDSCs. These findings suggest that PD-1 inhibitors should be used with caution in non-responsive patients, and that modulation of neutrophil activity and inhibiting inflammatory signaling may represent promising alternative strategies.
