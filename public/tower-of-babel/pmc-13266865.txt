Clinical implementation of amino acids infusion for perioperative renal protection
Authors: Stefano Fresilli, Rosario Losiggio, Giovanni Landoni
Published: 2026

We thank Van den Eynde for his thoughtful Editorial Commentary (1) on the beneficial effects of amino acids infusion to prevent acute kidney injury (AKI) in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) undergoing cardiac surgery (2).

We agree that recruitment of renal functional reserve represents only one component of a broader and more complex spectrum of renal responses induced by amino acids infusion. Beyond changes in glomerular filtration, amino acids have been shown to increase total renal blood flow and modulate intrarenal perfusion, with relevant effects on renal oxygenation (3). Importantly, improvements in cortical and medullary oxygen delivery were demonstrated experimentally, suggesting a dissociation between hyperfiltration and tissue oxygenation (4). These mechanisms may be particularly relevant in conditions of impaired autoregulation and perioperative hemodynamic instability, where increases in glomerular filtration alone may not translate into effective renal protection (5). In addition, amino acids infusion influences tubular workload and renal metabolic demand, potentially improving the balance between oxygen supply and consumption in vulnerable nephron segments (3).

The key finding from the CKD subgroup analysis of the PROTECTION trial is that amino acids infusion reduced AKI in patients with CKD with similar effect in magnitude and direction to that observed in the overall trial population (2). At first glance, these findings may appear to generate a mechanistic paradox. If amino acids-mediated renoprotection were driven predominantly by recruitment of renal functional reserve, and if renal functional reserve is progressively attenuated in CKD, a diminishing treatment effect with declining kidney function would be expected (6). When considered in light of the multiple renal hemodynamic and metabolic mechanisms elicited by amino acids infusion, the preservation of benefit observed in PROTECTION trial (7) no longer appears paradoxical. Rather, it suggests that patients with CKD may derive proportionally greater benefit from those protective pathways that remain operative despite reduced nephron number and attenuated renal functional reserve (3).

We also agree with the limitations appropriately outlined in the Editorial Commentary, many of which are inherent to perioperative AKI research and deserve careful consideration (1).

Urine output-based criteria are particularly susceptible to confounding in the perioperative setting, with special attention to cardiac surgical patients. In this context, fluid administration, diuretic use, and hemodynamic fluctuations are common, and bladder catheters are often removed soon after surgery, which prevents accurate monitoring of oliguria. Of note, the PROTECTION trial (6) included a sensitivity analysis on the primary outcome considering both the creatinine and urine output components of the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, which confirmed the beneficial renal effects of amino acids infusion and the negligible role of urinary output in defining AKI in this setting.

Even if the absence of tubular injury biomarkers such as NGAL, KIM-1, or TIMP-2·IGFBP7 limits mechanistic inference regarding tubular stress or injury, this aspect can be easily addressed by any center worldwide collecting data before and after the routine implementation of this strategy in clinical practice. However, the fact that this did not happen during the last 20 months further underlines the uncommon use of these biomarkers in clinical practice.

We also agree that hyperfiltration per se is not synonymous with renoprotection and may be maladaptive in CKD (6). Nevertheless, as discussed above, the renoprotective effects of amino acids infusion likely extend beyond glomerular filtration alone and include improvements in renal perfusion, intrarenal oxygenation, and metabolic efficiency (5).

Finally, the consistency of effect across estimated glomerular filtration rate strata, together with concordant findings in sensitivity analyses, supports the robustness of the observed association and strengthens confidence in the overall conclusion (2).

We agree with the author that the clinical implications of these findings are substantial, particularly in a population as vulnerable as patients with CKD undergoing cardiac surgery (1). In the CKD subgroup analysis, the relative reduction in AKI risk was comparable to that observed in the overall PROTECTION population, whereas the higher baseline risk of AKI in CKD translated into a greater absolute benefit and a more favorable number needed to treat (14 versus 21 in the overall cohort) (2,7).

This point is not merely statistical. AKI during hospitalization is consistently associated with worse long-term outcomes, including progressive decline in renal function, dialysis dependence, and increased mortality (8). Therefore, achieving a meaningful absolute reduction in perioperative AKI in CKD patients, who are the most likely to experience severe events, has clear clinical relevance and strengthens the case for implementation.

From a pragmatic standpoint, intravenous amino acids infusion is a simple and cheap (9) intervention that can be integrated into routine perioperative workflows, with broad availability and an overall favorable safety profile in the setting evaluated in the PROTECTION study (7). Notably, the treatment duration plays a key role. A secondary analysis of the PROTECTION trial showed that short-term (≤48 hours) infusion is at least equivalent and possibly superior to prolonged infusion (49–72 hours) (10). Indeed, authors demonstrated that the beneficial effects of amino acids on AKI were almost identical between patients who received less than 48 hours of infusion and those treated for 49 to 72 hours. In contrast, the effect of amino acids on intensive care unit mortality significantly differed between the two groups (interaction P=0.04) favoring the shorter duration of infusion (10).

Several important avenues for future investigation arise from this work (1).

As amino acids infusion progressively enters clinical routine, this will also provide a valuable opportunity to further explore its underlying mechanisms to better clarify which pathways are most relevant to amino acids–mediated renoprotection.

Finally, beyond the surgical setting, further investigations should focus on patients with ongoing AKI and on all those admitted to the intensive care unit, with special attention to doses and duration of amino acids infusion.
