Evaluating LeishPepThera (PepA) as an immuno-therapeutic for Leishmania major infection
Authors: Shweta Khandibharad, Shailza Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is regulated by cytokine reciprocity, the dynamic balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines that determines disease outcomes. The Interleukin-12 (IL-12) and Interleukin-10 (IL-10) axis governs the type 1 and type 2 T helper cell (Th1/Th2) immune response, where dysregulation favors parasite persistence. Src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase (SHP-1) modulates this axis to promote Leishmania survival. Using an artificial intelligence-driven approach, therapeutic peptides targeting SHP-1 were designed and evaluated in Leishmania major (L. major) infection model. Among these, Peptide A (PepA) exhibited potent SHP-1 inhibition, enhanced nuclear factor of activated T cells 5 (NFAT5) translocation, and increased nitric oxide (NO) production through inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS/Nos2) upregulation. PepA also induced M1 (classically activated) macrophages, restored IL-12, suppressed IL-10 expression, and significantly reduced parasite load. These findings identify PepA (“LeishPepThera”) as a promising immunotherapeutic candidate that rebalances host immune signaling to promote parasite clearance in CL.Subject areas: Cell biology, Immunology

Introduction

The protozoan parasite genus Leishmania has more than 15 species that cause leishmaniasis, a complicated disease with both cutaneous and visceral manifestations. Significant differences exist in these species sensitivity to both conventional medications, like miltefosine and pentavalent antimonials, and those undergoing clinical trials, like paromomycin.1 Although new drug formulations such as miltefosine, amphotericin B, and its lipid formulations have demonstrated remarkable success in treating leishmaniasis, their utility is limited by their high cost and therapeutic complications. Patients treated with pentavalent antimonials have exhibited a reduction in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations, and resistance to these drugs has emerged in the parasite. Cases of leishmaniasis relapse have been reported following treatment with amphotericin B, which also is an expensive treatment regimen. Treatment with miltefosine commonly results in gastrointestinal side effects, and the drug is known to have teratogenic potential.2 Despite the tremendous advancements in leishmanial research over the past few decades, new therapeutic targets and discovery and characterization of new drugs remain unsatisfactory. The development of effective antileishmanial therapeutics is urgently needed to lower the disease burden of this crippling endemic disease, given the limited therapeutic alternatives and lack of prophylactic or preventive possibilities.3 The most cost-effective approach to preventing infectious diseases is vaccination. Vaccines to prevent leishmaniasis have not yet been discovered, though. Patients with leishmaniasis and HIV co-infection have been offered combination therapy. There is now no vaccine or medication that may effectively guarantee immunity against leishmaniasis and give long-term protection.4

Immunotherapy for complex and acute cutaneous leishmaniasis caused by L. major comprises small molecules and biomolecules with immuno-modulatory activity to skew parasite-eliminating immune response.5 Immunotherapy involves cytokines, synthekines, monoclonal antibodies, cellular therapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, enzyme inhibitors, Toll-like Receptor agonist, and therapeutic vaccines.5,6 First-, second-, and third-generation vaccines like Leishvaccine, Leishmune, and ChAd63-KH, which focus on elicitation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, are also under investigation.7 A major obstacle to the development and use of cytokine immunotherapy as a treatment and vaccine is the difficulty of combining innate and adaptive immunity, which is rendered difficult by limited comprehension of the immune system.2 Recent report highlights the importance of soluble factors as promising noninvasive markers for treatment of leishmaniasis, which may have tailored interventions with immunomodulatory outcomes. This may increase efficacy of treatment and address prevalent challenges in leishmaniasis.8

Peptides are soluble factors, with over 100 approved drugs; they are considered a growing market.9 Peptides as therapy include structural modification, peptide synthesis, drug design, peptide drug discovery, and the relationship between these advancements and peptide bioactivities. More than 170 peptides currently have an active clinical development, in addition to several preclinical investigations. Therapeutic peptides are less immunogenic and more affordable to produce than biologics. Peptides usually exhibit greater potency and selectivity for their targets than small compounds.10 Tachyplesin is an example of a peptide that had shown anti-leishmanial activity that specifically targeted parasite membrane. It was also used as a cargo-carrier for delivery of recombinant DNA in Leishmania donovani infection.11 rLinKAP devised from truncated kinetoplast-associated protein was used as a therapeutic vaccine that reported reduction of splenic parasite burden by 75% in treated mice with Leishmania infantum infection.12 By using computational approaches, GH18-cpLeish peptide was designed and evaluated for eliciting immune response, which mediates anti-leishmanial effect as a potential second-generation or third-generation vaccine.13

Nuclear factor of activated T cells 5 (NFAT5) is a transcription factor that is induced in hypertonic environment and reportedly in Leishmania infection. NFAT5 upregulates expressions of IL-12 and Nos2 and decreases IL-10 to promote parasite elimination.14,15 Amino acid residues of SHP-1, T353, C455, S456, A457, G458, I459, G460, and R461, form a unique catalytic motif that has been reported to inhibit NFAT5 translocation to nucleus by dephosphorylating its Y143 residue on nuclear localization signal.16 SHP-1 is activated and regulated by parasite by cleaving SHP-1 at the S594 position near the C-terminal domain.17,18 Thus, SHP-1 activation by parasite Glycoprotein 63 to inhibit NFAT5 translocation in the nucleus to prevent parasite eliminating response serves a crucial interplay axis in host-parasite interaction. Hence, we designed small peptides as immuno-modulatory biomaterial to target the SHP-1 catalytic motif. Src Homology 2 domain-containing Phosphatases-2 (SHP-2), like SHP-1, is a family member of Src Homology 2 domain-containing phosphatase, which has also been reported as a critical target for parasite proliferation and survival.19 Hence, the SHP family of proteins are potential targets of therapeutic intervention in leishmaniasis.

In our previous work, we had reported SHP-1 as potential therapeutic target for intervention, which regulates IL-10 and IL-12 reciprocity mediated by NFAT5.20 We validated that the role of NFAT5 and SHP-1 in regulating host cell plasticity is by regulating IL-10 and IL-12 reciprocity using single-cell ATAC sequencing. We identified prominent macrophage population called “sleepy macrophages,” which possess a phenotype whereby they may switch to either M1 or M2 phenotypes upon parasite infection by modulating NFAT5.21 Hence, considering SHP-1 as host regulatory factor, we further performed sequence, structure, and evolutionary analysis of SHP-1 to devise synthetic immuno-modulatory peptides using AI and machine learning algorithms. The peptide library was screened against SHP-1 using knowledge-based docking and blind docking. The chosen peptides were evaluated for their evolutionary conservedness using various parameters involving evaluation of position-specific scoring matrix.22 The top 3 peptides were simulated using molecular dynamics simulation using isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble and relaxed fit model. The environment provided for simulation mimicked the cutaneous lesion environment with hypertonicity. The peptides were also evaluated for their stability with cholesterol-anchored Dimyristoylphosphatidylcholine (DMPC) membrane, which mimics host cell membrane.23 The interaction analysis of peptides with SHP-1 through molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation studies gave us sufficient insights to test the anti-leishmanial activity of PepA, PepB, and PepC under in vitro and in vivo settings. Our findings emphasize the immuno-modulatory role of PepA in enhancing IL-12 production and facilitating parasite elimination. This positions PepA as a promising candidate for incorporation into synthetic circuit tools, potentially enabling the precise spatiotemporal regulation of host responses to effectively combat parasite infections.

Results

Purification and characterization of peptides

Peptides were designed and optimized using a deterministic search approach that took into consideration their physiochemical characteristics. This approach is known as the DEE approach. The primary objective of DEE was to generate a peptide library by thoroughly investigating the motif sequence of SHP-1 and to identify and eliminate the peptide sequences from the created library that fail to meet the global minimal energy conformation. A library of 90 peptides was screened and PepA, PepB, and PepC were reported as top hits. PepA, PepB, and PepC showed selected reaction monitoring (SRM)/multiple reaction monitoring compatibility of “A” quality, which suggested these peptides had moderate hydrophobicity making it compatible for synthesis and purification without any issues.22 Hence, PepA, PepB, and PepC were synthesized and their purity and molecular weight (MW) were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS).

Peptide A is highly pure (99.947%) based on the HPLC profile. The sharp peak at 12.966 min confirms successful peptide separation. Minor peaks (<0.5%) indicate minimal impurities, showing good synthesis and purification (Figure S1 and Table S1). The presence of multiple charge states, 840[M+2H]2+, 560.4[M+3H]3+, and 420.5[M+4H]4+, were observed in the mass spectrometry (MS) spectrum, which is characteristic of peptides analyzed by ESI-MS (Figure S2 and Table S2). The observed MW of PepA was 1,678.2 Da, the calculated MW was 1,678.0 Da, and the estimated monoisotopic mass was ∼1,677.0–1,677.5 Da. PepB was observed to be highly pure (99.292%), with only 0.708% minor impurities. The major peak was sharp at 13.354 min, which dominated the chromatogram, confirming effective peptide purification (Figure S3 and Table S3). The mass spectrum confirms the identity of PepB, with an observed MW (1,678.2 Da) closely matching the theoretical MW (1,677.90 Da). Different charge states, 1,679.1 [M + H]+, 840.0 [M+2H]2+, 560.4 [M+3H]3+, and 420.5[M+4H]4+, are present, confirming the presence of a well-ionized peptide (Figure S4 and Table S4). PepC was observed to be highly pure, with a single dominant peak at 11.390 min (98.05% purity). Minimal impurities (∼1.95%) confirm a successful purification process (Figure S5 and Table S5). The observed MW of PepC was 1,682.7 Da, closely matching the theoretical MW 1,682.97 with the difference of only 0.27 Da, which confirms its identity (Figure S6 and Table S6).

Cytotoxicity studies of peptides on parasite and peritoneal macrophages

For the parasite load assay, DAPI staining is used, and parasite load can be quantified using laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM).24 We observed that a total of 6 intracellular parasites were present in peritoneal macrophages (PMs) infected with L. major promastigotes as the MOI used was 1:10 ratio (Figure 1A). In PepA-, PepB-, and PepC-treated PMs, we observed a significant reduction in the parasite load when treated at 125 μg/mL of PepA, 125 μg/mL of PepB, and 250 μg/mL of PepC. For PepA, we observed that, at 250 μg/mL and 500 μg/mL concentration, PMs survival and morphology were altered, suggesting that at 125 μg/mL the peptide not only significantly reduces parasite burden but also does not hamper PMs survival and morphology. For PepB, at 250 μg/mL concentration, we observed that the cell number of PMs was reduced, suggesting a potential effect on its survival. At 500 μg/mL, the PMs showed disrupted morphology, suggesting that PepB may affect PMs growth by promoting cell disruption. For PepC, we observed a reduced parasite burden at 250 μg/mL concentration and at 500 μg/mL we did not observe any significant decline in parasite load, although the number of PMs may appear reduced. Miltefosine showed significant parasite load reduction in the infected PMs. We observed parasite clearance from macrophage at 5 μM concentration of miltefosine, nonetheless we observed reduction in cell number of PMs. At 10 μM, 15 μM, and 20 μM we observed changes in macrophage cell number as well as morphology (Figures 1B and 1C).

Nitric oxide (NO) production serves as an indicator of the parasite-eliminating effect; therefore, we analyzed nitrite levels in the cell culture media of peptide-treated and miltefosine-treated samples. For PepA and PepB, media were collected from cells treated with 125 μg/mL; for PepC, media were collected from cells treated with 250 μg/mL, and for miltefosine, media were collected from cells treated with a concentration of 5 μM. PMs isolated from IL12KO and IL10KO mice were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The culture media from knockout PMs samples were used as controls. Results suggest that, among all peptide-treated samples, PepA treatment induced higher nitrite levels (21 μM) as compared with PepB (10 μM) and PepC (12 μM). Miltefosine-treated sample when compared with control sample showed nitrite production (14 μM), IL12KO showed the lowest nitrite levels at 2 μM, and IL10KO the highest at 35 μM (Figure 1D).

To check if the chosen treatment concentration of PepA, PepB, PepC, and miltefosine has any effect on macrophages, we performed macrophage toxicity assay by analyzing propidium iodide (PI) and Annexin V-positive apoptotic PMs. We observed that, as compared with control, the percentage population of PI and Annexin V double-positive cells was lower, suggesting that these concentrations may not be toxic to the PMs (Figures 1E1–1E9 and 1F). However, we observed that the population of PMs was highly positive for PI. Therefore, we measured the selectivity index of peptides. The selectivity index observed for PepA and PepB was greater than 1 and that of PepC was less than 1 (Figure 1G). These results suggested that PepA and PepB may have better inhibitory effect on parasites than macrophages.

Effect of peptides on physical parameters and organs of mice

The schematic representation provided in Figure 2A shows the experimentation flow of peptide treatments in experimental mice groups of PepA, PepB, and PepC. The evaluation of physical parameters was performed before and after administration of peptides in the mice; these parameters included estimation of lesion size and weight of the mice. Weight of the superficial parotid lymph node (LN) was recorded upon terminating the mice; here uninfected mice superficial parotid LN was taken as control. Superficial parotid LNs of infected IL10KO and IL12KO were taken as cytokine controls. When the ear pinna lesion reached 3 mm in size, the peptides were then administered subcutaneously in the lesion. The size of the lesion for all experimental mice groups were noted using a digital Vernier caliper. We observed that size of lesion of mice treated with PepA had approximately reduced to 1 mm. The size of infected ear pinna of IL10KO was observed to be the smallest among all experimental mice groups. IL12KO mice showed the largest lesion size of more than 4 mm. The lesion sizes of infected mice and PepC-treated mice were almost the same, suggesting that PepC might not have a significant impact on reducing lesion of mice. Lesion size of PepB-treated mice was smaller than infected and PepC-treated mice but it was still greater than 3 mm (Figures 2B and 2C). Hence, we infer that PepA treatment may have a role in reducing the lesion size of L. major-infected mice. Some drugs/biomolecules can impact the weight of mice.25 Hence, we compared the weight of mice to see if the peptide treatment is affecting mice physiology. We observed that the weights of all mice were similar, in the range of 19-20g, with no significant changes, suggesting that the peptides might not affect the weight of mice despite their role in regulation of infection (Figure 2D). Draining LN enlargement is associated with L. major infection.26,27 We observed that the weight of the LNs isolated from the infection group and the IL12KO group was highest with no significant difference in the weight of LNs from PepA, PepB, PepC, and IL10KO groups as compared with control group of mice (Figure 2E). This may be because the peptides were injected subcutaneously at the site of lesion, which could have affected the parasite burden more locally at the cellular level. Spleen and liver enlargement are often associated with the visceral form of leishmaniasis, and few reports highlight spleen enlargement has been observed in cutaneous leishmaniasis.28 Interestingly, recent reports highlight that L. major infection may have far-reaching effects than the localized lesion and promote splenomegaly in infected mice. Its presence in these visceral organs likely stems from dissemination from the initial infection site, with specific genetic determinants influencing how effectively each organ can control parasite burden and the resulting pathology. L. major may control splenomegaly and hepatomegaly in the infected mice by expression of Lmr13, Lmr14, and Lmr15 gene loci.29,30 We checked if spleen and liver enlargement occurs in our CL models. We observed spleen enlargement in infection, PepB, PepC, and IL12KO mice. Spleens from mice of PepA-treated, miltefosine-treated, and IL10KO groups were observed to appear smaller in size (Figure 2F). Significant difference in liver size of mice groups was not observed (Figure 2G).

Cytokine profiling of knockout mice

The cytokine profiling of L. major-infected IL12KO and IL10KO was performed to identify differential expression cytokine profile of pro-inflammatory cytokines and anti-inflammatory cytokines as compared with infected but untreated Balb/c mice. The qRT-PCR data show that tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-ɑ) and interferon gamma (IFN-γ) were upregulated in infected IL10KO mice by 0.02-fold and 26.21-fold, respectively. In IL12KO mice, both cytokines were downregulated, where TNF-ɑ showed 0.011-fold change and IFN-γ showed 4.37-fold change (Figures 3A and 3B). Both cytokines were expressed in IL12KO, although their levels were significantly low as compared with the expression in the IL10KO sample. The anti-inflammatory cytokines transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and IL-4 were upregulated in IL12KO mice showing 1.621- and 96.62-fold change. However, in IL10KO both cytokines were significantly downregulated as compared with cytokine levels in IL12KO. TGF-β expression had changed 0.13-fold, and IL-4 expression changed by 1.32-fold (Figures 3C and 3D). Nos2 expression was changed by 0.75-fold in the IL12KO sample and 8.12-fold in the IL10KO sample (Figure 3E). These results suggest the strong influence that IL-12 and IL-10 have on other pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines. IL-12 absence may have influenced downregulation of TNF-ɑ and IFN-γ, and IL-10 absence may have influenced downregulation of TGF-β and IL-4. The decreased expression of IL-10 and IL-12 may have influenced overexpression of their counter anti-/pro-inflammatory cytokines in both knockouts, which may have regulated the expression of Nos2. The levels of Nos2 in both knockouts suggest that, in IL12KO; parasite survival may be more efficient than in IL10KO.

Expression analysis of NFAT5 and SHP-1

The LSCM for NFAT5 and SHP-1 localized expression from PMs of experimental mice treatment groups was performed. Analysis showed that PepA-treated mice showed high NFAT5 localized expression at the nucleus of PMs, whereas SHP-1 localized expression is low in these cells. In PepB-treated mice groups, NFAT5 localization was in the nucleus but SHP-1 expression was observed to be present in the nucleus and cytoplasm, which was relatively higher than NFAT5. In PepC-treated mice, localized expression of NFAT5 was observed to be lower in nucleus as compared with PepA and PepB. The expression of NFAT5 was also observed in the cytoplasm of the PepC PMs. Expression of SHP-1 on the other hand was closer to the nuclear compartment. The quantitative data of the NFAT5 and SHP-1 localization show that NFAT5 expression was higher in PMs of PepA-treated group and lowest in the infected IL12KO. SHP-1 expression was highest in IL12KO mice and lowest in PMs of IL10KO and PepA groups. PMs from miltefosine-treated mice showed higher expression of NFAT5 as compared with SHP-1. Although comparative grouped analysis showed that SHP-1 level changes were non-significant, these results suggest that PepA treatment may have inhibited SHP-1, which alternatively might have promoted NFAT5 localization to nucleus of the PMs (Figures 4A–4C). We also extracted protein from the PMs of all experimental mice groups and performed western blot analysis for SHP-1 and P-564-SHP-1. Our results suggest that, in PMs of PepA-treated mice, SHP-1 and P-564-SHP-1 were downregulated. In IL10KO PMs sample we observed the similar results, where SHP-1 and P-SHP-1 levels were lower. Conversely, SHP-1 and P-SHP-1 expression levels were higher in the PMs isolated from the IL12KO group as well as the miltefosine-treated group. In infected mice PMs sample, PepB-treated PMs sample, and PepC-treated PMs sample we observed expression of both SHP-1 and P-564-SHP-1 were significantly upregulated. Therefore, from our LCSM and western blot data we observed that efficacy of PepA to inhibit SHP-1 is better than PepB and PepC (Figures 4D and 4E). To check if PepA binds to SHP-1, we performed streptavidin pull-down assay using control PMs (C) and PMs infected with L. major promastigotes for 6 h. The synthesis and purity of Biotinylated PepA was confirmed using HPLC and ESI-MS (Data S9 and S10). Biotinylated PepA was the bait and SHP-1 was the prey protein. We observed that, in L. major-infected sample, binding of PepA-SHP-1 was more prominent as compared with control (Figure 4F).

Identification of macrophage population in lesion of mice treated with peptides

To identify the macrophage population present in the lesion of all experimental mice groups, we performed fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using macrophage markers. Unstained cells from the infection mice group were used for basic gating strategy. According to the forward scatter and side scatter (SSC), 95.7% cells were gated followed by removal of larger granulated and small debris. Single cells were gated as per SSC-A and SSC-W. The selected population was 96%, and those were further gated to observe single stain positive controls (Figure 5A). We observed that macrophages in the ear lesion dominantly expressed CD45 macrophage marker than F4/80 and CD11b. The presence of CD45 signifies the infiltration of dermal macrophages.31 In all mice samples, mean CD45 population was 42% (Figure 5B1). F4/80 and CD11b showed low expression, which was around 1% and 0.4%, respectively, in the lesion cell population (Figures 5B2 and 5B3). From the CD45-positive control cells we checked the expression of CD80 and CD163 single positive cells (Figures 5B4 and 5B5). In infected mice we observed that the CD80 population constituted 0.6% and CD163 constituted 19% (Figures 5C1 and 5C2). We observed that CD80-positive macrophages comprised 12% of the population in the lesion of PepA-treated mice, whereas CD163-positive cells were lower, comprising 4% of the population (Figure 5C3). In PepB-treated lesion CD80-positive cells constituted 3.1% of the population and CD163-positive cells constituted 12% of the population (Figure 5C4). In Peptide C-treated lesion CD80-positive cells constituted 9.1% of the population and CD163-positive cells constituted 12% of the population (Figure 5C5). In IL12KO mice lesion CD80-positive cells constituted 5.7% of the population and CD163-positive cells constituted 40% of the population (Figure 5C6). In the miltefosine-treated group, CD80-positive cells constituted 4.48% of the population and CD163-positive cells constituted 0.55% of the population (Figure 5C7). These results suggested that PepA-treated lesion had higher CD80 population as compared with other peptide-treated groups. This statistical result suggests that PepA treatment might have promoted higher M1 macrophage marker expression in the localized macrophage lesion population (Figure 5D).

Since there was no significant difference in the weight of superficial parotid LN, we assessed the expression of Nos2 in the LNs and observed the parasite number through limiting dilution assay (Figure 6A). Immune response studies of peptide treatment in mice

ELISA for IL-10 and IL-12 was performed using the sera of experimental mice groups to assess the circulating levels of IL-12 and IL-10 cytokines (Figure 6A). As our work focuses on IL-10 and IL-12 reciprocal levels in early stages of leishmaniasis, we analyzed if upon peptide treatment IL-10 levels reduce and IL-12 levels increase. We observed sera isolated from the infected group showed high IL-10 (306.5 pg/mL) and low IL-12 (53 pg/mL). PepA-treated mice showed high IL-12 (1,144.5 pg/ml) and low IL-10 (244.6 pg/ml), suggesting a reciprocal shift in the IL-10 and IL-12 expression patterns. IL12KO mice showed the lowest expression of IL-12 (11.17 pg/ml) and the highest IL-10 expression (553.75 pg/ml). In case of IL10KO mice, IL-10 concentration was the lowest among all groups (170.4 pg/ml) and concentration of IL-12 was the highest (1,521.5 pg/mL). In miltefosine-treated mice, IL-10 levels were lower (200 pg/mL) and IL-12 levels were higher (500 pg/mL). IL-12 levels in PepB- and PepC-treated mice were 1,070.5 pg/ml and 324.5 pg/ml, respectively. IL-10 levels in PepB- and PepC-treated mice were 249.6 pg/mL and 237.9 pg/ml, respectively; the IL-10 levels in control were observed to be 228.2 pg/mL and in IL10KO mice were 170.4 pg/mL. The difference in IL-10 levels between control and IL10KO was smaller but was observed to be significant. Balb/c mice naturally have a higher baseline level of IL-10 because of their genetic background, whereas IL10KO mice are genetically modified to not produce IL-10 (Figures 6D and 6E). Thus, ELISA results show that PepA might show modulatory effect in regulating the reciprocity of IL-10 and IL-12 in leishmaniasis by switching the cytokine dynamics toward IL-12 production. Discussion

The peptides PepA, PepB, and PepC were previously evaluated based on their physico-chemical properties primarily focusing on their hydropathy index, SRM, and their predicted half-life. Furthermore, their efficiency to bind to the SHP-1 catalytic motif, which is responsible to dephosphorylate NFAT5, was evaluated using blind docking and site-specific docking to observe that all three peptides had high specificity and binding energy to bind to SHP-1.22 The binding stability was substantiated through molecular dynamics simulation, suggesting PepA showed most stable interactions with SHP-1 in mimicked hypertonic cutaneous leishmaniasis lesion environment. PepA notably showed stable interactions with DMPC membrane anchored with cholesterol and TIP3P solvent model, which suggested PepA may possess localization properties that may serve essential to cross the cell plasma membrane.23 To evaluate the efficacy of peptides for anti-leishmanial host-directed therapy, we got them synthesized and purified. The HPLC results of all peptides highlighted that the purity of synthesized peptides is very high with minimal impurities showing good synthesis and purification. It was observed that PepC eluted earlier than PepA and PepB, indicating that it may be less hydrophobic and have different polarity. These were further validated using ESI-MS. The mass spectrometry data confirm that PepA has a MW of approximately 1,678.2 Da, which is very close to the theoretical value. The difference in observed and calculated MW (0.3 Da) might be because of minor instrument calibration variations or natural isotope effects. These results suggest and confirm the peptide identity and that the peptide is intact and unmodified. For PepB, the most intense peak at 566.4 m/z ([M+3H]3+) suggests that the triply protonated form is the most stable or efficiently ionized species. The monoisotopic molecular ion peak ([M + H]+) at 1,679.1 confirms the MW of PepB. Multiple charge states confirm the peptide’s ionization behavior under ESI-MS conditions. The most intense peak of PepC was at 561.9 m/z ([M+3H]3+), suggesting this charge state is the most stable under the given ionization conditions. Multiple charge states of PepC (2+, 3+, 4+) confirm successful ionization and MS detection. The triply charged ion ([M+3H]3+, m/z 561.9) is the most abundant in the plot (Figure S6), indicating stability in the ESI conditions. Hence, from observation, all three peptides closely match their theoretical MW with minimal deviations. All peptides are >98% pure, indicating successful synthesis and purification. All peptides ionize efficiently with [M+2H]2+, [M+3H]3+, and [M+4H]4+ charge states.

Parasite load assay and nitrite estimation assay gave quantitative insight into the effect of peptides on parasites and hosts. PMs treated with 125 μg/mL of PepA showed higher nitrite levels and the lowest parasite load without hampering its morphology. The generation of NO by M1 macrophages can successfully kill parasites by activating (iNOS)/(Nos2) and other leishmanicidal chemicals such reactive oxygen species (ROS). In the macrophage defense mechanism against intracellular parasites, NO and ROS are two important components. Since the Griess assay depends on the formation of nitrites in cell culture supernatants, it was used to quantify NO.32 Despite its efficacy in eliminating parasites, miltefosine treatment has been demonstrated to decrease the generation of NO by macrophages in Leishmania infection. Miltefosine specifically reduced NO levels in PMs after L. major infection in studies involving C57BL/6 mice, suggesting a modulatory effect on macrophage activation. Although NO is a well-known effector molecule in the macrophage-mediated elimination of Leishmania, the antiparasitic action of the drug is not impacted by NO levels. Miltefosine in fact increases macrophage phagocytic capacity and effectively eliminates amastigotes from infected dendritic cells without compromising their immune function, indicating that its therapeutic effect may depend more on enhanced cellular uptake and intracellular killing mechanisms than on increased NO production.33,34,35 PepA also showed low toxicity toward PMs and its selectivity index was less than 1, suggesting that PepA may possess anti-leishmanial properties.

IL-12 is required to maintain Th1 cell response and to prevent Th2 cell response.36 The paradigm for studying the mechanisms underlying susceptibility to L. major has been BALB/c mice, while C57BL/6 mice have been used to explore resistance. As our model focuses on IL-10 and IL-12 reciprocity at early stages of infection, we used BALB/c mice as the in vivo model.37 In IL12KO mice it has been reported that TGF-β and IL-4 are upregulated in leishmaniasis, which upregulates Th2 cell response.38 Increased TGF-β may also be associated with decreased IFN-γ in IL12KO mice as absence of IL-12 has been known to prevent IFN-γ production.36,39 TNF-ɑ also shows downregulation in IL12KO mice with leishmaniasis, which leads to upregulation of parasite survival response.40 Thus, the anti-inflammatory cytokine upregulation may lead to susceptibility toward Leishmania infection in IL12KO mice. IL-12 production results in induction of NO; therefore, in IL12KO mice, expression of Nos2 was downregulated.41 On the contrary, IL10KO mice are reported to be less susceptible to L. major infection.39 IFN-γ and Nos2 mRNA expression are reported to be high in IL10KO mice, suggesting enhanced Th1 response.40 Absence of IL-10 also leads to upregulation of TNF-ɑ, which is essential for activation of M1 macrophages.42 In IL10KO mice, IL-4 and TGF-β are downregulated, which were directly responsible for parasite survival. Due to their absence, IFN-γ and NO are upregulated promoting parasite-eliminating response.43 Thus, both knockout mice were used as the cytokine control groups as we are addressing the immuno-modulatory effects on reciprocal expression of IL-10 and IL-12.

Leishmania activates SHP-1 by phosphorylating it on the tyrosine 536 residue16 and cleaving it at serine 594.17,18 This truncated SHP-1 may inhibit NFAT5 by dephosphorylating it at tyrosine 143 at auxiliary export domain, which prevents its localization to nucleus.16,22,44 This NFAT5 is essential for IL-12 expression by promoting H3K27me3 histone demethylation from the promoter region of IL12p40 Nucleosome 1 position.20 Studies involving our peptides, aided through AI and machine learning approach, have reported that PepA shows stable interaction with DMPC membrane as it closely resonates mammalian plasma membrane.45 PepA also specifically interacts with active amino acids of the catalytic domain of SHP-1.23 The significance of the SHP-1 catalytic residues was further confirmed using alanine scanning mutagenesis studies, which showed that catalytic residues are functionally crucial and PepA shows interaction with them. Thus, our results suggest that PepA might have interacted with SHP-1 and inhibited its catalytic domain because of which NFAT5 was able to localize to the nucleus. Interaction between PepA and SHP-1 was further confirmed using peptide-protein pull-down assay. Phosphorylation of SHP-1 at tyrosine 564 is essential for its maximal phosphatase activity. Mutation studies of SHP-1 at tyrosine 564 have shown to reduce its activity by 40%.46 Therefore, our immunoblotting results suggest that downregulation of SHP-1 and P-564-SHP-1 may be associated with Peptide A treatment, which may have inhibited SHP-1 activity. SHP-1 inhibition by PepA may promote translocation of NFAT5 to the nucleus, which may promote IL-12 expression. Reports of a lipidic peptide IK14004 suggested that the peptide can function as a cytokine regulator by fine-tuning the balance of immunological communication between effector and regulatory pathways by selectively inhibiting the synthesis of IL-12p40 and IFN-γ, increasing the levels of IL-12p70, and uncoupling IL-2 from IFN-γ secretion. IK14004 alters dendritic cell-T cell interaction, which raises the percentage of Foxp3-expressing CD4+ regulatory T cells and stimulates the expression of activating co-receptors on CD8+ T cells. This successfully regulates the cytokine milieu toward a balanced effector-regulatory T cell response.47

Administration of peptide subcutaneously at the site of infection in L. major infection is a prominently used technique to study parasite elimination effect in mice models.48,49 The concentration determined from parasite load assay, IC50 and CC50, which promotes parasite elimination without hampering host cell, can be considered for dosing the mice.50,51

In the lesion, L. major infection enables immune cells infiltration, which primarily include macrophages.52 Macrophages act as the sentinel cells as parasite prefers to infect and hijack host macrophage to multiply. These macrophages bear the potential to kill the parasite.53 Spleen acts as a sight for parasite replication. The infection is characterized by enlargement of spleen to block pro-inflammatory cytokine response mediated by macrophages.54,55 Hence, it is essential to identify the macrophage phenotype expressed in the mice in order to understand the probability of disease outcome. In L. major infection, CD45, F4/80, and CD11b are expressed as macrophage markers.56 These markers are used to characterize the presence of macrophages in ear lesion and spleen.57,58 CD163 is expressed as marker for M2 macrophages59,60 and CD80 is prominently expressed by M1 macrophages.61 In various inflammatory disease models, it has been reported that peptides can modulate macrophage phenotype. Peptide IDR-1018 was reported to modulate human macrophage differentiation by regulating IRF4.62 Furthermore, peptides were used to regulate proliferation of monocyte/macrophage THP-1 cell line in inflammatory pathway.63 Hence, we investigated the effect of peptide on infiltrated macrophages in lesion.

Upon Leishmania parasite infection in mice, it has been known that the parasite prevails its infectivity at the site of infection, which is ear and its draining LN. Quantification of Nos2 expression through qRT-PCR of LNs has been reportedly used to provide the estimate of parasite elimination.64 In alignment with the in vitro nitrite estimation results, we observed an upregulation of the Nos2 mRNA levels in the LNs of PepA-treated mice. The lowest Nos2 mRNA levels were detected in LNs of PepB- and PepC-treated mice. Additionally, LN sample from miltefosine-treated mice and IL10KO mice exhibited an upregulation of Nos2 mRNA expression, whereas LN sample of IL12KO mice showed a downregulation. These findings suggest that PepA effectively modulates NO responses in infected mouse models.

Mice that are infected at ear pinna show changes in cytokine production, which results in determination of parasite load and also affects pathological severity of lesion.65 Peptides are also reported to alter the cytokine expression profile in Leishmania-infected mice by acting as an immuno-therapeutic agent. Peptides can also impact the parasite load in draining LNs by altering NO levels.66 Hence, we performed ELISA for identification of IL-12 and IL-10 levels in mice sera as a response to peptide treatment. Serum IL-10 levels were highest in IL-12KO mice, and serum IL-12 levels were highest in IL10KO mice. IL-10 acts as a regulatory cytokine to inhibit IL-12 levels, and likewise IL-12 inhibits IL-10 levels.20 Upon treatment of infected mice with PepA, it was observed in the sera of the mice that IL-10 levels were lowest among all other experimental groups and sera levels of IL-12 were observed to be highest suggesting immuno-modulatory role of PepA in L. major infection in regulation of reciprocity of IL-10 and IL-12. The limiting dilution method is a highly sensitive and reliable method used to quantify the parasite burden in experimental leishmaniasis mice model.67,68 Our findings indicate that the LNs of miltefosine-treated mice and PepA-treated mice exhibited the lowest parasite load compared with all other experimental groups.

The long-lasting effect of peptide can be checked with appropriate delivery system, whereby peptides can be integrated with vector and can be delivered using an appropriate delivery system. PLGA-encapsulated HisDTC had exhibited a stable system for delivery of peptides in canine leishmaniasis model to achieve significant reduction in parasite load.69 Hence, we aim to devise a better strategy for delivery of peptides to understand its localization in vivo, monitor the changes in parasite load in real time, and achieve spatiotemporal expression of peptide to prevent repeated peptide dosage. We propose to design a biosynthetic circuit for the localized, stable, and efficient spatiotemporal expression of PepA. This circuit will utilize a doxycycline-inducible Tet-ON system, regulated by the TetR protein, with GFP serving as the reporter. Following transfection and induction, we will investigate the ability of the immuno-modulatory synthetic circuit to function as a biosensor for parasite infections and sustain PepA expression. The circuit’s performance will be assessed by its capacity to induce high IL-12 levels while suppressing IL-10 expression, promoting NO production, and inhibiting SHP-1 expression. These immuno-engineered macrophages will be tested for adoptive transfer in L. major-infected mice to evaluate the in vivo efficacy of the synthetic circuit. Additionally, the circuit can be encapsulated within nanomaterial polymers for delivery in mice to achieve effective parasite clearance. Thus, we propose PepA, termed LeishPepThera, not only as a promising tool for parasite elimination but also for its future potential as a synthetic device for precision therapeutics.

Limitations of the study

The results suggested that PepA and SHP-1 show interaction with each other, although direct measurements of binding affinity and kinetics would strengthen the PepA-SHP-1 binding further. Techniques such as surface plasmon resonance or isothermal titration calorimetry would provide quantitative evidence to strengthen the specificity and high-affinity binding. Additionally, although NFAT5 nuclear translocation was demonstrated, the study did not include functional assays directly linking NFAT5-mediated transcriptional activity to NO production and parasite killing. Comparative analyses with first-line therapies, including amphotericin B and pentavalent antimonial, were also not conducted, which may limit the context for evaluating relative therapeutic efficacy. Future studies could include detailed biophysical characterization of the PepA-SHP-1 interaction to confirm binding specificity and kinetics. Incorporating functional assays that directly associate NFAT5 activity to downstream effector mechanisms such as NO production and parasite clearance would provide deeper mechanistic insight. Comparative efficacy studies with clinically used first-line drugs might be crucial to evaluate PepA with the current therapeutic landscape. Lastly, translational investigations into pharmacokinetic properties and in vivo optimization would advance PepA toward potential clinical application. Regardless, our findings were consistent in evaluating PepA (LeishPepThera) as therapeutic peptide in L. major infection.

Experimental model and study participant details

L. major culture

L.major promastigotes (MHOM/Su73/5ASKH) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) media in 20% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). The parasites were cultured for 5 days at 26°C till they reached the stationary phase.

Animal models

The experimental animal groups consisted of female strains (3–4 weeks) of Balb/c mice, IL-12 knockout (KO) (C.129S1-Il12btm1Jm/J) mice (n = 8) and IL-10 KO (B6.129P2-Il10tm1Cgn/J) mice (n = 8). Balb/c mice were divided into 6 groups for peptide treatment: uninfected (C) (n = 8), infected and untreated (I) (n = 8), Peptide A treated infected Balb/c (PepA) (n = 8), Peptide B treated infected Balb/c (PepB) (n = 8), Peptide C treated infected Balb/c (PepC) (n = 8) and Miltefosine treated infected Balb/c mice (n = 8). The methodology for developing ear lesion and peptide treatment schedule was adapted from Guhe V and Singh S. 2024.51 Briefly, all mice strains except control were injected with 103 L.major promastigotes in the right ear pinna. The lesions were allowed to grow till 3 mm in size which was measured using a digital Vernier caliper. The concentrations of the peptides determined from parasite load assay and Nitrite estimation assay were; 125 μg of PepA and PepB per 20g of mice (6.250 mg/kg) and 250 μg of PepC per 20g mice (12.5 mg/kg). These peptides were likewise injected subcutaneously (intralesional) at the site of infection in ear pinna every alternate day for 45 days. Miltefosine was given orally to the infected Balb/c mice at a concentration of 40 mg/kg every alternate day for 45 days. Post dosing and treatment, the mice were given 7 days rest followed by experimentation.

All the mice strains were housed in Experimental Animal Facility (E.A.F.) at BRIC-NCCS, Pune. Method details

Peptides

Peptides designed using Dead end elimination method (DEE) (PepA, PepB and PepC) previously reported22,23 were synthesized and the purified form was obtained from CellBiOasis. N-terminal tagged biotinylated PepA was synthesized and the purified form was obtained from GenScript. The synthesis and purity was confirmed using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) and Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS). The peptides were reconstituted in Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) and were used for in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Parasite load assay

To identify parasite inhibiting concentration of peptides which does not affect macrophage morphology, we tested the peptides on infected peritoneal macrophages (PMs). Balb/c mice were injected intraperitoneally with 4% thioglycollate. Post 5 days, the mice were sacrificed to isolate PMs. 106 PMs were seeded in DMEM complete media [Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and penstrep [(50–100 units/mL penicillin and 50–100 μg/mL streptomycin] 8 well chamber slides. The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 in the incubator for 24 h. Post 24 h, PMs were infected with stationary phase L.major promastigotes with the multiplicity of infection (MOI) as (1 p.m.:10 L.major) ratio and the cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 in the incubator for 24 h. Later, excess parasites were removed from the media and the infected PMs were washed with 1X Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) thrice. Different concentrations of peptides (PepA, PepB and PepC) were added to the infected cells which were 500 μg/ml, 250 μg/ml, 125 μg/ml and 62.5 μg/ml. Miltefosine was used as a positive drug control, the concentrations used were 20 μM, 15 μM, 10 μM and 5 μM. These concentrations of miltefosine was used as per the concentrations reported by Kabra R and Singh S. 2021.70 After the addition of peptides and miltefosine, cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 in the incubator for 24 h.

The peptide-containing media was collected from the cells in a 1.5 mL tube which was later used for Nitrite estimation assay and the cells were washed with 1X PBS thrice. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at RT. The cells were further washed with 1X PBS thrice. To all the samples, 1 μg/mL of 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was added and the cells were incubated at RT in the dark for 10 min. Later, the cells were washed with 1X PBS thrice and distilled water twice. The slide was later acquired on Olympus FV 3000 laser scanning confocal microscope at 60X under oil immersion. A total of 100 PMs were counted for the presence of parasites in all samples. The number of parasites per macrophage was noted for all samples. Comparative analysis was performed between infection, peptide treated samples and miltefosine treated sample to identify the concentration of peptide required to clear parasites from macrophage without hampering macrophage morphology as compared to miltefosine.

Nitrite assay using greiss reagent

Cell culture media was collected post peptide treatment and were used to identify nitric oxide (NO) by identifying Nitrite concentration using Griess reagent kit. The supernatant from (125 μg/mL PepA and PepB treated PMs), (250 μg/mL PepC treated PMs) and (5 μM Miletfosine treated PMs) were used as they were the lowest concentration which promoted parasite elimination without compromising macrophage morphology. The 1 mM Nitrate standard solution was serially diluted in a 96-well plate to prepare a standard curve to measure the NO levels in serum samples. 100 μL of sera were added to the wells. Reagent A and Reagent B, from the kit were mixed in 1:1 ratio and 100 μL was added to the wells. The entire constituents of the wells were mixed by pipetting and the samples were incubated at RT for 30 min. A multi-plate reader was used to record the optical density of the samples at 548 nm wavelength. The standard curve was used for determining the concentration of nitrite in each sample.

Macrophage toxicity

Parasite resistance to many antibiotics is rapidly increasing globally, necessitating ongoing drug development in order to bring newer molecules to market. Monitoring toxicity of the molecules is crucial in ensuring the development of new drugs.71 To check if the peptides were toxic for macrophages, we performed Annexin V/PI apoptosis assay on PMs treated with 125 μg/mL of PepA and PepB, 250 μg/mL of PepC and 5 μM of miltefosine. Briefly, PMs were isolated from Balb/c mice and 106 cells were seeded in 60 mm petri plates. The cells were adhered for 3 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. Post incubation, the cells were treated with peptides and miltefosine for 24 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 24 h, media was discarded and cells were resuspended in 300 μL Annexin V buffer. To the cells 1.5 μL Annexin V dye and 1.5 μL PI was added and incubated at RT in the dark for 30 min. The cells were acquired for the identification of the percentage of apoptotic population on BD FACS Canto II. Results were analyzed using FlowJo 10.10.0.

Selectivity index

To determine selectivity index; we identified 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) and Cytotoxic concentration (CC50) values of PepA, PepB and PepC. To determine IC50, PMs were isolated from Balb/c mice using thioglycollate method and 106 cells/well was seeded in 96 well tissue culture plates. The cells were adhered for 3 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. Post incubation, the cells were washed with 1X PBS and infected with 107 cells of L.major stationary phase promastigotes MOI (1:10) for 24 h. Post 25 h incubation, cells were washed with 1X PBS thrice and they were treated with serially diluted, different concentration of peptides (mentioned as per parasite load assay) for 24 h 37°C with 5% CO2. Post incubation, samples were washed with 1X PBS thrice and incubated with 100 μL of 0.5 mg/mL 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) for 4 h at RT in the dark. Later, MTT was discarded and 100 μL DMSO was added to the wells to dissolve formazan. The plate was incubated at RT for 30 min in the dark. The readings were recorded on a multiplate reader at 570 nm.

To determine CC50, PMs were isolated from Balb/c mice using thioglycolate method and 106 cells/well was seeded in 96 well tissue culture plates. The cells were adhered for 3 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. Post incubation, the cells were washed with 1X PBS and they were treated with serially diluted, different concentration of peptides (mentioned as per parasite load assay) for 24 h 37°C with 5% CO2. Post incubation, samples were washed with 1X PBS thrice and incubated with 100 μL of 0.5 mg/mL MTT for 4 h at RT in the dark. Further, MTT was discarded and 100 μL DMSO was added to the wells. The plate was incubated at RT for 30 min in the dark. Later, the readings were recorded on a multiplate reader at 570 nm.

To calculate the selectivity index, we took the ratio of CC50/IC50 for PepA, PepB and PepC. The selectivity index >1 demonstrates that the compounds possesses potential as a therapeutic in the treatment of leishmaniasis.72

Physical parameters

The weight of the mice from all experimental mice groups was checked before infecting them with L. major at the ear pinna; using a digital weighing machine. Later, when the lesion was developed and the treatment regime of peptides was completed, the weight of mice was again checked on a digital weighing machine. The weight of each mouse from all the groups was noted and the average was calculated. The lesion diameters of all experimental mice groups were measured before and after peptide treatment using a Vernier caliper and subsequently reported. Lymph nodes (LNs) were harvested and their weight was calculated.

Isolation of spleen and liver from mice for imaging

Mice were euthanized using CO2 and the body was sanitized using 70% ethanol. The mice were laid on their right flank side. A scissor and forceps were used to cut the skin and expose the peritoneum and spleen. The spleen was harvested using forceps and scissors and placed on petri plate containing ice-cold PBS. External tissues attached to spleen were carefully removed and images of spleen from all experimental mice group were captured. This methodology was adopted from Alexandre, Y.O., and Mueller, S.N. 2022.73

The mice was placed on its front to expose the ventral area of the body and from the exposed peritoneum, larger incision was made to expose abdominal cavity and organs. The ribcage was exposed to gain access to liver. While gently holding the left lobe of liver with forceps, entire liver was excised with scissors. Liver was placed on ice-cold PBS. Other tissues connected to liver were removed and images of liver from all experimental mice groups were captured. The harvesting protocol was adopted from Mortimer et al. 2021.74

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) of cytokines and Nos2

Isolation of PMs

For cytokine profiling of IL10KO and IL12KO mice, PMs from infected but untreated Balb/c mice, IL-10KO and IL-12KO mice were isolated. The protocol for isolation of PMs was adapted from De Jesus et al. 2022.75 Briefly, euthanized mice were placed on sterile surface and 70% ethanol was sprayed on the abdominal region for sanitization. Sterile forceps and scissors were used and the skin overlaying the peritoneum was cut and pulled back gently to expose the peritoneal cavity inner linings beneath the skin. Later, using a 10 mL syringe, 5 mL ice-cold PBS was injected in the peritoneum cavity. The abdominal walls were massaged for 15-20 s to dislodge the PMs the PBS. The PBS was drawn back using the same syringe and the PMs were collected in 50 mL conical tubes on ice.

PMs collected were washed with 1X PBS twice by centrifuging the cells at 2000 rpm for 15 min at 24°C. The PMs were resuspended in DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotic. Total 106 cells seeded in 60 mm petri dishes and incubated for 3 h at 37°C, 5% CO2 to allow PMs to adhere. Post incubation, non-adherent cells were removed and the PMs were resuspended in 1 mL TRIzol reagent. The samples were processed for mRNA isolation, cDNA synthesis using high capacity cDNA kit and qRT-PCR using protocol of Khandibharad S and Singh S. 2024.76

mRNA isolation

To the PMs sample resuspended in TRIzol reagent, 200 μL of chloroform was added and the samples were gently mixed by inverting the 1.5 mL tube. The ratio of chloroform: TRIzol was 1:5. The samples were incubated at RT for 15 min. Later, samples were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The upper aqueous transparent layer was collected in a fresh 1.5 mL tube. To the aq. layer, 500 μL isopropanol was added and the tubes were mixed gently by inverting. The tubes were further incubated at RT for 10 min. Post incubation; the samples were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatant was discarded and the obtained pellet was washed twice with 75% chilled Ethanol by centrifuging the samples 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The pellet was air dried and resuspended in 20 μL diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC) water. The isolated RNA was quantified using nanodrop to note 260/280 and 260/230 ratio.

cDNA synthesis

From each sample, 2 μg mRNA was used to synthesize cDNA in 100 μL tubes. For preparation of 20 μL cDNA we used 0.8 μL of 25X dNTP, 2 μL 10X RT Primer, 2 μL 10X RT buffer, 0.5 μL Reverse transcriptase, 2 μg mRNA was added as per the quantification from nanodrop and the total volume was adjusted using DEPC water. The samples underwent thermal cycler process following these steps; 25°C for 10 min, followed by 2 h at 37°C, the third is 85°C for 5 min, and the final step was 4°C forever.

qRT-PCR

Expression profile for cytokines which include Tumor Necrosis Factor alpha (TNF-ɑ), Interferon gamma (IFN-γ), Tumor growth factor beta (TFG-β) and Interleukin-4 (IL-4) and Nos2 using TaqMan-FAM-MGB chemistry was performed. Total 2 μg cDNA from each sample was used for differential mRNA expression analysis. The reaction mixture of 10 μL was used. The reagents were added as follows; 5 μL of (2X) TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix, 0.5 μL of Taqman-FAM-MGB probes which are enlised above, 1 μL cDNA from samples. The total volume was made up with nuclease free water. The qRT-PCR was run using StepOne plus Real-Time PCR system and StepOne software version 2.3. The PCR cycle included the following steps; Holding at 50°C for 2 min, Holding at 95°C for 20 s, Cycling at 95°C for 3 s and Cycling at 60°C for 30 s. Total number of cycles were 40. Comparative CT (ΔΔ CT) was noted for each probe. Actin B was used as housekeeping gene. Relative fold change was calculated for expression of all probes.

Laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM)

PMs from all experimental mice groups were isolated as per the protocol mentioned in earlier section. A total 104 cells/well were seeded in 8 well chamber slides and the cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 in the incubator for 3 h allowing adhesion of PMs. Post incubation, the non-adherent cells were discarded, and the adhered PMs were washed with 1X PBS thrice. Further, the cells were washed thrice with 0.05% Tween 20 in 1X PBS. Cells were later fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at RT followed by washes with 1X PBS. Cells were treated with 1X PBST (0.1% Triton X- in 1X PBS) at RT for 10 min followed by blocking with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) prepared in 1X PBST for 30 min at RT. Blocking media was removed from the cells and washed thrice with 1X PBST. To the samples, SHP-1 and NFAT5 primary antibodies (1:1000) ratio were added and cells were incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The cells were treated for 1 h with mouse phalloidin Alexa Fluor 568 (1:500) and anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) F(ab′)2 fragment (Alexa Fluor 488 Conjugate) and were washed three times with 1X PBST. Cells were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min and then washed three times with 1× PBST. Later, cells were washed three times using distilled water. Cells were air-dried and the slides were mounted using mounting media and examined at 60x on a Zeiss LSM 880 equipped with an Airyscan microscope. The images were analyzed in Fiji 1.54f.

Western blot

From the peritoneal cavities of every experimental mouse group, PMs were isolated. PMs were adhered for 3 h in DMEM with 10% FBS in 60 mm cell culture dishes in 5% CO2 at 37°C. Post incubation. Media was discarded and cells were washed with 1X PBS to get rid of any non-adherent cells. In 1 mL of PBS, cells were isolated using a cell scraper, and the cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at 24°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 90 μL Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer with 10 μL of 1X protease inhibitor to isolate cellular protein. Further, cells were mixed thoroughly and incubated on ice for 30 min followed by centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant of all samples was collected in fresh 1.5 mL tubes. In order to estimate the concentration of proteins, the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay was used where 1 mg/ml BSA was used as standard. On a 12% gel, 20 μg protein was loaded and later transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (39129090, HiMedia). After blocking the membrane for an hour with 3% BSA, it was subsequently washed three times using Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST). Primary antibodies SHP-1 (1:1000) and phosphorylated tyrosine 564- SHP-1 (P-564-SHP-1) (1:1000) β-actin (1:1000) prepared in 3% BSA in 1X TBST were added to the nitrocellulose membrane. At 4°C, the blots were incubated for 18 h. After incubation, the nitrocellulose membrane was washed thrice with 1X TBST. Later, the nitrocellulose membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody, anti-rabbit IgG (whole molecule)-peroxidase antibody produced in goat at RT for 1 h on a shaker. Once the incubation was complete, the nitrocellulose membrane was washed thrice with 1X TBST. The nitrocellulose membrane was developed using Luminol-Enhancer Solution and H2O2, and the images were captured using Amersham ImageQuant 500 imager. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ 1.53k.

Alanine scanning mutagenesis studies

Computational alanine-scanning mutagenesis was performed using the BAlaS web application, which is powered by the underlying command-line tool BudeAlaScan. The target SHP-1-PepA complex structure in PDB format was uploaded to the BAlaS web interface, then SHP-1 was designated as ligand and PepA was specified as the receptor chain to be interrogated. The application subsequently invoked BudeAlaScan by utilizing Intelligent System for Analysis, Model Building And Rational Design (ISAMBARD) for input preparation and a customized BUDE force field to estimate binding free-energy changes by automatically mutating each residue of interest to alanine and computing ΔΔG values that reflected their contributions to the interaction. The results were displayed interactively in the browser, enabling rapid inspection of energetic hotspots and facilitating identification of residues with the most significant binding contributions. Optionally, we also employed the “constellation manual mode” to generate and assess combinations of simultaneous alanine substitutions, thereby highlighting clusters of cooperative residues whose combined ΔΔG exceeded the sum of individual contributions.

SHP-1 and PeptideA interactions

Pull-down assay was performed using the Pierce Pull-Down Biotinylated Protein:Protein Interaction Kit (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, immobilized streptavidin resin was equilibrated in Tris-buffered saline (TBS), after which biotinylated bait peptide was incubated with the resin to allow binding. Excess biotin sites were blocked with a biotin blocking solution, and prey proteins from cell lysates which included PMs (C) and 6 h L. major infected PMs (I) were subsequently incubated with the immobilized bait with gentle mixing at 4°C. After binding, columns were washed with wash buffer supplemented with 150 mM NaCl to minimize nonspecific interactions, while preserving bait–prey complexes. Bound prey proteins were eluted using a low-pH elution buffer (pH 2.0) and immediately neutralized with 10 μL Tris buffer. Eluates were prepared in SDS sample buffer and analyzed by western blot for presence of SHP-1.

Analysis of cells by flow cytometry

The isolation and processing of lesions formed in the ear pinna were carried out following the protocol from,23,67 Briefly, mice from all experimental groups were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. The external ear was excised from the mice. Isolated ears were cleaned with 70% ethanol followed by three washes with 1X PBS. Outer and inner cuticular sections of ear pinna were separated using forceps and the isolated lesions were placed on a polystyrene petri plate. The isolated lesions from each mouse of experimental groups were homogenized in ice-cold digestion solution (0.25 mg/mL Liberase, 1 μg/mL DNase in 1X PBS). The isolated cells were passed through 40 μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension of lesion’s cell population. The cells were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of 0.1% FBS in 1X PBS and was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 24°C. This step was repeated thrice. Further, cells were incubated with CD163-PE (1:100), CD80-APC (1:100), CD45-Brilliant Violet 421(1:100), CD11b-eFluor 506 (1:100) and F4/80 PE-Cyanine7(1:100) for 1 h at RT in dark. Post incubation, cells were washed thrice with 1 mL 0.1% FBS in 1X PBS. The samples were acquired for the identification of the percentage population on BD FACS Canto II. Results were analyzed using FlowJo 10.10.0.

Isolation of lymph nodes from mice

Superficial parotid lymph node was isolated from experimental mice groups. The isolation and processing of LNs were done using Giraud E and Melanitou E. 202077 protocol. Briefly, using forceps and scissors, a small incision was made to expose the superficial parotid lymph node was isolated. The LN was washed thrice with 70% ethanol followed by 3 washes with 1X PBS. The LNs were homogenized and incubated with 1 mg/mL collagenase and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I at 37°C for 30 min to obtain single cell suspension. The obtained cells were washed thrice with 1X PBS at 2000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded and cell pellet was distributed for RNA isolation and limiting dilution assay.

Isolation of mRNA, cDNA and qRT-PCR was performed76 for Nos2 as per the steps mentioned above in the methodology section. Relative fold change was calculated for Nos2 expression by using ActB as housekeeping gene.

Limiting dilution assay

The processed LNs were suspended in RPMI with 20% heat-inactivated FBS. Using Taswell’s method, briefly, 200 μL of LN suspension was added to top wells in 96-well microtiter plates. A series of eight serial dilutions (1:2) were made of 100 μL. The plate was incubated for 7 days at 27°C. Using a hemocytometer slide, cells were observed and counted on an inverted light microscope. The total number of positive wells (containing motile promastigotes) and negative wells (containing none) were estimated.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sera samples from each experimental group were examined for presence of IL-10 and IL-12 using the IL-12 Mouse ELISA Kit and the IL-10 Mouse ELISA Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, a 1:100 diluted Biotin-conjugated primary antibody was mixed with sera and diluted 1:1 ratio with sample diluent. The mixture was then incubated on coated plates for 2 h at RT on a shaker. The sera were removed from the wells and the wells were washed with wash buffer. Streptavidin-HRP secondary antibody diluted 1:100 was added to the wells and the wells were incubated for 1 h on a shaker at RT. The wells were then washed with the wash buffer thrice. TMB substrate was added to the wells and incubated in the dark for 10 min; later stop solution was added to the wells. The wells were acquired on a multi spectrophotometer at 450 nm and colorimetric readings were reported for each sample. Quantification of IL-10 and IL-12 were done by extrapolating on standard curve graph.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The quantitative data shown is based on results from experiments using eight animal pools that were carried out at least twice and produced similar results. The data reported includes the mean and standard deviation (SD) of at least two independent experiments. The dataset was analyzed using the Student’s t test for two independent samples, one-way ANOVA for three independent groups, and two-way ANOVA for data with two independent variables between groups. Asterisks denoted statistical significance for differences, with a p-value less than 0.05(∗), p-value is less than 0.01 (∗∗), p-value is less than 0.001 (∗∗∗) and p-value is less than 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗). The data showing non-significant p-value is denoted as (ns). The statistical details of tests and p-value is denoted in figure legends. All data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 for Windows, a program developed by La Jolla, California-based GraphPad Software (www.graphpad.com).
