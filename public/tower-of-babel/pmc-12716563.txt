Feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of an intervention on patient engagement in patient safety: a prospective, mixed-methods evaluation in patient and family advisory councils (PFACs)
Authors: Larissa Brust, Yannick Blum, Qëndresa Rramani Dervishi, Nikoloz Gambashidze, Matthias Weigl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of ‘Partners for Patient Safety’ (P4PS) programme for strengthening competencies and patient engagement at the organisational level.DesignProspective study with three measurement points (baseline, interim and follow-up) and an explanatory sequential mixed methods approach for formative and process evaluation.SettingOncology-focused patient and family advisory councils (PFACs) integrated into healthcare organisations and networks in five German federal states.ParticipantsInitially, 36 stakeholders of six PFACs were recruited. At follow-up, 27 participated in all intervention modules and completed all surveys. From those, 14 participated in follow-up interviews.InterventionThe P4PS programme consists of two sequentially implemented modules: (1) an e-learning module and (2) a 4-hour on-site workshop. The programme focuses on the following topics: patient safety (PS), communication strategies and PFAC engagement in respective care organisations.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcome measures were feasibility domains, assessed via standardised (acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility) and self-developed measures (relevance, acceptability and social validity, complexity and practicability, demand and implementation, and adaptability). Secondary outcome measure was preliminary effectiveness, measured via changes in self-assessed competencies in PS, communication and engagement.ResultsFeasibility ratings were high across standardised and self-developed measures (median range: 4–5 of 5). Background

Improving patient safety (PS) remains a global health priority, as emphasised by the WHO.1 Despite significant progress, one in six patients still reports safety incidents, significantly contributing to disease burden and healthcare costs.2 3 As many of these incidents are preventable, multifaceted approaches are required, involving providers, patients and their representatives.2,4

Patient engagement (PE) has emerged as a key strategy to strengthen PS by establishing collaborative relationships between patients and both clinical and organisational professionals.5 6 PE refers to the active and meaningful engagement of patients and their representatives at all levels of care, including organisations and governance.7 The significant value of PE has been repeatedly highlighted, particularly at the organisational level including strategic planning and quality management.8,10

One promising approach to embrace and institutionalise the various benefits of PE is the establishment of patient and family advisory councils (PFACs).9 11 These often consist of patients, family members and patient advocates and collaborate with healthcare organisations to improve care quality, safety and responsiveness.12 13 When implemented effectively, PFACs provide valuable input in a variety of areas, such as facility planning, patient education, clinical safety strategies and organisational decision-making.14 In some countries, PFACs have already demonstrated positive impacts on patient satisfaction, safety outcomes and development of user-centred services.12 15 16 However, despite these promising developments, the potential of PFACs often remains underutilised, primarily due to unclear roles, limited competencies and knowledge, insufficient organisational support and the lack of standardised practices across healthcare settings.9 14 16

The situation in Germany mirrors these international trends, with limited research into the structure, function and actual needs of PFAC stakeholders.17 In terms of structure, PFACs are established in various forms across institutions, yet systematic knowledge about how they are organised and embedded within hospital governance is scarce. Moreover, little is known about how PFACs function in practice. For example, the extent to which PFACs contribute to strategic decision-making, quality improvement or PS initiatives beyond their mere involvement in research.18,20 This limited understanding also refers to various challenges in collaboration between PFACs and healthcare organisations, including deficits in structured exchange and dialogue concerning quality and safety improvement.21 In addition, education and training for all stakeholders have been identified as key leverage points for successful PE.21 22 However, even internationally, structured programmes that address the specific competency needs of PFAC members remain scarce.21 23 This lack of targeted, evidence-based training limits the transformative potential of PE in health organisations and their strategies for improvement.2 8 24

To address this gap, we initiated the broader PEPS-3 initiative (‘Implementation of PE for the strategic promotion of PS’), which aims to develop and evaluate strategies to strengthen PE in PS at the organisational level. As part of PEPS-3, we conducted a Delphi-informed, mixed-methods needs and requirements analysis among PFAC members and identified competency gaps in five PS areas (including PS fundamentals, hygiene measures, quality and risk management, legal basis for PE, other patient organisations) and five communication areas (including respectful and precise communication, constructive feedback and objectivity).25 These previous findings highlighted relevant training needs and indicated a desire among PFAC stakeholders for deeper integration into organisational structures, processes and data flows. To complement this, a rapid scoping review examined existing educational resources aimed at supporting PFACs in PS.26 We identified few promising examples with significant variation in content, format and depth. Standardised, adaptable programmes tailored to PFAC needs were notably lacking.

In response to these findings, we codeveloped the ‘Partners for Patient Safety’ (P4PS) blended learning programme within the PEPS-3 framework, in close collaboration with PFAC stakeholders and organisational representatives.27 Its goal was to strengthen PFAC members’ competencies in PS and communication, promote their strategic engagement at the organisational level and ultimately enhance PE to support PS. The P4PS programme was intended to include modular, flexible educational formats and to incorporate theoretical backgrounds, real-world case examples, legal frameworks for PFAC’s work and interactive exercises tailored to the PFAC context.

This study reports the prospective and mixed-methods evaluation of the newly developed P4PS programme. Specifically, we sought to:

evaluate feasibility of implementing the P4PS programme in real-world healthcare settings and

evaluate feasibility of implementing the P4PS programme in real-world healthcare settings and

explore its preliminary effectiveness and potential impact on PFAC members’ competencies and their engagement in organisational PS activities.

Methods

Study design

For evaluation of the P4PS programme, we conducted a prospective, quasi-experimental feasibility and preliminary effectiveness study using an explanatory sequential mixed methods approach (ie, with quantitative survey results informing subsequent qualitative reports stemming from interviews). This sequence and embedding of qualitative data enriched and contextualised the interpretation of quantitative results.28 29

The design of this evaluation consisted of repeated measures at three time points: baseline assessment (T0, preintervention), interim assessment (T1, between both intervention modules) and follow-up assessment (T2, after the intervention). An overview of our evaluation design and study flow, including details on the sequence of our data collection steps and analysis, is provided in figure 1.

Study flow. Mixed methods motives: development, expansion and complementarity; M1, intervention module 1; M2, intervention module 2; PFAC, patient and family advisory council; P4PS, Partners for Patient Safety; T0, baseline; T1, interim; T2, follow-up assessment.

This evaluation was part of the larger research initiative on organisation-level PE in PS, that is, PEPS-3. This article reports evaluation of feasibility and initial effectiveness of the final version of the P4PS programme—a newly developed intervention programme.27

Study’s data protection procedures adhered to national and international standards.25 This investigation was preregistered in the German Clinical Trials Register (ID: DRKS00034733).

To safeguard comprehensibility of reporting and reproducibility, we used two established reporting guidelines: (1) CONsolidated Standards Of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) extension to pilot and feasibility trials,30 excluding not applicable items due to the non-randomised design31; and, (2) Mixed Methods Article Reporting Standards.32

Participants and eligibility

We sought to investigate PFACs that are structurally integrated into healthcare organisations or in hospital networks in Germany, including both PFAC members and affiliated organisational representatives (eg, directors, managers, senior physicians/nurses). Eligible participants were German-speaking adults (≥18 years) who had been active for at least 3 months in an oncology-focused PFAC—given that these are the most common PFACs in Germany.25 Roles included patients, caregivers, organisational representatives and other stakeholders (eg, coordinators, researchers). As this was a feasibility study to explore potential effects, we performed no sample size calculation prior to study start.

Recruitment

We used a convenience sampling approach. We initially aimed to recruit at least five PFACs, each represented by at least six stakeholders (n≥ 30), respectively. This sample size is deemed appropriate for exploratory research in a scarcely researched area.33 34 Previous participants of a needs analysis,25 on which the present study was based, were invited for study participation (during November–December 2024). Furthermore, PFAC coordinators were also invited to support further recruitment within their local or regional PFAC and affiliated healthcare organisation(s). After information, all participants provided written informed consent. Participants received individually €120 compensation. Additionally, each PFAC received €500 for consistently participating in all intervention modules.

Content and module structure of the P4PS programme intervention

Aim and content of the P4PS programme

The P4PS programme aims to strengthen PS and communication competencies among PFAC members and to enhance collaboration between PFACs and healthcare organisations. It was developed based on the needs of oncology-focused PFAC members,25 findings from a previous scoping review on PS training for PFACs,26 and a logic model35 outlining inputs, activities, outputs and outcomes of the programme (for details, see online supplemental supporting information S1 table A). A detailed description of the programme’s development and content is available elsewhere27 as detailed explanation of the programme falls outside the scope of this paper. In brief, the P4PS programme addresses three key topics: (1) fundamentals of PS, (2) communication and collaborative goal setting and (3) PFAC engagement. It consists of two sequential modules combining knowledge acquisition and mutual application in practice: an e-learning (module 1) and an on-site workshop (module 2). The e-learning module, including instructional workshop materials, is available in German language on our institute’s website.36

Module 1 of the P4PS programme: e-learning

The purpose of this module 1 is to inform PFAC members on three relevant topics through distinct, consecutive chapters27: Chapter 1 covers key PS concepts, including definitions, domains, impacts of adverse events, systemic error contributors, patient rights and PS strategies (eg, Swiss cheese model, hygiene protocols). Chapter 2 provides information on PFAC engagement with examples of effective structures and regulatory considerations. Chapter 3 covers communication in PS (eg, speak up initiative, frameworks of effective communication) and introduces strategies for decision-making and goal setting. Further resources, such as meeting protocols and orientation manuals, are also provided. This module 1 concludes with a scenario of a fictional PFAC effectively collaborating with a hospital to reduce readmissions, while applying tools and resources, all taught in previous chapters.

The e-learning module features multimedia content (videos, audios, graphics), interactive exercises and quizzes to reinforce learning. Developed in Rise 360 (Articulate Global, LLC, New York), it is accessible on all devices, self-paced and designed to be completed in approximately 2 hours. Participants had 1 month (January 2025) to complete the e-learning course. Completion of module 1 was required before attending the following workshop (Module 2).

Module 2 of the P4PS programme: workshop

This second module is an in-person workshop27 conducted on-site within the respective healthcare organisation, that is, academic hospital hosting the comprehensive cancer network. This workshop was offered for all members of all five participating PFACs, respectively. Its purpose is twofold: (1) to refresh and reinforce content from the previous module 1 and (2) to foster collaboration between PFAC and stakeholders from respective healthcare organisation(s). This workshop comprises of four steps: (1) shifting perspectives to jointly clarify stakeholder roles, (2) identifying barriers to collaborations, (3) developing future measures for effective communication between PFAC and healthcare organisation and (4) setting goals for future improvements.

Two moderators (ie, members of the study team) facilitated the local workshops that usually lasted for 4 hours. Each workshop consisted of a brief introduction, review of the e-learning content, group work, individual tasks and role plays. Necessary resources such as handouts and flipcharts were provided. Workshop dates were jointly determined with organisational representatives within the planned intervention period (February 2025).

Patient and public involvement

The P4PS programme was developed and evaluated by a multidisciplinary research team of five PS researchers (two psychologists, one physician, two physiotherapists) and supported by local academic partners and educational experts.27 An independent patient representative was permanently involved to foster relevance and enhance clarity for both the target group and a lay audience.27 She was actively involved in the design of the study and implementation of the P4PS programme intervention, including reviewing study materials, refining intervention resources and providing feedback on feasibility and potential burden for participants. Her input helped improve and tailor the content and procedures to the specific needs of both patients and lay audience. The patient representative and PFAC stakeholders also participated in planning the dissemination of study results, helping to determine which findings and formats would best for outreach to relevant patient communities and for support of future PE practices in Germany, with particular attention to patients’ priorities.37

Evaluation of the P4PS programme: data collection and outcomes

For evaluation of the P4PS programme, quantitative and qualitative data were collected sequentially to evaluate its feasibility (primary outcome) and preliminary effectiveness (secondary outcome). Data collection occurred between January 2025 and March 2025 (see figure 1).

Quantitative assessment of P4PS programme feasibility and effectiveness

To determine its feasibility and preliminary effectiveness, online surveys were repeatedly administered at three time points (figure 1). Additionally, participants provided at baseline information on their demographic characteristics, their current (scale range: 1=‘very good’ to 6=‘insufficient’) and desired future level of engagement and on their current and desired focus on PS issues (online supplemental supporting information S2 table A).

All surveys were provided online via web-based forms (Unipark, Tivian XI GmbH). We used a pseudonymisation procedure to match participant data across all three time points. All survey items (in detail described below) were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1=‘completely disagree’ to 5=‘completely agree’). Estimated completion times were ~10 min (T0), ~5 min (T1) and ~15–20 min (T2).

Measurements of P4PS programme’s feasibility

Feasibility was assessed separately for each module at T1 and T2. We used a mix of validated and self-developed instruments and applied first the German-language versions of three validated measures: feasibility of intervention measure (FIM; Cronbach’s α=0.91), acceptability of intervention measure (AIM; α=0.94) and intervention appropriateness measure (IAM; α=0.94).38 39 Each scale consists of four items, respectively. Additionally, at T1, participants reported which chapters of Module 1 they had completed and the total amount of time they spent on it.

Feasibility of the full P4PS programme was assessed at T2 using 17 self-developed items covering five distinct domains, derived from Orsmond and Cohn’s40 guiding questions for feasibility studies: (1) relevance (guiding question: ‘do study participants show evidence of need for the intervention?’), (2) acceptability and social validity (eg,‘do the participants have enough time and capacity to complete the intervention?’), (3) complexity and practicability (eg,‘does the intervention involve a reasonable amount of time or does it create a burden for the participants?’), (4) demand and implementation (eg,’does the intervention fit with the daily life activities of study participants?’) and (5) adaptability (eg,‘are the […] procedures and […] measures appropriate […]?’).40 Our survey items for the five domains were adapted from validated instruments, including the User Experience Questionnaire41 and the System Usability Scale42 and are summarised in table 1. To reduce response bias, some items were negatively worded. Finally, space for additional free-text comments and suggestions was provided.

Measurements of programme’s effectiveness

Effectiveness was assessed via changes in participants’ self-reported competencies referring to the three core domains of the P4PS programme: PS, communication and PFAC engagement. Fourteen self-developed items were administered at T0 and T2. These items were derived from the programme’s learning objectives (see online supplemental supporting information S1 table B) and structured along Bloom’s revised taxonomy levels, that is, remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, create.43 44

To complement this, participants were asked at T2 to rate the perceived impact of the intervention on future PFAC engagement. For this purpose, 13 survey items from the German Safety Attitudes Questionnaire45 and Speak-Up-Check46 were adapted to capture respondents’ perceived changes over time.

Participants’ qualitative assessment of key factors influencing feasibility and outlook

Following module 2 and follow-up (T2), semistructured individual interviews with participants were conducted to complement and enrich our quantitative findings (sequential, explanative mixed methods approach28). If individual responses in quantitative data were observed below the indifference point of the scale, respective statements were included in interviews for further reflection. The interview guide focused on four themes: (1) recruitment and reach, (2) improvement and further development, (3) feasibility and (4) scaling up and sustainability. Further questions referred to initial perceptions, recruitment, accessibility, relevance, feasibility as well as potential for independent implementation without project funding, dissemination, scalability and sustainability (outlook).

We aimed to interview at least two stakeholders per PFAC (ideally one patient and one organisational representative). Interviews (~30 min) were conducted via ZoomX (Zoom Video Communications, San José), recorded, transcribed verbatim (via MAXQDA Software; VERBI Software GmbH, Berlin), checked for accuracy, pseudonymised by replacing identifiable information (eg, names, institutions, locations) with neutral codes. The key linking pseudonyms to real identities was stored separately and securely and will be deleted after study completion to render the data fully anonymous. The full interview guide is provided in online supplemental supporting information S1 table C.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using R47 and RStudio.48 Descriptive statistics were calculated across all time points for all variables of interest. Pre–post changes in self-assessed competencies were tested using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as the skewed distribution (Shapiro-Wilk test, visual inspection) and the ordinal nature of the Likert scales warranted a conservative non-parametric approach.49 As further sensitivity analyses, we conducted paired t-tests on the raw as well as on the log-transformed data and calculated effect sizes (Cohen’s d based on t-test). Analyses on preliminary effectiveness were conducted on complete cases only (participants who had data on T0 and T2, where questions assessing effectiveness were asked); no imputation was performed. To account for multiple testing (14 comparisons across different competencies), observed p values were Bonferroni-adjusted (p×14) and interpreted against the standard significance level of α=0.05.50

The mixed methods analysis followed an explanatory sequential design: quantitative findings informed the qualitative interview guide; qualitative results contextualised and complemented the survey data, including contradictions (see figure 1).28 29 51 Interview data were analysed using qualitative content analysis, following a combined deductive-inductive and consensual approach.52 The main categories were derived from the interview guide topics: (a) recruitment, (b) feasibility and (c) sustainability and outlook. The latter was created by merging the previously separate topics improvements and further development and scaling and consolidation, which were subsequently used as subcategories within this main category. Two researchers independently coded 30% of transcripts, aiming for intercoder agreement of Kn≥0.80 (kappa coefficient, Kn)52,54; discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and the scheme was iteratively refined (final Kn=0.87). Subcategories were generated inductively from additional material (20%), piloted and revised through coding of further material (35%; final Kn=0.83). All interviews were then coded using this final scheme (online supplemental supporting information S1 table D).

Feasibility of the P4PS programme

Participants reported an average of 140.5 min (SD=65.3) for completing module 1 (ie, the e-learning). Completion rates across the eight chapters ranged from 93.9% to 100%.

Table 1 and online supplemental figure A (supporting information S2) show participants’ feasibility ratings and distribution: Overall, feasibility was rated as high across all modules and constructs. For module 1, median scores for acceptability (AIM), appropriateness (IAM) and feasibility (FIM) ranged between 4 (agree) and 5 (completely agree). For module 2 (ie, the workshop), all three constructs received a median score of 5 (completely agree). Rating distributions are visualised in online supplemental figure A (supporting information S2); item-level data are provided in online supplemental supporting information S2 table B. The full P4PS programme also showed median feasibility scores ranging from 4 to 5 across all assessed domains (table 1).

Key factors influencing recruitment

Given the dropout rate of 30.8%, recruitment was addressed first in participant interviews. While recruitment strategies were generally deemed appropriate, reported challenges included access to key contacts, limited involvement of decision-makers and the high workload of organisational representatives. Direct contact with PFAC members was perceived as more effective than hierarchical communication (ie, top down). Information materials were mostly seen as useful, though some were considered extensive or unclear. Detailed results, being organised according to the main and subcategories of the qualitative content analysis, are presented in online supplemental supporting information S2 table C.

Key factors influencing feasibility

Table 3 summarises the mixed methods findings for feasibility-related constructs rated less positively (ie, range fell below the indifference point). Interview findings did not confirm single negative survey ratings regarding the programme’s relevance. Participants rated the programme as generally useful and valuable for clarifying roles, establishing common standards and providing expertise for the work of the PFAC. Barriers to participation were primarily highlighted as time constraints rather than a lack of relevance to the topic. PFAC members often faced competing responsibilities (eg, other study invitations), making implementation difficult without internal task delegation. For organisational representatives, formal task assignments, time budgets and leadership support were considered essential facilitators for acceptability and social validity. Practicability and complexity of the programme were influenced by digital literacy, cognitive load and limited time, especially among older or less tech-savvy participants.

Sustainability and outlook

The interviews provided several suggestions for improvement and scaling of the P4PS programme:

Improvements and further development

Participants suggested improvements to the P4PS programme, particularly regarding practical relevance, modular structure, technical features and didactics. Module 1 was perceived as theoretical (eg, I would have liked to have seen more practical examples of the work of PFAC. INT#7). Others recommended breaking it into smaller, more flexible units (eg, […] that you can get back on track where you left off […] I want to take another step back […]. INT#9). A recurring theme was the desire for more interactivity (eg, I think some people might also be missing this: I have a question here. INT#12).

Scaling and consolidation

Participants proposed various strategies for disseminating the P4PS programme, including collaboration with national organisations such as patient umbrella and cancer organisations (eg, BAG Selbsthilfe—patient advocacy, DKG—cancer society, DKH—cancer aid, DKFZ—cancer research centre) and integration into existing initiatives such as the Patient Expert Academy, patient conferences or advocacy meetings (eg, Take part in ‘patient days’ or something […] just give a short talk. INT#14). A decentralised, regional approach was also recommended, using local networks and roundtables. Suggestions included building a network of committed patient representatives as multipliers (eg, Find people who can act as multipliers. […] I could offer to draw attention to this via this mailing list. INT#12), using social media, targeted emails and accessible online platforms. Some advocated for expanding the target group beyond formal PFACs (eg, I would extend it beyond the PFACs to patient representatives as a whole. INT#12).

A recurring theme was the need for sponsorship or a platform to maintain and further develop the programme, including ideas for a national support network with regional contacts (eg, people, i.e. really people from every federal state, who in turn volunteer or whatever they are willing to do. INT#10).

Discussion

Strengthening PE at level of healthcare organisations remains a well-recognised yet under-implemented strategy to improve PS. This study evaluated the feasibility (primary objective) and preliminary effectiveness (secondary objective) of the P4PS programme, a blended learning intervention codeveloped with various stakeholders to strengthen PS-related competencies and foster strategic engagement. Our prospective and explanatory sequential mixed methods design enabled comprehensive insights into both implementation dynamics and feasibility outcome patterns. Given the need for evidence-based interventions to improve patient involvement in healthcare organisations, our findings hold important contributions for understanding successful PE.

Participants rated the programme as largely feasible, with high scores on acceptability, appropriateness and usefulness. Feedback from interviews confirmed its relevance, clarity and practical value. However, barriers such as time constraints, limited organisational resources and a lack of institutional and leadership commitment affected implementation, particularly participation in the second module. These findings emphasise the importance of structural support, well-defined roles and leadership backup for sustainable PFAC engagement. Our insights also resonate with previous findings, where sustainability of PE often thrives on institutional support, not merely on participant motivation.25 55 56 Moreover, our investigation also revealed examples on how lack of managerial commitment potentially manifests itself through lower PFAC attendance, limited coordination and unclear responsibilities, eventually, highlighting the importance of consistent commitment of hospital leadership and organisational readiness for effective and sustainable PFAC implementation.

Our evaluation sought to test P4PS programme’s feasibility under specific, real-world conditions.40 57 58 While participants highly valued its modular structure and content, they emphasised repeatedly that its sustainable implementation is dependent on contextual facilitator factors such as organisational readiness and coordination. In healthcare organisations with clearly assigned responsibilities and highly engaged leadership, long-term implementation was deemed feasible. In contrast, fragmented communication, unclear roles and limited support were perceived as significant barriers to continuity and participation. This pattern underscores how contextual variation can moderate quantitative findings, highlighting that high feasibility ratings may not translate equally across settings. Such integration underscores the value of mixed methods for understanding successful PFAC involvement in course of dynamic and multilayered organisational changes. The observations echo frameworks that view feasibility as a dynamic, context-dependent process.59,61 And they further support the notion that normalisation of new practices requires not only perceived value but also alignment with existing intraorganisational routines, cultures and capacities.62,64 This highlights a potential ‘implementation paradox’: programmes may be well-designed and accepted but still struggle to gain traction without formalised institutional anchoring.

Challenges in PFAC recruitment and communication further reflected systemic constraints in institutionalising PE. Uncertainties about representation, coordination and decision-making authority limit persistent engagement. While some barriers, such as unclear recruitment materials, were within our responsibility, others, including undefined PFAC roles, limited support and structural constraints, point to broader systemic challenges in institutionalising PE.65 66 Moreover, these insights corroborate that the success of PE interventions not only depends on external conditions but also on internal PFAC structures, with clear governance and stakeholder communication.25

Another major contribution is our prospective evaluation that revealed among PFAC members’ significant improvements in key learning objectives of the programme, that is, safety-related knowledge and confidence. Our results corroborate that well-designed educational programmes are effective in informing and empowering patients and their representatives on PS-related concepts and approaches. Insights obtained from our qualitative interviews further contextualised these improvements, suggesting that participants not only gained knowledge but also potentially reframed their self-perception as legitimate partners in safety work. This interpretive shift might help explain the observed quantitative increase in confidence scores, and, potentially, demonstrating how learning processes can foster identity change and engagement motivation.67

However, hypothesised changes in communication and PFAC engagement competencies over time were moderate. Post-hoc, we assume that many participants were already familiar with basic knowledge in group interaction and communication, mostly likely due to their prior engagement in patient organisations. This is consistent with previous findings showing that individuals with experience in community organisations often demonstrate stronger participatory skills.68 Moreover, communication and engagement are challenging skills that typically require iterative application, consistent feedback and perpetuation for consolidation.69 70 Given the short-term nature of our programme, skill development in these domains may have been constrained. This aligns well with Bloom’s taxonomy,43 44 which highlights that higher order competencies require iterative practice and contextual integration. Future iterations of the P4PS programme could therefore benefit from longitudinal mentoring components or peer learning structures to support ongoing application and feedback. This is consistent with theories of experiential learning, which emphasise the recursive cycle of reflection and practice as essential for sustainable behavioural change.71 72

Notwithstanding the implementation challenges, participants at follow-up expressed greater motivation and confidence to engage as PFAC members in PS work and in collaboration with healthcare organisations. Many described the programme as empowering and legitimising their role, important conditions for long-term engagement.73 Whether these intentions lead to sustained behaviour remains uncertain and depends on further reinforcement and institutional integration.59 Integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence reveals that feasibility is not a static attribute but a relational topic shaped by learning, leadership and context in our programme. Altogether, our results suggest that the P4PS programme fosters basic PS competencies and initiates reflective engagement processes. However, observed behaviour-related gains highlight the need for continued development, practical application and structural embedding to ensure long-term impact.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include being, to our knowledge, the first intervention of its kind addressing the competencies and roles of PFAC members in PS, with findings providing promising initial evidence on which future research and programme development can build. Its explanatory sequential mixed methods design enabled a comprehensive understanding of effectiveness and real-world implementation challenges.28 29 51 The integration of qualitative findings further helped explain quantitative results, deepening the understanding of feasibility and guiding programme improvement. Moreover, the participatory design and evaluation process involved PFAC members and organisational representatives, ensuring relevance, patient orientation and user-centred educational design and procedures. The programme’s contents were based on needs identified prior with patients and patient advocates25 and drew further on available empirical and practical evidence.26 27 The intervention was implemented across structurally diverse PFACs in Germany, with broad distribution across regions and institutions. Outcome measures combined standardised self-reports instruments and competency-based indicators aligned with established frameworks (see the Methods section).

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our study design limits causal inferences and warrants replication with, ideally, control groups or alternative educational measures on PE. Although we sought to identify short-term and medium-term effects, our time lag of the follow-up period precludes conclusions about long-term effects. Our convenience sample included participants from oncology councils, thereby limiting generalisability to other specialties or domains of PE. Dropout rate was moderate, and participation of organisational representatives was lower than expected. Although these in themselves are useful data that highlight limitations associated with PE and PFACs in a broader sense, they should also be carefully considered when interpreting the presented results. Furthermore, PFACs varied in their maturity, structure and institutional integration. We acknowledge that policies and regulations differ substantially across nations and healthcare systems, particularly with regard to PE and involvement in healthcare organisations. This may significantly influence the formal requirements as well as needs of patients and their representatives in terms of participation, roles and expectations on involvement in organisational matters. Finally, we relied on self-reported outcomes what introduces potential bias. Since valid and reliable self-report instruments for most of the assessed constructs are still lacking, future investigations should seek to repeat our intervention and, ideally, replicate our findings.

Implications for future practice and research

Concerning healthcare practice, our findings encompass numerous important implications. First, educational formats and training programmes for PFACs should be designed in a user-friendly and modular format. Second, they should be tailored to the specific needs of this particular group, especially in topics related to PS and quality improvement. Didactic variability in content and format, such as blended learning, interactive elements and opportunities for exchange, is essential to address the diversity among members of PFACs. Third, institutional and leadership commitment remains a key success factor for implementation, including formalised roles, protected time and alignment with the existing structures.25 55 74 To ensure sustainability, measures such as permanent training and educational resources should be established with healthcare organisations, coupled with persistent exchange, evaluation and feedback—within and across institutions, including patient organisations. Finally, future implementation efforts may also consider tandem models, peer facilitation and adoption of digital tools to facilitate broader uptake. Insights from the workshop (module 2) underline these needs: instead of strategic planning, participants prioritised foundational issues such as role clarity and internal structures—pointing to the need for flexible formats and realistic expectations.75

Future research should advance our understanding of PFACs implementation within healthcare organisations, including their structure, stakeholder expectations and roles.25 Comparative studies across different organisational contexts are needed to explore variability in PFAC engagement and assess programme transferability to other healthcare settings and patient groups. This also includes further refinement of indicators and tools that allow for meaningful comparison of PFAC integration and PE at the organisational level. Longitudinal and controlled trials should evaluate long-term effects on PE and PS outcomes such as patient harm, safety culture or adverse events. Finally, utilisation of standardised tools for assessing implementation success including feasibility and effectiveness would enhance rigour and comparability of future evaluations.

Conclusion

Given the lack of comprehensive evidence on organisational-level interventions to promote PE in PS, our evaluation of the P4PS programme demonstrates promising feasibility and preliminary effectiveness. Our results provide significant insights into the facilitators (ie, role clarity, institutional support, alignment with existent structures and functions) for meaningful PE in safety-related organisational processes. To scale and sustain its impact, future efforts should focus on adaptive implementation, structural embedding and long-term evaluation across diverse healthcare settings.
