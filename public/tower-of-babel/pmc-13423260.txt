The Methodological Pitfalls Complicating the Analysis of Early Term Births
Authors: Ye'elah E. Berman, John P. Newnham, Dorota A. Doherty, Sarah V. Ward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEvaluating the success of policies aiming to reduce early term birth (37+0–38+6 weeks' gestation) is analytically challenging.ObjectivesDetermine the best method of reporting temporal changes of early term birth rates.MethodsWe analysed 54,646 singleton births at Western Australia's main tertiary hospital (2009–2019). Using interrupted time series, segmented and nominal regression, we assessed how varying gestational age outcomes, denominator, or statistical method affected conclusions regarding temporal changes (pre and post‐Initiative implementation) in the timing of birth.ResultsInterrupted time series showed yearly rates of birth at 37+0–38+6 weeks increased pre‐Initiative, with no instant level change or change in the slope post‐Initiative, regardless of denominator: all births (slope change −0.0, 95% CI −0.5, 0.5); births > 36+6 weeks (slope change: −0.4, 95% CI −1.0, 0.2), and no change to the number of early term births averted. Conversely, when examining births < 39+0 weeks, pre‐Initiative rates increased by 0.9% (95% CI 0.5, 1.3) per year, then stabilised post‐Initiative (slope change: −0.8, 95% CI −1.4, −0.3), with no change to the number of births < 39+0 weeks averted. Nominal logistic regression showed that pre‐Initiative, the rise in the early term birth rate was driven by a reduction in births at ≥ 39+0 weeks, and post‐Initiative by a reduction in preterm birth. Using segmented quantile regression, lengthening of gestational age at birth in days was observed more so at the lower percentiles, compared to the estimated counterfactual.ConclusionExamining early term birth rates in isolation can mask changes in birth timing in the presence of a changing preterm or ≥ 39+0 birth rate. Researchers should examine all births < 39+0 weeks rather than just early term births, or use nominal logistic regression. For gestational age in days as the outcome, segmented quantile regression can be used.

Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as birth < 37+0 completed weeks gestation, is the leading cause of death and disability in children less than 5 years of age globally [1, 2]. In response to increases in the preterm birth rate in the state [3], the Western Australian (WA) Preterm Birth Prevention Initiative (the Initiative) was launched in 2014 [4], with the primary aim of reducing preterm birth. In the first year following the introduction of the Initiative, the preterm birth rate was lowered across the state [4], and 5 years later it was still decreasing amongst non‐Aboriginal women at the tertiary hospital [5].

Timing of birth is a spectrum, and infants born just beyond the preterm period share many features with those born preterm [6]. Compared to birth at 39+0–40+6 weeks, birth at 37+0–38+6 weeks is associated with higher risks of morbidity [7] and mortality [8] during the perinatal period, as well as adverse outcomes during childhood [9, 10] and adulthood [11]. In light of these differences, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommend that “term” gestational ages be further broken down into early term (37+0–38+6), full term (39+0–40+6), and late term (41+0–41+6) [12, 13].

Due to the higher risks associated with early term birth, a secondary aim of the Initiative was to lower the rate of births between 37+0 and 38+6 weeks. We have previously published on assessing the efficacy of the Initiative in reducing the rates of preterm birth [4, 5], with further studies assessing reductions in the rate of early term birth ongoing. However, in evaluating the success of this policy, we faced complex analytical challenges due to the choice of denominator, method of statistical analysis and assessment of timing of birth in gestational weeks or days, plus boundary effects caused by categorisation of the continuous gestational age outcome. As such, this paper aimed to assess the effect that varying these parameters had on conclusions regarding changes in the early term birth rates. We hypothesised that different analytical methods and outcome categorisations would result in different conclusions about temporal changes in early term birth rates.

Methods

Study Population and Data Sources

This study used all singleton births to non‐Aboriginal women, who gave birth at the main tertiary hospital, King Edward Memorial Hospital, in WA between 2009 and 2019. Pregnancy data were obtained from the Midwives Notification System, a register of all births in WA attended by a midwife and occurring at ≥ 20 weeks gestation, or with a birthweight of ≥ 400 g.

Outcomes

The outcome for this study was gestational age at birth. For interrupted time series analyses, we examined rates of birth at < 37+0 weeks gestation (preterm), 37+0–38+6 weeks gestation (early term) and < 39+0 weeks gestation (early birth). For segmented nominal logistic regression, we assessed all births, categorised into < 37+0 weeks gestation, 37+0–38+6 weeks and ≥ 39+0 weeks. We also assessed changes in the gestational age at birth in days.

Exposure

The primary exposure was time‐period, contrasting birth rates from before (2009–2014) and after (2014–2019) implementation of the Initiative in May 2014. We manipulated the outcome category (< 37+0 weeks vs. 37+0–38+6 weeks vs. < 39+0 weeks vs. gestational age in days), the choice of denominator (all births vs. births > 36+6 weeks), and the type of analysis employed (interrupted time series vs. segmented nominal logistic or quantile regression) and assessed the effect this had on our conclusions regarding changes in the rate of birth at early term associated with the Initiative.

Statistical Analysis

Frequency distributions for demographic characteristics, maternal conditions and obstetric history were reported by time‐period (pre‐ and post‐Initiative). Interrupted time series analyses estimated changes in the trends and levels of the proportion of births that occurred at < 37+0, 37+0–38+6 and < 39+0 weeks gestation, following implementation of the Initiative. By extending the trend line from the pre‐Initiative time‐period, we created the counterfactual estimated trend line, representing the estimated number of births to occur at these gestations in the absence of the Initiative. The counterfactual was then compared to the line of the best fit that was plotted based on the actual births occurring at these gestations post‐Initiative. Multiplying the difference between the two lines by the actual number of births per month gave the estimated number of births averted in each gestational age group in the post‐Initiative period, with 95% confidence intervals (CI) derived using bootstrapping methods with 1000 iterations. Separate slopes (the average yearly change in the birth rate) for both the pre‐Initiative and post‐Initiative time‐periods were also derived. The percentage changes between the pre‐ and post‐Initiative slopes and one reflecting the immediate effect of the introduction of the Initiative (at the breakpoint in May 2014) were also calculated. For each analysis, we used a different combination of gestational age outcome and denominator, contrasted the resulting slope and change coefficients, and reported the number of births averted at that gestation.

Unadjusted segmented nominal logistic regression with time in months, intervention (pre‐ vs. post‐Initiative) and a time*intervention interaction was used to simultaneously model the predicted probabilities of birth at < 37+0, 37+0–38+6 and ≥ 39+0 weeks' gestation. Yearly changes in the log odds of preterm birth (< 37+0) and early term birth at 37+0–38+6 weeks, compared to birth at ≥ 39+0 weeks, were reported for the pre‐ and post‐Initiative time‐periods, as well as the level change in log odds at the time of the Initiative. Births averted at each gestation over the post‐Initiative period were calculated using the same methods as the interrupted time series analyses.

Segmented quantile regression, as described by Moineddin and colleagues in 2022 [14], was performed using the SAS function PROC QUANTREG, with the outcome as gestational age in days and covariates for time, intervention, and a time*intervention interaction included [14]. This methodology allowed us to plot the predicted values for the gestational length in days at the 25th, 40th, 60th, and 75th percentile of each month. These percentiles were chosen as they covered the gestational age range of interest. As in the previous analyses, the model yielded pre‐Initiative and post‐Initiative slopes representing the yearly change in gestational length in days at each quantile. We also reported a level change at the time of the Initiative, a slope change between the pre‐ and post‐Initiative slopes, and the difference in predicted gestational length between the actual values post‐Initiative and those predicted by the counterfactual line, averaged across the post Initiative time‐period. SAS (version 9.4, Cary, NC: SAS Institute Inc.) statistical software package was used for data analyses.

Missing Data and Sensitivity Analysis

The Index of Relative Socio‐Economic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD) score was missing for 993 (1.8%) births categorised as ‘unknown’ for reporting. There were 9227 (16.9%) births to multiparous women for whom a record of their previous pregnancy was not available. These women were classified as having an unknown history of preterm birth and no prior stillbirth. All other data were complete. Only monthly rates are reported, as sensitivity analyses indicated that these produced similar results to yearly rates.

Results

There were 64,993 births at the state's main tertiary hospital between 2009 and 2019, 55,065 of which were singleton births to non‐Aboriginal women. A further 419 births were excluded as terminations of pregnancy, and the final sample size consisted of 54,646 births. Table 1 shows the number and percentage of births in the study population by year. The rate of preterm birth (< 37+0 weeks gestation) increased from 18.5% in 2009 to a maximum of 20.1% in 2013, before reducing to a minimum of 14.7% in 2019. The rate of early term birth (37+0–38+6 weeks' gestation) was lowest in 2009 (27.7%) and rose steadily to a maximum of 33.3% in 2018. Births that were not considered early (≥ 39+0 weeks) reduced in the pre‐Initiative period (53.8% in 2009 to 48.9% in 2013) and then stabilised in the post Initiative period (50.7% in 2014 to 52.5% in 2019). Women who gave birth were slightly older and had slightly higher rates of pre‐existing diabetes in the post‐Initiative time‐period, compared to the pre‐Initiative time‐period (Table 2). Post‐Initiative, they also had lower rates of asthma and other pre‐existing conditions, fewer previous children, and substantially lower rates of smoking.

Comment

Principal Findings

The results of this paper demonstrate that when assessing temporal changes in the timing of birth at early term, the categorisation of the outcome and the method of analysis used can affect interpretation of changes associated with interventions to reduce early birth. When modelling gestational age at early term alone, our results showed a year‐on‐year increase in the proportion of births at 37+0–38+6 weeks, with negligible change in trajectory resulting from the Initiative. Consequently, no early‐term births were averted. Analysis of birth rates at < 39+0 and < 37+0 weeks showed that in fact the composition of early term births had changed substantially. Segmented nominal logistic and quantile regression were proposed as alternatives to interrupted time series analysis.

Strengths of the Study

Our study included 11 years of data covering all births at WA's major tertiary hospital, providing a large sample size and ample statistical power to perform robust time‐series analyses. Data were drawn from the Midwives Notification System, which is a validated register of all births in WA that are attended by a midwife, ensuring near complete case ascertainment. The study is also directly policy relevant, using the example of the Initiative to demonstrate how methodological choices can alter conclusions about policy effectiveness.

Limitations of the Data

Our study was limited by the lack of concurrent controls, but given we were working with administrative data, our study design was appropriate. We also only used data from King Edward Memorial Hospital as the preterm birth rate had decreased substantially at this site, and consequently it made an excellent case study for demonstrating how examining the early term birth rate in isolation can be misleading in the presence of a changing preterm birth rate. As King Edward Memorial Hospital is the major tertiary hospital in WA, it receives a significant number of high‐risk cases from across the whole state. As such, it has a much higher preterm birth rate than both the state as a whole and the national average, and is not representative of the state's preterm birth figures. As our study used administrative data, we do not have specific information on what method was used for dating of gestational age, but the gestational age in weeks variable used was calculated based on a validated algorithm using a combination of last menstrual period, expected due date and date of birth [16]. We also did not perform adjusted analyses, despite having identified several risk factors for early birth (including smoking, maternal age, diabetes, asthma and parity) that were different between the pre‐ and post‐Initiative periods. As the purpose of this paper was to demonstrate different methods for examining temporal changes in early birth, and not to evaluate the effectiveness of the WA Preterm Birth Prevention Initiative, they were not considered relevant to include here. Adjusted analyses examining the Initiative's effectiveness have been published elsewhere [4, 5, 8, 17].

Interpretation

In reporting results from only early term (37+0–38+6 weeks) and using interrupted time series analyses, an important shift in the timing of birth was obscured. Analyses of births at 37+0–38+6 weeks (regardless of denominator) showed no notable effect on the rate of births at early term following implementation of the Initiative. In contrast, examination of the rates of births at < 37+0 and < 39+0 weeks' gestation showed that prior to the Initiative, rates of both preterm birth and early birth at < 39+0 weeks were increasing each year. Then, following the Initiative, there was a change in trajectory and the change in these rates plateaued, with 1246 preterm births averted across the post‐Initiative time period. From these results, it is clear that the composition of the early term birth rate had changed substantially. Where previously the increase in early term birth was driven entirely by a reduction in births ≥ 39+0 weeks, after the Initiative, the increase in the early term birth rate was driven by the reduced preterm birth rate, as gestation length was increased and pushed into the early term timeframe. These results make it clear that it is not appropriate to assess whether interventions aiming to change the timing of birth have been effective by using early term birth alone as an outcome. Analysing births < 39+0 weeks cannot directly answer questions about associations between the introduction of an intervention and the rate of early term birth because the preterm births are also included in this categorisation. However, it did show us that there was a flattening in the year‐on‐year increase in the rate of overall birth < 39+0 weeks, which is of significant benefit.

The analytical problem we have identified is generalisable to any continuous outcome that has been categorised and has a lower and upper bound, indicating that a different method of analysis, such as nominal logistic regression, must be employed. Nominal logistic regression models are a preferable method of analysis as they have the advantage of being able to simultaneously model all three gestational age categories, and consequently the previously observed shifts in gestational age are apparent. However, while predicted probabilities can provide a convenient visual description of temporal changes in the timing of birth, only changes in log odds associated with the Initiative (or any intervention) can be reported. This makes year‐on‐year changes between time periods difficult to interpret, as they are in relation to the reference category (births ≥ 39+0 weeks gestation) rather than stand‐alone rates that can be reported as in an interrupted time series analysis. Probabilities can still be compared to the counterfactual predictions, and when combined with the actual birth rates that occurred, the estimated number of preterm and early term births averted can still be calculated. The results of the segmented nominal logistic regression were similar to those from the four interrupted time series analyses, showing many preterm births averted and no change in early term births. However, the use of nominal logistic regression makes it clear that the composition of early term births was entirely different between the pre‐ and post‐Initiative time periods and that despite there being no change in the early term birth rate, the Initiative did have an effect on the timing of birth.

Analysis of gestational age in days is similarly complicated. One cannot analyse the average gestation at birth across the whole gestational age range because interventions aimed at optimising perinatal outcomes seek to increase short gestational ages to as close to ≥ 39+0 weeks as possible, but do not seek to lengthen longer gestations. Analysing the average gestational age at < 39+0 weeks or at 37+0–38+6 weeks is also not an adequate solution because these populations are not fixed. A reduction in births ≥ 39+0 weeks can result in an artificial increase in the mean gestational age in days, while a reduction in the preterm birth rate can result in an artificial decrease. The use of segmented quantile regression can circumvent some of these problems. As this method doesn't require categorisation of gestational age, it allows us to model changes at a range of quantiles so that it is apparent if the intervention of interest affects different parts of the gestational age range differently. Our results showed that the trajectories of the gestational age in days in all calculated percentiles were getting shorter in the pre‐Initiative time period, with this trajectory changing in the post‐Initiative time period. At the 60th and 75th quantiles, which both fell above 39+0 weeks, the difference between the actual and counterfactual lines was smaller than at the 25th and 40th percentiles, and at the 60th percentile there was no change in the slope. This makes sense because once a pregnancy reaches 39+0 weeks (considered full term), attempts would not be made to delay delivery if spontaneous labour occurs or complications necessitating delivery arose. The only percentile in which there was a reduction in gestational age in days across the post‐Initiative period was the 25th percentile, falling within the 37 week gestational age period.

Conclusions

When assessing changes in the timing of early term birth, careful consideration must be given to the impact of denominators and boundaries imposed on continuous outcomes, as they can result in misleading conclusions, obscuring important results. Temporal changes in rates of birth at early term cannot be accurately commented on without considering the broader context in analyses. For analyses using completed weeks gestation as the outcome, we recommend either analysing all early births < 39+0 weeks instead of early term births. If early term births are of particular interest, we recommend using nominal logistic regression to simultaneously model probabilities of preterm birth, early term birth, and birth ≥ 39+0 weeks. If the outcome is gestational age at birth in days, segmented quantile regression can show how interventions can have differential effects at different gestational ages. We are unaware of any other papers that have investigated this issue. This paper makes an important contribution to the methodological literature for those attempting to assess temporal changes in the timing of birth.
