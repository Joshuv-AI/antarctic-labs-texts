Multi-UAV Flood Monitoring via CVT with Gaussian Mixture of Density Functions for Coverage Control
Jie Song, Yang Bai, Mikhail Svinin, Naoki Wakamiya

Abstract
This study presents a control strategy for coordinating multiple unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to monitor unknown flood regions and estimate the extent of inundation. The proposed method adopts a density-driven coverage framework based on Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT), in which the density function is modeled using a Gaussian Mixture of Density Functions (GMDF). This formulation provides a more accurate characterization of inundated areas compared to conventional axis-aligned Gaussian models. The performance of the two density modeling approaches is systematically evaluated under different UAV fleet sizes (16, 20, and 24), with multiple simulation trials conducted in the ROS/Gazebo environment. The results show that the GMDF-based formulation consistently achieves higher coverage rates, demonstrating its effectiveness in enhancing flood monitoring and improving UAV spatial distribution.

II
Flood monitoring strategy

For monitoring an unknown workspace, we design a multi-UAV controller to detect and optimally cover inundated regions. Each UAV is modeled as a mass point in the high-level controller, operating at a uniform altitude and equipped with a high-resolution camera that provides a bird’s-eye view of a limited area. The Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT) technique is then employed as the main tool to design the monitoring strategy and obtain an accurate estimation of the entire inundated workspace.

II-B

Flood detection and covering

The detection and monitoring of the inundation areas formulate a coverage problem, which aims to allocate multiple UAVs optimally within the flood region. Different from conventional coverage problems, the challenge of flood monitoring is that the environment (distribution of inundation areas) is not only initially unknown to the UAV but also time-varying. In our framework, this time-varying characteristic is addressed through the iterative update of the density function: once a UAV detects a flood, a new peak is generated whose magnitude depends on the proportion of inundation observed within its sensing range. As the density function evolves with new detections, the corresponding Voronoi tessellation and UAV goal positions are updated accordingly. Through this iterative process, the UAV team can continuously adapt their configuration to dynamic changes in the flood regions.

Coverage controller design

Based on the locational cost function (

4

\dot{\bm{p}}_{i}=-k\left(\bm{p}_{i}-\bm{c}_{i}\right),\quad k>0,

(9)

where
\bm{c}_{i}
is the density-weighted centroid of region
V_{i}
. This controller drives each UAV toward its centroid, ensuring that the swarm converges asymptotically to a Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT).

Since the density function

is updated in real time according to flood detections, the centroids

The representation of flood dynamics in Gazebo is particularly challenging, as the platform does not natively simulate liquids. To overcome this, the external environment is modeled with GeoBlender for terrain generation, while the Gazebo Actor plugin is used to animate vertical variations of the water surface. This setup allows flood regions to expand or contract during simulation, producing realistic inundation effects while remaining computationally efficient. Such a design ensures that the simulator is well-suited for testing multi-UAV coverage control strategies under dynamic flood conditions.

The objective of these simulations is to assess the ability of the UAV swarm to coordinate and achieve effective coverage of flood-affected workspaces. At the start of each simulation, a fleet of 16 UAVs is deployed and initially positioned in a uniform distribution across the designated workspace, which has been pre-identified as susceptible to flooding. However, the specific locations of inundated regions within the workspace remain unknown to the UAVs. Upon reaching their initial positions, the UAVs activate their onboard cameras to detect flooded areas through image segmentation techniques. The extracted information is then shared among UAVs via ROS topics, enabling cooperative situational awareness. Based on the shared data, a density function is dynamically generated to approximate the flood distribution and guide UAV motion. The control strategy continuously updates UAV positions to adapt to the detected flood boundaries, ensuring that the swarm converges toward a near-optimal coverage configuration. It should be noted that in the simulations, the flood regions themselves are time-varying, gradually expanding or contracting during the monitoring process. Although these variations may not be visually prominent in static snapshots, they are reflected in the evolution of the density function and the corresponding UAV reconfigurations, which together demonstrate the system’s ability to respond to dynamic flood changes.

The integration of cooperative perception, density-based coverage control, and CVT-based redistribution allows the UAVs to respond to both large-scale and localized inundations, thereby ensuring reliable monitoring of dynamic flood environments.

IV
Conclusions

This work presented a control strategy that enables multiple UAVs to monitor unknown flood regions and distribute themselves to achieve near-optimal coverage while concurrently estimating the inundated areas. The method relies on the CVT framework and a perception-driven density function that updates online as new observations arrive. Once the CVT structure is formed, the associated density distribution provides an estimate of the flooded regions and guides UAV reconfiguration.

The effectiveness of the proposed approach was validated in simulation with different shapes and time-varying extents of flood regions, showing that the UAV configuration converges toward CVT-based deployments and achieves reliable coverage of the workspace. A comparative study was conducted between two density-modeling choices: an axis-aligned Gaussian density function and a Gaussian mixture of density functions. Across three fleet sizes (16, 20, and 24 UAVs) with ten trials per setting, the Gaussian mixture model consistently achieved higher coverage rates for the same number of UAVs (see Fig. 3(e)), indicating that it provides more accurate guidance for UAV coverage control.

While the approach demonstrates strong performance, several limitations remain. In particular, as the fleet size increases, the system convergence time becomes significantly longer, which restricts the scalability of the current centralized framework to large-scale UAV deployments. Moreover, the reliance on centralized computation makes the system sensitive to individual UAV failures or communication disruptions.

As future work, we will extend the method toward a decentralized flood-monitoring scheme that maintains coverage performance while reducing convergence delays and improving resilience against vehicle dropouts and network limitations. This will involve distributed density estimation, decentralized CVT computation, and fault-tolerant reallocation mechanisms, thereby enabling scalable and reliable multi-UAV flood monitoring in real-world scenarios.
