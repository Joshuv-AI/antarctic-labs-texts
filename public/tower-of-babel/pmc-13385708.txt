Temporal changes in surface tension guide microtubule organization and accurate asymmetric division of Arabidopsis zygotes
Authors: Zichen Kang, Sakumi Nakagawa, Hikari Matsumoto, Yukitaka Ishimoto, Tomonobu Nonoyama, Yuga Hanaki, Satoru Tsugawa, Minako Ueda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In most plants, the zygote divides asymmetrically to define the body axis. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the zygote undergoes polar cell elongation with maintaining a transverse band of cortical microtubules (MTs) at the growing cell tip. How the MT band is maintained at the actively renewing tip and whether it contributes to the asymmetric zygote division remain elusive. By combining live-cell imaging, mechanical simulation, and various perturbation analyses, we demonstrate a bidirectionally interactive feedback in which the MT band forms a mechanical frame to support polar zygote elongation, and this growth increases the calculated surface tension at the zygote tip to induce MT band formation. Furthermore, after zygote elongation is complete, the MT band guides another MT band, preprophase band, to determine the accurate cell division site. Therefore, the MT band is maintained via mechanical feedback with polar cell growth, ensuring the proper asymmetric cell division of the zygote.

Introduction

Initiation of the main body axis is one of the first steps in developing an organism from a unicellular zygote. In most plants, ontogeny begins with the zygotic asymmetric cell division that prefigures the apical–basal axis. In Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), the zygote undergoes polar elongation before dividing asymmetrically; thus, the apical–basal direction is already evident during zygote growth1. Based on live-cell imaging of the zygote, we recently showed that the zygote tip, where the sperm cell fused to the egg cell, grows in the apical direction2. In plant cells that undergo polar elongation, such as root hairs and pollen tubes, actin filaments (F-actin) and microtubules (MTs) align longitudinally along the direction of cell growth3,4. Zygotes have longitudinally aligned F-actin but form a transverse band of cortical MTs at the subapical region of the growing cell tip5. The subapical MT band is not typical in polarly growing cells of angiosperms, but a similar structure is found in the elongating tip of protonema of southern maidenhair fern (Adiantum capillus-veneris)6. Both in Arabidopsis zygote and fern protonema, MT disruption caused tip swelling, implying an important role of the MT band in supporting cell elongation of a particular type of polarly elongating cells5,7. But the mechanism of the subapical MT band formation and its role in the zygote polarization are both unclear.

Another well-known type of MT band is the preprophase band (PPB), which is formed at the future cell division site in plant cells. The PPB is generally formed at the cortical region surrounding nucleus8. Indeed, the division plane is formed where the migrating nucleus arrives in asymmetrically dividing cells of Arabidopsis, such as initial cells of stomata and lateral root9,10. In the zygote, the nucleus migrates apically along F-actin, and the PPB and cell division plane form at the apical region5, but the relationship between these structures and the subapical MT band is unknown.

In this study, we found that the subapical MT band is retained at a constant position relative to the growing tip, and the MT band forms a mechanical frame to restrict the zygote growth direction. By combining quantitative live-cell imaging, computer simulation, and mutant analyses, we demonstrated that the polar zygote elongation induces a temporal change in calculated surface tension at the subapical region, which works as the driving force to maintain the MT band. Furthermore, after zygote growth is complete, PPB is formed near the MT band site, prior to the zygote division. These findings suggest a mechanical feedback between cell growth and MT organization underlies not only zygote polarization, but also the asymmetric division of the zygote.

Results

The MT band is maintained at a constant distance from the apical cell pole during zygote elongation

We analyzed the dynamics of zygote growth and MT structure in Arabidopsis based on time-lapse observations using MT/nucleus markers and two-photon excitation microscopy (2PEM) (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Movie 1). The MT/nucleus marker contains an MT marker (EC1p::Clover-TUA6, expressing a green fluorescent protein Clover fused with TUBULIN ALPHA6 under the control of EGG CELL1 promoter) and a nucleus marker (ABI4p::H2B-tdTomato, expressing HISTONE H2B fused with a red fluorescent protein tdTomato under the control of ABA INSENSITIVE4 promoter). As shown previously, we detected the subapical MT band during zygote elongation5. The MTs then form the mitotic apparatus, including the PPB, spindle, and phragmoplast, leading to asymmetric cell division (see below). We performed quantitative analysis of the images to capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of cell growth, such as cell contour, cell length (L), and tip radius (re), as well as detecting the MT band based on truncated Gaussian fitting of MT signal distribution along the cell centerline (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Movie 1, see Methods). As previously observed, the zygote growth rate (dL/dt) first increased and then decreased, while cell tip radius decreased and then increased, resulting in a slightly constricted cell shape (Fig. 1c, d)2. The subapical MT band is required for a mechanical frame to maintain the shape of the zygote tip

To investigate the role of the MT band in zygote growth, we analyzed the effects of a MT polymerization inhibitor (oryzalin) and a MT depolymerization inhibitor (taxol) (Fig. 2). In contrast to untreated zygotes (Fig. 2a–c), the oryzalin-treated zygotes swelled, with a continuous increase in cell radius and a decrease in growth rate, revealing the loss of radial growth restriction (Fig. 2e–g, Supplementary Movie 2)5. MT bands disappeared and were not detectable by Gaussian fitting (Fig. 2h; compare to Fig. 2d). By contrast, taxol treatment distorted the cell shape, leading to non-uniform growth (Fig. 2i–k, Supplementary Movie 3). The MT bands occasionally became wider and reached toward the cell apex (Fig. 2l). Cell division failed in oryzalin-treated zygotes (Fig. 2e), likely due to blocked formation of the mitotic apparatus. Taxol treatment led to a longer apical cell (Fig. 2i) and thus a more symmetric division compared to untreated zygotes (Fig. 2m), suggesting that the MT band contributes to the determination of asymmetric cell division. These features were observed in all samples examined (16 oryzalin-treated zygotes and 12 taxol-treated zygotes; Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). In general, cortical MTs are known to support rigid cell wall formation by guiding cellulose synthase complexes11. Therefore, to investigate whether the subapical MT band functions as a mechanical frame through cell wall formation, we performed dual observation of the MT/nucleus marker and cellulose-specific dye Calcofluor White (CFW) (Supplementary Fig. 4a–e). Ovules expressing the MT/nucleus marker were fixed, cleared using ClearSeeAlpha, and stained with the CFW12. Although fixation-induced damage slightly distorted the zygotes and attenuated MT signals, clear CFW staining was observed in the basal and lateral regions of elongating zygotes, whereas little or no signal was detected in the apical region above the MT band (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Movie 4). In dividing zygotes after the MT band had disappeared, CFW signals were observed across the entire cell surface, including the apical region and newly forming cell plate (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d), and this uniform signal persisted after division (Supplementary Fig. 4e). Since transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a continuous outer layer covering the entire cell surface at all stages examined before and after fertilization (Supplementary Fig. 4f), other cell wall materials, such as pectin and callose, might also contribute to maintaining the zygote tip structure, as in other polar growing cell13.

The MT band is located at the subapical region where the time changes of estimated surface tension reach a maximum

Next, we explored how the MT band is maintained at a constant position in the actively renewing zygote tip. MTs are proposed to act as tension sensors; thus, cortical MT alignment can change in response to cell surface tension14,15. Therefore, we estimated the mechanical properties of growing zygotes by combining computer modeling with time-lapse imaging. We recently found that zygote growth can be reconstructed using the viscoelasto-plastic deformation model16, which simulates cell morphogenesis (deformation from the initial to next shape) based on two mechanical parameters: the expansion force from inside the cell (turgor pressure: P) and the deformability of the cell surface (surface extensibility: Φ) (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 5a, b, see “Methods”)17. Although surface extensibility is a complex variable with spatial distribution, we determined that a single parameter defining the range of extensible area (extensibility range: lg), combined with turgor pressure, is sufficient to describe hemispherical tip deformation of the zygote (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d)16. Therefore, we searched for a suitable combination of P and lg at each time point that explains the actual dynamics of zygote deformation, i.e., changes in the growth rate and tip radius obtained from the wild type time-lapse data shown in Fig. 1 (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Fig. 5e), and reconstructed the model zygote (compare ‘Data’ and ‘Model’ in Fig. 3d). Based on the model zygote, we calculated the spatial distribution of circumferential surface tension (one of the surface mechanical stresses in the circumferential direction: σθ) with respect to the surface distance from the zygote apex. The value below the subapical region was clearly larger than that at the tip at all time points examined (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Movie 5). To precisely estimate the temporal change of σθ, we calculated the time derivative of the surface tension (Dσθ/Dt), considering the zygote tip as a moving coordinate system of surface points (see “Methods”). This time change markedly increased at the subapical region, which corresponds to the MT band position (Fig. 3f, g, Supplementary Movie 5), leading to the hypothesis that the MT band is maintained at the subapical region by the temporal change (i.e., the constant increase) of surface tension. Moreover, the temporal average of MT band width (6.6 µm, Fig. 3g) was wider than the region of maximum time change (2.2 µm, Fig. 3f). Tension-induced MT band formation requires balanced MT polymerization and depolymerization

To evaluate the usefulness of the viscoelasto-plastic deformation model and to further examine the relationship between cell growth, tension dynamics, and MT organization, we searched for a suitable combination of P and lg that reproduces the actual deformation of oryzalin- and taxol-treated zygotes shown in Fig. 2 (Fig. 4a–c, g–i). In oryzalin-treated zygotes, the estimated lg, the range of tip extensible area, ranged from 7.6 to 9.6 µm (Fig. 4b), which was larger than that in untreated zygotes (from 5.9 to 8.6 µm; Fig. 3c). In contrast, taxol-treated zygotes showed a smaller lg ranging from 5.4 to 6.5 µm (Fig. 4h). We further calculated the spatial distribution of estimated surface tension (σθ) and its time change (Dσθ/Dt) with respect to the zygote apex. Similar to untreated zygotes, both oryzalin- and taxol-treated samples exhibited an increase in Dσθ/Dt in the subapical region (Fig. 4d, e, j, k, Supplementary Movies 6, 7). However, as noted above, an MT band was not detected in this region in oryzalin-treated zygotes (Fig. 4f), whereas taxol-treated zygotes displayed a broader MT band (8.1 µm, Fig. 4l) than in untreated samples (6.6 µm, Fig. 3g). These results suggest that while growth-dependent changes in tension still occur under both drug treatments, abnormal MT stability disrupts the proper organization of MT bands in response to the mechanical cues.

Taken together, these findings lead us to propose bidirectional mechanical feedback between MT organization and cell growth: the subapical MT band forms a mechanical frame that restricts radial expansion at the zygote apex, while the growth-dependent increase in surface tension promotes MT band formation through the regulation of MT turnover.

Continuous zygote elongation is required to maintain the subapical MT band

To test this mechanical feedback hypothesis, we examined the importance of growth-dependent changes in tension for MT band maintenance (Fig. 5). For this purpose, we introduced the MT/nucleus marker described above into an Arabidopsis mutant in the mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase YODA (YDA), yda-2991, in which zygote growth arrests prematurely18,19. Live-cell imaging of yda showed that the subapical MT band was initially present in young zygotes (before 05:20 in Fig. 5d; compare to Fig. 5a, Supplementary Movie 8). To evaluate this further, we used the viscoelasto-plastic deformation model to identify parameter combinations of P and lg that reproduce the observed deformation of the yda zygote (Fig. 5g–i), and calculated the circumferential surface tension to compare with the MT band pattern (Fig. 5g–l, Supplementary Movie 9). We found that MT bands were maintained at the subapical region as long as cell elongation caused a large time change of estimated tension (Fig. 5k, l). Once this time change diminished (at 03:20), the MT band also disappeared within a few hours (by 05:40; Fig. 5k, l). These results support the notion that growth–driven changes in tension are critical for the maintenance of the subapical MT band.

Although it has been proposed that MTs can autonomously sense tension via tubulin itself, some regulatory factors are also known to rearrange MT patterns in response to tension; a well-known factor is the MT-severing enzyme KATANIN1 (KTN1)20,21. Therefore, we examined the behavior of zygotes of the KTN1 mutant ktn1-2, which shows abnormal cell division patterns during embryogenesis22. Since ktn1 homozygous plants produce very few seeds, we used self-fertilized ktn1 heterozygous mother plants, in which approximately one-quarter of the embryos exhibited cell division defects (25.0%, n = 64; Supplementary Fig. 7a). Similarly, approximately one-quarter of the zygotes derived from ktn1 heterozygous mothers expressing the MT/nucleus marker showed delayed phragmoplast expansion (28.6%, n = 14), consistent with the ktn1 phenotype reported in other tissues23, but none of these zygotes showed detectable abnormalities in MT band maintenance, cell elongation, or subsequent PPB formation (Supplementary Fig. 7b, Supplementary Movie 10). These results suggest that KTN1 may not be a crucial factor in the mechanical feedback between cell growth and MT organization.

MT bands guide PPB formation prior to asymmetric division of the zygote

In yda mutant zygotes, the PPB appeared, while after the disappearance of the subapical MT band (at 10:40; Fig. 5d, f, Supplementary Movie 8). The PPB formed away from the subapical region, resulting in a more symmetric than wild type (Fig. 5m). Since MT stabilization due to taxol treatment also affected asymmetric cell division (Fig. 2m), we tested whether the MT band is involved in determining the position of the PPB by examining the positional relationships among the MT band, PPB, nucleus, and cell division plane in wild-type zygotes (Fig. 6a, b). Before cell division, the PPB appeared slightly below the region containing the subapical MT band, above the nucleus (Figs. 1e, 6a and Supplementary Movie 1). The gap was subtle but consistent, as shown by significant differences of the center positions of the MT band, PPB, and nucleus (Fig. 6b). The spindle formed between the PPB and nucleus, establishing the cell division plane (Fig. 6a). In wild-type zygotes, the MT band and the nucleus are located close to each other, making it difficult to distinguish their individual contributions to PPB positioning. To overcome this limitation, we focused on the shoot gravitropism2-1 (sgr2-1) mutant, in which the nucleus remains distant from the apical region of the zygote19. The SGR2 gene encodes a phospholipase A1-like protein localized to the vacuolar membrane, and in sgr2, large and rigid vacuoles occupy the apical region in the zygote, preventing the nucleus from reaching the apical pole24–26. We performed live-cell imaging of MT/nucleus marker in the sgr2 background (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 8a–c and Supplementary Movie 11). During zygote elongation, the subapical MT band was properly maintained, while the large apical vacuole blocked the nucleus from approaching the apical region (Fig. 6c). The PPB formed at the subapical MT band site, rather than around the nucleus, indicating an effect of the subapical MT band on PPB positioning.

In sgr2 zygotes, the large vacuole persistently occupied the apical region during mitosis, and unlike in wild type, the spindle did not shift toward the PPB site (Fig. 6c). Consequently, the division plane formed at the nuclear position, resulting in a more symmetric division than in wild type (Fig. 6d). This situation hindered the assessment of the direct effect of the PPB on division plane positioning. Therefore, we then focused on the core regulator of PPB formation, TON1 RECRUITING MOTIF (TRM), whose triple mutant (trm678) lacks a visible PPB27. As bright transgenic plants were not obtained with the above-mentioned MT/nucleus marker in this background, we constructed a modified MT marker by replacing Clover with the brighter fluorescent protein tdStayGold (EC1p::tdStayGold-TUA6)28, and combined it with the above nuclear marker (ABI4p::H2B-tdTomato), hereafter referred to as MT(SG)/nucleus.

We confirmed that wild-type zygotes carrying the MT(SG)/nucleus marker showed comparable behavior to those expressing the original MT/nucleus marker (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 8d–f; compare to Figs. 6a, 1a–e). The trm678 zygotes showed proper cell elongation and retained subapical MT band, but this MT signal dispersed without forming the PPB before cell division (at 12:00; Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 8g–i, and Supplementary Movie 12). Consequently, the spindle and division plane formed at the nuclear position, resulting in a more symmetric division than wild type (Fig. 6f, g). These results indicate that in the absence of a PPB positioned apically relative to the nucleus, the spindle fails to relocate upward.

Taken together, our data indicate that the MT band contributes to PPB positioning and that the PPB, in turn, adjusts the spindle positioning to ensure precise asymmetric zygote division in wild-type Arabidopsis.

Discussion

We propose a bidirectionally interactive feedback mechanism whereby polar zygote growth produces a large time change in the estimated surface tension, which in turn restricts the zygote growth direction via MT band formation, ultimately supporting accurate asymmetric cell division (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Schematic representation of dynamics and the proposed mechanism.Diagram summarizing the mechanical feedback of polar zygote elongation and MT organization, and the role of the MT band in supporting the accurate asymmetric zygote division.

In most animal and plant cells, the dynamic behavior of the cortical cytoskeleton is crucial for growth dynamics. In plants, the rigid cell wall generally covers the entire cell surface. In contrast, we found that the zygote tip lacks a clear CFW signal, suggesting that the MT band itself and/or cell wall components whose synthesis is induced by the MT band may act as a mechanical frame to restrict the direction of zygote growth. However, the precise spatiotemporal relationship between the MT band and the cell wall, and the wall materials that maintain the zygote tip structure, remains unclear. Currently, no live-imaging method has been established for simultaneously visualizing both cell wall synthesis and MT band dynamics in zygotes, as commonly used cell wall dyes for living cells, such as propidium iodide and Pontamine Fast Scarlet 4B, cannot penetrate ovules29. Therefore, establishing such techniques, for example by generating fluorescent markers to visualize the region of cellulose synthesis in zygotes, and combining them with MT inhibitors, will help elucidate the role of the cell wall in mechanical feedback during zygote growth. In addition, it will be important to investigate cell wall components other than cellulose, particularly pectin, since a mutation in NIMNA, which encodes a polygalacturonase (a pectin-degrading enzyme), has been reported to impair zygote elongation30. Furthermore, in the tobacco pollen tube (another type of polar growing cell), the apical wall is largely composed of viscoelastic, methyl-esterified pectins, where cellulose is not detected, and this pectin-rich wall is thought to protect the growing cell tip from rupture during rapid cell elongation31–33.

We also showed that MT alignment depends on cell growth. The MTs were proposed to function as a tension sensor in various experiments. For example, in an in vivo experiment, forcing protoplasts to change shape led to MT rearrangement34. In the protoplasts, the tension-induced MT pattern was reoriented after several hours, indicating that MTs can respond to temporal changes in tension, rather than the absolute magnitude of tension, with a time lag. Similarly, in the current study, the MT band in yda zygotes disappeared a few hours after the loss of a large time change of estimated surface tension. Our observation that the MT band was wider than the region of maximum time change of estimated tension would also show the delayed response of MTs to the changing stress pattern. In plant tissues, where neighboring cells are tightly anchored by cell walls, damage to and growth of adjacent cells cause external changes in tension and promote MT-dependent cell morphogenesis to adjust the overall tissue shape15,35. By contrast, the zygote is surrounded by a fluidic giant cell, the endosperm, and thus would freely grow without significant external forces. Therefore, the zygote may induce internal changes in tension by regulating its own growth rate, resulting in cell polarization and, thus, asymmetric cell division. In this stand-alone situation, it seems reasonable that the zygote adopts a simple mechanical mechanism to autonomously amplify subtle polarity information, such as sperm entry from the apical end, leading to the formation of the apical–basal axis.

We also found that the MT band finally shifted toward the basal region, where the PPB formed, and that a division plane formed between the PPB and the nucleus. These events were overlooked in the previous study when the zygotic MT dynamics were first live-imaged5, but were discovered in this study by improved image quantification techniques, such as the MT signal tracking by Gaussian fitting and precise comparison of PPB and nucleus locations. Based on these findings, we proposed that the MT band has a dual function: supporting the polar zygote elongation via mechanical feedback and guiding PPB position for influencing cell division asymmetry (Fig. 7).

In zygotes, the nucleus migrates polarly to the apical region along longitudinally aligned F-actin, and thus disruption of F-actin leads to the nucleus being positioned at the cell center5. In such zygotes, cell division occurs at the nuclear position5, as observed in yda zygotes, indicating that nuclear position is a major determinant of the division plane. Accordingly, the MT band would act as a fine-tuning factor, guiding PPB formation slightly above the nucleus and thereby promoting an even more asymmetric division. The positional gap of PPB and the nucleus seems odd, since the PPB usually forms around the nucleus, but pre-adjusting PPB position via MT patterning should be an effective strategy to ensure asymmetric division. Indeed, in a recent study, polarly localizing proteins in the leaf epidermis depleted stable MTs from a particular cell domain and thus limited the area in which the PPB could form36. Nevertheless, this epidermis cell divides at the nuclear position37, highlighting the uniqueness of the zygote that forms the PPB and the division plane at a different location than the nucleus. In tobacco BY-2 cells, cytoplasmic MTs that link the PPB and the prophase nucleus promote the formation of a bipolar spindle oriented perpendicular to the PPB38. In wild-type zygotes, in which the PPB and nucleus are slightly offset, MTs accumulate predominantly on the nuclear surface distal to the PPB, whereas in sgr2 and trm678 zygotes, both of which lack the perinuclear PPB, MTs cover the entire nuclear surface instead of adopting a bipolar arrangement (Fig. 6). These observations suggest that a similar PPB-dependent mechanism operates to adjust spindle positioning in the zygote.

The findings that trm678 zygotes retained the MT band but failed to form PPB, and that yda zygotes formed the PPB in a different position several hours after the disappearance of the MT band indicate that the MT band and PPB form via different molecular mechanisms (Figs. 5, 6). However, we can only speculate about the mechanisms that determine the exact position of the PPB in zygotes; therefore, it will be important to establish methods to specifically disrupt the MT band prior to PPB formation and thereby clarify the relationship between the MT band, cell shape, and PPB positioning. As mentioned above, the pre-depletion of MTs inhibits PPB formation36. Therefore, it appears to make sense that the PPB forms near the MT band site, an abundant source of MTs. Since apical cell geometry is crucial for setting proper transverse division, an early event in radial axis formation39, the MT band–dependent setting of the precise division site in the zygote would be important for the formation of a small spherical apical cell for subsequent embryo patterning.

Our findings demonstrated that a simple, autonomous mechanism utilizing spatiotemporal mechanical changes and a cytoskeleton that precisely senses them directly leads to the initial event of plant ontogeny. Therefore, this work will facilitate the interdisciplinary research of plant biology with mechanical engineering, physics, and data science, which will enable the manipulation of seed development to enhance agriculture.

Methods

Plant growth conditions and transgenic lines

All Arabidopsis lines are in the Col-0 background. The yda-2991, ktn1-2 (SAIL_343_D12), sgr2-1, and trm678 triple mutants were described previously19,24,27,40. The MT marker (EC1p::Clover-TUA6, coded as MU2228) was previously described41. The MT(SG) marker (EC1p::tdStayGold-TUA6, coded as HM06) was generated by replacing Clover with tdStayGold28. These markers were combined with a nuclear marker ABA INSENSITIVE4 (ABI4)p::H2B-tdTomato (coded as MU2463), which contains the 981-bp ABI4 promoter42, the full-length coding region of H2B (AT1G07790), tdTomato, and the NOS terminator in the pPZP211 binary vector. Plants were grown at 18–22 °C under continuous light or long-day conditions (16-h light/8-h dark).

Time-lapse observations and histological analysis

The zygote live-cell imaging was performed using a laser-scanning inverted microscope A1 (Nikon) equipped with a pulse laser (Mai Tai DeepSee; Spectra-Physics) and AX (Nikon) equipped with an ultrafast tunable laser (InSight X3 Dual option; Spectra-Physics) as previously described43,44. Time-lapse images were acquired every 20 min at 25 or 31 z-stacks with 1-μm intervals and using a 40 × water-immersion objective lens (CFI Apo LWD WI; Nikon) with Immersol W 2010 (Zeiss) immersion medium. Fluorescence signals were detected by the external non-descanned GaAsP PMT detectors. In the A1 system, we used two dichroic mirrors (DM495 and DM560), a short-pass filter (492 nm/SP) for CFW stain (see below), a band-pass filter (525/50 nm) for Clover and tdStayGold, and a mirror for tdTomato. In AX system, we used three dichroic mirrors (DM488, DM560 and DM685), and three band-pass filters (457/70 nm for CFW stain, 525/50 nm for Clover and tdStayGold, and 605/70 nm for tdTomato).

For inhibitor treatment, 0.1% DMSO and individual inhibitors [0.1% DMSO containing 1 μM oryzalin (Chem Service) or 10 μM taxol (Wako, Paclitaxel)] were added to the media ∼1 h before observation, as previously described45.

For cell wall staining, tissue clearing was performed using ClearSeeAlpha solution12. The self-pollinated pistils were fixed with 4% (w/v) PFA for 1 h in PBS under vacuum (∼690 mmHg) at room temperature. Fixed pistils were washed in PBS and cleared with ClearSeeAlpha for 3–5 days. Then the cleared pistils were stained with 1 mg/ml CFW (Wako) in water overnight and then mounted in ClearSeeAlpha droplet. The observation was performed with the above microscope (AX). The images were acquired at 25 z-stacks of 1-μm intervals using the above objective lens.

For DIC microscopy of embryo morphology, ovules were cleared in a chloral hydrate solution and observed using a Zeiss Axio Imager A1, as previously described46.

Maximum intensity projection (MIP) and image processing were performed using NIS-Elements software (Nikon) or Fiji (https://fiji.sc/). MIP images were generated by projecting all 25 or 31 xy-images, except for Sgr2, where 14 out of 31 images containing the zygote were used to avoid background signal from the ovule.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Unfertilized and fertilized wild-type ovules were fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde, 4% paraformaldehyde, and 50 mM sodium cacodylate, pH 7.4, overnight at 4 °C. The tissue segments were washed in buffer and post-fixed for 3 h in 2% osmium tetroxide in 50 mM sodium cacodylate buffer at 4 °C. The tissue was then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and further incubated in ethanol, transferred into propylene oxide, infiltrated, and embedded in Quetol 651. A series of thin sections (80 nm) was stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead stain solution at room temperature and examined using a JEM-1400Plus transmission electron microscope (JEOL) at 80 kV, and the images were acquired with a VELETA CCD camera (Olympus Soft Imaging Solutions).

Cortical MTs agent-based model

We constructed an agent-based model of the cortical MTs (CMTs) where we took the CMTs as agents (elements) and analyzed the collective behavior of the agents based on the previous works51,52. We accounted for the following three main dynamics: single MT growth and shrinkage, MTs interaction, and orientational modification based on a given large surface tension as an external directional cue53.

For single MT growth and shrinkage, we used the growth speed of the plus end \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}^{+}$$\end{document}v+ = 0.08 µm/s, the shrinkage speed of the plus end \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}^{-}$$\end{document}v− = 0.16 µm/s, and the shrinkage speed of the minus end (so-called treadmilling speed) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}^{{tm}}$$\end{document}vtm = 0.01 μm/s. The switching rate from shrinkage to growth at the plus end (so-called rescue rate) is rr = 0.007 1/s, and the switching rate from growth to shrinkage (so-called spontaneous catastrophe rate) rc is in the range [0.001–0.01] 1/s. The nucleation rate of CMTs on the surface rn = 0.001 1/s.

For MTs interaction, we considered the interactions between CMTs based on the previous studies52,54. First, we took into account the zippering effect where the growing CMT changes the direction of the growing front parallel to another CMT with the colliding angle less than 40 degrees. Second, we took into account the induced catastrophe where it begins to depolymerize from the growing front with a probability 50% with the colliding angle more than 40 degrees. Third, we took the account for crossover where the growing CMT crosses another CMT with a probability 50%, with the colliding angle more than 40 degrees.
