Optimal matching under size priority
Nathanaël Enriquez, Mike Liu, Laurent Ménard, Vianney Perchet

Abstract
Past studies on the local limit of maximal weight matchings in edge-weighted large random graphs rely fundamentally on the assumption that the weights are atomless, which ensures that the maximal weight matching is unique. This excludes de facto maximal size matchings that correspond to equal edge-weights. In this work, we overcome this difficulty by assigning i.i.d. atomless weights to edges and choosing the maximal size matching that maximises the weight. We call these doubly constrained matchings optimal matchings . The natural generalisation of optimal matchings for infinite unimodular random graphs are unimodular matchings of maximal density at the root that maximise the expected weight at the root when it is matched. For unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson (UBGW) trees and for a broad class of weight distributions, we show existence and uniqueness in law of such matchings. We also prove that if a sequence of finite random weighted graphs converges locally to an UBGW tree with i.i.d. weights, then there exists a sequence of matchings on the finite graphs that converges locally to the optimal matching on the limiting tree. Finally, we identify a regime, depending only on the offspring distribution of the limiting tree, in which correlations between edge states in the optimal matching decay exponentially with their graph distance. In this regime, we strengthen the previous convergence to the convergence of optimal matchings of the finite graphs. As a by-product, we can explicitly compute the asymptotic densities of edges that belong to all maximal-density matchings, and of edges that belong to none.



Keywords:

Optimal matchings, sparse random graphs, unimodularity, local convergence, belief propagation, renormalisation.

05C70, 05C82, 60C05, 60K35.

Introduction and main results

A matching of a graph
G
is a collection of edges of
G
with no common vertices. A matching
M
of finite graph
G
is said to be
maximal
if it maximizes the cardinality of
M
among the set of matchings of
G
.
The study of maximal matchings on sparse graphs dates back to the foundational paper by Karp and Sipser
[
14
]
where they provide the asymptotic size of maximal matchings on sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs
G(n,\frac{c}{n})
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
for fixed
c>0
. More recently, Bordenave, Lelarge and Salez
[
8
]
established an asymptotic formula for the cardinality of maximal matchings on sparse graphs that converge locally to a locally finite tree
\mathbb{T}
. These results do not study the geometry of maximal matchings for a good reason: There are indeed typically an exponential (in the size of the graph) number of maximal matchings. This suggests that there are many possible geometries for the local limits of such matchings.

In a previous article by the same authors
[
11
]
the geometry of matchings maximising the sum of its edge weights with no consideration for the size is studied in depth. Namely, the maximal weight matching converges locally to a unimodular matching whose distribution is explicitly described. The method, originally devised by Aldous
[
3
,
2
]
, relies on using a message passing algorithm that finds such matchings on trees.

All these previous works rely fundamentally on the assumption that the weights are atomless, which ensures that the maximal weight matching is unique. This excludes de facto the study of maximal matchings.
In this work, we overcome this difficulty by assigning i.i.d. atomless weights to edges and choosing the maximal matching that maximises the weight. We call these doubly constrained matchings
optimal
matchings.

1.1
Optimal unimodular matchings

Informally (see Section
2
for precise definitions), a random matching on the unimodular tree
\mathbb{T}
rooted at a distinguished oriented Vertex
o\in V(\mathbb{T})
is unimodular if its law is invariant by re-rooting. If we assign i.i.d. weights
w=(w(e))_{e\in E(\mathbb{T})}
with law
\omega
to the edges of
\mathbb{T}
, we say that a unimodular matching
\mathbb{M}
of the weighted tree is optimal if the quantity

\perfE(\mathbb{T},o,w,\mathbb{M})=\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\exists v\sim o,\{o,v\}\in\mathbb{M}\right),\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\sim o}w(o,v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{o,v\}\in\mathbb{M}}\right]\right)

(1)

is maximal in
lexicographical order
among all unimodular matchings of
(\mathbb{T},o,w)
. Note that for finite graphs, when
o
is chosen uniformly among the vertices, this performance is maximal when the matching is maximal in size and maximal in total weight under this constraint.

Before stating our main result, we need to define more precisely Unimodular Bienaymé Galton Watson random trees (UBGW). Let
\pi
be a distribution on non-negative integers with finite expectation and generating function
\phi
. We denote by
\hat{\pi}
the size-biased version of
\pi
, whose generating function is
\hat{\phi}
, defined by
\hat{\phi}(x)=\frac{\phi^{\prime}(x)}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}
. We say that a rooted tree
(\mathbb{T},o)
is a UBGW with offspring distribution
\pi
if the law of the tree can be obtained as follows. Draw a sequence of independent vertex-rooted independent Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees with offspring distribution
\hat{\pi}
(that is, each vertex of these trees have an independent number of children distributed according to
\hat{\pi}
). Independently draw a random number
N
with distribution
\pi
. The tree
(\mathbb{T},o)
is then obtained by connecting the root of each of the
N
first Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees to an additional vertex that we call
o
.

Our first theorem states the existence and uniqueness in law of unimodular matchings on
\mathbb{T}
that are optimal in the sense of performance (
1
):

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\pi
be a reproduction law and
\omega
a distribution on
\mathbb{R}
such that:

•

both
\pi
and
\omega
have finite expectation,

•

the law
\omega
is atomless and admits a density on some open interval,

•

denoting by
\phi
the generating function of
\pi
and setting
\hat{\phi}=\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}
, the function

t\mapsto\hat{\phi}(1-\hat{\phi}(1-t))

has a finite number of fixed points.

Then there exists a unique (in law) unimodular optimal matching
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}})
whose marginal tree
(\mathbb{T},{o})
is a Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with reproduction law
\pi
and i.i.d. weights of law
\omega
. Furthermore the distribution of
(\mathbb{T},{o},\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbb{T}))
is described in Proposition
4.1
.

Remark 1
.

The third assumption about
\hat{\phi}
induces notable exclusions. Indeed, if one assumes that
\hat{\phi}
is analytic at
1
(this includes in particular laws with exponential moments), then the function
\hat{\phi}(1-\ \hat{\phi}(1-x))
has infinitely many fixed points iff it is equal to
x
on the whole interval
[0,1]
. The family of geometric laws

\hat{\phi}(x)=\frac{px}{1-(1-p)x}

falls into that exclusion. The family given by
\hat{\phi}(x)=1-(1-x^{k})^{1/k}
for

is another example. All such exceptions are also critical for assumption (
2

3
.

Our next main result states that if a sequence of random weighted random graphs converges in the Benjamini-Schramm sense to a unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree, then this optimal matching is the limit of a sequence of matchings on the finite graphs. This includes classical models of random graphs such as Erdős-Rényi random graphs, regular random graphs or configuration models that all converge to unimodular versions of Bienaymé-Galton-Watson random trees. See for instance the books by van der Hofstad
[
18
,

]
, the article by Benjamini, Lyons and Schramm

]
, or the monograph by Aldous and Lyons

, and references therein.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n})
be a sequence of random graphs and choose a root
\overset{\rightarrow}{o}_{n}
uniformly among the vertices.
Suppose that the sequence of rooted weighted random graphs
(G_{n},o_{n})
converges locally to an (vertex-rooted) Unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson (UBGW) tree
(\mathbb{T},o)
with reproduction law
\pi
. Assume further that the ratio
\frac{2|E_{n}|}{|V_{n}|}
converges in probability to the expectation of

holds on the UBGW tree. Now assign i.i.d weights

of distribution

. We have

1.

There exists a sequence

of matchings of

converges locally to

.
In particular, we have that:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\exists v\sim o_{n},\{o_{n},v\}\in M_{n}\right),\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\sim o_{n}}w_{n}(o_{n},v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{o_{n},v\}\in M_{n}}\right]\right)=\perfE(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}),

2.

Reciprocally, for any sequence
(M_{n})_{n}
of matchings of
G_{n}
, take
(a,b)
to be any subsequential limit of

\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\exists v\sim o_{n},\{o_{n},v\}\in M_{n}\right),\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\sim o_{n}}w_{n}(o_{n},v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{o_{n},v\}\in M_{n}}\right]\right),

then we must have that

(a,b)\overset{\mathrm{lex}}{\leq}\perfE(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}).

Remark 2
.

Let us stress that, in the first statement of Theorem
1.2
, the matching
M_{n}
is not necessarily a maximal matching of
G_{n}
, but only asymptotically maximal. An interesting and delicate question is then the existence of a sequence of maximal matchings of the graphs
G_{n}
that also have asymptotically optimal performance. This question will be completely answered in a regime that we call subcritical in the next section. We conjecture that it holds in general.

Remark 3
.

Bordenave, Lelarge and Salez proved in
[
17
]
that, if
G_{n}
is a sequence of
deterministic
graphs that converges locally in distribution when rooted uniformly, then there is almost sure convergence of the normalised size of the maximum size matching.
In the case of configuration models, which converge almost surely to UBGW trees under uniform rooting (see e.g.
[
9
]
),
the asymptotic size of maximal matchings on
G_{n}
converges almost surely to

2-\max_{u\in[0,1]}F_{\pi}(u)

with

F_{\pi}:x\mapsto\phi(1-x)+\phi(1-\hat{\phi}(1-x))+x\hat{\phi}(1-x)\phi^{\prime}(1).

The uniqueness of
M_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbb{T})
established in Theorem
1.1
ensures that this quantity is equal to
\mathbb{P}\left(\exists v\sim o,\{o,v\}\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}\right)
.
The first statement of the Theorem contains a weaker convergence in expectation when the graphs
G_{n}
are
random
and converge in distribution.
Note that this result also applies for the exceptions of Remark
1
, and we can observe that, in these cases, maximal matchings are in fact asymptotically perfect matchings.

Remark 4
.

A classical way to study maximum size matchings on a finite graph is to introduce a temperature parameter
\beta
and a Gibbs distribution on matchings of the form
\mu(M)\propto e^{-\beta|E(M)|}
(see
[
8
]
).
In the zero temperature limit, namely letting
\beta\to+\infty
, we recover a uniformly sampled maximum size matching.
We stress that our theorems are not about such measures. In fact, we will show in Proposition
7.1
that, in some strong sense, uniform maximum size matchings on UBGW trees are fundamentally different from maximum weight maximum size matchings on UBGW trees.

1.2
The subcritical regime

Our next statement gives a positive answer to the convergence question in a regime that we call
local
or
subcritical
for reasons that we will explain after the theorem. For a rooted graph
(G,o)
we will write
B_{H}(G,o)
to be the subgraph of
G
induced by vertices with graph distance at most
H
from
o
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Under the same notation and hypothesis as Theorem
1.2
, assume furthermore that for any random variable
X
taking values in
[0,1]
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\phi}^{\prime}(1-X)\right]\hat{\phi}^{\prime}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\phi}(1-X)\right]\right)<1.

(2)

Also assume that
(G_{n},o_{n})
converges locally in total variation to
(\mathbb{T},o)
, in the sense that for every
\varepsilon>0,H>0
, there exists a rank
N_{\varepsilon,H}
such that for any
n\geq N_{\varepsilon,H}
, there is a coupling
(\tilde{G}_{n},\tilde{o}_{n},\tilde{\mathbb{T}},\tilde{o})
between
(G_{n},o_{n})
and
(\mathbb{T},o)
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(B_{H}(\tilde{G}_{n},\tilde{o}_{n})=B_{H}(\tilde{\mathbb{T}},\tilde{o})\right)>1-\varepsilon.

(3)

For any
G_{n}
, define
M_{\mathrm{opt}}(G_{n})
to be the maximal weight maximum size matching on
G_{n}
, then we have that

(G_{n},o_{n},M_{\mathrm{opt}}(G_{n}))\longrightarrow(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}})

where the convergence is understood in the sense of local convergence in total variation.

Remark 5
.

Note that condition (
2
) does not depend on the weight distribution and depends only on the reproduction law of the tree. We do not know if this condition is sharp and it is likely that the true condition depends on both
\pi
and
\omega
.

We call a reproduction satisfying condition (
2
)
subcritical
.
For Erdős-Rényi random graphs
G(n,\tfrac{c}{n})
, the sub-criticality condition is equivalent to
c<e
. This
e
-threshold is the object of several phase transitions for the Erdős-Rényi model; see, for instance, the works of Karp-Sipser
[
14
]
for a geometric phenomenon or Coste-Salez
[
10
]
for a spectral phenomenon. More related to our work, Gamarnik-Nowicki-Swirszcz
[
13
]
proved that, for i.i.d. exponential weights, independent sets can be approximated by local functions when
c<e
and maximum weight matchings for all

. Theorem
1.3
is in fact a consequence of a generalisation of this locality property for optimal matchings and Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees that we state now. See also the recent paper by Lam and Sen
[
16
]
where they establish similar results for exponential weights and for graphs with degrees bounded by
3
, which remarkably include non tree-like graphs such as the hexagonal lattice.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(\mathbb{T},\overset{\rightarrow}{o})
be an edge-rooted unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with reproduction law
\pi
.
Set
\hat{\phi}
to be the generating function of
\hat{\pi}
. Assume that
\hat{\phi}
verifies condition (
2
). Then there exists a sequence of determinist functions
F_{H}
such that:

•

Each function
F_{H}
is
H-
local

•

Set

\rho_{\pi}:=\sup_{X\text{ r.v. in }[0,1]}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\phi}^{\prime}(1-X)\right]\hat{\phi}^{\prime}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\phi}(1-X)\right]\right)<1.

Then there exists
C>0
such that, for every
H>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left((\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}})\simeq F_{H}(\mathbb{T},o)\right)>1-C\rho_{\pi}^{H}.

For a sequence of finite weighted graphs

, this gives an annealed exponential decay for the correlation between the states of two edges relatively to their distance.
In particular, this implies asymptotic decorrelation of the state of two uniformly picked edges in

:

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{T},o)
be as in Theorem

and let
(G_{n},o_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence uniformly rooted finite graphs such that
(G_{n},o_{n})
converges locally to

. Assume in addition that if

is a uniform edge chosen independently from

, then
(G_{n},o_{n}^{\prime})
converges locally to an independent copy of
(\mathbb{T},o)
.
Then
(G_{n},M_{\mathrm{opt}}(G_{n}),o_{n})
and
(G_{n},M_{\mathrm{opt}}(G_{n}),o_{n}^{\prime})

converge to two independent copies
of
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}})
.

Remark 6
.

The condition is satisfied for configuration models (see subsection
2.2
for a definition). It is also not empty: One can consider for

,
G_{n}
to be
n
copies of the same Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with a Poisson reproduction law of parameter

. It is clear that

satisfies condition (
2

(G_{n},o_{n})
converges locally to
(\mathbb{T},o)
but two uniformly chosen vertices will see the same copy of the tree.

On mandatory and blocking edges

1.4
A glimpse at our approach: Belief propagation and renormalisation

Maximum weight matchings were studied in our previous article
[
11
]
with an approach based on belief propagation. Informally, directed message variables
\mathbf{Z}(u,v)_{(u,v)\in\overset{\rightarrow}{E}(\mathbb{T})}
on the weighted tree
(\mathbb{T},o,w)
satisfying

\mathbf{Z}(u,v)=\max\left(0,\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u^{\prime}\sim v\\
u^{\prime}\neq u\end{subarray}}\left(w(v,u^{\prime})-\mathbf{Z}(v,u^{\prime})\right)\right)

were defined and studied. In particular, it was shown in Theorem 1 of
[
11
]
that there is a unique stationary distribution for such messages and that they correspond to the unique (in distribution) unimodular matching
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\max})
on the tree that maximizes

\mathbb{E}\left[w(o)\mathbbm{1}_{o\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{max}}}\right].

The matching
\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{max}}
is constructed by the messages via the decision rule

\{u,v\}\in\mathbb{M}_{\max}\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{Z}(u,v)+\mathbf{Z}(v,u)<w(u,v).

See Subsection
3.1
for a more detailed presentation.

The main idea behind this paper is to introduce a renormalisation procedure on these messages when the weights are of the form
1+\varepsilon w
. This defines a tree, messages and matching that we call
(\mathbb{T},o,w,\mathbb{M}_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)})
As
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we find that the corresponding messages
\mathbf{Z}^{\varepsilon}
can be split into a macroscopic component
x(u,v)
and a microscopic component
Z(u,v)
, such that the messages
(x,Z)
verify a two dimensional equivalent to the previous recursion:

(x,Z)(u,v)=\maxlex\left((0,0),\maxlex_{\begin{subarray}{c}u^{\prime}\sim v\\
u^{\prime}\neq u\end{subarray}}\left((1,w(v,u^{\prime}))-(x(v,u^{\prime}),Z(v,u^{\prime}))\right)\right),

where
\mathrm{lex}
stands for the lexicographical order. The decision rule becomes

(u,v)\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}\Leftrightarrow(x,Z)(u,v)+(x,Z)(v,u)\overset{\mathrm{\lex}}{<}(1,w(u,v)).

A detailed heuristic of this renormalisation procedure is given in Section
3
.
We will show in Section
5
that under general conditions, it is possible to determine the explicit distribution of the macroscopic component and that there is a unique solution to the corresponding recursive distributional equation.

A by-product of our approach is that the maximum weight matching on the tree converges in law to the optimal matching:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
(\mathbb{T},o,w)
be a Unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with reproduction law
\pi
and i.i.d. weights of law
\omega
.
Assume that
\pi
and
\omega
have finite expectation, and that the generating function
\phi
of
\pi
is such that the function

x\in[0,1]\mapsto\phi(1-x)+\phi(1-\hat{\phi}(1-x))+\phi^{\prime}(1)x\hat{\phi}(1-x)

has at most two global maxima.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, denote by
(\mathbb{T},o,w,\mathbb{M}_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)})
the unique (in law) unimodular matching maximising

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\sim o}(1+\varepsilon w(o,v))\mathbbm{1}_{\{o,v\}\in\mathbb{M}_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)}}\right],

and by
(\mathbb{T},o,w,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}})
the unique (in law) optimal unimodular matching
given by Theorem
1.1
. We have the following weak local convergence

(\mathbb{T},o,w,\mathbb{M}_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)})\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbb{T},o,w,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}).

Combining Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.6
, we get the following corollary:

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
(G_{n},w_{n})
be a sequence of weighted finite graphs.
Under assumptions and notations of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.6
, define
M_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)}(G_{n})
to be the matching on
G_{n}
maximising the sum of

(G_{n},o_{n},M_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)}(G_{n})

1.5
Organisation of the paper

Section
2
presents the precise definitions and important notions used in the paper. In particular, we give definitions for unimodular graphs, local convergence, and optimal matchings.

As already mentioned, Section
3
gives detailed heuristics for our approach in the special case of UBGW trees. It also ends with an informal computation of the Karp-Sipser formula for the size of maximal matchings from the renormalised messages in the case of Poisson offspring distribution.

Section
4
formally builds the joint distribution
\left(\mathbb{T},o,w,(x,Z)(u,v)_{(u,v)\in\overset{\rightarrow}{E}}\right)
and shows that the matching defined by

2.1
Vertex-rooted and edge-rooted unimodularity

We now introduce the notion of unimodularity for (possibly decorated) random graphs (recall Definition
2.2
, in the following all graphs can be decorated).
To this end, we define the space of doubly rooted graphs up to isomorphism
\mathcal{G}^{\star\star}
similarly as before, but with two distinguished roots. It will be simpler to give separately specific definitions for vertex-rooted and edge-rooted graphs.
We refer to Aldous-Lyons
[
1
]
for a comprehensive exposition on the topic.

Definition 2.5

(Vertex-rooted unimodularity)
.

We say that a probability measure
\mu
on decorated vertex-rooted graphs is unimodular if the following statement holds for every measurable
f:\mathcal{G}^{\star\star}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\star}}\left(\sum_{v\in V(G)}f(G,o,v)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(G,o)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\star}}\left(\sum_{v\in V(G)}f(G,v,o)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(G,o).

The subspace of unimodular laws on vertex rooted-graph will be noted
\mathcal{L}_{U}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
.

The definition can be written alternatively as

\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o})\sim\mu}\left[\sum_{v\in V(G)}f(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},v)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o})\sim\mu}\left[\sum_{v\in V(G)}f(\mathbb{G},v,\mathbf{o})\right].

For edge-rooted graphs, we will use the following definition:

Definition 2.6

(Edge-rooted unimodularity)
.

Let
\hat{\mu}
be a probability measure on decorated edge-rooted graphs.
Let
(\mathbb{G},(\mathbf{o}_{-},\mathbf{o}_{+}))
be a random edge-weighted decorated graph with law
\hat{\mu}
. Let
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{1}
be a uniformly picked directed edge of the form
(\mathbf{o}_{+},\mathbf{v})
for
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{o}_{-}
.
We say that
\hat{\mu}
is:

•

stationary if
(\mathbb{G},(\mathbf{o}_{-},\mathbf{o}_{+}))\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}(\mathbb{G},\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{1})

•

revertible if
(\mathbb{G},(\mathbf{o}_{-},\mathbf{o}_{+}))\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}(\mathbb{G},(\mathbf{o}_{+},\mathbf{o}_{-}))

•

unimodular if it is both revertible and stationary.

The subspace of unimodular laws on edge-rooted graphs will be noted
\mathcal{L}_{U}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star})

To simplify notation, we will say that a random rooted graph (as a random variable) is unimodular when its corresponding law is.

Remark 10
.

Fix
G
a finite deterministic graph, and let
\mathbf{o}
be a random vertex (resp.
\overset{\rightarrow}{\mathbf{o}}
an oriented edge). It is straightforward to check that
(G,\mathbf{o})
(resp.
(G,\overset{\rightarrow}{\mathbf{o}})
) is vertex-rooted unimodular (resp. edge-rooted unimodular) iff
\mathbf{o}
is a uniform vertex (resp.
\overset{\rightarrow}{\mathbf{o}}
is a uniform oriented edge). Hence, unimodular graphs can be viewed as generalisations of uniformly rooted graphs, which is one of the fundamental findings of
[
6
]
.

We now present the classical transformation that maps a unimodular vertex-rooted graph to a unimodular edge-rooted graph. Heuristically, to transform a graph rooted at a uniform vertex into a graph rooted at a uniformly oriented edge, one has to pick an oriented edge starting at its root vertex, but this induces a bias by the degree of the root vertex. Indeed, a directed edge
(u,v)
will be less likely to be the new root the greater the degree of
u
is. This bias has to be taken into account to conserve unimodularity. This is done in the following transformations on the laws.

Definition 2.7
.

Take
\mu_{v}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
such that
0<m:=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\star}}\deg(o)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{v}(G,o)<\infty
. Let
R(\mu_{v})\in\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star})
be the unique measure such that,
for every
f
measurable from
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\int_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}}f(G,(o_{-},o_{+}))\,\mathrm{d}R(\mu_{v})(G,(o_{-},o_{+}))=\int_{\mathcal{G}*}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{u\sim o}f(G,o,u)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{v}(G,o).

Remark 11
.

The operator
R
is the composition of two transformations. The first operator consists of choosing a uniformly oriented edge started at the root vertex, giving a measure
R_{1}(\mu_{v})\in\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star})
:

\int_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}}f(G,(o_{-},o_{+}))\,\mathrm{d}R_{1}(\mu_{v})(G,(o_{-},o_{+})))=\int_{\mathcal{G}*}\frac{1}{\deg(o)}\sum_{u\sim o}f(\textbf{}G,o,u)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{v}(G,o).

The second step is then to cancel the bias by the degree of the vertex, giving
R(\mu_{v})=R_{2}\circ R_{1}(\mu_{v})\in\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star})
:

\int_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}}f(G,(o_{-},o_{+}))\mathrm{d}R_{2}\circ R_{1}(\mu_{v})(G,(o_{-},o_{+}))=\int_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}}\frac{\deg(o_{-})}{m}f(G,(o_{-},o_{+}))\,\mathrm{d}R_{1}(\mu_{v})(G,(o_{-},o_{+})).

Equivalently, to obtain an edge-rooted version of a vertex rooted random graph, one can consider the size-biased version of the original measure
\mathrm{d}\mu_{v}(G,o)
(namely sample a graph with measure

\frac{\deg(o)}{m}\mathrm{d}\mu_{v}(G,o)
) and then from this sampled graph, starting from the root, select an edge uniformly at random to get the edge measure
dR(\mu_{v})
.

The following proposition links vertex unimodularity and edge unimodularity.

Proposition 2.8

(Theorem 4.1 in
[
1
]
)
.

Let
\mu_{v}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
be such that
0<\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\star}}\deg(o)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{v}(G,o)<\infty
.
Then the measure
\mu_{v}
is unimodular if and only if
R(\mu_{v})
is unimodular.

2.3
Matchings, optimality and unimodularity

We start with the definition of matchings on a graph.

Definition 2.9
.

For any weighted graph
G=((V,E),w)
, a matching
M=(V,E^{\prime})
on
G
is a subgraph of
G
such that
E^{\prime}\subset E
and every vertex of
V
belongs to at most one edge of
E^{\prime}
. A matched graph is a pair
(G,M)
, where
M
is a matching on
G
.

We can extend the notion of unimodularity for random graphs to random matched graphs by transforming a matching into a canonical decoration. If
(G,M)=((V,E),(V,E^{\prime}))
is a matched graph then we can define the associated decoration
\mathbbm{1}_{M}:E\to\{0,1\}
with
\mathbbm{1}_{M}(u,v)=1
if and only if
(u,v)\in E^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{M},\mathbf{o})
be a random matched rooted graph.
We say that
(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{M},\mathbf{o})
is unimodular iff
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{M}})
is unimodular.

The correspondence between
(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{M},\mathbf{o})
and
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{M}})
gives a representation of the space of unimodular laws on decorated matched graphs as a subspace of unimodular laws on decorated graphs
\mathcal{L}_{U,\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{U}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
.

Definition 2.11
.

The subspace
\mathcal{L}_{U,\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{U}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
is defined as the set of
elements
\mu
of
\mathcal{L}_{U}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
such that for
(G,o,w,(f_{i})_{i\in\{1,..,I\}})\sim\mu
, we have that
f_{I}(u,v)
maps
E
to
\{0,1\}
and the subset
\{(u,v),f_{I}(u,v)=1\}
almost surely induces a matching on
G
in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists u,u^{\prime},v\in V,u\neq u^{\prime}\text{ and }f(v,u)=f(v,u^{\prime})=1\right)=0.

Similarly we can define the edge rooted version
\mathcal{L}_{U,\mathcal{M}}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star})
.

We recall that the central object of interest is optimal matching, which corresponds to the classical notion of maximal weight matching in finite graphs.
When
G
is infinite, this optimality is ill-defined. However, in the case of a unimodular random weighted graph, since the root edge is informally a typical edge, we can define optimality via its expected weight when it belongs to the matching. This leads to the following definition:

Definition 2.12
.

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbb{M})
be a unimodular random matched rooted graph, we define the performance of
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbb{M})
as:

\displaystyle\perfE(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbb{M})

\displaystyle:=\bigg(\mathbb{P}((\mathbf{o}_{-},\mathbf{o_{+}})\in\mathbb{M}),\mathbb{E}\left[w((\mathbf{o}_{-},\mathbf{o}_{+}))\mathbbm{1}_{(\mathbf{o}_{-},\mathbf{o}_{+})\in\mathbb{M}}\right]\bigg)\text{ in the edge-rooted setting,}

\displaystyle\perfV(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbb{M})

\displaystyle:=\bigg(\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{o}\text{ is matched by }\mathbb{M}),\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\sim o}w(\mathbf{o},v)\mathbbm{1}_{(\mathbf{o},v)\in\mathbb{M}}\right]\bigg)\text{in the vertex-rooted setting.}

When the context is clear, we will shorten the notation to
\perf(\mathbb{M})
.
By extension, since those quantities only depend on the law of
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbb{M})
, we will freely use the same notation
\perfE(\mu_{e}):=\perfE(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbb{M})
for
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{M}})\sim\mu_{e}
and
\perfV(\mu_{v}):=\perfV(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbb{M})
for
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{M}})\sim\mu_{v}
where
\mu_{e}\in\mathcal{L}_{U,\mathcal{M}}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\star})
and
\mu_{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{U,\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
.

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o})
be a (undecorated) unimodular vertex-rooted graph. We say that
(\mathbb{G}^{\prime},\mathbf{o}^{\prime},\mathbb{M})
is optimal if it is a unimodular matched vertex-rooted graph such that
\perfV((\mathbb{G}^{\prime},\mathbf{o}^{\prime}),\mathbb{M})
is lexicographically maximal among all unimodular rooted matched random graphs
(\mathbb{G}^{\prime},\mathbf{o}^{\prime},\mathbb{M}^{\prime})
such that
(\mathbb{G}^{\prime},\mathbf{o}^{\prime})
has the same law as
(\mathbb{G},\mathbf{o})
.
In the edge-rooted setting, optimality is defined similarly.
The next proposition shows that the operator
R
, introduced in Definition
2.7
, preserves optimality.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
\mu_{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{U,\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{G}^{\star})
, assume
m=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\star}}\deg(o)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{v}(G,o)<\infty
, then:

\perfV(\mu_{v})=m\perfE(R(\mu_{v})).

The proof of this proposition is nearly identical with the proof of
[
11
, Proposition 2.12]
with bidimensional weights
(1,w)
instead of unidimensional weights
w
so we omit it here.

Remark 12
.

If the graph is finite and the root is chosen uniformly either among the vertices or among the directed edges, then
\perfV
is simply the average contribution per vertex, and
\perfE
is the average contribution per directed edge.
It is then clear that the two quantities are proportional. The proposition shows that it generalises to unimodular matched graphs.

We state a useful property of unimodular graphs: events that have probability zero (resp. almost sure) at the root have probability zero (resp. almost sure) everywhere.

Proposition 2.14

(Lemma 2.3 in
[
1
]
)
.

Let
(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{o})
be a unimodular graph, let
H>0
and
f
be a non-negative H-local function on
\mathcal{G}^{\star}
.
Assume that

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{o})\right]=0.

Then almost surely, for all
v\in\mathbf{V}

f(\mathbf{G},v)=0.

3
Heuristics

In this section, we give more details about the heuristics behind our approach. We will look at a UBGW tree
(\mathbb{T},\overset{\rightarrow}{o})
some reproduction law
\pi
satisfying that

1.

The map
t\mapsto\hat{\phi}(1-\hat{\phi}(1-t))
has finite amount of fixed points.

2.

The tree has leaves, which is equivalent to
\hat{\phi}(0)>0
.

We will then try to find the "maximum size, maximum weight matching", in this order, on the tree.
The main idea is to see what happens when we consider the maximum weight matching, with no size constraints, when edge weights are of the form
w^{\varepsilon}(u,v)=1+\varepsilon w(u,v)
for
(u,v)\in E
for i.i.d. continuous variables
w(u,v)
and
\varepsilon\to 0
. The study of maximum weight matchings was the topic of our previous article
[
11
]
and we will first describe this setting in the next section as it is instrumental to our approach.

3.2
Renormalising the message variables

We return to maximum size, maximum weight matchings. We assign weights
w^{\varepsilon}:=1+\varepsilon\,w
to edges, where the variables
w
are i.i.d. and have a continuous distribution to use the approach described in the previous section. Indeed, Theorem
3.1
states that the unique (in law) unimodular matching
\mathbb{M}_{\varepsilon}
on
(\mathbb{T},\overset{\rightarrow}{o},w_{\varepsilon})
maximising
\mathbb{E}[w^{\varepsilon}(o)\mathbbm{1}_{o\in\mathbb{M}}]
can be generated by variables
\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}
verifying

\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}(u,v)=\max\left(0,\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u^{\prime}\sim v\\
u^{\prime}\neq u\end{subarray}}\left(w^{\varepsilon}(v,u^{\prime})-\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}(v,u^{\prime})\right)\right).

(11)

The cumulative distribution function
h^{\varepsilon}
of the common distribution of the variables
\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}
satisfies

\forall t\in\mathbb{R},h^{\varepsilon}(t)=\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq 0}\hat{\phi}(1-\mathbb{E}[h^{\varepsilon}(W^{\varepsilon}-t)]),

(12)

where
W^{\varepsilon}
has the distribution of
1+\varepsilon W
with
W\sim\omega
and
\hat{\phi}
denotes the probability generating function of the size-biased reproduction law of the tree.
Finally, the inclusion rule would be:

(u,v)\in\mathbb{M}^{\varepsilon}\Leftrightarrow w^{\varepsilon}(u,v)>\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}(u,v)+\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}(v,u).

(13)

If one naively took
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in the above equation, assuming
h^{\varepsilon}(t)\rightarrow h^{0}(t)
, we would get:

\forall t\in\mathbb{R},h^{0}(t)=\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq 0}\hat{\phi}(1-h^{0}(1-t)).

(14)

which, when iterated inside
]0,1[
to avoid the boundary conditions, gives that
h^{0}(t)
has to solve:

\forall t\in]0,1[,h^{0}(t)=\hat{\phi}(1-\hat{\phi}(1-h^{0}(t))).

(15)

This implies that the values of
h^{0}
at every point of continuity has to be a fixed point of
t\mapsto\hat{\phi}(1-\hat{\phi}(1-t))
. So
\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}
must be a discrete variable in
[0,1]
.
In general if
t\mapsto\hat{\phi}(1-\hat{\phi}(1-t))
has
p+1
fixed points
l_{0},...l_{p}
, we must have that
l_{k}=\hat{\phi}(1-l_{p-k})
so we will set
l^{\prime}_{k}=l_{p-k}
. We can then enumerate all the possible solutions for
h^{0}
with the conditions we have found so far. Let
p^{\prime}\leq\tfrac{p}{2}
be a integer,
l_{k_{0}}<...<l_{k_{p^{\prime}}}<l_{\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor}
be fixed points. Consider
x_{0}=0<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{p^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
be real numbers with
x_{p^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}
only if
{k_{p^{\prime}}}=\frac{p}{2}
. The solutions are parametrised with
(l_{k_{0}},\cdots,l_{k_{p^{\prime}}},x_{0},...,x_{p^{\prime}})
as follows:

•

If
x_{p}^{\prime}\neq\frac{1}{2}
, setting
l_{k_{-1}}=0
and
l^{\prime}_{k_{-1}}=1
,

h^{0}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{p^{\prime}}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq x_{n}}\left(l_{k_{n}}-l_{k_{(n-1)}}\right)+\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq 1-x_{n}}\left(l^{\prime}_{k_{n-1}}-l^{\prime}_{k_{n}}\right)\right).

•

Otherwise, if
x_{p}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}
,

h^{0}(t)=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{p^{\prime}-1}\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq x_{n}}\left(l_{k_{n}}-l_{k_{(n-1)}}\right)+\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq 1-x_{n}}\left(l^{\prime}_{k_{n-1}}-l^{\prime}_{k_{n}}\right)\right)+\left(l_{k_{p^{\prime}}}-l_{k_{p^{\prime}-1}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq\frac{1}{2}}.

In essence
\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}
is concentrated at
x_{0},...x_{p^{\prime}},1-x_{p^{\prime}},\dots,1-x_{0}
with probabilities given by the consecutive differences between the
l_{k_{n}}
.

The decision rule

1+\varepsilon w(u,v)>\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}(u,v)+\mathbf{Z}^{(\varepsilon)}(v,u)

then becomes as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,

1\geq\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}(u,v)+\mathbf{Z^{(0)}}(v,u).

The issue is that the event

1=\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}(u,v)+\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}(v,u)

now happens with positive probability, and when this event happens, there will be multiple edges
v^{\prime}
around
u
such that
\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}(u,v^{\prime})+\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}(v^{\prime},u)=1
. In this case, the rule does not define a matching anymore since multiple neighbours of
u
can be tied at
1
in the decision rule.

To break those ties, we will renormalise
h^{\varepsilon}
around the limiting atoms
1,x_{1},\dots,x_{p^{\prime}},1-x_{p^{\prime}},1-x_{1},\dots,1
. We will know
a posteriori
that for well behaved degree distributions, the only atoms will appear at
0,\frac{1}{2},1
. In this heuristics section, for simplicity’s sake, we assume as such. We refer to Figure
6
for an illustration of this discussion.

3.3
Recovering Karp-Sipser formula

In this subsection, we will see how to compute the known Karp-Sipser formula for maximum matchings from our renormalised message variables. We will thus assume that
\pi
is a Poisson distribution of parameter
c>0
. We focus on the harder case
c>e
, where we will assume that
\mathbf{Z}^{0}
concentrates around
0,\frac{1}{2}
and
1
with the relevant fixed points denoted by
\underline{\gamma}
for the smallest and
\overline{\gamma}
for the largest. The probability that a uniform edge is matched is then:

\mathbb{P}((i_{a},Z_{a})+(i_{b},Z_{b})\overset{\lex}{<}(1,w))

(28)

with
(i_{a},Z_{a}),(i_{b},Z_{b})
and
w
independent.
As we said before, the representation
(Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2})
is more convenient for computing probabilities, so we can transform
(i_{a},Z_{a})
into
(Z_{a,0},Z_{a,1},Z_{a,2})
and
(i_{b},Z_{b})
into
(Z_{b,0},Z_{b,1},Z_{b,2})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{2}(W-t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq 0}\frac{-\ln(h_{0}(t))}{c},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{1}(W-t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{-\ln(h_{1}(t))}{c},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{0}(W-t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{-\ln(h_{2}(t))}{c}.

\displaystyle=\int_{\beta}^{1}\frac{-\ln(u)}{c}\mathrm{d}u-\frac{\beta\ln(\beta)}{c}

at

It remains to express

\overline{\gamma}
and
\underline{\gamma}

Using the same trick as above gives the following:

\displaystyle\frac{\beta\ln(\beta)}{c}-\int_{\beta}^{\underline{\gamma}}\frac{\ln(u)}{c}=\overline{\gamma}\underline{\gamma}-\int_{\overline{\gamma}}^{1}\frac{\ln(u)}{c}\mathrm{d}u.

\displaystyle\underline{\gamma}\ln(\underline{\gamma})-\underline{\gamma}-\beta\ln(\beta)+\beta+\beta\ln(\beta)=\overline{\gamma}-1-\overline{\gamma}\ln(\overline{\gamma})-c\overline{\gamma}\underline{\gamma}.

Isolating

Recalling that

\frac{1}{c}\left[2-\overline{\gamma}-\underline{\gamma}-c\underline{\gamma}\overline{\gamma}\right].

4.2
Adding Self-Loops

In the remainder of this section, we will adopt the vertex-rooted point of view. Dealing with partial matchings rather than perfect matchings will be very cumbersome in future proofs. For example, when comparing two matchings, we would need to discuss several cases depending on whether a vertex is matched or not for the two matchings. A simple solution to deal with this is to add self-loops, in which case the graphs always have perfect matchings, and any partial matching on the graph without self-loops can be augmented into a perfect matching on the graph with self-loops. Reciprocally, a perfect matching on a graph with self-loops can be restricted to a partial matching on the corresponding graph without self-loops.

We need to choose adequate weights for the self-loops so that a partial matching on the graph without self-loops is optimal among all partial matchings of the original graph if and only if the corresponding perfect matching on the augmented graph with self-loops is optimal among all perfect matchings of the augmented graph.

Starting with a system of variables

for

that satisfies recursion (

), we want to extend it to variables

for each self-loop so that we get a similar recursion as (
37
) and the decision rule (
38
) constructs the perfect matching of the augmented graph associated to the matching defined by the original variables.

In Recursion (
37
), the variable

l_{\mathcal{H},j}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}+\int_{l_{\mathcal{H},j}}^{l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}}(1-\hat{\phi}^{-1}(u))\mathrm{d}u=l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}+\int_{l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}}^{l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}}(1-\hat{\phi}^{-1}(u))\mathrm{d}u.

Similarly as before, recall that for any
0<j\leq k
,
l_{\mathcal{H},j}=\mathcal{F}(l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1})
. Using integral of reciprocal functions, we get that:

\displaystyle\int_{l_{\mathcal{H},j}}^{l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{F}(l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1})}^{\mathcal{F}(l_{\mathcal{H},k-j})}\mathcal{F}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=-\int_{l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}}^{l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}}\mathcal{F}(u)\mathrm{d}u+l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}-l_{\mathcal{H},j}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}.

Recall that a primitive of
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathcal{G}(u)=-\frac{\phi(1-u)}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}
, so we have that:

\displaystyle\int_{l_{\mathcal{H},j}}^{l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\phi(1-u)}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}\right]_{l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}}^{l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}}+l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}-l_{\mathcal{H},j}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},k-j})-\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1})}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}+l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}-l_{\mathcal{H},j}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}.

Evaluating this equality at
j
and
k-j
and injecting this into the first expression, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},k-j})-\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1})}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}+l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}=\frac{\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},j})-\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},j+1})}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}+l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},j}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},k-j})+\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},j+1})}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}+l_{\mathcal{H},j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},k-j}=\frac{\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},j})+\phi(1-l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1})}{\phi^{\prime}(1)}+l_{\mathcal{H},k-j+1}l_{\mathcal{H},j}.

This is precisely the statement that the desired quantity is preserved when changing
j
by
j+1
. The conclusion follows by induction.
∎

\{u,v\}\in\mathbb{M}\implies\exists i\in\{0,1,2\},\,Z_{i}(u,v)+Z_{2-i}(v,u)\ <w(u,v).

The reverse implication clearly holds:

\displaystyle\exists i\in\{0,1,2\},\,\,Z_{i}(u,v)+Z_{2-i}(v,u)\ <w(u,v)\implies

\displaystyle\exists\varepsilon_{0}>0,\,\forall\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0},\,\exists i\in\{0,1,2\},\,\,\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{(\varepsilon)}(u,v)+\mathbf{Z}_{2-i}^{(\varepsilon)}(v,u)<w(u,v).

Hence,

\{u,v\}\in\mathbb{M}\iff\exists\in\{0,1,2\},\,Z_{i}(u,v)+Z_{2-i}(v,u)\ <w(u,v).

So,
\mathbb{M}
agrees with
\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}
. As
\mathbb{M}
was chosen as any subsequent limit of
\mathbb{M}_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)}
, we have thus obtained that

\left(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\max}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\left(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}\right)

as required.
∎

6
The subcritical regimes

Recall that a reproduction law is subcritical for optimal matchings if it satisfies, for every random variable
X
with values in
[0,1]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\phi}^{\prime}(1-X)\right]\hat{\phi}^{\prime}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\phi}(1-X)\right]\right)<1.

(
2

In the first subsection, under this sub-criticality condition, we strengthen the approximation in law of optimal matchings for the local topology obtained in Theorem
1.2
to a convergence in total variation of
finite
optimal matchings. On the way we prove that, in this regime, the messages and the state of edges in the optimal matching have uniform exponential
point to set
correlation decay.

Similarly, we say that a reproduction law is subcritical for mandatory and blocking edges if it satisfies

\exists!t\in(0,1):t=\hat{\phi}(1-\hat{\phi}(1-t)).

(
’
‣
1.5
)

In the second subsection, we prove that in this regime, there is local convergence in total variation of mandatory and blocking edges along with local convergence in total variation of a sequence of random graphs to the corresponding UBGW tree.

Note that in both regimes, we must have a unique fixed point
\gamma=\hat{\phi}\left(1-\hat{\phi}(1-\gamma)\right)
that also verifies
\gamma=\hat{\phi}(1-\gamma)
, hence
k=1
and
i\sim\gamma\delta_{0}+(1-\gamma)\delta_{1}
.

The main arguments in both settings can be broken down as follows:

1.

First, for a given graph
G
and
v\in V(G)
such that
B_{H}(G_{n},v)
is a tree, we can represent the influence of the optimal matching outside the ball
B_{H}(G_{n},v)
by a well-chosen boundary condition on the messages on the ball. Namely, take a boundary vertex

and denote by

its unique neighbour in
B_{H}(G,v)
. If the vertex

is matched to an exterior vertex, we can set

, if on the contrary

. Then, the restriction of the optimal matching to
B_{H}(G,v)
coincides with the matching induced by the messages inside
B_{H}(G,v)
defined by these boundary conditions.

2.

Another general fact is that inside

, the messages around the root vertex

are deterministically increasing with respect to the boundary conditions when

is even, and decreasing when
H
is odd. So as

(1,+\infty)

3.

This motivates the search for a regime where these two extremal families converge almost surely to the same limit, we will call this regime subcritical. For Theorems
1.3
and
1.4

2

and

decay exponentially and uniformly in

.

7.1
Generalisation to lexicographic weights, min cost max gain matching

In this subsection, we introduce the formalism that is the natural generalisation of the previous results to the min cost max gain matching problem. As we will see, the situation is very similar to this article and our methods should apply. We only present the general framework and will not go into a detailed analysis.

The main idea is that we treated the case where we lexicographically maximize the sum of
(1,w(u,v))
on every edge

\displaystyle(Z_{1},Z_{2})(u,v)=\maxlex\left((0,0),\maxlex_{\begin{subarray}{c}u^{\prime}\sim v\\
u^{\prime}\neq u\end{subarray}}\left((w_{1},w_{2})(v,u^{\prime})-(Z_{1},Z_{2})(v,u^{\prime})\right)\right)

(76)

By projecting on the first component, the variable
Z_{1}
satisfies the recursion of the classic unidimensional RDE when one only seeks to optimize one weight. However, without specifying anything on the distribution
w_{1}
, one cannot hope to say more on solutions of Equation (
76
). Furthermore, there are problems when trying to solve the corresponding RDE with existence theorems using compactness since
\mathbb{R}^{2}
equipped with the lexicographical topology is not a Polish space. One needs to proceed by studying the maximum matching under weights
w_{1}+\varepsilon w_{2}
and let
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. This crucial step is not straightforward and is not a consequence of the present work. Nevertheless, if one managed to obtain the existence of those limits, the rest of the analysis would be similar with
(i_{\mathcal{H}},Z_{\mathcal{H}})
replaced by
(Z_{1,{\mathcal{H}}},Z_{2,{\mathcal{H}}})
.

7.2
Relationship between maximum weight maximum size matching and uniform maximum size matchings

In this subsection we show that, in a precise sense, maximum weight maximum size matchings and uniform maximum size matchings are fundamentally different.

Let
G_{n}
be a sequence of
unweighted
random graphs that converge in Benjamini-Schramm sense to a unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree
(\mathbb{T},o)
with reproduction law
\pi
.
For any
n
, we can consider
M_{n,\mathcal{U}}
to be a maximum size matching uniformly picked among all maximum size matchings of
G_{n}
.

From compactness arguments, the sequence
(G_{n},M_{n,\mathcal{U}})
gives rise to unimodular subsequent limits
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{U}})
where
\mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{U}}
must be a unimodular matching with the same size as the ones we have constructed in this paper.

For
\omega
with finite expectation, let us write
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}^{\omega})
to be the unique optimal matching distribution when one samples i.i.d. weights with distribution
\omega
on the edges of
\mathbb{T}
given by Theorem
1.1
.

A natural question to ask is the relationship between
\mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{U}}
and the set
\{\mathbb{M}^{\omega}\}_{\omega}
with
\omega
spanning distributions with finite expectation. The proposition below shows that they are well separated.

Proposition 7.1
.

Consider
C_{w}(\pi)
the convex hull of the distributions of variables of the form

\bigg\{(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}^{\omega}),\omega\text{ with finite expectation}\bigg\}.

Let
C_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi)
to be the convex hull of subsequent Benjamini-Schramm limits of
(G_{n},M_{n,\mathcal{U}}(G_{n}))
where
(G_{n})
is any finite graph sequence converging in Benjamini-Schramm sense to
(\mathbb{T},o)
.
For two distributions
\mu
of a variable
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M})
and
\mu^{\prime}
of a variable
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}^{\prime})
, set the total variation distance

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\sup_{\text{ events }A}|\mu(A)-\mu^{\prime}(A)|.

Then for any
\pi
with finite expectation, satisfying
\hat{\pi}\neq\delta_{0}
and
\hat{\pi}_{0}>0
, we have

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(C_{w}(\pi),C_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi))>0.

As a consequence, we deduce that it is impossible to approach uniform maximum size matchings with maximum weight matchings on i.i.d. weights.

Proof.

Let
p=\min\{j>0:\hat{\pi}_{j}>0\}
. Define
A
to be the event that
\deg(o)=p+1
, every neighbour
v_{o},....,v_{p}\sim o
has degree
p+1
and every neighbour
v_{j,1},\dots,v_{j,p}\sim v_{j},v_{j,j^{\prime}}\neq o
is a leaf. Set
B
the event that
o
is matched to one of its neighbours
v_{j}
. See Figure
17
for an illustration of this event.

Figure 17:
The event
A

On the event
A
, where
\mathbb{T}
is finite, there are
p^{p+1}
maximum size matchings that leave
o
unmatched, and
(p+1)p^{p}
maximum size matchings that matches
o
with one of its neighbours. Hence,

\mathbb{P}_{M_{\mathcal{U}}}(B|A)=\frac{(p+1)p^{p}}{(p+1)p^{p}+p^{p+1}}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{p}{p+1}}.

For the weighted matching problem, pick any weight distribution
\omega
and sample
w_{j}=w(o,v_{j})
and
w_{j,j^{\prime}}=w(v_{j},v_{j,j^{\prime}})
independently according to
\omega
.
The event
B
can then be written as the disjoint union

\bigcup_{j=0}^{p}\bigg\{(i,Z)(o,v_{j})+(i,Z)(v_{j},o)\overset{\lex}{<}(1,w_{j})\bigg\}.

It is easy to see that every edge is neither mandatory nor blocking, so the first component sums to
1
.
By using the recursion, the messages can be expressed as a function of the weights:

\displaystyle Z(o,v_{j})

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq p}w_{j,j^{\prime}},

\displaystyle Z(v_{j},o)

\displaystyle=\max\left(0,\max_{j^{\prime\prime}\neq j}(w_{j^{\prime\prime}}-\max_{1\leq j^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq p}w_{j^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime\prime}})\right).

The event
B
then rewrites into

\bigcup_{j=0}^{p}\bigg\{\max_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq p}(w_{j,j^{\prime}})+\max(0,\max_{j^{\prime\prime}\neq j}(w_{j^{\prime\prime}}-\max_{1\leq j^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq p}w_{j^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime\prime}}))<w_{j}\bigg\}.

We thus obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{M}^{\omega}}(B|A)=(p+1)\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}-\max_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq p}(w_{0,j^{\prime}})>\max(0,\max_{0<j^{\prime\prime}<p}(w_{j^{\prime\prime}}-\max_{1\leq j^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq p}w_{j^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime\prime}}))\right).

Set

\displaystyle\forall\,0\leq j\leq p,X_{j}

\displaystyle:=(w_{j}-\max_{1\leq j^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq p}w_{j,j^{\prime\prime\prime}}).

The variables
(X_{j})_{0\leq j\leq p}
are i.i.d. and
\mathbb{P}(X_{j}>0)
is exactly the probability that
w_{j}
is the largest among
p+1
i.i.d. variables of distribution
\omega
, and is thus
\tfrac{1}{p+1}
. With this notation,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{M}^{\omega}}(B|A)
rewrites as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{M}^{\omega}}(B|A)

\displaystyle=(p+1)\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}>\max(0,\max_{1\leq j\leq p}X_{j})\right).

We will now decompose with respect to which of the
X_{j}
are positive:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{M}^{\omega}}(B|A)

\displaystyle=(p+1)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,p\}}\left\{\forall j^{\prime}\in S,X_{j^{\prime}}>0,\forall j^{\prime\prime}\notin S,X_{j^{\prime\prime}}\leq 0,X_{0}>0,X_{0}>\max_{j^{\prime}\in S}X_{j^{\prime}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=(p+1)\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,p\}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}>\max_{j^{\prime}\in S}X_{j^{\prime}}|X_{0}>0,\forall j^{\prime}\in S,X_{j}^{\prime}>0\right)\mathbb{P}(X_{0}>0)^{|S|+1}\mathbb{P}(X_{0}\leq 0)^{p-|S|}

\displaystyle=(p+1)\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,p\}}\left(\frac{1}{|S|+1}\right)\frac{p^{p-|S|}}{(p+1)^{p+1}},

where we used the fact that conditionally on
X_{0}>0
and
X_{j^{\prime}}>0
for
j^{\prime}\in S
, the family
(X_{0},(X_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}\in S})
are i.i.d. so that the probability that
X_{0}
is the largest is
\tfrac{1}{|S|+1}
. Remarkably, this quantity does not depend on the distribution
\omega
.
Finally, decomposing with respect to the cardinal of
S
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{M}^{\omega}}(B|A)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{p}}{(p+1)^{p}}\sum_{l=0}^{p}\binom{p}{l}\frac{1}{l+1}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)^{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{p}}{(p+1)^{p}}\frac{1}{p+1}\frac{(1+\frac{1}{p})^{p+1}-1}{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p+1)^{p+1}-p^{p+1}}{(p+1)^{p+1}}

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-\frac{1}{p+1}\right)^{p+1}.

Since
A
is only a conditioning on
\mathbb{T}
, we get that for any
\omega
,

\mathbb{P}_{(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}^{\omega})}(B\cap A)=\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{p+1}\right)^{p+1}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{T}}(A)

whereas for any subsequent limit
(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}^{\mathcal{U}})
of uniform maximum size matchings, we have that:

\mathbb{P}_{(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbb{M}^{\mathcal{U}})}(B\cap A)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{p}{p+1}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{T}}(A).

From this we deduce that

\forall\mu\in C_{w}(\pi),\mu(A\cap B)=\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{p+1}\right)^{p+1}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{T}}(A)

and

\forall\mu^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi),\mu^{\prime}(A\cap B)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{p}{p+1}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{T}}(A).

As a conclusion, as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{T}}(A)=\pi_{p+1}\hat{\pi}_{p}^{p+1}\hat{\pi}_{0}^{p(p+1)}
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(C_{w}(\pi),C_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi))

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\mu\in C_{w}(\pi),\mu^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi)}|\mu(A\cap B)-\mu^{\prime}(A\cap B)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{1+\frac{p}{p+1}}-1+\left(1-\frac{1}{p+1}\right)^{p+1}\right|\pi_{p+1}\hat{\pi}_{p}^{p+1}\hat{\pi}_{0}^{p(p+1)}>0.

∎

Remark 18
.

We find an effective lower bound in this proof. By considering every possible "central symmetric" configuration of finite trees, one could, in theory, further improve this lower bound.
To remove the condition
\hat{\pi}_{0}>0
, one would need to condition on infinite trees which would require studying Benjamini-Schramm limits of uniform maximum size matchings.

7.3
A conjecture on mandatory and blocking edges

In the previous subsection, we have shown that uniform maximum size matchings and maximum weight matching maximum size are well separated. Nevertheless, in Theorem
1.5

It is natural to ask whether these results hold outside of the subcritical regime.

For Erdős-Renyí random graphs, Theorem
1.5
reads:

Corollary 7.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{T},o)

Conjecture 3

\left(G_{n},\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{\max}(G_{n})}M},\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcap_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{\max}(G_{n})}M^{\complement}}\right)

converges in Benjamini Schramm sense to

\left(\mathbb{T},o,\mathbbm{1}_{i(\overset{\rightarrow}{e})+i(\overset{\leftarrow}{e})<k},\mathbbm{1}_{i(\overset{\rightarrow}{e})+i(\overset{\leftarrow}{e})>k}\right).

Proof of Proposition
4.5

Recall
4.5
:
See
4.5

We will first prove the equivalent proposition for unidimensional variables:

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be two real-valued random variables such that
Y\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Y^{\prime}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}[(Y-Y^{\prime})_{+}]=\mathbb{E}[(Y-Y^{\prime})_{-}].

Proof of Lemma
8.1
.

We will break down the proof into three steps, we will first prove it assuming
Y-Y^{\prime}
is integrable, then nonnegative, then without any assumptions on
Y-Y^{\prime}
.

Let us assume
Y-Y^{\prime}
is integrable, we will show that
\mathbb{E}[Y-Y^{\prime}]=0
.
Write
Y^{\prime}+(Y-Y^{\prime})=Y
, set
H=Y-Y^{\prime}
, then:

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it(Y+H)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itY}\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itY}\left(e^{itH}-1\right)\right]=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itY}\frac{\left(e^{itH}-1\right)}{t}\right]=0.

The integrand is dominated by
|H|
which is integrable, so by dominated convergence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itY}\frac{\left(e^{itH}-1\right)}{t}\right]\underset{t\rightarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{E}[iH].

Hence
\mathbb{E}[H]=0
.

Now let us assume that
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are nonnegative. If the difference is integrable then we are already done. Assume
(Y-Y^{\prime})
is not integrable, we will proceed by contradiction.
Let us assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{E}\left[H_{+}\right]<+\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\left[H_{-}\right]=+\infty
.

Since the variables
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are nonnegative, we can define their Laplace transform on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
:

\displaystyle\forall a\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-a(Y+H)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-aY}\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\forall a\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-aY}\left(e^{-aH}-1\right)\right]=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\forall a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-aY}\frac{\left(e^{-aH}-1\right)}{a}\right]=0.

We decompose the above expectation into positive and negative parts:

\displaystyle\forall a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-aY}\frac{\left(e^{-aH}-1\right)}{a}\mathbbm{1}_{H>0}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-aY}\frac{\left(e^{-aH}-1\right)}{a}\mathbbm{1}_{H<0}\right]:=I_{+}(a)+I_{-}(a)=0.

The integrand in
I_{+}(a)
is dominated by
H_{+}
so by dominated convergence theorem:

\displaystyle\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}I_{+}(a)=-\mathbb{E}[H_{+}].

For
I_{-}(a)
, for every
M>0
, define
Z_{M}=\max(H_{-},M)
, then:

\displaystyle I_{-}(a)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-aY}\frac{\left(e^{aZ_{M}}-1\right)}{a}\mathbbm{1}_{H_{-}>0}\right].

Now in the second expectation, the integrand is bounded by
Me^{M}
, so fixing
M
and taking
a\rightarrow 0^{+}
yields by dominated convergence theorem:

\displaystyle\liminf_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}I_{-}(a)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{M}\mathbbm{1}_{H_{-}>0}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[H_{-}\mathbbm{1}_{H_{-}\leq M}\right].

Taking
M\rightarrow\infty
yields:

\displaystyle\liminf_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}I_{-}(a)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[H_{-}\right]=+\infty.

In conclusion we have shown that
I_{-}(a)+I_{+}(a)=0
for every
a>0
, that
I_{+}(a)
is bounded but that
I_{-}(a)
diverges to
+\infty
as
a\rightarrow 0^{+}
. This is clearly a contradiction.

Now we will lift the nonnegativity requirement by decomposing
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
into positive and negative parts. We assume that
H_{+}
is integrable and we will show that
H_{-}
is also integrable.
Writing
Y=Y_{+}-Y_{-}
and
Y^{\prime}=Y_{+}^{\prime}-Y_{-}^{\prime}
we have that:

\displaystyle(Y_{+}-Y_{+}^{\prime})_{+}\leq(Y-Y^{\prime})_{+}.

Indeed

1.

If
Y\geq 0
and
Y^{\prime}\geq 0
, there is equality.

2.

If
Y<0
then the left-hand side is
(-Y_{+}^{\prime})_{+}=0
and the right-hand side is positive, so it holds.

3.

If
Y\geq 0
and
Y^{\prime}<0
then the left hand side is
Y_{+}
and the right hand side is
Y_{+}-Y^{\prime}>Y_{+}
so it still holds.

Since
H_{+}
is integrable by hypothesis, the previous discussion thus implies that
(Y_{+}-Y_{+}^{\prime})_{+}
is also integrable.
By symmetry, we deduce that
(Y_{-}^{\prime}-Y_{-})_{+}
must also be integrable.

Since
Y_{+}
and
Y_{+}^{\prime}
have the same law, applying the nonnegative result to the couple
(Y_{+},Y_{+}^{\prime})
yields that
(Y_{+}-Y_{+}^{\prime})
must be integrable.
By symmetry,
(Y_{-}^{\prime}-Y_{-})
is also integrable.

Now writing that
H=Y-Y^{\prime}=(Y_{+}-Y_{+}^{\prime})-(Y_{-}-Y_{-}^{\prime})
, we have that
H
is integrable, the first step yields the conclusion.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain the following unidimensional version of Proposition
4.5
:

Proposition 8.2
.

If
X,X^{\prime},Y,Y^{\prime}
are four real valued random variables such that
X
is integrable,
Y\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Y^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime}\leq 0
almost surely.
If almost surely,
X+X^{\prime}\geq Y-Y^{\prime}
, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]\geq 0

with equality implying
X^{\prime}=0
almost surely.

Proof of Proposition
8.2
.

Since
X^{\prime}\leq 0
, we have that
X\geq Y-Y^{\prime}
which in turn implies that
(Y-Y^{\prime})_{+}
must be integrable.
Applying Lemma
8.1
, we get that
(Y-Y^{\prime})_{-}
is integrable and hence
Y-Y^{\prime}
is integrable and of zero expectation.
Taking expectation in
X\geq Y-Y^{\prime}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]\geq\mathbb{E}[Y-Y^{\prime}]=0.

Now if
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
then
X-(Y-Y^{\prime})\geq-X^{\prime}\geq 0
implies that
X^{\prime}
is integrable and that
\mathbb{E}[-X^{\prime}]=0
. Therefore
X^{\prime}=0
almost surely.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
4.5
:

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

First let us look at the projection on the first marginal. The projection on the first marginal converts lexicographic order into the usual order on
\mathbb{R}
.
Let us write
X=(X_{1},X_{2}),X^{\prime}=(X_{1}^{\prime},X_{2}^{\prime}),Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})
and
Y^{\prime}=(Y_{1}^{\prime},Y_{2}^{\prime})
we then have:

X_{1}+X_{1}^{\prime}\geq Y_{1}-Y_{1}^{\prime}
with
X_{1}
integrable,
X_{1}^{\prime}\leq 0
almost surely, and
Y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Y_{1}^{\prime}
.
Applying Lemma
8.2
to
X_{1},X_{1}^{\prime},Y_{1},Y_{1}^{\prime}
, we obtain that
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\geq 0
with equality if and only if
X_{1}^{\prime}=0
almost surely.

If
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]>0
then we must have
\mathbb{E}[X]\overset{\lex}{>}(0,0)
and we are done. Else,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=0
in which case
X_{1}^{\prime}=0
almost surely.
Furthermore, by the inequality
X_{1}-Y_{1}+Y_{1}^{\prime}\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-Y_{1}+Y_{1}]=0
we deduce that almost surely,
X_{1}=Y_{1}-Y_{1}^{\prime}
.
In this case,
X+X^{\prime}\overset{\lex}{\geq}Y-Y^{\prime}
becomes
X_{2}+X_{2}^{\prime}\geq Y_{2}-Y_{2}^{\prime}
as there is always equality on the first marginal.
Once again,
X_{2}
is integrable,
X_{2}^{\prime}\leq 0
almost surely since
X_{1}^{\prime}=0
and
X^{\prime}\overset{\lex}{\leq}(0,0)
, and
Y_{2}
and
Y_{2}^{\prime}
have the same law as the images of the second projection of
Y,Y^{\prime}
.
Now we simply apply Proposition
8.2
to
X_{2},X_{2}^{\prime},Y_{2},Y_{2}^{\prime}
to deduce that
\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]\geq 0
with equality implying
\mathbb{E}[X_{2}^{\prime}]=0
.

Putting everything together, we have shown that
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]>0
or
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]\geq 0
which is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}[X]\overset{\lex}{\geq}(0,0)
. Furthermore, if there is equality then
X_{1}^{\prime}=X_{2}^{\prime}=0
almost surely which means that
X^{\prime}=(0,0)
almost surely.
