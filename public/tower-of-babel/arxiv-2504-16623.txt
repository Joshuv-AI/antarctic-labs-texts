Censored lifespans in a double-truncated sample: Maximum likelihood inference for the exponential distribution
Fiete Sieg & Anne-Marie Toparkus & Rafael Weißbach

Abstract
The analysis of a truncated sample can be hindered by censoring. Survival information may be lost to follow-up or the birthdate may be missing. The data can still be modeled as a truncated point process and it is close to a Poisson process, in the Hellinger distance, as long as the sample is small relative to the population. We assume an exponential distribution for the lifespan, derive the likelihood and profile out the unobservable sample size. Identification of the exponential parameter is shown, together with consistency and asymptotic normality of its M-estimator. Even though the estimator sequence is indexed in the sample size, both the point estimator and the standard error are observable. Enterprise lifespans in Germany constitute our example. Keywords: Truncation, censoring, point process, maximum likelihood, asymptotic inference



1
Introduction and sampling design

In a simple random sample, each individual of the population has the same and parameter-independent selection probability. A left-truncated panel causes two probabilities, individuals who had died before the study have a probability of zero and those remaining have a parameter-dependent probability. The design has prominent applications in economics
(
Heckman 1976
)
and medicine
(
Kalbfleisch and Lawless 1989
)
and the statistical analysis has a long history
(

;

;

;

;

;

;

;
Eriksson et al. 2015
;
Weißbach et al. 2024
)
. Panel data are double-truncated when an individual who dies after the study also has a probability of zero
(

;

;
Shen 2010
;
Moreira and de Uña-Álvarez 2010
;
Shen 2014
;
Moreira et al. 2016
;
Emura et al. 2017
;
Frank et al. 2019
;
Emura and Pan 2020
;
Weißbach and Wied 2022
;
Weißbach and Dörre 2022
)
. The European Commission has published business activity since 2018 with a new definition of an enterprise. Together with the national statistical offices, enterprise foundations and closures are reported, although the mere status of enterprise existence is not reported and the data is hence doubly truncated.

The additional obstacle caused by censoring is that the lifespans of observable individuals are only partially available. The lifespan of an enterprise that was founded in 2018 and is still active when a study ends, here after 2019, is right-censored. s

G-s

(A1)

Let
\Theta=[{\varepsilon},1/{\varepsilon}]

{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)

(A2)

Let

(A3)

Let

By the design of the study, a sample unit is only observable subject to additional conditions. We follow

for the case of a unit born before the study, i.e. for

4.

(L_{i},R_{i})=(1,1)

3
Identification and estimation

The elementary definition of identification in the population assumes the data to be a simple random sample. However, following
Andersen et al. 1988
, Assumptions
(A1)
-
4
formulate the data
\{(y^{obs}_{j},l^{obs}_{j},r^{obs}_{j})\}_{j\leq m}
to be a truncated sample.
Efron and Petrosian 1999
instead assume the data to be a simple random sample from the population subject to the restriction
D
. Sampling and truncation are not commutative
(see
Toparkus and Weißbach 2025
, Figure 2)
. In order to prove consistency when minimizing (
3
),
van der Vaart 1998
formulates a generalized definition for identification. A preliminary step in order to prove the generalized definition is to prove identification of
\theta
in the ordinary sense while assuming that
\{(y^{obs}_{j},l^{obs}_{j},r^{obs}_{j})\}_{j\leq m}
is a simple random sample from the
D
-restricted population.

Lemma 1
.

The parameter
\theta
is identified in the
D
-restricted population.

Proof.

The probability measure in the restricted population is
{{\mathbb{P}}}^{obs}_{\theta}={\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}/\alpha_{\theta}
. We need to show that from
{{\mathbb{P}}}^{obs}_{\theta_{1}}={{\mathbb{P}}}^{obs}_{\theta_{2}}
with
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
immediately follows
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
. Let be
{{\mathbb{P}}}^{obs}_{\theta_{1}}={{\mathbb{P}}}^{obs}_{\theta_{2}}
, so that for arbitrary
(y,l,r)
in the support
D
it follows for the observable data that
{{\mathbb{P}}}^{obs}_{\theta_{1}}(Y^{obs}_{j}\leq y,L^{obs}_{j}=l,R^{obs}_{j}=r)={{\mathbb{P}}}^{obs}_{\theta_{2}}(Y^{obs}_{j}\leq y,L^{obs}_{j}=l,R^{obs}_{j}=r)
, which means
F_{\theta_{1}}=F_{\theta_{2}}
. Hence it is by (
2
)

1=\frac{f_{\theta_{1}}(y,l,r)}{f_{\theta_{2}}(y,l,r)}=\frac{\alpha_{\theta_{2}}}{\alpha_{\theta_{1}}}e^{-y(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})}\left\{\frac{1-e^{-\theta_{1}(G-s)}}{1-e^{-\theta_{2}(G-s)}}\right\}^{l}\left\{\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}\right\}^{(1-l)(1-r)}

on the support of
f_{\theta}
. It now suffices to re-sort the
y
-dependent part on the left and differentiate once with respect to
y
, to result in
(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})e^{y(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})}=0
which is equivalent to
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
.
∎

Maximization of likelihood (
3
) yields
n=m/\alpha_{\theta}
and we profile out
n
. Define
m_{\theta}(y,l,r):=\chi_{D}(y,l,r)\log f_{\theta}(y,l,r)-\chi_{D}(y,l,r)C(y,l,r)
, with
\theta
-independent

C(y,l,r):=-\log(G)+\log(s-y)(1-l)(1-r)
. Then (
3
) can be represented as

\log L(data|\theta,n)\big|_{n=m/\alpha_{\theta}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}m_{\theta}(y^{obs}_{j},l^{obs}_{j},r^{obs}_{j})+C_{m}^{\prime}

(4)

with parameter-independent
C^{\prime}_{m}:=m\log(m)-m-m\log(G)+3s+\sum_{j=1}^{m}(1-l_{j})(1-r^{obs}_{j})\log(s-y^{obs}_{j})
. Define further
M_{n}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{\theta}(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})
and
M(\theta):={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}M_{n}(\theta)
(where
{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}
is the expectation with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
),
so that maximizing arguments of (
4
) and
M_{n}(\theta)
are identical. The first does not need the unobserved
n
and can be used for the computation, whereas the second is an average and can be used for the analysis.
Under the Assumptions
(A1)
-
4
, short calculations yield the following properties (and especially use Lemma
1
for (e)):

(a)

For
0<s<G
, the function
\theta\mapsto m_{\theta}(y,l,r)
is continuous on
\Theta
for all
(y,l,r)\in D
.

(b)

The function
\theta\mapsto m_{\theta}
is dominated by a
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}
-integrable
\theta
-independent function.

(c)

It is
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|M_{n}(\theta)-M(\theta)|\overset{p}{\to}0
.

(d)

The function
\theta\mapsto M(\theta)
is continuous on
\Theta
.

(e)

The true parameter
\theta_{0}
is the unique maximizer of
M(\theta)
.

As maximizing argument of the criterion function
M_{n}(\theta)
define

\hat{\theta}_{n}:=\inf\big\{\hat{\theta}\in\Theta\,|\,\forall\theta\in\Theta:\,M_{n}(\hat{\theta})\geq M_{n}(\theta)\big\}.

(5)

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumptions
(A1)
-
4
and
0<s<G
it is
\hat{\theta}_{n}\overset{p}{\to}\theta_{0}
for
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

The first condition of
van der Vaart 1998
is
(c)
. The second condition is the generalized identification definition. Note that
\theta_{0}
uniquely maximizes

due to
(e)
, and that the continuity of

. Hence, each sequence
(\tilde{\theta}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

converges in probability against

and hence

∎

The consistency is one condition for the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}_{n}
and we now state the remaining conditions.

Corollary 1
.

It is
\theta\mapsto m_{\theta}(y,l,r)
two times differentiable in
\theta
on
({\varepsilon},1/{\varepsilon})
for all
(y,l,r)\in D

Corollary 2
.

It is
\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}M(\theta)={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}\,\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}m_{\theta}(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})

For all

(6)

for a measurable bound
\dot{m}
with
{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}\dot{m}(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})^{2}<\infty
.

As final preparation, and similar to the Fisher information in maximum likelihood theory, we have (with proof in
A

Lemma 2
.

For
0<s<G
and for any
\theta_{0}\in\Theta

\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}M(\theta_{0})\neq 0

Here we write as usual for a function

\frac{d}{dx}g(a)

\frac{d}{dx}g(x)|_{x=a}
.

Theorem 2
.

Under the Assumptions
(A1)
-
4
and
0<s<G

\{\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\sigma^{2}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}[\frac{d}{d\theta}m_{\theta_{0}}(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})^{2}]/{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}[\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}m_{\theta_{0}}(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})]^{2}

The proof applies Theorem 5.23 of

∎

B.3
Derivation of likelihood approximation (Formula
3
)

Let denote
{\varepsilon}_{P}
the Dirac-measure in point
P
. The data are now described by the truncated point process

N_{n,D}(\cdot):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\varepsilon}_{(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})}(\cdot\cap D)
, which equals in distribution a Binomial process according
Reiss 1993
. It is a mapping
\Omega^{n}\to\mathbb{M}(S,\mathcal{B})
with the point measure over
(S,\mathcal{B})
as image, and
\mathcal{B}
as
\sigma
-field over
S
. Note that
N_{n,D}(B):\Omega^{n}\to\{0,\dots,n\}
with
\omega\mapsto N_{n,D}^{\omega}(B)
for each
B\in\mathcal{B}
is the random number of
\{(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, that belong to
B\cap D
. Denote the intensity measure by
\nu_{n,D}
and we will approximate the density of
N_{n,D}
, i.e. the likelihood, by that of a Poisson process
N_{n}^{*}
which has an identical intensity measure. The likelihood approximation can than be maximized in
(n,\theta)
. Both processes are close in Hellinger distance as
\alpha_{\theta_{0}}
is small
(
Reiss 1993
, Theorem 1.4.2)
, for instance if
s\ll G
.

Define
Z_{n}
to be Poisson-distributed with parameter
n\alpha_{\theta_{0}}
and to be independent of a sequence of independent and identically distributed random vectors
\{(Y_{l}^{*},L_{l}^{*},R_{l}^{*})\}_{l\geq 1}
, each with density (
2
). Define
N_{n}^{*}(\cdot):=\sum_{l=1}^{Z_{n}}{\varepsilon}_{(Y_{l}^{*},L_{l}^{*},R_{l}^{*})}(\cdot)
.

Lemma 3
.

The intensity measure
\nu_{n}^{*}
of
N_{n}^{*}
is equal to

\nu_{n,D}
.

Proof.

For
B\in\mathcal{B}
it is
\nu_{n}^{*}(B)={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}N_{n}^{*}(B)={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}\sum_{l=1}^{Z_{n}}{\varepsilon}_{(Y_{l}^{*},L_{l}^{*},R_{l}^{*})}(B)
.
Due to the independence of
Z_{n}
, the distribution
Z_{n}\sim\text{Poi}(n\alpha_{\theta_{0}})
and the identical distributions of the
(Y_{l}^{*},L_{l}^{*},R_{l}^{*})
follows

\displaystyle\nu_{n}^{*}(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}Z_{n}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}{\varepsilon}_{(Y^{*},L^{*},R^{*})}(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\alpha_{\theta_{0}}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y^{*},L^{*},R^{*})\in B\big)=n\alpha_{\theta_{0}}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y^{obs},L^{obs},R^{obs})\in B\big).

And furthermore
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y^{obs},L^{obs},R^{obs})\in B\big)={\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y,L,R)\in B\,|\,(Y,L,R)\in D\big)
so that

\displaystyle\nu_{n}^{*}(B)

\displaystyle=n\alpha_{\theta_{0}}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y,L,R)\in B\,\big|\,(Y,L,R)\in D\big)

\displaystyle=n\alpha_{\theta_{0}}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y,L,R)\in B\cap D\big)\big/\alpha_{\theta_{0}}=n\cdot{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y,L,R)\in B\cap D\big).

Finally, the last expression is
\nu_{n,D}(B)
, because
\nu_{n,D}(B)={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}N_{n,D}(B)={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\varepsilon}_{(Y_{i},L_{i},R_{i})}(B\cap D)=n\cdot{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\big((Y,L,R)\in B\cap D\big)
.
∎

In order to derive the Radon-Nikodym derivative of

the dominating measure is chosen to be that of a parameter-independent Poisson process

.
To this end, define
(Y_{i}^{0},L_{i}^{0},R_{i}^{0})
with

and thereof independent

\displaystyle=3s\cdot\frac{1}{s}\,\lambda_{[0,s]}(B_{1})\cdot\frac{1}{3}\,\text{card}\big(B_{2}\cap\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0)\}\big)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{[0,s]}(B_{1})\cdot\big\{{\varepsilon}_{(0,0)}(B_{2})+{\varepsilon}_{(0,1)}(B_{2})+{\varepsilon}_{(1,0)}(B_{2})\big\}=:\lambda_{[0,s]}(B_{1})\cdot\mu_{0}(B_{2}),
