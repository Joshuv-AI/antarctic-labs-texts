Acute effects of moderate intensity exercise on uric acid excretion in underexcretion hyperuricemia
Authors: Wenlang Yu, Yusha Ma, Yichao Yu, Wenxing Wang, Qinlong Li, Yuanhui Zhao, Hong Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Uric acid excretion insufficiency is a major etiology of Hyperuricemia (HUA). While exercise intervention serves as a non-pharmacological uric acid-lowering strategy, acute exercise may cause transient serum uric acid (SUA) elevation, posing risks to HUA patients. This pilot study investigated the acute effects of a single bout of moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) on SUA and uric acid excretion indices in young men with underexcretion hyperuricemia, aiming to characterize the acute physiological responses, with a focus on potential safety implications. This was a pilot study using a self-controlled pre-post design. Prior to the experiment, 24-hour urine collection and fasting venous blood sampling were conducted. Patients were classified based on 24-hour urinary uric acid (UUA) excretion and fractional excretion of urate (FEUA). A total of 18 patients with Underexcretion Hyperuricemia were finally included in the acute MICT intervention. Blood/urine samples were collected pre-intervention, post-intervention, and 30 min post-intervention. No statistically significant changes were observed in SUA or UUA following the acute exercise intervention (P > 0.05). Notably, key uric acid excretion indices increased significantly at 30 min post-intervention: fractional excretion of urate (FEUA, P < 0.001), UUA/creatinine ratio (UUA/UCr, P < 0.01), and Simkin index (P < 0.001) were all markedly higher than pre-intervention levels. Additionally, blood lactate was significantly elevated immediately post-intervention (P < 0.01), and serum creatinine (SCr) increased significantly both immediately and 30 min post-intervention (both P < 0.05). MICT did not induce a statistically significant acute increase in SUA in this pilot cohort, while uric acid excretion indices increased after exercise. Although no apparent large acute SUA surge was observed within the post-exercise window, these findings are preliminary, and confirmatory controlled trials in larger and more diverse populations are required.

Introduction

Uric acid excretion insufficiency is a major etiology of Hyperuricemia (HUA), yet clinical management of HUA often lacks subtype-specific interventions—a gap highlighted by key guidelines. Both the 2006 EULAR and the 2012 ACR gout management guidelines highlight the importance of etiological classification of hyperuricemia, distinguishing between uric acid overproduction and underexcretion, as a foundation for individualized urate-lowering therapy. Each subtype reflects distinct pathophysiological mechanisms that warrant targeted treatment approaches1,2.Hyperuricemia has traditionally been classified according to urinary urate excretion parameters, including 24-hour urinary uric acid excretion (UUA) and fractional excretion of urate (FEUA), into underexcretion, overproduction, combined, and normal types1–3. Ichida et al. further suggested that a substantial proportion of patients previously categorized as “overproduction type” may actually represent “renal overload type” hyperuricemia caused by impaired extra-renal urate excretion, particularly involving ABCG2 dysfunction3. Nevertheless, impaired renal urate excretion remains widely recognized as the predominant mechanism underlying hyperuricemia, and approximately 70–90% of patients are classified as having underexcretion hyperuricemia4.

From a pathophysiological perspective, underexcretion hyperuricemia is primarily characterized by an imbalance in renal tubular urate transport, in which the balance between urate secretion and reabsorption shifts toward enhanced reabsorption5,6. Under physiological conditions, urate is transported from the bloodstream into proximal tubular epithelial cells via organic anion transporters such as OAT1 and OAT3, while apical transporters including NPT1, NPT4, ABCG2, and ABCC4 mediate urate secretion into the tubular lumen. In contrast, transporters such as URAT1, OAT4, and GLUT9 participate in the reabsorption of urate across the apical membrane of proximal tubular cells5–7. When secretory transport pathways are impaired or reabsorptive transport is enhanced, renal urate clearance becomes insufficient. Consequently, even without a substantial increase in uric acid production, serum uric acid levels may remain elevated, resulting in the clinical phenotype of hyperuricemia primarily driven by impaired renal excretion5,6. Clinically, patients with this subtype face unique challenges, as they may derive limited benefit from conventional purine-restricted dietary strategies. In addition, although urate-lowering therapy remains the cornerstone of HUA management, the long-term safety profiles and cardiovascular safety of certain urate-lowering agents remain under ongoing investigation and debate8–12. Therefore, exploring adjunctive non-pharmacological strategies, including exercise intervention, may have potential clinical value. Epidemiological evidence consistently shows that regular exercise confers protective effects against HUA, while chronic sedentariness increases susceptibility9,13–15.

However, existing exercise studies on HUA rarely stratify patients by subtype; most evidence comes from healthy individuals, those with hypertension, or those with hyperlipidemia, in which SUA is considered only as a secondary endpoint13,16,17. Acute exercise has been shown to influence SUA metabolism through multiple physiological pathways, including accelerated purine nucleotide degradation, increased lactate accumulation, oxidative stress, and transient alterations in renal urate handling16,18–23. In particular, high-intensity or exhaustive exercise may transiently elevate SUA due to increased ATP turnover and reduced renal urate clearance18,22,23. In contrast, moderate aerobic exercise has generally been associated with improved urate metabolism and lower long-term SUA levels13,24,25. Importantly, abrupt fluctuations in SUA have been implicated as potential triggers for gout flares14,15. Therefore, understanding the acute SUA response to exercise may be particularly relevant in individuals with underexcretion hyperuricemia, whose impaired renal urate excretion could theoretically limit compensation for acute metabolic perturbations during exercise. However, no studies have specifically investigated the acute metabolic responses to exercise in this subtype of HUA patients.

This study aimed to investigate the acute effects of moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) on serum uric acid and uric acid excretion indices in individuals with underexcretion hyperuricemia, with the goal of characterizing the acute metabolic responses to MICT and providing preliminary evidence regarding its safety in this high-risk subtype.

Methods

Study design

A prospective, single-arm, pre-post intervention study was conducted. No concurrent non-exercise control group was included. Therefore, observed post-exercise changes cannot be attributed exclusively to MICT, and potential influences of circadian variation and short-term hydration-related effects cannot be fully excluded. Participants with hyperuricemia were screened and classified into subtypes using urinary uric acid excretion parameters. Eligible subjects with Underexcretion Hyperuricemia (n = 18) underwent acute MICT. The study flow is shown in Fig. 1. Biological samples (venous blood and urine) were collected at baseline, post-exercise termination, and 30 min post-exercise. All participants furnished written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Beijing Sport University Ethics Committee (No.2022179 H), and all procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Subject

This study recruited male hyperuricemia patients from a certain hospital in Beijing between September and December 2022. Participants underwent hyperuricemia subtyping to identify those with Underexcretion Hyperuricemia as study subjects.

Participants’ Inclusion Criteria: (1) Adult males aged 18–35 years; (2) Diagnosed with HUA via fasting SUA > 420 µmol/L on two non-consecutive days; (3) Classified as Underexcretion Hyperuricemia based on uric acid excretion parameters; (4) Not currently receiving any uric acid-lowering therapy; (5) No history of gout attacks within one month prior to testing; (6) Commitment to a low-purine diet during the study period; (7) Willingness to provide written informed consent.

Participants’ Exclusion Criteria: (1) Presence of comorbidities with hyperuricemia: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, obesity, stroke, coronary heart disease, cardiac insufficiency, uric acid nephrolithiasis, renal dysfunction, etc.; (2) Acute gout attack or other inflammatory conditions at the time of screening; (3) Current use of medications affecting uric acid metabolism (e.g., colchicine, allopurinol, febuxostat, benzbromarone); (4) Presence of exercise contraindications or musculoskeletal injuries precluding completion of the acute exercise intervention. Subtyping method for hyperuricemia

Screening sample collection (24-hour urine and fasting blood)

A 24-hour urine sample collection was first performed, starting after the first morning urination on day 1 and ending with the first morning urination on day 2. Participants discontinued medications interfering with SUA (e.g., colchicine, allopurinol, febuxostat, benzbromarone) two weeks prior to urine collection. Five days before collection, drugs affecting uric acid excretion (e.g., aspirin, diuretics, insulin, penicillin, vitamins B1, B2, and C) were ceased, and a low-purine diet was initiated. Before sample collection, trained personnel conducted a structured interview with the subjects to confirm the need for a low-purine diet and medication discontinuation. One day before collection, strenuous exercise and excessive sweating were avoided. On the collection day, participants were advised to drink adequate water. Total 24-hour urine volume was accurately recorded, with preservative added to the collection container. In cases of dehydration (e.g., diarrhea, vomiting), fever, urinary tract infection, or other acute illnesses on the collection day, the procedure was rescheduled.

Following urine collection, fasting blood samples were obtained. After 12 h of fasting, 5 ml of sterile cubital venous blood was collected in the morning (7:00–8:00) under resting conditions. Samples were allowed to clot without anticoagulant, then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to separate serum. Measured indices included UUA, urine volume (UV), serum creatinine (SCr), urine creatinine (UCr), Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG), Total Cholesterol (TC), Triglyceride (TG), Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C), High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C) and Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), analyzed using an automated biochemical analyzer (UniCel DxC 800 Synchron, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Uric acid clearance (CUr) and creatinine clearance (CCr) were calculated. Etiological subtyping was determined using 24-hour urinary uric acid excretion (24hUUE) and fractional excretion of urate (FEUA), derived from the screening 24-hour urine collection, according to established criteria (Table 2). Test program

Exercise intervention protocol

This study utilized moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) as the exercise intervention, administered via cycle ergometry on a Monark 894E cycle ergometer (Monark, Sweden). The exercise duration was 30 min. The objective criterion for exercise intensity was blood lactate (< 4 mmol/L)26. Polar H10 (Polar, Finland) was used throughout the exercise to continuously record heart rate (HR) in real time. Prior to the intervention, participants wore the Polar H10 chest strap in a seated, quiet resting position for 5 min to measure resting heart rate (RHR). Maximum heart rate (HRmax) was estimated as 207 − (0.7 × age), and heart rate reserve (HRR) was calculated as HRR = HRmax − RHR. The target heart rate during MICT was determined using the Karvonen formula: Target HR = (HRR × intensity percentage) + RHR27,28, where the intensity percentage ranged from 40 to 59%29. HR was continuously monitored during exercise, and ergometer resistance was adjusted as necessary to maintain values within the moderate-intensity target range.

Sample collection during exercise intervention

Pre-exercise sampling: Mid-stream urine samples (30–50 ml) were collected, mixed thoroughly, and 5–10 ml aliquots were stored. Following urine collection, 5 ml of sterile cubital venous blood was drawn at rest, allowed to clot without anticoagulant, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected.

Immediate post-exercise sampling: Within 1 min of exercise termination, 5 ml of venous blood was collected using the same protocol. Urine sampling (30–50 ml mid-stream, 5–10 ml aliquot) was performed immediately after blood collection.

30-minute post-exercise sampling: At 30 min after exercise, a repeat blood draw and urine collection were conducted using identical procedures. The detailed sampling workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2. All blood and urine samples were analyzed using an automated biochemical analyzer (UniCel DxC 800 Synchron, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Additionally, participant body weight was measured before and after the intervention, and water intake during exercise was recorded to calculate fluid loss. The specific testing items and time points are summarized in Table 3.

Uric acid excretion evaluation indices

During the acute MICT trial, uric acid handling was evaluated using spot urine–derived indices collected at standardized time points (pre-exercise, immediately post-exercise, and 30 min post-exercise). Importantly, spot urine–derived FEUA during the trial was used to quantify within-subject acute responses and was not intended to be numerically interchangeable with 24-hour urine–derived FEUA used for etiological subtyping at screening; accordingly, spot FEUA was not used for reclassification.

Uric acid handling during the acute trial was evaluated using multiple spot urine–derived indices, including fractional excretion of urate (FEUA), the urine uric acid-to-creatinine ratio (UUA/UCr), and the Simkin index. The UUA/UCr ratio serves as a creatinine-adjusted measure of urinary uric acid excretion, which may reduce variability related to urine dilution30. The Simkin index reflects the uric acid content per unit of glomerular filtrate and integrates urinary uric acid concentration with contemporaneous serum creatinine levels31. Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., USA), and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA) was used for data visualization. Normality of variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant for omnibus (primary) tests.

For normally distributed outcomes, changes across the three time points (pre-exercise, immediately post-exercise, and 30 min post-exercise) were evaluated using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA with time as the within-subject factor. If the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. When the omnibus test was significant, pairwise comparisons between time points were conducted using paired-samples t-tests.

For non-normally distributed outcomes, the Friedman test was used to assess overall time effects. When significant, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

To control the family-wise error rate for multiple pairwise comparisons, the Holm–Bonferroni sequential procedure (Holm, 1979) was applied to the post hoc P values, and Holm-adjusted P values were reported.

Effect sizes for within-subject comparisons were expressed as Cohen’s dz (standardized mean difference for paired samples) for parametric pairwise comparisons. For nonparametric comparisons, effect sizes were reported as r derived from the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen (1988): 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium), and 0.8 (large)32.

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7; Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Germany) for a repeated-measures ANOVA (within-subject design with three time points), assuming α = 0.05 and power (1 − β) = 0.80. Based on a large effect size assumption (f = 0.4)8, the minimum required sample size was 12 participants. To evaluate the robustness of this assumption, we conducted a sensitivity power analysis under smaller effect size scenarios while holding other parameters constant (three measurements, one group, correlation among repeated measures = 0.5, nonsphericity correction ε = 1). Under a moderate effect size assumption (f = 0.25), the required sample size increased to 28 participants; under a small effect size assumption (f = 0.20), the required sample size increased to 42 participants. Therefore, the present sample size (n = 18) was adequately powered to detect large within-subject effects but may be underpowered to detect small-to-moderate changes in SUA.

Results

Changes in monitoring indicators before and after exercise

The Friedman test showed no overall time effect on SUA (χ² = 0.761, P = 0.684), with a negligible effect size (Kendall’s W = 0.021), indicating no evidence of time-dependent change. The mean ranks were similar across time points (baseline: 1.92; immediately post-exercise: 2.17; 30 min post-exercise: 1.92). Post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm–Bonferroni adjustment (k = 3) detected no significant pairwise differences: baseline vs. immediately post-exercise (Holm-adjusted P = 0.845, r = 0.05, negligible effect), baseline vs. 30 min post-exercise (Holm-adjusted P = 0.687, r = 0.09, small effect), and immediately post-exercise vs. 30 min post-exercise (Holm-adjusted P = 0.255, r = 0.41, medium effect). The specific changes are shown in Table 4.

For SCr, repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to examine time effects. Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was met (W = 0.878, P = 0.354), and therefore the uncorrected ANOVA results were reported. A significant overall effect of time on SCr was observed (F = 3.963, P = 0.028), with a large effect size (partial η² = 0.189). Polynomial contrast analysis further revealed a significant linear trend across time points (F = 6.112, P = 0.024; partial η² = 0.264), whereas the quadratic component was not significant (F = 1.378, P = 0.257), indicating a progressive increase in SCr over time. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using paired-samples t-tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction (k = 3). SCr immediately after exercise was significantly higher than that at baseline (Holm-adjusted P = 0.039), with a medium effect size, Cohen’s dz = 0.65, 95% CI (0.16, 1.15). Similarly, SCr at 30 min post-exercise was also significantly higher than that at baseline (Holm-adjusted P = 0.048), with a medium effect size, Cohen’s dz = 0.58, 95% CI (0.07, 1.08). However, no significant difference was observed between immediately post-exercise and 30 min post-exercise (Holm-adjusted P = 0.776). The specific changes are shown in Table 4.

Discussion

In the present study, a single bout of moderate-intensity continuous training did not induce a statistically significant acute increase in serum uric acid in young men with underexcretion hyperuricemia. This finding is somewhat unexpected, as both serum lactate and serum creatinine increased significantly after exercise, changes that are generally considered to favor transient SUA elevation during acute exercise. Previous studies have shown that acute high-intensity or exhaustive exercise may transiently elevate SUA through accelerated purine degradation, lactate accumulation, oxidative stress, and reduced renal urate clearance16,18,19,33. In contrast, despite these exercise-related metabolic changes, SUA remained relatively stable in the present study, suggesting that concurrent changes in urate handling may have helped maintain relatively stable SUA levels during the post-exercise period.

Exercise intensity may partially explain the relatively stable SUA response observed in the present study. Controlling exercise intensity to avoid excessive anaerobic glycolysis and oxidative stress may partially mitigate exercise-induced transient uric acid elevation. A meta-analysis on physical activity and hyperuricemia showed that moderate-to-low-intensity physical activity reduces the risk of uric acid elevation by 12%25. Studies in healthy populations have found that aerobic exercise enhances the activity of hypoxanthine guanine phosphoribosyl transferase (HGPRT), allowing the uric acid precursor Hx to replenish the adenylate nucleotides (AdN) pool through the HGPRT salvage pathway, reducing uric acid production by improving purine metabolic economy22,23. Experimental studies have also suggested that aerobic exercise may modulate renal urate transporter expression and antioxidant pathways34. Therefore, although serum lactate increased after exercise in the present study, the overall metabolic stress induced by MICT may not have been sufficient to provoke a substantial acute accumulation of SUA.

A potential explanation for the stable SUA response observed in the present study is the concurrent enhancement of uric acid excretion following exercise. FEUA, the UUA/UCr ratio, and the Simkin Index all demonstrated progressive increases during the post-exercise period, suggesting an acute enhancement of renal urate excretion after MICT. Notably, FEUA increased from 4.95% at baseline to 6.55% at 30 min post-exercise, approaching values commonly reported in healthy populations, whereas gout patients typically exhibit FEUA values of approximately 3–5%19. Previous studies have similarly reported that moderate aerobic exercise may improve urate metabolism and promote uric acid excretion8,24 In addition, Wołyniec et al.35 observed a mild increase in FEUA during prolonged endurance exercise, which is generally consistent with the trend observed in the present study. Because participants in our study had underexcretion hyperuricemia and relatively low baseline urate excretion capacity, the post-exercise increase in urate excretion indices may have been more readily detectable. Collectively, these findings suggest that enhanced urate excretion may have partially counteracted the metabolic tendency toward exercise-induced SUA accumulation.

Interestingly, the increase in urate excretion indices occurred despite a modest elevation in SCr after exercise. Although an increase in SCr is often interpreted as suggesting a reduction in glomerular filtration, this interpretation should be made cautiously in the context of acute exercise. Exercise-related SCr elevations may reflect not only changes in renal filtration, but also transient alterations in renal hemodynamics, plasma volume, and muscle creatinine production36,37. Indeed, previous endurance-exercise studies have shown that post-exercise SCr can increase even when creatinine clearance is unchanged or increased, indicating that SCr alone may not reliably reflect GFR during acute exercise37–39. In the present study, the magnitude of SCr increase was small, and FEUA, UUA/UCr, and the Simkin Index increased rather than decreased. Therefore, the modest post-exercise rise in SCr does not necessarily indicate reduced urate excretory capacity, and the concurrent increase in urate excretion indices suggests that enhanced renal urate handling may have partially offset the metabolic tendency toward SUA elevation.

Hydration status may also have contributed to the relatively stable SUA response observed in the present study. Previous studies have suggested that dehydration and electrolyte imbalance during exercise may impair urate excretion and promote transient SUA elevation19,40–42. Acute exercise-induced fluid loss may increase renal urate reabsorption and reduce uric acid excretion, potentially through alterations in renal hemodynamics and electrolyte balance16,19,41,42. In contrast, participants in the present study maintained relatively stable hydration status throughout the intervention, with mean fluid loss remaining below 1% of body weight. Adequate fluid replacement and limited dehydration may therefore have helped preserve urate excretion during exercise, which may partly explain the observed increase in urate excretion indices and the absence of a significant acute SUA elevation.

Limitations and external validity

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 18), limiting statistical power and generalizability. Sensitivity analysis indicated that substantially larger samples would be required to reliably detect small-to-moderate acute changes in SUA. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant SUA increase should not be interpreted as definitive evidence regarding exercise safety, and subtle acute effects cannot be excluded. Second, the study used a single-arm pre–post design without a non-exercise control group. Consequently, observed post-exercise changes cannot be attributed exclusively to MICT, and potential influences of circadian variation and short-term hydration-related effects cannot be fully excluded. Third, only young male participants without major comorbidities were included, limiting external validity. Future studies should include women, older adults, and individuals with metabolic or cardiovascular comorbidities. Finally, only short-term responses to a single exercise session were evaluated. Larger controlled studies are required to clarify both the acute and long-term effects of exercise in patients with underexcretion hyperuricemia.

Clinical and practical implications

These results provide preliminary guidance for clinicians and exercise professionals in designing safe, moderate-intensity exercise programs for individuals with underexcretion hyperuricemia. Ensuring adequate hydration and controlling exercise intensity may help prevent transient SUA elevations and promote long-term urate regulation.

Conclusion

In this pilot single-arm study, a single bout of moderate-intensity continuous training did not induce a statistically significant acute increase in serum uric acid in young men with underexcretion hyperuricemia. In contrast, FEUA, the UUA/UCr ratio, and the Simkin Index showed time-dependent increases, suggesting a transient post-exercise increase in uric acid excretion indices.

These findings provide only preliminary evidence and should be interpreted with caution. Because no concurrent non-exercise control group was included, the observed post-exercise changes cannot be attributed exclusively to MICT, and potential influences of circadian variation and hydration-related effects cannot be fully excluded. Larger controlled studies in broader populations are required to confirm these findings and to further evaluate the acute safety and clinical relevance of exercise in patients with underexcretion hyperuricemia.
