Genome-wide identification of the MAPK gene family in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) and functional characterization of AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 in plant innate immunity
Authors: Li Yang, Junjie Shi, Yuxin Yang, Sixian Hu, Heyi Lu, Hainan Jiang, Yao Cai, Like Wang, Ailing Ben, Tingli Liu, Yazhou Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade is a fundamental signaling module that translates environmental stimuli into cellular responses in eukaryotes. Despite its importance, a comprehensive analysis of this family in the allotetraploid peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) remains limited.ResultsIn this study, 32 AhMAPK family members (AhMPK1-AhMPK32) were identified in the peanut genome. These genes are non-uniformly distributed across 14 chromosomes and are characterized by a widespread absence of introns. Phylogenetic analysis categorized the AhMPK proteins into four distinct subgroups (Groups I, II, III, and Ⅳ), with high conservation in gene structures and protein motifs within each group. Promoter analysis revealed an abundance of cis-acting elements responsive to light, phytohormones, and abiotic stresses. Transcriptomic profiling across 19 tissues and multiple stress conditions showed that while most AhMPK genes are constitutively expressed, AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 maintain high transcript abundance and are significantly induced by various environmental stimuli. Functional characterization through Agrobacterium-mediated transient and stable expression demonstrated that AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 positively regulate plant basal immunity. Overexpression of these genes significantly enhanced PAMP-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, up-regulated the defense marker gene FRK1, and conferred increased resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pst) DC3000.ConclusionsCollectively, our results provide a systematic genomic framework for the peanut AhMAPK family and identify AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 as critical candidates for the genetic improvement of disease resistance and stress resilience in peanut.Keywords: Peanut, AhMAPK cascade, Genome-wide identification, Expression profiling, Abiotic stress, Plant innate immunity

Introduction

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade represents a highly conserved and essential signal transduction module in eukaryotes. Positioned downstream of various receptors, these cascades regulate both normal growth and development as well as cellular adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions by coordinating specific responsive signals [1]. A canonical MAPK cascade is composed of at least three tiers of protein kinases: a MAPKK kinase (MAPKKK, MKKK, or MEKK), a MAPK kinase (MAPKK, MKK, or MEK), and a MAPK (MPK) [2]. Within each tier, multiple members often exist with redundant or partially overlapping functions, thereby diversifying the signal transduction pathways. Upon stimulation, the MAPKKK at the apex of the cascade is activated, which subsequently phosphorylates and activates the downstream MAPKK. The activated MAPKK then dual-phosphorylates the MAPK at the bottom of the cascade. Once activated, the MAPK can phosphorylate a wide array of downstream substrates, including transcription factors, other protein kinases, enzymes, and structural proteins, ultimately triggering specific cellular responses [2].

Typical MAPK proteins possess a conserved TX[Y/F]VATR activation loop and 11 kinase subdomains. Their kinase activity specifically depends on the phosphorylation of the characteristic motif within the T-loop located between subdomains VII and VIII. To date, only a few MAPKs, such as AtMPK3/4/6 in Arabidopsis thaliana, have been experimentally verified for their canonical biochemical and physiological functions. The AtMPK3/6 module, for instance, is not only involved in stomatal development and immune regulation but also plays pivotal roles in drought and salt stress responses, highlighting the functional diversity of this gene family in plant environmental adaptation [3–5].

Phylogenetically, plant MAPKs are categorized into four main groups: A, B, C, and D. The phosphorylation loops of Groups A through C feature a characteristic Thr-Glu-Tyr (TEY) motif, whereas Group D exhibits a variant Thr-Asp-Tyr (TDY) domain. Because these kinases are integral to plant adaptation under suboptimal environments, comprehensive genomic analyses have been conducted on this gene family [6]. Current genomic studies indicate that the Arabidopsis genome encodes 20 MAPK genes. In other plant species, such as cucumber (Cucumis sativus), Brassica rapa, cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), and rice (Oryza sativa), 14, 34, 43, and 17 MAPK genes have been identified, respectively [7–10]. Comparative genomics analysis suggests that the MAPK family often undergoes significant gene duplication and functional divergence in allotetraploid crops. Notably, specific family members exhibit distinct stress-induced expression profiles; for example, the overexpression of cotton GhMPK2 enhances salt tolerance in transgenic plants [11], and wheat TaMPK4 has been shown to regulate responses to various abiotic stresses [12]. These findings designate the MAPK family as a reservoir of potential genetic resources for stress-tolerance breeding in crops.

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.), a globally important oilseed crop, frequently faces growth and yield limitations due to drought and salinity [13, 14]. Despite its significance, a comprehensive analysis of the phylogenetic relationships, structural characteristics, and functional divergence mechanisms of the AhMAPK family in allotetraploid peanut remains limited. In this study, using the cultivated peanut variety ‘Fuhuasheng’ as the research material, we conducted a genome-wide identification of AhMAPK family members. By integrating phylogenetic analysis, gene structure, conserved motifs, cis-acting elements, and transcriptomic profiling under multiple conditions, we aimed to elucidate their evolutionary traits, expansion mechanisms, and transcriptional regulatory patterns. Furthermore, based on these findings, we performed functional characterization of candidate genes, specifically AhMPK3 and AhMPK18, revealing their positive regulatory roles in plant innate immunity. Our results provide theoretical insights into the molecular mechanisms by which peanut MAPKs respond to environmental stress and offer valuable genetic resources and data for the genetic improvement of stress resilience in peanut.

Materials and methods

Materials

The peanut cultivar ‘Fuhuasheng’, utilized throughout this study, has undergone comprehensive whole-genome assembly and sequencing. Its publicly available reference genome database (Peanut Genome Resource, PGR: https://pgr.itps.ncku.edu.tw) served as the primary resource for analyses. For experimental studies, seeds of ‘Fuhuasheng’ were obtained from the laboratory collection of Nanjing Xiaozhuang University (Nanjing, China). ‘Fuhuasheng’ plants were cultivated in a growth chamber under a 14-h light/10-h dark (25 °C) photoperiod, with a light intensity of 350 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and 60–70% relative humidity. Plants were grown in a standard peat-based potting mix.

Seeds of Nicotiana benthamiana and A. thaliana (ecotype Col-0) were both provided by the laboratory collection of Nanjing Xiaozhuang University (Nanjing, China). N. benthamiana and A. thaliana plants were maintained in a controlled-environment growth room at 22 °C with 70% relative humidity and a 14-h light/10-h dark photoperiod. A light intensity of 120 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ was provided by cool-white fluorescent lamps. N. benthamiana plants were grown in a commercial potting soil mixture and used for transient expression assays at the 4–5 leaf stage. Arabidopsis plants were grown on a 1:1 mixture of peat moss and vermiculite, and were used for Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation after flowering.

Identification of AhMAPK family members

Peanut whole-genome sequences and corresponding annotation files, encompassing basic gene structure information such as introns, exons, and CDS regions, were obtained from the public PGR database. Using the full-length protein sequences of 20 A. thaliana AtMPK genes as queries, a homology search was performed against the peanut genome protein annotation data utilizing the BLASTP algorithm [15]. After the removal of redundant sequences, the SMART database was employed to verify the domains of the candidate sequences, ensuring they possessed characteristic features of the MAPK family prior to subsequent analyses [16].

Sequence analysis and chromosomal localization of AhMAPKs

Chromosomal localization information and protein sequence lengths for each gene were extracted from the PGR database. Physicochemical parameters, including the isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (Mw) of the AhMAPK proteins, were calculated using the Compute pI/Mw tool on the ExPASy online platform (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/). Subcellular localization analysis was conducted using the Plant-mPLoc prediction system (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/). Chromosomal position data was used to map the gene distribution on chromosomes, illustrating their physical distribution characteristics within the peanut genome.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of MAPKs

For the phylogenetic analysis and classification of MAPKs, multiple sequence alignments of the full-length amino acid sequences of 32 AhMAPK and 20 AtMAPK proteins were performed using the ClustalW program (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw) [17]. Based on the alignment results, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA 10.2 software. The reliability of each branch node was evaluated utilizing 1000 bootstrap replicates [18].

Structural characterization of AhMAPKs

Conserved motifs within the AhMAPK protein sequences were systematically identified using the MEME 5.3.3 online program (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) [19], with all parameters set to their default values. Exon-intron structural information for the AhMAPK genes was directly retrieved from the PGR database.

Analysis of cis-acting elements in AhMAPK gene promoters

To analyze the composition of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of AhMAPK genes, the 2 kb promoter sequences upstream of the start codon (ATG) for each gene were extracted using TBtools software [20]. Subsequently, the obtained sequences were submitted to the PlantCARE online analysis platform (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) for the systematic identification and functional annotation of cis-acting elements [21].

Expression pattern analysis of AhMAPK genes

Based on the transcriptome data provided by the PGR database, the expression patterns of AhMAPK genes across various tissues and under different stress conditions were systematically analyzed. Tissue expression profiling encompassed the following 19 sample types: Root, Root tip, Root nodule, Root and stem, Stem, Stem tip, Cotyledon, Leaf, Florescence, Gynophore, Pericarp-I, Pericarp-II, Pericarp-III, Embryo-I, Embryo-II, Embryo-III, Embryo-IV, Testa-I, and Testa-II. Stress expression analysis included the following treatment conditions: (1) Drought stress: Leaf with normal irrigation (control) and leaf with drought treatment; (2) Hormone treatments: Leaf treated with ddH₂O (control), leaf treated with salicylic acid (SA), leaf treated with paclobutrazol (PAC), leaf treated with ethephon (ETH), leaf treated with brassinolide (BL), and leaf treated with abscisic acid (ABA); (3) Temperature stress: Leaf at room temperature (control) and leaf with low-temperature treatment.

Peanut RNA extraction and qRT-PCR validation

Peanut plants were cultivated at 25 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. Upon reaching the three-leaf stage, the plants were subjected to room temperature (control) and low-temperature (10 °C) treatments for 24 h, respectively. Samples were immediately ground in liquid nitrogen and stored. Total RNA was extracted, and cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR kit. qPCR validation of the expression patterns under low-temperature stress was performed using the following primers: AhMPK1-FP: TCGACCGGCAACAGAAGC; AhMPK1-RP: ATCGGGCGCTATCTTGGC; AhMPK2-FP: AATGCAGACCCCCTTGCC; AhMPK2-RP: ACTGGCTGAGCAGAAGGC; AhMPK13-FP: TGCCCAGCCATCATTCCC; AhMPK13-RP: CCCGGTTTTGCTGTTGGC; AhMPK18-FP: TGGCCGGCGTTAATCCAA; AhMPK18-RP: GGCATGATCGGAGGACGG. After the peanut plants had fully flowered, RNA was extracted separately from the roots, stems, leaves, and flowers. Following cDNA synthesis using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR kit, qPCR validation of tissue expression patterns was conducted using the following primers: AhMPK3-FP: GCTGGTTGCTGTTAAGAA; AhMPK3-RP: CCAAACATCAATTGCAGA; AhMPK9-FP: TCACCTGATGACGCCAGC; AhMPK9-RP: CAACAGCACCGGGAGACA; AhMPK18-FP: TGGCCGGCGTTAATCCAA; AhMPK18-RP: GGCATGATCGGAGGACGG; AhMPK27-FP: TGTCTCCCGGTGCTGTTG; AhMPK27-RP: TGAAGCGGTGCCAGGTAC. All qRT-PCR assays utilized ELF1B as the internal reference gene, employing the primers ELF1B-FP: AAGCTTCCCTGGCAAAGCTCAA and ELF1B-RP: TTCCTCAGCTGCCTTCTTATCC.

ROS detection assay

The complete coding regions of specific AhMAPK genes were PCR-amplified and inserted into the pCAMBIA1300-35 S-HA-RBS plasmid. These constructs were then introduced into the leaves of 5-6-week-old N. benthamiana via Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain GV3101) to facilitate transient expression. After 48 h of infiltration, 8-mm leaf discs were collected using a sterile cork borer (Sigma-Aldrich). To minimize wounding responses, the discs were placed in 96-well plates containing 200 µL of sterile ultrapure water and incubated overnight for equilibration. Following this, the water was replaced with 200 µL of an assay solution comprising 1 µM flg22, 20 µM L-012 probe (Wako Chemicals), and 10 µg/mL horseradish peroxidase (HRP; Sigma-Aldrich). The reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst was continuously monitored by measuring the luminescence every 30 s over a 15-minute period utilizing a Tecan F200 microplate reader. Each experiment was conducted with three independent biological replicates. Data were statistically evaluated utilizing a one-way ANOVA coupled with Tukey’s post hoc test, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

In vitro autophosphorylation assays and western blotting

Recombinant HIS-tagged AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 proteins were heterologously expressed in Escherichia coli and subsequently purified via Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Qiagen). The autophosphorylation activity of the purified kinases was then evaluated using the pIMAGO-biotin phosphoprotein detection kit (Tymora) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Western blotting was performed using mouse monoclonal anti-HIS or anti-FLAG-peroxidase conjugates.

Pathogen infection assays

For the pathogen resistance evaluations, soil-grown Arabidopsis plants aged 4 to 5 weeks were inoculated with Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pst) DC3000. A bacterial suspension adjusted to a density of 5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL was uniformly spray-inoculated onto the rosette leaves. In planta bacterial populations were subsequently quantified at 3 days post-inoculation (dpi). To determine bacterial growth, three leaf discs (0.5 cm in diameter) were harvested from the inoculated leaves of each plant using a cork borer, with three independent plants per genotype serving as three biological replicates. The collected discs were homogenized in 500 µL of sterile 10 mM MgCl2 using a motorized tissue grinder. The resulting homogenates were serially diluted, and 10 µL aliquots of each dilution were spotted onto KB agar medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL rifampicin. After incubation at 28 °C for 48 h, the number of colonies was recorded. The bacterial titers were log-transformed and expressed as log10(CFU/leaf disk). Data were presented as the mean ± SD from three biological replicates.

Results and analysis

Identification and physicochemical characterization of AhMAPK family members in the peanut genome

A systematic screening of the peanut whole-genome annotation files was performed using the BLASTP algorithm, leading to the identification of 32 AhMAPK family members. These members were designated as AhMPK1-AhMPK32 based on their respective chromosomal locations (Table 1). Subsequent analysis of their physicochemical properties encompassed amino acid sequence length (aa), molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and predicted subcellular localization. The results revealed a certain degree of variation in sequence length among family members, though the overall range remained relatively narrow. The amino acid lengths ranged from 368 aa (AhMPK25) to 612 aa (AhMPK1), with corresponding molecular weights between 32.86 kDa (AhMPK15) and 68.87 kDa (AhMPK23). The theoretical pI exhibited a broader range, from 4.97 (AhMPK4) to 9.24 (AhMPK12). Subcellular localization predictions indicated that most AhMAPK proteins are localized in the nucleus. Detailed characteristics of the 32 peanut AhMAPK family members are summarized in Table 1.

Characterization of 32 MAPK gene family members in peanut

Chromosomal distribution of peanut AhMAPK family members

Basic information for the 32 AhMAPK genes was retrieved from the PGR database (Table 1). Chromosomal mapping analysis demonstrated that these genes are widely but non-uniformly distributed across 14 specific chromosomes, including Chr01, Chr03, Chr04, Chr05, Chr07, Chr08, Chr10, Chr11, Chr13, Chr14, Chr15, Chr16, Chr17, and Chr18. Significant differences in gene density were observed among chromosomes: Chr13 contained the highest number of AhMAPK genes (8), identifying it as a gene-rich cluster. This was followed by Chr03 with 5 genes. In contrast, Chr08, Chr10, and Chr18 each harbored only a single AhMAPK gene, representing regions of sparse distribution (Fig. 1).

Phylogenetic analysis of the AhMAPK and AtMAPK family

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships between MAPKs in peanut and Arabidopsis, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method based on multiple sequence alignments of full-length amino acid sequences from 32 peanut and 20 Arabidopsis MAPK proteins. Based on sequence similarity and topological features, the 32 peanut MAPK genes were categorized into four major subgroups: Group I, Group II, Group III, and Group Ⅳ. As shown in Fig. 2, Group I consists of 9 peanut AhMAPKs and 5 AtMAPKs; Group II includes 4 peanut and 3 Arabidopsis members; Group III comprises 4 AhMAPKs and 4 AtMAPKs; and Group Ⅳ contains 15 peanut and 8 Arabidopsis members.

Analysis of gene structure and conserved protein motifs

To further understand the evolutionary characteristics of the peanut MAPK family, we analyzed their exon-intron structures and conserved motifs in the context of their phylogenetic relationships (Fig. 3A). Gene structure analysis revealed a widespread absence of introns among peanut MAPK genes (Fig. 3B); for the majority of members, the coding regions and non-coding regions exist as continuous blocks without intervening introns. Only a few Group Ⅳ members contained very short introns, which accounted for a minimal proportion of the total gene length.

Evolutionary relationships A, gene structure B, and conserved motifs (C) of AhMAPK family members in peanut

Conserved protein motifs are critical for the structure and function of AhMAPK family members. Using the MEME online database, 10 conserved motifs were identified (Fig. 3C). Alignment results showed that Motifs 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7 constitute the core conserved motifs of the family, being present in all 32 members. Interestingly, Motif 10 exhibited significant subgroup specificity: it was present in 27 members but absent in AhMPK14, AhMPK15, AhMPK24, AhMPK25, and AhMPK31. Integration with the phylogenetic analysis revealed that these five genes all cluster within Group Ⅲ, suggesting that the presence or absence of Motif 10 is a key structural hallmark distinguishing Group Ⅲ from other subgroups.

These findings indicate that the evolutionary classification of the peanut MAPK family is highly correlated with gene structure and motif distribution, providing a structural basis for future studies on the functional divergence of different AhMAPK subgroups.

Stress-related cis-acting elements in the promoters of peanut AhMAPK genes

To investigate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of peanut AhMAPK genes in response to abiotic stress and phytohormones, 2 kb promoter sequences upstream of the start codon (ATG) were extracted from the PGR database. Cis-acting elements were identified using the PlantCARE database. The analysis identified 13 types of responsive elements, including those related to abscisic acid (ABA), anaerobic induction, defense and stress, zein metabolism, light, low temperature, methyl jasmonate (MeJA), salicylic acid (SA), auxin, gibberellin (GA), elicitors, wound, and anoxic-specific induction.

As shown in Fig. 4, all 32 AhMAPK genes contained at least 11 stress-related cis-elements, suggesting widespread involvement in various stress responses. Light-responsive elements were the most abundant, ranging from 5 (AhMPK11) to 20 (AhMPK1) per gene. Conversely, anoxic-specific induction elements were the least frequent, found only in AhMPK4 and AhMPK20. Elicitor-responsive elements were also rare, appearing only in AhMPK2, AhMPK16, and AhMPK31. Additionally, ABA-responsive elements were identified in 27 genes, while anaerobic induction elements were found in 22 genes, suggesting roles in ABA signaling and hypoxia stress responses. The presence of varied numbers of MeJA (68), low-temperature (21), and SA (16) responsive elements further indicates that peanut AhMAPKs are central to diverse biological processes and environmental adaptation.

Expression patterns of peanut MAPK genes across different tissues

Spatial variation in gene expression is fundamental to plant physiological regulation. We analyzed the transcript abundance of the 32 AhMAPK members across 19 different tissues, including roots, reproductive organs at various developmental stages, and vegetative tissues (Fig. 5).

Heat map showing the expression levels of MAPK genes in various tissues of peanut. A Color intensity reflects log2-transformed FPKM values. B RT-qPCR validation for AhMPK1, 2, 13, and 18. one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test; different letters indicate significant difference at p < 0.05

The expression heatmap revealed differential expression levels for all members across the sampled tissues. Notably, 29 genes showed constitutive expression characteristics, with detectable signals (FPKM > 0) in all tissues, including root nodules, stem tips, and developing pericarps. Only AhMPK5, AhMPK11, and AhMPK21 exhibited tissue-specific low expression patterns. While no single gene was highly expressed (FPKM > 3) across all tissues, ten genes (AhMPK1, 2, 3, 7, 12, 13, 18, 22, 27, and 28) maintained moderate-to-high expression levels in both vegetative and reproductive organs. In particular, AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 exhibited significantly higher expression levels (FPKM ≈ 10) across these vegetative and reproductive samples. RT-qPCR validation for AhMPK1, 2, 13, and 18 showed high consistency with the RNA-seq data (Fig. 5B), confirming the reliability of the transcriptome analysis.

Expression patterns of AhMAPK genes under phytohormone and abiotic stress treatments

To clarify the biological functions of peanut AhMAPKs in stress responses, we analyzed their expression under treatments with SA, ethephon (ETH), brassinolide (BL), ABA, paclobutrazol (PAC), drought, and low-temperature. Transcriptome analysis revealed distinct responsive features (Fig. 6): (1) 13 genes were up-regulated under SA; (2) 15 genes showed altered expression in response to PAC; (3) AhMPK3 and AhMPK16 responded to ETH; (4) AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 were induced by BL; (5) ABA significantly regulated AhMPK2 and AhMPK16, while also inducing AhMPK3 and AhMPK18; (6) Drought stress significantly altered the expression of 8 genes; (7) Low temperature affected 9 genes, including AhMPK3, 9, 18, and 27. Notably, AhMPK3 exhibited significant expression changes across all seven treatments, suggesting it serves as a central hub in hormone signaling and stress crosstalk. RT-qPCR results for AhMPK3, 9, 18, and 27 under cold stress were consistent with the RNA-seq data (Fig. 6B).

AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 positively regulate plant immunity

To date, two major MAPK cascades, MEKK1-MKK1/2-MPK4 and MAPKKK3/4/5-MKK4/5-MPK3/6, have been well characterized for their roles in regulating plant immunity [22–25].To identify peanut MAPK members involved in immune regulation, we performed Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression of AhMPK3, 6, 9, 18, 22, and 27 in N. benthamiana leaves and monitored flg22-induced ROS bursts. Among the tested candidates, only AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 significantly enhanced the flg22-induced ROS burst, suggesting their involvement in peanut immune responses (Fig. 7).

As typical members of the MAPK family, AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 contain highly conserved kinase domains that are predicted to possess catalytic activity. To experimentally verify this, recombinant HIS-tagged AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 proteins were heterologously expressed in E. coli and purified via Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. Results of the in vitro kinase reaction assay, utilizing the pIMAGO-biotin phosphoprotein detection system, demonstrated robust autophosphorylation activity for both purified proteins (Fig. 8A). BIK1, known to possess strong kinase activity, was used as a positive control [26].These biochemical findings confirm that AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 are functional protein kinases, providing a foundational basis for their active roles in peanut immune signaling.

To further evaluate the functional roles of AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 in plant immunity, we generated stable A. transgenic lines overexpressing AhMPK3-FLAG or AhMPK18-FLAG. We subsequently assessed PAMP-triggered ROS production, defense-related gene expression, and resistance to Pst DC3000. Our results demonstrated that both flg22- and chitin-induced ROS bursts were significantly potentiated in the AhMPK3- and AhMPK18-overexpressing plants compared to the wild-type Col-0 (Fig. 8B, C). Consistently, the flg22-elicited expression of FRK1, a canonical defense marker gene, was markedly elevated in these transgenic lines (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, upon inoculation with Pst DC3000, the transgenic lines exhibited significantly enhanced resistance as evidenced by lower bacterial titers compared to Col-0 (Fig. 8E). Western blot analysis confirmed the stable expression of the corresponding proteins in independent T3 lines (Fig. 8F). Collectively, these findings indicate that AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 function as positive regulators of basal immunity in Arabidopsis.

Discussion

The MAPK cascade is a highly conserved signal transduction module in eukaryotes. Through the hierarchical phosphorylation cascade of MAPKKK-MAPKK-MAPK, it coordinates cellular responses to developmental signals and environmental stresses, acting as one of the core regulatory networks for plant adaptation to complex environments [6, 27]. In recent years, the MAPK families in model plants and various crops have been systematically identified, and the functions of their members in processes such as stress resistance and plant growth and development have gradually been elucidated [28]. However, as an important oilseed crop, the phylogenetic characteristics, structural divergence, and stress response mechanisms of the MAPK family in allotetraploid peanut have not yet been comprehensively resolved. In this study, using the cultivated peanut variety ‘Fuhuasheng’ as materials, we revealed the core characteristics of the peanut MAPK family through genome-wide identification, phylogenetic analysis, structural characterization, and multi-condition expression profiling.

The genome-wide identification results revealed the presence of 32 MAPK family members in cultivated peanut. This number is slightly higher than that in the diploid wild species Arachis duranensis (16) and Arachis ipaensis (15). Physicochemical property analysis indicated significant variations in the amino acid lengths, molecular weights, and isoelectric points of AhMAPK proteins (368–612 aa, 32.86–68.87 kDa, and 4.97–9.24, respectively). Furthermore, they are primarily localized in the nucleus, which is consistent with their roles in intracellular signal transduction [29]. It is worth noting that our study identified a total of 32 AhMPK genes in the peanut cultivar ‘Fuhuasheng’, which is slightly more than the 30 members previously reported in the reference genome of A. hypogaea cv. Tifrunner [30]. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to the use of different reference genomes. Different cultivars within the same species frequently exhibit genomic variations, such as gene presence/absence variations (PAVs), as well as differences in genome assembly and annotation resolution. Thus, the identification of two additional AhMAPK members in ‘Fuhuasheng’ likely reflects the inherent genetic diversity and specific genomic architectures between these two distinct peanut cultivars.

Phylogenetic analysis categorized the 32 AhMAPK genes into four subgroups (Groups I–Ⅳ), whereas previous studies divided peanut MAPKs into four subfamilies (A–D) based on the TEY/TDY activation loop motif [6, 27]. By integrating the inter-species synteny analysis between peanut and Arabidopsis, Group I and Ⅱ in this study correspond to Group A of the A–D subfamilies, encompassing members homologous to Arabidopsis AtMAPK3/6(such as AhMPK18). These genes have been proven to participate in biotic and abiotic stress responses in Arabidopsis [31, 32]. Group Ⅲ corresponds to Group B, including AhMPK14, AhMPK31, and others, which are homologous to AtMAPK4/11 and are speculated to be involved in regulating cell division during peanut growth and development [33]. Group Ⅳ covers Groups C and D, among which members homologous to AtMAPK1/2/7 (such as AhMPK2) may be involved in the ABA signaling pathway [34]. This classification correlation corroborates the evolutionary conservation of the peanut MAPK family and provides a reliable basis for functional inference.

Chromosomal localization analysis demonstrated that AhMAPK genes are unevenly distributed across 14 chromosomes, with Chr13 enriched with 8 genes, serving as the core distribution region of the family. This characteristic has also been reported in the MAPK families of crops such as cotton and Brassica [32, 35] and is usually associated with tandem gene duplication events.

The analysis of gene structures and conserved motifs is crucial for elucidating the functional conservation and divergence of the family. This study found a widespread absence of introns within the peanut MAPK family, with only a few Group Ⅳ members containing extremely short introns. This structural feature can abbreviate the time required for gene transcription and mRNA processing, enabling rapid gene expression under stress conditions [28]. Conserved motif analysis identified 10 core motifs, among which Motifs 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7 are stably present in all members. These motifs correspond to the core kinase domains of MAPK proteins (such as the ATP-binding site and the phosphorylation active center) [6, 27], and their high conservation ensures the kinase activity of the family members. Conversely, the subgroup-specific distribution of Motif 10 (absent only in Group Ⅲ) may serve as the structural basis for the functional divergence of this subgroup.

Analysis of cis-acting elements revealed that the promoter regions of AhMAPK genes contain 13 types of stress- and hormone-responsive elements. Among them, light-responsive elements are the most abundant, while abscisic acid (ABA)-responsive, low-temperature-responsive, and methyl jasmonate (MeJA)-responsive elements are widely distributed. This is consistent with previous findings that peanut MAPK promoters contain stress-responsive elements such as MYB, MYC, and ABRE [30], indicating that AhMAPK genes can perceive multiple environmental and hormonal signals through diversified cis-elements to construct complex transcriptional regulatory networks.

Tissue expression profiling demonstrated that 29 AhMAPK genes exhibited constitutive expression across 19 tissues, with only AhMPK5, AhMPK11, and AhMPK21 displaying tissue-specific low expression. This aligns with the general rule that MAPK genes are widely involved in the regulation of plant growth and development [29]. Notably, 9 genes, including AhMPK1, AhMPK2 and AhMPK18, maintained high expression levels in vegetative organs (roots, stems, leaves) as well as reproductive organs (flowers, fruits). Such broadly and highly expressed genes are typically considered core members essential for maintaining basic cellular functions [32].

Expression analysis under different stress and hormone treatments revealed the response specificity and synergistic crosstalk of AhMAPK genes. Under salicylic acid treatment, 13 genes exhibited an up-regulated expression trend, and under paclobutrazol treatment, 15 genes showed altered expression patterns. Ethephon only induced the up-regulation of AhMPK3 and AhMPK16, while brassinolide specifically activated AhMPK3 and AhMPK18. This confirms the general principle that the hormone response of MAPK genes possesses receptor specificity [28].Among them, AhMPK3 displayed significant expression changes across all treatments. Its homologous gene, AtMAPK4, has been previously verified as a core gene in drought response [32], leading to the hypothesis that AhMPK3 may act as a signal crosstalk node, integrating hormone and abiotic stress signals.

Under drought stress, the expression patterns of 8 genes, including AhMPK3, AhMPK14, and AhMPK31, were significantly altered. The up-regulation trend of these genes is consistent with the marked up-regulation of AtMAPK4 in drought-tolerance treatments [35], suggesting that they may enhance drought tolerance in peanut by activating downstream antioxidant enzymes or osmoprotectant synthesis genes. Low-temperature treatment primarily affected the expression of genes such as AhMPK2, AhMPK3, and AhMPK18, further corroborating the regulatory logic that the distribution of cis-elements determines the specificity of gene responses [30].

Phylogenetically, plant MAPKs are primarily organized into two functional modules: the MAPKKK3/4/5-MKK4/5-MPK3/6 pathway, which positively regulates defense, and the MEKK1-MKK1/2-MPK4 pathway, typically associated with negative regulation [22–25]. Our findings demonstrate that AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 cluster tightly within Group II and share high homology with AtMPK1 and AtMPK4. The robust in vitro autophosphorylation activity and the significantly enhanced immune responses observed in Arabidopsis overexpressing lines, including potentiated ROS bursts, elevated FRK1 expression, and increased resistance to Pst DC3000, confirm that AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 function as active positive regulators of basal immunity. This functional conservation underscores the stability of Group II MAPK signaling across the Brassicaceae and Fabaceae families. Interestingly, while Group Ⅲ members in Arabidopsis, such as AtMPK2, often act as negative regulators of systemic acquired resistance [24, 25], our results for AhMPK9 and AhMPK27 (Group I) did not reveal similar negative regulatory patterns in the ROS assays. It implies a higher degree of functional specialization or sub-functionalization within the Group I clade of the allotetraploid peanut genome. It is probable that the negative regulatory roles in peanut are sustained by other untested Group I members, or that this module has evolved distinct regulatory nuances in legumes. Overall, this study establishes a robust biochemical and functional foundation for the peanut MAPK family, identifying AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 as critical components of the peanut immune system and offering promising targets for future molecular breeding aimed at enhancing disease resistance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified 32 MAPK family members in the peanut genome, which are unevenly distributed across 14 chromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis categorized these members into four subgroups. Subcellular localization predictions and physicochemical analysis confirmed that most AhMPKs are nuclear-localized proteins with characteristic kinase domains. Transcriptional analysis revealed that the majority of AhMPK genes are constitutively expressed across diverse tissues, with AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 exhibiting consistently high abundance. The identification of numerous stress-responsive cis-acting elements in their promoters, coupled with expression profiling under various abiotic and phytohormone treatments, suggests that the peanut MAPK family plays a versatile role in environmental adaptation. Specifically, AhMPK3 emerged as a potential central hub in hormone signaling and stress crosstalk. Functional characterization further demonstrated that AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 act as positive regulators of plant basal immunity. Their overexpression in Arabidopsis significantly enhanced PAMP-induced ROS bursts, elevated the expression of the defense marker FRK1, and conferred robust resistance against Pst DC3000. Collectively, these findings provide a comprehensive genomic and functional framework for the peanut MAPK family, highlighting AhMPK3 and AhMPK18 as promising candidates for the molecular breeding of stress-resilient and disease-resistant peanut cultivars.
