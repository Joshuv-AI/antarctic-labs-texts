Transport of Golgi-localized β-catenin p-S47 by KIF11 or KIFC3 induces primary ciliogenesis
Authors: Na Yeong Lee, Mi-Lang Kyun, Ji Eun Yu, Sun-Ok Kim, Key-Hwan Lim, Kyung Ho Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Primary cilium is an important hub for cell signaling and dysregulation of primary cilia assembly and disassembly is associated with the development of cancer and chemotherapeutic drug resistance, as well as the genetic disorders collectively known as ciliopathy. β-catenin plays a major role in canonical Wnt signaling; however, its association with primary cilia has only recently been highlighted in reports of β-catenin-mediated primary ciliogenesis. In this study, we found that β-catenin p-S47 was localized to the Golgi apparatus and the nucleus, and the amount of β-catenin p-S47 at these locations was significantly higher during primary ciliogenesis compared with asynchronous cell growth conditions. In addition, the novel β-catenin-binding motor proteins KIF11 and KIFC3 were shown to have a lower binding affinity in β-catenin S47A than in β-catenin wild-type. Knockdown of KIF11 or KIFC3 resulted in primary cilia deficiency and increased β-catenin p-S47 levels in the Golgi apparatus and were accompanied by a decrease in β-catenin p-S47 at the centrosome. The accumulation of β-catenin p-S47 in the nucleus was increased during primary ciliogenesis along with β-catenin-dependent transcriptional activity. The collective findings indicate the existence of a novel mechanism of primary ciliogenesis involving KIF11-/KIFC3-associated β-catenin p-S47 in the Golgi apparatus and β-catenin p-S47 transcriptional activity in the nucleus. This study revealed a new mechanism for the study of ciliopathies, cancer, and chemotherapeutic drug resistance caused by primary ciliogenesis dysregulation and provides new targets for drug development to treat these diseases.

INTRODUCTION

β-catenin is recognized as an important regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway. Wnt signaling is a major cellular signaling pathway as it regulates cell fate determination and homeostasis in animal cells from embryogenesis to organogenesis (Valenta et al., 2012). Thus, disturbances in Wnt signaling are associated with a range of human degenerative diseases, and aberrant β-catenin regulation, particularly constitutive β-catenin activation, is a major contributor to tumorigenesis (Clevers, 2006, Logan and Nusse, 2004, Moon et al., 2004). Wnt signaling has traditionally been divided into 2 categories: (1) canonical Wnt signaling, which involves β-catenin, and (2) noncanonical Wnt signaling, which does not include β-catenin (alternatively referred to as Wnt/PCP, planar cell polarity) and Wnt-dependent/protein kinase C-dependent pathways (Angers and Moon, 2009, Gao and Chen, 2010, Logan and Nusse, 2004, van Amerongen et al., 2008, Zhang et al., 2007). Recent studies indicate that the phosphorylation of serine 60 residue in β-catenin catalyzed by Plk1 is involved in the regulation of cytokinesis (Yu et al., 2021), whereas the phosphorylation of serine 47 residue of β-catenin by CK1δ is associated with the regulation of primary ciliogenesis (Kyun et al., 2020).

The primary cilium has been proposed to act as an intracellular antenna, functioning as a hub for cell signaling (Goetz and Anderson, 2010, Seeley and Nachury, 2010). Primary cilia represent axonemes, consisting of a 9+0-type microtubule skeleton and a membrane surrounded by a dense array of signaling receptors (Afzelius, 2004, Davenport and Yoder, 2005, Fisch and Dupuis-Williams, 2011, Ke and Yang, 2014, Lee, 2023, Pedersen et al., 2016). In contrast to motile cilia, which are permanent structures, they are produced during the G0/G1 phase of the cell cycle and disassembled upon entry into mitosis (Dawe et al., 2007, Goetz and Anderson, 2010, Lee, 2020, Pearson et al., 2007, Seeley and Nachury, 2010). Although the detailed mechanisms underlying the assembly and disassembly of primary cilia remain largely unknown, studies have shown that defects in the assembly/disassembly cycle of primary cilia cause a wide range of human genetic disorders called ciliopathies (Badano et al., 2006). Therefore, further studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the degradation and assembly of disease-related primary cilia.

A significant number of kinesin superfamily proteins (KIFs) have been identified as having a regulatory role in the formation of primary cilia. In particular, the trafficking of the ciliary membrane by heterotrimeric kinesin-2 motors (KIF3A, KIF3B, and kinesin superfamily-associated protein in vertebrates) represents a central process for the intraflagellar transport system, which is responsible for the extension of the primary cilium axoneme (Cole et al., 1998, Scholey, 2013). Other kinesins, including KIF7 and KIF27, are also involved in primary cilium formation, suggesting a broader role for kinesins in this process (Cheung et al., 2009, Endoh-Yamagami et al., 2009, Liem et al., 2009, Morthorst et al., 2018, Wilson et al., 2009).

Increasing evidence suggests a connection between the regulation of primary ciliogenesis and the development of cancer (Lee, 2020). The development of cancer has been reported to be associated with primary ciliogenesis deficiency, with some exceptions (Lee, 2023). In particular, excessive primary cilia formation and elongated primary cilia may contribute to tumor cell resistance to chemotherapy (Jenks et al., 2018, Kim et al., 2022, Kyun et al., 2020, Lee, 2023). This suggests that primary cilia may represent a novel target for overcoming drug resistance. The study of primary cilia assembly and disassembly may provide valuable insights into the treatment of various human diseases, with the potential for wide-scale applicability in diverse fields.

This study builds upon our previous research on the Wnt3a-CK1δ-β-catenin p-S47-centriolar satellite axis in primary ciliogenesis (Kyun et al., 2020). It reveals a previously unidentified mechanism involving β-catenin p-S47, which may lead to new drug targets and fundamental insights for understanding how to address diseases associated with primary ciliary dysregulation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plasmid Construction and Mutagenesis

The murine wild-type (WT) form of β-catenin and an alanine substitution mutant (designated the “A” mutant) were subcloned into the BamI-NotI site of pcDNA-FLAG (Invitrogen). The full-length and “A” mutant versions were created through polymerase chain reaction, utilizing the pCDNA3-Flag-β-catenin WT template (a gift of Eric R. Fearon, University of Michigan) and the pEGFP-C1-β-catenin WT template (a gift of W. James Nelson, Stanford University).

Cell Culture and Transfection

The HEK293T and HeLa cervical carcinoma cell lines CCL2 were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum in accordance with the recommendations of the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). hTERT-immortalized retinal pigment epithelial (hTERT-RPE) cells were cultured in a medium comprising 10% Tet System Approved fetal bovine serum and DME/F-12 1:1 medium (HyClone). The transfection of plasmids and siRNAs into cells was conducted using the X-tremeGENE HP DNA transfection reagent (Roche), Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen), or Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturers. The siRNA target sequences were siKIF11-#1 5′-CAGAUUGAUGUUUACCGAATT-3′, siKIF11-#2 5′-GAAACUUACUGAUAAUGGUTT-3′, and siKIF11 #3 SASI_Hs01_00161690 [368] (Sigma-Aldrich). The siRNA target sequences were siKIFC3-#1 5′-GGCUGUGCACGAGAAUCUATT-3′, siKIFC3-#2 SASI_Hs01_00046906 [2064] (Sigma-Aldrich), and siKIF11 #3 SASI_Hs01_00046907 [2820] (Sigma-Aldrich). Following transfection, the cells were utilized for a variety of biochemical assays, including immunoprecipitation, western blot analysis, and immunofluorescence analysis.

Immunoprecipitation and Immunoblot Analysis

Immunoprecipitation was conducted in accordance with the methodology previously described by Lee et al. (2018). In brief, hTERT-RPE cells were harvested with 1× ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lyzed in 1× TBSN buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-Cl [pH 8.0], 5 mM EGTA, 0.5% NP-40, 1.5 mM EDTA, and 10 mg/ml pNPP [p-nitrophenyl phosphate] Sigma) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 ×g for 20 minutes at 4°C. The cleared cell lysates were incubated with the indicated antibodies for a period of 6 hours at 4°C. Following this, the antibodies were bound to protein A- or G-sepharose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnologies) for an additional 4 hours at the same temperature. The beads were precipitated by brief centrifugation and subsequently washed on more than 4 occasions with 1× TBSN buffer. The beads were combined with 2× Laemmli sample buffer (comprising 4% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 120 mM Tris-Cl [pH 6.8], 10% 2-mercaptoethanol, 20% glycerol, and 0.02% bromophenol blue) and heated for 15 minutes at 95°C to 100°C. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).

The immunoprecipitates were subjected to separation on 6% to 12% (SDS-PAGE) gels and subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies for 2 to 4 hours at room temperature (or overnight at 4°C). Following this, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (Amersham Biosciences) for 1 hour at room temperature. The membranes were washed with 1× TBST (0.05% Tween 20, 50 mM Tris-Cl [pH 7.5], and 150 mM NaCl) more than 3 times. Immunoreactive signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Pierce). The list of antibodies utilized in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Serum Starvation–Based Ciliogenesis Assay, Immunofluorescence Assay, and Image Analyses

Counting of cells containing cilia was conducted in accordance with the methodology previously outlined by Lee et al. (2017). In brief, hTERT-RPE cells were cultivated on coverslips in 12-well plates for a period of 24 hours. Thereafter, the cells were washed 3 times with 1× PBS and subsequently cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium-only medium for a duration of 24 to 48 hours. To observe primary cilia, the resulting cells were coimmunostained with antiacetylated α-tubulin antibody (Sigma, 1:200) and anti-γ-tubulin antibody (Sigma, 1:200), as previously described (Lee et al., 2017).

The primary cilia-containing cells were enumerated manually using a Nikon Eclipse ti-u inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon) or a Zeiss AxioObserver Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss). To detect the centrosomal localization of β-catenin p-S47, the hTERT-RPE cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes at room temperature and subsequently permeabilized with methanol cooled to −20°C for 2 minutes. Subsequently, the resulting samples were washed 4 times with 1× PBS. An immunofluorescence assay was conducted in accordance with the previously described methodology (Lee et al., 2017). The primary antibodies were incubated for a period of 2 to 4 hours at room temperature (or for a period of 16-20 hours at 4°C for phospho-antibody), after which they were washed 3 times with 1× PBS. Fluorescent dye–conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. DNA was stained with 1 μg/ml of 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma) solution for 10 minutes at room temperature. The resulting coverslips were mounted with Fluoro-Gel mounting medium (EMS), observed, and imaged with either a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope, Zeiss AxioObserver, or a Nikon Eclipse ti-u inverted fluorescence microscope system. The antibodies utilized for immunofluorescence analysis are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

To quantify the signal intensity of β-catenin p-S47, digital images were captured using a Zeiss AxioObserver or a Nikon Eclipse ti-u inverted fluorescence microscope system. The microscope systems were operated at 1,388 × 1,040 pixels and a 12-bit resolution, or 1,280 × 1,024 pixels and a 16-bit resolution, respectively. The digital images were subsequently analyzed using either ZEN v2.1 (Carl Zeiss) or ImageJ software.

Luciferase Reporter Assay

To monitor the β-catenin-dependent transcriptional activity in serum-starved HeLa CCL2 cells, a TopFlash/FopFlash luciferase reporter system was employed. For the luciferase assays, HeLa cells were transfected with pSuperTopFlash (TCF reporter plasmid) and the respective negative control pFopFlash (TCF-binding site mutant) reporter plasmids, which were kindly provided by Randall T. Moon of University of Washington. To assess the transcription efficiency, Renilla luciferase (pRL-TK) (Promega) was cotransfected. A dual luciferase assay kit (Promega) was utilized to quantify the activity, which was then analyzed using a Veritas microplate luminometer (Turner Biosystems).

Subcellular Protein Fractionation

The cytoplasmic, membrane-bound (Golgi, ER), nuclear-soluble, chromatin-bound, and cytoskeletal protein extracts from hTERT-RPE cells were separated and prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#. 78840). Briefly, after the addition of CEB to the cell pellet, the samples were incubated at 4°C for 10 minutes with gentle mixing, and then centrifuged at 500 ×g for 5 minutes. The supernatant was promptly transferred (cytoplasmic extract) to a clean, prechilled tube on ice. An ice-cold solution of MEB-containing protease inhibitors was then added to the pellet. The tube was vortexed for a period of seconds at the highest setting, incubated at 4°C for a period of 10 minutes with gentle mixing, and then centrifuged at 3,000 ×g for a period of 5 minutes. Subsequently, the supernatant (membrane extract) was transferred to a clean, prechilled tube on ice. Ice-cold NEB-containing protease inhibitors were then added to the pellet. Following vortexing, the tube was incubated at 4°C for 30 minutes with gentle mixing and then centrifuged at 5,000 ×g for 5 minutes. The supernatant (soluble nuclear extract) was then transferred to a clean prechilled tube on ice. The chromatin-bound extraction buffer was prepared by adding 5 μl of 100 mM CaCl2 and 3 μl of micrococcal nuclease (300 units) per 100 μl of room temperature NEB. The pellet was then treated with NEB-containing protease inhibitors, CaCl2, and micrococcal nuclease. Subsequently, the tube was vortexed and incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes or in a 37°C water bath for 5 minutes. Subsequently, the tube was vortexed for 15 seconds at the highest setting and then centrifuged at 16,000 ×g (highest setting of microcentrifuge) for 5 minutes. The supernatant was then transferred to a clean, prechilled tube on ice, which was designated as the chromatin-bound nuclear extract. The prepared samples were subjected to immunoblotting analysis.

In-Gel Digestion With Trypsin, Peptide Extraction, and Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry

The protein bands from the SDS-PAGE gels were excised and subjected to in-gel digestion with trypsin, as previously described (Bahk et al., 2004, Kim et al., 2021) (ProteomeTech, Inc). The extracted solution was concentrated using a vacuum concentrator, resulting in a concentrated solution. Before mass spectrometry was conducted, the peptide solution was subjected to desalting via a reversed-phase column (Bahk et al., 2004, Kim et al., 2021).

An liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was conducted using a nano ACQUITY UPLC and LTQ-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Electron and ProteomeTech, Inc). In the case of tandem mass spectrometry, the mass spectra were obtained through the use of data-dependent acquisition with a full mass scan (300-2,000 m/z), which was subsequently followed by MS/MS scans. Each scan was acquired with an average of 1 microscan on the LTQ. The temperature of the ion transfer tube was maintained at 275°C, and the spray voltage was set at 2.0 kV. The normalization of collision energy was set at 35% for MS/MS. The individual spectra were subjected to processing using SEQUEST software (Thermo Quest) and the generated peak lists were utilized to query an in-house database via the MASCOT program (Matrix Science Ltd) (Kim et al., 2021).

Real-Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from hTERT-RPE cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). Quantitative Real-Time PCR assays were carried out in MicroAmp Optical 96-Well Reaction Plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on the Quantstudio 3 Real-Time PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific) utilizing PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The expression levels of target genes were normalized against GAPDH RNA levels in each sample. Relative expression was determined using the 2(−△△Ct) method. The primers used in these experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using Excel (Microsoft) for the unpaired 2-tailed t-test of Figures 2B and C, 4C, Supplementary Figure S2, and SigmaPlot 13.0 (Systat Software) for the 1-way ANOVA of Figure 5B and D.

RESULTS

β-Catenin p-S47 Is Increased in the Golgi Apparatus and Nucleus Through the Induction of Primary Ciliogenesis

Previously, we reported a mechanism for the induction of primary ciliogenesis by CK1δ-induced β-catenin Ser 47 phosphorylation (Kyun et al., 2020). In this study, the subcellular localization of β-catenin p-S47 was determined by various immunostaining methods to identify the mechanism of primary ciliogenesis by β-catenin p-S47. Of the immunostaining methods, the 0.2% Triton X-100 extraction method revealed the localization of β-catenin p-S47 in the Golgi apparatus (Fig. 1). Soluble proteins were extracted using a 0.2% Triton X-100 extraction method before fixation of the asynchronously growing hTERT-RPE cells. β-catenin p-S47 was identified in centrosome/basal bodies as well as Golgi-like structures by coimmunostaining with acetylated α-tubulin, which represents the axoneme of primary cilia. To confirm that the Golgi-like structures where β-catenin p-S47 is localized are indeed the Golgi apparatus, we performed coimmunostaining with β-catenin p-S47 using an antibody against GM130, which is a cis–Golgi marker protein (Fig. 1). The results indicated that the Golgi-like structure where β-catenin p-S47 was localized almost perfectly matched the Golgi apparatus immunostained with GM130. Next, to determine whether β-catenin p-S47 localized in the Golgi apparatus is involved in primary ciliogenesis, we examined Golgi apparatus localization of β-catenin p-S47 under serum-deficient conditions, which is a condition required for primary ciliogenesis.

The β-catenin p-S47 signal in the Golgi apparatus was identified in asynchronous growing cells and was significantly increased under serum-starved conditions for 24 hours (Fig. 2A). The β-catenin p-S47 signal was quantified and found to be approximately 2-fold higher under serum starvation conditions compared with asynchronous growing conditions (Fig. 2B). In addition to the increase in β-catenin p-S47 signals in the Golgi apparatus that occur as a result of the induction of primary ciliogenesis, changes in β-catenin p-S47 levels were also observed in the nucleus (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, under serum starvation conditions, there was an increase in the β-catenin p-S47 signal in the nucleus, which was approximately 3-fold higher compared with that under normal cell growth conditions (Fig. 2C). This suggests that a considerable quantity of β-catenin p-S47 accumulates in the nucleus under primary ciliogenesis conditions. To further validate the findings from our previous report (Kyun et al., 2020), we conducted an experiment involving Wnt3a treatment. Treatment with Wnt3a-conditioned medium (CM) led to an increase in β-catenin p-S47 levels in both the Golgi and nucleus, compared with cells treated with control CM (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To further confirm the increase in β-catenin p-S47 levels in response to primary ciliogenesis conditions, the distribution of the protein within the Golgi apparatus and nucleus was quantified in various subcellular fractions. The results indicated that the quantity of β-catenin p-S47 was considerably augmented in the Golgi apparatus fraction under conditions of primary ciliogenesis compared with cells undergoing normal cell growth (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. S2). The quantity of β-catenin p-S47 in the nuclear fraction was also significantly increased under conditions of primary ciliogenesis. The total amount of β-catenin also increased by the conditions of primary ciliogenesis in the Golgi and nuclear cell fractions. Nevertheless, this increase was more pronounced for the phosphorylated form of β-catenin, p-S47, compared with total β-catenin. No increase in β-catenin p-S47 was observed in the cytosolic, chromatin-bound, or intermediate filament fractions following primary ciliogenesis induction, except in the Golgi apparatus and nucleus cell fractions. Based on these findings and our previous research (Kyun et al., 2020), we propose the following scenario: upon Wnt3a stimulation, CK1δ phosphorylates β-catenin at serine 47, resulting in the accumulation of phosphorylated β-catenin (p-S47) in both the nucleus and Golgi apparatus. In the nucleus, β-catenin p-S47 may promote the transcription of genes involved in primary ciliogenesis. Simultaneously, the accumulation of β-catenin p-S47 in the Golgi apparatus may contribute to ciliogenesis by translocating to the centrosome or basal body, likely mediated by the motor proteins, along with other ciliogenesis-related factors and vesicles.

As discussed above, the function of β-catenin p-S47, which accumulates in the nucleus in response to primary ciliogenesis conditions, was predicted through changes in β-catenin-dependent transcriptional activity. Alterations in β-catenin-dependent gene expression resulting from conditions of primary ciliogenesis were validated using a TopFlash luciferase assay (Supplementary Fig. S3). HeLa CCL2 cells were transfected with the pTopFlash plasmid, which is a normal TCF reporter plasmid, and the pFopFlash plasmid, which has a TCF-binding site mutant. Following serum starvation for 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours, the expression of the TopFlash and FopFlash reporter genes was measured using a luciferase assay (Supplementary Fig. S3A). In FopFlash, the respective negative control, luciferase activity was unchanged over time during serum deprivation. In contrast, TopFlash activity was approximately 10-fold higher after 48 hours of serum starvation conditions compared with the 0-hour control (Supplementary Fig. S3B). The results indicate that the transcriptional activity of β-catenin is more pronounced under conditions of serum starvation, which are primary ciliogenesis conditions. Next, to determine whether the expression of ciliogenesis-related genes is upregulated under primary ciliogenesis conditions, such as serum starvation, we monitored the mRNA levels of Hedgehog signaling components, including GLI, Patched, and Smoothened, using real-time PCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4). As a result of our experiment, we found that the mRNA expression levels of all tested genes increased under the 48-hour serum starvation condition compared with the asynchronously growing condition. However, the level of expression did not reach that of the β-catenin target gene, Axin2. A more than 4-fold increase in Axin2 mRNA expression was observed under serum starvation compared with the asynchronously growing condition. In contrast, other genes such as GLI, Patched, and Smoothened showed less than a 2-fold increase in mRNA expression between the 2 conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4).

The Identification of a Novel KIF11-/KIFC3-Associated β-Catenin Transport Mechanism

To elucidate the mechanism of primary ciliogenesis involving β-catenin p-S47 accumulation in the Golgi, we immunoprecipitated exogenous β-catenin from cell lysates overexpressing Flag-tagged β-catenin and identified the novel factors that bind to β-catenin by LC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. 3A). A total of 125 candidate β-catenin-binding proteins were identified (Supplementary Table S1, Excel file). Among these, we focused on motor proteins that may be involved in the movement of β-catenin p-S47 from the Golgi to the centrosome and selected KIF11 (Zalenski et al., 2020), which is involved in primary cilia formation, and KIFC3 (Xu et al., 2002), which is involved in Golgi positioning and integration, and examined their functional relevance to β-catenin p-S47. Furthermore, we analyzed the putative β-catenin-binding proteins using the STRING database network and found that no direct interaction between β-catenin (CTNNB1) and KIF11/KIFC3 has been reported (Fig. 3A, bottom left). Subsequently, we determined whether a physical interaction occurs between β-catenin and KIF11 or KIFC3. Endogenous protein immunoprecipitation resulted in a β-catenin-KIF11 complex and a β-catenin-KIFC3 complex (Fig. 3B). Immunoprecipitation of endogenous KIF11 or KIFC3 using anti-KIF11 or anti-KIFC3 antibodies under growth conditions revealed the binding of endogenous β-catenin to endogenous KIF11/KIFC3. To determine whether binding was associated with the serine 47 phosphorylation of β-catenin, we overexpressed WT (Flag-β-catenin WT) or a phosphorylation-deficient mutant (Flag-β-catenin S47A) and immunoprecipitated exogenous β-catenin with anti-Flag antibodies. More KIF11 and KIFC3 were bound to WT β-catenin compared with the phosphorylation-deficient mutant (S47A) (Fig. 3C). To further validate the findings from our previous report (Kyun et al., 2020), we conducted an experiment involving Wnt3a treatment. Treatment with Wnt3a CM resulted in an increase in β-catenin p-S47 levels in both the Golgi and centrosome, compared with cells treated with control CM (Fig. 5A-D right). DISCUSSION

In the present study, we found that the subcellular localization of β-catenin p-S47 is associated with primary ciliogenesis. In a previous study, we identified that Wnt3a-induced CK1δ activity phosphorylates serine 47 of β-catenin. This leads to basal body recruitment of centriolar satellites, which drives primary ciliogenesis (Kyun et al., 2020). We identified a novel β-catenin p-S47-related mechanism that further expands our understanding of the primary ciliogenesis pathway. Following the phosphorylation of β-catenin by CK1δ, we investigated the potential induction of primary ciliogenesis by the transport of β-catenin p-S47 from the Golgi apparatus to the centrosome, which is regulated by KIF11 or KIFC3 motor proteins. Additionally, we examined the induction of primary ciliogenesis by the transcriptional activity of β-catenin p-S47 in the nucleus (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Proposed mechanism of β-catenin p-S47 transportation by KIF11/KIFC3 to generate primary cilia. Centrosome/basal body–localized β-catenin p-S47 induces primary ciliogenesis. In addition, the generated β-catenin p-S47 is translocated to the nucleus. We found that the amount of β-catenin p-S47 was increased in the Golgi apparatus and nucleus during serum starvation, a condition required for primary ciliogenesis. These findings further support the link between primary ciliogenesis and β-catenin serine 47 phosphorylation.

We identified KIF11 and KIFC3 as novel binding partners of β-catenin. This study proposes an association between β-catenin p-S47 and KIF11 at the Golgi apparatus during primary ciliogenesis. The previously known basal body localization of KIF11 and the regulatory role of KIF11 in primary ciliogenesis (Zalenski et al., 2020) appear to be related to the function of the β-catenin p-S47-KIF11 complex identified in this study. Previous studies have indicated that KIFC3 plays a role in Golgi positioning and integration (Xu et al., 2002). Our study is the first to suggest that KIFC3 may interact with β-catenin p-S47 to regulate primary ciliogenesis. Moreover, because KIFC1, a member of a similar kinesin family, induces primary cilia formation by transporting membrane proteins from the Golgi apparatus to the primary cilia (Lee et al., 2018), the β-catenin p-S47-KIFC3 complex may also act on primary cilia formation by delivering membrane proteins to the primary cilia. In addition, β-catenin may function as an adaptor protein between kinesin motor proteins (KIF11/KIFC3) and membrane-forming cargo proteins during this transport process. Serine 47 phosphorylation of β-catenin may regulate its adaptor function; however, further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis by measuring the binding of the motor proteins KIF11 and KIFC3 to their cargo membrane proteins in the presence of β-catenin p-S47. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine which cargoes bind to the β-catenin p-S47-KIF11/KIFC3 complex and are transported from the Golgi apparatus to the centrosome, thereby inducing primary ciliogenesis.

Several previous studies have reported on the transcriptional activity of β-catenin (Valenta et al., 2012). In the present study, we observed an increase in β-catenin p-S47 in the nucleus under primary ciliogenesis conditions as well as an increase in β-catenin transcriptional activity; however, despite a significant increase in β-catenin transcriptional activity induced by primary ciliogenesis condition (Supplementary Fig. S3), as determined by the TopFlash/FopFlash luciferase assay, primary ciliogenesis-related Hedgehog signaling components such as GLI, Patched, and Smoothened did not exhibit a substantial increase in expression following serum starvation. In contrast, the β-catenin target gene Axin2 showed a robust increase in expression (Supplementary Fig. S4). This suggests the possibility that other ciliogenesis-related genes, beyond those we selected, may be regulated by nucleus-localized β-catenin p-S47. Further studies are required to elucidate the expression of primary cilia-related genes and their association with β-catenin p-S47.

In the present study, we identified the localization of β-catenin p-S47 in the Golgi apparatus and nucleus, its association with KIF11/KIFC3 in the Golgi apparatus, and its transcriptional activity in the nucleus. These findings represent a follow-up to our previous results showing Wnt3a-CK1δ-β-catenin p-S47-centriolar satellite axis–mediated primary ciliogenesis (Kyun et al., 2020) and provide further evidence for the function of β-catenin in primary ciliogenesis. In particular, we confirmed the importance of β-catenin serine 47 phosphorylation in this process. Furthermore, we identified the kinesin motor proteins, KIF11 and KIFC3, as novel binding partners of β-catenin. This provides a basis for future studies to clarify the mechanism of primary cilia formation by identifying the cargoes transported by the β-catenin-KIF11/KIFC3 complex during primary ciliogenesis. Of the 125 candidate β-catenin-binding factors identified in this study, factors other than KIF11 and KIFC3 are also worthy of further study for their potential association with β-catenin and regulation of primary ciliogenesis.

The dysregulation of primary ciliogenesis has been implicated in numerous diseases, including ciliopathies and cancer (Lee, 2023). Furthermore, recent studies indicate that the regulation of primary cilia represents an important area for the development of novel strategies to overcome drug resistance in cancer (Jenks et al., 2018, Kyun et al., 2020, Lee, 2023). Consequently, the identification of primary ciliogenesis regulatory mechanisms will provide valuable insights for the study of a range of diseases, including ciliopathies and cancer.
