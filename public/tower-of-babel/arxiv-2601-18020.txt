A generalization of the notion of helix
Pascual LUCAS 1 , José-Antonio ORTEGA-YAGÜES 2 1 Departamento de Matemáticas, Universidad de Murcia, Campus de Espinardo, 30100 Murcia, Spain, ORCID iD: https://orcid.org/0000-0002-4354-9736 2 Departamento de Matemáticas, Universidad de Murcia, Campus de Espinardo, 30100 Murcia, Spain, ORCID iD: https://orcid.org/0000-0001-9521-1051 \rec .201 \acc .201 \finv ..201

Abstract
In this paper we generalize the notion of helix in the three-dimensional Euclidean space, which we define as that curve α \alpha for which there is an F F -constant vector field W W along α \alpha that forms a constant angle with a fixed direction V V (called an axis of the helix). We find the natural equation and the geometric integration of helices α \alpha where the F F -constant vector field W W is orthogonal to its axis.

1
Introduction

Let
\alpha:I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a differentiable unit speed curve and let
\mathfrak{X}(\alpha)
denote the set of differentiable vector fields along the curve. Let
F(s)=\{F_{1}(s),F_{2}(s),F_{3}(s)\}
be a moving orthonormal frame along
\alpha
, so we can see
F
as a differentiable map
F:I\to SO(3)
. It is easy to show (see e.g.
[
3
, pp. 43–45]
) that there exists a unique vector field
D_{F}(s)
along
\alpha
satisfying the equations

F_{1}^{\prime}=D_{F}\times F_{1},\quad F_{2}^{\prime}=D_{F}\times F_{2},\quad F_{3}^{\prime}=D_{F}\times F_{3},

(1.1)

where
()^{\prime}
is the usual derivative in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\times
stands for the cross product. The vector field
D_{F}
is called the
Darboux vector associated to the frame
F
.

From a physical point of view, along the curve
\alpha
we have two coordinate systems associated to two references: (a) One associated to the frame
F
, that may be imagined as being fixed on the curve. This system rotates and is thus accelerating, it is a non-inertial frame. (b) The other is the canonical rectangular coordinates
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, associated to the usual canonical basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
, that is fixed to the space and is an inertial frame. Obviously, given a vector field
W
along
\alpha
, the variation in
s
of its coordinate functions in the two coordinate systems is not the same. In fact, they are related by the following equation:

W^{\prime}=\frac{d_{r}}{ds}(W)+D_{F}\times W,

where
\frac{d_{r}}{ds}(W)
denotes the rate of change of
W
as observed in the rotating coordinate system (i.e., in the frame
F
). The above equation is usually called the
Transport Theorem
in analytical dynamics (see
[
6
, p. 11]
). If we think of
W
as a curve in the 3-space, then the above formula tells us that its (absolute) velocity in the inertial frame
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
is equal to the velocity relative to the moving (rotating) frame
\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\}
plus the velocity of the rotating coordinate system itself.

Motivated by the equations (
1.1
) and the Transport Theorem, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.1

A vector field
W
along
\alpha
is said to be constant with respect to the frame
F
(or
F
-constant vector field
) if
\frac{d_{r}}{ds}(W)=0
(or, equivalently,
W^{\prime}=D_{F}\times W
).

The set of
F
-constant vector fields along
\alpha
will be denoted by
\mathfrak{X}_{F}(\alpha)
. In
[
5
]
, this kind of vector fields are said to be invariably attached to every point of the curve. If we think of the curve
\alpha
as the trajectory of a rigid body, then a
F
-constant vector can be imagined as a body-fixed vector,
[
6
]
. The following result is a straightforward computation.

Proposition 1.2

The following properties of
F
-constant vector fields hold:

1)

If
W
is a
F
-constant vector field, then
W
has constant length.

2)

If
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are
F
-constant vector fields, then
W_{1}+W_{2}
is a
F
-constant vector field.

3)

Let
W
be a nowhere zero
F
-constant vector field and
h
a differentiable function, then
hW
is a
F
-constant vector field if and only if
h
is a constant function.

4)

W
is a
F
-constant vector field if and only if
W=a_{1}F_{1}+a_{2}F_{2}+a_{3}F_{3}
, for certain constants
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence,
\mathfrak{X}_{F}(\alpha)
is a 3-dimensional real vector space.

A special moving frame is the Frenet frame
F=\{T_{\alpha},N_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}\}
, satisfying the well-known Frenet-Serret equations

T_{\alpha}^{\prime}(s)=\kappa_{\alpha}(s)\,N_{\alpha}(s),\quad N_{\alpha}^{\prime}(s)=-\kappa_{\alpha}(s)\,T_{\alpha}(s)+\tau_{\alpha}(s)\,B_{\alpha}(s),\quad B_{\alpha}^{\prime}(s)=-\tau_{\alpha}(s)\,N_{\alpha}(s),

(1.2)

where
\kappa_{\alpha}
and
\tau_{\alpha}
stand for the curvature and torsion functions, respectively. The vector fields
T_{\alpha},N_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}
are trivial examples of
F
-constant vector fields. Throughout this paper we will assume that our curves are non-planar, i.e., with non-zero torsion. For this frame, the Darboux vector is simply denoted by
D_{\alpha}
and is given by
D_{\alpha}=\tau_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}+\kappa_{\alpha}B_{\alpha}
. This vector can be interpreted as the angular velocity of the Frenet frame as a whole. In fact, the rate of change of the frame
\{T_{\alpha},N_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}\}
with
s
can be characterized as an instantaneous rotation about the vector
D_{\alpha}
, with angular velocity equal to the
total curvature
specified by

\omega=|D_{\alpha}|=\sqrt{\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\tau_{\alpha}^{2}}.

Let
W
be a nonzero differentiable vector field along the curve
\alpha
.
W
is said to be a normal vector field if
W(s)
belongs to the normal plane for every
s
; similarly, we have rectifying or osculating vector fields, depending on whether
W(s)
belongs to the rectifying or osculating plane, respectively.

The term helix (or general helix, or cylindrical helix, or curve of constant slope) has traditionally been used to define curves whose tangent vector field
T_{\alpha}
forms a constant angle with a fixed direction in the 3-space,
[
7
, p. 33]
. The concept of helix has been extended by considering vector fields other than the tangent vector field
T_{\alpha}
, such as the principal normal vector field
N_{\alpha}
(thus giving rise to slant helices,
[
2
,
4
]
). In this paper, we propose a new extension of the concept by considering
F
-constant vector fields.

Definition 1.3

A curve
\alpha
is said to be a
helix
if there exists a
F
-constant vector field
W
along
\alpha
that forms a constant angle with a fixed direction
V
, called an
axis
of the helix.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
W
is a unit vector field. In the particular case that
W
is a normal (osculating or rectifying, resp.) vector field then
\alpha
is called a normal (osculating or rectifying, resp.) helix. Note that we recover the notion of cylindrical helix or slant helix when the
F
-constant vector field
W
is given by
T_{\alpha}
or
N_{\alpha}
, respectively. Note also that the term osculating helix has been previously used to refer to the circular helix passing through a point of a curve, having the same tangent, curvature vector and torsion,
[
7
, p. 42]
.

In this paper, we solve the following problem:

How are characterized the helices
\alpha
when the
F
-constant vector field
W
is orthogonal to its axis
V
?

Note that when the vector field
W
is
T_{\alpha}
,
N_{\alpha}
or
B_{\alpha}
, then the curve
\alpha
is nothing but a plane curve or a cylindrical helix. Therefore, in the following sections we will address the question when the vector field
W
is expressed as a linear combination of at least two vector fields of the Frenet frame.

2
Normal helices

2.1
Natural equation of normal helices

Let
\alpha
be a nonplanar curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Frenet apparatus
\{\kappa_{\alpha},\,\tau_{\alpha};T_{\alpha},N_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}\}
, and assume that
\alpha
is a normal helix with axis
V
,
V
being a constant vector. Suppose that there is a nonzero constant angle
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
such that
W=\cos\theta\,N_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,B_{\alpha}
is orthogonal to
V
. Hence we can write

V=\lambda\,T_{\alpha}+\mu(\sin\theta\,N_{\alpha}-\cos\theta\,B_{\alpha}),

(2.1)

for certain differentiable functions
\lambda
and
\mu
. By taking derivative in (
2.1
) we get

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}-\mu\sin\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\,\mu^{\prime}+\lambda\,\kappa_{\alpha}+\mu\cos\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle-\cos\theta\,\mu^{\prime}+\mu\sin\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0.

(2.4)

From equation (
2.4
) we have

\mu=e^{\tan\theta\,\int\tau_{\alpha}},

(2.5)

that jointly with (
2.3
) leads to

\lambda=-\sec\theta\,\rho\,e^{\tan\theta\,\int\tau_{\alpha}},

(2.6)

where
\rho=\tau_{\alpha}/\kappa_{\alpha}
is called the Lancret curvature. Finally, putting equations (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) in (
2.2
) yields

-\sec\theta(\rho^{\prime}+\tan\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}\,\rho)=\sin\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha},

(2.7)

and then

-\rho^{\prime}=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha}+\tan\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}\,\rho.

(2.8)

Therefore

\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{\cos^{2}\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\tau^{2}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\right)^{\prime}=-\tan\theta.

(2.9)

Conversely, let
\alpha
be a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying equation (
2.9
), for a nonzero constant
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. Let
V
be the vector field given in (
2.1
), where
\mu
and
\lambda
are defined by equations (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), respectively. Then equations (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) are satisfied. On the other hand, from equation (
2.9
) we easily get (
2.7
), and then we deduce (
2.2
) since

\mu\sin\theta\;\kappa_{\alpha}=-\sec\theta\big(\rho^{\prime}\mu+\tan\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}\,\rho\,\mu\big)=\lambda^{\prime}.

Hence, there is a constant
\theta
such that the vector field
\cos\theta\,N_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,B_{\alpha}
is orthogonal to a constant direction
V
, so
\alpha
is a normal helix.

Summing up, we have shown the following result (we also include the case
\theta=0
).

Theorem 2.1

Let
\alpha
be a nonplanar arclength parametrized curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with curvature
\kappa_{\alpha}>0
and torsion
\tau_{\alpha}
. Then
\alpha
is a normal helix (with
W
orthogonal to
V
) if and only if the following equation holds

\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{\cos^{2}\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\tau^{2}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\right)^{\prime}=-\tan\theta,

for a certain constant
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
.

2.2
Geometric interpretation of normal helices

Let
\alpha
be a normal helix with axis
V
, and assume that
V
is orthogonal to the
F
-constant vector field
W=\cos\theta\,N_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,B_{\alpha}
,
\theta
being a constant. Let us consider
C_{\alpha,V}
the cylinder parametrized by
X(t,z)=\alpha(t)+z\,V
, then we have

N=X_{t}\times X_{z}=T_{\alpha}\times V=\cos\theta\,N_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,B_{\alpha}
, up to a sign.
This shows that the principal normal vector field
N_{\alpha}
of the curve
\alpha
makes a constant angle
\theta
with the unit vector field
N
normal to the cylinder
C_{\alpha,V}
. It is not difficult to see that this condition characterizes the normal helices.

Let
M=C_{\beta,V}
be a general cylinder parametrized by
X(t,z)=\beta(t)+zV
, where
\beta
is a unit planar curve and
V
is a unit vector orthogonal to that plane. If
\{T_{\beta},N_{\beta}\}
is the Frenet frame of
\beta
, assume that the unit normal vector to the cylinder is given by
N(t,z)=T_{\beta}(t)\times V=N_{\beta}(t)
. Let us assume that
\alpha(s)=X(t(s),z(s))
,
s\in I
, is an arclength parametrized curve in
M
such that
N_{\alpha}
makes a constant angle
\theta
with
N
. A straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\cos\varphi(s)\,T_{\beta}(t(s))+\sin\varphi(s)\,V,

(a)

\displaystyle N_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\sin\theta\,\big(-\sin\varphi(s)\,T_{\beta}(t(s))+\cos\varphi(s)\,V\big)+\cos\theta\,N,

(b)

(2.10)

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=-\cos\theta\,\big(-\sin\varphi(s)\,T_{\beta}(t(s))+\cos\varphi(s)\,V\big)+\sin\theta\,N,

(c)

where
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I)
is a differentiable function with
t^{\prime}(s)=\cos\varphi(s)
and
z^{\prime}(s)=\sin\varphi(s)
. It is easy to see that

V=\sin\varphi(s)\,T_{\alpha}(s)+\cos\varphi(s)\big(\sin\theta\,N_{\alpha}(s)-\cos\theta\,B_{\alpha}(s)\big),

(2.11)

and then we can define the
F
-constant vector field
W=\cos\theta\,N_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,B_{\alpha}
satisfying
\left<W,V\right>=0
, showing that
\alpha
is a normal helix. Therefore, we have proved the following result.

Theorem 2.2

A curve
\alpha
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a normal helix with axis
V
if and only if
\alpha
lies on a cylinder
C
and its principal normal vector field makes a constant angle with the normal vector field to the cylinder.

Note that when
\theta=0
then
\alpha
is a geodesic of the cylinder, and so it is a cylindrical helix. Hence, Theorem
2.2
is an extension of the well known theorem of Lancret that characterizes the cylindrical helices as the geodesics of the cylinders.

We finish this section with the following result about curves
\alpha(s)
in a cylinder
C_{\beta,V}
. By taking derivative in equations (a) and (c) of (
2.2
) we get

\displaystyle\kappa_{\alpha}(s)N_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}(s)(-\sin\varphi(s)\,T_{\beta}(t(s))+\cos\varphi(s)\,V)+\cos^{2}\varphi(s)\kappa_{\beta}(t(s))\,N_{\beta}(t(s)),

\displaystyle-\tau_{\alpha}(s)N_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}(s)\cos\theta(\cos\varphi(s)\,T_{\beta}(t(s))+\sin\varphi(s)\,V)+

\displaystyle\kern 10.00002pt+\cos\varphi\kappa_{\beta}(-\sin\theta\,T_{\beta}(t(s))+\cos\theta\sin\varphi\,N_{\beta}(t(s))).

These two equations lead to the following result.

Proposition 2.3

Let
\alpha(s)=X(t(s),z(s))
be an arclength parametrized curve in a cylinder
C_{\beta,V}
. The principal normal vector field
N_{\alpha}
makes a constant angle
\theta
with the normal to the cylinder if and only if there is a differentiable function
\varphi
such that the following equations hold

\displaystyle t^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\cos\varphi(s),

(2.12)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\sin\varphi(s),

(2.13)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\tan\theta\,\cos^{2}(\varphi(s))\,\kappa_{\beta}(t(s)).

(2.14)

Moreover, the curvature and torsion of
\alpha
are given by

\kappa_{\alpha}(s)=\frac{\cos^{2}\varphi(s)}{\cos\theta}\kappa_{\beta}(t(s)),\quad\tau_{\alpha}(s)=-\sin\varphi(s)\cos\varphi(s)\,\kappa_{\beta}(t(s)).

(2.15)

On the other hand, from (
2.15
) we get

\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}(s)=-\cos\theta\tan\varphi(s),

and since
(\tan\varphi)^{\prime}(s)=\tan\theta\,\kappa_{\beta}(t(s))
, we have that only in circular cylinders there exist normal helices that are also rectifying curves, see
[
1
]
.

2.3
An example: normal helices in circular cylinders

Let
C_{\beta,V}
be a cylinder over a circle
\beta
of radius one. Then from Proposition
2.3
we get

\displaystyle\varphi(s)

\displaystyle=\arctan(\tan(\theta)s),

\displaystyle t(s)

\displaystyle=\cot(\theta)\sinh^{-1}(\tan(\theta)s)+t_{0},

\displaystyle z(s)

\displaystyle=\cot(\theta)\sqrt{1+\tan^{2}(\theta)s^{2}}+z_{0},

where
\theta,t_{0},z_{0}
are constants. Hence, a family of normal helices in the cylinder is given by

\displaystyle\alpha(s)

\displaystyle=\Big(\cos(\cot(\theta)\sinh^{-1}(\tan(\theta)s)+t_{0}),

\displaystyle\kern 20.00003pt\sin(\cot(\theta)\sinh^{-1}(\tan(\theta)s)+t_{0}),

\displaystyle\kern 20.00003pt\cot(\theta)\sqrt{1+\tan^{2}(\theta)s^{2}}+z_{0}\Big).

Moreover, from (
2.15
) we obtain that the curvature and torsion of
\alpha
are given by

\kappa_{\alpha}(s)=\frac{\cos\theta}{\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}(\theta)\,s^{2}},\quad\tau_{\alpha}(s)=\frac{-\sin\theta\cos(\theta)\,s}{\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}(\theta)\,s^{2}}.

(2.16)

Note that these curves verify that
\tau_{\alpha}/\kappa_{\alpha}(s)=-\sin(\theta)s
, so they are rectifying curves.

We can reparametrize the curves
\alpha
to obtain a simpler expression. Indeed, let us consider the change of parameter
\tan(\theta)s=\sinh(\tan(\theta)t)
, then

\alpha(t)=\big(\cos(t+t_{0}),\sin(t+t_{0}),\cot(\theta)\cosh(\tan(\theta)t)+z_{0}\big),

and the curvature and torsion can be computed as follows

\kappa_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{\sec\theta}{\cosh^{2}(\tan(\theta)t)},\quad\tau_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{-\sinh(\tan(\theta)t)}{\cosh^{2}(\tan(\theta)t)}.

(2.17)

1
.

3
Osculating helices

3.1
Natural equation of osculating helices

Let
\alpha
be a nonplanar osculating helix with axis
V
. Then there is an osculating vector field
W=\cos\theta\,T_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,N_{\alpha}
, for a nonzero constant angle
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, which is orthogonal to
V
. Hence we can write

V=\mu(-\sin\theta\;T_{\alpha}+\cos\theta\;N_{\alpha})+\lambda\,B_{\alpha},

(3.1)

for certain differentiable functions
\lambda
and
\mu
. By derivating here we obtain the following equations

\displaystyle-\sin\theta\;\mu^{\prime}-\mu\cos\theta\;\kappa_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\cos\theta\;\mu^{\prime}-\lambda\,\tau_{\alpha}-\mu\sin\theta\;\kappa_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}+\mu\cos\theta\;\tau_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0.

(3.4)

From equation (
3.2
) we get

\mu=e^{-\cot\theta\,\int\kappa_{\alpha}},

(3.5)

that jointly with (
3.3
) yields

\lambda=\frac{-1}{\sin\theta\rho}\,e^{-\cot\theta\,\int\kappa_{\alpha}},

(3.6)

where
\rho
is the Lancret curvature. On the other hand, by putting equations (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) in (
3.4
), we get

\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\left(\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho^{2}}+\cot\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha}\,\frac{\rho}{\rho^{2}}\right)=-\cos\theta\;\tau_{\alpha},

and then

-\rho^{\prime}=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}\rho^{2}+\cot\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha}\,\rho,

which can be rewritten as

\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}}{\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\tau^{2}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\kappa_{\alpha}}\right)^{\prime}=-\cot\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}.

(3.7)

Note that this equation is equivalent to

\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\tau^{2}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{\tau_{\alpha}}\right)^{\prime}=\cot\theta.

(3.8)

Now, we will see that this equation characterizes the osculating helices. Indeed,
let
\alpha
be a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying (
3.8
) for a nonzero constant
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. Let us define a vector field
V
as in (
3.1
), where
\lambda
and
\mu
are given by (
3.6
) and (
3.5
), respectively. Then equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are satisfied. Finally, it is straightforward to see that equation (
3.8
) leads to (
3.4
). Then there exists a constant
\theta
such that
\cos\theta\,T_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,N_{\alpha}
is orthogonal to the constant direction
V
, that is,
\alpha
is an osculating helix.

We have proved the following characterization of the osculating helices (we also include the cases
\theta=\pm\pi/2
).

Theorem 3.1

Let
\alpha
be a nonplanar arclength parametrized curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with curvature
\kappa_{\alpha}>0
and torsion
\tau_{\alpha}
. Then
\alpha
is an osculating helix (with
W
orthogonal to
V
) if and only if the following equation holds

\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\tau^{2}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{\tau_{\alpha}}\right)^{\prime}=\cot\theta,

for a nonzero constant angle
\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
.

3.2
Normal helices and osculating helices

Let
\alpha(s)
be a normal helix with Frenet apparatous
\{\kappa_{\alpha},\tau_{\alpha};T_{\alpha},N_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}\}
. Then the curve

{\overline{\alpha}}(s)=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}B_{\alpha}(t)\,dt

(3.9)

is an arclength parametrized curve, and without loss of generality its Frenet frame is given by

T_{\overline{\alpha}}=B_{\alpha},\quad N_{\overline{\alpha}}=-N_{\alpha},\quad B_{\overline{\alpha}}=T_{\alpha}.

Hence their curvature and torsion are given by
\kappa_{\overline{\alpha}}=\tau_{\alpha}
and
\tau_{\overline{\alpha}}=\kappa_{\alpha}
. It is straightforward to see that
{\overline{\alpha}}
satisfies (
3.8
), and so it is an osculating helix.

On the other hand, and following a similar reasoning, it can be proved that if
{\overline{\alpha}}
is an osculating helix then the curve

\alpha(s)=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}B_{\overline{\alpha}}(t)\,dt

(3.10)

is a normal helix. Therefore, and in a certain sense, normal helices and osculating helices can be considered as duals of each other.

4
Rectifying helices

Let
\alpha
be a nonplanar curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Frenet apparatous
\{\kappa_{\alpha},\,\tau_{\alpha};T_{\alpha},N_{\alpha},B_{\alpha}\}
, and assume that
\alpha
is a rectifying helix with axis
V
,
V
being a constant vector. Let us suppose there is a constant angle
\theta
such that the rectifying vector field
W=\cos\theta\,B_{\alpha}+\sin\theta\,T_{\alpha}
is orthogonal to
V
. Hence we can write

V=\lambda\,N_{\alpha}+\mu(\sin\theta\,B_{\alpha}-\cos\theta\,T_{\alpha}),

for certain differentiable functions
\lambda
and
\mu
. By taking derivative there we get

\displaystyle-\lambda\,\kappa_{\alpha}-\mu^{\prime}\,\cos\theta

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}-\mu\,\cos\theta\,\kappa_{\alpha}-\mu\sin\theta\,\tau_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lambda\,\tau_{\alpha}+\mu^{\prime}\,\sin\theta

\displaystyle=0.

From these equations we easily deduce that
\tau_{\alpha}/\kappa_{\alpha}
is constant, and so
\alpha
is a cylindrical helix. Since every cylindrical helix is also a rectifying helix, then we have proved the following result.

Theorem 4.1

Let
\alpha
be an arclength parametrized curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with curvature
\kappa_{\alpha}>0
. Then
\alpha
is a rectifying helix (with
W
orthogonal to
V
) if and only if it is a cylindrical helix.

5
The general case

Let us assume, in this section, that the unit
F
-constant vector field
W
along
\alpha
is given by
W=aT_{\alpha}+bN_{\alpha}+cB_{\alpha}
, where
a,b,c
are nonzero constants with
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}=1
(the other cases have already been analyzed in the preceding sections). Since we are assuming that
\left<W,V\right>=0
, then there exists two differentiable functions
\lambda,\mu
such that

V=\lambda(-cT_{\alpha}+aB_{\alpha})+\mu(-cN_{\alpha}+bB_{\alpha}),

(5.1)

and by derivating here we obtain the following equations:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}-\mu\kappa_{\alpha},

(5.2)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c\mu^{\prime}+\lambda(c\kappa_{\alpha}+a\tau_{\alpha})+b\mu\tau_{\alpha},
