Feasibility of Minimally Invasive Scoliosis Surgery in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis Exceeding 80° of Curve Magnitude
Authors: Hong Jin Kim, Jungwook Lim, Dong Yun Kim, Joung Woo Shin, Jae Hyuk Yang, Seung Woo Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate feasibility and effectiveness of minimally invasive scoliosis surgery (MISS) via posterior approach in patients with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) presenting with very severe curve exceeding 80°. MethodsA total of 257 flexible AIS patients who underwent deformity correction using posterior MISS were retrospectively categorized into 3 groups based on the curve magnitude: moderate (M group, main Cobb angle <60°, n=142), severe (S group, 60°–80°, n=91), and very severe (VS group, ≥80°, n=24). Radiological parameters, perioperative outcomes, and complication rates were compared among the 3 groups. ResultsThe VS group required significantly longer operative times (p<0.001), more fused segments (p<0.001), and a greater number of rib resections during thoracoplasty (p=0.032) compared to the other groups. Despite these surgical demands, there were no statistical differences in the correction rate (p=0.954) and last follow-up thoracic kyphosis (p=0.830) among the 3 groups. Regarding complications, while the overall complication rate showed no statistical difference, the VS group required significantly more invasive management for pleural effusions (p=0.012). ConclusionPosterior MISS demonstrated acceptable radiological and perioperative outcomes even in flexible AIS with Cobb angles ≥80°. These findings suggest the posterior MISS can be a feasible surgical option for selected very severe AIS patients with sufficient flexibility.Keywords: Minimally invasive scoliosis surgery, Posterior spinal fusion, Severe curve magnitude, Feasibility, Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis

INTRODUCTION

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a complex 3-dimensional (3D) spinal deformity characterized by lateral deviation and axial rotation of the vertebrae [1]. In severe cases, surgical correction is indicated to restore trunk balance by correcting the 3D curve and prevent long-term sequelae caused by the deformed spinal curve and thorax [1,2]. Posterior spinal fusion (PSF) remains the mainstay of surgical treatment for curves exceeding 45° in the thoracolumbar region or 50° in the thoracic region [1]. However, conventional open scoliosis surgery (COSS) necessitates one long skin incision with extensive soft tissue dissection, which leads to substantial morbidity, cosmetic dissatisfaction, and psychological distress [1-5].

Minimally invasive scoliosis surgery (MISS) has been introduced to address these limitations of COSS in AIS. This encompasses vertebral body tethering via an anterior approach for motion preservation, and posterior MISS, which performs PSF through 2 or 3 small (3–4 cm) skin incisions [2,6]. Posterior MISS, in particular, offers advantages over COSS by minimizing soft tissue dissection and skin incisions while allowing for the application of established perioperative planning principles, such as the determination of the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) [2]. Previous studies have demonstrated that in flexible AIS with moderate-to-severe curves, posterior MISS achieves radiological outcomes comparable to COSS, along with benefits in perioperative management and cosmetic satisfaction [7]. However, limitations associated with the restricted surgical vision and space of mini-incisions—such as wound disruption, skipped pedicle screws even on the concave side, and technical difficulties in very severe curves—have been raised as concerns with posterior MISS [8].

The coin-hole technique of posterior MISS was devised to mitigate these risks [5]. By utilizing a tubular retractor at the miniincision site to protect soft tissues and expose targeted facet joints, this technique allows for pedicle screw insertion at all functional segments, thereby reducing the risk of complications such as wound disruption, surgical site infection, and rod dislodgement [9]. While this coin-hole technique has yielded complication rates comparable to COSS, its current indication is generally limited to moderate-to-severe curves (<80°) [7]. Nevertheless, in very severe cases of AIS where the curve remains flexible, posterior MISS may still be a feasible option as additional osteotomies are not required. However, it remains unclear whether this approach yields outcomes equivalent to those observed in flexible AIS with moderate-to-severe curves. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of posterior MISS in patients with flexible AIS presenting with very severe curves exceeding 80°.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study Design, Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria, and Group Allocation

After approval of the protocol from institutional review board (IRB) of Korea University Guro Hospital (IRB number: 2026GR0010), this single-center retrospective study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the current guidelines of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology [10].

We retrospectively reviewed hospital data from 469 patients who underwent posterior MISS in our center. All surgical procedures were performed by a single senior surgeon with vast experience in deformity correction, utilizing both COSS and posterior MISS techniques. The inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of AIS treated with posterior MISS between March 2015 and August 2023; and (2) curve flexibility of >30% as confirmed by preoperative standing lateral bending or supine radiographs, which was calculated as {[(preoperative main Cobb angle)–(minimum main Cobb angle on bending or supine radiographs)]/preoperative main Cobb angle}×100. The exclusion criteria were: (1) age <10 years or >18 years (n=168), (2) history of previous spinal surgery or infection (n=4), (3) incomplete medical records due to irregular follow-up (n=6), and (4) a follow-up period less than 2 years (n=34). A total of 257 patients with flexible AIS who underwent deformity correction using posterior MISS were finally included and categorized into 3 groups based on the magnitude of the main Cobb angle: moderate (M group, <60°, n=142), severe (S group, 60°–80°, n=91), and very severe (VS group, ≥80°, n=24) groups (Fig. 1).

2. Surgical Technique of Posterior MISS

All posterior MISS was conducted in AIS patients with curve flexibility >30% using preoperative lateral bending or supine radiographs (Fig. 2A and B) [11,12]. Under general anesthesia, patients were placed in a prone position on a hyperextended radiolucent table to increase skin redundancy and facilitate instrument handling. After checking the uppermost instrumented vertebra (UIV) and LIV levels using a fluoroscope, 2 or 3 midline skin incisions, approximately 3–4 cm in length, were made to instrument the 4 to 7 thoracic vertebrae and/or 3 to 4 lumbar vertebrae (Fig. 2C and D). After splitting the thoracolumbar fascia, sequential dilatation was performed, and a 20- to 24-mm diameter tubular retractor system (or right-angled retractors) was placed to expose the lateral side of the facets to approach the pedicle screw entry points. The entry point of the pedicle screw was marked at the lateral one-third of the superior articular process base in the thoracic spine and just lateral to the base of the facet joint in the lumbar spine. Facet joint capsules were removed, and a hole was created at the pedicle screw entry point using a drill. After inserting a guidewire through the trajectory of the pedicle screw, a specially designed reamer with a cutting head was inserted over the guidewire to prepare the fusion bed. Bone graft (chipped cancellous bone allografts or graft substitute) was applied over the facet using a cut-open 5-mL syringe and impactor. Cannulated polyaxial long-head pedicle screws were inserted over the guidewire in all functional segments using the freehand technique. After precontoured rods were inserted from the cranial to caudal direction, a rod derotation maneuver was conducted to achieve curve correction (Fig. 2E). Residual rib hump was resolved by thoracoplasty, which was conducted using 3 to 5 rib resections with a 5-mm diamond burr at the most prominent region [5]. We adopted the short apical rib resection thoracoplasty technique through the preexisting mini-open corridors [13]. Postoperatively, walker ambulation was encouraged 1–2 days after surgery, depending on the patient’s condition. Patients were discharged after confirming their ability to ambulate independently, typically 7–8 days postoperatively. All patients were required to wear a brace for 3 months.

3. Data Collection

From our hospital medical records, demographic variables comprised sex, age, body mass index (BMI), follow-up period, Lenke classification, and Risser stage. Perioperative variables included fused segments, number of skin incisions, scar length, thoracoplasty, number of resected ribs, hospital days, operative time, estimated blood loss (EBL), postoperative blood transfusion, and transfused volume. The EBL was calculated using weighting calculation method (total weight of gauze after surgery– total weight of gauze before surgery+amount fluid in suction bucket–weight of flushing fluid) [14]. Radiological parameters were measured preoperatively, at 6 weeks postoperatively, and at the last follow-up using whole-spine anteroposterior and lateral plain radiographs. Coronal parameters included main Cobb angle, coronal balance (CB, the distance between the C7 plumb line and the central sacral vertical line), clavicle angle (the angle between the intersection of a horizontal line and a line connecting the highest 2 points of each clavicle), and T1 tilt (the angle between a horizontal line and a line drawn along the parallel to the superior end plate of the T1 vertebrae). Sagittal parameters included sagittal vertical axis (SVA), thoracic kyphosis (TK, sagittal Cobb angle between T5 and T12), and lumbar lordosis (LL, sagittal Cobb angle between L1 and S1). Complications were assessed based on pleural effusion, pneumothorax, pneumonia, venous thromboembolism, wound destruction, deep wound infection, abnormal intraoperative motor evoked potential signal, neurological deficit, and metal failure (screw/rod breakage and/or locking cap dislodgement).

4. Statistical Analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed using Python (ver. 3.11.5; Python Software Foundation, USA) with matplotlib library (ver. 3.7.2). Continuous variables are presented as mean± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Continuous variables among the 3 groups were compared using the 1-way analysis of variance test with post hoc analysis using Bonferroni correction, and categorical variables were compared using chi-square test as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

2. Perioperative Outcome

The mean number of fused segments was 11.3 in the M group, 11.9 in the S group, and 12.5 in the VS group, showing a statistically significant difference (p<0.001). Most MISS procedures were performed through 2 skin incisions in all groups, with no significant difference observed (p=0.074). The rate of thoracoplasty (p=0.015) and the mean number of resected ribs (p=0.032) were significantly higher in the VS group compared to the other 2 groups. Operative time was significantly shorter in the M group (289.5 minutes) than in the S group (321.4 minutes) and the VS group (365.6 minutes) (p<0.001). However, there were no statistical differences in EBL (p=0.175), postoperative blood transfusion rates (p=0.154), and transfused volume (p=0.065) among the 3 groups (Table 2).

4. Complications

The occurrence rate of pleural effusion as the most common complication following thoracoplasty was 71.8% in the M group, 76.9% in the S group, and 70.8% in the VS group, with no statistical difference (p=0.657). However, invasive management for pleural effusion showed the greatest rate in the VS group, followed by the S and M groups (p=0.012). Other complications, including wound destruction (p=0.958), deep wound infection (p=0.533), and metal failure (p=0.200), showed no statistical differences among the 3 groups (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study focused on whether posterior MISS could be extended to flexible, very severe AIS curves exceeding 80°, which have traditionally been considered challenging for limited-exposure techniques. We demonstrated that posterior MISS achieved sufficient correction rates comparable to those observed in moderate- to-severe curves, without increasing the overall complication rate. However, very severe curves were associated with a greater need for thoracoplasty-related invasive management, likely due to the larger number of ribs resected. Therefore, our findings suggest that, in flexible curves, posterior MISS may not be limited by curve magnitude alone, supporting the potential expansion of its indications beyond moderate-to-severe AIS (Fig. 3).

In the past, posterior MISS has been indicated primarily for moderate curves, typically less than 70° or 80°, due to technical challenges raised by limited visualization through small incisions [2]. Severe curve AIS often requires extensive osteotomy and releases to resolve rigidity, which are generally considered contraindications for MISS [15]. However, from the perspective of curve magnitude, an increase in the Cobb angle leads to greater apical translation, which conversely shortens the distance between the end vertebrae along the longitudinal axis. Considering this geometric characteristic for posterior MISS, which utilizes longitudinal mini-open incisions, we hypothesized that the curve magnitude itself could not be a significant constraint in the application of posterior MISS. Although fusion levels tended to increase with curve severity, this was partially offset by these geometric advantages. Accordingly, posterior MISS in our study did not necessitate an increase in either the number or size of incisions as curve magnitude increased. Therefore, our results suggest that curve flexibility, rather than absolute magnitude, is the critical determinant for the feasibility of MISS.

In the very severe curve exceeding 80°, anchoring strategies via high-density instrumentation are essential to minimize mechanical failures such as screw pull-out or breakage [15]. Notably, this directly conforms to our surgical strategy for AIS, which emphasizes pedicle screw instrumentation across all functional segments. However, the severe axial rotation with distorted trans-verse process inherent to these deformities obscure conventional landmarks, compounding the technical difficulty of screw insertion through limited mini-open corridors. To address these visual and anatomical constraints safely, we adopted a facet jointbased pedicle screw entry point [16]. Because the facet joint itself undergoes relatively less anatomical distortion even in highly rotated vertebrae, this landmark can be consistently applied across most vertebrae and enables a less-extensive procedure without requiring exposure of the transverse process. Furthermore, the surgical procedure is initiated on a hyperextended operating table to maximize skin redundancy and mobility during pedicle screw insertion. Crucially, after all screws are securely placed, the table is reverted to a neutral position to safely execute the subsequent rod derotation maneuvers in this posterior MISS technique. Notably, our limited-exposure technique continues to evolve, with the potential to improve procedural safety and expand its applicability to very severe AIS curves.

Although supine side-bending radiographs are most commonly used for the measurement of flexibility, the reliability of flexibility measurement methods may vary depending on the curve magnitude and location [17,18]. A literature review of 11 methods of spine flexibility assessment in AIS suggested that the traction method in severe curves and the lateral bending method in moderate curve should be considered to estimate curve flexibility according to the curve magnitude [18]. Furthermore, the appropriate method could be chosen based on curve location: the fulcrum bending in thoracic curves and lateral bending in thoracolumbar/lumbar curves [18]. The established cutoff for defining a rigid curve remains unclear, with reported flexibility thresholds ranging from 20% to 50% [11,12,19]. Severe and rigid scoliosis has commonly been defined as a large curve, typically ≥80° or ≥90°, with flexibility <25% or ≤30% on preoperative flexibility radiographs, often requiring more extensive procedures such as Ponte osteotomy [15]. Therefore, in the present study, we applied a criterion of >30% flexibility on side-bending radiographs using the lateral bending method or supine radiographs as a minimum threshold for selecting patients in whom adequate correction could reasonably be attempted using posterior-based MISS. Given that curve flexibility is a key factor for the determination of indication for posterior MISS, future research is warranted to establish standardized guidelines for measuring flexibility in severe AIS.

The operative time for posterior MISS was substantially longer in AIS patients with very severe curves. This finding may be attributable to several factors, primarily the need for instrumentation over a longer fusion length and the additional time required for extensive thoracoplasty, rather than technical difficulties arising from increased curve magnitude, especially when considering the previously mentioned geometric characteristics for longitudinal axis. Notably, EBL and transfusion rates did not differ from those in the less severe groups despite the prolonged operative time. Although the mean EBL in the present study was approximately 1,000 mL, which may appear relatively high, this value was consistent with preliminary findings and remained substantially lower than that reported for COSS [7]. This should be interpreted in the context of the weight-based calculation method used in this study, which may overestimate blood loss [14]. Hospital stay ranged from 9.9 to 11.0 days across the 3 groups, consistent with a previous study on posterior-based MISS and shorter than that typically reported for COSS [7]. In our country, hospital stay is influenced by the low-cost national health insurance system, as patients often remain hospitalized until ambulation, early rehabilitation, and wound healing are achieved [20]. Therefore, our finding underscores the advantages of MISS regarding perioperative outcomes, likely stemming from its soft tissue-preserving nature [3].

Sarwahi et al. [21-23] reported a high rate of wound destruction and rod dislodgement following posterior MISS. These complications are often associated with the forceful traction required, particularly in the distal portion of incised skin. The initial studies of posterior MISS introduced skipped levels of pedicle screw instrumentation and did not utilize soft tissue preservation systems such as tubular retractors [23]. To address these issues, the coin-hole technique was developed, which includes pedicle screw insertion in all functional segments and the use of tubular retractors in coin-sized mini-open lesions [5,9]. These technical refinements were effective even in very severe curves, considering no substantial differences in complication rates observed in our study. In our cohort of posterior MISS, all cases of metal failure consisted of screw breakage or locking cap dislodgement at the LIV. Although allograft bone can provide comparable fusion rates, its fusion potential may be limited compared with that of autograft. In addition, pedicle screw insertion at the most distal one or 2 lumbar levels can occasionally be technically challenging in MISS [24]. In such cases, pinpoint incisions are created at the planned drain sites, allowing freehand pedicle screw insertion using an assembly-type screw system. Accordingly, future studies are needed to compare these complication rates between MISS and COSS in AIS patients with curves exceeding 80° [25].

In our cohort, thoracoplasty was performed in most patients to correct the rib hump deformity. The relatively high incidence of pleural effusion and pneumothorax was mainly attributable to concurrent thoracoplasty, rather than being an inherent complication of the posterior MISS procedure itself [26,27]. Although pleural effusion and pneumothorax were observed, most cases were mild and resolved with observation during hospitalization, without requiring additional invasive treatment procedures. Meanwhile, it was performed frequently and extensively in very severe curves of AIS to address rib hump deformity [28]. Consequently, the management of postoperative pleural effusion was more invasive in this group, often necessitating chest tube or pigtail catheter insertion, in contrast to the closed observation typically sufficient for the other groups. Although the frequent use of thoracoplasty and the extent of rib resection may contribute to increased procedural invasiveness and greater postoperative pleural effusion volume, these effusions were effectively manageable within the standard hospitalization period [26,27]. Nevertheless, our study emphasizes the need for meticulous postoperative surveillance and management of pleural effusion in patients with very severe AIS undergoing posterior MISS.

The mean Risser stage was significantly lower in patients with curves >80°. In these patients, remaining growth potential may further increase the risk of rapid curve progression rather than justify delaying surgery. Therefore, surgical decision-making in skeletally immature patients, including the choice between growth-friendly surgery and definitive fusion, was comprehensively determined based on curve magnitude, expected curve progression, remaining growth potential, global imbalance, and documented complications [1,29]. In this context, posterior-based MISS may offer an important cosmetic advantage by achieving deformity correction through limited skin incisions, which may positively influence the long-term psychosocial impact of scoliosis in patients with very severe curve magnitude.

This study has several limitations. First, selection bias may have been introduced due to its retrospective nature. Second, considering the relatively small sample size of the VS group and that all posterior MISS procedures were performed by a single surgeon, the generalizability of these results cannot be guaranteed. Third, our study did not standardize the flexibility measurement method to define the indications for posterior MISS. However, preoperative flexibility assessment methods vary widely, including side bending, supine, and traction radiographs, which highlights the need for further evaluation regarding their utility in posterior MISS [15]. Fourth, pedicle screw malposition was not evaluated in this study and should be addressed in future studies using computed tomography scans. Last, this study did not include the patients who underwent COSS, which makes it difficult to evaluate comparability of MISS in curves exceeding 80°. However, the principal objective of this study is not to definitively establish the superiority of MISS, but rather to demonstrate its surgical feasibility in the flexible AIS curves exceeding 80° as a concept of indication expansion. Furthermore, we did not consider patient-reported outcome measures in this study, which should be focused on in a comparative analysis between posterior MISS and COSS with respect to self-image and mental health. Therefore, future prospective studies considering these limitations are warranted to validate our findings.

CONCLUSION

Posterior MISS demonstrated acceptable radiological and perioperative outcomes even in flexible AIS with Cobb angles ≥80°. These findings suggest the posterior MISS can be a feasible surgical option for selected very severe AIS patients with sufficient flexibility.
