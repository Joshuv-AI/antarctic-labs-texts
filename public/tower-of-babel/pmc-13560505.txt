Evaluation of the Effect of Cendakimab on the Pharmacokinetics of CYP Probe Drugs in Patients With Eosinophilic Esophagitis
Authors: Peijin Zhang, Christina M. Charriez, Bindu Murthy, Shenita Basdeo, Yanying Wang, Leonel Villa‐Caballero, Hamza Kandoussi, Claudia H. M. C. De Oliveira, David A. Leiman, Jie Li, Urvi Aras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCendakimab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that inhibits interleukin‐13 binding to its receptors. Although not expected to share clearance pathways with small molecules, its use in eosinophilic esophagitis, a type 2 inflammatory disease, may indirectly affect cytochrome P450 activity by modulating cytokine signaling affecting certain cytochrome P450 enzymes. This open‐label, single‐sequence study evaluated the pharmacokinetics of cytochrome P450 substrates in adults with active eosinophilic esophagitis, evidenced by a peak eosinophil count of ≥ 15 per high‐power field in esophageal biopsies and clinical symptoms of esophageal dysfunction, before and after 16 weeks of cendakimab treatment. Patients received a cocktail of probe substrates (caffeine 200 mg, warfarin 10 mg + vitamin K 10 mg, omeprazole 40 mg, dextromethorphan 30 mg, midazolam 5 mg) for cytochrome P450 1A2, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, and 3A in two sequential periods, separated by 16 weeks of cendakimab treatment. Blood samples were analyzed to assess pharmacokinetics. Sixteen patients were treated; 15 completed the study. Exposures (AUC and C max) of caffeine, S‐warfarin, midazolam, and omeprazole were comparable before and after treatment. Dextromethorphan exposure showed no clinically meaningful change, although interpretation is limited by small sample size. Cendakimab was well tolerated. All adverse events were mild to moderate, with no serious adverse events, deaths or discontinuations from adverse events. No clinically relevant changes in laboratory tests, vital signs, or electrocardiograms were observed. Cendakimab had no clinically meaningful effects on cytochrome P450 enzyme activities and was generally safe and well tolerated with or without probe substrates in adults with active eosinophilic esophagitis.

Introduction

Cendakimab (CC‐93538, previously known as RPC4046 and ABT‐308) is a recombinant, humanized monoclonal antibody (IgG1κ) with high affinity and neutralizing activity against interleukin (IL) 13. It selectively binds both the wild‐type IL‐13 and the Q110 sequence variant [1], blocking IL‐13 interactions with its receptors, IL‐13Rα1 and IL‐13Rα2. These receptors are implicated in inflammatory responses and tissue remodeling or fibrosis in eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) [2, 3, 4, 5].

Cendakimab was developed for the treatment of EoE, a type 2 cell‐mediated inflammatory disease in which IL‐13 plays a central role in immunopathogenesis [6]. Like other monoclonal antibodies, cendakimab is expected to be metabolized via lysosomal degradation into amino acids, which are reabsorbed into the endogenous amino acid pool. It does not share metabolic pathways with small‐molecule drugs. Furthermore, IL‐13 is not known to directly regulate cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme activity, which is responsible for the metabolism of most small‐molecule drugs. Therefore, cendakimab is not anticipated to have direct drug–drug interactions (DDIs) with small‐molecule therapies.

Proinflammatory cytokines, such as interferon gamma (IFN‐γ), tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα), IL‐6, and IL‐1β, are known to downregulate CYPs [7]. Although eosinophilic esophagitis is a type 2 inflammatory disease evidence regarding systemic cytokine levels that may influence CYP activity has been inconsistent. For example, Blanchard et al. [8] reported elevated circulating levels of several cytokines, including IL‐6, whereas Dellon et al. [9] observed no significant differences in serum IL‐6 or other inflammatory biomarkers between patients with EoE and non‐EoE controls. Therefore, the potential for cytokine‐mediated modulation of CYP activity in EoE could not be ruled out. Treatment with cendakimab may help restore the inflammatory environment by neutralizing IL‐13. This restoration could indirectly modulate CYP activity, a phenomenon referred to as a disease–drug interaction [10]. Consistent with the 2023 Food and Drug Administration Guidance for Industry on Drug–Drug Interaction Assessment for Therapeutic Proteins, which recommends evaluating the potential for disease‐mediated effects on CYP activity when therapeutic proteins modify inflammatory cytokine pathways, a clinical DDI study was warranted [11]. To investigate this potential, a dedicated clinical study was conducted to evaluate the effect of cendakimab treatment on the activity of five major CYP enzymes (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A) in adults with active EoE. The study also assessed the safety, exploratory efficacy, and immunogenicity of cendakimab.

Methods

Study Design

This was an open‐label, single‐sequence, multicenter study planned to enroll approximately 15 adults with confirmed EoE. The study included two treatment periods for a total of 18 weeks; one period to assess the pharmacokinetics (PK) of CYP probe substrates alone and a second period to assess the PK of CYP probe substrates coadministered with cendakimab after 16 weeks of cendakimab once‐weekly treatment (Figure 1). This study was conducted at 11 centers in the United States in compliance with International Conference on Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use/Good Clinical Practices and in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent before participating in the study. The protocol, amendments, and informed consent were reviewed and approved by institutional review board/independent ethics committee before study initiation. Institutional review boards included Advarra IRB (Pro00058973; first center approval date 24 Jan 2022) and Duke University Health System Institutional Review Board (Pro00110864; approval date 26 Sep 2023). See the Supporting Information 1 for a full list of centers. This study (NCT05175352) is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov.

Study Population

The study enrolled male and female patients aged 18 to 75 years, inclusive, with a body weight of at least 40.0 kg and a confirmed diagnosis of active EoE. Disease activity was supported by histologic evidence, defined as a peak count of ≥ 15 eosinophils per high‐power field (hpf) at any two levels of the esophagus (proximal, mid, and/or distal) obtained during screening esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) biopsy collected 8 to 3 weeks before day 1, along with clinical evidence of at least 4 dysphagia days (DDs) during the 14‐day screening collection period (day −15 to day −2), as recorded using the modified Daily Symptom Diary (mDSD) [12] via electronic diary. Women who were pregnant or breastfeeding were excluded, and those of childbearing potential were eligible only if they agreed to use highly effective contraceptive methods throughout the study. Patients were excluded if they had known hypersensitivity to, or contraindications for, any of the CYP probe substrates used in the study (caffeine, warfarin/vitamin K, omeprazole, dextromethorphan, or midazolam). The use of CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A inhibitors or inducers was not allowed during the study period. Additional exclusions included use of known CYP modulators within 14 days or five half‐lives (whichever was longer) before administration of the CYP probe substrates, use of systemic or swallowed topical corticosteroids, or a genotype indicating poor metabolizer status for CYP2C9, CYP2C19, or CYP2D6.

Interventions

Approximately 15 eligible patients were planned for enrollment to receive a CYP probe substrate cocktail administered orally across two sequential treatment periods (periods 1 and 2; see Figure 1). These periods were separated by 16 weeks of cendakimab once‐weekly treatment. The CYP probe substrate cocktail included caffeine (200 mg [tablet]), warfarin (10 mg [tablet]) coadministered with vitamin K (10 mg [5 mg tablets]), omeprazole (40 mg [delayed release capsule]), dextromethorphan (30 mg [30 mg/5 mL polistirex extended‐release suspension]), and midazolam (5 mg [5 mg/2.5 mL oral syrup]). The selected probe substrate doses were based on previously published and validated CYP cocktail studies and have been used safely and successfully to assess CYP enzyme activity in clinical pharmacology studies [13, 14]. All CYP probe substrates were administered as close together as possible within 5 min. Patients who completed period 2 and continued to meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria (e.g., had not permanently discontinued cendakimab) were offered the opportunity to enroll in an optional open‐label extension study (NCT04991935) to evaluate the long‐term efficacy and safety of cendakimab. Patients who chose not to enroll in the open‐label extension underwent safety follow‐up visits at 8 and 16 weeks after the last dose of cendakimab, allowing for a full washout period.

Patients reported to the clinical site on the morning of CYP probe substrate cocktail administration in each of the two periods (days 1 and 120) and returned daily for PK sample collection for up to 96 h post‐dose. Cendakimab 360 mg was administered subcutaneously once weekly beginning on day 8 of period 2, for a total of 17 doses. EoE disease condition was assessed during the screening period, through 2 weeks of mDSD data collection before the period two cocktail administration (at week 16–17), and by EGD at week 14. Genotyping for CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6 was performed during screening. Safety and immunogenicity were monitored throughout the study.

PK Sampling Schedule

Blood samples were collected in periods 1 and 2 at 0 (within 1 h before dosing), 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after CYP probe substrate cocktail administration. Sampling durations varied by analyte: omeprazole was collected up to 24 h; caffeine and midazolam up to 48 h; and dextromethorphan and warfarin up to 96. Cendakimab PK, antidrug antibody (ADA), and biomarker samples were collected predose on days 8, 36, 64, 92, 120, and at 8‐week and 16‐week follow‐up safety visits.

Bioanalyses

The quantification of omeprazole, dextromethorphan, caffeine, midazolam, and S‐warfarin in human plasma was performed using standalone validated LC–MS/MS assays. See Supporting Information 2 for the analytical methods' precision and detection ranges.

The quantification of cendakimab in human serum was performed using a validated electrochemiluminescence assay, quantitatively measuring cendakimab levels using biotin conjugated recombinant human IL‐13 as the capture reagent and a ruthenylated mouse anti‐human IgG Fc for detection. The detection of anti‐cendakimab antibodies in human serum was performed using a validated homogenous bridging electrochemiluminescence immunoassay as per regulatory expectations [15]. All sample analysis was conducted in accordance with the applicable regulations, standard operating procedures, and study design. Quality control samples run during sample analysis met the method acceptance criteria.

PK Parameters

PK parameters were estimated using noncompartmental analysis and actual PK collection time using Phoenix WinNonlin, version 8.3 (Certara USA Inc., Princeton, New Jersey). The PK parameters for caffeine, S‐warfarin, omeprazole, dextromethorphan, and midazolam that were evaluated, as appropriate, included maximum observed concentration (C max), area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) from time zero to 12 h postdose (AUC0–12) for caffeine only, and AUC from time zero extrapolated to infinity (AUCINF); AUCINF values were excluded when > 20% of AUCINF was determined by extrapolation or when adjusted coefficient of determination (R 2) was < 0.8.

Safety and Immunogenicity

Safety was monitored throughout the study. Safety evaluations included, but were not limited to, adverse events (AEs), serious AEs (SAEs), physical exam (PEs), vital signs, 12‐lead safety ECGs, clinical laboratory safety tests, concomitant medications/procedures, and pregnancy tests for women of childbearing potential. Immunogenicity (ADA and neutralizing antibody [NAb]) was evaluated from predose through the duration of the study, including the safety follow‐up period for those who did not enroll in the open‐label extension. The impact of immunogenicity on the safety and PK of cendakimab was evaluated.

Exploratory Efficacy Assessments

For this DDI study, the treatment response to multiple doses of cendakimab was explored by measuring the change in EoE clinical symptoms and eosinophilic histologic response suggestive of a reduction in inflammatory burden. Dysphagia symptoms were evaluated by patient‐reported outcome using the mDSD [12] through the number of DDs and the mDSD composite score [16], both assessed over a 14‐day period before the CYP probe substrate cocktail administration in period 1 (baseline) and over 16 weeks of cendakimab treatment from days 106 to 119 in period 2. The number of DDs assesses the frequency of dysphagia by asking the patient to confirm the presence of dysphagia symptoms (with scores ranging from 0–14). The mDSD composite score is representative of both frequency and severity of dysphagia by incorporating an assessment on the need to seek relief (with scores ranging from 0–70). Esophageal eosinophil count was obtained through EGD biopsies at baseline (within 8 weeks to 3 weeks before day 1) and after 12 weeks of cendakimab treatment (week 14/day 92), as this treatment duration previously resulted in histologic improvement in patients with EoE [17]. The protocol required biopsy evaluation to be through central read, with the exception that the screening assessment could be alternatively collected using a historical assessment within the same time frame before day 1. The screening central read was performed in 15 of 16 patients enrolled in the study. Biopsy fragments were obtained from three levels of the esophagus—proximal, mid, and distal.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical comparison of PK parameters of CYP probe substrates was performed on natural log transformed C max and AUC using a linear mixed‐effect model analysis with period (period 1 vs. period 2) as a fixed effect and patient as a random effect. The geometric least squares mean (LSM) of AUC and C max and the associated 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of the LSM ratios were calculated. In addition, the PK parameters were summarized descriptively. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina). All safety and exploratory efficacy analyses were performed in patients who had at least one dose of cendakimab or any dose of the CYP probe substrates. Safety analyses are presented descriptively. The proportion of patients having an AE was calculated as the number of patients having the event divided by the total number of patients in the reported time interval. Treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) are AEs that occur after dosing with any of the study drugs. Exploratory efficacy analyses are presented as descriptive summaries for both continuous and categorical endpoints per visit and assessment time, where appropriate.

Results

Sixteen patients (seven male, nine female) with confirmed EoE were treated in this study. The majority of the patients were White and not Hispanic or Latino (15 patients [93.8%]). The mean age of the study population was 43.2 years. The mean times since EoE symptoms onset and diagnosis were 13.9 and 8.9 years, respectively. The baseline mean (standard deviation [SD]) peak eosinophil count (PEC) values per hpf were 57.2 (52.46) for the proximal esophagus, 93.3 (61.21) for the mid esophagus, and 85.1 (51.55) for the distal esophagus. The mean (SD) PEC across all three levels (using the highest value across the available levels for each patient) was 118.2 (57.05) at baseline. Nine patients (56.3%) had a previous esophageal dilation procedure. With respect to dysphagia symptoms, the mean (SD) number of DDs at baseline was 11.5 (3.28) days, and the mean (SD) mDSD composite score was 23.82 (8.26) points; baseline values were not included for 1 patient who did not meet the required minimum number of days for the assessment. Fifteen patients (93.8%) completed the treatment, and 1 patient (6.3%) discontinued the treatment due to withdrawal of consent by the patient. Overall, the mean duration of treatment with cendakimab was 107.2 days. The mean treatment compliance (based on number of doses expected) reported for cendakimab was 100% with a mean number of 16.0 doses received per patient. Within the treatment compliance categories, all 16 patients (100.0%) achieved compliance within the range of 80% to 120%.

Of the 16 enrolled subjects, 1 subject completed Period 1 but discontinued shortly after entering Period 2 prior to probe substrate administration. For this subject, only caffeine PK data were reportable in Period 1.

For caffeine, 12 subjects in Period 1 and 11 subjects in Period 2 had reportable C max or AUC0‐12 values. Four subjects in Period 1 and 5 subjects in Period 2 had predose caffeine concentrations exceeding 5% of C max and were excluded from the PK analysis. Eight subjects had reportable C max and AUC0‐12 in both treatment periods and were included in the comparative PK analysis. Based on these 8 subjects, the 90% CIs for the geometric mean ratios were slightly out of the 80% and 125% range. However, the differences between the two treatment periods were not considered clinically meaningful.

For S‐warfarin, three subjects did not report AUCINF because more than 20% of AUCINF was extrapolated and/or the adjusted R 2 of the terminal phase regression was < 0.8. One additional subject did not have reportable PK concentration data. Consequently, 15 subjects had reportable C max and 12 subjects had reportable AUCINF in both periods. The 90% CIs for the geometric mean ratios were entirely within the 80% and 125% range, indicating the exposure to S‐warfarin was comparable between the two treatment periods.

For omeprazole, five subjects did not have reportable AUCINF because more than 20% was extrapolated and/or the adjusted R 2 of the terminal phase regression was < 0.8. Two subjects either had no PK sample collected or no reportable PK concentration in one or both periods. Consequently, 14 subjects had reportable C max and nine subjects had reportable AUCINF in both periods. The 90% CIs for the geometric mean ratios included 1.00 and the upper limit exceeded 125% for all parameters; however, these differences were not considered clinically meaningful.

Plasma dextromethorphan concentrations were unexpectedly low and were often near the lower limit of quantification. Consequently, 11 subjects had reportable C max values and four subjects had reportable AUCINF values for dextromethorphan in both treatment periods. The 90% CIs for the geometric mean ratios included 1.00; however, the 90% CIs were wide. These differences were not considered clinically meaningful.

For midazolam, 13 subjects had reportable C max and AUCINF in both treatment periods. Three subjects were excluded from the PK analysis because of missing PK data or predose concentrations exceeding 5% of C max in both periods. The 90% CIs for the geometric mean ratios for AUCINF were within the 80% and 125% range; however, the upper limit of 90% CI for C max was slightly higher than 125%. Exposure to midazolam was comparable between the two treatment periods.

The sensitivity analysis, which included all subjects with data from at least one period, confirmed similar findings (data not shown).

Genotyping of CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6 on Pharmacokinetics

Among the 16 patients, CYP2C9 genotyping identified 12 as normal metabolizers and 4 as intermediate metabolizers. For CYP2C19, seven were normal metabolizers, four intermediate metabolizers, four rapid, and one ultra‐rapid metabolizers. Regarding CYP2D6, 12 were normal metabolizers, two intermediate metabolizers, and two had unknown metabolizer status.

No differences in PK were observed between genotype and phenotype groups, either in the absence or presence of cendakimab. Genotyping and phenotyping of CYP enzymes had no apparent impact on the PK of CYP probe drugs (data not shown).

Safety

Overall, 10 out of 16 patients experienced at least one TEAE during periods 1 (three patients [18.8%]) and two (10 patients [62.5%]). All TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity. No SAEs or deaths were observed. No patient had TEAEs leading to permanent discontinuation of the study drug. One patient had a mild TEAE of palpitations that led to temporary interruption of cendakimab during period 2. A list of all TEAEs occurring in ≥ 5% of patients (at least one patient) in periods 1 and 2 of the study is included in the Supporting Information 3. There were no TEAEs of special interest (categories monitored included anaphylactic reaction, systemic or severe hypersensitivity, severe injection‐site reactions lasting > 1 day, malignancy events, helminthic or parasitic infections, opportunistic infections, and infections of special interest). No clinically relevant changes were observed in serum chemistry, hematology, or urinalysis. No clinically relevant changes in PE or vital signs were observed. Further, no EoE flares or use of rescue procedures such as esophageal dilation were reported during the study.

Exploratory Efficacy Assessments

EoE clinical symptoms and histologic efficacy parameters were explored after treatment with cendakimab in period 2. Consistent improvements were demonstrated in patient reported symptoms and eosinophilic histologic response. Change from baseline in dysphagia symptoms was evaluable in 11 patients; a mean (SD) decrease in the number of DDs over a 14‐day period of ⎯4.9 (3.05) days and a mean (SD) decrease in the mDSD composite score of ⎯12.29 (7.63) points was observed from the baseline assessment to the 2‐week period before week 18/day 120 (collected from days 106–119). A reduction in PEC across all available esophageal levels from baseline to week 14 (day 92) was demonstrated. The PEC mean (SD) changes from baseline were −56.8 (54.69) for proximal, −65.1 (82.52) for mid, and −68.1 (50.18) for distal esophageal levels. The mean change in PEC from baseline across all esophageal levels was −85.1 (67.10) (Figure 3) in 14 patients with histologic data (by central read) at both timepoints. Moreover, at week 14 (day 92), the PEC of three patients (18.8%) decreased to ≤ 6 eos/hpf and the PEC of four patients (25.0%) decreased to < 15 eos/hpf, noting that all 16 patients had a PEC of > 15 eos/hpf consistent with the diagnosis of EoE at baseline.

Pharmacodynamics

A panel of 10 pro‐inflammatory cytokine analytes (IFN‐γ, IL‐1β, IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐12, IL‐13, and TNF‐α) was analyzed in patients' plasma samples collected at weeks 2 (baseline for cytokines before initiation of cendakimab treatment), 6, 10, 14, and 18. Most (72.4%; 521 out of 720 samples) of the cytokine analytes at all the time points were below the limit of quantification (Figure 4), suggesting that the cytokine levels were very low in patients with EoE. Moreover, there were no clinically meaningful differences observed in any of the analytes between any timepoints. These results demonstrated that a DDI due to modulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines is unlikely to exist with cendakimab treatment in patients with EoE.

Immunogenicity

Anti‐cendakimab antibody and NAb were monitored over the treatment period and were detected in two out of 16 patients post cendakimab treatment. No preexisting ADAs were detected. There was no association between the presence of ADAs/NAbs and the safety, exploratory efficacy, or PK of cendakimab.

Discussion

Cendakimab does not directly interact with CYP enzymes. However, indirect effects on CYP activity may occur due to modulation of proinflammatory cytokines associated with EoE, which could influence hepatic enzyme expression. Both dose and treatment duration are critical factors in evaluating potential DDIs. In this study, CYP enzyme activity was assessed before and after 16 weeks of cendakimab once‐weekly treatment. This time frame was selected based on evidence from the cendakimab phase II trial in patients with EoE, where the primary endpoint was evaluated at week 16 in the double‐blind treatment period, and therapeutic effects were demonstrated by week 12 in the additional open‐label treatment period [17]. The dose of cendakimab used, 360 mg subcutaneously once weekly, represents the target dose for this indication. Measuring CYP activity at baseline and after 16 weeks allows for capturing the clinically meaningful change in disease state and the greatest potential for disease‐mediated effects on drug metabolism, if present. The exploratory efficacy data from this study indicated improvements in clinical symptoms and histologic features after cendakimab treatment. Therefore, the 16‐week treatment period was an appropriate setting to evaluate potential DDIs in patients with EoE.

For therapeutic proteins that modulate proinflammatory cytokines in disease states characterized by elevated cytokine levels, inflammatory markers can be useful in assessing the risk of DDIs [18]. IL‐6 ≥ 20 pg/mL [19, 20], for example, has been associated with more than a two‐fold increase in the AUC of sensitive CYP3A substrates. In this study, levels of a panel of 10 proinflammatory cytokines—including IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, and IL‐6—were found to be low, supporting a low risk of DDIs for cendakimab in the treatment of EoE. The low circulating cytokine levels observed may in fact represent an important study finding that helps explain the absence of meaningful effects of cendakimab treatment on CYP activity. Given the conflicting literature regarding systemic markers of inflammation in EoE, characterization of circulating cytokines may provide useful information to contextualize the nature of inflammation in EoE. This may aid future drug development programs, potentially informing the need to assess cytokine‐mediated drug–drug interaction risk in this patient population. Furthermore, low immunogenicity was observed in this study, with no apparent association between ADA/NAb status and PK, exploratory efficacy, or safety outcomes.

The study protocol predefined AUC0–12 for caffeine and AUCINF for the other CYP substrates as primary endpoints to evaluate the effect of cendakimab treatment. AUC0–12 was selected for caffeine due to the potential for patients to consume coffee after the 12‐h confinement period at the clinical site.

The geometric mean ratios for exposure (AUC0–12 for caffeine and AUCINF for the other CYP probe substrates) were all within the range of 80% to 125%, indicating that none of the five CYP probe substrates were inhibited or induced by cendakimab treatment in patients with EoE. C max and AUC values for most CYP probe substrates were within or near the 80% to 125% range, except for dextromethorphan. Exposure to dextromethorphan appeared lower after 16 weeks of cendakimab 360 mg once weekly compared with baseline. However, this apparent reduction may not reflect a true PK change. Plasma concentrations of dextromethorphan were low and often near the lower limit of quantification, suggesting that the analytic sensitivity may have been insufficient to reliably detect dextromethorphan levels in some patients. This led to nonreportable exposures and substantial variability, limiting the number of evaluable patients and the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the effect of cendakimab on CYP2D6 activity.

This study has several limitations. First, it was not designed to support formal hypothesis testing. The sample size of 12 evaluable subjects was relatively small but was deemed acceptable based on the precision of the half‐width of the 90% confidence interval for the difference in log‐transformed means, targeted to be within 18% of AUC, assuming the intra‐subject SD of the natural log transformed data of 0.26 for AUC. This small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study used a single‐sequence design, which allowed within‐subject comparisons before and after cendakimab treatment. This approach reduced variability and, consequently, the required sample size. However, the single‐sequence design does not allow a separation of treatment and period effects; thus, the observed differences in pharmacokinetic parameters may have been confounded by period effects. However, given the long half‐life of cendakimab (20 to 21 days) [21], a 2 × 2 crossover design was not feasible. Third, the low dextromethorphan concentrations were not anticipated at the time of study design, as the extent of cytokine‐mediated modulation of CYP2D6 activity in EoE was uncertain. Accordingly, the bioanalytical lower limit of quantification was therefore selected based on expected exposure levels. As a result, most subjects had extremely low dextromethorphan concentrations, close to the bioanalytical detection limits, which limited the evaluability of the PK data. Therefore, the dextromethorphan data should be interpreted with caution. However, the results for the other probe drugs are unlikely to be affected. Finally, the study was conducted using an open‐label design to enable study recruitment in patients with EoE; however, as the primary endpoints were objective pharmacokinetic parameters, the potential for bias due to the open‐label design is considered minimal. Further, safety and exploratory efficacy results following cendakimab treatment should be interpreted in the context of a small, open‐label patient cohort but are still helpful to contextualize broader inflammatory states and are largely concordant with results from the larger, definitive phase III randomized control trial of cendakimab versus placebo in patients with EoE [16]. In addition, these findings were generated in patients with EoE, a predominantly type 2 inflammatory disease, and may not be directly generalizable to disease states with distinct inflammatory mechanisms, cytokine profiles, or inflammatory burden, such as rheumatoid arthritis. Nevertheless, the findings may have relevance to other diseases characterized by similar type 2 inflammatory pathways and comparable levels of systemic inflammation.

Overall, cendakimab treatment in adults with active EoE had no clinically meaningful effect on the activity of CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, or CYP3A, and the results were consistent across genotypes. The treatment was generally safe and well tolerated, both with and without coadministration of CYP substrates.
