Impact of the gut microbiome on health impacts of Haskap berries: study protocol for a randomized control trial
Authors: Morgan L. Chamberlin, Meghan L. Spears, Gwendolyn Cooper, Zachariah J. Miller, Brian Bothner, Seth T. Walk, Carl J. Yeoman, Mary P. Miles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHaskap berries have great potential as a superfood due to high polyphenolic content which confers both anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity. These health impacts are mitigated, at least in part, by the gut microbiome as most ingested polyphenols pass to the large intestine for microbial enzymatic action and conversion to secondary phenolic metabolites. These microbial actions mediate both the bioavailability and the bioefficacy of Haskap-derived phenolics. However, clinical intervention trials characterizing the impact of long-term Haskap consumption on human health and the interaction between Haskap-derived phenolics and the gut microbiome are limited. This study aims to determine the impact of Haskap consumption on gut microbiome composition, gut microbial and serum metabolites, and other health outcome metrics in a cohort of adults with both low and high risk of metabolic syndrome.MethodsThis is a four-armed, randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted in a cohort of adults with both low and high risk of metabolic syndrome. A total of 120 participants (60 metabolically healthy, 60 metabolically unhealthy) will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to consume a daily dose of either Haskap or placebo juice for 8 weeks. Outcome measures will be collected before and after the intervention period to determine the health impacts of Haskap in both groups. Primary outcome measures include fasting blood markers of glucose and lipid metabolism and inflammation, fat oxidation rates during submaximal exercise, 16S rRNA fecal microbial composition data, and mass spectrometry-acquired fecal and serum metabolomic data. Secondary outcome measures include anthropometric and sleep quality measures as well as acute and habitual dietary intake data.DiscussionInvestigating how the gut microbiome influences the health benefits of consuming Haskap berries will help elucidate potential mechanisms of Haskap-induced metabolic health benefits and help inform the development of effective strategies to decrease metabolic disease risk through Haskap consumption.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06546020. Registered on 1 August 2024.

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Metabolic conditions such as increased abdominal adiposity, insulin resistance, elevated blood lipids, and inflammation are common components of metabolic syndrome and drive progression toward metabolic diseases including type 2 diabetes (T2D), and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [1, 2]. An estimated 34% and 9% of the US adult population are prediabetic and diabetic, with T2D comprising the vast majority of diabetes cases [3]. Furthermore, there is a 2–4-fold elevation in risk for CVD in individuals with T2D [4]. Polyphenols are bioactive compounds found in a wide variety of foods that have been shown to reduce inflammation [5], combat oxidative stress [6], and improve blood pressure and lipid profiles [7]. As a result, interest in polyphenol-rich “superfood” consumption as a dietary strategy to combat metabolic disease progression is growing.

Lonicera caerulae, also known as Haskap, is a fruit-producing shrub commonly planted in Canada, Eastern Europe, and Asia. It is ideal for commercial production as the shrub is resistant to cold and pests and the fruit is hardy enough to withstand automated cultivation processes without damage [8, 9]. Haskap berries are a rich source of dietary polyphenols, primarily derived from phenolic acids, flavonols, and anthocyanins with the latter being the most abundant [10]. The total phenolic content of Haskap fruit far exceeds that of other fruits with one study identifying values as high as (1111.17 mg/100 g) which exceeded the values found in bilberries (778.47 mg/100 g), wild strawberries (437.04 mg/100 g) and purple raspberries (638.09 mg/100 g) [11].

Dietary polyphenols exert a great impact on health and are therefore the subject of many research studies. However, a limited number of studies have assessed the positive health effects of Haskap berry consumption in humans [12, 13]—though evidence suggests they have great potential to do so. In vitro and murine studies have shown that Haskap berries and their isolated polyphenols inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhance gut barrier function, modify gut microbiota, and improve various metabolic markers, including reductions in lipids and adipose tissue [14–20]. While Haskap has demonstrated wide ranging health impacts, the overall physiologic and metabolic impact of Haskap consumption in humans has not been explored. Importantly, we are not aware of any studies investigating how long-term Haskap supplementation influences physiologic responses in individuals with metabolic risk factors—a population ideally poised to benefit from supplementation. Furthermore, research is still needed to better understand the mechanism responsible for demonstrated health benefits of Haskap.

The effectiveness of polyphenol consumption in improving human health is closely tied to microbial activity within the gut [14, 21, 22]. While some polyphenols are absorbed in the small intestine, most travel to the colon, where gut microbes convert them into secondary phenolic metabolites through enzymatic processes [23, 24]. These metabolites often surpass the parent compounds in both bioavailability and biological activity [25–27]. Additionally, research indicates that polyphenol consumption can reshape the gut microbiota composition by acting both as a prebiotic and an antimicrobial agent [28]. These changes can influence the gut metabolome [22] and affect postprandial responses in the host [29]. Thus, shifts in microbial populations and the metabolites they produce are critical mechanisms underlying the health effects associated with polyphenol intake [21].

There is a critical gap in knowledge regarding the relationship between Haskap-derived polyphenols and the gut microbiome and how this interaction impacts health benefits. To address this gap, we designed a four-armed, randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial in a cohort of adults with both low and high risk of metabolic syndrome to comprehensively assess the health impacts of long-term Haskap consumption. In this trial, we examined the impact of Haskap juice consumption on fecal microbial composition, host and microbial metabolomic profiles, and measures of host metabolic health including markers of glucose and lipid metabolism and inflammation. These measurements provide a comprehensive characterization of health responses to Haskap consumption and allow for the assessment of the interaction between gut microbiota and Haskap-derived polyphenols to determine microbiome-driven health impacts.

Trial design {8}

The study is a prospective, four-armed, randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted to determine the impact of Haskap consumption on gut microbiome composition, serum and gut microbial metabolites, and health outcome metrics in a cohort of adults with both low and high risk of metabolic syndrome (MetS). A total of 120 participants (60 MH, 60 MUH) will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to consume a daily dose of either Haskap (HA) or placebo (PL) juice for 8 weeks. Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Study co-investigators or authorized representatives are responsible for obtaining informed consent. The process involves a detailed explanation of the study’s background, risks, and benefits. Participants are provided with a copy of the informed consent document, which contains details about the voluntary nature of participation, the confidentiality of personal data, and the right to withdraw at any time. All forms are approved by the ethics committee before the commencement of the trial. Consent is obtained at each research visit.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

The blood and stool samples collected as part of this study may be utilized for future studies that are not yet planned. Future analyses will not include participant DNA genetic analysis. All samples collected during the study are coded to maintain participant anonymity and information linking the participant to the coded samples will be destroyed at the end of the current study. Samples will be owned and controlled by the principal investigator of this study who will be the only researcher with the authority to approve use of samples for future studies. All participants are provided the right to refuse consent for future use of study samples with no impact to their participation in the current study.

Intervention description {11a}

Haskap berries

Haskap berries utilized in this study are obtained from berries grown at or in the region of the Western Agricultural Research Center (WARC) in Corvallis, MT. Haskap berries are bulked by cultivar variety and transported frozen to the Nutrition Research Lab in Bozeman, MT. Different berry batches and cultivars are analyzed for polyphenolic and sugar concentrations and then normalized to body mass of participants accordingly for consistent dosing for the duration of the study.

Intervention juices

Based on previous work examining similar outcome measures [21], a daily polyphenol dose of 1.94 g berry·kg−1 was set for the study. The daily Haskap juice dose (mL/day) required to meet the target polyphenol intake was calculated based on the total polyphenol content of the berries, determined as described below. The determined dose translates to 136 g of berries or ~1 cup of Haskap berries per day for a participant weighing 70 kg and reflects both a standard fruit serving size as recommended by the American Heart Association [22] and a feasible intake level for individuals seeking to obtain potential health benefits. The determined daily juice dose is then divided into two doses to be consumed 12 h apart (morning and evening dose). The Haskap juice is prepared by blending 500 g of frozen Haskap berries with 1000 mL water in a commercial blender.

The placebo juice utilized in this study is flavor, color, and carbohydrate-matched to the Haskap juice. The placebo juice is prepared by blending 30 g glucose, 35 g fructose, 10 g black cherry Koolaid© mix (no sugar added), and 1500 mL water in a commercial blender. Placebo juice volumes are matched to Haskap juice volumes based on body mass.

All study juices are prepared by a third-party research associate in the Nutrition Research Laboratory so that researchers performing study visits remain blind to condition. After preparation, juice is kept frozen (−20 °C) until time of participant pick-up. Participants are provided with juice as needed during the 8-week intervention and instructed to refrigerate the juice.

Haskap juice polyphenol and sugar analysis

Haskap berries are analyzed to determine both phenolic and carbohydrate content. Polyphenol content was determined using the Fast Blue BB assay based on methods described previously [22]. Briefly, a gallic acid standard curve was used to quantify total polyphenolic content in Haskap juice using 0.01% Fast Blue B Salt solution and 1 N KOH on an EPOCH microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA). Carbohydrate content was determined by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis using a Bruker 600 MHz (1H Larmor frequency) AVANCE III solution NMR spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm triple resonance (1H, 13C, 15N) liquid helium-cooled cryoprobe, Top-Spin software (Billerica, MA, USA, Bruker version 3.2) and an automatic sample loading system (SampleJet). Determined carbohydrate concentrations were used to develop matching placebo juice.

Intervention period

The intervention period will start on the first day participants consume the juice and span 8 weeks. A 3-day buffer is included at the end of the period to provide flexibility in scheduling the final visit. If a participant becomes ill, the final visit will be scheduled at least 1 week after symptoms resolve. During the 8-week intervention period, participants are instructed to consume their standard diet with the exception of omitting foods with high polyphenolic content. Participants are provided with a list of foods to avoid at visit 1 (Table 2). Adherence to diet guidelines is verified with self-reported diet adherence questionnaires completed on a weekly basis during the intervention period. Participants are asked to keep logs of daily juice consumption as well as consumption of high-polyphenolic foods for the duration of the intervention period. These adherence logs are turned in to research personnel during juice pick-ups and at visit 3 after the final week of the intervention.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

All participants are informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason without consequence. Should this occur, study procedures are immediately discontinued and the participant is compensated for their time as agreed upon in the study consent. Data collected up to date of the time of withdrawal will be kept. In certain situations, the primary investigator may decide to withdraw a participant from the study. This decision can be made if there is a significant negative reaction to any study procedure, if a participant reports significant illness, or if the investigator determines that withdrawal from the study is in the participant’s best interest. Record of participant termination from the study and explanation for withdrawal will be kept.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Participants are provided with a list of restricted foods and the quantities that may be consumed as well as suggested alternative foods. A dietary adherence log will be provided to each participant to record any instances where they did not follow the recommended diet. In addition, a daily compliance log to track juice ingestion will be provided to each participant as a reminder to consume their twice daily juice dose and to record any instances of missed or incomplete dose consumption. A research member will go over each of these documents with study participants in person during visit 2 prior to starting the intervention period. Finally, reminders for each visit to the Nutrition Research Laboratory are sent to participants 48 h before each visit.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

During the study period, participants may continue medical and social care as usual. However, initiation of treatment plans that conflict with exclusion criteria such as the prescription of antibiotics or lipid-lowering medication will result in removal from the study. Blood test results are provided to participants, and they are advised to share test results with their primary care providers throughout the study.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

The likelihood of participants encountering any harm during the study is low. Risks associated with study participation are described to participants during each study visit during the consenting process. In the event that a participant sustains any harm as a result of study participation, they will be referred to an appropriate health care service. Outside of this circumstance, post-trial care is not provided.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome measures in this study include fasting blood markers of glucose and lipid metabolism and inflammation, fat oxidation rates during submaximal exercise, fecal microbial composition, and mass spectrometry-acquired fecal and serum metabolomic data. Secondary outcome measures include anthropometric, sleep quality, and physical activity measures as well as acute and habitual dietary intake data. All outcome measures are collected at baseline (pre-intervention) and again after the 8-week intervention period (post-intervention). Sample size {14}

Pilot data from a similarly designed study in our laboratory investigating the health impacts of Aronia juice consumption was utilized to perform a power analysis for the current study. Power was calculated a posteriori using untargeted serum metabolomic data from fasting blood samples at pre- and post-intervention timepoints. Top metabolites (features) over time and intervention group were identified with multivariate empirical Bayes analysis of variance (MEBA) [23]. MEBA is a time series modeling technique that compares temporal metabolic profiles across different conditions and ranks features based on degree of change over time. Features identified by MEBA analysis were ranked from most significant to least based on differences in patterns over time. Features from this list were then binned into ten groups (10th percentiles) to capture a range of most to least effective features based on how well they discriminated between intervention groups over time. To quantify the effect of selected features, Cohen’s d effect sizes of each feature were calculated and averaged across the 10 percentile bins. These averaged effect sizes represent expected effect sizes based on discriminatory power of representative features from each percentile ranking. Power was then calculated from Cohen’s d effect sizes of selected features to find a difference in means (post–pre measurement) between the Aronia and placebo group. These power values are therefore representative of the expected discriminatory power from different ranks of features. Using this method, a power of β = 0.80 for linear regression modeling to detect true differences for the top 60% of metabolites is achieved with a sample size of n = 25 per group. Additional a posteriori sample size calculations were made using the outcome measures of cholesterol and interleukins-17 and 23 (cytokines most relevant to inflammation in the gut) in G*Power software (version 3.1). The statistical test utilized was an ANOVA for repeated measures (within-between interaction) with an effect size (f) = 0.2, α = 0.05, and power = 0.95. The total sample size calculated with these inputs was 56 (n = 56) with 28 participants per group. To provide a small cushion to allow for the possibility of greater variability in these outcome measures, 30 participants per group for a total of 120 study participants will be enrolled in the study.

Recruitment {15}

Potential participants are recruited on a rolling basis in Bozeman, Montana and the surrounding area via flyer advertisements, word of mouth, and snowball recruitment methods. Interested individuals will be directed to a screening survey through the online platform REDCap [24, 25]. The screening survey briefly describes the trial and asks questions to obtain information regarding inclusion and exclusion criteria. The survey also contains contact information for study staff so potential participants may contact the research team with any questions. Study staff monitoring the online survey will follow-up with potentially eligible participants via email to verify eligibility. Participants who are determined to be potentially eligible based on the online screening survey will be scheduled for a screening visit in the Nutrition Research Lab where a blood draw, blood pressure, and anthropometric assessment will be performed to determine eligibility for enrollment into MH or MUH study groups.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Block randomization to study condition (HA vs PL) will be completed using the blockrand function in the blockrand package in R statistical software. Blocks of ten with two equally numbered levels will be utilized, with the seed for each block individually set based on the last five integers returned by the Sys.time function in base R. Eligible participants are randomized to condition after the initial screening visit.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The allocation sequence will be implemented via an online data sheet. Only the individual in charge of creating the randomization sequence and the individual in charge of participant allocation and juice making will have access to the sheet.

Implementation {16c}

Creation of the randomization sequence will be performed by a designated research team member who does not interact with participants. The treatment allocation sequence generated from the randomization process will be provided to a third-party research member who is responsible for participant allocation and preparation of Haskap and placebo juices. Prepared juices will be labeled only by blinded code in order for investigators working on the clinical trial to remain blinded as well as study participants.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Study participants and research investigators will be blinded to study intervention assignments. Research investigators completing data analysis will remain blind until after initial data analysis is completed.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Unblinding will only be conducted if a relevant medical reason arises that is believed to be directly related to the participant’s involvement in the study.

Data management {19}

Trial data is collected by trained research assistants using standardized data collection forms designed for the study. Identifying information (name, contact information) about potential and enrolled participants will be collected by trained research personnel on a web-based datasheet stored in a secured OneDrive file. Upon enrollment into the study, each participant will be assigned an ID number. Participant name and ID number will be contained on a master code which is the only document linking participant name and ID and stored in a secured OneDrive file. All data collected and recorded for the duration of the study will solely be identified with participant ID. All physical collection sheets are stored in a locked file cabinet in the Nutrition Research Laboratory for the duration of the study. Participant data generated or stored in web-based spreadsheets and other electronic media are stored in secured OneDrive files. Metabolomic data from the Montana State University Proteomics, Metabolomics, and Mass Spectrometry core facility are stored on secure servers within the facility before storage as secured OneDrive files. When all participants for the study have completed the research protocol, the master code datasheet linking names and IDs is destroyed. Preservation of cyberinfrastructure including secured OneDrive files is provided by Montana State University’s IT Center. Access to files is restricted by the Principal Investigator and permission is required to access files.

Confidentiality {27}

All participant data will be treated confidentially by the trial personnel. Data will be collected via web-based questionnaires and established assessment forms. The data will then be stored as described above.

However, samples collected as a part of this study, including fecal and blood samples, may be utilized for future or ancillary studies. Participant identification will remain confidential as stored samples for future analyses will remain coded. Samples will be owned and controlled by the principal investigator of this study and stored in the Nutrition Research Laboratory. Participants are provided this information during the consenting process and are informed of their right to refuse consent to use of samples in future studies.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Statistical analyses will be conducted using R statistical software unless designated otherwise. Unless specified below, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons is planned. All primary endpoints will be reported with exact p-values, 95% confidence intervals, and effect sizes to allow readers to interpret the results in context. The five secondary endpoints are considered exploratory and are reported without adjustment for multiplicity. Descriptive summary statistics will be performed to summarize participant characteristics by intervention group at baseline. General linear models will be used to determine if physical and biological characteristics differed between groups before the intervention began and to determine the impact of the dietary intervention on outcome measures. Specifications of statistical testing for each outcome variable are provided below. Validity conditions will be confirmed through visualizations of the response and model residuals. Appropriate data transformations or alternative analytical approaches will be applied as warranted.

Clinical assessment data

Fasting lipid and glucose markers as well as secondary outcome measures including anthropometric measures, sleep quality, and physical activity will be summarized as delta values (post-intervention–pre-intervention). General linear models will then be used to assess the impact of the dietary intervention on each outcome measure with an interaction predictor between juice condition (PL or HA) and health group (MUH or MH) as well as main effects underlying the interaction. Variables with known biological relevance will be included as covariates in models as needed.

Inflammation data

Each of the seven cytokine markers will be summarized as delta values (post-intervention–pre-intervention). General linear models will then be used to assess the impact of the dietary intervention on each cytokine with an interaction predictor between juice condition (PL or HA) and health group (MUH or MH) as well as main effects underlying the interaction. Visceral adipose will be included as a predictor variable in inflammation models as it represents a common difference between genders and is a key contributor to systemic inflammation levels [31]. The false discovery rate (FDR) will be applied to correct for multiple testing.

Dietary intake data

Acute dietary intake data from the ASA24 will be assessed to determine if each participant well-matched their diet in the 24-h period prior to blood collection visits (Visits 2 and 3). Significant deviations in intake may result in exclusion of participant data from analysis. Habitual dietary patterns, represented by DHQ-III healthy eating index scores (HEI), will be analyzed at baseline through general linear models to detect existing dietary differences in habitual diet between intervention groups. To determine if the intervention changed participant dietary intake, linear mixed effects models will be used to assess both HEI scores and pertinent dietary components with an interaction predictor between juice condition, health group and time (pre- or post-intervention), main effects underlying the interaction, and a random effect for subject. Diet components that may be expected to change with the required low-polyphenol intervention diet such as fiber (g) and fruit (cups) will similarly be assessed. Post-hoc multiple pairwise comparisons will be performed to detect significant differences by group using Tukey’s HSD.

Exercise data

Fat oxidation rates will be calculated as net AUC (trapezoidal) during the 30-min submaximal exercise test and then summarized delta values (post-intervention–pre-intervention). General linear models will be used to assess the impact of intervention on fat oxidation rates using an interaction predictor between juice condition and health group and main effects underlying the interaction.

Metabolomic data

Statistical analysis of untargeted metabolomic data will be completed using MetaboAnalyst [32] (metaboanalyst.ca). Preprocessing steps will include normalization and filtering. Statistical analyses will include t-tests and ANOVAs to determine significant features, which will be screened using the FDR to correct for multiple testing. Identified significant features will be examined as delta values (post-intervention relative concentration–pre-intervention relative concentration) by health group and juice condition. These statistical techniques will also be utilized in the targeted analysis to determine the change in F2-isoprostane concentrations completed in the targeted analysis.

Microbial taxonomic data

Baseline community composition will be represented by the average of the fecal samples collected at Visits 1 and 2 and the post-intervention represented by the fecal sample collected at Visit 3. Ecological analyses including alpha diversity (richness and Pielou’s evenness) will be calculated at the OTU level using MOTHUR (mothur.org) and R package vegan. Beta-diversity analyses will be performed on subsampled data with filtering of OTUs less than 3 counts in at least 20% of the samples. Permutational multivariate analysis of distance matrices, or PERMANOVA, will be performed with 999 permutations using adonis2 in vegan (version 2.6–4). Fixed effects will include an interaction between juice condition, health group and time, underlying main effects in the interaction, and subject. Community composition will be examined at each taxonomic level (phylum–genus) in addition to taxa selected by hierarchical feature engineering using taxaHFE without the permuted random forest [33]. Beta diversity will be visualized using non-metric dimensional scaling from phyloseq [34].

A differential abundance analysis will also be performed to identify possible changes in genera from 8 weeks of juice consumption. To do so, MaAsLin2 [35] with the subject as a random effect and an interaction between juice condition, health group, and time (pre- or post- intervention) as fixed effects will be utilized. The interaction output will be extracted and q-values re-calculated for multiple testing adjustment with the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Network analysis

To determine if relationships exist between microbial taxonomy data, fecal and serum metabolomics data, and clinical assessment data, a correlation-based network analysis will be conducted. Correlation network analysis utilizes mathematically derived correlations to evaluate the degree of similarity between all variables in a dataset [36]. Relationships between variables are then visualized via a network analysis to enable biological interpretation of data. Each node in the network represents a variable (clinical measurement, metabolite, lipid, etc.) which is connected to other nodes by an edge. The color of the edge reflects if it is a positive or negative relationship (i.e., correlation coefficient). In-house developed R code for correlation matrix calculation and network generation are publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/IDSS-NIAID/MetLipNet). The “rcorr” function from the Hmisc package calculates correlation matrices from integrated datasets consisting of all variables of interest. The “cor_gather” function from the rstatix package enables coercion of correlation matrices to a format that is compatible with the igraph package for network visualization. Networks can be optimized using the igraph package to trim nodes with no connections to other nodes or to reduce complexity of the network. In addition, specific nodes of interest can be interrogated by examining their first- and second-degree neighbors using the “ego” function from the igraph package [37]. Networks are plotted using the ggraph package.

Discussion

Identifying foods with bioactive components that decrease inflammation and improve metabolism is an important strategy for reducing metabolic disease burden. Berries high in polyphenols promote human health through a variety of mechanisms, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, that may be dependent on interactions with the gut microbiota. Polyphenol-rich Haskap berries have therapeutic potential to improve human health but the impact of long-term Haskap consumption and subsequent changes to metabolic health and interaction with the gut microbiota are understudied. This study therefore investigates the interaction of bioactive components in Haskap with gut microbiota and resultant changes to fecal microbial and serum metabolites, inflammation, and metabolic health. Furthermore, as the impact of Haskap consumption may be dependent on gut microbial composition, this study compares gut microbiota composition and health impacts of Haskap berry consumption between metabolically healthy and unhealthy groups. This study significantly advances research by elucidating microbiome-dependent and independent impacts of long-term Haskap consumption on metabolic health in two distinct populations.
