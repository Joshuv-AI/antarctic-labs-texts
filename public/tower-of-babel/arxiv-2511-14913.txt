Stochastic Kinetics of Protein Molecules in a Gene Expression Model under Burst Approximation
Yuntao Lu, Yunxin Zhang

Abstract
The burst approximation is a widely-used technique to simplify stochastic gene expression models. However, both analytical results and efficient algorithms are currently unavailable for general models under burst approximation. In this article, we systematically analyze surrogate models with multiple gene states. Analytical solution to the chemical master equation is provided, which is further exploited from two perspectives. Theoretically, several inequalities are established using functional analysis. We conclude that the steady-state distribution of protein copy number is bounded from above by a constant multiple of some negative binomial distribution if the burst size is geometrically distributed. Computationally, efficient algorithms are developed under three circumstances based on the standard binomial moment method.



1
Introduction

Gene expression is a fundamental process that underpins living systems. Physically, gene expression can be studied experimentally, or using computational approaches including molecular dynamics simulation
[
24
]
.
Significant randomness at single-molecule level is clearly observed
[
12
]
. This intrinsic stochasticity raises the question of how the living systems achieve precise and reliable outcomes despite the underlying fluctuation of every individual process. To quantitatively understand and predict gene expression, appropriate mathematical models are necessary. In particular, probability, especially the theory of stochastic process, should be utilized to account for the intrinsic stochasticity.

In principle, a fine-grained description of a complicated living system is far beyond the reach of mathematical analysis, and we can either consider only the statistical properties of the system or propose coarse-grained models. The continuous-time Markov chain is widely used to model the interaction of different molecules in a living system, whose Kolmogorov forward equation is also named the chemical master equation
[
5
,
25
]
. Additionally, the Markov chain is frequently used in the study of biomolecules as a coarse-graining technique to describe the conformational dynamics
[
19
,
21
]
. Based on the above two theoretical frameworks, appropriate models of gene expression can be readily proposed. Nevertheless, further analysis poses mathematical challenges. Stochastic gene expression models have a long history, yet are widely regarded as analytically intractable
[
22
]
until our recent development of a new approach
[
13
]
. Therefore, approximation methods and numerical approaches are more commonly used.

The burst approximation is an approximation technique aiming to simplify the mathematical structure of complete gene expression models, which is, roughly speaking, based on the experimental conclusion that mRNAs have much shorter life periods than proteins in almost all cells
[
18
,
22
]
. In complete gene expression models
[
13
]
, both the numbers of mRNA molecules and protein molecules are tracked; while under the burst approximation, only the protein copy number needs to be considered, since mRNA molecules are assumed to hydrolyze instantly. In this sense, the burst approximation reduces the dimension of the corresponding stochastic process. Moreover, based on the equivalence of queueing systems and stochastic transcription models established in
[
23
]
, the burst approximation reduces the problem to the well-studied infinite-server queueing system with batch arrivals
[
16
]
. Therefore, both existing results and mathematical techniques from queueing theory can be introduced to analyze gene expression models.

Models under the burst approximation admit fruitful analytical results. The simplest cases where the gene remains active or switches between active and inactive states are studied in
[
22
]
, and analytical expressions are derived for the steady-state distribution of protein copy number. Recently, the standard binomial moment method has been employed to analyze models featuring multiple gene states
[
4
]
. In
[
4
]
, a recurrence relation among binomial moments is derived for general models with multiple gene states, while analytical expressions are obtained in special cases where only one gene state is active. Non-Markovian models also exist
[
1
]
, but only low order moments can be effectively analyzed.

Notably, our recent work has enabled direct analysis of general gene expression models without resorting to any approximation techniques
[
13
]
. In particular, our recent analysis
[
13
]
shows that, in some cases, the burst approximation is extremely inaccurate compared with complete gene expression models even if mRNAs have significantly shorter lifetimes than proteins. However, stochastic gene expression models under the burst approximation still possess independent theoretical value for both stochastic process and queueing theory.

In this article, we systematically analyze general gene expression models under the burst approximation using a novel approach, which is mathematically rigorous and reveals the dynamical behaviors of the system. We construct the time-dependent analytical solution to the chemical master equation and further calculate binomial moments based on the probability mass function. The steady-state binomial moments are found to have concise expressions. We then proceed from two different perspectives. Theoretically, we asymptotically analyze the analytical expressions using functional analysis, concluding that the steady-state distribution of protein copy number is always bounded from above by a negative binomial distribution if the burst size is geometrically distributed. Computationally, we exploit the recurrence relation among adjacent binomial moments and develop efficient algorithms under three circumstances. In the end, we connect our general results with existing literature by showing the consistency of our results with those in
[
22
]
. In principle, this article generalizes our previous work on transcription models
[
14
]
to gene expression models under the burst approximation, and takes a similar approach. However, additional and novel techniques are introduced to analyze the significantly more complicated results.

Model Description

In this article, we propose and analyze a general gene expression model under the burst approximation, represented as a reaction system (
1
). In this model, the gene admits
N

\mathcal{S}_{i}\;(1\leq i\leq N)

Within each metastable state, proteins are produced in bursts according to different rates, and the switch of the gene state is allowed upon protein production. Note that during one burst, multiple protein molecules can be generated simultaneously, and the exact number follows some probability distribution instead of being deterministic. Existing protein molecules undergo hydrolysis at a given rate, which is assumed to be independent of the gene state and the protein count. In this article, autoregulation of proteins on the expression is not considered for simplification. Following the stochastic description of chemical reaction kinetics
[
5
]

Therefore, an elegant expression for binomial moments exists in many cases

]

\displaystyle B_{m}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\

\displaystyle C_{r}=\frac{\lambda^{r-1}}{(1-\lambda)^{r+1}}D_{1}\in\mathbb{R^{N\times N}},\;\;r\geq 1.

If the burst size follows a Poisson distribution, namely,
D_{r}=(\alpha^{r-1}/r!)D_{1}\;(r\geq 1)
for some positive real number
\alpha
, we can also obtain the analytical expression for
\{C_{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
. To be specific,

C_{r}=\frac{\alpha^{r-1}}{r!}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha}D_{1},\;\;r\geq 1.

(14)

See the Appendix for detailed calculation. Note that,

\displaystyle D\equiv D_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}D_{r}=D_{0}+\frac{1}{\alpha}(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha}-1)D_{1}.

(15)

5.3
Burst Becomes Negligible for Large Size

During practical gene expression, chances are extremely low that a large number of proteins are simultaneously produced. Therefore, we may assume
D_{r}=\bm{0}_{N\times N}
for
r
strictly larger than a given threshold, denoted as
r_{\mathrm{max}}
. In such a case,
C_{r}=\bm{0}_{N\times N}
for
r\geq r_{\mathrm{max}}+1
, and computation of
C_{r}=\sum_{n=r}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\binom{n}{r}D_{n}\;(1\leq r\leq r_{\mathrm{max}})
only involves finite summations. This assumption is also a prerequisite for numerically studying models in (
2
) via the stochastic simulation algorithm (SSA)
[
7
,
6
]
, since SSA only admits a finite number of reaction pathways. In
Figure 3
, we verify our result using SSA. In the example shown in
Figure 4
, the truncation at relatively small
r_{\mathrm{max}}
already yields results that are highly accurate compared to the full (non-truncated) model.

A Special Case

In this section, we connect our general conclusion to the classic results established in
[
22
]
through studying the special case where
N=1
in (
1
) and the burst number of protein is geometrically distributed. This special case has been thoroughly analyzed in
[
22
]
, and we show the results therein are consistent with the general expression (
6
).

As in (
4
), we assume
\{D_{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
follows a geometric distribution with parameter
\lambda\in(0,1)
. We now also assume
N=1
in this section. Therefore
D_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
is a positive real number.

Based on (
5
), and (
6
), we prove that the steady-state distribution of protein count is actually a negative binomial distribution, namely,

\displaystyle P_{n}=\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\left[\frac{D_{1}}{\delta\lambda(1-\lambda)}\right]^{(n)}(1-\lambda)^{D_{1}/[\delta\lambda(1-\lambda)]},

(16)

consistent with the previous results
[
22
]
. See the Appendix for detailed calculation. We especially point out that (
10
) is also used in the proof, which overcomes the complexity bottleneck.

7
Discussion and Conclusion

In this article, we generalize our results in
[
14
]
to systematically analyze gene expression models under the burst approximation. Analytical solution to the corresponding chemical master equation is presented and further exploited in two different ways. Theoretically, we generalize the inequalities obtained in
[
14
]
, obtaining an analogue to the Heavy-Tailed Law originally proposed for transcription models. We find that the probability distribution of protein copy number is always bounded by a constant multiple of some negative binomial distribution if the burst size is geometrically distributed. Computationally, we propose fast solvers for the steady-state distribution of protein counts in three special cases, namely, where the burst size is geometrically distributed, Poisson-distributed, or becomes negligible after a certain threshold. Additionally, we show the consistency of our result with
[
22
]
, by studying the special case where
N=1
and the burst size follows a geometric distribution. As shown in
[
13
]
, although (
1
) is usually seen as the surrogate model to complete gene expression models, the approximation error may become unbounded. However, it actually remains unknown whether either model reasonably models the real physical process of gene expression. The validity of the model is usually evaluated following a top-down approach. For example, fitting the generated probability distribution to the experimental data can help determine the parameters in the model
[
11
,
15
,
20
]
. However, when the model becomes general or the number of parameters becomes arbitrarily large, this top-down approach may not be able to fully assess the reliability of the model since the model may adequately fit any given distribution that is compactly supported. Moreover, models formulated via chemical master equation implicitly assume the Markov property, but many physical systems are actually highly non-Markovian
[
26
]
. To fit the model to real gene expression process, a bottom-up approach using molecular dynamics simulation is theoretically feasible, although the current computational resources are still insufficient to support simulations of such a complex process. The output of molecular dynamics simulation can be connected to the chemical master equation through a discretization procedure like Markov State Model (MSM)
[
19
,
21
]
. To model such complex systems in practice, a more general theoretical framework may be required.

Appendix A
Analytical Solution to Chemical Master Equation

Converting (
2
) into matrix-form and using the standard generating function method, the original chemical master equation can be neatly expressed as the following partial differential equation system.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{G}(t,z)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t,z)D_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}z^{r}\mathcal{G}(t,z)D_{r}+\delta(1-z)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{G}(t,z).

(17)

Recall that the generating function
\mathcal{G}(t,z):=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{m}\mathbb{P}(m;t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.

We now demonstrate that (
3
) and (
4
) are exactly the unique solution to (
2
). Firstly, notice that the generating function of (
3
) and (
4
) has the following expression,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t,z)=\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t}+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{i})}\right]^{l_{i}}

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t_{1}}D_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}D_{l_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}D_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t-t_{k})}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.

We then prove that (
18
) is the unique solution to (
17
) under initial condition
\mathcal{G}(0,z)=\bm{I}_{N}
. The initial condition is obviously satisfied. The following subsections calculate
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{G}(t,z)
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{G}(t,z)
, respectively.

For convenience, let
\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)
denote the integrand in (
18
). Bold letter
\bm{t}
denotes the tuple
(t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{k})
; while bold letter
\bm{l}
denotes the tuple
(l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k})
. Namely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{i})}\right]^{l_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t_{1}}D_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}D_{l_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}D_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t-t_{k})}.

(19)

A.1
Calculation of
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{G}(t,z)

On the one hand, according to the expression in (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{G}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}\bm{t}

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}\bm{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{j})}l_{j}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{j})}\right]^{l_{j}-1}

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{i})}\right]^{l_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t_{1}}D_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}D_{l_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}D_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t-t_{k})}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.

A.2
Calculation of
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{G}(t,z)

On the other hand, calculating
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{G}(t,z)
is much more involved. Recall that integral domain
\Omega_{k}=\{(t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{k})\mid 0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\cdots\leq t_{k}\leq t\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
is related to
t
.
In the first place, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{G}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t}D_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.

(21)

Using the same techniques as those in
[
14
]
, we calculate
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}\bm{t}
as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}\bm{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\cdots\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}t_{k}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{2}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\right]

(22)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\cdots\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}t_{k}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t_{1}

\displaystyle+\left[\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\dots\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}t_{k}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{2}\right]\bigg|_{t_{1}=t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\cdots\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}t_{k}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t_{1}

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\cdots\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)dt_{k}\right]\mathrm{d}t_{k-1}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}\bm{t}+\int_{\Omega_{k-1}}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\bigg|_{t_{k}=t}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.

Plugging in the exact expression of
\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(1-z)\delta\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{j})}l_{j}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{j})}\right]^{l_{j}-1}

(23)

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{i})}\right]^{l_{i}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t_{1}}D_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}D_{l_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}D_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t-t_{k})}

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{i})}\right]^{l_{i}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t_{1}}D_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}D_{l_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}D_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t-t_{k})}D_{0}.

Therefore, we have established

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t}D_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\mathrm{d}\bm{t}=\mathcal{G}(z,t)D_{0}+\delta(1-z)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{G}(z,t).

(24)

In addition,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{k-1}}\mathcal{F}(t,k;\bm{t},\bm{l};z)\bigg|_{t_{k}=t}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{k-1}}z^{l_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left[1+(z-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\delta(t-t_{i})}\right]^{l_{i}}

(25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t_{1}}D_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}D_{l_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}(t-t_{k-1})}D_{l_{k}}\mathrm{d}\bm{t},k\geq 2.

When
k=1
, special attention is needed and (
25
) degenerates into
z^{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D_{0}t}D_{l_{1}}
.
Hence, in general,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}\int_{\Omega_{k-1}}\mathcal{F}(t,k,\bm{l},\bm{t},z)\bigg|_{t_{k}=t}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}z^{r}\mathcal{G}(z,t)D_{r}.

(26)

Combining (
20
) and (
26
), we have proven that the generating function given by (
18
) satisfies the partial differential equation system (
17
), thus concluding our proof.

Appendix B
Derivation of Steady-State Binomial Moments (
6
)

The matrix-form
m
-th order binomial moment
\mathcal{B}(m,t)
is defined as the
m
-th coefficient of the Taylor expansion of
\mathcal{G}(t,z)
at
z=1
(should it exists). Let
w:=z-1
, according to (
17
),
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w):=\mathcal{G}(t,w+1)
is the unique solution (with initial condition
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(0,w)=\bm{I}_{N}
) to the partial differential equation system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w)=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w)D+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}[(w+1)^{r}-1]\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w)D_{r}-w\delta\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w).

(27)

Similar to
Appendix A
, we can prove that
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w)
has explicit expression as stated below.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{Dt}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}w^{\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}(t-t_{j})}

(28)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{Dt_{1}}C_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D(t_{2}-t_{1})}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}C_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{D(t-t_{k})}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.

Perform the change of variables
x_{i}=t-t_{k+1-i}\;(i=1,2,\cdots,k)
, (
28
) is arranged as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(t,w)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{Dt}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1}w^{\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}x_{k+1-j}}

(29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{D(t-x_{k})}C_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{k}-x_{k-1})}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{2}-x_{1})}C_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{Dx_{1}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

Since (
29
) naturally takes the form of a power series, we assemble the terms according to the power of
w
, obtaining the analytical expression for binomial moments (
m\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(m,t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\
l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}x_{k+1-j}}\mathrm{e}^{D(t-x_{k})}C_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{k}-x_{k-1})}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{2}-x_{1})}C_{l_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{Dx_{1}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(30)

In particular,
\mathcal{B}(0,t)=\mathrm{e}^{Dt}
.

Recall from the main text that coarse-grained binomial moments of protein copy number are defined as
B_{m}(t):=\bm{\pi}^{\top}\mathcal{B}_{m}(t)\bm{1},\;m\in\mathbb{N}
, where the invariant distribution
\bm{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
is determined by
\bm{\pi}^{\top}D=\bm{0}_{1\times N}
and
\bm{\pi}^{\top}\bm{1}=1
. Assume
D
is irreducible, then
\bm{\pi}
is unique. By definition,

\displaystyle B_{m}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}x_{k+1-j}}\bm{\pi}^{\top}C_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{k}-x_{k-1})}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{2}-x_{1})}C_{l_{k}}\bm{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(31)

To calculate steady-state binomial moments (should they converge)
B_{m}:=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}B_{m}(t)
, we first recall some important results from matrix analysis. Lévy-Desplanques theorem states that any strictly diagonally dominant matrix is nonsingular, which in particular applies to
k\delta\bm{I}_{N}-D
when
\delta>0,k\geq 1
and
D
is a
Q
-matrix. Also, it follows from Geršgorin disc theorem that all eigenvalues of a strictly diagonally dominant matrix with negative diagonal entries have strictly negative real part. Furthermore, given any matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
whose eigenvalues all have strictly negative real part, we have
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}e^{Xt}=\bm{0}_{N\times N}
. This can be readily proven by using the Jordan canonical form (see Chapter
9
in
[
8
]
). In particular, we have

\displaystyle\int_{x_{i-1}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-k\delta x_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{i}-x_{i-1})}\mathrm{d}x_{i}

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{x_{i-1}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{(D-k\delta\bm{I}_{N})x_{i}-Dx_{i-1}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(D-k\delta\bm{I}_{N}\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{(D-k\delta\bm{I}_{N})x_{i}}\bigg|_{x_{i}=x_{i-1}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-Dx_{i-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(k\delta\bm{I}_{N}-D\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{(D-k\delta\bm{I}_{N})x_{i-1}}\mathrm{e}^{-Dx_{i-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(k\delta\bm{I}_{N}-D\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-k\delta x_{i-1}}.

According to (
31
), we have (
m\geq 1
)

\displaystyle B_{m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}B_{m}(t)

(33)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{x_{k}}\cdots\int_{0}^{x_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}x_{k+1-j}}

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}^{\top}C_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{k}-x_{k-1})}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{2}-x_{1})}C_{l_{k}}\bm{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k-1}\mathrm{d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\sum_{j=1}^{k}l_{j}x_{k+1-j}}

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}^{\top}C_{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{k}-x_{k-1})}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{2}-x_{1})}C_{l_{k}}\bm{1}\mathrm{d}x_{k}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{x_{k-2}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\sum_{j=2}^{k}l_{j}x_{k+1-j}}\bm{\pi}^{\top}C_{l_{1}}\left(l_{1}\delta\bm{I}_{N}-D\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-l_{1}\delta x_{k-1}}C_{l_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{k-1}-x_{k-2})}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{D(x_{2}-x_{1})}C_{l_{k}}\bm{1}\mathrm{d}x_{k-1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m\delta}\bm{\pi}^{\top}C_{l_{1}}\left(l_{1}\delta\bm{I}_{N}-D\right)^{-1}C_{l_{2}}\left[(l_{1}+l_{2})\delta\bm{I}_{N}-D\right]^{-1}C_{l_{3}}

\displaystyle\cdots\left[(l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{k-1})\delta\bm{I}_{N}-D\right]^{-1}C_{l_{k}}\bm{1}.

Up to this point, (
6
) has been established.

Appendix F
Proof of Combinatorial Identity (

)

LEMMA
.

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=m\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\zeta^{k}}{l_{1}(l_{1}+l_{2})\cdots(l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{k})}=\frac{\zeta^{(m)}}{m!},

where
\zeta
is any given real number and
m\geq 1
.

We prove (
10
) by induction. When
m=1,2
, the above equality can be verified by hand. Assume now that (

) holds for any
m\leq M
where
M
is a given integer. When
m=M+1
, we organize the terms within the summation according to the value of
l_{k}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=M+1\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\zeta^{k}}{l_{1}(l_{1}+l_{2})\cdots(l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{k})}

(43)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta}{M+1}+\sum_{l=1}^{M}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}=M+1-l\\
l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\zeta}{M+1}\frac{\zeta^{k}}{l_{1}(l_{1}+l_{2})\cdots(l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{k})}

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta^{(M+1-l)}}{(M+1-l)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\zeta+1)^{(M+1-l)}}{(M+1-l)!}-\frac{(M+1-l)(\zeta+1)^{(M-l)}}{(M+1-l)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\zeta+1)^{(M+1-l)}}{(M+1-l)!}-\frac{(\zeta+1)^{(M-l)}}{(M-l)!}.

Sum up (

to
M

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{M}\frac{\zeta^{(M+1-l)}}{(M+1-l)!}=\frac{(\zeta+1)^{(M-1)}}{M!}-1.

(45)

Substituting (

) is proved.
