Waldenstrom’s macroglobulinemia in siblings: a single institutional experience with 10 cases at 25 years and a review of the literature
Authors: Lingling Wang, Yongfen Huang, Hui Chen, Jianming Dong, Hao Xu, Yuqing Miao, Yuexin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWaldenstrom’s macroglobulinemia (WM) is characterized by familial aggregation. The study displayed the characteristics and outcomes of patients with WM in siblings. Ten cases (five pairs) were recognized as WM in siblings. The earliest pair (cases 3 and 4) was identified in 2002 and 2005, respectively, while the recent pair (cases 7 and 8) was confirmed in 2022. Males accounted for 90%. The median age at diagnosis was 65.5 years (range, 51–71 years). Rituximab-based immunochemotherapy was initiated in three patients, whereas regimens based on nucleoside analogs and alkylating drugs were initiated in five patients. As of December 2024, three patients died from severe infection, while one died from traumatic injury. Our investigation discovered no significant difference between WM in siblings and sporadic WM in terms of gender, age, ECOG score, B symptoms, with or without BTKi exposure, IPSS-WM score, Hb, PLT, LDH, β2-MG, IgM, albumin, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and MYD88L265P. Median overall survival was 230 months for patients with familial WM among siblings and 96 months for those with sporadic WM (p = 0.2, HR: 0.6, 95% CIs: 0.2-1). This study provided an overview of the screening initiatives for WM in siblings in the hospital population and follow-up results for five pairs of WM in siblings. Introduction

Waldenstrom’s macroglobulinemia (WM) is a rare B-cell lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma characterized by a slow disease progression. Most patients develop progressive anemia accompanied by mild lymphadenopathy, and sometimes symptoms associated with hyperviscosity. Complications of WM include hyperviscosity syndrome, which can induce fatigue, dizziness, and blurred vision, as well as mucosal hemorrhage, altered mental status, and perhaps coma [1]. Additionally, problems like cryoglobulinemia, which can result in amyloidosis, peripheral neuropathy, etc., are possible [2]. However, WM remains incurable, and treatment-related toxicity and drug resistance may arise [3]. Prior research has indicated that prompt identification and diagnosis of WM may improve patient outcomes, and population-based WM screening may be able to detect the development of the disease before complications develop [4–6]. Several studies have revealed familial clustering of WM or other B-lymphocyte proliferative diseases (B-LPD), including non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), multiple myeloma (MM), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance (MGUS), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), and Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), among first-degree relatives of WM patients [7–11]. Environmental and genetic susceptibility genes may play crucial roles in familial WM. However, evidence on the impact of screening WM in siblings is still limited, especially in the Chinese population. Research on the predisposition of WM in families and the necessity of screening family members is limited. MGUS is a precancerous clonal plasma cell or lymphoplasmacytic proliferative disorder characterized by the presence of monoclonal immunoglobulins and/or an abnormal proportion of free immunoglobulin light chains in the blood and/or urine [12]. IgM-MGUS is more common in the population and can progress to WM [13]. The clinical follow-up of detected MGUS by serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) as a screening method may be able to detect WM in siblings earlier.

The SPEP-based screening system among siblings of confirmed WM has been performed in the hospital population at The First People’s Hospital of Yancheng. This study presents 25-year follow-up findings of five pairs of WM in siblings in our center. This is the first longitudinal, real-world report of an active screening program for WM in siblings within a Chinese hospital setting.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study investigated WM in siblings who were treated at The First People’s Hospital of Yancheng between February 2000 and November 2024. We examined patients’ medical records by reviewing both outpatient and inpatient records. To find pertinent patients, the terms “M protein”, “WM”, and “MGUS” were chosen as keywords. The SPEP-based screening system among siblings of confirmed WM has been performed in the hospital population at The First People’s Hospital of Yancheng. Every patient with a diagnosis of WM or IgM-MGUS was registered at our facility, together with the names, ages, genders, and identification numbers of their siblings. At least one SPEP test was administered to these siblings each year. When an increased monoclonal IgM was identified, they were screened for MGUS or WM. Patients of WM in siblings were followed up every 3 months. Figure 1 illustrates the screening process. The diagnosis of WM is confirmed by the presence of IgM monoclonal protein, clonal lymphoplasmacytic cells in bone marrow as well as immunophenotyping which suggests CD19(+), CD20(+), sIgM(+), CD5(-), CD10(-), CD22(+), CD23(-), CD25(+), CD27(+), FMC7(+), CD38 and/or CD138(+), and CD103(-). MYD88L265P is not essential for diagnosis. IgM-MGUS must meet the following requirements: clonal lymphoplasmacytic cells in bone marrow are less than 10%, IgM monoclonal protein is less than 30 g/L, and there are no indications of organ damage, such as anemia, destructive bone lesions, hypercalcemia, or kidney injury. The trial was conducted according to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. The Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yancheng approved this study (approval number: 2023-J-022). All patients provided written informed consent. Follow-up data were obtained by reviewing outpatient and inpatient medical records, supplemented by telephone follow-ups for all patients until December 27, 2024. Baseline clinical data, including gender, age at diagnosis, year at diagnosis, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), IgM, leukocytes, hemoglobin, platelets, β2-MG, serum albumin, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score, B symptoms, IPSS-WM score, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, treatments, complications, modifications in treatment patterns, survival outcomes, and best response (complete remission [CR], very good partial remission [VGPR], partial remission [PR], stable disease [SD], or progressive disease [PD]), were collected from all patients.

Paradigm of screening system for WM in siblings

Treatment and assessments

Asymptomatic WM patients went into the wait-and-watch stage. Patients with the following therapeutic indications required treatment, including B symptoms, symptomatic hyperviscosity, amyloidosis, cold agglutinin disease, cryoglobulinemia, disease-associated hemocytopenia, peripheral neuropathy, organomegaly, and extramedullary lesions, particularly those affecting the CNS (Bing-Neel syndrome). Massive lymphadenopathy and changes in the disease are also included. Elevated serum IgM levels alone do not indicate the need for therapy. Conventional chemotherapy, which was based on alkylating drugs and nucleoside analogs, included CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone), FC (fludarabine, cyclophosphamide), TD (thalidomide and prednisone), fludarabine, and bendamustine. The novel treatment based on proteasome inhibitors, and CD20 monoclonal antibodies, including R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone), FR (fludarabine and rituximab), DRC (dexamethasone, rituximab, and cyclophosphamide), BR (bendamustine, rituximab), BCR (bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab), and BDR (bortezomib, dexamethasone, and rituximab). Patients recently diagnosed were treated with BTKi as the primary treatment. Disease progression was defined using the efficacy assessment criteria developed by the 11th International Workshop on WM (IWWM-11).

Statistical analysis

The patients’ characteristics were compiled using descriptive statistical methods. SPSS version 26 was utilized to perform statistical analysis. Medians were computed to provide statistical data. Overall survival (OS) time was defined as the time from patient initiation of first-line therapy to death or final follow-up. Multivariate analysis was done using Cox’s proportional hazards model. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered a significant difference.

Results

General information

Between February 2000 and November 2024, 125 patients with WM and 37 patients with IgM-MGUS were identified at The First People’s Hospital of Yancheng. According to our findings, 75.2% (n = 94) of those with WM were diagnosed after 2017 (Fig. 2). Three hundred and forty-five of these patients’ siblings participated in the screening. By verifying identity card number, gender, age, and name, ten cases (five pairs) were recognized as WM in siblings (WM, n = 9; IgM-MGUS, n = 1). The earliest pair (cases 3 and 4) was identified in 2002 and 2005, respectively, while the most recent pair (cases 7 and 8) was confirmed in 2022.

Baseline characteristics

Proband and whole-exome sequencing (WES)

In the first pair, the proband was the older brother (case 1), who was diagnosed with WM in March 2009 at the age of 65. The primary symptom was fatigue. In May 2017, the younger brother (case 2) was diagnosed with WM. He was 70 years old at the time of the diagnosis and had a fever and severe anemia. Case 3 was the proband in the second pair, who were also brothers. He was 59 years old when diagnosed with WM in August 2002, and his primary symptoms were fatigue and dizziness. His 51-year-old younger brother (case 4) was diagnosed with WM in March 2005 after exhibiting similar symptoms. The third pair were identical twins, with case 5 being the proband, who was diagnosed in June 2018 with fatigue and dizziness as the first symptoms, and laboratory tests revealed mild anemia. His identical twin brother (case 6) received SPEP tests immediately. In line with our suspicions, case 6 was diagnosed with IgM-MGUS at the same time. WES tests of the twins revealed ten uncommon, explicit non-synonymous mononucleotide variants (NEUROG1, FHL2, BRWD1, MICALL1, NR1H3, LARGE1, PCDHA5, CHST4, ZMAT5, and DOCK2). The M-CAP score was used to predict the pathogenicity of these mutations, with mutations that were common in twins and were more harmful in descending order of value given priority. FHL2g226a was shown to be the most important mutation for familial WM, while ZMAT5 and FHL2 were found to be novel mutations that had not been previously documented. The twins were meticulously observed, and it’s interesting to find that after 44 months of follow-up, case 6 advanced to WM. In pair 4, the younger brother (case 7), the proband, was treated in the respiratory department in June 2022 for exhaustion, dizziness, and recurring massive pleural effusions, and was subsequently referred to the hematology department due to increased monoclonal IgM levels. He was diagnosed with WM. Three months after the initial symptom of edema in both lower limbs, his sister (case 8) was ultimately diagnosed with WM. Additionally, WES testing on the two patients revealed 15 shared mutant genes, including AXL, DNMT3B, SCRIB, ANK2, CHD6, CMYA5, DNAH12, HERC2, HIF3A, KDM5A, NSD2, PARP2, RYR2, TLR2, and UTRN. Case 8 also detected a TP53 mutation. Pair 5 were brothers. Case 9 was the proband, who was diagnosed with IgM-MGUS in July 2017 and advanced to WM in July 2020. Because of frequent skin rashes, his brother was also diagnosed with IgM-MGUS in March 2017. As of December 2024, he was still in the MGUS stage and had not progressed to WM (Supplemental Table 1).

Disease courses and treatment outcomes

Five patients (cases 1, 2, 3, 4, 10) were started on regimens based on nucleoside analogs and alkylating agents. In cases 5, 7, 8, and 9, rituximab-based immunochemotherapy was administered. Bortezomib-based therapy was conducted for case 5. Zanubrutinib was administered orally to case 6. Regarding the initial treatment, patients who received traditional chemotherapy had up to five lines of treatment and endured frequent relapses during the disease course. Rituximab-based and/or bortezomib-based therapy were the most commonly used therapy regimens following relapse. Three (cases 1, 3, 4) of these patients achieved the best treatment response of PR, while patients (cases 5, 6, 7, 8, 9) who began with a regimen based on CD20 monoclonal antibody and targeted therapies had a lower proportion of relapse, and all attained PR or VGPR. As of December 2024, three patients (cases 1, 2, 3) died from severe infection, while case 8 died from traumatic injury (Supplemental Table 2).

Clinical characteristics of WM in siblings and sporadic WM

This study included 115 patients with sporadic WM and 10 patients with WM in siblings between February 2000 and November 2024. Median overall survival was 230 months for patients with familial WM among siblings and 96 months for those with sporadic WM (p = 0.2, HR: 0.6, 95% CIs: 0.2-1) (Fig. 3). Subgroup analysis showed no significant difference between groups of WM in siblings and sporadic WM in terms of gender, age, ECOG score, B symptoms, with or without BTKi exposure, IPSS-WM score, Hb, PLT, LDH, β2-MG, IgM, albumin, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and MYD88L265P (Fig. 4).

Discussion

WM is a rare and indolent B-cell lymphoma primarily observed in the elderly that causes multisystem damage as the disease progresses. 4.3% of patients with WM had a family history of lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma or WM, and that family history is associated with a worse prognosis than the familial form [1]. Treon SP and Steingrímsson V, et al. found that patients with a family history of WM had a worse prognosis and were more likely to experience disease progression [14, 15]. However, familial WM is very rare. The earliest description of familial WM dates back to 1962, when Massari et al. reported a pair of siblings with WM [16]. The description of familial WM in siblings has been mainly in the form of case reports (Table 2) [8, 16–29].

We anticipated that early identification of WM may improve survival and reduce complications. It may be feasible to identify the development of WM before complications appear by performing a population-based screening for the disease. Screening for WM/MGUS in siblings of patients diagnosed with WM may be crucial. For the following grounds, we selected siblings of WM patients to participate in the screening program. One is that the majority of these patients’ parents had already died and were too elderly to be routinely admitted to the hospital for SPEP testing. Second, the children of these patients were young and not the main group of affected individuals, and long-term surveillance of these individuals was time-consuming and expensive. Finally, siblings with WM had a short age gap and had been raised in comparable circumstances throughout childhood. There is currently little data on how screening programs affect patients, though. To investigate the differences in clinical characteristics between WM in siblings and sporadic cases, as well as the evolution of treatment patterns in the age of novel drugs, our study gathered 25 years of real-world data from our institute. The SPEP screening for siblings of confirmed WM is uncomplicated due to the low incidence of WM. We meticulously registered these cases and, as soon as potential cases were identified, we started the IgM-MGUS/WM screening process. For cases with globulin levels exceeding 30 g/L, the laboratory would give an early warning and recommend further refinement of immunoglobulin classification and quantitative testing. If the tests indicated an increased monoclonal IgM, they would be referred to the hematology department. The electronic medical record system of our hospital is highly convenient since each patient’s inpatient and outpatient information is connected to his or her identity card number, and it is feasible to review the patient’s record of visits to any hospital in Jiangsu Province. We were able to get streamlined follow-up data thanks to the system. It should be mentioned that there were only 10 patients of WM in siblings within twenty-five years in our study. Due to selection bias, the true incidence may be controversial, given that the study was conducted in a single institute and a hospital population. According to our investigation conducted at several hospitals in Jiangsu province, few centers screened and followed up with the siblings of WM, yet barely conducted SPEP in the siblings of confirmed WM. Consequently, the prevalence of WM in siblings in the real world may be underestimated. Given the convenience and mature technology, SPEP testing may be able to raise the detection rate in this specific population.

In our study, subgroup analysis showed no significant difference between groups of WM in siblings and sporadic WM in terms of gender, age, ECOG score, B symptoms, with or without BTKi exposure, IPSS-WM score, Hb, PLT, LDH, β2-MG, IgM, albumin, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and MYD88L265P. The OS between the two groups was not significantly different (230 months vs. 96 months, p = 0.2, HR: 0.6, 95% CIs: 0.2-1). Groves FD et al. discovered that individuals with familial WM were 10 years younger than those with sporadic cases and tended to affect men more frequently [10, 30]. Recent case reports indicate that IgM values seem to be higher in familial WM cases, yet there were no obvious differences in age, gender, the existence of family history, clinical presentation, laboratory tests, or genetics [9, 10, 21, 26, 31, 32].

In this study, Cases 5 and 6 are identical twins, and our previous study found that FHL2g226a is likely to be a mutation associated with familial WM. Some studies have suggested that the shared exposure of family members to the living environment may be a potential susceptibility factor [21, 24, 25, 33, 34]. On the other hand, WM had been described in several generations [8, 18, 19, 25, 35–37]. It is yet unknown if the living environment is linked to the development of familial WM. In a subset of families with WM, a paraprotein termed hyperphosphorylated paratarg-y (pP-7) was discovered to be dominantly inherited as a somatic mutation [38–41]. Aldo M. Roccaro et al. performed WES of germline DNA samples from three cases of familial WM and 246 patients with non-familial WM. They screened LAPTM5c403t and HCLS1g496a as candidate genes and hypothesized that these two genes might be the most pertinent targets for familial WM [42].

Almost all patients with WM experienced an IgM-MGUS stage before diagnosis. The clonal B cells in MGUS already exhibit molecular markers of malignant clones. It has been identified as an independent risk factor for the development of WM, with an annual rate of progression to WM of roughly 1.5% [5, 43]. Some researchers consider that there is little clinical benefit of screening the population for IgM-MGUS because familial WM is rare, and many WM patients who meet the diagnostic criteria do not need treatment immediately if they are asymptomatic. In fact, because of the indolent nature of the disease and the advanced age of diagnosis, some cases of WM in siblings likely remain ignored. The prevalence of WM in siblings in the real world may be underestimated.

To the best of our knowledge, it is the first longitudinal, real-world report of an active screening program for WM in siblings within a Chinese hospital setting. We compiled the features of WM in siblings based on a single-center in-hospital screening of siblings of patients with WM using the SPEP test. However, there may be some limitations. This retrospective study was conducted at a single center, which may limit its applicability to other settings. Besides, only siblings of patients with WM were investigated, while siblings of patients with MGUS were excluded from the study; selection bias may also have an impact on our findings. The true incidence rate needs to be confirmed in multi-center and larger population samples in the future. These 10 patients were diagnosed based on symptoms rather than through screening. Although we have started a screening program for siblings of patients who have been diagnosed with WM, no new cases of WM in siblings have been found thus far. Furthermore, only four of the 10 patients underwent WES testing, while the other six patients failed to undergo WES testing due to financial reasons and the inaccessibility of testing in earlier years.

Conclusions

This study presented the follow-up results for 10 patients with WM in siblings and 115 patients with sporadic WM. The incidence of WM in siblings in the real world may be underestimated.
