Investigating the factors related to the brain executive function change during pregnancy
Authors: Saeideh Iranshahi, Maliheh Arab, Fariba Arabgol, Reza Shekarriz-Foumani, Nasim Nouri, Behnaz Ghavami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSome studies indicate cognitive defects during pregnancy, but the factors affecting these defects have not been addressed. Therefore, the present study aims to determine the impressive factors in executive function during pregnancy.Materials and methodsThis study was conducted on pregnant women referred to Imam Hossein and Taleghani Hospital in Tehran between October 2019 and October 2021. Eligible people completed the Brain Executive Function Questionnaire of the adult self-report form at the following three points of follow-up: the first trimester of pregnancy, the third trimester of pregnancy, and postpartum. The executive performance scores of the studied subjects and the influencing factors were reported.ResultsIn this study, 210 pregnant women with a mean age of 28.70 ± 7.08 years filled out the questionnaire three times. The Global Executive Composite (GEC) of the study subjects in the third trimester of pregnancy was significantly higher than in the first trimester of pregnancy and after delivery (a higher score indicates a greater impairment of the brain’s executive function), which indicates lower executive function during the third trimester. The GEC of the study subjects after delivery was significantly higher than in the first trimester of pregnancy. The level of education of the subjects was the covariate of the changes in the study subjects’ executive performance.ConclusionScore of the postpartum period in comparison to the first trimester was significantly decreased, yet. Also, the changes in the executive function of the brain during pregnancy were influenced by the level of education, including better function and lower decline in the high-educated group.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Pregnancy is associated with significant neuropsychological changes, particularly in cognitive domains such as memory, attention, and executive function. A growing body of evidence suggests that some women experience a decline in cognitive performance during pregnancy, especially in tasks requiring working memory and complex attention [1–7].

Individual studies of memory performance decline and cognitive issues during pregnancy have been published in various articles. In a survey conducted at Sunderland Royal Hospital, UK, in January 2008, pregnant women reported a decline in memory, attention skills, and processing speed, particularly in the third trimester [8].

In another report from the University of British Columbia in 2011, an attempt has been made to examine the evidence indicating or refuting memory loss in pregnant women, because these changes are felt in the public belief of society as well as the pregnant women themselves. They found both subjective complaints and objective evidence of reduced cognitive performance, though the extent and persistence of these changes varied [9].

Studies mainly in the fields of Neuroscience, Basic science, and Psychology have addressed this issue [10–17]. For instance, Hoekzema et al. (2017) showed a reduction in gray matter volume in the medial prefrontal cortex, which was correlated with maternal attachment and persisted for at least two years postpartum [18]. In the field of midwifery, investigations have also been carried out and in a review article published in March 2018, information from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology and Fertility of Harvard Medical School in Boston and Ohio University in Columbus has been collected in this matter, concluding that cognitive changes in pregnancy and its effect on daily life are on debate [15]. Other studies have documented impaired performance in executive tasks such as planning, verbal fluency, and response inhibition during late pregnancy, with partial or complete recovery in the postpartum period [19–22].

Therefore, due to the controversial data, this study aims to determine the factors related to the change in the executive function of the brain during pregnancy.

In this study, the executive function of the brain includes behavioral regulations and metacognitive issues, which are measured using the executive function of the brain self-report form for adults. Related factors, including age, gestational age, level of education, and body mass index, are measured as mentioned below.

This study aims to determine the changes in the global executive composite of the brain (GEC) in pregnancy and according to age, trimester of pregnancy, level of education, and body mass index. The results of this study might finally lead to more family and social support for pregnant women, with more attention and understanding of the physiological changes of brain executive function in pregnancy.

Materials and methods

This study was a prospective cohort study including pregnant women referred to Imam Hossein and Taleghani Hospitals in Tehran between October 2019 and October 2021. A total of 210 pregnant women in their first trimester were recruited using convenience sampling after confirming pregnancy via β-hCG testing.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Pregnant women who referred to the prenatal care (PNC) clinics of Imam Hossein and Taleghani Hospitals in Tehran during their first trimester of pregnancy (confirmed by a positive β-hCG test) were considered eligible for this study. Participants were required to be literate (able to read and write) and to have no known comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or systemic illnesses, including lupus and epilepsy. Women with a history of assisted reproductive techniques, such as in vitro fertilization (IVF), and those with any form of addiction to smoking, alcohol, or psychotropic substances were excluded at the time of enrollment.

Further exclusion criteria included the development of severe hyperemesis gravidarum requiring hospitalization, pregnancies resulting from assisted reproductive technologies (ART), and known psychiatric illnesses. During the follow-up period, any participants who developed severe preeclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) requiring hospitalization and insulin therapy, or experienced spontaneous abortion were excluded from the final analysis. Additionally, participants who were lost to follow-up despite multiple phone contact attempts were also excluded from the study. These criteria were implemented to minimize confounding variables that could independently influence cognitive or executive functioning.

Sampling method, sample size, and how to calculate it

In this study, a convenience sampling method was employed to recruit pregnant women who were referred to the prenatal care clinics of Imam Hossein and Taleghani Hospitals in Tehran. Based on data extracted from previous studies and statistical parameters (power = 0.80 and type I error = 0.05), the required sample size was calculated to be 210 participants, accounting for an expected 20% attrition rate.

During the recruitment period, 245 pregnant women were initially approached. Among them, 15 were excluded based on the study’s predefined eligibility criteria (such as history of chronic diseases, assisted reproductive techniques, or substance use), and 20 women declined participation after receiving detailed information about the study. As a result, 210 eligible and consenting participants in their first trimester of pregnancy were enrolled.

Throughout the study, some participants were lost to follow-up or excluded due to the development of pregnancy-related complications, including severe preeclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus requiring hospitalization and insulin treatment, and spontaneous abortion. However, ongoing recruitment continued throughout the data collection period to maintain the sample size, and the final number of participants who completed all three study phases (first trimester, third trimester, and postpartum) remained at 210.

Method

In this study, the changes in the executive function of the brain in 210 pregnant women were measured. Each of the samples was followed up three times, including the first trimester of pregnancy, the third trimester of pregnancy, and the postpartum period (the 42nd day to 2 years after delivery). The last visit was not conducted within 42 days after the delivery. After giving complete explanations about the study to the subjects, written informed consent was obtained. Basic data of the study population were recorded in checklists, including data such as age, level of education, gestational age, height, and weight. The tool used in the research was a self-report questionnaire that was completed three times, as mentioned above.

Study tool

The main international questionnaire used in this study was the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function-Adult Version (BRIEF-A), which has been translated and localized under the name of the Brain Executive Function Questionnaire, a self-report form for adults. The BRIEF is a daily executive functioning test developed by Juvia in 2000 for parents and teachers of elementary school-aged children, preschoolers, and then adults. All 75 questions of the adult questionnaire have the same score. The scoring of the questions was based on a three-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 3 (often). The adult questionnaire has three main indexes: the List of Behavioral Regulation Inventory (BRI) which measures the ability to control behaviors and impulses; the metacognitive index (MI) which measures functions related to maintaining attention and working memory and organization; the Global executive composite of the brain (GEC) that shows the overall level of executive functioning. BRI includes four scales (inhibition, change, emotional control, and self-monitoring), and MI includes five scales (initiation, working memory, planning/organizing, monitoring of tasks, and material organization) [23]. Table 1 shows the questions related to BRI and MI and the related scales. To evaluate the validity of completion of the questionnaire completed by each person three indicators of negativity, infrequency, and inconsistency were used. To analyze BRIEF-A, the T Score (M = 50 and SD = 10) was used. These scores are changes in raw scale scores. The Persian version of the questionnaire was evaluated by Mohammadnia et al. in 2020, and it had acceptable validity and reliability [24], showing Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.92 for the GEC, 0.88 for the BRI, and 0.90 for the MI. In our study population, internal consistency was also assessed, and Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.89 for the GEC, 0.86 for the BRI, and 0.87 for the MI at baseline, indicating good reliability of the instrument in pregnant women.

SPSS/23 statistical software was used for data analysis. Mean, standard deviation, frequency, percentage, mean difference, and 95% confidence interval were used to describe the data. The paired t-test was used to compare the scores of the questionnaire at different time points. Analysis of variance with repeated measurements and follow-up tests was used to investigate the time trend of changes in executive function and the effect of various factors on it. Multivariate linear regression was also used to determine the predictor variables of executive performance. All tests were performed at a significance level of 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Ethical code: IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1401.495). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 2000 Declaration of Helsinki. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study, with all data accessible only to the research team and reported in aggregate form. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after providing adequate information regarding the study’s aims and procedures.

Result

In this study, 210 pregnant women answered the questions of the questionnaire questions on three occasions, and according to the acceptable validity scores (infrequency, negativity, and inconsistency), they entered the final analysis. The mean age of the studied subjects was 28.70 ± 7.08 years (range = 18–45). Among the study population, 113 subjects (53.8%) had a normal body mass index (BMI < 25 kg/m2), 66 cases were overweight (BMI 25–30 kg/m2) (31.4%) and other 31 subjects were obese (BMI > 30 kg/m2) (14.8%).

Most of the studied cases had primary education (26.7%) or middle-level education (31.4%). Other people had a diploma or post-diploma (26.2%), bachelor’s degree (12.9%), or master’s degree and above (2.9%), respectively.

Predictors of executive function

To assess potential predictors of executive function, a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was performed, including age, BMI, and level of education as independent variables. As shown in Table 3, educational level was the only significant predictor, with higher education associated with better executive functioning (F = 7.404, p = 0.007, ŋ² = 0.035). Age and BMI were not significantly associated with changes in executive function (F = 2.713, p = 0.101, ŋ² = 0.013 and F = 0.010, p = 0.921, ŋ² < 0.001, respectively).

Discussion

According to the findings of the present study, executive function significantly declines in the third trimester of pregnancy and improves in the postpartum period, although it is different from the first trimester, yet. In 2021, Barda et al. conducted a prospective cohort study on 80 women between 20 and 40 years of age with the same race and language (40 pregnant and 40 non-pregnant). The findings showed that during pregnancy, learning, memory, attention, and language skills are impaired. These cognitive changes were found to be unrelated to age or educational level [4]. The above changes can be attributed to the effect of sex hormones and corticosteroids secreted from the adrenal gland.

In 2019, a systematic review was conducted in Ontario, Canada by Ouellette and his colleagues (reviewing related articles since 1990), A small but consistent effect of pregnancy on working memory was found, and no effect on conceptual memory was shown [16]. The above-mentioned decrease was probably due to simultaneous depression. Probably, memory loss in such a critical period of a woman’s life is due to lifestyle changes in addition to hormonal changes. During pregnancy, women have a lot of worries about childbirth, the postpartum period, coping strategies for motherhood, and changes in sleep patterns, all of which may lead to anxiety or depression. One study reported decreased memory performance in a specific subset of pregnant women who showed symptoms of pregnancy-related depression.

The current study was conducted following the observation of changes in the behavior of pregnant women at the end of pregnancy compared to the beginning of pregnancy, and due to the inadequacy of previous studies. This study, including 210 subjects, harbored a larger sample size in comparison to previous studies. In addition, each pregnant woman was evaluated 3 times, instead of in comparison to the non-pregnant control group, which can decrease the confounding of the results and add to the validity.

According to the investigations that have been done so far, the role of the change in the level of hormones (especially estrogen) that occurs in pregnancy seems to be strong on the changes in brain function, therefore, in a group of studies, an attempt has been made to measure the role of the change in the level of hormones on the structure of the brain by MRI. Several neuroimaging studies have evaluated the hormonal effects on the brain during pregnancy. Hoekzema et al. (2017) conducted a longitudinal MRI study and demonstrated significant gray matter volume reductions in regions associated with social cognition, particularly in the anterior and posterior cortical midline and bilateral lateral prefrontal and temporal cortex. These changes persisted for at least two years after birth and were proposed to reflect a hormone-driven process of synaptic pruning aimed at enhancing maternal behavior [18]. Similarly, Kim et al. (2021) used surface-based morphometry and observed cortical thinning in frontal and parietal regions during pregnancy compared to non-pregnant women, reinforcing the notion of neuroanatomical adaptations in gestational brain structure [25].

The findings from these studies support the theory that neuroendocrine changes, including elevated levels of estrogen, progesterone, and cortisol, may underlie observed cognitive and behavioral adaptations during pregnancy.

Overall, it seems that cognitive changes during pregnancy do not reflect dementia, but are the consequence of subtle cognitive changes that occur during pregnancy and probably resolve a few months after delivery. In the current study, postpartum visits were performed between 7 weeks and 12 weeks after delivery; Therefore, it is expected that with the passage of more time, performance might be increased to the level of the first trimester.

In our study, the level of education was included as a predictor in the multivariate linear regression analysis. The results showed a statistically significant association between higher education levels and better executive function scores across time points. In contrast, age and BMI were not significantly associated with executive function scores. We interpret this as an observed statistical association, not as a causal relationship. It is possible that higher education may be related to better-developed cognitive reserve or coping strategies, but further research is necessary to explore underlying mechanisms. In the study conducted by Barda et al., executive changes in pregnancy were unrelated to education. These inconsistencies highlight the need for more robust longitudinal studies investigating how education and other sociocognitive factors might moderate neuropsychological adaptations during pregnancy.

Study Limitations.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from only two centers in Tehran using a convenience sampling method, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although validated tools were used, subjective self-reporting may have introduced response bias. Third, hormonal levels were not measured, preventing a direct correlation between endocrine changes and cognitive outcomes. Finally, the follow-up duration postpartum was limited to 7–12 weeks, and longer-term cognitive recovery could not be assessed. Future studies should incorporate hormonal assays, objective cognitive testing, and long-term follow-up to better elucidate the trajectory of cognitive function during and after pregnancy.

Conclusion

The findings of our study showed that the executive function of the brain in the third trimester of pregnancy significantly decreased compared to the first trimester of pregnancy and postpartum, and its recovery after delivery was not complete until 12 weeks postpartum.

In addition, changes in brain executive function during pregnancy were influenced by the level of education. That is, better executive function in higher education cases was reported, although impaired function in pregnancy was shown in this subgroup, as well from a practical point of view, it is necessary to recognize brain function changes as one of the physiological changes of pregnancy and provide necessary support for pregnant women, including in the work and life environment.
