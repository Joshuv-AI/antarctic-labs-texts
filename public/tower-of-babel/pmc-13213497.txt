The design process for orthopaedic implants
Authors: Radovan Zdero, Pawel Brzozowski, Emil H. Schemitsch, Irene Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Orthopaedic implants consist of engineered devices for various surgical uses, such as plates or nails used to fix fractures; plates, rods, or cages used for spinal fusion, total hip or knee arthroplasties, and osteoconductive bone scaffolds. The implant design process typically involves the complicated interaction of interdisciplinary teams, client consultation, concept generation, concept ranking, prototype fabrication, engineering analysis, etc. However, the process can also be described as having four major aspects, each of which has a feedback loop if modifications are needed. The first is the design and fabrication, which can include factors such as geometry, surface finish, special features, material selection, and methods of fabrication. The second involves mechanical/engineering analysis, which could involve factors such as stress risers, stress shielding, elastic stiffness, failure strength, and fatigue life. The third is biological/clinical analysis, which may involve factors such as biocompatibility, bioresorbability, bone remodelling, surgical simplicity, and clinical outcomes. The fourth is marketplace evaluation, which might involve factors such as cost-effectiveness, patent acquisition, regulatory approval, demographic suitability, and ecological sustainability. These aspects, or the specific factors within each aspect, can occur sequentially step by step, simultaneously side by side, in different orders, or by some combination of all of these options. This article is a brief practical introduction to the design process for orthopaedic implants.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(5):577–582.

Introduction

Orthopaedic implants involve diverse technologies.1 For example, cables, nails, plates, and screws provide mechanical stability for the fragments of a fracture to facilitate primary healing via mature bone cell formation, or secondary healing via callus formation. Cages, plates, rods, and screws are used for the fixation of one or more vertebral fractures or for the fusion of two or more adjacent vertebrae. Arthroplasties are used to replace damaged articulating surfaces of the shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, or ankle. Osteoconductive scaffolds made of ceramics, metals, or polymers are inserted into bony defects for mechanical support until new bone forms on their surfaces.

Designing these implants usually involves the complex interplay of multidisciplinary teams (e.g. engineers, surgeons), client consultation (e.g. objectives), concept generation (e.g. morphology charts), concept ranking (e.g. Pugh charts), prototype fabrication (e.g. 3D printing), and engineering analysis (e.g. mechanical testing).2 The process may also be described more concisely as having four major aspects: implant design and fabrication, mechanical engineering analysis, biological and clinical analysis, and marketplace evaluation, plus feedback loops for redesign (Figure 1). The aim of this article is to provide a short introduction to the design process for orthopaedic implants.

Implant design and fabrication

Geometry

The geometry of the implant includes the outline of the silhouette, cross-sectional form, internal architecture, length, width, thickness, and diameter. These should be chosen to reduce an implant’s excessive motion and risk of breaking. For example, classic mechanical formulae can predict the role of geometry, as follows: plates for the fixation of fractures with 5% greater thickness generate 15.8% higher stiffness and 9.3% lower tensile surface stress during bending,3 spinal fusion rods with 5% greater diameter produce 21.6% higher stiffness and 13.6% lower shear surface stress during torsion,3 and modular total hip arthroplasties (THAs) with 14.3% more head-trunnion interface area require 3.8% more force during head pull-off failure.4

Surface finish

The surface finish is designed to be smooth, corrugated, or textured.1,2 For instance, nails for the treatment of fractures, plates, and spinal fusion rods could have smooth surfaces to prevent bone ongrowth so they can be easily removed if there are postoperative complications; however, plates used for fractures and those for spinal fusion could have corrugated surfaces to avoid too much contact and compression of the bone’s periosteal ‘skin’ which could impair the blood flow.1 Similarly, the stem of an arthroplasty could have smooth surfaces for easy removal if there are complications; however, the stem could be textured for bone ongrowth and better anchorage in low-quality bone or when replacing a smooth stem.1

Special features

Implants could be designed to have special features to give surgeons options for different scenarios or complex cases, such as in patients with advanced osteoporosis, severe osteoarthritis, bone, joint, or spinal deformities, and complex multifragment fractures. For example, hybrid plates can have smooth and threaded holes, respectively, for non-locking screws with smooth screw heads enabling flexible fixation, or locking screws with threaded heads enabling rigid fixation.5 Similarly, ‘far cortical locking’ plates can have screws with smaller shaft diameters to permit back-and-forth toggle micromotion within larger pilot holes in bone for quicker callus formation.5 Moreover, sliding plates can have a sliding segment, so that the length of the plate can be adjusted intraoperatively using a screw-spring mechanism, controlling the compressive force at the site of the fracture.6

Material selection

The implants could be designed from ceramics, metals, polymers, or composites for different applications based on their chemical, mechanical, and other properties.1,2 For instance, ceramics are often used for scaffolds to plug bony defects because they encourage the ingrowth of bone for better mechanical stability or for the articulating surfaces of arthroplasties because of their low wear rate.1 Metals like cobalt-chromium, stainless steel, or titanium alloy are usually used because of their high load-bearing capacity for the cables, nails, plates, or screws to fix fractures, spinal fusion cages, plates, or rods, and the stems of arthroplasties.1 Polymers like high-density polyethylene are commonly used for articulating surfaces of arthroplasties due to their low friction.1 There is also an increasing interest in new materials, such as fibre-reinforced polymers, due to their excellent resistance to corrosion, high strength-to-weight ratios, and custom-made mechanical properties.7

Fabrication methods

The method of fabrication of an implant may be chosen based on its geometry, material properties, and costs.2,8 For example, metal implants are made using one or more forming methods: 1) casting (molten metal poured into a mould); 2) drawing (heated metal pulled through a hole); 3) forging (cooled or heated metal compressed between dies); 4) rolling (heated metal passed between rollers); or 5) machining (metal is cut, drilled, and milled). Then, one or more methods of finishing are used: 1) annealing (heating or cooling to restore ductility and toughness); 2) polishing (removal of surface defects); 3) passivating (protection against chemical corrosion); 4) nitriding (hardening the surface chemically); or 5) irradiating (antimicrobial sterilization). However, new 3D printing technology for ceramics, composites, metals, or polymers can be rapid, offer excellent control of geometry and create patient-specific implants, although the load-bearing capacity of the implant must be sufficient.9

Implant mechanical/engineering analysis

Stress risers

Stress risers at implant-bone interfaces are geometrical discontinuities which create areas of increased stress which may fail if they exceed the material failure limits (e.g. ultimate tensile stress).1 Consequently, implants should be designed to reduce or eliminate these discontinuities, such as sharp points or edges, jagged surfaces, corners, curves, defects, gaps, grooves, and holes. Thus, periprosthetic femoral fractures can occur around the stem of the femoral component of a THA as in a Vancouver B fracture, requiring further surgery to reconstruct the bone using a plate and screws.7 However, the screws which are used to secure the plate can also create stress risers around their sharp threads and cause loosening via toggle motion, screw pullout from bone, or pushout through bone.1,10

Stress shielding

Stress shielding at the implant-bone interface occurs because too much load is carried by overly rigid implants relative to the adjacent bone leading to loss of bone density, bone resorption, loosening, and damage to the implant or bone.1 Implants should be designed to reduce or eliminate stress shielding by increasing bone stress to be equal to, or greater than, an intact non-implanted bone control, although the bone stress must stay below the bone’s material failure limit (e.g. ultimate tensile stress). For example, plates used for the fixation of a femoral fracture made from more flexible fibre-reinforced polymer (vs metal) can reduce stress shielding under the plate by 1.8 to 2.7×,7 while THAs made from more flexible porous (vs solid) metal can decrease stress shielding around the implant’s stem by a factor of four.11

Elastic stiffness

Elastic stiffness (physical flexibility) is the slope (the change in load/change in displacement) of the initial linear part of the load-displacement graph during quasi-static, cyclical, or impact loading.10 In order to restore function, the implant should be designed so that the implant-bone construct’s elastic stiffness is immediately or eventually similar to that of an intact non-implanted bone control. However, depending on the type of implant, bone quality, and the time after operation, sometimes this is not possible. For instance, an intact femur’s elastic stiffness (1× intact) can rise after the introduction of a metal THA (1.28× intact), then it can drop after the femur sustains a fracture which is stabilized using a metal plate and screws (0.52× intact), and finally it can rise again after the fracture heals (1.39× intact).7

Failure strength

Failure strength (breakage resistance) is the peak load on the load-displacement graph before failure during quasi-static, cyclical, or impact loading.10 In order to restore function, the implant must be designed so that the failure strength of the implant-bone construct is immediately or eventually comparable to that of an intact non-implanted bone control. This is sometimes, however, not achievable due to the type of the implant, the bone quality, or time after operation. For example, the failure strength of an intact humerus (1× intact) can decrease after the removal of a tumour which leaves a circular defect at the mid-shaft (0.17× intact), but the failure strength can rise after the defect is stabilized using a plate and screws (0.50× intact) or a nail and screws (0.67× intact) without reaching its intact magnitude.12

Fatigue life

The fatigue life (longevity) is the number of loading cycles at a given load which is withstood before failure.1 In order to restore function, the implant could be designed so that the fatigue life of the implant-bone construct is greater than 150,000 cycles to simulate two months of use (thus, enough time for some healing of the fracture) or one million cycles to mimic one year of use (thus, enough time for complete bone remodelling).13 This may not, however, be reached because of the type of implant, the quality of the bone, or the time after the operation. For instance, femoral fractures which are stabilized using nails with metaphyseal rather than diaphyseal screws can achieve a 209,000 (vs 80,000) cycle fatigue life at 700 N, as during partial weightbearing after surgery.14

Implant biological/clinical analysis

Biocompatibility

Biocompatible implants should have no effects (thus be bioinert) or beneficial effects (thus be bioactive) on biological tissue. Testing protocols for biocompatibility and related properties can include the following: 1) carcinogenesis tests detect tumour cells; 2) cytotoxicity tests analyze cell survival; 3) genotoxicity tests identify genetic damage; 4) haemocompatibility tests determine blood cell damage or coagulation; 5) hydrophilicity tests evaluate the absorption of water; 6) irritation tests identify skin or mucous membrane reactions; 7) osteogenesis tests measure new bone formation; and 8) sensitivity tests quantify immunological response.15 For example, novel plates for the treatment of fractures in long bones made from carbon fibre, flax fibre, or epoxy resin, can have similar cytotoxicity (with between 75% and 130% survival of osteoblasts) and osteogenesis (between 96% and 104% formation of osteoblasts) versus stainless steel, but its 8.3% water weight uptake could degrade the mechanical properties.16

Bioresorbability

Bioresorbable implants are made from semi-rigid materials composed of larger molecules which are hydrolyzed into smaller molecules and then metabolized by the liver into byproducts like CO2 which the body expels.17 Advantages include less stress shielding of bone, no implant removal, and full visibility of bone during imaging; however, the bioresorption process must allow enough time for sufficient remodelling of bone so that it can withstand mechanical load on its own. This is feasible for implants used for the treatment of fractures, bony scaffolds, and spinal fusion cages which protect the bone until sufficient remodelling has occurred, but may not be suitable for an arthroplasty which permanently replaces the damaged bone, cartilage, and menisci which cover the articulating surfaces. For instance, novel nails for the treatment of fractured long bones made from bioresorbable polylactide (plus bioresorbable magnesium alloy) can retain 93% and 60%, respectively, of the original bending failure stress after six and 12 weeks in a water bath, which might be enough time for sufficient healing of the fracture.18

Bone remodelling

Bone remodelling occurs as osteoblasts deposit new minerals and collagen, whereas osteoclasts destroy minerals and collagen, in response to changing mechanical load (Wolff’s Law).1 For example, implants used to treat fractures (cables, nails, plates and screws) or those used for spinal fusion (cages, plates and rods) could be designed and/or mounted to permit 0.2 to 1 mm of axial interfragmentary motion (IFM) and ensure shear IFM is below 1.6 × axial IFM to stimulate earlier callus formation leading to quicker healed fractures or completed fusions.19 Similarly, arthroplasties could be designed and/or implanted to equalize load sharing between the implant and bone, avoiding stress shielding, which occurs through loss of bone density along the implant, the resorption of bone, loosening of the implant away from the bone, and damage to the implant or bone.11

Surgical simplicity

Surgical simplicity means that the implant can be easily inserted, adjusted, repaired, removed, and/or replaced. Although an implant may have attractive qualities, such as low purchase price, high mechanical performance, and minimal ecological impact, it should be redesigned if surgeons consider it difficult to use. This can be quantified by the number of manual tasks, number of surgical tools, amount of soft-tissue stripping and preparation of bone, volume of blood loss, and operating time. For instance, nail-nail (vs nail-plate, plate-nail, or plate-plate) reconstructions for stabilizing forearm fractures require between 38% and 46% less operating time, between 58% and 70% shorter length of incision, and between 44% and 60% smaller area of periosteal stripping,20 while nails (vs sliding hip screws) for fixing trochanteric hip fractures require 8% shorter operating times and result in 20% less blood loss during surgery.21

Clinical outcomes

Patient-reported and surgeon-reported clinical outcomes after surgery include the healing of a fracture, muscle strength, the level of pain, range of motion, wound infection, and satisfaction, as well as damage to, or the breaking of an implant, its malfunction, migration, and prominence.22 For example, proximal tibial fractures stabilized using locking plates which involve inserting the screws at variable angles (vs fixed-angle) can capture bony fragments more effectively for improved stability of the reconstruction leading to better outcomes.23 Consequently, at six months of follow-up, the use of variable-angle (vs fixed-angle) plates can result in the following: 1) 9% more patients with a Rasmussen radiological score of 18,23 which is defined as excellent (thus leading to less OA, articular depression, condylar widening, and varus/valgus deformation); 2) 33% fewer patients with complications (i.e., medial collapse, wound infection); 3) 18% more patients with an Oxford Knee Score of between 40 and 48, which is defined as excellent (i.e., more function, less discomfort);24,25 and 4) 4% more patients who achieve full weightbearing.23

Implant marketplace evaluation

Cost-effectiveness

The cost effectiveness of an implant involves more than reducing the price, since stakeholders wish to minimize money, time, and the resources associated with the implant.2,26 For instance, university researchers developing new implants spend time or effort, purchase equipment or materials, and/or recruit animal or human subjects. Medical companies incur costs to develop, patent, fabricate, sterilize, package, store, advertize, sell, and distribute implants. Government agencies pay salaries to personnel and use resources to evaluate applications from university researchers and medical companies wishing to market implants in their country. Clinics and hospitals purchase implants and have associated costs, such as paying surgeons and nurses, buying pain medications and antibiotics, surgical instruments and monitors, allocating operating theatre time and hospital beds, and discarding or incinerating surgically removed implants.

Patent acquisition

Implants with unique designs are presumably the intellectual property of the inventor, such as an individual designer or medical company.2,27,28 However, intellectual property can only be legally protected by obtaining a certificate called a patent from a national (e.g., U.S. Patent and Trademark Office), regional (e.g., European Patent Office), or international (e.g., World Intellectual Property Organization) agency. The owner of the patent then has exclusive, but time-limited, control of the implant’s fabrication, marketing and sales, receives financial rewards through sales or royalties, and/or gains prestige as the inventor or owner. Patents cannot, however, always guarantee legal protection. Fraudulent individuals or companies may copy an implant’s design which is patented in one country to make, advertise, and sell the implant in a different country, in which the original patent is not legally binding.

Regulatory approval

The approval of an implant could be considered to occur through a network of international, national, and local regulatory bodies.29,30 Internationally, scientific regulatory bodies recommend criteria regarding an implant’s technical quality and clinical suitability; however, these recommendations are not mandatory for national or local stakeholders. Nationally, government regulatory bodies often consult international recommendations when establishing countrywide laws about which implants can legally be fabricated, marketed, sold, and/or used in clinics or hospitals. However, government-run or government-funded research can, in turn, improve the accuracy of international recommendations. Locally, clinic- or hospital-based regulatory bodies must abide by national laws when creating policies for their local context regarding the purchase, storage, sterilization, usage, and/or disposal of implants, and local research can influence international recommendations and national laws.

Morphology and phenotype variations

While custom-made implants can be designed to accommodate extreme clinical conditions (e.g. skeletal dysplasia), this can be prohibitively expensive,31 and might not account for the more subtle variations which may exist between patients, such as anatomical variations. There are many examples in the literature of the importance of accounting for subtle anatomical differences which may directly influence clinical outcomes. For example, changing the design of the Oxford Domed lateral unicompartmental knee arthroplasty from a flat to a more anatomically based tibial component reduced the dislocation rate from 17% (flat) to 3.7% (domed).32 In another example, using an oversized tibial component in a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is associated with reduced functional outcomes.33 It is therefore important that the designs of implants accommodate morphological and phenotypical variations between individuals and more generally among the patients for whom the implant is intended. Important variations to consider include, but are not limited to: age, sex, genetics, environmental factors, variations in skeletal morphology (e.g. bone/joint anatomy), the bone/joint condition (e.g. healthy vs pathological) and variable functional demands (e.g. the type and level of activity).

Ecological sustainability

The design of an implant should be ecologically sustainable in order to address growing interest among companies, governments, and society about the management of global resources.34,35 Waste audits involve weighing materials used during surgery which are later discarded, incinerated or recycled. For example, a TKA generates 13.3 kg of waste in the form of regular solid waste (64.5% of total weight), biohazard waste (19.2%), recyclable blue sterile wraps (12.1%), recyclable plastics (2.2%), and incinerable sharps (2.0%).36 Life cycle assessments involve estimating CO2 production for raw material extraction/processing, manufacturing, sterilization, packaging, transportation, use, maintenance, disposal, incineration, recycling, or reuse. For instance, additive (vs traditional) manufacturing of the femoral component of a TKA produces 14.9 kg (vs 46.9 kg) of CO2.37 It has been suggested that implant ecological sustainability may be improved by additive manufacturing, having fewer surgical instruments in trays, reusable instruments, correct waste separation and routine recycling.34,35

Conclusion

This article presents a brief introduction to many important aspects of the design processes for orthopaedic implants. A more complete process might also consider other design-and-fabrication factors in the design and fabrication (e.g. implant storage), mechanical/engineering factors (e.g. the wear of an arthroplasty), biological/clinical factors (e.g. the influence on postoperative rehabilitation), and marketplace factors (e.g. the number of patients who could benefit from the implant).
