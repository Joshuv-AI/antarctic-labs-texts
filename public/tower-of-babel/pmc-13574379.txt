Chronic postsurgical pain after living donor hepatectomy
Authors: Tufan Gumus, Mustafa Nuri Deniz, Elvan Erhan, Alper Uguz, Sezgin Ulukaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative pain lasting longer than 90 days is defined as chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) and is known to be a long-term bothersome problem for surgical patients. The study presents the incidence, characteristics, and risk factors of CPSP in living donor hepatectomy (LDH). This was a single-center, retrospective study using the institution’s electronic files and the CPSP questionnaire completed during the telephone interview. Of 123 patients assessed, 114 (92.6%) had acute postoperative pain, 15 (12.2%) reported CPSP, 11 (8.9%) reported ongoing pain, and 3 (2.4%) indicated that pain was their most significant health concern. Neuropathic pain was identified in 9 (7.3%), with 6 still requiring medication for pain relief. Analyses of univariate (age, height, comorbidities, previous surgery, and features of acute postoperative pain) and logistic regression (age [β = 1.065; P = .033], comorbidities [β = 5.875; P = .003], previous abdominal surgery [β = 3.234; P = .039], acute pain severity [β = 1.368; P = .021], and duration of pain [β = 1.044; P = .001]) revealed significant associations between the variables and CPSP. A subgroup of patients with a relatively lower rate of CPSP continues to endure moderate to mild CPSP with neuropathic features in the long term. We think that attention to donor’s previous features and effective treatment of acute pain severity that takes into account the neuropathic component may reduce the development of CPSP in living liver donors.

1. Introduction

Living donor hepatectomy (LDH) plays a crucial role in addressing the shortage of deceased organ donation. Living liver donors deserve special care as they are selected from healthy volunteers. However, delays in returning to preoperative health of up to 6 months, fatigue lasting up to 2 years after donation, and other physical and psychosocial issues are significant causes of concern for living liver donors. Chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) is notable with a frequency of 30% and 27% in LDH patients at 6-month and 12-month follow-ups.[1-3] CPSP is defined as pain that persists for more than 90 days.[4] Recently, an observational multicenter study reported that the overall incidence of CPSP was 11.8% and mostly had neuropathic pain features. The prediction and prevention of CPSP are also debated in terms of postoperative analgesia methods.[5] Patients undergoing LDH should be examined in terms of CPSP causes and characteristics in order to be protected from CPSP. For this reason, this study seeks to present the incidence, causes, and clinical characteristics of CPSP in LDH patients who were managed with an institutional protocol for patient care and pain management.

2. Materials and methods

This study employs an analytical cross-sectional design, approved by the University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (decision number 23-7T/34). The study cohort comprises living liver donors who underwent donor hepatectomy under general anesthesia at the Organ Transplantation Center between February 2017 and December 2021.

Information on the donors was sourced from electronic patient files and paper documents. A CPSP-related questionnaire was also administered through telephone interviews. Exclusions from the study encompassed individuals with medical conditions impeding evaluation, those unreachable, individuals who had undergone different surgical procedures post-donor hepatectomy, or those who had passed away (Fig. 1). Prior to participating, verbal consent was obtained from cases by a physician not affiliated with the transplant team, who provided information about the study and administered the prepared questionnaire.

2.1. Donor hepatectomy organization

All living donor hepatectomies were conducted via open surgery utilizing the Makuuchi incision. Following the incision, bilateral abdominal retractors were positioned beneath the rib curves throughout the procedure to enhance surgical field access and facilitate liver dissection. Post-operation, a drain was inserted at the hepatectomy site, and the abdominal layers were closed using a continuous Polydioxanone suture. Finally, the skin was closed with staples.

2.2. Anesthesia and postoperative analgesia protocol

The study encompassed cases where the standard institutional practice involved inhalational-based general anesthesia. Patient’s analgesia was provided with thoracic epidural (thoracic epidural analgesia [TEA]) or intravenous (intravenous analgesia [IVA]) methods, which served both as anesthetic support during surgery and as postoperative analgesia. TEA was delivered via an epidural catheter at T8 to T10 intervals. A single dose with a solution of 10 to 12 mL containing 0.25% bupivacaine hydrochloride and 100 mcg fentanyl citrate was administered at the beginning of the surgery. Postoperatively, TEA was maintained with intermittent dosing, with a maximum of 4 doses per day for patients reporting pain severity 4 and above on the visual analog scale (VAS). Each dose consisted of 3 mg morphine and 0.125% to 0.2% bupivacaine via the epidural catheter, 7–9 mL in total. If the patient refused the epidural catheter or the catheter could not be placed, IVA was administered. IVA was maintained with a remifentanil hydrochloride infusion (Ultiva 5 mg vial, GlaxoSmithKline, Istanbul, Turkey) at doses ranging from 0.1 to 1 µg/kg/min, following administration of fentanyl 2 µg/kg during induction. The remifentanil infusion was continued throughout the surgical procedure. Postoperative analgesia was initiated toward the end of surgery. The postoperative analgesic regimen consisted of intravenous paracetamol (Perfalgan 1 g vial, Bristol-Myers Squibb Pharmaceuticals Ltd) administered at 1 g every 6 hours (maximum 4 g/d) and intravenous tramadol hydrochloride (Contramal 100 mg, Abdi İbrahim, Türkiye) at 50 to 100 mg every 4 to 6 hours (maximum 400 mg/d). Intramuscular diclofenac sodium (75 mg) was administered as a rescue analgesic in both analgesia groups.

2.3. Variables

Data extracted from electronic patient files and archival records included donor age, height, weight, body mass index, gender, marital status, co-morbidities, previous surgeries, type (right hepatectomy, left lateral segmentectomy, or left hepatectomy) and timing (elective or emergent) of surgery, the duration of the operation, postoperative analgesia method (thoracic epidural or intravenous), hospital stay, and surgical complications based on the Clavien-Dindo classification.

During telephone interviews, a questionnaire form was used to gather data on the presence, location, duration, severity, and quality of acute pain in the early postoperative period. An 11-grade Numeric Rating Scale (NRS – 0: no pain; 10: the most severe pain experienced) was employed to evaluate pain severity.

Pain that persisted for more than 3 months after the operation was defined as CPSP.[4,6] CPSP was further evaluated for severity, neuropathic component, analgesic treatment administered, and its impact on the donor’s quality of life (QoL). The S-LANSS (Self-Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms & Signs) score was calculated for all patients with CPSP, based on the first 5 questions (Questions 12–16 of the survey).[7]

2.4. Statistics

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS statistical software package for Windows (version 21.0; IBM). Categorical data were presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%), while continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) or median (minimum − maximum). Univariate associations were assessed using Pearson chi-square or Fisher exact tests (as appropriate) for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables. A logistic regression analysis was used to examine the associations between the independent continuous or categorical variables and CPSP, expressed as β, 95% CI (upper and lower interquantile range), P value. A two sided P value <.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

3. Results

From the initial pool of 187 individuals (Fig. 1), 51 were unreachable by phone, 10 remained uncontactable, and 2 had passed away due to natural causes. There were no surgery-related deaths. Additionally, 1 patient was excluded due to prior incisional hernia repair. None of the patients had a chronic pain before the operation. The study focused on 123 individuals.

3.1. Donor characteristics

Donors, with a mean age of 37 years (SD 9.5), were predominantly male (53.6%) and 63.4% married. Most had elective surgeries (91.8%) lasting around 281 minutes. Procedures included right hepatectomy in 101, left hepatectomy in 5, and left lateral segmentectomy in 17. Postoperative analgesia was administered through TEA in 108 (87.8%) and IVA in 15 (12.2%) of donors. Hospital stay was 6 (4–17) days. Complications were observed in 26% (32 donors). The postoperative follow-up was 55 (17–78) months.

3.2. Postoperative pain

3.2.1. Acute postoperative pain

A total of 114 (92.6%) donors had acute postoperative pain, and 9 had no pain. Pain intensity was severe in 69 (56.1%), moderate in 36 (29.2%) and mild in 9 (7.3%). Surgical site pain (92.7%) was universal. The frequencies of acute pain were similar in patients with and without CPSP, but the severity (P = .01), duration (P = .000), and frequency of ongoing pain (P = .000) were significantly higher in patients with CPSP (Table 1). All individuals noted a gradual reduction in postoperative pain intensity over time. Apart from surgical pain, 10 donors with TEA reported postoperative low back pain, and 5 reported accompanying right shoulder pain with transient numbness in the right hand in 2 donors. All these symptoms were in the early postoperative period and resolved.

3.2.2. CPSP

CPSP was identified in 15 (12.2%) cases (Table 2). Eleven (8.9%) of them, whose survey questioning times were 14–50 months, had ongoing pain. Pain intensity was moderate in 5 cases and mild in 6. Among those with CPSP, 6 (4.8%) sought treatment at the pain outpatient clinic, and for 3 (2.4%) patients, pain was their most significant health concern. Univariate analysis revealed that age, height, comorbidities, previous surgery, and features of acute postoperative pain were significantly related to CPSP. The gender, marriage, weight, body mass index of patients, type, timing, surgical duration and complications of hepatectomies, incidence of acute postoperative pain, analgesia methods, and hospital stay were not associated with CPSP. Logistic regression analysis also revealed positive associations between age (β = 1.065; 95% CI, 1.005–1.128; P = .033), comorbidities (β = 5.875 95% CI, 1.843–18.730; P = .003), previous abdominal surgery (β = 3.234; 95% CI, 1.061–9.852; P = .039), acute pain severity (β = 1.368; 95% CI, 1.49–1.784; P = .021 and duration of pain (β = 1.044; 95% CI, 1.017–1.072; P = .001) and the development of CPSP (Table 3).

3.2.3. Neuropathic pain

Nine (7.3%) patients with CPSP reported uncomfortable sensations such as pins and needles, stinging, or tingling in the painful skin area. Of these, 3 (2.4%) cases described sudden onset explosive, throbbing, electric shock-like pain, and 1 (0.8%) reported hypersensitivity to touch and temperature in the painful area. The S-LANSS score exceeded 12 in 3 patients whose pain was not relieved by analgesics such as paracetamol and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, prompting their referral to the pain clinic.

3.2.4. Non-CPSP sensations

Among donors (n = 108) who did not develop CPSP, 21.1% (26 donors) reported a non-disturbing sensation at the surgical incision site in cold weather but did not define it as pain. This cold sensation was reported by 9 (60%) with CPSP (P = .004, vs non-CPSP group). Additionally, 35.7% (44 cases) of individuals without CPSP reported numbness around the surgical incision.

4. Discussion

In our relatively large study evaluating 123 individuals who had open living donor hepatectomy and were examined an average of 55 months after surgery, 114 donors (92.6%) had acute postoperative pain. Fifteen donors (12.2%) experienced CPSP.

Acute postoperative pain emerges as a crucial risk factor for CPSP. Bonnet et al., in 65 hepatectomy patients, found higher (37% at movement) early postoperative pain scores in 9% who reported CPSP, emphasizing early postoperative pain as a significant CPSP risk factor.[8] Holtzman et al reported that, after donor hepatectomy, 42% at rest and 72% at movement experienced moderate-to-severe pain on day 1 in patients with CPSP.[3] Aggarwal et al also reported that moderate-to-severe acute pain on day 1 after hepatectomy is as high as 72%.[9] In our study, donors were able to express their early postoperative pain experiences easily. Although there was no difference in the incidence of acute pain, the severity and duration of acute pain were found to be significantly higher among patients with CPSP than without, as expected.

In general, the incidence of CPSP after abdominal surgery ranges widely, from 5% to 50%, depending on the surgical procedure.[10-12] Recently, in a European survey, the mean incidence of CPSP at 6 months was 10.5%, with variations depending on the type of surgery. One-third of patients experienced CPSP at both 3 and 6 months. Verbesey et al conducted a prospective study on 47 liver donors, reporting 5% with moderate pain and 20% with mild pain 6 months post-hepatectomy, with 17% still having mild pain after 12 months.[13] Chan et al found 24% of 25 patients had ongoing incision site pain 1 year post-surgery.[5] In a larger study with an average 69-month follow-up, 6% reported incision site pain requiring regular analgesic use.[14] Holtzman et al reported CPSP with incidences of 31% at 6-month and 27% at 12-month after LDH.[3] Imamura et al described CPSP in 42% of donors, numbness in 3%, and partial cutaneous sensory loss in 47% as scar-related complications at an 86-month follow-up.[15] In our study, in which all pain-related components were questioned in detail, the detection of CPSP in 12.2% of the cases can be considered a satisfactory result.

The transition of acute postoperative pain to chronic postsurgical pain is a complex and poorly understood developmental process, involving biological, psychological, and social-environmental factors.[11] Young patients and women have been reported to experience more severe acute postoperative pain, hence CPSP.[16-19] Unlike other studies, increasing age appeared to be a confounder in our study, and gender was not found to have an effect on CPSP. The high acute pain intensity and CPSP rate in patients who underwent living donor hepatectomy are also consistent with their logistic regression data. The patients’ previous experiences over time may have also led to a higher rate of CPSP. Compared to patients without CPSP, the number of patients with previous abdominal surgery (β = 3.234, P = .039) and concomitant diseases (β = 5.875, P = .003) were risk factors for CPSP patients. The fact that prolonged pain was also identified as a significant risk factor (β = 1.044, P = .001) in our study may mean that patients need to receive better pain management to prevent CPSP. Therefore, we can say that they experienced more severe acute pain and therefore resulted in CPSP. In our series, perioperative, patient-experienced factors such as duration, timing, and type of hepatectomy, complication rates, and hospital stay seem to have no effect on CPSP.

Our study also assessed whether the postoperative analgesia method contributed to the development of CPSP. In accordance with the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocol, combined analgesia methods such as TEA and IVA are recommended for pain control, including live donor procedures and other surgical interventions.[20-24] It has been assumed that sufficient pain relief will improve the surgical outcome.[21] Thoracic epidural decreases endocrine, metabolic, and inflammatory responses after abdominal surgery. Epidural analgesia provides better postoperative pain relief than intravenous analgesia after live liver donation.[20] In a small study comparing patients undergoing donor hepatectomy and major hepatectomy, donor hepatectomies were found to be more painful than major hepatectomies under thoracic epidural analgesia.[25] Epidural analgesia has even been reported to be effective in reducing the incidence of chronic pain at 6-month follow-up.[26] It should be acknowledged that failure to provide effective epidural analgesia may vary depending on the patient, practitioner, or drug doses. Frankly, there still seems to be a need for high-quality randomized controlled trials for open liver surgery analgesia.[24] Although epidural analgesia administered by a dedicated anesthetist is preferred as a routine protocol in this patient group for 3 days in our organ transplant center, all patients with CPSP in our study had TEA. In our study, we made a noteworthy observation: 56.1% of the patients reported experiencing severe pain in a questionnaire administered an average of 55 months (range: 17 to 78 months) after surgery. It could be related to the single-dose thoracic epidural analgesia administered at the beginning of the surgery. During routine postoperative follow-up, these patients had described brief but intense pain immediately after surgery, which subsided rapidly. However, they continued to report this acute pain intensity even years later. This finding suggests that the severity of acute postoperative pain, even when short-lived, may be associated with the development of chronic pain.

Donors managed with IVA did not report CPSP. However, more patients were needed to understand whether the combination of postoperative tramadol and paracetamol after intraoperative remifentanil infusion had a significant effect on CPSP.

An important consideration in all patients with CPSP is that the initial incisional pain subsequently acquires neuropathic features. The incidence of neuropathic pain (7.3%) after LDH performed with open surgery is quite satisfactory considering the total number of patients.[27] Of these, 4.8% had to apply to pain clinics to relieve their ongoing pain. Three patients (2.4%) with S-LANSS scores >12 distressingly reported ongoing uncomfortable sensations in the painful area. It was understood from these patients with neuropathic pain that their pain relief was unresponsive to standard treatments. In the follow-up of such sensitive patients, it is necessary to apply different pharmacological or non-pharmacological treatments targeting neuropathic pain pathways to prevent hyperalgesia.[28,29] Unfortunately, the importance of effective suppression of early acute pain, especially severe pain, and not prolonging the duration of pain needs to be referred to again with the data of our study.

Our study also found a high incidence of partial sensory loss (35.7%, n = 44) and increased sensitivity to cold weather not defined as pain (28.4%, n = 35). Apart from pain, addressing issues related to scarring that makes itself felt constantly is important in evaluating the QoL of donors.[30]

As a most important postoperative issue, the majority of liver donors are expected to recover their preoperative QoL within 6 months following surgery.[1] A multicenter study involving a substantial number of LDH patients revealed that longer post-donation hospitalization and concerns about donation-related health effects were associated with an increased risk of various physical and mental health problems.[31] Because of the potential impact of somatic and neuropathic postoperative pain on QoL and long-term psychosocial and physical health, living liver donors deserve effective pain management.[13,32] It is also suggested that the distinct pattern of pain perception, negative emotional distress to pain in donors, and interventions used in other surgical populations may not be equally effective in them.[33]

Our study covered various aspects of chronic pain including prevalence, associated causes, and neuropathic pain components. The utilization of phone interviews enhances the reliability of our findings. However, we acknowledge that this retrospective study has some limitations, including the lack of preoperative evaluation, the full range of analgesic types and doses used long-term, the omission of analyses of preoperative psychosocial factors, and differences in the timing of case evaluations.

5. Conclusions

It seems that a subgroup of patients with a relatively lower rate of CPSP continues to endure moderate to mild CPSP with neuropathic features in the long term. We think that attention to donor’s previous features and effective treatment of acute severe pain that takes into account the neuropathic component may reduce the development of CPSP in living liver donors.
