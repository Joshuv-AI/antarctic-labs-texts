Nursing workforce in Southeast Asia: a scoping review of historical trends, contemporary issues, and regional solutions
Authors: Hanif Abdul Rahman, Siti Nurzaimah Nazhirah Zaim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe nursing workforce is pivotal to sustaining healthcare systems, yet Southeast Asia faces persistent challenges in recruitment, retention, and competency development. Historical legacies of workforce shortages, migration, and uneven training standards continue to shape the present, as rising demands from demographic transitions, pandemics, and evolving care needs have intensified pressures on the profession.ObjectiveThis scoping review aims to synthesize historical and current evidence on nursing workforce issues in Southeast Asia, with a focus on occupational competencies, workforce distribution, and policy responses.MethodsGuided by established scoping review frameworks, literature was systematically mapped from regional and international databases, policy documents, and grey sources spanning the colonial era to 2025. Data were charted to capture temporal trends, thematic issues, and multilevel solutions.FindingsWorkforce issues include long-standing shortages, skill mix imbalances, migration to higher-income regions, limited continuing education, and uneven regulatory capacity. Emerging themes highlight the importance of advanced practice roles, digital health competencies, and resilience-building in crisis contexts. Regional solutions are evident at multiple levels: (i) Macro-level—policy harmonization, regional cooperation through ASEAN frameworks, and investment in nursing education infrastructure; (ii) Meso-level—institutional initiatives to strengthen workplace environments, interprofessional collaboration, and retention strategies; and (iii) Micro-level—competency-based training, mentorship, and leadership development programs tailored to local health system needs.ConclusionAddressing nursing workforce issues in Southeast Asia requires historically informed, context-sensitive strategies that integrate regional collaboration with practical solutions across policy, institutional, and individual levels. Such approaches are essential for advancing occupational competency, strengthening health systems, and achieving universal health coverage in the region.Keywords: Nursing workforce, Workforce retention, Southeast Asia, Scoping review, Health policy, Advanced practice nursing

Introduction

The nursing profession stands at the nexus of healthcare delivery in Southeast Asia, representing a critical component of health systems that serve over 680 million people across the region [1]. This scoping review synthesizes historical and contemporary evidence on nursing workforce challenges and competencies in Southeast Asia, examining how colonial legacies continue to shape modern nursing practices while contemporary pressures from demographic transitions, migration patterns, and evolving care demands create unprecedented challenges for the profession [2].

Southeast Asia comprises eleven countries with varying levels of economic development, healthcare infrastructure, and nursing education. The region faces a paradoxical situation where some countries experience nursing surpluses while simultaneously confronting geographical maldistribution, skill mix imbalances, and significant outmigration to higher-income regions [3]. These challenges are compounded by historical factors rooted in colonial healthcare systems that established nursing as a subordinate profession to medicine, creating enduring professional identity issues that persist today [4–6].

The significance of addressing nursing workforce challenges extends beyond professional interests to fundamental questions of healthcare equity and universal health coverage. The World Health Organization estimates that the South-East Asia Region accounts for a disproportionate share of the global nursing shortage, with the region needing an additional 7.6 million nurses and midwives by 2030 [2]. This shortage is particularly acute in rural areas, where healthcare access remains limited despite regional economic growth [5].

To ensure analytical precision throughout this review, three key terms are defined as used in this paper. Competency refers to the integration of knowledge, skills, attitudes, and judgement required to perform nursing roles to an agreed standard within a defined scope of practice. Capacity denotes the quantitative dimension of workforce development, encompassing the overall number and distribution of nursing personnel and the structural systems (education, regulation, governance) that produce and sustain them. Skill mix describes the proportion and combination of different categories of nursing staff (e.g., registered nurses, enrolled nurses, auxiliary personnel, advanced practice nurses) deployed within a care setting or system, and the alignment between staff competencies and patient care needs. These distinctions are particularly important in the Southeast Asian context, where overlapping terminology has contributed to inconsistencies in policy formulation and cross-country comparison.

Historical context of nursing development

Colonial foundations and their lasting impact

The development of nursing in Southeast Asia cannot be separated from the region’s colonial history, which profoundly shaped professional hierarchies and educational approaches that continue to influence contemporary practice [5]. During the Dutch colonial period in Indonesia, nursing education began in the 1800s with the establishment of basic training programs designed primarily to serve European staff and soldiers. The Dutch Indies introduced the Mantri nurse system, creating a three-year diploma program that produced first-class certified nurses who were authorized to provide basic medical care, particularly in rural areas where European physicians were scarce [5, 7].

This colonial model established several enduring characteristics of Southeast Asian nursing. First, it positioned nursing as subordinate to medicine, with nurses functioning primarily as “doctor helpers” rather than autonomous professionals. Second, it created a hierarchical system where European-trained nurses held higher status than locally trained personnel. Third, it emphasized technical skills over theoretical knowledge, a orientation that persisted well into the post-independence era [5, 6].

The British colonial influence in Singapore and Malaysia followed similar patterns, with nursing initially performed by chained convicts in the 1820’s before evolving into more formalized training under religious orders. French nuns from the Convent of the Holy Infant Jesus began nursing duties at Singapore’s General Hospital in 1885, marking the beginning of professional nursing in the colony. This religious foundation contributed to the perception of nursing as a charitable vocation rather than a professional career, influencing recruitment patterns and professional status for decades [9].

Post-independence transformation and American influence

The period following independence marked significant shifts in nursing education and practice across Southeast Asia. Indonesia’s collaboration with Project HOPE in the 1960s exemplified the transition from European to American nursing models. This eight-month program trained thirty Indonesian nurses in American procedures and techniques, fundamentally altering nursing education curricula and practice standards [5, 6].

The influence of American nursing education introduced several key changes: emphasis on the nursing process rather than purely technical skills, integration of theoretical knowledge with clinical practice, and promotion of nursing as an independent profession rather than merely a medical auxiliary. However, implementation of these changes varied significantly across countries and regions, with many areas continuing to rely on traditional Mantri nurse models due to physician shortages [5, 6].

Thailand’s nursing development followed a different trajectory, building upon its status as the only Southeast Asian country to avoid direct colonization [10]. The establishment of nursing schools in the mid-20th century incorporated elements from both Western and traditional healing systems, creating a more integrated approach to nursing education. This foundation contributed to Thailand’s relatively stable nursing workforce and lower outmigration rates compared to regional neighbors [10, 11].

Contemporary workforce challenges

Geographical distribution and density disparities

Contemporary nursing workforce challenges in Southeast Asia are characterized by significant disparities in density and distribution. Current data reveals substantial variations in nursing density across the region, ranging from 1.03 nurses per 1,000 population in Cambodia to 6.71 per 1,000 in Brunei (Fig. 1). These disparities reflect both economic development levels and historical investment in nursing education infrastructure [8].

Nursing workforce density per 1000 population across Southeast Asian countries, highlighting significant disparities between high-income and lower-middle-income nations [2, 8]

The distribution challenges extend beyond national averages to significant subnational variations (Fig. 2). Indonesia, despite having over one million registered nurses, experiences a fourfold variation in health worker density across provinces, with remote areas severely underserved. Similar patterns exist in Thailand, Malaysia, and the Philippines, where urban areas concentrate nursing resources while rural populations face critical shortages [1, 12] (Fig. 2).

Historical growth trajectories of nursing workforces in three major Southeast Asian countries from 2006–2024, showing sustained expansion despite varying scales [8]

These geographical imbalances are perpetuated by several factors including limited rural career advancement opportunities, inadequate infrastructure, and social isolation. Rural retention strategies implemented across the region have shown mixed results, with some countries reporting improved distribution through targeted incentives and mandatory rural service programs [2, 10].

Migration patterns and brain drain

Nursing migration represents one of the most significant challenges facing Southeast Asian health systems. The Philippines dominates global nurse migration, with an outmigration rate of 45.2%, followed by Myanmar at 18.7% and Malaysia at 15.3% (Fig. 3). This migration is driven by substantial wage differentials, better working conditions, and career advancement opportunities in destination countries [13].

Nursing outmigration rates from Southeast Asian countries, revealing the Philippines as the dominant source of migrant nurses globally [13]

The migration pattern follows established corridors, with Filipino nurses primarily moving to the United States, United Kingdom, and Middle East, while Indonesian and Thai nurses often migrate to Singapore, Malaysia, and Japan. This creates a cascading effect where middle-income countries like Singapore and Malaysia become both destination and transit countries, recruiting nurses from lower-income neighbors while losing their own nurses to higher-income destinations [13].

The COVID-19 pandemic intensified migration pressures, with the Philippines temporarily banning nurse deployment abroad in April 2020 due to domestic shortages. This policy sparked significant debate about balancing domestic healthcare needs with economic benefits from remittances, highlighting the complex relationship between migration and national health security [11].

Educational system challenges

Nursing education systems across Southeast Asia face multiple challenges related to capacity, quality, and standardization. Indonesia operates the largest nursing education system in the region, with 909 schools producing approximately 28,825 graduates annually. Yet quality concerns persist due to variations in accreditation standards and clinical training opportunities.

The relationship between educational infrastructure and graduate output reveals significant efficiency variations across countries (Fig. 4). Indonesia’s system, while producing the highest absolute numbers, operates with mixed public-private provision that creates quality inconsistencies. Thailand’s more centralized system produces fewer graduates but maintains higher standardization through government oversight [11].

Relationship between nursing education infrastructure and graduate output in Southeast Asian countries, revealing efficiency variations across different educational systems [11]

Curriculum modernization remains a persistent challenge, with many programs still emphasizing technical skills over critical thinking and evidence-based practice. The integration of digital health competencies, interprofessional collaboration, and advanced practice roles into curricula has been slow and uneven across the region [5, 14].

Regional response mechanisms

ASEAN mutual recognition arrangements

The ASEAN Mutual Recognition Arrangement (MRA) on Nursing Services, signed in 2006, represents the most significant regional policy response to nursing workforce challenges [15]. The MRA aims to facilitate professional mobility within ASEAN while maintaining quality standards through harmonized competency frameworks and mutual recognition of qualifications [15].

Implementation of the MRA has been gradual and uneven across member states. Brunei and Thailand achieved full conformity early, while Indonesia, Cambodia, and Myanmar demonstrated largely consistent implementation, and Vietnam remained half-complete as of recent assessments [16]. The arrangement has facilitated some nurse mobility, particularly benefiting countries like Brunei, where 15% of nurses are from overseas, including 50 registered through the ASEAN MRA [16].

The MRA established the ASEAN Joint Coordinating Committee on Nursing (AJCCN) to oversee implementation and promote best practices. This committee has worked to develop common core competencies across five domains: professional nursing practice, leadership and management, education and research, professional development, and ethical-legal practice. However, variations in licensing examinations, language requirements, and regulatory frameworks continue to limit the MRA’s effectiveness [15, 17].

National policy innovations

Individual countries have developed diverse policy responses to nursing workforce challenges. Singapore’s Advanced Practice Nurse (APN) program, launched in 2003, exemplifies proactive workforce development to address aging population pressures [18]. The program combines master’s-level education with specialized clinical training, producing nurses capable of diagnosing and managing common conditions. Singapore plans to increase APN numbers to over 200, representing approximately 1.4% of the nursing population [18].

Thailand has implemented comprehensive rural retention strategies combining financial incentives, career development opportunities, and mandatory service requirements. The country’s approach includes providing housing allowances, educational support, and structured career pathways that have shown measurable improvements in rural nurse retention rates [10, 19].

Malaysia’s systematic approach to workforce planning demonstrates another model for regional emulation. The country has maintained detailed nursing workforce databases since 2006, tracking registration, deployment, and retention patterns [20]. This data-driven approach enables targeted interventions and resource allocation based on evidence rather than assumptions [20].

Multilevel solutions framework

Effective responses to nursing workforce challenges require coordinated action across multiple levels. At the macro level, regional cooperation through ASEAN frameworks, policy harmonization, and investment in nursing education infrastructure provide the foundation for sustainable workforce development. The ASEAN Socio-Cultural Community blueprint’s focus on health workforce development creates institutional mechanisms for sustained cooperation [21, 22].

Meso-level solutions center on institutional initiatives to strengthen workplace environments, interprofessional collaboration, and retention strategies [23]. Evidence from recent studies demonstrates that organizational factors significantly influence nurse retention, with supportive leadership, adequate staffing ratios, and professional development opportunities being critical determinants [23, 24].

Micro-level interventions focus on competency-based training, mentorship programs, and leadership development tailored to local health system needs. Singapore’s structured APN development program exemplifies this approach, combining rigorous selection criteria, comprehensive training, and ongoing professional support [18].

Integrative Conceptual Synthesis. The evidence synthesized across this review reveals that nursing workforce challenges in Southeast Asia are not discrete, parallel problems but deeply interconnected phenomena operating through reciprocal feedback loops. Structural legacies of colonialism established hierarchical professional identities and subordinated nursing education to medical priorities, laying the groundwork for persistent competency deficits and low professional status that continue to drive outmigration. Migration, in turn, depletes human capital from source countries, weakening educational systems and institutional mentorship capacity—thereby perpetuating the competency gaps that originally pushed nurses toward migration. These structural-educational dynamics interact with workforce distribution challenges: inequitable deployment of nurses to rural and underserved settings reflects not only incentive failures at the meso level, but also the educational pipeline’s historical orientation toward hospital-based, urban-centric training models rooted in colonial curricula. Meanwhile, workforce shortages intensify workload burdens on remaining nurses, eroding retention and accelerating burnout—a pattern starkly amplified during the COVID-19 pandemic. Regional policy mechanisms such as the ASEAN MRA attempt to manage mobility and harmonize competency standards, yet their effectiveness is constrained upstream by the very educational and governance disparities they seek to resolve. Taken together, these findings suggest that the Southeast Asian nursing workforce crisis is best understood as a system-level phenomenon, in which historical legacies, structural conditions, educational quality, professional identity, migration pressures, and policy responses are co-constitutive rather than independently addressable. Sustainable improvement requires interventions that simultaneously target multiple levels of this system, recognizing that progress at one level may be undermined or amplified by dynamics at another.

Competency development and professional standards

Core competency frameworks

Southeast Asian countries have developed increasingly sophisticated competency frameworks to guide nursing education and practice. The ASEAN nursing competency framework identifies five core domains that provide structure for regional harmonization while allowing national adaptation [20]. These domains encompass professional, ethical, and legal practice; nursing care provision; care management; and professional development [25].

Singapore has developed one of the most comprehensive competency frameworks, organizing registered nurse competencies into four domains: professional, legal and ethical nursing practice; management of care; leadership and nursing management; and professional development. Each domain includes specific competency standards and indicators that guide curriculum development and performance assessment [25].

The evolution toward competency-based education represents a significant shift from traditional task-oriented training to outcome-focused professional development. This transition requires substantial investment in faculty development, assessment methods, and clinical partnerships to ensure competency attainment [14, 25].

Advanced practice role development

The development of advanced practice nursing roles across Southeast Asia reflects recognition of the need for more skilled practitioners to address complex health challenges. Singapore’s APN model has influenced similar developments in other countries, demonstrating the feasibility of expanding nursing scope of practice within existing regulatory frameworks [18].

APNs in Singapore function across four specialties: acute care, medical/surgical, community, and mental health. Their scope includes advanced physical assessment, diagnosis development, laboratory interpretation, care management, and limited prescriptive authority under protocols. This model bridges traditional nursing practice and physician care, addressing workforce gaps while maintaining quality standards [18, 26].

Thailand’s approach to advanced practice development emphasizes integration with existing healthcare teams rather than creation of independent practitioner roles. This model focuses on enhancing clinical expertise within traditional nursing hierarchies, potentially offering greater acceptance and sustainability in contexts where professional boundaries are more rigid [19].

Digital health competency integration

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated recognition of digital health competencies as essential for contemporary nursing practice [14, 23]. Countries across Southeast Asia have begun integrating telehealth, electronic health records, and digital patient monitoring into nursing curricula and continuing education programs.

Singapore has emerged as a regional leader in digital health training for healthcare professionals, offering structured telemedicine courses and telehealth service provision guidelines. The Ministry of Health’s approach prioritizes patient safety while enabling nurses to develop confidence in digital service delivery .

Thailand’s Digital Skill Development Academy (DISDA) has developed specific programs for healthcare workforce digital upskilling, including nursing-focused training modules. This institutional approach ensures systematic competency development rather than ad hoc training initiatives [14].

Crisis response and resilience building

COVID-19 impact and lessons

The COVID-19 pandemic provided an unprecedented test of Southeast Asian nursing workforce resilience and adaptability. Healthcare workers across the region faced extraordinary demands, with nurses bearing particular responsibility for frontline patient care while managing personal and family health risks [23].

Research examining healthcare worker resilience during the pandemic revealed significant variations across countries, with Vietnamese healthcare workers demonstrating the highest resilience levels, followed by Taiwan and Hong Kong, while Nepal scored lowest. These differences were associated with organizational support quality, workplace policy satisfaction, and prior pandemic experience [23].

Countries with previous SARS experience, including Thailand, Vietnam, and Singapore, demonstrated better adaptive capacity during COVID-19, suggesting that organizational learning from previous crises enhances pandemic preparedness (Fig. 5). This finding has implications for regional preparedness strategies and knowledge sharing mechanisms [23].

Impact of COVID-19 pandemic on nursing retention rates across Southeast Asian countries, showing universal decline in workforce stability [23]

The pandemic also highlighted the importance of mental health support for healthcare workers. Studies revealed significant increases in burnout rates, with Indonesian healthcare workers experiencing rates between 68 and 85%, compared to 56–64% in India [27]. These findings underscore the need for systematic approaches to healthcare worker well-being that extend beyond crisis periods .

Organizational resilience strategies

Effective crisis response requires organizational-level resilience building that encompasses multiple dimensions of workforce support. Research from the pandemic period identifies five key retention strategies that proved effective: ensuring safe flexible staffing and quality work environments, enhancing organizational mental health and wellness supports, improved leadership and communication, increasing professional development and mentorship opportunities, and better compensation and incentives [24].

Safe staffing emerged as a critical factor, with healthcare workers in settings with adequate nurse-to-patient ratios demonstrating greater resilience and lower turnover intentions. Several countries have since implemented legislative approaches to mandated staffing ratios, following California’s pioneering model [24].

Mental health and wellness supports became particularly important during the pandemic, with successful programs including psychological coaching, peer support networks, and structured debriefing sessions. These interventions showed measurable impacts on nurse retention and job satisfaction [24].

Regional cooperation in crisis response

The pandemic demonstrated both the potential for and limitations of regional cooperation in health emergency response. ASEAN’s health cooperation mechanisms, while providing frameworks for information sharing and policy coordination, were tested by the scale and urgency of the crisis [28].

Successful regional initiatives included coordinated vaccine procurement, shared clinical protocols, and knowledge exchange platforms that enabled countries to learn from each other’s experiences. However, the predominance of national responses over regional coordination highlighted the need for stronger institutional mechanisms and pre-positioned resources for future emergencies [28].

The nursing workforce’s role in pandemic response has elevated professional recognition and political support in several countries. This enhanced visibility provides opportunities for addressing longstanding workforce challenges through increased investment and policy attention [29].

Contemporary issues and emerging challenges

Demographic transitions and care demands

Southeast Asia is experiencing rapid demographic transitions that fundamentally alter healthcare demands and nursing workforce requirements. The WHO projects that by 2030, 13.7% of Southeast Asians will be over 60 years old, rising to 20.3% by 2050 [30]. This aging trajectory creates unprecedented demands for chronic care management, rehabilitation services, and long-term care support [31].

Countries at different stages of demographic transition face distinct challenges. Singapore and Thailand, with relatively advanced aging populations, are developing specialized geriatric nursing competencies and care delivery models. Malaysia’s Geriatric Nursing Competency Framework provides a template for regional adaptation, emphasizing specialized knowledge and skills for older adult care [25].

The rise of non-communicable diseases compounds demographic pressures. Diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer rates are increasing across the region, requiring nurses with specialized knowledge in chronic disease management, patient education, and family support. These conditions demand different competencies than traditional acute care nursing, necessitating curriculum reforms and continuing education programs [32].

Technology integration and digital health

Digital transformation in healthcare presents both opportunities and challenges for nursing workforce development. Electronic health records, telemedicine platforms, and artificial intelligence applications are reshaping nursing practice while requiring new competencies [14, 33].

ASEAN countries show varying levels of digital health adoption, with Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand leading in infrastructure development and nurse training programs. However, significant gaps remain in rural areas and among older nurses who may lack digital literacy skills [14].

The integration of artificial intelligence into nursing practice represents an emerging frontier that requires careful consideration of professional roles and ethical implications. Research on AI perspectives among Asian nurses reveals both enthusiasm for efficiency gains and concerns about maintaining human-centered care [33].

Professional identity and autonomy

Despite decades of professionalization efforts, nursing in Southeast Asia continues to struggle with professional identity and autonomy issues rooted in historical hierarchies and cultural expectations. The persistence of task-oriented practice models limits nurses’ ability to function as independent practitioners and contributes to job dissatisfaction and turnover [5, 34].

Recent research on Indonesian emergency nurses reveals how practitioners develop personal sustainability strategies to maintain professional commitment despite challenging conditions. These findings suggest that individual resilience, while important, cannot substitute for systematic professional development and workplace improvement [34].

The development of advanced practice roles offers one pathway to enhanced professional autonomy, but implementation requires careful attention to regulatory frameworks, interprofessional relationships, and public acceptance. Singapore’s experience demonstrates that successful APN programs require sustained institutional support and clear scope of practice definitions [18].

Regional variations and country-specific challenges

Philippines: Migration and domestic shortages

The Philippines presents perhaps the most complex nursing workforce dynamics in Southeast Asia, simultaneously serving as the world’s largest source of migrant nurses while experiencing critical domestic shortages. The country produces approximately 15,000 nursing graduates annually but faces a shortfall of 127,000 nurses domestically [35].

This paradox reflects structural issues, including limited domestic employment opportunities, low wages, and poor working conditions, that drive outmigration. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted these tensions when the government temporarily banned the deployment of nurses abroad to address domestic shortages, sparking significant professional and political debate [36, 37].

Recent projections suggest that nurse shortages in the Philippines will reach 90,000 by 2028, requiring 23 years to fill the gap at current production rates [35]. This timeline underscores the need for comprehensive workforce planning that addresses both education capacity and retention strategies.

Indonesia: Scale and distribution challenges

Indonesia’s nursing workforce challenges are fundamentally shaped by the country’s geographic scale and diversity. With over 1 million registered nurses serving a population of 270 million across thousands of islands, distribution rather than absolute numbers represents the primary challenge [3].

The country has achieved its national target of 2.46 nurses per 1,000 population, yet significant regional variations persist. Some provinces experience surpluses while others face critical shortages, particularly in remote areas where infrastructure limitations and cultural barriers complicate nurse deployment [3].

Indonesia’s decentralized governance structure compounds these challenges, with local governments responsible for health workforce planning but dependent on national civil service quotas for nurse hiring. This system creates mismatches between local needs and hiring capacity, contributing to unemployment among new graduates in some areas while shortages persist in others [3].

Thailand: Stability and rural retention

Thailand represents a relative success story in Southeast Asian nursing workforce development, achieving better balance between supply and demand than most regional neighbors. The country produces approximately 9,800 nursing graduates annually and maintains a density of 3.08 nurses per 1,000 population [11].

Thailand’s success stems from comprehensive workforce planning that includes production targets aligned with population needs, structured career development pathways, and effective rural retention strategies. The country’s experience with mandatory rural service, combined with financial incentives and professional development opportunities, has improved geographical distribution [10, 11, 19].

However, Thailand faces emerging challenges related to aging population demands and competition from other sectors for nursing graduates. Recent studies indicate increasing turnover rates and job dissatisfaction among hospital nurses, particularly related to autonomy and workplace relationships [19].

Singapore: Advanced practice leadership

Singapore’s approach to nursing workforce development emphasizes quality over quantity, focusing on advanced practice roles and specialized competencies to address population health needs efficiently. The country’s APN program serves as a regional model for professional development and scope of practice expansion [18, 26].

The success of Singapore’s approach reflects substantial investment in nursing education infrastructure, including partnerships with international universities and structured postgraduate training programs. However, the model’s transferability to other regional contexts remains questionable given Singapore’s unique economic and institutional characteristics [18].

Singapore’s experience demonstrates the importance of regulatory clarity, professional support, and institutional commitment in developing advanced nursing roles. These lessons inform regional discussions about professional development and scope of practice expansion.

Policy implications and recommendations

Strengthening regional cooperation

The evidence reviewed suggests that regional cooperation through ASEAN mechanisms provides essential frameworks for addressing nursing workforce challenges, but implementation requires strengthening institutional capacity and member state commitment. The MRA on Nursing Services has facilitated some professional mobility but remains limited by regulatory differences and bureaucratic barriers [16, 21].

Recommendations for strengthening regional cooperation include expanding the ASEAN Health Division’s human resources to match the scope of regional challenges, developing common standards for nursing education accreditation, and creating regional funding mechanisms for nursing education infrastructure development [21].

The establishment of regional nursing workforce databases would enable evidence-based planning and resource allocation while facilitating monitoring of migration patterns and their impacts on health systems. Such systems require sustained political commitment and technical support to ensure data quality and accessibility .

Investing in education infrastructure

The analysis reveals significant variations in nursing education capacity and quality across Southeast Asia, with implications for workforce competency and regional mobility. Addressing these disparities requires targeted investment in faculty development, clinical training facilities, and curriculum modernization [17, 38].

Priority areas for investment include integration of digital health competencies into all nursing programs, development of advanced practice education tracks, and establishment of regional centers of excellence for nursing education [13, 14, 26]. These initiatives require coordination between government agencies, educational institutions, and healthcare organizations.

Faculty development emerges as a critical bottleneck, with many countries lacking sufficient qualified educators to support program expansion or quality improvement. Regional exchange programs, distance learning initiatives, and partnership arrangements with international institutions offer potential solutions [2].

Addressing retention and working conditions

The evidence consistently identifies working conditions, professional development opportunities, and organizational support as key determinants of nursing retention. Policy responses must address these factors systematically rather than through isolated interventions [13, 19, 24].

Safe staffing ratios emerge as a fundamental requirement for sustainable nursing practice, with legislative approaches showing promise in several jurisdictions. Implementation requires careful attention to context-specific factors including resource availability, skill mix requirements, and institutional capacity [24].

Professional development opportunities, including continuing education, career advancement pathways, and recognition programs, significantly influence retention decisions. Countries should develop comprehensive career frameworks that provide clear progression opportunities within nursing rather than only through movement to other professions [13, 24].

Developing crisis preparedness

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted both individual and systemic resilience factors that enable nursing workforces to respond effectively to health emergencies. Building on these lessons requires systematic attention to organizational capacity, workforce well-being, and surge planning [23].

Regional preparedness strategies should include pre-positioned human resources, coordinated training programs, and mutual aid agreements for health emergency response [28]. These mechanisms require advance planning and resource commitment but prove essential during crisis periods.

Mental health support for healthcare workers must extend beyond crisis periods to include routine organizational wellness programs, peer support networks, and professional counseling services. Evidence from pandemic response demonstrates that proactive well-being support enhances both individual resilience and organizational effectiveness [24, 27].

Future directions and research priorities

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. Data availability and quality vary significantly across Southeast Asian countries, with some nations having limited published research on nursing workforce issues. The rapidly evolving nature of nursing workforce challenges means some data sources may not fully capture recent developments, particularly COVID-19 impacts and recovery patterns. The heterogeneity of political systems, healthcare structures, and economic contexts across Southeast Asia limits generalizability of specific interventions. Several areas require additional research and policy attention: migration impact assessments, competency development research, technology integration and workforce implications, rigorous longitudinal evaluation tracking career trajectories, and regional nursing workforce monitoring systems.

Toward an evolving paradigm of workforce design The evidence mapped in this review charts the traditional landscape of supply, distribution, and competency challenges. However, the field is undergoing a deeper conceptual transformation that this review’s findings must be situated within. Emerging scholarship is moving beyond static supply-and-demand models toward dynamic, systems-level frameworks that reconceptualize nursing staffing as an adaptive equilibrium shaped by workload variability, competency configuration, and health system efficiency. Of particular relevance to Southeast Asia is the accelerating integration of artificial intelligence and digital health technologies into clinical environments, which is beginning to reconfigure the nature of nursing work itself. AI-assisted clinical decision support, automated documentation, remote monitoring platforms, and the emerging domain of physical AI (i.e., robotic systems operating within care environments) are creating new forms of human–technology collaboration that challenge conventional assumptions about skill mix and role boundaries. In these technology-enabled environments, workforce planning can no longer be reduced to questions of nurse-to-patient ratios or qualification levels alone; it must also account for the co-optimization of human competencies and technological capabilities to achieve safe, efficient, and equitable care. For Southeast Asia, where healthcare digitalization is advancing unevenly but rapidly, this transition presents both an opportunity and a risk: countries that proactively develop adaptive nursing competencies for technology-integrated environments may leapfrog traditional workforce bottlenecks, while those that do not may find that digital adoption widens rather than narrows existing inequities. Future research and policy work in the region should therefore extend beyond the workforce challenges identified in this review to explore how nursing roles, competency frameworks, and educational systems can be redesigned for health systems in which human and technological capacities are increasingly interdependent.

Conclusion

Addressing nursing workforce challenges in Southeast Asia is complex. While progress has been made in expanding educational capacity and ASEAN cooperation, persistent challenges related to geographical distribution, outmigration, and quality standards demand sustained coordinated responses. The COVID-19 pandemic elevated nursing’s profile and demonstrated workforce adaptability, creating opportunities for increased investment and policy attention. Success depends on comprehensive workforce planning, investment in educational infrastructure, strengthened working conditions, and effective regional cooperation. Moving forward, countries must balance international nurse mobility with domestic health system requirements while developing advanced practice roles and digital health competencies that support professional growth and contributions to regional health goals. The distinctive contribution of this review lies not only in mapping what is known about the region’s nursing workforce, but in surfacing how these well-documented challenges are structurally interrelated. By tracing the reciprocal relationships among colonial legacies, educational capacity, professional identity, migration, and policy responses across macro, meso, and micro levels, this review offers an integrated explanatory framework—rather than a catalogue of problems—that can inform a new generation of evidence-based, systemically coherent workforce strategies for Southeast Asia. Crucially, the review also positions these findings at the threshold of an emerging transformation in healthcare work, one in which the configuration of nursing roles, competencies, and skill mix must increasingly account for technology-enabled environments and the co-optimization of human and digital capacities. Addressing nursing workforce challenges in this evolving landscape requires not only the historically-informed, multi-level strategies identified in this review, but also a forward-looking commitment to redesigning nursing’s role within health systems of the future.
