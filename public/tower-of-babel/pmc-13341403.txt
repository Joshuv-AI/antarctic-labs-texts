Maternal Hypoglycemia and Reduced Foetal Growth in Pregnancies Following Roux‐en‐Y Gastric Bypass: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Louise L. Stentebjerg, Lene R. Madsen, René K. Støving, Lise Lotte T. Andersen, Claus B. Juhl, Christina A. Vinter, Dorte M. Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe risk of delivering a small‐for‐gestational‐age (SGA) neonate is increased after Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass (RYGB). Because glucose is the foetus's primary energy source and postprandial hypoglycemia is common after RYGB, the Bariatric surgery And consequences for Mother and Baby In pregnancy (BAMBI) study examined associations between trimester specific maternal hypoglycemia and offspring birthweight as well as other perinatal outcomes.Materials and MethodsTwenty‐three pregnant women with RYGB and 23 BMI‐ and parity‐matched controls were followed prospectively with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and foetal ultrasounds in each trimester. At birth, neonatal anthropometric measures, including skinfold measurements, were obtained. Exposure to hypoglycaemia was defined as spending > 4% of the time with interstitial glucose < 3.5 mmol/L. Multivariable regression was used to examine associations between exposure to hypoglycaemia in each trimester and birthweight, adjusting for maternal age, parity, pre‐pregnancy body mass index, gestational weight gain and surgery‐to‐conception interval.ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar regardless of exposure to hypoglycemia. Spending > 4% of the time in hypoglycemia during the second or third trimester was associated with a 550 g lower birthweight (95% CI [−1031–−69], p = 0.03), while no such association was observed for the first trimester. Infants exposed to second/third trimester hypoglycemia had lower abdominal circumference percentiles (20th vs. 55th, p = 0.01) and lighter placentas (580 g vs. 705 g, p = 0.01).ConclusionIncreased maternal hypoglycemia in the second or third trimester of pregnancy post‐RYGB appears to be associated with reduced foetal growth. Optimising glucose levels through CGM may help prevent SGA births in this population. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03713060

Introduction

Undernutrition increases the risk of chronic diseases later in life [1, 2]. While Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass (RYGB) before pregnancy improves obstetrical and neonatal outcomes related to obesity [3], it is also associated with reduced foetal growth and increased risk of small‐for‐gestational age (SGA) offspring [4, 5]. By definition, 10% of offspring should be classified as SGA at delivery (below the 10th percentile), but 15%–26% of offspring are SGA in pregnancies following RYGB [5, 6, 7, 8]. Given the prevalence of RYGB in women of reproductive age, identifying modifiable factors to reduce SGA risk is crucial [9].

Maternal nutrient deficiency and insufficient gestational weight gain (GWG) following RYGB are known contributors to SGA [10, 11]. About half of SGA cases occur in women with insufficient GWG [6, 7, 12], indicating that other factors also contribute. The rapid weight loss phase within the first year post‐surgery may impair foetal growth, potentially increasing SGA risk with shorter surgery‐to‐conception intervals. However, evidence is conflicting and a meta‐analysis of 981 women found no increased SGA risk with conception within 1 year post‐RYGB [13].

Recently, we have reported an increased risk of hypoglycemia during pregnancy in women after RYGB [14, 15]. Glucose serves as the primary source of energy for foetal and placental growth [16]. As foetal gluconeogenesis is minimal, the foetus relies entirely on maternal glucose delivery and maternal hypoglycemia could affect birthweight [16]. Indeed, lower glucose nadirs during OGTT in pregnancies following bariatric surgery have been correlated with birthweight [17, 18]. A study based on the Dutch war‐induced famine of 1944–1945 showed that nutritional restriction in the first trimester led to larger placentas, compensating for foetal undernutrition. In contrast, famine in late pregnancy reduced both birthweight, placental weight and placenta‐to‐birthweight ratio, indicating a lack of compensation [19]. Maric et al. [18] assessed placental function by uterine artery Doppler and found that it was unaffected by bariatric surgery, but placental size has not been studied.

The association between maternal postbariatric hypoglycemia and foetal growth remains underexplored. Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether offspring birthweight is associated with maternal hypoglycemia in the first, second or third trimester in women with RYGB. Furthermore, we wanted to explore the impact of maternal hypoglycemia on other perinatal outcomes and whether exposure to hypoglycemia correlates with being SGA during pregnancy, as assessed by estimated foetal weight (EFW).

Methods

Study Population

This was a prospective, observational study performed as part of the Bariatric surgery And consequences for Mother and Baby In pregnancy (BAMBI) study registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03713060). The BAMBI cohort was recruited from Odense University Hospital (Odense, Denmark) from April 2019 to November 2021. Pregnant women who had undergone RYGB surgery before conception were eligible for inclusion. For each of these women, a pregnant control without a history of bariatric surgery was recruited, matched by age, parity, and pre‐pregnancy body mass index (BMI).

Women aged 18 to 45 years with a singleton pregnancy who were booked for antenatal care before 14 weeks of gestation were recruited. We excluded women with overt diabetes, a history of type 2 diabetes, severe medical illness, severe psychiatric illness, alcohol consumption or smoking. Furthermore, pregnancies resulting in miscarriage, termination, stillbirth or delivery of a phenotypically abnormal infant were excluded from further analyses. Gestational diabetes mellitus in a previous pregnancy was not cause for exclusion. Medical history of all women was extracted from medical records and validated by interview.

Continuous Glucose Monitoring

We applied the Dexcom G6 sensor (Dexcom Inc., San Diego, CA) in gestational week 12–14 (first trimester), 24 (second trimester), 34 (third trimester) and 4–6 weeks postpartum. The sensor was inserted in the subcutaneous tissue on the abdomen for 10 days. With one reading every 5 min, the sensor provided up to 288 readings of interstitial glucose (IG) per day. We excluded data from the initial 24 h of the monitoring.

The mean IG, time in range (TIR), time above range (TAR), time below range (TBR), and time with very low IG were determined. The target glucose range was defined as IG 3.5–7.8 mmol/L and very low as IG < 3.0 mmol/L in accordance with the international consensus on glucose targets in pregnancy for women with type 1 or 2 diabetes as well as GDM, as specific CGM targets for healthy pregnant women or women treated with RYGB are not currently available [20]. Additional CGM metrics including coefficient of variation, standard deviation, and diurnal variation have been comprehensively reported for the BAMBI cohort and are therefore not repeated [14].

Foetal Ultrasound

Foetal ultrasound examinations were performed at five time points during pregnancy. A first‐trimester ultrasound (gestational weeks 11 + 3 to 13 + 6) was conducted as part of combined screening for major chromosomal abnormalities, with gestational age determined by crown–rump length. A second trimester screening (gestational weeks 18 + 0 to 22 + 0) was performed according to national guidelines. Ultrasounds for foetal weight estimation were conducted at gestational weeks 28, 32 and 36 including measurements of head circumference (HC), biparietal diameter (BPD), abdominal circumference (AC) and femur length (FL). The head/abdominal circumference ratio was calculated to assess disproportionate intrauterine foetal growth. The HC, BPD, AC and FL percentiles were calculated using the formula described by Verburg et al. [21]. Foetal weight was estimated using the Hadlock D formula [22, 23] and EFW percentiles were calculated using the Marsál formula [24], adjusted for gestational age and sex. SGA was defined as weight below the 10th percentile, based on ultrasound‐estimated foetal weight or birthweight [25].

Experienced sonographers from the antenatal clinic at Odense University Hospital performed all foetal ultrasounds using the Voluson GE Ultrasound System (GE Healthcare, Arlington, TX, USA).

Body Fat Estimation by Skinfold Measurements

Within 72 h after delivery, skinfold measurements to estimate neonatal fat mass were performed using the Harpenden skinfold calliper (Baty International, West Sussex, UK). The skinfolds were measured by two examiners at four locations: flank, thigh, subscapular, and triceps. All measurements were performed on the right side of the body of the neonate. We calculated the neonatal body fat percent using the neonate's birthweight, length and flank skinfold as proposed by Catalano et al. [26].

Statistical Analysis

This study represents a post hoc analysis of the BAMBI cohort. The sample size was originally determined based on nadir plasma glucose levels during a mixed meal test, as previously described [15]. To assess statistical power for this post hoc regression analysis, a sensitivity power analysis for multiple linear regression was performed to estimate the minimum detectable effect size given the sample size and number of predictors. Model stability was further evaluated using bootstrap resampling with 2000 replications to obtain bias‐corrected confidence intervals for regression coefficients. The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05.

We used the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) software hosted at Open data Explorative Network (OPEN), Odense University Hospital (Odense, Denmark) for collection and management of data. Medians and interquartile ranges were calculated for continuous variables, and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for comparison between groups. For categorical variables, counts and percentages are reported, and the Fisher's exact test was used for comparison. We pooled all CGM measurements for the groups and calculated means and SDs for TIR, TAR, TBR and time below very low range. Student's t‐test was used for comparison of these. The 24‐h mean profiles of IG were calculated by pooling 30‐min IG means of each participant, and afterwards calculating 30‐min group means. We used standard errors of the difference in means to calculate 95% CIs. Lastly, we investigated the association between increased time spent in hypoglycemia (yes/no) and foetal growth by multiple linear regression adjusted for age, parity, pre‐pregnancy BMI, GWG and surgery‐to‐conception interval. Increased time spent below range was set at > 4% and below very low > 1% of the time [20]. In case of missing data on skinfold measurements, the women with a missing match were re‐matched. Holm‐Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple testing.

Ethical Approval

The BAMBI study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Region of Southern Denmark (27 July 2017; S‐20160134). All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

We enrolled 50 pregnant women in the BAMBI cohort. Of these, 46 completed the BAMBI protocol—23 in each group, matched one‐to‐one by BMI and parity. The women with RYGB were 4 years (IQR: 0–7) older than the matched controls. No congenital malformations, stillbirths, or shoulder dystocia were detected. During the study period, four women dropped out owing to pregnancy resulting in miscarriage (n = 2), fear of contracting COVID‐19 (n = 1) and nausea/vomiting (n = 1).

Prior to conception, women with RYGB reduced their BMI from 45 kg/m2 (IQR 42–54) pre‐surgery to 32 kg/m2 (IQR 27–39), corresponding to a total weight loss of 35%. The median surgery‐to‐conception interval was 30 months (IQR 15–89). Regression analysis showed that spending > 4% of the time with IG < 3.5 mmol/L in the second or third trimester of pregnancy was associated with a 550 g lower birthweight (95% CI [−1031–−69 g], p = 0.03), adjusted for parity, pre‐pregnancy BMI, age, GWG, and surgery‐to‐conception interval (Table 2). GWG was positively associated with birthweight with a coefficient of 24 g per kg weight change (95% CI [3–45], p = 0.03). Surgery‐to‐conception interval tended to be positively associated with birthweight with an increase of 5 g per month (95% CI [−2–13], p = 0.14).

Due to low numbers of SGA neonates, regression analysis on this outcome was not performed. In the RYGB group, six neonates were born SGA (five males, one female), compared to one in the control group (male) (Table 1A).

The results of the offspring's anthropometrics are summarised in Table 1B. The AC percentile was lower (20th vs. 55th percentile, p = 0.01), and the head circumference percentile and the length of the neonate were shorter (50th vs. 55th, p = 0.07, and 49 vs. 51 cm, p = 0.11, respectively), although these differences were not statistically significant. There were no differences observed in body fat mass or body fat percentage.

Placenta weight was similar between women treated with RYGB and matched controls (Table 1A), yet significantly lower among the RYGB‐treated women with increased time in hypoglycemia compared to those with acceptable time in hypoglycemia during the second or third trimester (580 vs. 705 g, p = 0.01) (Table 1B). There were no significant differences between groups on placenta‐to‐birthweight ratio, but the placenta‐to‐birthweight ratio was increased in the RYGB group compared to controls (Table 1A), driven by the women with acceptable time in hypoglycemia (Table 1B).

Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity power analysis indicated that, with the available sample size (n = 23) and six predictors in the model, the study had 80% power to detect a birthweight difference of approximately 252 g. Bootstrap resampling (2000 replications) of the multivariable regression yielded a birthweight coefficient of similar magnitude with a 95% bootstrap CI [−1113–14 g], p = 0.056. One potentially influential observation was identified among the seven women with increased time in hypoglycaemia. Excluding this observation from the multivariable analysis reduced the birthweight regression coefficient from 550 g to 425 g, p = 0.12.

Significant associations remained statistically significant after Holm‐Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.

Discussion

We showed that spending > 4% of the time with interstitial glucose < 3.5 mmol/L in the second or third trimester of pregnancy was associated with a 550 g decrease in birthweight. After adjusting for other established contributors to reduced foetal growth, increased time spent in hypoglycemia remained an independent risk factor of reduced foetal growth. Additionally, we observed that the placental weight was lower in women with RYGB who spent increased time in hypoglycemia.

In order to reduce the number of SGA neonates born to women treated with RYGB, we need to clarify the aetiology. Adherence to the IOM guidelines for appropriate GWG [6, 7] and postponement of pregnancy for 1 year post surgery have been recommended [7]. However, the latter is still under discussion [13]. Results from our study suggest a third target of intervention: glycemic monitoring and optimisation of glucose metabolism in the second and third trimester. Given the potential for a 550 g increase in birthweight by optimising glucose levels, interventions targeting glucose metabolism in these women appear to be clinically relevant. Raising awareness of hypoglycemic symptoms among pregnant women treated with RYGB, screening for hypoglycemia, and giving dietary advice to avoid postprandial hypoglycemia could be first line of action.

Exposure to hypoglycemia during the second or third trimester was determining for foetal growth, as previously observed in studies of the Dutch famine [19]. The offspring of women treated with RYGB with increased time in hypoglycemia in the second or third trimester had lower placenta weight and a tendency towards lower birthweight and placenta‐to‐birthweight ratio compared to those with acceptable time in hypoglycemia like the offspring of the women who experienced the Dutch famine in mid‐ and late‐pregnancy [19]. It is therefore conceivable that in pregnancies after RYGB, women not exposed to significant hypoglycemia may be able to compensate for potential restricted foetal growth by increasing placental size, whereas women with ongoing hypoglycemia cannot. This is in line with the placenta using glucose as its primary source of energy [16].

The aetiology of reduced foetal growth is multifactorial. Owing to the eligibility criteria, some of the known causes of foetal growth restriction are ruled out in advance for the women in the BAMBI cohort (smoking, alcohol, substance abuse, renal disease, diabetes with vascular disease etc.) [27]. Notably, five out of six SGA neonates born to the women with RYGB were male. Female foetuses are more insulin resistant and have the same amount of subcutaneous fat as their larger male counterparts [28]. Consistent with this, female neonates in the BAMBI study tended to have a higher body fat percentage than male neonates (data not shown). We speculate that in the BAMBI cohort female foetuses are protected against reduced growth, which could be due to their increased insulin resistance. However, the BAMBI study does not have power to stratify for gender, and we did not find a difference in body fat mass or body fat percentage between the women with RYGB and increased time in hypoglycemia compared to those with acceptable time in hypoglycemia. Thus, on the question of whether hypoglycemia engages in the tendency towards a reduction in body fat mass and body fat percentage we observed in the offspring born to women with RYGB, our results should be challenged in a larger cohort.

The BAMBI study has several strengths. First, the prospective cohort design allowed for collection of detailed longitudinal data. The thoroughly matched control group enhanced interpretation of the results. All women were examined by ultrasound at the antenatal clinic of experienced sonographers. Additionally, the repeated use of CGM during pregnancy and postpartum provided an extensive dataset of women's glucose profiles. Finally, we employed state‐of‐the‐art CGM technology, validated even in the hypoglycemic range, although abdominal sensor placement may have slightly limited the precision during late pregnancy [29, 30]. It was technically impossible to disable the urgent low alarm on the sensor, why the women were alerted if their IG dropped below 3.1 mmol/L. Thus, if the women have responded to the alarm by ingesting carbohydrates, the time in hypoglycemia might be underestimated. Theoretically, the foetus will benefit from this ingestion of carbohydrates as well and the association between hypoglycemia and foetal growth will be unaffected by the urgent low alarms. The results of the study are solely based on women with gastric bypass. It would be of interest to investigate if a similar association is present for pregnant women who have undergone gastric sleeve surgery. Owing to local COVID‐19 restrictions during the BAMBI study period, skinfold measurements were subject to missing data, which compromised the power of the results. Furthermore, as these analyses were conducted post hoc, the sample size was not determined a priori for this specific research question. While sensitivity power analyses indicated adequate power to detect a clinically meaningful effect, and the observed estimate exceeded this threshold, the small sample size limits the precision and stability of the estimates. This is reflected in the wide confidence intervals observed in bootstrap analyses. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, but indicate a clinically relevant association that warrants confirmation in larger, prospectively designed studies.

These data suggest that maternal hypoglycemia independently contributes to restricted foetal growth among the offspring of women with RYGB, with notable clinical relevance. Whether hypoglycemia affects body composition as well should be investigated in a larger cohort. Assessment of the effect of maternal hypoglycemia on offspring lean body mass and fat mass in male and female neonates is of interest. We have previously shown that longer surgery‐to‐conception interval, lower nadir weight post‐surgery, and greater weight loss are associated with hypoglycemia [14]. CGM could be used to screen for hypoglycemia in defined risk groups. Reducing the incidence of SGA neonates among women with RYGB may help mitigate the associated risks and improve long‐term health outcomes in the offspring.
