Revisiting the facial root exit zone in hemifacial spasm
Authors: Ken Matsushima, Toshio Matsushima, Marc Sindou
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Drs. Kawalec and Kaufmann for their thoughtful and anatomically precise comments on our narrative review, and for further clarifying the operative target in microvascular decompression (MVD) for hemifacial spasm (HFS) [4, 5]. We also appreciate their generous comments regarding our attempt to describe the evolution of MVD through the complementary concepts of anatomical “maps” and physiological “compasses.”

The purpose of our review was not to redefine the operative target in HFS in technical detail, but to summarize the historical evolution by which anatomical knowledge of the cerebellopontine angle and physiological monitoring have shaped contemporary MVD. In that context, we intended to describe how, in the early period of MVD, the concept of the root entry/exit zone (REZ) was not yet uniformly emphasized across neurovascular compression syndromes, and how accumulated operative experience gradually brought increasing attention to the REZ as a physiologically vulnerable region. We therefore used the term REZ in its broad historical and physiological sense, referring to the region of central myelin vulnerability where neurovascular compression may generate hyperactive cranial nerve syndromes [2, 5]. Indeed, Guclu et al. noted that the REZ, although lacking a formal taxonomic definition for cranial nerves, is generally considered to include the transitional zone of Obersteiner–Redlich, the central myelin portion of the root, and the adjacent subpial surface of the brainstem [2].

When considering the facial REZ specifically, however, as Drs. Kawalec and Kaufmann emphasize, the physiologically vulnerable region in HFS can be considered not only in relation to the short segment between the root detachment point and the transitional zone, but also in relation to its more proximal extension along the facial root. In this context, the terminology of the root exit point, attached segment, root detachment point, and transitional zone provides an additional clinically relevant anatomical framework [3, 4]. A focus limited to the distal cisternal facial nerve risks missing the true culprit compression; therefore, systematic inspection and decompression of the proximal facial root exit zone from the pontomedullary sulcus toward the transitional zone are important.

On the other hand, although uncommon, vascular compression of the cisternal facial nerve distal to the transitional zone has also been reported as a cause of HFS [6]. Thus, the site of culprit compression and the pathophysiology of HFS should not be oversimplified. The anatomical and physiological significance of the REZ/facial REZ, as well as the relationship between the site of culprit compression and symptom generation in HFS, still require further clarification.

This discussion reinforces one of the central messages of our review: the importance of selecting individualized anatomical corridors for each neurovascular compression syndrome. The infrafloccular route for HFS is important precisely because it facilitates access to the proximal facial root exit zone by following the lower cranial nerve region toward the brainstem, thereby avoiding a misguided focus on the distal facial nerve alone [1]. Thus, the expanded concept of the facial root exit zone proposed by Drs. Kawalec and Kaufmann is complementary to the anatomical “map” emphasized in our review.

We welcome this Letter as an important refinement of the anatomical terminology and operative target in HFS. Further anatomical, physiological, and clinical investigations are warranted to deepen our understanding of the REZ/facial REZ and the pathophysiology of HFS.
