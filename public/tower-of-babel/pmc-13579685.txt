Long-term outcomes of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survivors in New Zealand: A prospective, longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Rachael L Parke, Glenn Eastwood, Anna Hunt, Anne Turner, Leanlove Navarra, Anais Charles-Nelson, Michael Bailey, Shay McGuinness, TAME study investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOut-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a devastating event that can have ongoing neurological and functional sequelae for survivors, which impact their whānau/family and society. Information on long-term survival remains incomplete. We aimed to understand the long-term neurological, functional, and health-related quality of life outcomes and survival of patients with OHCA at 24 months in New Zealand.DesignWe performed a preplanned prospective, longitudinal cohort study. Setting New Zealand Participants Participants enrolled in the Targeted Therapeutic Mild Hypercapnia after resuscitated Cardiac Arrest (TAME trial) in New Zealand.Main outcome measuresParticipants had a comprehensive follow-up at 6 and 24 months using the Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended to determine neurological outcome as well as multiple other patient-centred functional outcome assessment tools.ResultsWe included all 200 TAME participants enrolled in New Zealand, of whom 194 (97%) had 24-month data. There was no difference in the primary and secondary TAME study outcomes between groups, with overall 56.3% of participants having a favourable neurological outcome at 6 months. In the New Zealand subpopulation, functional and health-related quality of life status at 24 months was not worse than at 6 months for most survivors, with very few participants reporting more than slight disability.ConclusionsAmong New Zealand TAME trial participants, neurological and functional outcomes measured across multiple domains generally remained stable or improved between 6 and 24 months. These findings provide important information for survivors of OHCA, their whānau/family, clinicians, and researchers on the timing and trajectory of neurological and functional recovery.Keywords: Cognitive outcome, Functional outcome, Neurological outcome, Hypercapnia, Normocapnia

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a common and catastrophic event. In New Zealand, in 2023–2024, there were 2547 cardiac arrest (CA) events in adults aged >15 years where resuscitation was attempted.1 Of these, 24% were admitted to hospital, and 11% survived to 30 days.1 The overall incidence is 134/100 000 person-years and is 30% more frequent in Māori (Indigenous people of New Zealand).1

Devastating neurological injury, leading to death or ongoing severe neurological impairment, is a common outcome for those admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).2,3 For those who survive to hospital discharge, neurological injury ranges from cognitive impairment to permanent, severe disability.4,5 The disabling effects of CA may persist, and the human and financial costs are substantial.6

While evidence exists of near-term health-related quality of life and functional outcomes,7,8 information on longer-term survival and recovery remains poorly described. To our knowledge, only one recent study has reported extended follow-up to 24 months after OHCA.8 Knowledge of recovery trajectories is important to inform patient- and family-centred care and goals-of-care conversations and to describe the ongoing burden of recovery and long-term sequelae for patients, families and the broader community.

The Targeted Therapeutic Mild Hypercapnia after resuscitated Cardiac Arrest (TAME) trial assessed targeted mild hypercapnia (TTMH) compared to targeted normocapnia in patients with coma after OHCA and found that targeted mild hypercapnia did not improve neurological outcomes at 6 months.5 We aimed to describe the neurological, functional, health-related quality of life and mortality status of participants from New Zealand enrolled in TAME 2 years postrandomisation.

Methods

Study design and participants

We performed a preplanned nested cohort study of participants from New Zealand who were enrolled in the TAME trial.5 TAME enrolled 1700 participants from 63 ICUs in 17 countries from March 2018 to September 2021 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03114033; registered November 4, 2017). The study was approved in New Zealand by the Northern A Health and Disability Ethics Committee (#17NTA134, September 21, 2017).

The protocol, statistical analysis plan, and primary results of the TAME study have been previously published.5,9 Prospective informed consent was not possible; however, written informed consent for continued participation and follow-up was obtained from survivors who regained capacity to do so. Where participants did not regain capacity, consultation took place with relatives/friends/family members to ascertain the participant's likely views. Although we planned to undertake this study in Australia and New Zealand, funding was only obtained to complete this work in New Zealand.

Participants were aged ≥18 years, resuscitated after an OHCA of presumed cardiac or unknown cause who had a sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) for ≥20 min without chest compressions and were eligible for intensive care without restriction. Patients were excluded if time from ROSC to screening was more than 180 min, or CA was unwitnessed with an initial rhythm of asystole. Participants were randomly assigned to targeted mild hypercapnia (PaCO2 50–55 mmHg) or to targeted normocapnia (PaCO2 35–45 mmHg) for a 24-h period from the time of randomisation. Further details on TAME eligibility, intervention, and follow-up procedures are presented elsewhere.5,9

Data collection

Participants were followed for 24 months postrandomisation and contacted by a local site research coordinator to confirm contact details and agreement to ongoing participation before interview. Centrally based, trained, independent outcome assessors blinded to allocation contacted the participant (as the primary candidate) or their nominated relative/friend/whānau member at 24 months. Data were entered into a web-based case report form provided by Spinnaker Software, Wellington, New Zealand. A risk management protocol guided escalation of care in case of reports of distress or identified risk to the participant.

Study outcomes

Outcomes at 24 months were assessed using the same tools that were used at 6 months in the TAME trial. The primary outcome measure was the difference in a favourable neurological outcome, defined as a Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOSE) score of 5–8 at 6 months between groups.10

The secondary outcomes included mortality, functional recovery assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS),11 participant-assessed quality of life using EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L), and cognitive function using Montreal Cognitive Assessment - Blind (MoCA-Blind), Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE), and Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT)[12], [13], [14], [15] (see Supplemental Table S1).

Vital status at 24 months was assessed either by the research coordinator reviewing medical records, including the New Zealand National Death Index or by telephone contact with participants, a nominated family member or a general practitioner.

Statistical analyses

The sample size was fixed by the number of TAME participants enrolled in Aotearoa New Zealand. Consequently, the study was not prospectively powered for the 24-month treatment comparisons, and effect estimates are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to indicate their precision.

Binary variables are reported as absolute numbers and percentages, normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation), and discrete or nonnormally distributed variables are reported as medians (interquartile range). Participants with missing primary outcome were excluded from the analysis of the primary end point. Missing data were not imputed.

The primary outcome (favourable neurological outcome) and binomial secondary outcomes were analysed using generalised linear regression with binomial distribution and log link function and robust sandwich errors to account for clustering at a site level. Results are presented as relative risks (95% CI).

Ordinal secondary outcomes (mRS and GOSE) were analysed using a cumulative logistic model or a partial proportional odds ratio (OR) in accordance with proportionality assumptions, which were assessed using the Brant–Wald test. Results are presented as cumulative odds ratio or partial OR (95% CI).

Continuous outcomes (EQ-5D, MoCA, and IQCODE) were analysed using quantile regression with standard errors estimated using the clustered version of bootstrap. The results are presented as median difference (95% CI). Time to death was analysed using Cox regression, including a random effect for site with proportionality assumptions assessed by Schoenfeld residuals. The results are presented as hazard ratio and 95% CI estimated by the bootstrap method. Predefined multivariate sensitivity analysis for all outcomes included co-enrolment in the TTM trial as a covariate. Analysis was undertaken using R version 4.3.2 and a two-sided P-value of 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Similar to the TAME trial,5 we found no difference in favourable neurological outcome between groups in the New Zealand subpopulation at 24 months: 46/87 (52.9%) vs 48/88 (54.5%; Relative risk (RR) 0.97: 95% CI 0.67–1.40, P = 0.869). Baseline characteristics of the entire New Zealand population are shown by group and outcome in Supplemental Tables S2 and S3. The primary and secondary outcomes by group are shown in Supplemental Table S4.

To describe the trajectory of patients with OHCA in New Zealand over time, we report the data as a single cohort rather than by treatment allocation to reflect the above result.

Participants

Of 1700 TAME trial participants, 200 (11.8%) were recruited from eight sites in New Zealand. Six participants had no outcome data available at either 6 or 24 months as they had left New Zealand (alive) before 6 months and could not be contacted. Of the remaining 194 (97%), all had at least partial outcome data at 6 and 24 months. Survival status was available for 194/200 (97%) participants at both 6 and 24 months; GOSE in 190/200 (95%) and 175/200 (88%) at 6 and 24 months respectively; and quality-of-life assessments (EQ5D and mRS) were available for 190/200 (95%) and 175/200 (88%) at 6 and 24 months, respectively (see Fig. 1).

Survival

Of 200 participants, 148 (74%) left ICU alive, and 138 (69%) left hospital alive. At 30 days, 134/198 (67%) were alive, 127/194 (65.4%) were alive at 6 months, and 119/194 (61.3%) were alive at 24 months.

Neurological outcome

At 6 and 24 months, 107/190 (56.3%) and 94/175 (53.7%) participants, respectively, had a favourable neurological outcome (GOSE ≥5) (Table 2). Excluding participants who had died, the number of participants reporting a poor neurological outcome (GOSE 2–4) was 16/123 (13%) at 6 months and 6/100 (6%) at 24 months.

Functional status

mRS collected at baseline from family members showed 196/200 (98%) had a favourable functional status (mRS of 0–2), and 4/200 (2%) had an mRS of 3. At 6 months, 105/190 (55.3%) had a favourable functional status, and at 24 months, 95/175 (54.3%) had a favourable functional status. Excluding participants who had died, the number of participants reporting a poor functional status (mRS 3–5) was 18/123 (14.6%) at 6 months and 5/100 (5%) at 24 months (Table 2).

Health-related quality of life

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) showed a median (IQR) EQ-5D-5L in surviving participants of 0.8 (0.0, 0.9) and 0.8 (0.0, 1.0) at 6 and 24 months, respectively. Participants reported a median (IQR) EQ-5D-5L visual analogue score of 80.0 (70, 90) at both 6 and 24 months.

Cognitive function

Cognitive function (MoCA Blind with dysfunction defined as a score of <18) was present at 6 months in 37/118 participants (31.3%) and 20/97 (20.6%) at 24 months (Table 2). Cognitive decline (IQCODE with the respondent answering the questions with reference being the participants’ pre-OHCA function at both time points, with cognitive decline defined as a score ≥3.01) occurred in 63/120 (52.5%) participants at 6 months and in 29/92 (31.5%) at 24 months.

Comparison of neurological and functional outcomes over time

Participants exhibiting good to moderate recovery at 1 month either improved or remained stable when GOSE and mRS were assessed at 6 months (Fig. 2), and improvement appeared to continue out to 24 months. GOSE at 1 month was only available for the subset of participants who were also enrolled in the TTM II study.

Functional and HRQoL status improved from randomisation to 1 month and then did not differ significantly between 6 and 24 months. Of note, measures of cognitive decline, cognitive dysfunction, and HRQoL did not change significantly over time by ethnicity (Supplemental Table S6).

Discussion

This preplanned, nested cohort study assessed neurological, functional, HRQoL, and mortality outcomes in TAME participants in New Zealand. This is one of the few studies to prospectively assess outcomes in OHCA survivors in the setting of a randomised controlled trial using blinded, independent outcome assessors. There were three key findings. First, there were no between-group differences for all outcomes at 24 months. Second, a favourable neurological outcome at 6 months persisted until 24 months. Third, a surprising number of participants who reported deficits in functional outcomes at 6 months reported improvements in symptoms at 24 months using patient-centred metrics.

Limited evidence is available to guide clinicians, survivors, and families regarding neurological, functional, HRQoL, and cognitive recovery following OHCA, particularly beyond 6 months.

External validity of these findings should be considered in light of the selected TAME-NZ cohort, which included only patients with OHCA who survived resuscitation, achieved sustained ROSC, and were eligible for enrolment. This cohort differs from that reported in a New Zealand–wide OHCA registry;16 for example, TAME-NZ participants were younger, more often men, more likely to have had a resuscitation attempt, and ethnicity was differently distributed. These differences may reflect survivor and selection biases inherent in enrolling only resuscitated patients who reached hospital and met trial eligibility. Nonetheless, the cohort's demographic and clinical characteristics are consistent with other longitudinal OHCA trial populations.8,17,18 Although a Hawthorne effect cannot be excluded, it was not measured, and its impact on long-term outcomes is uncertain.

One previous study showed no significant change in functional status between 6 and 12 months.19 That single-centre study assessed 45 survivors of CA; however, only 26 completed follow-up at all time points (1, 3, 6, and 12 months).19 Recently, Hultgren et al. reported functional and cognitive outcomes to 24 months in 835 OHCA survivors.8,20 They reported targeted temperature management after OHCA did not affect cognitive function at 24 months.8

We found cognitive dysfunction in 31% of participants at 6 months and 21% at 24 months. There is little to inform regarding this outcome longer term; however, our 6-month incidence is lower than previous reports of 26.4–56%.18,21,22

Ongoing cognitive impairment has been found in general ICU populations,23 including those with septic shock.24 A systematic review including 19 studies found a wide range of cognitive impairment identified in 4–62% of adult general, medical and surgical ICU survivors in studies that undertook follow-up for 2–156 months.23 Similar to our study, the review found little deterioration or improvement in cognitive function at multiple time points after ICU admission. The reasons for the development of cognitive dysfunction may be multifactorial and include development of multiorgan failure,25 sepsis,25 hypoxaemia,26 use of sedative and analgesic agents,27 dysglycaemia,28 and delirium.29 Thus, it remains difficult to determine whether ongoing cognitive dysfunction is related to the OHCA or the overall ICU experience.

Our study has three key strengths. First, it leveraged enrolment into a large, international, multicentre randomised trial to generate national data that have not been previously collected. This facilitates unique insight into longer-term outcomes of OHCA survivors in this country. Second, all outcome assessments were conducted by trained, centralised assessors so consistency of assessment was ensured. Third, assessments were conducted personally by telephone and not by electronic survey, ensuring a robust dataset was available for analysis, and addressing concerns of both response bias and effects of cognitive impairment on responses to electronic methods raised in previous papers.30 Because of these strategies, loss to follow-up was remarkably low, reducing the potential for bias.

We acknowledge several limitations. This study was limited by its modest sample size and inclusion of participants from a single country. The resulting CIs were wide and did not exclude potentially important between-group differences. In addition, the characteristics of CA, resuscitation systems and postarrest care in Aotearoa New Zealand may differ from those in other settings, limiting the generalisability of the findings. Generalisability may be further limited by the relatively high frequency of witnessed arrests, bystander CPR and initial shockable rhythms in this cohort. However, it is still a large, comprehensive long-term follow-up study of survivors of OHCA. Our initial plan was for all TAME participants in New Zealand and Australia to participate in this nested cohort study, but funding was only obtained in New Zealand. Although we used validated tools previously shown to be sensitive measurements of these outcomes in this population, such as the mRS,19 some tools have not been properly evaluated in this population, such as the EQ-5D-5L.31 These tools may still not measure what is important to an OHCA survivor or their family in terms of HRQoL and recovery. This has been recognised previously, and work is underway to develop robust, relevant, co-produced measures of survivorship and HRQoL for this cohort.32 Of interest, none of the tools used in our study has been developed or validated in populations in New Zealand, and therefore, they may not have been sensitive enough to distinguish specific differences. There may also be cultural nuances in the questions used to identify deficits that should be identified and addressed. Likewise, the proposed Core Outcome Set for Cardiac Arrest has been identified through patient and public partners that no single tool comprehensively captures the lived experience of the aftermath of OHCA.31 Future research to validate patient-reported outcome measures in unique countries and ethnicities should occur to determine whether these tools are robust and generalisable to this population. One additional limitation is not having prospectively collected baseline measurements of these outcomes; obviously, this is not possible in this patient population. Like previously published research, there is an assumption made around pre-OHCA function, which may not be accurate. Finally, findings may be subject to recall and survivor bias.

Our study findings will be of interest to OHCA survivors, their family, clinicians, and researchers. Importantly, the findings of our study provide information on the timing and trajectory of neurological and functional recovery in adults resuscitated after OHCA. It is important to recognise that the experience of an event as traumatic as OHCA also impacts those surrounding the survivor. Ongoing brain injury following OHCA has been described as “a family affair” and ongoing emotional disturbances experienced to include sleep disturbances, loss of libido and appetite, and dramatic changes to employment, income, and living conditions associated with caregiver requirements.33 We must quantify the true cost to survivors, families, and society of both short- and long-term costs of survivorship through assessment of health economics.

These findings can be used to inform family of the likelihood of change in these outcomes out to 24 months, with the key message being that recovery is gradual but may continue for many months. This should provide reassurance to patients and family and may assist them to set realistic expectations in their recovery. It may also suggest that ongoing resources and rehabilitation be directed towards this cohort to support and reassess following discharge from the ICU and hospital. Comprehensive rehabilitation services directed at survivors of OHCA could result in more rapid and/or more complete recovery, but further work is clearly required to evaluate the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of such an approach, noting that the study suggests that ongoing rehabilitation and recovery may well extend for at least 2 years after the initial ICU admission.

Conclusion

In this preplanned prospective longitudinal survivorship study, participant-reported neurological and functional outcomes remained stable or improved slightly for survivors of OHCA between 6 and 24 months. Collectively, these findings inform OHCA survivors, their family, clinicians, and researchers on the timing and trajectory of survivorship following OHCA in New Zealand.
