Multi-omics profiling reveals EMT-driven fibroblast activation in the renal injury niche
Authors: Songmei Zhai, Keru Li, Kaidong Liu, Tianyi Ren, Chen Lv, Qizhe Sun, Jiayi Sun, Yuan Zhang, Wenyuan Zhao, Tianyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent renal injury creates conditions for activating fibroblasts. However, due to the complexity of the renal injury microenvironment, the mechanisms driving fibroblast activation remain poorly understood. Here, we use single-nucleus RNA sequencing data and spatial transcriptomic data to investigate the mechanisms of fibroblast activation in renal injury. By integrating 619,137 single-cell nuclei from 80 samples, we identify 10 fibroblast subtypes within the renal injury environment. The FIBEMT (Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transitional Fibroblast) subtype is characterized by elevated scores in EMT-related gene programs, TGF-β signaling pathway, and extracellular matrix (ECM) evaluation, and it is also linked to poorer renal function. Our analysis suggests that SOX4 may promote the activation of FIBEMT subtype by upregulating EMT and ECM-associated pathways. The interaction between the FIBEMT subtype and the CYP24A1+ descending thin limb cell (DTL) subtype with mesenchymal cell characteristics is enhanced in acute injury. Additionally, the analysis of spatial transcriptomic data further reveals that the FIBEMT subtype, along with EMT and ECM features, as well as CYP24A1+ DTL subtype, are primarily concentrated in the renal papilla region. Introduction

The kidney is a urinary organ with regional responses to injury influenced by unique structures and functions [1]. It comprises the renal cortex and medulla, including the papilla. Renal filtration, electrolyte balance, and hormone release for blood pressure regulation are linked to the activity of various cell types along the corticomedullary axis [2]. Renal injury plays an important role in the progression of renal fibrosis. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a potentially fatal condition in which the kidneys undergo a complex series of pathophysiological changes that ultimately lead to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and renal fibrosis [3]. The injury process involves alterations in transcriptional patterns, various biological processes, and interactions with cells in the injury microenvironment. Therefore, it is essential to understand the mechanisms of cell activity to gain novel insights and develop appropriate preventive measures.

In recent years, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomic (ST) technology have revolutionized renal fibrosis research by providing profound insights into the renal injury microenvironment. Several studies utilizing scRNA-seq and ST have focused on renal fibrosis, revealing that the tubule cell landscapes are highly heterogeneous. Abedini A et al. identified two subclusters of injury proximal tubule (iPT) cells using scRNA-seq and ST, highlighting that the HAVCR1+ iPT subcluster is more enriched in disease samples than the VCAM1+ iPT subcluster [4]. Furthermore, following renal injury, local regions may exhibit pro-fibrotic characteristics, with fibroblasts playing a crucial role [5]. The cells in these regions are activated from a quiescent state, contributing to the progression of renal injury [6, 7].

A recent study using single-cell transcriptomics revealed three distinctive fibroblast clusters with "secretory", "contractile" and "vascular" transcriptional features in renal fibrosis [8]. Another study suggested that injured renal tubular epithelial cells can release specific signaling molecules, such as FGF2, as paracrine factors to activate neighboring fibroblasts [9]. Although fibroblasts are the primary target for fibrotic kidney disease, the underlying regulatory mechanisms in different fibroblast subtypes and their interplay with the renal injury remain incompletely understood. In addition, the majority of existing research has predominantly focused on the renal cortex, largely overlooking the medulla and papilla regions. This oversight represents a gap in the current understanding of kidney injury.

In this study, we constructed an atlas of the renal injury microenvironment through multi-omics analysis, and systematically investigated the activation mechanisms of fibroblasts. We employed single-cell transcriptomic analysis to elucidate their intrinsic functional properties and uncover their multifaceted roles in AKI and CKD. Additionally, spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed the preferential distribution of fibroblasts across different renal structural regions. In conclusion, our comprehensive analysis of the cellular and molecular dynamics of fibroblasts under different injury states provides novel insights into strategies for alleviating kidney injury, promoting repair, and potentially elucidating the progression of long-term renal fibrosis.

Material and methods

Data sources

All data of kidney samples was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The integrated single-nucleus datasets consisted of 80 samples from GSE234788 and GSE183277. Spatial transcriptomic data (GSE183456, GSE226533 and GSE154107) were obtained from 4 kidney sections, including three human samples and one mouse sample. We also collected two human bulk transcriptomic datasets (GSE217427 and GSE137570) comprising 68 samples that included information on renal function. The detailed data information used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

snRNA-seq processing

The R package "Seurat" (v4.4.0) was employed to process the single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data [10]. The quality of cells in each dataset was assessed based on three metrics: (1) the number of total UMI count per cell was above 300 and below 20,000; (2) the number of detected genes was above 200 and below 5,000; (3) the percentage of mitochondrial genes was below 15%. To mitigate batch effects, we utilized the Harmony algorithm (v1.2.0) to integrate the gene-cell matrices derived from different datasets [11]. After batch effect correction, a shared nearest-neighbor graph was generated using the FindNeighbors function with the parameter: dims = 1:25, and clusters were identified using the FindClusters function. The identified clusters were visualized using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method. Subsequently, cluster-specific genes were calculated using the FindAllMarkers function. We annotated the clusters into specific cell types by comparing the cluster-specific genes with canonical cell markers.

Cell type preference distribution

To quantify the enrichment of cell types in different types of renal injury (Normal, AKI and CKD), we compared the observed and expected cell numbers for each cell type by computing the Ro/e value [12]. We considered a cell type to be enriched in a specific injury type if Ro/e > 1.

Functional enrichment analysis

We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cluster using the FindMarkers function in Seurat. DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and HALLMARK enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package (v4.8.3) with default settings [13].

Gene set scoring

Scores were calculated using the AddModuleScore algorithm in Seurat, based on gene sets from the EMT and TGF-β signaling pathways, obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). In addition, the features of ECM were derived from a comprehensive review of the literature [14].

Spatial colocalization

The R package 'Seurat' (v4.4.0) was utilized to process the ST data. The data were standardized using the SCTransform function. We extracted the top 100 DEGs of the FIBEMT subtype using snRNA-seq analysis. We then intersected the genes detected in the ST data with the top 100 DEGs, as well as the EMT and ECM feature sets. This intersection allowed us to determine the final gene set for analysis in the ST data. Through gene set scoring, we mapped the FIBEMT, EMT, and ECM features onto tissue slices. Significant overlap observed in specific tissue compartments was indicative of spatial colocalization. For mouse data, we performed gene homologous conversion using the R package 'homologene' and then mapped these to mouse spatial data in the same method.

Clinical application

To investigate the correlation between FIBEMT subtype and renal function, we collected the Clinical index of the kidney patients, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), Creatine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Next, we classified the samples into high-score or low-score groups based on the median enrichment score of top 20 FIBEMT features. T test was conducted to assess whether there were significant differences in renal function between the high-score and low-score groups. In addition, pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the correlation between SOX4 expression and clinical indicators.

SCENIC analysis

The regulon network was explored using the pySCENIC (v0.12.1), which analyzed the co-expression of transcription factors (TFs) and their putative target genes [15]. First, the grnboost2 function was used to predict potential regulatory interactions between TFs and their target genes. Then, the inferred regulatory networks were pruned using cis-regulatory motifs, resulting in the identification of regulons. Finally, the AUCell function was used to score the activity of each regulon in each cell. The regulon specificity score (RSS) between subtypes was calculated using the calcRSS function in R. We ended up preserving TFs with the highest scores for visualization. CYTOSCAPE software (v3.9.1, https://cytoscape.org/) was used to visualize the gene regulatory networks.

Trajectory analysis

We applied the Monocle2 (v2.28.0) algorithm to investigate dynamic biological processes, such as potential differentiation trajectories of different cell types [16]. The DEGs identified by the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat were used to order the cells in the trajectory. The Ggridges package (v0.5.6) was utilized to analyze the frequency of distributed cells across various subtypes along the pseudotime axis. The CytoTRACE (v0.3.3) algorithm was utilized to predict and visualize the cell differentiation scores along the trajectory [17]. The branch displaying high cell differentiation scores was selected as the root.

Cell interaction analysis

We utilized the CellChat (v1.6.1) algorithm to investigate the potential interactions between different cell types [18]. The combined Seurat object, comprising both fibroblasts and other cell types in the kidney injury microenvironment, was used as input to the algorithm. After creating the CellChat object, we set up the reference database using the secretory signaling, ECM-Receptor and Cell–Cell contact. The specific ligand-receptor interactions and communication probabilities between different cell types were inferred using the computeCommunProb and computeCommunProbPathway functions, respectively.

Cell culture

The rat renal fibroblast cell line NRK-49F was obtained from a commercial cell repository and maintained under standard culture conditions. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged at approximately 70–80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA. SOX4 overexpression plasmid and control vector pcDNA3.1 were purchased from HanBio (Shanghai, China). During SOX4 overexpression experiments, NRK-49F cells were seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to reach approximately 60–70% confluence prior to transfection. Cells were then transfected with a plasmid encoding SOX4 or an empty vector control using a liposome-based transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, cells were incubated for 6 h before downstream RNA extraction and gene expression analyses.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from transfected NRK-49F cells using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically. Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcription kit according to the supplier’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed using a SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on a real-time PCR detection system. The amplification conditions were set according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The mRNA expression levels of target genes (COL6A2, VCAN, BGN, and SPARC) and EMT markers (FN1 and ACTA2) were measured. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Apart from the statistical analyses described above, all other statistical analyses were performed using the R language (v4.3.0). Statistical significance was calculated using the T-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Pearson correlation analysis. The significance of the p value is indicated as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns: No significance.

Results

Atlas of kidney injury from snRNA-seq analysis

To understand the pathogenic mechanisms involving fibroblasts in the renal injury microenvironment, we first developed a three-step approach: (1) generating a human renal injury atlas by integrating snRNA-seq data; (2) conducting comprehensive bioinformatics analyses to investigate the cellular and molecular networks between fibroblasts and other cell types; (3) examining the clinical relevance and spatial co-localization of fibroblasts (Fig. 1A).

To generate a comprehensive single-cell atlas of renal injury, we collected a total of 80 snRNA-seq samples from normal kidneys, AKI, and CKD [14, 19]. After strict quality control and filtration, we obtained 619,137 high-quality nuclei. To ensure robust integration across the two datasets, we applied the Harmony algorithm to correct for batch effects (Supplementary Fig. 1). Using graph-based unsupervised clustering, we identified 16 subclasses of epithelial, endothelial, stromal, and immune cell types based on canonical cell markers (Fig. 1B, C).

The complex interactions among renal injury microenvironment play a critical role in the progression of fibrosis and treatment response [20]. CellChat-based analysis was carried out to assess the interactions between the major microenvironment components in the two dimensions of injury state and anatomical region. Notably, crosstalk between stromal cells and endothelial cells was predominant in the injury microenvironment (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 2A). The interaction was particularly obvious in specific cases, with heightened activity primarily in the cortex during AKI and predominantly in the medulla during CKD (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). In addition, fibroblasts within stromal cells presented prolific interactions with other components, particularly in AKI compared to normal and CKD (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). These findings suggested that stromal cells and other cell types may play a significant role in the renal injury and fibrosis microenvironment, highlighting inter-disease and inter-regional heterogeneity.

Furthermore, cell types exhibited distinct injury-type preferences across different regions. Based on the Ro/e score, we characterized the distribution bias of cells in various states. Compared to normal state, fibroblasts tended to be distributed in injury states across all regions of the kidney (Fig. 1E). In AKI, they were mainly found in the cortical and papillary regions, while in CKD, they were predominantly in the medulla (Fig. 1E). Collectively, fibroblasts engaged in frequent interactions and displayed a distribution bias that may exhibit distinct effects on the renal injury microenvironment.

Fibroblast subtypes exhibit heterogeneous transcriptional patterns

To investigate the changes in gene expression patterns and specific mechanisms of fibroblasts within the renal injury microenvironment, we categorized a total of 34,380 fibroblasts into 10 distinct subtypes, which exhibited clear tissue separation (Fig. 2A). Based on the differences in the distribution of cell types, fibroblasts predominantly derived from normal tissues were referred to as FIBNor (Normal Fibroblast), while the remaining 9 subtypes were considered by their distinct gene signatures and the biological functions they were involved in (Fig. 2B, C; Supplementary Fig. 2B; Supplementary Tables 3, 4). The FIBAdi (Adipogenic Fibroblast) subtype specifically expressed genes related to adipogenesis, such as CFD, and was significantly enriched in adipogenic functions. The COL7A1+ FIB (COL7A1+ Fibroblast) subtype exhibited significant changes in collagen deposition, renal system development, and tissue cell migration. The FIBEMT (Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transitional Fibroblast) was a subtype specifically involved in TGF-β and antigen-presenting signaling pathways. Additionally, the FIBEMT subtype played a crucial role in the proliferation and differentiation of epithelial cells, mesenchymal cells, and fibroblasts. This hinted that FIBEMT may represent a highly activated fibroblast state that orchestrates a pro-fibrotic microenvironment characterized by strong EMT signatures.

In addition, fibroblast subtypes were also involved in alterations to various metabolic pathways, indicating that injury to the microenvironment is associated with metabolic disorders (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 5). COL7A1+ FIB was involved in the alteration of alpha linolenic acid metabolism and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 2C). The metabolism of alpha linolenic acid metabolism and arachidonic acid has been reported to regulate renal inflammatory response [21]. Oxidative stress-related selenoamino acid metabolism, RNA metabolism and TP53-regulated metabolic activity were up-regulated in FIBEMT subtype (Supplementary Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 4). In renal injury research, Oxidative-related metabolic alterations are common indicators [22, 23]. Furthermore, the metabolism of inositol phosphate in RGS5+ FIB (RGS5+ Fibroblast) was interfered (Fig. 2C). It was a key link in intracellular signaling and energy metabolism, affecting the energy metabolism of kidney cells [24].

FIBEMT subtype displays fibrosis-related molecular features

Ten fibroblast subtypes were shared among Normal, AKI and CKD samples. However, they exhibited heterogeneous cell proportions (Fig. 2D). PKHD1+ FIB (PKHD1+ Fibroblast), FREM1+ FIB (FREM1+ Fibroblast) and ABCA8+ FIB (ABCA8+ Fibroblast) subtypes showed an increasing trend along the Normal-AKI-CKD axis, while the proportion of RGS5+ FIB subtype decreased significantly (Fig. 2D). FIBEMT was predominantly observed in patients with AKI. SERPINE1+ FIB (SERPINE1+ Fibroblast) represented the majority in normal and CKD. To further assess the potential of the ten fibroblast subtypes in the process of renal injury, we scored them based on the signature genes associated with the classical signaling pathways of TGF-β and ECM from previous fibrosis research [25, 26]. The FIBEMT subtype ranked highest in both TGF-β and ECM feature scores (p < 2.2e-16, Kruskal–Wallis test, Fig. 2E). Furthermore, The FIBEMT subtype exhibited notably high expression of signature genes associated with the TGF-β and ECM pathways, including TGFBI, TGFB1, COL7A1, COL3A1 and LAMC3 (Fig. 2F).

Then, we aimed to determine whether the FIBEMT subtype could assist in prognostic assessment. Here, we constructed a patient grouping model based on the scores of the top 20 DEGs associated with FIBEMT. As expected, we observed significant differences in renal function indicators between the groups with high and low scores. The group with high feature enrichment scores was significantly associated with poor renal function (GSE234788: p = 0.045, p = 0.065; GSE217427: p = 0.03; GSE137570: p = 0.0099; T test, Fig. 3A-C). In addition, we identified a subtype similar to the human FIBEMT subtype in an independent mouse dataset that specifically expressed FIBEMT subtype features, including S100a6, Mgp, Bgn, and Clu (Supplementary Fig. 2D-I). This indicated the conservation of feature genes in both humans and mice.

Activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway facilitates the recruitment of fibroblasts to the injury site and their differentiation into myofibroblasts. This process stimulates the secretion of ECM components, contributing to tissue injury and fibrosis [27]. In order to elucidate the differentiation potential of the FIBEMT subtype in injury environment, we successfully revealed the transition potential of FIBEMT to FIBMyo (Myofibroblast) through trajectory analysis. Along the differentiation path, we also found the evolutionary process of fibroblasts transitioning from normal state to AKI state and further to CKD state (Fig. 3D). This finding aligned with previous studies [28, 29]. With the differentiation process, the fibroblast marker LUM and characteristics of FIBEMT were predominantly expressed in the early stages of differentiation, while the myofibroblast marker ACTA2 was prominently expressed in the later stages (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, TGFBI, a gene associated with TGF-β signaling, was primarily expressed in the middle of the differentiation pathway, facilitating and promoting the transition of FIBEMT into FIBMyo. Trajectory analysis positioned FIBEMT as a precursor to the more differentiated myofibroblast state (Fig. 3D-E). In conclusion, the above results indicated that the FIBEMT subtype, defined by its pronounced EMT and fibrosis-related molecular signature, was closely associated with fibrosis and renal dysfunction. This encouraged us to further explore its pathogenic mechanisms in renal injury.

SOX4 is associated with and potentially regulates FIBEMT subtype behavior through EMT process

Next, we examined the regulon activity across fibroblast subtypes and extracted the top three TFs with the highest activity in each subtype (Fig. 3F). Based on our analysis, we particularly focused on the TFs within the FIBEMT, especially SOX4, which also exhibited significantly high expression at the transcriptomic level (Fig. 3G). SOX4 is a member of the SOX (SRY-related HMG-box) transcription factor family, whose main function is to regulate gene expression and participate in cell differentiation and development [30]. We found that SOX4 was not only one of the DEGs in FIBEMT, but also a self-regulating target gene (Supplementary Fig. 2J). Furthermore, its target genes, HMGB2 and HOXD9, were also expressed in FIBEMT (Supplementary Fig. 2K). In the FIBEMT subtype, SOX4 expression level was up-regulated in disease state compared to normal state (p = 0.08, T test, Fig. 3H). Previous researches have demonstrated that SOX4 was abnormally expressed under injury conditions [8, 31]. At the same time, the expression level of SOX4 was significantly positively correlated with the severity of fibrosis and inversely correlated with the level of eGFR (Fig. 3I). These results confirmed that high expression of SOX4 was associated with a poor prognosis in kidney injury.

To investigate deeper into the function of SOX4, we identified a gene set comprising 40 DEGs that exhibited a significant positive correlation with SOX4 in FIBEMT (Fig. 3J, M). VIM has been identified as a target gene of SOX4 and is involved in the EMT process [30]. Through enrichment analysis using multiple databases, we discovered that the gene set was involved in various cellular functions, focusing on two key aspects: immune-related functions, and fibrosis-related functions (Fig. 3K and Supplementary Table 6). HALLMARK enriched only to a pathway, the EMT program. In addition, SOX4 expression was significantly positively correlated with the scores of the ECM and EMT pathways, except for the TGF-β pathway (Fig. 3L). This suggested that SOX4 may mediate the ECM and EMT pathways to influence FIBEMT subtype behavior. Then, we took the intersection among the three feature sets to further evaluate the SOX4 mechanism. Ultimately, we obtained five common genes: COL6A2, COL3A1, VCAN, BGN, and SPARC, which were closely related to collagen deposition and EMT process and may be potential target genes of SOX4 (Fig. 3M). In the FIBEMT subtype, potential SOX4 target genes, including COL6A2 and VCAN, were significantly upregulated in SOX4-positive compared to SOX4-negative cells (Supplementary Fig. 2L; Wilcoxon rank-sum test). This differential expression is consistent with a positive association between SOX4, reinforcing the role of SOX4 in driving the FIBEMT subtype behavior (Fig. 3J, M and Supplementary Fig. 2L). To validate the predicted regulatory role of SOX4, we examined the effects of SOX4 overexpression in the rat renal fibroblast cell line NRK-49F (Fig. 3N). SOX4 overexpression led to a significant upregulation of the potential target genes COL6A2, VCAN, BGN, and SPARC compared with control cells. In addition, the EMT-associated marker FN1 and ACTA2 were also markedly increased (Fig. 3N). These findings are consistent with our bioinformatic predictions and support the notion that SOX4 contributes to the activation of fibroblast pro-fibrotic transcriptional programs and the FIBEMT phenotype.

FIBEMT subtype in the papilla region exhibits EMT and ECM features

Interestingly, fibroblasts can be categorized based on their anatomical regions, exhibiting clear regional specificity. Cells originated from the same anatomical area tended to cluster together (Fig. 2A). The distinct regions of the kidney influenced the differences in transcriptomic features [14, 19]. In the cortex region, genes including NEGR1, ADAMTSL3, LAMA2, and NOX4 were highly expressed (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the medulla region showed high expression levels of FREM1, TNC, ADAMTSL1, CLMP, and AGTR1, while the papilla region exhibited elevated expression of CA8, LTBP1, HPSE2, ITGA8 (Fig. 4A). This heterogeneity in transcriptomic features directly reflected the diversity of regional functions (Supplementary Fig. 2M). The WNT pathway, wound healing, and collagen activation functions were significantly enriched in the papilla region, indicating that papilla fibroblasts play a vital role in promoting the progression of renal disease. The FIBEMT subtype was more prevalent in the papilla region and corresponds to the functions of cells in that region (Fig. 4B).

We confirmed the preference of the FIBEMT subtype in the papilla/medulla region through the co-localization of spatial transcriptomic slices from both human and mouse samples (Fig. 4C-E). Simultaneously, we identified ECM and EMT signals in the same spatial transcriptomic data. As anticipated, the scores for both pathways were concentrated in the same region as the FIBEMT subtype. This further demonstrated that FIBEMT can promote the development of the injury microenvironment by disrupting EMT and ECM signals.

CYP24A1+ DTL subtype: a key epithelial player in EMT process

The mechanistic analysis of the FIBEMT subtype in this study suggested that EMT played an important role in kidney injury. However, epithelial cells were a crucial component of the EMT process[32]. We found that descending thin limb cell (DTL) in epithelial cells was significantly correlated with BUN and creatinine (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In the epithelial cell types, DTL exhibited the third-highest EMT score (Supplementary Fig. 3B). And it was predominantly found in the medulla and papilla regions (Fig. 1E). DTL is a key part of the loop of Henle [33, 34]. We conducted unsupervised clustering of DTL cells and divided them into 5 different DTL subtypes based on their characteristic gene expression (Fig. 5A). Although each subtype was distributed across various states, the proportions of these subtypes were not uniform. CYP24A1+ DTL was particularly prominent, primarily observed in AKI, and found in all three renal regions, especially in the cortex and papilla (Fig. 5B).

We identified a series of highly expressed DEGs in DTL subtypes. Notably, genes related to collagen synthesis, immune regulation, and metabolism in the CYP24A1+ DTL subtype were significantly upregulated, such as CD44, ADAMTS1, SPP1, ITGB6, EFNA5, and CYP24A1 (Fig. 5C). Moreover, the CYP24A1+ DTL subtype highlighted fibrosis-related and immune-related signaling pathways and was involved in a variety of complex pathways in the context of kidney injury, underscoring its significance in the renal injury state.

By calculating the RSS, we observed that FOXO1 and ZEB1 displayed the highest RSS in the CYP24A1+ DTL (Fig. 5D). ZEB1 was an important EMT-inducing factor [35]. Based on the SCENIC results, we constructed a transcriptional regulatory network to explore potential mechanisms of CYP24A1+ DTL (Fig. 5E). Our results indicated that ZEB1 regulated several DEGs associated with EMT processes, such as MAP4K4, ETV6 and MECOM. The ETV6-MECOM fusion protein acted as a key regulator in inducing the EMT process and could upregulate the expression of key EMT transcription factors [36]. MECOM was regulated by multiple transcription factors in our research (Fig. 5E). ATF2 and SOX6 were specifically enriched in the NALCN+ DTL and UNC5D+ DTL subtypes, respectively (Fig. 5D). The intricate regulatory relationships between transcription factors and target genes drove the development of various phenotypes of DTL and collectively contributed to the formation of the injury microenvironment.

Interaction potential between CYP24A1+ DTL and FIBEMT in renal injury

The primary focus of this study was to investigate the key mechanisms of EMT in the renal injury microenvironment. Among DTL subtypes, CYP24A1+ DTL ranked at the top, further emphasizing the general dedifferentiation of these specific subtypes may have undergone during the EMT process (p < 2.2e-16, Kruskal–Wallis test, Fig. 5F). This underscored the plasticity through the EMT process. Furthermore, the CYP24A1⁺ DTL specifically expressed a prominent mesenchymal phenotype, characterized by elevated expression of canonical mesenchymal markers—including ZEB1,ETS1,CD44, and FN1. This subtype also expressed potential SOX4 target genes(COL6A2,VCAN,COL3A1) identified in our prior analysis, alongside established DTL signature genes (Fig. 5C, G). Our results showed that SOX4 was upregulated in the FIBEMT subtype, which subsequently modulated potential target genes expression. These findings led us to speculate that FIBEMT may promote the EMT of CYP24A1⁺ DTL in kidney injury. Similarly, among various fibroblast subtypes, FIBMyo and FIBEMT were prominent, with high EMT scores, indicating their significant role in fibroblast activation (p < 2.2e-16, Kruskal–Wallis test, Supplementary Fig. 3C). We found that FIBEMT and CYP24A1+ DTL were enriched in the same area of the ST section, specifically in the papilla/medulla region (Fig. 5H). This finding implied the possibility of crosstalk between them.

Analyzing intercellular communication, we found significant differences in interactions during various injury states, especially in AKI (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Notably, the interaction frequency between CYP24A1+ DTL, acting as a signal sender, and FIBEMT has increased. This communication mainly involved ligand-receptor pairs like LAMA1-CD44, LAMB1-CD44, and LAMC1-CD44 (Supplementary Fig. 4B). CYP24A1+ DTL, as a receiver, has limited interactions with FIBEMT and FIBMyo, while interactions between FIBEMT and FIBMyo have increased (Supplementary Fig. 4A). CYP24A1+ DTL and FIBMyo interacted with FIBEMT via PDGFC-PDGFRA (Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Persistent transcriptomic alterations of endothelial cell in AKI and CKD

Apart from fibroblasts, endothelial cells showed significant changes in specific injury areas, particularly in the cortex during AKI and the medulla in CKD (Supplementary Fig. 2A). We extracted these regions along with their respective normal endothelial cells for further exploration (Fig. 6A). We identified two genes, FTH1 and AKAP12, that exhibited significant expression changes across various stages of kidney injury (Fig. 6B, C). FTH1 expression increased with injury progression from normal to AKI and CKD. Spatial transcriptomic data showed FTH1 colocalizes with the endothelial cell marker PECAM1 in the kidney (Fig. 6D). Conversely, the diminished expression of AKAP12 suggested ongoing endothelial cell damage, potentially contributing to the shift from acute to chronic kidney injury. And the expression levels of these genes correspond to different regions of the kidney (Fig. 6C). Endothelial cells' functions changed with the severity of kidney injury, showing unique and shared traits. We noticed that key functions like angiogenesis, kidney development, and cell differentiation started to alter in AKI and continued in CKD (Fig. 6E). In CKD, endothelial activity affected kidney balance and energy metabolism. In AKI, pathways like integrin, TGF-β, and VEGF signaling were highly active. As CKD progresses, significant shifts were observed in the p53 signaling and apoptosis pathways (Fig. 6F).

Figure 6E showed that cell differentiation and migration functions remained continuously active, particularly in CKD. Additionally, fibroblast proliferation was observed, underscoring the key role of EndoMT in promoting the fibrotic process. We collected EndoMT-related pathways and factors from the literature [37–39]. With the progression of renal injury, the pathway scores associated with EndoMT gradually increased (Fig. 6G). The activity of transcription factors SNAI1 and ZEB1 dropped in AKI and increased in CKD. This indicated that transcription factor activity might be influenced by multiple reasons, not solely by signaling pathway activation.

Then, endothelial and fibroblast interactions varied significantly by injury type and showed regional preferences. In AKI, interactions between FIBAdi, ABCA8+ FIB, and FIBMyo with endothelial cells were enhanced (Fig. 6H). FIBAdi and ABCA8+ FIB were primarily located in the cortex and interacted with endothelial cells via PTPRM-PTPRM, PDGFD-PDGFRB, and collagen-related ligand-receptor pairs (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). In CKD, interactions between FREM1+ FIB and endothelial cells were most notable (Fig. 6H). FREM1+ FIB were concentrated in the medulla and interacted with endothelial cells via TNC-(ITGA9 + ITGB1) and ANGPT1-TEK pairs (Supplementary Fig. 5B). The expression of these ligand-receptor genes also changed with the differentiation of endothelium-fibroblasts (Fig. 6I and Supplementary Fig. 5C). The evidence described above suggested that the crosstalk between endothelial cells and fibroblasts promoted the change of renal injury environment through different modes, depending on distinct regions.

Discussion

The study explored the role of fibroblast subtypes in the renal injury microenvironment, focusing on their involvement in kidney injury development through the EMT program. We profiled and named cell subpopulations, such as FIBEMT, FIBAdi, RGS5+ FIB, based on a variety of considerations, including characteristic genes, functions, and distribution preferences, while also referencing studies related to fibroblasts [40]. Based on the analysis of multiple omics datasets, we demonstrated a significant correlation between the characteristics of the FIBEMT subtype and renal function indicators. The FIBEMT subtype was prominently involved in fibrosis-related signaling pathways, such as TGF-β and ECM, highlighting its importance in the injury microenvironment. Furthermore, transcription factor module analysis revealed the upregulation of SOX4 activity, a key transcription factor, in FIBEMT and highlighted the specificity of its expression level. Our analysis demonstrated that in the injury state, the upregulation of SOX4 was positively associated with the degree of fibrosis, and such upregulation can result in the dysregulation of eGFR. We elucidated that SOX4 can influence the role of FIBEMT in promoting kidney injury progression by mediating EMT and ECM signaling pathways. Additionally, the FIBEMT subtype features colocalized with EMT and ECM features in the renal papilla/medulla region, as revealed by spatial transcriptomics data.

Moreover, fibroblasts implied alternative origins from epithelial cells, endothelial cells. The important role of the CYP24A1+ DTL subtype has been emphasized. CYP24A1+ DTL represented a specific subtype of DTL, which was itself a subclass of epithelial cells. Our research indicated that DTL was closely associated with kidney function in epithelial cells. In the CYP24A1+ DTL subtype, genes related to collagen synthesis, immune regulation, and metabolism were notably upregulated. Crucially, we found that this FIBEMT subtype not only spatially colocalizes with CYP24A1⁺ DTL cells but also exhibits enhanced interactions with them in AKI. Notably, among all DTL subtypes, CYP24A1⁺ DTL itself emerged as the top scorer for the EMT signaling pathway. This co-occurrence positioned the FIBEMT as a potential stromal orchestrator of the EMT niche in renal injury, likely facilitating epithelial transition through molecular signaling. Furthermore, Endothelial cells played a multifaceted role in renal fibrosis, participating in processes such as EndoMT, disruption of vascular integrity, metabolic reprogramming, and immune regulation [41, 42]. Our findings demonstrated the interrelationship and developmental trajectory of endothelial and fibroblast subtypes in AKI and CKD states, further confirming the central role of fibroblasts in the renal injury microenvironment.

Our study also found the prevalence of the SERPINE1⁺ fibroblast subtype in normal kidney tissue. SERPINE1 as a known marker of TGF-β activation is well-noted [43, 44]. This finding aligns with the emerging understanding that a basal, homeostatic level of TGF-β signaling operates in healthy organs, where it participates in routine tissue maintenance, injury surveillance, and repair preparedness [45]. In this context, SERPINE1⁺ fibroblasts may represent a poised stromal population. The transition to a pro-fibrotic state during kidney injury, therefore, likely involves not the de novo activation of the TGF-β pathway, but rather its dysregulated amplification and functional diversion. This conceptual framework suggests that fibrotic diseases may stem from the pathological hijacking and exaggeration of a fundamental repair mechanism, shifting the balance from controlled healing to uncontrolled matrix deposition.

Although this study explored cell dynamics within the kidney injury microenvironment, it also had certain limitations. One notable limitation of this study was the lack of direct in vitro or in vivo perturbation experiments to establish causality for key regulatory candidates such as SOX4. While our findings were based on robust bioinformatic analyses, definitive proof of regulatory function requires experimental validation. Importantly, the convergence of multi-layered evidence presented—including statistically significant differential expression of target genes in SOX4-positive versus negative FIBEMT cells, continuous correlation between SOX4 and its putative targets across the population, and its association with clinical fibrosis markers—provided a strong and testable hypothesis. This integrated computational foundation explicitly pointed to targeted in vitro gain- or loss-of-function studies as the essential and logical next step. Specifically, future work should include manipulating SOX4 expression in relevant fibroblast models to directly assess its impact on the expression of ECM/EMT genes and on functional phenotypes such as collagen production and cell migration. On the other hand, the complexity of kidney injury suggested that there may be additional critical cells and interactions that have not yet been fully identified, such as immune cells and their associated microenvironments, which warrant further exploration in future studies. Furthermore, we acknowledged that at the spatial transcriptomic level, there was potential for more in-depth analysis. This analysis should not only focus on the colocalization of gene expression but may also encompass the construction of intercellular communication networks, detailed examinations of signal transduction pathways, and the dynamic changes in cell states within the microenvironment. By fully utilizing these methods, we can gain a more comprehensive understanding of the complexities of the kidney injury microenvironment and establish a more precise scientific foundation for the diagnosis and treatment of kidney disease.

Our results offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying kidney injury and long-term fibrosis, as well as potential strategies for preventing and treating the transition from AKI to CKD. As a potential molecular target, SOX4 mediated EMT and ECM signaling. Targeted therapy aimed at SOX4 may help mitigate kidney injury, enhance kidney function, and potentially prevent or delay the progression of chronic kidney disease.
