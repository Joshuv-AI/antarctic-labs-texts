A New Species and Record of Erioscyphella (Lachnaceae, Helotiales) from Korea
Authors: Dae Ho Kim, Young-Nam Kwag, Hyung So Kim, Chang-Sun Kim, Sang-Kuk Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Erioscyphella (Lachnaceae, Helotiales), characterized by minute apothecia, is distributed in subtropical regions of East Asia; however, relatively little is known about species in Korea. In this study, Korean collections of Erioscyphella were examined using morphological observations and multilocus phylogenetic analyses based on ITS, LSU, and RPB2 sequences. One new taxon, Erioscyphella koreana sp. nov., has been described, and Erioscyphella hainanensis has been newly recorded in Korea. The Korean specimens of E. hainanensis showed similar morphological characteristics to those of previously reported E. hainanensis and high sequence similarity in the combined ITS–LSU–RPB2 analysis. In the multilocus phylogeny, E. koreana was recovered as a distinct clade closely related to Erioscyphella boninensis, Erioscyphella lushanensis, and Erioscyphella paralushanensis. However, it was distinguishable from these species by a combination of morphological characteristics, including apothecial coloration, ascus morphology, and ascospore dimensions. Korean Erioscyphella abnormis, E. hainanensis, and E. koreana shared similar apothecial structures but differed in ascospore septation, paraphysis morphology, and substrate preference. These findings expand our knowledge of Erioscyphella diversity in Korea and highlight the value of integrative taxonomy for species delimitation.Keywords: Erioscyphella hainanensis, Erioscyphella koreana, Erioscyphella, lachnaceae

Introduction

Erioscyphella Kirschst. (Lachnaceae, Helotiales) is a genus of fungi reported mainly in temperate to subtropical regions of East Asia. Species in this genus form minute apothecia, are saprobic, and occur on a variety of plant-derived substrates, including decaying wood, fallen branches, bamboo remains, and leaf litter [1,2].

Morphologically, Erioscyphella is characterized by small apothecia that are cupulate to discoid, with the receptacle and margin densely covered by conspicuous hairs. These hairs are generally cylindrical, septate, and often bear amorphous or resinous material at the apex [2,3]. In addition, species of Erioscyphella produce relatively long, filiform, multiseptate ascospores [2]. This combination of characters is the primary diagnostic basis for distinguishing Erioscyphella from morphologically similar lachnaceous genera, particularly Lachnum and its allies [3,4]. However, at the species level, morphological differences among taxa are often subtle and may be insufficient to clearly resolve species boundaries [2].

Recent studies of Erioscyphella have increasingly adopted integrative taxonomic approaches that combine detailed morphological observations with multilocus phylogenetic analyses based on internal transcribed spacer region of nuclear ribosomal DNA (ITS), large subunit nuclear ribosomal RNA gene (LSU), mitochondrial small subunit (mtSSU), and second largest subunit of nuclear RNA polymerase II (RPB2) sequence data [2,5–7]. A comprehensive revision based on Japanese materials has redefined the generic concept of Erioscyphella through the integration of morphological, ecological, and multilocus phylogenetic evidence and proposed numerous new species and combinations, providing a framework for species delimitation within the genus [2]. Subsequently, additional new species have been reported from southwestern China using combined morphological and molecular data, indicating a gradual expansion of the known species diversity of Erioscyphella [6,7].

Taxonomic studies and records of Erioscyphella in Korea are limited. To date, only E. abnormis has been reported in Korea based on morphological characteristics and an ITS phylogenetic analysis [8], prior to the widespread application of multilocus phylogenetic analyses. Accordingly, a reexamination of Korean specimens using updated taxonomic frameworks is required.

In this study, we reevaluated Korean collections of Erioscyphella through detailed observations of macro- and micromorphological characters and phylogenetic analyses based on multilocus sequence data (ITS, LSU, and RPB2). One taxon exhibiting distinct morphological characteristics and phylogenetic divergence was identified as a new species of Erioscyphella, and E. hainanensis was newly recorded in Korea. This study provides baseline data on the species diversity and distribution of Erioscyphella in Korea.

Morphological observations

Field photographs taken during collection were used to record habitat and substrate information. Macro- and micromorphological features of the Erioscyphella specimens were examined and images were obtained using a stereomicroscope (Leica Z16 APO, Wetzlar, Germany) and a compound microscope (Leica DM 6000 FS). For microscopic observations, apothecia were sectioned into thin hand-cut slices and mounted in distilled water, Melzer’s reagent, and lactic acid.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Genomic DNA was isolated from dried specimens using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). For molecular identification, three loci were amplified: ITS, LSU, and RPB2. The ITS region was amplified with ITS5/ITS4 [9], LSU with LR0R/LR5 [10], and RPB2 with fRPB2-5F/fRPB2-7cR [11].

PCR was performed using AccuPower® Taq PCR PreMix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) or AccuPower® Gold HotStart Taq PCR PreMix & Master Mix (Bioneer Inc.). Reactions were performed in a 20 μL volume containing 1 μL of each primer, 1 μL of template DNA, and nuclease-free water, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each premix contained Taq DNA polymerase, dNTPs, Tris-HCl, KCl, MgCl2, stabilizers, and tracking dye. Thermal cycling consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, 30 cycles of 94 °C for 20 s, annealing at 55 °C for ITS and LSU or 52 °C for RPB2 for 20 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 3 min. PCR products were then held at 4 °C until further processing.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

PCR amplicons were purified and sequenced by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea). Chromatograms were inspected, and contigs were assembled using Geneious Prime®. For initial identification, BLASTn searches (v. 2.13.0) were performed against the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) GenBank database.

Multiple sequence alignments were generated using MAFFT v. 7 and adjusted manually prior to phylogenetic inference. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) under the GTR + I + G substitution model, which was selected as the best-fit model using jModelTest v. 2.1.10 [12]. For BI, Markov chain Monte Carlo analyses were run for 1,000,000 generations, and the first 250,000 generations were discarded as burn-in. The resulting trees were visualized using FigTree v. 1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). Neodasyscypha cerina TNS-F-65625 [2] was designated as the outgroup.

Results

Molecular characteristics

To re-identify Erioscyphella specimens in KH, ITS, LSU, and RPB2 nucleotide sequences were analyzed though BLAST searches against the NCBI GenBank database.

Specimens KA25-0264, KA25-0289, KA25-0299, KA25-0364, and KA25-0432 formed a single genetic group in the phylogeny. The ITS region was 827 bp in length, and a single nucleotide difference was detected only in KA25-0289, whereas the LSU and RPB2 sequences were identical among all specimens. BLAST searches of the ITS sequences showed that KA25-0264, KA25-0299, KA25-0364, and KA25-0432 shared 97.25–100% sequence identity with E. hainanensis reference sequences. Sequence identity with Erioscyphella sinensis TNS:F-32161 (LC669449) was lower, at 95.12% (507/533 bp). The ITS sequence of KA25-0289 and E. hainanensis reference sequences also showed high similarity, with identities ranging from 97.25% to 99.88%, whereas its similarity to E. sinensis TNS:F-32161 (LC669449) was 95.12% (506/533 bp). BLAST searches of the LSU sequences indicated that all specimens except KA25-0246 showed 99.64–100% sequence identity with E. hainanensis reference sequences, while similarity to E. abnormis NBRC 114466 (LC533139) was 99.42% (852/857 bp). The LSU sequence of KA25-0246 also exhibited high similarity to E. hainanensis reference sequences, ranging from 99.74% to 100%, and showed 99.37% identity (783/788 bp) with E. abnormis SS2403_04 (LC862156). In BLAST searches of the RPB2 sequences, all specimens showed 95.09–100% sequence identity with E. hainanensis reference sequences, whereas similarity to E. sinensis NBRC 104390 (LC533218) was lower, at 89.96% (627/697 bp). In the phylogenetic tree based on the concatenated dataset (Figure 1), these specimens clustered together with reference sequences of E. hainanensis, forming a distinct clade with 100% bootstrap support, clearly separated from other species.

Specimens KA25-0451 and KA25-0600 shared identical sequences across the ITS, LSU, and RPB2 regions. BLAST searches of the ITS sequences revealed that both specimens showed the highest similarity to E. lushanensis 3631 (JF937582), with 96.07% identity (513/534 bp), followed by E. boninensis TNS:F-26520 (NR_185389) with 95.84% identity (553/577 bp), and E. paralushanensis TNS:F-61920 (NR_185390) with 95.76% identity (497/519 bp). Sequence identity with E. hainanensis TNS:F-80356 (LC669465) was 91.89% (714/777 bp). BLAST searches of the LSU sequences showed high similarity to multiple reference sequences of E. abnormis, with identities ranging from 99.65% to 99.77%. High similarity was also observed with Erioscyphella sasibrevispora NBRC 114475 (LC533174) and E. boninensis NBRC 114447 (LC533151), both showing 99.53% identity (853/857 bp). Sequence identity with E. hainanensis NBRC 114470 (LC533172) was 99.42% (852/857 bp). The RPB2 sequences of KA25-0451 and KA25-0600 were 929 and 1,049 bp in length, respectively, resulting in slight differences in similarity values. For KA25-0451, sequence identity was the highest with E. boninensis NBRC 114447 (LC533196) at 96.44% (705/731 bp), followed by E. paralushanensis NBRC 114468 (LC533220) at 95.31% (711/746 bp). Similarly, KA25-0600 showed 96.21% identity (710/738 bp) with E. boninensis NBRC 114447 (LC533196) and 95.23% identity (718/754 bp) with E. paralushanensis NBRC 114468 (LC533220). In contrast, both specimens showed less than 90% sequence identity with other reference sequences. In the phylogenetic analysis based on the combined ITS, LSU, and RPB2 dataset (Figure 1), KA25-0451 and KA25-0600 formed a single clade that was distinct from previously described species.

Remarks:

E. hainanensis is distinguished from congeners by its minute yellow apothecia; relatively short, aseptate ascospores; and lanceolate paraphyses. The morphologically similar Lachnum haematinum has a more distinct amyloid reaction at the ascus apex, smaller ascospores, and filiform paraphyses, thereby separating it from the present species [19]. It is also distinguished from E. sinensis by ascospore size (E. sinensis, 21–35 × 0.5–0.8 μm) [2]. In the combined ITS–LSU–RPB2 phylogeny (Figure 1), E. hainanensis formed a strongly supported monophyletic clade and was recovered as sister to E. sinensis. Compared with the phylogenetically closely related Erioscyphella euterpes, E. hainanensis is readily distinguished by its much shorter, aseptate ascospores and substrate preference, as E. euterpes has been reported on fronds of Prestoea montana (=Euterpe globosa) [4].

Remarks: E. koreana is distinguished from other species of Erioscyphella by a unique combination of characters, including finely granulate, cylindrical hairs with rounded to obtuse, non-swollen apices, a distinct amyloid apical apparatus in the asci, and filiform to narrowly fusiform aseptate ascospores. The new species and E. boninensis share granulate hairs and aseptate ascospores but differ in the overall configuration of the hairs, asci, and ascospores. The species is readily distinguished from E. lushanensis and E. paralushanensis, both of which produce red to dark red hairs, by the presence of hyaline, non-pigmented hairs. In addition, E. paralushanensis differs by producing septate ascospores. The new species is also separated from Erioscyphella papillaris by the absence of papillate, swollen hair apices. In the combined ITS–LSU–RPB2 phylogeny (Figure 1), E. koreana forms a distinct clade within Erioscyphella and is recovered as closely related to E. boninensis, E. lushanensis, and E. paralushanensis, supporting its recognition as a distinct species.

Discussion

In this study, Korean collections of Erioscyphella were examined using detailed morphological observations combined with multilocus phylogenetic analyses based on ITS, LSU, and RPB2 sequence data. One new species was described, E. koreana sp. nov., and E. hainanensis was newly recorded in Korea. These findings indicate that the species diversity of Erioscyphella in Korea is likely higher than previously recognized.

Phylogenetic analyses showed that E. koreana is closely related to E. boninensis, E. lushanensis, and E. paralushanensis, sharing generally similar morphological characteristics with these taxa. However, in the combined ITS–LSU–RPB2 phylogeny, E. koreana was recovered as a distinct clade, clearly separated from its closest relatives. Although the overall morphology was comparable, E. koreana could be distinguished from these species by a combination of morphological characteristics, including apothecial coloration, ascus morphology and size, and ascospore length. These results support the recognition of E. koreana as a separate species within the genus.

A comparison of Korean species of Erioscyphella (Table 2) further highlighted interspecific differences within the country. Although E. abnormis, E. hainanensis, and E. koreana shared a generally similar apothecial structure and hyaline hairs, they differed in several important morphological characteristics. E. abnormis was characterized by yellowish apothecia, relatively large asci, and multiseptate ascospores and typically occurred on woody substrates. E. hainanensis and E. koreana produced whitish to pale-colored apothecia and aseptate ascospores and were primarily associated with fallen leaves. Furthermore, while E. hainanensis and E. koreana exhibited broadly similar overall morphologies, they differed in paraphysis morphology, providing additional diagnostic characters for species delimitation. These comparisons demonstrate that even among morphologically similar species, differences in combinations of characteristics and substrate preference contribute to species-level recognition.

E. hainanensis, previously reported in Japan and China, is documented for the first time in Korea in this study. The Korean specimens examined in this study were morphologically consistent with previous descriptions and formed a strongly supported clade with reference sequences in a multilocus phylogenetic analysis. This finding extends the known distribution of E. hainanensis to the Korean Peninsula and suggests that the species is more widely distributed in East Asia than previously understood.

In conclusion, this study expands the taxonomic knowledge of Erioscyphella in Korea and confirms the effectiveness of an integrative approach combining morphological characteristics with multilocus phylogenetic analyses. The results demonstrate that, even when overall morphology is similar, species can be reliably delimited through the combined evaluation of phylogenetic position and subtle but consistent morphological characteristics. Continued sampling across diverse regions and substrates, together with the accumulation of additional molecular data, will further clarify the species diversity and distribution patterns of Erioscyphella in Korea.
