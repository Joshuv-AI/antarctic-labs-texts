The HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis mediates aberrant osteogenic-tenogenic differentiation imbalance of tendon stem/progenitor cells in diabetic tendinopathy
Authors: Panpan Lu, Yucheng Gao, Hao Wang, Liu Shi, Yingjuan Li, Yunfeng Rui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTendinopathy is a common musculoskeletal disorder in individuals with diabetes. Tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) play an essential role in tendon repair, regeneration and homeostasis maintenance. Although studies have demonstrated that diabetic tendinopathy is closely associated with the altered differentiation of TSPCs, the underlying mechanisms remain largely unknown. This study was designed to investigate the role of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs and diabetic tendinopathy.MethodsRat diabetes models were induced with streptozotocin (65 mg/kg). TSPCs were isolated at week 2 and the patellar tendons were isolated at weeks 2 and 4 for histological analysis. The activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in TSPCs and diabetic tendons was determined by western blot, ELISA, qRT-PCR and immunohistochemical staining. TSPCs and the diabetic tendons were then treated with lentivirus targeting HMGB1 or glycyrrhizin or FPS-ZM1 or PNU-74654 to demonstrate the role of this axis in TSPCs differentiation and diabetic tendinopathy. Alizarin red staining was performed to evaluate the calcium nodule formation. The mRNA expression of the osteogenic and tenogenic genes in TSPCs and diabetic tendons was examined by qRT-PCR and immunohistochemical staining. Recombinant HMGB1 was injected around the patellar tendons to further study the role of HMGB1 in tendinopathy, the histological changes were evaluated by HE stating and the expression of RAGE, β-catenin and the osteogenic genes in tendons was detected by immunohistochemical staining at weeks 2 and 4.ResultsWe established a rat diabetes model. In diabetic TSPCs, the activity of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis was increased. Diabetic TSPCs exhibited increased osteogenic differentiation potential and reduced tenogenic differentiation ability. Blockade of this axis attenuated the differentiation imbalance of diabetic TSPCs. In addition, we also demonstrated the activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in diabetic tendons. The expression of tendon-related markers was decreased and the expression of osteogenic markers was increased in diabetic tendons. Inhibition of this axis could ameliorate these non-tenogenic alterations. Furthermore, injection of recombinant HMGB1 promoted the development of tendinopathy, increased the expression of RAGE and β-catenin and upregulated the expression of osteogenic markers in tendon tissue.ConclusionsOur findings revealed the critical role of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs and diabetic tendinopathy, highlighting a novel essential mechanism involved in the pathogenesis of diabetic tendinopathy and providing a promising therapeutic target and approach for diabetic tendinopathy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06572-8.

Introduction

Tendons facilitate the transmission of muscular forces to bones, enabling joint movement [1, 2]. Tendinopathy, a major category of tendon pathology alongside laceration, poses a significant therapeutic challenge [3, 4]. Tendinopathy is common in individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM), leading to chronic pain, tendon rupture and restricted mobility [5, 6]. In diabetic tendons, the structural and functional properties are compromised, contributing to a greater susceptibility to tendon degeneration and injury [6, 7]. Previous studies have shown that the expression of tenogenic genes is decreased in diabetic tendons, while the expression of osteochondrogenic genes is increased [8, 9]. The expression of tenogenic genes serves as a functional readout for the tendon’s self-healing capacity, namely collagen production in response to mechanical stress [1]. Conversely, osteochondrogenic gene expression is linked to the aberrant outcome of heterotopic ossification after tendon damage [10]. However, the pathogenesis of diabetic tendinopathy remains largely unknown [7, 11].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) possess multipotent differentiation capabilities, which can be modulated by endogenous and exogenous factors [12, 13]. Tenogenic induction of MSCs derived from various tissue precursors is utilized to achieve regeneration and repair of tendon tissue [14–16]. Tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs), as a kind of tendon-specific MSCs, which are capable of self-renewal and multilineage differentiation and have been isolated from various species [17–19], play a critical role in tendon repair, regeneration and homeostasis maintenance [20–24]. Studies have shown that diabetic tendinopathy is closely associated with functional alterations in TSPCs [7, 11]. It has been reported that a high-glucose environment can suppress the proliferation and tenogenic differentiation ability of TSPCs [25, 26]. Compared with healthy cells, in addition to reduced self-renewal ability, TSPCs isolated from diabetic rat tendons exhibit differentiation imbalance with increased osteochondrogenic differentiation ability and reduced tenogenic differentiation capacity, demonstrating that TSPCs are involved in the pathogenesis of diabetic tendinopathy [9]. These functional changes in TSPCs in diabetic tendinopathy may impair tendon healing and regeneration capacity. Although the association between diabetic tendinopathy and TSPCs has been well recognized, the specific mechanisms underlying the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs in diabetic tendinopathy remain unclear. The restoration of the original properties of diabetic tendons is still a major clinical challenge due to limited endogenous regeneration and repair ability.

High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is a member of the high mobility group protein family and a widely expressed nuclear protein that plays a crucial role in various biological and pathological processes, such as inflammation [27], differentiation and migration [28]. However, HMGB1 can be secreted into the extracellular milieu in response to tissue damage or cellular stress and acts as a novel multifunctional cytokine involved in inflammation, tissue injury and regeneration [29–31], as well as in several cellular processes, such as cell migration, differentiation and proliferation [32]. Previous studies have indicated that the level of HMGB1 is upregulated in diabetic patients and animal models and is involved in the pathogenesis of many diabetes-related diseases [32–34]. Intriguingly, recent studies have also shown that HMGB1, as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) or an alarmin, is increased and plays a vital role in human [30, 35], mouse [36, 37] and rat [38, 39] tendinopathy, such as proinflammatory responses and matrix regulation [40]. In addition, it has been shown that extracellular HMGB1 is also involved in regulating the proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation of MSCs [31, 41, 42]. Although previous studies have shown the significant role of HMGB1 in tendinopathy and the regulation of stem cells, no studies have focused on the role of HMGB1 in TSPCs during diabetic tendinopathy. The receptor for advanced glycation end-product (RAGE) is a functional receptor for HMGB1, to which HMGB1 can bind to regulate stem cell proliferation, migration and differentiation [28]. HMGB1 stimulation could upregulate RAGE expression during osteogenic differentiation of human bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs) [41]. In addition, intracellular β-catenin signaling has emerged as a critical regulator of the osteogenic differentiation of stem cells [43, 44]. In the nucleus, β-catenin is known to interact with transcription factor 4 (TCF-4) and activate the transcription of osteogenic genes [45]. Studies have shown that HMGB1 promotes osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs [46] and vascular calcification in patients with chronic kidney disease [47] through β-catenin signaling. Increased RAGE expression could promote osteogenic differentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells and vascular calcification by modulating downstream β-catenin signaling [48, 49].

This study was designed to investigate the role of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs and diabetic tendinopathy. In the present study, we confirmed the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs in diabetic tendinopathy. We further showed that the enhanced differentiation imbalance of diabetic TSPCs was dependent on the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis. Furthermore, the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis also played a critical role in the increased osteogenic genes and decreased tenogenic genes in diabetic tendons. Inhibition of this axis could ameliorate the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs and these nontenogenic alterations in diabetic tendons. These findings revealed the critical role of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs and diabetic tendinopathy, highlighting a novel essential mechanism involved in the pathogenesis of diabetic tendinopathy and providing a promising therapeutic target and approach for diabetic tendinopathy.

Materials and methods

Induction of DM and animal experiment

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Southeast University. Sixty-six 8-week-old male Sprague Dawley rats were randomly assigned to five groups using a computer-generated random number sequence: control group, DM group, DM group receiving daily intraperitoneal injection of glycyrrhizin (50 mg/kg body weight, Sigma Aldrich) [39], DM group receiving daily injection of FPS-ZM1 (1 mg/kg body weight, MedChemExpresss) [50] around the patellar tendon and DM group receiving daily injection of PNU-74654 (50 μM, 200 μl; Abcam) [45] around the patellar tendon. This ensured that each animal had an equal probability of being assigned to any group, minimizing selection bias. At week 2, three rats were sacrificed in the control and DM groups for the isolation and culture of TSPCs. Six rats from each group were sacrificed after anesthetization at weeks 2 and 4 after induction or injection, and the patellar tendons were isolated for histological analysis and detection of mRNA or protein expression. During the intervention and housing, the investigator responsible for the daily administration of treatments and animal care was blinded to the group allocation throughout the experiment. During data analysis, the statistician analyzing the final datasets was also kept blinded to the group codes until the statistical comparisons were completed.

The method of rat DM model induction with streptozotocin (STZ, Sigma Aldrich) has been well established in our previous study [9]. Briefly, the rats were intraperitoneally injected with STZ-citrate buffer solution (65 mg/kg) to induce DM, while the control rats only received citrate buffer. All rats were subjected to an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) after fasting for 8 h prior to induction and on day 3 postinjection. The persistent phenotype of DM was considered to be sustained blood glucose levels equal to or higher than 250 mg/dL. Blood glucose levels of all rats were measured with a glucometer every two days.

For short-term HMGB1 administration, twenty-four 8-week-old male Sprague Dawley rats were randomly divided into two groups: the control group injected with physiological saline and the HMGB1 group injected daily with recombinant HMGB1 (1 μg/kg body weight, R&D Systems) around the patellar tendon [39]. Rats in each group were sacrificed after anesthetization at weeks 2 and 4 postinjection, and the patellar tendons were isolated for histological analysis. The sample sizes for the animal tendon tissues were n = 3 for Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses, whereas n = 5 was used for IHC analysis.

TSPC isolation and culture

The methods of TSPC isolation and culture have been well established in our previous studies [9, 17]. Briefly, the middle substance of the patellar tendons was minced and digested with type I collagenase (3 mg/ml, Sigma Aldrich). Then, the mixture was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer (Becton Dickinson) to yield a single-cell suspension. The cells were cultured in complete medium containing low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin (all from Gibco). Passage 3 (P3) to P5 of healthy TSPCs (hTSPCs) from the control group and diabetic TSPCs (dTSPCs) from the DM group were used for all experiments. The medium was changed every 3 days. For all cellular assays, the sample size was defined as n = 3 independent biological replicates.

Osteogenic differentiation assay

The osteogenic differentiation induction of TSPCs has been well described in our previous studies [9, 17]. TSPCs were seeded in 6-well plates at 4 × 103 cells/cm2, cultured in complete medium until confluence, and then cultured in complete basal medium (BM) or osteogenic induction medium (OIM), which was complete basal medium supplemented with 20 mM β-glycerolphosphate, 1 nM dexamethasone and 50 mM ascorbic acid (all from Sigma Aldrich), for 14 days. Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was used to detect the mRNA expression of runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), osteopontin (OPN) and osteocalcin (OCN). Alizarin red S (ARS) staining was performed to evaluate calcium nodule formation.

mRNA expression of tenogenic genes

TSPCs were seeded in 6-well plates at 4 × 103 cells/cm2 and cultured in complete medium. On day 7, TSPCs were harvested, and the mRNA expression of the tenogenic genes collagen type I A 1 chain (Col 1A1), scleraxis (Scx) and tenomodulin (Tnmd) was measured by qRT-PCR.

qRT-PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from cell or tendon tissue samples with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed to cDNA using a HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. One microliter of total cDNA from each sample was amplified in a 20 μl reaction mixture containing ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) and specific primers for target genes using the ABI Step One Plus system (Applied Biosystems). The relative expression of each target gene was normalized to the β-actin gene and calculated as the fold change using the 2−ΔΔCT formula. The sequences of primer used in this study are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Cell transduction

The procedures of cell transduction were well established in our previous study [51]. Lentiviruses targeting HMGB1 and negative control vectors were purchased from GeneChem Corporation (Shanghai, China). A total of 8 × 104 cells were seeded in 6-well plates; cells at 20–30% confluence were transfected with the lentiviral vector using HitransG Transduction Reagent P (GeneChem) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transduction efficiency was verified by western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cell and tendon tissue samples using a Total Protein Extraction Kit (Keygen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The protein concentrations were detected by a BCA protein assay kit (Keygen Biotech). The protein was fractionated by SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% fat-free milk in TBST solution and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies at 4 C overnight. Primary antibodies are shown in Additional file 1: Table S2. After incubation with secondary antibodies (Proteintech) for 2 h at room temperature, the blots were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Keygen Biotech). The gray value of each band was examined, and data were normalized to β-actin and presented as fold change.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The supernatant of the cell samples was collected and centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min to remove cell debris. The levels of HMGB1 in each group were measured using an ELISA kit (Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

General histology and immunohistochemical staining

After being isolated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, the patellar tendons were dehydrated through gradient alcohol, embedded in paraffin, and cut longitudinally into 5-μm-thick slices. After deparaffinization and hydration, the slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Immunohistochemical staining was performed as previously described [52]. The primary antibodies are listed in Additional file 1: Table S2. Goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam) and 3,3' diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark) were used for section incubation. The sections were visualized under a light microscope (Leica Cambridge). Quantitative analysis of positive signals was performed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) by examining six randomly selected high-power fields (HPF) per section.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 16.0. All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Differences in mean values between groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison tests. P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

Induction of DM

We established rat DM models according to our previous study [9]. Before DM induction, there was no significant difference in blood glucose levels at each time point of IPGTT (Fig. S1A) or the mean area under the curve (AUC) of IPGTT (Fig. S1B) between the control and DM group subjects, indicating no difference in glucose tolerance and regulation between the control and DM group rats. However, the blood glucose levels at each time point of the IPGTT and the AUC of the IPGTT of DM subjects were significantly higher than those of controls three days post-STZ induction (Fig. S1C, D), indicating the severity of hyperglycemia in DM subjects. Moreover, the blood glucose values in DM subjects were sustained higher than 250 mg/dL during the following four weeks, which was markedly higher than that of the control group (Fig. S1E), suggesting sustained hyperglycemia of DM rats after STZ induction. In addition, the body weight of DM rats consistently declined during the first two weeks and then rose gradually; however, the body weight of control rats increased continuously and was significantly higher than that of DM rats in the four weeks after DM induction (Fig. S1F). By gross observation, the diabetic tendons appeared grayish compared to the healthy control tendons (Fig. S1G). The diabetic tendons appeared to be fragile and brittle when sliced. Histologically, collagen fiber disorganization, rounded tendon cells, red blood cells and blood vessels were observed in diabetic tendons (Fig. S1H). These results suggested the successful establishment of rat DM models and showed early pathological changes in the tendons of diabetic rats.

Activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin signaling pathway in dTSPCs

Next, we explored the activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin signaling pathway in dTSPCs. The results showed that the protein level of HMGB1 was significantly higher in dTSPCs than in hTSPCs (Fig. 1A, B), as was the extracellular HMGB1 level in the culture supernatant (Fig. 1C). In addition, the protein expression of RAGE, β-catenin and TCF-4 was also significantly increased in dTSPCs compared with hTSPCs (Fig. 1D–G). To further validate the role of HMGB1 in the activation of downstream RAGE/β-catenin signaling, we analyzed the pathway-related molecular changes in dTSPCs after HMGB1 knockdown (Fig. 1A–C). We found that HMGB1 knockdown reduced the expression of RAGE, β-catenin and TCF-4 in dTSPCs (Fig. 1D-G). Furthermore, inhibition of RAGE by the inhibitor FPS-ZM1 downregulated the levels of β-catenin and TCF-4 in dTSPCs (Fig. 1H–J). To further investigate the nuclear activation of β-catenin, we performed immunofluorescence staining. The results revealed that β-catenin expression was predominantly cytoplasmic in hTSPCs. In contrast, dTSPCs exhibited extensive nuclear localization of β-catenin. Notably, HMGB1 knockdown reduced the nuclear expression of β-catenin in dTSPCs (Fig. S2). Blockade of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis ameliorates the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs

To further investigate the role of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin signaling pathway in the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs during diabetic tendinopathy, we analyzed the differentiation ability of TSPCs after HMGB1 knockdown in dTSPCs or treatment with RAGE inhibitor FPS-ZM1 or β-catenin inhibitor PNU-74654. The results showed that the ARS-positive calcium nodules were significantly increased in dTSPCs compared with hTSPCs (Fig. 2A, B). The mRNA expression of the osteogenic genes Runx2, OPN and OCN was also markedly increased in dTSPCs (Fig. 2C–E), whereas the mRNA levels of the tenogenic markers Col 1A1, Scx and Tnmd were markedly decreased in dTSPCs (Fig. 2F–H). These results indicated the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs with enhanced osteogenic differentiation potential and reduced tenogenic differentiation ability of dTSPCs. HMGB1 knockdown significantly attenuated the intensity of ARS staining (Fig. 2A, B), and downregulated the mRNA expression of osteogenic genes (Fig. 2C–E). Activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in diabetic tendons

Given that the inhibition of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis could ameliorate the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs, to further investigate the role of this axis in diabetic tendinopathy, we first examined the activity of the axis by measuring the expression of HMGB1, RAGE and β-catenin in the tendons of diabetic rats. Immunohistochemical staining showed that compared with the control group, the expression of HMGB1 and β-catenin was increased in diabetic tendons at weeks 2 and 4, with HMGB1 mainly distributed in the extracellular matrix, while there was no significant change in the expression of RAGE (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis showed that the protein levels of HMGB1, RAGE and β-catenin were significantly higher in diabetic tendons at week 4 than in control tendons (Fig. 4B–E). In addition, qRT-PCR showed increased mRNA expression of HMGB1, RAGE and β-catenin in diabetic tendons at week 4 compared with the control group (Fig. 4F–H). To further determine the activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin pathway in vivo, we injected tendons with either the HMGB1 inhibitor glycyrrhizin or the RAGE inhibitor FPS-ZM1. The results showed that the injection of glycyrrhizin reduced the expression of HMGB1, RAGE and β-catenin in diabetic tendons (Fig. 4A–H). Moreover, FPS-ZM1 treatment markedly downregulated the mRNA and protein expression of β-catenin in diabetic tendons at week 4 (Fig. 4I–K). Discussion

Tendinopathy is formally diagnosed based on the constellation of symptoms including pain, swelling, and impaired performance [53]. Current consensus has shifted to view tendinopathy as a microenvironment of low-grade, chronic inflammation, accompanied by increased expression of inflammatory cytokines and growth factors [54]. The pathogenesis of tendinopathy has been linked to functional impairment of TSPCs during the development and progression of several factors [55]. Similar to MSCs, TSPCs exhibit self-renewal capacity and multidifferentiation potential [17, 18]. TSPCs are superior for tendon homeostasis maintenance and repair due to their tendon tissue-specific differentiation properties [18]. Spontaneous tenogenic differentiation has been reported as a specific feature of TSPCs [56], demonstrating the vital role of TSPCs in tendon homeostasis maintenance and repair. In the present study, we demonstrated that TSPCs isolated from diabetic tendons exhibited a phenotype of differentiation imbalance, as evidenced by enhanced osteogenic differentiation potential and reduced tenogenic differentiation ability, which is consistent with previous studies [9]. Rui et al. [57] first proposed the differentiation imbalance theory of TSPCs and elucidated its potential role in the pathogenesis of tendinopathy. A recent study using single-cell transcriptomics identified the Tppp3/Pdgfra-positive cell population as TSPCs, which could generate new tenocytes and undergo self-renewal upon tenon injury [58]. Yin et al. [59] reported that compared with nestin-negative TSPCs, nestin-positive TSPCs exhibited superior self-renewal and tenogenic differentiation potential, which was involved in the development and endogenous repair of tendon tissues. The differentiation imbalance of TSPCs into chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation rather than tenogenic differentiation has been reported to contribute to the pathogenesis of chronic tendinopathy [57, 60] and may also play an essential role in the pathological changes of diabetic tendons [6, 7, 11]. Kim et al. [8] reported that hyperglycemic conditions could affect tendon homeostasis due to increased osteochondrogenic differentiation and decreased tenogenic differentiation of TSPCs. Therefore, the altered differentiation fate of TSPCs is likely to affect tendon components and homeostasis [61], thereby impairing tendon repair and regeneration and facilitating the development and procession of diabetic tendinopathy.

Here, we demonstrated that activation of the HMGB1/ RAGE/β-catenin axis was closely associated with the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs and diabetic tendinopathy (Fig. 8). HMGB1 is widely expressed, resides primarily in the nucleus and participates in various physiological and pathological processes [27, 62]. Upon tissue injury or cellular stress, HMGB1 can be secreted into the extracellular environment passively from dead cells or actively from activated immune cells and other cell types. Extracellular HMGB1 acts as a DAMP or an alarmin or a novel cytokine involved in inflammation, tissue injury and regeneration [27, 62]. Previous studies reported that HMGB1 was increased in diabetic patients and involved in the pathogenesis of many diabetes-related diseases [32, 33]. High glucose treatment in vitro could increase the expression of HMGB1 in MSCs [63] and human retinal endothelial cells [64]. Recent studies have indicated that HMGB1 is involved in the regulation of stem cells. Exogeneous HMGB1 could inhibit the proliferation and promote the migration and osteogenic differentiation of human MSCs [31, 41, 42]. HMGB1 can bind with RAGE to regulate the differentiation of stem cells [28]. Moreover, HMGB1 treatment markedly increased RAGE expression in human BMSCs during osteogenic differentiation [41]. In addition, HMGB1 could promote osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs [46] and vascular calcification [47] through intracellular β-catenin signaling. Increased RAGE expression could promote osteogenic differentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells and vascular calcification by modulating downstream β-catenin signaling [48, 49]. In this study, we showed the activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in dTSPCs and further demonstrated that the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs was dependent on this axis. Intriguingly, blockade of this axis ameliorated the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs. Notably, β-catenin inhibition primarily played a vital role in mitigating the osteogenic differentiation of dTSPCs and at least partially reversed tenogenic differentiation, indicating that there may be other intracellular signals that regulate the tenogenic differentiation of TSPCs. Collectively, these results highlight the role of this axis in the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs and provide a potentially promising target and strategy for antagonizing the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs.Fig. 8Schematic diagram illustrating the role of HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in erroneous differentiation of TSPCs and diabetic tendinopathy

Histologically, previous studies reported an unparalleled arrangement of collagen fibers, disorganized tendon cells in the extracellular matrix, microtears and red blood cell accumulation in diabetic tendons [9], as well as rounded and chondrocyte-like cells [9, 65] and adipogenic transdifferentiating of tenocytes [66]. Increased vessels were also observed in the tendons of both diabetes patients and animal models, which may be associated with increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor in diabetic tendons [9, 67]. In the current study, we also observed a similar appearance, including collagen fiber disorganization, rounded tendon cells, red blood cells and increased blood vessels in rat diabetic tendons. Phenotypic variations in tendon cells may lead to homeostasis imbalance and accelerate tendon degeneration. Studies have also reported focal collagen degeneration [68], uneven glycoprotein deposition [69], lipid deposits [70] and calcium deposition [71] in diabetic tendons. These findings are consistent with the characteristic alternations of tendinopathy, indicating the degeneration of diabetic tendons. Previous studies showed that the expression of tendon-related markers was decreased and the expression of osteochondrogenic genes was increased in diabetic tendons [8, 9]. Our results were in line with previous studies [8, 9], indicating nontenogenic and structural alterations in diabetic tendons. These findings suggest that some tenocytes under diabetic conditions may exhibit increased nontenogenic differentiation potential. Calcium deposition or tendon calcification is also a common occurrence of tendinopathy in patients with DM [71, 72], but the mechanism of tendon calcification has not yet been fully illuminated. In the present study, we did not observe calcification in the tendons of diabetic rats, probably due to the insufficient duration of DM. These histological changes may lead to impaired structure and function, as well as reduced biomechanical properties of diabetic tendons, resulting in chronic pain and even tendon rupture. It has been reported that the enhanced osteogenic differentiation potential of TSPCs contributes to increased ossification in tendons [73]. Feng et al. [74] showed that a subpopulation of TSPCs expressing Cathepsin K was a primary source of cells involved in tendon ossification. Therefore, we speculated that the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs may account for the structural and nontenogenic alternations and calcification in diabetic tendons, providing new insight into the pathogenesis of diabetic tendinopathy.

In recent years, accumulating evidence has suggested that HMGB1 is increased in human [30, 35], rat [38] and mouse [36, 37, 75] tendinopathy models, and plays a variety of roles in tendon disorders such as proinflammation and matrix degeneration [40]. RAGE has also been found to increase in diseased human tendons and injured rat rotator cuff tendons, exacerbating tendinopathy and suppressing tendon healing [38, 76]. This study confirms the activation of the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis in diabetic tendons. HMGB1 inhibition suppressed RAGE/β-catenin signaling, while β-catenin blockade reduced osteogenic gene expression, indicating this axis drives aberrant differentiation. Importantly, targeting this pathway with glycyrrhizin, FPS-ZM1, or PNU-74654 ameliorated non-tenogenic alterations, highlighting its therapeutic potential for diabetic tendinopathy.

Extracellular matrix disorganization was associated with HMGB1 upregulation in injured rat rotator cuff tendons [38]. Akbar et al. [30] reported increased HMGB1 in human tendinopathic tissue, with the greatest expression in early tendinopathic tissues, regulating inflammatory cytokines and matrix changes. Zhang et al. [39] showed that the tendons administered with recombinant HMGB1 exhibited hypercellularity after 2 weeks, and the tendon tissues at the HMGB1 injection site showed more extensive proliferation of tendon cells. In addition, they also found that the time-dependent increase in cell growth was accompanied by the formation of vascular-like structures and increased infiltration of immune cells. In the present study, to further investigate the role of HMGB1, we injected HMGB1 around healthy patellar tendons to investigate the histiocyte alternations. The results showed notably increased fiber disorganization and hypercellularity and increased nuclear size and rounding of tendon cells in tendons administered with exogenous HMGB1 injection. We also observed a significant increase in vascular formation in tendons after treating with recombinant HMGB1, but no significant change was observed after 2 weeks. These appearances are consistent with the characteristics of tendon degenerative features. These alterations in tendon morphology, tissue structure and inflammatory response were significantly restored after treatment with the HMGB1 inhibitor glycyrrhizin [39], suggesting an essential involvement of HMGB1 in promoting the early development of tendinopathy. Furthermore, exogenous HMGB1 treatment significantly inhibit collagen generation and increase collagen catabolism, leading to impaired matrix remodeling [39]. Notably, we also found that exogenous HMGB1 significantly increased the expression of osteogenic markers. Nontenogenic structural changes combined with damaged matrix remodeling may ultimately synergize to accelerate the occurrence and progression of tendinopathy. Meanwhile, the expression of RAGE and β-catenin was also increased in tendons treated with exogenous HMGB1, further demonstrating the role of HMGB1 in the activation of RAGE/β-catenin signaling. Collectively, these results suggest that HMGB1 is a critical cytokine contributing to the development and progression of tendinopathy and promotes the expression of osteogenic markers in tendons through RAGE/β-catenin signaling, providing a potential therapeutic target.

Several limitations in the current study should be acknowledged. First, while our data implicate TSPC differentiation imbalance in diabetic tendinopathy, further in vivo lineage-tracing studies are required to definitively establish that the observed nontenogenic changes in diabetic tendons originate from TSPCs and to identify which specific TSPC subpopulations are critically involved. The positive cells in IHC may include not only TSPCs but also mature tenocytes and potentially some infiltrating cells, and that the lack of a definitive TSPC-specific marker is a challenge for the entire field. In the future, we will employ more advanced techniques to unequivocally determine the contribution of specific tendon cell subpopulations in vivo. Second, our findings indicate that β-catenin signaling primarily regulates osteogenic differentiation, with a limited role in tenogenic differentiation. This suggests the involvement of other, yet-to-be-identified intracellular signals in driving tenogenic differentiation under diabetic conditions, which warrants future investigation. Thirdly, although our findings are strongly supported by complementary pharmacological and genetic inhibition of HMGB1, we did not explore other orthogonal validation methods, such as independent siRNA constructs or neutralizing antibodies. Fourth, our study lacked in vivo functional analyses, such as biomechanical testing and gait analysis, to correlate the molecular changes with tendon functional properties. Finally, the use of only male animals limits the exploration of potential sex-related differences in diabetic tendinopathy. These limitations represents an important direction for our future work.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that the differentiation imbalance of dTSPCs was dependent on the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis. Moreover, the HMGB1/RAGE/β-catenin axis also played a critical role in the nontenogenic alteration in diabetic tendons with increased expression of osteogenic genes and decreased expression of tendon-related genes. Inhibition of this axis could attenuate the differentiation imbalance of TSPCs and ameliorate nontenogenic alterations in diabetic tendons. Our findings suggest a novel essential mechanism involved in the pathogenesis of diabetic tendinopathy and provide a promising therapeutic target and approach for diabetic tendinopathy.
