Interaction between methylated arginine metabolites and oxidative stress in periodontitis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bugra Balkan, Zeliha Guney, Ezgi Balkan, Elif Polat Balkan, Sivge Kurgan, Nur Balci, Muhittin A. Serdar, Meral Gunhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease associated with systemic conditions such as endothelial dysfunction. Nitric oxide (NO) plays a central role in inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are critical in periodontal pathogenesis. Arginine (Arg) is the precursor of NO, while its methylated derivatives, asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) and symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA), inhibit NO synthesis. This study investigated the interplay between NO metabolism and oxidative stress in periodontitis.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 20 patients with periodontitis and 20 healthy controls. Clinical periodontal assessments were performed, and salivary and serum levels of the oxidative stress index (OSI), ADMA, SDMA, and arginine were measured using Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsGroups did not differ by age or sex (p = 0.053 and p = 0.507). Clinical periodontal parameters were significantly higher in the periodontitis group (p < 0.001). Salivary OSI, ADMA, SDMA, and Arg were significantly elevated in periodontitis patients (p = 0.026, p = 0.002, p = 0.006, and p < 0.001), while serum SDMA levels were reduced (p = 0.026). Salivary OSI, ADMA, and Arg correlated positively with all clinical periodontal parameters (p < 0.05). Salivary SDMA correlated with probing depth (r = 0.329, p = 0.039), and strong associations were observed between salivary ADMA and SDMA (r = 0.722, p < 0.001) and between serum ADMA and Arg (r = 0.461, p = 0.003).ConclusionThis study underscores the complex interplay between NO metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation in periodontitis, suggesting that ADMA- and SDMA-mediated disruption of Arg metabolism may exacerbate oxidative stress and inflammation, worsening the disease.Clinical relevanceThis study provides insights into the interplay between NO metabolism and oxidative stress in periodontitis, emphasizing the potential role of ADMA and SDMA in disease progression. Understanding these mechanisms may contribute to a better comprehension of the systemic impact of periodontitis.Trial registrationThe trial protocol was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT06973148) on April 28, 2025.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by an imbalance in pro- and antioxidant activity and is an independent risk factor contributing to the pathophysiology of endothelial dysfunction [1–4]. Recent studies have reported that nitric oxide (NO) is vital in inflammatory diseases [5–8]. Nitric oxide is involved in preventing platelet aggregation, adhesion, and activation and eliminating the toxic effects of superoxide ions; it also plays a key role in endothelial dysfunction [9].

Arginine is an important amino acid in the mechanism of the inflammatory response and is the only amino acid substrate used to produce NO by all isoforms of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) [10]. Methylated arginines (asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) and symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA)) inhibit the production of NO [11]. Under average ADMA concentrations, intracellular nitric oxide synthase (NOS) effectively utilizes the substrate L-arginine (L-Arg), producing physiological NO levels. However, when confronted with elevated levels of ADMA associated with pathology, NOS activity diminishes, leading to a decrease in NO production [12].

In the meantime, ADMA can cleave NOS isoenzymes to produce superoxide, which contributes to the oxidative stress load [13]. In addition, SDMA inhibits the uptake of L-Arg into the cell, indirectly affecting the production of NOS. In addition to inhibiting NO production and pro-inflammatory effects [10, 13], SDMA may exhibit prooxidant properties by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) in monocytes [14] and increasing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase (NADPH-oxidase) [15].

The first and most important pathway that is effective in ADMA catabolism is the breakdown of ADMA into citrulline and dimethylamines by the dimethylarginine dimethylaminohydrolase (DDAH) enzyme, which is responsible for 70% of ADMA catabolism in the body. A small part of it is excreted from the kidneys without any change [16]. Oxidative stress impairs DDAH activity by oxidizing its reactive cysteine residues, leading to a decline in enzymatic function [17]. Consequently, this reduction in DDAH activity results in an accumulation of free ADMA levels [18].

Based on the available literature and our previous studies, increased levels of ADMA and SDMA appear to play a role in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease [19, 20]. Moreover, previous studies have highlighted the interplay between oxidative stress and methylated arginines in systemic conditions such as kidney disease [21] and hypertension [22, 23]. However, no study to date has simultaneously evaluated ADMA, SDMA, and the oxidative stress index (OSI) in the context of periodontitis. This represents a critical gap in the literature, as such an integrated assessment could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the biochemical mechanisms linking oxidative stress and NO metabolism in periodontal inflammation.

Building on this evidence, we hypothesized that in periodontitis, disruption of arginine metabolism, driven by elevated ADMA and SDMA, reduces nitric oxide bioavailability, thereby amplifying oxidative stress and contributing to periodontal inflammation with systemic consequences. Accordingly, our study aimed to investigate the relationship between NO metabolism (via ADMA, SDMA, and Arg) and oxidative stress (via OSI), and to evaluate their reciprocal effects.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study included 20 individuals diagnosed with Stage III Grade B periodontitis (P group; n = 20; female/male: 14/6; mean age: 38.8 ± 12.6 years) and 20 periodontally healthy participants as controls (C group; female/male: 12/8; mean age: 31.3 ± 9.0 years), in accordance with the STROBE Checklist. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Ankara University. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision), under protocol number 04/11 dated February 17, 2021. Participant recruitment took place at the Department of Periodontology, Ankara University Faculty of Dentistry, from June 2021 to December 2022. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Radiographic and clinical periodontal examinations were applied, and probing depth (PD) [24], plaque index (PI) [25], bleeding on probing (BOP) [26], and clinical attachment loss (CAL) [27] were recorded at six tooth sites (mesio-buccal, mid-buccal, disto-buccal, mesio-lingual, mid-lingual, and disto-lingual) by a sole investigator (NB) at baseline. Examiner calibration was conducted prior to data collection, and intra-examiner agreement for periodontal measurements achieved a Cohen’s kappa value of 0.84. Participants who had at least 20 natural teeth were included.

Periodontal diagnosis was made according to the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions [28]. Periodontal health was defined as the absence of a history of periodontitis or clinical symptoms, BOP < 10%, PD ≤ 3 mm, no clinical signs of gingival inflammation, and overall well-maintained oral hygiene [57]. Periodontitis was defined by the presence of interdental CAL at ≥ 2 non-adjacent teeth or buccal/oral CAL ≥ 3 mm with PD > 3 mm at ≥ 2 teeth [29]. Stage III periodontitis was diagnosed based on the following criteria: radiographic bone loss extending to the middle or apical third of the root, tooth loss due to periodontitis (≤ 4 teeth), and interdental CAL ≥ 5 mm at the most severely affected site. Grade B was assigned if the bone loss-to-age ratio ranged from 0.25 to 1.

The exclusion criteria were (1) patients having diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and/or any autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, (2) pregnant and lactating mothers, (3) tobacco users and vapers, (4) patients using antibiotics, anti-inflammatory medications, immunosuppressive drugs, contraceptives within three months, (5) patients who had periodontal treatment during the last six months. Clinical periodontal parameters were evaluated and recorded at the first visit. Following group assignment and participant consent, saliva and serum samples were collected on the subsequent day between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. to minimize any potential influence of clinical measurements on salivary components.

Saliva and serum sampling

Unstimulated saliva samples were obtained from each participant following a detailed explanation of the collection procedure. Initially, participants rinsed their mouths thoroughly with filtered water and were then seated in a relaxed position to drool into a disposable tube for five minutes passively. The collected saliva was centrifuged at 2800 × g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was transferred into Eppendorf tubes for storage [30].

Blood samples were obtained via standard venipuncture and left at room temperature for 30 min to allow for clot formation. Serum was then separated by centrifuging the blood at 4000 × g for 10 min and subsequently transferred into Eppendorf tubes. All serum and saliva samples were stored at −80 °C until the time of analysis.

Determination of salivary and serum oxidative stress index (OSI)

The samples' total antioxidant and oxidant capacity levels were evaluated with the method developed by Erel [31, 32].

The principle of the test is based on the decrease of the blue-green color formed by the 2,2'-azinobis-3- ethylebenzothiazoline-6-sulfonate radical cation (ABTS + radical) with the antioxidants in the sample added to the medium. 2,2'-azinobis-3- ethylebenzothiazoline-6-sulfonate (ABTS) is incubated with a peroxidase metmyoglobin (HX-Fe+) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to form the ABTS + radical. To form the ABTS + radical, the resultant myoglobin reacts with ABTS. ABTS + radical is blue-green and partly stable. The color formation inhibition occurs according to the ratio of antioxidants in the added sample. Varioskan Multimode Reader1 was used to measure the color change at a wavelength of 600 nm and calibrated with ascorbic acid, which is traditionally used to provide the standard for total antioxidant status (TAS) measurement. The results of the measure are united as mmol ascorbic acid equivalent/l.

In the presence of several different oxidant species in an acidic medium and measurements of ferric ions with xylenol orange, a total oxidative level assessment shall be based on oxidation to ferrous ions. In this method, oxidants oxidize the iron-o-dianisidine complex to iron ions. A colored xylenol orange = ferric ion complex is formed by the produced ferric ions. The sample's oxidant level influences the intensity of color. This color change was measured with a Varioskan Multimode Reader1 at a wavelength of 530 nm. H2O2 was used as a standard for total oxidant status (TOS) calculation. Results are expressed as μmol H2O2 equivalent/l.

The OSI was calculated using the formula OSI = TOS/TASx10.

Determination of salivary and serum methylated arginine metabolites

Salivary and serum levels of arginine and methylated arginines were measured using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS, ESI Source, Thermo Scientific Accessmax), with methodological adjustments based on the protocol described by Di Gangi et al. [33].

Statistical analysis

A priori sample size analysis was conducted using specialized software. Using a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.029) for two groups, based on differences in the salivary ADMA levels reported by Şengül et al. [19], a 0.05 α-error, and 95% power, the required total sample size was calculated as 32 participants (n = 16 per group). To account for potential confounding factors and possible incomplete data collection, the target sample size was set at 20 rather than the minimum requirement.

All statistical analyses were run by Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 22.0.2 Normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For intergroup comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to non-normally distributed variables, and the Student t-test was applied to normally distributed variables. Spearman’s correlation test was used to evaluate the correlation between groups. The α = 0.05 significance level has been used for all tests.

Discussion

Our study aimed to evaluate the link between NO metabolism and oxidative stress regarding periodontal inflammation. Salivary ADMA, SDMA, Arg levels, and OSI were significantly higher in the periodontitis group than in the control group, with large effect sizes, indicating not only statistical but also clinical significance. In contrast, serum markers showed smaller or negligible effects, with only SDMA significantly lower in periodontitis, highlighting saliva as a more sensitive medium for detecting biochemical alterations.

Oxidative stress is an inflammatory process caused by an imbalance between ROS and antioxidant systems, and this process can damage cellular and extracellular components [34]. ROS can induce inflammation and cause direct tissue damage, including lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and oxidation of essential enzymes [35]. Clinical and basic experimental studies have shown a strong association between oxidative stress and periodontitis in the last decade [36–41]. In 2005, OSI, measured as TOS/TAS, was developed to demonstrate the amount of oxidative stress [32]. In this study, salivary OSI was significantly increased in the periodontitis group (d = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.11–1.49), supporting its role as a sensitive biomarker of oxidative burden [42, 43]. Increased OSI can be explained by increased NADPH oxidase activity during phagocytosis [35] or the hyper-reactive phenotype of neutrophils [34, 43] during periodontitis. In addition, fibroblasts activated by periodontopathogens also produce excessive ROS [44, 45]. Due to increased OSI, the expression of NOS is paradoxically increased, causing the formation of uncoupled NOS [46]. In this uncoupled state, producing superoxide rather than NO leads to oxidative stress.

The activity of the enzymes involved in the formation and degradation of ADMA, such as protein arginine N-methyltransferases (PRMTs) and DDAH, has been shown to be regulated in a redox-sensitive fashion [47]. Thus, activating the above-mentioned enzymes and the subsequently increased vascular superoxide production should consistently lead to increased ADMA concentrations [48, 49]. Parallel to this information, ADMA levels were found to be high in this study and our previous studies [19, 20], with a large effect size, reinforcing its relevance as a robust marker of oxidative stress–related NO dysregulation.

Unlike ADMA, the pathophysiological implications of SDMA have received relatively limited consideration. In addition to its role in inhibiting NO production [15], SDMA may exhibit pro-inflammatory effects [50], including the initiation of ROS generation [8]. The increased SDMA levels in the current study are consistent with our previous research [19, 20], and the effect size suggests that this elevation is both statistically and clinically meaningful. ADMA and SDMA have a proinflammatory effect and increase reactive oxygen species, although they restrict NO production through different mechanisms. The elevation of proinflammatory cytokines might lead to an increase in Arg concentration [51, 52]. Similar to these studies, we observed that Arg levels increased in periodontal disease, with a particularly large effect size, making Arg one of the most distinctive biochemical features of periodontitis in this cohort. Increased Arg levels can be explained by increased SDMA levels due to the ability of SDMA to inhibit Arg uptake into the cell.

To our knowledge, no periodontal study has directly investigated the mechanistic relationship between ADMA, SDMA, and OSI. However, data from other chronic immuno-inflammatory and systemic disease models shed important light. In rheumatoid arthritis [53, 54] and obstructive sleep apnea (OSAS) [55], elevated ADMA and SDMA levels have been closely linked with increased OSI, impaired NO bioavailability, and endothelial dysfunction. These conditions demonstrate the same bidirectional interaction we propose here: oxidative stress promotes dimethylarginine accumulation, while ADMA/SDMA, in turn, amplifies oxidative burden, sustaining inflammation. The moderate-to-large effect sizes we observed across salivary ADMA, SDMA, and OSI in periodontitis provide strong support for this model within the oral inflammatory context.

More recent studies extend this relationship to hepatotoxicity [56, 57], sepsis [58], and cardiovascular disease [59]. In hepatotoxicity models, antioxidant modulation of ADMA and SDMA metabolism reduced OSI and protected against ROS-mediated hepatocyte injury [56, 57]. In sepsis, plasma ADMA and SDMA levels correlate with severity and mortality, reflecting their integration into oxidative stress–driven systemic inflammation [58]. In heart failure, SDMA is an independent predictor of endothelial dysfunction, with OSI rising in parallel [59]. Across these diverse contexts, the ADMA/SDMA–OSI axis emerges as a unifying pathogenic pathway in oxidative stress–mediated tissue injury. Our findings extend this axis to periodontitis, and the magnitude of observed effect sizes suggests that these alterations are not minor but clinically relevant, linking local disease with systemic inflammatory patterns.

Our findings in periodontitis thus align with this broader evidence: the concomitant increase of ADMA, SDMA, and OSI in saliva suggests that periodontal tissues share the same pathophysiological mechanisms observed in systemic diseases. This positions ADMA and SDMA not only as local markers of periodontal inflammation but also as potential cross-disease biomarkers of oxidative stress activity with systemic relevance. Taken together, the observed increases in OSI, ADMA, SDMA, and Arg may contribute to multiple stages of periodontal tissue destruction: oxidative stress and NO dysregulation can initiate inflammatory signaling, perpetuate connective tissue breakdown, and ultimately accelerate attachment loss and bone resorption. Importantly, the positive correlations of salivary OSI, ADMA, and Arg with clinical periodontal parameters suggest that these biomarkers may not only reflect underlying biochemical mechanisms but also hold potential diagnostic and prognostic value. Monitoring these parameters could therefore aid in assessing disease activity and progression, as well as in evaluating treatment outcomes in clinical practice. The large effect sizes for clinical parameters, together with significant correlations between salivary biomarkers and CAL, PD, and BOP, emphasize the close interplay between biochemical mechanisms and clinical expression of disease.

Periodontal disease exerts systemic effects; however, many biomarkers indicative of these effects do not demonstrate significant differences in serum samples [60, 61]. Notably, while salivary levels of ADMA and Arg show statistically significant differences, the corresponding serum levels remain unchanged. In contrast, only serum SDMA levels exhibit a significant difference, which may reflect a regulatory mechanism that contributes to overall host homeostasis. Under oxidative stress, NOS becomes uncoupled, leading to an increased production of superoxide anions. These anions react with NO to form peroxynitrite, a highly reactive free radical [62]. Peroxynitrite further induces NOS uncoupling, resulting in additional production of ADMA and SDMA, along with increased urea formation [63]. This cascade enhances urinary excretion, accounting for 100% of SDMA clearance and approximately 20% of ADMA clearance [64]. Consequently, while serum ADMA and Arg levels remain stable, the observed decrease in serum SDMA levels can be attributed to this enhanced clearance mechanism, which aligns with the negative effect size detected.

This study has certain limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes drawing causal inferences regarding the relationship between NO metabolism, oxidative stress, and periodontal inflammation. Second, although the power analysis confirmed that a total of 40 participants was the minimum sample size required, the relatively modest cohort may still limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, while salivary and serum biomarkers provide valuable insights, they represent single-time-point measurements and may not fully capture the dynamic changes occurring during periodontal inflammation. In addition, although examiner calibration was performed, some degree of measurement variability in clinical periodontal parameters cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, while we excluded all systemic conditions that could influence periodontal health and included only systemically healthy individuals, other unmeasured factors such as dietary habits, local oral conditions, or genetic predispositions may still have had an impact. Future longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and to further clarify the underlying biological mechanisms.

This study highlights the critical role of nitric oxide metabolism and oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. The interplay between methylated arginines and oxidative stress may contribute to disease progression by influencing inflammatory responses and cellular homeostasis. While these findings reinforce the connection between periodontal inflammation and systemic health, further research is needed to fully elucidate the mechanisms involved, particularly the role of DDAH and NO metabolism. A more comprehensive understanding of these pathways could provide deeper insights into the systemic implications of periodontitis and its association with endothelial dysfunction. By integrating effect sizes and correlation data, our study demonstrates that salivary OSI, ADMA, SDMA, and Arg are not only statistically distinct between groups but also clinically meaningful, offering promising avenues for non-invasive diagnosis, monitoring, and potentially prognostic evaluation in periodontitis.

Conclusion

This study highlights the interplay between nitric oxide metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation in Stage III Grade B periodontitis, suggesting that ADMA- and SDMA-mediated disruptions in arginine metabolism may intensify oxidative burden and inflammatory responses. Together with the strong associations between salivary biomarkers and clinical parameters, these findings support their potential value as non-invasive indicators of disease activity and progression, while also pointing to shared mechanisms with systemic inflammatory conditions.
