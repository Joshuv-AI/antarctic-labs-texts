Clarifying the Longitudinal Interpretation of Wind-Up Ratio After Lower-Limb Amputation [Letter]
Authors: Mingyang Wang, Xiaoyu Yang, Li Yang
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with interest the article by Wagner et al, who used repeated quantitative sensory testing (QST) to examine sensory changes after lower-limb amputation.1 Repeated QST and the participant-level trajectories permit examination of within-person change in a clinically heterogeneous pain condition. However, interpretation of the wind-up ratio (WUR) findings by phantom limb pain (PLP) status depends on the grouping rule used in the longitudinal analyses and on whether the reported contrasts support the conclusion.

PLP status was assigned post hoc at each assessment using a Multidimensional Pain Inventory Phantom Pain Severity score of at least 1. Among the ten participants with T2 QST data, Table 1 indicates four persistent PLP trajectories (IDs 2, 3, 5, and 9), three incident trajectories (IDs 1, 8, and 10), one remitting trajectory (ID 7), and two persistently absent trajectories (IDs 4 and 11). ID 6 withdrew before T2. Supplementary Information 2 states that only T1 data are available for this participant, apparently in relation to the eight QST trajectories, whereas Table 1 reports a T2 PLP score and T2 medication. It would be helpful to clarify whether these clinical data were collected without T2 QST and how ID 6 was handled in descriptions of PLP trajectories. Figure 3 otherwise presents time-specific groups: PLP+ N = 5 and PLP-N = 6 at T1, compared with PLP+ N = 7 and PLP-N = 3 at T2.

Table 4 appears to use a different grouping rule. Its WUR comparisons report t(4) for both PLP+ and PLP-, indicating five paired observations per group. Supplementary Information 2 shows paired WUR values for the five participants who were PLP+ at T1 (IDs 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9) and the five T1 PLP- completers (IDs 1, 4, 8, 10, and 11). Thus, Table 4 appears to anchor PLP status at T1, although this is not stated in the Statistical Analysis section or table note. Under that rule, the PLP+ group includes a participant whose PLP remitted, whereas the PLP- group includes three who developed PLP by T2. Figure 3 and Table 4 therefore address different questions: concurrent group differences and change within baseline-defined groups, respectively. This distinction affects interpretation.2 We ask the authors to confirm the grouping anchor, report participant IDs and outcome-specific exclusions for each paired comparison, and provide the exact individual WUR changes.

The inferential contrast also warrants attention. A significant within-group WUR change in PLP+ (P = 0.003) and a non-significant change in PLP- (P = 0.199) do not show that change differed between groups.3 If the conclusion concerns evolution according to baseline PLP status, it should be supported by a direct comparison of individual WUR changes or a group-by-time interaction. Given the ten T2 QST completers, exact individual changes would be informative; when feasible, the difference in WUR change between the two baseline groups should be reported with a confidence interval. This avoids unstable inferential estimates for the persistent, incident, remitting, and persistently absent subgroups, some of which contain only one or two participants.

The cross-sectional evidence cited with the longitudinal finding also raises a test-direction inconsistency. The Methods state that tests were two-tailed unless otherwise described, but the T2 PLP+ versus PLP- WUR comparison in Table 2 reports t(8) = 2.057, P = 0.037. The corresponding two-sided P value is approximately 0.074; P = 0.037 is the one-sided value, while the reported 95% confidence interval (−0.03 to 0.50) includes zero. The same pattern appears elsewhere in the PLP+ versus PLP- subset: for example, t(8) = 1.851 with P = 0.051 for T1 WDT also corresponds to a one-sided calculation. We suggest auditing this comparison subset, stating whether directional hypotheses and their directions were prespecified, and aligning the Methods, P values, confidence intervals, and table note. This issue is separate from, and does not negate, the paired WUR result.

Clarifying the grouping anchor, analysis sets, change contrast, and test direction would allow readers to judge the WUR findings more accurately. Until then, the conclusion that central sensitization became more prominent over time should remain exploratory, particularly because its relation to persistent, incident, or remitting PLP is uncertain. WUR is best described here as a QST marker interpreted as reflecting central sensitization rather than independent confirmation of that mechanism. A focused clarification and reanalysis would strengthen the study’s contribution to understanding sensory change after amputation.
