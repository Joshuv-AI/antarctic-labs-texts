Rescuing Acute Myocardial Infarction–Related Left Ventricular Rupture and Severe Mitral Regurgitation With ECMO
Authors: Xiang Qu, Changxi Chen, Chuhuan Zhao, Xiangbin Pan, Hao Zhou
Published: 2026

Ventricular free wall rupture (VFWR) is a catastrophic mechanical complication after acute myocardial infarction (AMI) that typically leads to death if left untreated. Although surgical repair is the primary treatment, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) can serve as a bridge to treatment, providing temporary circulatory support for patients with hemodynamic instability or those who are not suitable for immediate surgery.3 Concurrently, mitral regurgitation (MR) is a common complication after AMI, increasing cardiac workload and affecting long-term prognosis.1 For high-risk patients unable to tolerate traditional surgery, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) of the mitral valve has emerged as a promising alternative.2 We describe an elderly female patient who developed a small VFWR and severe MR after AMI, whose condition was stabilized with conservative treatment supported by ECMO, and who subsequently underwent successful TEER to control the MR. This case aims to explore the feasibility and effectiveness of ECMO support combined with TEER in patients with small VFWR and severe MR after AMI, providing new ideas and options for clinical practice.

History of Presentation and Past Medical History

An 81-year-old woman with a history of hypertension presented with acute inferior wall ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) (Figure 1A). She was scheduled for emergency percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Intraoperatively, a 90% segmental stenosis with a large thrombus was observed in the mid right coronary artery, with some thrombus dislodging and occluding the posterior ventricular branch. After repeated thrombus aspiration of the right coronary artery, a stent was implanted (4.0 × 24 mm Excrossal, Jiwei Inc) (Figure 2).

Investigations

On day 1 after PCI, the patient suddenly experienced a drop in blood pressure, jugular venous distention, and loss of consciousness. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a large pericardial effusion with cardiac tamponade, a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 45%, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) of 41 mm, and moderate MR (Figure 3A).

Management

VFWR was suspected. Antiplatelet medications were discontinued, fluid resuscitation was accelerated, vasopressors were administered, and the patient was intubated and venoarterial ECMO support was initiated. A 21-F drainage catheter was placed in the right femoral vein and a 15-F perfusion catheter was placed in the right femoral artery. The ECMO flow rate was 3.25 to 3.5 L/min, and the rotational speed was 3,700 to 4,000 rpm. Right lower limb Doppler ultrasound was performed to assess arterial flow velocity and volume, which showed a right common femoral artery internal diameter of 6.6 mm and a peak systolic velocity of 22 cm/s. During ECMO support, if the patient's distal limb skin is pink, limb temperature is warm, and capillary refill of the nail bed returns within 2 seconds after pressure, the patient's peripheral microcirculation is considered to be good. Pericardiocentesis was performed under ultrasound guidance, and 200 mL of bloody pericardial fluid was drained. Subsequently, the pericardial effusion was drained at the rate of 5 mL/h. Following this, the patient's hemodynamics stabilized. Given the high surgical risk of emergent VFWR repair after STEMI and the slowing rate of pericardial fluid drainage, conservative treatment was temporarily administered under close monitoring of vital signs.

According to the volume of pericardial effusion indicated by TTE and the drainage volume of the pericardial puncture tube (Table 1), bivalirudin was used for anticoagulation on day 2 after PCI, and the activated partial thromboplastin time was maintained at 1.5 to 2.5 times the normal value. On day 3 post-PCI, the patient was weaned from ventilator support, and clopidogrel 75 mg once daily was added. TTE indicated that the LVEF was 33%, the ECMO was adjusted to 3.0 L/min, and the rotational speed was adjusted to 3,500 rpm. On day 8 post-PCI, colchicine 0.5 mg twice daily was added to reduce inflammatory exudation in the pericardial cavity. The ECMO flow rate was adjusted to 2.0 L/min, and the rotational speed was adjusted to 3,000 rpm. On day 9, TTE showed an LVEF of 42%, left ventricular outflow tract velocity time integral of 13.9 cm, and moderate to severe MR (Figure 3B), after which ECMO support was subsequently weaned off. On day 15 post-PCI, based on the drainage volume of pericardial effusion (Table 1), considering the VFWR had spontaneously repaired, the pericardial drainage tube was removed.

On day 25 post-PCI, the patient developed refractory heart failure. Despite volume removal via continuous renal replacement therapy, the heart failure symptoms did not improve. Hypotension also developed, requiring norepinephrine and vasopressin to maintain blood pressure. Consequently, an intra-aortic balloon pump was used, which provided partial relief from the heart failure. A follow-up electrocardiogram showed no recurrence of myocardial ischemia (Figure 1B), and TTE revealed no pericardial effusion, an LVEF of 49%, an LVEDD of 52 mm, and severe MR (Figure 3C). We considered that the MR was the primary contributor to the heart failure. Therefore, on day 44 after PCI, the patient underwent TEER of the mitral valve. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography revealed severe (4+) MR in the A2 and P2 regions (Figure 3D), and 2 clips were deployed in this area. Postoperatively, the MR was reduced to mild (1+) (Figure 3E), with a mean mitral valve gradient of 2 mm Hg and a maximum of 4 mm Hg. Predischarge TTE showed an LVEF of 40%, an LVEDD of 57 mm, and mild MR (Figure 3F).

Outcome and Follow-Up

On day 4 after TEER, the patient was transferred to general ward for rehabilitation, and on day 11 after TEER, the patient was discharged. At the outpatient follow-up 3 months after discharge, the patient's cardiac function was classified as NYHA functional class I. Echocardiography revealed an LVEF of 48%, mild MR (1+), an LVEDD of 51 mm, and no pericardial effusion.

Discussion

VFWR and severe MR after AMI are rare but life-threatening complications. This case report describes the successful management of an 81-year-old female patient who developed VFWR and severe MR after acute STEMI. A comprehensive approach using conservative therapy, ECMO support, and TEER achieved a favorable outcome.

According to an analysis of 6,712 STEMI patients, 78 cases (approximately 1.16%) experienced VFWR, with a high in-hospital mortality rate of 92.3%.3 Even with surgical intervention, mortality rates as high as 44% to 67% have been reported.4 Treatment strategies for VFWR primarily include surgical and conservative management. Surgical repair is generally considered the preferred treatment option,5 but conservative treatment may also be successful in patients with hemodynamically stable conditions, localized or small-sized VFWR, subacute or delayed rupture, or oozing-type VFWR.6 This 81-year-old patient developed acute pericardial tamponade from VFWR 1 day post-PCI, but only 200 mL of bloody effusion was drained, with a slow subsequent rate (5 mL/h), suggesting a small, thrombus-sealed rupture—a “pseudo-healing” that enabled conservative management. Immediate intubation and venoarterial ECMO support provided cardiopulmonary rest, reducing myocardial oxygen demand and preventing further tearing. The inferior wall AMI location likely contributed to lower ventricular pressure and slower bleeding, while the absence of extensive cardiomyopathy preserved local repair capacity. Given the high surgical risk in this elderly patient, conservative therapy was pursued under close monitoring, aided by colchicine to suppress pericardial inflammation and promote healing, along with careful adjustment of anticoagulant and antiplatelet regimens.

In this STEMI case, intensified dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated post-PCI to prevent stent thrombosis. However, VFWR detected on day 1 necessitated immediate dual antiplatelet therapy discontinuation to prioritize hemostasis. Limited pericardial drainage suggested transient “pseudo-healing” of the VFWR, prompting the use of bivalirudin—rather than heparin—for anticoagulation during ECMO, given its association with lower risks of circuit thrombosis, bleeding, and mortality in ECMO patients.7 With no existing guidance on antiplatelet resumption timing in VFWR, clopidogrel was cautiously restarted on day 3 after VFWR detection to mitigate high in-stent thrombosis risk while minimizing fatal bleeding.

In this case, the patient developed severe ischemic MR after AMI, primarily because of myocardial necrosis, fibrosis, left ventricular remodeling, and tethering of the mitral apparatus—without papillary muscle or chordal rupture. Altered left ventricular geometry led to papillary muscle displacement and impaired leaflet coaptation, resulting in Carpentier IIIb MR. TEER is a safe, effective, minimally invasive alternative for patients at high surgical risk, especially elderly individuals with cardiogenic shock, as it avoids cardiopulmonary bypass and reduces perioperative complications. TEER has demonstrated a 28% reduction in 5-year all-cause mortality and 47% fewer heart failure hospitalizations.8 For MR complicated by recent VFWR, optimal TEER timing is undefined. Early intervention may be limited by small left atrial volume—impeding clip delivery—and it could increase the risk of VFWR rerupture.9 However, excessive delay risks irreversible left ventricular remodeling and worsening heart failure. As a left atrial diameter of >55 mm correlates with higher post-TEER mortality,10 TEER should be performed as soon as hemodynamic stability is achieved and VFWR is controlled.

Conclusions

This case highlights the successful management of a rare and life-threatening complication—VFWR with severe MR—after AMI. Through a combination of emergency PCI, timely venoarterial ECMO support, careful anticoagulation strategy, and ultimately TEER intervention, the patient achieved hemodynamic stability and improvement in cardiac function. The favorable outcome underscores the importance of a stepwise, individualized approach and close hemodynamic monitoring in high-risk post-MI complications.
