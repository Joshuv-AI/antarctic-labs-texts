Integrating Dietary Inflammatory Index and Triglyceride-Glucose Body Mass Index for Mortality Association in Hypertension
Authors: Jianjun Wu, Youqi Zhang, Ning Liu, Liu Ji, Huimin Xian, Ziqian Weng, Dawei Yang, Fan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiet-related inflammation and metabolic dysfunction may influence long-term outcomes in hypertension.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to evaluate whether the DII (Dietary Inflammatory Index) and TyG-BMI (triglyceride-glucose body mass index), individually and jointly, are associated with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in adults with hypertension.MethodsThe authors analyzed 8,024 hypertensive adults (≥18 years) from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2001 to 2018 with a median follow-up of 9.91 years. DII was calculated from 27 nutrient intakes (24-hour recalls) and categorized into tertiles; TyG-BMI was derived from fasting triglycerides, fasting glucose, and BMI.ResultsProinflammatory diets were associated with higher risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.00-1.85) and all-cause mortality (HR: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.08-1.54). Elevated TyG-BMI was associated with higher CVD mortality (HR: 1.46; 95% CI: 1.10-1.94), whereas its association with all-cause mortality was not statistically significant (HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 0.96-1.32). Participants with both high DII and high TyG-BMI had nearly a 2-fold higher risk of CVD mortality (HR: 1.98; 95% CI: 1.33-2.94). In subgroup analyses, risks appeared stronger in men (CVD HR: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.27-3.63) and among adults ≥60 years, where the highest CVD risk was observed for high DII + low TyG-BMI (HR: 1.91; 95% CI: 1.41-2.59; reference: low DII + middle TyG-BMI).ConclusionsDII and TyG-BMI are significant indices that may enhance personalized strategies for CVD prevention and management.

According to the 2023 Global Hypertension Report by the World Health Organization, the number of adults with hypertension increased from 650 million in 1990 to 1.3 billion in 2019. Elevated blood pressure (BP) contributes to more than 10 million deaths each year, primarily due to its contribution to cardiovascular disease (CVD) and related complications.1,2 Although genetic predisposition contributes to the development of hypertension, extensive evidence supports that individuals with unhealthy lifestyles, such as high-salt, low-fiber, and high-fat diets, have substantially higher incidence of hypertension compared to those with healthier habits.3 Despite therapeutic advances in hypertension management, a large proportion of hypertensive individuals remain at a high risk of cardiovascular events and mortality.

Dietary patterns are modifiable determinants of BP regulation and vascular health.4,5 Higher dietary fiber intake is associated with modest reductions in BP and improved vascular health. Dietary fiber also lowers plasma cholesterol levels, improves blood rheology, and reduces vascular resistance, thereby mitigating cardiovascular stress and enhancing hemodynamic stability.6, 7, 8

Proinflammatory diets, as reflected by higher DII (Dietary Inflammatory Index) scores, promote systemic inflammation, which contributes to endothelial dysfunction, increased arterial stiffness, and heightened sympathetic activity-hallmarks of hypertensive pathophysiology.9, 10, 11 For example, Vissers et al. found that proinflammatory diets can increase hypertension risk by 24%, with each SD increase in the DII corresponding to a 7% rise in hypertension risk.12 Furthermore, high DII scores are closely linked to an increased CVD risk via mechanisms involving oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance.13 Concurrently, metabolic disturbances, particularly insulin resistance, play a pivotal role in sodium retention, vascular remodeling, and impaired nitric oxide bioavailability, further exacerbating BP elevation. The TyG-BMI (triglyceride-glucose body mass index), which integrates markers of lipid and glucose metabolism, serves as a practical and reliable surrogate for insulin resistance in epidemiological studies.14,15 Elevated TyG-BMI values reflect lipid and glucose metabolism disturbances that collectively promote atherosclerosis and vascular injury.16, 17, 18 Impaired lipid metabolism and glucose dysregulation exacerbates oxidative stress and inflammation, accelerating cardiovascular damage.19 In a cohort of 46,652 French women, elevated DII scores were associated with increased hypertension risk, particularly among those with normal BMI.20

However, most investigations have examined DII or TyG-based metrics in isolation or focused on incident hypertension or intermediate outcomes. Evidence integrating inflammatory and metabolic indices to evaluate long-term cardiovascular and all-cause mortality among individuals with hypertension remains limited. Therefore, our study aimed to examine the independent and joint associations of DII and TyG-BMI with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in adults with hypertension, and to assess whether their combined evaluation may facilitate more precise risk stratification and targeted preventive strategies.

Material and methods

Study design and population

The NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) conducted by the NCHS (National Center for Health Statistics), was designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the noninstitutionalized U.S. population.21 Data from 9 continuous NHANES cycles (2001-2018) were included, with mortality follow-up through December 31, 2019.

Initially, participants completed structured in-home interviews, followed by assessments at mobile examination centers, which included additional standardized questionnaires, medical examinations, and biological sample collection. NHANES adheres to stringent protocols to minimize nonsampling and measurement errors throughout survey planning, data collection, and processing. Standardized questionnaires gathered information on demographics, previous diagnoses of hypertension and use of antihypertensive medications, prior diagnoses of high blood cholesterol and use of lipid-lowering drugs, and diabetes mellitus. A total of 91,351 participants were initially screened from 9 consecutive NHANES cycles spanning 2001 to 2018. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, missing data on fasting glucose, triglycerides (TGs), BMI, or mortality status. Blood pressure measurement

During the medical examination, BP was measured using a standardized protocol by trained personnel after the participant had rested for 5 minutes in a seated position. Per NHANES protocol, we used the average of all available valid readings (up to 4). If 2 readings were available, their mean was used; if only 1 reading was available, that value was used. Measurements were obtained in the mobile examination center from the right arm unless contraindicated.

Definition of hypertension and antihypertensive medications

Hypertension was defined based on BP measurements and/or self-reported use of antihypertensive medications.22 Participants with an average systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg and/or an average diastolic BP ≥90 mm Hg, or those who self-reported the current use of antihypertensive medications, were classified as hypertensive. We considered the primary agents listed by the established guidelines, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, and thiazide or thiazide-type diuretic agents, as well as secondary agents. To assess the association of DII and TyG-BMI for hypertension-related outcomes, we evaluated heterogeneity by clinic BP control using full-sample interaction models and additionally display stratum-specific estimates for well-controlled vs uncontrolled BP. Patients with systolic BP <140 mm Hg and diastolic BP <90 mm Hg were classified as “well controlled,” whereas those with systolic ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic ≥90 mm Hg were classified as “uncontrolled.”

Ascertainment of mortality outcomes

Participants' survival status was ascertained through linkage with the National Death Index, maintained by the NCHS. Participant mortality data and the corresponding causes of death are systematically updated, with the most recent update being recorded on December 31, 2019. Cause-specific mortality was classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision. CVD mortality included heart disease (ICD-10 I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51) and cerebrovascular disease (I60-I69). Malignant neoplasms were coded as C00-C97, diabetes mortality under codes E10-E14, and other causes categorized in alignment with the standard NCHS criteria.23

Assessment of covariates

Demographic and health-related data were collected through structured household interviews conducted as part of NHANES. Covariates selected for inclusion in our analyses were based on established associations with mortality risk among hypertensive populations reported in prior literature. Specifically, these covariates encompassed demographic factors including sex (male, female), age (continuous), and race/ethnicity (categorized as Mexican American, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or other); anthropometric data; socioeconomic status indicators such as education attainment (less than high school, high school or equivalent, or more than high school) and the poverty-income ratio; lifestyle variables comprising physical activity level and alcohol consumption (yes/no); as well as clinical histories including self-reported CVD (yes/no) and diabetes mellitus (yes/no).

Clinical indicators including fasting glucose, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), TGs, total cholesterol low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were measured in NHANES laboratories. Self-reported diseases included myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and cancer. Diabetes mellitus was defined according to the American Diabetes Association’s diagnostic criteria as follows: 1) either the fasting plasma glucose value ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or the 2-h plasma glucose (2-h PG) ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) during the Oral Glucose Tolerance Test, or A1C ≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or 2) a self-reported diagnosis, indicated by an affirmative response to the questions, "Has a doctor ever told you that you have diabetes?" or "Are you currently taking insulin?" Glycemic control, assessed using HbA1c, was categorized as well controlled (<7.0% [<53 mmol/mol]) and poorly controlled (≥7.0% [≥53 mmol/mol]). Physical activity was assessed based on the frequency of moderate-to-vigorous intensity activities (eg, walking, jogging, running, swimming, cycling, dancing, or yard work) reported weekly, with physical inactivity defined as never engaging in such activities.24 Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current smoker. Drinking status was categorized as heavy drinker, binge drinking, moderate drinker, mild drinker, nondrinker, or history of daily binge drinking. CVD was ascertained from prior diagnoses of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, or stroke.

Assessment of TyG indices

The TyG-BMI has been considered as a reliable alternative indicator for evaluating insulin resistance;25 however, its associations with CVD or all-cause mortality rates in hypertensive patients remain unclear. The TyG-BMI index was calculated as Ln [fasting TGs (mg/dL) × fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL)/2]. TGs and fasting plasma glucose levels were measured using enzymatic assays on Roche Modular P and Roche Cobas 6,000 chemistry analyzers, respectively. The TyG-BMI index was calculated as BMI multiplied by the TyG-BMI index, where BMI was defined as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (BMI = weight [kg]/height [m]2). Patients were classified into tertiles based on their TyG-BMI (T1, T2, and T3). TyG-waist circumference (WC) index was defined as Ln [fasting TGs (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2] × WC (cm). Analyses with TyG-WC were considered exploratory and reported in Supplement Tables 4-5. For all models using TyG-BMI or DII×TyG-BMI as exposures, BMI was not entered as a covariate to avoid overadjustment.

Dietary inflammatory index

As previously described in NHANES, dietary intake was collected via 24-hour recalls and used to calculate the DII.9,26 Nutrient intake was estimated by averaging 2 reliable 24-hour recalls or, when only 1 reliable recall was available, using that single recall. Supplements and medications were excluded. Briefly, the DII incorporates a total of 45 dietary parameters, each assigned a specific inflammatory score based on its effect on 6 major inflammatory biomarkers (interleukin [IL]-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-α, and C-reactive protein). Shivappa et al have demonstrated that fewer than 30 dietary parameters remain sufficient to reliably predict diet-related inflammatory potential.27 Our study used 27 dietary components to calculate the DII: carbohydrates, protein, total fat, saturated fat, monounsaturated fat, polyunsaturated fat, total n-3 fatty acids, total n-6 fatty acids, dietary fiber, cholesterol, alcohol, caffeine, vitamin A, β-carotene, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, folate, iron, magnesium, zinc, and selenium.

The overall DII score was calculated by summing the individual component scores, with positive values reflecting a proinflammatory diet, negative values indicating an anti-inflammatory diet, and a score of zero representing a neutral inflammatory potential. The theoretical range of DII scores extends from −8.87 (maximally anti-inflammatory) to +7.89 (maximally pro-inflammatory).28 Each participant's intake was compared to a global reference database, with Z-scores calculated for each food parameter by subtracting the global mean and dividing by the SD.29 These Z-scores were converted to percentiles, centered, and adjusted to minimize outliers, then multiplied by the inflammatory effect score of each food parameter and summed to obtain an overall DII score.30 The DII can still be calculated if fewer than 30 nutrients are available.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were performed in R (version 4.3.2, R Software). Two-sided α = 0.05. The false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Survey-design Cox models (svycoxph) accounted for combined fasting subsample weights, strata, and primary sampling units. When TyG-BMI was the exposure (alone or in DII × TyG-BMI joint categories), we excluded BMI, fasting plasma glucose, and TGs from adjustment to avoid overadjustment and multicollinearity. Continuous variables are presented as medians [IQR]; categorical variables as survey-weighted counts (weighted %). Linear regression analysis was used to assess differences in continuous variables, and the Wald chi-square test was used for categorical variables. We calculated Spearman correlation coefficients to evaluate the association between the DII and various cardiovascular risk factors.

Survey design and weighting

All analyses used combined fasting subsample weights appropriate for pooled 2001 to 2018 NHANES cycles (WTSAF2YR divided by 9), with design variables (SDMVSTRA, SDMVPSU) specified for variance estimation; design-based (svycoxph) Cox models were used, and hypertensive adults were handled.

Time-to-event and competing-risk analyses

For all-cause mortality, we estimated survey-weighted Kaplan-Meier curves and compared strata with log-rank tests. For cause-specific mortality (CVD, diabetes, and cancer), deaths from other causes were treated as competing events; we therefore fitted design-based cause-specific Cox models, censoring at competing deaths, and reported HRs with 95% CIs. Because Kaplan-Meier can overestimate cumulative incidence in the presence of competing risks, we did not present Kaplan-Meier curves for cause-specific outcomes. As a sensitivity analysis, Fine-Gray subdistribution models yielded qualitatively similar inferences (results available on request).

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

HRs and corresponding 95% CIs were computed using Cox proportional hazards regression to examine the associations of DII and TyG-BMI with mortality endpoints (all-cause, CVD, diabetes, and cancer mortality). Covariate adjustments were comprehensive, including demographic (sex, age, and race), socioeconomic (education and poverty-income ratio), lifestyle (physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption), and clinical (diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and CVD) variables, to mitigate potential confounding effects. Proportional hazards were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and log (-log) plots. The functional form was examined with restricted cubic splines and martingale residuals; where nonlinearity was not supported; linear terms were retained. Influence diagnostics (deviance residuals and difference in betas) were inspected; results were robust to these checks.

We constructed 3 sequential models for covariate adjustment: model 1 (age, sex, race/ethnicity); model 2 (model 1 + education, alcohol, smoking, diabetes medications, antihypertensive medications); and model 3 (model 2 + HbA1c, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol). When TyG-BMI (or TyG-WC, or DII × TyG-BMI/-WC joint categories) was the exposure, BMI, fasting plasma glucose, and TGs (and waist circumference for TyG-WC) were not included as covariates to avoid overadjustment.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis

We first modeled continuous DII and TyG-BMI within survey-weighted Cox models and evaluated functional form usingRCSs. Decisions on linear vs nonlinear specification considered statistical evidence (overall and nonlinearity tests) and model fit (AIC/BIC).

By contrast, the differences were even more pronounced when comparing TyG-BMI groups. The highest TyG-BMI group (>289) had significantly higher levels of glycated hemoglobin (5.90% vs 5.50%, P < 0.001) and fasting blood glucose (111 vs 99 mg/dL, P < 0.001). Diabetes prevalence in T3 TyG-BMI group reached 42.3% (vs 15.8%, P < 0.001), accompanied by an increased use of glucose-lowering and antihypertensive medications. These findings indicate that a higher TyG-BMI is not only linked to greater metabolic burden, but also more strongly associated with elevated cardiometabolic risk and the need for pharmacological intervention.

Sensitivity analyses

Excluding deaths within 2 years of follow-up and excluding participants with baseline CVD yielded qualitatively similar inferences (Supplement Tables 1 and 2). In fully adjusted models, high DII + high TyG-BMI remained associated with higher CVD mortality (HR: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.38-3.00 and HR: 2.22; 95% CI: 1.24-3.96, respectively) and with higher all-cause mortality (HR: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.07-1.75 and HR: 1.33; 95% CI: 0.98-1.81), with dose-response trends (P for trend ≤0.003).

For all-cause mortality, males with high DII and high TyG-BMI showed a significantly increased risk (HR: 1.86; 95% CI: 1.38-2.51). In addition, individuals with HbA1c levels <7.0 in the high DII and middle TyG-BMI group exhibited increased all-cause mortality risk (HR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.40-2.10), and those with pre-existing CVD in the high DII and middle TyG-BMI group had an elevated risk (HR: 2.10; 95% CI: 1.52-2.88).

For diabetes-related mortality, males with high DII and high TyG-BMI had a significantly higher risk (HR: 2.56; 95% CI: 1.07-6.11). In terms of cancer-related mortality, the highest risk was observed among males in the low DII and high TyG-BMI group (HR: 1.73; 95% CI: 1.02-2.94). Heavy drinkers with high DII and middle TyG-BMI also showed increased cancer-related mortality risk (HR: 2.39; 95% CI: 1.19-4.83). Among participants with HbA1c <7.0%, high DII + middle TyG-BMI was associated with higher cancer mortality (HR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.10-2.43).

In Supplement Table 3, we compared the prognostic impact of combined DII and TyG-BMI categories on mortality in well-controlled vs uncontrolled hypertensive adults (Model 3). In uncontrolled hypertensives, mortality rose stepwise across DII × TyG-BMI strata for both all-cause (high DII + high TyG-BMI: HR: 1.71 [95% CI: 1.26-2.32]; P < 0.001) and CVD mortality (HR: 2.41 [95% CI: 1.52-3.80]; P = 0.003). In well-controlled patients, these associations were attenuated and nonsignificant (all-cause P = 0.078; CVD P = 0.204).

Discussion

Although pharmacological treatment of established hypertension is well documented to reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, such interventions can be costly, require ongoing medical supervision, and may be associated with adverse effects. Notably, only 31.8% of individuals with hypertension in the United States had adequately controlled BP in 1999 to 2000, with the proportion rising to a peak of 53.8% by 2013 to 2014, before declining to 43.7% in 2017 to 2018, despite ongoing public health efforts.31, 32, 33 Our study revealed that both the DII and TyG-BMI are significantly associated with mortality risk. For DII tertiles, T3 vs T1 was associated with CVD (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.00-1.85) and all-cause (HR: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.08-1.54) mortality (Table 2). As shown in Table 3, for the combined exposure, high DII + high TyG-BMI vs low DII + middle TyG-BMI was associated with higher risks (CVD HR: 1.98; 95% CI: 1.33-2.94; all-cause HR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.14-1.80). Similarly, elevated TyG-BMI, an indicator of insulin resistance, was independently associated with higher cardiovascular mortality. For all-cause mortality, the association was modest and did not reach statistical significance after full adjustment, although spline analyses suggested a nonlinear increase at higher TyG-BMI levels. These findings indicate that proinflammatory dietary patterns and metabolic dysfunction are jointly associated with higher mortality risk in hypertensive adults, and support consideration of individualized dietary counseling to lower dietary inflammatory potential alongside optimization of metabolic health as part of risk management.

Liu et al reported a significant association between TyG and hypertension in individuals with normal BMI, but not in those with elevated BMI.34 Elevated TyG-BMI, indicative of insulin resistance and adiposity, may contribute to higher CVD mortality in hypertensive individuals. Managing metabolic health could be crucial in reducing mortality risk (Table 2) (CVD, HR: 1.98; 95% CI: 1.33-2.94, all-cause, HR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.14-1.80). The association remained significant after adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and lifestyle factors in Table 2 (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.00-1.85). Subgroup analysis reveals that males with high DII and high TyG-BMI had increased risks for cardiovascular mortality (HR: 2.15) and diabetes-related mortality (HR: 2.56), whereas older adults and specific racial/ethnic groups also showed elevated risks. In our analysis, the highest cancer mortality risk was indeed observed in participants with high DII but middle TyG-BMI rather than the high TyG-BMI group. One plausible explanation is that extreme metabolic dysfunction indicated by high TyG-BMI may primarily elevate cardiovascular rather than cancer-related mortality, whereas moderate metabolic dysfunction (middle TyG-BMI) combined with high dietary inflammatory potential (high DII) might increase chronic inflammation linked specifically to cancer pathogenesis. However, this intriguing observation warrants further investigation to clarify the underlying biological mechanisms.

Given that dietary modifications–such as adopting anti-inflammatory or Mediterranean-style diets–may beneficially impact both systemic inflammation and metabolic health, improving dietary quality holds dual potential to lower DII and mitigate TyG-BMI-related metabolic disturbances. Diet-induced inflammation significantly exacerbates systemic inflammatory responses, potentially increasing mortality risk or accelerating disease progression in patients with diabetes and acute coronary syndrome.35 Individuals with well-controlled HbA1c (<7.0%) in the low DII and high TyG-BMI group showed a higher risk of cancer mortality (HR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.10-2.43). It may be driven by insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, which are hallmarks of TyG-BMI and have been independently associated with cancer development and progression through mechanisms such as hyperinsulinemia, activation of the insulin-like growth factor 1 axis, and pro-proliferative signaling pathways.36,37

Similarly, those without CVD but with high DII and low TyG-BMI also had increased cancer mortality (HR: 1.85; 95% CI: 1.15-2.97) suggests that dietary inflammation alone—independent of marked metabolic dysfunction—may contribute to cancer risk through mechanisms such as enhanced oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and promotion of a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment.38,39 We also noticed that high levels of TyG-BMI were significantly associated with increased cardiovascular mortality. For all-cause mortality, the association was not statistically significant in fully adjusted models; however, risk was higher when high TyG-BMI combined with high DII, highlighting a potential joint effect. The association with diabetes-related mortality weakened after adjustment for confounders, whereas no significant predictive effect was observed for cancer-related mortality (Supplement Table 6).

These observations suggest that both inflammatory and metabolic pathways may influence cancer mortality by distinct yet overlapping mechanisms, and highlight the importance of evaluating them jointly in risk assessment. Our study expands on this by demonstrating the substantial impact of high DII scores in hypertensive populations (Central Illustration). To our knowledge, evidence on the association between elevated TyG-BMI and mortality specifically among patients with hypertension is limited. To translate these findings into practice, clinicians should promote dietary interventions aimed at reducing dietary inflammatory potential. Anti-inflammatory diets rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, healthy fats (eg, omega-3 fatty acids), and dietary fiber should be encouraged, while reducing the intake of pro-inflammatory foods such as saturated fats, trans fats, and added sugars. This approach not only reduces inflammation but also enhances metabolic health, both of which are essential for effective hypertension management.

Study limitations

First, although the NHANES study provides a large sample size, potential selection bias, and limitations in sample distribution and sampling methods may affect the validity of our results. Second, future studies should explore these associations across populations with diverse genetic backgrounds to further address potential selection bias and improve external validity. Third, our analyses relied on single baseline measurements of DII and TyG-BMI, which may not capture intraindividual changes over time, as glucose, lipid profiles, and BMI are subject to age-related variations and treatment effects. Both indices are widely used in clinical and epidemiological studies and exhibit relatively low short-term biological variability under stable conditions. Furthermore, considering our substantial sample size and prolonged follow-up period, random measurement variability would more likely attenuate. Fourth, the absence of detailed dietary supplementation data (such as dietary salt, sugars, or all kinds of saturated fats), specific medication adherence records, and genetic predisposition information restricts a comprehensive understanding of metabolism-related mechanisms. Lastly, given the observational design of this study, the reported association between TyG-BMI and mortality risk must not be interpreted as causal. Despite these limitations, our study contributes to the understanding of mortality associations by DII and TyG-BMI, which may complement traditional BP risk factors. Further research is warranted to comprehensively elucidate their clinical value.

Conclusions

Elevated dietary inflammatory potential and metabolic dysfunction, as measured by DII and TyG-BMI, were jointly associated with increased risks of CVD and all-cause mortality among hypertensive adults, highlighting the potential utility of integrating dietary and metabolic interventions to improve patient outcomes.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE AND PROCEDURAL SKILLS: The combined presence of elevated dietary inflammatory potential and metabolic dysfunction, as reflected by high DII and high TyG-BMI, was associated with significantly increased risks of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in adults with hypertension. Incorporating DII and TyG-BMI into electronic health records and population health strategies may enhance personalized preventive care and optimize long-term cardiovascular risk reduction.

COMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE AND PROCEDURAL SKILLS: The combined presence of elevated dietary inflammatory potential and metabolic dysfunction, as reflected by high DII and high TyG-BMI, was associated with significantly increased risks of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in adults with hypertension. Joint assessment of dietary inflammation and metabolic dysfunction may improve mortality risk stratification in hypertension and support integrated dietary and metabolic interventions. Patients with poor dietary inflammatory profiles and insulin resistance–related phenotypes may benefit from integrated dietary and metabolic interventions beyond standard antihypertensive therapies.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: DII and TyG-BMI are easily obtainable, noninvasive, and scalable markers that may complement traditional clinical tools to identify high-risk hypertensive individuals. Future research should explore whether targeted dietary modification (eg, Mediterranean or anti-inflammatory diets) and insulin resistance management can improve outcomes among hypertensive populations with high DII and TyG-BMI profiles. Incorporating DII and TyG-BMI into electronic health records and population health strategies may enhance personalized preventive care and optimize long-term cardiovascular risk reduction.
