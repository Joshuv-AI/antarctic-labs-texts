The extent of lymph node dissection in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer: current controversies, evidence gaps, and future directions in the immunotherapy era—a narrative review

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases, and surgical resection continues to be the cornerstone of curative treatment for patients with early-stage disease (stage I–II) (2-4). Central to the oncologic quality of lung cancer surgery is the adequacy of lymph node assessment, as nodal status represents one of the most powerful prognostic factors and directly guides adjuvant therapy decisions (5,6).

The evolution of lymph node dissection (LND) in NSCLC has been shaped by two fundamental objectives: accurate pathologic staging and potential therapeutic benefit through the removal of occult metastatic deposits (7,8). Systematic lymph node dissection (SLND)—the complete removal of all ipsilateral hilar and mediastinal lymph node stations—has been widely endorsed as the standard of care (9,10). However, this approach has never been unequivocally validated by high-level evidence. Five randomized prospective comparative studies examining SLND versus lymph node sampling (LNS) have yielded conflicting results: two reported improved overall survival (OS) with SLND, whereas three found no significant survival difference (11). Moreover, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reached divergent conclusions, with some demonstrating survival benefit in cohort studies but not in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (11-14).

The clinical landscape has shifted dramatically over the past decade. Three landmark RCTs—JCOG0802, CALGB 140503, and DRKS00004897—have established sublobar resection as a non-inferior oncologic alternative to lobectomy for carefully selected patients with peripheral stage IA NSCLC, challenging the long-held paradigm that lobectomy is the only standard (15-17). This paradigm shift compels a re-evaluation of the requisite scope of LND, particularly because the nodal staging requirements in these trials were protocol-mandated and may not reflect real-world practice variability.

Furthermore, the success of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in both adjuvant and neoadjuvant settings has introduced an entirely new consideration. Tumor-draining lymph nodes (TdLNs) harbor tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes and serve as critical reservoirs for anti-tumor immunity. Preliminary evidence suggests that extensive removal of non-metastatic lymph nodes may disrupt these immune reservoirs and potentially impair the efficacy of subsequent immunotherapy (18). This emerging immunologic perspective compels a fundamental rethinking of what constitutes “optimal” lymphadenectomy.

This review aims to critically synthesize the current evidence on LND extent in early-stage NSCLC, delineate persistent controversies and evidence gaps, and chart future directions toward personalized, biologically informed nodal management strategies. Therefore, in this review, we focus primarily on the extent of mediastinal LND—systematic, lobe-specific, and selective—in patients with stage I–II NSCLC. In addition, immunotherapy is also discussed as an emerging biological factor that may affect the risk-benefit balance of nodal clearance and as a future direction that warrants further investigation. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1275/rc).

Methods

We conducted a narrative review following the standard methodology for review articles. A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from January 2000 to December 2025. The search strategy combined terms for non-small cell lung cancer (e.g., “NSCLC”, “lung neoplasm”), lymph node dissection (e.g., “lymphadenectomy”, “systematic lymph node dissection”, “lobe-specific lymph node dissection”, “lymph node sampling”, “sentinel lymph node”), early-stage disease (e.g., “stage I”, “stage II”, “sublobar resection”, “segmentectomy”), and immunotherapy-related terms (e.g., “immune checkpoint inhibitor”, “neoadjuvant”, “adjuvant”). Only English-language publications were considered. We included RCTs, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, prospective cohort studies, and retrospective studies with propensity score matching. Case reports, case series with fewer than 20 patients, editorials, and conference abstracts without full text were excluded. Two independent reviewers (L.L. and Y.Z.) screened titles and abstracts; full texts of potentially eligible articles were obtained and assessed. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (Y.W.). In addition, reference lists of retrieved articles and relevant reviews were hand-searched for additional studies. Given the narrative nature of this review, we prioritized high-level evidence but also incorporated key observational data where randomized evidence was lacking, with explicit acknowledgment of methodological heterogeneity. The spectrum of LND strategies

SLND

SLND mandates the complete removal of all ipsilateral hilar and mediastinal lymph node stations (19). The rationale is twofold: maximal detection of occult nodal metastases (thereby improving staging accuracy) and potential survival benefit through the removal of microscopic residual disease (20,21). Proponents argue that SLND enables reliable identification of patients requiring adjuvant therapy and reduces the risk of locoregional recurrence (22,23).

However, the survival benefit of SLND has been questioned. In a comprehensive narrative review of five RCTs, two demonstrated OS improvement with SLND, while three showed no significant difference (11). Specifically, the ACOSOG Z0030 trial, one of the largest randomized studies comparing SLND versus LNS in patients with N0 or N1 NSCLC, found no survival difference between the two approaches, although more extensive dissection was associated with improved nodal upstaging in certain subgroups (12). Other RCTs have similarly failed to demonstrate a consistent OS advantage for SLND over less extensive nodal assessment (13,14). A best evidence topic analysis examining 211 papers concluded that while retrospective cohort studies implied survival benefit with SLND, this was not substantiated by RCTs or meta-analyses (13). Furthermore, SLND is associated with increased operative time and complication rates, including chylothorax and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, without demonstrating consistent oncologic superiority.

LNS

LNS involves the selective removal of representative lymph nodes from hilar and mediastinal stations rather than complete station clearance. The potential advantages include reduced operative time and morbidity, while the disadvantages include the risk of understaging and missing isolated metastases.

The available evidence suggests no significant difference in recurrence rates between SLND and LNS in stage I NSCLC patients. The ACOSOG Z0030 trial, which randomized 1,023 patients with N0 or N1 NSCLC to either SLND or LNS, found no difference in OS at 5 years (primary endpoint), but did identify improved nodal upstaging—from 5.1% to 8.3%—with more extensive dissection in certain subgroups (12). A meta-analysis by Li et al., including both cohort studies and RCTs, demonstrated that LNS was associated with inferior survival compared to SLND in early-stage patients, although significant heterogeneity across included studies (I2=68%) limits interpretation of this finding (24). The conflicting results between the ACOSOG Z0030 trial and subsequent meta-analyses may reflect differences in patient selection, definition of LNS, and pathological processing protocols.

Lobe-specific LND (L-SLND)

L-SLND represents a middle ground between radical dissection and minimal sampling. This approach is based on observations that lymphatic drainage from each pulmonary lobe follows predictable patterns, and the risk of metastases to certain mediastinal stations is negligible for specific tumor locations. For example, tumors in the right upper lobe rarely metastasize to the subcarinal station, whereas lower lobe tumors have a higher propensity for subcarinal involvement.

A meta-analysis by Wang et al., including one RCT and eight retrospective cohort studies with 8,499 patients, found that L-SLND was associated with a lower postoperative complication rate [risk ratio (RR) 0.83, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.72–0.95, P=0.006] compared to SLND, with no significant difference in OS [hazard ratio (HR) 1.12, 95% CI: 0.81–1.54, P=0.501] (25). However, the analysis was characterized by high heterogeneity (I2=71.9%), underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and rigorous patient selection. A more recent retrospective study comparing L-SLND and SLND in 107 stage I NSCLC patients found comparable 5-year OS (82% vs. 84%, P>0.05) and disease-free survival (DFS) (70% vs. 65%, P>0.05), suggesting L-SLND could be a viable treatment choice (26).

Ongoing prospective trials, including the LESSON trial (ChiCTR2100048415), a single-institution RCT investigating L-SLND in stage IA1–2 NSCLC with ≥50% ground-glass opacity components, are expected to provide higher-quality evidence (27).

Selective/individualized LND

The ultimate evolution of LND lies in abandoning a “one-size-fits-all” approach in favor of truly individualized strategies that integrate preoperative imaging characteristics, intraoperative frozen section analysis, and emerging molecular biomarkers. The rationale for such personalization stems from increasing awareness that the oncologic benefit of removing a given nodal station is not uniform across all patients, whereas the associated morbidity—including chylothorax, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, and prolonged air leak—varies with the extent and location of dissection. Consequently, selective LND aims to dissect only those nodal regions with a meaningful probability of harboring metastases, while preserving stations with negligible metastatic risk, thereby optimizing the therapeutic ratio of the procedure.

A comprehensive review by Jiang et al. systematically articulated that excessive dissection of non-metastatic lymph nodes offers uncertain clinical benefit while simultaneously increasing complication risks and potentially disturbing systemic immune homeostasis, a consideration particularly relevant in the era of perioperative immunotherapy (28). Preoperative predictors of nodal involvement, such as consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) on computed tomography (CT) and maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) on positron emission tomography (PET), have demonstrated utility in identifying patients with extremely low risk of occult mediastinal disease. For instance, in patients undergoing segmentectomy for peripheral clinical stage IA NSCLC with CTR <1.0 and SUVmax <2.0, the incidence of occult N2 metastasis has been reported as low as 0.9%, raising the possibility of safely omitting mediastinal dissection altogether in such highly selected individuals (29). Beyond conventional imaging, radiomics and machine learning algorithms applied to preoperative CT or PET/CT scans are being explored to generate individualized risk scores for nodal metastasis, potentially guiding dissection decisions with greater accuracy than clinical judgment alone.

Intraoperatively, frozen section analysis of sampled hilar or mediastinal nodes can further refine the extent of dissection. If multiple sampled N1 stations prove negative on frozen section, and the primary tumor exhibits favorable radiographic features, the surgeon may reasonably limit mediastinal LND to only those stations with established drainage patterns for the affected lobe—thereby transitioning from systematic to selective dissection in real time. Furthermore, the emerging technique of sentinel lymph node (SLN) mapping using near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence indocyanine green (ICG) or radioisotope tracers holds promise for identifying the first draining node, which, if negative on intraoperative molecular analysis [e.g., one-step nucleic acid amplification for cytokeratin 19 messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA)], may obviate the need for complete station clearance. Although not yet ready for routine adoption, SLN-guided selective dissection represents a conceptually attractive paradigm that could significantly reduce surgical overtreatment.

Nevertheless, the transition to selective or individualized LND is not without challenges. The lack of validated, prospective risk stratification tools, the absence of randomized trials comparing selective versus systematic LND in prespecified risk subgroups, and concerns about missing skip metastases (N2 without N1 involvement) remain substantial barriers. Moreover, the optimal trade-off between reducing morbidity and preserving adequate nodal staging for accurate prognostic classification and adjuvant therapy decisions has not been quantitatively defined. Current evidence thus supports selective LND only in narrowly defined, low-risk populations—typically those with ground-glass-predominant peripheral stage IA tumors—and only within the framework of clinical protocols or experienced high-volume centers. Future research must focus on developing and prospectively validating robust predictive models that incorporate clinicoradiologic, molecular, and intraoperative pathological data, enabling surgeons to tailor the extent of LND to the individual patient’s tumor biology, lymphatic drainage pattern, and immune status. Only through such an evidence-based, individualized strategy can the field reconcile the competing goals of oncologic radicality, patient safety, and immune preservation (Figure 1, Table 2).

Current controversies and conflicting evidence

The 4L lymph node debate

Perhaps no single nodal station has generated more controversy than the left lower paratracheal (station 4L) lymph nodes in left-sided NSCLC. Current guidelines provide no clear consensus on whether 4L LND should be performed routinely. Two recent meta-analyses have arrived at divergent conclusions.

Deng et al., in a meta-analysis of six studies encompassing 4,253 patients, reported that 4L LND was associated with improved OS (HR 0.65, 95% CI: 0.52–0.81, P<0.0001) and DFS (HR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.71–0.95, P=0.008), with similar postoperative complication rates between groups (30). Conversely, Gryszko et al., in an updated meta-analysis, found improved OS in the 4LND+ group (HR 0.77, 95% CI: 0.62–0.96, P=0.02) but with significantly higher total complication rates (RR 1.45, 95% CI: 1.20–1.74, P<0.001) and vocal cord palsy incidence (RR 3.64, 95% CI: 1.96–6.78, P<0.001) (31). Critically, when only propensity score-matched studies were analyzed, the OS benefit lost statistical significance (HR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.66–1.02, P=0.07). Furthermore, no significant OS difference was observed in stage I NSCLC patients (HR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.79–1.26, P=0.96). When interpreting these findings, the Will Rogers phenomenon—whereby more extensive nodal dissection leads to upstaging of patients who would otherwise be classified as lower stage, thereby improving survival outcomes within each stage without a true biological benefit—should be considered. The observation that the OS benefit of 4L dissection lost statistical significance in propensity score-matched analyses (HR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.66–1.02, P=0.07) supports this interpretation (31). Furthermore, the absence of an OS benefit specifically in stage I patients (HR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.79–1.26, P=0.96) suggests that any survival advantage attributed to 4L dissection may be partly driven by stage migration rather than a genuine therapeutic effect. This underscores the need for cautious interpretation of the existing evidence and highlights the importance of high-quality prospective data. Sublobar resection and the adequacy of nodal assessment

The shift toward sublobar resection for early-stage NSCLC has reignited debate regarding the minimum acceptable extent of LND. While both JCOG0802 and CALGB 140503 mandated protocol-specified nodal staging (systematic sampling or dissection) (15,16), whether these results are generalizable to real-world practice where nodal assessment may be less rigorous remains unclear. Several studies have examined the prognostic role of LND quantity in sublobar resection, but findings have been inconsistent, suggesting that node count alone may be an inadequate quality metric and that selection of appropriate nodal stations may be more critical than sheer quantity (32,33). For segmentectomy in highly selected patients—those with tumors showing consolidation-to-tumor ratio <1.0 and SUVmax <2.0—mediastinal LND may be unnecessary, as the risk of cN0-pN2 upstaging is extremely low (0.9%) (29). This finding supports the feasibility of selective nodal dissection in carefully stratified populations (Table 2).

Minimally invasive surgery: does approach affect nodal clearance?

Concerns have been raised that video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) may yield inferior lymphadenectomy quality compared to open thoracotomy. A large retrospective multicenter study of 6,265 stage I lung cancer patients found that the average number of lymph nodes removed was significantly lower in VATS lobectomy (10.9) than in open thoracotomy (12.9, P<0.001) (34). Counterintuitively, however, 5-year survival was better in the VATS group (78.6% vs. 73.8%, P=0.002), suggesting that lymph node quantity may not be a reliable surrogate for oncologic quality in the minimally invasive setting (34).

Data from the phase 3 JCOG 1413 trial found no significant differences in N2 upstaging proportions between VATS and open thoracotomy groups for clinical stage I–II NSCLC (35). Meta-analyses have generally concluded that VATS is a highly efficient alternative to thoracotomy without compromising oncologic outcomes, although open thoracotomy may achieve slightly better N2 nodal assessment (36,37). Robotic-assisted thoracic surgery appears to offer advantages over VATS, with studies demonstrating a higher number of lymph nodes dissected and more thorough nodal clearance (38,39) (Table 2).

Emerging concepts and future directions

SLN mapping

SLN biopsy revolutionized the surgical management of melanoma and breast cancer by enabling targeted nodal evaluation with reduced morbidity. For over two decades, thoracic surgeons have attempted to apply this technique to lung cancer, but success has remained elusive due to the complex lymphatic drainage of the lung and technical challenges.

Recent advances have renewed optimism. NIR fluorescence imaging using ICG has emerged as a promising technique for intraoperative SLN identification. A phase 1 dose-escalation trial demonstrated that ICG doses greater than 1,000 μg yielded nearly 90% intrathoracic SLN visualization, with SLN status directly correlating with final nodal status of the lymphadenectomy specimen (40). Preliminary long-term outcomes suggest that N0 status established via NIR-guided SLN mapping may be associated with decreased recurrence and improved survival (41).

More recently, a pilot study combining robotic lung resection with intraoperative SLN identification using radioisotope labeling and one-step nucleic acid amplification for CK19 mRNA detection demonstrated nodal upstaging in 3 of 8 patients (37.5%), including one case where only the SLN was positive (42). However, a feasibility study using navigation bronchoscopy-guided injection of 99mTc-nanocolloid identified an SLN in only 10 of 31 cases (32.3%), highlighting the need for further technical refinement (43).

Key challenges remain: standardization of tracer injection techniques, optimization of detection sensitivity, and validation against systematic lymphadenectomy as the reference standard. The sentinel node approach holds particular promise for sublobar resections and for patients at high risk for complications from extensive LND (Table 3).

Preoperative prediction and risk stratification

The ability to accurately predict pathologic nodal status prior to surgical resection represents a cornerstone of individualized LND strategies, as it would enable surgeons to selectively limit or extend dissection based on an individual patient’s estimated risk of occult nodal metastases. Currently, the most widely available and evidence-supported preoperative predictors are derived from conventional CT and PET imaging. Among CT-derived parameters, the CTR has emerged as a robust surrogate for tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential, particularly in small peripheral nodules. The SUVmax on 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose PET similarly reflects tumor metabolic activity and has been consistently associated with lymph node involvement. Adachi and colleagues demonstrated that among patients undergoing segmentectomy for clinical stage IA NSCLC, those with both CTR <1.0 and SUVmax <2.0 exhibited an extremely low 5-year cumulative recurrence incidence of only 2.0%, compared to 15.9% in those with CTR =1.0 and SUVmax ≥2.0 (29). Moreover, in this ultra-low-risk subgroup, the incidence of occult N2 upstaging was reported as 0.9%, suggesting that systematic mediastinal LND might be safely omitted without compromising oncologic outcomes. These observations have provided the rationale for ongoing prospective trials that stratify LND extent based on preoperative imaging profiles.

Despite their utility, conventional imaging metrics suffer from inherent limitations, including inter-observer variability, suboptimal sensitivity for micrometastases, and an inability to reliably differentiate inflammatory from malignant nodal enlargement. To overcome these constraints, radiomics—the high-throughput extraction of quantitative imaging features that are imperceptible to the human eye—has gained increasing attention. By analyzing texture, shape, intensity, and wavelet-transformed features from CT or PET/CT images, radiomic models can capture tumor heterogeneity and peritumoral characteristics that correlate with nodal metastatic risk. Preliminary studies have demonstrated that radiomic signatures outperform conventional imaging parameters in predicting occult lymph node involvement, with some models achieving area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.85 in validation cohorts. Furthermore, deep learning approaches, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been applied to preoperative CT images and have shown superior performance compared to radiologists in detecting lymph node metastasis, albeit with the caveat that most models have been developed in single-institution cohorts and lack prospective external validation.

In addition to imaging-based prediction, circulating biomarkers and molecular profiling of preoperative biopsy specimens offer complementary avenues for risk stratification. For instance, the detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) or the expression profiles of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related genes in endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) samples may provide biologic information that refines nodal risk assessment beyond imaging alone. However, these approaches are not yet ready for routine clinical implementation due to issues of assay sensitivity, standardization, and cost-effectiveness.

Crucially, the clinical utility of any preoperative prediction model depends on its ability to reliably identify a subgroup of patients with such a low probability of nodal metastasis that the harms of systematic LND—including operative time, morbidity, and potential immune disruption—outweigh the benefits of removing a currently undetectable focus of disease. To achieve this, future research must prioritize the development of rigorously validated, multi-institutional predictive models that integrate clinical, radiomic, and molecular features, and that are prospectively tested in decision-impact studies. Ideally, such models would provide individualized risk estimates that can be updated intraoperatively with frozen section findings, enabling a dynamic, risk-adaptive approach to LND. Until such tools are available, preoperative risk stratification should be applied cautiously, primarily within clinical protocols or in the context of shared decision-making where patients are fully informed of the residual uncertainty regarding occult nodal disease (Table 3).

Immunotherapy considerations: lymph node preservation in the immunotherapy era

TdLNs are active immune organs where tumor-specific T cells are primed and maintained. Preclinical and emerging clinical data suggest that extensive removal of non-metastatic lymph nodes may disrupt these immune reservoirs (18). A retrospective study by Zhou et al. of 131 cN0/N1 NSCLC patients who received neoadjuvant immunotherapy followed by surgery found that systemic mediastinal LND did not confer survival benefit compared to selective LND and was associated with a slightly higher postoperative recurrence risk (18). Single-cell RNA/T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing revealed that extensive nodal dissection may reduce the repertoire of stem-like exhausted CD8+ memory T cells, which are considered important for sustained anti-tumor immune responses.

These observations raise the question of whether extensive LND might affect the efficacy of subsequent immunotherapy, although this remains to be confirmed in prospective studies. Evidence gaps and research priorities

Despite decades of investigation into the optimal extent of LND for early-stage NSCLC, several fundamental questions remain unanswered. The following subsections delineate the most pressing evidence gaps and corresponding research priorities, which collectively define the agenda for future clinical and translational studies in this field.

Defining the minimal acceptable LND for sublobar resection

The paradigm shift toward sublobar resection—segmentectomy or wide wedge resection—for selected peripheral stage IA NSCLC, validated by the JCOG0802 and CALGB 140503 trials (15,16), has not been accompanied by parallel refinements in nodal staging requirements. Current guidelines lack specific recommendations regarding the minimum extent or quality of LND that should accompany sublobar resection. The protocol-mandated nodal assessment in these landmark trials (systematic sampling or complete dissection) may not reflect real-world variability, raising concerns about the generalizability of non-inferiority outcomes to practices with less rigorous nodal evaluation. Moreover, the optimal number of nodal stations to remove during segmentectomy, and whether mediastinal dissection can be safely omitted in ultra-low-risk subgroups (e.g., pure ground-glass nodules or those with CTR <0.5), remain undefined. Prospective, randomized trials comparing systematic LND versus L-SLND or selective LND specifically in patients undergoing sublobar resection are urgently needed. In particular, studies should stratify patients by preoperative imaging features (e.g., CTR, SUVmax) and incorporate intraoperative frozen section analysis to identify subgroups in whom mediastinal dissection offers negligible staging or therapeutic benefit. The ongoing MELDSIG trial in China, evaluating spared mediastinal LND versus full LND for stage IA NSCLC presenting as ground-glass nodules, represents a step in this direction, but broader international efforts are required to establish evidence-based, procedure-specific nodal staging standards (Table 4).

Re-evaluating quantitative thresholds as quality metrics

The use of absolute lymph node counts (e.g., ≥10 or ≥16 nodes) as a surrogate for adequate LND quality has been widely adopted in quality improvement initiatives and retrospective research. However, accumulating evidence challenges the validity of these thresholds in the modern era. Several studies have failed to demonstrate a consistent prognostic association between node count and survival in early-stage NSCLC, particularly following sublobar resection or minimally invasive approaches (33,34). The conflicting findings likely stem from the biological heterogeneity of node counts—a high number may simply reflect fragmentation of a single station rather than true multi-station clearance—and from the fact that node count is heavily influenced by surgical technique, pathological processing, and patient anatomy rather than oncologic thoroughness. The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer has recommended that quality assessment should emphasize the examination of specific nodal stations (e.g., stations 2R, 4R, 7, 10R for right-sided tumors) rather than absolute node numbers (45). Nevertheless, this recommendation has not been universally adopted, and the optimal set of stations per tumor location remains debated. Future research should prioritize the development and validation of station-based quality metrics that are reproducible, clinically meaningful, and applicable across surgical approaches. Additionally, prospective studies are needed to determine whether routine pathological assessment of node counts can be replaced or supplemented by more rigorous measures, such as the number of distinct nodal stations examined or the use of molecular techniques (e.g., one-step nucleic acid amplification) to detect micrometastases in sampled nodes (Table 4).

Validating SLN mapping as a replacement for systematic LND

SLN mapping, which has revolutionized nodal staging in melanoma and breast cancer, remains an unfulfilled promise in lung cancer. Despite over two decades of research, technical and logistical barriers have prevented its routine clinical adoption. The complex and variable lymphatic drainage patterns of the lung, the difficulty of injecting tracers without contaminating the surgical field, and the lack of a highly sensitive intraoperative assay for micrometastases have all contributed to slow progress. Recent advances in NIR fluorescence imaging with ICG and in molecular detection techniques (e.g., one-step nucleic acid amplification for cytokeratin 19 mRNA) have rekindled interest (40,42). However, a large, multicenter, prospective trial that directly compares SLN-guided selective dissection against systematic LND as the reference standard has never been conducted. The primary endpoint of such a trial should be the false-negative rate of SLN biopsy—that is, the proportion of patients with a negative SLN but positive non-SLN nodes. An acceptable false-negative rate (e.g., <5%) would justify abandoning complete station clearance. Secondary endpoints should include operative time, complication rates, upstaging frequency, and long-term recurrence-free survival. Until such validation is achieved, SLN mapping should remain a research tool. The research priority is therefore to design and execute a phase III non-inferiority trial that enrolls patients with clinical stage I NSCLC and systematically compares SLN mapping with thorough intraoperative pathological assessment against full LND, ideally employing standardized tracer injection protocols and centralized pathology review (Table 4).

Determining the optimal LND extent in the era of perioperative immunotherapy

The expanding use of ICIs in both neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings has raised questions about the relationship between LND extent and immunotherapy efficacy. Retrospective data suggest that extensive removal of non-metastatic mediastinal nodes may correlate with higher recurrence risk in patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy (18), though these findings require prospective confirmation, Key evidence gaps include: (I) whether the number or location of preserved lymph nodes correlates with circulating tumor-specific T cell clonal diversity; (II) whether patients receiving neoadjuvant ICIs followed by surgery have different patterns of nodal metastasis (e.g., higher rates of pathological complete response within nodes) that might permit less extensive LND; and (III) whether adjuvant ICI efficacy is dose-dependently reduced by the extent of prior LND. Prospective trials with correlative immune monitoring (e.g., TCR sequencing, single-cell analysis of resected nodes) are needed to address these questions (Table 4).

Clarifying the incidence and clinical significance of skip metastases

Skip N2 metastases—defined as mediastinal lymph node involvement without concomitant ipsilateral hilar (N1) nodal disease—represent an important clinical scenario because they challenge the logic of L-SLND or selective LND that relies on stepwise progression of tumor spread. The reported incidence of skip metastases among patients with proven N2 disease varies widely across studies, ranging from approximately 20% to 30% (46). This wide range reflects differences in pathological examination thoroughness, patient selection, and staging protocols. The clinical significance of skip metastases also remains debated. Some studies suggest that isolated N2 involvement without N1 disease carries a more favorable prognosis than continuous N1–2 involvement, whereas others report equivalent or worse outcomes. The biologic basis for skip metastases—whether they arise from alternative lymphatic drainage pathways, direct embolization, or different tumor biology (e.g., increased lymphatic invasion capacity)—is incompletely understood. The primary evidence gap is the lack of a modern, prospective, multi-institutional study that systematically examines skip metastasis incidence in early-stage NSCLC using uniform pathological protocols (e.g., serial sectioning and immunohistochemistry for cytokeratin). Furthermore, it is unknown whether patients with clinically suspected (by PET/CT) but pathologically unconfirmed skip metastases derive differential benefit from complete mediastinal dissection versus lobe-specific dissection. Research priorities include: (I) a prospective cohort study to accurately establish the incidence and risk factors for skip metastases in clinical stage I NSCLC; (II) molecular profiling of primary tumors to identify genomic signatures associated with the skip metastasis phenomenon; and (III) comparative effectiveness analyses (ideally from randomized data) of different LND strategies in the subset of patients found intraoperatively to have N0 hilar nodes but suspicious mediastinal nodes. Until these gaps are addressed, surgeons should continue to perform at least sampling of mediastinal stations (including subcarinal and lower paratracheal nodes) even when hilar nodes appear negative on frozen section, particularly for patients with higher-risk features such as larger tumor size, elevated SUVmax, or central location (Table 4).

Methodological limitations of the existing evidence

Several methodological limitations in the existing literature should be acknowledged when interpreting the evidence presented in this review. First, the definition of systematic LND varies substantially across studies, with some requiring removal of all mediastinal fat pads and others targeting only a predefined set of stations. Second, the distinction between intentional LNS and incomplete LND is often blurred in retrospective studies, introducing potential misclassification bias. Third, most studies lack central pathology review and standardized node-processing protocols, resulting in significant variability in node count and upstaging rates across institutions. Fourth, the majority of available evidence is retrospective or derived from subgroup analyses of trials not designed to compare LND strategies. These limitations highlight the need for prospective, standardized, and adequately powered trials to inform clinical practice (Table 4).

Discussion

The optimal extent of LND in early-stage NSCLC has remained a subject of enduring debate for nearly three decades. As the present review has illustrated, the available evidence—derived from RCTs, meta-analyses, retrospective cohort studies, and emerging translational investigations—does not support a uniform recommendation for all patients. Instead, the accumulated data increasingly favor a nuanced, risk-adapted approach that weighs the competing priorities of accurate pathologic staging, potential therapeutic benefit, perioperative morbidity, and preservation of anti-tumor immune architecture. The persistent failure of systematic LND to demonstrate a consistent survival advantage over less extensive strategies in level I evidence, coupled with its well-documented association with increased complications such as chylothorax and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, challenges the long-held dogma that “more is always better” in nodal surgery. At the same time, the non-negligible rate of occult N2 metastases in certain subgroups (e.g., solid-dominant tumors or those with elevated PET avidity) precludes a wholesale abandonment of mediastinal evaluation. Thus, the central clinical dilemma is not whether to perform LND, but rather how to individualize its extent with sufficient precision to maximize oncologic benefit while minimizing harm.

A critical observation from the literature is that the therapeutic value of LND appears to be highly context-dependent. In patients with low-risk features—such as peripheral ground-glass-predominant stage IA tumors with CTR <0.5 and normal or low SUVmax—the incidence of occult mediastinal nodal disease approaches zero, and the removal of non-involved lymph nodes is unlikely to confer any survival benefit (29). In this population, systematic mediastinal dissection may represent overtreatment, exposing patients to unnecessary risk without compensating oncologic gain. Conversely, in patients with high-risk features (e.g., larger tumor size, central location, solid radiologic appearance, or high PET avidity), the probability of nodal involvement is substantially higher, and thorough LND may provide both staging and therapeutic advantages. Importantly, even in high-risk patients, the incremental benefit of dissecting every mediastinal station (e.g., the controversial 4L station in left-sided tumors) versus a lobe-specific approach remains uncertain. The meta-analyses by Deng et al. and Gryszko et al. on the 4L node exemplify this uncertainty: while some data suggest survival benefit, others indicate increased morbidity without clear oncologic advantage, particularly when propensity score-matched analyses are considered (30,31). These conflicting findings underscore the limitations of aggregate-level evidence and the need for better risk stratification tools.

The emergence of sublobar resection as a standard of care for selected patients has added further complexity. In the landmark JCOG0802 and CALGB 140503 trials, protocol-mandated nodal staging included systematic sampling or complete dissection (15,16). Whether the non-inferiority outcomes of sublobar resection would hold if less rigorous nodal assessment were performed remains unknown. This question is particularly pressing because real-world practice patterns show considerable variability in the extent of LND during segmentectomy. Preliminary data from Adachi and colleagues suggest that in ultra-low-risk patients undergoing segmentectomy, the absence of mediastinal LND is associated with a 5-year recurrence incidence of only 2.0%, raising the possibility that formal mediastinal dissection might be safely omitted in this narrowly defined subgroup (29). Future guidelines will need to incorporate such risk stratification, moving away from dichotomous recommendations (“dissect vs. sample”) toward a graded approach based on preoperative and intraoperative predictors.

The expanding role of immunotherapy adds another layer of complexity to this risk-benefit calculus. TdLNs serve as reservoirs for stem-like exhausted CD8+ memory T cells, and retrospective data suggest that extensive removal of non-metastatic mediastinal nodes may correlate with higher recurrence risk in patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy (18). These observations raise the question of whether extensive LND could affect the efficacy of subsequent immunotherapy, although this remains preliminary and requires prospective validation. For now, this immunologic perspective reinforces the importance of selective dissection in appropriate candidates, alongside considerations of morbidity and staging accuracy.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that the ultimate goal of LND is not merely node removal but accurate staging that informs adjuvant therapy and prognostic estimation. The current TNM staging system relies heavily on the number of involved nodal stations and the presence of skip metastases, yet these elements are themselves dependent on the thoroughness of the pathologic examination. A less extensive LND may understage a proportion of patients, denying them potentially life-saving adjuvant chemotherapy or immunotherapy. Conversely, an overly extensive LND may upstage patients through the detection of isolated tumor cells or micrometastases of uncertain clinical significance, exposing them to unnecessary treatment toxicity. The development of more sensitive and specific intraoperative molecular assays (e.g., one-step nucleic acid amplification for cytokeratin 19 mRNA) could help resolve this dilemma by providing real-time detection of occult tumor deposits, enabling the surgeon to selectively complete dissection of a station only when molecular positivity is confirmed. This “diagnosis-directed” LND represents a promising future direction that merges the principles of precision oncology with surgical decision-making.

Conclusions

In summary, the discussion above highlights that the extent of LND in early-stage NSCLC cannot be considered in isolation from patient-specific factors, tumor biology, surgical approach, and evolving adjuvant treatment paradigms. The field is moving away from a binary debate (sampling versus dissection) toward a more sophisticated framework that integrates preoperative risk prediction, intraoperative diagnostics, and immunologic considerations. While high-quality evidence remains incomplete, existing data provide a reasonable foundation for individualized decision-making in experienced centers, with the understanding that future research will continue to refine the optimal balance between thoroughness and restraint (Table 4).
