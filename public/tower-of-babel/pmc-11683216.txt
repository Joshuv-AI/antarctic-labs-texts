A database for the outer sizes of tropical cyclones over the Middle Americas
Authors: Adolfo Perez-Estrada, Christian Dominguez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTropical cyclones (TCs) are catastrophic phenomena that constantly threaten populations settled in the tropics. Their direct effects (strong winds, storm surges, and intense precipitation) are confined near the TC center. On the other hand, the indirect effects are due to extreme rainfall events associated with rainbands distant from the TC center. Diverse TC-size definitions do not consider these indirect effects, although these definitions are used for disaster risk reduction strategies. Risk management based on TC size needs an appropriate definition of TC hazards for adequately planning early actions. Here, we developed two algorithms, Radius of the Outermost CLOUD (ROCLOUD) and Radius of Precipitation Band (RPB), to define outer TC sizes using the HURDAT data, GPM satellite imagery, and a radial wind profile. We analyzed 191 and 336 TCs over the North Atlantic and the Eastern North Pacific basins, respectively, during the 2000–2020 period. We create a database for outer TC sizes that presents 6-hourly information during the TC lifetime, from pre-cyclonic to decaying states. This database also provides the TC location, TC outer sizes defined by the two algorithms, and TC shape metrics (asymmetry, dispersion and solidity) every 6 h. The database is freely available in a text format at Mendeley data [1].We present statistics for the four quadrant radii and mean radius defined by the ROCLOUD and RBP algorithms and the shape metrics. We expect this database can be useful to Early Warning Systems for Tropical Cyclones and regional risk management studies in the Middle Americas.Keywords: Indirect effects, Shape metrics, Hydrometeorological hazards, Risk management

Background

The tropical cyclone (TC) inner size is confined to surrounding regions near the center, where the most intense winds and rainfall are located [2]. The outer size is a broad external non-convective region that can extend far from the center. It does not vary with intensity and is larger than the inner size [3]. Until now, the inner and outer size definitions do not include the maximum rainband extent for each TC quadrant in their definitions. The extreme rainfall events associated with TCs mainly occur in regions with complex topography (i.e., located in high mountain ranges) [4]. In addition, the rainfall distribution inside the TC circulation is not homogeneous and can vary across TC quadrants [5].

The main objective is to describe the database for outer sizes of TCs over the Middle Americas presented here. We used the radial wind profile from Pérez-Alarcón et al. [6] and infrared satellite imagery from the Global Precipitation Measurement mission (GPM) [7] to calculate outer TC sizes during the 2000–2020 period by using our two developed algorithms: ROCLOUD and RPB. We used the processed data to compute TC shape metrics (asymmetry, dispersion and solidity) that are included in this dataset.

Data Description

We collected 4526 TC positions over the North Atlantic (NA) Ocean and 6923 TC positions over the Eastern Pacific (EP) Ocean that affected the Middle Americas region during the 2000–2020 period. We created two files, one for each oceanic basin: NA_TCSize_2000–2020.dat and EP_TCSize_2000–2020.dat, respectively [1]. We name each TC according to an identifier provided by the best-track dataset from the National Hurricane Center (HURDAT2), depending on the oceanic basin. The identifier is composed of the oceanic basin + the TC number for that basin + the year of formation. For example, AL012010 means that the TC formed over NA, it was the first one in this oceanic basin, and it occurred in 2010. The outer TC sizes defined by our Radius of the Outermost CLOUD (ROCLOUD) and Radius of Precipitation Band (RPB) algorithms are computed for four quadrant radii. This information is presented every 6 h and is organized in the following order: date, time in UTC, TC center position (latitude and longitude), maximum sustained winds and minimum sea level pressure, HURDAT2 reports all of the above variables; four quadrant radii (northeast, northwest, southwest, and southeast) and the mean radius defined by ROCLOUD, TC shape metrics (i.e., asymmetry, dispersion and solidity), four quadrant radii (northeast, northwest, southwest, and southeast) and the mean radius defined by RPB. It is important to mention that there are missing values, represented by the value −9999, due to the temporal unavailability of GPM products. All the data is presented in a text format that is easy-readable, portable, and transferable, following the ICON principles [8] (Table 1).

The dataset follows the HURDAT2 format, featuring two types of lines. Each TC system is named by the aforementioned identifier, followed by the TC official name and the number of lines that corresponds to that TC. For example:

AL012010, ALEX, 32

The first 8 characters are the identifier, the following characters indicate the official name provided by HURDAT2, and the number 32 means that Hurricane Alex has 32 lines of information. The subsequent lines detail the TC position data defined by HURDAT2; the four quadrant radii and mean radius defined by ROCLOD and RPB algorithms; and the asymmetry, dispersion and solidity values. The first column is the date of the TC position, expressed in an 8-digit format: 4 for the year, 2 for the month, and 2 for the day. The second column represents the hour in UTC using 4 digits. The third and fourth columns indicate the latitude and longitude TC position, respectively. The latitude is in a North orientation, expressed in 4 digits. The point is considered a digit. The longitude is in a West-East orientation, expressed in 6 digits. The fifth column indicates the maximum sustained wind speed, expressed in 2 digits (integer values), and its units are knots. The sixth column corresponds to the minimum sea level pressure, expressed in millibars. The seventh, eighth, ninth and tenth columns show the northeastern, northwestern, southwestern, and southeastern quadrant radii defined by the ROCLOUD algorithm, respectively. These four quadrant radii are expressed in kilometers. The eleventh column represents the mean radius of the four quadrant radii expressed in kilometers, using 7 digits. The twelfth, thirteenth and fourteenth columns indicate the asymmetry, dispersion, and solidity of the TC, respectively. These shape metrics are dimensionless, expressed in 4 digits, and can vary from 0 to 1.

The last five columns correspond to the northeastern, northwestern, southwestern, and southeastern quadrant radii, defined by the RPB algorithm, and the mean radius of these four radii. All five radii are expressed in a 7-digit format, and their units are kilometers.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

HURDAT and satellite data

TC positions from NA and EP during the 2000–2000 period were obtained from HURDAT2 [9]. This database is based on a post-seasonal analysis and contains 6-hourly information about the geographical position, date, and time in UTC. It is stored in the https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/hurdat website. We only included TCs that are over the region 5°N-35°N and 60°W-130°W, defined as the Middle Americas for this study. The main criterion for selecting TCs from both basins was that their trajectories should report at least two points within the study region. However, we analyzed their whole lifetime, even when the track moved northwards to the study region. As a result, 191 (336) TCs from NA (EP) were examined during the study period.

The satellite imagery and precipitation products were obtained from GPM [10], which has a quasi-global coverage (60°N - 60°S). GPM is based on the combination of passive microwave (PMW) sensors, PMW-calibrated infrared (IR) sensors, and their merger at different spatial and temporal resolutions [11]. We employ IR satellite images at a 4-km spatial resolution and 30-min temporal resolution to define the threshold of 233 K in brightness temperature (BT) to define TC deep convection during the 2000–2020 period. We also use the IMERG (Integrated Multi-satellitE Retrievals) data obtained from a GPM algorithm [12]. This algorithm combines the Tropical Rainfall Measuring Mission (TRMM), Geosynchronous Operational Environmental Satellites (GOES), and Geosynchronous Meteorological Satellite (GMS) rainfall data, among other products, into a global mesh [13]. The final product is the Final Precipitation L3 Half Hourly 0.1° × 0.1° V06B, also known as GPM_3IMERGHH. It is available from 1999 onward at https://disc.gsfc.nasa.gov/datasets/GPM_MERGIR_1/summary.

TC wind profile size data

Pérez-Alarcon et al. [6] developed a database of outer TC radius for all the global cyclogenetic basins using an exponentially decaying radial wind profile. Pérez-Alarcon et al. [6] follows the method proposed by Willoughby et al. [14] and limits the outer TC radius to the distance from the TC center where the tangential wind speed is 2 m/s. The database produced by Pérez-Alarcon et al. [6] presents the radii where surface winds reach 34 kt (R34), 50 kt (R50), 60 kt (R60) and the resulting outer TC radius. The data is freely available from Mendeley data (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/8997r89fbf/1). We used the outer TC radii from the NA and EP basins during the study period. However, real TCs observed in satellite imagery are distinct from the theoretical circular shape obtained by Pérez-Alarcon et al. [15], which only considers winds to define an outer TC radius. Our ROCLOUD and RPB algorithms not only examine this wind-based TC radius, but also incorporate the TC cloud band extension and TC rainfall, respectively, for computing each quadrant radii. These two algorithms consider TC interactions with landmass and complex topography, which represents an advantage over Pérez-Alarcon et al. [15].

ROCLOUD algorithm

We developed the ROCLOUD algorithm. This technique is based on the Python programming language and determines the outer TC size by combining wind field data from Pérez-Alarcon et al. [6] and convective cluster locations. The ROCLOUD technique measures the distance between the TC center reported by HURDAT2 and the farthest convective cluster (i.e. clouds) inside the outer TC radius obtained by Pérez-Alarcon et al. [6]. The algorithm automatically carries out the following steps for each TC position:

1.IR images from the GPM are segmented based on BTs < 233 K (−40 °C). This threshold is usually related to deep TC convection and used for defining TC intense precipitation [20]. The technique segments pixels or groups of pixels with similar characteristics inside the outer TC radius obtained by Pérez-Alarcon et al. [6].

RPB algorithm

The RBP algorithm was developed as a modification of ROCLOUD. The RPB algorithm follows steps from 1 to 5 of the ROCLOUD algorithm but considers the IMERG rainfall data to define the maximum extent per quadrant. The RPB algorithm aims to determine how the TC precipitation is distributed inside the outer TC radius from Pérez-Alarcon et al. [6]. The results could be applied to improve the forecast of the TC rainfall-outer size relationship. The RBP algorithm followed Zhou and Matyas [5] and Matyas and VanShoick [16], who segmented TRMM data and defined the TC induced-rainfall using different hourly rainfall thresholds: 2.0 mm/h, 2.5 mm/h, 3 mm/h, 5mm/h. We used IMERG because it has the best precipitation detection compared to other satellite products. However, IMERG underestimates extreme precipitation amounts [18]. We selected the 2.5 mm/h threshold to determine moderate TC-induced rainfall, following previous studies [5,16,17], as this threshold provides the best TC rainfall estimation and allows to compare the TC rainfall with the extend of TC cloud bands defined by ROCLOUD [17].

TC spatial metrics

The distribution of TC cloud bands can be investigated using mathematical equations [19]. Three spatial metrics were selected to analyze TC cloud band: dispersion, asymmetry, and solidity. These metrics are relevant for determining the role of TC shape on rainfall over landmass [19]. These spatial metrics could be critical factors for predicting TC-induced damage and forecasting TC hazards [17].

The Asymmetry (A) describes the organization of TC cloud-bands as seen by satellites along the TC trajectory (Eq. (1)). The maximum (Rmax) and minimum (Rmin) extent from the TC center are obtained from ROCLOUD. Its dimensionless values vary from 0 to 1 [19]. The lowest the value, the most symmetric the TC. For example, values near 0 mean that TCs are almost a perfect circle. Values near 1 mean that TCs are asymmetric or non-circular.

Dispersion (D) is a metric that measures the spread of closed contour areas where BT < 233 K from the TC center [19]. “D” results from the multiplication of two terms: the area of each closed contour (Ari) divided by the total area of closed contours (Ar), and the distance from the centroid of each closed contour (Rcentroid) divided by the search radius (Rsearch) defined per quadrant by ROCLOUD (Eq. (2)). The “D” values range from 0 to 1. The lower the value, the closer the convective regions to the TC center. For example, values near 0 mean that the closed contour areas are located near the TC center. Values near 1 mean that the closed contour areas are located far away from the TC center. For D close to 1, TC cloud bands are dispersed from the TC center. Zick & Matyas [19] further describe the Dispersion metric.

Finally, Solidity (S) is a metric that relates the area of TC closed contour areas, known as cumulus polygons (ATC cumulus polygons), to the total area of closed contours, known as the cumulus field (Atotal area of the cumulus field) (Eq. (3)). Its dimensionless values vary from 0 to 1. Lower values indicate that some TC areas do not have cloud bands or that the rainfall field is less structured [20]. For example, values near 0 mean that the outer TC size has empty spaces without cloud bands. Values near 1 mean that cloud bands almost fill out the outer TC size.

It is worth mentioning that Zhou and Matyas [20] used TRMM satellite products to define these metrics only for TCs from the NA that made landfall over the USA and the Middle American countries during the 1998–2014 period. The metrics presented in this study are computed for all TCs from NA and EP that passed through the Middle Americas from 2000 to 2020. Furthermore, we analyze GPM satellite products whose spatial resolution is finer than TRMM precipitation.
