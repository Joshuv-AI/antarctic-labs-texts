Conjugating trivial automorphisms of 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℕ ) / fin \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})/\mathrm{fin}
Will Brian, Ilijas Farah

Abstract
. A trivial automorphism of the Boolean algebra 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℕ ) / Fin \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})/\mathrm{Fin} is an automorphism induced by the action of some function ℕ → ℕ \mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N} . The forcing axiom 𝖮𝖢𝖠 T \mathsf{OCA}_{\mathrm{T}} implies all automorphisms are trivial, and therefore two trivial automorphisms are conjugate if and only if they have the same (modulo finite) orbit structure. We show that the Continuum Hypothesis implies that two trivial automorphisms are conjugate if and only if there are no first-order obstructions to their conjugacy and their indices have the same parity, if and only if the given trivial automorphisms are conugate in some forcing extension of the universe. To each automorphism α \alpha of 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℕ ) / Fin \nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}} we associate the first-order structure 𝔄 α = ( 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℕ ) / Fin , α ) \mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}=(\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}},\alpha) and compute the existential theories of these structures. These results are applied to resolve a question of Braga, Farah, and Vignati and prove that there are coarse metric spaces X X and Y Y such that the isomorphism between their uniform Roe coronas is independent of 𝖹𝖥𝖢 \ZFC .

2.
Almost permutations

The power set of
{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, when ordered by inclusion, is a complete Boolean algebra. The Boolean algebra
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}

is the quotient of this Boolean algebra by the ideal of finite sets.
For
A,B{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
A=^{*}B
to mean that
A
and
B
differ by finitely many elements, i.e.
\left\lvert(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)\right\rvert<\aleph_{0}
.
The members of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are equivalence classes of the
=^{*}
relation, and we denote the equivalence class of
A{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
by
[A]_{\text{Fin}}
.
The Boolean-algebraic relation
[A]_{\text{Fin}}\leq[B]_{\text{Fin}}
is denoted on the level of representatives by writing
A{\,\subseteq\,}^{*}B
: that is,
A{\,\subseteq\,}^{*}B
means that
[A]_{\text{Fin}}\leq[B]_{\text{Fin}}
, or (equivalently) that
B
contains all but finitely many members of
A
.

The Stone space of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is
\beta{\mathbb{N}}
, the Čech-Stone compactification of the countable discrete space
{\mathbb{N}}
. The Stone space of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is
{\mathbb{N}}^{*}=\beta{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathbb{N}}
.

While our main interest is in almost permutations of
{\mathbb{N}}
, it is helpful to begin with a more general definition.

If

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose

Consequently,

If

For the “consequently” part: the previous paragraph shows the map

, we say the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like part of
f
is empty, and
\mathrm{Z}(f)
is defined to be the empty map.
☕

Note that
\mathrm{R}(f)
is only defined up to equivalence, as it depends on the enumeration
\bar{n}
.
In what follows (in the next lemma and in the remainder of the paper), every statement about
\mathrm{R}(f)
is invariant under equivalence.

Lemma 2.10
.

Every almost permutation
f
of
{\mathbb{N}}
is equivalent to
\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)
.
Moreover, given two almost permutations
f
and
g
of
{\mathbb{N}}
,
f\sim g
if and only if
\mathrm{R}(f)\sim\mathrm{R}(g)
,
\mathrm{S}(f)=\mathrm{S}(g)
, and
\mathrm{Z}(f)=\mathrm{Z}(g)
.

Proof.

If
O_{f}(n)
is
{\mathbb{N}}
-like and
O_{f}(m)
is reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like, then the restriction of
f
to
O_{f}(n)\cup O_{f}(m)
is equivalent to
s_{\mathbb{Z}}
, a single
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit. (To see this, extend
f
to
O_{f}(m)\cup O_{f}(n)
by sending the unique element of
f[O_{f}(n)]\setminus O_{f}(n)
to the unique element of
O_{f}(m)\setminus f[O_{f}(m)]
.)
Bearing this in mind, the desired conclusion is an almost immediate consequence of Definition
2.9
, as follows.
First, decompose
f
into orbits.
Because
f
is an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
, it can have only finitely many finite orbits that are not finite cycles. Delete these (if any exist),
and delete the other finite orbits as well if there are only finitely many of them.
Next, exchange any pair of
{\mathbb{N}}
-like and reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbits for a single
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit.
These changes result in a map equivalent to
f
, and this map is equivalent to
\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)
.

To prove the “moreover” part, we first consider the case when
g
is the restriction of
f
to a cofinite set. Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
be the complement of this set. Then
S
intersects only finitely many orbits of
f
, and by induction it suffices to consider the case when it intersects only one orbit,
O_{f}(n)
. The only nontrivial case is when this is a two-sided infinite orbit, in which case passing from
f
to
g
breaks it into two one-sided infinite orbits, one
{\mathbb{N}}
-like and the other reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like, together with finitely many (possibly zero) finite orbits. It is a feature of our definition that these two infinite orbits contribute to
\mathrm{S}(g)
and
\mathrm{Z}(g)
in the same way that the single
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit contributes to
\mathrm{S}(f)
and
\mathrm{Z}(f)
. This proves the special case.

For the general case, fix equivalent
f
and
g
and an almost bijection
h
witnessing the equivalence. Let
f^{\prime}=f\restriction\dom(h)
and
g^{\prime}=g\restriction\ran(h)
. By the previous paragraph,
f\sim f^{\prime}
and
g\sim g^{\prime}
. As
h
witnesses
f^{\prime}\sim g^{\prime}
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Definition 2.11
.

If
f
is an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
, define

\Index(f)\,=\,|{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\dom(f)|-|{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\ran(f)|

This is the
index
of
f
, or more precisely the
prodigal index
of
f
.
The (
index
)
parity
of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{par}(f)
, is
0
if
\Index(f)
is even, and
1
if
\Index(f)
is odd.
☕

The index first appeared in a rudimentary form in a 1915 theorem of Andreoli (see
[
9
, (12) on p. 25]
).
The quantity
-\Index(f)
is called the
banker index
of
f
; see the discussion in
[
9
, §1.C]
. The two indices are clearly interchangeable, and our choice of prodigal index over banker index is dictated by the analogy with the Fredholm index of operators on a Hilbert space (see e.g.,
[
26
, 3.3.12]
).

As is well-known, the Fredholm index of Fredholm operators on a Hilbert space is preserved under compact perturbations (
[
26
, Theorem 3.3.17]
). Similarly, the index of almost permutations is preserved under the
=^{*}
relation. To prove this, it suffices to notice that every almost permutation
f
has the same index as
f\restriction A
for any cofinite
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(f)
; this implies that if
f=^{*}g
then
f
and
g
both have the same index as
f\cap g
, hence the same index as each other.
Similar reasoning shows that the index of an almost permutation is invariant under equivalence as well: i.e.,
f\sim g
implies
\Index(f)=\Index(g)
.

Furthermore,
\Index(f\circ g)=\Index(f)+\Index(g)
(see
[
32
, Theorem 6.1]
).
Thus the index is a group homomorphism from
\mathfrak{S}^{*}({\mathbb{N}})
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
whose kernel is
\mathfrak{S}_{0}^{*}({\mathbb{N}})
, the group of permutations of
{\mathbb{N}}
modulo almost equality.
It follows that the parity function is a group homomorphism from
\mathfrak{S}^{*}({\mathbb{N}})
to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
whose kernel is the group of parity zero almost permutations.

In light of Lemma
2.2
, and the fact that
\Index(f)=\Index(g)
whenever
f=^{*}g
, we can define

\Index(\alpha_{f})\,=\,\Index(f)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathrm{par}(\alpha_{f})\,=\,\mathrm{par}(f).

Just like the Fredholm index of operators on an infinite-dimensional, separable Hilbert space, the prodigal index is a group homomorphism from the group of trivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
into
({\mathbb{Z}},+)
; in other words,

\Index(\alpha_{f}\circ\alpha_{g})\,=\,\Index(\alpha_{f})+\Index(\alpha_{g}).

(This follows from the facts that
[f]\mapsto\alpha_{f}
is a group isomorphism from
\mathfrak{S}^{*}(X)
to
\TrAut(\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}(X)}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}})
, and the index is a group homomorphism from
\mathfrak{S}^{*}({\mathbb{N}})
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
. See
[
32
, Section 6]
for further details.)
In particular, this proves:

Proposition 2.12
.

If two trivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are trivially conjugate, then they have the same index. ∎

Similarly, from this and Lemma
2.5
, it follows that equivalent almost permutations always have the same index.

Because equivalent permutations have the same index, Lemma
2.10
implies

\displaystyle\Index(f)

\displaystyle\,=\,\Index(\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f))

\displaystyle\,=\,\Index(\mathrm{R}(f))+\Index(\mathrm{Z}(f))+\Index(\mathrm{S}(f))\,=\,\Index(\mathrm{S}(f)).

In other words,
\Index(f)
depends only on
\mathrm{S}(f)
.
Specifically,

\displaystyle\Index(f)

\displaystyle=k>0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathrm{S}(f)=s_{k{\mathbb{N}}},

\displaystyle\Index(f)

\displaystyle=k<0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathrm{S}(f)=s_{|k|{\mathbb{N}}}^{-1},

\displaystyle\Index(f)

\displaystyle=0\hskip 28.73729pt\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathrm{S}(f)\text{ is empty}.

One could paraphrase Proposition
2.12
as saying that the index provides an “obstruction” to trivial conjugacy: automorphisms with different indices are not trivially conjugate.
Let us point out, though, that the index is not an obstruction to conjugacy in general. This was proved recently by Brian
[
5
]
, by showing that
\mathsf{CH}
implies
{\sigma}
and
{\sigma}^{-1}
are conjugate. (Note that these maps have index
1
and
-1
, respectively.)
In the following section we show that while the index does not provide an obstruction to conjugacy, the parity of the index does.

3.
The parity obstruction

The goal of this section is to prove that conjugate trivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
have the same index parity. Note that this is a theorem of
\mathsf{ZFC}
, and does not require
\mathsf{CH}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha
be an automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. Define

\mathrm{FIX}_{\alpha}=\left\{c\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}\colon\alpha(c)=c\right\}.

\FIX_{\alpha}
is a Boolean subalgebra of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, and the atoms of
\FIX_{\alpha}
are called the
components
of
\alpha
. That is,
c
is a component of
\alpha
if
c\in\mathrm{FIX}_{\alpha}\setminus\{[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\}
and if
x\notin\FIX_{\alpha}
whenever
[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}<x<c
.
☕

Observe that
\FIX_{\sigma}
and
\FIX_{{\sigma}^{-1}}
are both the trivial algebra
\{[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}},[{\mathbb{N}}]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\}
.
In fact,
{\sigma}
and
{\sigma}^{-1}
both enjoy a stronger property: they are
incompressible
, which means that if
x\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
and
x\neq[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}},[{\mathbb{N}}]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, then
{\sigma}(x),{\sigma}^{-1}(x)\not\leq x
.
This is not difficult to see; a proof can be found in
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]
or
[
6
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Furthermore,
{\sigma}
and
{\sigma}^{-1}
are the only trivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
for which
\FIX_{\alpha}=\{[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}},[{\mathbb{N}}]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\}
.
(It is worth noting, however, that
\mathsf{CH}
implies there are non-trivial automorphisms
\alpha
with
\FIX_{\alpha}
the trivial subalgebra; see
[
6
, Theorem 5.7]
.)
With this in mind, the idea behind Definition
3.1
is that when
\alpha
is trivial, each component of
\alpha
is simply the clopen set corresponding to some
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit in the almost permutation inducing
\alpha
. This is made more precise in Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
below.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. If
\gamma
is a conjugacy map from
\alpha
to
\beta
, then
\FIX_{\beta}=\{\gamma(c)\mid\,c\in\FIX_{\alpha}\}
. In other words, conjugacy mappings send fixed points to fixed points. Furthermore,
c
is a component of
\alpha
if and only if
\gamma(c)
is a component of
\beta
.

Proof.

If
\gamma
is a conjugacy mapping from
\alpha
to
\beta
, then
\alpha(c)=\gamma^{-1}\circ\beta\circ\gamma(c)
for all
c\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
. Because
\gamma
is injective,
\alpha(c)=c
if and only if
\beta(\gamma(c))=\gamma(c)
.

For the “furthermore” part, note that
\gamma
, being an automorphism, preserves the
\leq
relation. Thus
c
is an atom of
\FIX_{\alpha}
if and only if
\gamma(c)
is an atom of
\FIX_{\beta}
.
∎

Given an almost permutation
f
of
{\mathbb{N}}
and some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit, let us denote
O^{+}_{f}(n)=\left\{f^{i}(n)\colon i>0\right\}
and
O^{-}_{f}(n)=\left\{f^{i}(n)\colon i<0\right\}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f
be an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
O
be either an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a set of the form
O^{+}_{f}(n)
or
O^{-}_{f}(n)
for some
n
with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit.
If
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
and
O\cap A
and
O\setminus A
are both infinite, then
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\notin\FIX_{\alpha_{f}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
n
has either an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit or a reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, and suppose that
O_{f}(n)\cap A
and
O_{f}(n)\setminus A
are both infinite. Let

B=\left\{f^{i}(n)\in O_{f}(n)\colon f^{i}(n)\in A\text{ and }f^{i+1}(n)\notin A\right\}.

Because
O_{f}(n)\cap A
and
O_{f}(n)\setminus A
are both infinite,
B
is infinite.
But
B\subseteq A
, while
\alpha_{f}([B]_{\mathrm{Fin}})=[f[B]]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\not\leq[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
.
Hence
\alpha_{f}([A]_{\mathrm{Fin}})\neq[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
.

If
n
has a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit, the same argument works, verbatim, if we simply replace
O_{f}(n)
with either
O^{+}_{f}(n)
or
O^{-}_{f}(n)
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f
be an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
. Then components of
\alpha_{f}
are precisely the sets
[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
where
O
be either an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a set of the form
O^{+}_{f}(n)
or
O^{-}_{f}(n)
for some
n
with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit.

Proof.

Let
O
be either an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a set of the form
O^{+}_{f}(n)
or
O^{-}_{f}(n)
for some
n
with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit. Clearly
\alpha_{f}([O]_{\mathrm{Fin}})=[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, i.e.,
[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in\FIX_{\alpha_{f}}
. Furthermore, the previous lemma implies that if
A{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
and
[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}<[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}<[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, then
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\notin\FIX_{\alpha_{f}}
. Thus
[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is a component of
\alpha_{f}
.

It remains to show
\alpha_{f}
has no other components.
Fix
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
.

Let
O
be either an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a set of the form
O^{+}_{f}(n)
or
O^{-}_{f}(n)
for some
n
with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit.
If
[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}<[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
then (because
[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in\FIX_{\alpha_{f}}
)
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is not a component of
\alpha_{f}
.
If
[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}<[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\wedge[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}<[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, then
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is not a component of
\alpha_{f}
by the previous lemma.
Hence if
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is a component of
\alpha_{f}
, we must have either
[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}=[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
or
[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\cap[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}=[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
.

Suppose, then, that
A\cap O
is finite whenever
O
is an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit, or a set of the form
O^{+}_{f}(n)
or
O^{-}_{f}(n)
for some
n
with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit.
This implies
A
meets every orbit in a finite set. So either
A
is finite or it meets infinitely many orbits. If
A
is finite,
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}=[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is not a component of
\alpha_{f}
. If
A
meets infinitely many orbits, then there is a partition of

into two infinite subsets
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
such that if
n\in A_{i}
then
O_{f}(n)\cap A{\,\subseteq\,}A_{i}
. If
\alpha_{f}([A]_{\mathrm{Fin}})=[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, then this implies
\alpha_{f}([A_{i}]_{\mathrm{Fin}})=[A_{i}]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
for

. Consequently, either
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\notin\FIX_{\alpha_{f}}
, or else
[A_{0}]_{\mathrm{Fin}},[A_{1}]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\in\FIX_{\alpha_{f}}
. Either way,
[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is not a component.
∎

Recall from Lemma
2.10
that every almost permutation
f
of
{\mathbb{N}}
is equivalent to
\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)
. If
f=\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)
then, by the definition of the direct sum (Definition
2.6
), the domain of
\mathrm{R}(f)
is the subset of
{\mathbb{N}}
containing those
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with finite
f
-orbits.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f
be an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
with
f=\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)
, and let
D=\mathrm{dom}(\mathrm{R}(f))
. Then
[{\mathbb{N}}\setminus D]_{\Fin}
is the supremum of all components of
\alpha_{f}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the previous lemma.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

If two trivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
have different index parity, then they are not conjugate.

Proof.

Consider the following property of an automorphism
\alpha
of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
:

(*)

There is a partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of the components of
\alpha
into pairs, such that for any
A{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathcal{P}}
(any set of these pairs), there is some
d_{A}\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
such that

\circ

If
c
is a component of
\alpha
and
c\in\bigcup A
, then
c\leq d_{A}
.

\circ

If
c
is a component of
\alpha
and
c\notin\bigcup A
, then
c\wedge d_{A}=[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
.

\circ

\alpha(d_{A})=d_{A}
.

This property
(*)
is conjugacy-invariant.
One can see this simply by noting that
(*)
describes a property of the algebraic dynamical system
\langle\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}},\alpha\rangle
that does not depend on the names of members of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
; as conjugacy is the natural notion of isomorphism in the category of dynamical systems, any such property is conjugacy-invariant.
For a more hands-on way of seeing that
(*)
is conjugacy-invariant, we noted already in Lemma
3.2
that a conjugacy mapping
\gamma
sends the components of
\alpha
to the components of a conjugate map
\beta=\phi\circ\alpha\circ\phi^{-1}
, in the sense that
c
is a component of
\alpha
if and only if
\gamma(c)
is a component of
\beta
.
Using this fact, it is not difficult to check that if
{\mathcal{P}}
is a partition witnessing
(*)
for
\alpha
, then
\left\{\{\gamma(c),\gamma(d)\}\colon\{c,d\}\in{\mathcal{P}}\right\}
is a partition witnessing
(*)
for
\gamma\circ\alpha\circ\gamma^{-1}
.

The plan of this proof is to show that property
(*)
detects the index parity of a trivial map, in the sense that if
f
is an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\alpha_{f}
satisfies
(*)
if and only if
\mathrm{par}(f)=0
. Because
(*)
is conjugacy-invariant, this suffices to prove the theorem.

First let us show that if
\mathrm{par}(f)=0
then
\alpha_{f}
satisfies
(*)
.
Let
f
be an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathrm{par}(f)=0
.
Recall that
\alpha_{f}
is trivially conjugate to
\alpha_{f^{\prime}}
for any almost permutation
f^{\prime}
of
{\mathbb{N}}
that is equivalent to
f
.
Because
(*)
is conjugacy-invariant, this means that replacing
f
with an equivalent almost permutation does not change whether it satisfies
(*)
.
Thus, applying Lemma
2.10
, we may (and do) assume
f=\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)
.
By the remarks near the end of Section
2
, the index of
f
depends only on
\mathrm{S}(f)
.
In particular, because
\mathrm{par}(f)=0
, either
\mathrm{S}(f)=s_{k{\mathbb{N}}}
or
\mathrm{S}(f)=s_{k{\mathbb{N}}}^{-1}
for some even
k\geq 0
.

Suppose first that
f
has only finitely many
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbits.
By Lemma
3.4
,
\alpha_{f}
has two components for every
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit of
f
and one for every
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit.
In particular,
\alpha_{f}
has a finite, even number of components.
In this case, any partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of these components into pairs witnesses that
\alpha_{f}
satisfies
(*)
, because if
A{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathcal{P}}
then
d_{A}=\bigvee\big(\bigcup A\big)
has all the required properties.

Suppose next that
f
has infinitely many
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbits.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}
denote a partition of the components of
\alpha_{f}
such that

\circ

if
O
is an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit of
f
, then there is another
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit
O^{\prime}
such that
\{[O]_{\mathrm{Fin}},[O^{\prime}]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\}\in{\mathcal{P}}
; and

\circ

for each
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit
O_{f}(n)
,
\{[O^{-}_{f}(n)]_{\mathrm{Fin}},[O^{+}_{f}(n)]_{\mathrm{Fin}},\}\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

In other words, the components of
\alpha_{f}
that come from
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbits are paired up in the natural way, and the components coming from
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbits are paired with each other arbitrarily. This covers all components of
\alpha_{f}
by Lemma
3.4
.
To see that
{\mathcal{P}}
witnesses
(*)
, fix

.
For each pair

either
(1)
there are two
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbits from

, or
(2)
the two components of

are formed from the two ends of a single
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit.
Let

denote the union of all these orbits: i.e.,

\textstyle D_{A}\,=\,\bigcup\left\{O_{f}(n)\colon[O_{f}(n)]_{\Fin}\in\bigcup A\text{ or }[O^{-}_{f}(n)]_{\Fin},[O^{+}_{f}(n)]_{\Fin}\in\bigcup A\right\}.

Using Lemmas
3.4
and the assumption that

, it is not difficult to see that

has the three properties required in
(*)
.

Hence, whether

has finitely many or infinitely many
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbits, if
\mathrm{par}(f)=0
then
\alpha_{f}
satisfies
(*)
.

Next, let us show that if
\mathrm{par}(f)=1
then
\alpha_{f}
does not satisfy
(*)
.
Let
f
be an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathrm{par}(f)=1
.
As before, we may (and do) assume

.
Because
\mathrm{par}(f)=1
, either
\mathrm{S}(f)=s_{k{\mathbb{N}}}
or
\mathrm{S}(f)=s_{k{\mathbb{N}}}^{-1}
for some odd

.

By Lemma
3.4
,
\alpha_{f}
has two components for every
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit of
f
and one for every
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit.
But
f
has an odd number of

-like or reverse

-like orbits, so if

has only finitely many

-like orbits, then
\alpha_{f}
has a finite, odd number of components.
In particular, the components of
\alpha_{f}
cannot be partitioned into pairs, which means that
(*)
fails for trivial reasons.

Suppose instead that
f
has infinitely many
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbits.
Because
\mathrm{par}(f)=1
, the number of
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbits of

is odd.
Thus
\alpha_{f}
has
\aleph_{0}
components coming from the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbits of
f
, and it has a finite odd number of components coming from the
{\mathbb{N}}
-like and reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbits of
f
.

Each
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit

gives rise to two components of
\alpha_{f}
,
[O^{-}_{f}(n)]_{\Fin}
and
[O^{+}_{f}(n)]_{\Fin}
. Let us say that these two components are
natural partners
. Furthermore, let us say that any component of
\alpha_{f}
arising from
{\mathbb{N}}
-like or reverse

-like orbit does not have a natural partner.

Suppose

is a partition of the components of
\alpha_{f}
into pairs.
Because the number of
{\mathbb{N}}
-like and reverse
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbits of

is odd, the number of components without a natural partner is odd.
This implies that there is an infinite

such that each member of
A_{0}
has a natural partner, but is not paired with its natural partner by
{\mathcal{P}}
.
(To see this, note that if

were to pair all but finitely many components with their natural partners, then there would be a finite, odd number of components left over, and so no way to pair them off.)
If we color

by setting

if some member of

is the natural partner of some member of

, and

-homogeneous subset of

.
Thus, by Ramsey’s theorem, this coloring has an infinite
0
-homogeneous set
A{\,\subseteq\,}A_{0}
.
That is, there is an infinite

such that each member of
\bigcup A
has a natural partner, but no two members of
\bigcup A
are natural partners of each other.

Concretely, there is an infinite set
\left\{n_{k}\colon k\in{\omega}\right\}{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
such that the

are all distinct
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbits of

(hence disjoint from each other), and such that
\bigcup A
contains exactly one of

or
[O^{+}_{f}(n_{k})]_{\Fin}
for each

.

Fix

, and fix

with
[D]_{\Fin}=d_{A}
.
Assume that
d_{A}
satisfies the first two of the three properties listed in the statement of
(*)
.
We will show that
d_{A}
cannot also satisfy the third property.

Our assumption that

satisfies the first two properties in
(*)
means that for any component

of
\alpha_{f}
, either
c\in\bigcup A
and
c\leq d_{A}
, or else
c\notin\bigcup A
and
c\wedge d_{A}=[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
.
Given our description of the components of
\alpha_{f}
above, this means that for each
k\in{\omega}
, either
D
contains only finitely many members
O^{-}_{f}(n_{k})
and all but finitely many members of or
O^{+}_{f}(n_{k})
, or else
D
contains only finitely many members
O^{+}_{f}(n_{k})
and all but finitely many members of or
O^{-}_{f}(n_{k})
.
This implies that, for each

such that either

and

and

[\left\{f^{i_{k}+1}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin}\,=\,\alpha_{f}([\left\{f^{i_{k}}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin})\,\leq\,\alpha_{f}([D]_{\Fin})\,=\,\alpha_{f}(d_{A}),

\displaystyle[\left\{f^{i_{k}+1}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin}\wedge d_{A}

\displaystyle\,=\,[\left\{f^{i_{k}+1}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin}\wedge[D]_{\Fin}

\displaystyle\,=\,[\left\{f^{i_{k}+1}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}\cap D]_{\Fin}\,=\,[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}.

Because

, and therefore

.
If on the other hand
f^{i_{k}}(n_{k})\notin D
and
f^{i_{k}+1}(n_{k})\in D
for infinitely many

[\left\{f^{i_{k}}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin}=\alpha_{f}^{-1}([\left\{f^{i_{k}+1}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin})\leq\alpha_{f}^{-1}([D]_{\Fin})=\alpha_{f}^{-1}(d_{A}),

while at the same time

\displaystyle[\left\{f^{i_{k}}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin}\wedge d_{A}

\displaystyle\,=\,[\left\{f^{i_{k}}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}]_{\Fin}\wedge[D]_{\Fin}

\displaystyle\,=\,[\left\{f^{i_{k}}(n_{k})\colon k\in K\right\}\cap D]_{\Fin}\,=\,[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}.

As before,

.
This shows that any

satisfying the first two bullet points listed in statement

∎

4.
Saturation

By the
language of dynamical systems
we mean the formal logical language that extends the language of Boolean algebras by adding a function symbol representing an automorphism of a Boolean algebra.
A model for this language is a dynamical system
\langle\mathbb{A},\alpha\rangle
, where
\mathbb{A}
is a Boolean algebra and
\alpha:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
an automorphism. Things like
+
or
\leq
are understood as part of the language of the Boolean algebra
\mathbb{A}
, and not listed explicitly.

Definition 4.1
.

If
\alpha
is an automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, let

\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}\,=\,\langle\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}},\alpha\rangle.

let
\mathrm{Th}(\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha})
denote the first-order theory of this structure in the language of dynamical systems, and let
\mathrm{Th}_{\exists}(\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha})
denote its existential theory.
For convenience,
if
f
is an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
rather than
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha_{f}}
.
Similarly, if
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
is a rotary automorphism, we write
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
rather than
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha_{\bar{n}}}
.
☕

As usual,
\mathrm{Th}(\mathfrak{A}_{f})=\mathrm{Th}(\mathfrak{A}_{g})
can also be expressed by saying
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{g}
are
elementarily equivalent
, denoted
\mathfrak{A}_{f}\equiv\mathfrak{A}_{g}
.
Furthermore, observe that the following important fact follows immediately from the definitions:

Proposition 4.2
.

Given
\phi,\psi\in\Aut(\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}})
, the structures
\mathfrak{A}_{\phi}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{\psi}
are isomorphic if and only if the maps
\phi
and
\psi
are conjugate.

The main result of this section, Theorem
4.10
below, states:
assuming
\mathsf{CH}
, if
\mathrm{par}(f)=\mathrm{par}(g)
and
\mathfrak{A}_{f}\equiv\mathfrak{A}_{g}
then
\mathfrak{A}_{f}\cong\mathfrak{A}_{g}
.
A special case of this is the main result of
[
5
]
, which states that
\mathsf{CH}
implies
\mathfrak{A}_{\sigma}\cong\mathfrak{A}_{{\sigma}^{-1}}
.
We do not claim to give an alternative proof of the results in
[
5
]
; rather, our theorem extends the main theorem of
[
5
]
, and its proof relies directly on
[
5
]
. The proof of Theorem
4.10
uses
[
5
]
as a black box, so that the reader does not need to be familiar with the details in
[
5
]
in order to understand the proof.

The proof of Theorem
4.10
relies on a saturation argument. Since some of our readership may not care about
\aleph_{1}
and since we will need
\kappa
-saturated models for
\kappa>\aleph_{1}
only once, we adopt the following variation on the standard terminology (already standard in the theory of
\mathrm{C^{*}}
-algebras).

Definition 4.3
.

We say that a first-order structure
\mathfrak{A}
is
countably saturated
if every consistent type over a countable subset of
\mathfrak{A}
is realized in
\mathfrak{A}
.

We show that rotary automorphisms correspond to countably saturated structures (Lemma
4.4
). To complement this argument, we also classify precisely which trivial maps give rise to countably saturated structures:
the structure
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
is countably saturated if and only if
\mathrm{Z}(f)\neq s_{{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}}
(the permutation with infinitely many bi-infinite orbits, Definition
2.7
)
This is Theorem
4.16
below, whose proof also relies on
[
5
]
. Unlike the proof of Theorem
4.10
, the proof of Theorem
4.16
requires the reader to be familiar with some of the details of
[
5
]
.

We begin by showing that if
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
is a (trivial) rotary automorphism of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, then the structure
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
is countably saturated.
For the statement of the following lemma, let
{\mathcal{P}}(n)
denote the power set of
n=\{0,1,2,\dots,n-1\}
, which is a finite Boolean algebra of size
2^{n}
, and let
r_{n}
denote the automorphism induced on
{\mathcal{P}}(n)
by the function
i\mapsto i+1\ \,(\text{mod }n)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For every sequence
\bar{n}=\left\langle n_{j}\colon n\in{\omega}\right\rangle
of natural numbers,

\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}\,\cong\,\textstyle\Big(\prod_{j\in{\omega}}\langle{\mathcal{P}}(n_{j}),r_{n_{j}}\rangle\Big)/\Fin.

Consequently,
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
is countably saturated.

Proof.

As in Definition
2.8
,
let
I_{0},I_{1},I_{2},\dots
be the sequence of adjacent intervals with
|I_{j}|=n_{j}
for all
j
, and let
r_{\bar{n}}
denote the map

r_{\bar{n}}(i)\,=\,\begin{cases}i+1&\text{ if }i\neq\max I_{k}\text{ for any $k$,}\\
\min I_{k}&\text{ if }i=\max I_{k}.\end{cases}

For each
j
, this map induces a function
A\mapsto r_{\bar{n}}[A]
on
{\mathcal{P}}(I_{n_{j}})
; let us denote this function
R_{j}
. Clearly
\langle{\mathcal{P}}(I_{j}),R_{j}\rangle\cong\langle{\mathcal{P}}(n),r_{n}\rangle
.

Define a function
f\colon{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\to\prod_{j\in{\omega}}{\mathcal{P}}(I_{j})
by setting

f(X)\,=\,\left\langle X\cap I_{j}\colon j\in{\omega}\right\rangle.

If
[X]_{\mathrm{Fin}}=[Y]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
then
X\cap I_{j}=Y\cap I_{j}
for all but finitely many
j
.
Thus
f
lifts to an isomorphism
mapping
\langle\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}},\alpha_{\bar{n}}\rangle
to
\prod_{j}\langle{\mathcal{P}}(I_{j}),R_{j}\rangle/\Fin
, namely the function
[X]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\,\mapsto\,[f(X)]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
.
The first assertion of the lemma follows.

Every reduced product over
\Fin
is countably saturated by
[
19
]
(a proof of this result in a more general context can be found in
[
11
, Theorem 16.5.1]
,
and an elegant direct proof can be found in
[
25
, Theorem 4]
), so the second assertion of the lemma follows from the first.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha_{\bar{m}}
and
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
be rotary automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
Assuming
\mathsf{CH}
,
\alpha_{\bar{m}}
and
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
are conjugate if and only if
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}}\equiv\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
.

Proof.

Recall (Proposition
4.2
)
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
are isomorphic if and only if
\alpha_{\bar{m}}
and
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
are conjugate.
With this in mind, the forward implication of the lemma is trivial: isomorphic structures have the same theory.
For the reverse implication, assume
\mathsf{CH}
, which means
|\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}|=\aleph_{1}
.
Any two elementarily equivalent countably saturated structures of size
\aleph_{1}
are isomorphic (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 5.1.13]
, keeping in mind that our ‘countably saturated’ translates as ‘
\aleph_{1}
-saturated’).
Thus, applying Lemma
4.4
, if
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}}\equiv\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
then
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}}\cong\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
, i.e.
\alpha_{\bar{m}}
and
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
are conjugate.
∎

Proposition
4.6
below is based on
[
16
, Lemma 5.2]
. An analogous fact is true for metric structures, with an essentially identical proof.

Proposition 4.6

(Ghasemi’s Trick)
.

Suppose that
A_{n}
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is a sequence of structures in the same countable language
\mathcal{L}
. There is a sequence
n(k)
, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that for every further subsequence
n(k(i))
, for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the reduced products
\prod_{k}A_{n(k)}/\Fin
and
\prod_{i}A_{n(k(i))}/\Fin
are elementarily equivalent and therefore isomorphic, provided that
\mathsf{CH}
holds and each structure has cardinality
\leq\!2^{\aleph_{0}}
.

Proof.

Since the language of dynamical systems is countable, there are only countably many
\mathcal{L}
-sentences. Enumerate these sentences as
\varphi_{j}
, for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Using this fact, a standard diagonalization argument shows that there is an infinite set
D=\{n(k)\mid k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
such that for every
j
,
\varphi_{j}^{A_{n(k)}}
is either true for all but finitely many
k
or it is false for all but finitely many
k
. (This is essentially identical to the usual argument that the splitting number
\mathfrak{s}
is uncountable.)

To see that this subsequence is as required, choose a further subsequence
n(k(i))
of this sequence, and fix an
\mathcal{L}
-sentence
\varphi
.
By the Feferman–Vaught theorem (
[
13
]
, also
[
8
, Proposition 6.3.2]
) there are a finite set
F_{\varphi}=\{\psi_{i}\mid i<m\}
of
\mathcal{L}
-sentences and a formula
\Theta(Z_{0},\dots,Z_{m-1})
in the language of Boolean algebras such that, with
Z(\psi)
denoting the
\Fin
-equivalence class of the set
\{n\mid B_{n}\models\psi\}
, we have that
\big(\prod_{n}B_{n}\big)/\Fin\models\varphi
if and only if
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}\models\Theta(Z(\psi_{0}),\dots,Z(\psi_{m-1}))
.

If
\psi
is a Boolean combination of the
\psi_{j}
, for
j<m
, then it is either true in all but finitely many of the
A_{n(k)}
or it is false in all but finitely many of the
A_{n(k)}
, and the same is true for every further subsequence.
Therefore
\big(\prod_{k}A_{n(k)}\big)/\Fin\models\varphi
if and only if
\big(\prod_{i}A_{n(k(i))}\big)/\Fin\models\varphi
. Since
\varphi
was arbitrary, this proves that the reduced products are elementarily equivalent.
By the countable saturation of reduced products over
\Fin
(already used in the proof of Lemma
4.4
), these structures are countably saturated. Since they are also elementarily equivalent, as in the proof of Lemma
4.4
,
\mathsf{CH}
implies they are isomorphic.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
\bar{n}
is a sequence in
{\mathbb{N}}
, and
f_{\bar{n}}
the corresponding trivial automorphism of
{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
d\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
-invariant if and only if
d=[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
for some
D{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
that is a union of
f_{\bar{n}}
-orbits.
Furthermore, if
d
is a nonzero
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
-invariant set, then the restriction of
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
to
d
is conjugate to
\alpha_{\bar{m}}
for some subsequence
\bar{m}
of
\bar{n}
.

Proof.

If
D{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
is a union of
f_{\bar{n}}
-orbits, then
f_{\bar{n}}[D]=D
, and therefore
\alpha_{\bar{n}}([D]_{\mathrm{Fin}})=[f_{\bar{n}}[D]]_{\mathrm{Fin}}=[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, i.e.,
[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
-invariant.
Conversely, suppose
[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
is not
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
-invariant. Then either
\alpha_{\bar{n}}([D]_{\mathrm{Fin}})-[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\neq[\emptyset]
or
[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}-\alpha_{\bar{n}}([D]_{\mathrm{Fin}})\neq[\emptyset]
. In the former case,

\alpha_{\bar{n}}([D]_{\mathrm{Fin}})-[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\,=\,[f_{\bar{n}}[D]]_{\mathrm{Fin}}-[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\,=\,[f_{\bar{n}}[D]\setminus D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\,\neq\,[\emptyset],

which means
f_{\bar{n}}[D]\setminus D
is infinite. In particular
D
is not
f_{\bar{n}}
-invariant, and therefore is not a union of
f_{\bar{n}}
-orbits. Similarly, in the latter case
[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}-\alpha_{\bar{n}}([D]_{\mathrm{Fin}})\neq[\emptyset]
implies
D\setminus f_{\bar{n}}[D]
is infinite, which means
D
is not
f_{\bar{n}}
-invariant, and therefore is not a union of
f_{\bar{n}}
-orbits.

For the “furthermore” part of the lemma, fix a nonzero
d\in\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
such that
\alpha_{\bar{n}}(d)=d
.
By the previous paragraph, there is some
D{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
with
d=[D]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
such that
D
is a union of
f_{\bar{n}}
-orbits. Letting
\bar{m}
denote the subsequence of
\bar{n}
consisting of those orbits contained in
D
, it is clear that
f_{\bar{m}}
is equivalent to
f_{\bar{n}}\restriction D
, which implies that
\alpha_{\bar{m}}
is conjugate to
\alpha_{\bar{n}}\restriction d
.
∎

Applying Proposition

4.1.
Potential conjugacy

Given an almost permutation

of
{\mathbb{N}}
, the definition of

is absolute between different models of set theory, in the following sense.
If

or

Definition 4.12
.

Two trivial automorphisms
\alpha_{f}
and
\alpha_{g}
of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
are

if there is a set forcing extension in which
\alpha_{f}
and
\alpha_{g}
are conjugate.

Corollary 4.13

For two trivial automorphisms

(1)

\alpha
and

\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}\equiv\mathfrak{A}_{\beta}

(2)

\alpha

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha

\beta

\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}

\mathrm{par}(\alpha)=\mathrm{par}(\beta)

For the converse direction, let
\alpha

\beta

The parity of

4.2.
On the failure of countable saturation in
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}

Lemma
4.5
might cause one to hope that
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
is countably saturated for every trivial automorphism
\alpha
. This fleeting hope is dispelled in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.14
.

If
f=s_{{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}}
then then
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha_{f}}
is not countably saturated.

Proof.

For convenience, let us take
\mathrm{dom}(f)={\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
f(m,n)=(m+1,n)
.
Let
A={\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
. The Boolean algebra

\mathbb{A}\,=\,\left\{c\wedge[A]_{\mathrm{Fin}}\colon c\in\FIX_{\alpha_{f}}\right\}

is a definable substructure of
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
. (We do not care about how the map
\alpha_{f}
acts on
\mathbb{A}
, so we interpret this structure in the reduced language of Boolean algebras.)
Applying Lemma
3.2
,
\mathbb{A}\cong{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. But
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})
is not a countably saturated Boolean algebra. Because
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
has a definable substructure that is not countably saturated (in the reduced language),
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
itself is not countably saturated.
∎

This proposition, together with Lemma
4.4
, raises the question of exactly when
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
is countably saturated.
Towards answering this question, we turn next to the shift map
{\sigma}
.
The following is a consequence of the main result of
[
5
]
.

Proposition 4.15
.

\mathfrak{A}_{\sigma}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{{\sigma}^{-1}}
are countably saturated, and their theories are model complete.

Proof.

For this proof (and this proof only), we assume familiarity with the terminology and notation in
[
5
]
.
The proof is no different for
{\sigma}
and for
{\sigma}^{-1}
, so we give the argument just for
{\sigma}
.
A structure is both countably universal and countably homogeneous if and only if it is countably saturated (
[
8
, Theorem 5.1.14]
). This is how we shall prove
\mathfrak{A}_{\sigma}
is countably saturated.
To begin, we need the following strengthening of Lemma 6.2 (the Lifting Lemma) from
[
5
]
:

Claim
.

Let
(\langle\mathbb{A},\alpha\rangle,\langle\mathbb{B},\beta\rangle,\iota,\eta)
be an instance of the lifting problem for
\langle\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}},{\sigma}\rangle
. If
\langle\mathbb{A},\alpha\rangle
is elementarily equivalent to
\langle\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}},{\sigma}\rangle
, then this instance of the lifting problem has a solution.

Proof of the Claim.

The key observation is that the proof of Theorem 10.2 in
[
5
]
(all theorem numbers in this proof refer to
[
5
]
) can be improved by putting an explicit upper bound on the size of the digraph
\langle\mathcal{C},\overset{{}_{\mathcal{C}}}{\to}\rangle
.
Specifically, the construction in the main part of the proof of Theorem 10.2 shows

|\mathcal{C}|\,\leq\,1+2^{2^{2\cdot|\mathcal{B}|^{2}}}.

This bound is not meant to be tight, and comes from just following through the stages of the construction of
\mathcal{C}
in the proof of Theorem 10.2, and computing the trivial bounds at every step.
Specifically, there are
2^{|\mathcal{B}|^{2}}
possibilities for each
\mathcal{P}^{i}
and
\mathcal{F}^{i}
, simply because these are binary relations on
\mathcal{B}
. Hence there are
\big(2^{|\mathcal{B}|^{2}}\big)^{2}=2^{2\cdot|\mathcal{B}|^{2}}
possibilities for
(\mathcal{P}^{i},\mathcal{F}^{i})
, and thus
2^{2^{2\cdot|\mathcal{B}|^{2}}}
possibilities for the
\mathcal{L}^{k}
. Because
\mathcal{C}
is formed from a subset of the
\mathcal{L}^{k}
, plus one extra vertex, the bound follows.

The observant reader of
[
5
]
may notice that Theorem 10.8 forms part of the proof of Theorem 10.2, and also contains a construction of a digraph
\langle\mathcal{C},\overset{{}_{\mathcal{C}}}{\to}\rangle
to cover a particular case of Theorem 10.2. This does not ruin the above bound, though, because the digraph constructed in the proof of Theorem 10.8 is even smaller. Going through the construction in the proof of Theorem 10.8, and taking easy bounds at every step, shows
|\mathcal{C}|\leq|\mathcal{A}|+2^{|\mathcal{B}|}\leq|\mathcal{B}|+2^{|\mathcal{B}|}
.

With this improvement to Theorem 10.2 in mind, one can make a similar change to Theorem 11.2: not only is
\langle\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}},\sigma\rangle
polarized, but for any given partition
\mathcal{A}
, there is a partition
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
witnessing polarization for
\mathcal{A}
with
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\leq 1+2^{2^{2\cdot|\mathcal{V}|^{2}}}
.

Having explicit bounds like these, rather than mere existence statements, does not much affect the proof of the main result of
[
5
]
.
It is relevant here, however, because it means that the statement “the virtual refinement
(\phi,\langle\mathcal{V},\to\rangle)
is polarized” can be expressed not with an unbounded quantification over all partitions of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, but rather only by quantifying over partitions of a certain specific size. And roughly speaking, unbounded quantification over all partitions is second-order in the language of dynamical systems, but bounded quantification over partitions of a certain size is first-order.

Specifically, note that for any given
n
, “there is a partition of size
n
with some first-order property” is first-order expressible in the language of dynamical systems. Thus “there is a partition of size
\leq N
with some first-order property” is also first-order expressible. In light of the previous paragraph, this means that for every virtual refinement
(\phi,\langle\mathcal{V},\to\rangle)
of a partition
\mathcal{A}
, the statement “the virtual refinement
(\phi,\langle\mathcal{V},\to\rangle)
is polarized” can be expressed by a sentence of first-order logic, provided that “is polarized” is also first-order expressible.
And it is, because while this assertion involves a quantification over partitions, it is not difficult to check that two virtual refinements
(\phi,\langle\mathcal{V},\to\rangle)
and
(\psi,\langle\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\to^{\prime}\rangle)
are compatible if and only if there is a partition of size at most
|\mathcal{V}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}^{\prime}|
witnessing their compatibility.

Consequently, the statement that “every virtual refinement of every partition of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
is polarized” is expressible as a scheme of first-order sentences (one sentence for every virtual refinement). If

is elementarily equivalent to

Because each particular sentence in this scheme is sufficiently absolute (see the proof of Theorem 11.3 in

]
), it follows that every virtual refinement of every partition of
\mathbb{A}
is polarized.
By Theorem 9.7 in
[

has a solution.
∎

The proof of Theorem 12.3 in

relies on the Lifting Lemma, Lemma 6.2. If we substitute the use of Lemma 6.2 in that proof with the previous claim instead (which strengthens Lemma 6.2), then we get a strengthening of Theorem 12.3, namely:

implies that if

This domino falls into others.
The proof of Theorem 12.4 in

elementarily equivalent to

is an elementary substructure.
(Recall that, generally speaking, a model can have a substructure satisfying the same theory but failing to be an elementary substructure. But apparently this cannot happen with

In like manner, the proof of Theorem 12.5 in

relies on Theorem 12.4, and if we substitute the stronger version from the previous paragraph, we get a strengthening of Theorem 12.5, namely:

It would be desirable to find a direct proof that

Theorem 4.16
.

If

is an almost permutation of
{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
is countably saturated if and only if
\mathrm{Z}(f)\neq s_{{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
, we may replace

with any equivalent almost permutation without changing the structure
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
.
Thus, applying Lemma
2.10
, let us assume
f=\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)
.

First suppose that
\mathrm{Z}(f)\neq s_{{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
Because
f=\mathrm{R}(f)\oplus\mathrm{Z}(f)\oplus\mathrm{S}(f)
, it is clear that
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
is the direct product of a rotary automorphism
\mathfrak{A}_{\mathrm{R}(f)}
and finitely many copies of
\mathfrak{A}_{\sigma}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{{\sigma}^{-1}}
. (Recall that a single
{\mathbb{Z}}
-like orbit is equivalent to the sum of an
{\mathbb{N}}
-like orbit and a reverse

-like orbit.)
Since the direct product of finitely many countably saturated structures (naturally, of the same language) is countably saturated, by Proposition
4.15

Next suppose that
\mathrm{Z}(f)=s_{{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
This means that

is a definable substructure of
\mathfrak{A}_{f}

to

5.
The theory of a trivial automorphism

Results of the previous section show that potential isomorphism between dynamical systems associated with trivial automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
reduces to elementary equivalence under
\mathsf{CH}
. This raises the question of what properties of such
\alpha
are first order. In this section we provide some partial answers to this question.
We shall be abusing the language and writing “the theory of an automorphism
\alpha
” instead of “the theory of the dynamical system
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
”.
Following
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
, or
[
17
]
, given an automorphism
\alpha:\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}\to\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
and a partition of unity
\mathcal{A}
for
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
, we can represent the action of
\alpha
on
\mathcal{A}
as a directed graph. The edge relation for this graph is the “hitting relation”

a\overset{{}_{\alpha}}{\longrightarrow}b\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\alpha(a)\wedge b\neq[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}.

Such a digraph is said to be
represented
in
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
.
This definition and the following lemma apply to automorphisms that are not necessarily trivial.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
\alpha
and
\beta
are two automorphisms of
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.

(1)

\ThE(\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha})\subseteq\ThE(\mathfrak{A}_{\beta})
if and only if every digraph represented in
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
is represented in
\mathfrak{A}_{\beta}
.

(2)

\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{\beta}
have the same existential theory if and only if the same digraphs are represented in
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{\beta}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the first part.
A digraph being represented in
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
by some partition
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
can be expressed by an existential sentence:

\exists a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
such that
a_{i}\wedge a_{j}=[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
whenever
i\neq j
,
\bigvee_{i\leq n}a_{i}=[{\mathbb{N}}]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
, and
\alpha(a_{i})\wedge a_{j}\neq[\emptyset]_{\mathrm{Fin}}
if and only if
a_{i}\overset{{}_{\alpha}}{\longrightarrow}a_{j}
for all
i,j\leq n
.

It therefore suffices to prove the converse implication. Fix an existential sentence
\exists x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\ \varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
, where
\varphi
is quantifier-free, in the laguage of dynamical systems. Assume that this sentence is satisfied in
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
by some
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
.
Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the (finite) partition of unity generated by
\alpha^{i}(b_{j})
for all
j\leq n
and
i
such that
\alpha^{i}(x_{j})
occurs in
\varphi
. Consider the digraph associated with
{\mathcal{A}}
. For each
j\leq n
let
{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
be the set of pieces in
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
b_{j}=\bigvee{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
. Assume that this digraph is represented in some
\mathfrak{A}_{\beta}
, by a partition
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
, and let
f\colon{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
be a digraph isomorphism. Then the elements
c_{j}=\bigvee f[{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{j}]
for
j\leq n
satisfy
\varphi
in
\mathfrak{A}_{\beta}
.
∎

In analogy with almost permutations, let us say a function
f:{\omega}\to{\omega}
is an
almost surjection
if
{\omega}\setminus\mathrm{image}(f)
is finite.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\bar{m}=\left\langle m_{j}\colon j\in{\omega}\right\rangle
and
\bar{n}=\left\langle n_{j}\colon j\in{\omega}\right\rangle
be sequences in
{\mathbb{N}}
, and suppose there is a finite-to-one almost surjection

such that

\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}}
embeds in
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}

\mathrm{Th}_{\exists}(\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}})\subseteq\mathrm{Th}_{\exists}(\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}})

Proof.

Fix
\bar{m}
,
\bar{n}
, and
f
as described.
Let
I_{k}=\big[\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}m_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\big)
for all
k
, so that
I_{0},I_{1},I_{2},\dots
is the sequence of intervals defined for
\bar{m}
as in Definition
2.8
, and let
J_{k}=\big[\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}n_{j},\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}\big)
for all
k
, so that
J_{0},J_{1},J_{2},\dots
is the sequence of intervals defined for
\bar{n}
as in Definition
2.8
.
Our condition on
f
asserts that for some
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if
k\geq k_{0}
then there is some
d_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
|J_{k}|=d_{k}|I_{f(k)}|
.

Define a partial function

as follows:
\mathrm{dom}(e)=\bigcup_{k\geq k_{0}}J_{k}
, and

e(j)\,=\,\min I_{f(k)}+j^{\prime}\ \ \text{ when $j\in J_{k}$ and $0\leq j^{\prime}<|I_{f(k)}|$ with $j=j^{\prime}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ |I_{f(k)}|)$}.

In other words, if we think of the

and

as cyclically ordered, then

is simply the function that takes each

and wraps it precisely

times around

.
Observe that

is finite-to-one, because

is: the preimage of a point in

has size

.
Observe also that
\mathrm{dom}(e)
is a cofinite subset of
{\mathbb{N}}
.

Define a function
\eta:\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}\to\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}

\eta([A]_{\mathrm{Fin}})\,=\,\big[e^{-1}[A]\big]_{\mathrm{Fin}}

for all
A{\,\subseteq\,}{\mathbb{N}}
.
Note that
\eta
is well-defined because
e
is finite-to-one.
Because function pullbacks preserve unions and intersections,
\eta
preserves the Boolean-algebraic operations on
\nicefrac{{\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{N}})}}{{\mathrm{Fin}}}
.
Furthermore, because the domain of

is cofinite in

then

; i.e.,

is injective. Hence

is a Boolean-algebraic embedding

.

Because the permutation
f_{\bar{m}}
cyclically permutes the
I_{k}
, and likewise
f_{\bar{n}}
cyclically permutes the

e\circ f_{\bar{n}}(i)=f_{\bar{m}}\circ e(i)
for all

. From this, and our definition of
\alpha_{\bar{m}}
and
\alpha_{\bar{n}}
, it follows that
\eta\circ\alpha_{\bar{n}}=\alpha_{\bar{m}}\circ\eta
.

Thus
\eta
is an embedding of
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}}
into
\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}}
.
The second assertion of the lemma, that
\mathrm{Th}_{\exists}(\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{m}})\subseteq\mathrm{Th}_{\exists}(\mathfrak{A}_{\bar{n}})
, follows immediately from this, because existential statements are upwards absolute.
∎
