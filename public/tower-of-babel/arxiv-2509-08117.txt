Online Learning and Coverage of Unknown Fields Using Random-Feature Gaussian Processes
Ruijie Du, Ruoyu Lin, Yanning Shen 1, Magnus Egerstedt

Abstract
This paper proposes a framework for multi-robot systems to perform simultaneous learning and coverage of the domain of interest characterized by an unknown and potentially time-varying density function. To overcome the limitations of Gaussian Process (GP) regression, we employ Random Feature GP (RFGP) and its online variant (O-RFGP) that enables online and incremental inference. By integrating these with Voronoi-based coverage control and Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) sampling strategy, a team of robots can adaptively focus on important regions while refining the learned spatial field for efficient coverage. Under mild assumptions, we provide theoretical guarantees and evaluate the framework through simulations in time-invariant scenarios. Furthermore, its effectiveness in time-varying settings is demonstrated through additional simulations and a physical experiment.



I
Introduction

Environmental exploration and monitoring play a vital role in applications ranging from disaster response to ecological preservation, where multi-robot systems provide significant advantages due to their resilience
and effectiveness in data collection and coverage across large domains, e.g.,
[
1
]
. To guide the deployment of robots, a common approach to characterize the relative importance or informativeness of the environment is through a density distribution function across the domain of interest, i.e., a spatial field. For example, in wildfire monitoring or fighting
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, the density function can represent the heat distribution
or fuel accumulation, while in sensor network deployment
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
,
it can encode the spatial concentration of target-relevant information.

However, in many real-world situations like search and rescue, such a density distribution is not available a priori. This introduces a fundamental challenge: robots must perform environmental exploration to learn the underlying spatial field, rather than simply reacting to a pre-defined map. To address this challenge, recent approaches leverage Bayesian inference to learn such unknown spatial fields while simultaneously deploying robots to efficiently cover it in a way that more robots are concentrated in areas with higher importance characterized by density values, e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. This creates a dual process of exploration and exploitation, in which cooperative robots gradually improve their knowledge of the environment through learning the unknown density while simultaneously covering relatively more important or informative areas.

Bayesian inference typically employs the
Gaussian Process (GP)
as a probabilistic surrogate that learns unknown functions with uncertainty quantification, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. Combined with the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) sampling technique, e.g.,
[
14
]
, Voronoi-based coverage control
[
15
,
16
]
and GP can be coupled to enable the estimation of an unknown density function from noisy measurements and the coordinated deployment of robots to achieve an optimal coverage over the learned spatial field., e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
However, the methods proposed in
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
for multi-robot learning and coverage of unknown spatial fields are based on the assumption that the density function is time-invariant, which limits their applicability in dynamic scenarios such as wildfire monitoring, where the density function is time-varying.

Moreover, the applicability of GP-based methods is limited by the cubic complexity in the number of training data
[
12
]
, especially with the requirement of real-time implementations. Although decentralized algorithm design can alleviate the computational burden related to the number of agents, e.g.,
[
10
]
, it does not address the fundamental issue of the cubic complexity introduced by GP, which primarily results from the size of the training data that is typically much larger than the number of agents.

To handle this fundamental challenge of GP, recent work explored the use of random feature approximations, e.g.,
[
13
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Random Feature Gaussian Process (RFGP)
is an approximation method that enables scalable inference in GP. Instead of directly computing the kernel matrix, RFGP approximates the GP kernel using random Fourier features, e.g.,
[
20
,
21
]
, transforming the problem into a finite-dimensional regression. RFGP brings significant improvements in the computational cost with acceptable or even almost no performance loss. However, RFGP still requires all collected measurements to be associated with outputs, which limits its applicability in learning time-varying density functions.

Online RFGP (O-RFGP)
is a scalable approach that allows
training data to arrive ‘on the fly’ and updates the learning results
accordingly, e.g.,
[
19
,
21
]
. By leveraging the random Fourier features
[
19
,
21
]
, O-RFGP is able to approximate the GP kernel in a computationally efficient manner and avoid the need to store historical
training data through an iterative update manner. O-RFGP updates incrementally as new training data
become available, making it potential for learning time-varying density functions in real-time.

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

A simultaneous learning and coverage algorithm
for efficient environmental exploration and monitoring of unknown spatial fields
based on the RFGP and O-RFGP combined with Voronoi-based coverage control and UCB sampling technique;

•

Theoretical justifications of the performance of the proposed algorithm under mild
assumptions in the time-invariant setting, demonstrating asymptotic no-regret with respect to the coverage obtained with a known density function; and

•

Evaluation of the performance
of the proposed algorithm in simulations for both time-invariant and time-varying settings, as well as a physical experiment in time-varying scenario demonstrating the effectiveness of the algorithm.

II
Problem Formulation

Consider a group of
n
planar robots operating in a convex domain of interest
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The position of robot
i\in N\triangleq\{1,\dots,n\}
is denoted as
{x}^{i}\in\mathcal{D}
, and the positions of all robots are grouped into a matrix
\mathbf{x}\triangleq\left[{x}^{1},\cdots,{x}^{n}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
.
The problem of coverage control involves designing strategies for a group of robots to achieve optimal coverage of
\mathcal{D}
associated with a density distribution. Specifically, the density distribution function,
f(x):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, encodes the relative importance or informativeness of a location
x\in\mathcal{D}
in the sense that the higher the value of
f(x)
, the more important or informative the location
x
is. Typically, the subdomain, or region of dominance, that robot
i
is in charge of is given by the Voronoi cell, e.g.,
[
16
]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{i}

\displaystyle\triangleq\big\{{q}\in\mathcal{D}:\lVert{q}-{x}^{i}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert{q}-{x}^{j}\rVert_{2},\,\,\forall j\neq i\in N\big\}.

(1)

When the density function
f
is known, an optimal coverage can be asymptotically achieved by each robot
i
following the gradient flow of the locational cost function

\displaystyle\ell(\mathbf{x},f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{x}\in\mathcal{V}^{i}}\!\lVert{x}-{x}^{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}f({x})\,d{x}

(2)

with respect to
x^{i}
, e.g.,
[
16
]
, resulting in a centroidal Voronoi tessellation (CVT)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{i}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{V}^{i}}\!xf({x})\,d{x}}{\int_{\mathcal{V}^{i}}\!f({x})\,d{x}},\,\,\forall i\in N,

(3)

where
\mathbf{c}^{i}
is the centroid (i.e., center of mass) of
\mathcal{V}^{i}
with respect to the density function
f
. We denote the CVT by the matrix
\mathbf{c}\triangleq\left[\mathbf{c}^{1},\cdots,\mathbf{c}^{n}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
.

However, in practice, as discussed in Section
I
, the density function
f
can be unknown a priori. Then,
the group of robots needs to achieve an optimal coverage of
a surrogate density function learned from their measurements. Specifically, at position
{x}^{i}
, robot
i
takes the measurement

\displaystyle y^{i}=f({x}^{i})+\epsilon,

(4)

where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. As a robot typically takes independent measurements
at each time using the same sensor, the measurement noise
\epsilon
in (
4
) is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) across time and space, e.g.,
[
8
]
.

Let time be indexed by
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, and all variables related to time
t
be affixed with a subscript
(\cdot)_{t}
. For example,
\mathbf{x}_{t}\triangleq\left[{x}_{t}^{1},\cdots,{x}_{t}^{n}\right]^{\top}
denotes the locations of all the
n
robots at time
t
. As each robot takes a measurement
y^{i}_{t}
at time
t
, all measurements at time
t
are grouped in a vector
\mathbf{y}_{t}\triangleq\left[y^{1}_{t},\cdots,y^{n}_{t}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We use the subscript
(\cdot)_{:t}
to indicate the variables with the range of time steps from the beginning to
t
. For example,
the positions of all the
n
robots from
t=1
to
t=T

are grouped into the matrix
\mathbf{x}_{:T}\in\mathbb{R}^{nT\times 2}
and their corresponding measurements
are denoted as the vector
\mathbf{y}_{:T}\in\mathbb{R}^{nT}
. Let
f_{t}({x})
denote the time-varying density value at location
{x}
at time
t
, whereas
f({x})
represents the time-invariant density function at location
{x}
whose value remains the same over time.

As the true density function is unknown, the robots need to simultaneously learn and update the surrogate density function
\hat{f}_{t}
at each time step based on collected training data, i.e., measurements of all robots, and follow a control law to efficiently cover the domain associated with
\hat{f}_{t}
. The centroid of
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{i}
with respect to
\hat{f}_{t}
becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{i}_{t}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{V}_{t}^{i}}{x}\hat{f}_{t}({x})\,d{x}}{\int_{\mathcal{V}_{t}^{i}}\hat{f}_{t}({x})\,d{x}},

(5)

and the control law for the team of robots is given by

[
16
]

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\kappa(\mathbf{c}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}),

(6)

where

\mathbf{c}_{t}\triangleq\left[\mathbf{c}^{1}_{t},\cdots,\mathbf{c}^{n}_{t}\right]^{\top}
, and

is a control gain.

III
Density Learning

In this section, we discuss the density learning methods in detail. The key idea is to model the unknown density function

as the realization of a GP, specified by a mean function,
\mu(\cdot):\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and a covariance (kernel) function,

\sigma^{2}({x})=k({x},{x})
. We assume that the structures of
\mu
and

are characterized by certain hyperparameters

and
\tau^{*}

\displaystyle f(\cdot)\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu(\cdot;\rho^{*}),k(\cdot,\cdot;\tau^{*})).

For time-varying density, (

\displaystyle f_{t}(\cdot)\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu(\cdot;\rho_{t}^{*}),k(\cdot,\cdot;\tau_{t}^{*})),

(8)

where
\rho^{*}_{t}
and
\tau_{t}^{*}
are hyperparameters at
t

To learn an unknown function

that has a GP prior,
the noisy measurements

at positions

are leveraged. Then, the learned density value

at location

at time

can be represented by

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{t}({x})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_{t}({x}),k_{t}({x},{x})),

where

and

are mean and covariance functions calculated based on

approach in Section

III-B

Random Feature Gaussian Process (RFGP)

Random feature Gaussian Process (RFGP) provides an efficient way to scale GP by approximating the kernel using random features. It is more suitable for applications requiring GPs but constrained by computational resources, such as physical robotics implementations in real-time.

We define the real-valued
2D
-dimensional random feature (RF) vector as

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathbf{v}}({x})\triangleq

(12)

\displaystyle~~~\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}[\sin(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}{x}),\cos(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}{x})\dots,\sin(\mathbf{v}_{D}^{\top}{x}),\cos(\mathbf{v}_{D}^{\top}{x})]^{\top}.

And we define
\phi_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq[\phi_{\mathbf{v}}({x^{1}}),\cdots,\phi_{\mathbf{v}}({x^{n}})]
.
A low-rank approximation of
\mathbf{K}_{t}
can be achieved by using the standardized shift-invariant
\bar{k}

[
19
]

\displaystyle\bar{k}({x},{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}e^{j\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}({x}-{x}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x})\phi_{\mathbf{v}}({x}^{\prime}),

and
k=\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\bar{k}
, where
\sigma^{2}_{\theta}
is the magnitude of
k
.
A low-rank (
2D
) approximation of
\mathbf{K}^{t}

\bar{\mathbf{K}}^{t}
, is shown as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}{\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}^{\top},

(13)

where
\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}\triangleq[\phi_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\cdots,\phi_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})]^{\top}
.
Then, the
\mu^{\text{RF}}
and
k^{\text{RF}}
at time step

can be calculated by

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{\text{RF}}({x})

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x})\left({\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}+\frac{\tau_{\text{RF}}\mathbf{I}_{2D}}{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{:t},

(14)

\displaystyle k_{t}^{\text{RF}}({x},{x})

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x})\left(\frac{{\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}{\tau_{\text{RF}}}+\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2D}}{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\phi_{\mathbf{v}}({x}).

(15)

Let
\mathbf{\theta}_{t}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}
be

\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}+\frac{\tau_{\text{RF}}\mathbf{I}_{2D}}{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{:t},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{{\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}}{\tau_{\text{RF}}}+\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2D}}{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}\right)^{-1},

(17)

\mu_{t}^{\text{RF}}
and
k_{t}^{\text{RF}}
at time step

can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{\text{RF}}({x})

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x})\mathbf{\theta}_{t},

(18)

\displaystyle k_{t}^{\text{RF}}({x},{x})

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\phi_{\mathbf{v}}({x}).

(19)

Then,
\mathbf{\theta}_{t}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}
can be considered as the parameters of RFGP. For example, if

, ideally,
\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x})\mathbf{\theta}^{*}=\rho^{\top}{x}
, where
\mathbf{\theta}^{*}
denotes the optimal model parameters. As such,
RFGP hugely reduces computational complexity and memory cost by using a fixed dimension RF vector to approximate the kernel, i.e., at time

\mathcal{O}(TD)
and the computational complexity is

is much smaller than

as summarized in Table

III-C

Online Random Feature Gaussian Process (O-RFGP)

The RF-based function approximant
\mu^{\text{RF}}
and
k^{\text{RF}}
readily accommodates online operation
[
19
]
, which is necessary for many time-critical applications, such as simultaneous learning and covering time-varying density functions.
The online approach allows interactive learning, i.e., the prediction at time step
t
receives
\mathbf{x}_{t}
at the beginning of slot
t
and then updates after receiving measurements
at the end of slot
t
. Specifically, the online learning procedure is
shown as follows:

1.

Predict mean and covariance at location
\mathbf{x}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\mu^{\text{O}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{\theta}_{t-1},

(20)

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=k^{\text{O}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}\phi_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}).

(21)

2.

Upon receiving
\mathbf{y}_{t}
, update
\mathbf{\theta}_{t}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\theta}_{t-1}+\sigma_{t}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\phi_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})(\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mu^{\text{O}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})),

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}-\sigma_{t}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}\phi_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}.

(23)

The O-RFGP can significantly save data storage and further reduce the computational complexity in the sense that at each time step, the computational complexity and the memory cost are both
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
, as summarized in Table
I
. This iterative update mechanism enables O-RFGP to adapt to time-varying function learning by relying solely on the current measurements. Meanwhile, the low computational complexity of O-RFGP allows real-time robotic implementations.

IV
Learning-Aided Coverage Algorithm and Regret Analysis

In this section, first, we propose an algorithm that enables robots to simultaneously learn an unknown spatial field and efficiently cover the most recently acquired estimate of it. The algorithm, which integrates GP-based learning and Voronoi-based coverage control, combined with the UCB sampling strategy, guides the robots’ sensing and movement, incrementally driving them toward a CVT with respect to the evolving learned density function. Specifically, this is achieved by alternating between a prediction step, which allows the multi-robot team to select new points sampled, and a correction step, which allows it to update the learned density to be covered. VI
Conclusion

This paper puts forth ORC (Algorithm
1
), a framework that integrates O-RFGP and Voronoi-based coverage control to enable a team of robots to learn an unknown density function and achieve efficient spatial coverage. Under mild assumptions, we provide regret analysis to theoretically guarantee the performance of the proposed framework in time-invariant settings, whose effectiveness is demonstrated through simulations. This framework also shows promise in efficient learning and coverage of unknown time-varying spatial fields, as demonstrated in additional simulations and a physical experiment. As a general framework, ORC can be extended with alternative sampling strategies to further improve performance in complex and dynamic environments.

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}\leq\sqrt{c_{1}(n+1)T\beta_{T}\gamma_{T}}.

Lemma D.1

\displaystyle R_{\text{pred}}(T)

\displaystyle~~~-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\mu({x};\mathbf{\theta}^{*});f({x}_{t}))

(48)

where

To establish the regret bound for O-RFGP, we also need the following lemma.

Lemma D.2
.

Under Assumptions
4
, for a fixed
\epsilon_{2}>0
, the following bound holds with probability at least

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{v}}({x}_{t})\mathbf{\theta}^{*};f({x}_{t}))

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{\lVert\mathbf{\theta}^{*}\rVert^{2}}{2\sigma_{\theta}}+D\log(1+\frac{T\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{2D}).

Proof.

Defining the cumulative loss over T slots with a fixed
\mathbf{\theta}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{\theta}}\triangleq-\log(f(\mathbf{x}_{:T})|\mathbf{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{:T})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x}_{t}))\mathbf{\theta};f({x}_{t})),

(50)

the expected cumulative loss over
q(\mathbf{\theta})
, a pdf of the fixed strategy
\mathbf{\theta}
, can be defined as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{q_{\mathbf{\theta}}}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{\theta}}]=\int_{\mathbf{\theta}}q(\mathbf{\theta})\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{\theta}}d\mathbf{\theta}.

(51)

Based on Bayes rule, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\mu({x};\mathbf{\theta});f({x}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\log(f({x}_{t})|f({x}_{t-1});\mathbf{x}_{:t})

\displaystyle=-\log p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t});\mathbf{x}_{:t}).

(52)

Employing the definition of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\mu({x};\mathbf{\theta});f({x}))-\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{q_{\mathbf{\theta}}}=\int_{\mathbf{\theta}}q(\mathbf{\theta})\log\frac{p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t})|\mathbf{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{:t})}{p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t});\mathbf{x}_{:t})}d\mathbf{\theta}.

(53)

Since
p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t}),\mathbf{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{:t})=p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t})|\mathbf{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{:t})p(\mathbf{\theta})=p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t});\mathbf{x}_{:t})p(\mathbf{\theta}|f(\mathbf{x}_{:t});\mathbf{x}_{:t})
, the RHS of (
53
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{\theta}}q(\mathbf{\theta})\log\frac{p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t})|\mathbf{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{:t})}{p(f(\mathbf{x}_{:t});\mathbf{x}_{:t})}d\mathbf{\theta}

\displaystyle~~=\int_{\mathbf{\theta}}q(\mathbf{\theta})\log\frac{p(\mathbf{\theta}|f(\mathbf{x}_{:t});\mathbf{x}_{:t})}{p(\mathbf{\theta})}d\mathbf{\theta}

\displaystyle~~=\int_{\mathbf{\theta}}q(\mathbf{\theta})\log\frac{q(\mathbf{\theta})}{p(\mathbf{\theta})}d\mathbf{\theta}-\int_{\mathbf{\theta}}q(\mathbf{\theta})\log\frac{q(\mathbf{\theta})}{p(\mathbf{\theta}|f(\mathbf{x}_{:t});\mathbf{x}_{:t})}d\mathbf{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq KL(q(\mathbf{\theta})||p(\mathbf{\theta})).

Hence, it holds for any

(
Lemma 2
of
[

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\mu({x};\mathbf{\theta});f({x}))\leq\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{q_{\mathbf{\theta}}}+KL(q(\mathbf{\theta})||p(\mathbf{\theta})).

(54)

Let

\displaystyle~=2D\log\sigma_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\theta}}(\lVert\mathbf{\theta}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2D\xi^{2})-2D-2D\log\xi.

(55)

Let

and
z_{t}^{*}=\phi_{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}({x}_{t})\mathbf{\theta}^{*}
. Taking Taylor’s expansion of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(z_{t};f({x}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(z^{*}_{t};f(f{x}))+\frac{d\mathcal{L}(z^{*}_{t};f({x}))}{dz_{t}}(z_{t}-z^{*}_{t})

\displaystyle~~+\frac{d^{2}}{dz_{t}^{2}}\mathcal{L}(h(z_{t});f({x}))\frac{(z_{t}-z^{*}_{t})^{2}}{2},

(56)

where
h(z_{t})
is some function lying between
z_{t}
and
z^{*}_{t}
. Taking the expectation of (

) with respect to
q(\mathbf{\theta})
leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(z^{*}_{t};f({x}))+\frac{d\mathcal{L}(z^{*}_{t};f({x}))}{dz_{t}}\times 0

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dz_{t}^{2}}\mathcal{L}(h(z_{t});f({x}))\frac{(z_{t}-z^{*}_{t})^{2}}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\mathcal{L}(z^{*}_{t};f({x}))+c_{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(z_{t}-z^{*}_{t})^{2}}{2}\right]

(57)

where

Combining with (

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\mu({x};\mathbf{\theta});f({x}))\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{\theta}^{*}}+\frac{Tc_{4}\xi^{2}}{2}+2D\log\sigma_{\theta}

\displaystyle~~~~~+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\theta}}(\lVert\mathbf{\theta}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2D\xi^{2})-2D-2D\log\xi.

Theorem D.3

provides the upper bound of prediction for O-RFGP:

while
\mathcal{L}
is Bayesian loss. Assuming the loss of optimal estimator
\hat{f}^{*}
is relatively negligible,
\mathcal{L}(\hat{f}^{*}({x}_{t});f({x}_{t}))\approx 0
, we can have
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(\mu({x};\mathbf{\theta});f({x}))=\mathcal{O}(\log T)
. Then,
\mathcal{L}(\mu_{t}({x});f({x}))
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mu_{t}({x}_{t});f({x}_{t}))

\displaystyle=-\log p(f({x}_{t};\mathbf{\theta})|\mu_{t}({x}_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{(f({x}_{t})-\mu_{t}({x}_{t}))^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{t}^{2}).

Since
\sigma_{t}^{2}
is bounded due to the bounded kernel, the cumulative squared error also scales as
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
:
by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}|f({x}_{t})-\mu_{t}({x}_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}(f({x}_{t})-\mu_{t}({x}_{t}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T\log T}).

Since the cumulative absolute error is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T\log T})
, the per-step error is

\displaystyle\left|f({x})-\mu_{t}({x})\right|=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{T}}).

Although it converges slower than
\mathcal{O}({\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}})
, it is still fast enough for the requirement in Lemma

.
