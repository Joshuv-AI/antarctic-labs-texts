Early-onset type 1 diabetes mellitus in a child with a pathogenic CTLA4 variant complicated by generalized lipodystrophy and severe insulin resistance: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yu Ding, Qun Li, Qianwen Zhang, Shiyang Gao, Juan Li, Lingwen Ying, Ru-En Yao, Tingting Yu, Xiumin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe co-occurrence of early-onset type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), acquired generalized lipodystrophy (AGL), and severe insulin resistance (SIR) is extremely rare, poses significant therapeutic challenges and no cases associated with pathogenic Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated 4 (CTLA4) variants have been previously documented.Case DescriptionWe performed genetic testing, flow cytometric immunophenotyping, leptin measurement and a 4.5-year clinical follow-up in a 3-year-3-month-old boy with early-onset T1DM, insulin allergy following initial insulin therapy, progressive lipodystrophy, SIR and mild hepatic dysfunction. We additionally conducted a systematic literature review to summarize CTLA4 variant-related autoimmune endocrine disorders and fat metabolism abnormalities. Pathological biopsy of subcutaneous adipose tissue revealed scattered lymphocytes and histiocytes infiltrating the fat septa and perivascular areas. A heterozygous pathogenic c.151C>T (p.Arg51*) variant in the CTLA4 gene was identified by whole-exome sequencing (WES) analysis. His father and sister carried the same variant with incomplete penetrance. The patient showed markedly reduced CTLA4 expression on regulatory T cells and undetectable serum leptin. Treatment with abatacept normalized liver function but did not improve glycemic control or insulin resistance. During the follow-up, the patient’s growth consistently exceeded the 97th percentile for age and gender.ConclusionsThis report established the first association between a pathogenic CTLA4 variant and AGL, which expands the known clinical phenotypic spectrum of CTLA4-related immune dysregulation disorders. CTLA4 genetic testing should be considered in patients presenting with early-onset T1DM and unexplained lipodystrophy to enable early diagnosis and guide personalized management.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is a common childhood diabetes caused by genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and immune-mediated destruction of pancreatic β-cells (1). Early-onset T1DM (onset <2 years) is frequently associated with germline pathogenic variants in immune dysregulation genes (2). Generalized lipodystrophy (GL) is a rare disorder characterized by generalized adipose tissue loss, accompanied by severe insulin resistance (SIR), metabolic disturbances, and multi-organ damage. It has both genetic (most commonly AGPAT2 and BSCL2 variants) and acquired etiologies (3). The co-occurrence of T1DM, GL, and SIR is extremely rare, posing substantial challenges to etiological diagnosis and precision therapy.

Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated 4 (CTLA4) is a key immune regulator predominantly expressed on activated T cells. It inhibits excessive T-cell activation by binding B7 molecules to maintain immune homeostasis (4). Pathogenic variants in the CTLA4 gene cause a heterogeneous syndrome of immune dysregulation, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, and lymphoproliferation (5,6). To date, no cases of T1DM, GL, and SIR occurring concurrently in patients with CTLA4 variants have been reported in the literature.

Traditional diagnostic approaches often fail to identify the underlying cause in children with early-onset T1DM and rare comorbidities, whereas genetic testing enables precision diagnosis. We present a pediatric patient with early-onset T1DM who subsequently developed progressive lipodystrophy, SIR, and liver dysfunction. Genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic CTLA4 variant, and partial therapeutic response was achieved with tailored treatment. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of CTLA4-related disorders and provides preliminary clinical insights for the diagnosis and management of similar rare presentations. Case presentation

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center (No. SCMCIRB-K2025193-1). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s parents for publication of this case report and accompanying images. Clinical description (Figure 1)

A 3-year-3-month-old boy presented with T1DM complicated by progressive lipodystrophy. At approximately 1 year of age, he developed polyuria, polydipsia, and weight loss, accompanied by subtle subcutaneous fat loss that was unrecognized by his parents (Figure 1A,1B). At 1 year and 6 months of age, he was admitted to a local hospital due to altered mental status. Laboratory evaluation confirmed diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): random blood glucose 26.7 mmol/L, pH 7.12, serum ketones 5.6 mmol/L, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) 12.4%. Fasting insulin (<2.00 µIU/mL) and C-peptide (<0.10 ng/mL) were markedly reduced, with weakly positive anti-insulin autoantibodies. He was diagnosed with T1DM and initiated on subcutaneous insulin therapy (insulin aspart + insulin glargine), achieving glycemic control of 6–12 mmol/L.

At 1 year and 7 months of age (1 month after the initial diagnosis), he developed generalized urticaria consistent with insulin allergy (Figure 1C). The treatment was switched to insulin glulisine combined with insulin degludec, and the dose was gradually increased. The rash gradually subsided, but the subcutaneous lipodystrophy continued to worsen. At 2 years and 2 months of age, body composition analysis showed severely reduced body fat mass (1.0 kg; normal 2.1–4.3 kg). Liver enzymes were elevated, and abdominal ultrasound revealed mild hepatosplenomegaly. Skin and subcutaneous tissue biopsy (posterior shoulder/back region) showed dermal fibrous hyperplasia with mild perivascular and periadnexal lymphocytic infiltrate; subcutaneous fat distribution is unremarkable, with scattered lymphocytes and histiocytes within fat septa and perivascular stroma. Treatment with cyclophosphamide pulse therapy (350 mg) resulted in severe drug-induced liver injury [alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 583 IU/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 233 IU/L], which resolved after discontinuation and hepatoprotective therapy.

At 3 years and 3 months of age, he was transferred to our center due to refractory hyperglycemia despite high-dose insulin (5.1 U/kg/day). Oral glucose tolerance test confirmed SIR with persistently undetectable C-peptide (<0.01 ng/mL) and high insulin level (>300.0 µIU/mL). Diabetes autoantibodies [insulin autoantibody (IAA), glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA)] were elevated. Lipid profile showed dyslipidemia: total cholesterol 5.31 mmol/L, triglycerides 2.01 mmol/L, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) 0.80 mmol/L, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) 3.60 mmol/L. Immunoglobulin A (IgA) and complement C4 were elevated, while other immunoglobulins and complements were normal. Autoimmune hepatitis antibodies and antinuclear antibody panel were negative. Echocardiography was normal.

Physical examination on first admission to our hospital: Diffuse subcutaneous fat loss with multiple buttock folds (Figure 1D), axillary acanthosis nigricans. Height and weight were both above the 97th percentile for age and gender, with body mass index (BMI) in the 50th–85th percentile range. No hepatosplenomegaly or lymphadenopathy was detected.

The patient was born via full-term spontaneous vaginal delivery with a birth weight of 4,000 g and birth length of 50 cm, with no history of neonatal resuscitation or asphyxia. Family history was notable for his father, who had asymptomatic cervical lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly on imaging; his mother and elder sister were healthy.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and data analysis

WES was performed on genomic DNA from the patient using the SureSelectXT Human All Exon Kit v6 (Agilent Technologies) and HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina). Single nucleotide variant (SNV) data were preprocessed with NextGENe® software and analyzed via Ingenuity® Variant Analysis™. Candidate variants were validated by Sanger sequencing in the patient and his family members (parents and elder sister).

WES identified a heterozygous CTLA4 c.151C>T (p.Arg51*) nonsense variant (NM_005214.4). This variant has been previously reported to cause immune dysregulation (7). Sanger sequencing validation confirmed that the patient’s father and elder sister also carried the same heterozygous variant (Figure 2A). According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) variant classification criteria, this variant is classified as likely pathogenic. This pedigree demonstrates the variable penetrance and phenotypic heterogeneity characteristic of CTLA4-related disorders (Figure 2B).

Cellular immunological assays and leptin measurement

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from the patient and his family members underwent flow cytometric immunophenotyping for CD3, CD4, CD25, FOXP3, and CTLA4. After fixation and permeabilization with the BD Foxp3 Staining Buffer Kit, cells were stained with FOXP3-PE and CTLA4-BV786. Data were acquired on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo v10.8.

CD4+ T cell subsets and CTLA4 expression were analyzed as shown in Figure 2C. The frequency of CD25+ FOXP3+ canonical regulatory T (Treg) cells among peripheral blood CD4+ T cells in the patient was 8.64%, higher than that in his elder sister (5.14%), father (6.95%), and mother (5.90%). The patient also had a slightly elevated proportion of CD25+ FOXP3− effector T cells (10.4%) relative to other family members, indicating dysregulated CD4+ T cell homeostasis. Notably, CTLA4 positivity in the patient’s CD4+ T cells was only 4.4%, markedly lower than levels in his sister (28.1%), father (35.2%) and mother (23.2%), confirming a severe defect of this core negative immune regulator.

Serum leptin was measured by ELISA (Human Leptin Quantikine Kit, R&D Systems) in the proband and 5 age- and sex-matched healthy children. The patient’s serum leptin was undetectable (healthy reference range: 1.6–2.0 ng/mL), consistent with the clinical features of lipodystrophy (Figure 2D).

Metformin (250 mg twice daily) was added to high-dose insulin for SIR, but glycemic control remained suboptimal and liver enzymes were persistently elevated. Given the underlying immune dysregulation, abatacept (87.5 mg weekly subcutaneously) was initiated after parental informed consent. After 4 months of therapy, liver function had normalized, but no improvement was observed in glycemic control or lipodystrophy, and abatacept was discontinued. At 5 years and 10 months of age (Figure 1E), pioglitazone (15 mg daily) was added to metformin, resulting in transient glycemic improvement but persistent HbA1c elevation. Metreleptin replacement was recommended for GL and undetectable serum leptin, but was declined by the parents for financial and personal reasons. At the last follow-up (7 years 9 months of age), the patient’s HbA1c was 9.5%, ALT was mildly elevated, and lipid profile was normal. Throughout follow-up, he maintained normal neurodevelopment and age-appropriate growth, with height consistently above the 97th percentile for age and gender (Figure 1F).

Literature review

We performed a systematic literature search in PubMed, EMBASE and Web of Science (from database inception to April 2026) using keywords including “CTLA4”, “cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated 4”, “CTLA-4”, “immune dysregulation”, “endocrine autoimmunity” and “lipodystrophy”. Autoimmune endocrine diseases (most commonly T1DM) are frequent manifestations of CTLA4 haploinsufficiency, whereas no AGL associated with pathogenic CTLA4 variants has been previously reported.

Discussion

CTLA4 encodes a critical immune checkpoint molecule that maintains immune homeostasis by negatively regulating T-cell activation. Pathogenic variants can impair its ligand-binding capacity and lower the T-cell activation threshold (8). Polymorphisms in CTLA4 are well-established risk factors for T1DM. Multiple studies, including a systematic review and meta-analysis of childhood T1DM, have confirmed that the CTLA4 +49A/G polymorphism significantly increases disease risk (8-10).

Notably, the patient’s father and elder sister both carry the same pathogenic CTLA4 variant but exhibit incomplete penetrance: the father has only asymptomatic cervical lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly, while the sister is entirely unaffected. This observation is consistent with previous literature: Schwab et al. reported that only 67.6% (90/133) of CTLA4 mutation carriers develop clinical manifestations (11). These findings highlight the importance of considering incomplete penetrance in genetic counseling and suggest that additional genetic or environmental modifiers are required for the development of overt disease, including T1DM.

Lipodystrophy syndromes comprise a heterogeneous group of rare disorders characterized by partial or complete loss of adipose tissue, resulting in abnormal body habitus, SIR, steatohepatitis, and hypertriglyceridemia. Acquired GL (AGL) is broadly classified into three subtypes: panniculitis-associated, autoimmune-associated, and idiopathic (12). Histopathologically, our case with septal/perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltration without overt fat necrosis is consistent with autoimmune-associated AGL, and distinct from panniculitis-type (extensive fat necrosis) and idiopathic (minimal inflammation) subtypes. Well-documented autoimmune comorbidities of AGL include juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Sjögren’s syndrome, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and immune thrombocytopenia (13). Based on the integrated analysis of genetic, histopathological, and clinical features in this case, we hypothesize that the pathogenic CTLA4 variant drives an autoimmune response targeting adipose tissue, leading to the development of autoimmune-associated AGL. Biopsy-confirmed septal and perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltration injures adipocytes, causing generalized fat loss and secondary leptin deficiency.

The co-occurrence of T1DM and AGL is extremely rare, with only three pediatric cases reported worldwide to date (14,15). This combined disorder is characterized by the paradoxical coexistence of absolute insulin deficiency and SIR, creating unique therapeutic challenges. All prior cases had onset at 6–11 years of age with variable onset sequences. Park et al. described two patients: one developed T1DM at 8 years followed by generalized lipoatrophy at 11 years, and the other had simultaneous onset at 6 years. Both required extremely high insulin doses (5 and 32 U/kg/day) and developed hypertriglyceridemia, elevated liver enzymes, and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis. Kumar et al. reported an 8-year-old boy with progressive lipodystrophy who developed GAD-positive T1DM at 10 years. Despite high-dose insulin (5.7 U/kg/day) combined with metformin and acarbose, he had persistent poor glycemic control (peak HbA1c 14.4%) and developed severe hypertriglyceridemia, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and retroperitoneal fibrosis. Notably, none of these prior cases carried confirmed pathogenic variants in immune regulatory genes. In contrast, our patient presented with DKA at 18 months of age, followed by lipodystrophy months later. This significantly earlier onset suggests CTLA4 variant-mediated immune dysregulation as a potential driver. Furthermore, our patient developed insulin allergy, a previously unreported complication in AGL-T1DM, potentially linked to Treg dysfunction secondary to CTLA4 haploinsufficiency. CTLA4 haploinsufficiency disrupts peripheral immune tolerance and activates polyclonal autoreactive T cells; thus, the concurrent involvement of the islets and adipose tissue likely represents parallel target-organ damage under the same autoimmune setting.

Abatacept is a CTLA4-Ig fusion protein that competitively inhibits CD28-mediated T-cell co-stimulation by binding CD80/CD86 on antigen-presenting cells, thereby suppressing excessive T-cell activation and partially compensating for endogenous CTLA4 deficiency (16,17). It is well-established as effective for CTLA4 haploinsufficiency, rapidly improving multi-organ autoimmunity, lymphocytic infiltration, and inflammatory symptoms, even in cases refractory to conventional immunosuppressants (18,19). Abatacept also shows promise in the treatment of newly diagnosed T1DM. It reduces circulating memory CD4+ T cells and follicular helper T cells, thereby decreasing autoantibody production (e.g., anti-insulin antibodies). Clinical trials have shown that abatacept attenuates the decline in stimulated C-peptide secretion and delays β-cell function deterioration in newly diagnosed T1DM patients, although it fails to induce long-term immune tolerance (20-22). Based on these considerations, the patient in this study received abatacept for 4 months. Liver function normalized during treatment, indicating that immunomodulatory therapy is effective for ameliorating hepatic inflammation. However, no significant improvement was observed in glycemic control or insulin resistance. This discrepancy may be attributed to the inability of immunomodulation alone to reverse irreversible pancreatic β-cell exhaustion and adipose tissue damage. This differential effect guides clinical use: abatacept targets active autoimmune injury, with no benefit for established metabolic sequelae.

Lipodystrophy impairs adipose tissue lipid storage capacity, leading to ectopic lipid deposition in the liver and skeletal muscle. This lipotoxicity disrupts insulin signaling, constituting the primary mechanism of insulin resistance (23). Ectopic lipid metabolites further trigger low-grade inflammation and alter adipokine secretion, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates insulin resistance (24). In our patient, SIR was confirmed by high insulin requirements (up to 5.5 U/kg/day), elevated insulin levels during oral glucose tolerance test, and acanthosis nigricans. Management of T1DM complicated by AGL and SIR remains challenging, with conventional glucose-lowering agents showing limited efficacy. High-dose insulin combined with metformin is the cornerstone of treatment, but glycemic control remains poor in most patients. Thiazolidinediones (e.g., pioglitazone) partially improve insulin sensitivity and reduce HbA1c and liver enzymes, but may exacerbate hypertriglyceridemia and worsen hepatic steatosis in patients with complete lipoatrophy, necessitating cautious use (25,26). In our patient with mild dyslipidemia, metformin-pioglitazone combination therapy resulted in a modest reduction in insulin dose but failed to achieve optimal glycemic control.

Metreleptin, a recombinant human leptin, is Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for the treatment of GL and significantly improves insulin resistance and metabolic parameters (27). A prospective study by Park et al. (14) showed that 1 year of leptin therapy in patients with T1DM and AGL reduced insulin requirements from 3.3–5 to 1 U/kg/day, improved HbA1c from 10.6–10.7% to 7.3–8.4%, and substantially ameliorated hypertriglyceridemia and liver dysfunction, with sustained benefits confirmed at 5-year follow-up. Our patient met the criteria for leptin therapy, but parental refusal prevented evaluation of this potentially effective treatment.

CTLA4 haploinsufficiency, caused by pathogenic CTLA4 variants, typically presents with infections, autoimmunity, lymphoproliferation, and increased cancer risk. Our patient presented with autoimmune T1DM and hepatomegaly, both well-established manifestations of this disorder. Notably, AGL has not been previously reported in patients with CTLA4 deficiency. Conclusions

We report a rare case of early-onset autoimmune T1DM with AGL, SIR, and mild liver function impairment, harboring a pathogenic heterozygous CTLA4 variant. CTLA4 haploinsufficiency-related immune dysregulation likely underlies concurrent T1DM and AGL. Diagnosis requires integration of clinical, laboratory, and genetic evaluation. Importantly, CTLA4 variants should be considered in the genetic testing for patients with T1DM and unexplained AGL. Abatacept normalized liver function but failed to improve glycemic control or insulin resistance in this patient. Metreleptin or other targeted metabolic therapies may correct underlying metabolic defects. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of CTLA4-related disorders and provides preliminary diagnostic and management insights for similar rare cases. However, further studies are warranted to elucidate its long-term prognosis and optimize therapeutic regimens.
