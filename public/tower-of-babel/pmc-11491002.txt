Clinical characteristics of HIV-associated tracheobronchial Talaromyces marneffei infection in seven patients in Guangxi, China
Authors: Mianluan Pan, Hongjie Wu, Hairong Lin, Zhimin Peng, Jie Huang, Jianquan Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTracheobronchial Talaromyces marneffei (T. marneffei) infections among HIV-infected patients are rare. To improve understanding, we analyzed the clinical features, immune mechanisms, treatment, and prognosis of these patients.MethodsWe collected clinical information from HIV-positive patients with talaromycosis admitted to the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning from January 2015 to June 2022. Patients who presented with culture and/or histopathological proof of tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection were included.ResultsA total of 108 patients with respiratory infections who underwent bronchoscopy were enrolled. Seven patients with tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection, all of whom were men with a median age of 48 years (range 39–50 years), were analyzed. Cough, sputum, fever, and weight loss were the most common symptoms. The total white blood cell count was normal or decreased, and all lymphocyte counts were decreased. All patients had reduced CD4+ T-cell counts, with values less than 50 cells/µL. The chest CT imaging signs included patchy signals or large areas of exudation, bronchial stenosis and occlusion. This was different from the lack of bronchial involvement. Endoscopically, the trachea and bronchial mucosa showed congestion, edema, surface attachment, nodules, lumen stenosis, obstruction, etc. T. marneffei spores were found via bronchial mucosal pathology in all 7 patients. Five patients were initially treated with intravenous infusion of amphotericin B for 2 weeks, followed by oral itraconazole capsules (200 mg) twice daily, and two patients were initially treated with itraconazole. Six patients were remission, and 1 died.ConclusionsThe clinical features of trachea invasion and nontracheal invasion are not unique, but chest CT reveals manifestations such as masses, solid shadows, and bronchial stenosis/obstruction. Bronchoscopy should be performed if possible, and the presence or absence of trachea T. marneffei infection should be confirmed. Antiviral therapy combined with antifungal therapy can improve patient prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10046-3.Keywords: Talaromyces marneffei, Tracheobronchial, Bronchoscopy, Human immunodeficiency virus

Introduction

Talaromycosis is a rare invasive mycosis caused by Talaromyces marneffei. In Southeast Asia, T. marneffei is the third most opportunistic infection related to AIDS after tuberculosis and Cryptococcus [1], with the main risk factor being CD4 + T cells < 100 cells/µL [2]. In the Guangdong and Guangxi regions, 18.8% and 16%, respectively, of hospitalized AIDS patients with CD4 + T lymphocyte counts less than 200 cells/µL were infected with T. marneffei [3]. For a long time, the ability of hospitals to provide specialized diagnosis and treatment of T. marneffei infection in AIDS patients has been limited by many factors, such as instruments and equipment. There is less information about tracheoscopy findings, and more, but limited information about endoscopic biopsy pathology, which has greatly affected the diagnosis and treatment of tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection in AIDS patients. Disseminated T. marneffei infection disseminates in a hematogenous manner or via the lymphatic system throughout the body, involving the respiratory system, blood, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, skin, etc. The respiratory system is one of the earliest and most common systems involved [4], and the lungs are often affected. However, there are few reports of tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection [5–8], which can cause the serious consequence of tracheal obstruction. Therefore, to remind clinicians to pay more attention to the characteristics of T. marneffei infection in the trachea and provide more references and suggestions for related clinical diagnosis and treatment, we report seven cases of HIV-positive hosts diagnosed with T. marneffei infection involving the trachea and bronchi through bronchoscopy, tissue pathology, and/or culture.

Materials and methods

Study population

We reviewed the medical records of 1567 HIV/AIDS patients with T. marneffei infection admitted to the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning from January 1, 2015, to June 30, 2022. Among these patients, 108 patients who underwent chest computed tomography (CT) had pathological changes, and bronchoscopy was performed; ultimately, 7 patients with tracheobronchial T. marneffei infections were selected for inclusion in this study. In this article, we describe the clinical and laboratory features, imaging examinations, treatments, and prognoses of these seven patients. In addition, we compared the main characteristics and outcomes of patients with respiratory system T. marneffei infection who had bronchial involvement with those of patients who did not.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) HIV infection; (2) tracheal and/or bronchial (segmental and above) lesions based on bronchoscopy and respiratory specimens of patients with a culture-confirmed or histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of talaromycosis; and (3) age ≥ 18 years. Patients with incomplete clinical data, laboratory results or therapy information were excluded from the analyses.

This study was approved by the HIV/AIDS Clinical Treatment Center of Guangxi (Nanning) and The Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning. All patients provided written informed consent. The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All the authors vouched for the completeness and accuracy of the data and for the fidelity of the study to the protocol.

Methods used to diagnose T. marneffei infection

To diagnose T. marneffei infection, microbiological or pathological findings identified from clinical samples consistent with any of the following manifestations were utilized: (a) isolation of pathogens from culture; (b) visible detection of fungi (intracellular yeast-like or sausage-shaped cells measuring 2–3 mm in diameter with transverse septa) via microscopy after periodic acid–Schiff or Wright staining.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a descriptive analysis of demographic and clinical characteristics via R software (version 4.2.2). Continuous variables are summarized as the means with standard errors (SEs), whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies. To evaluate the significance of the associations between each demographic and clinical variable and T. marneffei, we employed analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Rao‒Scott chi-square test.

Results

Demographic data and clinical characteristics

The seven patients were all men, with the median age of 48 years (range 39–50 years), and it was 41 years (35, 50) of the patients without bronchial involvement. The time from the onset of clinical symptoms to the diagnosis of T. marneffei infection was 30–50 days (median: 45 days). The comorbidities included pulmonary tuberculosis (n = 2); 1 patient was complicated with tuberculous pleurisy and intestinal tuberculosis, and one patient underwent partial intestinal resection due to intestinal perforation. The most common clinical sign of tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection was cough, and four patients each had sputum, fever and weight loss. There were 3 cases of fatigue, 3 cases of rash, 2 cases of sore throat, 2 cases of poor appetite, and 1 case each of chest pain, shortness of breath, nausea, skin pruritus, anemia, hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy. In contrast, patients without tracheobronchial involvement presented with fever (90.1%), cough (65.3%), expectoration (57.4%), fatigue (52.5%), poor appetite (44.6%) and shortness of breath (32.8%). However, there was no significant difference in symptoms between the two groups. Chest CT of tracheobronchial T. marneffei-infected patients revealed that all patients had pulmonary lesions. There were 6 cases of patchy increased-density shadows (Fig. 1A), 3 cases of pleural thickening and consolidation shadows (Fig. 1B), and 2 cases of bronchial stenosis/occlusion. There were also nodular shadows, hilar enlargement, bronchial wall thickening, mass shadows (Fig. 1C), hilar and mediastinal lymph node enlargement, Et cetera. However, chest CT of patients without tracheobronchial involvement revealed patchy increased-density shadows (72.2%), pleural effusion (42.6%), nodular shadows (14.9%) and hilar and mediastinal lymph node enlargement (14.9%). Patients who had tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection more typically presented with solid shadows and bronchial stenosis/occlusion. Congestion, edema and nodules were observed in the trachea via left and right bronchoscopy. Among them, there were 5 cases of mucosal congestion, edema, and thickening, 4 cases of mucosal surface attachments (Fig. 1D), 3 cases of nodule (Fig. 1E) masses in the bronchial lumen, 3 cases of purulent secretions, 2 cases of uneven mucosal surfaces (Fig. 1F), and 2 cases of bronchial stenosis. Unlike patients with T. marneffei infection with trachea invasion, most patients without trachea invasion were endoscopically normal, whereas a minority presented with bronchial mucosal edema and viscous secretions. The common features of bronchial mucosa histopathology are focal coagulation necrosis, proliferation of histiocytes in the lamina propria, fungal spores in the cytoplasm, and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) and D-PAS staining positivity. All patients underwent mucosal specimen culture and/or pathological diagnosis through airway biopsy. T. marneffei spores were detected via bronchial mucosal pathology in 7 patients, and positive results were detected via BALF culture in 5 patients, blood culture in 3 patients, sputum culture in 2 patients, and a pharyngeal swab in 1 patient. Laboratory examination results

The total white blood cell count was normal in 4 patients and decreased in 3 patients, and the lowest white blood cell count was 2.0 × 109/L. All lymphocyte counts were decreased. Both hemoglobin and serum albumin levels decreased, whereas globulin levels increased in 4 patients. Five patients (5/7) had a positive serum fungal β-glucan test (G test), three patients (3/5) had a positive serum Aspergillus galactomannan antigen test (GM test), and four patients had elevated ferritin. In addition, all patients had a decrease in the CD4+ T-cell count to less than 50 cells/µL. Three patients had a decrease in the CD8 + T-cell count (Table 2).

Treatments and outcomes

Among the 7 patients, 4 had received antiretroviral therapy (ART) treatment before anti-T. marneffei treatment, and 3 had received ART treatment simultaneously with antifungal therapy. Five patients were initially treated with intravenous infusion of amphotericin B for 2 weeks, followed by oral itraconazole capsules (200 mg) twice daily, and two patients were initially treated with 0.2 g of itraconazole twice daily. The course of treatment was 12 months (range 12–16 months). Four patients were cured, 2 improved, and 1 (14.3%) died of respiratory failure. A follow-up bronchoscopy examination of the cured patients revealed that everything had returned to normal and that there were no complications, such as stenosis. All patients without tracheobronchial involvement received antifungal treatment; 21 patients (20.8%) died, and 80 patients (79.2%) achieved remission.

Discussion

Among infectious diseases, fungal infection has become one of the greatest threats to public health. On October 25, 2021, the World Health Organization (WHO) issued the first list of major fungal pathogens (FPPLs) for invasive fungal diseases (IFDSs). The relevant factors included antifungal resistance, mortality, evidence-based treatment, availability of diagnostic methods, annual morbidity, complications and sequelae of T. marneffei. The WHO listed T. marneffei as having a medium response priority among the 19 fungi on the list; this resource is designed to appeal to governments and researchers to make significant efforts to focus on and promote further research and policy interventions [9]. Talaromycosis is one of the most harmful fungal infections in Southeast Asia and southern China, especially in Guangxi, Guangdong and Hong Kong. T. marneffei is the third most common pathogen causing opportunistic infection in AIDS patients in Southeast Asia, after Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Cryptococcus.

Nittayananta et al. [9] suggested that the inhalation of T. marneffei conidia may be the main route of transmission. The respiratory system is one of the earliest and most commonly involved systems, and pulmonary involvement is also common. Tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection of HIV-positive hosts has rarely been reported, although these parts of the respiratory tract come into contact with T. marneffei spores before the lungs do. The possible cause of this infection is contact with infected hosts; owing to the epidemic prevention requirements of various countries, patients with talaromycosis often visit specialized infectious disease hospitals and obtain highly positive results through blood culture, whereas clinicians often fail to check the trachea and bronchus. Therefore, understanding the clinical features of airway involvement by T. marneffei is important for the prevention and treatment of talaromycosis.

A total of 108 AIDS patients infected with T. marneffei during the past 7 years were examined via bronchoscopy in an infectious disease hospital in Guangxi because of severe lung lesions, and airway-bronchial involvement was found in 7 patients (0.4%). These findings suggest that this type of occult clinical feature is worthy of clinical attention. The median time from onset to diagnosis was 45 days, reflecting both the lack of awareness and the challenge of early diagnosis. The common clinical manifestations were cough, expectoration, fever and emaciation, followed by fatigue, rash and anorexia, as well as lymphadenopathy, papules with central necrosis, and hepatosplenomegaly. The most common clinical signs without tracheobronchial involvement were fever, cough, expectoration, fatigue, poor appetite and shortness of breath. All patients had a normal or reduced total white blood cell count, decreased lymphocyte counts, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, and increased globulin; however, the CD4 + T lymphocyte count was significantly lower, and the CD4 + T lymphocyte count was less than 50 cells/µl. CD4 + T lymphocytes less than 100 cells/µL in HIV-positive hosts are a major risk factor for T. marneffei infection [10]. In a retrospective study of 1,079 patients with HIV-associated talaromycosis, 91.9% had CD4 + T lymphocyte counts less than 50 cells/µL [11]. In the late stage of AIDS, the number of CD4 + T cells in the body is reduced, and the preexisting impairment of cell-mediated immune responses that occurs in patients with AIDS results in severely reduced fungicidal activity, which diminishes the capacity of host phagocytes to eradicate this pathogen. Under these conditions, T. marneffei can multiply and spread throughout the body via the lymphatic system and bloodstream.

A recent study indicated that 72.5% of patients tested positive for serum galactomannan [11]. In this study, 71.4% of the G test indices and 42.9% of the GM test indices increased, suggesting that the G test and GM test results may be used as screening tests for T. marneffei infection in HIV-positive hosts. Patients without tracheobronchial involvement presented with patchy increased-density shadows, pleural effusion, nodular shadows and hilar and mediastinal lymph node enlargement. However, patients with tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection typically present with solid shadows and bronchial stenosis/occlusion. Therefore, bronchial stenosis/occlusion might be crucial for confirming tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection. Endoscopically, the trachea and bronchial mucosa show congestion, edema, surface attachment, nodules, lumen stenosis, obstruction, etc. Therefore, these conditions are difficult to differentiate from other pulmonary infections, such as pulmonary tuberculosis, pulmonary cryptococcosis, aspergillosis, and even lung cancer and airway tumors. Zong reported a case of HIV-positive disseminated T. marneffei infection with cough, fever and emaciation, which was misdiagnosed as pulmonary tuberculosis, and the diagnosis of T. marneffei infection was confirmed by histopathology [12]. The histopathological features of T. marneffei infection include focal coagulative necrosis and hyperplasia of histiocytes in the lamina propria of the mucosa, and fungal spores can be observed in the cytoplasm. Typical features of the septa can be clearly observed via PAS or d-PAS. It can be distinguished from granulomas, caseous necrosis and lymphocyte infiltration caused by tuberculosis infection. Negative acid‒fast staining can further exclude tuberculosis. A lack of heterologous cells could rule out tumors. Moreover, BALF can be cultured, or metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) can be performed, improving the value of differential diagnosis. Because AIDS is often associated with other opportunistic infections, such as tuberculosis, Pneumocystis pneumonia, and cryptococcosis, bronchoscopy may be necessary to obtain lung samples.

At present, the diagnosis of T. marneffei infection is still based on the gold standard of identifying the pathogen in culture. The positive rate of blood culture is extremely high, and it is easy to diagnose T. marneffei infection [13]. In the clinic, bronchoscopy and tissue biopsy are often not performed, which may lead to a missed diagnosis of tracheobronchial infection. In our research, T. marneffei was isolated from BALF cultures from 5 patients, from blood cultures from 3 patients, and from sputum cultures from 2 patients with bronchial involvement. Therefore, not all blood cultures are positive, and respiratory specimens are an important diagnostic criterion. Therefore, in patients with respiratory symptoms, especially those with bronchial stenosis/occlusion on chest CT, bronchoscopy should be performed whenever possible, and bronchoalveolar lavage and/or tissue biopsy should be performed to determine whether there is airway involvement.

For people with HIV-positive T. marneffei infection, the guidelines recommend starting treatment with amphotericin B 0.6–1.0 mg kg/d for 2 weeks, or itraconazole 600 mg/d for 3 days, followed by 400 mg/d for 11 days, a sequential itraconazole of 400 mg/d for 10 weeks, followed by itraconazole 200 mg/d until the CD4 + T lymphocyte count is > 100 cells/µl for at least 6 months, or a lifetime of itraconazole [14]. In our research, all patients received antifungal therapy, following the guidelines for HIV-positive T. marneffei infection, and were treated with amphotericin B for 2 weeks followed by sequential itraconazole therapy. Two patients received direct itraconazole therapy, and both improved. However, 1 patient died during the study period, with a mortality rate of 14.3%. In addition, patients without tracheobronchial involvement had a mortality rate of 20.8%. These findings suggest that respiratory symptom infection affects the prognosis of T. marneffei infection and may be one of the causes of high mortality. It has been suggested that the use of systemic antifungal therapy alone is not sufficient in Talaromycosis marneffei patients with tracheobronchial involvement. The use of nebulized inhalation or intratracheal instillation of amphotericin B in the treatment of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis has been reported in the literature [15]. Therefore, for HIV-positive hosts with tracheobronchial T. marneffei infection, early local administration of antifungals may reduce mortality and is worth exploring.

In 2011, a multicenter randomized controlled study from China assessed the optimal timing for initiating retrovirus therapy in patients with HIV-associated T. marneffei. Compared with the delayed ART group (median time from antifungal treatment to initiation of ART was 21 days), the early ART group (median time from antifungal therapy to initiation of ART was 11 days) had a significant reduction in mortality at 48 weeks [16]. In our study, 4 patients received antiretroviral therapy (ART) before anti-T. marneffei treatment, and their symptoms improved. Therefore, early anti-infective therapy and HAART can improve the prognosis of patients with T. marneffei infection.

This was a single-center retrospective study with a small number of included cases, which is a shortcoming of this study. More clinicians need to pay attention to the particularities of T. marneffei-infected airways and accumulate experience to cope with the clinical prevention and treatment of AIDS patients with talaromycosis.

Conclusions

In summary, T. marneffei involvement of the airway system should be considered an important clinical feature of talaromycosis marneffei. When the chest CT findings are bronchostenosis/occlusion and consolidation, T. marneffei infection should be considered, and further bronchoscopy should be recommended.
