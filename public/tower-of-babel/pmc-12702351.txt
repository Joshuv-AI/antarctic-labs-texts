Bifunctional 3D-printed PEEK scaffolds incorporating hydrogel-encapsulated magnesium-quinone nanosheets for antibacterial activity and mandibular bone regeneration
Authors: Qiao Su, Tianying Luo, Meng Pan, Jun Liu, Hui Yang, Xulin Hu, Zhiyong Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Infectious mandibular bone defects present significant clinical challenges due to the complex pathological microenvironment, risk of persistent bacterial colonization, and insufficient bone regeneration. Poly (ether ketone) (PEEK) is considered a promising alternative to metallic implants owing to its bone-mimetic mechanical properties; however, its intrinsic hydrophobicity and bioinertness hinder osseointegration and functional recovery. Here, a bifunctional 3D-printed porous PEEK scaffold coated with an alginate hydrogel encapsulating magnesium-aloe emodin (MgAe) nanosheets (AP@MgAe) was developed to achieve simultaneous antibacterial and osteogenic functions. MgAe nanosheets were synthesized via metal-quinone coordination-driven self-assembly, enabling the co-delivery of bioactive Mg2+ and aloe emodin in a pH-responsive and sustained-release manner within the hydrogel coating. In vitro, AP@MgAe scaffolds exhibited potent antibacterial activity against S. aureus, excellent cytocompatibility, and enhanced osteogenic differentiation of rat bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). In vivo, AP@MgAe scaffolds effectively suppressed infection, attenuated inflammation, and promoted bone formation and osseointegration in a rat model of infectious mandibular bone defects. Collectively, this work introduces a novel metal-quinone framework-integrated 3D-printed PEEK platform, offering a clinically promising, antibiotic-free, and bifunctional strategy for treating infection-associated bone defects.

Introduction

Infectious mandibular bone defects, most frequently caused by Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), remain a significant clinical challenge due to the combined threat of microbial colonization and impaired bone healing [1,2]. Left untreated, such infections can result in osteonecrosis and sequestrum formation. Current strategies seek to achieve both effective infection control and functional bone regeneration [3]. Autologous fibula-free flap (FFF) transplantation remains the gold standard because of its inherent osteoconductive and osteoinductive properties [4]. However, its application is restricted by donor site morbidity and limited graft availability. Although readily accessible, allografts and xenografts require stringent processing to reduce immunogenicity and the risk of disease transmission, and they often integrate suboptimally with host tissue. Titanium implants are another common alternative, offering excellent strength and biocompatibility. Yet, their high elastic modulus (102–110 GPa) can cause stress shielding and subsequent bone resorption, while their radiopacity complicates postoperative imaging [5,6].

Poly (ether ketone) (PEEK), a thermoplastic polymer approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), has emerged as a promising alternative to titanium implants for craniofacial reconstruction [7,8]. PEEK combines chemical resistance with radiolucency, and its elastic modulus (3–4 GPa) approaches that of human cortical bone (7–30 GPa) and trabecular bone (∼1 GPa), supporting favorable load-bearing compatibility [[9], [10], [11]]. Recently, fused deposition modeling (FDM)-based 3D printing technology has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional methods. It enables rapid fabrication of precisely designed and fully interconnected scaffolds that support cell proliferation and personalized repair of complex maxillofacial defects [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, the intrinsic hydrophobicity and bioinertness of unmodified PEEK limit cell adhesion and osseointegration [16]. Compared with composite melt blending strategies that may compromise the polymer structural integrity of PEEK, the use of bioactive surface coatings, particularly hydrogel-based layers, offers a safer and more effective approach to enhance bioactivity while maintaining the intrinsic mechanical strength of PEEK [[16], [17], [18]]. Hydrogels improve surface wettability, which facilitates protein adsorption and cell adhesion. Among various candidates, alginate-based hydrogels are widely studied due to their outstanding biocompatibility, FDA-approved safety for human applications [20], low cost, prolonged and localized delivery of active agents, and mild in situ ionic crosslinking capacity [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. These features make them highly suitable for biomedical surface modification.

Infection control remains a primary concern in mandibular defect management. Although systemic antibiotics and antimicrobial peptides are commonly employed, the rise of drug-resistant strains and the limitations of systemic delivery necessitate alternative strategies [26,27]. One promising strategy involves metal-quinone frameworks, which feature distinct coordination for responding more sensitively to stimuli [28]. Both quinones and metal ions, as the building blocks, possess inherent biological activities [[29], [30], [31]], enabling the self-assembled products to exert therapeutic effects without additional medications [32]. Aloe emodin (Ae), a natural anthraquinone derivative from Chinese herbs, exhibits antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic properties, but its clinical application is hindered by poor bioavailability and potential toxicity [33,34]. Magnesium ions (Mg2+), the fourth most abundant cation in the human body, participate in bone metabolism, immune modulation, and vascularization [35,36], though rapid release may induce local toxicity [37]. By assembling Ae and Mg2+ into nanoscale metal-quinone frameworks (MgAe), these issues can be mitigated through improved stability, sustained release, and synergistic bioactivity [38,39]. Despite their potential, the use of such coordination systems for bone regeneration remains at an early stage.

To address these challenges, recent research has focused on enhancing the biological performance of 3D-printed PEEK scaffolds through surface functionalization with bioactive molecules or ions [[40], [41], [42]]. Nevertheless, limitations persist regarding therapeutic loading efficiency, controlled release profiles, optimized bioactive design, and long-term biocompatibility. In this study, we engineered a bifunctional 3D-printed porous PEEK scaffold by integrating an alginate hydrogel encapsulating magnesium-aloe emodin (MgAe) nanosheets (AP@MgAe) through a facile post-printing surface modification strategy (Scheme 1). FDM-based fabrication allowed precise architectural control and patient-specific customization conformed to irregular bone defect morphologies, while the hydrogel coating ensured close biological integration with the surrounding host tissue. Moreover, MgAe nanosheets, synthesized via a one-step self-assembly process, exhibited enhanced stability and excellent therapeutic synergy while avoiding conventional antibiotics or complex synthetic procedures. Thus, this strategy introduces a new paradigm of bifunctional PEEK scaffolds that first incorporate metal-quinone coordination, integrating mechanical reliability, antibacterial defense, and osteogenesis potential, and holding strong promise for clinical translation in infection-related bone repair.

Fabrication and characterization of MgAe nanosheets

Bioactive MgAe nanosheets, based on a metal-quinone framework, were synthesized via self-assembly under alkaline conditions. Specifically, 5 mL of 3 mM MgCl2 was added to 25 mL of 0.5 mg mL−1 Ae and reacted at 45 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 5 mL of 0.5 M NaOH was introduced into the mixture, and the reduction reaction proceeded for an additional 8 h. The resulting dark purple precipitate was collected using an ultrafiltration spin column (MWCO = 3000 Da) by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 20 min to remove free ions, washed three times with deionized water, and vacuum-dried overnight at 60 °C. The purified product was stored at 4 °C under light-protected conditions for long-term preservation.

A 50 μL aliquot of MgAe suspension (500 μg mL−1) was dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to adsorb and air-dry for 10 min with filter paper. The morphology was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) (Talos F200S, FEI, USA). Particle size distributions were estimated from TEM micrographs using ImageJ. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, UK). Elemental composition was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with a Thermo Fisher K-ALPHA system (USA). The crystal structures were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). The surface states were further characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher, USA). Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra and fluorescence emission spectra were recorded using a U-2900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan) and an F-7000 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan), respectively. The quantitative analysis of Mg2+ was performed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 725, Agilent Technologies, USA). The UV–vis absorption at 440 nm was used to quantify the content of Ae in MgAe nanosheets via a standard curve.

Fabrication and characterization of PEEK (P), alginate hydrogel-coated PEEK (AP), and AP group loaded with MgAe nanosheets (AP@MgAe)

Porous PEEK scaffolds were fabricated using 1.75 mm filaments via FDM-based 3D printing. The scaffold prototype was designed using 3ds Max 2024 (Autodesk, Canada), then sliced and exported in G-code format using Cura 15.04.6 (Ultimaker, Netherlands). The processed data was subsequently imported into a 3D printer (3DTobe, China) to complete the printing process. The optimal printing parameters and porous dimensions were delineated through preliminary experiments. Scaffolds with varying porosities, designated as 50 % PEEK, 60 % PEEK, and 70 % PEEK, were successfully fabricated. Among these, the 60 % PEEK scaffolds exhibited superior printability and physicochemical properties, making them suitable for subsequent experiments. These scaffolds were denoted as the P group. For characterization analysis, as well as in vitro antibacterial and cellular experiments, 5 × 5 × 1.2 mm specimens were used, whereas 10 × 10 × 10 mm cubic samples were prepared for mechanical testing. Additionally, cylindrical scaffolds (Ø 5 × 1.2 mm) were fabricated for implantation in rat models of infectious mandibular defects.

The 60 % PEEK scaffolds (P group) were placed in molds matching their dimensions and immersed in a 2 % (w/v) sodium alginate aqueous solution until the entire porous structure was fully coated. The scaffolds were then crosslinked with 0.6 % (w/v) CaCl2 solution at room temperature for 2 h. Following freeze-drying for 24 h, the crosslinked hydrogel-coated PEEK scaffolds, designated as the AP group, were incubated with 30 μL of MgAe nanosheet solutions at varying concentrations (0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg mL−1) for 1.5 h to allow complete adsorption. The scaffolds were subsequently removed, lyophilized again, and stored at 4 °C under light-protected conditions, forming the corresponding AP@MgAe group.

The micromorphology and pore structure of the scaffolds under different printing conditions were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Apreo 2 S LoVac, Thermo Fisher, USA). Porosity was determined using the Archimedes method. A porous scaffold with an apparent volume of V1 was immersed in anhydrous ethanol, and the difference in liquid level before and after immersion was measured as V2. Porosity was calculated using Equation (1):(1)Porosity%=V1−V2V1×100%

Compression tests were performed using a universal mechanical testing machine (MTS Systems, America). Cubic porous scaffolds were tested along the Z-axis at room temperature (25 °C) following the national standard GB/T 1041–2008. The compression speed was set to 1 mm min−1, with a preloading force of 50 N applied to the scaffold positioned on the test bench. SEM and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford Instruments, UK) were used to examine surface morphology and elemental distribution. FTIR was employed to analyze functional groups. The surface hydrophilicity was evaluated using an optical contact angle measurement instrument (Theta Flow, Biolin Scientific, Sweden). The interfacial adhesion strength (kPa) between the alginate hydrogel and the PEEK scaffold was evaluated using the universal testing machine at a speed of 2 mm min−1.

To evaluate hydrogel long-term stability, the AP scaffolds were immersed in deionized water, PBS, α-MEM, and α-MEM +15 % (v/v) FBS at 37 °C for 7 days. Morphological changes in the hydrogel coating were observed and recorded to assess swelling or detachment.

For the swelling test, the freeze-dried AP scaffold was weighed (W1) and immersed in PBS at room temperature. At specific time intervals, the sample was removed, excess moisture was blotted off, and the sample was reweighed (W2). The swelling ratio and water content were calculated using Equations (2), (3), respectively:(2)Swellingratio%=W2−W1W1×100%(3)WaterContent%=W2−W1W2×100%

Release behavior

Release profiles of MgAe and Mg2+ from hydrogel-coated AP@MgAe scaffolds were evaluated under sink conditions. Freeze-dried AP@MgAe scaffolds (loaded with 30 μL of 4 mg mL−1 MgAe) were immersed in PBS (pH 7.4 or pH 5.5) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. For MgAe release, 1 mL PBS was used, and 0.2 mL of supernatant was collected at predetermined intervals (0–72 h), followed by equal-volume replenishment. The content was determined by measuring the absorbance at 440 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan Sky, Thermo Fisher, USA). Similarly, for Mg2+ release, the scaffolds were immersed in 10 mL PBS. Then, 3 mL aliquots were withdrawn at the same time points, and Mg2+ content was quantified by ICP mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), also with medium replenishment after each sampling.

To assess morphological changes after release under physiological and acidic conditions, MgAe nanosheets (20 μL, 4 mg mL−1) were incubated in 0.2 mL PBS at pH 7.4 or 5.5 for 24 h, followed by TEM observation.

Antibacterial performance in vitro

Gram-positive S. aureus and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli) were well cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C under aerobic conditions with constant shaking at 100 rpm. The growth medium was supplemented with tryptone (10 g/L), yeast extract (5 g/L), and NaCl (10 g/L).

Colony-forming unit (CFU) counting

The antibacterial activity of MgAe nanosheets and AP@MgAe scaffolds was assessed using the spread plate method. Briefly, a bacterial suspension (500 μL, 1 × 106 CFU mL−1) was co-incubated with the test materials at varying concentrations (MgAe nanosheets: 0, 5, 10, 25, and 50 μg mL−1; AP@MgAe scaffolds: 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg mL−1) for 24 h. Following incubation, 200 μL of bacterial supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate, and its optical density (OD) at 600 nm was measured. The remaining suspension was serially diluted 106-fold in LB broth. Subsequently, 50 μL of the diluted bacterial suspension was evenly spread onto LB agar plates for CFU counting. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the CFUs on each plate were recorded.

The bacteriostatic efficiency was calculated using Equation (4), where Nc represents the number of colonies in the control group (without test materials), and Ne represents the number of colonies in the experimental group:(4)Antibacterialrate%=Nc−NeNc×100%

Analysis of bacterial morphology

To further examine bacterial survival, the morphology of pathogenic bacteria incubated with MgAe nanosheets or AP@MgAe scaffolds was observed using SEM. For samples treated with MgAe nanosheets, the treated bacterial suspension was collected, centrifuged (5000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C), and fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, the bacterial pellet was sequentially dehydrated in graded ethanol solutions (30, 50, 70, 85, and 95 % v/v, 10 min each) and treated twice with 100 % ethanol. Finally, 5 μL of the sample was dropped onto a silicon wafer, dried via critical point drying, and imaged using SEM (Regulus 8100, Hitachi, Japan). Similarly, bio-transmission electron microscopy (bio-TEM, HT7800, Hitachi, Japan) was employed to examine bacterial morphologies. For samples co-cultured with AP@MgAe scaffolds, the removed scaffolds were also fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After rinsing with PBS to remove non-adherent or loosely attached bacteria, the samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol solutions. Following air drying and gold sputter-coating, the distribution and morphology of bacteria on different scaffolds were examined using SEM (Apreo 2 S LoVac, Thermo Fisher, USA).

Live/dead bacterial staining

Briefly, 500 μL of bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU mL−1) was incubated with MgAe nanosheets at varying concentrations for 24 h at 37 °C under shaking at 100 rpm. Following incubation, the suspension was centrifuged (5000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C), and the bacterial pellets were washed twice with 0.85 % (w/v) NaCl solution and resuspended in 0.5 mL of the same solution. Live/dead bacterial staining was performed using a bacterial viability assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions and visualized using inverted fluorescence microscopy (DMi8, Leica, Germany), where live bacteria appeared green and dead bacteria appeared red. The fluorescence intensity was semi-quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Cytocompatibility assessment

Rat bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were isolated using conventional methods. Briefly, 3-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were euthanized and soaked in 75 % ethanol for disinfection. The femurs were then carefully exposed and completely separated. The epiphyses at both ends of the femurs were excised using sterile surgical scissors to expose the marrow cavity, which was subsequently flushed with a syringe filled with culture medium to collect the cells. The cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove tissue debris and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The pelleted BMSCs were resuspended and cultured in a complete α-MEM medium supplemented with 15 % (v/v) FBS and 1 % (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin under 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. The culture medium was refreshed every 3 days, and passages 3–5 were used for subsequent experiments.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

BMSCs were seeded onto 96-well plates for cell proliferation assay using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, BMSCs (1 × 104 cells per well) were co-cultured with different concentrations (0–100 μg mL−1) of MgAe nanosheets. The culture medium for BMSCs was replaced with a mixture of CCK-8 reagent and fresh medium (1:10, v/v) after 1 day of incubation. The plates were then incubated in the dark for 2 h, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured. Similarly, the viability of BMSCs after 48 and 72 h of incubation with various concentrations (0–150 μg mL−1) of MgAe nanosheets was also evaluated using the CCK-8 assay.

Live/dead cell staining and SEM analysis

The biocompatibility of the scaffolds was evaluated by seeding cells (1 × 105 cells per well) onto sterile scaffolds placed in a 24-well plate. After incubation for 7 days, the scaffolds were stained using a live/dead assay according to standard protocols. Viable cells (green fluorescence) and non-viable cells (red fluorescence) were visualized using fluorescence microscopy. For SEM observation, these treated samples were fixed with 4 % PFA overnight, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, dried, and subsequently imaged at 1000 × magnification for cellular adhesion.

Osteogenic evaluation in vitro

BMSCs were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 onto sterile scaffolds in 12-well plates and incubated for 48 h. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with osteoinductive differentiation medium, consisting of a complete medium supplemented with 10 mM β-glycerol phosphate disodium salt pentahydrate, 100 nM dexamethasone, and 50 μg mL−1 L-ascorbic acid, and refreshed every 3 days.

ALP activity

ALP activity analysis and staining were performed to evaluate the effects of AP@MgAe on the early osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. BMSCs were co-cultured with different scaffolds in osteogenic medium for 5 and 7 days, followed by PBS washing at the designated time points. For quantitative analysis, cells were lysed using cell lysis buffer, and the lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected to assess ALP activity using a commercial assay kit following the manufacturer's instructions. Meanwhile, the total intracellular protein content was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. ALP activity was normalized to total protein levels to calculate relative ALP activity. For ALP staining, cells were fixed with 4 % PFA for 30 min at room temperature, stained with the BCIP/NBT ALP staining kit, and imaged using a light microscope (Leica, Germany).

ARS staining

ARS staining and semi-quantitative analysis were performed to assess late-stage calcium deposition. After 14 days of osteoinductive co-culture, BMSCs were washed with PBS and fixed with 4 % PFA for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then washed with deionized water, stained with ARS staining solution according to the manufacturer's instructions, and imaged. For semi-quantitative analysis, 1 mL of 10 % (m/v) cetylpyridinium chloride monohydrate was added to dissolve calcium nodules, and the absorbance of 200 μL of the extracted solution was measured at 562 nm.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis of osteogenesis-related genes

After co-culturing with different scaffolds for 7 and 14 days, the expression levels of osteogenesis-related genes in BMSCs, including bone morphogenetic protein 2 (Bmp2), type I collagen alpha 2 chain (Col1), osteopontin (Opn), and runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), were analyzed using qRT-PCR, with β-actin as the housekeeping gene. The primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from BMSCs cultured in the osteoinductive medium using TRIzol reagent, and RNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit, followed by qPCR amplification on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 PCR system (Thermo Fisher, USA). Relative gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

In vivo treatment of infectious mandibular defects

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (Approval Number: WCHSIRB-D-2024-739). Eight-week-old male SD rats (∼250 g) were obtained from HFK (China) and housed in a clean animal facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to sterile food and water. After one week of acclimatization, 40 rats were anesthetized using isoflurane gas via a small-animal general anesthesia machine from RWD (China) and underwent surgical procedures on their right mandibles following local hair removal. By identifying the right mandibular angle, a parallel incision (∼2 cm in length) was made along its edge. The skin and subcutaneous tissues were incised layer by layer, followed by blunt dissection to separate the muscle tissue and retract the periosteum, exposing most of the mandibular bone. A trephine drill was then used to create a critical-sized full-thickness bone defect (Ø = 5 mm) under continuous irrigation with pre-cooled sterile saline. To induce bacterial infection, an absorbable gelatin sponge soaked with 20 μL of S. aureus suspension (108 CFU mL−1) was implanted into the defect. The surgical site was then sutured, and the rats were placed on a soft diet for 3 days postoperatively.

Two weeks after the initial surgery, the same incision site was reopened to re-expose the mandibular bone defect. All nonviable tissues were carefully removed, and thorough irrigation and drainage were performed to eliminate residual infection. During the second surgical procedure, sterile scaffolds (Ø 5 × 1.2 mm) were implanted into the defect site, and the skin was sutured. Rats continued on a soft diet for 3 days postoperatively. Rats with bacterial infections were randomly divided into four groups, each receiving a different treatment following thorough debridement: (1) control: without scaffold implantation in the infected area; (2) P: implanted with the P scaffold; (3) AP: implanted with the AP scaffold; (4) AP@MgAe: implanted with the AP scaffold loaded with 4 mg mL−1 MgAe nanosheets.

Antibacterial performance in vivo

Three days after the second surgery, infected tissues from the defect site were swabbed in 1 mL of sterile saline and homogenized. A 50 μL aliquot was then plated onto LB agar plates after appropriate dilution to determine bacterial colony counts and calculate the in vivo antibacterial rate. Simultaneously, a routine blood test was conducted and compared with the results obtained from infected rats two weeks after the initial surgery.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis

All rats were euthanized by an overdose of gas anesthesia at 6 and 12 weeks postoperatively. The right mandibular specimens containing the implanted scaffolds were collected, rinsed with PBS, and fixed in 4 % PFA. Micro-CT (μCT50, Scanco Medical AG, Bassersdorf, Switzerland) was performed to assess in vivo bone regeneration within the defect area. Scanning was conducted at an isotropic voxel size of 10 μm, using a 70 kVp energy setting and a 300 ms integration time. The original images were reconstructed into serial cross-sections, and histomorphometric parameters, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), were analyzed within the specified cylindrical volume of interest (VOI) using Scanco Medical Evaluation and Visualizer software.

Histological examination

To assess bone tissue regeneration, the fixed mandibular specimens were decalcified using a slow decalcification solution at room temperature for 3 weeks, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. The paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned using a microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany) and mounted onto glass slides. Subsequently, hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and Masson's trichrome staining were performed using standard histological techniques. In addition, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was conducted using anti-ALP (1:100) and anti-OCN (1:100) antibodies. The stained sections were imaged using a slide microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the semi-quantitative analysis was performed to assess ALP- and OCN-positive cells. To evaluate the local inflammatory response, IHC for IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β was performed on mandibular tissues, and semi-quantitative analysis was also performed.

In vivo biosafety

To assess the biosafety of the implanted scaffolds, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected from the rats at 12 weeks postoperatively for HE staining. Additionally, blood biochemical analysis was performed to further evaluate systemic toxicity and biocompatibility.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently conducted at least three times, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data visualization was performed using Origin 2024 (OriginLab, USA), followed by statistical analysis. For comparisons between the two groups, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was used. For multiple-group comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and structural characterization of MgAe nanosheets

The main challenge in treating infectious bone defects lies in initially correcting the infected microenvironment and subsequently promoting bone regeneration through effective drug delivery. Ae exhibits notable antibacterial properties, while Mg2+ has the potential to enhance neovascularization and bone regeneration. Furthermore, strategies involving metal-organic complexes have demonstrated superior synergistic effects on vascularized bone regeneration compared to simple Mg2+ ions alone [43,44]. To integrate their respective functionalities for repairing infectious bone defects, MgAe nanosheets composed of self-assembled metal Mg2+ and Ae were successfully fabricated via a one-step method in an alkaline environment. This assembly was likely driven by diverse supramolecular interactions [28]. Specifically, the carbonyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups of quinones serve as coordination sites, facilitating the formation of a metal quinone framework between Mg2+ and Ae. The nanosheets were uniformly dispersed in the solution, resulting in the formation of a purplish-red solution (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B and C, TEM images revealed that the MgAe metal-quinone framework exhibited relatively uniform sheet-like nanostructures with an average size of 148.21 ± 44.24 nm. In the full-range XPS survey (Fig. 1E), the presence of Mg, O, and C elements in the MgAe nanosheets was confirmed by the characteristic binding energy peaks corresponding to Mg 1s (1301 eV), O 1s (529 eV), and C 1s (282 eV), respectively [45]. High-resolution XPS spectrum of C 1s showed peaks at 284.8 eV (C-C/C=C), 286.7 eV (C-O), and 289.4 eV (C=O) [46]. The O 1s and Mg 1s spectra, respectively, showed the characteristic peaks of Mg-O at around 531.1 eV and 1304.0 eV, indicating successful coordination between Mg2+ and oxygen-containing functional groups within the nanosheets [[47], [48], [49]]. To further verify the chemical interaction between Ae and Mg2+, FTIR spectroscopy was conducted (Fig. 1F). Both the Ae monomer and its complex with Mg2+ exhibited broad and intense O-H stretching vibration bands at 3405 and 3407 cm−1, respectively, and characteristic C-O stretching vibrations from phenolic hydroxyl groups at 1274 and 1281 cm−1. These results suggested that phenolic hydroxyl groups are retained in both the free ligand and the complexed state. Interestingly, the O-H stretching band in MgAe displayed a slight red shift compared to that in free Ae, which can be attributed to hydrogen bonding enhancement and possible electron delocalization effects induced by metal-ligand interactions [50]. In contrast, the carbonyl stretching bands of MgAe at 1673 and 1623 cm−1 exhibited a modest blue shift relative to those of Ae (1674 and 1627 cm−1), which may be due to decreased electron density around the carbonyl group as a result of coordination with Mg2+, leading to C=O bond weakening [51]. Additionally, variations in the C=C stretching vibrations imply a partial π-conjugation perturbation upon metal chelation, and a new weak absorption band observed at 454 cm−1 in the low-frequency region further confirms the successful self-assembly due to the Mg-O coordination between Mg2+ and the hydroxyl and carbonyl groups of Ae [52]. Furthermore, XRD patterns of the Ae monomer, MgCl2, and the MgAe complex (Fig. 1G) demonstrated distinct changes in crystallinity and diffraction peaks, collectively confirming the successful formation of the MgAe nanosheet crystalline structure.

Both Ae and MgAe exhibited a strong UV–Vis absorption peak at 440 nm, likely originating from the C=O groups in the anthraquinone framework, and the yellow color of Ae solution turned dark claret after coordination with Mg2+ and self-assembly, which is consistent with the observed shoulder peak at ∼505 nm in the MgAe sample [52] (Fig. 1H). Fluorescence emission spectra revealed a maximum emission near 550 nm for both Ae and MgAe (Fig. 1I). ICP-OES (Table S2) and UV–vis (Fig. S2) analysis revealed that the MgAe complex contained ∼1.68 % Mg2+ and ∼75.5 % Ae by mass, corresponding to a Mg2+: Ae molar ratio of ∼1:4. Structurally, the 1,8-dihydroxy groups of Ae can potentially participate in coordination, but due to the electron-donating nature of the 3-hydroxymethyl group, the 8-hydroxy is more reactive and preferentially involved in metal binding. Based on existing research, it is postulated that the MgAe nanosheet is formed via the coordination of the 8-hydroxy and 9-carbonyl groups of two Ae molecules with one Mg2+ ion [53,54]. These findings support the formation of a stable, well-dispersed MgAe supramolecular structure, which ensures a high loading of bioactive Ae while maintaining controlled Mg2+ release for antibacterial and osteogenic functionality [52].

Optimization and mechanical assessment of FDM-printed PEEK scaffolds

To develop a structurally robust and biologically adaptable scaffold for mandibular defect repair, porous PEEK scaffolds were fabricated using FDM technology under optimized printing parameters (Fig. 2A). Given that the thermal and mechanical properties of PEEK are highly sensitive to processing conditions, a systematic optimization of nozzle temperature and printing speed was conducted. As shown in Fig. 2B, nozzle temperatures exceeding 385 °C led to filament fusion and pore collapse, while temperatures below 375 °C resulted in filament breakage due to insufficient thermal fluidity. Only within a narrow temperature range (375–385 °C) could structurally intact scaffolds be reproducibly obtained. SEM imaging confirmed that scaffolds printed at 380 °C with a print speed of 12 mm s−1 yielded well-defined porous architectures. Mechanical testing (Fig. 2C) revealed that scaffolds fabricated under these conditions maintained consistent compressive strength and modulus. An appropriate pore architecture is essential for facilitating ion exchange, protein adsorption, and the regulation of cellular behaviors such as adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [19,55,56]. Previous studies have indicated that pores larger than 100 μm are sufficient to support vascular ingrowth, nutrient diffusion, and metabolic waste removal, while interconnected macropores in the range of 300–500 μm are considered optimal for bone tissue ingrowth and neovascularization [41]. To investigate the structural fidelity of scaffolds fabricated under optimized printing conditions, PEEK scaffolds with designed porosities of 50 %, 60 %, and 70 % were printed and analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2D, all groups exhibited clearly defined interconnected architectures. Quantitative measurements revealed actual porosity values of 50.0 ± 7.0 %, 63.3 ± 3.5 %, and 82.0 ± 1.7 %, respectively (Fig. 2E), which aligned well with their designed values. The slightly greater deviation observed in the 70 % group may be attributed to the limited resolution and flow control at high porosity during FDM printing, as well as the high melting point and high viscosity of PEEK material [57].

Since PEEK scaffolds are intended for load-bearing applications in mandibular reconstruction, sufficient mechanical strength is essential. The compressive strength of the 50 %, 60 %, and 70 % scaffolds was measured as 34.3 ± 2.2 MPa, 19.4 ± 1.7 MPa, and 10.6 ± 1.5 MPa, respectively, while their corresponding compressive moduli were 564.4 ± 31.9 MPa, 439.8 ± 102.0 MPa, and 342.8 ± 15.9 MPa (Fig. 2F). These results demonstrate that all FDM-printed scaffolds retained sufficient structural integrity under axial loading, although a reduction in mechanical performance was observed with increasing porosity. According to the literature, the compressive modulus of mandibular trabecular bone varies widely depending on anatomical location and cortical support, ranging from 3.5 to 240 MPa [[58], [59], [60]]. Meanwhile, O'Mahony et al. reported direction-dependent elastic moduli of mandibular bone as 114, 511, and 907 MPa along the infero-superior, bucco-lingual, and mesio-distal axes, respectively [61]. Based on these mechanical benchmarks, the 60 % porous PEEK scaffold, with a measured pore size of 620.0 ± 38.3 μm, was chosen as it provided a suitable compromise between mechanical strength, hydrogel loading capacity, and space for cell infiltration in mandibular bone regeneration.

Hydrogel-mediated functionalization and characterization of MgAe-loaded PEEK scaffolds

To achieve prolonged release of Ae and Mg2+ for sustained treatment of infectious bone defects and to enhance the bioactivity of the inherently bioinert PEEK substrate, a hydrogel-based delivery system was developed via a freeze-drying approach. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, alginate hydrogel was first infused into the porous architecture of the 3D-printed PEEK scaffold. Following complete ionic crosslinking and lyophilization, the resultant hydrogel-modified scaffold was incubated with the MgAe nanosheets solution to facilitate loading. The scaffold maintained its original yellowish appearance after hydrogel modification, while the incorporation of MgAe induced a characteristic purplish hue, indicating successful uptake of the nanosheets (Fig. 3B). SEM revealed a well-preserved interconnected macroporous architecture, suitable for subsequent tissue ingrowth and hydrogel accommodation. Notably, MgAe aggregates were observed on the surface of the lyophilized hydrogel matrix in the AP@MgAe group. To investigate the chemical composition and surface modifications, FTIR spectroscopy was performed (Fig. 3D). The pristine PEEK scaffold exhibited characteristic absorption peaks at 1647 cm−1 (C=O stretching of diphenylketone), 1594 and 1486 cm−1 (C=C stretching in aromatic rings), and 1276, 1216, and 1184 cm−1 (asymmetric stretching of C-O-C in diaryl ethers) [19,62]. In the AP group, peaks at 1595 and 1430 cm−1 were assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric -COO- stretching vibrations, respectively, arising from calcium-ion-crosslinked alginate hydrogels [63]. Similarly, AP@MgAe exhibited -COO- absorption bands at 1595 and 1415 cm−1, indicating that the PEEK substrate was effectively coated by the alginate hydrogel. Additionally, a new peak at 454 cm−1, corresponding to Mg-O stretching, was detected exclusively in the AP@MgAe group, corroborating the successful incorporation of MgAe nanosheets [35,52]. Furthermore, an intensified and broadened O-H stretching band between 3500 and 3200 cm−1 was observed in AP@MgAe, suggesting increased hydrogen bonding interactions likely due to water adsorption and the hydrophilic nature of the modified surface [64].

In biological systems, enhanced hydrophilicity of biomaterials is critical for improving interfacial interactions with surrounding cells, thereby facilitating cell adhesion and proliferation [35]. Water contact angle measurements were performed to evaluate the surface wettability of the scaffolds (Fig. 3E). The pristine PEEK scaffold (P group) exhibited a high contact angle of 93.05 ± 7.06°, indicative of its intrinsic hydrophobicity. In contrast, the AP and AP@MgAe scaffolds demonstrated markedly reduced contact angles of 24.04 ± 5.31° and 23.34 ± 3.77°, respectively, confirming the successful incorporation of hydrophilic alginate hydrogel coatings and the resultant improvement in surface wettability. To further characterize the hydrogel behavior, the swelling ratio and water content of the AP scaffold were assessed over time (Fig. 3F). After immersion in PBS solution, the scaffold reached a relatively saturated swelling state (149.31 ± 13.35 %) within 1.5 h. Accordingly, a 1.5 h duration was selected for subsequent MgAe nanosheet loading, as it provided an optimal balance between sufficient hydrogel hydration and structural integrity and reproducibility of the loading process. Besides, the interfacial adhesion test showed an average adhesion strength of 7.49 ± 0.36 kPa for the freeze-dried hydrogel scaffold and 1.55 ± 0.10 kPa for the fully swollen hydrogel scaffold (Table S3). Such interfacial stability is critical for maintaining hydrogel layer integrity and ensuring consistent release of MgAe nanosheets during long-term implantation [65,66]. Meanwhile, the hydrogel layer remained structurally stable across all tested media (deionized water, PBS, α-MEM, and α-MEM + 15 % FBS), showing no noticeable dissolution or detachment (Fig. S5).

The incorporation of a porous PEEK architecture combined with a lyophilized, crosslinked hydrogel matrix provided an effective platform for the sustained delivery of bioactive agents from the AP@MgAe scaffold. This hierarchical structure enabled efficient encapsulation and gradual release of therapeutic molecules to the scaffold surface, which is essential for early-stage infection control and modulation of the local inflammatory microenvironment. As shown in Fig. 3G, under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the Mg2+ release from AP@MgAe exhibited an initial burst, reaching 26.21 ± 3.25 % within the first 18 h. This was followed by a slower release phase, with cumulative release reaching 29.31 ± 4.02 % at 72 h. Notably, the release behavior was pH-responsive, with significantly accelerated release (49.09 ± 7.17 % at 18 h and 60.44 ± 4.73 % at 72 h) observed under acidic conditions (pH 5.5). This acid-responsive release is likely attributed to the sensitivity of the metal-ligand coordination structure to acidic environments [52]. The release of MgAe was assessed by UV–vis (Fig. S6), and its release behavior was consistent with that of Mg2+. The combination of an initial burst and a subsequent prolonged release creates a therapeutic window that favors rapid bacterial suppression in the early stage while providing long-term support for late osteogenesis and tissue regeneration.

Besides, as shown in TEM images (Fig. S3), MgAe nanosheets exhibited lamellar structures after incubation at pH 7.4 for 24 h, indicating good stability under physiological conditions. In contrast, exposure to acidic PBS (pH 5.5) led to noticeable disintegration, consistent with accelerated release behavior under infection-mimicking environments.

In vitro antibacterial performance

Infectious mandibular bone defects are frequently associated with complex bacterial colonization, among which S. aureus, a Gram-positive bacterium, is the most prevalent pathogen [67]. It can invade bone tissue and significantly delay the healing process. The antibacterial activity of MgAe nanosheets and AP@MgAe scaffolds against S. aureus was initially assessed by agar plate colony formation assays (Fig. 4A and E). A dose-dependent reduction in bacterial colonies was observed with increasing concentrations of MgAe nanosheets. Quantitative analysis revealed that the highest bacterial inhibition rates exceeded 80 % for the 50 μg mL−1 MgAe group and approached 70 % in the AP@4MgAe scaffold group (Fig. 4B and G). These trends were consistent with OD600 measurements, which showed progressive suppression of bacterial growth in response to increasing MgAe concentrations. SEM imaging further confirmed the morphological disruption of S. aureus after exposure to MgAe or AP@MgAe. Untreated control groups displayed smooth, intact, and round bacterial morphologies. In contrast, bacteria treated with higher MgAe concentrations exhibited visible deformation, including membrane shrinkage and surface collapse (Fig. 4C). Notably, bio-TEM images of bacteria exposed to a higher concentration of MgAe nanosheets (50 μg mL−1) revealed severely distorted cell membranes and indistinct cytoplasmic regions with numerous vacuoles (Fig. S7). These observations suggest that MgAe nanosheets inhibit bacterial proliferation by disrupting membrane integrity and inducing cytoplasmic leakage [68]. Similarly, bacteria adhered to AP@MgAe scaffolds with higher MgAe loading appeared distorted or lysed, with significantly reduced numbers of intact cells (Fig. 4F). These results collectively suggest that MgAe-mediated antibacterial activity may involve membrane disruption (Fig. 4H). In vitro cytocompatibility and osteogenic evaluation

Biocompatibility is an essential prerequisite for any scaffold intended for bone tissue engineering applications. While divalent cations such as Mg2+ are known to promote osteogenesis, excessive concentrations may induce cytotoxic effects [43]. As shown in Fig. 5A, cell viability remained above 85 % across all tested concentrations, with no statistically significant decline observed even at the highest dose of 100 μg mL−1. The relatively long-term assays showed that cell viability remained above 80 % in all tested groups over 48–72 h, indicating negligible cytotoxicity even at higher concentrations (Fig. S9). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MgAe nanosheets are highly cytocompatible and well tolerated by BMSCs. Further assessment using live/dead staining revealed abundant viable cells on all AP@MgAe-modified scaffolds after 7 days (Fig. 5B), indicating that the incorporation of MgAe did not compromise cell survival. Notably, modified scaffolds with low to moderate MgAe concentrations (AP@0.5MgAe and AP@1MgAe) supported greater cell attachment and spreading, as evidenced by SEM imaging (Fig. 5C). In contrast, AP@2MgAe showed relatively fewer adherent cells, suggesting a dose-dependent effect of MgAe on cell behavior. To evaluate the osteogenic capacity of AP@MgAe scaffolds, a series of in vitro assays was conducted, including ALP staining and activity measurement, ARS staining, and osteogenesis-related gene expression profiling. ALP activity, an early marker of osteogenic differentiation, was observed via staining on days 5 and 7 (Fig. 5D). ALP positivity was detected in all treatment groups, with the AP@2MgAe group showing the strongest signal at both time points. Notably, ALP staining intensity was higher on day 7 than on day 5, indicating a time-dependent increase in osteogenic activity. Quantitative ALP activity assays corroborated these findings, showing significantly elevated enzyme activity in the AP@0.5MgAe, AP@1MgAe, and AP@2MgAe groups relative to the control (Fig. 5E). Mineralized matrix deposition, a late-stage osteogenic marker, was assessed on day 14 using ARS staining. The AP@2MgAe group demonstrated the highest degree of calcium nodule formation, both macroscopically and microscopically (Fig. 5D), with semi-quantitative analysis confirming significantly higher ARS density compared to other groups (Fig. 5F).

To gain insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying osteogenic induction, qRT-PCR analysis was conducted to quantify the expression of osteogenic genes, including Bmp2, Col1, Opn, and Runx2 (Fig. 5G). Overall, expression levels increased from day 7–14 across all groups, with the AP@MgAe groups showing significantly higher expression than the control group. The AP@2MgAe group consistently demonstrated the strongest upregulation.

Taken together, these findings highlight the osteoconductive potential of AP@MgAe scaffolds, which may be attributed to the sustained release of bioactive Mg2+ and Ae from the hydrogel matrix. This dual functionality supports both osteogenesis and antibacterial activity, positioning the scaffold as a promising platform for the repair of infectious bone defects.

To investigate the osteogenic mechanism, IHC staining of ALP and OCN was performed (Fig. 7C and D). Semi-quantitative analysis revealed a significant increase in ALP- and OCN-positive cells in the AP@MgAe group compared to the control and other scaffold groups (Fig. 7E and F). Notably, ALP expression peaked at 6 weeks and declined by 12 weeks, consistent with the natural course of bone maturation. OCN, a late-stage marker, showed progressive upregulation over time, reflecting enhanced osteoblast activity and matrix mineralization. Since osteogenesis is closely associated with the local microenvironment, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines was further examined. As shown in Fig. S11, the control group showed extensive brown staining, indicating high levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. In contrast, the AP@MgAe group exhibited significantly lower expression, demonstrating its effectiveness in controlling infection and reducing inflammation. Additionally, biosafety evaluations conducted at 12 weeks revealed no pathological abnormalities in major organs, as confirmed by HE staining and blood biochemistry analysis (Figs. S12 and S13), suggesting favorable biocompatibility of the AP@MgAe system for in vivo application. From a mechanical standpoint, the 3D-printed PEEK framework provides load-bearing compatibility that stabilizes the defect and transmits physiological strains to the regenerating tissue. This reduces stress shielding and limits pathological micromotion, thereby supporting osteogenic adhesion, spreading, and matrix deposition within the interconnected pores. Together with the alginate/MgAe interphase that improves early cell-material interactions, the mechanical environment created by the PEEK lattice aligns with the elevated ALP and OCN signals and the improved micro-CT metrics, indicating that appropriate stiffness and stable load transfer are integral to the observed bone regeneration.

In summary, the AP@MgAe scaffolds outperformed other groups by (i) mitigating local infection, (ii) reducing inflammatory responses, and (iii) accelerating osteointegration [[73], [74], [75], [76]]. The antibacterial activity of the AP@MgAe scaffold aids in controlling local infection, which in turn facilitates osteogenesis by preventing infection-induced inflammation and maintaining a conducive environment for bone formation. The synergy between infection suppression and osteogenic stimulation ensures optimal healing and bone regeneration in infected defects. These effects were supported by the gradual disappearance of defect margins, enhanced tissue continuity with surrounding bone, and increased deposition of mineralized matrix. The 3D-printed PEEK scaffold provides the mechanical strength and structural support needed for bone tissue regeneration, with its porous structure facilitating cell infiltration and tissue growth. MgAe nanosheet modification imparts antibacterial and osteogenic functions to the scaffold. The pH-responsive release ensures sustained antibacterial activity under mildly acidic conditions, and the release of Mg2+ promotes osteogenesis. These two functions work synergistically, combining mechanical support with biological functionality, to provide an effective solution for infection control and bone regeneration. Furthermore, compared with representative Mg-based and polyphenol-modified scaffolds reported in recent years [35,41,43,45], the AP@MgAe scaffold uniquely integrates a load-bearing 3D-printed PEEK with a pH-responsive alginate interphase that embeds negatively charged MgAe nanosheets, thereby confirming the dual antibacterial and osteogenic efficacy of the proposed antibiotic-free strategy under mildly acidic infectious conditions.

Conclusions

This study addresses the clinical challenges of infectious mandibular bone defects by developing a functional scaffold system capable of simultaneous antibacterial activity and osteogenesis. A bioinspired porous PEEK scaffold was fabricated via FDM, providing favorable mechanical properties and architectural support for bone regeneration. To endow the scaffold with therapeutic functionality, an alginate hydrogel was integrated within the porous framework, followed by the incorporation of MgAe nanosheets formed via the supramolecular self-assembly of Ae and Mg2+. This hydrogel-mediated modification enabled the synergistic and sustained release of bioactive agents within an infected and inflammatory microenvironment, simultaneously suppressing bacterial proliferation and promoting osteogenic differentiation. The facile fabrication process, synergistic antibacterial and osteogenic properties, and favorable cytocompatibility of the AP@MgAe highlight its potential as an antibiotic-free strategy for treating infection-associated bone defects.

In vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrated that the AP@MgAe scaffold possessed excellent cytocompatibility, effective antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, and enhanced osteogenic capacity. Notably, the scaffold significantly reduced bacterial burden, mitigated inflammatory responses, and promoted new bone formation. Micro-CT reconstruction, histological analysis, and IHC staining all confirmed superior bone regeneration in the AP@MgAe group. Moreover, biosafety evaluations based on serum biochemistry and histopathological examination of major organs revealed no detectable systemic toxicity.

Taken together, these findings highlight the translational potential of the AP@MgAe scaffold as a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for infectious bone defects. By eliminating the need for secondary surgeries or high-dose antibiotic administration, this bifunctional platform provides a clinically relevant solution for promoting antibacterial protection and bone regeneration. Leveraging 3D printing, the FDM-printed PEEK scaffolds allow scalable and customizable fabrication, aligning with the principles of precision medicine. Looking ahead, we will engineer smart, triggerable systems to achieve precise, on-demand delivery. Although our data support the osteogenic potential of MgAe, the molecular basis requires further study. Future studies will delineate Mg2+-dependent osteogenic and angiogenic pathways, and clarify Ae-mediated regulation of bone remodeling and osteoclast activity, thereby strengthening the translational promise of the AP@MgAe scaffold.
