Decision aid reduces caregivers' preference for acid‐suppressive medication in infants with symptoms suggestive of reflux
Authors: Anna de Geus, Marinde van Lennep, Quirine M van Dellen, Andrieke C Knottnerus, Sarah T A Teklenburg‐Roord, Marc A Benninga, Merit M Tabbers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCaregivers of infants with symptoms suggestive of gastroesophageal reflux (GER), such as regurgitation and excessive crying, often feel uncertain and seek treatment, though it is usually unnecessary. This study evaluated the effect of a decision aid on reducing caregivers’ preference for acid suppressive medication for infants referred because of potential GER symptoms and its effect on their decision certainty.MethodsInfants (<18 months) referred to pediatric outpatient clinics with symptoms suggestive of GER were included. The decision aid provided information on gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and three treatment options: lifestyle advice, thickened feeding, and acid‐suppressive medication. Caregivers completed questionnaires assessing treatment preference and decision certainty before (T1a) and after using the decision aid (T1b), and after the outpatient visit (T2). Decision certainty was assessed using the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS).ResultsA total of 110 infants were included (median age 4 months [interquartile range 2–6]; 48% female; 78% general hospital). Pre‐visit questionnaires (T1a, T1b) were completed by 79 caregivers (72%) and 53 (48%) also completed T2. Preference for acid‐suppressive medication decreased from 46/79 (58%) at T1a to 34/79 (43%) at T1b (p < 0.05), and to 6/53 (11%) at T2 (p < 0.001). Mean DCS‐score (scale 0–100) decreased from 47.2 (standard deviation [SD] 16.1) at T1a to 34.4 (SD 17.4) at T1b, and to 19.0 (SD 12.3) post‐visit (p < 0.001), indicating improved decision certainty.ConclusionsThe decision aid reduced caregivers’ preference for acid‐suppressive treatment in infants with symptoms suggestive of GER and increased decision certainty. This supports the potential role of a decision aid in promoting appropriate care.Keywords: decision certainty, gastroesophageal reflux, pediatrics, shared decision making, treatment

INTRODUCTION

Gastroesophageal reflux (GER) is defined as the passage of gastric contents into the esophagus with or without regurgitation which is a normal physiologic process occurring several times a day in all infants. 1 , 2 When GER leads to troublesome symptoms and/or complications, such as failure to thrive, hematemesis, esophagitis or structuring, it is defined as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). 1 , 2 Especially in infants, it is difficult to differentiate between GER and GERD, because a golden standard is lacking and clinical manifestation of GER and GERD can be very similar. Symptoms of infant GERD may include regurgitation, excessive crying, back arching, and irritability but many of these symptoms also occur in otherwise healthy infants. In 90% of infants, these symptoms disappear by the first year of life without any intervention. 3 However, caregivers of infants with symptoms such as regurgitation and excessive crying feel uncertain and often seek treatment, even though treatment is usually not required. It is therefore crucial to educate caregivers on the benign character of the symptoms and to explain that in most infants, the symptoms will resolve spontaneously. Acid‐suppressive medications should not be prescribed in infants with physiological GER, due to uncertain efficacy and an increased risk of pneumonia, Clostridium difficile infections, and viral gastroenteritis. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 Acid‐suppressive medications are only indicated in cases of reflux‐related erosive esophagitis, or in the absence of esophagitis when pathological acid exposure or a symptom association with acid reflux is documented using esophageal pH‐impedance monitoring. 2 , 7 Despite the current recommendations in the GER(D) guideline, acid‐suppressive medications are still prescribed for healthy children with symptoms suggestive of GER. 8 In addition, the abundance of online (mis)information can confuse caregivers, increasing their uncertainty about the nature of the symptoms and treatment options. Providing clear information from a reliable source could help reduce this uncertainty. Decision aids are evidence‐based tools designed to assist patients in making informed and thoughtful choices between healthcare options. Several studies in different decision contexts have shown that decision aids lead to less decisional conflict and a greater sense of being informed. 9 Therefore, an online decision aid for caregivers of infants with symptoms suggestive of GER was developed and a prospective cohort study was performed to investigate the effect of this decision aid on reducing caregivers’ preference for acid suppressive medication and their decision certainty.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The requirement for Institutional Review Board approval was waived by the Ethics Committees of Amsterdam UMC. The ethics committee of Amsterdam UMC and general hospitals approved the study.

Study design and subjects

After a pilot phase, the decision aid was provided to all caregivers of infants presenting with symptoms suggestive of GER at pediatric outpatient clinics. Infants were recruited at one academic hospital (Emma Children's Hospital/Amsterdam UMC) and three general hospitals (Isala, Onze Lieve Vrouwe Gasthuis, and Spaarne Gasthuis). Infants were eligible if they were (1) <18 months old and (2) referred with symptoms suggestive of GER and if GER was part of the differential diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included (1) an indication for acid suppressive therapy according to the GER(D) guideline (e.g., hematemesis); (2) caregivers with insufficient knowledge of the Dutch language; (3) when caregivers did not have access to a computer, smartphone, or tablet with internet. For each eligible infant, one caregiver was invited to participate and, upon providing consent, received the questionnaires and decision aid. Since no studies have been published on comparable interventions within this population, a sample size calculation was not possible. Therefore, the sample size was determined based on the feasibility of participant inclusion within the recruitment period, with the target set at 100 inclusions.

Intervention

All caregivers received a link to the decision aid and questionnaires via email before their visit to the pediatrician. The decision aid provided information on GER(D) including three treatment options: lifestyle advice, thickened feeding, and acid suppressive medication (Supporting Information S1: Appendix S1). First, it introduced the definitions of GER and GERD and their differences. Then, each treatment option was briefly discussed, including its pros and cons. Lifestyle advice included practical tips that parents could implement in daily life. For thickened feeding, the rationale for its use and guidance on how to apply it were provided. For acid suppressive medication, the mechanism of action, the lack of evidence, and potential adverse events were described. The decision aid concluded with an overview summarizing the three treatment options, including their respective pros and cons. All information was based on national and international guidelines on GER(D) in children. 2 , 10 and was written in concise, plain language.

Data collection

Data were collected from January 2022 to February 2024 through online questionnaires, which were completed by caregivers at three time points (Figure 1):

Before visit to the pediatrician

Caregivers were sent a package with both questionnaires and the decision aid. Before caregivers could read the decision aid, they were asked to complete questionnaires (T1a), which included (1) a short knowledge test regarding GER in infants comprising three questions (Supporting Information S1: Appendix S2), (2) a questionnaire regarding treatment preferences, and (3) the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS) questionnaire. Subsequently, caregivers were able to read the decision aid and asked to fill in the same three questionnaires (T1b).

After visit to the pediatrician

Caregivers were sent the same set of questionnaires immediately after the consultation, with an additional feedback form. Caregivers were reminded via multiple emails and a phone call to complete the questionnaires. Data were collected via Castor, an electronic data capture (EDC) system.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measure was the proportion of caregiver's preferring acid‐suppressive medication. Caregivers were asked which treatment option they would choose for their infant at T1a, T1b, and T2: lifestyle advice, thickened feeding, or acid suppressive medication. The difference in the proportion of caregivers preferring acid suppressive medication was compared between time points T1a and T1b, and between T1b and T2.

Secondary outcome measure was the DCS score, a validated questionnaire developed to assess a person's perceptions of their decision certainty regarding a choice about healthcare options. 11 , 12 The questionnaire consists of 16 questions, measuring five dimensions of decision making: feeling uncertain, feeling uninformed, feeling unclear about values, and feeling unsupported, as well as ineffective decision making. 11 A five‐point Likert scale was used for each question (0 = totally agree, 4 = totally disagree). A total score (range 0–100) was calculated in which a score 0 indicates no decisional conflict, equivalent to high certainty, and a score of 100 indicates a lot decisional conflict. In addition, subscale scores per dimension of decision making were calculated. DCS scores were compared between time points T1a and T1b, and between T1b and T2. The Dutch version of the questionnaire, which was translated and used by Metz et al. was used for this study (Supporting Information S1: Appendix S3). 13

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 28. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Continuous data are reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR), and categorical data as counts and percentages.

The McNemar test evaluated changes in incorrect responses on the knowledge test and parental treatment preferences between T1a–T1b and T1b–T2.

DCS total and subscale scores at T1a, T1b, and T2 were compared using paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, depending on distribution. Results were qualitatively similar with both parametric and non‐parametric tests.

Only fully completed questionnaires were analyzed. Logistic regression assessed demographic differences between included and excluded participants. Parental feedback was analyzed descriptively. When group sizes were sufficient, subgroup analyses were conducted by hospital type (academic vs. general). In addition, exploratory post‐hoc analyses were performed by infant age (0–3, 4–6, ≥7 months) for the primary outcome. A p‐value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patients

In total, 153 eligible infants were identified of whom caregivers were asked for participation (Figure 2). 110/153 caregivers gave consent and were included in the study, reasons for exclusion are provided in Figure 2. Twenty‐four patients (22%) were recruited from an academic hospital and 86 (78%) patients from general hospitals (Table 1). Median age was 4 months (IQR 2–6) and 48% was female. Details of patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table S1. Of all children who were recruited from a general hospital, 88% were referred by the child health clinic or a general practitioner. 52% (n = 53/103) of caregivers believed their child had GERD before reading the decision aid, 14% had no opinion, and 35% believed their child had GER and not GERD. Subgroup analyses showed that this differed between caregivers of infants referred to an academic hospital or a general hospital, 83% (n = 19/23) and 43% (n = 34/80) believed their child had GERD, respectively.

Response rate

Of the 110 included caregivers, 79 (71%) fully completed the questionnaires from T1a and T1b. Of these 79 caregivers, 53 (67%) also completed the questionnaire from T2. In total, 48% of the caregivers completed all questionnaires. Seventy‐nine patients were included in the analyses for T1a versus T1b and 53 patients were included in the analyses for T1b versus T2. There were no significant differences in relevant demographic characteristics between participants included in the analysis and those who were excluded, as determined by logistic regression. The mean interval between completing the pre‐ and post‐visit questionnaires was 14 days (SD 13, range 0–65 days).

Caregivers' treatment preferences

The proportion of caregivers with treatment preference for acid suppressive medication significantly decreased after caregivers read the decision aid (T1a 46/79 vs. T1b 34/79, p = 0.004) and after they visited the pediatrician (T1b 22/53 vs. T2 6/53, p < 0.001) (Figure 3). Subgroup analysis for children seen at an academic hospital (n = 20) showed that the proportion of caregivers preferring acid‐suppressive medication remained unchanged; none of the parents changed their treatment preference, regardless of their initial preference. Results remained significant when children referred to an academic hospital were excluded from the analysis. In the T1a versus T1b analysis, a significant decrease in parental preference was observed in infants aged 0–3 months (n = 35, p = 0.012), whereas no significant changes were seen in the 4–6 months (n = 24, p = 0.625) or ≥7 months groups (n = 19, p = 1.000). In the T1b versus T2 analysis, the decrease remained significant for the youngest group (n = 26, p = 0.008) but not for the older age groups. In all age subgroups, there was a trend toward decreased parental preference for acid suppressive medication.

Decision certainty

The mean total DCS‐score significantly decreased after caregivers read the decision aid, from 47.2 (SD 16.1) at T1a to 34.4 (SD 17.4) at T1b, indicating an increased decision certainty. In addition, all subscores significantly decreased (Supporting Information S1: Table S2). Total DCS‐score and all subscores further decreased post‐visit (Supporting Information S1: Table S2). Subgroup analysis showed that mean total DCS‐scores at T1a did not differ between children referred to an academic hospital or general hospital, 47.2 (SD 16.1) versis 54.2 (SD 16.1), respectively. In both groups, the mean total DCS scores significantly decreased from T1a to T1b. The decrease in DCS‐scores did not significantly differ between the academic and general hospitals (7.6 SD = 15.7 vs. 14.5 SD = 14.1, p = 0.070). Only two DCS‐subscores (informed and values clarity) decreased in the academic hospital group, whereas all subscores decreased in the general hospital group.

Short knowledge test

From the three questions asked in the knowledge test, the number of wrong answers per question only significantly reduced for the question “Can thickened feeding reduce reflux symptoms?” after reading the decision aid (Supporting Information S1: Table S3). 19/79 (24%) answered this question with “no” at T1a before reading the decision aid and 6/79 (8%) answered this question with “no” after reading the decision aid (p < 0.001).

Feedback on the decision aid

A total of 50 caregivers completed the feedback form. Eighty‐eight percent of caregivers found the decision aid useful and 88% would recommend the decision aid to other caregivers of children experiencing symptoms suggestive for GER. Of the six caregivers who indicated they would not recommend the decision aid, reasons included were that one caregiver already had read the national guideline on GER(D), two caregivers reported a need for more information on thickened feeding and medications, and one parent consulted the pediatrician for a broader perspective on their child's symptoms, rather than solely focusing on GER.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate a decision aid for caregivers of infants with symptoms suggestive of GER. Our results clearly show that the decision aid significantly reduced the proportion of caregivers who preferred acid‐suppressive medication and significantly increased caregivers’ decision certainty. These findings highlight the potential of this tool to reduce caregiver's demand for inappropriate and potentially harmful pharmacological treatment and to support them in their decision‐making.

This decision aid was specifically designed to help caregivers to make well‐informed decisions regarding treatment, in alignment with pediatric GER(D) guidelines that discourage the use of acid‐suppressive medication in otherwise healthy infants. Despite these guidelines, such medications are still frequently prescribed, which is concerning given the lack of evidence supporting their efficacy in the absence of acid‐induced disease and the risk of adverse effects. 2 , 14 , 15 , 16

Previous interventions aimed at reducing inappropriate prescriptions, such as guideline dissemination and educational efforts targeting healthcare providers, have shown mixed results. 8 , 17 , 18 , 19 Notably, these approaches have primarily focused on clinicians, whereas our study uniquely targets caregivers. By directly addressing caregiver preferences, our decision aid may complement existing clinician‐focused strategies. Indeed, we observed that caregiver preference for acid‐suppressive medication significantly decreased after reading the decision aid, with a further reduction following the visit to the outpatient pediatric clinic. Although our study design does not allow to determine whether this led to an actual reduction in prescriptions, the findings suggest a promising strategy to reduce overtreatment.

Our decision aid provides evidence‐based information from a reliable source to enhance shared decision making helping caregivers feel informed and confident about treatment choices. Excessive infantile crying, a common GER‐related symptom, is associated with mental health challenges among parents, such as anxiety, depression, and elevated stress during the first year of life. 20 , 21 , 22 This psychological burden may drive the high demand for treatment by caregivers, as confirmed in our study, as 58% of caregivers initially preferred acid‐suppressive medication. Furthermore, many parents attribute common physiological symptoms in infants, such as crying and regurgitation, to GERD, which can further increase the demand for treatment. Clinicians may also prescribe proton pump inhibitors for unexplained infant distress or crying due to limited awareness of guidelines or a desire to reassure parents. 14 , 15 , 23 In this context, shared decision making plays an important role in aligning parental expectations with evidence‐based care. Shared decision‐making is increasingly recognized in pediatric care, as it is an evidence‐based approach that emphasizes collaboration between caregivers and healthcare professionals. A recent systematic review in child care identified poor‐quality information as a key barrier to shared decision making, underscoring the potential of decision aids. 24 Decision aids improve knowledge, foster realistic risk perceptions, encourage active participation in decision‐making, and reduce decisional conflict. 9 , 25 , 26 Similar to these studies, our study showed knowledge improved on one of three questions after reading the decision aid (incorrect responses dropped from 24% to 8%) and decisional conflict scores decreased significantly across all five domains.

Our baseline DCS‐score was relatively high (mean 47.2, SD 16.1) compared to previous studies involving surrogate decision‐making for children (mean 30.2, SD 14.2), possibly reflecting high caregiver stress in our sample. 26 Higher DCS‐scores have also been reported in primary care settings (mean 40.6, SD 18.3), which could also have contributed to our high baseline DCS‐score given that the majority of our population was only seen by a primary care provider prior to receiving the decision aid. 26 DCS‐scores exceeding 37.5 are associated with decision delay or uncertainty, while scores below 25 are associated with decision implementation. 11 In our study, the mean DCS dropped from 47.2 pre‐decision aid to 34.4 post‐decision aid and further to 19.0 post‐visit, suggesting caregivers became more certain of their choice and more likely to implement their decision. While the absence of a control group precludes determining the precise effect of the decision aid on post‐visit DCS scores, the greatest reduction in mean total DCS score and parental preference for acid suppressive medication were observed after the outpatient visit, underscoring the importance of the visit and discussion with the physician.

This study has several strengths. First, the decision aid was developed using evidence‐based guidelines, ensuring its content is evidence‐based and likely to be endorsed by healthcare professionals. Second, we recruited participants from both academic and general hospitals, enhancing the generalizability of our findings to real‐world settings where caregivers may have varying levels of prior information. However, several limitations should be noted. The decision aid was only available online, in Dutch, and not adapted for caregivers with low literacy levels, introducing potential selection bias. We also observed a relatively high rate of incomplete post‐visit questionnaires despite follow‐up efforts. While logistic regression found no significant differences between completers and non‐completers, lost to follow‐up may have introduced selection bias, potentially overestimating the intervention's effect. Only five caregivers provided reasons for not filling in the questionnaires at T2, as they were informed that their child did not have GER, making the final questionnaire no longer applicable. Caregivers provided feedback on the questionnaires, noting that some questions were not completely applicable to their situation and that the questionnaires were too long, suggesting that questionnaire fatigue contributed to the low response rate at T2. Last, the absence of a control group precludes isolating the effect of the decision aid on post‐visit outcomes.

Our findings can have important implications for clinical practice. The decision aid was effective in reducing caregiver preference for acid‐suppressive medication and improving decision certainty among those referred to pediatricians in secondary and tertiary care. However, its utility may extend even further when implemented earlier in the care pathway. In particular, we believe the decision aid could be integrated into general practice or child health clinic settings, either as a handout for caregivers or as part of the referral process. As these are often the first healthcare professionals caregivers visit, this early intervention may help reassure caregivers, promote appropriate management strategies, foster greater confidence in the management approach, improve consultation efficiency, and reduce unnecessary referrals to pediatricians. This is supported by the findings of our post hoc subgroup analyses, which suggest that the decision aid may have a greater effect in caregivers of infants aged 0–3 months. These findings should be interpreted with caution as the statistical power is low due to the limited number of patients in each subgroup. Interestingly, we observed that caregiver preferences did not change among those referred to an academic hospital. This suggests that caregivers who have already received comprehensive information from multiple healthcare providers may benefit less from additional decision support, underscoring the value of introducing such tools earlier in the care pathway. As this study represents the first use of the decision aid in clinical practice, future studies or implementation may require refinement or local adaptation to optimize parental education and infant management.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that providing evidence‐based information in a clear and structured manner helps reducing caregiver preference for acid‐suppressive medication and increases decision certainty in the management of infants <18 months old presenting with symptoms suggestive of GER. While further research is needed to evaluate the impact of such tools on actual prescribing behavior, our study supports the potential role of caregiver‐facing decision aids in promoting appropriate care and reducing unnecessary treatment. The decision aid may be of most value when implemented early in the care pathway, for example, by the family doctor or child health clinic.
