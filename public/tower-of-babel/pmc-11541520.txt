A Comprehensive Comparison Analysis between Ammonium-Based and Phosphonium-Based Bifunctional Ionic Liquids for Metal Extraction and Separation Processes
Authors: Zaheer Abbas, Simon MoonGeun Jung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA phosphonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid (IL) Cyp-IL was synthesized to recover molybdenum and vanadium from industrial waste resources, such as the spent hydrodesulfurization catalysts. In comparison with the reported versatile Aliquat 336-based ionic liquid, the developed ionic liquid Cyp-IL was investigated for its potential application recovery of vanadium and molybdenum from industrial wastes. To assess the efficiency of the comparative extraction process, a comprehensive comparison analysis was conducted between the ammonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid Aliquat 336 and the developed phosphonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid Cyp-IL. Vanadium and molybdenum in the ionic liquid-loaded phases were selectively precipitated using NH4Cl and BaCl2 solutions in a basic medium. Compared to Aliquat 336-based Ali-IL, Cyp-IL exhibited superior performance in the extraction and separation processes of molybdenum and vanadium such as a recovery rate of Mo 91% and V 82.5% vs Mo 85% and V 72% by the ammonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid Ali-IL. The recovered components, such as molybdenum and vanadium, were characterized by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). This method capacitates the sustainable recovery of pure vanadium and molybdenum compounds while minimizing the environmental impact.

Introduction

Ionic liquids are promising eco-friendly media and substitutes for hydrometallurgical methods. This approach, termed ionometallurgy, involves utilizing ionic liquids for extraction instead of the aqueous phase traditionally used in hydrometallurgy.1,2 Unlike aqueous systems, nonaqueous solvent systems comprise organic and inorganic solvents including ionic liquids, deep-eutectic solvents, supercritical carbon dioxide, and ammonia solutions.3−5 Considering this, ionic liquids were assessed for their potential in the metal recovery process from waste resources. These ionic liquids were utilized in the extraction phase, originating from either acid-leached or basic media-leached solutions. However, this approach involves several stages that produce a significant amount of wastewater containing acids and bases. To tackle this issue, ionic liquids can be utilized in the initial leaching phase. Nonetheless, the utilization of ionic liquid in the leaching process has been limited largely due to its low leaching efficiency.6−9

Industrial wastes such as spent process catalysts contain a considerable amount of molybdenum, vanadium, aluminum, cobalt, iron, and nickel. These inorganic and toxic wastes have been recycled and used as an important secondary resource for the recovery of valuable metals.10,11 These valuable metals have broad applications ranging from metal alloys, aircraft components, electrical parts, filaments, synthesis, and catalysis.12,13 The retrieval of valuable metals from the disposed process catalyst is environmentally and economically significant.14,15 However, according to “the Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal”,16,17 due to the toxic properties of these industrial inorganic wastes, most countries have designated the spent process catalysts as hazardous waste materials.18,19

Several methodologies have been investigated and implemented to recover valuable metals like vanadium (V), nickel (Ni), molybdenum (Mo), and cobalt (Co) from industrial waste resources such as spent process catalysts.20 Most of the proposed techniques or methods for metal recovery from industrial waste resources rely heavily on pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical processes. Among the pyrometallurgical methods, the roasting-leaching process is the widely used method, which is attributed to its high efficacy in metal recovery. However, this process has several drawbacks despite high metal recoveries. For instance, pyrometallurgical processes often involve high temperatures and the release of gases, leading to environmental concerns. Emissions of sulfur dioxide (SO2) and other pollutants contribute to air pollution and acid rain, posing risks to the environment and human health.21−23

Molybdenum and vanadium were recovered through the hydrometallurgical process involving the dissolving of these metals from the spent catalysts using acid, alkaline, and bioleaching processes.24−28 For this purpose, inorganic acidic and alkaline solutions are employed; however, these exhibit poor selectivity for certain metals such as Mo, V, and Ni due to the dissolution of the matrix, which ultimately produces impure leachates.29,30 To overcome such issues, the inorganic or mineral acids were replaced with organic acids.31−33 However, the leaching recovery of metals remained relatively low, not exceeding 50%.34 Furthermore, a significant amount of wastewater is frequently produced because numerous extraction and separation steps are required in the hydrometallurgical processes used to recover metals. Since the hydrometallurgical and pyrometallurgical processes have their shortcomings, to reduce environmental problems, a novel innovative process should be sought in addition to both selective and efficient leaching.

In this work, we conducted a comparative analysis of the phosphonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid “Cyp-IL” and the ammonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid “Ali-IL” to evaluate the effectiveness of the leaching process using ionic liquids. For this purpose, in tandem with Aliquat 336-based Ali-IL, Cyp-IL was synthesized and compared on the extraction and separation efficiencies of vanadium and molybdenum. These two ionic liquids were employed as a leaching solution for a spent hydrodesulfurization (HDS) catalyst consisting of a mixture of ionic liquids and hydrogen peroxide. H2O2 serves as an oxidizing agent, facilitating the dissolution of metals from the spent catalyst matrix.35 The leaching solution offers distinct advantages in the processing of spent lithium-ion batteries, eliminating the need for acid or base treatment in the leaching step. As the solubilities of Al3+ and Ni2+ in such solutions are notably limited, they are capable of facilitating the selective recovery of molybdenum and vanadium. In addition, molybdenum and vanadium were complexed with a cation of the bifunctional ionic liquid facilitating their leaching from industrial waste resources. Both bifunctional ionic liquids (Bif-IL) have demonstrated effective extraction and separation of metal ions by their dual functionality, acting both as cationic and anionic extractants.8,36,37

However, the phosphonium-based ionic liquid Cyp-IL shows higher leaching efficiency compared to its ammonium-based ion liquid Ali-IL. The enhanced leaching efficiency of phosphonium-based ionic liquids such as Cyp-IL can be attributed to the unique chemical and physical properties of phosphonium-based ionic liquids, i.e., higher thermal stability and greater hydrophobicity, which facilitate better solubility for metal-containing species. The larger size and lower charge density of the phosphonium cation contribute to stronger interactions with metal ions, which ultimately improve the extraction and separation efficiency during the metal recovery process. After leaching, vanadium and molybdenum compounds can be precipitated from the metal-ion-loaded phase by adding ammonium salt. The leaching parameters, namely, the concentration of both ionic liquids, H2O2 ratio, and various operational factors including temperature, reaction time, and S/L ratio (w/v), were subjected to systematic investigation. The details of this investigation are given in a subsequent discussion.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Chemicals

Trihexyl phosphine (>90.0%) was purchased from TCI (Japan), while Aliquat 336 (≥97.0%) and ammonium chloride (>99.0%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Di(2-ethylhexyl)phosphoric acid (D2EHPA, 95%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Kerosene was employed as a diluent for ionic liquid, which was supplied by Daejung Chemicals (Korea). H2O2 (30%) was purchased from Duksan Chemicals (Korea).

Pretreatment of Industrial Waste Resources

The spent HDS catalyst obtained from the Korean Petroleum Corporation was first treated with kerosene to remove oil and other organic matter, deposited on the spent catalyst during the petroleum refining process.38 The spent catalyst was dried in a vacuum oven overnight, and then, it was calcined in a muffle furnace at 700 °C for 5 h. Subsequently, the calcined spent catalyst was ground to ensure a uniform size for analysis and the leaching process.

The primary chemical composition of the spent catalysts was examined using an X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer, and its composition is listed in Table 1. Vanadium and aluminum are the predominant elements, constituting the majority of the composition. Additionally, the catalysts contain other elements, including sulfur, molybdenum, nickel, and iron. The prevailing forms of these metal ions are primarily oxides and sulfides. The spent HDS catalysts typically contain sulfur compounds, such as metal sulfides.8 Desulfurization occurs during calcination, and it also facilitates the removal of organic deposits. Sulfur compounds undergo oxidation, converting metal sulfides into metal oxides and releasing sulfur gases. The transition of metal sulfides to metal oxides occurs as sulfur is removed, altering the catalyst’s chemical composition.

Extraction and Separation Processes

In each trial, a fixed concentration of ionic liquids in kerosene was introduced into a two-neck flask connected to a condenser. The leaching process was conducted at 50 °C for 3 h. The solid-to-liquid ratio was set at 1:20, with stirring at a speed of 600–700 rpm. Once the temperature reached the designated level, H2O2 was cautiously added drop by drop to prevent a potentially hazardous exothermic reaction. Due to the highly exothermic nature of the reaction, H2O2 was added gradually. Following the leaching duration, the mixture underwent vacuum filtration, separating the residue from the leaching solution, which was then washed with acetone. Finally, the residue was dried at 80 °C in a vacuum overnight, and the chemical composition was analyzed by an XRF spectrometer.

Synthesis Procedure of the Ionic Liquids

Ammonium-based and phosphonium-based bifunctional ionic liquids such as Ali-IL and Cyp-IL were synthesized, respectively.36,39 The synthetic scheme and molecular structures of bifunctional ILs are shown in Figure 1a,b. We first synthesized the Cyphos-EH moiety using trihexyl phosphine and 2-ethylhexyl chloride to synthesize the phosphonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid such as Cyp-IL. After that, the same molar concentration of Cyphos-EH and D2EHPA in heptane was allowed to stir at room temperature for 2 h and transferred to a separating funnel. Then, a 0.5 M NaHCO3 solution was added to promote Cyp-IL formation. This process of adding the 0.5 M NaHCO3 solution was repeated twice. NaHCO3 was added in a volume equal to that of the reaction mixture. Solvent and other impurities were removed at reduced pressure with a rotary evaporator, and the ionic liquids were dried in a vacuum for 5 h at 70 °C.

Results and Discussion

Characterizations of Ionic Liquids

The structural features of both ionic liquids were examined by comparing FT-IR spectra of Cyp-EH, Aliquat 336, D2EHPA, and the bifunctional ionic liquids, as shown in Figure S1. This characterization was mainly focused on changes in three different functional groups, such as P–OH, P=O, and the hydrogen bond peak. The peaks appearing within the range of 1064–1282 cm–1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of P=O bonds.36

These P=O, P–O, and P–OH bond changes are likely due to the interactions between organophosphate anions and Cyp-EH and Aliquat 336 cations. This evidence further confirms the formation of bifunctional ionic liquids.40,41

The bifunctional ionic liquids were further characterized by 1HNMR as illustrated in Figure S2a,b. The number of protons corresponds appropriately to the structures.

Optimization of Leaching Conditions

The effect of different parameters, for instance, ionic liquid concentrations, leaching time, temperature, solid–liquid (S/L ratio), and H2O2 percentage, on leaching efficiency was studied to optimize the leaching conditions for both ionic liquids. Figure 2a demonstrates a continuous rise in the leaching percentage of Mo and V with an increasing reaction time. Following a 3 h leaching period, vanadium and molybdenum exhibited maximum leaching efficiencies exceeding 80 and 90% for Cyp-IL and over 70 and 80% for Ali-IL, respectively. Thus, an optimal leaching duration of 3 h is considered.

Effect of (a) leaching time, (b) temperature, (c) H2O2 percentage, and (d) S/L ratio on the leaching efficiencies of Mo and V.

Figure 2b illustrates the impact of temperature variation on the leaching process of both Mo and V by Cyp-IL and Ali-IL. Optimal leaching efficiencies for both metals were observed at 50 °C as higher temperatures are conducive to enhanced leaching. Hence, after evaluating the temperature’s influence on Mo and V leaching, 50 °C was identified as the most suitable temperature for both Cyp-IL and Ali-IL.

The leaching efficiencies of molybdenum and vanadium were relatively low in the absence of H2O2 as shown in Figure 2c. However, as the ratio of H2O2 was enhanced, the leaching of both Mo and V notably improved. This enhancement was due to the anionic complex formation of vanadium and molybdenum facilitated by the oxidizing nature of H2O2. Considering the exothermic nature of the H2O2 reaction, we opted for a 30% v/v ratio as being the most suitable for the leaching process.

We also optimized the effect of the solid–liquid ratio varying from 1:10 to 1:40 as shown in Figure 2d. It was noted that the most efficient and optimized solid-to-liquid (S/L) ratio was 1:30. Subsequent alterations in the solid/liquid ratios resulted in minimal changes in leaching efficiency.

Comparison of the Leaching Efficiency between Ali-IL and Cyp-IL

In the early time of the extraction and separation reaction, the increase in the concentration of ionic liquids improved the leaching performance of Mo and V, whereas other metals like nickel, iron, and aluminum were not leached significantly. Therefore, we included only Mo and V in the optimization process as shown in Figures 2 and 3. The increased leaching efficiencies of Mo and V with the concentration of ionic liquids were ascribed to the enhanced dissolution of metal ions formed due to the interaction of metal oxides and H2O2 in both ionic liquid solutions.

To assess and compare the leaching efficacy of the phosphonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid such as Cyp-IL, it was compared with the previously reported ammonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid Ali-IL.8,42,43 The structures of both ionic liquids are shown in Figure 1. The extraction and separation process were conducted under optimized conditions specifically employing 0.3 M Cyp-IL and H2O2 30%, an S/L ratio of 1/20 at 50 °C and 600 rpm for 3 h.

As depicted in Figure 3b, a comparative analysis using the previously reported ionic liquid Ali-IL reveals that the leaching efficiency of the developed ionic liquid Cyp-IL is superior for Mo and V extraction. Cyp-IL indicates a recovery rate of Mo (91%) and V (82%), whereas Ali-IL reveals a lower recovery rate of Mo (84%) and V (72%).

The enhanced leaching efficiency of phosphonium-based ionic liquids such as Cyp-IL over ammonium-based Ali-IL counterparts can be attributed to the unique chemical and physical properties of the phosphonium cations. Phosphonium-based ionic liquids generally possess higher thermal stability, lower melting points, and greater hydrophobicity compared with their ammonium counterparts. Phosphonium cations help stabilize the metal-anion complexes in the solution. While they do not directly participate in metal coordination due to the lack of lone pairs, their larger size and lower charge density can create a more stable ionic environment, promoting the solubility of these complexes. The bulky phosphonium cations can influence the solvation environment, affecting the overall ionic strength and viscosity of the IL. This can impact the leaching kinetics and efficiency. Anions containing oxygen, such as those derived from carboxylates or phosphates, have lone pairs that can coordinate with metal ions. This coordination helps to solubilize the metal ions from the spent catalyst into the IL solution. Anions can also participate in the redox reactions required for leaching. For example, they can interact with oxidizing agents like H2O2 to enhance the oxidative leaching of metal ions. These properties facilitate better solubility for metal-containing species and improve the extraction and separation efficiency during the metal recovery process. The specific molecular structure and properties of phosphonium-based ionic liquids make them more effective for metal recovery applications than their ammonium-based counterparts. Similar to phosphonium cations, ammonium cations stabilize anion-metal complexes. They help maintain the solubility of these complexes within the IL solution. Ammonium cations are generally more hydrophilic compared with phosphonium cations. This can lead to differences in solubility and leaching dynamics, particularly when water is involved as a cosolvent or reaction medium. In summary, the effectiveness of phosphonium- and ammonium-based ILs in leaching metal ions from spent catalysts largely depends on the specific interactions of the anions with the metal ions, while the cations play supportive roles in stabilizing the solution and influencing the solvation environment.

Recovery of V and Mo

Vanadium can be recovered by selective precipitation from the mixture of other metal species from an alkaline medium.39,46,47 This can be done by stirring an equal volume of the organic phase and NH4Cl solution (2 mol/L) at 45 °C for 1 h maintaining the pH of 8–9. This method simultaneously enables the stripping and precipitation of V from the Cyp-IL- and Ali-IL-loaded phases. The precipitation percentage of vanadium for Cyp-IL- and Ali-IL-loaded phases was 94.8 and 94.5, respectively, with over 95% purity in both cases. The precipitation percentage was calculated following the same procedure as reported previously.8 The filtrate showed elevated levels of molybdenum (Mo) as well as small quantities of vanadium (V), nickel (Ni), and other metal ions which were determined by ICP-OES. After V(V) precipitation, the concentration of metal ions in the filtrate was in the range of 45–45 ppm for V(V), 940–955 ppm for Mo(VI), and 67–75 ppm for Ni(II) in the case of both ionic liquid-leached solutions. The precipitated compound was characterized as NH4VO3. The precipitate was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM).

The morphological feature of the precipitate was elucidated through scanning electron microscopy (SEM) as shown in Figure 5c. All the analysis data (Figures 4a and 5a,c) for NH4VO3 closely align with the data in previously reported studies.8,48,49

Following the recovery of vanadium via precipitation, the concentration of metal ions in the filtrate was measured. The filtrate exhibited a higher concentration of molybdenum (Mo) along with minor amounts of vanadium (V) and nickel (Ni) and small quantities of other metal ions. Selective precipitation of Mo over other metals in an alkaline environment can be easily achieved.8,50 BaCl2 was used to recover Mo from the filtrate for this purpose. An equal volume of a 0.2 mol/L BaCl2 solution and the filtrate was combined at 50 °C for 1 h. The findings indicated that the precipitation percentage of Mo(VI) was around 98% with a purity of over 90%, and it was determined to have the chemical formula of barium molybdate (BaMoO4) through XRD (Figure 5b), EDS (Figure 4b), and SEM (Figure 5d) analysis.

Techno-Economic Analysis of the Process

The techno-economic analysis of the proposed method using the process for leaching and recovering Mo and V from spent HDS catalysts involves several key considerations. First, we achieved high leaching efficiencies with Cyp-IL (over 90% for Mo and over 80% for V). This high efficiency implies an effective resource utilization. Cyp-IL performs superior to Ali-IL in selectively leaching and recovering V and Mo. A noteworthy aspect is the recyclability of the ionic liquid, allowing for its reuse in subsequent leaching processes, contributing to cost savings, and minimizing environmental impact. This method not only achieves efficient metal recovery but also aligns with sustainability goals by reducing the environmental footprints associated with traditional recovery methods. However, a comprehensive techno-economic analysis should delve into the production costs, market values of recovered metals, scalability, and regulatory considerations to provide a holistic understanding of the economic viability of this green resource recovery approach.

Conclusions

The ammonium-based ionic liquid Aliquat 336 is known for its unique characteristics of versatility and affordability. For the comparison study in this work, an ammonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid Ali-IL, derived from Aliquat 336, was compared with a phosphonium-based bifunctional ionic liquid Cyp-IL for the recovery of molybdenum and vanadium from industrial waste resources. We found that Cyp-IL indicates a higher recovery rate of Mo 91% and V 82.5%. In comparison, Ali-IL reveals a lower recovery rate of Mo 85% and V 72%. As the phosphonium-based ionic liquids possess higher thermal stability, lower melting points, and greater hydrophobicity compared with ammonium-based ILs, they have better solubility for metal-containing species. The larger size and lower charge density of phosphonium cations also contribute to stronger interactions with metal ions, which culminate in enhanced extraction and separation processes. These extraction and separation processes using the phosphonium-based bifunctional ILs enable the sustainable and eco-friendly recovery of vanadium and molybdenum compounds from industrial waste resources.
