Personalized External Aortic Root Support Before Pregnancy in Heritable Thoracic Aortic Disease
Authors: Juul Molendijk, Marieke I. van Schie, Daniëlle Robbers-Visser, Berto J. Bouma, Dave R. Koolbergen, Anke Heitkamp, Carolien Roos, Martijn A. Oudijk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeritable thoracic aortic disease (HTAD) increases the risk of aortic dissection, particularly during pregnancy and the puerperium. Standard management includes beta-blockers and, in selected cases, prophylactic aortic root surgery. Personalized external aortic root support (PEARS) offers an extravascular approach to aortic root support, but pregnancy data are scarce. This case series describes pregnancies in women with HTAD after PEARS.Case SummaryThree pregnancies in 2 women with Marfan syndrome and familial thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection who underwent preconception PEARS are presented. No dissections occurred. Aortic dimensions remained stable throughout pregnancy and follow-up (5 and 34 months). Pregnancies were uncomplicated, and all births were vaginal. Postpartum hemorrhage due to retained placenta occurred in all cases and was managed medically and by manual removal at the operating theater. Neonatal outcomes were favorable.ConclusionIn this case series, PEARS seems to be a promising preconception strategy, with favorable cardiovascular, obstetric, and neonatal outcomes.

Heritable thoracic aortic disease (HTAD) comprises a group of genetic aortopathies characterized by progressive aortic root dilatation and an increased risk of dissection, particularly during pregnancy and the puerperium.1 HTAD includes syndromic forms, such as Marfan syndrome (MFS), and non-syndromic or familial thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (FTAAD). Preventive strategies in pregnancy rely on beta-blockers and prophylactic aortic root surgery in selected patients. Current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend prophylactic aortic root replacement in women with HTAD contemplating pregnancy when the aortic diameter exceeds 45 mm, and suggest that it may be considered for diameters between 40 and 45 mm.Take-Home Messages•The 3 successful pregnancies reported contribute to the growing evidence that pregnancy after personalized external aortic root support can be safe in selected women with heritable thoracic aortic disease, although additional data are needed.•Obstetric outcomes are generally favorable, though postpartum hemorrhage may occur and should be anticipated and managed according to standard care.

Conventional surgical options include composite valve-graft replacement (Bentall procedure) and valve-sparing root replacement (VSRR). Mechanical valves require lifelong anticoagulation, with attendant maternal and fetal risks, whereas biological valves and VSRR avoid this requirement. Personalized external aortic root support (PEARS) is a tissue-sparing alternative in which a custom macroporous mesh, designed using 3-dimensional imaging, is placed externally around the aortic root and ascending aorta, preserving the native valve and often avoiding cardiopulmonary bypass during surgery (Figures 1 and 2).2 PEARS has been associated with a lower perioperative risk profile, faster recovery, and a reduced risk of late infection, as the prosthesis does not come into contact with blood.2 Multicenter data of the first 200 consecutive patients (median age: 33 years, 73.5% MFS) reported a perioperative mortality rate of 0.5%, no occurrences of ascending (type A) dissections over 596 postoperative patient-years, and favorable valve function.3Figure 1PEARS ProsthesisA microporous, compliant polyester mesh sleeve designed to cease aortic dilatation and preserve aortic valve function. Pregnancy outcomes after PEARS remain limited and are derived from small case series. These emerging data emphasize the importance of documenting individual cases to expand the currently scarce evidence. Previous reports of pregnancies after PEARS have described favorable outcomes;4, 5, 6 however, several clinically relevant parameters, including birth weight and gestational age at delivery, were not reported. Accordingly, in this case series, we present 3 pregnancies in 2 women with HTAD after PEARS, managed by the pregnancy heart team at Amsterdam University Medical Center, reporting both cardiovascular and obstetric outcomes.

Methods

A retrospective case series was conducted including all pregnancies in women with HTAD who had undergone PEARS before conception and subsequently received antenatal and peripartum care from the pregnancy heart team at Amsterdam University Medical Center. Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records and included demographic characteristics, medical and obstetric history, imaging findings (echocardiography, computed tomography [CT], and/or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), and medication use. Given the small sample size, analyses were descriptive. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for publication of their clinical data.

Patient 1

A 33-year-old nulliparous woman (G1P0) with FTAAD without an identified pathogenic variant was evaluated at our center. Her family history was notable for her father, who carries a pathogenic MYH11 variant and presented with an acute type A aortic dissection requiring surgical repair with a Bentall procedure, followed by a subsequent type B aortic dissection. The patient received genetic counseling for the familial variant but declined genetic testing. At age 32, she was evaluated for surgical management of ascending aortic dilatation measuring 42 mm, given her family history of aortic dissection and desire for future pregnancy. The aortic valve was structurally normal with preserved function. During preoperative counseling, surgical options including VSRR and PEARS were discussed. The advantages and limitations of both strategies were reviewed, including the relative novelty of PEARS and the possibility of intraoperative conversion to cardiopulmonary bypass with root replacement if required. After shared decision-making, the patient opted to undergo PEARS. The patient conceived 6 months after the procedure. After PEARS, pregnancy risk was classified as modified World Health Organization (mWHO) II-III based on a postoperative aortic diameter <40 mm, indicating an intermediate increased risk of maternal mortality or moderate to severe morbidity.7 Blood pressure remained within normal limits throughout pregnancy. Beta-blockers were not administered because she preferred to avoid medication unless strictly necessary. The pregnancy progressed without complications, and she awaited spontaneous vaginal birth at a gestational age of 41 weeks and 6 days. No analgesia was administered despite the recommendation of the pregnancy heart team to use early epidural anesthesia. The postpartum period was complicated by hemorrhage due to a retained placenta, necessitating manual removal in the operating theater. Total blood loss was 1,700 mL. The patient was discharged home after 48 hours of postpartum observation. The neonate was small for gestational age, with a birth weight of 3,060 g (6th percentile), despite normal fetal growth and Doppler measurements during pregnancy. Serial echocardiography, CT, and/or MRI demonstrated stable aortic dimensions during pregnancy and the postpartum period, with the ascending aorta measuring 35 mm. No aortic dissection occurred during 13 months of postpartum follow-up.

Patient 2

Pregnancy 1

A 31-year-old nulliparous woman (G1P0) with MFS caused by a de novo FBN1 mutation was evaluated for progressive aortic root dilatation. Preoperative imaging demonstrated an aortic root diameter of 50 mm. The aortic valve was structurally normal with preserved function. During preoperative counseling, surgical options including VSRR and PEARS were discussed. The potential advantages and limitations of both procedures were discussed, as well as the possibility of intraoperative conversion to cardiopulmonary bypass with root replacement if required. After shared decision-making, the patient elected to undergo PEARS at age 29. CT imaging before and after PEARS is shown in Figures 3C and 3D.

The patient conceived 18 months after the procedure. After PEARS, pregnancy risk was classified as mWHO III based on a postoperative aortic root diameter of 45 mm, indicating a severely increased risk of maternal mortality or severe morbidity.7 Blood pressure remained well controlled throughout pregnancy with beta-blocker therapy consisting of metoprolol 25 mg daily. The pregnancy was uncomplicated, and an assisted vaginal delivery was performed because of suspected fetal distress after spontaneous labor onset at 39 weeks and 6 days of gestation. Epidural analgesia was administered early during labor. The postpartum period was complicated by hemorrhage due to a partly retained placenta, necessitating manual removal in the operating theater. Total blood loss was 1,100 mL. The patient was discharged home after 48 hours of postpartum observation. The neonate had a normal birth weight of 3,345 g (41st percentile). Serial echocardiography, CT, and/or MRI demonstrated stable aortic dimensions during pregnancy and the postpartum period, with the aortic root measuring 42 mm. No aortic dissection occurred during 3 years of postpartum follow-up.

Pregnancy 2

At age 33, the woman completed a second pregnancy, which occurred 33 months after the PEARS procedure and again was classified as mWHO III. Blood pressure remained well controlled throughout pregnancy with beta-blocker therapy consisting of metoprolol 25 mg daily. The pregnancy progressed without complications, and vaginal birth occurred after induction of labor at a gestational age of 41 weeks and 2 days. Epidural analgesia was administered early during labor. The postpartum period was complicated by hemorrhage due to a partly retained placenta, necessitating manual removal in the operating theater. Total blood loss was 2,150 mL. She was discharged home after 48 hours of postpartum observation. The neonate was appropriate for gestational age with a birth weight 3,530 g (27th percentile). Serial echocardiography, CT, and/or MRI demonstrated stable aortic dimensions during pregnancy and the postpartum period, with the aortic root measuring 43 mm. No aortic dissection occurred during 5 months of postpartum follow-up.

Discussion

This case series describes 3 successful pregnancies in 2 women with HTAD after PEARS, contributing to the limited evidence on cardiovascular and obstetric outcomes of pregnancy after PEARS. In all cases, aortic dimensions remained stable throughout pregnancy and postpartum follow-up, and no dissections occurred. All births were vaginal at term; retained placenta and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) complicated each delivery and were managed without escalation beyond standard care.

Pregnancy is associated with hemodynamic and hormonal changes that increase shear stress on the vasculature, predisposing to aortic root dilatation. In the general population, the risk of aortic dissection increases 4- to 25-fold during pregnancy.7 In women with HTAD, this risk is further amplified, underscoring the importance of preconception counseling and, in selected cases, prophylactic aortic intervention. PEARS is as a tissue-sparing alternative to conventional root surgery. It preserves the native aortic valve, typically avoids cardiopulmonary bypass, and eliminates the need for lifelong anticoagulation, making it an appealing option for women contemplating pregnancy. As the implant is positioned external to the circulation, it is not associated with a risk of endocarditis.2

At our institution, PEARS is considered in patients with genetic aortic disease and moderate aortic dilatation with preserved valve function or mild to moderate aortic regurgitation. Patients are counseled regarding alternative strategies, including VSRR, and management is individualized based on anatomy, valve function, and patient preference. In this setting, PEARS is typically selected as a less invasive, valve-preserving option.

PEARS stabilizes the aortic root, reduces wall stress, and prevents further dilatation.2 Although the aortic root is not resected, a reduction in diameter may be observed after implantation because of external support from the custom-made prosthesis, typically sized to match or slightly undersize the native aortic dimensions. In our cohort, serial imaging demonstrated reduced aortic dimensions that remained stable throughout pregnancy.

Outside pregnancy, observational data from the first 200 PEARS patients, evaluated over several hundred patient-years, reported 1 asymptomatic Stanford type B dissection 3 years postsurgery, consistent with our findings. Aortic root interventions, including PEARS, reduce type A dissection risk; however, type B dissections may occur despite prior surgery.8 Beyond 2 single-case reports,4,5 a recent series of 9 pregnancies in 7 women with HTAD (predominantly MFS) demonstrated stable aortic dimensions and no dissections during a mean postpartum follow-up of 4.3 years.6 As no intervention eliminates distal risk, pregnancies in women with HTAD require individualized counseling and structured imaging surveillance in specialized centers, in accordance with European Society of Cardiology guidelines.7

Obstetric outcomes in our patients were favorable, with no hypertensive disorders and well-controlled blood pressure. Adequate analgesia during labor is generally recommended to minimize hemodynamic stress.5 One patient declined neuraxial analgesia without complications. Preterm birth occurs in approximately 15% of HTAD pregnancies versus 7% in the general population,1,9 and neonates small for gestational age occur in approximately 14% versus 10%.1,9 In our series, all infants were delivered at term, with 1 neonate small for gestational age. Patient 2, with MFS, received low-dose beta-blockers and delivered neonates appropriate for gestational age, although beta-blockers have been associated with lower birth weight.1,7 Prior reports of pregnancy after PEARS did not include detailed obstetric outcomes; our study adds these data.

All deliveries were complicated by PPH due to retained placenta, necessitating manual removal. Previous studies have reported an increased incidence of PPH in women with MFS compared with the general population,10 although data in FTAAD remain scarce. In these studies, PPH was defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL. Although uterotonic agents are standard in the prevention and management of PPH, agents such as ergometrine are often avoided in patients with aortopathies because of their potential to induce hypertension or vasospasm.7 This cautious approach may contribute to a higher observed incidence of PPH. There is no evidence of increased uterine atony in this population.

Conclusions

Pregnancy after PEARS was associated with favorable outcomes in our case series. These findings support PEARS as a potential option for selected women with HTAD contemplating pregnancy. However, the retrospective design, small sample size, and limited follow-up remain important limitations. Larger prospective studies are needed to better define long-term outcomes in this high-risk population.
