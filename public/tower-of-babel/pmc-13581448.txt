Strengthening Parenting Among Refugees in Europe (SPARE): Feasibility of an Adapted Parenting Intervention for Ukrainian Refugees in Iceland
Authors: Margrét Sigmarsdóttir, Bergljót Gyða Guðmundsdóttir, Ásthildur Helga Bragadóttir, Ársæll Már Arnarsson, Marion S Forgatch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn recent years, the flow of refugees has grown considerably due to increasing upheaval worldwide. Host countries can help mitigate adjustment problems for refugee families by offering evidence‐based support programs. The Strengthening Parenting Among Refugees in Europe (SPARE) program builds on and adapts the intervention Generation Parent Management Training—Oregon Model (GenerationPMTO). SPARE supports parents in their parenting role and helps them adapt to a new cultural environment. The aim of this study was to investigate the feasibility, acceptability, and impact of the intervention on Ukrainian refugee parents in Iceland. The adaptations address trauma, culture, and context. A total of 14 families participated, with 22 children up to 18 years old. Parents attended SPARE parent group sessions for 12 weeks and completed questionnaires before and after the program, as well as at the end of each session. Additionally, participants were asked to share their views and experiences in a focus group interview. Participants found SPARE to be feasible for Ukrainian refugees in Iceland; they were satisfied with the program and reported that it had a positive impact on their families. These findings are consistent with those of previous feasibility studies regarding SPARE in Europe, providing more support for continued research and development. SPARE may be an important resource for refugee‐hosting countries to offer Ukrainian refugee families to cope with trauma and adjust to new surroundings.Keywords: adaptation, evidence‐based intervention, parenting practices, refugees, Ukraine

Introduction

Over the past decade, the number of refugees fleeing their homes has risen significantly. According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), almost 43 million people were forcibly displaced in 2012. By June 2024, this number had grown to over 122 million people, of whom nearly 44 million were refugees (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) 2024). Icelandic authorities define refugees as people fleeing their homeland due to a legitimate fear of being persecuted for their race, religion, nationality, or political opinion and who are unable to obtain protection from their home country, those at risk of serious harm or death if returned to their home country (e.g., due to armed conflict), as well as stateless individuals (Foreign Nationals Act No. 80/2016, n.d.). Individuals from war‐torn areas often have experienced a variety of stress factors in their homeland as well as repeated exposure to life‐threatening challenges as refugees. Upon arrival in a host country, they can face additional difficulties, such as precarious living conditions, unemployment, learning a new language, and adjusting to a new culture. These experiences may be particularly stressful for families with children, with an increased risk of disruption in effective child rearing practices (Fegert et al. 2018).

The Russian invasion of Ukraine and resulting war has significantly increased the number of Ukrainian refugees in Europe, who receive temporary protection in several European countries, including Iceland (Government of Iceland 2022). Temporary protection includes access to healthcare, social services, housing, and work and residence permits (Foreign Nationals' Right to Work Act No. 97/2002, n.d.). Because of the increased risk for adjustment problems among refugee families (Fegert et al. 2018; Gewirtz et al. 2022), it is important to offer this group effective and tailored support programs. This study focuses on one such program, Strengthening Parenting Among Refugees in Europe (SPARE; Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025), an adaptation of the evidence‐based method Generation Parent Management Training—Oregon model (GenerationPMTO; Forgatch and Patterson 2010). Here, results are presented from a feasibility study of an adaptation of SPARE for Ukrainian parents in Iceland.

Parenting Interventions and Adaptations

Reviews of evidence‐based treatments for child adjustment problems find that behavioral interventions that strengthen parenting skills generally yield positive results for children and their families (e.g., Dishion et al. 2016; Mingebach et al. 2018). GenerationPMTO has been successfully adapted to suit the needs of different groups worldwide (Rains et al. 2021), and several programs based on GenerationPMTO have undergone extensive study showing its positive effects on child adjustment, parenting practices, and parent well‐being (e.g., Cai et al. 2022). SPARE is based on a group version of GenerationPMTO, Parenting Through Change (PTC; Forgatch and Patterson 2010; Forgatch et al. 2009) with the addition of material to address trauma from another adaptation of GenerationPMTO, After Deployment Adaptive Parenting Tools (ADAPT; Gewirtz et al. 2014, 2016, 2018).

The original evidence‐based intervention GenerationPMTO was developed by Patterson and colleagues at the Oregon Social Learning Center in the United States (e.g., Forgatch and Patterson 2010). GenerationPMTO helps refine core parenting practices, including skill encouragement, setting limits, monitoring and supervision, problem solving, and positive involvement. Parents also enhance their skills in giving clear directions, emotion regulation, communication, and effective home‐school collaboration (Forgatch and Patterson 2010; Rains et al. 2021). Details can be found in articles, chapters, and parent books (e.g., Dishion et al. 2016; Domenech Rodríguez et al. 2026; Forgatch et al. 2017; Rains et al. 2021). The program has been translated, provided to families in several languages, and adapted to many specific populations (e.g., Bjørknes et al. 2012; Gewirtz et al. 2016, 2018; Parra‐Cardona et al. 2017; Wieling et al. 2015). Within Europe, Norway, Iceland, the Netherlands, and Denmark have successfully implemented GenerationPMTO (Askeland et al. 2019; Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2019, 2026) for a quarter century. SPARE is the result of these four countries' collaboration. SPARE has been shown to be feasible for Arabic‐speaking refugees in those countries (Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025), wherein the main adaptations addressed the following: (a) Trauma, by adding two components (mindfulness and emotion coaching) from the ADAPT program (Gewirtz et al. 2016, 2018); (b) Culture, with more time and attention in each session devoted to cultural discussions and the experience of being a refugee; (c) Language, metaphors, goals, concepts, and illustrations being adapted to be made relevant to the particular group of refugees; (d) Employment of link‐workers who are selected from the refugees' culture and work with the therapists to bridge the gap between cultures. More information about the adaptation is provided in the Method section.

Current Study

Feasibility studies of adaptations of evidence‐based programs evaluate a program's viability and potential for success. Such studies can identify changes that may better suit the needs of a focal group with respect to cultural/ethnic background, age, or religion, and suggested changes (Bowen et al. 2009). This study, the first SPARE investigation conducted with Ukrainian families in Iceland, examines whether SPARE is feasible as a support for these families. It is a part of a larger project examining SPARE among refugee families in Europe (Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025). The following research questions are addressed: (1) What was the parents' experience of participating in SPARE? and (2) Did they find the program suitable for supporting Ukrainian parents and children following relocation to a new country and culture? The Ecological Validity Model by Bernal et al. (1995) (see also Domenech Rodríguez et al. 2011) guided the adaptation process of SPARE for Arabic‐speaking refugees as well as for Ukrainian refugee families.

Method

Participants

Of the 15 parents who started the course, 14 completed and participated in the present study. Parents ranged in age between 26 and 55. Of the 14 study participants, nine were single mothers and five participants were married, with one father in the married group. Note that the limited representation of fathers may be due to the fact that many men were required to stay in Ukraine under wartime mobilization restrictions. Participants represented a total of 22 children (59% boys), up to the age of 18. Most participants spoke Ukrainian in their home (53%), followed by Russian (27%). All families had lived in Iceland for up to 1 year. Based on extant data, the study sample appears characteristic of the larger population of Ukrainian refugees in Iceland (Statistics Iceland 2024). Measures

Attendance, Homework Completion, and Participant Satisfaction

SPARE therapists monitored participant attendance (present/not present) and homework completion for each session. Homework completion was recorded as a yes if parents completed any part of the homework assigned; otherwise as a no.

Parent satisfaction was assessed using self‐report questionnaires completed at the end of each session, administered by a research team member once therapists and link‐workers had left the room to mitigate risk of social desirability bias. Satisfaction questionnaires for each session were adapted from Adapt Parenting Tools (Gewirtz et al. 2018), where questions are rated on a 5‐point scale ranging from not true to very true. Satisfaction measures administered in sessions 1–11 comprised five subscales (see also Table 2): Parent satisfaction (three items), Positive group experiences (eight items), Home practice satisfaction (four items, administered in sessions 2–11), Negative group experiences (three items), and Homework difficulty (two items, administered in sessions 2–11). Satisfaction scores are computed by calculating the mean of relevant item ratings (negatively worded items are reverse scored).

Focus Group Interview

Focus group interviews are an efficient data collection method (Hennink et al. 2011), particularly suitable when researching topics on which limited information exists, including evaluating specific services (Bender 2021). Interviewers must be flexible and ask open‐ended questions that allow the discussion to flow (Fontana and Frey 1994).

All participants were invited to participate in a focus group interview immediately following the last SPARE session. Thirteen parents, all of whom had completed the course, took part in the interview. The interview lasted approximately 90 min and addressed three major topics: SPARE methods and materials, parent group experiences, and the importance of examining cultural differences in relation to refugee parent education. The interview was conducted by two research team members in English; the link‐worker simultaneously interpreted questions and answers from English to Ukrainian between interviewers and parents.

Intervention

The Ukrainian adaptation of SPARE followed the same content and procedures as the original version provided to Arabic‐speaking families. Sessions were delivered in English by two Icelandic therapists who were experienced psychologists and trained GenerationPMTO therapists with a 2‐day additional training in delivering SPARE. A total of two link‐workers supported the group, one at a time, translating and culturally adapting material for the parents. Although Ukrainian was the link‐workers' native language, one of them was also proficient in Russian. Both had a university degree and received brief training in the program and in how to serve as a link‐worker. Both link‐workers had arrived in Iceland as refugees.

In addition to strengthening parenting practices and minimizing coercion, SPARE goals include helping families address trauma, promoting effective emotion regulation, and supporting adjustment to new customs and culture (Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025). The 12 parent group sessions include all key components of GenerationPMTO (Forgatch and Patterson 2010). Every session followed an agenda in which homework assignments were reviewed and a new component was introduced. Session topics covered positive parenting skills, including encouragement (i.e., incentive charts and token systems using a token economy), limit setting (i.e., time‐out, privilege removals, work chores), monitoring, problem solving, and positive involvement (see session topics in Table 4; for more detailed information see Forgatch and Patterson 2010). Two ADAPT program components were added to address trauma (Gewirtz et al. 2016, 2018), namely mindfulness (observing) and emotion coaching. Guided mindfulness exercises in Ukrainian were played in every session (Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025); emotion coaching was a specific focus of sessions 8 and 11, emphasizing emotion regulation related to traumatic experiences (Gewirtz et al. 2018). Parents had access to mindfulness exercises at home and were encouraged to complete them with their children.

To encourage participation, parents arriving on time could participate in a lottery and win small prizes (e.g., snacks, stickers, coloring books, card games, candles, and gift cards from restaurants, community swimming pools, and amusement parks). Between sessions, parents received a brief phone call from a link‐worker reminding them of their homework and helping them solve any problems they experienced with the home practice assignment. The link‐worker also would offer further explanation about topics from the previous session if needed. At the end of each session, parents received homework to be reviewed at the beginning of the next session. Homework revolved around the topic of each session; for example, following the session on encouragement, parents were supported to try out an incentive chart or a token economy with their children (see Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025).

Parent material was translated and back translated to Ukrainian from the English SPARE version, and language and illustrations were adapted by a Ukrainian translator and a link‐worker. There was a special focus on the wording, especially regarding the limit setting component, given that this may be a sensitive issue among Ukrainian parents, based on link‐worker expertise and extant literature (e.g., Burlaka et al. 2025; Grogan‐Kaylor et al. 2018). Homework was structured, simple, and contained less text and more illustrations that were made more suitable to Ukrainian culture. All sessions were held in the same location, after business hours, based on parent availability. Transport was made available for parents after each session and children could come with parents if needed. Before each session, therapists and link‐workers prepared the session; for example, they would discuss culturally appropriate role‐play themes (e.g., regarding setting limits) and relevant routines and skills to work with in an incentive chart or token system. Examples of role plays of special importance were practicing how to observe and regulate emotions and how to use active communication skills and emotion coaching with children. Link‐workers were trained in translating each role directly and parents then practiced what they had observed.

Procedure

The study was approved by the National Bioethics Committee in Iceland (reference no. VSNb2020010017/03.01). Accordingly, all participants provided informed consent for participation in the study.

Participant recruitment was conducted soon after the first Ukrainian refugees arrived in Iceland. Recruitment strategies were aligned with welfare system procedures that were developed, and participants were recruited only from one municipality, the capital of Iceland. Following recruitment, SPARE was introduced to prospective participants by the GenerationPMTO center in the city, a week before the first SPARE session. SPARE therapists, link‐workers, and research team members from the University of Iceland delivered the introduction. Parents were informed that SPARE was an educational course for refugee families intended to support them through the hardships and stress of having had to flee their home country. Additionally, parents received practical information such as how many weeks the intervention would last, how often and for how long the group would meet, and that telephone contact would be made between sessions for support, especially in relation to home practice assignments. At the end of the meeting, parents were invited to participate in the present study. Interested parents signed an informed consent form and completed a demographics questionnaire. The study was conducted in fall 2023. Although not a focus of the current study, therapist fidelity was monitored and found to be high, consistent with findings reported by Sigmarsdóttir et al. (2025).

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses of parent demographics and satisfaction survey data were collected, including means and standard deviations for total scores and subscales using Microsoft Excel. The focus group interview was conducted in English and simultaneously translated to Ukrainian by the link‐worker. The interview was recorded and transcribed verbatim in English and read and reread in detail for qualitative analysis. Transcribed interview data were coded and analyzed according to qualitative content analysis (Graneheim and Lundman 2004) independently by a research team member, emphasizing manifest or descriptive rather than latent content. To enhance rigor, two additional research team members reviewed the analysis including the interview recording and transcript and collaboratively resolved any inconsistencies. To identify and analyze patterns related to the three main themes of the study, an interview guide based on a template by Parra‐Cardona et al. (2016) was used, following procedures reported by Sigmarsdóttir et al. (2023, 2025). Focus group findings are divided into three sections per the major themes of the interview guide: (a) Importance of parenting materials and SPARE methods; (b) Parents' reflections and experiences of participating in the group; and (c) Importance of examining cultural differences when working with refugee parents on parenting education.

Positionality Statement

Members of the research team, all of whom were born and raised in Iceland or the United States, have professional backgrounds in education, social, and/or health sciences and extensive experience working with families of diverse backgrounds in both research and applied settings. Although many of the authors are also parents, none of them are refugees or have experienced forced migration; hence, their perspectives may differ from those of participants, which may have influenced the research process, including data collection and analysis. To address this, the research team engaged in ongoing reflection and discussion, considering alternative perspectives, and critically examining their assumptions and interpretations. Moreover, the authors recognize that the intersectionality of human identities and backgrounds, as well as systemic inequities, shape family experiences and access to resources, including psychosocial support. The research team is committed to expanding access to culturally responsive and effective parenting support for diverse families and acknowledges that this commitment, as well as their own perspectives, may impact their research and the knowledge it yields.

Results

Session attendance averaged 82% (median = 84%). Homework completion, assessed by therapists, was estimated at 86% average across participants and sessions (median = 87%). For several sessions, full (100%) attendance or homework completion were recorded (see Table 4).

Participant program satisfaction based on their ratings in each Session 1–11 was high (see Table 2). Parents agreed with course ideas, found the group useful, and had positive experiences. Parents felt that they actively participated, were open to new information, and that the therapists encouraged participation and were understanding. Participants also were mostly satisfied with homework assignments (see Table 2), completed them successfully, found them useful, and felt that they suited their family life well and that their children responded well to them. Regarding negative group experiences, participants indicated they did not feel irritated, sad, or criticized.

At the end of the program, participants rated their overall satisfaction, how helpful or useful they found it to be with respect to specific sessions (see Table 3). Overall, participants were satisfied with the course, found it helpful, felt that the therapists understood them, and reported they would participate in a future parenting group based on their experience. Participants noticed positive changes in their children's behavior and found course components helpful overall. Additionally, participants liked giving clear directions, finding solutions, communicating with children, and managing emotions. On average, limit setting was rated somewhat lower than the other components, albeit with greater variability. A closer examination of satisfaction data revealed that lower scores for limit setting resulted from lower ratings provided by four participants, whereas the remaining 10 participants rated it more positively. Homework was considered useful overall, and participants found the therapists to be skillful. Participants said they would recommend SPARE to others (for more details see Table 3).

Participants were asked what they would like to change to increase the program's accessibility. A few noted they would have liked the course to be held in a more central location for more convenient access. One said she would have liked the therapists to speak her native language, as occasionally they had difficulty understanding each other. Most were happy with the program; however, they felt no changes were needed. They also said it was interesting and useful as an aid in raising their children. When asked what changes would make the course more helpful, several participants recommended that the children join the course for a few sessions and suggested dividing the group by the children's age. A few would have liked an advanced course for the same group, and some said the course needed no improvement.

Focus Group Interview

Results from the focus group interview are divided into three sections per the major themes of the interview guide: (a) Importance of parenting materials and SPARE methods, following the same order as presented to parents (see Table 4); (b) Parents' reflections and experiences of participating in the parenting group; (c) Importance of examining cultural differences when parenting materials and education are communicated to refugee parents.

Importance of Parenting Materials and SPARE Methods

Overall, participants were satisfied with SPARE methods and materials. Although most were able to use the tools in everyday situations, some noted that their applicability depended upon the child's age and emphasized that child‐rearing should be tailored to each child's needs. Participant A said: “Not all tools can be used for all children. It depends on the family, on the child's situation”. On a related note, Participant B stated that the tools were helpful to “organize which demands to put on the child.” Parents felt it was important to be able to react appropriately to a given situation and remarked that different tools might be suitable for different circumstances, even later in a child's life (e.g., adolescence).

With respect to specific tools, most parents agreed on the importance of giving clear directions. All of them described prior experiences of ineffective communication with their children, resulting in conflict. Recognizing the benefits of clear directions, the parents understood why their children had appeared not to be listening prior to attending SPARE and reported improvements in parent–child communication following SPARE participation. Participant C said:

I often give orders when I'm busy and often don't understand what orders I'm giving. Then my child ignores my orders, it's very stressful for me [and the child], as my feelings are very sensitive. So now I'm starting to pay attention to how I'm giving directions and stop myself if I'm giving unclear instructions.

Participant C's story is characteristic of many participants who had experienced a certain level of exhaustion, even resignation, in raising their children. This, in turn, caused emotional distress and an escalation in negative communication, which created tension in the home. Participants were surprised at how effective clear directions were at increasing the child's willingness to cooperate and thus improving communication in the home.

Regarding the encouragement tool, parents usually agreed on the importance of praising their children, although it was often on the back burner given the stresses of life as a refugee family. SPARE reminded them of the significance of praise and encouraged them to point out to their children what they did well. Participant D said: “Sometimes we forget to encourage our children” and Participant E added: “I think it is very important to praise our children.” Participants agreed that praise was a good way to increase children's self‐confidence. The parents found the incentive chart useful, and some adopted it informally or without much conscious thought. Participant F said: “We didn't do it in writing, but verbally we did, it just came out somehow.” In contrast, some participants didn't think the incentive chart was suitable for older children and teenagers. Participant B said, “And again, it works for small children, less [so] for older ones.” More parents than not, however, adopted the incentive chart and started giving their children tokens to encourage positive behavior. Many had positive experiences with the token system and used it not only to encourage desired behavior but also to spend time together. Thus, the reward often became a source of quality time. Participant F shared her success: “My daughter has collected 15 elastic bands [tokens], and we went together to buy a present after that”. Participant D added: “For my child the token system works, she loves getting elastic bands.” Thus, the token system worked well and both parents and children appeared happy with it.

Participants were asked about limit setting to correct challenging behavior. Most were satisfied with it, although some parents had mixed feelings about the time‐out element and feared it would negatively affect the children. Discussions arose about the parents' own childhood experiences in Ukraine of harsh exclusionary discipline practices, where they were “put in a corner” for long periods of time. Parents associated this experience with limit setting and had difficulty distinguishing between the tool on one hand and how they felt excluded as children on the other. Many were hurt by this experience and feared going too far in disciplining their children. Participant F said:

In our childhood, exclusion was used; we were punished; we had to ‘stay in a corner’ for some time. There wasn't even a chair in the corner, just standing in the corner. And it wasn't just five minutes; it was an hour. I don't want my child to feel the way I felt in my childhood, because there was violence.

After discussing limit setting and its application in SPARE, however, many people's minds changed. Most agreed there was a big difference between the time‐out taught in SPARE and the exclusionary discipline that was common in their childhood. Participants were also more open to using privilege loss (e.g., screen time) to correct unwanted behavior. Participant F said: “Something that the child is enjoying, you take it away for some time” and Participant A said: “If you want to have some kind of punishment, we are doing it, like we don't let them go and visit friends.” Parents usually took the loss of privileges rather seriously and often it could last for a few days. Participant A said: “It varies for how long, depending on the situation. It's unlike in Iceland, just for five minutes. We often take away privileges from the child for three, four days at a time.”

As for monitoring, most parents believed they provide good supervision and appreciated SPARE's confirmation of its importance, especially as some felt that supervision in Iceland was not as strict as in Ukraine. Most felt competent in monitoring and supervising their child and even found it unnecessary to address it in the program. Participant A, however, said: “Of course it needs to be mentioned in the framework of this course, but I don't think we need this, we know this.” Despite these sentiments, they connected with the tool and saw its potential in further improving their approach.

When asked about the problem‐solving tool, parents emphasized the importance of finding solutions in conversation with the children, which fostered positive communication and solidarity between parents and children. Participant C described her experience:

My son has various problems at school. When something comes up, we sit down together and discuss the issues. He just sits in front of me, and we try to focus on the positive and even make fun of things. I always like to give examples and mention situations from my life. I always try to explain how it would have been better to act.

Participant D said she noticed a big difference in her daughter after they started talking things over instead of her lecturing her daughter. As a result, their relationship improved considerably, and she said, “My child says to me: ‘Mom, I'm so grateful for you. You have started to have such good conversations with me’, and [she] said that I am such a wonderful mother.” The parents agreed on the importance of meeting children where they are, in calm conversation, whenever possible.

Regarding mindfulness and emotion regulation, some found that the mindfulness exercises helped them find an inner calm that supported making better parenting decisions. Everyone agreed that dealing with one's own feelings is crucial. Participant F said: “We should all know that it is difficult to deal with our feelings, it is the most important but also the most difficult, we have to start with ourselves”, and Participant G agreed. Despite generally agreeing on the value of knowing one's own feelings, many found it difficult to find time for the introspection it requires. Participant E said: “Sometimes you just feel like you've finally found ten minutes to yourself, to be in privacy and relax, you don't necessarily have the energy for that.” Participants D and F agreed that it is complicated to concentrate amid the pressures of daily life. Participant H mentioned needing to work on feeling better as a parent in a new environment: “Because this is a completely new situation for us, we not only have to deal with our responsibilities but also support our children.”

Parents' Reflections and Experiences of Participating in the Group

Most participants had positive experiences of SPARE, especially due to the solidarity and peer support that they experienced. Participant G verbalized the experience of many parents: “In the beginning, we met and didn't know each other at all, and then we got to know each other and became closer, and it has become a kind of tradition to meet once a week.” Participants had good experiences with SPARE therapists and link‐workers. Participant D stated: “We love our teachers on the course. They are also such good actors; they have the ability to make us relax… [they] have given us a lot of support.” All participants indicated they would recommend the course to others and felt they had learned a lot that supported them in becoming better parents. Some, however, pointed out there were no Ukrainian therapists, which meant that the therapists may have lacked understanding of the Ukrainian mentality. At times, participant F felt a lack of connection with the therapist. She said: “I had difficulty with the fact that two of the teachers were Icelandic, their mentality and experience is different from ours, sometimes it was a bit difficult to connect.” Conversely, another participant remarked that “being a parent is everywhere the same”, such that in her mind, cultural differences were virtually nonexistent. These comments demonstrate how some participants may have viewed this aspect of SPARE differently.

When asked if the course could be improved, some parents would have liked the children to be more involved. Participant A said: “Maybe the children should come along for some time. We can also do something special together with them. I think that would be beneficial.” Many of the participants believed their children's participation could enhance their well‐being. Participant E said: “For example, maybe there is a specific session for teenagers, so they get help from professionals to understand their feelings, or one session for children without parents, but of course age should be considered”. Parents also felt the groups should be arranged based on the children's age, as they found it to be relevant regarding the parenting tools.

Importance of Examining Cultural Differences When Working With Refugee Parents on Parenting Education

The parenting approaches taught in SPARE were in many ways different from what participants were used to. They did not always agree with course content, nor with what they had seen Icelandic parents do. Despite that, they generally felt positively about raising their children in Iceland. Differences included Ukrainian parents maintaining a lot of control over their children as well as closely monitoring the children's well‐being, especially after being forcibly displaced. Participant C said:

There was no need to teach us [monitoring] because we are always monitoring the physical and emotional state of our children. We are always watching them and talking to them. And when our children grow up, we will watch them all the time and they already know that we are going to watch them always.

Participants agreed that parenting was more complicated since the war. Many of them had a significant trauma history, resulting in discomfort that caused numerous challenges, including fatigue and a shorter fuse. They believed SPARE had helped them to better handle their role as parents, especially the support and encouragement tools. Participants all agreed that being forced to leave their home country was difficult and often felt that others did not understand them. The war not only changed their place of residence, but also their identity and understanding. On that note, Participant H said: “Our situation is very, very difficult, I feel that we are not the same people as we were in Ukraine. Ukrainian people are very anxious and under a lot of stress, I don't think Icelanders understand how we feel”. Participants talked about being very worried, feeling distressed, and needing more help. Participant F said: “You are not able to help us with all the problems we are facing”. Nevertheless, participants generally felt good in Iceland, and SPARE helped to reduce the marginalization they experienced. Participant D shared that “this course has helped me a lot to understand and feel that I'm just a normal person”. She added that having to run away from her home country was very difficult as she lost everything. Further, she said: “I feel like I died and now I'm starting my life over, like I have to learn everything all over again, learn Icelandic, find friends, try to adapt, look for a job and stuff like that”.

Discussion

This study examined the feasibility of SPARE as a support for Ukrainian refugee parents in Iceland. The following research questions were proposed: (1) What was the parents' experience of participating in SPARE? and (2) Did they find the program suitable for supporting Ukrainian parents and children following relocation to a new country and culture? Results indicate that SPARE is feasible for Ukrainian refugee families. Parent attendance, homework completion, and satisfaction throughout the course were good overall. For example, attendance was 82%, homework completion measured at 86%, and parent satisfaction after each session averaged 92%. This is in keeping with reports from prior feasibility studies on SPARE in Europe (Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025). Retention rate was high, with only one dropout. Factors such as between‐session contact, support in sessions, problem‐solving with parents, and providing them with useful tools could all have contributed to the high retention rate. Creating a welcoming meeting place for parents with a shared cultural background when facing displacement is also a likely contributing factor, mirroring what Parra‐Cardona and colleagues had shown with Latine parents (2017).

Both quantitative and qualitative results showed general satisfaction with the intervention, which also is in accordance with previous research on SPARE among Arabic‐speaking refugees (Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025) and other studies among minoritized groups (e.g., Parra‐Cardona et al. 2017; Parra‐Cardona 2019; Wieling et al. 2015). Specifically, participants noted the usefulness of the parent material and mindfulness exercises, consistent with reports by Gewirtz et al. (2016, 2018). Most participants associated course attendance with positive changes in their children's behavior and felt they could recommend it to other parents. Parents found the therapists to be understanding in complex situations. These findings are comparable, for example, to feasibility study results by Wieling et al. (2015) on PMTO implementation in Northern Uganda.

In the focus group interview, parents expressed great satisfaction with SPARE, in line with satisfaction data gathered throughout the program. They liked the atmosphere, found the therapists and link‐workers to be positive and supportive, and looked forward to attending classes. Parents applied the tools covered in SPARE (i.e., clear directions, encouragement, monitoring, problem‐solving, setting limits) and taught their children new skills. They found the tools and group role plays useful. Parents were also satisfied with the mindfulness exercises and found them helpful in getting out of the daily grind. Several of them emphasized that active listening and emotion regulation skills helped both themselves and their children find peace, to be more involved and appreciative, and to better regulate emotions. Parents also said they felt communication with their children had improved, and they directly linked it to more positive parenting methods at home. Parents felt that time spent with their children was now more enjoyable and that pressure and stress in relation to communication and parenting were reduced. These results are similar to previous findings regarding the experiences of mothers who participated in the original PTC group (Forgatch et al. 2009), where core components of the intervention strengthened their parenting and bolstered their relationship with the children.

Parents were generally satisfied with their participation in the program and rated the therapists and link‐workers positively. Some parents even mentioned they would have liked the course to be longer. When discussing how the course could be refined, some mentioned that they would have liked to see more child involvement as appropriate based on different age‐related needs. Everyone said they would recommend SPARE to others because they felt they came out as stronger parents, echoing results reported by Sigmarsdóttir et al. (2023, 2025).

Many participants felt there were differences between the parenting approaches presented in SPARE versus Ukraine, albeit not profound. This is noteworthy, however, because even though they felt Icelandic children lack discipline, they also found certain approaches within SPARE to be rather strict. Time‐out was mentioned specifically, because at first, some parents found it humiliating. It is relevant that many parents reported having had a coercive upbringing, in which they were excluded for long periods of time. They applied this experience to time‐out as presented in SPARE. A study by Koprowicz and Ponomarova (2026) indicated that Ukrainian parents hold a more authoritarian approach to parenting than Polish parents, for example. Similar differences may exist between Ukrainian parenting styles and those of their Nordic counterparts, with Nordic parents possibly sharing more similarities with Polish parents. In fact, data reported by Berkers and Sieben (2020) indicated that people from the Nordic countries and from Poland place greater value on child autonomy than obedience, whereas the opposite was true among Ukrainian participants. This is an interesting possibility requiring further study, which potentially normalizes the experiences reported in the group. Upon further explanation, the parents understood that time‐out is not an exclusionary discipline strategy; rather, it is a solution to help the child to cool down and find peace by taking a break, supported by numerous research studies, including among children with a history of trauma (Dadds and Tully 2019). These results reveal how crucial it is to introduce strategies such as time‐out accurately and delicately, to validate thoughts and feelings that may arise among parents with a history of trauma and simultaneously address potential misconceptions.

Cultural Considerations

The theoretical foundation of GenerationPMTO, Social Interaction Action Learning theory, informs the intervention's core components in all its adaptations (Domenech Rodríguez et al. 2026; Forgatch and Patterson 2010). The current adaptation of GenerationPMTO, SPARE, embodies the Ecological Validity Model (EVM), which ensures that research and intervention match cultural needs (Bernal et al. 1995). The EVM lists eight relevant dimensions: language, persons, metaphors, goals, concepts, context, content, and methods. Adding relevant cultural content to an evidence‐based program has benefits. For example, Parra‐Cardona et al. (2017) examined the efficacy of a cultural adaptation of GenerationPMTO in a randomized controlled trial with three conditions (waitlist control, culturally adapted, culturally adapted and enhanced). Both culturally adapted conditions reduced child internalizing concerns compared to the control condition. The culturally enhanced version, however, with two added culture‐specific sessions focusing on the ways in which immigration challenges and biculturalism may impact parenting, outperformed the other adaptation with significant improvements in father reports of child externalizing behavior (Parra‐Cardona et al. 2017). In SPARE, components were added to address trauma, a feature that cannot be ignored for refugee families, which seems to have been well received by the Ukrainian parents.

Despite feeling a sense of well‐being in Iceland, the parents experienced multiple challenges adjusting to new surroundings. Some parents would have liked for the therapists to speak Ukrainian, to allow for a more mutual understanding of everyone's cultural experiences and backgrounds. This is a noteworthy consideration for future development and implementation of SPARE, stressing the importance of the link‐workers' role. Moreover, and maybe a part of the explanation, although all parents in the group understood and spoke Ukrainian, some of them spoke Russian as their primary language, with Russian or a combination of Russian/Ukrainian spoken at home. Practical implications for future delivery of SPARE among Ukrainian parents might include ensuring that at least one parent who speaks Ukrainian as a native language be recruited to the group. Despite this, the participants found SPARE to be helpful, not only because it supported them in their parenting, but also because it facilitated their integration into Icelandic society, similar to results reported by Sigmarsdóttir et al. (2023, 2025) on SPARE for Arabic‐speaking parents. Throughout the course, the participants also connected with peers that allowed them to build their network and better integrate into society.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of the present study is that the intervention produced both quantitative and qualitative positive outcomes. Parents showed up for sessions, engaged in the home practice assignments, and reported satisfaction with the program. Another strength is that SPARE for Ukrainian refugees replicates positive findings from a similar SPARE adaptation for Arabic‐speaking refugees that was feasibility tested in Iceland, Norway, the Netherlands, and Denmark (Sigmarsdóttir et al. 2023, 2025).

A weakness of this study is the small sample size limiting generalizability. Can these positive findings be replicated with other groups of Ukrainian refugees in Iceland? Will this adaptation of SPARE for Ukrainian families in Iceland generalize to other host nations? Another limitation is that despite efforts to minimize social desirability risk (e.g., having research team members administer satisfaction measures in the absence of therapists and link‐workers), the possibility of such biases cannot be excluded entirely. For example, to facilitate a mutual understanding between participants and the interviewer, link‐workers had to be present for the focus group interview, which may have impacted participants' willingness to be forthcoming about their experiences. Additionally, the wide age range of children could be seen as a limitation. Some of the parents mentioned that they would have liked more age‐specific guidance; the absence of which may have prevented them from trying out some of the skills.

Future Directions

Findings from this study suggest many directions to advance the program. One next step is to replicate these findings with more groups in Iceland and expand to other nations where GenerationPMTO‐trained therapists are available. Besides Iceland, SPARE has been feasibility tested in Norway, the Netherlands, and Denmark, creating an excellent opportunity to examine SPARE among Ukrainian families there as well. Another step is to collect outcome data that assesses parenting practices and parent and child adjustment. Yet another step is to incorporate culturally specific issues related to immigration challenges and biculturalism, as suggested by Parra‐Cardona et al. (2017). Other future research and implementation of SPARE may include testing the applicability of these findings to other groups, including those of marginalized backgrounds and identities and/or with a history of other trauma, with a focus on outcomes. This is important in the light of the fact that there is a dearth of evidence‐based parenting programs for refugees in Europe (Gewirtz et al. 2022), a group in an extreme need for effective services. Research could include expanding SPARE to suit the needs of additional refugee populations (e.g., from South America), as well as exploring the feasibility of other program formats (e.g., online delivery). Finally, more rigorous investigations of SPARE and its impact on family outcomes (e.g., via randomized controlled trials) are needed.

On a practical level, the program could benefit from the following: Ensuring therapists and link‐workers have relevant professional coaching and time to prepare; if possible, dividing parents into groups based on child age; and making sure meetings are held in a central, accessible location. Furthermore, given the adversities that refugee families have experienced, the importance of therapist expertise and skill in fostering participant connectedness cannot be overstated. Similarly, the interplay between link‐workers and participants merits consideration, especially with respect to shared or non‐shared traumatic experiences and their influence on participant outcomes.

Conclusion

In this era when many families are forced to flee their homeland and settle in foreign countries, they must adjust to their new contexts. Most refugees have encountered complex, repeated trauma in their search for safety. They have had to cope with consequences such as post‐traumatic stress and their children tend to show elevated internalizing and externalizing symptoms (Gewirtz et al. 2022). Thus, they sorely need support in the country of arrival, including a culturally responsive orientation to a new environment for themselves and their children to facilitate their adjustment and inclusion. Programs like SPARE can offer group support to refugees while introducing effective parenting strategies that can help families adapt to their new circumstances. Results of the current study indicate that SPARE may be a valuable resource for Ukrainian refugee families relocating to Iceland and possibly to other Northern European countries, helping them to address various challenges and strengthening their parenting. Feasibility studies are preliminary tests of the potential success of a venture. With positive findings in the present study for Ukrainian families, it is time to apply the lessons learned from this study and test the program with more families in more countries.
