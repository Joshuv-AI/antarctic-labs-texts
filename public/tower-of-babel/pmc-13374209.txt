Co-constructing meaning within the family system: a longitudinal dyadic interpretative phenomenological study of adolescents with depression and non-suicidal self-injury and their primary caregivers
Authors: Shihan Fang, Yanbo Wang, Xiaoying Zhu, Xiaoyu Huang, Kaitao Yu, Ruobing Qi, Linli Wang, Pu Rao, Yating Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent depression accompanied by non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is commonly recognized, addressed, and continuously reshaped within the family context. Existing studies predominantly focus on risk factors or caregiver burden, with limited attention to how family members jointly construct and renegotiate meanings of illness, self-injury, and safety over time. This study aimed to explore the interactional experiences of adolescents with depression and NSSI and their primary caregivers across illness stages, and to examine how psychological meanings and relational regulation processes are co-constructed within the family system.MethodsA longitudinal dyadic interpretative phenomenological analysis was conducted. Ten adolescent-caregiver dyads (N = 20) were interviewed during hospitalization (T1) and at 1 month (T2), 3 months (T3), and 6 months (T4) post-discharge, with optional joint interviews at T3–T4. Analyses followed an idiographic-to-dyadic and cross-temporal trajectory approach, attending to psychological convergence, divergence, and critical turning points across time.ResultsA temporal trajectory of family meaning co-construction was identified. During hospitalization (T1), family interactions were often reorganized around risk containment, with NSSI-related distress commonly treated by caregivers as requiring immediate safety-oriented responses. In the post-discharge middle phase (T2–T3), some families entered a seemingly “quiet period,” which emerged as a key divergence point in dyadic adjustment. For some, reduced overt conflict functioned as a buffer that enabled tentative relational adjustments; for others, the same quietness was experienced as heightened uncertainty, which could intensify vigilance and avoidance and maintain less workable interactional cycles. By the later follow-up (T3–T4), several families negotiated lower-intensity yet sustainable interactional patterns through waiting, reduced reactivity, and shared safety cues, which, in some families, appeared to support a form of limited stability and more workable distress co-regulation.ConclusionsFamily experiences of adolescent depression with NSSI may be understood as a dynamic process of relational meaning-making and psychological adaptation. These findings should be read as interpretive and context-bound. They tentatively suggest that clinical work may benefit from attending not only to safety management, including suicide-risk assessment where clinically indicated, but also to how family members renegotiate boundaries, signals, and responses under ongoing uncertainty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04757-2.

Background

Adolescent depressive disorders and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) have shown a sustained increase worldwide, emerging as a critical priority in public health and clinical practice [1–3]. Epidemiological evidence consistently indicates that depressive disorders are among the most common mental disorders in adolescence, and that the prevalence of NSSI is substantially higher among adolescents with depression than in the general population [1, 4, 5]. Prior research further suggests that comorbid depression and NSSI are associated with more severe affective symptoms and a markedly elevated risk of subsequent suicidal behavior [6, 7]. Crucially, accumulating evidence indicates that NSSI should not be understood merely as a “dangerous behavior” or pathological impulsivity, but rather as a psychosocial phenomenon with identifiable functions. Its occurrence is closely linked to adolescents’ emotion regulation needs, interpersonal interaction patterns, and meaning-making processes embedded in specific relational and contextual settings [8–10].

During adolescence, understandings of emotional experiences, illness states, and coping strategies are strongly shaped within the family system. The family is not only the context in which symptoms are often first recognized, but also the primary setting for everyday caregiving, risk management, and recovery-oriented support [11]. A substantial body of research suggests that family communication patterns, emotional responsiveness, and caregivers’ interpretations of the illness are closely related to the persistence of depressive symptoms and the recurrence of self-injury [12–14]. In this process, family members are not simply passive responders to symptoms; rather, they continuously form, revise, and negotiate their understandings of depression, self-injury, and each other’s roles through ongoing interaction [15]. From a psychological perspective, these interactional processes reflect dynamic practices of emotional co-regulation, boundary negotiation, and relational meaning-making. However, existing studies have largely examined family influences on adolescent depression and NSSI from the perspectives of risk factors or caregiving burden, focusing primarily on how family characteristics predict individual symptom outcomes [11, 16, 17]. By contrast, how family members develop shared—or divergent—meanings of NSSI through sustained interaction across the course of illness, and how such meanings subsequently shape family interactional adjustments, warrants deeper exploration.

Notably, current research on NSSI also presents methodological and perspectival limitations. First, while quantitative studies have documented statistical associations between family functioning, frequency of self-injury, and severity of depression, they are limited in their ability to capture how family members interpret each other’s behaviors in situ, and how these interpretations influence interactional processes in everyday life [18]. Second, qualitative research has provided important insights into adolescents’ experiences of depression and self-injury; however, most studies continue to adopt a single-actor analytic focus—either foregrounding adolescents’ subjective experiences or emphasizing parents’ distress [19]. Recent qualitative work has begun to include broader participant groups—such as parents and healthcare providers—in examining youth self-harm and its meanings. In that literature, self-harm may include NSSI and suicidal self-harm, depending on study definitions. However, such work remains largely cross-sectional rather than longitudinal and dyadic [20]. This fragmentation of perspectives constrains the field’s ability to reconstruct the relational dynamics of parent–adolescent interaction. Specifically, the precise psychological mechanisms through which adolescents experiencing psychological distress and their primary caregivers perceive, interpret, and respond to each other within dynamic, unfolding interactions remain largely underexplored. As a result, complex processes of meaning negotiation, interactional tension, and relational adjustment are often reduced to static descriptors such as “poor communication” or “overprotection” [21]. Such static categorizations obscure the underlying psychological processes through which relational patterns evolve, stabilize, or deteriorate over time.

Recent theoretical developments in family systems and meaning-making frameworks emphasize that illness does not exist in isolation within the individual; rather, it is continuously interpreted, named, and reshaped through family interactions [22, 23]. For adolescents with depressive disorders accompanied by NSSI, self-injury often introduces heightened uncertainty and emotional disruption within the family system. Caregivers may oscillate between efforts to protect, control, or step back, while adolescents simultaneously re-evaluate issues of intimacy, autonomy, boundaries, and the possibility of being understood [24, 25]. These meanings and interactional practices are not fixed; instead, they often shift across illness-related phases, including symptom recognition, fluctuations in perceived risk, and trajectories of treatment and recovery [26]. Nevertheless, there remains a notable lack of longitudinal, relationally oriented empirical research examining how families develop and adjust interaction patterns through ongoing everyday negotiations and relational adaptations over time [27].

To address these gaps, the present study adopts a longitudinal Dyadic Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (Dyadic IPA) approach [28], focusing on adolescents with depressive disorders accompanied by NSSI and their primary caregivers. We systematically examine their interactional experiences and processes of meaning co-construction across different stages of the illness. Specifically, this study is guided by two primary research questions:

How do adolescents with depression and NSSI and their primary caregivers experience and make sense of their evolving interactions across different stages of the illness?How are the meanings of illness, self-injury, and safety co-constructed, understood differently, and renegotiated within the family dyad over time?

By analyzing adolescents’ and caregivers’ narratives concurrently at multiple time points, this study examines how each party makes sense of themselves and the other over time, with particular attention to relational processes such as convergence, divergence, and renegotiation of meaning. Compared with cross-sectional or single-perspective research, this study aims to advance understanding of how family systems gradually adjust their patterns of relational regulation and caregiving under conditions of ongoing uncertainty, thereby providing context-sensitive empirical grounding for family-centered psychological interventions and support practices.

Methods

Aim, design, and methodological orientation

To answer the research questions outlined in the introduction, we employed a longitudinal Dyadic IPA, which focuses on meaning-making processes—both shared and divergent—by conducting interpretative analysis at individual and dyadic levels [28]. Within a longitudinal research framework, data were collected and analyzed from the same adolescent-caregiver dyads at multiple time points. This approach allowed us to capture individual experiential meanings while simultaneously comparing and integrating convergence, divergence, and silence and absence within the dyads, thereby tracing the relational trajectories of the dyads over time [29, 30].

Family Systems Theory and Meaning-Making Theory served as sensitizing concepts [22, 23] to guide interview probing and analytical focus, rather than as pre-determined conclusions. Family Systems Theory directed attention toward interactional cycles, boundaries, role distribution, and systemic adjustments, while Meaning-Making Theory facilitated an understanding of how meanings were interpreted, shared, and transformed during periods of uncertainty and crisis. In the analysis and writing process, these theories functioned as conceptual tools to orient the researchers’ inquiry, with every interpretation remaining grounded in the original text to avoid substituting participants’ lived experiences with theoretical assumptions.

To enhance analytic transparency and auditability within the longitudinal Dyadic IPA design [31], the adolescent-caregiver dyad was established as the core unit of analysis. The temporal dimension (T1–T4) and relational dimension (individual–dyad–family system) were integrated into a stepwise analytical chain moving from transcript immersion to individual-level coding, within-time-point dyadic integration, within-family cross-temporal comparison, identification of turning points and trajectories, and final cross-case synthesis. Setting and recruitment

Participants were primarily recruited from the inpatient ward of a specialized psychiatric hospital in China and its follow-up channels. Trained clinicians or primary nurses initially screened families based on eligibility criteria during routine care. Upon obtaining permission, the research team contacted potential participants to explain the study’s purpose, procedures, risks, and protective measures, obtaining written informed consent. For the post-discharge phases (T2–T4), the research team invited participants to continue with follow-up interviews via outpatient visits or encrypted video calls, aiming to maintain continuous tracking of the same family dyads across all four time points.

To mitigate the influence of power dynamics on narratives, a clear distinction was maintained between the research team and the clinical treatment team. Researchers were not involved in clinical decision-making. Interviews and analyses were conducted by researchers with backgrounds in psychiatry, psychology, or nursing who were trained in IPA and safe communication with adolescents. The research team did not provide therapeutic advice or interventions; all clinical management remained the responsibility of the attending medical team. All adolescents received routine inpatient psychiatric care followed by usual post-discharge outpatient follow-up as clinically indicated. However, treatment content and intensity were not standardized across families, and no family-based intervention was delivered as part of the study protocol. Treatment-related changes were documented as contextual background for interpretation rather than analyzed as intervention variables.

To ensure clinical accuracy, a dual-physician independent assessment and consensus meeting process was employed. Any diagnostic discrepancies were resolved through a clinical consensus meeting, recorded, and archived (see Appendix 1).

Sampling strategy

We employed purposive sampling combined with maximum variation sampling to ensure diversity in key characteristics. We sought differentiation in family structure (nuclear, single-parent, or grandparent-led families); illness duration (< 6 months, 6–24 months, > 24 months); school status (attending, suspended, or dropped out); residential context (urban, township, or rural); caregiver educational level; adolescent depression severity (assessed using the Children’s Depression Inventory-Second Edition, CDI-2 [33, 34]); NSSI severity (assessed using the Ottawa Self-Injury Inventory, OSI [35, 36]); and parental marital status. This variation was intended to enrich the observation of meaning co-construction across different contexts and to enhance sample heterogeneity and contextual richness, rather than to serve as criteria for hypothesis testing.

Sample size and information power

Following IPA’s idiographic orientation, sample size was determined based on information power in conjunction with the longitudinal design of the study [37]. Information power was evaluated at the level of the family dyad rather than individual interviews. After completing data collection through at least T2 and conducting preliminary analyses, we assessed information power by considering the specificity of the research aim, sample heterogeneity, clarity of the theoretical framework, and data quality [37, 38]. Recruitment was discontinued when, after the addition of several consecutive dyads, the core thematic framework no longer demonstrated substantive extension—that is, newly added dyads primarily reiterated existing themes without contributing new interpretative dimensions or key turning points. To buffer against attrition during follow-up, two additional families were recruited beyond the point at which information power was judged sufficient. All decisions regarding sample size were documented in the audit trail.

Data collection

Interview timing and format

A longitudinal design was adopted with interviews conducted at key treatment and recovery milestones [39–41]: T1 (during hospitalization), T2 (1 month post-discharge), T3 (3 months post-discharge), and T4 (6 months post-discharge). At each time point, individual semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted separately with adolescents and their primary caregivers (approximately 40–80 min each). T1 interviews were conducted in a private room within the ward, while T2–T4 interviews took place in outpatient clinics, community settings, or other secure private spaces. When in-person follow-up was not feasible because of geographical distance, scheduling barriers, or public health restrictions, individual interviews were conducted via Tencent Meeting, an encrypted video-conferencing platform, in accordance with the ethics-approved study protocol. Access was restricted to the interviewer and participant, and all recordings and transferred files were handled using access-restricted, password-protected data-management procedures approved by the institutional ethics committee. Across the study, 14 interviews were completed remotely in this way, primarily during follow-up; joint interviews were conducted in person. To facilitate rapport building and sensitivity to longitudinal changes, the same trained researcher followed the same family throughout the study whenever possible.

Joint interviews and safeguarding protocol

During the T3 and T4 phases, an optional joint interview involving both the adolescent and the primary caregiver was conducted to directly observe interactional dynamics and the emergence of co-constructed understandings. Participation in joint interviews was voluntary and did not affect continued participation in individual interviews.

Joint interviews were initiated only when: (a) individual interviews at T1–T2 had been completed; (b) pre-interview risk screening indicated a level below moderate; and (c) separate informed consent was obtained from both parties, with explicit clarification of topics they did not wish to discuss together.

Researchers avoided introducing sensitive information from individual interviews that was not already mutually known. Interview prompts focused on shared or jointly recognizable interactional scenarios. Explicit “skip” or “pause” options were provided to maintain psychological safety. Brief post-interview check-ins were conducted separately with the adolescent and caregiver to assess emotional state and support needs.

Interview guide and quality control

The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the theoretical frameworks [28, 29] and relevant literature [13, 31, 38, 42], then refined through pilot interviews with two families (pilot data were excluded from analysis) and expert consultation (see Appendix 2). The final guide focused on four core domains: (a) key events and subjective experiences related to depression and NSSI; (b) interpretations of symptoms and meanings of self-injury; (c) specific interactional scenarios, conflicts, and sources of support; and (d) changes over time, turning points, and future expectations.

Interviewers were required to pass a role-play assessment covering methodological competence, emotional safety, and ethical reflexivity prior to data collection. The first two formal interviews conducted by each researcher were reviewed by senior supervisors using an Interview Consistency Checklist (Appendix 3).

Instruments and risk screening tools

At baseline (T1), depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Children’s Depression Inventory–Second Edition (CDI-2) [33, 34], and NSSI severity was assessed using the Ottawa Self-Injury Inventory (OSI) [35, 36]. These instruments were used solely to describe sample characteristics and contextualize interpretation and were not used to generate themes, assess treatment effects, or support causal inference. Additionally, the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) Screener Version [43] was administered prior to every interview to continuously monitor psychological safety (see Sect. Ethical considerations for the specific emergency response protocol).

Data analysis

Data analysis followed IPA principles and a multiperspectival approach to IPA adapted for dyadic work [44], with the family dyad treated as the core unit of analysis. To make the analytic pathway more transparent, analysis was conducted in six sequential and traceable steps (Table 1), moving from raw interview material to individual themes, dyadic interpretations, longitudinal trajectories, and the final family-system interpretative model.

At each time point, each family was organized as a dyadic data package, including two verbatim transcripts (adolescent and primary caregiver), corresponding suicide and self-harm risk screening records, and researchers’ contextual field notes (e.g., significant life events, treatment changes). Analytic outputs from each step were documented in case summaries, dyadic comparison memos, temporal matrices, and audit-trail records so that later interpretations could be traced back to earlier analytic decisions. Joint interviews conducted at T3–T4 were treated as interactional materials used to supplement and refine reconstructions of dyadic interaction patterns and co-constructed meanings, rather than to replace individual narratives.

Step 1. data preparation, transcription, and immersion

All interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese and audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Verbatim transcription was completed within 24 h after each interview, retaining para-verbal features such as pauses, emphasis, emotional expressions, and notable non-verbal cues where identifiable. Researchers engaged in repeated close reading of each transcript to achieve immersion in participants’ experiential accounts, attending to language use, emotional flow, and contextual nuances.

At the longitudinal level, separate text corpora were established for each time point (T1–T4), accompanied by synchronized records of contextual changes and observational notes to support temporally grounded interpretation. For English-language reporting, quoted excerpts followed a structured translation protocol: Chinese coding first, followed by professional translation and bilingual verification. Ambiguities were resolved through iterative comparison with original transcripts and analytic memos to ensure cross-linguistic equivalence. All English quotations were traceable to their original Chinese sources and included in the audit trail.

Following detailed annotation, individual case summaries were developed to synthesize emergent themes and preserve a traceable link between raw extracts, preliminary comments, and higher-order interpretations; these summaries then served as the foundation for dyadic integration. NVivo 14.0 was used to support data management, coding organization, and the writing of analytic memos.

Step 3. within-time-point dyadic analysis

Dyadic analysis focused on the adolescent-caregiver pair as a relational unit and was conducted independently by two researchers, followed by comparison and consensus discussions.

Theme comparison involved systematic identification of convergence, divergence, and silence and absence across dyadic narratives. Convergence referred to shared understandings of key experiences; divergence captured discrepant interpretations or emotional responses; and silence and absence indicated themes emphasized by one member but minimally addressed by the other, treated as analytic cues requiring contextual verification rather than direct indicators of relational dysfunction.

Interactional pattern reconstruction integrated both narratives to identify recurring interactional patterns (e.g., cycles of mutual blame, emotional avoidance, protective silence), aiming to capture the underlying psychological mechanisms of relational regulation discussed in the theoretical framework. An event-response chain approach was used to align triggering events, interpretations, emotional reactions, and behavioral responses across dyad members.

Co-constructed meaning was examined by identifying instances in which meanings of illness, NSSI, caregiving roles, or recovery were jointly articulated, mutually negotiated, or reflected in sustained interactional adjustments. Co-constructed meaning was defined as observable within the narratives through reciprocal engagement, rather than inferred by the researchers.

Steps 4–6. within-family longitudinal comparison, turning point identification, and cross-case synthesis

Longitudinal analysis extended the dyadic findings by tracing how meanings, interactional responses, and relational positions shifted across T1, T2, T3, and T4 within each family.

Step 4 focused on within-family cross-temporal comparison. At the individual level, shifts in cognitive understanding, emotional experiences, and perceived family roles were examined from T1 to T4. At the dyadic level, temporal matrices were used to compare how each adolescent-caregiver pair interpreted the same events across time, allowing the team to identify points of convergence, divergence, and emergent re-negotiation.

Step 5 involved identifying turning points and phases in the trajectory of each family. Turning points were identified only when: (a) directional changes in meaning-making were evident; (b) interactional changes were sustained across subsequent time points; and (c) narrative shifts were corroborated by contextual records.

Step 6 then compared trajectories across families to identify recurrent phases, divergent pathways, and higher-order patterns of meaning co-construction at the family-system level. Cross-case synthesis remained idiographically grounded by returning to original transcripts, case summaries, and dyadic memos whenever a higher-order interpretation was proposed. All determinations were reviewed in team meetings and documented in the audit trail. At each move from idiographic coding to higher-order interpretation, the team revisited reflexive notes to examine whether proposed patterns were grounded in participants’ language and longitudinal case material rather than in prior theoretical expectations.

Researcher positionality, reflexivity and trustworthiness

Researcher positionality and reflexive practice

Given the interpretative orientation of IPA, researcher positionality was treated as part of the analytic process rather than as a procedural add-on. The interview and analytic team was multidisciplinary and predominantly female, with backgrounds in psychiatry, psychology, and nursing, and with training in IPA and safe interviewing with adolescents. These backgrounds supported sensitivity to clinical risk, caregiver burden, and family interactional processes, but also meant that researchers could enter the field with assumptions that silence, withdrawal, parental monitoring, or family tension primarily signaled dysfunction or escalating danger. To reduce the risk of imposing these assumptions, Family Systems Theory and Meaning-Making Theory were used as sensitizing concepts rather than explanatory templates, and interviewers prioritized participants’ own language and experiential meanings before moving to interpretative probing. In doing so, they worked reflexively to bracket, as far as possible, their prior clinical assumptions and authority, positioning themselves as curious learners rather than diagnostic experts so that participants’ lived experiences could remain foregrounded.

The team also remained attentive to the effects of setting and power asymmetry. Although researchers were not members of the treatment team and had no role in clinical decision-making, interviews beginning during hospitalization could still lead participants to perceive interviewers as institutionally aligned with care providers. Researchers therefore reiterated their independent role, emphasized that participation or refusal would not affect treatment, and documented signs of guardedness, deference, or abrupt topic shifts in field notes. During analysis, reflexive journals and consensus discussions were used to examine how disciplinary expectations, emotional responses to adolescent risk, and prior commitments to relational or systemic interpretations might be shaping coding decisions. Particular attention was paid not to automatically read silence as resistance, monitoring as pathology, or temporary calm as recovery without checking these interpretations against within-dyad and cross-time-point data. For example, in early analytic discussions, initial clinically informed readings tentatively treated reduced overt conflict at T2–T3 as possible improvement. After returning to paired narratives, field notes, and later time points, we noted that, in some families, the same quietness coexisted with concealment, vigilance, and emotional distance. We therefore refined this interpretation, treating the quiet period not as straightforward stabilization but as a branching point that could reflect either tentative relational adjustment or unresolved tension and avoidance.

Trustworthiness

The trustworthiness of this study was addressed in accordance with the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba [45].

Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement enabled by longitudinal follow-up, researcher triangulation (independent coding by at least two analysts followed by consensus discussions), and systematic documentation of analytic decisions. Where appropriate and without compromising psychological safety, member reflection was conducted with 18 participants (8 adolescents and 10 caregivers) by inviting them to comment on thematic summaries or interpretative syntheses, focusing on resonance rather than verification of findings.

Transferability was supported through thick description of participants’ demographic characteristics, illness contexts, and family environments, while maintaining strict de-identification. This approach enables readers to assess the applicability of findings to other clinical or cultural contexts.

Dependability and audit trail were ensured by comprehensive documentation of the analytic process, including iterative versions of coding frameworks, analytic memos, and records of team discussions.

Confirmability was strengthened through continuous reflexive journaling, in which researchers documented assumptions, emotional responses, and potential biases. Reflexive records were revisited at key analytic stages to distinguish participants’ accounts from researchers’ interpretations, with interpretations consistently anchored in textual evidence.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Hangzhou Seventh People’s Hospital (Approval No.: Research [2024] Ethics Review No. 060) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, including legal guardians for minors, after providing detailed explanations of study aims, procedures, potential risks, and data use. Participation was voluntary, with the right to withdraw at any time without consequences.

All data were anonymized using coded identifiers (e.g., F1). Identifiable information was accessible only to the core research team. Digital files were encrypted, stored on password-protected devices, and transmitted via access-restricted channels. Data were destroyed according to a predefined plan upon study completion.

To safeguard participants’ psychological well-being, suicide and self-harm risk screening was conducted before each interview using the C-SSRS Screener Version [43]. If moderate or higher risk was identified, the interview was immediately suspended and the clinical response protocol was activated, including notification of the attending clinician, initiation of institutional emergency procedures, and provision of appropriate psychological support or referral. Interviews were resumed only after clinical clearance.

For joint interviews, additional safeguards were implemented. Participation was voluntary, boundaries regarding sensitive topics were clarified in advance, and researchers continuously monitored emotional responses. Interviews were terminated immediately if signs of distress or safety concerns emerged.

Reporting standards

This qualitative study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), a 32-item checklist for interviews and focus groups [46] (see Appendix 5).

Results

Participants and data sources

Data collection was conducted between August 2024 and July 2025. Initially, 15 adolescent-primary caregiver dyads meeting the inclusion criteria were recruited. Two dyads participated in pilot interviews to refine the interview guide; data from these pilot interviews were excluded from the final analysis. Of the remaining 13 dyads, three withdrew during the follow-up period (T3–T4) due to personal reasons or changes in family circumstances. Consequently, the final sample consisted of 10 adolescent-primary caregiver dyads (20 participants) who provided complete longitudinal data. Guided by the principle of information power [37], the sample was considered sufficient to support an in-depth interpretation of meaning co-construction within the family system, given the study’s focused aim, rich data density, and a stable analytic framework.

We conducted 100 in-depth interviews (audio-recorded), totaling approximately 108 h, with verbatim transcripts amounting to approximately 1.12 million Chinese characters. This dataset included 80 individual interviews (10 families × 4 time points × 2 participants) and 20 joint interviews. All 10 final dyads completed the full set of planned individual interviews at T1–T4 and participated in one joint interview at both T3 and T4. Overall, 86 interviews were conducted in person and 14 were conducted remotely via Tencent Meeting; all remote interviews were individual interviews, whereas all joint interviews were conducted in person. The final analytic dataset was therefore longitudinally complete, with variation pertaining to interview modality rather than missing data. Joint interviews were conducted at both T3 and T4 to elicit shared interpretations and responses to the same interactional events; they were initiated only when risk assessments indicated that interview conditions were appropriate and when both participants provided consent.

To enhance rigor and trustworthiness, we maintained a triangulated audit trail during data collection. This included approximately 100 post-interview field notes focusing on nonverbal behaviors and interactional atmospheres, as well as reflexive journals (approximately 30,000 Chinese characters) to support ongoing monitoring of researchers’ assumptions and potential biases. In addition, risk-screening records and clinical consistency assessment forms across study phases were archived. These supplementary materials were not used for quantitative validation of conclusions; rather, they served to enhance thick description, support traceability of key interactional episodes, and strengthen interpretive coherence.

Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 2. Adolescents were aged 12–18 years and were predominantly female. Most had a mid-to-long illness duration. At baseline, the majority presented with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms alongside varying levels of NSSI. Marked impairment in academic functioning was common prior to hospitalization. Family contexts and caregiving arrangements were heterogeneous, including nuclear families, divorced or separated-parent families, and grandparent-led caregiving situations.

Thematic findings

The findings are presented across three analytic levels: adolescent, caregiver, and dyadic. These levels are examined within an overarching temporal trajectory consisting of three phases. The early period (T1 and early T2) was dominated by crisis response and risk containment. During the middle period (T2–T3), overt conflict often quieted, but the meaning of this quiet began to diverge across families. By the later period (T3–T4), some dyads had begun to develop more workable, lower-burden ways of staying connected. As shown in Fig. 1, the analytic model is best understood not as a simple stage sequence, but as a temporal pattern. Within this pattern, the T2–T3 quiet period functions as a branching point. The T3–T4 phase then reflects whether dyads could sustain negotiated but limited stability through low-burden rules and shared safety cues.

Adolescent level: from overwhelm and interruption to cautious re-expression

Theme 1. Distress often exceeded language, and NSSI was described as a way of interrupting emotion rather than expressing a wish to die (T1–T2)

During the early phase, adolescents commonly described distress as something that outstripped language. At moments of peak intensity, many felt mentally chaotic or unable to organize experience into words. As N1 recalled, her “mind was chaotic,” causing her to quickly “get stuck” and retreat into silence (F1, N1). Within this context, NSSI was usually described in functional terms—such as N9 framing it as a “tranquilizer” to silence incessant internal “voices” (F9, N9)—rather than as an expression of suicidal intent. Thus, participants’ accounts distinguished NSSI as an affect-regulation practice from suicidal behavior, while suicide risk was monitored separately through clinical safety procedures. By T2, these meanings had not changed substantially, but adolescents had become more aware that disclosure could alter family interactions.

Theme 2. After discharge, silence and masking became increasingly relational and strategic (T1–T2)

Adolescents did not describe silence simply as withdrawal. Especially after discharge, silence, humor, shortened replies, controlled facial expressions, or staying behind a closed door became ways of limiting escalation and protecting already strained relationships. For example, N7 described deliberately controlling her facial expressions and tone so that her grandfather would not panic. For some, silence was also a way of conserving emotional energy; as one adolescent put it, “explaining itself is exhausting.” What became clearer from T1 to T2 was not symptom resolution, but adolescents’ growing awareness that expression could be emotionally costly and relationally risky.

Theme 3. By T3–T4, some adolescents began to re-express distress through lower-burden signals

Later changes were usually gradual. Around T3, some adolescents began to test more manageable forms of expression, such as sending a text message, offering a brief statement of safety, or explicitly asking caregivers not to “rush in like a fire alarm” when distress became visible. By T4, these signals were somewhat more recognizable within some dyads, but they remained fragile and conditional. What mattered was not that disclosure had become easy, but that less burdensome ways of making distress visible had begun to emerge.

Primary caregiver level: from vigilance and control to effortful restraint

Theme 1. Crisis containment in hospital extended into everyday vigilance after discharge (T1–T2)

At T1, caregivers commonly described fear, uncertainty, and responsibility so intense that care became organized around watching and checking. For instance, the father in F8 described himself as a “monitor,” fearing any lapse might lead to severe consequences (F8, C8). After discharge, this vigilance rarely disappeared. Instead, it became folded into ordinary family life as caregivers assumed greater day-to-day responsibility for safety outside the ward.

Theme 2. Loosening control usually began with concrete interactional setbacks (T2–T3)

Between T2 and T3, some caregivers began to realize that immediate questioning or intervention did not necessarily reduce risk and could sometimes intensify distress. This recognition usually did not arise as a general insight, but through specific episodes. In Family F9, the mother described waiting outside the bathroom door rather than entering immediately; when the feared outcome did not occur, that moment became a small turning point in how she approached later distress. The shift was tentative, but it marked an important change in how care was enacted.

Theme 3. By T3–T4, restraint had become part of caregiving work, but fear remained

By the later phase, some caregivers had become more deliberate in tone, timing, and proximity. Restraint did not mean the absence of fear; it became a form of caregiving work. As the F8 father noted, even as he learned to “say less,” his “heart is still suspended whenever the child is unhappy” (F8, C8). T4 therefore looked less like relief than like a more sustained attempt to hold back without disengaging.

Family-system level (Dyadic IPA): from risk-centered reorganization to negotiated but limited stability

Theme 1. Early family life was reorganized around risk and often reinforced a monitoring–withdrawal cycle (T1–T2)

At the dyadic level, early family life was reorganized around confirming safety through an interlocking monitoring–withdrawal loop. Behaviors that adolescents used as self-protection—such as silence, withdrawal, or avoidance—were often read by caregivers as warning signs requiring checking; that checking, in turn, was often experienced by adolescents as intrusive or escalating. As the bounded context of hospitalization transitioned to home routines, this dynamic tended to position the adolescent primarily as someone to be watched.

Theme 2. The T2–T3 quiet period was not simply a sign of improvement, but a branching point

As overt conflict lessened, many families entered this quiet period. Yet this quiet did not mean the same thing across cases. In some dyads, reduced interactional intensity created breathing room; N4 described this period as “more comfortable,” a space where the relationship temporarily ceased to be a burden (F4, N4). In others, the same quiet was interpreted as concealment; the grandmother in F5 interpreted it as the adolescent “holding things in,” which paradoxically heightened her observation (F5, C5). For this reason, we treated the T2–T3 quiet period as a point of divergence rather than as straightforward recovery. In some families, this period opened space for small interactional shifts; in others, it preserved distance and uncertainty beneath a calmer surface.

Theme 3. By T3–T4, some dyads developed low-burden rules and shared safety cues

Where greater stability emerged, it usually did so through small, repeatable rules rather than through the resolution of distress. In some families, caregivers first asked whether companionship or solitude was needed. In others, text messages were accepted as valid signals of distress. In Family F10, everyday cues—such as the sound of keys being placed in the tray by the entryway—came to function as sufficient reassurance of safety, reducing the need for repeated verbal checking. In some dyads, such rules were explicitly co-constructed. For example, in Family F1, when the adolescent proposed that she might say, “I’m just feeling awful right now—I won’t do anything foolish,” the caregiver immediately responded, “If I hear that, I won’t panic.” Even so, later stability remained limited. Tension did not disappear; in some dyads, what changed was the capacity to remain in contact while carrying tension together.

Dynamic trajectory model

Taken together, the findings suggest a dynamic trajectory moving from early risk-centered reorganization, through a T2–T3 quiet period that functioned as a branching point, and toward a later phase of negotiated but limited stability. This was not a linear movement from crisis to resolution. Rather, Fig. 1 integrates the adolescent-, caregiver-, and dyadic-level findings into a temporal interpretive model. Within this model, T3–T4 reflects not full recovery, but whether limited stability could be negotiated and sustained.

Discussion

From symptom change to relational regulation: repositioning under persistent uncertainty

In these families, symptom improvement did not automatically restore pre-illness interactional patterns. Instead, adolescents and primary caregivers appeared to renegotiate roles, responsibilities, and relational boundaries under ongoing uncertainty regarding distress, self-injury, and safety. Because this was a longitudinal qualitative study of ten dyads in one specific Chinese clinical setting, the findings are best read as interpretive, context-bound accounts of family meaning-making rather than as definitive or universally applicable stages. The broader adolescent self-harm literature, in which definitions may include NSSI and suicidal self-harm depending on study scope, similarly indicates that self-harm-related experiences can place sustained psychological demands on caregivers, often characterized by fear, vigilance, helplessness, and repeated adjustments in caregiving responses [26, 47].

During the crisis and hospitalization phase (T1–T2), family interaction was predominantly organized around preventing further harm. Caregivers shifted rapidly toward monitoring, checking, and environmental control, transforming everyday relational support into safety-centered functioning. Adolescents were implicitly positioned as individuals requiring continuous safety confirmation, and their emotions and behaviors were increasingly interpreted within a risk-assessment framework rather than a relational one. Within this sample, however, participants did not usually narrate NSSI as synonymous with suicidal behavior. Rather, family responses often folded depression, NSSI-related distress, and possible suicide risk into a broader field of danger, even though adolescents more often described NSSI as a way of managing overwhelming affect. Similar patterns have been documented in previous studies describing parental confusion, distress, and heightened safety concerns in response to self-harm [48, 49].

When symptoms partially alleviated during T2–T3, families did not immediately enter stability. Instead, many moved into a superficially calm yet internally fragile phase. The meaning of this apparently calm period diverged: in some families, reduced conflict signaled relational respite; in others, silence was interpreted as latent danger and was accompanied by intensified checking. In this sense, quietness could function either as a tentative form of adaptive stabilization or as a surface marker of unresolved tension, concealment, and avoidance. Across these families, symptom reduction did not necessarily translate into a greater sense of relational safety. Even in the absence of overt NSSI episodes, caregivers could remain highly vigilant, reflecting the enduring psychological impact of perceived risk [47].

Importantly, relational turning points rarely emerged from explicit verbal agreements. Rather, they were observed in shifts in moment-to-moment responses. When caregivers refrained from immediate control—choosing to pause, wait, or reduce response intensity—escalating cycles were sometimes interrupted. By T3–T4, some families established relatively stable yet bounded relational patterns. This stability was not based on risk elimination but on shared acknowledgment of uncertainty and negotiation of tolerable interactional rules. In some families, what seemed to change was not the persistence of vulnerability, but their growing ability to remain in contact without immediately trying to remove the discomfort.

Reorganization of interaction: meaning co-construction and limited stability

Across families, care did not always keep the same form. What first appeared as checking, following, and intervening sometimes gave way to waiting, asking more carefully, or leaving a little space. These shifts did not arrive as formal techniques. They emerged through repeated moments in which both parties tested what could be tolerated without escalating distress. In several families, care began to move from control-oriented interruption toward restraint and emotional containment. Situated within a Chinese sociocultural context, this shift is particularly salient. Prior research has described a cultural logic of zeren wuxianhua (limitless responsibility) that may shape parent–child dynamics in some families, where “good parenting” can become implicitly linked to assuming primary responsibility for preventing harm. During the crisis phase, this cultural script appeared to resonate with caregivers’ tendency to equate care with high-intensity control, at times manifesting as intrusive monitoring (e.g., Families F5 and F8). However, the longitudinal data suggest a more cautious interpretation: more workable patterns appeared when caregivers gradually relinquished the expectation of immediate problem resolution. Support came to include the ability to tolerate anxiety while preserving space for the adolescent. Across these accounts, the family rule seemed to shift from “intervene immediately” to “allow temporary distance within safety limits.” Consistent with the concept of second-order change in family systems theory [50], families altered the governing rules rather than intensifying existing control strategies. Our findings resonate with literature linking reduced high expressed emotion to improved adolescent adjustment [39, 51], offering a contextualized understanding of how such emotional shifts unfold within the dyad.

Second, relational stabilization was underpinned by the development of low-burden shared safety signals. Families that achieved relational easing often developed subtle, repeatable cues during T3–T4. For example, in Family F10, the sound of the key dropping into the tray by the entryway gradually became a shared safety cue, signaling arrival without the need for repeated verbal checking.

In Family F8, asking before approaching gradually became a recognizable interactional practice. These everyday gestures became shared relational anchors, simultaneously preserving adolescent autonomy and providing minimal caregiver reassurance. In some dyads, these tacit agreements reduced the need for repeated checking and made everyday contact somewhat easier to sustain [52].

Importantly, the limited stability observed here did not imply the absence of tension. Caregivers continued to expend psychological effort to regulate vigilance, and adolescents remained cautious in self-expression. Stability therefore involved coexistence with manageable strain rather than full emotional resolution. This pattern also resonates with Walsh’s framework of family resilience [53, 54]. In this context, resilience did not signify a simple return to a pre-illness baseline but the emergence of a more workable relational equilibrium that accommodated ongoing vulnerability while reducing the likelihood of renewed escalation. What seemed to matter was not the disappearance of strain, but whether both parties could live with some strain without the interaction escalating again.

Clinical implications: supporting negotiated stability rather than absolute risk elimination

Within the scope of this study, clinical work with families of adolescents with depression and NSSI may benefit from attending not only to immediate risk containment, but also to relational negotiation under persistent uncertainty.

While clinical guidelines such as those from National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) appropriately emphasize safety management [55], our findings raise the possibility that, in some families, an exclusive focus on monitoring may inadvertently reinforce crisis-based relational patterns. When risk management becomes the dominant organizing principle, adolescents may come to be positioned primarily as needing continuous monitoring, while caregivers may remain in prolonged hypervigilance, increasing relational strain.

Alongside maintaining safety thresholds, the findings may suggest that some families could benefit when caregivers are supported to step back from a sustained crisis stance and tolerate uncertainty a little longer before acting. The example of the mother in Family F9—waiting outside the bathroom rather than immediately entering—may illustrate a form of trust held under continuing worry: the caregiver tolerated uncertainty and delayed control responses while still maintaining safety thresholds. One possible implication is that clinicians might attend to whether this capacity is already beginning to emerge in caregivers, and consider how to support it without assuming that it can be quickly consolidated. Similar processes of caregiver adjustment have been described in recent qualitative work [27].

Furthermore, long-term relational stabilization may depend less on intensive emotional disclosure and more on the consolidation of low-intensity, mutually understood interactional rules. A more tentative implication is that clinicians might first look for small agreements already taking shape in family life, rather than assuming that more intensive or standardized communication techniques are always required [56]. These may include a brief text message, asking before approaching, or an everyday cue that signals safe return. Clinicians might then consider helping families make these responses more recognizable and consistent. This may be one way of supporting a limited, more sustainable form of stability, without assuming that risk can be fully eliminated.

Methodological reflections, limitations, and future directions

Capturing relational evolution through a longitudinal dyadic lens

The longitudinal dyadic IPA design enabled the examination of temporal shifts within family systems under crisis. Compared with cross-sectional research focusing on static associations between symptoms and family functioning [15], this approach revealed iterative processes of probing, withdrawal, and renegotiation that shape dyadic psychological adjustment over time.

Longitudinal follow-up was particularly valuable in clarifying the T2–T3 “quiet period,” which might otherwise be misinterpreted as straightforward recovery. Continued engagement suggested that this period could reflect suppressed avoidance, temporary buffering, or preparation for relational adjustment. Consistent with prior methodological work on longitudinal IPA, this approach is particularly useful for examining how meanings, continuity, and change unfold across time and relational contexts [57, 58].

The dyadic perspective also enabled us to move beyond single-sided narratives. Prior qualitative research on adolescent NSSI has often focused either on adolescents’ subjective distress or on caregivers’ burden in isolation [59], potentially overlooking interactional discrepancies within the family system. By placing adolescents and primary caregivers within the same analytic framework, this study illuminated misalignments and gradual shifts in mutual understanding. For example, in Family F9, what the mother experienced as “cold rejection” was described by the daughter as “necessary distance to avoid collapse.” Such relational misinterpretations respond to recent calls for multi-perspective approaches in NSSI research and address limitations inherent in single-perspective designs [60].

Limitations and future research

Participants were recruited from psychiatric inpatient and follow-up settings, potentially limiting generalizability to families without access to specialized care. In addition, treatment exposures during hospitalization and follow-up were not standardized across families. Variation in care context may therefore have influenced family meaning-making and adjustment trajectories and should be understood as part of the interpretive context rather than as a controlled explanatory factor. Despite maximum variation sampling, most caregivers were mothers, reflecting broader gendered caregiving patterns [24, 59]. Perspectives of fathers and other family members remain underrepresented.

Attrition during T3–T4 may have resulted in an overrepresentation of families demonstrating relative stability. Additionally, although joint interviews enabled the observation of in-the-moment negotiation, they may also have influenced the expression of conflict despite safeguards. Joint interviews were conducted only at T3–T4 and required continuing participation, separate consent, and psychologically safe conditions. Later-phase interactional material may therefore reflect dyads who were able to remain engaged in the study and to participate together under these conditions; this should be considered when interpreting co-constructed interaction in the later phase.

Future studies may extend longitudinal dyadic approaches across diverse cultural contexts and family structures, incorporating fathers, siblings, and extended kin. Family-involved intervention research in adolescents with NSSI also remains preliminary, despite early mixed-method evidence suggesting possible improvements in parent–child interaction and parenting-related processes [61]. Longer-term follow-up would further clarify how families renegotiate interactional rules during symptom recurrence or new stressors and whether such changes can be supported and sustained over time.

Conclusions

Using a longitudinal dyadic interpretative phenomenological analysis, this study explored how adolescents with depressive disorders accompanied by non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and their primary caregivers experienced and understood the illness process across different stages. The findings suggest that, within this sample, NSSI-related distress was embedded in ongoing family interactions rather than confined to the adolescent’s individual experience. Through these interactions, meanings of self-injury, risk, caregiving, and safety were continuously interpreted and renegotiated over time.

Family adaptation did not progress linearly alongside symptom change. Instead, families gradually formed more workable patterns through repeated interactional adjustments. A clinically meaningful finding was the T2–T3 quiet period. Reduced overt conflict during this period could signal temporary relational respite in some families, but it could also coexist with concealment, vigilance, and unresolved tension in others. Adolescents often regulated relational tension through silence, distancing, or indirect expression, whereas caregivers repeatedly balanced heightened vigilance with restrained presence. In participants’ accounts, NSSI was usually narrated as a way of managing overwhelming affect or relational strain rather than as suicidal behavior per se. This distinction did not eliminate the broader clinical need for ongoing suicide-risk assessment and safety management. Shifts in family interaction were closely linked to everyday responses to uncertainty. Interactions appeared easier to sustain when they became more predictable and less intrusive, enabling family members to maintain emotional connection and co-regulate distress without escalating around risk.

By adopting a longitudinal and dyadic lens, this study extends existing research that has primarily examined adolescents’ experiences or caregivers’ burdens in isolation. The findings suggest the value of conceptualizing NSSI-related distress as an evolving relational process within the family system. Clinically, beyond ensuring safety, it may be useful to attend to how family members manage uncertainty in everyday interactions—how they read signals, set limits, and stay in contact without escalating—rather than assuming that risk elimination alone is sufficient to reorganize family life.
