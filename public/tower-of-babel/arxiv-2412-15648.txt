A sufficient condition for local limit theorem
Kaoru Yoneda, Tsuyoshi Yoneda

Abstract
. We give a sufficient condition for the local limit theorem. To construct it, we employ infinite times of convolutions of probability density functions.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 42A38; Secondary 60F05

1.
Introduction

There have been a lot of attempts to prove the central limit theorem (CLT) without using characteristic functions (i.e. Fourier transform). See
[
1
,
2
]
for the recent development of CLT and references therein.
In spite of the difficulty of building
the theory of characteristic functions, in this paper, we focus on local limit theorem,
and give a sufficient condition for the local limit theorem.

To be self-contained,
first we reformulate
the infinite sum of independent random variables into infinite times of convolutions of probability density functions.
For a random variable
X
, let us define the corresponding monotone increasing function
\mu
such that

\mu(x):=P(X\leq x).

Note that
0\leq\mu(x)\leq 1

and
P(y<X\leq x)=P(X\leq x)-P(X\leq y)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
By Lebesgue’s decomposition theorem,
we can split
\mu
into three parts:

\mu=\mu^{p}+\mu^{ac}+\mu^{sc},

where
\mu^{p}
is the point mass part,
\mu^{ac}
is the absolute continuous part
and
\mu^{sc}
is the singular continuous part.
The typical example of the singular continuous part is the Cantor distribution.
Since we focus on the local limit theorem, in what follows, we assume
\mu^{p}=\mu^{sc}=0
.
Then we obtain the following probability density function (PDF):

\rho^{ac}:=\frac{d\mu^{ac}}{dx}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\rho^{ac}\geq 0
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{ac}(x)dx=1
.
Let
X_{1}(x)=x
and
X_{2}(x)=x
(
x\in\mathbb{R}
) be independent random variables,
and
\rho^{X_{1}}

\rho^{X_{2}}
be the corresponding PDFs.
Let
Y=X_{1}+X_{2}
,
and
\mu^{Y}
be the corresponding PDF.
By Benn’s diagram, we have

\begin{split}&P\left(x-\delta_{1}/2+\delta_{2}/2<Y\leq x+\delta_{1}/2-\delta_{2}/2\right)\\
\leq&\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}P\left(x-\delta_{2}j-\delta_{1}/2<X_{1}\leq x-\delta_{2}j+\delta_{1}/2,\ \delta_{2}j-\delta_{2}/2<X_{2}\leq\delta_{2}j+\delta_{2}/2\right)\\
\leq&P\left(x-\delta_{1}/2-\delta_{2}/2<Y\leq x+\delta_{1}/2+\delta_{2}/2\right)\\
\end{split}

for
\delta_{1}\gg\delta_{2}>0
.
On the other hand, by the statistical independence of random variable, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}P\left(x-\delta_{2}j-\delta_{1}/2<X_{1}\leq x-\delta_{2}j+\delta_{1}/2,\ \delta_{2}j-\delta_{2}/2<X_{2}\leq\delta_{2}j+\delta_{2}/2\right)\\
=&\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}P\left(x-\delta_{2}j-\delta_{1}/2<X_{1}\leq x-\delta_{2}j+\delta_{1}/2)P(\delta_{2}j-\delta_{2}/2<X_{2}\leq\delta_{2}j+\delta_{2}/2\right).\\
\end{split}

Then, taking
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\to 0
, we have

\rho^{Y}=\rho^{X_{1}}\ast\rho^{X_{2}}\quad a.e.

Repeating this argument, for
Y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}
, we have

(1)

\rho^{Y}=\rho^{X_{1}}\ast\rho^{X_{2}}\ast\cdots\ast\rho^{X_{n}}.

Remark 1
.

The formula of (
1
), that is, infinite times of convolutions have already been studied, in the different context.
The second author
[
8
,
9
]
constructed special smooth functions
which are compactly supported, and are satisfying the corresponding functional-differential equations of advanced type.
To construct such special functions, he employed the formula (
1
) with various scalings.
See also
[
6
,
7
]
.

2.
Sufficient condition for local limit theorem

In this section, we give a sufficient condition for
the local limit theorem.
For any fixed
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
we set
h_{N}:=\sqrt{\sigma/N}
(
N=\sigma,\sigma+1,\cdots
).
Let
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be such that

(2)

\rho\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\rho(x)dx=0,\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}\rho(x)dx=:\gamma<\infty,

where this
\sigma_{\pm}
means
\sigma\pm\delta
for some sufficiently small
\delta>0
.
Note that
\hat{\rho}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
due to (
2
).
Applying the Taylor expansion to
(\hat{\rho})^{2}
, we see that

|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}=((\hat{\rho}(t))^{2})^{1/2t^{2}}=\left(1+\frac{(\hat{\rho}^{2})^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)t^{2}}{2!}\right)^{\frac{1}{2t^{2}}}.

Since

(\hat{\rho}^{2})^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)|_{t=0}=2\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-2\gamma,

we have

\begin{split}1+\frac{(\hat{\rho}^{2})^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)t^{2}}{2!}&\leq 1-\gamma_{-}t^{2}\quad(0<t<T)\\
\end{split}

for some
\theta\in(0,1)
and
T(<\sqrt{e})
.

Thus we have

|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}\leq\left(1-\gamma_{-}t^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}t^{2}}\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}}\to e^{-\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}}<1\quad(t\to 0+).

Remark 2
.

The function
(1-s)^{1/s}
(
s=t^{2}\gamma_{-}
) is monotone decreasing, thus

(3)

|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}\leq e^{-\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}}<1\quad\text{for}\quad 0<t<T.

This is due to the fact that

(1-s)^{\frac{1}{s}}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{s}\log(1-s)\right)

and

\begin{split}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{1}{s}\log(1-s)\right)&=-\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{1}{1-s}+\frac{1}{s}\log(1-s)\right)\\
&=-\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{1}{2}s+\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\right)s^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\right)s^{3}+\cdots\right)<0.\end{split}

With the aid of the above observation, in this paper we also assume the following:

(4)

|\hat{\rho}(t)|\leq\frac{C}{|t|^{\frac{1}{\sigma_{-}}}}\quad\text{for}\quad T<t<\infty,

where
C=|T|^{\frac{1}{\sigma_{-}}}(1-\gamma_{-}T^{2})
.
For
\ell>0
, let
\rho_{\ell}(x):=\ell^{-1}\rho(\ell^{-1}x)
and let

\Phi_{N}(x):=\underbrace{\rho_{h_{N}}\ast\rho_{h_{N}}\ast\cdots\ast\rho_{h_{N}}}_{N}(x).

Then we can state the main theorem as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Assume
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
(
2
) and (
4
).
Then we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Phi_{N}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\gamma}\right)\quad\text{uniformly}.

To prove this local limit theorem, it is enough to show the following lemma,
due to Bernstein’s inequality.

Lemma 2
.

We have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|\hat{\Phi}(y)-e^{-\gamma y^{2}/2}\|_{L^{1}_{y}(\mathbb{R})}=0.

Proof.

To prove this key lemma, we just apply Lebesgue’s convergence theorem.
Thus it is enough to show the following two assertions:

(5)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\hat{\Phi}_{N}(y)=e^{-\gamma y^{2}/2}\quad\text{for any}\quad y\in\mathbb{R}

and there exists
M(y)\in L^{1}_{y}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(6)

|\hat{\Phi}_{N}(y)|\leq\sup_{N\geq\sigma}\left|\left(\hat{\rho}(h_{N}y)\right)^{N}\right|\leq M(y).

First we show (
5
).
We see that

(7)

\left(\hat{\rho}(h_{N}y)\right)^{N}=\left(\hat{\rho}(h_{N}y)\right)^{\left(\frac{1}{h_{N}y}\right)^{2}\sigma y^{2}}=\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\right)^{\frac{\sigma y^{2}}{t^{2}}},

where
t:=h_{N}y
. Thus, to show the pointwise convergence for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
,
it is enough to show that the complex function

\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\right)^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}

converges for
t\to 0+
.
By the assumption
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\rho(x)dx=0
, we have

\begin{split}\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\right)^{1/t^{2}}&=\left(1+\frac{\hat{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)t^{2}}{2!}\right)^{1/t^{2}}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{\hat{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)t^{2}}{2!}\right)\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(\frac{\hat{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)t^{2}}{2!}+O(t^{3})\right)\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\frac{\hat{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(\theta t)}{2}+O(t)\right)\end{split}

for some
\theta\in(0,1)
depending on
t
.
Thus

\lim_{t\to 0+}\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\right)^{1/t^{2}}=e^{-\gamma/2}.

This gives (
5
). Next we show (
6
).
By (
7
),
it is enough to show

\left(\sup_{0<t\leq y}|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}\right)^{\sigma y^{2}}\in L^{1}_{y}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}).

This is due to the fact that

N\geq\sigma\Leftrightarrow\sqrt{N/\sigma}\geq 1\Leftrightarrow y\geq t.

For
t\in(T,\infty)
, we see that

\left|\hat{\rho}(t)\right|^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}\leq\left|\frac{C}{t}\right|^{\frac{1}{\sigma_{-}t^{2}}}=\exp\left(-(1/\sigma_{-}t^{2})\log(t/C)\right)=:\zeta(t)

by (
4
)
and

-\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{-}t^{2}}\log(t/C)\right)=-\frac{C}{\sigma_{-}t^{3}}(1-2\log t).

Thus this function
\zeta
is monotone decreasing up to
t=\sqrt{e}
,
and monotone increasing from there.
Note that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\zeta(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\log(t/C)}{\sigma_{-}t^{2}}\right)=1

and then we can find a unique
y_{0}\in(\sqrt{e},\infty)
satisfying

\zeta(y_{0})=e^{-\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}}
.
Therefore

\left(\sup_{0<t<y}|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}\right)^{\sigma y^{2}}\leq e^{-\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad y\in[0,y_{0}]

and

\left(\sup_{0<t<y}|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}\right)^{\sigma y^{2}}\leq\left(\left|\frac{C}{y}\right|^{\frac{1}{\sigma_{-}y^{2}}}\right)^{\sigma y^{2}}\leq Cy^{-(1_{+})}\quad\text{for}\quad y\in(y_{0},\infty),

and this means there exists a
function
M(y)\in L^{1}_{y}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying

M(y)=\begin{cases}e^{-\frac{\gamma_{-}}{2}}\quad(y\leq y_{0}),\\
Cy^{-(1_{+})}\quad(y_{0}<y).\end{cases}

This is the desired function satisfying (
6
) and
we complete the proof of the Lemma
2
.
∎
