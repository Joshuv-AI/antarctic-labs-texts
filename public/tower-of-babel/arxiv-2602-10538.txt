Why Agentic Theorem Prover Works: A Statistical Provability Theory of Mathematical Reasoning Models
Sho Sonoda sho.sonoda@riken.jp, Shunta Akiyama akiyama_shunta@cyberagent.co.jp, Yuya Uezato uezato_yuya@cyberagent.co.jp

Abstract
Agentic theorem provers—pipelines that couple a mathematical reasoning model with library retrieval, subgoal-decomposition/search planner, and a proof assistant verifier—have recently achieved striking empirical success, yet it remains unclear which components drive performance and why such systems work at all despite classical hardness of proof search. We propose a distributional viewpoint and introduce statistical provability , defined as the finite-horizon success probability of reaching a verified proof, averaged over an instance distribution, and formalize modern theorem-proving pipelines as time-bounded MDPs. Exploiting Bellman structure, we prove existence of optimal policies under mild regularity, derive provability certificates via sub-/super-solution inequalities, and bound the performance gap of score-guided planning (greedy/top- k k /beam/rollouts) in terms of approximation error, sequential statistical complexity, representation geometry (metric entropy/doubling structure), and action-gap margin tails. Together, our theory provides a principled, component-sensitive explanation of when and why agentic theorem provers succeed on biased real-world problem distributions, while clarifying limitations in worst-case or adversarial regimes.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly improved at mathematical
reasoning
(
Barreto 2026
;
Bryan et al. 2026
;
Epoch AI 2026
)
, and a parallel effort has produced increasingly capable

agentic theorem provers
: systems that combine an LLM policy, a
planner for decomposition, a retriever over large libraries (such as
Mathlib), and a proof assistant (such as Lean, Rocq, and Isabelle, often with SAT/SMT
solvers) that provides rigorous feedback. Despite striking empirical
progress, these pipelines are complex and highly modular, and it remains
difficult to explain
which components affect how
—and in what
sense they can overcome, or fail to overcome, the classical hardness
barriers surrounding proof search. This gap between practice and theory
is now a bottleneck: many design choices are guided by heuristics and
ablations, while a principled understanding of
provability under
realistic distributions and finite compute
is still missing.

Formalization itself has a long history, from the early
foundations of logic (Frege) through Hilbert’s program and Gödel’s
incompleteness results. It is also well known that even very simple
fragments of logic already exhibit computational hardness: for example,

3
-SAT belongs to propositional logic yet is NP-complete, and
decision problems for first-order logic are undecidable in general.
Meanwhile, contemporary reasoning models can solve not only
competition-style mathematics but also problems that look “modern” in
flavor, and agentic systems can sometimes translate such reasoning into

machine-checked proofs
. This creates a tension. A formal proof
is a discrete object—a finite sequence of symbolic
transformations—so why should a stochastic generator plus a search
loop succeed at all? Has the emergence of LLM-based reasoning somehow
transcended complexity theory? The answer is no: classical lower bounds
and undecidability results remain valid. The key question is therefore
more subtle:

What changes when we replace worst-case, unbiased settings by
the highly structured, biased distributions of theorem statements and
proof patterns that appear in practice?

We propose the working hypothesis that resolves the apparent paradox:

Statistical provability.
Real-world theorem
instances are
not
distributed uniformly over “all statements of
a given size.” Instead, they concentrate on a highly structured,
repetitive subset of mathematics (libraries, idioms, canonical proof
patterns). Under such biased distributions, there exist regimes where
statistical methods—learned policies and learned scoring
functions—can outperform generic decision procedures and brute-force
search, even though unbiased/uniform formulations remain hard.

Traditional complexity theory has emphasized worst-case analysis
(uniform norm over all instances) and, when considering average-case
settings, often uses distributions designed to be unbiased or “flat.”
In those settings, the implication “unbiased/uniform
\Rightarrow

hard” is difficult to escape. Our focus is complementary: we ask when
and why “typical/frequent
\Rightarrow
easy” might hold, and how
modern agentic pipelines exploit this. Of course, if an instance
distribution were supported on worst cases, no such conclusion should be
expected. The point is not to deny classical hardness, but to

explain empirical success
in the regimes where these systems
actually operate.

1.1
Our approach: provability as a finite-horizon reachability
problem

To make such statements precise, we model an agentic theorem prover as a

finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP)
. The state encodes
the current proof obligation and verifier feedback (e.g., goals,
subgoals, error messages), the action encodes the next proof step
(tactic/lemma invocation, parameters, retrieval prompt, decoding
controls), and the transition is induced by the proof assistant’s
execution and checking. The evaluation target is
time-bounded
success
: reach the solved state within a budget of
B
interaction
steps (or verifier calls). This leads to a canonical object:
the
provability
of an instance
x_{0}
under budget
B
,

V_{B}^{\pi}(x_{0}):=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\exists t\leq B:X_{t}\in G\mid X_{0}=x_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad V_{B}^{*}(x_{0}):=\sup_{\pi}V_{B}^{\pi}(x_{0})

where
G
is the goal/success set and
\pi
ranges over policies.

We then define
statistical provability
as a distributional
notion—provability averaged over an instance distribution
Q
:

\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q}[V_{B}^{\pi}(x_{0})]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q}[V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})].

This formalizes the central object that agentic systems implicitly
optimize: not worst-case solvability, but success probability on a
(highly biased) stream of problems under a fixed compute budget.

1.2
Why Bellman structure matters: certificates and
component-wise effects

A major advantage of the MDP view is that it exposes the
Bellman
structure
of the objective. This enables two kinds of results that are
difficult to obtain from purely heuristic narratives:

1.

Existence and optimality:
under mild regularity
(compactness/Feller-type continuity), the optimal finite-horizon value

V_{B}^{*}
exists and is attained by a deterministic Markov policy.

2.

Provability certificates:
rather than only predicting success
rates, we construct
upper and lower
bounds on
V_{B}^{*}
using
Bellman inequalities (sub-/super-solutions). This yields a certificate
interval

L_{B}(x_{0})\leq V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})\leq U_{B}(x_{0}),

whose
gap

quantifies uncertainty about provability given an approximate
planner/value surrogate.

To connect these certificates to learning and search, we treat the
reasoning model as producing a
score function
(or a family of
score functions)
h_{b}(x,a)
that guides greedy selection, top-
k

filtering, beam search, or rollouts. Deviations from the optimal value
can then be controlled in terms of (i) approximation error of
h_{b}

relative to the true one-step optimal value
Q_{b}^{*}
, (ii) statistical
estimation error under adaptive data collection, and (iii) action-gap
(“margin”) properties that determine when mis-ranking actually harms
success. In a representative form, the analysis yields bounds where
performance degrades roughly like

\text{(effective proof length)}\times\text{(score error)}^{\text{(margin exponent)}},

making explicit how design choices change success probability through a
small set of interpretable parameters.

1.3
Contributions

This paper develops a statistical provability theory for agentic theorem
proving. Concretely:

•

MDP formalization and the notion of statistical provability.

We formalize modern agentic theorem provers as finite-horizon
reachability MDPs and define
provability
as optimal
time-bounded success probability, together with its distributional
counterpart over instance distributions.

•

Bellman-structure evaluation and certificates.
We use Bellman
recursions and inequalities to derive provability certificates and to
bound the deviation between the optimal success probability and that
achieved by score-guided planning algorithms.

•

A component-sensitive bound on success probability.
We show
how proof complexity, representation geometry (via metric entropy /
doubling-type conditions), tool feedback, retrieval, test-time search,
and sequential statistical complexity (e.g., sequential entropy /
sequential Rademacher-type quantities) enter the provability
bounds.

•

A bridge between logical hardness and statistical complexity.

We position these results as a step toward connecting classical
complexity barriers with the statistical structure of real
mathematical corpora, clarifying when “typical” instances can be
tractable for learned, agentic systems.

2
Problem Formulation

We formalize interactive formal theorem proving (with an LLM agent and an exact verifier/proof assistant) as a time-bounded reachability problem in a Markov decision process (MDP).
We then develop a Bellman-structure–based evaluation of (i)
provability (optimal success probability), (ii) deviation from
optimality of score-based planning (greedy/top-
k
/beam/rollout), and
(iii) certificate-style upper/lower bounds.

Notation and standing conventions

Let
(\mathcal{G},d_{\mathcal{G}})
denote a compact metric space used as an
embedding space for individual goals
,
and let
M(\mathcal{G})
denote the set of finite positive Borel measures on
\mathcal{G}
.
For
W>0
, put

M_{\leq W}(\mathcal{G}):=\{\mu\in M(\mathcal{G}):\mu(\mathcal{G})\leq W\}.

For a bounded function
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
, write

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}}|f(x)|,\quad\lip(f):=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d_{\mathcal{G}}(x,y)},\quad\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}:=\max\{\|f\|_{\infty},\lip(f)\}.

The
bounded Lipschitz metric
on
M(\mathcal{G})
is

d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1}\int_{\mathcal{G}}fd(\mu-\mu^{\prime}).

“Goal solved” is represented by the
zero measure

0\in M(\mathcal{G})
.

All probabilities are defined on an underlying probability space on
which the agent’s randomness (LLM sampling, exploration) and any
environment randomness live.

3
From formal proof search to a time-bounded reachability MDP

3.1
Informal picture

At each step the agent observes a proof assistant state (goals + local
context + error messages) and chooses an action (e.g., a tactic/lemma
invocation, or an LLM query template with retrieval parameters). The
proof assistant deterministically (or stochastically, if external tools
are randomized) returns the next state. We are interested in the
probability of reaching a solved state
within a fixed time/step
budget
.

3.2
Measure-valued encoding of “a finite set of goals”

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
denote the (discrete) set of syntactic goals (or
goal+context objects). Fix an embedding map

\phi:\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathcal{G}.

If a proof state
x
contains goals
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{G}}

(allowing multiplicity), define the
goal measure

x:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\phi(g_{i})}\in M(\mathcal{G}).

We note:
(1) We use symbol
x
, rather than
\mu
, to mention the goal measure.
(2) The solved state (“no goals”) corresponds to
x=0
(zero-measure).
(3) The number of goals is the total mass:
x(\mathcal{G})=n
.
(4) If we enforce (or truncate to)
x(\mathcal{G})\leq W
, then

x\in M_{\leq W}(\mathcal{G})
.

Especially, we use the following compactness in
Section

4
to guarantee the existence of optimal policies via dynamic programming with maximizers.

Theorem 1

(Compactness)
.

If
(\mathcal{G},d_{\mathcal{G}})
is compact, then
(M_{\leq W}(\mathcal{G}),d_{\mathrm{BL}})
is a compact metric space.

A full proof is given in
Appendix

B
.

This encoding is
permutation-invariant
and naturally supports
variable goal counts.

3.3
MDP

We define a finite-horizon MDP:

State space
:
\mathcal{X}:=M_{\leq W}(\mathcal{G})
(or a truncated
version; see
Section

8
).

Action space
: a compact metric space

(\mathcal{A},d_{\mathcal{A}})
representing admissible control
choices (tactic calls, parameters, retrieval settings, decoding
strategy, etc.).

Transition kernel
:
P(\cdot\mid x,a)
is a Borel
probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
.

Goal set
:
G:=\{0\}\subset\mathcal{X}
(closed in

d_{\mathrm{BL}}
).

We assume the proof assistant acts as a
verifier
: it produces
correct successor states, so that the MDP’s transition is a faithful
abstraction of actual interaction.

3.4
Provability as a time-bounded reachability objective

Definition 1

(Time-bounded success / provability)
.

Fix a horizon
B\in\mathbb{N}
. For a policy
\pi
and initial state

x\in\mathcal{X}
, define the
time-bounded success
probability

V_{B}^{\pi}(x):=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}\left(\exists t\in\{0,1,\dots,B\}\ \text{such that}\ X_{t}\in G\mid X_{0}=x\right).

The
optimal provability
is

V_{B}^{*}(x):=\sup_{\pi}V_{B}^{\pi}(x).

Definition 2

(Finite-horizon value functions)
.

For
b=0,1,\dots,B
define

V_{b}^{*}(x):=\sup_{\pi}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}\left(\exists t\in\{0,1,\dots,b\}\ \text{such that}\ X_{t}\in G\mid X_{0}=x\right).

Then
V_{0}^{*}(x)=\mathbf{1}[x\in G]
.

Definition 3

(One-step optimal action-value)
.

For
b\geq 1
define

Q_{b}^{*}(x,a):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}V_{b-1}^{*}(x^{\prime})P(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a).

We have Bellman recursion (finite-horizon reachability).
For
b\geq 0
:

V_{b+1}^{*}(x)=\mathbf{1}[x\in G]\ \vee\ \sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{b+1}^{*}(x,a).

Lemma 1

(Bounds and monotonicity)
.

For all
b
and
x
,
0\leq V_{b}^{*}(x)\leq 1
and

V_{b}^{*}(x)\leq V_{b+1}^{*}(x)
.

Proof.

Immediate from the definition of reachability
probabilities. ∎

4
Existence of optimal value functions and optimal Markov
policies

We state mild continuity assumptions ensuring that the supremum over
actions is attained.

Assumption 1

(Weak continuity / Feller-type condition)
.

For every bounded continuous
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the map

(x,a)\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x^{\prime})P(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a)

is continuous on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
.

This implies that
(x_{n},a_{n})\to(x,a)
entails

P(\cdot\mid x_{n},a_{n})\Rightarrow P(\cdot\mid x,a)
weakly.

Theorem 2

(Existence of maximizers and optimal Markov policies)
.

Assume:
(1)
\mathcal{X}=M_{\leq W}(\mathcal{G})
is compact under
d_{\mathrm{BL}}

(
Theorem

1
),
(2)
\mathcal{A}
is compact,
(3) the kernel
P
satisfies
1
,
(4)
G=\{0\}
is closed (hence
\mathbf{1}[x\in G]
is upper semicontinuous).

Then for each
b=1,\dots,B
:
(a) The Bellman recursion defines a Borel-measurable
V_{b}^{*}
,
(b) For each
x
, the supremum
\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)

is attained by some
a_{b}^{*}(x)\in\mathcal{A}
,
(c) hence there exists an optimal deterministic Markov policy

\pi^{*}=(a_{b}^{*})_{b=1}^{B}
achieving
V_{B}^{*}
.

The proof is based on backward induction. See
Appendix

C
for the full proof.

5
Bellman inequalities and provability certificates

The following monotonicity principle is central: it yields

upper/lower bound certificates
without solving the full MDP.

Definition 4

(Bellman operator)
.

Define
T
on bounded measurable
V:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
by

(TV)(x):=\mathbf{1}[x\in G]\ \vee\ \sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}V(x^{\prime})P(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a).

Then
V_{b+1}^{*}=TV_{b}^{*}
with
V_{0}^{*}=\mathbf{1}[\cdot\in G]
.

Lemma 2

(Monotonicity)
.

If
V\leq W
pointwise then
TV\leq TW
pointwise.

Proof.

Supremum and expectation preserve order. ∎

Theorem 3

(Sub-/super-solution certificates)
.

Let
(U_{b})_{b=0}^{B}
and
(L_{b})_{b=0}^{B}
be sequences of functions

\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
.

If
U_{0}\geq\mathbf{1}[\cdot\in G]
and

U_{b+1}\geq TU_{b}\quad\text{for all }b=0,\dots,B-1,

then

U_{b}\geq V_{b}^{*}
for all
b
.

If
L_{0}\leq\mathbf{1}[\cdot\in G]
and

L_{b+1}\leq TL_{b}\quad\text{for all }b=0,\dots,B-1,

then

L_{b}\leq V_{b}^{*}
for all
b
.

Proof.

Induction using
Lemma

2
.
∎

Definition 5

(provability certificate and certificate gap)
.

For an initial state
x_{0}
, any pair
(L_{B}(x_{0}),U_{B}(x_{0}))

such that

L_{B}(x_{0})\leq V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})\leq U_{B}(x_{0})

is a

provability certificate
, and

\mathrm{Gap}(x_{0}):=U_{B}(x_{0})-L_{B}(x_{0})

is the

certificate gap
.

6
Score-based planning: deviation from optimal provability

We now connect LLM-driven planning to provability via
score
functions
.

6.1
Score functions and induced policies

Let
(h_{b})_{b=1}^{B}
be score functions

h_{b}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1].

Define the greedy policy

\pi_{h}(x,b)\in\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}h_{b}(x,a).

Similarly,
define top-
k
candidate sets

\mathrm{Top}\text{-}k(h_{b}(x,\cdot))
and beam-search variants (beam
introduces a tree policy, but for analysis we track how often
near-optimal actions remain in the candidate set).

6.2
Uniform score approximation and worst-case regret

Assumption 2

(Uniform approximation of
Q_{b}^{*}
on a domain)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
be a compact
“relevant” domain (e.g., reachable pairs). Assume that for each
b
,

\sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{D}}\big|h_{b}(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)\big|\leq\varepsilon_{b}.

(How to obtain such bounds with high probability is addressed in
Section

7
)

Lemma 3

(One-step suboptimality bound)
.

Under
2
, for every
x
such that

(x,a)\in\mathcal{D}
for all relevant
a
,

\sup_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,\pi_{h}(x,b))\leq 2\varepsilon_{b}.

Proof.

Let
a^{*}\in\arg\max_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)
and

\hat{a}\in\arg\max_{a}h_{b}(x,a)
.
Then

Q_{b}^{*}(x,a^{*})\leq h_{b}(x,a^{*})+\varepsilon_{b}\leq h_{b}(x,\hat{a})+\varepsilon_{b}\leq Q_{b}^{*}(x,\hat{a})+2\varepsilon_{b}
.
Rearrange. ∎

Theorem 4

(Worst-case provability regret bound)
.

Under
2
, for any initial
x_{0}
whose relevant
trajectories remain inside
\mathcal{D}
,

0\leq V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})-V_{B}^{\pi_{h}}(x_{0})\leq 2\sum_{b=1}^{B}\varepsilon_{b}.

Proof.

At each step
b
, the greedy action loses at most

2\varepsilon_{b}
in the optimal one-step value (
Lemma

3
). Since
finite-horizon reachability accumulates these losses additively under
dynamic programming, summing yields the bound. ∎

6.3
Certificate construction from score bounds

Corollary 1

(Score-based certificate)
.

Under
2
, define for
b\geq 1
:

U_{b}(x):=\mathbf{1}[x\in G]\ \vee\ \mathrm{clip}\Big(\sup_{a}h_{b}(x,a)+\varepsilon_{b}\Big),

L_{b}(x):=\mathbf{1}[x\in G]\ \vee\ \mathrm{clip}\Big(\sup_{a}h_{b}(x,a)-\varepsilon_{b}\Big),

with
\mathrm{clip}(t)=\min\{1,\max\{0,t\}\}
. Then

L_{b}(x)\leq V_{b}^{*}(x)\leq U_{b}(x),\qquad U_{b}(x)-L_{b}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon_{b}.

Proof.

For each
x
,

\sup_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)\in[\sup_{a}h_{b}(x,a)-\varepsilon_{b},\ \sup_{a}h_{b}(x,a)+\varepsilon_{b}]
,
then apply
Theorem

3
. ∎

6.4
Top-
k
/beam and a margin condition (fast
rates)

Define the
(k+1)
-gap

\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(x):=Q_{b}^{*}(x,a_{1}^{*}(x))-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a_{k+1}^{*}(x)),

where
a_{i}^{*}(x)
are actions sorted by
Q_{b}^{*}(x,\cdot)
.

Assumption 3

(Small-margin tail under occupancy)
.

Let
\nu_{b}
be an occupancy distribution over states at depth
b

(induced by the algorithm or a reference policy). Assume there exist

C_{\Delta},\beta>0
such that for small
t
,

\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(x)\leq t)\leq C_{\Delta}t^{\beta}.

Theorem 5

(Expected fast-rate regret under margin)
.

If
2
and
3

hold,
then for a top-
k
policy (and similarly for a
beam that preserves the top-
k
set at each node),

\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q}\big[V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})-V_{B}^{\pi_{h,k}}(x_{0})\big]\ \leq\ C\sum_{b=1}^{B}\varepsilon_{b}^{\beta+1}

for a constant
C
depending on
C_{\Delta},\beta
.

Proof.

If
\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(x)>2\varepsilon_{b}
, uniform
perturbation of size
\varepsilon_{b}
cannot push the best action below
rank
k
, so top-
k
contains an optimal action. Errors only occur
on
{\Delta_{b}^{(k)}\leq 2\varepsilon_{b}}
, which has probability

\leq C_{\Delta}(2\varepsilon_{b})^{\beta}
, and the loss there is

O(\varepsilon_{b})
. Multiply and sum over
b
. ∎

We refer to
Appendix

D
for more details on occupancy distribution, margin condition, fast rates, and what if margin fails.

7
High-probability control of approximation error: sufficient
conditions

This section provides sufficient conditions under which
2

holds
with high probability
, in settings that include:
(1) offline pretraining from proof libraries (supervised on expert
traces),
(2) interactive pretraining using the verifier (rollouts, counterexamples,
self-play),
(3) adaptive data collection via beam search / rollouts.

7.1
Assumptions

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
be the compact
domain on which we need uniform control.

Assumption 4

(Geometry of the relevant domain)
.

Assume:

\mathcal{D}
has
doubling dimension

d_{\mathcal{D}}

(equivalently, covering numbers satisfy)

\mathcal{N}(\rho;\mathcal{D})\leq\left(\frac{C_{\mathcal{D}}}{\rho}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{D}}}\quad(0<\rho\leq 1).

Assumption 5

(Lipschitz score class)
.

Assume each hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}_{b}
is uniformly Lipschitz on

\mathcal{D}
:

\mathcal{H}_{b}\subseteq\lip_{L_{H}}(\mathcal{D},[0,1])
.

Definition 6

(Approximation error (expressivity term))
.

Define

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app},b}:=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{b}}\ \sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{D}}|h(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)|.

This is a
modeling
term: it depends on the representation and
architecture. Offline library pretraining and richer encoders can be
viewed as reducing
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app},b}
(or localizing the
effective hypothesis class).

7.2
Statistical estimation error (high probability)

We now state a generic “adaptive uniform deviation” lemma sufficient
for non-i.i.d. data produced by exploration.

Lemma 4

(Adaptive uniform deviation via covering numbers)
.

Let
(Z_{t})_{t=1}^{n}
be a process adapted to a filtration

(\mathcal{F}_{t})
taking values in
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\mathcal{F}

be a class of functions
f:\mathcal{D}\to[-1,1]
. Then for any

\varepsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Big(f(Z_{t})-\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\Big)\right|\ \leq\ 2\varepsilon+\sqrt{\frac{2\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon;\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})+2\log(2/\delta)}{n}}.

The proof is given in
Appendix

E
.

Lemma 5

(Metric entropy of Lipschitz score classes on doubling domains)
.

There exists a constant
C
depending on

C_{\mathcal{D}},d_{\mathcal{D}}
such that for all

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon;\mathcal{H}_{b},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq C\left(\frac{L_{H}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{D}}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

Proof.

Discretize
\mathcal{D}
by an
\eta
-net and
quantize function values; Lipschitzness controls interpolation error.
∎

Theorem 6

(A high-probability uniform approximation bound; sufficient condition)
.

Suppose that for each
(x,a)\in\mathcal{D}
we can produce bounded
targets
Y\in[0,1]
such that

\mathbb{E}[Y\mid x,a]=Q_{b}^{*}(x,a),

and we fit
\hat{h}_{b}\in\mathcal{H}_{b}
using empirical risk minimization
for squared loss (or any method achieving the uniform deviation
guarantees of
Lemma

4
). Then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{D}}|\hat{h}_{b}(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app},b}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}(n,\delta),

where (hiding polylog factors)

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}(n,\delta)=O\left(L_{H}^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{D}}}{d_{\mathcal{D}}+2}}\left(\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{D}}+2}}\right).

The proof is given in
Appendix

F
.

Remark 1

(What is
Y
)
.

Here
Y
is a training dataset for hypothesis
\hat{h}
.
With verifiers such as Lean and Rocq,
a canonical construction is the following
Monte Carlo rollout labeling
:
(1) Fix a horizon
b-1
.
(2) Start from proof state
x
.
(3) Apply action
a
once (tactic invocation / lemma call / tool query).
(4) Then follow some continuation procedure for at most
b-1
further steps.
(5) Output
Y=1
if the proof is solved within the remaining steps, else
Y=0
.

8
Variable number of goals: truncation and tail control
(compactness-preserving treatment)

The measure encoding naturally supports variable goal counts via

x(\mathcal{G})=|\text{goals}|
. However, compactness requires a mass bound.

8.1
Truncated state space and overflow event

Let
W>0
and define
\mathcal{X}:=M_{\leq W}(\mathcal{G})
. Consider the

overflow event

\mathsf{Overflow}:=\left\{\max_{t\leq B}X_{t}(\mathcal{G})>W\right\}.

In practice,
W
can correspond to:
(1) a hard cap induced by the prover (goal management),
(2) a cap on maintained subgoals due to beam limits,
(3) or a high-probability cap (quantile) justified empirically.

Proposition 1

(High-probability truncation)
.

Let
\pi
be any policy. If

\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{x_{0}}(\mathsf{Overflow})\leq\delta_{W},

then the true
time-bounded success probability satisfies

V_{B}^{\pi}(x_{0})\geq V_{B,\leq W}^{\pi}(x_{0})-\delta_{W},

where
V_{B,\leq W}^{\pi}
is the success probability in the

truncated MDP
that sends all overflow states to an absorbing
failure state.

Proof.

Couple the true and truncated processes until the first
overflow time. They agree on the complement of
\mathsf{Overflow}
. On

\mathsf{Overflow}
, truncated success is
\leq
true success (since
truncation forces failure). Hence the difference is at most

\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Overflow})
. ∎

Consequence.
All bounds proved on the compact space

M_{\leq W}(\mathcal{G})
extend to the original system with an additive

-\delta_{W}
(for lower bounds) or
+\delta_{W}
(for upper bounds),
depending on the direction.

9
Discussion on representation learning via certificate gap minimization

This section formalizes an objective for learning a
continuous
embedding/representation
that makes provability certificates tight.

9.1
Embedding as a decision variable

Let
\Phi
be a class of embeddings
\phi:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to K
(or
more generally, a class of metrics/feature maps inducing
K
and

d_{K}
). Each
\phi
induces:
(1) a state representation

\mu_{\phi}(x)=\sum_{i}\delta_{\phi(g_{i})}\in M_{\leq W}(K)
,
(2) a domain
\mathcal{D}_{\phi}\subseteq M_{\leq W}(K)\times\mathcal{A}
,
(3) hypothesis classes

\mathcal{H}_{\phi,b}\subseteq\mathrm{Lip}_{L_{H}}(\mathcal{D}_{\phi},[0,1])
.

9.2
The “best certificate” within a restricted function
family

Fix a function family
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}
(e.g., all
L
-Lipschitz
functions on
\mathcal{X}_{\phi}
) from which we construct
sub-/super-solutions. Define the
tightest
certificate bounds
within
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}
:

\displaystyle U_{B,\phi}^{*}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{U_{B}(\mu_{0}):(U_{b})_{b=0}^{B}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\phi},\ U_{b+1}\geq TU_{b},\ U_{0}\geq\mathbf{1}_{G}\right\},

\displaystyle L_{B,\phi}^{*}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle:=\sup\left\{L_{B}(\mu_{0}):(L_{b})_{b=0}^{B}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\phi},\ L_{b+1}\leq TL_{b},\ L_{0}\leq\mathbf{1}_{G}\right\}.

These are well-defined as optimization problems over a convex cone of
inequalities (when
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}
is convex).

9.3
Certificate-gap minimization objective

A natural representation-learning objective is:

\min_{\phi\in\Phi}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}\sim Q}\Big[U_{B,\phi}^{*}(\mu_{0})-L_{B,\phi}^{*}(\mu_{0})\Big]\ +\ \lambda\mathsf{Comp}(\phi),

where
\mathsf{Comp}(\phi)
penalizes representations that are
statistically difficult, e.g. via:
(1) doubling dimension / covering numbers of
\mathcal{D}_{\phi}
,
(2) Lipschitz constants required to represent
Q^{*}
,
(3) distortion/bi-Lipschitz constraints to prevent degenerate embeddings.

Interpretation.
This objective makes explicit the tradeoff:
better representations reduce approximation error and increase margins
(tight certificates),
but overly complex geometries increase statistical error (loose
certificates).

10
Discussions on scaling laws

Even without cut, the bounds already yield clean scaling relationships.

10.1
Estimation-error scaling on doubling domains

From
Theorem

6
, with doubling dimension
d_{\mathcal{D}}
,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}(n)\ \approx\ n^{-1/(d_{\mathcal{D}}+2)}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(n).

10.2
Regret scaling (worst-case vs margin)

Worst case (
Theorem

4
): if
\varepsilon_{b}\approx\varepsilon
,

V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})-V_{B}^{\pi_{h}}(x_{0})\ \lesssim\ B\varepsilon.

Under a margin condition (
Theorem

5
):

\mathbb{E}[V_{B}^{*}-V_{B}^{\pi_{h,k}}]\ \lesssim\ B\varepsilon^{\beta+1}.

Plugging

\varepsilon\approx\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}(n)

yields sample-complexity scaling for achieving a target regret
\eta
:

If
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}
is negligible and we use worst-case
regret:

Bn^{-1/(d_{\mathcal{D}}+2)}\lesssim\eta\quad\Rightarrow\quad n\gtrsim(B/\eta)^{d_{\mathcal{D}}+2}.

Under margin:

Bn^{-(\beta+1)/(d_{\mathcal{D}}+2)}\lesssim\eta\quad\Rightarrow\quad n\gtrsim(B/\eta)^{(d_{\mathcal{D}}+2)/(\beta+1)}.

10.3
Where “exponential improvements” can come from

The above formulas show that performance depends critically on:
the effective horizon
B
(or a smaller
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
),
the effective dimension
d_{\mathcal{D}}
,
and the margin exponent
\beta
.

Therefore, any structural mechanism (e.g., cut-introduction) that:
(1) reduces
B
to

L_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx O(\log\mathrm{Complexity})
, or
(2) reduces
d_{\mathcal{D}}
by localizing to a low-dimensional
manifold, or
(3) increases
\beta
by making correct actions more separated,
can change required sample sizes from

\mathrm{poly}(\mathrm{Complexity})
to

\mathrm{polylog}(\mathrm{Complexity})
, which is a standard sense in
which one observes
exponential-scale improvements
.

11
Discussions on easy vs hard instances

The bounds in
Sections

6
and
7
show that the achievable success probability
is governed by a small set of parameters:
(1) effective horizon / proof length (
B
or an effective

L_{\mathrm{eff}}
),
(2) approximation error
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}
,
(3) statistical error
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}(n)
driven by
geometry (doubling dimension, Lipschitz constants),
(4) margin parameters
(C_{\Delta},\beta)
for top-
k
/beam stability,
(5) overflow tail
\delta_{W}
.

A representative schematic lower bound is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q}[V_{B}^{\pi_{h,k}}(x_{0})]\ \gtrsim\ \mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q}[V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})]-L_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}})^{\beta+1}-\delta_{W}-\text{(beam/top-$k$ bias terms)}.

11.1
Easier instances (high success probability)

Instances tend to be easier when:

Short effective proofs
: small
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
(few steps
to solve).

Large action margins
: the correct next action is clearly
better, yielding larger
\beta
and smaller
C_{\Delta}
.

Low geometric complexity of reachable states
: small

d_{\mathcal{D}}
(local low-dimensional structure), improving

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
.

Low approximation error
:
Q^{*}
is smooth w.r.t. the chosen
representation and expressible by
\mathcal{H}
.

Informative verifier feedback
: quickly prunes invalid
branches and localizes the relevant domain
\mathcal{D}
.

11.2
Harder instances (low success probability)

Instances tend to be hard when:

Long required proofs
: large
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
, causing
error accumulation.

Small margins / many ambiguous states
:
\Delta_{b}^{(k)}

often near
0
, making top-
k
/beam fragile.

High geometric complexity
: large
d_{\mathcal{D}}
or
near-discrete geometry (huge covering numbers), inflating

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
.

Out-of-distribution (OOD)
: the relevant domain
\mathcal{D}

is poorly covered by training data, inflating both

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}
.

Computationally hard subproblems embedded in the proof
: if
the proof requires solving tasks believed hard (e.g., certain
cryptographic prediction tasks, or expensive computations not
automated by libraries), then
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
effectively
explodes and margins flatten, driving success probability down under
time limits.

12
Conclusion

We introduced a statistical provability framework for agentic theorem
proving, treating formal proof generation as a finite-horizon
reachability MDP and evaluating success by time-bounded probability
rather than worst-case decidability. The Bellman structure yields both
existence results and practical
provability certificates
, while
our deviation bounds make explicit how pipeline components—verifier
feedback, retrieval, test-time search, and representation
geometry—shape success through approximation error, sequential
complexity, and margin effects.
Overall, the theory explains why modern agentic systems can be effective on biased, structured theorem
distributions without contradicting classical hardness, and it
highlights where limitations remain inevitable under uniform or
adversarial instance regimes.

Appendix A
Literature Overview

This section reviews (i) learning-based theorem proving before modern
LLMs, (ii) the transition to language-model-driven formal provers, (iii)
recent “agentic” pipelines centered on decomposition and lemma
synthesis, and (iv) emerging theory for test-time scaling (TTS) and
verifier-guided inference. The goal is not an exhaustive survey but a
map of the design patterns most relevant to our probabilistic analysis
of end-to-end proof success.

A.1
From symbolic automation to learning-guided proof search
(pre-LLM)

Before LLMs, a major theme in automated and interactive theorem
proving was heuristics to guide symbolic search. Two
recurring bottlenecks were (a) selecting the right
premises
from
a large library and (b) selecting the next
proof step

(tactic/rule) so that search does not explode combinatorially.

TacticToe
(HOL4)
(
Gauthier et al. 2017
;
Gauthier et al. 2021
)
learns from
human proofs to predict tactics and then uses
Monte Carlo tree
search
to explore tactic-level proof paths. In its reported
experiments, TacticToe proves a substantial fraction of a
standard-library benchmark under a tight CPU budget, illustrating how
learned heuristics can dramatically outperform unguided search.

A second line of work developed
environments and datasets
that
make learning-guided proving reproducible at scale. The
HOList

environment (HOL Light)
(
Bansal et al. 2019
)
and related systems (e.g., DeepHOL) emphasized
“learning to prove” via imitation learning and reinforcement learning
in a higher-order logic setting.
In Lean specifically,

LeanDojo

(
Yang et al. 2023
)
provides programmatic access to proof states, tactics,
and premise annotations—lowering the barrier to training
retrieval-augmented provers and to evaluating them under standardized
conditions.

Finally, benchmark construction shaped the field’s trajectory by
defining what “success” means operationally.
miniF2F

(
Zheng et al. 2022
)
is
influential here because it targets olympiad-style mathematics across
multiple proof assistants and has become a common testbed for neural and
LLM-based provers.

Relevance to this study.
These pre-LLM systems already
instantiate the MDP perspective: a proof state evolves under
discrete actions, search is sequential/adaptive, and learned policies
are evaluated by
success probability under a compute budget
. What
changes in the LLM era is (i) the expressivity of the policy class, (ii)
the role of natural-language reasoning and intermediate “thought”
tokens, and (iii) the extent to which decomposition and retrieval become
first-class citizens of the pipeline.

A.2
Early neural language modeling for formal proof

GPT-f
(Metamath)
(
Polu and Sutskever 2020
)
is one of the earliest studies connecting LLM to
formal proof.
They found new short proofs, which were accepted into the main Metamath library.

Two methodological shifts occur in this era:

1.

From feature engineering to representation learning.
Proof
states and tactics can be encoded as sequences (or structured
objects), letting models learn latent representations of “what to do
next.”

2.

From local decisions to global strategies.
Generative models
naturally produce
multi-step
outputs (proof sketches, tactic
scripts), which encourages pipeline designs that interleave proposal,
verification, and refinement.

These shifts set the stage for today’s agentic provers, where “one-step
prediction” is only a component in a broader search-and-feedback loop.

A.3
Agentic theorem proving and the decomposition trend

Recent systems increasingly treat theorem proving as an
agentic
workflow
: propose a plan, decompose into subgoals, retrieve
lemmas/premises, attempt formal steps, and iterate using proof assistant
feedback. A particularly visible trend is
explicit subgoal
decomposition / lemma synthesis
—operationally analogous to
introducing “cuts” in proof theory.

A few representative approaches:

•

Draft, Sketch, and Prove (DSP).

(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
DSP maps informal proofs
(human-written or LLM-generated) into
formal proof sketches

that guide an automated prover by steering search toward easier
subproblems; reported results show substantial gains on
competition-style problem collections when guided by sketches.

•

DeepSeek-Prover and DeepSeek-Prover-V2.

(
Xin et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2025
)

DeepSeek-Prover
focuses on data generation and training for Lean 4 theorem proving;
DeepSeek-Prover-V2 explicitly emphasizes
reinforcement
learning for subgoal decomposition
, describing a recursive pipeline
that decomposes complex problems into subgoals and then synthesizes
proofs of subgoals into longer reasoning traces.

•

Hilbert.

(
Varambally et al. 2025
)
Hilbert is presented as an agentic framework that
aims to bridge informal reasoning and formal verification, explicitly
positioning itself as combining complementary strengths of
natural-language reasoning and proof assistant checking.

•

Prover Agent.

(
Baba et al. 2025
)
Prover Agent integrates LLMs with Lean and
explicitly coordinates multiple roles (informal reasoning, formal
proving, feedback usage), including auxiliary lemma generation.

•

Seed-Prover.

(
Chen et al. 2025
)
Seed-Prover frames itself as lemma-style
whole-proof reasoning that iteratively refines proofs based on Lean
feedback and intermediate lemmas, coupled with explicit test-time
inference strategies enabling “deep and broad” reasoning.

•

Aristotle.

(
Achim et al. 2025
)
Aristotle is described as combining formal
verification with informal reasoning and dedicated components such as
geometry solvers, targeting IMO-level performance.

Unifying view.
Despite varied engineering choices, these
systems share a structural pattern:

1.

Policy
proposes a candidate step or a short script.

2.

Planner
introduces intermediate structure (subgoals, lemmas,
proof sketches).

3.

Retriever
supplies context from a library (premises,
analogous proofs).

4.

Verifier
(proof assistant kernel and/or solver) filters and
provides feedback.

5.

The loop repeats under a
test-time compute budget
.

This is precisely the structure we model as an MDP with an augmented
agent, where success probability emerges from the interaction between
(i) the base policy’s quality, (ii) decomposition effectiveness, (iii)
retrieval accuracy, and (iv) the amplification provided by search and
verification.

A.4
Test-time scaling and reasoning models: empirical and
theoretical lenses

A second literature stream, partly orthogonal but increasingly
intertwined with theorem proving, studies
test-time scaling

(TTS): improving performance by allocating more compute during
inference, often via longer reasoning traces, repeated sampling, search,
or verifier-guided selection.

A.4.1
Chain-of-thought prompting as a control knob

Zero-shot chain-of-thought
prompting (“Let’s think step by
step”,
Kojima et al. 2022
) was popularized as a surprisingly strong baseline demonstrating
that a single instruction can elicit multi-step reasoning behavior even
without exemplars.
From
our standpoint, this is an early indication that the policy class
at test time is not fixed: prompts and inference procedures can
effectively change the hypothesis being deployed.

A.4.2
CoT as increased computational expressivity

Theoretical work has argued that producing intermediate reasoning tokens
can expand what constant-depth transformer computations can realize,
especially for tasks that are inherently serial
(
Feng et al. 2023
)
.
This resonates with
theorem proving, where long-horizon dependencies and serial structure
are fundamental: proofs are not merely long outputs, but adaptive
processes where future steps depend on earlier derived subgoals.

A.4.3
Latent-variable and variance-reduction viewpoints

A complementary theoretical lens treats rationales / chains-of-thought
as
latent variables
and studies training or inference by
marginalizing over them. In particular,
Phan et al. 2023
proposes
maximizing marginal likelihood over rationales and discusses variance
reduction in gradient estimates as models improve—connecting
conceptually to self-consistency as “Monte Carlo marginalization” at
inference time.

A.4.4
Which TTS strategy works depends on problem structure

Recent empirical studies argue that test-time compute does not translate
into uniform gains: different tasks benefit from different allocations
across sampling, self-correction, verifier-guided selection, or adaptive
procedures.
Wu et al. 2024
motivates one of our central modeling choices: the “difficulty” of a
theorem should not be summarized solely by a single scalar (e.g.,
length), but also by
structure
—including decomposability and
availability of useful lemmas in the library.

A.4.5
Verifiers can change scaling laws

Verifier-guided selection is often the mechanism that makes TTS
effective in high-stakes settings. Several authors
(
Beirami et al. 2025
;
Setlur et al. 2025
;
Botta et al. 2025
)

formalize
Best-of-
N
(BoN)
selection and characterize how
performance depends on verifier quality.
They argue for
separations between verifier-based and verifier-free approaches under
comparable compute/data budgets.
In theorem proving, the
proof assistant kernel plays the role of a near-perfect verifier for
syntactic correctness, but “semantic” verifiers (e.g., premise
relevance, lemma usefulness, intermediate progress) may be
imperfect—so the verifier-quality dependence remains central.

A.5
Statistical theory with computational constraints

Classical computational learning theory already highlights the role of

interaction
and
queries
. A canonical example is Angluin’s

L^{\ast}
algorithm

(
Angluin 1987
)

for learning regular languages from membership and
equivalence queries to a “teacher,” connecting sample efficiency to an
interactive protocol rather than passive i.i.d. data.
This viewpoint is highly suggestive for theorem proving: the proof
assistant and solver feedback constitute an interactive information
channel, and the agent’s objective is to adaptively extract the right
information to complete a proof.

More recently, several directions attempt to incorporate computational
constraints into information and generalization theory. For instance,

Finzi et al. 2026
proposes “epiplexity”, an information-like quantity intended to capture
what
computationally bounded observers
can extract from data,
explicitly motivated by tensions between classical information theory
and modern practice. Separately, several authors
(
Strobl et al. 2024
)
relate
transformer expressivity to formal languages and circuit classes, offering a
vocabulary to discuss which forms of reasoning are representationally
plausible under given architectural and precision constraints.

Positioning.
Our work sits at the intersection: we analyze
theorem proving as an interactive sequential decision process, where
success depends on both (i) computational/proof-theoretic structure
(e.g., the availability of short proofs via cut/lemmas) and (ii)
statistical/learning-theoretic quantities suited to adaptive interaction
(sequential complexity, covering numbers). We do not claim a full
unification, but we aim to put these components into a single framework
where their contributions to proof success probability can be compared
on the same scale.

Proof of

We prove

.

Lemma 6

(Arzelà–Ascoli compactness of the BL unit ball)

Let

\mathcal{F}:=\{f:K\to\mathbb{R}:\ \|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1\}.

Then

\mathcal{F}
is compact in
(C(K),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
.

Proof.

Functions in
\mathcal{F}
are uniformly bounded
(
\|f\|\leq 1
) and equicontinuous (Lipschitz constant
\leq 1

Lemma 7

(
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
metrizes weak convergence on
M_{\leq W}(K)
)
.

Let
(\mu_{n})
and
\mu
be in
M_{\leq W}(K)
.
Then

\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu\ \text{(weakly)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0.

Proof.

we show

•

(
\Leftarrow
) If
d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
, then

\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\to 0
for all bounded Lipschitz
f
. Bounded
Lipschitz functions are dense in
C(K)
on compact metric spaces, so
convergence holds for all continuous
f
, i.e.,

\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
.

•

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
. Suppose

d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\not\to 0

\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence (still indexed by

\left|\int f_{n}d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }n.

By Lemma A.1,
(f_{n})

\displaystyle\left|\int f_{n}d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|+\left|\int(f_{n}-f)d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|+\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\mu_{n}-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

\mu_{n},\mu

. Also,

\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\to 0
by weak convergence. Hence the right-hand

\geq\varepsilon

Therefore

. ∎

Lemma 8

(Compactness)
.

M_{\leq W}(K)
is compact under
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
.

Proof.

M_{\leq W}(K)
is tight and weakly sequentially

Appendix D
Occupancy distribution, margin condition, fast rates, and what if margin fails

D.1
Occupancy distribution
\nu_{b}
: definition and meaning

In finite-horizon control, the
occupancy distribution
captures

where the algorithm actually visits
, and is the correct object
for distributional assumptions (like margins).

For a (possibly randomized) policy
\pi
and an initial distribution

Q
over states, define the depth-
b
state occupancy as the law of

X_{b-1}
:

\nu_{b}^{\pi}(\cdot):=\Pr^{\pi}(X_{b-1}\in\cdot\mid X_{0}\sim Q).

For reachability tasks, it is often more appropriate to condition on
“not solved yet”:

\tilde{\nu}_{b}^{\pi}(\cdot):=\Pr^{\pi}(X_{b-1}\in\cdot\mid X_{0}\sim Q,\ T^{\pi}\geq b),

because decisions after termination are irrelevant.

For beam search, one can analogously define an occupancy over expanded
nodes at depth
b
induced by the beam’s node-selection rule; the key
point is the same:
\nu_{b}
is the distribution of states that
matter at depth
b
under the algorithm.

D.2
Margin condition: what it says

Let
Q_{b}^{*}(x,\cdot)
be the optimal one-step value at depth
b
. For
top-
k
analysis, define the
(k+1)
-gap

\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(x):=Q_{b}^{*}(x,a_{1}^{*}(x))-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a_{k+1}^{*}(x)),

where actions are ranked by
Q_{b}^{*}
.

The
margin condition

(
Arlot and Bartlett 2011
, Mammen-Tsybakov-style, see e.g.)
states that near-ties are
rare:

\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(X)\leq t)\ \leq\ C_{\Delta}t^{\beta}\quad\text{for small }t.

Interpretation:

•

\beta
large
\Rightarrow
“clear separation”: ambiguous
states are rare.

•

\beta
small (or
0
)
\Rightarrow
“many near-ties”: small
score errors often change rankings.

This is exactly the condition under which plug-in decision rules achieve

fast rates
in classification, transplanted here to action
selection.

D.3
Proof of
Theorem

5
: fast-rate proof (for the plug-in greedy policy)

To make the argument transparent, we first present the
k=1
(greedy)
case; the top-
k
version is the same with
\Delta_{b}^{(k)}
.

Assume a uniform score approximation at depth
b
:

\displaystyle\sup_{x,a\in\mathcal{D}}|h_{b}(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)|\leq\varepsilon.

(1)

Let
\hat{a}(x)\in\arg\max_{a}h_{b}(x,a)
and

a^{*}(x)\in\arg\max_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)
. Define the gap

\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x):=Q_{b}^{*}(x,a^{*}(x))-\max_{a\neq a^{*}(x)}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)
.

Lemma 9

(Mis-selection implies small gap)
.

Under
Equation

1
, if
\hat{a}(x)\neq a^{*}(x)
then

\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon
.

Proof.

If
\hat{a}\neq a^{*}
, then

Q_{b}^{*}(x,a^{*})\leq h_{b}(x,a^{*})+\varepsilon\leq h_{b}(x,\hat{a})+\varepsilon\leq Q_{b}^{*}(x,\hat{a})+2\varepsilon.

Thus
Q_{b}^{*}(x,a^{*})-Q_{b}^{*}(x,\hat{a})\leq 2\varepsilon
. Since

\hat{a}
is not optimal, the left side is at least the gap

\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)
, giving
\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon
.
∎

Lemma 10

(Pointwise regret bound)
.

Define the one-step regret at
x
:

r_{b}(x):=\max_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,\hat{a}(x)).

Then under
Equation

1
,

r_{b}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon\ \mathbf{1}{\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon}.

Proof.

If
\hat{a}=a^{*}
, then
r_{b}(x)=0
. Otherwise, by

Lemma

9
,

\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon
and also

r_{b}(x)\leq\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon
. Combine. ∎

Now take expectation under occupancy
\nu_{b}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{b}}[r_{b}(X)]\leq 2\varepsilon\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(1)}\leq 2\varepsilon)\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot C_{\Delta}(2\varepsilon)^{\beta}=O(\varepsilon^{\beta+1}).

This is the
fast rate
: the expected regret is smaller than
linear in
\varepsilon
when
\beta>0
.

Finally, summing over depths (and propagating through Bellman recursion)
yields an overall provability gap bound of the form

\mathbb{E}[V_{B}^{*}(X_{0})-V_{B}^{\pi_{h}}(X_{0})]\ \lesssim\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{b}}[r_{b}(X)]\ =\ O\left(B\varepsilon^{\beta+1}\right),

up to constants and technical details about termination (see Section
4 below).

Remark 2

(Top-
k
/ beam version)
.

Replace
\Delta_{b}^{(1)}
by
\Delta_{b}^{(k)}
. The same logic shows:

•

if the optimal action is pruned from top-
k
, then

\Delta_{b}^{(k)}\leq 2\varepsilon
;

•

the loss incurred by missing the optimal action is at most

\Delta_{b}^{(k)}\leq 2\varepsilon
on that event;

•

thus expected loss scales as

\varepsilon\cdot\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(k)}\leq 2\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{\beta+1})
.

D.4
If the margin condition fails: what can we still say?

If the margin tail does
not
decay (effectively
\beta=0
),
then the above argument collapses to the worst-case linear behavior:

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{b}}[r_{b}(X)]\ \lesssim\ O(\varepsilon).

More pessimistically, if there is non-negligible mass of exact ties
(e.g.,
\nu_{b}(\Delta=0)>0
), then ranking-based selection can be
intrinsically unstable: arbitrarily small perturbations can flip the
argmax on that set. In that case:

•

fast rates are not information-theoretically available
from
plug-in ranking alone,

•

and one must rely on
search
(keeping multiple candidates),

additional structure
(tie-breaking information, richer
feedback), or accept slower rates.

Beam/top-
k
can still help when margins fail, but then the analysis
must track
set coverage
rather than “gap-based stability.” A
common alternative is to bound success in terms of the probability that

at least one
of the retained actions is “good enough,” which
leads to different regularity assumptions (e.g., mass of

\varepsilon
-optimal actions) rather than gap tails.

Appendix E
Proof of
Lemma

4
(Adaptive uniform deviation via covering numbers)

Statement.
Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration and
let
Z_{t}
be
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variables taking
values in a set
\mathcal{Z}
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of
functions
f:\mathcal{Z}\to[0,1]
. For
f\in\mathcal{F}
, define the
martingale difference

D_{t}(f):=f(Z_{t})-\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{M}\}
be an
\varepsilon
-net
of
\mathcal{F}
in
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
(so

M=\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon;\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
). Then for any

\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq 2\varepsilon+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}}.

Proof.

we show

1.

Reduction to a finite net.
For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, choose

f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
such that

\|f-f_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
. Then for each
t
,

\displaystyle|D_{t}(f)-D_{t}(f_{i})|

\displaystyle=\left|\big(f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})\big)-\mathbb{E}\big[f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\right|

\displaystyle\leq|f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})|+\mathbb{E}\big[|f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})|\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\varepsilon=2\varepsilon.

Averaging over
t
gives

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|+2\varepsilon.

Hence

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq M}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|+2\varepsilon.

2.

Concentration for each net element.
For fixed
i
, the
sequence
D_{t}(f_{i})
is a martingale difference adapted to

\mathcal{F}_{t}
and satisfies
D_{t}(f_{i})\in[-1,1]
because

f_{i}(Z_{t})\in[0,1]
and its conditional expectation lies in

[0,1]
. By Azuma–Hoeffding,

\Pr\left(\left|\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|\geq n\gamma\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\gamma^{2}}{2}\right).

3.

Union bound.
By union bound over
i=1,\dots,M
,

\Pr\left(\max_{i\leq M}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|\geq\gamma\right)\leq 2M\exp\left(-\frac{n\gamma^{2}}{2}\right).

Set the right-hand side to
\delta
and solve:

\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}}.

4.

Combine steps.
With this choice of
\gamma
, with
probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq 2\varepsilon+\gamma,

which is the desired bound.

∎

Appendix F
Proof of
Theorem

6
(High-probability uniform approximation)

Technically, this theorem implicitly mixes two issues:

1.

controlling estimation error under adaptive sampling, and

2.

converting that to a
uniform-in-domain sup norm bound
.

F.1
Setting

•

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
be compact.

•

Let
Q:\mathcal{D}\to[0,1]
denote the target function (in the main text,

Q=Q_{b}^{*}
).

•

Assume
Q
is
L_{Q}
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{D}
.

•

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathrm{Lip}_{L_{H}}(\mathcal{D},[0,1])
be
the hypothesis class.

•

Define approximation error

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}:=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\ \|h-Q\|_{\infty}.

F.2
Sampling assumption (coverage / generative access)

There exist an
\eta
-net
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
of
\mathcal{D}

(with
N=\mathcal{N}(\eta;\mathcal{D})
) and, for each net point

z_{i}
, we can collect
m
conditionally unbiased samples

Y_{i,1},\dots,Y_{i,m}\in[0,1],\qquad\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,j}\mid z_{i}]=Q(z_{i}),

independent across
j
given
z_{i}
. (In practice,
Y_{i,j}
can
be a rollout success indicator from
(x,a)=z_{i}
.)

Let
\hat{q}_{i}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}Y_{i,j}
.

F.3
Constructing an estimator

Define
\hat{h}
as a Lipschitz extension of the values

\{\hat{q}_{i}\}
onto
\mathcal{D}
with Lipschitz constant at most

L_{H}
(e.g., a McShane extension). Then

\hat{h}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{L_{H}}(\mathcal{D},[0,1])
. If we further
project
\hat{h}
onto
\mathcal{H}
(or assume
\mathcal{H}

contains such extensions), we can ensure
\hat{h}\in\mathcal{H}
up to

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}
.

F.4
Statement (Uniform sup norm bound; explicit form)

Theorem 7

(
Theorem

6
, refined)
.

Under the setting above, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|\hat{h}-Q\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}+(L_{H}+L_{Q})\eta+\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2m}}.

(2)

In particular, if
\mathcal{D}
has doubling dimension
d
so that

N\lesssim(C/\eta)^{d}
and
n:=mN
is the total number of samples,
optimizing
\eta
yields (up to polylog factors)

\|\hat{h}-Q\|_{\infty}\ \lesssim\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}+(L_{H}+L_{Q})^{\frac{d}{d+2}}\left(\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d+2}}.

Proof.

Fix an
\eta
-net
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of

\mathcal{D}
. For each
i
, by Hoeffding’s inequality,

\Pr\left(|\hat{q}_{i}-Q(z_{i})|\geq\tau\right)\leq 2\exp(-2m\tau^{2}).

Union bound over
i=1,\dots,N
gives: with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\max_{i\leq N}|\hat{q}_{i}-Q(z_{i})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2m}}.

(3)

Now fix any
z\in\mathcal{D}
. Let
z_{i}
be a net point with

d(z,z_{i})\leq\eta
. Then

\displaystyle|\hat{h}(z)-Q(z)|

\displaystyle\leq|\hat{h}(z)-\hat{h}(z_{i})|+|\hat{h}(z_{i})-Q(z_{i})|+|Q(z_{i})-Q(z)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{H}d(z,z_{i})+|\hat{q}_{i}-Q(z_{i})|+L_{Q}d(z,z_{i})

\displaystyle\leq(L_{H}+L_{Q})\eta+\max_{j\leq N}|\hat{q}_{j}-Q(z_{j})|.

Taking supremum over
z\in\mathcal{D}
and using
Equation

3
yields the
bound for the extension estimator. If
\hat{h}
is then projected into

\mathcal{H}
, an additional
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}
term appears
by triangle inequality, giving
Equation

2
. ∎
