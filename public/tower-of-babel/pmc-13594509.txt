Impact of Cipepofol versus Propofol on Long-Term Cardiovascular Outcomes After TAVR: An Extended Follow-Up of a Randomized Trial
Authors: Tingting Ni, Yantian Lv, Laiying Zhou, Jing Yu, Xincheng Tao, Han Cao, Yuanyuan Yao, Min Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients undergoing transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) under general anesthesia are particularly vulnerable to hemodynamic instability. General anesthesia remains a common approach and is necessary in selected patients, although conscious sedation is increasingly used. A previous randomized trial showed greater peri-induction hemodynamic stability with cipepofol than with propofol, but whether this translates into improved clinical outcomes is uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a post hoc, exploratory extended follow-up of a single-center randomized trial. Patients were randomly assigned to cipepofol or propofol. In-hospital analyses included 122 TAVR-treated participants (61 per group), whereas the 30-day and 1-year analyses included 118 participants with follow-up data in the full analysis set (59 per group). The primary endpoint was the 1-year composite of all-cause mortality, stroke, acute kidney injury, myocardial infarction, and new-onset atrial fibrillation (NOAF). For this exploratory analysis, the components were grouped based on their hypothesized association with periprocedural hemodynamic instability and were also reported separately.ResultsAt 1 year, the primary composite endpoint occurred in 13/59 (22.0%) patients in the cipepofol group and 22/59 (37.3%) patients in the propofol group (hazard ratio [HR] 0.56; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.28 to 1.10; P = 0.093). NOAF, analyzed separately as an exploratory secondary endpoint, occurred less frequently with cipepofol than with propofol (7/59 [11.9%] vs 17/59 [28.8%]; HR 0.39; 95% CI 0.16 to 0.95; P = 0.037). At 30 days, the composite endpoint occurred in 9/59 (15.3%) and 14/59 (23.7%) patients, respectively (HR 0.63; 95% CI 0.27 to 1.45; P = 0.277). The in-hospital composite endpoints were 10/61 (16.4%) and 16/61 (26.2%), respectively (P = 0.185).ConclusionIn this exploratory extended follow-up, cipepofol did not significantly reduce the 1-year primary composite endpoint compared with propofol. The observed lower incidence of NOAF should be regarded as hypothesis-generating given the limited sample size, multiple comparisons, and intermittent rhythm surveillance. The applicability of these findings is limited to patients undergoing transfemoral TAVR under general anesthesia and does not extend to procedures performed under conscious sedation.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is an established therapy for patients with severe aortic stenosis.1,2 Intraoperative hypotension has been associated with acute kidney injury, myocardial injury, stroke, and mortality across high-risk surgical populations, with risk increasing according to both the depth and duration of exposure. Individualized blood pressure management reduced postoperative organ dysfunction in a randomized trial of high-risk surgical patients,3 while observational evidence demonstrates an exposure–response relationship. In noncardiac surgery, the risks of myocardial and kidney injury increased progressively with longer exposure to a mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg,4 and a meta-analysis associated intraoperative hypotension with acute kidney injury, myocardial injury, and mortality.5 In cardiac surgery, each additional 10 minutes below this threshold was associated with a 5% increase in the adjusted odds of a composite of stroke, acute kidney injury, and in-hospital mortality.6 Although these data were derived largely from broader surgical populations and do not directly establish the same exposure-outcome relationship specifically in TAVR, patients undergoing TAVR are typically older adults with substantial cardiovascular comorbidity and limited cardiac output reserve, making avoidance of hypotension particularly important for preserving coronary and cerebral perfusion.

Transfemoral TAVR may be performed under general anesthesia or local anesthesia with conscious sedation. In the randomized SOLVE-TAVI trial, a large head-to-head comparison, the two strategies met the prespecified criterion for equivalence for the 30-day composite endpoint.7 Related composite endpoints did not differ significantly at 1 or 5 years, although 5-year all-cause mortality was lower in the conscious sedation arm (41.5% vs 54.3%; HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.53–0.94; P = 0.02), a secondary long-term finding from a trial powered for a 30-day endpoint that requires confirmation.8,9 A large registry analysis associated conscious sedation with modestly better outcomes, although residual confounding cannot be excluded.10 General anesthesia nevertheless remained common during the 2016–2019 US registry period: approximately 35% of transfemoral procedures were performed under general anesthesia, and 17% of centers used it exclusively.10 General anesthesia remains necessary in selected patients, including those unable to tolerate conscious sedation or requiring transesophageal echocardiographic guidance, and when intraprocedural conversion is required. The choice of induction agent therefore remains clinically relevant wherever general anesthesia is used.

When TAVR is performed under general anesthesia, the choice of anesthetic agent is clinically important because patients with severe aortic stenosis tolerate abrupt reductions in systemic vascular resistance and cardiac output poorly. Within the broader framework of hemodynamic management, selection of an induction agent with less vasodilatory and cardiovascular depressant effects represents a modifiable pharmacological strategy that may be particularly relevant in this high-risk population. This approach complements other measures to minimize hypotension, including preload optimization, individualized blood pressure targets, careful anesthetic titration, and early vasopressor support. Although propofol is widely used in general anesthesia, it can reduce arterial pressure through vasodilation and cardiovascular depression and may precipitate hypotension in hemodynamically vulnerable patients.11,12 It has been hypothesized that the vasopressor support required to correct such hypotension may further increase myocardial oxygen demand and contribute to arrhythmogenic stress, although this pathway has not been established mechanistically in this setting.13,14

Cipepofol is a newer intravenous anesthetic related to propofol and has been shown to have a more favorable hemodynamic profile in perioperative studies.15,16 Most of these studies were, however, powered for sedation quality or short-term hemodynamic endpoints rather than for clinical outcomes, and few enrolled patients with severe valvular heart disease, so the evidence base in this population remains limited. In our previous randomized clinical trial of patients who underwent transfemoral TAVR under general anesthesia, compared to propofol, cipepofol was associated with a lower incidence of postinduction hypotension, a smaller reduction in mean arterial pressure, and lower norepinephrine requirements.17 Although similar hemodynamic goals may be pursued through other pharmacological and nonpharmacological strategies, the randomized comparison in the parent trial provided a specific opportunity to determine whether the early hemodynamic advantage associated with induction-agent selection translated into improved longer-term clinical outcomes.

Because clinically important outcomes after TAVR are not limited to the perioperative period, and because TAVR is increasingly offered to patients with longer life expectancy in whom outcomes beyond discharge carry greater weight, we extended follow-up of the randomized cohort to 1 year, the time point at which valve performance and health status are conventionally reassessed.18 We therefore compared 1-year outcomes after transfemoral TAVR with cipepofol or propofol.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Oversight

Ethical approval for this extended follow-up study (approval no. 2025–1849) was provided by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Hangzhou, China (Chairperson Prof. Zhiying Wu) on 1 December 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The parent randomized trial17 was approved at the same institution on 12 April 2023. Although long-term follow-up was not specified in the original trial protocol, all participants had been prospectively enrolled in the cardiology department’s structured cardiovascular registry.

Study Population and Randomization

The parent randomized trial screened 169 consecutive patients with severe aortic stenosis who were deemed suitable candidates for transfemoral TAVR by a multidisciplinary heart team. Briefly, eligible patients were aged 60 to 85 years, had an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status of III or IV, and were scheduled for elective transfemoral TAVR under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Major exclusion criteria included preoperative tracheal intubation, refractory hemodynamic instability, relevant drug allergies, and major neurologic or psychiatric disorders. Full eligibility criteria were reported previously.17

In total, 124 patients met the eligibility criteria and were randomized at a 1:1 ratio to receive general anesthesia with either cipepofol or propofol. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence in permuted blocks and concealed by sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes that were opened immediately before induction.

For the present extended follow-up analysis, in-hospital outcomes were analyzed in the TAVR-treated cohort, which included patients who underwent TAVR with the assigned study drug. Follow-up outcomes at 30 days and 1 year were analyzed in the full analysis set (FAS), which included randomized patients with available follow-up data. Participants without postdischarge follow-up data were excluded from the 30-day and 1-year analyses, and no outcome imputation was performed. Supportive follow-up analyses were performed in the per-protocol set (PPS), which excluded patients with major protocol deviations, including conversion to open-chest surgery. One-year echocardiographic outcomes were analyzed using available cases without imputation. Anesthesia Protocol and Periprocedural Management

The anesthetic protocol was described in detail in the original hemodynamic report. In brief, all procedures were performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation, and patients received cipepofol or propofol within a standardized regimen. Hemodynamic management was guided by continuous arterial pressure monitoring, with norepinephrine titrated to predefined targets. Procedural aspects of TAVR followed institutional standards and were independent of treatment allocation.

Follow-Up and Data Collection

Clinical follow-up was conducted as part of the institutional prospective TAVR registry. Patients were scheduled for standardized clinical evaluations at 30 days (±7 days) and 1 year (±30 days) after TAVR, which included an assessment of symptoms and adverse events, 12-lead electrocardiography, and transthoracic echocardiography. Health status was evaluated using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire-12 (KCCQ-12) at baseline and at 1 year in survivors. The KCCQ-12 instrument used in this study is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1.

If patients were unable to attend outpatient visits, structured telephone interviews were conducted using a standardized questionnaire. Stroke and myocardial infarction required medical-record documentation consistent with the prespecified VARC-3 definitions, whereas new-onset atrial fibrillation required electrocardiographic documentation consistent with the prespecified study definition.

Study Outcomes

Primary Endpoint

The primary endpoint of this extended follow-up was the 1-year composite endpoint, defined as all-cause mortality, stroke, acute kidney injury, myocardial infarction, or new-onset atrial fibrillation occurring within 1 year after TAVR. For this post hoc exploratory analysis, the components were selected after completion of follow-up data collection but before database lock and any between-group comparative analysis, based on a hypothesized relationship with periprocedural hemodynamic instability rather than equivalent clinical severity. The selected mortality, cardiovascular, neurologic, renal, and rhythm outcomes are recognized within the VARC-3 endpoint framework. New-onset atrial fibrillation was included as a clinically relevant rhythm outcome; although its relationship to the proposed hemodynamic mechanism remains unproven, new-onset atrial fibrillation after TAVR has been associated with increased risks of stroke, bleeding, and mortality. Because the components differ substantially in clinical severity, each was also reported separately.

Secondary Endpoints

Secondary endpoints included the primary composite at 30 days; its individual components at 30 days and 1 year; an in-hospital composite including postoperative delirium; 1-year “alive and well” status; cardiovascular and cerebrovascular readmissions; and 1-year echocardiographic parameters.

Definitions of Clinical Endpoints

Endpoint definitions were fixed before database lock and before any between-group comparative analysis, as described in the Statistical Analysis section. All-cause mortality, stroke, and myocardial infarction were defined in accordance with the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria.18 AKI was defined and staged according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria.19 Detailed operational definitions for all endpoints are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.

Statistical Analysis

Long-term follow-up was not part of the original trial protocol, and the composite endpoint reported here was not included in the trial registration, which specified a periprocedural hemodynamic primary endpoint. Endpoint definitions and the analysis plan were fixed before any between-group comparative analysis and before database lock, but after follow-up data collection had been completed. No sample size calculation was performed for the clinical outcomes reported here; the sample size was determined by the parent trial, which was powered for a periprocedural hemodynamic endpoint. The present analyses are therefore post hoc and exploratory. Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; variables consistent with a normal distribution in both groups are presented as the mean (standard deviation [SD]), and all others as the median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages). Between-group comparisons of continuous outcomes used the independent-samples t test for variables presented as mean (SD) and the Mann–Whitney U-test for variables presented as median (IQR). Categorical outcomes were compared using the Pearson χ2-test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Given the randomized design, generally similar baseline characteristics, and limited number of clinical events, unadjusted analyses were used to preserve the randomized comparison and avoid unstable multivariable adjustment and overfitting.

In-hospital outcomes were analyzed in the TAVR-treated cohort, which included all patients who underwent TAVR with the assigned study drug before any postdischarge loss to follow-up occurred. Follow-up outcomes at 30 days and 1 year were analyzed in the FAS, with supportive analyses performed in the PPS. For the primary composite endpoint at 30 days and 1 year, Kaplan–Meier–based cumulative event curves (1-minus-survival plots) were generated and compared using the Log rank test, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression. For new-onset atrial fibrillation, all-cause death was treated as a censoring event, and the relative treatment effect was estimated using a cause-specific Cox proportional hazards model. Because model-based estimates are unstable when events are few, effect estimates are reported in the main analysis only for the composite endpoint, for which 35 events occurred, and new-onset atrial fibrillation, for which 24 events occurred. Outcomes with fewer than 10 events in total are summarized descriptively as counts and percentages. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons.

Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were fitted for new-onset atrial fibrillation in the FAS and PPS as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the findings under a competing-risk framework (Tables S6–S9). Additional exploratory model outputs for sparse outcomes are included in these tables for completeness and transparency; they are not interpreted in the main manuscript or used to support clinical conclusions. All tests were 2-sided. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.2.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

From 29 June 2023 to 8 July 2024, 169 patients with severe aortic stenosis who were eligible for transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement were screened, 124 of whom underwent randomization to receive cipepofol (n = 62) or propofol (n = 62). One patient in each group did not receive the assigned study drug; therefore, 122 patients (61 per group) underwent TAVR with the assigned study drug and constituted the TAVR-treated cohort for perioperative and in-hospital outcome analyses. During follow-up, 2 patients in the cipepofol group and 2 in the propofol group were lost to follow-up. In addition, 1 patient in the propofol group underwent conversion to open-chest surgery and was excluded from the per-protocol set. Thus, the FAS for 30-day and 1-year follow-up outcome analyses included 118 patients (59 in each group), and the PPS included 117 patients (59 in the cipepofol group and 58 in the propofol group) (Figure 1).

In the FAS, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally well balanced between groups (Table 1). The mean (SD) age was 72.1 (4.4) years in the cipepofol group and 72.2 (5.3) years in the propofol group, and 28 patients (47.5%) and 23 patients (39.0%), respectively, were women. The median (IQR) STS-PROM score was 2.4% (1.6–3.9%) in the cipepofol group and 2.3% (1.5–3.9%) in the propofol group. In-Hospital and 30-Day Outcomes

In the TAVR-treated cohort, the in-hospital composite endpoint, defined as the primary composite plus postoperative delirium, occurred in 10 of 61 patients (16.4%) in the cipepofol group and 16 of 61 patients (26.2%) in the propofol group (P = 0.185). No all-cause deaths or strokes occurred during hospitalization in either group, and the individual in-hospital outcomes are summarized in Table S1.

At 30 days, in the FAS, the primary composite endpoint occurred in 9 of the 59 patients (15.3%) in the cipepofol group and 14 of the 59 patients (23.7%) in the propofol group (HR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.27–1.45; P = 0.277). No all-cause deaths occurred within 30 days in either group. Stroke occurred only in the propofol group (2 of 59 [3.4%]). The individual component outcomes at 30 days are summarized in Table S2.

Additional 1-Year Outcomes

Among survivors, 49 of 56 patients (87.5%) in the cipepofol group and 43 of 58 patients (74.1%) in the propofol group met the prespecified “alive and well” definition (P = 0.071) (Figure S1). Cardiovascular or cerebrovascular readmission occurred in 6 of the 59 patients (10.2%) in the cipepofol group and in 8 of the 59 patients (13.6%) in the propofol group (P = 0.569) (Figure S2). One-year echocardiographic data were unavailable for 8 patients in the cipepofol group and 10 in the propofol group. Of these, 3 and 1 patient, respectively, had died before the 1-year visit; the remaining 5 and 9 patients were alive but declined the protocol echocardiographic examination at the 1-year visit. Supportive Analyses in the per-Protocol Set

Supportive analyses in the PPS were directionally consistent with the primary FAS findings (Tables S3–S5). In the PPS, the 1-year primary composite endpoint occurred in 13 of 59 patients (22.0%) in the cipepofol group and 21 of 58 patients (36.2%) in the propofol group (HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.29–1.15; P = 0.120). New-onset atrial fibrillation likewise remained less frequent in the cipepofol group at 1 year (7 of 59 [11.9%] vs 16 of 58 [27.6%]; HR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.17–0.99; P = 0.048).

Sensitivity Analyses

Fine–Gray sensitivity analyses for new-onset atrial fibrillation yielded results consistent with the primary cause-specific Cox analyses in both the FAS and PPS. In the FAS, the subdistribution HR for new-onset atrial fibrillation was 0.57 (95% CI, 0.23–1.42; P = 0.230) at 30 days (Table S6) and 0.39 (95% CI, 0.16–0.95; P = 0.037) at 1 year (Table S7). In the PPS, the results of the Fine–Gray sensitivity analyses were also concordant with the primary results (Tables S8 and S9).

Discussion

This extended follow-up of a randomized clinical trial provides, to our knowledge, the first comparative 1-year clinical outcome data for cipepofol versus propofol in patients who underwent transfemoral TAVR under general anesthesia. Although the primary composite endpoint was not significantly reduced, the lower incidence of new-onset atrial fibrillation with cipepofol was a nominally significant secondary finding that should be interpreted as exploratory.

The 15.3-percentage-point absolute difference in the 1-year composite endpoint may represent a possible signal; however, the 95% confidence interval included no effect, and because the parent trial was powered for hemodynamic rather than clinical outcomes, this finding remains hypothesis-generating. The direction of the clinical findings was concordant with the earlier physiologic findings, but the present analysis cannot establish that greater peri-induction hemodynamic stability translated into improved 1-year clinical outcomes. In our cohort, the 1-year composite endpoint occurred in 22.0% of patients receiving cipepofol and 37.3% of those receiving propofol, and NOAF occurred in 11.9% and 28.8%, respectively. Although cross-trial comparisons are limited by differences in endpoint composition, the overall rates of death and stroke in the present study were broadly within the range reported in contemporary TAVR series, in which 1-year mortality ranged from approximately 3.1% to 4.6% and stroke from approximately 2.4% to 2.6%.20 A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled post-TAVR NOAF incidence of approximately 9.9%.21 However, this cross-study comparison is limited by heterogeneity in populations, detection methods, and follow-up durations, and the observed rates may also reflect study-specific factors.

The between-group difference in new-onset atrial fibrillation was the principal secondary signal observed in this analysis. Post-TAVR NOAF is clinically relevant because registry and claims-based studies have associated it with increased risks of stroke, bleeding, and mortality.22–24 However, given the small sample size, multiple comparisons, and intermittent rhythm surveillance, the present finding remains exploratory and requires confirmation in an adequately powered study.

The observed between-group difference in new-onset atrial fibrillation is biologically plausible within the contemporary substrate-trigger framework for postoperative atrial fibrillation.25,26 In severe aortic stenosis, chronic pressure overload and diastolic dysfunction may promote left atrial dilation and fibrotic remodeling, thereby creating a vulnerable substrate for atrial arrhythmia.25,27 Superimposed on this substrate, periprocedural hypotension, sympathetic activation, and greater vasopressor exposure may serve as triggers.25,28 Although derived from distributive shock rather than TAVR, randomized evidence has associated catecholamine-sparing vasopressor strategies with a lower incidence of atrial fibrillation.14 In the parent trial, cipepofol reduced the incidence of postinduction hypotension by 18 percentage points. Median norepinephrine doses were lower with cipepofol than with propofol both within 15 minutes after induction (6 [0–10] vs 10 [5–20] μg) and during the entire procedure (356 [156–553] vs 443 [264–724] μg).17 However, the duration of norepinephrine exposure and the participant-level relationships among hypotension, vasopressor exposure, and subsequent new-onset atrial fibrillation were not evaluated; no mediation analysis was performed, and the proposed pathway remains unproven. Although valve type and intraprocedural fluid volume were recorded and balanced between groups, rapid pacing was not systematically captured, and this or other unrecorded periprocedural variables may have influenced atrial fibrillation risk. Accordingly, this mechanism is offered as a hypothesis to be tested rather than as an explanation established by the present data. Baseline characteristics were generally well balanced, although chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and prior stroke were somewhat more frequent in the cipepofol group. Chance baseline imbalance cannot be excluded in this small sample.

The temporal pattern of this finding merits comment. Although the 30-day cause-specific estimate was not statistically significant, its direction was consistent with the 1-year estimate, and the Fine–Gray sensitivity analyses yielded concordant results. No new events occurred in the cipepofol group after 30 days, whereas five additional events accrued in the propofol group. The difference in statistical significance between the two time points is therefore consistent with increased statistical precision as events accrued and should not be interpreted as evidence that the association emerged only after 30 days. Whether the continued accrual of events in the propofol group reflects progressive atrial remodeling on a vulnerable substrate remains speculative and cannot be examined in these data.26,27

Event counts for mortality, stroke, acute kidney injury, and myocardial infarction were too low to support meaningful between-group inference; these outcomes are therefore presented descriptively as counts and percentages. Deaths were numerically more frequent with cipepofol (3 vs 1), although the very small number of events precludes any meaningful inference; mortality will require assessment in adequately powered studies. At 1 year, the proportion of patients meeting the “alive and well” definition was numerically higher in the cipepofol group than in the propofol group (87.5% vs 74.1%), although the difference was not statistically significant. Given the increasing recognition of health status and “alive and well” outcomes as meaningful patient-centered measures in contemporary TAVR research,29 this numerical difference should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and may inform the design of future adequately powered studies, rather than as confirmatory evidence of clinical benefit.

These findings have both clinical and translational implications. As summarized in the Introduction, randomized evidence supports conscious sedation and general anesthesia as comparable strategies for the 30-day composite endpoint, with a secondary long-term mortality signal favoring conscious sedation that requires confirmation; general anesthesia nevertheless remains in wide use. The present findings apply to patients managed under general anesthesia, whether by institutional practice or clinical necessity, and carry no implication for procedures performed under conscious sedation. These results support further evaluation in adequately powered multicenter trials with clinical outcomes and standardized rhythm and hemodynamic assessment.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the parent trial was designed and powered for the primary hemodynamic endpoint rather than for 30-day or 1-year clinical outcomes. Accordingly, the present follow-up analyses were exploratory, and the small number of clinical events—particularly deaths, stroke, myocardial infarction, and acute kidney injury—precluded meaningful between-group inference for these outcomes. Second, this was a single-center study, and the anesthesia protocol, hemodynamic targets, postprocedural care, and rhythm-surveillance practices were institution-specific, limiting external generalizability. Third, postdischarge ascertainment of atrial fibrillation relied on scheduled electrocardiography and routine clinical encounters rather than continuous rhythm monitoring; asymptomatic or paroxysmal episodes may therefore have been missed, and differential ascertainment cannot be excluded if follow-up attendance, symptom burden, or clinical evaluation differed between groups. Fourth, although 1 year is a clinically relevant intermediate-term follow-up horizon after TAVR, it is insufficient to assess treatment effects on low-incidence outcomes such as mortality and stroke; longer follow-up is required.

Conclusions

In this exploratory, post hoc extended follow-up, cipepofol did not significantly reduce the 1-year primary composite endpoint. The lower incidence of 1-year new-onset atrial fibrillation was a secondary, nominally significant finding and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating given the small sample size, multiple comparisons, and intermittent rhythm surveillance. Future confirmatory trials should prespecify either new-onset atrial fibrillation with standardized continuous rhythm monitoring or a harder clinical endpoint such as 1-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) or stroke. Although the increasing adoption of conscious sedation may narrow the population eligible for such trials, general anesthesia remains widely used in many centers and clinically necessary for selected patients; future studies should therefore focus on this clinically relevant subgroup, with interpretation confined to patients undergoing transfemoral TAVR under general anesthesia.
