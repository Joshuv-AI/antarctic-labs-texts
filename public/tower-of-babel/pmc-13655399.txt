Atopic Dermatitis and Sleep Disorders
Authors: Genlong Bai, Xinyi Shao, Yi Ou, Jin Chen, Zhaofu Tan, Aijun Chen, Jingbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a common inflammatory skin disease affecting 129 million people worldwide and is characterized by recurrent eczema, severe pruritus, and skin barrier damage. Previous studies on AD-sleep disorder associations were limited by small sample sizes or single-center designs, lacking large-scale epidemiological evidence. This study aimed to investigate the association between AD and sleep disorders using the UK Biobank prospective cohort and to explore effects of sex, race, and obesity on this relationship.MethodsA prospective cohort design was employed, including 329,796 participants for cross-sectional analysis and 330,875 for longitudinal analysis. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were used. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to validate robustness.ResultsCross-sectional analysis showed that AD was significantly associated with increased sleep disorder risk (adjusted odds ratio, 2.84; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.54–3.18; P < 0.001), while the longitudinal analysis confirmed AD as an independent predictor of sleep disorders (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.32–1.73; P < 0.001), with Kaplan-Meier curves further demonstrating a higher cumulative risk of sleep disorders in participants with AD. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations in females and participants with moderate body mass index (BMI), and sensitivity analyses validated the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsAD is an independent risk factor for sleep disorders, particularly in women and individuals with moderate BMI. Future research should further explore precision-targeted therapies and management strategies for specific populations.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, UK Biobank, sleep disorders, cohort study, eczema, pruritus, skin, obesity

INTRODUCTION

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common inflammatory skin disease that affects both infants and adults, having a prevalence of approximately 13% in children and 5% in adults.1 Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that AD affects 129 million populations worldwide and represents a significant public health challenge.2 The pathogenesis of AD is complex, involving skin barrier deficits, immune-mediated mechanisms, colonization by microbes, including bacteria and fungi, on the skin, neurological aspects, and the patterns of reactivity to environmental antigens.3,4 The characteristics of AD are recurrent eczema lesions, severe itching, and skin barrier damage, which often lead to secondary infections. In addition to dermatological manifestations, AD is also associated with various comorbidities, including cardiovascular diseases, psychiatric disorders, autoimmune diseases, and sleep disorders. Owing to intense pruritus, which frequently worsens at night, it disrupts sleep by causing frequent awakenings, longer time to fall asleep, and poorer sleep quality.5

Good-quality sleep is essential for health and well-being, serving as a cornerstone for restoring physiological and mental function throughout life. However, sleep disorders have become a widespread public health concern, affecting approximately 45% of the global population.6 Beyond their direct impact on quality of life, sleep disorders are associated with numerous physical and psychological complications, such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, mental illness, and psoriasis.7,8,9,10 Previous studies have shown that itching often leads to sleep disorders, anxiety, and depression in AD patients, and impairs quality of life. Esposito et al. 11 reported that compared to the healthy control group, AD patients reported lower sleep quality, more severe insomnia and depression. However, most previous studies on the association between AD and sleep disorders have used small sample sizes or single center designs. There is currently no research using nationally representative large-scale datasets to explore the correlation between AD and sleep disorders.

In this prospective cohort study, we analyzed the association between sleep disorders and the incidence of AD in the UK Biobank. We also examined whether sex, race, ethnicity, obesity, and other factors might influence the potential link between AD and sleep disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

This study was conducted using the UK Biobank Project, a large-scale prospective cohort that included over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years from England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. At baseline, participants completed touchscreen questionnaires, oral interviews, and physical measurements to collect sociodemographic characteristics, physical measurements, medical histories, and biological samples. Follow-up health outcome data were obtained by linking various medical records. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their recruitment. Ethical approval for the UK Biobank project was granted by the North West Multi-center Research Ethics Committee (reference No. 21/NW/0157). The analysis was performed using UK Biobank Resource data (application No. 747364) in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies.

In this study, we conducted both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. The flow chart of this study is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, which provides detailed information on the selection of participants. Final analytical samples included 329,796 participants for cross-sectional evaluation and 330,875 for longitudinal evaluation.

Exposure

According to previous studies, AD is diagnosed by hospital records, primary care data, self-reports, and death registrations, with diagnoses coded using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system (ICD-10 code: L20).12 Participants who self-reported AD after baseline or were diagnosed AD after baseline were excluded from the analysis.

Outcome

The primary outcome in our study was the presence of sleep disorders. Sleep disorder cases were identified by using validated algorithms based on the ICD-10 codes F51 and G47 (non-organic and organic sleep disorders) (Supplementary Table S1). Cross-sectional analysis excluded participants diagnosed with sleep disorders after enrollment, while longitudinal analysis excluded those with pre-existing or baseline sleep disorder diagnoses.

Covariates

According to previous research, several baseline factors associated with sleep disorders and AD are considered potential covariates, including age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), education level, average total household income before tax, race and ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), alcohol drinking status, smoking status, physical activity, history of diabetes or hypertension. The TDI was used as a proxy for material socioeconomic deprivation and calculated based on data of unemployment, car and homeownership, and household overcrowding.13 Physical activity was assessed using the validated Short International Physical Activity Questionnaire, which allowed us to acquire total metabolic equivalent minutes per week.14

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of participants were described as means ± standard deviation for continuous variables or frequency and percentage (N [%]) for categorical variables. The follow-up time was calculated from the baseline date to the date of the first AD occurrence, death, or update date of linkages, whichever occurred first. Statistical differences between groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous data and the χ2 test for categorical data.

For the cross-sectional analysis, multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence interval (CIs) for the relationship between AD and sleep disorders. For the longitudinal analysis, a multivariable Cox proportional-hazards model was used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CI for the risk of sleep disorders. For both analyses, we considered three models: Model 1 was a crude model. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex and ethnicity. In addition to the variables in Model 2, Model 3 was further adjusted for education level, BMI, household income, TDI, smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity, and history of hypertension or diabetes based on Model 2. Additionally, the Kaplan-Meier method was used to compare the cumulative risk of AD events between sleep disorders, and the logarithmic rank test was used for statistical evaluation. We defined follow-up duration as diagnosis of sleep disorders, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up on September 1, 2024, whichever occurred first.

Subgroup analysis was conducted to check the robustness of the research results. In both sets of analyses concerns, we conducted the stratification analyses by sex, BMI, race, smoke status, alcohol drinking status, physical activity, and history of diabetes or hypertension. Furthermore, to test the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses of longitudinal analysis (Model 3). First, we repeated the main analyses after excluding the AD cases with < 2 years of follow-up to minimize the effect of subclinical conditions on the observed associations. We excluded patients with bipolar disorder and depression to rule out the impact of mental disorders on sleep. Next, we also used a narrow definition of sleep disorders by ICD-10 G47 to investigate the robustness of our findings. Then, according to the previous study, we excluded participants without primary health records because UK Biobank participants are more likely to be older, female, and from less socioeconomically deprived areas, as well as having fewer self-reported health conditions than nonparticipants.15 To address missing covariate data, we implemented random forest-based multiple imputation (m = 5, using “mice” R package), subsequently repeating the association analysis with the first imputed dataset. In addition, we conducted inverse probability treatment weight (IPTW) analysis to reduce study bias.

We conducted the data analysis of this study from July 6, 2025, to September 1 of 2025. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

RESULTS

Basic characteristics of the participants

This study included a total of 329,796 participants in the cross-sectional cohort and 330,875 in the longitudinal cohort from the UK Biobank (Table 1). In the longitudinal cohort, compared to those without AD, participants with AD were significantly younger (mean age: 55.7 ± 8.2 years vs. 55.9 ± 8.1 years, although this difference was statistically not significant in the cross-sectional cohort. Females were more susceptible to AD than males in both cohorts (54.7% in the cross-sectional cohort; 54.6% in the longitudinal cohort, P < 0.001). Results also showed that participants with AD had lower TDI and higher household incomes compared to those without AD. Counterintuitively, a higher proportion of participants with AD had never smoked but were drinkers, and they had a lower prevalence of hypertension and diabetes. No significant differences were observed in race, educational level, physical activity, or the prevalence of hypertension or diabetes between the two groups. Compared to the participants we excluded, the participants we included in both cohorts were younger, more likely to be male and white, as well as having lower TDI scores and higher education levels (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

Association between AD and sleep disorders

Subsequently, we explored the association between AD and sleep disorders using logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression (Table 2). In the cross-sectional analysis, the results of all three models indicate that AD can significantly increase the risk of sleep disorders, and this association is most pronounced in Model 3, which fully adjustment for covariates (OR, 2.84; 95% CI, 2.54–3.18; P < 0.001). Consistent with cross-sectional analysis results, in the longitudinal analysis, Model 3 showed a significant association between AD and sleep disorders (HR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.32–1.73; P < 0.001). Supplementary Fig. S2 shows the cumulative risk of incident sleep disorders in AD participants during follow-up by using Kaplan-Meier curves. The results demonstrated that participants with AD exhibited a higher cumulative risk of developing sleep disorders during the follow-up period compared to those without AD.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the association between AD and sleep disorders and to identify population variances as presented in Figure. The subgroup analyses revealed a consistent association across most groups, whether in the cross-sectional cohort (Figure A) or the longitudinal cohort (Figure B). Significant interactions were noted in sex and BMI (P for interaction < 0.05). Female participants with AD had a significantly higher risk of developing sleep disorders compared to male participants with AD. Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our findings regarding the association between AD and sleep disorder, we performed the following sensitivity analyses (Table 3). To reduce bias from undiagnosed pre-existing conditions, we excluded participants who developed sleep disorders within 2 years of follow-up, participants with AD showed a significantly higher risk of sleep disorders compared to those without AD (adjusted HR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.14–1.55; P < 0.001). Even after excluding participants with bipolar disorders or major depression, or both, the association remained significant (adjusted HR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.31–1.75; P < 0.001). Moreover, using the diagnosis definition of ICD-10 G47 also confirmed the association between AD and sleep disorders (adjusted HR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.31–1.72; P < 0.001). Next, after excluding the participants without primary health records, participants with AD still showed a significantly higher risk of incident sleep disorders (adjusted HR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.03–1.36; P = 0.01). In the multiple imputation analysis for missing data in covariates, participants with AD still had a significantly higher risk of sleep disorders (adjusted HR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.35–1.68; P < 0.001). Finally, in the IPTW analyses, the association between AD and sleep disorders was also confirmed (adjusted HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.30–1.70; P < 0.001). These sensitivity analyses consistently demonstrated a significant association between AD and an increased risk of sleep disorders, even after accounting for potential biases and missing data.

DISCUSSION

This study, based on prospective cohort data from the UK Biobank, for the first large-scale confirmatory analysis demonstrates a significant association between AD and sleep disorders in a population of 300,000. The cross-sectional analysis shows that the risk of sleep disorders significantly increases in AD patients, and the longitudinal cohort analysis further verifies that AD is an independent risk factor for the occurrence of sleep disorders. The results of subgroup analysis indicate that the risk of sleep disorders is significantly higher in female AD patients than in male AD patients. This finding is consistent with findings from the Swedish BAMSE cohort study showing a higher proportion of sleep disturbance in female AD patients (20.5% vs. 14.8%).1 Furthermore, individuals with a moderate BMI exhibit the strongest association strength, suggesting that metabolic factors may amplify AD-related sleep issues through inflammatory pathways.

The results of this study are consistent with the conclusions of a small-scale case-control study conducted by Esposito et al.,11 which indicated that the risk of sleep disorders was significantly increased in AD patients. However, this study used a nationally representative sample (n > 300,000) to overcome the limitations of potential bias caused by small sample sizes and employed a prospective design to exclude reverse causation interference, clarifying that AD is a risk factor for sleep disorders rather than an outcome. In a study on the correlation between childhood AD and sleep quality, it was found that children with active AD had an adjusted OR of 1.48 for sleep disorders, while this study found an HR of 1.51,17 which may suggest that the risk of sleep disorders is significantly increased in both pediatric and adult AD patients.

The precise mechanism by which AD leads to sleep disorders remains unclear. Based on existing literature, several potential pathways have been hypothesized. The nighttime itching in AD may be the core mechanism triggering sleep disorders. Studies have reported that interleukin (IL)-31 is elevated in both AD patients and mouse models of AD.18 During the body’s nighttime core temperature decline, it may activate the temperature-sensitive protein transient receptor potential cation channel vanilloid (TRPV), leading to a reduced itching threshold.19,20 TRPV-1 is a key protein that constitutes interleukin-31 receptor A (IL-31RA). This hypothesis is indirectly supported by Phase III clinical data on Nemolizumab, an IL-31RA antagonist, which showed that 41% of patients experienced improved sleep after four weeks of treatment.21 Another hypothesized mechanism involves systemic inflammation influencing sleep-wake regulation. Studies have shown that pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α) can cross the blood-brain barrier, interfering with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and circadian rhythm regulation, leading to abnormalities in sleep architecture (such as reduced deep sleep and increased fragmentation of rapid eye movement sleep).22,23 Additionally, animal models suggest that IL-31 might directly act on the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, affecting the expression of biological clock genes (such as Clock and Bmal1), and subsequently influencing the body’s circadian rhythm.22

Our study has the following advantages and disadvantages. This study follows the STROBE statement guidelines and conducts both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, with results demonstrating internal consistency, and leverages the advantages of a large sample from the UK Biobank, which minimizes result bias caused by small samples as much as possible. The ICD-10 codes from the UK Biobank have been double-validated, and the accuracy of AD diagnosis is relatively high. However, this study’s reliance on ICD coding may miss mild AD patients who have not sought medical attention, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true association strength. The reliance on ICD codes likely skews samples toward more severe cases seeking medical care, which may lead to an overestimation of risk compared to the general AD population. Another notable limitation of this study is the heterogeneity of the primary outcome. We utilized ICD-10 codes F51/G47 to define sleep disorders, which encompass a broad spectrum of conditions with potentially distinct pathophysiological origins, such as insomnia, sleep apnea, and circadian rhythm disturbances. Future research should employ more granular assessments, including disease-specific sleep scales, and objective measures like polysomnography, to refine these associations. Additionally, the study population consists of European adults aged 37–73, which may limit the applicability of the results. In future research, we may further verify the causal role of the IL-31/IL-6 pathway in the association between AD and sleep through Mendelian randomization and conduct randomized controlled trials on the impact of biological agents on the sleep structure of AD patients in specific populations to further expand our research conclusions.

This prospective cohort study, through cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, confirms that AD is an independent risk factor for sleep disorders, and that this association is particularly significant in women and individuals with a moderate BMI. Future research should further explore precision-targeted therapies and management strategies for specific populations.
