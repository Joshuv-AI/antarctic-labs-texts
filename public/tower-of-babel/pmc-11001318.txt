The value of transthoracic echocardiography and chest X-ray in locating the tip of central venous catheter in dialysis patients: a comparative study with computed tomography imaging
Authors: Meng Zhang, Hui-Ling Liu, Wei-Hong Li, Mu-Zi Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo determine the tip position of the central venous catheter (CVC) in patients with dialysis, the guidelines recommend that it be determined using chest radiography (CXR) after catheterization, without fluoroscopy. However, some researchers have proposed that transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) can replace CXR, but this has not been widely adopted. This study aimed to determine which of the two aforementioned methods is more suitable for locating the tip position of the CVC. This prospective study included 160 patients who underwent hemodialysis at our hospital from March 2021 to December 2022. After inserting the CVC through the internal jugular vein, we used transthoracic echocardiography and CXR to determine the tip of the CVC and compared the results with those of computed tomography (CT). In the comparison between TTE and CXR for locating the CVC tip, we obtained three main findings. (1) TTE was associated with fewer misdiagnosed cases than CXR. (2) TTE provided higher sensitivity (similar sensitivity in position 2), specificity, positive/negative predictive values, and accuracy than CXR. (3) When comparing the receiver operating characteristic curves of TTE and CXR, the area under the curve (95% confidence interval) for the former was larger. Additionally, we made anatomical discoveries: the "hyperechoic triangle" recognized by TTE was equivalent to the entrance of the superior vena cava into the right atrium shown by transesophageal transthoracic echocardiography. TTE is more suitable than CXR as the first examination for CVC tip localization, as it improves diagnostic accuracy and reduces X-ray radiation damage.

Introduction

Central venous catheter (CVC) placement is widely used in intensive care units, operating rooms, and nephrology departments. A CVC allow hypertonic solution infusion and better control of the flow rate during clinical administration [1]. It is also used in hemodialysis treatment. Patients who have lost kidney function for various reasons can receive kidney replacement therapy to clear metabolites in the body, of which 69% are undergoing hemodialysis. In the 60 years since the creation of this method, hemodialysis technology has greatly improved [2]. For the insertion techniques for catheterization, we perform puncture according to anatomical markers [3], fluoroscopic puncture [4], ultrasound-guided puncture [5], and electrocardiogram-guided CVC tip positioning puncture [6]. These techniques can help evaluate the insertion depth of the catheter and monitor the CVC tip position during the catheter insertion process. The ideal location of the CVC tip at home and abroad remains inconclusive. According to the expert consensus on vascular access for hemodialysis in China in 2019, this location should be in the middle and upper right atria [7]. If placed in the lower right atrium, it can lead to arrhythmia, cardiac wall damage, and other serious complications [8]. If it is placed into the venae cavae system, it may lead to venous endothelial damage, pleural effusion, and thrombosis [9,10]. Therefore, it is important to correctly determine and promptly adjust the position of the CVC tip.

Currently, guidelines stipulate that the position of the CVC tip should be determined by chest radiography (CXR) after catheterization [11]. However, CXR determines the tip location on the basis of the anatomical markers of veins and bone, which means that it is inaccurate and limited (e.g. trauma patients who have lost bone markers) and inevitably causes radiation damage [12]. Transesophageal transthoracic echocardiography (TEE) can directly display the superior vena cava into the right atrium (SVC-RA) [13], and it is considered the gold standard for determining the position of the CVC tip. However, because of its invasive, time-consuming, and specialized nature, patient acceptance is not high [12]. Through comparative studies, Eng et al. demonstrated that three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) can provide more accurate heart measurements than TEE [14]. Therefore, CT was used as a reference. TTE has been proposed as an alternative to CXR but has not been widely adopted [15].

Herein, we compared the localization values of the CVC tip on TTE and CXR at five locations. This study aimed to determine Which is more suitable as a preliminary check to locate the CVC tip, TTE or CXR.

Materials and Methods

Study population

This prospective study was conducted on 160 patients randomly selected by drawing lots who underwent hemodialysis in our hospital between March 2021 and December 2022 and required CVC implantation. First, a population was selected from patients on hemodialysis in the Department of Nephrology according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, and then a sample was randomly selected from the population by drawing lots. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years, (2) clear awareness and voluntary cooperation, (3) indications for hemodialysis, (4) CT was deemed as a necessary clinical procedure. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of trauma or radiation therapy at the surface of the catheter; (2) poor coagulation function after clinical evaluation, preventing catheterization; (3) inability to cooperate with follow-up after replacement; (4) severe cardiac insufficiency; (5) mental disorders; and (6) severe infection.

Methods

Different nephrologists performed CVC placement under ultrasonographic guidance. Tunneled, 14.5-French dual-lumen, semipermanent CVCs were inserted percutaneously into the internal jugular vein in an anterograde manner. Postoperatively, a cardiac ultrasound physician (who was not directly involved in the placement of the CVC) performed tip localization of the CVC, and all patients underwent CXR and CT examinations after surgery.

Instruments and their operating methods

Echocardiographic techniques: Volkswagen E8 and LOGIQE8 ultrasound diagnostic instruments with a 3–5-MHz sector probe were used for examination. The patient was placed in the left-lying position, and we observed whether the CVC tip was located in the venae cava system or the right atrium in the intercostal inferior vena cava (IVC) section (Figure 1). If the CVC tip was located in the right atrium, the upper and lower diameters of the right atrium and the length of the CVC entering the right atrium were used to determine whether the CVC tip was located in the upper, middle, or lower parts of the right atrium. The catheter was identified as two "equal sign" high echo lines, with a pitch-black low echo lumen inside. The measurement of the upper and lower diameters of the right atrium was completed by identifying the "hyperechoic triangle" at the place where the superior vena cava enters the right atrium, which is equivalent to the SVC-RA observed visually on TEE. Through gross anatomy, we found that the "hyperechoic triangle" was the fat pad at the visceral and parietal folds of the pericardium in the serosal layer of the posterior wall of the superior IVC, entering the right atrium (Figure 2).

CXR: An FH-21HR medical X-ray generator was used. Scanning parameters were as follows: voltage, 220 V; current, 200 mA; rated frequency, 50 Hz; perspective tube voltage, 90 kv; tube current, 0.5–5 mA, continuously adjustable; and irradiation time, 0.04–6.3 s. The patient was placed in an upright position, and the position of the CVC tip was observed on the front and side films according to the veins and anatomical markers.

CT: A 64-row spiral CT scanner (Siemens, Munich, Germany) was used. Scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, 28–580 ms; layer thickness, 5 mm; and pitch, 1.2. The scanning range was from the entrance of the chest to the diaphragm. The patient was placed in a flat position, the method of breath holding was explained, and scanning began at the end of lung inhalation. We observed whether the CVC tip was located in the upper and lower chambers or in the right atrium on the iterative reconstruction interface. If the CVC tip was located in the right atrium, the upper and lower diameters of the right atrium and the length of the CVC entering the right atrium were used to determine whether the CVC tip was located in the upper, middle, or lower parts of the right atrium.

Image standard: The final result was determined by the chief physician of the cardiac ultrasound department, radiology department, and nephrology department.

Statistical analysis

Sensitivity was set to 90%, with a δ of 0.10, an α of 0.05, and power of 80%. From these data, it can be calculated that the minimum sample size required was 95 cases. Using the CT results as the reference, the experimental results were arranged into five sets of binary data on the basis of whether they were correctly placed at positions 1–5: the SVC-RA and the last 3 cm in front of it, upper right atrium, middle right atrium, lower right atrium, and IVC. True-positive results were defined as correct placements, whereas true-negative results were defined as incorrect placements. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, accuracy, and con­sistency of TTE and CXR with CT were determined. For consistency, the chi-square test and kappa value were used; the kappa value was divided into strong consistency (kappa ≥ 0.7), general consistency (0.4 ≤ kappa < 0.7), or weak consistency (Kappa < 0.4). Similarly, using CT as the reference, we defined five groups of receiver operating characteristic curves, calculated the area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), and evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of TTE and CXR. All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Patient and public involvement

The patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study initially included 200 patients; however, 40 patients dropped out of the study halfway. Ultimately, we collected data on 160 patients (sex, 60 males and 100 females; age range: 27.0–86.0 years; average age, 56.5 ± 29.5 years; height range: 146.0–187.0 cm; average height, 166.5 ± 20.5 cm; weight range, 44.2–82.6 kg; average weight, 63.4 ± 19.2 kg; average duration of dialysis, approximately 3 years; cumulative range of CVC catheterizations, 1–5 times. There were concerns regarding dialysis efficacy in 9 patients, and 16 patients had hypotensive complications.

Number of confirmed and misdiagnosed cases

The CT examination results showed that the tip of the CVC was located 3 cm before the SVC-RA, upper right atrium, middle right atrium, lower right atrium, and IVC in 10, 43, 55, 46, and 6 cases, respectively. The TTE examination results showed that 138 cases were correctly diagnosed and 22 cases were incorrectly diagnosed. The CXR examination results showed that 84 cases were correctly diagnosed and 76 cases were incorrectly diagnosed (Table 1).

Diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, accuracy, and kappa values

The sensitivity of TTE in locating the tip of CVC was similar to that of CXR in the upper right atrium, but higher than that of CXR in the other four positions. The specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy of TTE in locating the tip of CVC were higher than those of CXR in the last 3 cm before the SVC-RA, upper right atrium, middle right atrium, lower right atrium, and IVC. The consistency between TTE and CT was stronger in the last 3 cm before the SVC-RA, upper right atrium, middle right atrium, and lower right atrium (kappa ≥ 0.7) but slightly weaker in the IVC (kappa = 0.019). The consistency between CXR and CT was average (0.4 ≤ kappa < 0.7) or weak (kappa < 0.4). However, in the aforementioned five positions, the consistency between TTE and CT was better than that between TTE and CXR (Table 2).

Receiver operating characteristic curves of TTE and CXR

By comparing the receiver operating characteristic curves of TTE and CXR, we found that the AUC of TTE was larger than that of CXR at the SVC-RA and the last 3 cm in front of it, upper right atrium, middle right atrium, and lower right atrium. The AUC of TTE was smaller than that of CXR at the IVC position. Overall, TTE had higher diagnostic value than CXR (Figure 3).

Discussion

Appropriate positioning of the CVC tip is important. If the CVC tip is placed in the lower right atrium, serious complications may occur. When the CVC tip is close to the atrial wall, endocardial and even myocardial damage are very likely to occur when the atrium contracts; when the CVC tip is located in the corresponding atrial wall of the sinoatrial node, internodal bundle, and atrioventricular node, arrhythmia is likely to occur [8]. If the CVC tip is placed in the venae cavae system, venous endothelial damage and even secondary thrombosis may occur, and if the CVC tip stimulates or punctures the vein wall for a long time, it may cause inflammatory or bloody pleural effusion [9,10]. However, positioning the catheter tip may increase the proportion of catheter reset and catheter replacement; therefore, physicians should try to avoid this by aiming for the best dialysis efficacy rather than the ideal location.

According to the expert consensus in China in 2019, the location of vascular access for hemodialysis should be in the middle and upper right atrium [7]. Most previous studies have classified the position of the CVC tip as ideal or abnormal. However, the tip position should not be divided into as such. For patients with dialysis, the catheter should not be placed in an ideal position, but should be placed where the patient has the best dialysis effect. For example, in clinical practice, for some patients whose CVC tip is not in the ideal position but whose dialysis effect is good, the CVC tip position should not be changed. Therefore, combined with the distribution of CVC tip positions in clinical practice, we divided the CVC tip positions into five locations. The results showed that the diagnostic accuracy of TTE was higher than that of CXR at positions 1–5, which proves that TTE is more suitable than CXR for accurately locating the tip of the CVC in complex clinical practice.

TEE can be used to directly observe the SVC-RA and is considered the gold standard for locating CVC tips. However, it is invasive and specialized and cannot be routinely used to locate CVC tips [12,13]. Some scholars have confirmed that CT can provide a more accurate measurement of the heart than TEE [14], so we chose to use CT as the reference. For CVC tip positioning, TTE does not require a specialist in ultrasonography. Only a few ultrasonograms are required for one to learn this modality; therefore, it is easy to master and operate. Additionally, it is inexpensive, and bedside ultrasound machines are easy to use. Hence, TTE has a good general availability.

The number of patients with correctly placed catheters in this study was lower than that previously reported [16,17]; however, this was a comparative study of the diagnostic value of TTE and CXR compared to CT. We did not closely evaluate the accuracy of catheter placement; therefore, the low number of patients seems unlikely to affect the results. The misdiagnosis rate of CXR was 47.50%, whereas that of TTE was 13.75%, which was much lower than the misdiagnosis rate of CXR. However, we believe that the misdiagnosis rate of TTE is overestimated. With the rapid development of new ultrasound technologies such as three-dimensional imaging [18], the misdiagnosis rate of TTE will be further reduced, and we will continue to study this topic.

On the TTE image, the CVC tip is clearly displayed, and there are good methods to confirm this in a few unclear cases. For example, some researchers have used standard bubbles for rapid flushing and stirring of physiological saline to confirm CVC tips [19]. However, to observe the relative positions of the CVC tip and the SVC-RA, it is necessary to accurately identify the position of the SVC-RA while confirming the CVC tip. Therefore, TEE has become the gold standard for determining the position of the CVC tip. When measuring the distance between the CVC tip and the SVC-RA, we found that the SVC-RA is easier to identify in healthy hearts but more difficult to identify in abnormal hearts (such as those with right atrium enlargement and venae cava dilatation), which may be caused by the smaller angle of the SVC-RA. However, we found that this problem can be solved by identifying the "hyperechoic triangle" in the section of the superior intercostal IVC on TTE. Anatomically speaking, the "hyperechoic triangle" is the fat pad at the visceral and mural folds of the pericardium in the serosal layer behind the SVC-RA. Therefore, identifying it in the section of the superior intercostal IVC on TTE is equivalent to visualizing the SVC-RA on TEE. Moreover, TTE has many advantages, such as being noninvasive and non-radiative, allowing for observation of the CVC tip and its relationship with surrounding tissues from multiple angles. Spectral Doppler ultrasonography can be used to observe hemodynamic changes caused by catheterization at different stages of heart beating; inspection results can be immediately obtained, and multiple inspections can be repeated before and after adjusting the tube placement, avoiding multiple radiations. Additionally, the device is portable and can be used to perform bedside examinations in critically ill patients.

At present, the recommended method for evaluating the position of the catheter tip after CVC catheterization is CXR [11]. Many studies have shown that CXR has a high misdiagnosis rate because it judges the position of the CVC tip according to the route of the veins and anatomical landmarks of the bone, which is inaccurate, limited, and causes radiation damage [12,19–21]. Because of the repeated exposure of patients to radiation during radiography examination and the low incidence of catheter heterotopy after transthoracic ultrasound-guided catheterization, some scholars have proposed that CXR positioning of the CVC tip can be omitted after transthoracic ultrasound-guided catheterization, but this viewpoint is still highly controversial [22–24]. Some scholars have proposed that although the tip navigation system based on real-time ultrasound-guided intubation can identify the CVC tip, it cannot replace postoperative tip positioning [15, 25–27].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to rigorously compare TTE and CXR with CT. Our research results confirmed that TTE and CXR are more reliable than CT for identifying the tip of the catheter. TTE has good accuracy and high consistency with CT. Postoperative CXR is also feasible; however, its accuracy is poor. This study proves that the examination results of TTE can only be used as an indicator of whether patients need a radiography examination after catheterization because TTE is limited by technology and the observation range is limited. When the CVC tip is outside the observation range, radiography examination is required to view the superior vena cava system; Rossi advocated radiography examination in cases of procedural uncertainty or clinical doubt. This strategy greatly reduces the number of radiography examinations while obtaining clearer tip positions and reducing patient radiation and cost [28,29].

Limitations

The present study has limitations. First, we included a small number of patients with the tip of the CVC in the IVC, so our conclusion is highly contingent. Even if the kappa value at this location is 0.019 and the AUC is smaller than that of CXR, we cannot determine whether the CVC localization value of CXR is higher than that of TTE. Future research should increase the number of cases with the tip of the CVC in the IVC to validate our experimental conclusions. However, by combining the results of the five positions, we can still conclude that TTE has a higher value than CXR in locating the CVC tip. Second, the patients we studied were on hemodialysis, and the IVC section on TTE was easy to obtain. However, our results cannot be applied to all patients, such as those with chest wall trauma and those who are emotionally manic and unable to cooperate. In addition, tip position movement due to changes in patient position is not considered in this study.

Conclusions

This study’s findings indicate that TTE is an accurate technique for localizing the CVC tip position after internal jugular vein catheterization, and it has strong consistency with CT. CXR should be selected in cases where TTE is not feasible owing to technical limitations.

TTE and CXR complement each other and will increase the accuracy and safety of diagnosing CVC tip locations.
