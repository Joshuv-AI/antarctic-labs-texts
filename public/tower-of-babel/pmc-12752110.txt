Exploring the application of Heart to Heart Cards in hospice care education for nursing students: a qualitative study
Authors: Jinlong Liu, Binbin Hou, Yunjia Xu, Meirong Hong, Yun Xia, Wei Zhang, Yan Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospice care education is crucial for preparing nursing students to provide holistic end-of-life care. However, traditional pedagogical models often over-rely on one-way knowledge transmission, creating a theory-practice gap and neglecting emotional education. The Heart to Heart Card (HHC), an interactive card-based tool, presents a potential innovative solution. This study qualitatively explored undergraduate nursing students’ experiential perceptions of integrating the HHC into a hospice care course.MethodsA descriptive phenomenological design was employed. Seventeen undergraduate nursing students enrolled in a hospice care elective course were purposively sampled and participated in one-on-one, in-depth, semi-structured interviews between November 2024 and March 2025. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method.ResultsThree primary thematic categories emerged from the data analysis: (1) direct impressions and refinement recommendations for HHC within the course, (2) immersive learning and insights from the “Heart to Heart Tea House”, (3) practical challenges in transitioning HHC application from simulations to clinical settings.ConclusionThe HHC serves as an effective pedagogical tool for enhancing hospice care education, as it successfully bridges theory and practice, improving communication skills, and fostering humanistic competencies through its theoretical-practical integration. Further refinements in HHC design and implementation strategies are needed to optimize its applicability across diverse clinical scenarios and cultural contexts.Keywords: Heart to heart card, Hospice care, Qualitative study, Undergraduate nursing students

Background

Hospice care has become an indispensable component of modern healthcare, driven by the paradigm shift towards a biopsychosocial model of medicine and evolving societal perspectives on life and death [1, 2]. This form of care, which provides holistic support encompassing physiological, psychological, social, and spiritual needs for end-of-life patients, aims to alleviate suffering, preserve dignity, and facilitate a peaceful transition [3, 4]. The foundation of high-quality hospice care lies in robust education. Systematic training not only determines the competence of healthcare professionals but also directly impacts patient quality of life and family adjustment [5]. Recognizing this, China has elevated hospice care to a strategic national priority, as seen in the “Healthy China 2030” Planning Outline and the “14th Five-Year Plan for Healthy Aging,” both emphasizing the urgent need to strengthen professional workforce development [6, 7].

Nursing students, as future pillars of the healthcare team, play a crucial role in this context. Evidence confirms that those with systematic hospice care education demonstrate superior clinical proficiency, communication skills, and emotional resilience [4, 8]. While countries like the U.S., UK, and Australia have well-established, integrated hospice care curricula employing diverse and hierarchical teaching methods [9–12], China’s system is emerging and continues to develop, with ongoing efforts to enhance curriculum depth, content richness, and pedagogical diversity [13]. Therefore, advancing hospice care education for nursing students is a critical lever for improving end-of-life care in China. However, achieving this advancement requires overcoming significant challenges inherent in current pedagogical models, which often over-rely on one-way knowledge transmission, failing to actively engage students or bridge the theory-practice gap. This leads to difficulties in applying knowledge, such as avoiding death discussions in clinical practice despite knowing the communication principles [9, 14, 15]. Furthermore, existing approaches frequently neglect the holistic needs of patients and families [16, 17] and lack essential emotional education, leaving students psychologically vulnerable when confronting patient mortality and family grief [18, 19]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for innovative teaching models that integrate theory with practice, emphasize emotional learning, and align with complex clinical realities.

To address the limitations of current hospice care teaching methods, the Heart to Heart Card (HHC), an innovative educational tool, has been introduced in recent years. The HHC developed by the Chinese American Coalition for Compassionate Care (CACCC) in 2014, is an interactive pedagogical tool designed to facilitate discussions on life-and-death topics through a card-based game format in a relaxed atmosphere [20]. The HHC set comprises 54 cards, categorized into four dimensions: physical needs (♠, 13 cards), psychological needs (♦, 13 cards), social needs (♣, 13 cards), and spiritual needs (♥, 13 cards). Additionally, two special wish cards (wild cards) are included to express unlisted needs when the standard 52 cards are insufficient. Each card features a specific need statement at the center of its face [e.g., “I wish to remain clean and comfortable” (physical), “I want to participate in a favorite activity” (social), “I need professional psychological support” (psychological), “I’d like to reflect on my life” (spiritual)]. Based on this framework, two primary implementation modalities are frequently employed: the “Tea House” modality, featuring facilitator-guided group discussions (6–8 participants) on end-of-life preferences, and the “sharing” modality, involving a format where one sharer discusses their card choices with 1–2 listeners [21]. These structured approaches have demonstrated preliminary efficacy in transcending cultural barriers surrounding death discourse and fostering appropriate perspectives on life and death among students [22]. Despite its potential, the application of HHC in hospice care education remains nascent. For this study, the HHC was integrated into an elective Hospice Care course, with the aim of conducting a qualitative exploration into undergraduate nursing students’ experiential perceptions of its use, thereby providing foundational evidence for both its standardized implementation and the enhancement of pedagogical outcomes.

Methods

Design

This study integrated the HHC tool into the elective hospice care course through four sequential, progressive stages (see Fig. 1). First, during the theoretical teaching stage, the HHC’s design principles, functions, and its embedded holistic care philosophy were systematically introduced via the PowerPoint presentation. Subsequently, in the laboratory teaching stage, the “Tea House” modality was adopted to guide nursing students to engage in interactive discussions on perspectives regarding life and death within their small groups. Furthermore, in the simulation training stage, the “sharing” modality of HHC was combined with case-based simulation, utilizing cards to assist students in guiding simulated patients to express their core needs and concerns. Finally, during the clinical inspection stage, students attempted to use the topics on HHC cards to communicate and interact with real patients. This study employed a descriptive phenomenological design to explore nursing students’ experiences of using the HHC within the hospice care course.

Steps for integrating the HHC into the hospice care course

Sample and setting

Using purposive sampling, students enrolled in the hospice care elective course were selected as interview participants in December 2024. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) completion of the entire course curriculum, and (2) voluntary participation with demonstrated engagement capacity. Sample size was determined based on the principle of information saturation [23]. The estimated sample size in the research design was 15–20 participants, and the final sample size of 17 was determined based on the principle of information saturation. This sample size estimation was informed by previous qualitative studies in nursing education, where thematic saturation is commonly achieved with approximately 15–20 participants when examining experiential or perceptual data from a relatively homogeneous group [14].

Data collection

Aligned with the research objectives, we conducted a comprehensive literature review focusing on: (1) nursing students’ learning experiences in hospice care course, and (2) training interventions utilizing HHC for nurses/nursing students. Based on preliminary findings, we drafted initial interview questions which were subsequently refined through consultations with three experts in nursing education and hospice care. To ensure the instrument’s validity, we conducted pilot interviews with two eligible students meeting the inclusion criteria. The interview guide was iteratively revised based on pilot feedback, culminating in the finalized version presented in Table 1.

Interview guide

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently over a five-month period (November 2024 to March 2025). This study employed one-on-one in-depth interviews for data collection. To ensure standardization and scientific rigor, all research team members completed systematic qualitative research training prior to conducting interviews. Data were collected by at least two researchers trained in systematic qualitative research (One male, one female, registered nurses pursuing master’s degrees with extensive hospice care experience). Researchers established rapport with participants to foster trust and enable authentic sharing of their HHC experiences. Interviews were scheduled at participants’ convenience in private classroom settings after obtaining informed consent, with explicit assurances of confidentiality and data protection. A semi-structured protocol guided discussions, allowing flexible sequencing while maintaining focus. Researchers employed active listening, neutral probing, and non-leading questions, recording both audio recorder and in-person. Data collection continued until no new information emerged.

Data analysis

Within 24 h post-interview, dual researchers independently transcribed recordings and verified ambiguities with participants. Using Colaizzi’s 7-step phenomenological method, they: (1) repeatedly reviewed texts, (2) extracted significant statements, (3) formulated meanings, (4) clustered themes, (5) developed exhaustive descriptions, (6) distilled fundamental structures, and (7) member-checked findings. This rigorous process ensured analytical trustworthiness through coder consensus and participant validation [24]. The researchers coded all interview transcripts and then met to discuss if the objective of data saturation was reached. No new themes or ideas were found, which signified data saturation.

Rigor

This study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies. Rigor was ensured by employing strategies adhering to the central tenets of qualitative research [25]. Specifically, credibility was ensured by repeated and prolonged engagement with the interview transcripts and by investigator triangulation during the development of the initial coding framework. Plausibility was ensured with multiple sources of data and by the involvement of multiple researchers in interpreting the data through comparisons of the initial coding with the documentary sources and time-stamped video recordings. Finally, transferability was ensured by providing readers with raw data to infer the plausibility of our interpretations and by comparing our study results against broader research adopting complex-based pedagogical simulation designs [26]. With respect to researchers’ reflexivity, all the researchers were registered nurses and educators with extensive experience in qualitative research. Reflexivity as a team was ensured by having all members question themselves as they conducted analysis, by asking “What presumptions do I have of the data?” and “How do my assumptions shape the data analysis?”

Results

Demographic data

The study conducted interviews with 17 nursing students (15 female, 2 male), with a mean age of 21 years. None of the participants reported religious affiliations, and only two had prior experience accompanying hospice patients. The interviews lasted between 18 and 27 min, with a mean duration of 22.5 min. The variation in length was primarily due to differences in participants’ verbosity and the depth of their personal reflections on the HHC experience. We found these interview durations to be sufficient for in-depth exploration, as they allowed for comprehensive narratives from all participants and data collection continued until informational saturation was achieved. To ensure confidentiality, all participants were anonymized using alphanumeric codes (e.g., N1-N17).

Qualitative themes

Analysis of interview text yielded three key themes: (1) Direct impressions and refinement recommendations for HHC within the course, (2) Immersive learning and insights from the “Heart to Heart Tea House”, (3) Practical challenges in transitioning HHC application from simulations to clinical settings.

Theme 1: Direct impressions and refinement recommendations for HHC within the course

Interest stimulation

Multiple interviewees reported initial novelty when first exposed to the HHC, describing it as “an innovative tool resembling playing cards” that they had never encountered before. This distinctive format and content stimulated participants’ interest in the hospice care curriculum, making learning more engaging and dynamic while enhancing motivation and proactive engagement. N1: My first impression of HHC was remarkable novelty - its playing card-like appearance presented something entirely new. This innovative format sparked strong interest in the hospice course and significantly boosted my learning motivation. N5: Both the design and content of HHC were captivating, transforming course learning into a vivid experience. It increased my enthusiasm and active participation throughout the course.

Post-course insights: strategies for enhancement

Following the course, students proposed refining HHC content to ensure mutually exclusive and clearly defined options, thereby reducing user confusion. N14: I believe the content of HHC could be further refined to avoid overlap or inclusion relationships among options. Making each option more distinct, independent, and targeted would significantly reduce hesitation and confusion during use. Several students suggested developing tailored HHC versions for different patient populations, such as simplified or illustrated editions, to enhance clinical applicability and acceptability. N12: Considering the characteristics of patients across different age groups and cultural backgrounds, customized HHC versions—like simplified or cartoon-style editions—could better meet diverse patient needs and improve the tool’s clinical utility and acceptance. Some participants noted that during theoretical course, HHC was only briefly presented via PowerPoint slides, typically mentioned superficially without in-depth content explanation. Consequently, while the concept seemed refreshing, the initial exposure left limited cognitive imprint - constituting preliminary awareness rather than comprehensive understanding. N3: Although HHC’s concept appeared innovative, its classroom introduction through PPT was cursory without substantive elaboration. My impression remained superficial, merely forming a basic conceptual awareness. N14: While finding HHC intriguing initially, the lack of detailed classroom explanation prevented deeper comprehension. I hope future curricula could incorporate earlier and more thorough HHC content exploration. Additionally, some students emphasized the need to strengthen the connection between simulation training and clinical inspection. N2: After completing scenario-based simulations and transitioning to clinical practicum, there seems to be a disconnect. The actual patients we encounter differ significantly from the idealized cases in simulations, which don’t fully reflect real-world complexities.

Theme 2: Immersive learning and insights from the “Heart to Heart Tea House”

Creating a relaxed atmosphere and open sharing space for death-related discussions

When reviewing the experience of the Heart to Heart Tea House, most participants said that the design of the HHC using playing cards is novel and interesting, and because of its strong interactivity, participants generally feel a relaxed and pleasant atmosphere, which helps to relieve psychological burdens. N6: I thought the HHC was interesting when I heard the teacher introduce it in the theory class, but I had never seen the real thing. I finally used it in this practical class, and everyone participated together, and it felt very natural. Maybe it was because we were all classmates, the atmosphere was very relaxed, and I didn’t feel depressed at all. N7: I don’t think the atmosphere of the Heart to Heart Tea House makes me feel depressed, which helps me express my own thoughts on the topic of death. During the Heart to Heart Tea House, facilitators skillfully utilized the structured HHC format to guide participants in organically sharing personal perspectives and choices. This approach artfully circumvented the potential heaviness of direct death-related discussions, enabling more relaxed and open communication. N11: In the Heart to Heart Tea House event, the guide and host led us to choose the cards, so that we would not feel embarrassed or at a loss when talking about death. The whole process was quite natural. N17: HHC’s design brilliantly frames discussions as multiple-choice questions rather than open-ended essays. We simply selected cards that resonated with us and explained our choices - the entire process felt effortless and pressure-free.

Deep thinking and understanding of humanistic care

The participation experience inspired participants to think deeply personal needs and the death topic in a much more natural and relieved approach. This not only enabled a clearer understanding of their own end-of-life expectations, but also a deeper understanding of other people’s thoughts and feelings. N2: By participating in the Heart to Heart Tea House event, I have a clearer understanding of my end-of-life expectations and a deeper understanding of other people’s thoughts and feelings. This way of communication is very meaningful. N8: This way of communication is good. I can naturally understand some of the ideas of my friends around me. Their ideas will also give me some new thoughts, and it will not make people feel that discussing death is too heavy. N10: This activity not only provides us with a space to express ourselves, but also allows us to think deeply about our needs and some profound topics, such as death. Through the experience of the Heart to Heart Tea House, the participants gained that self-reflection and listening to others’ sharing helped to shape the understanding of notion of “whole-person care” emphasized in the end-of-life care. They realised that hospice care is not just about the physical care of the patient, but is a holistic concern that aims to meet the patient’s needs at all levels and to enhance the quality of his/her life. N9: The Heart to Heart Tea House gave me a deeper understanding of the humanistic care in hospice care. I realized that hospice care is not just about taking care of the patient’s body, but also about paying attention to them in all aspects, meeting their physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs, with the goal of improving their quality of life. N12: I used to think that hospice care was about taking care of the patient’s body and symptom management, but now I know that it is actually all-round, especially focusing on the patient’s comfort care and dignity.

Help to reflect on the meaning of life and plan the career path

Participants reflected on their life goals in the Heart to Heart Tea House and realized that the meaning of life lies in daily enrichment and contribution to society. This made them cherish life more and clarify their career and life direction. N15: I think young people who attend the Heart to Heart Tea House are more concerned with thinking and discussing about the value and meaning of life. I began to think about my own life goals and direction. I realized that the meaning of life is not just to live, but more importantly, how to make every day meaningful and contribute to others and society. N3:These Heart to Heart Tea House dialogues allowed me to understand peers’ perspectives while fostering greater life appreciation. The experience has progressively clarified my career path, marking significant emotional and psychological growth.

Theme 3: Practical challenges in transitioning HHC application from simulations to clinical settings

Exploration in the scenario simulation stage: using heart to heart cards for end-of-life communication

During the scenario simulation stage, students employed the HHC as an innovative communication tool to assist patients in articulating their inner thoughts and preferences. This approach significantly enhanced the clarity and effectiveness of nurse-patient interactions.

N4: In our group’s scenario simulation, the student playing the nurse role was particularly skilled in using HHC. She asked the student acting as the patient to select cards, which helped clearly identify the patient’s needs. Based on the chosen cards, the nurse was then able to provide precisely the assistance the patient required, allowing the entire simulation to progress smoothly and naturally. In simulated scenarios involving role conflicts—such as between patients and family members—HHC proved effective in promoting mutual understanding and easing tensions.

N16: HHC played a pivotal role in the simulated scenario, particularly in resolving conflicts between patients and family members. It acted like a key, unlocking the patient’s true thoughts and enabling both parties to better understand each other, ultimately diffusing the tension. By enabling patients to express their authentic thoughts and emotions through card selection, HHC not only deepened narrative development but also enhanced the emotional realism and resonance of the simulations. N11: HHC not only advanced the narrative development but also heightened the authenticity and emotional resonance of the simulation, transforming it into a profound and meaningful learning experience. This method allowed participants to better immerse themselves in the patient’s perspective, thereby increasing the depth and educational value of the simulation. N17: By having the patient select and interpret the cards, we were able to immerse ourselves more deeply into the patient’s perspective. As a result, the simulation gained greater realism and emotional engagement.

Practice gap in clinical internship

In the course of the inspection, due to the relatively limited opportunities for actual communication with patients, some participants tried to speculate on the possible needs of patients mainly by observing the details of patients’ outward behaviour and demeanour, interests and hobbies, and by combining them with the contents presented in the HHC. N12: During the internship, I didn’t have many opportunities to actually communicate with patients, so I could only observe the patients’ actions and learn about their hobbies from the head nurse, and then compare the content on the HHC to guess what they might need. But this method is not very reliable and the accuracy cannot be guaranteed. Only a few participants had the opportunity to have a brief communication with patients. During the communication, they would guide the patients to express their inner thoughts and needs based on the items on the HHC, and then achieve a deeper understanding of the patients. N1: Our group has the opportunity to chat with the patients briefly. I use the questions on the HHC to try to guide the patients to express their inner thoughts and needs, so that I can better understand the patients’ thoughts and needs.

Discussion

Innovative application and practical exploration of HHC in hospice care education

This study adopted a stepwise approach to systematically integrate HHC into an undergraduate nursing hospice care course. The qualitative findings revealed a nuanced reception of this integration across different phases of the course. In the theoretical phase, students generally reported that the HHC’s novel card-game format sparked initial interest and engagement. Several participants described their first exposure to the HHC as refreshing and motivating, noting that its distinctive design made the hospice care curriculum more engaging. However, some students also reflected that the initial theoretical introduction, delivered primarily through PowerPoint, felt somewhat cursory. They expressed a desire for more in-depth elaboration during this phase to establish a firmer foundational understanding of the tool and its underlying holistic care philosophy before moving to practical application.

During the practical phase, the “Heart to Heart Tea House” was widely perceived as an effective interactive teaching model. Participants frequently described the atmosphere it created as relaxed, open, and safe, which helped alleviate the psychological burden often associated with death-related discussions [27]. The structured format of the HHC was seen as instrumental in facilitating natural and pressure-free communication, allowing students to articulate personal perspectives on end-of-life preferences more comfortably.This finding suggests that the Tea House modality may help to mitigate some of the interaction limitations common in traditional hospice care education. Furthermore, integrating HHC into scenario-based simulations appeared to enhance the learning experience. Students reported that using the cards as a guide during role-playing helped them identify patient needs more systematically and added a layer of authenticity and emotional resonance to the simulations, thereby potentially deepening their engagement and practical comprehension [28, 29].

However, the transition from simulated environments to clinical practice revealed significant challenges. Some participants reported limited opportunities to apply the HHC directly in real patient interactions during their clinical inspection. When opportunities arose, they were often brief, and the complexities of actual clinical settings differed markedly from the more controlled simulation scenarios. This gap underscores that while the progressive “theory-simulation-practice” model is a valuable framework [30], the challenges identified by students precisely illuminate the path for its future refinement. We propose that its effectiveness in fully bridging educational and clinical realities could be enhanced by addressing these gaps, for instance, through increasing the authenticity of simulations and providing more structured support for clinical application.

HHC in hospice care education facilitates the development of communication skills

The findings of this study indicate that using HHC in hospice care education could provide a structured and supportive framework for developing nursing students’ communication skills in end-of-life care. Effective communication is paramount in this context for comprehensive symptom management and psychosocial support [31], yet students often feel anxious and underprepared for such sensitive conversations [32, 33]. In the simulated settings of this course, the HHC appeared to function as a valuable conversational aid. Participants indicated that the visual and categorical nature of the cards helped them structure interactions with simulated patients, making the daunting task of initiating end-of-life discussions more manageable. By providing concrete topics centered on patient needs, the tool seemed to create a conversational “buffer zone,” allowing the dialogue to focus initially on specific concerns rather than directly on death, thereby reducing initial discomfort. This approach was perceived as helping both students and simulated patients express thoughts more easily [34]. Nonetheless, consistent with feedback from participants who suggested card content refinements, the study acknowledges that the tool itself could be optimized—for instance, by minimizing content overlap and enhancing cultural adaptability—to better serve diverse populations and clinical situations [35].

HHC in hospice care education encourages reflection on humanistic care

The use of HHC in the course, particularly within the “Heart to Heart Tea House,” appeared to foster a reflective environment conducive to developing nursing students’ competence in humanistic care [36]. Through the activity, participants engaged in deep reflection on personal values, life goals, and the meaning of death. Many described gaining a clearer understanding of their own end-of-life expectations while also developing a deeper appreciation for the varied perspectives of their peers. This reflective and collaborative process was pivotal in shaping their understanding of the “whole-person care” model essential to hospice philosophy. Students reported a shifted perception of hospice care, moving beyond a focus solely on physical symptom management towards a more holistic view that encompasses the patient’s psychological, social, and spiritual well-being [37]. These experiences suggest that the HHC-facilitated dialogues can contribute to the internalization of humanistic care values, echoing findings from similar educational interventions like the Death Café [38]. For some students, this process also prompted reflection on their professional identity and future career path, marking a step in their development toward becoming more emotionally attuned and compassionate caregivers [39].

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted in a single university with a relatively small sample size, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Second, the elective nature and limited duration of the hospice care course might constrain the long-term assessment of HHC’s educational impact. Third, while students reported perceived improvements in their competencies, the study lacked objective measures to validate these self-assessed gains. Lastly, the potential for social desirability bias must be acknowledged, as participants’ awareness of the researchers’ roles may have inclined them to offer more favourable responses.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the HHC serves as an effective pedagogical tool for enhancing hospice care education among undergraduate nursing students. By integrating the HHC through a progressive “theory-simulation-clinical” model, the intervention successfully bridged the theory-practice gap, fostered multidimensional understanding of end-of-life care, and improved students’ communication skills and humanistic competencies. However, challenges such as item overlap in card content and idealized simulation scenarios highlight the need for further refinements in design and implementation strategies.

Future research should focus on optimizing the HHC’s applicability across diverse clinical and cultural contexts, while expanding empirical evidence to solidify its role in standardized hospice care curricula. In addition, regarding HHC application, two key enhancements were proposed: (1) augmenting simulation authenticity through diversified clinical scenarios and emergent case training, and (2) incorporating detailed theoretical instruction on HHC’s design rationale and cultural context in theory courses. This dual-pronged approach enables nursing students to concretely comprehend the congruence between HHC content and hospice care needs, thereby enhancing overall pedagogical outcomes.
