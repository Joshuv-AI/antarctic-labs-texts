Beyond “Middle Power”: Postcolonial Liminality and the Social Construction of Indonesia's Foreign Policy
Authors: Ahmad Rizky Mardhatillah Umar, Andrew Cooper, David Black, David Hornsby, Emel Parlar Dal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article proposes a new framework for understanding Indonesia's foreign policy. I argue that, rather than the widely accepted conception of Indonesia as a middle power, defined in terms of size, capability, identity, or status, Indonesia's foreign policy is better characterized by postcolonial liminality: a position in which Indonesia sits between multiple powers. Postcolonial liminality emerges from the tension between a postcolonial state's normative aspiration to transform international order and the pressures of great-power politics, leading the state to embrace a position of “non-belonging.” By historicizing Indonesia's foreign policy since its independence, I argue that Indonesia's foreign policy has been shaped by liminality resulting from a desire to advance global decolonization on the one hand and the pressures of great-power politics during the Cold War on the other. This tension has created a liminal position for Indonesia in its engagement with great powers, though it has maintained its autonomy and an independent foreign policy in the post-Cold War world. The story of Indonesia's postcolonial liminality offers a new perspective on the Global South's foreign policy in the changing global order.Keywords: Indonesia, middle power, postcolonial liminality, decolonization, Cold War, international order

Indonesia is widely considered a “middle power.” Scholars have argued that it is a country of intermediate size and capabilities, preferring coalition-building with like-minded countries, and using multilateralism to project influence in foreign policy. 1 This idea of “middle power” has shaped not only the study of Indonesia's foreign policy, but also the ways in which Indonesian policymakers view their country. In 2023, President Joko Widodo described the country as a rising middle power and articulated a certain degree of optimism about the country's role in global politics. 2

This article goes beyond this understanding of “middle power” by introducing the concept of postcolonial liminality as a framework for understanding the social construction of Indonesia's foreign policy. I argue that Indonesia's foreign policy has been shaped by evolving postcolonial liminality. As a postcolonial state, Indonesia navigates its position between multiple great powers to protect its autonomy, sovereignty, and security in international politics. In this context, Indonesia embraces a liminal position characterized by three key elements: (1) a constrained articulation of global decolonization; (2) a position of “non-belonging” in multiple camps or groupings in international politics; and (3) a desire for autonomy amid great-power politics. I bring a more nuanced theoretical understanding of Indonesia's foreign policy than the standard “middle power” argument. The framework of postcolonial liminality helps us to understand Indonesia's evolving position in international politics, not simply as a “middle power” but as a liminal power continuously negotiating its normative and political positions in the changing international order. The framework also exposes the agency of non-Western actors by highlighting their normative and ideational responses to international order.

To illustrate Indonesia's evolving postcolonial liminality in world politics, I trace the intellectual sources that shaped three key periods in Indonesia's foreign policy: (1) the early post-independence period beginning in 1945; (2) the Cold War, especially during the Suharto era; and (3) the post–Cold War period, with its changing international order. I begin by discussing the problem with “middle power” as a concept and the taken-for-granted assumption that Indonesia is a middle power. I then outline the concept of postcolonial liminality as an alternative conceptual framework for understanding Indonesia's foreign policy. The next three sections discuss three key aspects of postcolonial liminality that have shaped Indonesia's foreign policy since independence, namely the constrained articulation of global decolonization during the early independence period (1945–1966), the position of non-belonging during the Cold War, especially as articulated by Suharto's New Order government (1966–1980s), and the post–Cold War quest for autonomy through ASEAN 3 centrality (1990s–present).

The Problem with Middle Power

International Relations (IR) scholarship usually conceptualizes middle powerdom with three key criteria. First, middle powerdom is conceptualized in terms of the use of multilateralism by states to engage in international politics. Literature on middle powers usually characterizes “middle powers” as countries that use multilateralism, coalition-building, or diplomatic initiatives to leverage their power and negotiate their interests with great powers. From this perspective, countries are considered middle powers if (1) they possess limited material capabilities to effect change in international politics; and (2) their foreign policies prioritize coalition-building with other like-minded countries, with a preference for multilateralism and niche diplomacy. 4

Second, middle powerdom is also conceptualized in terms of states’ conceptions of their roles and status in world politics. From this theoretical perspective, scholars argue that middle power is related to the “status” that a country wants to achieve in international politics by playing certain roles. 5 Middle-power status thus represents a position that a state seeks to establish for itself in the international order. A state's self-identification as a middle power is argued to be essential for securing middle-power status because it enables the state to establish a place in the global hierarchy without assuming the roles, obligations, and responsibilities of great powers. 6

Third, middle powerdom is also typically differentiated according to differing agencies and identities that inform the articulation of states’ foreign policies, as seen in the distinction between “Western” and “non-Western” middle powers. From this perspective, Western/Northern middle powers, such as Canada or Australia, emphasize a rules-based international order and good international citizenship as a way to position themselves as middle powers. In contrast, non-Western/Southern middle powers, such as Indonesia or South Korea, are thought to pursue pragmatic engagement and diverse strategies of coalition-building to negotiate with great powers through multilateral channels. 7

Drawing on this conceptualization of middle power, scholars consider Indonesia a non-Western middle power that envisions a certain role in the liberal international order by employing multilateralism. 8 For example, according to Moch Faisal Karim, Indonesia has conceived of its role as a “middle power” in four role conceptions: as a regional leader, a voice for developing countries, an advocate of democracy, and a bridge-builder between the Global North and the Global South. 9 Other scholars consider Indonesia a middle power because of its commitment to regional leadership and good international citizenship, which has led to its preference for multilateralism. 10

Other scholars challenge this conception by arguing that Indonesia's leaders have always aspired to see Indonesia become a “great power” and attain great power status, despite maintaining a “low profile” in their gestures and pursuing inconsistent foreign policies. 11 From this perspective, Indonesia has articulated an aspiration to be considered a “great power,” at least by domestic audiences, as a means of establishing political legitimacy. According to Mohamad Rosyidin, Muhammad Rum, and Randy W. Nandyatama, Indonesia has never fully considered itself a middle power because “there have been significant impulses among Indonesian elites—and people—to regard their country as a great power, as shown in the current government's foreign policy to proclaim Indonesia's pre-eminence on the global stage.” 12

Both positions are problematic and rest on flawed assumptions. The argument that considers Indonesia a “middle power” due to its role conceptions of “good citizenship” or “regional leadership” takes for granted Indonesia's embeddedness in the liberal international order, thereby overlooking the wider historical and normative underpinnings of Indonesia's foreign policy. Similarly, the understanding of Indonesia as a “great power” on the basis of status rests on a false and simplistic distinction that contrasts “great power” with “middle power” and overlooks how policymakers respond to the changing global order and conceive of Indonesia's position within it.

These flaws illustrate two larger problems with the concept of “middle power.” First, it lacks analytical clarity. According to Eduard Jordaan, scholars of middle powers have a problematic obsession with identifying middle powerdom in terms of either “ranking” or “expectation of behaviour” in international politics, which has led scholars to overlook the socially constructed nature of these concepts. 13 Similarly, according to Jeffrey Robertson, it is unclear as to how these scholars define middle power and what counts as “middle” in middle powerdom, given the diversity of middle powers in terms of identity, size, and foreign policy orientation. 14

Second, the concept of middle power, as discussed in the literature, also fails to account for the historical and normative underpinnings of middle powers’ identities, especially the different political identities of states that are considered middle powers. “Middle power,” in this context, lacks an analytical framework that situates the normative and historical visions of order associated with “middle power” within the larger international order. 15 Recent works also criticize the “middle power” scholarship for overlooking domestic complexities, particularly by failing to account for “authoritarian” middle powers that do not necessarily have the same normative visions of international order as “democratic” middle powers. 16

Indonesia's Postcolonial Liminality

This article goes beyond the idea of “middle power” by proposing an alternative theoretical framework that accounts for the deeper normative, historical, and geopolitical positions of countries in the evolving structure of global order. Looking not at its size, capabilities, or status, but at its positionalities, I conceptualize Indonesia as a postcolonial liminal power. Instead of taking for granted states’ unchanging positions as “middle powers” in the international hierarchy, the concept of postcolonial liminality seeks to understand how states position themselves in international politics.

It is important to understand the concept of liminality in IR. Drawing on the works of Victor Turner and Anne Norton, Bahar Rumelili conceives of liminality as a condition of “in-betweenness.” In the words of Turner, liminal entities are “neither here nor there; they are betwixt and between the positions assigned and arranged by law, custom, convention, and ceremonies.” 17 The key to understanding liminality is to understand the complexity of agency: how states conceive of their positions in the changing international order. 18 Unlike approaches to agency in constructivist IR scholarship, liminality blurs the conception of “self” and “other” in world politics because of these states’ position between the “core” of the community—those who hold the most prominent identification in society—and the “other”—those who are excluded from society. 19 From this perspective, liminality can be conceptualized in terms of two key aspects: (1) “in-betweenness,” where agents reside between multiple powers and discursive forces that shape international politics; and (2) non-belonging, where agents cannot belong to one group, camp, or classification because they do not fit within these classifications of power.

Scholars have extended the concept of liminality to understand the agency of states that are considered “middle powers.” For example, Gabriele Abbondanza and Thomas Wilkins use the term “awkward powers” to characterize this liminality, in which countries fit partially within one category but do not fully belong to the group, resulting in awkward behaviour in the international order. 20 From this perspective, Sarah Teo has characterized Indonesia's “norm entrepreneurship” in ASEAN as awkward power because it does not fit fully with the expectation of liberal powers in shaping ASEAN's human rights and democratic ideas. 21 Postcolonial perspectives also use liminality to unpack the complexity of Global South agency, given the diverse and contested nature of “the Global South” and its complex interactions with other powers, especially after the Cold War. 22

Radityo Dharmaputra provides a clearer characterization of Indonesia's liminality in global politics. According to Dharmaputra, Indonesia's liminality has been characterized by the invocation of “middle-ness” across geography (“lying between the Indian and Pacific Oceans”), diplomacy (“middle power identity”), culture (“religious moderation”), and even foreign policy (the aspiration to posit Indonesia as a “bridge-builder” between the Global South and the Global North). These characteristics have led to Indonesia's “in-between foreign policy,” encapsulated in the sacrosanct principle of “independent and active foreign policy.” For Dharmaputra, “Indonesia sits in between different classifications,” which in turn has created “a recurring tendency to position Indonesia between competing camps, even when circumstances call for clearer alignment.” 23

Drawing on this extensive literature I reconceptualize Indonesia as a postcolonial liminal power. Indonesia's liminality emerged when its articulation of its normative vision of order was constrained by hostile great-power politics that affected its domestic politics, in turn forcing the country to adopt a position of non-belonging to maintain autonomy in international politics. I agree with Dharmaputra's characterization of liminality as “in-betweenness” among multiple powers. My argument, however, is more specific. I argue that Indonesia's liminality is postcolonial in nature, in the sense that Indonesia does not simply sit between multiple classifications and powers. Indonesia also actively negotiates its normative vision for postcolonial global order to adapt to the realities of great power politics, which has led to its non-belonging position in international politics.

Three key elements have shaped Indonesia's postcolonial liminality in the history of its foreign policy. First, Indonesia's liminality is shaped, primarily, by a constrained articulation of the normative vision of order in international politics. Since its independence, Indonesia has articulated a vision of postcolonial global order that is grounded in the normative vision of international order that has guided Indonesia's foreign policy, namely the rejection of colonialism, defence of sovereignty and sovereign equality in global politics, and a consistent embrace of multilateralism. 24 The articulation of this vision, however, is constrained by the pressures and demands of great powers to choose alignment in international politics, which has constrained Indonesia's push for a Third World bloc and global decolonization. 25

Second, Indonesia's liminality is shaped by a position of non-belonging: neither fully pursuing its agenda independently nor belonging to a particular power bloc due to power constraints in global politics. Scholars have largely characterized Indonesia's position as non-alignment: a rejection of formal alignment with great powers. 26 The idea of “non-belonging” is larger than non-alignment. Indonesia does not simply reject alignment politically; it was also unable and unwilling to belong socially to one camp during the Cold War due to its ambivalent attitude toward external powers and its aspiration to protect its autonomy from external interference. This led Indonesia's foreign-policymakers to avoid fixed classification of Indonesia in any grouping and to continuously engage with multiple camps by maintaining a certain distance. 27

Third, Indonesia's liminality is oriented toward maintaining an autonomous position in international politics. By autonomy, I mean Indonesia's ability to decide its own policies without external interference from great powers. Scholars have previously noted that Indonesia's foreign policy is based on the importance of sovereignty and non-interference. 28 However, in the context of unipolar or multipolar great power politics, autonomy is not always possible because of pressures. Thus, Indonesia not only maintains its distance from great powers; it also actively engages in certain strategies to protect its autonomous position, such as hedging or multi-alignment. 29 In this context, my conception of Indonesia's liminality is broader than the strategies of hedging or multi-alignment. As I will show in the penultimate section, Indonesia's foreign policy has been driven by a larger pursuit of autonomy to protect its identity and security, especially after the Cold War.

I will show these three aspects of liminality across three periods in the history of Indonesia's foreign policy: early independence under Sukarno (1945–1966), the height of the Cold War under Suharto (1966–1980s), and the post–Cold War era in the late Suharto and reformasi eras (1990s–present). In analyzing Indonesia's postcolonial liminality, I employ an intellectual-history approach by specifically tracing Indonesia's perceptions of its position in international politics. Through this approach, I show how liminality emerged from the engagement between foreign policy leaders and the structure of great power politics in international politics as reflected in their justifications for foreign policy. 30 I look at the intellectual sources behind Indonesia's foreign policy and assess (1) their conception of Indonesia's position in international politics; and (2) their strategies and actions in relation to Indonesia's position in international politics.

For this purpose, I trace Indonesia's key speech and policy documents, as well as the political thought of Indonesia's foreign policy leaders, to understand the evolution of Indonesia's liminality during and after the Cold War. For the early years of independence, I look at Sukarno, Mohammad Hatta, and Djuanda Kartawidjaja, especially in the context of independence and the global push for decolonization. For the height of the Cold War under Suharto, I turn to Adam Malik, Mochtar Kusumaatmadja, and Ali Moertopo, who articulated Indonesia's foreign policy during the Cold War, as well as the new post–Cold War international order in the 1990s. I then assess foreign policy leaders in the late Suharto period, particularly Ali Alatas, Jusuf Wanandi, and Juwono Sudarsono, who tried to defend Indonesia amid great power pressures. Finally, I discuss post-Suharto foreign policy leaders, particularly Dewi Fortuna Anwar, Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono, Marty Natalegawa, Retno L.P. Marsudi, and Prabowo Subianto, to analyze Indonesia's quest for autonomy and ASEAN centrality amid United States–China contestation.

Constrained Global Decolonization

To understand Indonesia's postcolonial liminality in world politics, first it is important to understand Indonesia's perceptions of its normative and historical identity as a postcolonial state. The intellectual roots of Indonesia's postcolonial liminality lie in its normative vision of postcolonial global order. Early post-independence leaders embraced an anti-colonial conception of world politics by emphasizing anti-colonialism, anti-imperialism, and rejection of Western dominance and intervention. As leaders of a nation liberated from colonialism, they envisioned an international order in which all countries would stand equally in international politics regardless of race and status, and would be free from colonial oppression and able to pursue development after independence. 31

Indonesia's early post-independence policymakers were indeed aware of the hostile great power politics during the Cold War. After independence, Indonesia's foreign-policymakers devised the principle of “independent and active” to navigate Indonesia's position amid the emerging US–Soviet rivalry. In his famous 1948 foreign policy speech, Hatta laid this foundation, making an analogy of Indonesia's foreign policy as “rowing between two reefs. Hatta considered two related aspects in positioning Indonesia after independence, including (1) Indonesia's geopolitical position as an archipelagic state located between competing great powers; and (2) Indonesia's consciousness as a postcolonial state with a need for international recognition, sovereignty, and development. 32

Hatta believed that Indonesia should maintain its independence by navigating between the great powers, allowing it to protect territorial sovereignty. From this perspective, Hatta outlined the principle of Indonesia's foreign policy that later became known as “independent and active,” arguing that Indonesia should not aim to be “an object in international political struggle,” but instead “a subject that can determine our position, [and] has a right to pursue our own purpose, that is Indonesia's complete independence.” 33

This “independent and active principle” has led Indonesian foreign-policymakers to envision “the Third World”: a third bloc comprising the postcolonial countries intended not only to navigate tensions between the Western and communist blocs, but also to advance decolonization in Asia and Africa. This push was evident with the 1955 Asian–African Conference in Bandung, which has been hailed as a pivotal feature of the modern international order. Indonesia also spearheaded an initiative to abolish colonialism at the 1960 United Nations General Assembly, along with fellow non-aligned countries Egypt, India, Ghana, and Yugoslavia. Indonesia took the leading role in the initiative, with Sukarno passionately calling for the abolition of colonialism at the UN General Assembly. 34 Supported by the Soviet bloc and China, the five leaders won the ensuing vote, and the UN General Assembly formally called for the end of colonialism through Resolution 1514. Later, Indonesia participated in the formal creation of the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961 and was involved in the formation of the Group of 77, consolidating the Third World bloc for newly decolonized countries and their supporters.

During the 1960s, however, Sukarno increasingly became close with the communist bloc amid the heightened tensions of the Cold War, although Indonesia still maintained engagement with the Third World countries. Sukarno proposed a “Jakarta-Peking-Pyongyang Axis” to establish new cooperation with China and North Korea, and later engaged with the Konfrontasi campaign against Malaysia under the pretext of resisting British imperialism. 35 Nevertheless, Sukarno remained commited to engaging with Third World countries. Speaking at the first Non-Aligned Summit in Belgrade, Yugoslavia, Sukarno enunciated what he called “the New Emerging Forces”: a coalition of new powers providing an alternative to the “old established forces” perpetuating colonialism. Sukarno believed the non-aligned states should ally with these emergent forces to fight against colonialism, injustice, and the “old equilibrium” of the great powers. 36

Indonesia's foreign policy during its early independence set the basis for postcolonial liminality in terms of constrained articulation of Indonesia's vision of a postcolonial global order due to the Cold War. As Hatta and Sukarno outlined, Indonesia was aware of the increased tensions between the Western and communist blocs. Nevertheless, Indonesia still aspired to push for global decolonization. This tension created a liminal position in early post-independence foreign policy in terms of a constrained commitment to decolonization amid the early Cold War tensions in world politics. This constrained vision was then pushed further into collapse when Sukarno himself was toppled by General Suharto and the military amid the political crisis of 1965–1966, thus putting an end to his ambitious decolonization project.

Non-belonging During the Cold War

Suharto created a new chapter in Indonesia's foreign affairs. In contrast to the anti-colonial nationalism of his predecessor Sukarno, Suharto had a pro-Western foreign policy outlook. Under Suharto, Indonesia embraced a position of non-belonging by engaging with the West but maintaining a distant position in order to protect its autonomy. Shortly after taking control, Suharto reversed his predecessor's anti-imperialist foreign policy, opened up the economy with foreign investment, and pivoted towards a more Western-friendly yet still cautious foreign policy. 37 Nevertheless, while Sukarno's anti-colonial and anti-imperialist foreign policy was largely dissolved by Suharto, Indonesia's non-aligned stance in international politics was still maintained by Suharto's foreign-policymakers.

Foreign Minister Adam Malik (1966–1978) outlined Indonesia's position as a departure from Sukarno's anti-colonialism. For Malik, Indonesia attempted to shift away from anti-colonial rhetoric, and embraced a new position as a “developing country” to become a bridge between the Global North and the Global South. 38 In articulating the idea of “becoming a bridge,” Malik envisioned a “good neighbour” relationship with Indonesia's Southeast Asian neighbours to contain the danger of communism and any effects of military intervention during the Cold War. This “good neighbour” foreign policy culminated in the creation of ASEAN in 1967 at a meeting of the foreign ministers of Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, the Philippines, and Thailand in Bangkok. According to Malik, who was also involved in the formation of ASEAN, regional cooperation was necessary to enable Southeast Asian countries to improve development without the fear of conflict and war because “the developing nations themselves have the primary responsibility for accelerating the speed of their recovery and improving their standard of living.” 39

ASEAN reshaped Indonesia's foreign policy under Suharto. Suharto's foreign policy and military advisors, especially Ali Moertopo, envisioned ASEAN as a “buffer” to protect Indonesia against the communist threat from the north, especially China and North Vietnam. 40 Therefore, Indonesia actively sought to strengthen ASEAN by establishing collective norms within the regional organization. Beginning in the early 1970s, Indonesia hosted a series of workshops with ASEAN policy leaders at the National Resilience Institute (LEMHANNAS), a military think tank, to discuss regional security developments. The result of these workshops was the concept of “regional resilience,” which later evolved into a set of principles that were adopted as the Treaty of Amity and Cooperation at the first ASEAN Summit in Bali in 1976, including respect for sovereignty, non-interference in internal and external affairs, peaceful settlement of disputes, renunciation of the use of force, and regional cooperation. 41

Despite these commitments, Indonesia was still able to use military force to invade—and later annex—East Timor in 1975 without any criticism from other ASEAN members, even though the invasion and annexation clearly violated ASEAN's basic principles of non-use of force. A document published by Indonesia's Ministry of foreign affairs in 1977 framed the invasion of East Timor as an act of “decolonization” from the Portuguese Empire and justified the invasion on the pretext of combatting communist terror from Frente Revolucionária do Timor-Leste Independente (FRETILIN), a major pro-independence party. 42 The invasion of East Timor thus exposed a contradiction between Indonesia's stated commitment to non-intervention and non-use of force and its Cold War anti-communism.

In addition to strengthening ASEAN, Indonesia also used the United Nations to defend its maritime territorial sovereignty and right to development. Indonesia advocated the concept of “archipelagic states” at the UN Conference on the Law of the Sea. 43 Indonesia had sought recognition of its maritime territorial boundaries and its right to an exclusive economic zone since the tenure of Prime Minister Djuanda, who declared Indonesia an archipelagic country and stated that “all territorial waters around, between, and connecting islands . . . are a part of Indonesia's territories.” 44 Under the leadership of Foreign Minister Mochtar Kusumaatmadja (1978–1988), Indonesia fought for this concept until its successful adoption as the UN Convention of the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) in 1982. In line with this position, Indonesia also supported the New International Economic Order to maintain its sovereignty over natural resources and pursue economic justice, even though Indonesia did not commit extensively to challenging Western dominance over the global economic order. 45

This consciousness of liminality also involved a military strategy prioritizing the protection of territorial sovereignty as the key objective in Indonesia's defence and security policies. From this perspective, realist thinkers, associated with the military and national security establishment, espoused a geostrategic conception of Indonesia as an archipelagic state located between great powers, necessitating a good neighbour policy. Ali Moertopo, a key advisor to President Suharto, emphasized that Indonesia should focus on its territorial integrity and pay attention to the contestation between the United States, Japan, the Soviet Union, and China in Southeast Asia. To respond to this political development, Moertopo argued, “the doctrine of national resilience should be developed to prevent any possible threat to the national security of the countries concerned.” 46

It was against this backdrop that Indonesia proposed the concept of “regional resilience” to frame the security of ASEAN. For Moertopo, Southeast Asian countries held responsibility for their own security and should cooperate with one another, meaning that Indonesia and its neighbours should defend their national interests together, since “small and developing countries can never rely on the power of other countries.” 47 One consequence of this regional resilience was the rejection of foreign military bases and military pacts considered detrimental to national security and stability and at risk of inviting intervention. It is important to note, however, that despite outlining his view, Moertopo did not directly criticize other Southeast Asian countries that had established alliances or even hosted military bases, such as the Philippines or Singapore, even though this contradicted the vision of neutrality in ASEAN.

Suharto's foreign policy illustrates the broader transformation of postcolonial liminality. Under Suharto, Indonesia shifted its foreign policy in a pro-Western direction and articulated an anti-communist foreign policy, which was prominent in its push for ASEAN and invasion of East Timor. Nevertheless, Indonesia was wary of spillover from conflict and military intervention during the Cold War, leading it to prioritize regionalism and multilateralism, particularly through ASEAN and the United Nations. Indonesia, more specifically, refused to be classified as either Global South or Global North, and instead played a role in bridging their cooperation to establish global interdependence at the height of the Cold War. This refusal reflected Indonesia's broader desire to maintain autonomy and non-alignment while at the same time avoiding any confrontation with the West.

Autonomy After the Cold War

The end of the Cold War brought significant changes to the configuration of great power politics with the consolidation of the liberal international order under American leadership. With these geopolitical changes, Indonesia found itself once again in a position of liminality. It faced pressure from the West to democratize and end its human rights abuses, which made it reluctant to engage with the liberal international order instead turning to managing its autonomy through ASEAN.

In the late 1980s, Indonesia began to face pressures from Western countries over widespread human rights abuses. For example, it faced heavy scrutiny from its civil society organizations and Western countries over the human rights abuses in East Timor perpetrated by the Indonesian military. The United States, in particular, imposed embargoes on the Indonesian military and barred some central military figures from entering the country. 48 Unsurprisingly, this pressure made Indonesia's leadership uncomfortable. Former foreign minister Ali Alatas called it a “pebble in the shoe” that constrained Indonesia's diplomatic engagement with the West. 49 Other Indonesian elites also rejected what they perceived as the selective application of human rights. Juwono Sudarsono, then deputy governor of the LEMHANNAS, described the issue of human rights as a “diplomatic scam” due to its selective application to non-Western countries. 50

However, not all Indonesians rejected human rights. Civil society organizations were among the most vocal in their support for human rights, using the new window of opportunity created by “openness” (keterbukaan) to engage in activism for human rights and democratization. 51 Prominent Indonesian think tank leader Jusuf Wanandi wrote that ASEAN nations, including Indonesia, should accept human rights but should have a forum to discuss human rights issues in their own way. 52 He even argued that ASEAN should establish its own commission on human rights that would enable ASEAN to approach the issue with reference to its members’ specific historic, economic, and political circumstances. 53

This pressure to embrace human rights created reluctance among Indonesia's governing elites to fully align with the liberal international order, even after Suharto fell from power. After the devastating Asian financial crisis that began in 1997 and severely weakened Indonesia's economy, leading to Suharto's fall from power in 1998, Indonesia adjusted its economic policies and embraced a structural adjustment programme from the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank. This move was accompanied by legal and political reform under Suharto's successors, including B.J. Habibie (1998–1999), Abdurrahman Wahid (1999–2001), and Megawati Sukarnoputri (2001–2004).

President Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono (2004–2014) attempted to improve Indonesia's engagement with the West by articulating a willingness to embrace democracy and human rights while being reluctant to accept the universal values of liberal democracy. To reinforce Indonesia's commitment to democracy, Yudhoyono organized the Bali Democracy Forum, an annual meeting showcasing the experience of democracy from non-Western countries. Yudhoyono made use of Indonesia's successful democratic transition and democratic consolidation after Suharto as a model of “home-grown democracy,” and branded Indonesia as “the third largest democracy in the world” and “Southeast Asia's largest and arguably strongest democracy.” 54 However, cautious about simply importing democracy from the West, Yudhoyono emphasized “the importance of searching for the right political and economic format of governance in order to promote economic development and democracy that fits the socio-economic conditions of the countries in the region.” 55

Yudhoyono's caution signals a larger reluctance to formally align with the West, which has been Indonesia's position since early independence. This reluctance became increasingly prominent in the 2010s as the United States engaged in economic competition with China as its primary economic rival. Indonesia responded to this US–China competition by offering an ASEAN perspective on the Indo-Pacific. Foreign Minister Marty Natalegawa (2009–2014) initially proposed an Indo-Pacific Treaty between ASEAN and its Indo-Pacific partners, including China and the United States, to ensure the security of ASEAN member states. Natalegawa believed that US–China competition had created a “dynamic equilibrium,” in which “dominant power [was] absent owing to a lack of the promotion of block politics and often self-fulfilling geopolitical fault lines.” 56 To respond to this dynamic equilibrium, Natalegawa argued that a new treaty between ASEAN and all dialogue partners in the Indo-Pacific was necessary to reduce the trust deficit among major powers, resolve territorial claim disputes (with an indirect reference to the South China Sea), and establish positive changes among actors with ASEAN's facilitation. 57

This proposal failed to materialize prior to the end of Natalegawa's tenure as foreign minister. However, the idea that Indonesia should actively engage in navigating US–China competition through ASEAN was revived by his successor, Retno Marsudi (2014–2024), with the new ASEAN Outlook on the Indo-Pacific (AOIP). Indonesia proposed the AOIP in early 2019, and all ASEAN members agreed to adopt the concept in the same year. According to Marsudi the AOIP reflects Indonesia's commitment to ensure peace, stability, and prosperity in the Indian and Pacific Ocean regions, which have been increasingly threatened by internal disunity caused by regional tensions between great powers. 58 Marsudi's concerns reflect the justification that Natalegawa previously made for the Indo-Pacific Treaty. Unlike Natalegawa's proposal, however, the AOIP is not a legal framework. The Outlook reiterates ASEAN's existing commitment to resolving disputes with great powers through institutionalized mechanisms within ASEAN-led regional architecture, particularly the ASEAN Regional Forum, the East Asia Summit, the ASEAN Defence Ministers’ Meeting Plus, and separate ASEAN+1 mechanisms with dialogue partners. 59

Indonesia's push for stronger ASEAN engagement in the Indo-Pacific reflects Indonesia's liminality under the pressure of US–China strategic competition. Indonesia wants to assert its autonomy through ASEAN while maintaining engagement with the US and China in economic, security, and development matters. Marsudi has repeatedly described ASEAN as “the cornerstone of Indonesia's foreign policy,” in keeping with Indonesia's commitment to ASEAN. 60 According to Dewi Fortuna Anwar, Indonesia has also expressed reservations about China's maritime territorial claims in the South China Sea and US minilateral initiatives such as AUKUS 61 and the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (the Quad). Instead, Indonesia has urged each side to use ASEAN-led forums to ease tensions, either through the ASEAN Regional Forum, the East Asia Summit, or code of conduct negotiation to resolve the South China Sea disputes in line with AOIP principles. 62

Prabowo Subianto's election as president in 2024 has opened up a new chapter of Indonesian foreign policy. Prabowo has reshaped foreign policy by bringing Indonesia into BRICS to engage with Russia and China (along with other states) and participating in the Board of Peace, a new international initiative established by US President Donald Trump to address problems in Gaza. Nevertheless, while Prabowo has attempted to improve Indonesia's global standing through these new engagements, Indonesia's liminality persists. Prabowo has been reluctant to formally align with the United States and has maintained a “multi-aligned” strategy to engage both the United States and China, which has resulted in Indonesia's limited role in BRICS and the Board of Peace. 63

Conclusion: Liminality and Postcolonial Foreign Policy

This article has attempted to challenge the widely held conception of Indonesia as a “middle power.” Indonesia's international thinking has been shaped by a sense of postcolonial liminality, which is characterized by a tension between its normative identity as a postcolonial state and the hostile structure of great power politics. Indonesia's foreign policy has been shaped by a tension between its desire for global decolonization in the context of the Cold War and post–Cold War international order and the hostile great power politics that have forced Indonesia to align. This tension has resulted in evolving liminality: Indonesia's foreign policy leaders have embraced regionalism and multilateralism since the Cold War, while still maintaining a desire for autonomy and non-alignment with any great powers. This liminality has put Indonesia in an awkward position of “non-belonging” amid Cold War tensions and post–Cold War strategic competition between the United States and China.

The concept of postcolonial liminality offers a deeper understanding of postcolonial states’ normative identities and historical positions in global order than does the concept of “middle power.” From the perspective of liminality, postcolonial states do not simply become “middle powers” in the global hierarchy; they actively negotiate their positions with other powers. The Indonesian story in this article shows this negotiation in the context of regional and international initiatives in which Indonesia has participated, such as ASEAN, UNCLOS, and East Asian regional architecture. This story offers a larger lesson for rethinking other Global South countries in the changing global order. Countries such as India, Brazil, and South Korea (to name but a few) have also articulated liminality shaped by their histories, making them not simply “middle powers” but postcolonial liminal powers that actively negotiate their places in the changing global order. This requires IR scholars to go beyond the concept of “middle power” and rethink postcolonial foreign policy in terms of liminality.
