Rapid Diagnosis and Surgical Management of Catastrophic Stanford Type A Acute Aortic Dissection Resulting in Successful Lung Donation: A Case Report
Authors: Koki Yokawa, Ryota Abe, Tomonori Higuma, Kazunori Yoshida, Hidefumi Obo, Makoto Kadotani, Hidetaka Wakiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case highlights that rapid diagnosis and emergency surgical repair for catastrophic Stanford Type A acute aortic dissection may preserve the opportunity for successful lung donation even when neurological recovery is impossible. Transthoracic echocardiography played a critical role in establishing the diagnosis when computed tomography could not be performed.Keywords: acute aortic dissection, emergency department, lung transplantation, organ donation, transthoracic echocardiography

Introduction

Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a life‐threatening condition requiring immediate diagnosis and intervention. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) remains the standard diagnostic modality [1]; however, CT may not be feasible in patients presenting with severe hemodynamic instability, shock, or impaired consciousness. In such situations, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) can provide critical diagnostic information and facilitate timely decision‐making. Although rapid diagnosis and emergency surgery are primarily intended to improve patient survival, they may also preserve organ function even when neurological recovery is ultimately impossible. We report a rare case of catastrophic Stanford Type A acute aortic dissection in which emergency diagnosis by TTE enabled immediate surgical repair without preoperative computed tomography. Although the patient ultimately progressed to brain death, successful lung donation was achieved, highlighting an uncommon but important clinical outcome.

Case History/Examination

A 54‐year‐old woman developed sudden back pain at 5:00 p.m. on the day of symptom onset. She was evaluated at a nearby clinic and discharged after observation. However, her symptoms persisted, and at approximately 11:00 p.m. she collapsed at home and was transported to a local emergency hospital.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed at the referring hospital demonstrated pericardial effusion and a suspected intimal flap within the aorta, raising suspicion of acute aortic dissection. She was subsequently transferred to our institution for further management.

Upon arrival, the patient was severely agitated and demonstrated altered consciousness (Japan Coma Scale III‐100). Her blood pressure was 75/65 mmHg, and her heart rate was 75 beats/min. Emergency department TTE revealed pericardial effusion and an intimal flap within the aortic arch (Figure 1). Because profound shock and severe agitation precluded safe computed tomography (CT) examination, a diagnosis of Stanford Type A acute aortic dissection was established based on clinical findings and echocardiographic assessment alone. Because of the patient's profound hemodynamic instability, only a focused transthoracic echocardiographic examination could be performed in the emergency department. Massive pericardial effusion and a suspected intimal flap in the ascending aorta were identified. No further assessment of aortic valve function or ventricular function was feasible before emergency surgery.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Emergency surgery was immediately undertaken. After transfer to the operating room, induction of general anesthesia resulted in further hemodynamic deterioration, culminating in cardiopulmonary arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated immediately. Median sternotomy revealed cardiac tamponade caused by a large hematoma over the anterior surface of the right ventricle. Following evacuation of the hematoma, temporary hemodynamic improvement was observed. Massive bleeding from the posterior aspect of the ascending aorta subsequently identified rupture of the ascending aorta as the source of circulatory collapse.

Because central venous access could not be established promptly, a venous cannula was inserted directly into the right atrium for rapid volume infusion. Simultaneously, the left femoral artery was exposed and cannulated for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). The interval from sternotomy to CPB initiation was 15 min. During this period, no effective pulse pressure was detectable, and continuous external cardiac massage was required. After CPB establishment, manual compression of the rupture site was performed while systemic cooling proceeded. Under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest with retrograde cerebral perfusion, ascending aortic replacement was successfully completed (Figure 2). Spontaneous circulation returned, and the patient was weaned from CPB. Total operative time was 6 h and 55 min.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable; however, neurological recovery was not achieved. On postoperative Day 1, bilateral pupillary dilation was observed. Head CT demonstrated severe hypoxic encephalopathy and brainstem compression consistent with cerebral herniation (Figure 3). Despite maximal intensive care support, the patient never regained consciousness after surgery (GCS E1VTM1), and brain death was confirmed on postoperative Day 13 according to institutional protocols.

Computed tomography of the head after surgery for acute aortic dissection. (A) The findings shown are consistent with hypoxic encephalopathy; (B) The apparent brainstem compression is suggestive of cerebral herniation.

Following discussions with the patient's family, organ donation was pursued. Cardiac function remained preserved without significant ventricular dysfunction, and the heart was considered a potential transplantable organ; however, cardiac donation was declined by the family. Pulmonary function was maintained, and successful lung procurement and transplantation were performed.

The kidneys were also procured as marginal donor organs. Because contrast‐enhanced CT had not been performed before surgery, the renal arterial anatomy could not be adequately assessed. Subsequent evaluation demonstrated that the renal arteries originated from the false lumen of the dissected aorta. Consequently, conventional organ perfusion techniques were insufficient for organ preservation, and kidney transplantation could not be performed.

Discussion

This case highlights two important clinical lessons. First, timely diagnosis and surgical intervention may preserve extracerebral organ function and maintain opportunities for organ donation even when neurological recovery is impossible. Second, emergency department transthoracic echocardiography can facilitate rapid diagnosis of catastrophic acute Type A aortic dissection when computed tomography is not feasible, thereby enabling prompt surgical intervention.

The diagnostic utility of TTE in acute aortic syndromes has been reported previously [2, 3, 4, 5]. However, most reports emphasize its role in establishing the diagnosis and expediting surgical treatment. In the present case, TTE enabled immediate recognition of ATAAD in a patient whose profound shock and severe agitation precluded CT imaging. Without this rapid diagnosis, emergency surgical intervention would likely have been delayed.

Despite prompt diagnosis and surgery, the patient ultimately developed irreversible hypoxic encephalopathy. This outcome was most likely attributable to profound circulatory collapse associated with ascending aortic rupture and cardiac tamponade before definitive circulatory support could be established. However, cerebral malperfusion related to acute Type A aortic dissection cannot be completely excluded, particularly because preoperative neurological imaging could not be performed. Thus, early diagnosis alone cannot always prevent devastating neurological injury in patients presenting with catastrophic ATAAD [1].

Nevertheless, rapid diagnosis and multidisciplinary management preserved the function of other organs. Although the patient could not be neurologically salvaged, successful lung donation was ultimately achieved. This experience highlights an often‐overlooked aspect of acute aortic dissection management. In selected patients, timely diagnosis and organ‐preserving critical care may create opportunities for organ donation and transplantation, thereby extending the benefits of treatment beyond the individual patient.

Successful organ transplantation from donors with aortic dissection has been reported previously [6]. However, in many reported cases, detailed anatomical evaluation was available before organ procurement. In the present case, profound hemodynamic instability, severe agitation, and impending circulatory collapse precluded CT examination. Consequently, life‐saving surgery had to proceed without detailed anatomical assessment of the distal aorta and its branch vessels. Following emergency cardiac surgery, postoperative management focused on preserving donor organ function. Careful fluid management with diuretic therapy was used to minimize pulmonary congestion after cardiopulmonary bypass, while vasoactive agents were administered as needed to maintain hemodynamic stability because marked blood pressure fluctuations frequently occurred after brain death. These measures may have contributed to the preservation of lung function until organ procurement.

Although successful lung transplantation was achieved, the absence of preoperative CT created difficulties during donor organ evaluation. The kidneys were procured as marginal donor organs; however, subsequent assessment demonstrated that the renal arteries originated from the false lumen of the dissected aorta. In this case, bedside abdominal ultrasonography performed before organ procurement confirmed preserved blood flow to the abdominal organs. Nevertheless, the kidneys were ultimately unsuitable for transplantation. We speculate that this was related to the renal arteries originating from the false lumen. Although preservation solution was administered through the true lumen using the standard renal preservation technique, adequate renal perfusion may not have been achieved because the patent false lumen allowed dissipation of perfusion pressure, thereby limiting effective delivery of preservation solution to the kidneys. Although this mechanism cannot be confirmed, it may represent an important consideration when evaluating organ donors with residual aortic dissection.

This experience suggests that organ donor evaluation after ATAAD may be particularly challenging when emergent life‐saving treatment must be prioritized over comprehensive imaging. Cardiovascular surgeons should recognize that, although rapid diagnosis and surgical intervention may preserve transplantable organs, the lack of preoperative anatomical information can influence the feasibility of subsequent organ procurement and transplantation. Reports of successful organ transplantation from donors with aortic dissection are limited, and evidence specifically addressing organ donation following emergency surgical repair of catastrophic acute Type A aortic dissection is particularly scarce. Therefore, the present case should not be interpreted as establishing a general causal relationship between rapid surgical intervention and preservation of donor organ viability. Nevertheless, this single case illustrates that successful lung donation may still be possible following emergency surgical repair for catastrophic acute type A aortic dissection. When preoperative computed tomography cannot be performed because of profound hemodynamic instability, focused transthoracic echocardiography can provide sufficient diagnostic information to enable life‐saving surgery, which may preserve the opportunity for organ donation in carefully selected patients. This case highlights that, although uncommon, successful lung donation may remain feasible in carefully selected patients despite catastrophic neurological outcomes.
