Strategy for balancing quality and moderate sodium reduction in beef patties through Maillard reaction modulation by banana peel extract
Authors: Nayoon Park, Hye-Jin Kim, Jeong Beom Ju, Cheorun Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated banana peel extract as a byproduct-derived strategy for maintaining flavor development and product quality in moderately sodium-reduced beef patties through Maillard reaction enhancement. Patties were prepared as S (1.0% NaCl), LS (0.5% NaCl), KS (0.5% NaCl +0.5% KCl), and ES (0.5% NaCl +0.5% banana peel extract) and pan-grilled at 180 °C. Compared with S, LS and KS reduced sodium content by approximately 45%, whereas ES achieved a more moderate sodium reduction of approximately 22% while avoiding the potassium increase associated with KCl substitution. Maillard reaction indices showed that ES maintained intermediate- and late-stage markers comparable to S and KS, while LS exhibited weaker browning responses. ES preserved water-holding capacity, cooking loss, and texture. Sensory evaluation indicated that ES provided saltiness similar to KS with reduced bitterness, consistent with electronic tongue measurements. NMR-based metabolomics supported shifts in organic acids and umami-associated nucleotides. Overall, banana peel extract represents a byproduct-derived clean-label approach that improved taste balance and maintained product quality under reduced‑sodium conditions.

Introduction

Salt is an essential component of the human diet and is widely used in the food industry. In processed meat products, salt promotes myofibrillar protein solubilization and hydration, thereby improving binding, water-holding capacity, and emulsion stability (Ruusunen & Puolanne, 2005). These changes influence key quality attributes, including cooking loss, tenderness, and juiciness. Salt also provides a characteristic salty taste while contributing to the enhancement of umami and perceived sweetness (Wang et al., 2023). However, meat products can account for as much as approximately 20% of total dietary sodium intake in daily diets (Inguglia et al., 2017). At the same time, excessive sodium intake is linked to elevated blood pressure and higher risks of cardiovascular disease and stroke. To reduce diet-related disease burden, the World Health Organization recommends limiting salt intake to 5 g/day and sodium intake to 2 g/day for adults (World Health Organization, 2025). Nevertheless, reported population intakes remain above these targets, averaging approximately 8–12 g/day in many European countries and > 12 g/day in several Asian countries (Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, reducing sodium content in meat products remains a major challenge.

Reducing sodium content in meat products is commonly achieved using salt replacers or flavor-enhancing ingredients. A representative salt replacer is potassium chloride (KCl). Although KCl can partially substitute for NaCl to lower dietary sodium exposure, its use is often limited by bitterness and a metallic off-taste (Morris et al., 2010). Moreover, KCl substitution may not be recommended for people with limited potassium clearance or on potassium-raising medications because it can increase serum potassium levels (Lee, 2011). In addition, most of these substitutes are synthetic additives, which may not align with consumer preferences for natural ingredients (Ortega-Heras et al., 2020). As an alternative strategy, flavor enhancers can be used to mitigate the sensory drawbacks associated with NaCl reduction, including reduced saltiness and a less balanced overall flavor profile, because many flavor enhancers exhibit synergistic taste effects that help compensate for these losses and improve overall palatability (Wang et al., 2023). Flavor enhancers include yeast extracts, free amino acids and peptides, nucleotides, plant protein hydrolysates, and plant-derived extracts. Among these, plant-derived extracts such as rosemary and grape pomace extracts have been reported to enhance perceived saltiness in foods (Bi et al., 2024). Notably, plant-derived extracts can be obtained from agricultural and food-processing by-products, providing a value-added route for waste valorization while meeting clean-label demand. García-Lomillo et al. (2017) recovered an extract from red wine by-product residues and proposed it as a natural seasoning alternative to salt. When added to beef patties, it provided antioxidant and antibacterial effects, allowing salt reduction and delaying microbial growth. In addition to their direct taste-enhancing effects, plant-derived extracts can influence flavor development through chemical reactions during thermal processing.

The Maillard reaction is a non-enzymatic series of reactions between reducing sugars and amino compounds (Lee et al., 2025). Because it strongly shapes both sensory attributes and nutritional quality, it is a key reaction during cooking and food processing (Arihara et al., 2021). Maillard reaction products can contribute to saltiness enhancement and may also exhibit functional properties such as antioxidant and antimicrobial activities, supporting their potential use as functional ingredients (Ni et al., 2025; Xing et al., 2024). However, in meat systems, the Maillard reaction can be limited by the relatively low availability of reducing sugars compared with amino compounds, creating a need for natural sugar sources to promote the reaction without relying on refined sugars. Among the natural sugar sources, banana peel was selected as an abundant fruit by-product with potential as a natural ingredient and a source of reducing sugars (Rebello et al., 2014). In our previous study, the addition of banana peel extract (0.5–1.5%) to beef patties enhanced Maillard reaction progression and suggested a potential improvement in saltiness perception, with 0.5% being the lowest tested concentration showing these effects (Park et al., 2025). However, while our previous findings supported the potential of banana peel extract as a natural flavor enhancer, its feasibility as a salt-reduction alternative in real meat matrices remains to be validated. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether banana peel extract can function as a salt-reduction alternative in beef patties while maintaining overall product quality.

Materials and methods

Preparation of banana peel extract

Banana peel extract was prepared as described previously (Park et al., 2025). Commercial Cavendish (Musa acuminata) bananas imported from Vietnam and the Philippines were selected at ripening stage 5. To minimize potential batch-to-batch variability in chemical composition, the banana peels were pooled from a large number of individual fruits and thoroughly homogenized to form a representative composite sample. The homogenized peels were washed, cut (2 × 2 × 1 cm3), dried (60 °C, 24 h), and milled (80 mesh). The powder was subjected to alkaline hydrogen peroxide treatment using 2% (w/w) H₂O₂ at pH 11.5 (adjusted with 4 M NaOH) at 50 °C for 3 h, with a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:25 (w/w). The mixture was then filtered, and the supernatant was adjusted to pH 5.5 with 6 N HCl, freeze-dried, and milled. The treated extract was hydrolyzed with 0.5% (w/w) Viscozyme L (Novozymes, Copenhagen, Denmark) in 0.05 M acetate buffer (pH 4.0) at 50 °C for 24 h, followed by centrifugation (2000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C), filtration (Whatman No. 4; Whatman International Ltd., Maidstone, Kent, UK), and freeze-drying to obtain banana peel extract. The overall extraction yield of the final banana peel extract was 59.86 g/100 g dry peel. All compositional data are expressed on a dry matter basis. Banana peel extract yielded glucose (68.68 mg/g), mannose (21.86 mg/g), arabinose (6.04 mg/g), and galactose (1.65 mg/g). Sodium, potassium, and phosphorus contents were 150.42, 46.04, and 1.25 mg/g, respectively. Total phenolic content was 4.14 mg gallic acid equivalents/g. In our previous study, the total reducing sugar content of the extract was 5.8 mg/mL as determined by the DNS method (Park et al., 2026). The reconstituted banana peel extract showed a pH of 4.0.

Preparation of low-salt beef patties

Semimembranosus muscles (approximately 2 kg each) were obtained from three individual cattle imported from Australia and purchased at a local market in Seoul, Republic of Korea. Each carcass was regarded as one independent batch replicate. Pork back fats (approximately 1 kg each), sourced from pigs slaughtered in Pocheon, Republic of Korea, were also purchased at a local market. The muscles and pork back fats were ground using a 6-mm plate prior to treatment. From each batch, four treatment groups were prepared: S (salt; 1.0% NaCl), LS (low-salt; 0.5% NaCl), KS (KCl with low-salt; 0.5% NaCl +0.5% KCl) and ES (extract with low-salt; 0.5% NaCl +0.5% banana peel extract). For each treatment, the patty batter was formulated with a ratio of lean meat:fat:water:garlic powder:onion powder:black pepper of 70:20:7.2:0.5:2.0:0.3 (w/w). All seasoning ingredients were added at identical levels across all treatments and batches. The mixture was homogenized using a food processor for at least 1 min. The resulting mixture (600 g per treatment) was divided into six portions (100 g each) and shaped using a manual patty press to obtain patties with a diameter of 10 cm and a thickness of 1.2 cm. Each patty was wrapped in polyethylene film and stored at −80 °C until analysis. For cooking, the patties were pan-grilled on an electric grill preheated to 180 °C with 5 mL of refined soybean oil (Beksul soybean oil, CJ CheilJedang Co., Incheon, Republic of Korea) for a total of 10 min (5 min per side) until the core temperature reached 72 °C. Both raw and cooked patties were minced prior to subsequent analyses.

Mineral content of low-salt beef patties

Mineral analysis was conducted based on Sulaiman et al. (2011) with minor modifications. Briefly, 1 g of cooked patty sample was accurately weighed into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The sample was digested with 10 mL of concentrated nitric acid until complete mineralization, thereby removing carbonaceous material. After cooling to room temperature, the digest was transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask and brought to volume with distilled water. The solution was then diluted as appropriate for analysis.

K, Na, and P contents were quantified using ICP–OES following Menezes et al. (2018) with slight modifications. The instrumental parameters were set to 1.2 kW RF power, 12 L/min plasma gas flow, 1.0 L/min auxiliary gas flow, and 0.7 L/min nebulizer gas flow, with radial viewing mode at an 8 mm viewing height. Measurements were performed with a 5 s read time and three replicates, using 25 s sample uptake, 15 s stabilization, and 30 s rinse (pump speed: 12 rpm). The selected emission wavelengths were 766.491 nm (K), 589.592 nm (Na), and 213.618 nm (P).

Physicochemical characteristics of low-salt beef patties

Maillard reaction intensity and fluorescence

For absorbance measurements, an aqueous extract of cooked patties was prepared. Cooked patty (4 g) was homogenized with deionized water (16 mL) at 9600 rpm for 30 s (T25 basic, IKA GmbH & Co., Staufen, Germany). The homogenate was centrifuged (2265 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C), and the resulting supernatant was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper (Whatman International Ltd.).

The intermediate and end-products of Maillard reaction were measured by absorbance of 294 nm and 420 nm, respectively. The filtrate was read directly at 420 nm, whereas a 5-fold dilution with deionized water was used for absorbance at 294 nm (Cao et al., 2022). Absorbance was recorded using a spectrophotometer (X-ma 3100, Human Co. Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Early-stage Maillard reaction intermediates were evaluated by fluorescence spectroscopy (Huang et al., 2022). Fluorescence was measured using the undiluted filtrate by fluorescence spectrophotometer (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 347 nm while scanning emission from 380 to 550 nm (Cao et al., 2022).

pH

Raw patty (3 g) was mixed with deionized water (27 mL) and homogenized at 9600 rpm for 30 s using a homogenizer (T25 basic). The homogenate was centrifuged at 2265 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was filtered through a Whatman No. 4 filter (Whatman International Ltd.). The filtrate pH was then measured using a calibrated pH meter (Seven2Go, Mettler Toledo Inc., Switzerland).

Water holding capacity (WHC) and cooking weight loss

Water holding capacity (WHC) of raw patties was measured following Park et al. (2025). Moisture content was determined by oven-drying at 105 °C to constant weight. Raw patties were centrifuged at 2000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and WHC was calculated as the percentage of retained moisture using the equation in Park et al. (2025).

For cooking, the patties were pan-grilled on an electric grill preheated to 180 °C with 5 mL of vegetable oil for a total of 10 min (5 min per side) until the core temperature reached 72 °C. Cooking loss of patties was determined following Parafati et al. (2021) with minor modifications. Patties were weighed before cooking (C1) and after cooking (C2), following cooling to room temperature. Cooking loss was expressed as the percentage of weight loss and calculated using the following equation:Cooking weight loss%=C1−C2C1×100

where C1 is the weight before cooking and C2 is the weight after cooking.

Color measurement

Surface color of raw and cooked patties was assessed using a colorimeter (CM-5, Konica Minolta Censing Inc., Osaka, Japan) after allowing samples to bloom for 30 min. Measurements were conducted with a D65 illuminant, a 10° standard observer, and a 30-mm aperture. Color values were reported in the CIE L*, a*, and b* system. For each treatment, six readings were collected from three patties, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

The ΔE was calculated to compare each treatment (LS, KS, and ES) with the S group. The ΔE value was calculated using the following equation (Mancini et al., 2015):ΔE=Lt∗−Ls∗2+at∗−as∗2+bt∗−bs∗2

where Lt∗,at∗,bt∗ are the CIE values of the treatment group (LS, KS, or ES), and Ls∗,as∗,bs∗are those of the S group.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Texture profile analysis (TPA) of cooked patties was conducted following Jung et al. (2022) with slight modifications. Patty samples were cut into rectangular pieces (20 × 20 × 15 mm) and analyzed using a texture analyzer (TA1, AMETEK Lloyd Instruments Ltd., UK). The test was performed with a 70% double-compression cycle at a test speed of 5 mm/s, a trigger force of 0.05 N, and a 3 s interval between compressions. The pre-test, post-test, and compression speeds were set to 1.0 mm/s, 3.0 mm/s, and 180 mm/min, respectively. Hardness (N), springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess (N), and chewiness (N) were recorded.

Taste attributes of low-salt beef patties

Descriptive sensory evaluation

The experimental design was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University (IRB No. 2306/001–003). A total of thirteen trained panelists (8 men and 5 women; 23–33 years) who had previous experience in descriptive sensory evaluation of meat products participated in the sensory evaluation, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to testing. Prior to evaluation, panelists participated in at least two preliminary training sessions to familiarize themselves with the sensory attributes, reference standards, and use of the intensity scale. Panelists rated the intensity of five attributes (saltiness, umami, bitterness, flavor, and off-flavor) on a 7-point scale (1 = weak, 7 = strong). To standardize scoring, panelists were calibrated in advance using reference solutions for saltiness, umami, and bitterness. Specifically, NaCl solutions of 0.06%, 0.25%, and 1.00% (w/v) represented intensity scores of 1, 4, and 7, respectively; MSG solutions of 0.03%, 0.125%, and 0.50% (w/v) represented scores of 1, 4, and 7; and caffeine solutions of 0.01%, 0.02%, and 0.04% (w/v) represented scores of 1, 4, and 7. Samples were labeled with randomized three-digit codes, and panelists cleansed their palates with water and plain crackers between samples.

Electronic tongue analysis

Electronic tongue analysis was performed using an Astree system (Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France) following Lee et al. (2023). The sensors AHS, CTS, and NMS primarily respond to sourness, saltiness, and umami-related compounds, respectively, whereas ANS, SCS, PKS, and CPS function as cross-selective sensors for overall taste pattern recognition (Zan et al., 2025). Prior to analysis, the sensors underwent autotest, conditioning, and calibration procedures according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cooked patty (5 g) was mixed with 100 mL of deionized water and homogenized at 10,000 rpm for 30 s using a homogenizer (T25 basic). The homogenate was centrifuged at 2265 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. Taste-related responses were recorded with the Astree system and processed in AlphaSoft software (Alpha MOS) for data screening and analysis. Measurements were conducted on extracts from cooked patties, and taste intensities were expressed on a 10-point scale based on sensor responses using the same calculation procedure.

NMR analysis for small molecular compounds of low-salt beef patties

NMR analysis was performed according to the method of Hong et al. (2025) with slight modifications. A 5 g chopped sample was homogenized with 20 mL of 0.6 M HClO₄ at 16,000 rpm for 1 min using a homogenizer (T25 basic). The homogenate was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was adjusted to pH 7.0 ± 0.01 using 1 M KOH. After a second centrifugation under the same conditions, the neutralized supernatant was filtered through a No.4 filter paper (Whatman International Ltd.), freeze-dried.

For NMR spectroscopy, freeze-dried samples were reconstituted in 1 mL of D₂O containing 1 mM TSP and 20 mM phosphate buffer, incubated at 35 °C for 10 min, and centrifuged at 17,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred into a 5 mm NMR tube (Wilmad-LabGlass, NJ, USA). Spectra were recorded at 298 K on a Bruker 850 MHz cryogenic NMR spectrometer using a zg30 pulse sequence (65,000 data points, 128 scans, 17,006.803 Hz spectral width). Chemical shifts were referenced to TSP, and manual baseline correction was applied.

Metabolites were identified and quantified using Chenomx NMR Suite v11, with reference to COSY and HSQC 2D spectra for confirmation. Structural identification was supported by comparison with the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), and concentrations were calculated using the Profiler module with TSP as the internal standard for metabolite quantification.

Statistical analysis

Three independent cattle carcasses were used as biological replicates (n = 3). Replicate measurements were performed according to the requirements of each analytical method. Replicate measurements from the same carcass were averaged, and the carcass mean was considered the experimental unit for statistical analysis. Statistical analysis was carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a linear model in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and group means were compared using Tukey's multiple range test with p < 0.05.

For multivariate analysis, NMR data were analyzed by partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and electronic-tongue sensor outputs were evaluated by principal component analysis (PCA). Both datasets were processed in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 after log transformation and auto-scaling.

Results and discussion

Mineral content of low-salt beef patties

Mineral composition (K, Na, and P) of cooked patties under different sodium-reduction strategies is shown in Table 1. K and P were included because banana peel–derived ingredients are reported to be rich in these minerals, and Na was quantified as the target mineral for sodium reduction (Emaga et al., 2007; Oyeyinka & Afolayan, 2019). Potassium content was highest in KS among the treatments (p < 0.05). Potassium content in ES did not differ from those in S and LS, indicating that the ES formulation did not increase potassium intake relative to the S treatment. The S treatment exhibited the highest sodium content, followed by ES, whereas LS and KS showed significantly lower and comparable sodium contents (p < 0.05). The relatively higher sodium content in ES compared with LS and KS may primarily be attributed to sodium introduced during the neutralization step after extraction (Yang et al., 2019). The relatively higher sodium content in ES compared with LS and KS is directly attributed to sodium introduced during the neutralization step after alkaline extraction. The banana peel extract itself contained 150.42 mg/g sodium, which contributed an additional 75.2 mg of sodium per 100 g patty batter under the 0.5% addition level. Consequently, the actual degree of sodium reduction relative to S was approximately 22% for ES, whereas LS and KS achieved reductions of approximately 45%. Although the absolute sodium reduction in ES was lower than that of the other low-salt groups, ES represents a superior compromise by compensating for the quality defects of LS (such as reduced WHC and taste intensity) and avoiding the severe bitterness of KS. Phosphorus content did not differ among treatments, suggesting that the tested sodium-reduction strategies did not materially affect phosphorus levels in cooked patties. Partial replacement of NaCl with KCl is widely used to reduce sodium content in meat products; however, this approach inevitably increases potassium intake. Excessive potassium consumption is not recommended for patients with hyperkalemia or those at risk of hyperkalemia, such as individuals with chronic kidney disease (AlSahow, 2023). In this context, the KS treatment showed a significantly elevated potassium level, which may limit its applicability to potassium-restricted populations. By contrast, ES maintained potassium levels comparable to those of the S and LS treatments, indicating a more balanced mineral profile and potential applicability for consumers requiring potassium intake management. Nevertheless, because the sodium reduction efficiency in ES was limited by process-derived sodium, alternative extraction boundaries must be established. Physicochemical properties of low-salt beef patties

Maillard reaction intensity

The Maillard reaction intensity of cooked beef patties prepared with different sodium-reduction strategies was evaluated using absorbance and fluorescence measurements (Fig. 1). Based on our previous observation that the banana peel extract enhanced saltiness/umami together with increased Maillard reaction markers in beef patties (Park et al., 2025), we hypothesized that the extract could partially offset palatability loss under sodium reduction by promoting Maillard reaction–derived taste-active compounds during cooking. Accordingly, we assessed Maillard reaction indices (absorbance and fluorescence) in reduced‑sodium beef patties formulated with the extract to determine whether Maillard-related changes accompany taste retention. Absorbance at 294 nm reflects the formation of early/intermediate Maillard reaction products, whereas absorbance at 420 nm indicates the accumulation of brown, high-molecular-weight polymers in later stages (Hemmler et al., 2018). At 294 nm, ES was the only treatment that differed from LS (p < 0.05) and showed the highest absorbance among the treatments (Fig. 1A). In contrast, ES exhibited absorbance values comparable to those of S and KS. At 420 nm, absorbance was significantly lower in LS than in the other treatments, which did not differ significantly from each other (p < 0.05; Fig. 1B). Salt has been reported to influence the Maillard reaction, with its effect varying across food systems and salt levels. While high NaCl levels (≥ 5%) are often associated with suppression of Maillard browning, low levels (e.g., 0.5%) have been reported to promote Maillard reaction markers under certain model conditions (Liang et al., 2019). In meat systems, salt is also widely recognized to accelerate lipid oxidation (Mariutti & Bragagnolo, 2017). Because lipid oxidation is a major source of aroma-active volatiles in cooked meat, changes in salt-driven oxidation can directly influence the volatile profile and overall flavor quality (Domínguez et al., 2019). Reactive carbonyls generated during lipid oxidation, such as glyoxal and methylglyoxal, can also arise from Maillard chemistry and may participate in Maillard/Strecker type pathways (Zamora & Hidalgo, 2005). Therefore, the lower Maillard indices observed in LS may, at least in part, reflect reduced salt induced lipid oxidation and a consequent decrease in the availability of lipid-derived carbonyl intermediates that could otherwise contribute to Maillard reaction progression. Notably, ES showed A294 values comparable to those of S and KS, despite containing less NaCl than S. Fluorescence spectra further supported these trends. Across the 400–500 nm region, ES exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity, except in the 410–420 nm range, where S showed comparable or slightly higher intensity. Overall, the fluorescence intensity followed the order ES > S > KS = LS. Especially, the emission band around 400–450 nm is commonly used as an index of fluorescent Maillard reaction intermediates/advanced glycation–type fluorophores in food systems (Lertittikul et al., 2007). Notably, fluorescence in this region may not exclusively originate from Maillard reaction products, because intrinsic constituents in banana peel extract—such as riboflavin (around 460 nm) and polyphenol-related fluorophores spanning approximately 400–500 nm—can also contribute to the signal (Radotić et al., 2023). Nevertheless, absorbance-based Maillard reaction indices (A294 and A420), which are not affected by direct fluorescence contribution from the extract, also showed recovery of Maillard reaction markers in ES relative to LS. However, because the specific contribution of intrinsic extract-derived fluorophores to the fluorescence signal could not be quantitatively determined in the present study, further studies evaluating the fluorescence characteristics of the extract itself are warranted.

Taken together, although the fluorescence response in ES may have been partially influenced by intrinsic fluorophores present in the banana peel extract, the absorbance-based Maillard reaction indices suggest that Maillard reaction progression in ES was potentially enhanced relative to LS and maintained at a level comparable to S and KS under sodium-reduced conditions.

pH, WHC, and cooking loss

The effects of sodium-reduction strategies on pH, water-holding capacity (WHC), and cooking loss are presented in Table 2. The pH of raw patties ranged from 5.58 to 5.64, which is within the typical pH range of 5.5–6.5 reported for meat products (Saguer et al., 2007). S and KS showed higher pH values, whereas ES exhibited the lowest pH (p < 0.05). This decrease is consistent with the relatively low pH of banana peel extract, previously reported to be approximately pH 4.0 (Park et al., 2025). Similar trends were also observed in cooked patties, with ES showing the lowest cooked pH among treatments (p < 0.05). Although the pH differences among treatments were relatively small, lower pH conditions are generally considered less favorable for Maillard reaction progression (Ajandouz et al., 2001; Shengbu et al., 2024). WHC of raw patties was lowest in LS (p < 0.05), while S, KS, and ES showed similar WHC values. This reduction in WHC in LS is a well-recognized drawback of reduced‑sodium meat products, as lowering NaCl can reduce myofibrillar protein solubilization and weaken water binding in the meat matrix (Ruusunen & Puolanne, 2005). In this context, the comparable WHC observed in KS and ES relative to S represents a positive outcome, indicating that these formulations improved WHC compared with LS while maintaining a level similar to the full-salt control. Although the difference was not significant, ES exhibited the highest WHC among the treatments. This trend may be associated with constituents in banana peel extract and their functionality in the meat matrix; reducing sugars and polysaccharides/pectic substances can improve WHC by promoting myosin solubility and reinforcing protein gel networks, thereby alleviating a key quality deterioration typically observed in low-salt meat products. (Park et al., 2025).

Cooking loss did not differ among treatments, although ES showed the lowest cooking loss. Considering the limited number of biological replicates used in the present study, subtle treatment-related differences may not have been fully detected statistically. This trend is consistent with the relatively higher WHC observed in ES, because improved water retention in the raw matrix can reduce moisture and fat exudation during heating. Protein–carbohydrate conjugates have been reported to enhance water-holding and emulsion stability in meat systems, which can translate into reduced cooking loss (Kim et al., 2024). Thus, the relatively higher Maillard reaction indices observed in ES may be associated with the formation of protein–carbohydrate conjugates and related Maillard-derived structures that could contribute to water and fat retention during cooking.

Overall, sodium-reduction strategies using salt replacers and/or banana peel extract improved WHC relative to LS, whereas cooking loss showed comparable values among treatments.

Overall, the color of raw patty was similar across treatments, except for the lower a* value observed in ES, indicating reduced redness compared with S (Table 3). After cooking, ES showed a higher L* with lower a* and b* values than the other treatments (p < 0.05). Lightness (L*) in cooked meat is influenced not only by the formation of browning pigments during heating but also by moisture distribution and microstructure-driven light scattering (Hughes et al., 2014). Accordingly, the higher L* observed in ES may be associated with its higher WHC and lower cooking loss trend, which can contribute to a brighter appearance and reduced color intensity. The a* value after cooking was lowest in ES, consistent with the lower a* value observed in the raw state. This pattern may indicate greater heat-induced changes in pigment status, such as enhanced myoglobin denaturation and associated shifts in pigment chemistry. Conseqently, ES showed the greatest ΔE relative to S, whereas color differences between LS and KS were relatively small.

This discrepancy between instrumental color parameters and Maillard reaction indices may be attributed to the combined effects of reaction staging and matrix optics. Chemically, early-to-intermediate Maillard reaction intermediates detected via fluorescence do not directly correspond to visible brown melanoidin pigments, as fluorescence accumulation often precedes visual browning cascades (El Hosry et al., 2025). Physically, the lower a* and b* values accompanied by a higher L* in cooked ES are closely linked to its superior WHC and moisture retention profile. Lightness and chromatic coordinates in cooked meat are strongly dictated by matrix moisture distribution and microstructure-driven light scattering (Hughes et al., 2014). The high water retention in ES alters surface light reflection, which can mathematically lower instrumental a* and b* readouts. However, as confirmed by visual inspection (Fig. 2B), no noticeable differences in surface color were observed among the cooked patties, because heterogeneous surface browning and the dominance of localized Maillard-derived brown pigments effectively masked these instrumental variations.

Visual inspection of raw and cooked patties revealed that raw ES exhibited a slightly more grayish tone than the other treatments; however, no noticeable differences in surface color were observed among the cooked patties (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, ES showed the highest ΔE values relative to the S group in both raw and cooked patties, with the cooked ES exhibiting a ΔE value of 10.17. According to Mokrzycki and Tatol (2011), ΔE values greater than 5 are generally considered visually distinguishable as two different colors. However, instrumental color differences in meat products do not always directly correspond to visual perception because meat surfaces exhibit heterogeneous scattering and reflectance characteristics (Trinderup et al., 2015). Furthermore, the uneven progress of heat-induced Maillard browning and the extensive localized accumulation of brown pigments across the grilled crust effectively masked the mathematical colorimetric differences detected by the instrument aperture. Therefore, despite the high calculated ΔE value, the structural and chemical complexity of the cooked surface prevented any distinct or practical visual rejection during human inspection (Fig. 2B).

Along Component 2, KS was shifted toward the negative direction, while S and LS were distributed in the positive direction, and ES spanned both positive and negative values around zero. Overall, all four treatments were clearly separated in the 3D score space, indicating distinct taste-response profiles among treatments.

Sensory evaluation

Salt not only provides saltiness but also enhances the characteristic flavor and overall eating quality of meat products; therefore, sensory evaluation is particularly important when developing sodium-reduced formulations (Kim et al., 2021; Ruusunen & Puolanne, 2005). In addition, seasoning ingredients may have contributed to the baseline flavor profile and Maillard reaction precursors of the patties, although identical amounts were used across all treatments (Cardelle-Cobas et al., 2005; Mottram, 1998). Sensory attribute intensities of beef patties prepared under different sodium-reduction strategies are shown in Fig. 4. Saltiness was rated highest in S and lowest in LS (p < 0.05). Although ES exhibited lower perceived saltiness than S (p < 0.05), no significant difference was observed between ES and KS. This pattern was consistent with the electronic tongue results for the saltiness related sensor (CTS). For bitterness, S and KS received higher scores, whereas LS and ES were rated lower (p < 0.05). This pattern was consistent with the electronic tongue results for the bitterness-related sensor (SCS). Because KCl can impart a metallic and bitter taste, the higher bitterness intensity observed in KS may be attributable to this characteristic off-taste (Wang et al., 2023). Umami ratings did not differ among treatments. In line with this, the electronic tongue showed only modest separation in the umami-related sensor (NMS), suggesting that small sensor differences may not necessarily translate into perceivable differences in human evaluation. Overall flavor and off-flavor also did not differ among treatments. At the compositional level, total free amino acids were highest in S (p < 0.05). Salt is known to promote gelation and emulsification of myofibrillar proteins by increasing ionic strength, thereby enhancing the retention of water-soluble compounds during heating (Aprilia & Kim, 2022). Accordingly, the higher salt level in S may have reduced the loss of free amino acids during cooking, resulting in the highest total free amino acid content in the cooked patties.

Total organic acids were highest in ES, whereas LS and KS showed the lowest levels (p < 0.05). This pattern may reflect multiple contributing factors. Organic acids are naturally abundant in plant tissues and extracts, and thus organic acids inherently present in banana peel extract may have contributed to the elevated organic acid pool in ES. In addition, thermal reactions such as the Maillard reaction can generate low-molecular-weight organic acids (e.g., formic and acetic acids) during heating (Cardoso et al., 2023). However, Alvarez-Vasco and Zhang (2013) reported that alkaline hydrogen peroxide pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass can generate considerable amounts of low-molecular-weight carboxylic acids, including formic acid, lactic acid, glycolic acid, and succinic acid. Therefore, the markedly elevated formate level observed in ES was likely influenced substantially by intrinsic extract-derived compounds and/or degradation products generated during the extraction process rather than exclusively by thermal reactions during cooking. Given that organic acids have been reported to enhance saltiness perception (Bi et al., 2024), the saltiness effect observed in ES may have been influenced by organic acids originating from the plant extract and/or formed during thermal reactions. However, the free amino acid and organic acid profiles of the raw extract were not analyzed in this study, which remains a limitation in completely differentiating intrinsic constituents from thermally generated metabolites. Therefore, further studies characterizing the extract itself will be necessary to clarify the origin of formate and other organic acids in ES.

Total sugars were lowest in S (p < 0.05). In ES, the presence of banana peel extract may have contributed additional sugars, thereby increasing the residual sugar pool. Because all treatments except ES were formulated with comparable initial sugar levels, the lowest total sugar observed in S likely reflects greater sugar utilization through thermal reactions such as the Maillard reaction rather than differences in sugar leaching. Notably, S showed relatively high fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1) along with the second-highest total organic acid level after ES, which is consistent with a great conversion of sugar into downstream reaction products in S compared with LS and KS.

Among individual metabolites, 4-aminobutyrate was highest in ES (p < 0.05). This non-protein amino acid, commonly referred to as GABA, has been reported to accumulate in banana peel and to exert blood pressure–lowering effects (Ali et al., 2022; Ramos-Ruiz et al., 2018). Therefore, the elevated 4-aminobutyrate in ES is likely attributable to the contribution from banana peel extract. Sarcosine was highest in S (p < 0.05); this amino-acid derivative that has been described as sweet-tasting (Zhang et al., 2021). Arginine was highest in S and LS (p < 0.05). Arginine is known as a bitter-tasting amino acid (Melis & Tomassini Barbarossa, 2017). AMP was highest in ES (p < 0.05). Because AMP can contribute to umami synergy, and umami perception has been reported to enhance saltiness (Sun et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2016), elevated AMP levels may partly explain the maintained saltiness perception observed in ES. Notably, formate in ES was more than seven-fold higher than in the other treatments (p < 0.05). As discussed above, the markedly elevated formate level observed in ES was likely influenced substantially by extract-derived compounds and/or degradation products generated during the extraction process. Because sour–bitter interactions are concentration- and matrix-dependent, sourness can suppress bitterness in taste mixtures (Chen & Amrein, 2014). Accordingly, the elevated organic acid content in ES may have contributed to the reduced bitterness perception observed in sensory evaluation, although this effect likely depends on the specific acid composition and its relative intensity within the overall taste matrix.

Conclusion

This study evaluated banana peel extract as a clean-label approach for moderate sodium reduction in beef patties by comparing conventional salt, low-salt formulation, potassium chloride substitution, and banana peel extract addition under identical cooking conditions. Compared with the salt control, LS and KS achieved greater sodium reduction, whereas ES provided a more moderate sodium reduction while avoiding the potassium increase associated with potassium chloride substitution. Banana peel extract also maintained key quality traits, improved saltiness perception relative to the low-salt treatment, and exhibited lower bitterness than potassium chloride substitution. Instrumental analyses showed corresponding changes in taste-related chemical profiles that were generally consistent with the sensory observations. Overall, these findings indicate that banana peel extract can serve as a by-product-derived, clean-label strategy to support moderate sodium reduction in meat products without compromising product quality, while maintaining a more balanced mineral profile than potassium chloride substitution. Nevertheless, because an independent extract-only control was omitted to prioritize realistic low-salt manufacturing conditions, further studies utilizing isolated model systems are warranted to definitively clarify the discrete, unconfounded relative contributions of Maillard reaction–related changes versus the intrinsic taste-active compounds in the extract, including organic acids, sugars, and GABA.
