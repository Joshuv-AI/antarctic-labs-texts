Sequential Monte Carlo approximations of Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao gradient flows
Francesca Romana Crucinio, Sahani Pathiraja

Abstract
We consider the problem of sampling from a probability distribution π \pi . It is well known that this can be written as an optimisation problem over the space of probability distribution in which we aim to minimise the Kullback–Leibler divergence from π \pi . We consider several partial differential equations (PDEs) whose solution is a minimiser of the Kullback–Leibler divergence from π \pi and connect them to well-known Monte Carlo algorithms. We focus in particular on PDEs obtained by considering the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao geometry over the space of probabilities and show that these lead to a natural implementation using importance sampling and sequential Monte Carlo. We propose a novel algorithm to approximate the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao flow of the Kullback–Leibler divergence which empirically outperforms the current state-of-the-art. We study tempered versions of these PDEs obtained by replacing the target distribution with a geometric mixture of initial and target distribution and show that these do not lead to a convergence speed up.

Introduction

Sampling from a target probability distribution whose density is known up to a normalisation constant is a fundamental task in computational statistics and machine learning. A natural way to formulate this task is optimisation of a functional measuring the dissimilarity to the target probability distribution, typically the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence.

Following this point of view, one can derive many popular sampling frameworks including variational inference
[
BKM17
]
, algorithms based on the Langevin dynamics
[
RS02
,
DMM19
]
and on kernelised Langevin dynamics
[
LW16
]
.
When the space over which the optimisation is carried out is the Wasserstein space, i.e. probability distributions with bounded second moments equipped with the Wasserstein-2 distance
[
AGS08
]
, the resulting gradient descent scheme is referred to as Wasserstein (W) gradient flow. It is well-known that the W gradient flow of the KL can be implemented by a Langevin diffusion
[
JKO98
,
Wib18
]
and easily discretised in time, resulting for instance in the Unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA;
[
RT96
,
DMM19
]
) and in the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA;
[
RS02
]
) when a Metropolis–Hastings accept reject step is added after each discretisation step.
Convergence of the W gradient flow and of the Unadjusted Langevin algorithm is guaranteed when the target distribution is sufficiently smooth and verifies a log-Sobolev assumption
[
VW19
]
.
Alternative time discretisations of the KL Wasserstein gradient flow
[
SKL20
,
MMW+21
]
or its gradient flow with respect to similar optimal transport geometries have been considered in the literature to propose alternative algorithms

[
LW16
,
GIHLS20
]
, but their convergence also depends strongly on the log-concavity of the target.

A second class of gradient flows which has gained popularity in recent years is that of Fisher–Rao (FR) gradient flows, i.e. gradient descent w.r.t. the Fisher–Rao or Hellinger–Kakutani distance.
Contrary to the Wasserstein matric, which leads to a diffusive behaviour, the Fisher–Rao metric leads to birth-death dynamics in which mass is created or removed. FR gradient flows perform natural gradient descent (see, e.g.,
[
Nüs24
, Appendix D]
and
[
FA95
,
HS98
,
Har09a
]
).

Exponential convergence of the FR gradient flow for KL does not require a log-Sobolev assumption and can be established under a bounded second moment assumption and mild regularity of the ratio between target and initial distribution
[
CHH+23b
, Theorem 4.1]
, and therefore holds for a wider class of targets. The FR gradient flow for KL is intimately tied to tempering or annealing due to its unit time rescaling
[
DEP23
,
CHH+24
]
and can also be connected to a mirror descent optimisation scheme for KL
[
CCK24
]
.
While superior in terms of theoretical behaviour, the FR gradient flow does not naturally lead to stable algorithms, in fact, as noted in
[
CHH+24
]
, pure FR dynamics cannot change the support of the distribution, so additional steps need to be added to explore the space. These steps can change the dynamics and can become problematic in high dimensions.

One option to address this shortcoming is offered by kernel methods
[
Nüs24
,
MM24
,
WN24
]
which naturally introduce a diffusive part into the (unit time) FR dynamics.
A second option, and the main focus of this paper, is to combine the W and the FR gradient flows by making use of the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao metric
[
GM17
]
leading to the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (WFR) gradient flow.
The WFR flow combines the diffusive behaviour of the W gradient flow with the FR flow and enjoys more favourable convergence properties, combining the rate of convergence of the W flow with that of the FR flow
[
DEP23
,
LLN19
]
; its numerical approximation can considerably speed up the convergence of gradient based methods for multimodal targets
[
LLN19
,
LSW23
]
and has been successfully employed to learn Gaussian mixtures
[
YWR24
]
.
Despite its favourable convergence properties, the WFR flow of the KL has remained relatively understudied compared to the W and the FR flow. This is likely due to the difficulty of obtaining stable numerical approximations for the WFR flow. To the best of our knowledge, the only numerical approximations of the WFR flow for the reverse KL are given by the birth-death Langevin algorithms developed in
[
LLN19
,
LSW23
]
.

In this paper we review the main gradient flows used for sampling with particular focus on the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao flow due to its superior convergence properties (Section
2
).
We propose an algorithm to approximate the WFR flow of the KL divergence which combines the diffusive behaviour of Langevin based algorithms with importance sampling (Section
3
) and empirically show its superior performance w.r.t. the birth-death Langevin dynamics of
[
LLN19
,
LSW23
]
.

We further explore the connections between the FR gradient flow, importance sampling and sequential Monte Carlo samplers (SMC;
[
dMDJ06
]
) and identify in the latter a general framework to approximate different FR flows. While connections between sequential Bayesian inference and FR flows have been explored in the discrete time and discrete space setting
[
DM97
,
Sha09
,
Har09b
,
Aky17
,
CGTS22
]
and more recently in continuous time and continuous space
[
PW24
]
; our focus here is on the sampling problem, in which no underlying sequential structure is present. We also propose a new method whereby importance sampling is used for the FR part.

To further explore the connection with SMC we consider also
tempered
versions of the W, FR and WFR PDEs obtained by considering a time varying target
\pi_{t}\propto\pi^{\lambda_{t}}\mu_{0}^{1-\lambda_{t}}
for
\lambda_{t}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
. We explore their gradient flow structure and show that these tempered PDEs do not improve the continuous time convergence rate of their non-tempered counterparts as shown in
[
CKSV25
]
for the W flow.

To summarise, the main contributions of this work are

•

We show that in the continuous time setting, tempering the target
\pi
does not improve convergence speed for the Wasserstein and the Fisher–Rao flow (Proposition
2.1
and
2.2
).

•

We propose stable algorithms to approximate the WFR flow which combine the diffusive behaviour of Langevin samplers and importance sampling (Algorithm
1
) and establish their convergence properties (Proposition
3.1
and
3.2
). We empirically show that they outperform the current approximations of the WFR flow. We then show that these algorithms are a particular instance of sequential Monte Carlo samplers.

•

We provide an optimisation point of view on sequential Monte Carlo samplers, showing that they provide numerical approximations of many FR type gradient flows of the Kullback–Leibler divergence. We thus establish a parallel result to that of
[
JKO98
,
Wib18
]
which shows that algorithms based on the Langevin diffusion can be seen as numerical approximations of the Wasserstein gradient flow.

Notation

We define some notation that will be used throughout the manuscript.
We endow
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to
the Euclidean norm

\|\cdot\|
when
d
is clear from context. We denote by
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of continuously differentiable functions,
for all
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we denote the gradient by
\nabla f
. Furthermore, if
f
is twice differentiable we
denote by
\nabla^{2}f
its Hessian and by
\Delta f
its Laplacian.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of probability measures over
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which admit a density w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure.
The Kullback–Leibler divergence is defined as follows: for
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu||\mu)=\int\log(\nicefrac{{\nu(x)}}{{\mu(x)}})\nu(x)dx
.
Throughout this manuscript we denote the target by
\pi
and assume
\pi\propto\exp(-V)
for some
V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The initial distribution of each flow is denoted by
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{t}
is the density at time
t
, with unnormalised density
\rho_{t}
.

2
Gradient flow PDEs for sampling

Let us denote the target distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\pi
and assume that it admits a density w.r.t. the Lebsegue measure also denoted by
\pi
, then a natural way to recover
\pi
is to consider the optimisation problem

\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi),

which can be solved by following the direction of steepest descent of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot||\pi)
, given by the gradient flow partial differential equation (PDE)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{m}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi),

where
\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{m}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
denotes the gradient of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot||\pi)
w.r.t. metric
\mathtt{m}

[
AGS08
,
CCH+24
]
.
Depending on the choice of metric
\mathtt{m}
, the gradient
\textrm{grad}_{\mathtt{m}}\mathcal{F}(\mu)
takes different shapes;
we primarily focus on Wasserstein (W), Fisher–Rao (FR) and Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (WFR) flows of the Kullback–Leibler divergence in infinite time.

The gradient flow of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
w.r.t. the geometry induced by the Wasserstein distance is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty)

(2.1)

and corresponds to the Fokker-Planck equation of a Langevin diffusion targeting
\pi

[
JKO98
]
. Convergence of the W gradient flow has been widely studied
[
DMM19
,
VW19
]
and is guaranteed whenever
\pi
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality (e.g.,
[
CEL+24
]
).

The Fisher–Rao (FR) gradient flow of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
is obtained by considering the Fisher–Rao (also known as Hellinger) metric
[
LLN19
]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)=\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty),

(2.2)

and is closely tied to mirror descent
[
CCK24
,
DEP23
]
and birth-death
[
LLN19
,
LSW23
]
dynamics.

Combining the two geometries one obtains the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (WFR) gradient flow
[
LLN19
, Theorem 3.1]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty),

(2.3)

where the addition of the two components is justified because each is
\pi
-invariant. As we will see later, from a sampling point of view the W flow diffuses the samples “horizontally” and promotes exploration of the space while the FR flows “vertically” weights the samples according to their closeness to the target
\pi
. Intuitively, the combination of these two strategies will improve on each one of them.

Fisher–Rao gradient flows are easily rescaled to the unit time interval as shown in
[
DEP23
]
and in Lemma
A.2
in a slightly more condensed form.
The unit time FR PDE takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)=\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{0}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}\right)\right]\right)\quad t\in[0,1]

(2.4)

and is the basis of recently developed kernel-based algorithms
[
MM24
,
Nüs24
]
.
This PDE is not a gradient flow for
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
but minimises the negative log-likelihood

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{1}(\mu)=\int\log\left(\frac{\mu_{0}(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)\mu(x)dx

(2.5)

w.r.t. the Fisher–Rao distance
[
PW24
, Lemma 2.1]
. This functional is not lower bounded and thus the corresponding minimisation problem is not well-posed.
However, at time
t=1
the solution of the unit-time FR flow coincides with
\pi
.

The unit time FR flow is intimately tied to geometric interpolations or annealing
[
CCK24
,
DEP23
]
which are at the basis of sequential Monte Carlo (SMC;
[
dMDJ06
]
), annealed importance sampling (AIS;
[
Nea01
]
), parallel tempering (PT;
[
Gey91
]
) and the homotopy method
[
DH08
]
.
This can be seen assuming that the unnormalised sequence of densities is constructed via a geometric interpolation
\rho_{t}=\pi^{t}\mu_{0}^{1-t}=e^{t\log\pi}e^{(1-t)\log\mu_{0}}
for
t\in[0,1]
.
Differentiating
\rho_{t}
with respect to time and applying Lemma
A.1
with
f(x)=\log\pi(x)-\log\mu_{0}(x)
yields the unit time FR PDE (
2.4
).

2.1
Convergence properties

We briefly review the convergence properties of the WFR flow to showcase its superior theoretical properties compared to the FR and the W flow.
The infinite-time FR flow (
2.2
) minimises
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
w.r.t. the Fisher–Rao distance (e.g.,
[
PW24
, Lemma 2.1]
); a similar results holds for the W flow (
2.1
) w.r.t. the Wasserstein-2 distance under a log-Sobolev assumption on the target
\pi
(e.g.,
[
CEL+24
]
):

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)\leq\frac{C_{\textrm{LSI}}}{2}\int\mu\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu}{\pi}\|^{2}

(2.6)

where
C_{\textrm{LSI}}
denotes the log-Sobolev constant of
\pi
.
The convergence of (
2.2
) to
\pi
has been investigated in
[
LLN19
,
LSW23
,
DEP23
,
CHH+23b
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the most general convergence result is given in
[
CHH+23b
, Theorem 4.1]
and requires that
\mu_{0},\pi
have bounded second moments
\int\|x\|^{2}\mu_{0}(x)dx\leq B
,
\int\|x\|^{2}\pi(x)dx\leq B

and

\displaystyle e^{-M(1+\|x\|^{2})}\leq\frac{\mu_{0}(x)}{\pi(x)}\leq e^{M(1+\|x\|^{2})}

(2.7)

for some
M>0
.
Under these assumptions, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)\leq M(2+B+Be^{M(1+B)e^{-t}})e^{-t}.

(2.8)

We point out that this rate is not sharp in general, as can be seen by setting
t=0
.
To obtain the rate of decay of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
for the WFR flow we consider the time derivative of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)
along the WFR flow

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle=\int\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\int\mu_{t}\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\|^{2}-\int\mu_{t}\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi_{t}}\right)^{2}+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)^{2}.

(2.9)

As observed in
[
GM17
, page 13]
, the first term corresponds to the negative gradient of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
w.r.t. the Wasserstein-2 metric, while the remaining terms give the negative gradient of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
w.r.t. the Fisher–Rao geometry, implying that the dissipation for the WFR metric is the sum of the W and the FR dissipation.
As a consequence, the convergence rate of WFR gradient flow (
2.3
) can be upper bounded by

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)\leq\min\left\{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{FR}}|\pi),D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{W}}|\pi)\right\},

where
\mu_{t}^{\textrm{FR}},\mu_{t}^{\textrm{W}}
denote the solutions of the FR and W flow PDEs respectively.
Using (
2.8
) and known results for the convergence of the W flow (e.g.,
[
CEL+24
]
) we find that

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}|\pi)\leq\min\left\{M(2+B+Be^{M(1+B)e^{-t}})e^{-t},D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{0}|\pi)e^{-2C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}t}\right\},

(2.10)

where
C_{\textrm{LSI}}
denotes the log-Sobolev constant of
\pi
.

Under stronger assumptions on the ratio
\mu_{0}/\pi
and a warm-start condition detailed in Appendix
B.1
,
[
LLN19
]
obtain a tighter convergence rate for all
t\geq t_{0}:=\log(M/\delta^{3})
and
\delta>0
:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\left(2C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}+(2-3\delta)\right)(t-t_{0})}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{0}||\pi).

(2.11)

This result obtained in
[
LLN19
, Appendix B]
confirms the rate conjectured by
[
DEP23
]
based on their numerical experiments and shows that combining the W flow and the FR flow leads to a considerable speed up.

We point out that neither (
2.10
) nor (
2.11
) provide a sharp rate of decay for
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)
. In fact, (
2.10
) shows that the rate of WFR is never worse than that of W or FR, looking at (
2.9
) however it is evident that the rate of decay of the WFR flow should be considerably higher than that of the two singular flows. Similarly, (
2.11
) shows that the decay in the case of the WFR flow is faster than that of the W flow but the rate depends on an arbitrary constant
\delta
which makes (
2.11
) harder to interpret.

2.2
Tempered PDEs for sampling

We consider a second class of PDEs in which the target
\pi
is replaced by a sequence of moving targets
(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. These samplers have become popular in the literature with the hope that the introduction of moving targets will improve the convergence of the sampler
[
NVPB24
,
AVE24
,
CKSV25
,
GTC25
]
.

We focus here on the sequence
(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by the geometric tempering iteration
\pi_{t}\propto\pi^{\lambda_{t}}\mu_{0}^{1-\lambda_{t}}
where the tempering schedule
\lambda_{t}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
,
\lambda_{t}
is non-decreasing in
t
and weakly differentiable. This corresponds to the solution of the unit time FR flow (
2.4
) and has been popularised in the sampling literature through its use in sequential Monte Carlo (SMC;
[
dMDJ06
]
), annealed importance sampling (AIS;
[
Nea01
]
), parallel tempering (PT;
[
Gey91
]
) and the homotopy method
[
DH08
]
.

Recently,
[
CKSV25
]
proposed tempered unadjusted Langevin algorithms as an approximation of the FR flow in unit time when
\mu_{0},\pi
are continuously differentiable. The corresponding PDE is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi_{t}(x)}\right)\right)\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(2.12)

This PDE is not
a gradient flow of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
and thus decay of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
along the flow needs to be checked directly.

Tempered unadjusted Langevin algorithms (Tempered ULA;
[
CKSV25
]
) provide an approximation of the tempered PDE (
2.12
) by using an Euler discretisation of the associated SDE

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\log\pi_{t}(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}.

(2.13)

[
CKSV25
, Corollary 5 and 6]
show that if
V_{\pi}
is strongly log-convex with constant
c_{\pi}
and similarly for
V_{\mu_{0}}
, and
c_{\pi}\geq c_{\mu_{0}}/2
then
\lambda_{t}\equiv 1
is optimal and the tempered W PDE does not improve on the standard W flow (
2.1
).

Given the tempering sequence
\pi_{t}
, one can also define the tempered FR PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]\right).

(2.14)

Similarly to (
2.12
), the PDE (
2.14
) is not a gradient flow of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
. As we show later, (
2.12
) reduces
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
at a rate which is always worse than that of the standard FR PDE (
2.2
).

Given the tempered W and tempered FR PDE one can naturally define a tempered WFR PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi_{t}(x)}\right)\right)+\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]\right).

(2.15)

As we show in the following sections, a particular discretisation of this PDE coincides with the commonly used tempering approaches in sequential Monte Carlo samplers
[
dMDJ06
]
. Appendix
B.1.5
establishes that the tempered PDEs can be seen as gradient flows of a functional
\mathcal{F}
defined on the product space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,1]
.

We now analyse the decay of the Kullback–Leibler along the different tempered PDEs and compare it with their standard non-tempered counterparts.
In order to obtain our results and allow comparison between the tempered PDEs (
2.12
) and (
2.14
) and their standard counterpart (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) we make the following assumptions on
\pi,\mu_{0}
.
These assumptions are strong but somewhat classical in the study of convergence of Langevin based samplers (e.g.,
[
DMM19
,
CKSV25
]
).

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\pi\propto e^{-V_{\pi}}
with
V_{\pi}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(a)

there exists
L_{\pi}>0
such that
\|\nabla V_{\pi}(x)-\nabla V_{\pi}(x^{\prime})\|\leq L_{\pi}\|x-x^{\prime}\|
;

(b)

there exists
c_{\pi}>0
such that
\left\langle x-x^{\prime},\nabla V_{\pi}(x)-\nabla V_{\pi}(x^{\prime})\right\rangle\geq c_{\pi}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that
\mu_{0}\propto e^{-V_{\mu_{0}}}
with
V_{\mu_{0}}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(a)

there exists
L_{\mu_{0}}>0
such that
\|\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}(x)-\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}(x^{\prime})\|\leq L_{0}\|x-x^{\prime}\|
;

(b)

there exists
c_{\mu_{0}}>0
such that
\left\langle x-x^{\prime},\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}(x)-\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}(x^{\prime})\right\rangle\geq c_{\mu_{0}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}
.

Assumption
1
-
(b)
implies that
\pi
satisfies (
2.6
) with
C_{\textrm{LSI}}=c_{\pi}^{-1}
.
Assumption
2
combined with Assumption
1
implies that
\nabla V_{\pi_{t}}:=(1-\lambda_{t})\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}+\lambda_{t}\nabla V_{\pi}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L_{\pi_{t}}=(1-\lambda_{t})L_{\mu_{0}}+\lambda_{t}L_{\pi}
and strongly convex with constant
c_{\pi_{t}}\geq(1-\lambda_{t})c_{\mu_{0}}+\lambda_{t}c_{\pi}
.

Under Assumption
1
and
2
we can obtain a result similar to
[
CKSV25
, Theorem 1]
(see Appendix
B.1
for a proof). Our result carries the same message as
[
CKSV25
, Theorem 1]
but allows for an easier comparison between tempered and standard PDEs.

Proposition 2.1

(Tempered W)
.

Under Assumption
1
and
2
, the tempered W PDE (
2.12
) reduces the Kullback–Leibler divergence at rate

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2tC_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{0}||\pi)+A\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2(t-s)C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}}(1-\lambda_{s})ds,

(2.16)

where
C_{\textrm{LSI}}=c_{\pi}^{-1}
,
A<\infty
is a constant which only depends on the convexity and Lipschitz properties of
V_{\pi},V_{\mu_{0}}
.
The convergence rate of the standard W PDE (
2.1
) is recovered for
\lambda_{s}\equiv 1
.

The first term corresponds to the rate of the classical W PDE (
2.1
), to which a bias term is added due to the fact that
\pi_{t}
approaches
\pi
only as
t\to\infty
.

Under the same assumptions required to obtain the convergence results for the standard FR PDE (
2.8
), we can obtain convergence of the tempered FR PDE (
2.14
) (see Appendix

An importance sampling approach to approximate the WFR PDE

The infinite time Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao PDE takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t})+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t})

where
f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu):=\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla\log\frac{\mu}{\pi})
and
f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu):=\mu(\log\frac{\pi}{\mu}-\int\log\frac{\pi}{\mu}d\mu)
correspond to the Wasserstein and Fisher–Rao operators respectively, and takes the form of a reaction-diffusion equation.

To obtain a time discretisation of the WFR PDE we consider the solution operator of the W flow and the FR flow separately. Assume that for certain discrete time
n-1
the solution of the WFR PDE is given by
\mu_{n-1}
, then applying the W flow for a time step
\gamma
leads to

\displaystyle\mu_{n-1/2}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{N}(0,2\gamma\cdot\textsf{Id})*\left[(\textsf{Id}+\gamma\nabla\log\pi)_{\#}\mu_{n-1}\right](x)

(3.1)

\displaystyle\propto\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x-y-\gamma\nabla\log\pi(y)\|^{2}\right)\mu_{n-1}(y)dy,

see, e.g.
[
Wib18
]
. The FR flow is not a linear operator, therefore to obtain an explicit expression for its action on
\mu_{n-1/2}
we consider the solution of the infinite time unnormalised FR PDE,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\rho_{t}\log\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}}

(3.2)

over time period
t
, where the above is obtained by applying Lemma
A.1
to (
2.2
). We can evaluate (
3.2
) directly using the substitution
\nu_{t}:=\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}
which transforms (
3.2
) into a linear PDE
[
CHH+23b
, Eq. (B.1)]
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nu_{t}=\frac{\partial_{t}\rho_{t}}{\rho_{t}}=\log\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}}=-\nu_{t}

which has explicit solution
\nu_{t}=\nu_{0}e^{-t}=e^{-t}\log\frac{\rho_{0}}{\pi}
. Transforming back yields

\displaystyle e^{-t}\log\frac{\rho_{0}}{\pi}=\log\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\implies\rho_{t}=\pi^{1-e^{-t}}\cdot\rho_{0}^{e^{-t}}.

Therefore, we immediately obtain that the FR flow with time step
\gamma
applied to
\mu_{n-1/2}
gives

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(x):=\pi(x)^{1-e^{-\gamma}}\mu_{n-1/2}(x)^{e^{-\gamma}}=\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{n-1/2}(x)}\right)^{1-e^{-\gamma}}\mu_{n-1/2}(x).

(3.3)

The evolution in (
3.1

where

4.3
Sequential Monte Carlo as Approximation of WFR Flows

We introduce in this section a class of algorithms known as sequential Monte Carlo samplers
[

]
and highlight how different algorithmic choices within this class lead to approximations of the PDE flows described in Section

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) samplers
[
dMDJ06
]
provide a particle approximation of the sequence of distributions
(\hat{\eta}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
using clouds of
N
weighted particles
\{X_{n}^{i},W_{n}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
To build an SMC sampler approximating the sequence
(\hat{\eta}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
one needs a family of Markov kernels
(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
used to propagate forward the particles, a sequence of backward kernels
(L_{n-1})_{n\geq 1}
and a resampling scheme.

A resampling scheme is a selection mechanism which given a set of weighted samples
\{X^{i},W^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
outputs a sequence of equally weighted samples
\{\widetilde{X}^{i},1/N\}_{i=1}^{N}
in which
\widetilde{X}^{i}=X^{j}
for some
j
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
. For a review of commonly used resampling schemes see
[
GCW19
]
.

At iteration
n
the weighted particle set
\{X_{n-1}^{i},W_{n-1}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
approximating
\hat{\eta}_{n-1}
is resampled to obtain the equally weighted particle set
\{\widetilde{X}_{n-1}^{i},1/N\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the kernel
M_{n}
is applied to propose new particle locations
X_{n}^{i}\sim M_{n}(\widetilde{X}_{n-1}^{i},\cdot)
. At this stage the particle set approximates
\hat{\eta}_{n-1}
, to obtain an approximation of
\hat{\eta}_{n}
an importance sampling step is applied with weights proportional to

\displaystyle w_{n}(x_{n-1},x_{n})=\frac{\hat{\eta}_{n}(x_{n})L_{n-1}(x_{n},x_{n-1})}{\hat{\eta}_{n-1}(x_{n-1})M_{n}(x_{n-1},x_{n})}.

(4.3)

The weight functions (
4.3
) simplify to
w_{n}(x_{n})=\hat{\eta}_{n}(x_{n})/\hat{\eta}_{n-1}(x_{n})
if
M_{n}
is a
\hat{\eta}_{n-1}
-invariant Markov kernel and
L_{n}
the corresponding backward kernel. Due to the renormalisation of the weights we do not need the normalising constants of
\hat{\eta}_{n},\hat{\eta}_{n-1}
.

The resulting procedure is summarised in Algorithm
2
whose output is a set of weighted samples which approximate
\hat{\eta}_{n}
. We refer the reader to
[
CP20
]
for a comprehensive introduction to SMC. Annealed importance sampling can be obtained from Algorithm

by omitting the resampling step; however, since it has been empirically
[
JSDT11
]
and theoretically
[
SBCCD24
]
shown that the addition of the resampling step improves the accuracy of the algorithm we do not further consider AIS.

sequences of distributions

Consider Algorithm

\displaystyle w_{n}^{\textrm{WFR}}(x_{n})

\displaystyle\log(\mu_{n+1})-\log(\mu_{n})

4.3.4
Convergence Properties

Algorithm
2
provides an approximation of the WFR and tempered WFR flow for particular choices of the sequence
(\hat{\eta}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and of proposal kernel
M_{n}
; however, the weights in (
4.5
) and (

) are intractable due to the presence of the convolution in the denominator and need to be approximated by

and
w_{n}^{\textrm{T-WFR},N}

Convergence of the numerical approximations to
\hat{\eta}_{n}
provided by Algorithm
2
follow from the rich literature on convergence of SMC methods (e.g.
[
dM04
]
) taking into account the additional approximation in the weight function
w_{n}
.
We provide here a simple result to validate our proposed approach and refer to the vast literature on convergence of SMC methods and their variants for further results (e.g.,
[
dM04
]
).
For simplicity, we focus on the case of measurable bounded test functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi(x)|<\infty
, a set we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We make the following assumption on the approximated weights
w_{n}^{N}
. While strong, this assumption is standard in the SMC literature and corresponds to the assumption used in
[
LLN19
, Theorem 3.2]
to prove the sharper convergence rate in (
2.11
).

Assumption 3
.

The weights
w_{n}^{N}
are positive everywhere,
w_{n}^{N}(x_{n})>0
for every
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and upper bounded
\|w_{n}^{N}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w_{n}^{N}(x)|<\infty
.

This assumption and some mild regularity on
\pi
(implied by Assumption
1
–
(b)
) allow us to obtain the following weak law of large numbers (WLLN) whose proof is provided in Appendix
B.3
:

Proposition 4.1

(Weak law of large numbers)
.

Under Assumption
3
, if
\pi
is upper bounded, for all
n\geq 0
and for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have the following convergence in probability

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{n}^{i}\varphi(X_{n}^{i})\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\int\varphi(x)\hat{\eta}_{n}(x)dx.

6
Conclusion

In this paper we study the connection between sampling algorithms and gradient flows in the space of probability measure, with focus on the Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao geometry. We identify a natural parallel between Fisher–Rao dynamics and importance sampling and use this idea to propose several sequential Monte Carlo algorithms which approximate the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao dynamics. These algorithm have convergence guarantees (Proposition
4.1
) and show superior performance to other approximations (Section
5
).
Connections between the WFR flow and a combination of diffusion and importance weighting have also been explored in
[
CNWR25
,
LCB+22
,
YWR24
]
. The main difference between our approach and the one of
[
CNWR25
, Section 5.8]
is in the order in which the space and time discretisations are performed:
[
CNWR25
, Section 5.8]
and
[
LCB+22
, Section H]
propose to first replace
\mu
with a weighted empirical measure and derives ODEs for the evolution of weights and locations; we first discretise time and connect the solution operators of the W and the FR flow in discrete time to ULA and importance sampling, respectively. This perspective allows us to directly connect the WFR flow with sequential Monte Carlo samplers.

The main drawback of approximating the WFR flow via Algorithm
1
is the
\mathcal{O}(N)
cost required to compute the weights (
4.5
); a cheaper alternative could be obtained by selecting a backward kernel
L_{n-1}
which is not the optimal one (see
[
dMDJ06
]
for some examples). This is likely to slow down convergence but would lead to weights which can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
. The best trade off between convergence speed and computational cost will likely be application dependent.

We also consider tempered dynamics and show that, both in continuous and in discrete time this dynamics do not improve the convergence speed of the standard, untempered ones (Proposition
2.1
,
2.2
,
3.2
).
Even if our theoretical and empirical results (Section
5
) suggest that tempered dynamics are not beneficial for sampling, we point out that the idea of tempering, i.e. to slowly move the initial distribution
\mu_{0}
to the target
\pi
via a geometric path, coincides with the FR flow, as highlighted in
[
CCK24
,
DEP23
]
(see also the discussion in Section
3
). Our results show that adding the FR flow to the W flow dramatically improves convergence by acting in a similar fashion to tempering. Therefore, changing the target in time using
\pi_{t}\propto\mu_{0}^{1-\lambda_{t}}\pi^{\lambda_{t}}
only introduces a bias and does not speed up the WFR dynamics.

Appendix A
Supplementary Lemmata

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\rho_{t}
denote an unnormalised probability density function, and assume its time evolution is governed by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)=\rho_{t}(x)f(x)

(A.1)

for some function
f(x)
, also known as the “fitness function” in the evolutionary biology literature. The evolution of the normalised probability density
\mu_{t}(x)=\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\int\rho_{t}(y)dy}
is given by the replicator PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)=\mu_{t}(x)\left(f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[f]\right)

(A.2)

Proof.

Differentiating the expression for
\mu_{t}(x)
with respect to
t
and substituting in (
A.1
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)}{\int\rho_{t}(y)dy}-\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{(\int\rho_{t}(y)dy)^{2}}\int\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{t}(x)f(x)}{\int\rho_{t}(y)dy}-\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{(\int\rho_{t}(y)dy)^{2}}\int f(y)\rho_{t}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)f(x)-\mu_{t}(x)\int f(y)\mu_{t}(y)dy

yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Unit time FR as a deterministic time rescaling of infinite time FR.)
.

Consider the unit time FR PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)=\mu_{t}(x)\log\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{0}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{0}(x)}\right],\quad t\in[0,1]

(A.3)

Let
\mu_{\tau}^{\ast}=\mu_{t}
where
\tau=-\log(1-t)
and
\mu_{t}
is a solution of (
A.3
). Then formally,
\mu_{\tau}^{\ast}
is a solution of the infinite time FR PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{\tau}^{\ast}(x)=\mu_{\tau}^{\ast}(x)\log\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{\tau}^{\ast}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\tau}^{\ast}}\left[\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{\tau}^{\ast}(x)}\right],\quad\tau\in[0,\infty)

Proof.

Consider the geometric interpolation

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(x)=\pi^{t}(x)\rho_{0}^{1-t}(x)=\exp[t\log\pi(x)+(1-t)\log\rho_{0}(x)]

(A.4)

where
\rho_{t}
denotes an unnormalised probability density. Differentiating both sides with respect to
t
yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\rho_{t}(x)\log\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\rho_{0}(x)}\right),\quad t\in[0,1],

(A.5)

that is, the unit time Fisher–Rao PDE in unnormalised form (see Lemma
A.1
). Consider the deterministic time change
\tau=-\log\left(1-t\right)
where
\tau\in[0,\infty)
such that
\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}=\rho_{t}
. Then using (
A.4
),

\displaystyle\log\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}=\log\rho_{t(\tau)}=(1-\exp(-\tau))\log\pi\,+\,\exp(-\tau)\log\rho_{0}

(A.6)

and
\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\exp(-\tau)
. Combining these results and applying chain rule to (
A.5
),

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}=\frac{dt}{d\tau}\partial_{t}\rho_{t(\tau)}

\displaystyle=\exp(-\tau)\rho_{t(\tau)}\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho_{t(\tau)}\left[\exp(-\tau)\log\pi-\exp(-\tau)\log{\rho_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle=\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}\left[\exp(-\tau)\log\pi-\exp(-\tau)\log{\rho_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle=\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}\left[\exp(-\tau)\log\pi+(1-\exp(-\tau))\log\pi-\log\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}\right]

\displaystyle=\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}\left[\log\pi-\log\rho_{\tau}^{\ast}\right]

yields the unnormalised form of the infinite time FR PDE. Applying the normalisation Lemma
A.1
yields the result.

∎

Appendix B
Convergence Results

B.1
Convergence of Continuous Time Schemes

B.1.1
Wasserstein Fisher Rao Flow

We consider the time derivative of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)
along the WFR flow

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle=\int\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\int\mu_{t}\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\|^{2}-\int\mu_{t}\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi_{t}}\right)^{2}+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)^{2}.

If
\pi
satisfies a Log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C_{\textrm{LSI}}
and
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\mu_{0}(x)}{\pi(x)}\geq e^{-M}
, using the proof strategy of
[
LLN19
, Theorem 3.2]
we find

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-2C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)-(2-2\delta)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)^{2},

for some
\delta>0
and
t\geq|\log(\delta/M)|
.
Further assuming that
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{0}||\pi)\leq 1
and using
[
LLN19
, Eq. (B.7)]
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq-\left(2C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}+(2-3\delta)\right)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi),

t\geq t_{0}:=\log(M/\delta^{3})
. Applying Grönwall’s Lemma we obtain

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\left(2C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}+(2-3\delta)\right)(t-t_{0})}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{0}||\pi),

showing that combining W with FR improves the convergence rate.

B.1.2
Tempered Wasserstein PDE

Consider the W PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi_{t}}\right)\right)

where
\pi_{t}\propto\pi^{\lambda_{t}}\mu_{0}^{1-\lambda_{t}}
and
\lambda_{t}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
,
\lambda_{t}
is non-decreasing in
t
and weakly differentiable.

A direct computation of the decay of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)
along this PDE gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle=-\int\mu_{t}\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\|^{2}+(1-\lambda_{t})\int\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\int\mu_{t}\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\|^{2}+(1-\lambda_{t})\int\mu_{t}\left[\Delta V_{\mu_{0}}-\Delta V_{\pi}-\langle\nabla V_{\pi},\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}-\nabla V_{\pi}\rangle\right]

where we used the expression for
\pi_{t}
and multiple applications of integrations by parts.

To proceed we recall that Assumption
1
implies the dissipativity condition

\displaystyle\langle x,\nabla V_{\pi}(x)\rangle\geq a_{\pi}\|x\|^{2}-b_{\pi},

(B.1)

with
a_{\pi}=c_{\pi}-1/(2\epsilon)>0
and
b_{\pi}=\epsilon\|\nabla V_{\pi}(0)\|^{2}/2>0
for
\epsilon>1/(2c_{\pi})
(see, e.g.,
[
CDDJ24
, Appendix B.3]
for a proof). A consequence of the dissipativity (
B.1
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|X\|^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{a_{\pi}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\langle X,\nabla V_{\pi}(X)\rangle]+\frac{b_{\pi}}{a_{\pi}}=\frac{d+b_{\pi}}{a_{\pi}}.

(B.2)

In addition,
V_{\pi}
must be minimised at some point
z_{0}\in B(0,\sqrt{\frac{b_{\pi}}{a_{\pi}}})
, thus the Lipschitz assumption implies

\displaystyle\|\nabla V_{\pi}(z)\|^{2}\leq 2L_{\pi}^{2}\left(\|z\|^{2}+\frac{b_{\pi}}{a_{\pi}}\right).

(B.3)

The same properties follow from Assumption
2
for
\mu_{0}
.

Using the above and
[
CKSV25
, Lemma 15]
we have

\displaystyle\int\mu_{t}\left[\Delta V_{\mu_{0}}-\Delta V_{\pi}-\langle\nabla V_{\pi},\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}-\nabla V_{\pi}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq d(L_{\mu_{0}}-c_{\pi})+2L_{\pi}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[\|x\|^{2}]+\frac{b_{\pi}}{a_{\pi}}\right)+\int\mu_{t}(\|\nabla V_{\pi}\|^{2}\vee\|\nabla V_{\mu_{0}}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq d(L_{\mu_{0}}-c_{\pi})+4(L_{\pi}^{2}\vee L_{\mu_{0}}^{2})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[\|x\|^{2}]+\left(\frac{b_{\pi}}{a_{\pi}}\vee\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq d(L_{\mu_{0}}-c_{\pi})+4(L_{\pi}^{2}\vee L_{\mu_{0}}^{2})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}[\|x\|^{2}]\vee\frac{d+b_{\mu_{0}}+b_{\pi}}{a_{\mu_{0}}\wedge a_{\pi}}+\left(\frac{b_{\pi}}{a_{\pi}}\vee\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)\right):=A.

Using Grönwall’s Lemma we obtain

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2tC_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{0}||\pi)+A\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2(t-s)C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}}(1-\lambda_{s})ds,

with
C_{\textrm{LSI}}=c_{\pi}^{-1}
.

B.1.3
Tempered Fisher Rao PDE

Consider the FR PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]\right),

where
\pi_{t}\propto\pi^{\lambda_{t}}\mu_{0}^{1-\lambda_{t}}
and
\lambda_{t}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
,
\lambda_{t}
is non-decreasing in
t
and weakly differentiable.
Using an argument similar to that of
[
CHH+23b
, Appendix B.1]
we can show that this PDE reduces the Kullback–Leibler divergence w.r.t.
\pi
.

Using the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial e^{t}\log\mu_{t}(x)}{\partial t}=e^{t}\log\pi_{t}(x)-e^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]

and the expression for
\pi_{t}
we have

\displaystyle\log\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\log\mu_{0}(x)(\int e^{s-t}(1-\lambda_{s})ds+e^{-t})+\log\pi(x)\int e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{s}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{s}}{\mu_{s}}\right)\right]ds.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mu_{t}\propto\mu_{0}^{e^{-t}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}(1-\lambda_{s})ds}\pi^{\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}.

(B.4)

The normalising constant of
\mu_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle:=\int\mu_{0}(x)^{1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}\pi(x)^{\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}dx

\displaystyle=\int\pi(x)\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{0}(x)}\right)^{1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}dx

\displaystyle\geq\int\pi(x)\exp\left(-M(1+\|x\|^{2})(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)\right)dx

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(\int\pi(x)\left(-M(1+\|x\|^{2})(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)\right)dx\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-M(1+B)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)\right),

where we used (
2.7
) and Jensen’s inequality.

Since
\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds<1
we also have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\frac{\mu_{0}(x)^{1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}\pi(x)^{\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)^{1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(M(1+B)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)+M(1+\|x\|^{2})(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)\right)

from which we obtain the result

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq M(1+B)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)+M(1+\int\mu_{t}(x)\|x\|^{2}dx)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)

\displaystyle\leq M(1+B)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)+M(1+B\exp\left(M(1+B)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)\right))(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)

using Proposition
B.1
.

Proposition B.1
.

Assume that
\int\|x\|^{2}\mu_{0}(x)dx\leq B
,
\int\|x\|^{2}\pi(x)dx\leq B

and (
2.7
), then

\displaystyle\int\mu_{t}(x)\|x\|^{2}dx\leq B\exp\left(M(1+B)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)\right).

Proof.

Using the expression for
\mu_{t}
in (
B.4
) and Hölder inequality with
p=(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)^{-1}
and
q=(\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)^{-1}
gives

\displaystyle\int\mu_{t}(x)\|x\|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\int(\mu_{0}(x)\|x\|^{2})^{1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}(\pi(x)\|x\|^{2})^{\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\left(\int\mu_{0}(x)\|x\|^{2}dx\right)^{1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}\left(\int\pi(x)\|x\|^{2}dx\right)^{\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds}

\displaystyle\leq B\exp\left(M(1+B)(1-\int_{0}^{t}e^{s-t}\lambda_{s}ds)\right).

∎

B.1.4
Tempered Wasserstein Fisher Rao PDE

As in the case of the other tempered PDEs, we consider the decay of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
along the flow described by (
2.15
):

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle=\int\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi_{t}}\right)\right)+\int\mu_{t}\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\log\frac{\pi_{t}}{\mu_{t}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}||\pi)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\int\mu_{t}\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\|^{2}-\textrm{var}_{\mu_{t}}\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\lambda_{t})\left[\int\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}\right)\right)-\textrm{cov}_{\mu_{t}}\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi},\log\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}\right)\right],

where we used the expression for
\pi_{t}
and multiple applications of integrations by parts.

We recognise the derivative of the standard WFR flow in the first term, the second term is due to the use of the tempered targets
\pi_{t}
and in fact is 0 for
\lambda_{t}\equiv 1
.
By defining the derivative of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
along the tempered W flow (
2.12
) and that along the tempered FR flow (
2.14
)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-W}}||\pi)

\displaystyle:=-\int\mu_{t}\|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\|^{2}+(1-\lambda_{t})\int\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-FR}}||\pi)

\displaystyle:=-\textrm{var}_{\mu_{t}}\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\right)-(1-\lambda_{t})\textrm{cov}_{\mu_{t}}\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi},\log\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}\right)

we can write

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-WFR}}||\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-W}}||\pi)+\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-FR}}||\pi),

showing that the W and FR geometries are indeed orthogonal as shown in
[
GM17
]
.

It follows that

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-WFR}}|\pi)\leq\min\left\{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-W}}||\pi),D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{T-FR}}||\pi)\right\}.

B.1.5
Gradient flow structure of tempered PDEs

The tempered PDEs described above do not correspond to gradient flows of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
. To identify a gradient flow structure, we consider the functional
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\lambda):\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\lambda):=D_{KL}(\mu||\pi_{\lambda})
where optimisation occurs both on
\mu
and on
\lambda
, whose evolution satisfies

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int\mu_{t}\log\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}-\int\pi_{t}\log\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}.

(B.5)

Gradient flows on joint spaces have been recently considered by
[
CKPJ25
,
KLJ23
]
which establish conditions on
\mathcal{F}
to guarantee convergence of the corresponding Wasserstein gradient flow.
Under the assumption that both
\mu_{0}
and
\pi
are strongly log-concave (see Assumption
1
and
2
below),
[
CKPJ25
, Theorem 1]
guarantees that the Wasserstein gradient flow of
\mathcal{F}
given by (
2.12
) and (
B.5
) converges exponentially:

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{F}(\mu_{t},\lambda_{t})\leq\mathcal{F}(\nu,\lambda_{0})e^{-2t(c_{\mu_{0}}\wedge c_{\pi})}

(B.6)

where
c_{\mu_{0}},c_{\pi}
denote the convexity constants of
\mu_{0},\pi
, respectively, and
\nu
is the initial condition of (
2.12
) (see Appendix
B.1
for a proof).

To apply
[
CKPJ25
, Theorem 1]
we check Assumption 1 and 2 therein as well as the extended log-Sobolev inequality (Eq. (11) therein).

The mapping
\lambda\mapsto\pi_{\lambda}=\mu_{0}^{1-\lambda}\pi^{\lambda}/Z_{\lambda}
is twice continuously differentiable with derivatives

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\log\pi_{\lambda}(x)=\log\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{0}(x)}-\int\pi_{\lambda}\log\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{0}(x)},\qquad\partial^{2}_{\lambda}\log\pi_{\lambda}(x)=\textrm{var}_{\pi_{\lambda}}\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{0}}\right)

which are finite quantities under Assumption
1
and
2
.
In addition,
\pi_{\lambda}(x)>0
for all
\lambda,x
if
\mu_{0}(x)>0
and
\pi(x)>0
. The Lipschitz continuity in Assumption
1
and
2
then guarantees that
[
CKPJ25
, Assumption 2]
is satisfied.

To check the extended log-Sobolev inequality in
[
CKPJ25
, Eq. (11)]
we note that
\mathcal{F}^{\star}=0
and, since
\pi_{\lambda}
is strongly log-concave under Assumption
1
and
2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mu,\lambda)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi_{\lambda})\leq\frac{1}{2c_{\lambda}}\int\|\nabla_{x}\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi_{\lambda}(x)}\|^{2}\mu(x)dx\leq\frac{1}{2(c_{\mu_{0}}\wedge c_{\pi})}\int\|\nabla_{x}\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi_{\lambda}(x)}\|^{2}\mu(x)dx

where
c_{\lambda}\geq(1-\lambda)c_{\mu_{0}}+\lambda c_{\pi}\geq c_{\mu_{0}}\wedge c_{\pi}
is the convexity constant of
\pi_{\lambda}
.

B.2
Convergence of Discrete Time Schemes

B.2.1
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Proof.

Let us denote
\mathcal{F}(\mu)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu||\pi)
.
To show that the mirror descent update (
3.5
) reduces
\mathcal{F}
we reproduce the argument of
[
CCK24
, Proposition 1]
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is 1-relatively smooth with respect to
\phi:\mu\mapsto\int\log(\mu(x))d\mu(x)

[
AFKL22
]
and since
\delta=1-e^{-\gamma}\leq 1
, for all
\gamma>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2})+\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2}),\mu_{n}-\mu_{n-1/2}\rangle+B_{\phi}(\mu_{n}|\mu_{n-1/2})

(B.7)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2})+\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2}),\mu_{n}-\mu_{n-1/2}\rangle+\frac{1}{\delta}B_{\phi}(\mu_{n}|\mu_{n-1/2}),

where
B_{\phi}(\nu|\mu)=\phi(\nu)-\phi(\mu)-\langle\nabla\phi(\mu),\nu-\mu\rangle
denotes the Bregman divergence and
\nabla\phi
the first variation.
Applying
[
AFKL22
, Lemma 3]
to the convex function
G(\nu)=\delta\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2}),\nu-\mu_{n-1/2}\rangle
, with
\mu=\mu_{n-1/2}
and
\bar{\nu}=\mu_{n}
yields

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2}),\mu_{n}-\mu_{n-1/2}\rangle+\frac{1}{\delta}B_{\phi}(\mu_{n}|\mu_{n-1/2})\leq

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1}),\nu-\mu_{n-1/2}\rangle+\frac{1}{\delta}B_{\phi}(\nu|\mu_{n-1/2})-\frac{1}{\delta}B_{\phi}(\nu|\mu_{n}).

Fix
\nu
, then (
B.7
) becomes:

\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n})\leq\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2})+\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2}),\nu-\mu_{n-1/2}\rangle+\frac{1}{\delta}B_{\phi}(\nu|\mu_{n-1/2})-\frac{1}{\delta}B_{\phi}(\nu|\mu_{n}).

This shows in particular, by substituting
\nu=\mu_{n-1/2}
and since
B_{\phi}(\nu|\mu_{n})\geq 0
, that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n})\leq\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2})-\frac{1}{\delta}B_{\phi}(\mu_{n-1/2}|\mu_{n}).

(B.8)

To deal with the W flow update we observe that
\mu_{n-1/2}
in (
3.4
) with fixed is obtained as

\displaystyle\mu_{n-1/2}(x)=\mu_{n-1}R_{\gamma}(x):=(4\uppi\gamma)^{-d/2}\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\|x-y-\gamma\nabla\log\pi(y)\|^{2}\right)\mu_{n-1}(y)dy

and using
[
VW19
, Lemma 3]

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\gamma/C_{\textrm{LSI}}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1})+6\gamma^{2}dL_{\pi}^{2},

with
\gamma\leq C_{\textrm{LSI}}^{-1}/(4L_{\pi}^{2})
.
Using (
B.8
), the result above and the fact that
B_{\phi}\geq 0
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n})\leq e^{-\gamma/C_{\textrm{LSI}}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n-1})+6\gamma^{2}dL_{\pi}^{2}.

Proceeding by induction we then find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mu_{n})

\displaystyle\leq e^{-n\gamma/C_{\textrm{LSI}}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{0})+6\gamma^{2}dL_{\pi}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-k\gamma/C_{\textrm{LSI}}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-n\gamma/C_{\textrm{LSI}}}\mathcal{F}(\mu_{0})+6\gamma^{2}dL_{\pi}^{2}\left(\frac{1-e^{-(n-1)}}{1-e^{-\gamma/C_{\textrm{LSI}}}}\right)

(B.18)

\displaystyle\leq b(p)^{1/p}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}4\left(\sup|\Delta_{n}^{i}|\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq b(p)^{1/p}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}16\|\varphi\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 4b(p)^{1/p}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}

with
b(p)
as in (
B.17
).

Using (
B.18
) and Markov’s inequality, it follows that
\eta^{N}_{n}(\varphi)-\hat{\eta}_{n-1}^{N}M_{n}(\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}0
.
We can use the hypothesis to get
\hat{\eta}_{n-1}^{N}M_{n}(\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\hat{\eta}_{n-1}M_{n}(\varphi)
.
Combining the two results above and using Slutzky’s lemma we obtain
\eta^{N}_{n}(\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\eta_{n}(\varphi)
.
∎

Proposition B.6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}
denote the
\sigma
-field generated by the weighted samples up to (and including) time
n-1
. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]=\int\varphi(x_{n})\left(M_{n}\Psi_{w_{n-1}^{N}}(\eta_{n-1}^{N})(x_{n})\right)^{1-\delta}\pi^{\delta}(x_{n})dx_{n},

for all
n\geq 1
and all
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In addition,
\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi)=\int\varphi(x_{n})\left(\hat{\eta}_{n-1}M_{n}(x_{n})\right)^{1-\delta}\pi^{\delta}(x_{n})dx_{n}
.

Proof.

Consider the conditional expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[w_{n}^{N}(X_{n}^{i})\varphi(X_{n}^{i})|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[w_{n}^{N}(X_{n}^{i})\varphi(X_{n}^{i})|\mathcal{G}_{n-1}^{N}\right]|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\int M_{n}(\widetilde{X}_{n-1}^{i},dx_{n})w_{n}^{N}(x_{n})\varphi(x_{n})|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{w_{n-1}^{N}(X_{n-1}^{j})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{n-1}^{N}(X_{n-1}^{k})}\int M_{n}(X_{n-1}^{j},x_{n})w_{n}^{N}(x_{n})\varphi(x_{n})dx_{n},

where the third equality follows from the fact that
X_{n}^{i}|\mathcal{G}_{n-1}^{N}\sim M_{n}(\widetilde{X}_{n-1}^{i},\cdot)
for each
i=1,\dots,N
and the fourth from the fact that
\{X_{n-1}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
and
w_{n-1}^{N}
are
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}
-measurable and conditionally each
\widetilde{X}_{n-1}^{i}
is drawn independently from the categorical distribution with probabilities given by the weights.
Plugging the definition of
w_{n}^{N}
in (
B.16
) into the above we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\int M_{n}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\varphi(x_{n})\Psi_{w_{n-1}^{N}}(\eta_{n-1}^{N})(x_{n-1})\frac{\left(\int M_{n}(z,x_{n})\Psi_{w_{n-1}^{N}}(\eta_{n-1}^{N})(z)dz\right)^{1-\delta}\pi^{\delta}(x_{n})}{\int M_{n}(z,x_{n})\Psi_{w_{n-1}^{N}}(\eta_{n-1}^{N})(z)dz}dx_{n}dx_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(x_{n})\left(\int M_{n}(z,x_{n})\Psi_{w_{n-1}^{N}}(\eta_{n-1}^{N})(z)dz\right)^{1-\delta}\pi^{\delta}(x_{n})dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(x_{n})\left(M_{n}\Psi_{w_{n-1}^{N}}(\eta_{n-1}^{N})(x_{n})\right)^{1-\delta}\pi^{\delta}(x_{n})dx_{n}.

We have that, for all
n\geq 1
and all
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(x_{n})G_{n}(x_{n})\eta_{n}(x_{n})dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\int\int M_{n}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\varphi(x_{n})\frac{\left(\hat{\eta}_{n-1}M_{n}(x_{n})\right)^{1-\delta}\pi^{\delta}(x_{n})}{\hat{\eta}_{n-1}M_{n}(x_{n})}\hat{\eta}_{n-1}(x_{n-1})dx_{n}dx_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(x_{n})\left(\hat{\eta}_{n-1}M_{n}(x_{n})\right)^{1-\delta}\pi^{\delta}(x_{n})dx_{n}.

∎

Lemma B.7

(Weight Comparison)
.

Assume that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\Psi_{w_{n-1}^{N}}(\eta_{n-1}^{N})(\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\Psi_{w_{n-1}}(\eta_{n-1})(\varphi)
, then
\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi)
, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Consider the decomposition

\displaystyle\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)-\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi)

\displaystyle=\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)-\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]-\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi).

For the first term, consider the sequence of functions
\Delta_{n}^{i}:E\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
\Delta_{n}^{i}(x):=w_{n}^{N}(x)\varphi(x)-\mathbb{E}\left[w_{n}^{N}(X_{n}^{i})\varphi(X_{n}^{i})\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]
, for
i=1,\ldots,N
.
Conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}
,
\Delta_{n}^{i}(X_{n}^{i})

i=1,\ldots,N
are independent and have expectation equal to 0, moreover

\displaystyle\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)-\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{n}^{i}(X_{n}^{i}).

Using Lemma
B.4
, we have almost surely

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)-\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]|^{p}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq 4b(p)^{1/p}\|w_{n}^{N}\|_{\infty}\|\varphi\|_{\infty},

where
b(p)
is given in (
B.17
).
Hence, by Markov’s inequality,
\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)-\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]\overset{p}{\rightarrow}0
.

Using Proposition
B.6
, the dominated convergence theorem and the hypothesis we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{N}\right]\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi),

from which the result follows.
∎

Lemma B.8

(Approximate reweighting)
.

Assume that, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi)
, then
\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}(\eta^{N}_{n})(\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\Psi_{w_{n}}(\eta_{n})(\varphi)

for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Take the definition of
\Psi_{w_{n}}
and
\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}
,
\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}(\eta^{N}_{n})(\varphi)-\Psi_{w_{n}}(\eta_{n})(\varphi)=\frac{\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)}{\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N})}-\frac{\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi)}{\eta_{n}(w_{n})}
.
A simple application of the continuous mapping theorem to the hypothesis gives
\frac{\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N}\varphi)}{\eta^{N}_{n}(w_{n}^{N})}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\frac{\eta_{n}(w_{n}\varphi)}{\eta_{n}(w_{n})}
,
and the result follows.
∎

Lemma B.9

(Multinomial resampling)
.

Assume that, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}(\eta^{N}_{n})(\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\hat{\eta}_{n}(\varphi)
,
then after the resampling step performed through multinomial resampling
\hat{\eta}_{n}^{N}(\varphi)\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\hat{\eta}_{n}(\varphi)

for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Consider the decomposition

\hat{\eta}_{n}^{N}(\varphi)-\hat{\eta}_{n}(\varphi)=\hat{\eta}_{n}^{N}(\varphi)-\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}(\eta^{N}_{n})(\varphi)+\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}(\eta^{N}_{n})(\varphi)-\hat{\eta}_{n}(\varphi).

Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{N}

the
\sigma
-field generated by the weighted samples up to (and including) time
n
,
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{N}:=\sigma\left(X_{n}^{i}:i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\right)\vee\mathcal{G}_{n-1}^{N}
and consider the sequence of functions
\Delta_{n}^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
\Delta_{n}^{i}(x):=\varphi(x)-\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i})\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}^{N}\right]
, for
i=1,\ldots,N
.
Conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{N}
,
\Delta_{n}^{i}(\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i})

i=1,\ldots,N
are independent and have expectation equal to 0, moreover

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{n}^{N}(\varphi)-\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}(\eta^{N}_{n})(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i})-\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i})\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}^{N}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{n}^{i}(\widetilde{X}_{n}^{i}).

Using Lemma
B.4
, we find

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\eta}_{n}^{N}(\varphi)-\Psi_{w_{n}^{N}}(\eta^{N}_{n})(\varphi)|^{p}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}^{N}\right]^{1/p}

Since

Appendix C
Analytic solutions: Multivariate Gaussian case

This section focuses on comparing the evolution of the PDEs introduce in this section for the special case of transporting a Gaussian
\mu_{0}(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;m_{0},C_{0})
to target Gaussian
\pi(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;m_{\pi},C_{\pi})
. In this case, we can obtain nice expressions for the moments along the flow described by each PDE.

In particular, observing that both the tempered W flow and the tempered FR flow preserve gaussianity, we have that
\mu_{t}(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;m_{t},C_{t})
for each time
t\geq 0
.

We detail here the results needed to obtain the ODEs describing the tempered and standard PDEs for the special case of transporting a Gaussian
\mu_{0}(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;m_{0},C_{0})
to target Gaussian
\pi(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;m_{\pi},C_{\pi})
.

Wasserstein Flow (Langevin)

The PDE in this case is the Fokker-Planck equation for the overdamped Langevin SDE

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla V_{\pi}(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{\pi}^{-1}(X_{t}-m_{\pi})dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}.

Since the potential takes the form
V_{\pi}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(x-m_{\pi})^{\top}C_{\pi}^{-1}(x-m_{\pi})
, the moment equations are standard as this essentially boils down to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. They are given by

\displaystyle\dot{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{\pi}^{-1}(m_{t}-m_{\pi})

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+2I

and the explicit solution can be found as

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{-tC_{\pi}^{-1}}m_{0}+(I-e^{-tC_{\pi}^{-1}})m_{\pi}

\displaystyle C_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{-tC_{\pi}^{-1}}\left(2\int_{0}^{t}e^{sC_{\pi}^{-1}}(e^{sC_{\pi}^{-1}})^{\top}ds+C_{0}\right)(e^{-tC_{\pi}^{-1}})^{\top}.

In the 1D case, we can easily evaluate the expression for
C_{t}
as

\displaystyle C_{t}=e^{-2tC_{\pi}^{-1}}[C_{0}+C_{\pi}(e^{2tC_{\pi}^{-1}}-1)].

Fisher–Rao flow

In the Gaussian case, the ODEs for the mean and covariance respectively are given by

\displaystyle\dot{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}(m_{t}-m_{\pi})

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}+C_{t}.

The explicit solutions in this case are known (see e.g.
[
CHH+23a
, Proposition 4.11]
),

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=m_{\pi}+e^{-t}\left((1-e^{-t})C_{\pi}^{-1}+e^{-t}C_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}C_{0}^{-1}(m_{0}-m_{\pi})

\displaystyle=m_{\pi}+e^{-t}C_{t}C_{0}^{-1}(m_{0}-m_{\pi})

\displaystyle C_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=C_{\pi}^{-1}+e^{-t}(C_{0}^{-1}-C_{\pi}^{-1}).

Unit time Fisher–Rao flow

The evolution of
m_{t},C_{t}
along the unit time FR flow (
2.4
) is given by

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=C_{t}\left[(1-t)C_{0}^{-1}m_{0}+tC_{\pi}^{-1}m_{\pi}\right]

\displaystyle C_{t}

\displaystyle=[(1-t)C_{0}^{-1}+tC_{\pi}^{-1}]^{-1}

Clearly at
t=1,m_{t}=m_{\pi},C_{t}=C_{\pi}
. The unit time FR and the infinite time FR follow the same path but over different time-scales which can be connected using the time scaling in Lemma
A.2
.

Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao flow

The mean and covariance equations in the Gaussian case for the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao flow can be obtained using the results for the pure Wasserstein and pure Fisher–Rao flows as shown in
[
LMTZ25
, Proposition 2.2]
,

\displaystyle\dot{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=-(C_{t}+I)C_{\pi}^{-1}(m_{t}-m_{\pi})

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}+C_{t}-C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+2I

The explicit solution in the 1D case is given by

\displaystyle C_{t}

\displaystyle=C_{\pi}+\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1}{C_{0}-C_{\pi}}+\frac{1}{C_{\pi}+2}\right)e^{\left(1+\frac{2}{C_{\pi}}\right)t}-\frac{1}{C_{\pi}+2}}

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=m_{\pi}+(m_{0}-m_{\pi})\exp\left(\frac{t}{C_{\pi}}\right)\frac{C_{t}-C_{\pi}}{C_{0}-C_{\pi}},

showing that
C_{t}
must approach
C_{\pi}
faster than
\exp(t/C_{\pi})
grows, which is guaranteed by the factor 2 in the expression for
C_{t}
.
Explicit solution of the covariance evolution under the WFR flow are give in
[
LMTZ25
, Remark 2.3]
.

Tempered Wasserstein flow (Tempered Langevin;
[
CKSV25
]
)

Recall the diffusion process characterising Tempered Langevin is given by

\displaystyle dX_{t}=-[(1-\lambda_{t})\nabla_{x}V_{\mu_{0}}+\lambda_{t}\nabla_{x}V_{\pi}]dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}

where
\lambda_{t}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1)
is a non-decreasing function of
t
. The corresponding evolution PDE given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi_{t}(x)}\right)\right)=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)^{\lambda_{t}}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\mu_{0}(x)}\right)^{1-\lambda_{t}}\right)

where
\pi_{t}\propto\pi^{\lambda_{t}}\mu_{0}^{1-\lambda_{t}}
. The second equality shows that it can be seen as a sum of two Wasserstein parts with temperatures
\lambda_{t}
and
1-\lambda_{t}
.

Note that
\pi_{t}
is a product of 2 Gaussian densities
\mathcal{N}\left(m_{\pi},\frac{C_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}\right)
and
\mathcal{N}\left(m_{0},\frac{C_{0}}{1-\lambda_{t}}\right)
therefore
\pi_{t}\propto\mathcal{N}(a_{t},P_{t})
where

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(m_{0}\frac{C_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}+m_{\pi}\frac{C_{0}}{1-\lambda_{t}}\right)\left(\frac{C_{0}}{1-\lambda_{t}}+\frac{C_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle P_{t}

\displaystyle=C_{\pi}(\lambda_{t}C_{0}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{\pi})^{-1}C_{0}

and the intermediate densities are still Gaussian. Then we have the same structure for the moment ODEs as for infinite time Wasserstein gradient flow, except with
m_{\pi},C_{\pi}
replaced by
a_{t},P_{t}
respectively,

\displaystyle\dot{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\left(C_{\pi}(\lambda_{t}C_{0}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{\pi})^{-1}C_{0}\right)^{-1}\left(m_{t}-\left(m_{0}\frac{C_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}+m_{\pi}\frac{C_{0}}{1-\lambda_{t}}\right)\left(\frac{C_{0}}{1-\lambda_{t}}+\frac{C_{\pi}}{\lambda_{t}}\right)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-(\lambda_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{0}^{-1})\left(m_{t}-\left((1-\lambda_{t})m_{0}C_{\pi}+\lambda_{t}m_{\pi}C_{0}\right)\left(\lambda_{t}C_{0}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{\pi}\right)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\left(C_{\pi}(\lambda_{t}C_{0}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{\pi})^{-1}C_{0}\right)^{-1}C_{t}-C_{t}\left(C_{\pi}(\lambda_{t}C_{0}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{\pi})^{-1}C_{0}\right)^{-1}+2I

\displaystyle=-(\lambda_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{0}^{-1})C_{t}-C_{t}(\lambda_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{0}^{-1})+2I.

In the 1D case, the explicit solution is given by

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=m_{0}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}(1-\lambda_{s})dsC_{0}^{-1}-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}dsC_{\pi}^{-1}}

\displaystyle+e^{-\int_{0}^{t}(1-\lambda_{s})dsC_{0}^{-1}-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}dsC_{\pi}^{-1}}\int_{0}^{t}[(1-\lambda_{u})C_{0}^{-1}m_{0}+\lambda_{u}C_{\pi}^{-1}m_{\pi}]e^{\int_{0}^{u}(1-\lambda_{s})dsC_{0}^{-1}+\int_{0}^{u}\lambda_{s}dsC_{\pi}^{-1}}du

\displaystyle C_{t}

\displaystyle=C_{0}e^{-2\int_{0}^{t}(1-\lambda_{s})dsC_{0}^{-1}-2\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}dsC_{\pi}^{-1}}

\displaystyle+2e^{-2\int_{0}^{t}(1-\lambda_{s})dsC_{0}^{-1}-2\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}dsC_{\pi}^{-1}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\int_{0}^{u}(1-\lambda_{s})dsC_{0}^{-1}+2\int_{0}^{u}\lambda_{s}dsC_{\pi}^{-1}}du.

Tempered Fisher–Rao flow

Recalling that
\pi_{t}\propto\mathcal{N}(a_{t},P_{t})
with
a_{t},P_{t}
as before and observing that tempered FR PDE can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)^{\lambda_{t}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{\lambda_{t}}\right]\right)+\mu_{t}(x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\mu_{0}(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)^{1-\lambda_{t}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{1-\lambda_{t}}\right]\right),

we see that the tempered FR PDE is the sum of two Fisher–Rao paths with temperatures
\lambda_{t}
and
1-\lambda_{t}
.

The ODEs describing the evolution of the moments of
\mu_{t}
are obtained from those for the standard FR flow replacing
m_{\pi},C_{\pi}
with
a_{t},P_{t}

\displaystyle\dot{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{t}(\lambda_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{0}^{-1})(m_{t}-m_{\pi})

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=-C_{t}(\lambda_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+(1-\lambda_{t})C_{0}^{-1})C_{t}+C_{t}.
