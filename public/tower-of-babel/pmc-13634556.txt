Different regulatory mechanisms of hepatitis B- and C-positive hepatocellular carcinoma by single-cell RNA sequencing
Authors: Su-Jin Baek, Seung-Jin Park, Minho Lee, Won Park, Jong-Hwan Kim, Youngjin Shin, Ok-Hee Kim, Ho Joong Choi, Say-June Kim, Yeun-Jun Chung, Seon-Young Kim
Published: 2026

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. It accounts for nearly 90% of all primary liver cancers. The incidence of HCC is primarily driven by chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections, which together are responsible for approximately 80% of global cases.1,2 HBV, a DNA virus, and HCV, an RNA virus, possess fundamentally different genomic structures and replication strategies. This suggests they may trigger carcinogenesis through distinct molecular pathways. However, virus-specific molecular distinctions in HCC pathogenesis remain poorly defined. Historically, bulk RNA sequencing has obscured critical cellular heterogeneity by averaging signals across mixed cell populations. The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has overcome this limitation, enabling high-resolution profiling of individual cells. While scRNA-seq has been applied to HCC, for instance, to profile the diverse immune cell subsets within the HBV/HCV microenvironment,3 and to identify unique, immunosuppressive tumor cell phenotypes specific to HBV-HCC,4 a systematic comparison of their distinct cellular ecosystems remains poorly characterized. To fill this gap, we conducted an integrative scRNA-seq analysis to systematically investigate the distinct molecular characteristics of HBV- and HCV-HCC. We integrated data from normal, HBV- and HCV-HCC tissues to identify cell-type-specific transcriptional alterations.

We constructed a comprehensive single-cell atlas by integrating data from 51 samples, comprising 23 HBV-HCC patients, 19 HCV-HCC patients, and 9 normal liver controls (Fig. 1A and Table S1). Patients with known HBV/HCV co-infection were excluded from this cohort. A total of 196,658 high-quality cells were retained for analysis. These cells were clustered using the top 20 principal components and visualized via UMAP (Fig. 1B). Using established canonical markers, we identified 48 cell clusters annotated into 10 major cell types (Fig. 1C; Fig. S1 and Table S2). Additionally, due to the distinct distributions of the Normal, HBV, and HCV groups, the proportions of cell types also varied significantly across the datasets (Fig. S2). Comparison of cell types within each group revealed that the most abundant populations across all samples were T cells (68,684 cells; 34.93%) and hepatocytes (73,745 cells; 37.5%) (Fig. 1D). Normal liver samples had a higher proportion of hepatocytes, whereas both HBV- and HCV-HCCs showed a marked decrease in hepatocytes and a corresponding increase in T cells. Critically, we observed a virus-specific difference: the proportion of cholangiocytes was higher in HCV-HCC (2%) compared with HBV-HCC (1%) (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, differentially expressed gene analysis comparing the viral subtypes revealed that cholangiocytes, along with smooth muscle cells and neutrophils, showed a high number of differentially expressed genes, underscoring their significant transcriptional divergence (Fig. S3 and Table S3). Given their high transcriptional divergence, we focused on cholangiocytes to uncover virus-specific mechanisms.

To understand the functional consequences of these transcriptional changes, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) across all cell types using the comprehensive Hallmark gene set. This analysis allowed us to categorize key dysregulated pathways into three groups: HBV-specific, HCV-specific, and common, representing those significant in HBV-HCC only, HCV-HCC only, or in both types. HBV-specific pathways included down-regulated "E2F TARGETS" and "P53 PATHWAY", and up-regulated "BILE ACID METABOLISM" and "HYPOXIA". HCV-specific pathways included up-regulated "ADIPOGENESIS" and "TGF BETA SIGNALING", and down-regulated "INTERFERON ALPHA RESPONSE" (macrophages/smooth muscle cells). Common pathways included down-regulated "ANGIOGENESIS" and "IL2 STAT5 SIGNALING", and up-regulated "COAGULATION" and "XENOBIOTIC METABOLISM" (Fig. 1F; Fig. S4 and Table S4). While these broad changes are informative, a cell-type-specific GSEA highlighted our central finding. Within cholangiocytes, while most pathways showed similar enrichment patterns, the "TNFA SIGNALING VIA NFKB" pathway exhibited an opposite expression pattern (Fig. 1G). This critical inflammatory pathway was significantly decreased in HBV-positive cells but significantly increased in HCV-positive cells compared with normal cholangiocytes. We investigated the 52 differentially expressed genes within the "TNFA SIGNALING VIA NFKB" pathway and found that most genes followed this divergent pattern (Figs. S5 and S6). Key genes, including MARCKS, JUN, EGR1, CCND1, FOS, and NFKBIA, showed decreased expression in HBV-cells but increased expression in HCV-positive cells. This demonstrated a fundamental, virus-specific re-wiring of TNF-α signaling specifically within the cholangiocyte population. To assess the clinical relevance of these findings, we performed survival analysis using TCGA LIHC data, stratifying by viral status (confirmed via clinical data and read alignment) (Fig. S7). This analysis identified prognostic genes that were effective only in a virus-dependent manner. Low expression of IFIT2, a gene decreased in our HBV-positive data, correlated with significantly shorter overall survival only in HBV-HCC patients (p = 0.027). IFIT2 limits HBV replication.5 Conversely, high expression of the chemokines CXCL1 and CXCL3, genes up-regulated in our HCV-positive data, correlated with reduced survival only in HCV-HCC patients (p = 0.048 and p = 0.015, respectively). These findings confirm that the distinct transcriptional programs we identified have tangible, virus-specific prognostic implications.

To investigate which genes regulated this divergence, we selected cell types that best reflected this divergence, and it was observed that cholangiocytes showed the clearest relationship among the normal, HBV, and HCV groups (Fig. S8). We performed trajectory analysis on the 1587 cholangiocytes (Fig. 1H) and, when ordering cells in pseudotime, normal cholangiocytes differentiated along a trajectory that bifurcated into two distinct malignant states: an HBV-positive branch and an HCV-positive branch. We identified 22 branch-dependent genes belonging to the "TNFA SIGNALING VIA NFKB" by applying the Branched Expression Analysis Modeling (BEAM) function to detect genes with significant expression changes between the HBV-HCC and HCV-HCC branches (Fig. 1I). According to the expression pattern by pseudotime, the genes were classified into four clusters with increased (HBV-enriched)/decreased (HBV-depleted) expression when progressing from normal to HBV and increased (HCV-enriched)/decreased (HCV-depleted) expression when progressing from normal to HCV. We focused on the key clusters defining the divergence: "HBV-depleted" (e.g., CCND1) and "HCV-enriched" (e.g., SOD2, SQSTM1) (Fig. 1J and Table S5). We discovered CEBPB as the candidate transcription factor regulating the genes that increase from Normal to HCV (Fig. S9A). We also identified EGR1 as the candidate transcription factor regulating genes decreasing from Normal to HBV (Fig. S9B). GSEA performed on the top 100 genes highly correlated with these transcription factors (Tables S6 and S7) supported their distinct functional programs (Fig. 1K and Table S7). The EGR1–HBV axis was associated with "Hypoxia", "TNF-α signaling via NF-kB", and "Apoptosis", while the CEBPB–HCV axis was linked to "Oxidative phosphorylation", "ROS pathway", and "Adipogenesis".

In conclusion, this study provides one of the first direct scRNA-seq comparisons of HBV- and HCV-HCC, revealing distinct cellular and molecular landscapes. Our key finding is the divergent regulation of the TNF-α signaling pathway in cholangiocytes, which is down-regulated in HBV-HCC and up-regulated in HCV-HCC. We suggest this divergence may be orchestrated by distinct candidate transcription factors—EGR1 in HBV-HCC and CEBPB in HCV-HCC. These transcription factors, in turn, are associated with different target genes (CCND1 vs. SOD2/SQSTM1) and downstream functional pathways (hypoxia/apoptosis vs. oxidative phosphorylation/ROS pathway) (Fig. 1L). This suggests HBV suppresses this pathway (potentially evading apoptosis), while HCV activates it (potentially promoting chronic inflammation). These findings provide correlative insights into the divergent oncogenic roles of HBV and HCV and offer valuable, virus-specific gene signatures.
