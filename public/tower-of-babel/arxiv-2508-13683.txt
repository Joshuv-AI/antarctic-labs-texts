A convergent Fourier spectral Galerkin method for the fractional Camassa-Holm equation
Mukul Dwivedi and Andreas Rupp

Abstract
. We analyze a Fourier spectral Galerkin method for the fractional Camassa-Holm (fCH) equation involving a fractional Laplacian of exponent α ∈ [ 1 , 2 ] \alpha\in[1,2] with periodic boundary conditions. The semi-discrete scheme preserves both mass and energy invariants of the fCH equation. For the fractional Benjamin-Bona-Mahony reduction, we establish existence and uniqueness of semi-discrete solutions and prove strong convergence to the unique solution in C 1 ​ ( [ 0 , T ] , H per α ​ ( I ) ) C^{1}([0,T];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)) for given T > 0 T>0 . For the general fCH equation, we demonstrate spectral accuracy in spatial discretization with optimal error estimates 𝒪 ⁡ ( N − r ) \mathcal{O}(N^{-r}) for initial data u 0 ∈ H r ​ ( I ) u_{0}\in H^{r}(I) with r ≥ α + 2 r\geq\alpha+2 and exponential convergence 𝒪 ⁡ ( e − c ​ N ) \mathcal{O}(e^{-cN}) for smooth solutions. Numerical experiments validate orbital stability of solitary waves achieving optimal convergence, confirming theoretical findings.

Introduction

For
\alpha\in[1,2]
,
T>0
, and
I:=[-\pi,\pi]

\displaystyle u_{t}+f(u)_{x}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{t}=-\kappa_{2}\left[2\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(uu_{x})+u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}\right],\quad\;\;x\in I,\;\;t\in(0,T],

(1.1)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in I,

where
f(u)=\kappa_{1}u+\frac{3}{2}\gamma u^{2}
,
u_{0}
is the given initial datum, and

denotes the
2\pi

and

are physical parameters. The fractional Laplacian
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}:=(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
for
\alpha>0

[
23
]
, i.e.,

\widehat{\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u}(k)=|k|^{\alpha}\hat{u}(k),

(1.2)

\hat{u}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{I}u(x)e^{-ikx}dx

The formulation (

[
6

u_{t}+2\omega u_{x}+3uu_{x}-u_{xxt}=2u_{x}u_{xx}+uu_{xxx},\quad x\in I,\;\;t\in(0,T],

(1.3)

subject to periodic boundary and initial conditions, which corresponds to the parameter choice
\kappa_{1}=2\omega
,
\gamma=1
,
\alpha=2
,
\kappa_{2}=1/3
. With
\omega>0
, the CH equation (

) describes the propagation of unidirectional gravitational waves in the shallow water approximation
[
12
,
10
,
17
]
. The equation possesses remarkable mathematical properties including complete integrability
[

]

[

[
29

\omega=0
.

We also study a special case of (
1.1
) when
\kappa_{1}=1
,
\gamma=1/3
, and
\kappa_{2}=0
, which yields the
2\pi
-periodic fractional Benjamin-Bona-Mahony (fBBM) equation
[

]

\displaystyle u_{t}+u_{x}+uu_{x}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{t}=0,\quad x\in I,\;t\in(0,T],

(1.4)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in I.

For
\alpha=2
, the fBBM equation (

[

for modeling long surface gravity waves of small amplitude
[

[

[

Recent work by Klein and Oruc
[

has extended the CH framework to fractional derivatives, where
\alpha<2
corresponds to sub-dispersive regimes relevant to nonlinear elastic waves
[
14
]
.
Despite theoretical advances in well-posedness
[

,
25
,
16
]
, numerical analysis of fractional CH equations remains underdeveloped, particularly for
\alpha<2
. The classical CH equation has been extensively studied numerically, with various approaches developed including finite difference methods
[
20
,
9
]
, discontinuous Galerkin schemes
[
32
,
24
]
, finite element methods
[
3
]
, and variational discretizations
[
18
]
. Spectral methods have also been applied to the classical CH equation (
1.3
), with Kalisch and Raynaud
[

]
analyzing spectral projections and establishing

order of accuracy for initial data

develop generalized Laguerre spectral approximations. For the BBM equation, a hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin scheme is presented in
[
15
]
and error analysis of a spectral projection of the rBO equation is given in
[
21
]

For fractional extensions, numerical studies are notably scarce. Klein and Oruc
[
23
]
conducted pioneering numerical investigations of fractional Camassa-Holm equations but without complete numerical analysis. For the fractional BBM equation (
1.1
), analytical studies of solitary waves exist
[
1
,
28
]
, but comprehensive numerical analysis remains lacking. This gap is particularly significant given the physical relevance of sub-dispersive regimes (
\alpha<2
) to nonlinear elastic waves
[
14
]
and other applications where fractional operators model nonlocal interactions and anomalous dispersion.

Fourier spectral Galerkin methods present a natural approach to address these gaps, offering three key advantages for fractional equations. First, they exactly represent fractional operators through Fourier multipliers, avoiding approximation errors in the principal linear term. Second, they inherently preserve geometric structures and conservation laws when properly formulated. Third, they achieve spectral accuracy and provide exponential convergence superior to algebraic rates of finite difference or finite element methods
[
7
,
19
]
for smooth solutions. These advantages make FSG methods particularly suitable for long-time simulations of nonlinear wave phenomena where conservation properties are crucial.

This work develops a comprehensive Fourier spectral Galerkin framework for both the general fractional Camassa-Holm equation (
1.1
) and its fractional BBM reduction (
1.4

For the fractional BBM case, we prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the semi-discrete problem and demonstrate strong convergence to the unique solution of the continuous problem (
1.4
). For the general fCH equation (
1.1
) with initial data
u_{0}\in H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
,
r\geq\alpha
, we establish spectral accuracy in spatial discretization with optimal error estimates. Numerical investigations validate the theoretical framework, including orbital stability of solitary waves and finite-time cusp formation in sub-dispersive regimes. The method effectively handles both smooth solutions and non-smooth peakons, providing a versatile tool for exploring rich solution behaviors across fractional orders.

2.
Preliminaries: Function Spaces and Fractional Operators

This section introduces the function spaces and operators essential for our analysis. We consider
2\pi
-periodic functions defined on the interval
I=[-\pi,\pi]
. The space of square-integrable periodic functions, denoted by
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
, is equipped with the inner product

(u,v)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u(x)\overline{v(x)}dx,

and the induced norm
\|u\|=(u,u)^{1/2}
. For any real number
r\geq 0
, the periodic Sobolev space
H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
is defined as the set of periodic functions for which the norm

\|u\|_{r}=\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+|k|^{2})^{r}|\hat{u}(k)|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

is finite. Every function
\xi\in H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
admits a Fourier series expansion

\xi(x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\xi}(k)e^{ikx},

(2.1)

which converges almost everywhere in
I
, see
[
8
]
. Note that
H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
is a subspace of the standard Sobolev space
H^{r}(I)
, and
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)=H^{0}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
and the norm
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{0}
.Z

For the purpose of spectral approximation, we define the finite-dimensional subspace of trigonometric polynomials

V_{N}:=\operatorname{span}\left\{e^{ikx}:-N\leq k\leq N\right\},\quad N\in\mathbb{N}.

The spaces
V_{N}
serve as natural approximations to
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
. The basis functions
\{e^{ikx}\}_{k=-N}^{N}
are orthogonal with respect to the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
. The orthogonal projection operator
P_{N}:L^{2}(I)\to V_{N}
is defined by

P_{N}\xi(x)=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\hat{\xi}(k)e^{ikx}.

(2.2)

The orthogonality of the basis implies that
P_{N}
satisfies the condition

(P_{N}\xi-\xi,\phi)=0\quad\text{for all}\quad\phi\in V_{N}.

(2.3)

We define the fractional Laplacian
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\geq 0
using the Fourier series expansion (
2.1
) of a function
\xi\in H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(I),~r\geq 0,
as follows

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\xi(x)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{\xi}(k)e^{ikx}.

(2.4)

Since
\phi\in V_{N}
, it has the representation:

\phi(x)=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}c_{k}e^{ikx},

where
c_{k}
are coefficients. Moreover, using the orthogonality of
e^{ikx}
, the Fourier coefficients of
\phi
are

\hat{\phi}(m)=\begin{cases}c_{m}&|m|\leq N,\\
0&|m|>N.\end{cases}

Now, observe that, if
\phi\in V_{N}
, we get

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\phi(x)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{\phi}(k)e^{ikx}=\sum\limits_{k=-N}^{N}|k|^{\alpha}c_{k}e^{ikx}.

Thus,
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\phi\in V_{N}
. The following lemma states several important properties of the fractional Laplacian (
2.4
).

Lemma 2.1

(See
[
13
]
)
.

The fractional Laplacian (
2.4
) satisfies the following properties

i)

For
\xi,\eta\in H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
,
\alpha\geq 0
, we have

\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\xi,\eta\right)=\left(\xi,\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\eta\right),

(2.5)

and

\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\xi_{x},\xi\right)=0.

(2.6)

ii)

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
, then for all
\xi,\eta\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}(I)
, we have

\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\xi,\eta\right)=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}}\xi,\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{2}}\eta\right),

(2.7)

and equivalently,

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\xi=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{2}}\xi=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{2}}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}}\xi.

(2.8)

iii)

For an orthogonal projection
P_{N}
defined by (
2.3
) and
\xi\in H^{r}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
,
r\geq\alpha\geq 0
, the fractional Laplacian (
2.4
) with exponent
\alpha
commutes with
P_{N}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(P_{N}\xi(x))=P_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\xi(x).

(2.9)

Lemma 2.2

(See
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\xi,\eta\in H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
,
\alpha\geq 0
. Then the following estimate holds

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(\xi\eta)\leq C(\alpha)\left(\xi\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\eta+\eta\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\xi\right),

(2.10)

where
C(\alpha)
is a constant depending on
\alpha
. Furthermore, there holds

\norm{\mathcal{D}^\alpha(\xi\eta)}\leq C(\alpha)\left(\norm{\xi}_{\infty}\norm{\mathcal{D}^\alpha\eta}+\norm{\eta}_{\infty}\norm{\mathcal{D}^\alpha\xi}\right),

(2.11)

where
C(\alpha)=\max\left\{1,2^{\alpha-1}\right\}.

The convergence properties of the Fourier projection operator are fundamental to our error analysis. We first state a key lemma quantifying spectral approximation accuracy.

Lemma 2.3

(Spectral Approximation; cf. Chapter 13 in
[
19
]
)
.

For any real
r\geq s\geq 0
and
u\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{r}(I)
, there exists
C>0
independent of
N
such that

\norm{u - P_N u}_{s}\leq CN^{s-r}\norm{u}_{r}.

If
u
is analytic on
I
, there exist positive constants
C,\sigma
independent of
N
such that

\norm{u - P_N u}\leq Ce^{-\sigma N}\norm{u}.

While this result is standard
[
19
]
, we provide a proof for the specific case
\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-P_{N}u)_{x}\|
and
r\geq\alpha+2
required in subsequent analysis.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Using Parseval’s identity, the projection property (
2.3
), and the fact that
P_{N}
commutes with derivatives and the fractional Laplacian from Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\mathcal D^\alpha(u-P_Nu)_x}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\mathcal{D}^\alpha u_x}^{2}-(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x},P_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x})-(P_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x})+\norm{P_N \mathcal D^\alpha u_x}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{2}|k|^{2\alpha}|\hat{u}(k)|^{2}-2\pi\sum_{k=-N}^{N}k^{2}|k|^{2\alpha}|\hat{u}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{|k|>N}k^{2}|k|^{2\alpha}|\hat{u}(k)|^{2}=2\pi\sum_{|k|>N}\frac{|k|^{2r}}{|k|^{2r-2(\alpha+1)}}|\hat{u}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi N^{-2r+2(\alpha+1)}\sum_{|k|>N}|k|^{2r}|\hat{u}(k)|^{2}\leq CN^{-2r+2(\alpha+1)}\norm{\partial_x^ru}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-2r+2(\alpha+1)}\norm{u}_{r}^{2}.

Therefore, we have

\norm{\mathcal D^\alpha(u-P_Nu)_x}\leq CN^{(\alpha+1)-r}\norm{u}_{r}.

(2.12)

Furthermore, let
u
be analytic on
I
, utilizing the above bound and Stirling’s formula
[
19
, Theorem 13.3]
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\mathcal D^\alpha(u-P_Nu)_x}\leq CN^{-r}N^{1+\alpha}\norm{\partial_x^ru}\leq C\frac{r!}{N^{r}}N^{1+\alpha}\norm{u}\leq C\frac{r^{r}e^{-r}}{N^{r}}N^{1+\alpha}\norm{u}\leq CN^{1+\alpha}e^{-cN}\norm{u},

(2.13)

where we have assumed that
r
is proportional to
N
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.4

(See
[
7
]
)
.

Let
u\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{s}(I)
for some
s>0
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
N
such that

\norm{u - P_N u}_{\infty}\leq CN^{-(s-1/2)}\norm{\partial^s_x u}.

(2.14)

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
v_{N}\in V_{N}
. Then

\norm{v_N}_{\infty}\leq CN^{1/2}\norm{v_N},

(2.15)

where
C
is independent of
N
.

Proof.

Express
v_{N}
as

v_{N}(x)=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}c_{k}e^{ikx}.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\absolutevalue{v_N(x)}\leq\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\absolutevalue{c_k}=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}1\cdot\absolutevalue{c_k}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{k=-N}^{N}1^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\absolutevalue{c_k}^{2}},

which implies

\absolutevalue{v_N(x)}\leq\sqrt{2N+1}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}\norm{v_N}\leq CN^{1/2}\norm{v_N}.

Thus

\norm{v_N}_{\infty}\leq CN^{1/2}\norm{v_N}.

∎

Utilizing Theorem
2.4
and
2.5
, the following inequality holds.
For
u_{N}\in V_{N}
and
u\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{s}(I)
for some
s>0
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{u - u_N}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{u - P_N u}_{\infty}+\norm{P_N u - u_N}_{\infty}

(2.16)

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-(s-1/2)}\norm{\partial_x^su}+CN^{1/2}\norm{P_N u - u_N}

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-(s-1/2)}\norm{\partial_x^su}+CN^{1/2}\norm{ u - u_N}.

3.
Fourier Spectral Galerkin Scheme

The variational form of (
1.1
) is obtained by multiplying by a test function
\phi\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{\alpha}(I)
and integrating over
I

(u_{t},\phi)+(u_{t},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\phi)=(f(u),\phi_{x})-\kappa_{2}\left[2(uu_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\phi)+(u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x},\phi)\right].

(3.1)

The Fourier spectral Galerkin approximation seeks solutions
u_{N}(\cdot,t)\in V_{N}
satisfying the variational formulation for all
t>0
and test functions
\phi\in V_{N}
:

\bigl((u_{N})_{t},\phi\bigr)+\bigl((u_{N})_{t},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\phi\bigr)=\bigl(f(u_{N}),\phi_{x}\bigr)-\kappa_{2}\left[2\bigl(u_{N}(u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\phi\bigr)+\bigl(u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x},\phi\bigr)\right].

(3.2)

subject to the initial condition
u_{N}(x,0)=P_{N}u_{0}(x)
. Expressing the numerical solution in Fourier series form
u_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\hat{u}_{k}(t)e^{ikx}
where
\hat{u}_{k}:=\hat{u}(k)
, and choosing
\phi_{m}=e^{imx}
for
m=-N,\dots,N
as test functions, utilizing
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}e^{imx}=|m|^{\alpha}e^{imx}
, we obtain

\frac{d\hat{u}_{m}}{dt}=\frac{1}{1+|m|^{\alpha}}\left(i\cdot m\widehat{f(u_{N})}(m)-\kappa_{2}\left[2|m|^{\alpha}\widehat{u_{N}(u_{N})_{x}}(m)+\widehat{u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x}}(m)\right]\right),

(3.3)

Note that,
\widehat{f(u_{N})}(m)
,
\widehat{u_{N}(u_{N})_{x}}(m)
, and
\widehat{u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x}}(m)
are nonlinear functions of the Fourier coefficients
\hat{u}_{k}
. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{u_{N}(u_{N})_{x}}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l=m\\
|k|,|l|\leq N\end{subarray}}(i\cdot l)\hat{u}_{k}\hat{u}_{l},

\displaystyle\widehat{u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x}}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l=m\\
|k|,|l|\leq N\end{subarray}}|l|^{\alpha}(i\cdot l)\hat{u}_{k}\hat{u}_{l},

\displaystyle\widehat{f(u_{N})}(m)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\hat{u}_{m}+\frac{3}{2}\gamma\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+l=m\\
|k|,|l|\leq N\end{subarray}}\hat{u}_{k}\hat{u}_{l}.

Defining the coefficient vector
\mathbf{U}(t)=[\hat{u}_{-N}(t),\dots,\hat{u}_{N}(t)]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N+1}
, the system (
3.3
) for
m=-N,\dots,N
constitutes the semi-discrete ODE system

\frac{d\mathbf{U}}{dt}(t)=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U}(t)),\quad\mathbf{g}=[g_{-N},\dots,g_{N}]^{T},

(3.4)

where each
g_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{2N+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, for
m=-N,\cdots,N
, are smooth and locally Lipschitz continuous, as the right-hand side involves finite sums of products of Fourier coefficients
\hat{u}_{\ell}
and bounded multipliers
|l|^{\alpha}
for
|l|\leq N
. Initial conditions are specified via the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection
u_{N}(x,0)=P_{N}u_{0}(x)
that follows

\mathbf{U}(0)=\bigl\{\hat{u}_{k}(0)\bigr\}_{k=-N}^{N}=\bigl\{(u_{0},e^{iNx})\bigr\}_{k=-N}^{N},

By the Picard-Lindelöf theorem, there exists
T_{\max}>0
such that (
3.4
) admits a unique solution for
t\in[0,T_{\max})
. As established in Theorem
3.1
, the solution remains uniformly bounded for all
t>0
, permitting global unique extension to
[0,\infty)
.

The semi-discrete scheme preserves the geometric invariants of the continuous equation.

Theorem 3.1

(Conservation)
.

Solutions to the semi-discrete Fourier spectral Galerkin scheme (
3.2
) satisfy:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{I}u_{N}\,dx

\displaystyle=0,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{I}\left(u_{N}^{2}+|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}u_{N}|^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=0.

(3.6)

Proof.

Choose
\phi\equiv 1
in (
3.2
), then using the property (
2.6
) of the fractional Laplacian and
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\phi=0
yields

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{I}u_{N}\,dx=-\kappa_{2}\int_{I}u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x}\,dx=0,

which proves (
3.5
).
Next, choose
\phi=u_{N}
in (
3.2
), and using the fractional Laplacian property (
2.5
), we get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\|u_{N}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}u_{N}\|^{2}\bigr)=\bigl(f(u_{N}),(u_{N})_{x}\bigr)-\kappa_{2}\left[2\bigl(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N}(u_{N})_{x}),u_{N}\bigr)+\bigl(u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x},u_{N}\bigr)\right].

In the right-hand side of the above equation, we employ integration by parts, periodicity of
u_{N}
, and property (
2.5
), which implies

\displaystyle\bigl(f(u_{N}),(u_{N})_{x}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\left(-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}u_{N}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}u_{N}^{3}\right)_{x}dx=0,

\displaystyle 2\kappa_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N}(u_{N})_{x}),u_{N}\bigr)+\kappa_{2}\bigl(u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x},u_{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{2}\bigl(u_{N}^{2},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x}\bigr)+\kappa_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x},u_{N}^{2}\bigr)=0.

Thus

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{I}\left(u_{N}^{2}+|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}u_{N}|^{2}\right)dx=0.

∎

3.1.
Convergence analysis of the FSG scheme

The convergence analysis of the general fCH equation (
1.1
) presents substantial challenges due to the complex nonlinear coupling between the solution
u
and the fractional operator
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}
in the terms
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(uu_{x})
and
u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}
. These nonlinearities introduce analytical difficulties in establishing full well-posedness and convergence results for the spectral method. To overcome these obstacles while retaining essential fractional dynamics, we consider the special case
\kappa_{2}=0
, the fBBM equation (
1.4
). This simplified model eliminates the problematic nonlinear coupling terms while preserving the fractional dissipation through
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{t}
. The resulting structure allows us to establish full convergence results; we prove that the spectral solution
u_{N}
converges to the unique solution of the fBBM equation (
1.4
) using compactness arguments.

Theorem 3.2

(Well-posedness)
.

For initial data
u_{0}\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{r}(I)
with
r\geq\alpha
and
T>0
is finite, there exists a unique solution

u\in C^{1}\!\bigl([0,T];H_{\mathrm{per}}^{r}(I)\bigr)

to (
1.4
).

The constructive proof of Theorem
3.2
follows from the next lemma together with the extension argument formulated in Remark
3.4
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u_{N}
denote the approximate solution to the fBBM equation (
1.4
) obtained via the semi-discrete FSG scheme (
3.2
) with parameters
(\kappa_{1},\gamma,\kappa_{2})=(1,1/3,0)
. For initial data
u_{0}\in H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)
, there exists a time
\bar{T}
and a positive constant
C=C(\|u_{0}\|_{\alpha})
such that the following bounds hold

\displaystyle\|u_{N}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|u_{N}(t)\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|(u_{N})_{t}(t)\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.9)

for all
t\in[0,\bar{T}]
.
Furthermore, as
N\to\infty
, the sequence
\{u_{N}\}
converges to the unique solution of the fBBM equation (
1.4
) in the topology
C^{1}([0,\bar{T}];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)).

Proof.

The bound (
3.7
) follows from the Theorem (
3.1
) for all
t>0
.
To bound
\norm{u_N(t)}_{\alpha}
, consider the FSG scheme (
3.2
) with parameters
(\kappa_{1},\gamma,\kappa_{2})=(1,1/3,0)
and choose test function
\phi=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}
. We have

\displaystyle\left((u_{N})_{t},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\right)+\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\right)=\left(f(u_{N}),(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})_{x}\right)=\left(u_{N},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left((u_{N})^{2},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\right).

Since
u_{N}
is periodic, using integration by parts, property (
2.6
), and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|^{2}\bigr)=-\left(u_{N}(u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\right)\leq\|u_{N}\|_{\infty}\|(u_{N})_{x}\|\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|.

Employing Sobolev inequalities
\|u_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq C\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}
and
\|(u_{N})_{x}\|\leq C\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|\leq C\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}
from
[
13
, Lemma A.1]
, Young’s inequality, and then adding some positive quantity to the right-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{2}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|\leq C\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{4}+C\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|^{2}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(\|u_{N}\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{2}\bigr)+C\bigl(\|u_{N}\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{2}\bigr)^{2}.

Applying analogous energy estimates to the lower-order derivative terms in the full Sobolev norm and combining with (
3.10
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\|u_{N}\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{2}\bigr)\leq C(\|u_{N}\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{2})+C(\|u_{N}\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{2})^{2}.

(3.11)

Define the functional
E(t):=\|u_{N}(t)\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{N}(t)\|_{\alpha}^{2}
. From the differential inequality (
3.11
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dt}\leq 2CE+2CE^{2}=2CE(1+E),

(3.12)

with initial condition
E(0)=E_{0}:=\|u_{0}\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{0}\|_{\alpha}^{2}
. Consider the differential equation

\frac{dz}{dt}=2Cz(1+z),\quad z(0)=E_{0},

(3.13)

where C is independent of
t
. The solution
z(t)=(E_{0}e^{2Ct})/(1+E_{0}-E_{0}e^{2Ct})
is increasing and convex for all time
t<\frac{1}{2C}\ln(1 + E_0^{-1})=:T_{b}
. Define
\bar{T}:=\frac{T_{b}}{2}
. For
t\leq\bar{T}
, we have

\displaystyle e^{2Ct}\leq e^{2C\bar{T}}=\left(1+\frac{1}{E_{0}}\right)^{1/2},

(3.14)

which yields the bound

\displaystyle z(t)\leq z(\bar{T})=\frac{E_{0}(1+E_{0}^{-1})^{1/2}}{1+E_{0}-E_{0}(1+E_{0}^{-1})^{1/2}}\leq 2(1+E_{0}).

(3.15)

Now consider the function
w(t)=z(t)-E(t)
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{dz}{dt}-\frac{dE}{dt}\geq 2Cz(1+z)-2CE(1+E)=2C\left[(z-E)+(z^{2}-E^{2})\right]=2Cw\left(1+z+E\right).

Since
z(t)>0
and
E(t)\geq 0
for
t<\frac{1}{2C}\ln(1 + E_0^{-1})
, we have
1+z+E>0
. With the initial condition
w(0)=z(0)-E(0)=0
, the differential inequality
\frac{dw}{dt}\geq K(t)w
(where
K(t)=2C(1+z+E)>0
) implies
w(t)\geq 0
for
t\in[0,\bar{T}]
by the following argument.
Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists some
t_{1}>0
such that
w(t_{1})<0
. Since
w(0)=0
and
w
is continuous, let
t_{0}=\inf\{t>0:w(t)<0\}
. By continuity,
w(t_{0})=0
, and there exists
\delta>0
such that
w(t)<0
for all
t\in(t_{0},t_{0}+\delta)
.

For
t\in(t_{0},t_{0}+\delta)
, since
w(t)<0
and
K(t)=2C(1+z+E)>0
, the inequality
\frac{dw}{dt}\geq K(t)w
holds. Set
y(t)=-w(t)>0
. Then
\frac{dw}{dt}=-\frac{dy}{dt}
, and substituting gives

\frac{dy}{dt}\leq K(t)y.

Define
v(t)=y(t)\exp\left(-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}K(s)ds\right)
. Then

\frac{dv}{dt}=\left(\frac{dy}{dt}-K(t)y\right)\exp\left(-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}K(s)ds\right)\leq 0,

since
\frac{dy}{dt}\leq K(t)y
. Thus,
v
is non-increasing. At
t=t_{0}
,
y(t_{0})=-w(t_{0})=0
, so
v(t_{0})=0
. Therefore, for
t>t_{0}
,
v(t)\leq v(t_{0})=0
. However, since
y(t)>0
and
\exp\left(-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}K(s)ds\right)>0
for
t>t_{0}
, it follows that
v(t)>0
. This contradiction implies that
w(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,\bar{T}]
.

Thus
E(t)\leq z(t)\leq 2(1+E_{0})
on
[0,\bar{T}]
.
Therefore, we have

\|u_{N}(t)\|_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{2}+\|u_{N}(t)\|_{\alpha}^{2}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\alpha}),

(3.16)

for all
t\leq\bar{T}
. This proves the bound (
3.8
). Now consider the FSG scheme (
3.2
) again with parameters
(\kappa_{1},\gamma,\kappa_{2})=(1,1/3,0)
, and choose test function
\phi=(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})_{t}
that yields

\displaystyle\left((u_{N})_{t},(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})_{t}\right)+\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})_{t},(u_{N})_{t}\right)=\left(f(u_{N}),(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})_{tx}\right).

Once again by the integration by parts and using the periodicity
u_{N}
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the Sobolev inequalities
[
13
, Lemma A.1]
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u_{N})_{t}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left((u_{N})_{x},(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})_{t}\right)-\left(u_{N}(u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|(u_{N})_{x}\|\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\|+\|u_{N}\|_{\infty}\|(u_{N})_{x}\|\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\|+\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{2}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\delta}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\|^{2}+\delta\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\|^{2}+C_{\delta}\|u_{N}\|_{\alpha}^{4}+\delta\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\|^{2},

where we have used Young’s inequality and
\delta>0
is a small constant. Eventually, using the bound (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N})_{t}\|^{2}+\|(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})_{t}\|^{2}\leq C,

for all
t\leq\bar{T}
. Similarly, we get the estimate for the lower-order derivatives. This proves the bound (
3.9
).

The bound (
3.9
) implies that
u_{N}\in\text{Lip}([0,\bar{T}];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I))
for all
N
. Then using (
3.7
), we employ an application of the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, which ensures the sequential compactness of
\{u_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
C([0,\bar{T}];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I))
. Consequently, there exists a subsequence
N_{k}
such that

u_{N_{k}}\to\bar{u}\text{ uniformly in }C([0,\bar{T}];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I))\text{ as }N_{k}\to\infty.

(3.17)

We now show that
\bar{u}
is the unique solution of the fBBM equation (
1.4
). First, claim that
\bar{u}
satisfies the following

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}(\bar{u}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\bar{u})\varphi_{t}\,dx\,dt+\int_{I}(u_{0}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{0})\varphi(\cdot,0)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(\bar{u})\varphi_{x}\,dx\,dt,

(3.18)

for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(I\times[0,\bar{T}])
. Consider a function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(I\times[0,\bar{T}])
. Using the projection
P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t)
as the test function in the FSG scheme (
3.2
) and integrating over
[0,\bar{T}]
, we get

\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}((u_{N})_{t}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t})P_{N}\varphi\,dt=\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(u_{N})(P_{N}\varphi)_{x}\,dt.

(3.19)

Integrating by parts in time for the left-hand side gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}(u_{N})_{t}\varphi\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}u_{N}\varphi_{t}\,dx\,dt-\int_{I}u_{N}(\cdot,0)\varphi(\cdot,0)\,dx,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{t}\varphi\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}\varphi_{t}\,dx\,dt-\int_{I}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}(\cdot,0)\varphi(\cdot,0)\,dx.

For the right-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(u_{N})(P_{N}\varphi)_{x}\,dx\,dt=\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(u_{N})\varphi_{x}\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(u_{N})_{x}(P_{N}\varphi-\varphi)\,dx\,dt.

Now substituting the above identities in (
3.19
) implies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}(u_{N}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N})\varphi_{t}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{I}(u_{N}(\cdot,0)+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}(\cdot,0))\varphi(\cdot,0)\,dx

(3.20)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(u_{N})\varphi_{x}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(u_{N})_{x}(P_{N}\varphi-\varphi)\,dx\,dt.

Since
P_{N}\varphi\to\varphi
and
u_{N}\to\bar{u}
in
C([0,\bar{T}];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I))
, passing to the limit as
N\to\infty
yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}(\bar{u}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\bar{u})\varphi_{t}\,dx\,dt+\int_{I}(u_{0}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{0})\varphi(\cdot,0)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}f(\bar{u})\varphi_{x}\,dx\,dt,

(3.21)

for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(I\times[0,\bar{T}])
, where initial conditions converge as
u_{N}(\cdot,0)=P_{N}u_{0}\to u_{0}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{N}(\cdot,0)\to\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{0}
. Finally, the regularity
\bar{u}\in C^{1}([0,\bar{T}];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I))
implies that
\bar{u}
is actually a strong solution and satisfies the fBBM equation (
1.4
) as an
L^{2}
-identity.

For uniqueness, let
\bar{u}
and
\bar{w}
be solutions of the fBBM equation (
1.4
) with
\bar{u}(x,0)=\bar{w}(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
. Define
v=\bar{u}-\bar{w}
, which satisfies
v(x,0)=0
, periodic condition, and

(1+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha})v_{t}=-(v_{x}+(\bar{u}\bar{u}_{x}-\bar{w}\bar{w}_{x})).

Taking the inner product with
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v
and using Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}v\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-(v_{x}+(\bar{u}\bar{u}_{x}-\bar{w}\bar{w}_{x}),\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v)

\displaystyle=-(v\bar{u}_{x}+\bar{w}v_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v)

\displaystyle=-(\bar{u}_{x},v\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v)-(\bar{w},v_{x}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v).

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Sobolev embeddings
\|v\|_{\infty}\leq C\|v\|_{\alpha}\leq C(\|\bar{u}\|_{\alpha}+\|\bar{w}\|_{\alpha})
and
\|v_{x}\|\leq C\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v\|
from
[
13
, Lemma A.1]
, and Young’s inequality imply

\left|(\bar{u}_{x},v\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v)+(\bar{w},v_{x}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v)\right|\leq K(\|v\|_{\infty}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v\|+\|v_{x}\|\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v\|)\leq KC\left(\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}v\|^{2}\right),

where
K=C\sup_{t\in[0,\bar{T}]}(\|\bar{u}(t)\|_{\alpha}+\|\bar{w}(t)\|_{\alpha})
. The Grönwall’s inequality with
v(0)=0
yields
v=0
. Thus,
\bar{u}
is unique, and the entire sequence
u_{N}
converges to
\bar{u}
in
C^{1}([0,\bar{T}];H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{per}}(I))
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Sjöberg’s technique
[
30
]
can be adapted to extend the local existence result for the fBBM equation to any finite time interval. The crucial point is that the length of the existence interval
[0,\bar{T}]
depends only on
\|u_{0}\|_{H_{\mathrm{per}}^{\alpha}}
. Since the
H_{\mathrm{per}}^{\alpha}
-norm of the exact fBBM solution is uniformly bounded in time
[
1
]
, we can extend the solution iteratively, and one may piece together successive local solutions to obtain a global solution. More precisely, at time
\bar{T}
we define a new initial condition by projection,

u_{\bar{T}}:=P_{N}u(\cdot,\bar{T}),

and solve the fBBM equation with data
u_{\bar{T}}
on the interval
[\bar{T},2\bar{T}]
. Since each such step preserves the
H_{\mathrm{per}}^{\alpha}
-norm, repeating this procedure inductively yields a solution on
[0,T]
for a given finite
T>0
. Also see the approach given in
[
11
]
for finite time wave breaking for similar nonlinear nonlocal shallow water equations.

4.
Optimal error analysis

This section establishes the spectral convergence of the FSG scheme (
3.2
) for the fCH equation (
1.1
). To derive optimal spectral convergence rates, we introduce an
a priori
assumption on the approximation error and subsequently justify its validity through a continuity argument.

Assume the exact solution
u
of (
1.1
) possesses sufficient regularity such that
u\in C([0,T];H^{r}(I))
with
r\geq\alpha+2
. We assume the following error bound for the semi-discrete approximation:

\|u-u_{N}\|\leq CN^{-(\alpha+3/2)}.

(4.1)

A direct consequence of inequality (
2.16
) and the approximation property (
2.3
) yields

\|u-u_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq CN^{-(\alpha+1)}.

(4.2)

The admissibility of (
4.1
) is established in the Remark
4.2
. Define the projection error
\mathcal{E}_{N}(t)\in V_{N}
as

\mathcal{E}_{N}(t):=P_{N}u(t)-u_{N}(t),\quad\mathcal{E}_{N}(0)=P_{N}u_{0}-u_{N}(0)=0.

This quantity represents the discrepancy between the spectral projection of the exact solution and the numerical approximation.

Theorem 4.1

(Spectral convergence)
.

Let
u
denote the exact solution of (
1.1
) satisfying
u\in C([0,T];H^{r}(I))
with regularity index
r\geq\alpha+2
, and let
u_{N}
be the semi-discrete approximation obtained via (
3.2
). There exists a constant
C=C(T,r,\|u\|_{C([0,T];H^{r})})
, independent of the discretization parameter
N
, such that

\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|\leq CN^{-r},

(4.3)

for all
t<T
. If, in addition, the initial data
u_{0}
is real-analytic, then there exist positive constants
C
and
c
, independent of
N
, such that

\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\,\mathrm{e}^{-cN},

for all
t<T
.

Proof.

The error evolution equation is derived by choosing the test function
\phi=\mathcal{E}_{N}
in the weak formulation (
3.2
) and the analysis leverages the commutativity of
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}
with
P_{N}
(Lemma
2.1
). This yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(P_{N}u_{t},\mathcal{E}_{N})+(P_{N}(\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)_{t},\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N})-\bigl((u_{N})_{t},\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigl)-\bigl((\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u_{N})_{t},\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\bigl(f(u)-f(u_{N}),(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\bigr)-\kappa_{2}\Bigl[\bigl(\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{2}_{x}-\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u^{2}_{N})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle+\bigl(u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}-u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\bigl(f^{\prime}(u)(\mathcal{E}_{N}-(P_{N}u-u))+\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}_{u}(u-U_{N})^{2},(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\bigr)}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\kappa_{2}\Bigl[\bigl(\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(2u(\mathcal{E}_{N}-(P_{N}u-u))-(u-u_{N})^{2})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\bigl((u-u_{N})\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}-(u-u_{N})\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-u_{N})_{x}+u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)}_{I_{3}}\Bigr],

where the right-hand side is decomposed using the following Taylor expansion and algebraic identities

\displaystyle f(u)-f(U_{N})

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(u)(u-U_{N})+\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}_{u}(u-U_{N})^{2}=f^{\prime}(u)(\mathcal{E}_{N}-(P_{N}u-u))+\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}_{u}(u-U_{N})^{2},

(4.5)

\displaystyle u^{2}-u^{2}_{N}

\displaystyle=2u(u-u_{N})-(u-u_{N})^{2}=2u(\mathcal{E}_{N}-(P_{N}u-u))-(u-u_{N})^{2},

\displaystyle u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}-u_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u_{N})_{x}

\displaystyle=(u-u_{N})\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}-(u-u_{N})\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-u_{N})_{x}+u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-u_{N})_{x},

where
\tilde{f^{\prime\prime}}_{u}=-\frac{3}{2}\gamma
is the Taylor remainder.
Now, each term
\mathcal{I}_{k}
is estimated using projection properties (
2.3
), the
a priori
assumption (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, smoothness of the exact solution
u
, and Young’s inequality. Consequently for
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\prime}(u)-P_{N}f^{\prime}(u)\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\|(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|+\|P_{N}u-u\|\|(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|\bigr)

\displaystyle+\bigl(P_{N}f^{\prime}(u)(\mathcal{E}_{N}-(P_{N}u-u)),(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\bigr)+C\|u-U_{N}\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\|(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|+\|P_{N}u-u\|\|(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-2r}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2},

where we used the inverse inequality
\|(\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|\leq CN\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|
for
\mathcal{E}_{N}\in V_{N}
. Similar arguments imply

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u-P_{N}u\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\|(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|+\|P_{N}u-u\|\|(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|\bigr)+2\kappa_{2}\bigl(P_{N}u\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N},(\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\bigr)

\displaystyle-2\kappa_{2}\bigl(P_{N}u(P_{N}u-u),(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\bigr)+C\|u-u_{N}\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\|(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|+\|P_{N}u-u\|\|(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-2r}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+C\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}\|_{\infty}\|u-u_{N}\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|-\bigl((u-u_{N})\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle+\bigl((u-P_{N}u)\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)+\bigl(P_{N}u\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(u-u_{N})_{x},\mathcal{E}_{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+\|P_{N}u-u\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\bigr)

\displaystyle+C\|u-u_{N}\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(P_{N}u-u)_{x}\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\bigr)

\displaystyle+C\|u-P_{N}u\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(P_{N}u-u)_{x}\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\bigr)-\bigl(P_{N}u\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N},(\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\bigr)

\displaystyle+\bigl(P_{N}u(P_{N}u-u),(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{N})_{x}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}\|_{\infty}\bigl(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+\|P_{N}u-u\|\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\bigr)

\displaystyle+2CN^{-(\alpha+1)}\bigl(CN^{(\alpha+1)}\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+CN^{-r+(\alpha+1)}\|u\|_{r}\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-2r}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

Substituting estimates on
\mathcal{I}_{1}
,
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{3}
into (
4.4
) produces the differential inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}\right)\leq CN^{-2r}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+C\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

(4.6)

An application of Grönwall’s inequality to (
4.6
) over
[0,T]
gives

\|\mathcal{E}_{N}(t)\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}\mathcal{E}_{N}(t)\|^{2}\leq e^{CT}N^{-2r},\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

The triangle inequality and projection estimate (
2.3
) yield

\displaystyle\|u-u_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq CN^{-r},

which establishes (
4.3
) for all
t\in[0,T]
. When
u
is analytic, using the analytic projection estimate (
2.13
) in the estimates (
4.4
)-(
4
) and similar analysis yields

\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\!\left(\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}\right)\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-2cN}+C\|\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}+C\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}\mathcal{E}_{N}\|^{2}.

An application of Grönwall’s inequality over
[0,T]
then shows

\|\mathcal{E}_{N}(t)\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}\mathcal{E}_{N}(t)\|^{2}\leq\mathrm{e}^{CT}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2cN},\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

Finally, by the triangle inequality and the analytic projection estimate (cf. Lemma
2.3
) we obtain

\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\mathcal{E}_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|u(t)-P_{N}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-cN},

which establishes the exponential convergence claim and completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The admissibility of the assumption (
4.1
) is established via a continuity argument. For
r\geq\alpha+2
and
N>N_{0}
sufficiently large, the constant
C=C(T,r,\|u\|_{C([0,T];H^{r}(I))})
in the error estimate (
4.3
), which can be exactly determined by the final time
T
, satisfies
CN^{-r}<\frac{1}{2}N^{-(\alpha+3/2)}
. Define the critical time

t^{*}:=\sup\left\{\tau\in[0,T]:\|u(t)-u_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq N^{-(\alpha+3/2)}\ \forall t\in[0,\tau]\right\}.

By continuity, we would have

\|u(t^{*})-u_{N}(t^{*})\|_{L^{2}}=N^{-(\alpha+3/2)}.

Now suppose, for contradiction, that
t^{*}<T
. Then the Theorem
4.1
holds for
t\in[0,t^{*}]
, and it implies

\|u(t^{*})-u_{N}(t^{*})\|_{L^{2}}\leq CN^{-r}<\tfrac{1}{2}N^{-(\alpha+3/2)},

contradicting the equality above. Hence
t^{*}\geq T
, validating (
4.1
) throughout
[0,T]
.

6.
Conclusion

We have developed and analyzed a Fourier spectral Galerkin method for the fractional Camassa–Holm equation with periodic boundary conditions. By representing the fractional derivative exactly through Fourier multipliers, the semi–discrete scheme inherits the mass and energy conservation laws of the continuous problem. In the simplified fractional Benjamin–Bona–Mahony setting, we established existence, uniqueness, and convergence of the numerical solution. For general fractional Camassa–Holm equations, we derived optimal error bounds: when the initial data lies in
H^{r}(I)
with
r\geq\alpha+2
, the
L^{2}
–error decays algebraically like
N^{-r}
; for analytic (hence smooth) solutions, the error decays exponentially like
\mathrm{e}^{-cN}
, confirming the spectral accuracy of the method. The numerical experiments corroborated these theoretical findings. Smooth travelling–wave solutions exhibited the expected rapid, nearly machine–precision convergence, while peakon solutions, whose regularity is limited to
H^{1}
, showed the anticipated algebraic decay.

Future research will extend this framework in several directions. One goal is to adapt the spectral method to higher–dimensional generalizations such as the Camassa–Holm–Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation to model two–dimensional wave dynamics. For non–periodic or irregular domains, mesh-free approaches such as radial basis function discretizations could provide a flexible alternative. Additional avenues include designing structure–preserving time–integration schemes for long–time simulations, developing adaptive refinement strategies to resolve singularities, and incorporating variable–order fractional operators to model heterogeneous media. Altogether, the present work lays a rigorous foundation for high–precision numerical exploration of a broad class of nonlinear wave phenomena.
