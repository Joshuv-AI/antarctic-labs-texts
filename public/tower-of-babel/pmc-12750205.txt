Heavy Metals and Microbiological Assessment of the Soil–Plant System of Flooded Areas Applied on Chard (Beta vulgaris)
Authors: Josip Jurković, Mersiha Alkić-Subašić, Emina Sijahović, Hamdija Čivić, Fejzo Bašić
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sewage, animal feces, heavy metals, pathogenic microbes, and other pollutants can contaminate floodwater. Fresh vegetables that have been submerged in floodwater or that might have been exposed to contaminated water are not safe to consume. Pollutants may enter plant tissues in addition to being on the outside of fruits and vegetables. In the autumn of 2024, there were great floods in central Bosnia and Herzegovina. The aim of this study was to determine the effect of floods on heavy metals and microbe content in the soil and plant tissue. Twenty-one samples of soil and sludge were prepared by extraction with aqua regia and EDTA, for the measurement of pseudo-total and available amounts of metals. Samples of chard were taken from flooded areas and from areas outside of floods (controls). The chard samples were divided into roots, stems, and leaves. The quantity of nine heavy metals (Cu, Zn, Ni, Co, Pb, Cd, Fe, Cr, and Mn) was measured by means of Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy with flame atomization (FAAS). Statistical evaluation included descriptive statistics (maximum, minimum, and average), principal component analysis (PCA), correlations, bioaccumulation factor, and translocation factor. The total bacterial count was determined using R2A agar, while the Most Probable Number method was applied for quantifying total coliforms and spore-forming bacteria. The CN analyzer measured the total content of C and N. The average concentrations of metals followed the sequence Cd < Co < Cr < Cu < Ni < Zn < Pb < Mn < Fe. The sludge had the highest average content of Pb, Fe, Cu, and Zn. Soil samples showed high background concentrations of Pb and Cd, and in some cases, concentrations were higher than permissible amounts. In plants, the highest concentrations of heavy metals were found in roots and leaves. Zn, Mn, and Co had positive (root/shoot) translocation factors in all cases. The highest total bacterial count, exceeding log 11, was observed in three soil samples. In contrast, two samples of sludge exhibited a significantly lower bacterial abundance, falling below log 6. Chard showed high ability to accumulate Cu, Zn, Ni, Fe, Mn, and Co. In some cases, chard has amounts of Cd higher than permissible, but it is not a consequence of flooding. Although high concentrations of lead are found in the soil samples, it is mostly kept within the root system.

Introduction

Floods represent the most common form of natural disaster, which occurs when water overflows and inundates land that is typically dry. These events are frequently triggered by intense rainfall, quick melting of snow, or storm surges caused by tropical cyclones or tsunamis in coastal regions. Floods brought on by catastrophic occurrences (such as intense rains and tsunamis) as a result of sudden changes in the global climate can also have an impact on how pollutants are distributed throughout the environment. Some heavy metals are absorbed by particulate matter and retained in sediments after entering the body of water. Heavy metals are released into the environment by sediments when hydrodynamic conditions change (due to dredging or erosion, for example). Pollutants that were previously temporarily stored in the channel are swiftly entrained and moved to the floodplain during floods. The most significant factor in the movement of heavy-metal pollutants linked to particulate matter, especially in catchments with high levels of pollution, is flooding. Particularly in the early stages of floods, the concentrations of suspended particle matter and pollutant contents rise in such river systems as discharge increases. , Because an increase in the water content of polluted soils causes changes in their physicochemical properties, flooding events in floodplains are particularly concerning. The potential accumulation of heavy metals such as Cd, Pb, Cu, Ni, Mn, and Zn into agricultural soils and their uptake by plants are of concern because it may lead to their transfer through the food chain, which can affect human health. Compared to nonleafy vegetables, leafy vegetables generally have higher and more polluted metal uptake rates. Because of their higher capacity for absorption through deposition in contaminated soil, water, and air, leafy vegetables are hyperaccumulators of harmful heavy metals. Chard is a regularly used vegetable in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Chard (Beta vulgaris subsp. cicla) is a biennial plant typically cultivated as an annual for its nutrient-rich, edible foliage. Compared to Spinacia oleracea (true spinach), it exhibits greater resistance to bolting under elevated temperatures, making it a valuable alternative for leafy vegetable production during warmer seasons, when other species are less viable. Propagation is achieved through clustered seeds, necessitating early stage thinning to optimize plant spacing. This initial thinning is suitable for consumption as leafy greens. Harvesting of mature leaves and petioles generally occurs 50–60 days post sowing, with the remaining crown capable of producing subsequent foliage for extended harvesting periods.

Beta vulgaris is known to uptake heavy metals from soil. The amount of metals accessible to biota, also known as the “bioavailable fraction,” is not represented by a measure of the overall concentration of metals in the soil. Except heavy metal contamination, flooding has great effect on soil microbiota. Soil oxygen is depleted during flooding, as a result of microbial activity and water saturation. This results in decreased oxygen levels as well as changes to the nutrient cycle and a decrease in soil nitrogen, manganese, iron, and sulfur.

Analyzing the diversity and abundance of microbial populations in environmental samples, particularly soil and water, is essential for understanding the beneficial roles microorganisms play in supporting the health of plants, animals, and humans. , Although extensive evidence supports the influence of carbon availability and soil moisture on specific microbial populations and ecological processes, the hypothesis that these factors are primary determinants of the microbial community structure and function in soils has been relatively underexplored. Ecological and evolutionary processes within microbial communities tend to select those organisms that respond to changes in moisture and rainfall, though the overall functional response may vary depending on local adaptations. , Soil typically contains an enormous number of microbial cells, ranging from 107 to 1012 cells per gram of dry soil. These microorganisms play a crucial role in decomposing organic matter, releasing mineral nutrients, and driving biogeochemical cycling. Additionally, they are involved in the transformation and degradation of waste materials and synthetic organic compounds. , The risk of fecal contamination in horticultural soils is a common and significant microbial food safety concern due to the potential presence of pathogenic bacteria. Because testing soils for specific pathogens can be costly and technically complex, fecal indicator bacteria, such as total coliforms (TC), fecal coliforms, Escherichia coli, enterococci, and spore-forming bacteria (SFB), are frequently used to indicate the possible presence of fecal matter and associated pathogens. − Traditional culture-based methods, including the spread plate technique and the Most Probable Number (MPN) method or its modifications, remain fundamental tools in environmental microbiology. These approaches offer valuable insights into microbial communities and support efforts in sustainable soil and water management. However, the pour plate method may be unsuitable for cultivating microorganisms that are sensitive to the elevated temperatures involved in mixing samples with molten agar. , From all of the facts stated above, the aims of this research arise:1.To determine the effect of flooding on the pseudo-total and bioavailable forms of heavy metals in soil, and their distribution within chard.2.To assess microbiological characteristics of flooded agricultural areas

Materials and Methods

A total of 21 soil samples were collected from a flood-affected region in Bosnia and Herzegovina (B&H), including nine control (unflooded) soil samples, nine flooded soil samples, and three sewage-contaminated samples (Figure .). All samples were obtained directly from agricultural fields within 3–4 days following the recession of heavy rainfall. However, detailed records of soil management practices prior to the flooding event are not available. On the flooded areas and in the controls, chard samples were also taken. Chard samples were divided into three parts: roots, stem, and leaves and dried in an oven for 48 h at a temperature of 40 °C. After drying, the samples were milled to fine powder.

Map of Bosnia and Herzegovina, with sampling positions.

After sampling, soil samples were air-dried, milled, and sieved. One part of the soil samples was used for heavy metal measurement and the other for microbiological assessment.

Methods of Analysis

Soil Analysis

Basic Quality Parameters

The pH value was measured using a potentiometric method on a Mettler Toledo MP 220. The instrument was calibrated with buffers having pH values of 4.21 and 7.00. The soil extraction solution ratio was 1:5 (10 g of soil:50 mL of extraction solution), prepared as described in literature. The measurement was made using two different extraction solutions: ultrapure water and potassium chloride (0.1 mol/L). C and N content is a paremeter like pH. Elemental analysis was performed using a Velp Scientifica CN 802 analyzer with a sample size of 50–100 mg. Helium was used as the carrier gas. The software used for our device is CNSoft software.

Heavy Metal Analysis

Pretreatment and Analysis of Soil Samples

Milled soil samples were prepared for determination of pseudo-total (M-t) and available EDTA extractable (M-a) content. Pseudo-total amounts of metals were extracted in aqua regia (concentrated HNO3 and HCl in a 1:3 ratio), in accordance to ISOISO 11466:1995. Bioavailable contents of heavy metals are often determined by chelating of metal ions with EDTA. Extraction of available forms of heavy metals from soil was performed using the EDTA solution (a mixture of 1 M (NH4)2CO3 and 0.01 M EDTA (ethylene-diamino-tetra acetic acid) with the pH of the solution adjusted to 8.6 using HCl or NH4OH). 10 g of soil with a diameter of 2 mm was poured with 20 mL of EDTA solution and placed on a shaker for 30 min, after which it was filtered and measured. Concentrations of heavy metals were measured by means of FAAS and a Shimadzu A-7000 instrument, in accordance to ISO 11047, 1998.

Pretreatment and Microbiological Analysis

After collection, soil samples were air-dried at 20 °C in a dark, well-ventilated room. For microbiological analysis, 30 g of each sample was placed into a sterile plastic bag and then manually shaken to homogenize the material and facilitate the separation of bacterial cells from clay particles, organic matter, and microaggregates within the soil matrix. Two culture-dependent methods were employed to quantify soil bacterial populations: (A) total bacterial counts, expressed as colony-forming units per gram of soil (CFU g–1), and (B) the MPN technique to assess fecal contamination, including TC and SFB. ,, (A)For the total bacterial count (CFU g–1), 1 g of dried soil was mixed with 9 mL of sterile 0.85% NaCl solution (HiMedia, India) in a 15 mL conical tube. The suspension was vortexed at the maximum speed for 5 min, followed by a 10-fold serial dilution in sterile saline to achieve appropriate bacterial concentrations. From each dilution, 100 μL was plated in duplicate on R2A agar (Reasoner’s 2A, HiMedia, India).(B)For the MPN method, a 3 × 3 protocol was followed, consisting of three 10-fold serial dilutions (1:10, 1:100, 1:1000), with three replicate tubes per dilution. For TC, 1 mL from each dilution was inoculated into 10 mL of sterile MacConkey Broth Purple (HiMedia, India) in 16 mL × 160 mm glass tubes. For SFB, 1 g of undiluted soil and 1 mL of the 1:10 and 1:100 dilutions were placed into sterile 16 mL × 160 mm glass tubes. Each tube was then supplemented with 10 mL of sterile Sulfite Polymyxin Sulfadiazine (SPS) agar (HiMedia, India) and cooled to 50 °C. The tubes were heat-treated in a water bath at 85 °C for 20 min to inactivate vegetative cells and stimulate spore germination and then cooled at 15 °C for 10 min. A modified MPN method integrating agar-based media was developed for the routine enumeration of heterotrophic bacteria in environmental samples and compared with the traditional spread plate technique for microbial recovery efficiency. ,

Culture Media and Conditions

All microbiological media were prepared according to the manufacturer’s specifications and sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min prior to use.

Inoculated R2A agar plates were air-dried for 5 min and then incubated in an inverted position at 30 °C for 7 days under aerobic conditions. Colony counts were recorded when the number of colonies per plate ranged between 30 and 300. , MacConkey Broth tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 2 days. A positive result for TC was indicated by a color change from purple to yellow and pink. SPS agar tubes were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 7 days (AnaeroGen, Oxoid, UK), with positive results indicated by a color change from yellow to black.

Descriptive Statistics of the Available Heavy Metal Content

Plant Analysis

The extraction of heavy metals from the plant material was done with the use of nitric and sulfuric acid (in a ratio of 2:1) according to the method specified in the research paper of Momen et al., 2006. The determination of the content of heavy metals in plant material samples and the content of total and accessible forms of heavy metals in soil samples was performed using the method of flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry on a Shimadzu 7000 AA apparatus (ISO 11047, 1998). ,

Statistical Evaluation

Statistical analysis was performed using statistical software PAST (version 4.06b) and SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics is presented by the maximum, minimum, and average concentrations of heavy metals in soil and plant tissue. Pearson’s correlation was employed to assess the extent of similarity between two sets of results and to see if the principal component analysis (PCA) was justified. Following is a range of correlation coefficients (r) that show how strong the trends and correlations under analysis are: A relationship is considered extremely weak if it is between 0.0 and 0.19, weak if it is between 0.20 and 0.39, moderate if it is between 0.40 and 0.59, strong if it is between 0.60 and 0.79, and very strong if it is between 0.80 and 1.0. A smaller collection of observed variablesthat is, groups represented as principal components (PCs)that drive the multivariate correlations between the various metal concentrations was found using PCA. In ArcGis Pro (v 3.4.0), inverse distance weighting (IDW) was used to visualize the findings spatially. The heavy metal concentrations in soil were spatially interpolated using this method, which demonstrated the distribution of heavy metals after flooding. The bioaccumulation factor (BCF) is defined as the ratio of the metal concentration in the plant material (silver birch leaves) to the metal concentration in the soil (mine waste). BCF provides information about the availability of silver birch to accumulate chalcophile elements in its tissue. , According to Kabata-Pendias & Dudka, BCF is characterized as follows: intensive accumulation (BCF >1), medium accumulation (0.1 < BCF <1), weak accumulation (0.01 < BCF <0.1), no probability of accumulation (BCF <0.01). In order to evaluate the capacity of chard to transfer metals from the roots to its aboveground tissues, the leaf/root metal concentration ratios were calculated. This ratio has different names such as translocation factor (TF), transfer factor, or transportation index. A high TF value indicates the ability of the plants to efficiently extract and transport the element from the root to the shoot system.

Results and Discussion

Soil Analysis

Basic Quality Parameters

The results of basic quality parameter determination are presented in Table .

The pH value ranged from very slightly acidic (6.7) to moderately basic (8.1). pH in water was always higher than the pH in KCl. The reason lies in the fact that soil pH in KCl measures the acidity of the soil solution, as well as the reserve acidity in the soil colloids. The results obtained for pH value are in accordance to other studies in close area. ,, Flooded areas exhibited a reduction in the total carbon (C) content and the lowest average carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio among the studied sites. Notably, the highest total carbon content was observed in the flood-associated sludge deposits. A higher C/N ratio in soil indicates a greater proportion of carbon relative to nitrogen, which is typically associated with slower rates of organic matter decomposition and an increased potential for nitrogen immobilization. Under such conditions, soil microbial communities may sequester available nitrogen to decompose carbon-rich substrates, thereby reducing the nitrogen availability for plant uptake. In contrast, when organic substrates possess a C/N ratio between approximately 1 and 15, rapid nitrogen mineralization tends to occur, enhancing the immediate availability of nitrogen for plant absorption. Thus, lower C/N ratios are generally associated with greater nitrogen release into the soil, supporting short-term crop nutrient demands.

Heavy Metal Content in Soil Samples

Pseudo-Total Amounts (M-t)

Descriptive statistics of the pseudo-total amounts of heavy metals determined in soil samples is shown by Table .

The average concentrations of heavy metals gained with aqua regia extraction lie in the following sequence: Mn > Fe > Pb > Zn > Ni > Cu > Cr > Co > Cd. Among heavy metals, manganese had the highest average value. Manganese content falls within the natural range in soil (20–3000 mg/kg). When three different sampling subjects are compared, the sludge is depleted in manganese content. The second most abundant element was iron. The fact that manganese and iron are the most abundant is expected for agricultural soils because they play a significant role regarding soil particulate matter. Lead took third place by abundance as a surprise. The average content of lead in investigated soils (flooded, controls, and sludge) was above the maximum permissible amount (80 mg/kg). Zinc showed quite uniform concentrations in all three of the investigated areas. The concentrations of zinc in soils (17–125 mg/kg) presented by Kabata-Pendias , are applicable to the results of this research. In one case, nickel showed higher concentrations than permissible (35 mg/kg). Chromium, cobalt, and cadmium showed lower concentrations than maximum permissible. − The results showed that sediments transported by floodwaters were high in Pb, Cu, Co, and Fe. In terms of potentially harmful exposure to trace metals, only the levels of Pb seem to be alarming. Even minimal amounts of Pb can significantly jeopardize human health. Pb has several toxic effects in both humans and animals, including interference with hemoglobin synthesis, gastrointestinal disorders, and damage to the central nervous system. Sludge was depleted in Mn, Ni, Zn, and Cr content. The depletion of certain heavy metals is determined by the change in redox conditions

Available Amounts (M-a)

Descriptive statistics of pseudo-total amounts of heavy metals determined in soil samples is shown by Table .

The average concentrations of heavy metals gained with EDTA extraction lie in the following sequence: Pb > Mn > Fe > Cu > Zn > Ni > Co > Cr > Cd. All investigated heavy metals had much lower available concentrations. The decreasing trend of extractability is presented in the following sequence: Cu > Pb > Zn > Ni > Fe > Co > Mn > Cr > Cd.

In the case of cadmium, there were no detectable amounts by means of AAS. Copper showed the highest extractability with EDTA compared to aqua regia (average: 46.53%). Copper is in a large portion present as easily extractable fractions. A similar result (50% extractability) was reported by Manouchehri et al. and Di Palma and Medici. , Straightforward Cu extraction methods commonly utilized in soil analysis, like total Cu or EDTA-extractable Cu measurements, offer sufficient indicators of Cu bioavailability for specific soil types. Interestingly, lead was also highly extractable (average: 18.7%), which means that lead is partially present as soluble fractions, which form complexes with EDTA. EDTA has high binding affinity toward Pb. , Instead of EDTA, some other complexing agents can be used to assess the bioavailability of heavy metals. To enhance the solubility of heavy metals in soil, EDTA is the most effective chelator for Pb, DPTA and EDTA are more suitable for Zn and Cd, and EDDS, EDTA, and DTPA are effective for Cu.

The highest concentration of iron was found in the sludge and the lowest in the flooded area. Sludge was depleted in manganese content and richer in lead content. Floods brought significantly higher amounts of iron and lead than was present as background (controls). The level of contamination with lead could be increased by precipitation of the sludge after flooding.

The sludge, after flooding, showed a decrease in the content of zinc, nickel, and chromium. This suggests that the flooding process may have led to leaching, dilution, or redistribution of these metals. Conversely, the sludge became enriched in copper and cobalt, indicating that these metals either concentrated due to flooding or were introduced into the sludge from external sources during or after the flood event. The overall metal composition in the sludge has undergone a significant change, with certain elements being depleted while others are enriched, which could be due to environmental factors, such as water flow, chemical reactions, or the introduction of new materials during the flooding process.

Metal mapping showed that Fe concentrations are the highest in sludge and in controls, gradually decreasing toward the flooded areas (Figure ). The spatial trends of Pb, Ni, Cu, and Zn were similar but less gradual than that of Fe, with hotspots (i.e., elevated concentrations) and depleted zones (i.e., low concentrations). However, care must be taken when interpreting the spatial interpolation of heavy metal pollution. From southeastern to northwestern directions, the maps show the geographical distribution of heavy metal pollution in the soil along the sampling spots. However, a denser sampling grid is required because the extrapolation beyond the sampling points (northeast, southeast) is unreliable.

From ecologically significant correlations, we can highlight the following correlations: the second highest positive correlation is found in the case of Cu and C (r = 0.80), which means that copper is probably mainly bonded to organic matter, which was also described earlier. − Copper also showed high correlations to pHw and pHK, 0.71 and 0.65, respectively. This means that adsorption to soil particles of copper increases with an increase of pH, and a decrease of pH results in desorption of copper. Sanders showed that at a given copper concentration the quantity of copper adsorbed increased with pH increase. Copper showed a high positive correlation with nickel (r = 0.77) and a high negative correlation with cadmium (r = −0.85) and manganese (r = −0.72). The negative correlation with cadmium could be explained with the fact that cadmium is much more mobile (desorbed) than copper. Manganese is very important metal regarding soil particulate matter (together with organic matter). Regarding its high negative correlation with copper, it is another proof that copper is mainly connected to organic matter. Lead showed moderate correlations with zinc (r = 0.53) and cobalt (r = 0.50). Lead, zinc, and cobalt are frequently found together in soil due to their similar chemical behavior and tendency to form similar mineral compounds, particularly sulfides. , Both metals can be mobilized by the same processes and can also be found in similar locations within the soil profile, such as in subsoils, where they are less influenced by anthropogenic contamination. However, they also exhibit distinct differences in their behavior and impact on the soil ecosystem. Except with copper, nickel showed strong positive correlations with chromium. This finding could be the result of agricultural practices which was described earlier by Flem et al. Nickel showed a strong negative correlation with manganese (r = −0.65), which could be explained by nickel not bonding primarily with manganese oxides but rather with organic matter (r = 0.67). Nickel and lead showed a moderate correlation with cobalt (r = 0.58 and r = 0.50). They all can originate from sulfide ores within the soil. Chromium showed a very strong negative correlation with manganese (r = −0.89) and a strong correlation with total carbon (r = 0.79). Based on the correlations, it can be concluded that chromium is mainly adsorbed onto organic matter and not onto manganese oxides. The geochemical behavior of chromium is highly affected by soil organic matter. Opposite behavioral characteristics were shown by cadmium, with a strong positive correlation with manganese (r = 0.66) and a strong negative correlation with carbon (r = −0.76). The obtained results strongly indicate that cadmium is mainly adsorbed onto manganese compounds, which is also described in the research paper of. Manganese showed a very strong negative correlation with total carbon (r = −0.87) and again a strong negative correlation with nitrogen (r = −0.64). Based on these correlations, it can be concluded that manganese as a particulate matter compound behaves differently compared to organic particulate matter. Total carbon and total nitrogen (TN) show strong and positive correlations (r = 0.83). Since they can be used to differentiate between marine and terrestrial sources of organic matter, environmental depositional conditions, pollution indices, and indicators of soil quality and productivity, total organic carbon and TN are the most significant components of sediments and soils.

Microbiological Soil Assessment

The average viable bacterial counts (log CFU g–1) were determined by identifying the lowest variation in cell numbers among replicates cultured on the R2A agar medium (Table ). Among the 21 soil samples analyzed, the highest total bacterial counts, exceeding log 11, were observed in samples 13, 4, and 9. In contrast, the lowest counts, below log 6, were recorded in samples 19 and 21, both collected from a nearby location within the same municipality. Notably, all samples with high bacterial counts were dominated by Actinomycetes following cultivation on R2A agar.

Fecal contamination in the soil samples was evaluated by quantifying TC and total SFB using the MPN technique. This method is particularly effective for detecting the low concentrations of microorganisms when direct enumeration is impractical.

Of the 21 soil samples analyzed, 17 showed no detectable levels of TC. Detectable concentrations were found in two control samples (6 and 7) and one sludge sample (21), although all were below 10 MPN per gram. Only one flooded soil sample (14) exhibited a higher concentration, measuring 16 MPN per gram (Table ). The presence of TC, fecal coliforms, and E. coli can occur in soils not directly influenced by agricultural activities or human waste. However, most studies on the persistence of fecal indicator bacteria and pathogenic microorganisms in soil have focused on environments impacted by the application of animal manure or slurry.

The highest MPN index for SFB was recorded in sample 7, with values significantly exceeding those of all other soil samples (Table .). The MPN analysis for SFB was conducted following the S2 protocol developed for farm milk and soil samples. , According to Heyndrickx, total anaerobic spore-forming bacterial counts in various soil types typically range from 2.0 to 5.2 log CFU g–1, while the counts of aerobic/facultative anaerobic Bacillus cereus spores in some soil samples range from 4.9 to 5.0 log CFU g–1.

In control soil samples, the total bacterial count showed a weak positive correlation with both pH measured in potassium chloride (pH KCl; r = 0.31) and pH measured in water (pH H2O; r = 0.15). Similarly, weak positive correlations were observed between the total bacterial count and available carbon content (C %) in relation to pH KCl (r = 0.31) and pH H2O (r = 0.38). A comparable trend was noted between the total bacterial count and available nitrogen content (N %), though correlations with pH H2O were low (r = 0.15 and r = 0.04, respectively).

Soil moisture is a critical driver of microbial activity and significantly influences carbon (C) emissions to the atmosphere. In flooded soil samples, total heterotrophic bacterial counts exhibited strong negative correlations with both pH H2O (r = −0.80) and pH KCl (r = −0.87). A moderate negative correlation was found between bacterial counts and available carbon content (r = −0.68), while a weaker negative correlation was observed with available nitrogen content (r = −0.44). These results align with previous findings that wetland soil microbial communities are primarily composed of heterotrophic organisms, whose growth is often limited by the availability of carbon and essential nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus.

In sludge samples, no correlation was observed between the total bacterial count and pH H2O (r = 0), while a strong negative correlation was found with pH KCl (r = −0.87). This was accompanied by a moderate negative correlation between the bacterial count and available carbon content (r = −0.50) and a moderate positive correlation with available nitrogen content (r = 0.50). Across all analyzed samples, significant correlations, both positive and negative, were identified among total bacterial count, pH (both H2O and KCl), and the availability of carbon and nitrogen.

Soil carbon content indirectly affects a range of soil physicochemical properties, including water retention, aggregation, pH buffering, and cation exchange capacity, all of which are influenced by the microbial community structure and activity. , According to Wilson et al., flooding leads to a rapid increase in carbon turnover and microbial activity, characterized by enhanced carbon mineralization, enzyme production, and substrate utilization, likely driven by opportunistic bacteria. Within 3 to 7 days post flooding, carbon availability increased, enzyme activity peaked, and microbial substrate diversity declined. By day 7, the first detectable shifts in the microbial community composition were observed.

Because of the substantial variation in heavy-metal concentrations, the results for the plant material are presented separately in Figures and .

In most cases, zinc and manganese had the highest concentrations in the leaves, while the concentration of iron varied significantly. The iron concentration in most cases was the highest in roots and less in green tissues such as leaves and stems. The analysis of iron concentrations across various organs or components of more than 1000 plant species has revealed significant variations both between and within species, with few identifiable patterns, if any. Zinc, manganese, and iron are essential micronutrients for plant health, impacting photosynthesis, enzyme activity, and overall growth. Lead concentration was higher in the roots. After being absorbed through soil solutions, Pb-ions primarily gather in the roots because of the barriers created by Casparian strips in the root endodermis.

Copper, nickel, and cobalt were higher in leaves in most cases. Copper and nickel are essential metals for plant growth, and cobalt is potentially essential. The higher concentrations of these metals in the leaves suggest that they may play an important role in plant metabolism or may be actively transported to the leaves for physiological processes, such as photosynthesis or enzyme activation. The presence of cobalt in the leaves might also indicate that it serves a functional, though less well-defined, role in plant health. Cobalt is potentially essential. Chromium was higher in roots, while cadmium varied among different parts of the chard. Chromium is toxic to plants, and it is mainly accumulated within the roots. The variability of the cadmium distribution across different parts of the chard may suggest that cadmium uptake and translocation within the plant are more complex. Its distribution could be influenced by environmental factors, plant age, or even specific mechanisms of detoxification of cadmium in various tissues.

EDTA extraction can mobilize metals that are otherwise tightly bound to soil particles, making them available for extraction and potentially for uptake by plants and other organisms.

Available copper showed a good correlation with amounts present within the plants, while other metals were higher in the plant tissue, meaning that they might be bioaccumulated by chard. The discrepancy observed between EDTA-extractable metals and the concentration in the plant tissue could be due to the specific plant uptake mechanisms, the interaction between metals and the soil matrix, and the plant’s ability to selectively accumulate metals from the soil. It is important to consider these dynamics when assessing the potential risks of metal contamination in plants and to use both chemical extraction methods and biological analyses for a fuller understanding of metal bioavailability and toxicity.

Higher TF means that plants have a higher ability to accumulate heavy metals. A TF greater than 1 shows that the plant stands against the contaminant as well as used the pollutant in a positive way. Copper, zinc, nickel, iron, manganese, and cobalt had in most cases a TF greater than 1. This result is expected since those metals are essential for plants. Except lead, other toxic elements, cadmium and chromium in four cases, had a TF greater than one, meaning that cadmium and chromium, although toxic, are accumulated by chard. Toxic metals (Cd and Pb) often accumulate heavily in roots because plants limit their translocation to protect photosynthetic tissues. They are sequestered in vacuoles or bound to cell wall components. , Essential metals like Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn also accumulate in roots initially but are actively transported to shoots as needed. Lower accumulation is often found in stems. Essential metals like Fe, Cu, and Mn often accumulate in leaves to support chloroplast function, redox homeostasis, and antioxidant enzyme activity (e.g., SODs use Cu/Zn, Mn).
