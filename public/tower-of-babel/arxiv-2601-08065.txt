A New Strategy for Verifying Reach-Avoid Specifications in Neural Feedback Systems
Samuel I. Akinwande, Sydney M. Katz, Mykel J. Kochenderfer, and Clark Barrett

Abstract
Forward reachability analysis is the predominant approach for verifying reach-avoid properties in neural feedback systems—dynamical systems controlled by neural networks. This dominance stems from the limited scalability of existing backward reachability methods. In this work, we introduce new algorithms that compute both over- and under-approximations of backward reachable sets for such systems. We further integrate these backward algorithms with established forward analysis techniques to yield a unified verification framework for neural feedback systems.



1
Introduction

Neural feedback systems—dynamical systems controlled by neural networks—are becoming increasingly prevalent, with applications in robotics, autonomous driving
[
3
]
, aerospace autonomy
[
4
]
, and beyond. As these systems proliferate, the need to ensure compliance with safety and design specifications has become increasingly critical. In particular, it is essential to verify that neural feedback systems operate as intended under all relevant conditions.

Common approaches for verifying specification satisfaction include sampling, invariance analysis, and reachability analysis
[
1
,
2
,
5
]
.
Sampling, a form of falsification, seeks to identify violations of a desired specification by evaluating a finite set of scenarios.
This approach is widely used in industrial practice due to its scalability and generality, but it cannot provide formal guarantees.
Invariance analysis, by contrast, offers formal guarantees by synthesizing a certificate function—typically a Lyapunov and/or barrier function—that certifies specification satisfaction.
However, such certificates are often difficult to construct in practice.
Reachability analysis evaluates all possible system states to determine whether a given property is satisfied or violated.
It provides formal guarantees and is applicable to a broad class of systems, but suffers from a trade-off between precision and scalability: precise methods scale poorly due to the curse of dimensionality.

Most existing methods for reachability analysis of neural feedback systems rely on forward analysis, propagating the system dynamics forward to compute all possible states.
This reliance arises because computing a backward pass through a neural network is particularly challenging
[
6
]
.
However, the exclusive use of forward analysis often amplifies the inherent limitations of reachability analysis.

In this work, we explore a strategy that combines an existing forward analysis method with new techniques for backward analysis of neural feedback systems, aiming to mitigate some of these limitations.

2
Problem Statement

Following
[
1
]
, we define a discrete-time neural feedback system
\mathcal{D}
as the tuple

\langle n,I,F,E,u,\delta,T,G,A\rangle,

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the
dimension
of the system (i.e., each state is an element of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
),

I\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the set of
initial states
,

F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is a sequence of
state update functions
with
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
perturbation set
(i.e., the set of admissible disturbance terms that may affect the next state),

u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
control function
,

\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the
time-step size
,

T\in\mathbb{N}
is the
number of time steps
,

G\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the set of
goal states
,
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the set of
avoid states
(i.e., unsafe states).

States evolve over a sequence of
T
discrete time steps, each of duration
\delta
, giving a total time horizon of
\delta\cdot T
.
For a current state
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the next-state function
\mathit{next}^{\mathcal{D}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
defines the possible successor states.
This set-valued mapping captures the nondeterminism introduced by the perturbation term.
For each
i\in[1,\ldots,n]
,

\displaystyle\mathit{next}^{\mathcal{D}}(\vec{x})_{i}=\big\{\vec{x}_{i}+\big(f_{i}(\vec{x})+u(\vec{x})_{i}+\epsilon_{i}\big)\cdot\delta\;\big|\;\epsilon\in E\big\},

(1)

Note that
f_{i}
and
u
may depend on the entire previous state
\vec{x}
, not only on the component
\vec{x}_{i}
.
We assume that the neural network controller
u
is a fully-connected, feed-forward network with
n
inputs,
n
outputs, and ReLU activations.

The previous-state function
\mathit{prev}^{\mathcal{D}}:2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
defines the
preimage
(or backward projection) of the forward evolution operator, and is given by

\displaystyle\mathit{prev}^{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{X})=\big\{\,\vec{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\big|\;\mathit{next}^{\mathcal{D}}(\vec{y})\subseteq\mathcal{X}\,\big\}.

(2)

We define a forward trajectory
\mathbf{F}^{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
, as the sequence of state sets
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{T})
, where
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathit{next}^{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{X}_{i-1})
for all
i\in[1,T]
.
Similarly, we define a backward trajectory
\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{X}_{T})
, where
\mathcal{X}_{T}
is a target set, as the sequence of state sets
(\mathcal{X}_{T},\mathcal{X}_{T-1},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{0})
, where
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathit{prev}^{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{X}_{i+1})
for all
i\in[0,T-1]
.

We say that a system
\mathcal{D}
is
safe
if it satisfies either the forward or backward reach-avoid properties.
The
forward reach-avoid properties
are given by Equations (
3
):

\displaystyle\forall\,\vec{x}_{0}\in I.\>\exists\,t\in[0..T].\>\mathbf{F}^{\mathcal{D}}(\{\vec{x}_{0}\})_{t}\subseteq G,

(3a)

\displaystyle\forall\,t\in[0..T].\>\mathbf{F}^{\mathcal{D}}(I)_{t}\cap A=\emptyset,

(3b)

and the
backward reach-avoid properties
are given by Equations (
4
):

\displaystyle\exists\,t\in[0..T].\>\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}(G)_{t}\supseteq I,

(4a)

\displaystyle\forall\,t\in[0..T].\>\mathbf{B}^{\mathcal{D}}(A)_{t}\cap I=\emptyset.

(4b)

Equations
3a
and
4a
are the
reach
properties.
They express that every forward trajectory starting from a state in the initial set eventually reaches the goal set, or that every backward trajectory starting from the terminal set traces back to the initial set within the specified number of time steps.
Conversely, Equations
3b
and
4b
are the
avoid
properties, which require that system trajectories avoid unsafe states at every time step within the given horizon.

Although the forward and backward properties are similar in intent, they differ subtly in interpretation.
Forward properties assert that all trajectories initiated from the initial set satisfy the reach-avoid conditions, whereas backward properties characterize the set of initial states from which such safe trajectories can originate.

Formally, we aim to establish that
\mathcal{D}
is safe.
Equations
3
and
4
are
sufficient
to verify safety, but relying solely on either forward or backward reachability analysis is limited by the challenges discussed earlier.
Our goal is to develop a verification strategy that jointly leverages Properties
3
and
4
to soundly and effectively establish system safety.

3
Proposed Methods

3.1
Forward Reachability Analysis

Forward reachability analysis verifies forward reach-avoid properties by solving Equation
1
over
T
time steps for all initial states in
I
.
We employ the method of
[
1
]
to compute an overapproximation of the true forward reachable set.

3.2
Backward Reachability Analysis

To compute backward reachable sets, we must solve Equation
2
.
The key challenge is that no scalable algorithms currently exist for directly solving Equation
2
.
Our objective, therefore, is to develop algorithms that can soundly approximate this equation in a computationally tractable manner.
Given a system
\mathcal{D}
and a target set
\mathcal{T}
, we can compute a lower bound on Equation
2
by solving the following problem.

\displaystyle\min\;\vec{x}_{i}^{t},

(5a)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\quad\vec{x}_{i}^{t+1}=\vec{x}_{i}^{t}+\big(f_{i}(\vec{x}^{t})+u(\vec{x}^{t})_{i}+\epsilon_{i}\big)\cdot\delta,

(5b)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n},\quad\epsilon\in E,\quad\vec{x}_{i}^{t+1}\in\mathcal{T}.

(5c)

Replacing the minimization with a maximization yields an upper bound.
Together, these bounds define a hyperrectangle that provides a guaranteed overapproximation of the true backwards reachable set (BRS).
We exploit this property to compute an under approximation of the BRS by formulating a constrained optimization problem: identify the largest subset of the overapproximation whose overapproximated forward image remains within the target set.
We outline three methods for solving this problem.

3.2.1
Golden Section Search (GSS)

This method estimates the largest admissible under hyperrectangle via a golden section search. The overapproximate hyperrectangle has center
\vec{c}
and radii
\vec{r}
, and the underapproximate hyperrectangle is parameterized as
\vec{c}\pm\rho\vec{r}
with
\rho\in(0,1)
. We seek the maximal
\rho
such that the overapproximated forward image of the under hyperrectangle remains within the target set.

3.2.2
Iterative Convex Hull (ICH)

Because GSS may require many forward queries, we propose a sampling-based alternative. We densely sample the overapproximate hyperrectangle, propagate each point using an inexpensive point query, and label samples as positive or negative depending on containment in the target set. The positive samples define a
candidate
underapproximate hyperrectangle, which is validated using an overapproximated query. If containment fails, the candidate becomes the new overapproximation, and the process repeats.

3.2.3
Largest Empty Box (LEB)

For highly nonconvex reachable sets, we instead solve a small MILP to find the largest
candidate
underapproximate hyperrectangle that excludes all negative samples from the ICH procedure. This candidate is then evaluated and refined using the same containment check.

3.3
The FaBRe Verification Strategy

Rather than exclusively computing forward or backward reachable sets, we propose verifying
\mathcal{D}
by combining both analyses.
Specifically, we partition the planning horizon into
T=F+B
steps, where
F
denotes the number of forward-analysis steps and
B
the number of backward-analysis steps.
The subdivision is treated as a configurable parameter of the solver. This yields the
F
orward
a
nd
B
ackward
Re
achability analysis strategy.

To establish that the system satisfies a reach property (Properties
3a
and
4a
), we perform
F
steps of forward analysis from
I
and
B
steps of backward analysis from
G
.
If the overapproximation of the forward reachable set at time step
F
is contained within the underapproximation of the backward reachable set at time step
B
, then the system is guaranteed to reach the goal set.

To verify that the system satisfies an avoid property at time
t
(Properties
3b
and
4b
), we compute
F
steps of overapproximated forward analysis from the initial set and
B
steps of overapproximated backward analysis from the avoid set
A
.
If the resulting forward and backward reachable sets are disjoint for all
t\in[1,T]
, then the system is guaranteed to avoid all unsafe states, thereby ensuring safety.
