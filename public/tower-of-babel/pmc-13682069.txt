Epigenetic modulation of NKG2D ligands enhances NKG2D-CAR T cell efficacy in neuroblastoma
Authors: Alfonso Navarro-Zapata, María Octavia De Santisteban, Rita María Regojo-Zapata, Marta Ibáñez-Navarro, Laura Clares-Villa, Marta de la Hoz, Andrea Romero, Carmen Mestre-Durán, Karima Al-Akioui Sanz, Halin Bareke, Sara Naharro, Jordi Minguillón, Lidia Pertíñez, Natalia Riestra, Mireia Masot, Carla Martín-Cortázar, Cristina Aguirre-Portolés, Cristina Ferreras, Adriana Mañas, Antonio Pérez-Martínez, Lucía Fernández Casanova

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children and is associated with high mortality despite aggressive multimodal therapies for high-risk patients. Novel treatments such as CAR T cell therapy offer promise, yet challenges persist due to immune evasion mechanisms by tumor cells. Specifically, the interaction between the natural killer group 2D (NKG2D) receptor on cytotoxic immune cells and its ligands (NKG2DL) is critical for antitumor immunity. However, the expression of NKG2DL is tightly regulated by different means, including epigenetic mechanisms. NB cells often downregulate NKG2DL expression to evade immune control, and this reduction is associated with poorer survival and worse prognosis.MethodsThe NKG2DL expression was assessed in a set of NB cell lines and patient-derived xenograft organoids (PDXOs) under basal conditions and following treatment with epigenetic drugs, valproic acid (VPA) and tazemetostat (TZM). NKG2D-CAR T cells were generated, and their proliferation, phenotype, cell-killing assays and cytokine production were performed in vitro. Cell-based assays were performed combining epigenetic drugs and NKG2D-CAR T cells in the different NB models.ResultsOur study revealed that NKG2DL expression in NB models and patient samples exhibited considerable heterogeneity, but it was low overall. Although NKG2DL expression does not necessarily preclude susceptibility, its downregulation may compromise the cytotoxicity of NKG2D-CAR T cells. Treatment with epigenetic drugs, VPA and TZM, significantly increased NKG2DL expression and improved NKG2D-CAR T cell-mediated killing in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionThis work suggests that targeting epigenetic pathways can enhance the susceptibility of NB cells to NKG2D-CAR T cell therapy, thereby providing a potential new approach to treat high-risk NB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08685-y.Keywords: Neuroblastoma, NKG2D, CAR T cells, Epigenetic pathways, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children. It accounts for 6–10% of all pediatric cancers and is responsible for 15% of childhood cancer-related deaths [1, 2]. Despite the implementation of intensive multimodal therapies, comprising high dose chemotherapy, surgery, radiation, autologous stem cell transplantation and anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody immunotherapy, high-risk NB patients still relapse and show poorer survival rates [3–5]. The intrinsic aggressiveness, heterogeneity, and therapy resistance of NB renders it one of the most challenging malignancies [4–6]. Early-phase clinical trials have established the safety and feasibility of CAR T cell therapy for NB, with GD2-targeting CAR T cells demonstrating promising activity [7–10]; however, substantial challenges to achieving robust therapeutic efficacy persist. These challenges include lack of specific tumor associated antigens (TAAs) or immune evasion and the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment [11].

Natural Killer Group 2, member D (NKG2D) is an activating receptor expressed on natural killer (NK) cells and some subsets of T cells, which recognizes stress-induced ligands, NKG2DL ligands (NKG2DL), including MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence A and B (MICA and MICB), and UL16-binding protein 1–6 (ULBP1-6), and plays a pivotal role in immune surveillance against infections and cancer [12]. NKG2D-CAR T cell therapy has previously demonstrated preclinical efficacy [13]. Furthermore, NKG2D-CAR T cells have been tested in patients suffering from hematological malignancies and solid tumors, demonstrating good tolerability with limited efficacy [14–16]. NB cells can evade NKG2D-mediated immune recognition by either reducing the surface expression of these ligands or releasing soluble forms (sNKG2DL) that inhibit NKG2D receptor function [17].

Epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling contribute to the silencing of genes involved in the recognition of NB cells by immune effector cells [2, 18]. In fact, epigenetic silencing of NKG2DLs leads to NK immune evasion [19]. DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) have been shown to be key mediators of these epigenetic changes [20]. HDAC inhibitors (HDACis) can reverse these modifications, restoring the expression of NKG2DLs and the susceptibility of tumor cells to immune recognition [21–24].

In this work, we hypothesized that treatment of NB cells with epigenetic drugs can increase NKG2DL expression to improve NKG2D-CAR T cells recognition and antitumor efficacy. We evaluated the potential of combining NKG2D-CAR T cells with epigenetic drugs (valproic acid and tazemetostat) for NB treatment. Our findings revealed that these drugs upregulate the expression of NKG2DL in NB cells and significantly enhance the cytotoxicity of NKG2D-CAR T cells in vitro. Our results suggest that integrating epigenetic therapy with NKG2D-CAR T cells could be a promising strategy for high-risk NB.

Materials and methods

Neuroblastoma sample genomics analysis

Gene expression analysis of NKG2DL was conducted via the R2 Genomics Analysis and Visualization Platform (Academic Medical Centre, Amsterdam; available at https://hgserver1.amc.nl/). Data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE45547; NCBI, Bethesda, MD, USA), comprising single-color gene expression profiles from 649 NB tumors, were used [25]. Tumors were classified according to the International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS) and by MYCN amplification status. Samples with incomplete clinical data were excluded from the analysis. For stage-based comparisons, 2log Z-score values were used. Additionally, a pan-NKG2DL expression signature was computed as the mean Z-score of all NKG2DL genes for each sample, providing a summary expression value for each dataset sample. Overall survival (OS) was analyzed in stages 3 and 4 patients, stratified by high or low gene expression (median cutoff). Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to assess group differences.

Determination of NKG2DL expression by immunohistochemistry in primary NB samples

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) primary NB tumors were obtained from the IdiPAZ Biobank in accordance with the Institutional Ethical Committee approvals (PI-2295) and Declaration of Helsinki. Patient characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Sections were (3 μm) were stained to detect NKG2DL expression by the Pathology Service of La Paz University Hospital (HULP).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using EnVision FLEX+ detection system (Dako, Agilent), with primary antibodies against MICA/B (Abcam; #ab203679), ULBP2/5/6 (R&D Systems; #AF1298), ULBP3 (Biorbyt; #orb5602) and ULBP4 (R&D Systems; #MAB6285) for 30 min at room temperature (RT). Images were captured using an Olympus BX-41 microscope. Staining intensity was scored by a pathologist as negative (N), weak (W), moderate (M), or high (H). The H-score was calculated with the following formula: H-score = (0 × N%) + (1 × W%) + (2 × M%) + (3 × H%), yielding values from 0 to 300, classified as null (0), low (1–100), medium (101–200), and high (201–300). Total NKG2DL expression was defined as the sum of the H-scores across all ligands, categorized as null (0), low (1–300), medium (301–600), or high (601–1200).

Healthy primary cells and cell lines

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were collected from healthy donors with approval from the Transfusions Centre of the Comunidad de Madrid (ethical approval: PI-2295). CCD-18Co normal human colon fibroblasts and NL20 normal human bronchial epithelial cells, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). NuLi-1 normal human lung epithelial cells were kindly provided by Dr. Paz-Ares (12 de Octubre Hospital, Spain).

Human NB cell lines LAN-1, LAN-5, NGP, CHP-134, and Kelly were obtained from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DMSZ). SK-N-AS, SK-N-SH, SH-SY5Y and HEK293T cells were obtained from ATCC. NB1691-Luc was kindly provided by Dr. A. Davidoff (St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital). For selected assays, CHP-134 and Kelly were lentivirally transduced to overexpress NKG2DL. All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing 4.5 g/L glucose (DMEM; Lonza) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA; Gibco Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (P/S; Lonza) in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Patient-derived xenograft organoids (PDXOs) of high-risk NB were generously provided by Dr. Daniel Bexell (Lund University, Sweden). Three PDXO models were used: LU-NB-2 (derived from a brain metastasis of NB), LU-NB-3 (a primary adrenal gland NB) and LU-NB-3R (a post-chemotherapy relapse derived from the primary LU-NB-3 model). All models have MYCN amplification [26, 27]. For selected experiments, PDXOs were transduced with GFP-luciferase or tdTomato-luciferase. Organoids were maintained in suspension culture (200,000 cells/ml) using Stem Cell Medium supplemented as previously described [28], and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 humid atmosphere. PDXOs were dissociated every 7 days with Accutase (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to maintain optimal growth.

Plasmid construction and lentiviral production

Lentiviral particles expressing MICA were generated using the lentiviral vector pHAGE-EF1a-MICA*009-IRES-ZsGreen, provided by Dr. Wucherpfennig (Plasmid #114007; Addgene). Corresponding vectors for ULBP2 and ULBP3 (pHAGE-EF1a-ULBP-2-IRES-ZsGreen and pHAGE-EF1a-ULBP-3-IRES-ZsGreen) were constructed by subcloning the sequences into the pUC57 vector (NZYTech), replacing the MICA insert. A mock pHAGE plasmid served as a negative control. NB cell lines expressing tdTomato-luciferase (NB-Tmt+ cells) were generated using the pCDH-EF1-Luc2-P2A-tdTomato vector, kindly provided by Dr. Raúl Torres (Spanish National Cancer Research Centre, CNIO, Spain).

The lentiviral vector containing NKG2D-4-1BB-CD3z CAR and production of lentiviral supernatants have been previously described [29, 30]. Following 48–72 h of transfection, the supernatants were collected, filtered and concentrated using the Lenti-X™ Concentrator (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentrated lentiviral supernatants were stored at -80 °C and titrated into HEK293T cells by serial dilution.

NKG2D-CAR transduction and CD45RA− isolation

CAR T cells were generated from PBMCs or isolated CD45RA− cells. PBMCs were isolated by using Ficoll-Paque (GE Healthcare) gradient centrifugation. CD45RA− cell isolation, priming and transduction of 1 x 106 cells were performed as previously described [30]. Transduction of the cells was performed with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) ranged from 2.5 to 10. Transduced cells were expanded in TEXMACS medium (Miltenyi Biotec) supplemented with 5% human AB serum (SAB; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis), and 250 IU/ml IL-2 premium grade (Miltenyi Biotec; #130-097-748) until day + 28. IL-2 was replenished every 3 days. NKG2D expression was assessed weekly by flow cytometry, and functional assays were performed from day + 5 post-transduction. Activated but not transduced T cells (UTD) served as controls for basal NKG2D expression and cytotoxicity assays.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

Cell phenotypes were determined by FCM using a Navios (Beckman Coulter), FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences) or Cytek® Aurora (Cytek Biosciences) flow cytometer. Fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) used are listed in Supplementary Table S2. For intracellular cytokine analysis, NKG2D-CAR T cells were co-cultured with NKG2DL-overexpressing CHP-134 cells at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 5:1 for 4 h in the presence of BD GolgiStop™ (1:200 dilution; #554724).Cells were then fixed and permeabilized with BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Kit (BD Biosciences; #554714) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended for FCM analysis. Specific fluorescence intensity (SFI) was determined as the ratio between MFI of stained samples and that of the corresponding isotype control or unstained cells. SFI ≥ 1.5 was considered positive. For healthy and non-tumorigenic cells, due to differences in basal NKG2D ligand expression across different cell types, values were log2-transformed to facilitate comparison and visualization. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v.10.7.1 (Tree Star Inc.).

Viability assay

Cell viability was assessed to determine the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of epigenetic drugs (epidrugs). Briefly, NB cells were seeded in flat-bottom 96-well plates and were treated with epidrugs (Supplementary Table S3) at serial dilutions in a final volume of 100 µl of complete DMEM. Then, NB cells were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. At the final timepoint, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent (MedChemExpress LLC) was added per well until the color change was visualized. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a Biotek Synergy 4 microplate reader (Biotek).

For PDXOs, cultures were dissociated and 5,000 cells/well were seeded in flat-bottom 96-well plates. After 48 h of incubation to allow organoid formation, they were treated with the corresponding compounds at graded concentrations. After 48 h of treatment, viability was assessed by adding luciferin (2 mM) and measuring luminescence on a ClarioStar Plus plate reader (BMG Labtech). Specific drug curves were fitted using GraphPad Prism v.8.0.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.).

Effect of epidrugs on NB cells and NKG2D-CAR T cells

NB cells (5 × 105 per well) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated the following day with selected epidrugs at previously determined doses for 24–72 h. In combination treatments, VPA and TZM were coadministered at the indicated concentrations. For PDXO assays, organoids were dissociated and 2 × 10⁶ cells were seeded in T25 flasks. After 48 h to allow organoid formation, PDXOs were treated with epidrugs for 48 h. To assess potential effects of epidrugs on immune cells, 1 × 106 NKG2D-CAR or UTD T cells (1 × 106 per well) exposed to VPA (2 mM) and TZM (5 µM) for 72 h in TEXMACS medium supplemented with 5% SAB. At the indicated time points, NB cells, NB organoids or immune cells were collected and analyzed by FCM. Untreated cells served as a negative control.

Cell-mediated cytotoxicity assays

The in vitro cytotoxic capacity of NKG2D-CAR T cells against NB cells was assessed at E: T ratios of 20:1, 10:1, 5:1 and 2.5:1 using the conventional 4-hour Europium-TDA assays (PerkinElmer; #AD0116) as previously described [29]. In selected experiments, NKG2DL blockade was performed with mouse anti-human MICA/B antibody (Clone 159207; R&D Systems; #MAB13001) or mouse anti-human ULBP2/5/6 antibody (Clone 165903; R&D Systems; #MAB1298). A non-specific mouse anti-human IgG2a isotype antibody (Clone 133304; R&D Systems; #MAB0031) was used as a control.

For luminescence-based assays, NB-tdTomato-Luc cells were treated with VPA, TZM or VPA + TZM for 48 h, harvested, washed two times, and co-cultured with NKG2D-CAR or UTD T cells at E: T ratio of 20:1 in 96-well plates for an additional 48 h. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS, and 100 µl of fresh DMEM containing 2 mM D-Luciferin sodium salt (MedChemExpress; #HY-12591) were added per well for bioluminescence measurement. For organoid assays, PDXOs were dissociated and 5,000 LU-NB-tdTomato-Luc cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates. After 48 h to allow organoids formation, PDXOs were treated with VPA, TZM, or VPA + TZM for 24 h, followed by addition of NKG2D-CAR or UTD T cells at E: T ratio of 1:1 in medium supplemented with IL-2 (250 IU/mL) + 5% human AB serum. After 24 h of co-culture, 2 mM D-luciferin was added to each well and bioluminescence was measured. NB viability was calculated relative to untreated controls.

IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-2 production assays

IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-2 secretion by NKG2D-CAR and UTD T cells was measured using the ELISA MAX™ Deluxe Set Human IFN-γ (#430104), TNF-α (#430204) and IL-2 (#431804) kits, all purchased from Biolegend, following the manufacturers’ instructions. Briefly, NB cell lines were pretreated with VPA, TZM or combination for 48 h, then harvested, washed, and co-cultured with NKG2D-CAR or UTD T cells at a 20:1 E: T ratio in 96-well plates in the presence of the epidrugs for 48 additional hours. Standard curves were generated using CurveExpert v1.4 (Cusabio).

Serial tumor cell challenge assay in the presence of VPA

NKG2D-CAR T cell function and persistence in the presence of VPA were evaluated using an in vitro serial co-culture (rechallenge) assay. Briefly, Kelly cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 0.25 × 106 cells per well (day − 2). After 24 h, on day − 1, cells were treated or not with VPA 1.9 µM. After 24 h of treatment, on day 0, NKG2D-CAR T cells were added to the treated and non-treated Kelly cells at a 1:1 E: T ratio and co-cultured for 3 days. On day 3, additional 0.25 × 10⁶ Kelly cells and 1.9 µM VPA were added to the corresponding wells, and co-cultures were maintained for an additional 3 days. The treatment and rechallenge were repeated on day 6 for an additional 3 days. Samples were collected on days 0, 3, 6 and 9 (endpoint) for FCM analysis of exhaustion markers PD-1, TIM-3 and LAG3. Viability of both NKG2D-CAR T and Kelly cells, was assessed using Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Kit (1:2500; BioLegend, no. 423105). Tumor cells were identified as GD2+CD3−, and NKG2D-CAR T cells as GD2−CD3+. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v.10.7.1 (Tree Star Inc.).

Infiltration and cytotoxic activity of NKG2D-CAR T cells in NB organoids

PDXOs were dissociated and 1 × 106 cells/flask were seeded in T25 flasks and incubated for 5 days to generate large organoids. On day 5, the PDXOs were treated with epidrugs at the indicated concentration for 24 h, followed by the addition of NKG2D-CAR and UTD T cells at an E: T ratio of 2:1. Untreated organoids were cultured in parallel to quantify cell numbers at the time of T cell addition. After 24 h of co-culture, the cells were gently centrifuged at 300 rpm for 3 min and washed, fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in 100 µl of Histogel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for paraffin processing. Double IHC staining was performed with polyclonal rabbit anti-human CD3 (Dako, #IR503), to recognize CAR T cells, and monoclonal mouse anti-human Granzyme B (GzmB; Dako #M7235), to evaluate their cytotoxic activity. FLEX DAB + and MAGENTA HRP chromogen were used for detection, with hematoxylin counterstain. Slides were scanned using the Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 scanner and analyzed using QuPath v.0.6.0. Infiltration rate was quantified as CD3+ cells per LU-NB cells, and activation as 100 × (GzmB+ cells/CD3+ cells).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.8.0 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as the mean ± SD, unless otherwise indicated. Data distribution was assessed using D’Agostino-Pearson or Shapiro-Wilk normality tests. IC50 values were calculated by nonlinear regression analysis using dose-response curve fitting. Comparison between two groups were performed using paired or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests or non-parametric Wilcoxon/Mann-Whitney tests, as appropriate. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using parametric one-way ANOVA or non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis with Tukey’s or Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test, as specified in the figure legends. Analyses involving two independent variables were performed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Šidák multiple-comparisons tests. SFI and the percentage of positive cells for each NKG2DL under different epidrugs treatments were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA. Correlation analyses were assessed using Pearson or Spearman’s correlation test. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests. Biological replicates correspond to independent donors or independent experiments. Exact sample sizes and statistical tests used for each experiment are specified in the corresponding figure legends. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NKG2DLs are heterogeneously expressed in neuroblastoma

To determine the suitability of NKG2D-CAR T cells for targeting NB cells, the expression of NKG2DL was comprehensively analyzed in NB samples. NKG2DL RNA expression levels were quantified in a publicly available cohort of 649 NB cases (Fig. 1). Stage 4 patients showed significantly reduced levels of NKG2DL transcripts compared to patients with less aggressive NB stages. Decreased expression levels were also seen in samples with MYCN amplification (Fig. 1A). Similar findings were observed when individual NKG2DLs were analyzed independently, with MICA, ULBP1 and ULBP2 showing significantly reduced transcript expression in stage 4 patients compared with patients at stages 1–2 (Fig. S1A). Although reduced total NKG2DL expression exhibited no significant trend, the prognostic impact was more evident when ligands were analyzed individually; notably, ULBP2 exhibited the most significant association with patient survival (Fig. 1B and S1B).

To assess the relevance of NKG2DL protein expression, we analyzed surface expression of NKG2DLs in an independent cohort of 23 primary NB patient samples by immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Supplementary Table S1). We detected heterogeneous protein expression of the individual ligands, with no significant differences regardless of NB stage or MYCN status (Fig. 1C, D and Fig. S2A, B). Only ULBP4 was highly expressed in most samples (Fig. S2C). Although not statistically significant, our results indicate that patients with low NKG2DL expression have reduced overall survival (Fig. 1E). This trend persisted when we examined the individual ligands MICA/B and ULBP3 (Fig. S3).

We also analyzed the expression of pan-NKG2DL and individual NKG2DL on nine NB cell lines (Fig. 1F and Fig. S4A). The percentage of expression and specific fluorescence intensity (SFI) for each individual NKG2DL in the NB cell lines are presented in Fig. S4B. Surface expression of at least one NKG2DL (SFI ≥ 1.5) was detected in all the studied NB cell lines but the expression was heterogenous and showed no correlation with MYCN amplification.

NKG2D-CAR T cells can target NB cells in vitro

CD45RA− T cells were successfully transduced with the NKG2D-CAR construct (Fig. S5A), resulting in a significant increase in the percentage of NKG2D-expressing cells after 21 days of transduction (Fig. S5B, C). Untransduced (UTD) CD45RA− T cells, which naturally express low levels of endogenous NKG2D, were used as controls for baseline expression. No significant differences in the CD4/CD8 distribution were detected between UTD and NKG2D-CART cells, except on day 21, when a greater percentage of CD8+ T cells was noted in the NKG2D-CAR T cell population (Fig. S5D). Additionally, a greater proportion of NKG2D-CAR T cells than UTD T cells exhibited an effector memory phenotype (CD45RA−CD27−) (Fig. S5E). However, no significant differences were observed in the expression of exhaustion markers (LAG3, PD-1, TIM-3) throughout cell expansion (Fig. S5F, G).

NKG2D-CAR T cells exhibited specific cytotoxic activity against SK-N-AS (MICA+, MICB+, ULBP2/5/6−, ULBP3−) and SK-N-SH (MICA−, MICB−, ULBP2/5/6−/+, ULBP3+) cells, with 56.8% and 30.1% lysis, respectively, at a 20:1 E: T ratio. However, at lower E: T ratios, significant cytotoxicity was only observed against the SK-N-AS cells, while the remaining NB cells lines were resistant (MICA−, MICB−, ULBP2/5/6+, ULBP3−/+) (Fig. 2A and Fig. S6A). Interestingly, NKG2DL-expressing cell lines, such as CHP-134 and Kelly, were resistant to NKG2D-CAR T cell-mediated lysis, even when NKG2D-CAR expression levels on the CD45RA− cells were increased by using higher MOIs (Fig. S6B, C).

To explore if the release of sNKG2DL could be accounting for tumor resistance, we measured the levels of sNKG2DL secreted by NB cells in vitro. We found no substantial increase of soluble ligands, suggesting this was not the main mechanism behind NB resistance (Fig. S6D). To determine if different NKG2DL could account differently for NKG2D recognition and targeting, we performed cytotoxicity experiments blocking specific ligands. We observed that upon MICA/B blockade, SK-N-AS cells become resistant to NKG2D-CAR T cells mediated cytotoxicity (Fig. 2B). Similarly, in SK-N-SH cells, ULBP2/5/6 blockade reduced NKG2D-CAR T cell cytotoxicity. These data suggest that a certain level of NKG2DL hierarchy may exist to induce effective targeting of NB cells by NKG2D-CAR T cells.

NKG2DL overexpression in NB cells enables the functionality of NKG2D-CAR T cells

To further explore the role of different NKG2DL on NKG2D-CAR T cells mediated lysis, we overexpressed MICA, ULBP2 and ULBP3 in the NKG2DL-expressing but NKG2D-CAR-resistant NB cell lines CHP-134 and Kelly (Fig. 3A and Fig. S7A) and evaluated NKG2D-CAR functionality. We found that induced overexpression of NKG2DL renders previously resistant NB cells susceptible to NKG2D-CAR T cells killing (Fig. 3B). However, not all the NKG2DL contributed equally, with MICA being the most effective at increasing NKG2D-CAR targeting, followed by ULBP2 and ULBP3 (Fig. 3B). In fact, MICA density correlated with the degree of NB cell lysis, suggesting a dominant role of this specific ligand in NB NKG2D-mediated immunosurveillance (Fig. 3C).

Additionally, we analyzed intracellular cytokine production by NKG2D-CAR T cells following co-culture with NB cells overexpressing NKG2DLs. The analysis revealed a significantly increased production of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 by NKG2D-CAR T cells co-cultured with NKG2DL-overexpressing NB cells (Fig. 3D, E). Similarly high percentages of cytokine-producing cells were observed in NKG2D-CAR T cells exposed to MICA- and ULBP2-overexpressing NB cells, whereas lower numbers were seen for those exposed to ULBP3-overexpressing NB cells (Fig. 3E). Finally, comparable downregulation of the NKG2D receptor was observed in NKG2D-CAR T cells after co-culture with NKG2DL-overexpressing NB cells for 4 h (Fig. S7D).

Targeting epigenetic pathways can modulate NKG2DL expression in NB cells

Targeting certain epigenetic pathways with pharmacological agents can alter the expression of NKG2DLs on tumor cells, affecting both the quantity and the balance ratio of different ligands [31–33]. Therefore, we explored if treatment of NB cells with epidrugs could increase NKG2DL expression on NB cells and enhance antitumor efficacy of NKG2D-CAR T cells. First, we determined the IC50 values for a collection of epidrugs (Fig. S7E) and analyzed NKG2DL expression in NB cells at different time intervals (Fig. S8A). The tested drugs showed a variable impact on NKG2DL modulation in NB cell lines across different time points. Notably, after 72 h of treatment, AZA, DAC, and TZM significantly upregulated ULBP2/5/6 expression in CHP134 cells, whereas JQ1 treatment reduced ULBP2/5/6 levels (Fig. S8B). Additionally, TZM significantly upregulated the ULBP3 expression in CHP-134. In Kelly cells, treatment with VPA resulted in increased MICB expression.

Next, we combined the two most effective drugs, VPA (HDACi) and TZM (EZH2i). The VPA + TZM combination significantly increased the expression of MICA, MICB, ULBP1 and ULBP3 in both cell lines (Fig. 4), and upregulated ULBP2/5/6 expression in CHP-134 cells (Fig. 4A). TZM alone induced the highest upregulation of ULBP2/5/6 in both cell lines, and upregulated ULBP4 expression.

To assess whether epidrugs increase NKG2DL expression in healthy cells, four non-malignant models were analyzed. First, PBMCs from three healthy donors showed no significant changes in NKG2D ligand expression upon treatment (Fig. S9A). Similarly, no relevant increases were observed in human bronchial epithelium (NL20), colon fibroblasts (CCD-18Co), or lung epithelium (NuLi-1) cells (Fig. S9B). The only exception was ULBP3 in NuLi-1 cells, which increased after VPA, TZM, or combined treatment.

We also investigated whether the combination of VPA + TZM exerts a synergistic effect on the acetylation and methylation of lysines 9 and 27 on H3 in NB-treated cells. Western blot analysis revealed that the combination enhanced acetylation levels of H3K9 and H3K27 (Fig. S10A, B), and a reduction in methylation was also observed. The levels of the HDAC1/2 and EZH2 enzymes remained unchanged (Fig. S10C, D). Given that NKG2DLs are considered aberrant HLA-I molecules and NB cells typically downregulate HLA-I, we evaluated whether the treatment could restore HLA-I expression in NB cells. A significant increase in HLA-ABC (HLA-I) expression was observed in both cell lines following treatment with the combination (Fig. S10E). Finally, we investigated the effect of the combination in cell death. Compared with the individual treatments, a minor increase in cell death was observed (Fig. S10F).

To exclude possible detrimental effects of the epigenetic drugs on effector cells, we evaluated the effect of the combination of VPA + TZM on the phenotype of NKG2D-CAR T cells. Our results showed that VPA treatment, both alone and in combination, significantly increases the total NKG2D expression on the NKG2D-CAR T cells but not for UTD T cells (Fig. S11A, B). No differences were observed in the CD4/CD8 or TCM/TEM subsets (Fig. S11C). Interestingly, VPA treatment, alone or in combination with TZM, resulted in an increased expression of the exhaustion marker LAG3 on NKG2D-CAR T cells (Fig. S11D), but no significant differences were observed for PD-1 and TIM-3. This effect may reflect enhanced cytotoxic capacity driven by increased NKG2D-CAR surface expression on T cells, while also being associated with the acquisition of a more exhausted phenotype prior to NB cell encounter.

NKG2D-CAR resistant NB cell lines were treated with VPA and/or TZM followed by co-culture with NKG2D-CAR T cells, and tumor cell viability was assessed (Fig. 5A). Pre-treatment with VPA and TZM significantly reduced tumor cell viability in Kelly cells when co-cultured with NKG2D-CAR T cells, indicating increased susceptibility to CAR T cell-mediated killing (Fig. 5B). Additionally, we quantified the production of IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-2 by the NKG2D-CAR and UTD T cells following co-culture with treated NB cell lines. A significant increase in IFN-γ and IL-2 levels was detected in the supernatants of co-cultures treated with VPA or VPA + TZM and NKG2D-CAR T cells for both cell lines (Fig. 5C). The effect on TNF-α production was more heterogeneous and not specific for the effector cells, with significantly higher levels of TNF-α observed only in the supernatants of co-cultures of Kelly treated with the combinations (Fig. 5C). These data suggest that the increased cytokine production by NKG2D-CAR T cells may be associated with improved target cell recognition, potentially driven by the upregulation of NKG2DL on NB cells.

To address this, we performed sequential in vitro rechallenges of NKG2D-CAR T cells from five different donors against Kelly cells treated or untreated with VPA over a 9-day period (Fig. 5D). We observed that Kelly cell viability was significantly reduced when co-cultured with NKG2D-CAR T cells in the presence of VPA (Fig. 5E), with the most pronounced effects happening at days 6 and 9. In addition, NKG2D-CAR T cell viability was also reduced in the VPA-treated condition, which may be associated with increased tonic signaling. Consistently, NKG2D-CAR T cells exposed to VPA exhibited a more exhausted phenotype (Fig. 5F), characterized by an increased frequency of triple-positive PD-1⁺TIM-3⁺LAG3⁺ cells over time.

These findings suggest that epigenetic modulation with VPA not only enhances tumor cell recognition and killing, but may also amplify CAR T cell activation, potentially promoting increased cytokine production at the cost of promoting exhaustion.

NKG2D-CAR T cells infiltrate and eliminate NB organoids treated with VPA or the combination

Once we had tested the effects of epidrugs on NB cell lines, we moved forward to a more clinically relevant model and used patient-derived xenograft organoids (PDXOs) to evaluate whether the epigenetic drugs could also enhance NKG2DL expression and improve the NKG2D-CAR T cells tumor infiltration ability (Fig. 6 and Fig. S12). We analyzed the impact on NKG2DL expression in NB organoids treated with VPA, TZM or VPA + TZM. Consistent with the cell line results, the combination of VPA + TZM increased the levels of MICA/B in NB organoid models (Fig. 6A), while VPA alone was enough to significantly elevate the expression of ULBP2/5/6. Next, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of NKG2D-CAR T cells against NB organoids. Those NB PDXOs treated with VPA and VPA + TZM showed increased sensitivity to NKG2D-CAR T cell-mediated lysis (Fig. 6B). The tumor infiltration capacity of the CAR T cells was analyzed by IHC staining of CD3+ T cells, and activation was assessed by staining for Granzyme B (GzmB+) (Fig. 6C and Fig. S12B). For LU-NB-2, infiltration of NKG2D-CAR T cells into the NB PDXOs was significantly higher when treated with VPA + TZM, and a significantly higher percentage of the infiltrated cells were activated with VPA and VPA + TZM (Fig. 6D). For LU-NB-3R, both VPA and the combination enhanced the infiltration and activation of NKG2D-CAR T cells (Fig. 6E). The infiltration and activation of the UTD T cells were not affected (Fig. S12C and S12D).

Treatment with VPA and NKG2D-CAR T cells extends survival in NB in vivo model

As a proof-of-concept, we evaluated the feasibility of combining VPA, a clinically approved drug with established use in pediatric patients, with NKG2D-CAR T cells in vivo. In this experiment, immunodeficient NSG mice engrafted with Kelly NB cells were treated with suboptimal doses of VPA (200 or 300 mg/kg) and NKG2D-CAR T cells were injected intratumorally (3 injections) (Fig. S13A). The treatment was well tolerated by all mice, with no evident signs of toxicity (Fig. S13B). All the combination treatments reduced tumor growth and, in some cases, led to tumor shrinkage. However, this effect was temporary, and the tumors quickly regrew after the treatment was discontinued (Fig. S13C). Both combined treatments prolonged the survival of mice respect to the control group (Fig. S13D). At the end point, tumors and organs were collected to assess the presence of NKG2D-CAR T cells. NKG2D-CAR T cells were found in spleen, bone marrow, blood and tumor for all mice analyzed (Fig S13E). Interestingly, the proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells varied between the treatment conditions only in the tumors (Fig S13E), and not in the organs, indicating specific activity within the tumor. Additionally, tumors treated with 300 mg/kg of VPA had a significantly smaller population of CD8+ T cells and higher of CD4+ than those treated with 200 mg/kg, which could indicate a more rapid exhaustion of CD8+ T cells due to a higher engagement with NKG2DL mediated by the higher dose of VPA. This was supported by an increment of PD-1 expression and TIM-3 expression on lymphocytes found within tumors treated with 300 mg/kg of VPA (Fig. S13F).

Overall, our results show that treatment of NB cells with epigenetic drugs changes NKG2DL expression, affecting quantity and potentially altering the ratio of different NKG2DL, which collectively, decrease the threshold of NKG2D-CAR recognition and targeting. Further studies should focus on confirming these findings and optimizing the therapeutic approach.

Discussion

Despite advancements, high-risk NB patients still face low survival rates, even with multimodal treatment regimens. Given the success of CAR T cell therapy in hematological malignancies, CAR T cell therapy has been explored for solid tumors such as NB [34]. Recently, a third generation GD2-CAR T has shown feasibility, safety and promising results for high-risk NB patients [7, 9]. However, fully effective CAR T cell therapy still requires countering the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and preventing antigen escape [35]. Our study aimed to assess the potential of CAR T cells targeting NKG2DL on NB, and the use of epigenetic drugs to enhance NKG2DL expression and improve NKG2D-CAR cytotoxicity.

NKG2D receptor-ligand interactions are essential for tumor immune surveillance [12]. In fact, expression loss of some NKG2DL such as MICA, MICB and ULBP2 is linked to poor clinical outcomes and metastasis in different types of cancer [36–39]. Consistent with prior studies, our findings indicate that the expression of NKG2DLs is not uniform in NB primary tumors and NB cell lines [40–42]. Additionally, we found that the presence of RNA transcripts does not always correlate with NKG2DL surface expression. This result was expected, as NKG2DL regulation involves a complex interplay between transcriptional activation, post-transcriptional repression, and post-translational modifications [43].

Such variations likely arise from distinct immune landscapes across tumors, where factors such as immune cell infiltration, or immunosuppressive cells influence the role of NKG2DL in treatment response. Besides heterogeneity, low or null expression of NKG2DLs correlates with decreased OS, MYCN amplification [44] and reduced NK infiltration [36, 45–47].

Our group has previously reported the preclinical efficacy of NKG2D-CAR T cells against osteosarcoma and leukemia, resulting in two phase I clinical trials: one for children, adolescents and young adults with advance sarcoma (NCT06087341), and another one for children with refractory/relapsed acute lymphoblastic leukemia (NCT06709469) [29, 30, 48]. However, when NKG2D-CAR T efficacy was tested against NB, we found no direct correlation between the overall NKG2DL expression and NKG2D-CAR T cells cytotoxicity. Similarly, De-Gang Song et al. did not find a correlation between NKG2DL expression in primary ovarian cancer cells and the cytotoxic capability of NKG2D-CAR T cells [33]. This may indicate that other signals and/or the ratios of expression of key NKG2DLs might be relevant for the cytolytic activity of NKG2D-CAR T cells.

The polymorphic nature of NKG2DLs could significantly influence immune activation. Studies suggest that NKG2DLs bind to NKG2D with a broad affinity range, which can differentially modulate NKG2D activation. Furthermore, high affinity does not always correspond with increased activation, as it may lead to receptor downregulation [49, 50]. As an example, ULBP3 leads to stronger NKG2D activation without increased binding affinity [51]. It has been described that MICA exhibits a more rapid, intense binding to NKG2D, resulting in high IFN-γ secretion by NK cells [52], as we observed with the blocking experiments. Additionally, the mechanical interactions of NKG2D with MICA/B induce conformational shifts, increasing ligand specificity and binding strength. Variants of ULBP2 also affect NK cell recognition and function due to different affinities [53]. These ligand differences influence immune synapse formation, potentially impacting the efficacy of NKG2D-CAR T cell therapy by altering ligand recognition [54].

In healthy tissues, a specific threshold of NKG2DL expression is needed to trigger the NKG2D activation signaling [55, 56]. A similar threshold in NB cells may explain the resistance to NKG2D-CAR T cells lysis that we have observed in some NB cell lines with low expression of NKG2DL. In our experiments, we observed that CAR T cell efficacy did not increase by augmenting NKG2D-CAR density suggesting that a balance between CAR density, antigen availability, and engineered signaling is needed [57–59]. However, we observed an increased NKG2D CAR T cell anti-tumor activity upon upregulation of NKG2DL, especially MICA/B (and to a lesser extent, ULBP2 and ULBP3). Although our data suggests a predominance of MICA/B over other NKG2DL, including ULBP2, which has a similar binding affinity, the mechanisms behind this observation need further research.

NKG2DL expression is tightly regulated through epigenetic control [12]. In fact, it has been described that epigenetic drugs, such as VPA and TZM, can alter the landscape of NKG2DL expression, affecting not only the quantity, but also the ratio of the different NKG2DL [31–33, 60, 61], which is aligned with our results. Importantly, the increase of NKG2DL expression induced by VPA appears to be restricted to tumor cells, with no effect observed in healthy tissues such as hepatic cells or hematopoietic stem cells [62, 63]. Our data revealed that TZM treatment can also upregulate NKG2DL in some NB cells, which is consistent with findings that using EZH2 inhibitors reduces H3K27 methylation, increasing NKG2DL expression and tumor cell sensitivity in other cancers [21, 64]. Furthermore, this indicates that VPA and the combination of VPA and TZM can enhance NKG2DL expression in various NB models. These drugs alter chromatin structure to increase tumor cell visibility to immune cells, with VPA possibly enhancing histone methylation [65].

However, in our experiments, we observed that the combined treatment did not increase the NKG2DL expression in healthy and non-tumorigenic cells. This result is expected since NKG2DL expression is linked to various signaling pathways including the PI3K/Akt, ERK, HER2/HER3, and p53 pathways that are frequently altered in tumor cells while in healthy cells remain unchanged [66]. Most importantly, previous reports from our group and others have demonstrated NKG2D-CAR T cells do not target healthy cells [29, 67]. Therefore, taken together, these findings do not suggest an increased risk of off-tumor toxicity with the combination treatment.

Combined VPA + TZM treatment also resulted in HLA-I overexpression in NB cells, consistent with findings in leukemia blasts [68]. Although increased HLA-I expression may dampen NK-cell-mediated recognition, HLA-I upregulation may be particularly relevant in the context of immunologically cold tumors and an immunosuppressive TME, as it may enhance tumor immunogenicity by promoting antigen presentation, facilitating immune infiltration, and increasing susceptibility to endogenous cytotoxic T-cell–mediated responses [69, 70]. Nevertheless, the mechanism linking increased HLA-I and NKG2DL expression remains unclear.

Notably, NKG2D-CAR T cells co-cultured with VPA-treated NB cells produced increased levels of IFN-γ, IL-2 and, slightly reduced, TNF-α, and these cytokines levels were associated with decreased NB cell viability in vitro, consistent with results in glioblastoma [66]. In fact, the combined treatment with NKG2D-CAR T cells and VPA also demonstrated sustained efficacy in long-term experiments, leading to a progressive reduction in NB cell viability over time, together with an increased exhaustion phenotype in CAR T cells during co-culture. Importantly, treatment with VPA or the combination and NKG2D-CAR T cells showed higher infiltration and activation rates in NB organoids. Although VPA and TZM treatment correlated with increased NKG2DL expression and enhanced tumor-cell killing, the improved cytotoxicity cannot be exclusively attributed to NKG2DL upregulation. Additional mechanisms, including treatment-induced apoptosis, phenotypic changes in NKG2D-CAR T cells, and broader unexplored epigenetic effects, may also contribute to the increased susceptibility of target cells.

Despite the strong synergistic effects observed with VPA and TZM in combination, VPA was prioritized due to its established clinical use, well-characterized pharmacokinetics and its immediate clinical translatability. Indeed, VPA + NKG2D-CAR T treatment was enough to significantly enhance cytotoxicity in NB PDXOs and extend the survival in mice, holding promise for improving the outcomes in patients with low NKG2DL expression. However, it is important to note that, because intratumoral injections of CAR T cells may overcome the limitations of trafficking and infiltration, it does not predict efficacy in metastatic disease, which is a still clinical challenge in high-risk neuroblastoma.

The study presented shows promise in the use of CAR T cells in combination with epigenetic drugs to treat NB, but it has several limitations. First, the in vitro preclinical models may not fully recapitulate the complex NB tumor microenvironment found in patients, where the immunosuppressive presence of myeloid-derived suppressor cells expressing NKG2DLs, along with other inhibitory cells and soluble factors, may hinder CAR T cell efficacy [71]. Regarding the in vivo assay, main limitations include the use of immunodeficient mice, incapable of fully mimicking the complex immune landscape of NB, the intratumoral infusion of CAR T cells and the short treatment window. While NKG2DL shedding and downregulation may play roles in immune evasion, other mechanisms, such as immune checkpoint molecule expression, require further investigation. Finally, studies to assess dosing, timing, on-target off-tumor effects, and long-term impacts of these treatments are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals that NKG2DLs are heterogeneously expressed in NB patient samples, with particularly low levels in high-risk and MYCN-amplified tumors. Using NB models, baseline NKG2DL expression does not correlate with susceptibility to NKG2D-CAR-mediated cytotoxicity. We also demonstrated that epigenetic drugs, particularly VPA and TZM, upregulate NKG2DL expression in NB and enhance NKG2D-CAR antitumor activity by increasing cytotoxicity, cytokine production and improving T cell infiltration and activation in NB organoids. These results indicate that combining selected epigenetic modulators with NKG2D-CAR T cells may improve neuroblastoma cell recognition, infiltration, and cytotoxicity by increasing NKG2DL expression. However, challenges such as tumor heterogeneity and immune evasion persist.
