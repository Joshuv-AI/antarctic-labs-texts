A dynamically consistent discrete-time model for monkeypox transmission with implications for quarantine policies
Authors: Kemal Türk, Mehmet Gümüş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonkeypox is a zoonotic viral infection with significant public health implications. Since epidemiological data are typically reported in discrete time intervals and public health interventions are implemented at specific decision points, discrete-time models provide a natural and policy-relevant modeling framework. Although numerous mathematical models for Mpox have been developed using differential equations, discrete-time approaches remain relatively unexplored, despite their practical importance. In this study, we introduce a discrete-time framework for Mpox by constructing a nonstandard finite difference (NSFD) scheme that preserves the key dynamical properties of the corresponding continuous model. The preservation of positivity, boundedness, and equilibrium behavior is particularly important for decision-support models, as numerical artifacts may otherwise lead to misleading interpretations in applied health and policy contexts. The basic reproduction number is explicitly computed and shown to depend on the human and animal reproduction numbers, denoted by and , respectively. Three equilibrium states are identified, and both the local and global dynamics of the system around these equilibria are rigorously analyzed. It is demonstrated that the condition alone may not be sufficient to guarantee disease eradication. Furthermore, it is proven that the condition is sufficient for the persistence of the disease in both human and animal populations. A sensitivity analysis is also performed to quantify the impact of model parameters on disease transmission dynamics. This analysis provides a clear interpretation of how quarantine measures and contact-reduction strategies influence disease persistence in human and animal populations. Numerical simulations confirm the theoretical findings and provide a comparative assessment of the proposed NSFD scheme against standard methods such as Euler and Runge-Kutta. The time-step-independent nature of the proposed framework ensures robust numerical outcomes and provides a reliable tool for supporting evidence-based public health decision-making and the evaluation of quarantine-oriented intervention strategies.Keywords: Monkeypox (Mpox), Discrete-time epidemic model, Nonstandard finite difference scheme, Zoonotic disease transmission, Quarantine strategies, Decision-support modeling, Numerical stability

Introduction

Monkeypox (Mpox) is a zoonotic viral infection that was first identified in 1958 among laboratory monkeys used for research in Copenhagen, Denmark. The first documented human case of Mpox occurred in 1970 in a nine-month-old child in the Democratic Republic of Congo. In 2003, the United States experienced its first outbreak of the disease. Since 2005, thousands of cases have been reported annually in the Democratic Republic of Congo. Due to increased international travel involving infected individuals, the virus has continued spread across different regions. A new global outbreak emerged in 2022, affecting Europe, the United States, and several other countries. Between January 2022 and June 2025, detailed case data for countries outside Africa accounted for 103,089 cases, representing approximately of all cases recorded during this period [1–3].

The natural reservoirs of the virus primarily various rodent species, such as rope squirrels (Funisciurus spp.), Gambian pouched rats (Cricetomys gambianus), and African dormice (Graphiurus spp.). In addition, non-human primates, including the sooty mangabey (Chlorocebus atys) and the cynomolgus macaque (Macaca fascicularis), have also been recognized as potential hosts [4, 5]. Transmission from animals to humans may occur through bites or scratches from infected animals, consumption of raw bushmeat, or ingestion of food contaminated by infected animals. The virus can spread from human to human through respiratory droplets, direct contact with bodily fluids, contaminated surfaces or personal items, or close contact with infected individuals. Common symptoms of Monkeypox (Mpox) infection include fever, headache, muscle aches, and fatigue. These symptoms are often followed by the development of a rash and raised lesions that may spread across various parts of the body. The incubation period of the virus is generally estimated to range from 7 to 21 days [5–7].

The Mpox epidemic has also caused significant socio-economic disruptions, particularly in African countries, which have been severely affected. In many parts of the region, hospitals and healthcare facilities lack the basic tools and equipment necessary for effective Mpox management. Furthermore, access to reliable diagnostic testing remains limited. In addition, insufficient public awareness and health education in many countries hinder the timely detection, reporting, and effective management of cases throughout the course of the disease [8].

Beyond its biological characteristics, the management of Mpox outbreaks requires timely decision-making based on routinely reported epidemiological data and well-defined intervention strategies. Vaccination constitutes a key component of Mpox control. Smallpox vaccination has been shown to provide substantial cross-protection against Mpox. However, particularly in rural areas where case numbers are high, the distribution of Mpox vaccines faces shortages and serious logistical constraints. In addition, certain specific antiviral agents have been used in the treatment of severe or high-risk Mpox patients [8]. Several countries have adopted quarantine measures to control the spread of Mpox. Belgium was the first to mandate a 21-day quarantine for confirmed cases, while the United Kingdom has recommended a 21-day self-isolation period for individuals with direct or household exposure to confirmed cases [9, 10].

Mathematical modeling is a crucial tool for analyzing the dynamics of infectious diseases. It offers a systematic framework for understanding transmission mechanisms, assessing the effectiveness of intervention strategies, and guiding public health decision-making. Numerous modeling studies have been conducted for a wide range of infectious diseases, including COVID-19, Hepatitis B, HIV, etc. [11–32]. Mathematical models of infectious diseases are most commonly formulated using systems of differential equations. However, public health surveillance data are typically collected and reported at discrete time intervals, and policy interventions such as quarantine or contact restrictions are implemented at specific decision points rather than continuously. These continuous time models typically assume that demographic processes such as births and deaths, as well as transitions between disease states, occur continuously over time. However, many plant and animal populations reproduce seasonally in discrete generations, with population counts therefore measured at specific intervals. Similarly, epidemic data are often collected in discrete time units (e.g., daily, monthly, or annually). This alignment with reporting cycles makes discrete-time models particularly suitable for evaluating intervention scenarios and supporting evidence-based public health planning. Discrete models can also serve as numerical approximations of continuous systems, offering computational efficiency while preserving the essential properties of the original model. For these reasons, continuous models may be discretized, or alternatively, models may be constructed directly in discrete form to more accurately capture the transmission dynamics of infectious diseases.

With the global spread of the Mpox outbreak, increasing attention has been directed toward understanding the population dynamics of the Mpox virus through mathematical modeling approaches. In [33], a mathematical model incorporating non-pharmaceutical interventions was developed and simulated using real-time data to investigate the transmission dynamics of Mpox. The study emphasized the importance of quarantine measures and increased testing coverage in reducing disease spread. Fractional-order models have also been explored, as in [34], where optimal control strategies were applied to assess the impact of vaccination and treatment. In [35], a model was proposed to analyze the impact of government interventions on the transmission dynamics of the disease. The study [36] examines a model involving human-to-animal transmission. Additional modeling studies on Mpox can be found in [9, 37–42].

Problem statement and motivation

When a disease model is formulated as a system of differential equations, it can be discretized using various numerical methods. Ideally, the discretized model should preserve as many of the dynamical properties of the continuous system as possible, such as positivity, boundedness, and the other qualitative properties of the solutions. Commonly used discretization techniques include the Euler and Runge-Kutta methods. However, these methods can lead to certain undesirable dynamical behaviors (such as convergence to incorrect equilibrium points or periodic cycles). To avoid these inconsistencies, certain restrictions on the time-step size are frequently imposed [12, 43, 44]. To address these issues, nonstandard finite difference (NSFD) methods can be applied. From an applied perspective, such dynamically consistent numerical schemes are essential, as spurious oscillations or negative population values may produce misleading conclusions in decision-support and policy-oriented studies. Discussion and conclusion

The proposed discrete-time framework can be readily adapted to different reporting resolutions and intervention scenarios, making it particularly suitable for public health planning contexts where data availability and decision timelines vary across regions. So, in this study, an Mpox model incorporating quarantine and treatment measures is considered. By developing an NSFD scheme that preserves the behavior of solutions, the model is transformed into a discrete form. Three different equilibrium states of the model are identified. The analysis establishes three threshold values, , , and , which play a central role in determining the stability behavior of these equilibria. It is shown that the disease-free equilibrium is LAS when and . However, these conditions alone are not sufficient for global eradication of the disease. Instead, the additional requirement must also be satisfied. This result indicates that reducing the human and animal reproduction numbers below unity may not be sufficient to eliminate the disease in both populations. Minimizing effective contact between susceptible humans and infected animals is also crucial. On the other hand, if either or exceeds unity, it is proven that the disease can persist in the human population or in both populations.

Sensitivity analysis highlights the importance of reducing the transmission rates () to control the disease, as well as the potential impact of population-level interventions, such as birth and migration control measures, that could be implemented by governments. Furthermore, the sensitivity index of the parameter emphasizes the effectiveness of quarantine policies in lowering the basic reproduction number . These findings indicate which intervention strategies may be most effective in mitigating the spread of the disease. All results are obtained independently of the time-step size k, unlike those derived from standard schemes. Several examples are provided to demonstrate the stability properties of the system and the independence of results from k. In these examples, the proposed NSFD scheme (3.1) is compared with Euler and RK4 schemes through simulations. Based on numerous simulation studies, the consistency results obtained for the NSFD, Euler, and RK4 schemes for various choices of k are presented in Table 3. As can be seen from the table and examples, Euler and RK4 schemes can cause inconsistencies, such as divergent or negative solutions, even at very small step sizes. To avoid these problems, constraints on the time step size are necessary. In contrast, the proposed NSFD scheme provides consistently reliable results regardless of the value k. This shows that the proposed scheme produces more reliable and accurate results than standard schemes. The independence of the results from the time-step size k, increases model’s applicability to long-term epidemiological studies. Consequently, this independence strengthens the validity of discrete-time approaches, providing a powerful framework for advancing disease modeling studies and clinical research.
