Stabilization of prefusion hMPV F improves manufacturability and protective immunity across hMPV lineages
Authors: Changbin Qu, Jingjing Zou, Dongyu Niu, Qingxin Wu, Lijie Liu, Mengting Yu, Qiqi Zhang, Long Zhang, Shuai Wei, Wei Zhang, Wei Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman metapneumovirus (hMPV) is a major cause of pediatric acute lower respiratory tract infections (ALRTIs), yet vaccine development has been hindered by the intrinsic metastability of the prefusion F glycoprotein (pre-F). Here, we used a structure-based design strategy to stabilize hMPV pre-F while preserving neutralization-sensitive epitopes. By applying combined stabilizing elements, including disulfide bonds, a designed salt bridge, and a trimer-interface sequence swap, we generated VM-874, a pre-F-stabilized trimer that was expressed at high levels in 293F cells and exhibited improved thermal stability and stress-resistant antigenicity. VM-874 also retained binding to multiple conformation-sensitive monoclonal antibodies following thermal and storage stress. VM-874 elicited high serum neutralizing titers against both hMPV A2 and B1 strains and conferred protection in BALB/c mice and cotton rats, as evidenced by reduced pulmonary viral burden and attenuated lung pathology after challenge. Although VM-874 elicited antibodies that cross-bound respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) F protein, no RSV-neutralizing activity was detected, underscoring the need to distinguish cross-reactivity from heterologous protection in combined RSV/hMPV vaccine strategies.Keywords: Human metapneumovirus, Prefusion F (Pre-F), Structure-guided vaccine design, Molecular dynamics, Thermal stability, Cross-lineage immunity

Introduction

Human metapneumovirus (hMPV) is an enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus in the genus Metapneumovirus within the family Pneumoviridae (Van Den Hoogen et al., 2001; Hamelin et al., 2004; Lianou et al., 2025; Tang, 2025). hMPV is an important cause of respiratory illness in children, particularly those under five years of age (Soto et al., 2018). In 2018, hMPV was estimated to be associated with approximately 14.2 million acute lower respiratory infection (ALRI) cases, 643,000 hospital admissions, and 7700 in-hospital deaths among children younger than five years worldwide (Wang et al., 2021). In addition, hMPV infection is frequently associated with coinfections with other respiratory viruses (Zhao et al., 2022), and some studies have reported that coinfected patients are more likely to develop fever than patients with hMPV monoinfection (Li et al., 2020; Kapandji et al., 2023). Circulating hMPV strains are classified into two major genetic subgroups, hMPV-A and hMPV-B, based largely on sequence variability in the attachment glycoprotein G (Panda et al., 2014; Muñoz-Escalante et al., 2022). However, antibodies targeting the G glycoprotein and the small hydrophobic (SH) protein generally exhibit weak neutralizing activity against hMPV (Skiadopoulos et al., 2006; Ryder et al., 2010; Xiao et al., 2022). In contrast, the fusion (F) glycoprotein is more conserved and is the principal target of neutralizing antibodies, supporting its potential as a vaccine antigen (Skiadopoulos et al., 2006; Wen et al., 2012; Más et al., 2016).

For both hMPV and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), the trimeric class I fusion protein F has become a central antigenic target in vaccine and monoclonal antibody (mAb) development (Wen et al., 2012; Rappazzo et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2023; Terstappen et al., 2024). Multiple major antigenic sites on RSV F have been defined through extensive mAb mapping, and the most potent neutralizing antibodies preferentially target prefusion-specific epitopes at the apex, including site Ø and neighboring pre-F-specific regions (Mclellan et al., 2013; Ngwuta et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2025). Although hMPV F and RSV F share a conserved overall architecture, their immunological profiles differ substantially (Rappazzo et al., 2022). Studies of natural infection and immunization have shown that a substantial fraction of hMPV-neutralizing antibodies target epitopes shared by pre-F and post-F, notably sites III and IV. Moreover, partial glycan shielding of the pre-F apex of hMPV F has been reported, and this feature has been proposed to limit the induction of RSV-like, site Ø-focused ultrapotent antibody responses (Battles et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2021).

Multiple structure-guided strategies have been explored to engineer hMPV F into a more stable pre-F immunogen. The first reported pre-F-stabilized hMPV F ectodomain used a proline substitution together with modification of the fusion-activating cleavage site and a C-terminal foldon trimerization domain. This design enabled structural characterization of hMPV pre-F and revealed important structural features, including the glycan shield at the trimer apex (Battles et al., 2017). It established the feasibility of producing hMPV F in the pre-F conformation, although its expression level and stability still required further improvement. Subsequent structure-based studies systematically evaluated proline substitutions, polar substitutions, cavity-filling mutations, and disulfide bonds, and combined beneficial substitutions into multi-mutation constructs. Among these designs, DS-CavEs2, generated by combining disulfide bonds, cavity-filling substitutions, and electrostatic interactions, showed improved expression, increased thermal stability, maintained MPE8 binding after thermal stress or storage, and elicited higher neutralizing antibody titers than post-F immunogens (Hsieh et al., 2022). These studies also indicated that different stabilizing elements need to be experimentally combined and validated to maintain expression, trimer formation, and pre-F antigenicity. Another representative strategy introduced interprotomer disulfide bonds to reduce interprotomer flexibility and stabilize the hMPV F trimer, thereby inducing high neutralizing antibody responses (Ou et al., 2023). However, the effect of interprotomer disulfide stabilization depends on appropriate disulfide positioning and requires experimental confirmation that the introduced constraints do not compromise protein expression or folding. More recent studies have further improved hMPV pre-F stabilization by using single-chain triple-disulfide-stabilized trimers, AI-guided pre-F trimer design, and computationally designed pre-F-stabilized antigens (Ou et al., 2023; Bakkers et al., 2024a; Kishko et al., 2025). These approaches have advanced hMPV F antigen design by improving trimer stability, expression, thermal stability, and immunogenicity, while also highlighting that cleavage-site control, trimer stabilization, interface stabilization, and preservation of pre-F antigenic sites remain important variables requiring experimental validation. Therefore, further rational optimization is still needed to improve the in vitro stability and in vivo performance of pre-F immunogens while supporting efficient antigen production. In addition, because hMPV and RSV F proteins share structural features and cross-reactive antibody responses have been documented, pre-existing RSV immunity may influence the specificity of responses elicited by hMPV F-based immunogens (Wen et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2022). Such effects could potentially limit the induction of protective hMPV-specific immunity. Addressing these issues will be critical for next-generation hMPV antigen design.

In this study, we applied a structure-based antigen design strategy to rationally optimize the hMPV F glycoprotein and performed molecular dynamics simulations on selected variants to assess conformational stability. Through iterative selection and comparison with the v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C pre-F comparator, VM-874 was identified as the lead candidate, showing higher expression in 293F cells and better preservation of neutralization-relevant, pre-F conformation-sensitive antibody-binding surfaces after heat stress. In immunogenicity studies, VM-874 elicited high titers of virus-neutralizing antibodies against both hMPV A2 and B1 strains in BALB/c mice and cotton rats and conferred robust protection in challenge experiments. Finally, given the structural similarity and partial epitope overlap between RSV F and hMPV F, we investigated whether VM-874 immunization could induce RSV cross-reactive antibody responses by assessing both binding and neutralizing activities. This analysis was undertaken to better define the immunological profile of VM-874 and to inform the rational development of RSV/hMPV vaccine strategies.

Results

Structure-guided screening of multi-substitution hMPV F antigen variants

To stabilize the metastable pre-F conformation of hMPV F, we performed a structure-guided screen focusing on three design strategies: local sequence replacement in the F1 N-terminal/fusion peptide-proximal region, disulfide-bond engineering in regions undergoing rearrangement during the pre-F-to-post-F transition, and optimization of potential interprotomer electrostatic interactions. All substitutions were introduced into a base construct comprising hMPV F A2 residues 1–489 (Fig. 1A). In total, 15 variants were designed, expressed, and characterized. Preservation of the pre-F antigenic surface was monitored via ELISA using the MPE8 antibody, a cross-reactive antibody targeting RSV-F and hMPV-F that preferentially recognizes the pre-F conformation.

ELISA screening in transiently transfected HEK293T cells revealed increased expression for seven disulfide variants relative to wild-type F (WT-F), including L378C/G432C, A140C/S149C, S65C/D183C, T127C/N153C, D54C/S265C, L66C/V191C, and S293C/S444C (Fig. 1B). To determine whether these improvements were maintained in a scalable expression system, the top-performing variants were further evaluated in suspension-adapted 293F cells. A140C/S149C showed a stronger expression-enhancing effect than the previously reported A140C/A147C pair (Supplementary Table S1), whereas L378C/G432C provided greater thermal stabilization than T365C/V463C (Supplementary Table S1). These results indicate that disulfide bonds introduced into regions involved in the pre-F-to-post-F transition can stabilize hMPV F through distinct effects on expression and thermal stability.

We next examined whether stabilization could be further improved by introducing additional substitutions at other sites. An alanine-to-aspartic acid substitution at position 225 (A225D) was introduced to potentially create a salt bridge with arginine 79 (R79) on a neighboring protomer, an interaction predicted to improve structural stability and expression (Supplementary Table S1). Comparison of the pre-F structures of hMPV F and RSV F revealed substantial divergence in the region corresponding to hMPV F residues 103–113. Because this region is adjacent to the fusion peptide and central cavity of the pre-F trimer, hMPV F residues 103–113 were replaced with the spatially corresponding RSV F sequence to examine whether modifying local interactions in this area could affect pre-F conformational stability. Based on these results, we combined the most beneficial changes into a single construct. This yielded VM-874, which contains A140C/S149C, A225D, L378C/G432C, and the 103–113 sequence replacement (Fig. 1C–F). WT-F and VM-874 exhibited comparable overall conformational stability over the 500 ns simulation, whereas VM-874 showed reduced local flexibility around the replacement region (Fig. 1G, H). Antigenic profiling further showed that A140C/S149C, L378C/G432C, and VM-874 retained reactivity with antibodies targeting pre-F-associated antigenic sites on hMPV F. In contrast, these stabilized constructs showed reduced binding to antibody 34, a rabbit-derived monoclonal antibody specific for the post-F conformation, indicating decreased exposure of the post-F-associated epitope (Fig. 1I).

Engineered VM-874 variant exhibits improved manufacturability-related properties

VM-874 was further constructed, purified, and evaluated for manufacturability (Fig. 2A). Relative to WT-F, VM-874 showed a 9.6-fold increase in expression in 293F cells (Supplementary Table S1). Thermal stability analysis showed that VM-874 had a melting temperature (Tm) of 66 °C, representing a 13 °C increase over WT-F (Fig. 2B), which was consistent with improved thermal stability. Functional integrity was assessed by measuring binding to a panel of pre-F- and post-F-specific antibodies targeting distinct epitopes using biolayer interferometry (BLI). VM-874 showed robust binding to epitope-specific antibodies, including MPE8 (Fig. 2C), MPV414 (Banerjee et al., 2022), ADI-61026, and 101F (Más et al., 2016), with low dissociation constants (KD) (Supplementary Fig. S1). Predominance of the pre-F conformation was further supported by negative-stain electron microscopy and cryo-EM 2D classification (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Fig. 2D). To further evaluate manufacturability, the binding of VM-874, v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C (pre-F comparator) (Ou et al., 2023), and WT-F to pre-F-specific antibodies was compared after heat treatment at 65 °C or 70 °C for 30 min. After heat treatment at 65 °C for 30 min, WT-F and v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C showed changes consistent with degradation or conformational transition, accompanied by substantially reduced binding to the pre-F-specific antibodies MPE8 and ADI-61026 (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S3). In contrast, under the same conditions, VM-874 retained structural integrity and showed no apparent loss of binding to any of the antibodies tested, indicating preservation of key neutralizing epitopes under thermal stress. After storage at 4 °C for 7 or 21 days, the binding of antibodies to VM-874 remained unchanged (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. S4), indicating sustained antigenicity during storage. Collectively, these results indicate that VM-874 exhibits improved manufacturability, combining enhanced expression in suspension-adapted 293F cells with increased thermal stability and favorable biophysical properties that are important for chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) development.

VM-874 elicits cross-lineage neutralizing antibodies against hMPV A2 and B1 and protects BALB/c mice against hMPV A2 challenge

The immunogenicity and protective efficacy of VM-874 were systematically evaluated in 6- to 8-week-old BALB/c mice (Fig. 3A). Mice were intramuscularly immunized with 5 μg of VM-874, post-F, or v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C formulated with 50 μg of aluminum hydroxide adjuvant, while control mice received adjuvant alone. A prime immunization was administered at week 0, followed by a booster immunization 3 weeks later. Serum samples were collected 2 weeks after the booster immunization for antibody analyses, and mice were subsequently challenged intranasally with 106 FFU of hMPV A2 to evaluate protective efficacy. Body weight was monitored daily after challenge (Fig. 3B).

Following booster immunization, serum IgG binding titers against both hMPV pre-F and post-F were significantly increased in the antigen-immunized groups relative to the adjuvant-only control group (Fig. 3C, D and Supplementary Fig. S5). Antibody functionality was further assessed by measuring serum neutralizing activity using a focus reduction neutralization test (FRNT50). VM-874 elicited robust neutralizing responses against both hMPV A2 and B1 strains. Against hMPV A2, VM-874 induced a neutralizing titer (GMT: 713) that 7-fold higher than post-F (GMT: 99) and 1.9-fold higher than that of the previously reported optimized pre-F comparator v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C (GMT: 369), although no significant difference was observed between VM-874 and v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S6). Against hMPV B1, VM-874 induced significantly higher neutralizing titers than post-F (GMT: 1020 vs. 79) and numerically higher neutralizing titers than v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C (GMT: 572), although no significant difference was observed between VM-874 and v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C (Fig. 3F).

Protective efficacy was then assessed in an hMPV A2 murine challenge model. Two weeks after the booster dose, all mice, including adjuvant-only controls, were challenged intranasally with 106 FFU of hMPV A2. Compared with the adjuvant-only group, the VM-874-immunized group showed significantly reduced infectious viral titers and viral RNA levels in lung homogenates (Fig. 3G and H). Consistent with previous reports in BALB/c hMPV challenge models, body weight declined after challenge in both immunized and control mice, with broadly comparable kinetics across groups (Fig. 3B). Histopathological analysis further showed that mice immunized with pre-F antigens, including VM-874 and v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C, exhibited attenuated inflammatory lung injury, as evidenced by reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and less pronounced alveolar septal thickening (Fig. 3I and J). Together, these findings indicate that VM-874 elicits protective immunity that limits viral replication and mitigates infection-associated lung pathology.

VM-874 elicits neutralizing antibodies and confers protection against hMPV challenge in cotton rats

Cotton rats are among the most widely used and highly permissive rodent models for hMPV research (Williams et al., 2005; Green et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2014). This model is particularly well suited for simultaneously assessing viral replication in both the upper respiratory tract (nasal turbinates) and lower respiratory tract (lungs), as well as lung inflammation and the in vivo efficacy of preventive or therapeutic interventions. VM-874 was therefore further evaluated in cotton rats using a prime-boost immunization regimen followed by viral challenge, with body weight monitored daily after challenge (Fig. 4A and B). Cotton rats were intramuscularly immunized with 5 μg of post-F, v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C, or VM-874 formulated with 50 μg of aluminum hydroxide adjuvant, while control animals received 50 μg of aluminum hydroxide adjuvant alone. A prime immunization was administered at week 0, followed by serum collection at week 2 and a booster immunization at week 3. Humoral immunogenicity was first assessed by antigen-binding assays, which showed readily detectable serum IgG responses against both pre-F (VM-874) and post-F following booster immunization (Fig. 4C and D). All antigen-immunized groups showed markedly increased hMPV F-reactive binding antibody responses compared with the adjuvant-only control group. Functional activity was then evaluated by neutralization assays, which showed that VM-874 elicited neutralizing antibodies against both hMPV A2 and B1 strains (Fig. 4E and F). Against hMPV A2, VM-874 elicited neutralizing titers with a GMT of 3,059, comparable to v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C (GMT: 2821) and higher than post-F (GMT: 1377). Against hMPV B1, VM-874 induced significantly higher neutralizing titers than both post-F (GMT: 8462 vs. 3317) and v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C (GMT: 8462 vs. 1971). Overall, under the corresponding immunization regimen, the neutralizing titers elicited by VM-874 in cotton rats were higher than those observed in BALB/c mice, supporting its ability to induce cross-lineage neutralizing responses in this model.

Protective efficacy was subsequently assessed following hMPV challenge. Compared with adjuvant-only controls, antigen-immunized cotton rats showed significantly reduced pulmonary viral RNA copy numbers (Fig. 4G). Infectious viral titers in lung homogenates were readily detected in the adjuvant-only group but were not detectable in the post-F-, v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C-, or VM-874-immunized groups (Fig. 4H), demonstrating effective suppression of viral replication in vivo. Consistent with the relatively mild clinical course of infection in this model, body weight remained broadly stable after challenge in both immunized and control animals (Fig. 4B), indicating the absence of overt systemic illness. To assess viral replication in the upper respiratory tract, viral burden in the nasal turbinates was quantified. Infectious virus was detected in the nasal turbinates of adjuvant-only controls but was undetectable in antigen-immunized animals (Fig. 4I). Concordantly, histopathological analysis showed attenuated inflammatory lung injury and reduced pathological scores in antigen-immunized animals, including VM-874-immunized cotton rats, compared with adjuvant-only controls (Fig. 4J and K). Together, these results indicate that VM-874 induces strong F-directed binding and neutralizing antibody responses and confers protection against hMPV infection in the cotton rat model.

VM-874 does not increase pulmonary RSV burden or exacerbate lung pathology following heterologous RSV challenge

Because RSV F and hMPV F share structurally conserved antigenic features and cross-reactive epitopes, we investigated whether the pre-F-stabilized hMPV F immunogen VM-874 could elicit RSV F-cross-reactive antibody responses and whether these responses mediated heterologous protection against RSV infection. To evaluate this, cotton rats and BALB/c mice were intramuscularly immunized with 5 μg of antigen formulated with 50 μg of aluminum hydroxide adjuvant. The immunization groups included hMPV post-F, the pre-F-stabilized hMPV F variant v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C, VM-874, pXCS847, and an aluminum hydroxide adjuvant-only control (Fig. 5A). pXCS847, a pre-F-stabilized RSV F antigen derived from Pfizer's RSV pre-F antigen-design program and corresponding to the optimized RSV pre-F construct 847, was included as a homologous RSV F positive-control immunogen (Mclellan et al., 2013; Che et al., 2023). Animals received a prime immunization at week 0 and a booster immunization 3 weeks later. Serum samples were collected 2 weeks after the booster immunization to assess binding antibody responses to RSV pre-F and RSV post-F antigens, as well as RSV-A2 neutralizing activity via FRNT50 assay. BALB/c mice were subsequently challenged intranasally with RSV-A2 to evaluate heterologous protection and potential vaccine-enhanced disease-associated pulmonary pathology.

In cotton rats, immunization with v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C or hMPV post-F induced detectable serum binding antibodies that bound both RSV pre-F and RSV post-F antigens, although the magnitude of these responses was lower than that induced by pXCS847 (Fig. 5B and C). In contrast, VM-874 immunization induced detectable serum antibodies against RSV pre-F, whereas little or no binding to RSV post-F was detected (Fig. 5B and C). As expected, pXCS847 elicited the strongest RSV F-binding responses and robust RSV-A2 neutralizing activity. In contrast, RSV-neutralizing activity remained undetectable in all hMPV F-immunized groups, including the VM-874, v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C, and hMPV post-F groups (Fig. 5D). A similar pattern was observed in BALB/c mice. Immunization with v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C or hMPV post-F induced measurable cross-binding antibodies against both RSV pre-F and RSV post-F, whereas VM-874 predominantly induced antibodies that bound RSV pre-F, with little or no detectable binding to RSV post-F. Nevertheless, only pXCS847 induced detectable RSV-A2-neutralizing antibodies (Fig. 5E–G). These findings indicate that VM-874 elicits antibodies that recognize RSV F; however, these cross-reactive responses are detectable by binding assays but non-neutralizing under the conditions tested.

To determine whether these cross-reactive antibody responses confer heterologous protection, immunized BALB/c mice were challenged intranasally with RSV-A2, and lung viral titers were measured as FFU/g. Consistent with its potent RSV-neutralizing activity, pXCS847 markedly reduced pulmonary RSV titers compared with the adjuvant control and hMPV F-immunized groups (Fig. 5H). In contrast, VM-874 did not significantly reduce lung RSV replication, and viral titers in the VM-874 group were comparable to those in the adjuvant control group (Fig. 5H). Importantly, VM-874 immunization did not increase pulmonary RSV burden relative to the adjuvant control. Histopathological analysis further showed that lung sections from VM-874-immunized mice did not show evident exacerbation relative to adjuvant-treated mice, with no obvious enhancement of inflammatory pathology (Fig. 5I). By contrast, hMPV post-F immunization was associated with more pronounced pulmonary inflammatory cell infiltration after RSV challenge (Fig. 5I), and additional quantitative analysis showed increased eosinophil and neutrophil infiltration in this group (Supplementary Fig. S7). Together, these results suggest that distinct conformational states of hMPV F may differentially influence the outcome of heterologous RSV exposure. The pre-F-stabilized hMPV F immunogen VM-874 induced RSV F-cross-reactive binding antibodies but did not elicit detectable RSV-neutralizing activity or heterologous protection. Nevertheless, VM-874 did not enhance pulmonary RSV replication or lung pathology following RSV-A2 challenge, whereas hMPV post-F immunization was associated with an unfavorable inflammatory phenotype in this heterologous challenge model.

Discussion

Pre-F stabilization has become a central design principle in the rational development of pneumovirus F-based vaccine antigens. Previous studies have demonstrated that hMPV F can be stabilized through proline substitutions, cleavage-site engineering, disulfide stabilization, cavity-filling mutations, electrostatic optimization, single-chain trimer design, AI-guided prefusion-trimer design, and computational antigen design (Hsieh et al., 2022; Ou et al., 2023; Bakkers et al., 2024b). These studies established important design principles for hMPV pre-F immunogens, but they also showed that improved stability alone may not fully ensure preserved antigenicity, efficient expression, manufacturability, or protective immunogenicity. Therefore, further antigen optimization remains necessary to preserve neutralization-related epitopes while maintaining efficient expression, trimer formation, and stability under stress conditions.

In this study, VM-874 was designed to improve both local conformational stability and trimer stability of hMPV F. Unlike designs that mainly rely on a single class of stabilizing mutations, VM-874 combines two engineered disulfide bonds, A140C/S149C and L378C/G432C, a designed electrostatic interaction through A225D, and replacement of hMPV F residues 103–113 with the corresponding RSV F sequence. The A140C/S149C disulfide bond is positioned in a region involved in conformational rearrangement during the pre-F-to-post-F transition and may restrict local refolding near HRA and the central helix. A225D was introduced to promote a potential interprotomer salt bridge with R79, whereas L378C/G432C and the 103–113 sequence replacement were designed to support trimer integrity. Thus, VM-874 integrates local disulfide stabilization, potential interprotomer electrostatic stabilization, and trimer-interface stabilization within a soluble trimeric antigen.

The antigenic evaluation of VM-874 further highlights its improved ability to maintain pre-F antigenic integrity under stress conditions compared with the v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C pre-F comparator. Because the DSF profile of v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C could not be reliably determined, we did not use Tm as the primary basis for direct comparison between the two pre-F antigens. Instead, their conformational quality was compared using conformation-sensitive monoclonal antibody binding and stress-condition antigenicity. VM-874 showed robust binding to multiple epitope-specific antibodies, including MPE8, MPV414, ADI-61026, and 101F, indicating preservation of several neutralization-relevant antigenic surfaces. Notably, after heat treatment, v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C showed markedly reduced binding to pre-F-specific antibodies, whereas VM-874 retained binding to the tested antibodies and maintained pre-F epitope integrity. VM-874 also preserved antibody binding after storage at 4 °C. These results suggest that VM-874 provides a more stress-resistant pre-F antigenic platform than the comparator antigen and has an improved capacity to maintain neutralization-relevant conformational epitopes during antigen handling and storage.

The immunogenicity results demonstrate that the structural stabilization strategy used for VM-874 translates into a functional antibody response in vivo. In BALB/c mice, VM-874 induced strong F-specific binding antibodies and generated a pre-F-biased serum IgG binding profile, as shown by the elevated pre-F/post-F binding IgG ratio (Supplementary Fig. S5). Moreover, VM-874 elicited neutralizing antibody responses against both hMPV A2 and B1 strains, with titers higher than those induced by post-F and numerically higher than those elicited by the v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C pre-F comparator. In cotton rats, VM-874 similarly induced robust cross-lineage neutralizing responses and reduced viral burden in both the upper and lower respiratory tract. These findings suggest that VM-874 preserves key pre-F antigenic features required for broad hMPV neutralization and in vivo protection.

Because hMPV F and RSV F share conserved structural features, hMPV F immunization may elicit antibodies capable of binding RSV F (Wen et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2022). In particular, antigenic sites III and IV are conserved regions shared by RSV F and hMPV F, and several antibodies, including MPE8, 101F, 54G10, and 17E10, have been reported to show RSV/hMPV cross-reactivity or cross-neutralization (Wen et al., 2017; Mousa et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2022). However, cross-binding does not necessarily indicate cross-neutralization or heterologous protection. This distinction is important because RSV and hMPV differ in the conformational distribution of protective antibody epitopes. For RSV, a large fraction of serum neutralizing activity is mediated by antibodies specific for the pre-F conformation, whereas hMPV-neutralizing antibodies more frequently recognize epitopes shared between pre-F and post-F conformations (Battles et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2019). Therefore, hMPV F-induced antibodies that bind RSV F may differ in epitope specificity, binding orientation, avidity, and functional activity from antibodies elicited directly by RSV F immunogens.

The observation that hMPV post-F immune serum bound RSV pre-F more strongly than RSV post-F may reflect conformational and epitope-presentation differences between the two pneumovirus F proteins. Although hMPV post-F may induce antibodies targeting conserved regions such as antigenic sites III and IV, these cross-reactive epitopes may be more accessible or more favorably displayed on RSV pre-F than on RSV post-F in the recombinant antigens used for binding assays. Antigenic site IV is present in both RSV pre-F and post-F, whereas site III includes antibodies with different conformation preferences, including pre-F-biased cross-reactive antibodies such as MPE8 (Mousa et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019). Thus, stronger binding of hMPV post-F immune serum to RSV pre-F does not necessarily mean that the serum contains RSV pre-F-specific neutralizing antibodies. Rather, it may indicate that some hMPV post-F-induced antibodies recognize conserved surfaces that are better presented on RSV pre-F in the binding assays. This interpretation is consistent with previous findings showing that antibody binding to RSV/hMPV F is influenced not only by sequence conservation, but also by conformational state and binding orientation (Mousa et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019).

Notably, VM-874 induced RSV F cross-binding antibodies but did not generate detectable RSV-neutralizing activity and did not increase pulmonary RSV burden after heterologous RSV challenge. In contrast, hMPV post-F immunization also induced RSV F cross-binding antibodies but was associated with higher lung viral titers and more pronounced inflammatory pathology after RSV challenge. These results suggest that the conformational state of the hMPV F immunogen may influence not only homologous neutralization, but also the functional quality of cross-reactive antibody responses during heterologous RSV exposure. One possible explanation is that VM-874, as a pre-F-stabilized trimer, may preferentially focus antibody responses toward structurally constrained and neutralization-related F epitopes, whereas post-F may elicit a higher proportion of non-neutralizing or poorly protective cross-binding antibodies. In the context of RSV vaccine-enhanced disease, non-protective or low-avidity antibodies, immune complex formation, complement activation, and Th2-biased pulmonary inflammation have been implicated as contributing factors (Polack et al., 2002; Delgado et al., 2009; Knudson et al., 2015). Therefore, the biological effect of RSV F cross-binding antibodies may depend less on binding titer alone and more on epitope specificity, avidity, IgG subclass distribution, Fc-mediated effector functions, complement activation, and the accompanying T cell response.

This interpretation may also explain why RSV F cross-binding antibodies induced by VM-874, despite lacking detectable RSV-neutralizing activity, were not associated with evident vaccine-associated enhanced viral diseases-like pathology, whereas hMPV post-F immunization was associated with enhanced pulmonary inflammation. VM-874-induced cross-binding antibodies may bind RSV F in a manner that is insufficient for neutralization but also insufficient to promote pathogenic immune complex deposition or pro-inflammatory Fc receptor- and complement-mediated activation. Conversely, hMPV post-F-induced antibodies may preferentially recognize non-neutralizing conserved surfaces that bind RSV F during infection without blocking fusion, thereby allowing viral replication to proceed in the presence of antibody-antigen complexes. Such a scenario could amplify local inflammation through Fc receptor engagement, complement activation, or recruitment of inflammatory leukocytes. Because these mechanisms were not directly tested in the present study, this explanation remains speculative. Future studies should evaluate RSV F cross-binding antibody avidity, epitope specificity, IgG subclasses, complement deposition, Fcγ receptor-dependent effector functions, cytokine profiles, and pulmonary inflammatory cell composition after heterologous RSV challenge.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although VM-874 was obtained through structure-guided design, molecular dynamics simulation, and experimental screening, it was not derived from exhaustive screening of all possible cysteine-pair, electrostatic, or multi-substitution combinations. Further combinatorial optimization may therefore identify variants with improved expression, thermal stability, antigenic durability, or manufacturability. Second, although VM-874 induced cross-lineage neutralizing antibody responses and reduced viral burden in BALB/c mice and cotton rats, these findings were mainly obtained from short-term preclinical animal experiments. Further studies are needed to evaluate immune durability, dose-sparing potential, adjuvant compatibility, the effects of repeated immunization, and protective efficacy in additional preclinical models. Third, the mechanisms underlying the different outcomes associated with RSV F cross-binding antibodies remain unclear. Future studies should therefore evaluate not only cross-binding and cross-neutralization, but also antibody avidity, epitope specificity, IgG subclass distribution, Fcγ receptor-dependent functions, complement deposition, cytokine profiles, and virological and pathological outcomes after sequential or heterologous virus exposure. These analyses will help define the functional boundaries and safety implications of hMPV/RSV cross-reactive immune responses.

Conclusions

Taken together, our findings support pre-F-focused hMPV F design as an important principle for next-generation hMPV vaccine development and suggest that this principle should also inform RSV/hMPV combination or sequential immunization strategies. This interpretation is consistent with structural-vaccinology studies showing that pre-F stabilization best preserves neutralization-relevant surfaces on pneumovirus F proteins and can improve the immunogenicity and protective performance of hMPV F immunogens. Because RSV and hMPV exhibit overlapping seasonal circulation and share structurally conserved, partly cross-reactive F epitopes, combined or sequential exposure may give rise to immune interactions that are not apparent in single-pathogen settings. Accordingly, preclinical evaluation of combined RSV/hMPV strategies should extend beyond homologous immunogenicity to include cross-binding versus cross-neutralization, the potential for sub-neutralizing or non-neutralizing cross-reactive responses, and virologic as well as pathologic outcomes in heterologous or sequential challenge models. Such benchmarking will be important to determine whether combined approaches can provide additive protection while minimizing unintended immune modulation.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

All hMPV F variants were cloned into a pcDNA3.1 expression vector containing a C-terminal 6 × His tag using homologous recombination, and all constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. Plasmids encoding F variants were transiently transfected into FreeStyle 293F cells using polyethyleneimine (PEI, MKbio, Shanghai, China). At 5 days post-transfection, culture supernatants were clarified by centrifugation at 5000×g (Beckman Coulter Avanti JXN-26, Brea, CA, USA) for 30 min and incubated with Ni-NTA resin (GenScript, L00666-100, Jiangsu, China) at 16 °C for 2 h. Bound proteins were eluted with PBS containing 500 mM imidazole. Protein purity (> 90%) was assessed by SDS-PAGE, and samples were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) equilibrated with PBS to obtain monodisperse fractions. The v3B_Δ12 D454C/V458C antigen was provided by the Beijing Institute of Biological Products.

Plasmids encoding the heavy and light chains of MPE8 (Corti et al., 2013), DS7 (Wen et al., 2012), ADI-61026 (Rappazzo et al., 2022), and MPV414 (Banerjee et al., 2022) were co-transfected into FreeStyle 293F cells at a 1:1 ratio using PEI. To purify IgG, clarified supernatants were applied to Protein A resin, and bound material was eluted with 100 mM glycine (pH 3.0). Eluates were immediately neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and further purified by SEC on a Superdex 200 column (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) in PBS to obtain monodisperse fractions. All protein samples were concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units (Merck Millipore, Ireland) to 2–4 mg/mL, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Molecular dynamics simulations of hMPV F

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the pre-F hMPV F trimer structure (PDB: 5WB0) as the starting model. Simulations were carried out with ACEMD using the AMBER14SB force field, the TIP3P water model, and a Langevin thermostat. The protein was solvated in an explicit water box with a 10 Å solvent buffer under periodic boundary conditions and simulated at 50 °C. After energy minimization, heavy-atom restraints were gradually released over the first 60 ns to allow equilibration. Three independent 500 ns production runs were then performed. Structural dynamics and stability were evaluated by analyzing backbone RMSD and per-residue RMSF using GROMACS (Bakkers et al., 2024a).

Antigen-coating ELISA for assessing pre-F epitope integrity after heat treatment

Heat-treated and untreated pre-F proteins were individually immobilized on 96-well ELISA plates (NEST, Jiangsu, China) at a concentration of 2 μg/mL (100 μL per well) overnight at 4 °C. Plates were then blocked with 2% (w/v) BSA in PBST for 2 h at 37 °C. A panel of hMPV F-specific monoclonal antibodies (MPE8, MPV414, 101F, and ADI-61026) was serially diluted from an initial concentration of 10 μg/mL, added to the pre-F protein-coated wells, and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed three times with PBST to remove unbound antibodies, and bound antibodies were detected using an appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed three times with PBST, developed with TMB Single-Component Substrate (Solarbio, PR1200, Beijing, China), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

ELISA for accelerated and long-term stability assessment

hMPV F proteins subjected to different treatments (e.g., accelerated or long-term stability conditions) were immobilized on 96-well ELISA plates at a concentration of 2 μg/mL (100 μL per well) overnight at 4 °C. Plates were washed with PBST, blocked with 2% (w/v) BSA for 2 h at 37 °C in PBST, and washed again. Serial dilutions of antibodies starting from 10 μg/mL (10-fold serial dilutions) were then added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, bound antibodies were detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG secondary antibody (Sino Biological, Beijing, China) at a dilution of 1:5000 for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by development with TMB substrate and measurement of absorbance at 450 nm.

Serum IgG binding ELISA

Serum IgG binding to hMPV F proteins was measured by ELISA. hMPV F proteins (2 μg/mL) were immobilized on 96-well ELISA plates overnight at 4 °C. Plates were washed with PBST, blocked with 2% (w/v) BSA in PBST, and washed again. Serial dilutions of sera (starting with an initial 1:80 dilution, followed by 5-fold serial dilutions) were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Bound IgG was detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (H+L) antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-1, USA) or an HRP-conjugated anti-cotton rat IgG (H+L) antibody (ICL Lab, CCOT-25P, Oregon, USA) at a dilution of 1:5000 for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by TMB development and measurement of absorbance at 450 nm.

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF)

SYPRO Orange dye (5000 × ; Invitrogen, Carlsbad MA, USA) was diluted in 1 × PBS to prepare a 50 × working solution. For each reaction, 3 μg of purified protein was diluted in 1 × PBS to a final volume of 27 μL, and 3 μL of 50 × SYPRO Orange was added to give a final reaction volume of 30 μL in a 96-well PCR plate. Wells containing 1 × PBS without protein served as negative controls. Plates were sealed with MicroAmp Optical Adhesive Film (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and fluorescence was measured on a real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument (Bio-Rad CFX96, Hercules, CA, USA). Samples were held at 25 °C for 3 min, followed by a temperature increment of 0.5 °C per minute within the range of 25 °C–95 °C. The melting temperature (Tm) was determined from the peak of the negative first derivative of the fluorescence signal with respect to temperature (−dF/dT), corresponding to the midpoint of thermal unfolding.

Affinity kinetics analysis by biolayer interferometry

Assays were performed on an Octet R8 system (Sartorius, Germany) at 25 °C using 1 × PBST as the running buffer in black 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One, Germany). Protein A biosensors (Sartorius, Germany) were pre-soaked in 1 × PBST for 10 min and equilibrated with a 60 s baseline step. Purified antibodies (8 μg/mL) were immobilized until a response of ∼1.2 nm was reached. After a second 60 s baseline in 1 × PBST, sensors were exposed to a 2-fold serial dilution series of antigen (seven concentrations, starting at 100 nM), including a buffer-only reference (0 nM antigen), for 180 s, followed by a 600 s dissociation step in 1 × PBST. Biosensors were regenerated between cycles using 10 mM glycine (pH 1.5). Data were analyzed using Octet Analysis Studio v13.0 with a 1:1 global fit model to determine Ka, Kd, and KD (KD = Kd/Ka).

Negative-stain transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Purified VM-874 was diluted to 10 μg/mL in 1 × PBS. A 4 μL aliquot was applied to a glow-discharged, carbon-coated grid (Zhongjingkeyi Technology; 60 s at 15 mA, 38 mbar) and incubated for 1 min to allow adsorption. Excess solution was removed by gentle blotting with filter paper. Uranyl acetate (3%, RXSV CHEM) was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min before use. A 100 μL volume was placed on parafilm and divided into three drops. The grid (carbon side down) was briefly dipped into each drop sequentially, with an incubation time of 30 s per drop. After the final dip, residual uranyl acetate was removed by gentle blotting, and the grid was air-dried at room temperature. TEM imaging was performed on a Talos L120C G2 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) operated at 120 kV. Representative micrographs were acquired at 120,000 × magnification, and particle morphology was assessed from the resulting images.

Cells

Baby hamster kidney (BHK-21-STD) cells were kindly provided by Guangzhou Starda Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China), whereas human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA; CRL-3216). Both cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma, MO, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin; Sigma, MO, USA). BHK-21-STD cells were seeded 1 day before infection and reached ∼80% confluence on the day of infection.

Viruses

The hMPV A2 strain (HMPV-A2/GZ/1804-183) and hMPV B1 strain (HMPV-B1/GZ/1712-70) were propagated in BHK-21-STD cells. BHK-21-STD cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.2. After adsorption for 1 h, the inoculum was removed and replaced with DMEM containing 0.1% BSA and 0.5 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin. After 2−3 days of incubation at 35 °C in 5% CO2, the culture supernatant was collected, clarified by centrifugation to remove cell debris, and stored at −80 °C.

HEp-2 cells were seeded in T75 flasks at 5 × 106 cells per flask and incubated overnight (12−20 h) to reach ∼80% confluence. RSV A2 was used to infect cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. Prior to infection, the culture medium was removed and the monolayer was rinsed once with 10 mL DMEM. Cells were then inoculated with 2 mL of RSV inoculum diluted in DMEM and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 3 h, with gentle rocking twice during the adsorption period. After adsorption, 8 mL of pre-warmed DMEM-based maintenance medium was added, and cultures were incubated at 33 °C with 5% CO2 for 2.5 days while monitoring for cytopathic effect (CPE). For virus harvest, infected cultures were subjected to one freeze–thaw cycle at −80 °C. Supernatants were collected, clarified by centrifugation at 2000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and stored at −80 °C.

Mice studies

Specific pathogen-free 6- to 8-week-old female BALB/c mice were used for immunization and viral challenge experiments. Animals were group-housed in ventilated racks under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 21 °C, with food and water available ad libitum. Groups of five mice were inoculated intramuscularly into both quadriceps muscles (100 μL per site; 5 μg total antigen per dose), formulated in PBS with 50 μg aluminum hydroxide adjuvant. Three weeks after the prime, mice were boosted with the same formulation, and blood was collected two weeks later by retro-orbital sampling. For the post-F construct, the protein was heated at 70 °C for 10 min before inoculation. Two weeks after booster immunization, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and challenged intranasally with 106 FFU of hMPV A2 or RSV A2 in a 50 μL volume. At 4 days post-challenge, mice were humanely euthanized, and lungs were collected to determine viral burden. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou National Laboratory (GZLAB-AUCP-2025-10-A06).

Cotton rat studies

Cotton rats (Sigmodon hispidus) used in this study were derived from breeding pairs purchased from ENVIGO RMS, LLC (Indianapolis, IN, USA), with authorized breeding rights. The animals were subsequently bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the animal facility of Guangzhou Laboratory. Animals were immunized using the same antigen dose and formulation described for mice. Two weeks after booster immunization, cotton rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and challenged intranasally with hMPV A2 (106 FFU in 100 μL), as indicated. At 4 days post-challenge, animals were humanely euthanized, and lungs and nasal turbinates were collected for determination of viral burden. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou National Laboratory (GZLAB-AUCP-2025-10-A06).

Focus reduction neutralization test (FRNT50)

BHK-21 cells or HEp-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates 1 day prior to infection. Serum samples were serially diluted in DMEM (starting with an initial 1:20 dilution, followed by 3-fold serial dilutions) and mixed with an equal volume of hMPV A2 or hMPV B1 (MOI = 0.2) or RSV A2 (MOI = 0.2). After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the serum-virus mixtures were added to BHK-21 monolayers (for hMPV) or HEp-2 monolayers (for RSV). FRNT50 was defined as the reciprocal serum dilution that reduced fluorescent focus counts by 50% relative to virus-only controls.

Focus-formation assay (FFA)

Virus titers were determined using a focus formation assay (FFA). For hMPV, BHK-21-STD cells were seeded in 96-well plates 1 day prior to infection. Undiluted virus stocks or lung homogenates, followed by four-fold serial dilutions, were added to BHK-21-STD cell monolayers and subjected to spinoculation at 1500 ×g for 90 min. The inoculum was then removed and replaced with 200 μL DMEM containing 0.1% BSA and 0.5 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with 5% BSA (Macklin, B824162, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. Cells were incubated with the human anti-F monoclonal antibody MPV414 for 2 h, followed by a FITC-labeled goat anti-human secondary antibody (Sino Biological, Beijing, China). Fluorescent foci were imaged and counted, and viral titers were calculated as focus-forming units (FFU) per mL. For RSV, HEp-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates 1 day prior to infection. Undiluted virus stocks or lung homogenates, followed by four-fold serial dilutions, were added to HEp-2 monolayers. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA (Macklin, B824162, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with an FITC-conjugated anti-RSV antibody (1:100). For tissue homogenates, viral titers were reported as FFU/g tissue.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissues using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA). Viral RNA levels were quantified by one-step qRT-PCR using gene-specific primers and the HiScript II One Step qRT-PCR SYBR Green Kit (Vazyme, Q221-01, Jiangsu, China) on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 Flex instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primer sequences (forward/reverse; 5′–3′) were as follows: hMPV N gene, ACAGCAGATTCTAAGAAACTCAGGC/TCTTTGTCTATCTCTTCCACC; RSV F gene, CGAGCCAGAAGAGAACTACCA/CCTTCTAGGTGCAGGACCTTA; Relative quantification was performed using β-actin as the internal control.

Histopathological analysis

Excised lungs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed lungs were processed for histological analysis, with the left lung lobe embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). H&E staining and quantification of eosinophils and neutrophils were performed by Wuhan Baiqiandu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Lung histopathological changes were evaluated by three independent blinded reviewers using a four-point ordinal scoring scale, with a score of 1 indicating normal tissue and a score of 4 indicating extensive or severe pathological changes. Representative histopathological images were selected from samples with pathology scores close to the median score of each group, in order to minimize subjective selection bias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Continuous variables, including antibody titers and viral titers, were log10-transformed when appropriate to reduce data skewness and better satisfy the assumptions of parametric tests. Geometric mean titers (GMTs) were calculated by back-transforming the mean values of log10-transformed titers. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student's t-test; when the assumption of equal variance was not met, Welch's corrected t-test was used. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by Dunnett's or Tukey's multiple-comparison test as appropriate. Values below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) were assigned the LLOD before statistical analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
