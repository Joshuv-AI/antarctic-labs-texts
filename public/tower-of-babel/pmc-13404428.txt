Clinical impact of wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet (WALANT) in carpal tunnel release: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Chia-You Chou, Wei-Ting Lin, Chi-Sheng Chien, Yu-Jung Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet (WALANT) is increasingly used in carpal tunnel release (CTR) because it avoids tourniquet-related discomfort and allows active intraoperative testing. Although WALANT improves perioperative experience, its effects on postoperative pain and functional recovery remain incompletely defined. Existing meta-analyses are limited by nonrandomized designs and heterogeneous hand procedures.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing WALANT with tourniquet-based anesthetic techniques for CTR. Outcomes included operative time, intraoperative pain, early postoperative pain, patient satisfaction, adverse events, and functional outcomes assessed using the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ). Random-effects models with prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses were applied.ResultsEight RCTs were included. Operative time did not differ between WALANT and comparator techniques overall, although operative time was longer with WALANT in open procedures. WALANT was consistently associated with lower intraoperative pain. Early postoperative pain showed substantial heterogeneity across studies, with smaller differences observed in endoscopic procedures. Overall patient satisfaction was higher with WALANT. Adverse events were uncommon and comparable between groups. Functional outcomes were reported in two trials; pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in the BCTQ Functional Status Scale favoring WALANT (mean difference [MD] − 1.17), while no difference was observed in the Symptom Severity Scale.ConclusionsBased on RCTs, WALANT for carpal tunnel release is associated with lower intraoperative pain and higher overall patient satisfaction. Early postoperative pain was lower with WALANT, although findings were heterogeneous across studies. WALANT may also be associated with improved postoperative hand function. Future randomized studies should systematically report operative and tourniquet duration and incorporate standardized functional outcome measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06974-2.

Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is one of the most common peripheral nerve entrapment disorders [1], and surgical decompression shows higher long-term effectiveness [2] and improves electrophysiological measures [3] compared with conservative treatment. For surgeons, the primary objective of carpal tunnel release is to achieve a complete release of the transverse carpal ligament to ensure symptom relief and functional recovery. Incomplete release remains the leading cause of failed surgery and subsequent revisions. Prior literature has identified incomplete release of the transverse carpal ligament as the most common cause of revision carpal tunnel surgery [4], attributed to insufficient exposure and suboptimal visualization [5].

Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet (WALANT) has gained popularity because it eliminates tourniquet pain and allows for active intraoperative testing [6, 7], thereby altering the surgical environment without changing the surgical procedure itself. While many studies have established that WALANT improves the perioperative experience, its impact on long-term functional recovery is still not fully understood.

Recent meta-analyses [8–10] have attempted to evaluate the efficacy of WALANT in hand surgery. However, these studies often include non-randomized data, which can lower the overall quality of evidence. Furthermore, many existing pooled analyses combine carpal tunnel release with other minor hand procedures, such as trigger finger or De Quervain’s release. Because each of these conditions follows a different recovery pattern, mixing them may hide the true impact of WALANT specifically on carpal tunnel outcomes. Recently published RCTs now allow for a more updated and focused analysis. We aim to use these new data to address the gaps in earlier meta-analyses.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis focused exclusively on RCTs involving carpal tunnel release. Our goal was to evaluate the clinical impact of WALANT on intraoperative and early postoperative pain and to explore whether its technical and experiential advantages may be associated with postoperative functional outcomes.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. A comprehensive literature search was performed to identify RCTs comparing WALANT with other anesthetic techniques for carpal tunnel release.

We searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for studies published from database inception through January 10, 2026, without restrictions on language or publication date. The search strategy incorporated a combination of free text, Medical Subject Headings and Emtree terms.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts using predefined eligibility criteria, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant articles. Any disagreements during the screening and selection process were resolved through discussion and consensus. Citation searching of included studies was additionally performed to identify eligible trials not captured by the electronic search.

Detailed information regarding the eligibility criteria, search strategy, and study selection process is provided in the Supplement. The systematic review protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD420261290147).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected according to predefined criteria based on the PICO framework. The population of interest consisted of adult patients undergoing carpal tunnel release for clinically diagnosed carpal tunnel syndrome. Only randomized controlled trials were eligible for inclusion, defined as studies with reported methodology consistent with randomized allocation.

The intervention of interest was WALANT, defined as local anesthetic infiltration with epinephrine performed without tourniquet application. Comparator interventions included anesthetic techniques involving tourniquet use, such as local anesthesia with tourniquet, intravenous regional anesthesia (IVRA), or regional nerve block.

Eligible outcomes included intraoperative pain, operative time, early postoperative pain, patient satisfaction, and functional outcomes. Studies were excluded if they were nonrandomized, observational, biomechanical or cadaveric studies, conference abstracts, review articles, or protocol-only registrations without completed trial data.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (CYC, YJS), and the extracted data were cross-checked to ensure accuracy. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, a third reviewer (WTL) was consulted to reach consensus.

The following data were extracted from each included study: study characteristics (first author, year of publication, country), patient demographics (sample size, age, sex), anesthetic technique (WALANT protocol, comparator type), and surgical approach (open or endoscopic carpal tunnel release). Outcome-related data included definitions and measurements of operative time, intraoperative pain, early postoperative pain, patient satisfaction, functional outcomes assessed by the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ) and reported adverse events.

In accordance with the prespecified PROSPERO protocol, no attempts were made to contact study authors for missing or unclear data. Analyses were therefore restricted to data available in the published reports.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias for included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [12] for RCTs. Assessments were performed independently by two reviewers (CYC, YJS), with disagreements resolved by consensus through discussion with a third reviewer (WTL).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the meta package [13]. Owing to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity among studies, random-effects models were applied for all quantitative syntheses. Meta-analysis was conducted only when data from two or more studies were available for a given outcome; otherwise, findings were summarized descriptively.

Continuous outcomes were pooled using mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). When outcomes were reported as medians with interquartile ranges, values were converted to estimated means and standard deviations using established statistical methods [14].

In some studies, satisfaction scores in the WALANT group demonstrated pronounced ceiling effects, resulting in a calculated standard deviation of zero after conversion. Because a variance of zero is not compatible with inverse-variance meta-analysis, a minimal non-zero standard deviation (SD = 0.001) was imputed in these instances to allow model convergence. This value was chosen to be sufficiently small so as not to materially influence effect estimates or study weighting.

Operative time was pooled only when studies explicitly defined the interval from surgical start to completion. Postoperative pain analyses were restricted to early assessments performed within 10–15 min after surgery or during the post-anesthesia care unit stay, based on the assumption that pain during this period predominantly reflects tourniquet-related discomfort. Pain assessments obtained more than one hour postoperatively were excluded from quantitative synthesis.

Functional outcomes at approximately three months postoperatively were analyzed using the BCTQ. Separate meta-analyses were conducted for the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) and Functional Status Scale (FSS) when data were available.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic, with a two-sided P value < 0.05 indicating statistically significant heterogeneity. The magnitude of heterogeneity was interpreted as follows: 0–40%, might not be important; 30–60%, may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90%, may represent substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100%, indicate considerable heterogeneity [15]. When substantial heterogeneity was observed, results were interpreted cautiously, and prespecified exploratory subgroup or sensitivity analyses were undertaken to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

In accordance with the prespecified PROSPERO protocol, exploratory subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted when data permitted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were primarily based on anesthetic classification, comparing WALANT with local anesthesia with tourniquet and with regional anesthesia techniques (IVRA or nerve block). Additional subgroup analyses based on surgical approach (open versus endoscopic) or WALANT injectate regimen were performed when relevant.

Formal subgroup interaction tests were conducted within the random-effects framework. When quantitative synthesis was not feasible due to limited data or heterogeneity in outcome definitions, a structured narrative synthesis was provided. Given the limited number of RCTs, all subgroup analyses were considered exploratory.

Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed because the number of studies available for individual outcomes was insufficient for reliable evaluation.

Certainty of evidence

We assessed the certainty of evidence using the GRADE framework [16] evaluating factors including risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. In alignment with GRADE methodology, we presented the findings within a minimally contextualized framework [17], assigning ratings to the strength of the observed effects.

Result

Study selection

The literature search identified 192 records from electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. After removal of duplicate records, the remaining records were screened by title and abstract. Studies that did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria were excluded. Full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility. In addition, citation searching of included studies was performed and identified one additional eligible study. One registered protocol without published results was excluded. Ultimately, eight randomized controlled trials met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and quantitative synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

A total of eight RCTs were included [18–25], published between 1993 and 2025 and conducted across Europe, North America, and South America. All studies compared WALANT with anesthetic techniques involving tourniquet use, including local anesthesia with tourniquet and regional anesthesia techniques such as intravenous regional anesthesia, distal nerve block, or ultrasound-guided axillary block. In all included studies, the tourniquet was applied at the upper arm.

Both open and endoscopic carpal tunnel release procedures were represented. WALANT protocols varied slightly in local anesthetic composition, including differences in epinephrine concentration and the use of sodium bicarbonate as a buffering agent. Comparator anesthetic techniques also varied across studies. Detailed study characteristics and anesthetic regimens are summarized in Table 1.

Risk of bias

Risk of bias assessments using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool are summarized in supplement. One RCT was judged to be at low risk of bias, while the remaining seven trials were judged to have some concerns. No study was rated as high risk of bias.

Some concerns were mainly related to the randomization process, outcome measurement, and selective reporting, largely due to incomplete reporting and lack of blinding for subjective outcomes. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions and missing outcome data was generally low across studies.

Operative time

The meta-analysis of operative time included 5 studies with 374 participants, all of which reported operative time using explicit definitions of surgical start and completion.

The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference in operative time between WALANT and comparator anesthetic techniques (MD: 0.71, 95% CI: − 0.32 to 1.75; Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis of operative time based on surgical approach. Forest plot showing subgroup analysis of operative time stratified by surgical approach (open vs. endoscopic carpal tunnel release). Effect estimates are presented as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals

Moderate heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I2 = 68%).

Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach demonstrated that sequential exclusion of individual studies did not materially change the pooled effect estimate or substantially reduce heterogeneity, indicating that no single study was the primary source of between-study variability.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on epinephrine concentration in the WALANT injectate (Fig. S2A), comparator anesthesia type (local vs. regional) (Fig. S2B), and surgical approach (open vs. endoscopic) (Fig. 2). The test for subgroup differences indicated that surgical approach contributed to variability in operative time, whereas anesthetic classification and injectate regimen did not demonstrate statistically significant interaction effects. Endoscopic procedures were associated with shorter operative time, and within the open subgroup, operative time was longer in the WALANT group.

Surgeon-reported visualization and bleeding

Three randomized trials reported surgeon-assessed operative visualization, perceived difficulty, or intraoperative bleeding. Compared with tourniquet-based anesthesia, WALANT was associated with slightly lower visualization scores or higher perceived operative difficulty in some studies, particularly in open procedures, while findings in endoscopic techniques were inconsistent across studies. However, all reported scores remained within acceptable ranges, and no study reported inability to complete the procedure because of bleeding or visualization issues (Table S5).

Intraoperative pain

The meta-analysis of intraoperative pain included 3 studies with 202 participants, with pain assessed using patient-reported scales (visual analog scale) recorded during or immediately after the procedure based on patient recall.

The pooled analysis showed that WALANT was associated with significantly lower intraoperative pain compared with anesthetic techniques involving tourniquet use (MD: − 1.58, 95% CI: − 2.17 to − 0.99; Fig. 3).

Forest plot of intraoperative pain. Forest plot comparing intraoperative pain scores between WALANT and tourniquet-based anesthetic techniques across randomized controlled trials

No heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I2 = 0%, p = 0.7938).

Subgroup analyses were performed based on surgical approach (open vs. endoscopic) and comparator anesthesia type (local vs. regional anesthesia). No statistically significant subgroup differences were observed (Fig. S3).

Early postoperative pain

The meta-analysis of early postoperative pain included 3 studies with 184 participants, with assessments performed within 10–15 min after surgery or in the recovery room.

The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between WALANT and comparator anesthetic techniques (MD: − 1.98, 95% CI: − 4.00 to 0.03; Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis of early postoperative pain based on surgical approach. Forest plot showing subgroup analysis of early postoperative pain stratified by surgical approach (open vs. endoscopic carpal tunnel release). Pain was assessed within 10–15 min after surgery or in the recovery room

Substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I2 = 94.2%).

Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach demonstrated that exclusion of one study substantially reduced heterogeneity (I2 = 37.9%) and resulted in a larger effect estimate favoring WALANT (MD: − 3.02, 95% CI: − 4.18 to − 1.85; Fig. S4).

Subgroup analyses were performed based on comparator anesthesia type (local vs. regional) and surgical approach (open vs. endoscopic). No significant subgroup interaction was observed for comparator anesthesia type. In contrast, a significant subgroup effect was observed for surgical approach (p < 0.0001), with a larger reduction in early postoperative pain in open procedures compared with endoscopic procedures (Fig. 4).

Patient satisfaction

The meta-analysis of patient satisfaction included 3 studies with 187 participants.

The pooled analysis showed significantly higher satisfaction scores in the WALANT group compared with anesthetic techniques involving tourniquet use (Fig. S6).

Low heterogeneity was observed among the included studies.

Although satisfaction was assessed at different time points across studies, two studies demonstrated higher satisfaction with WALANT, whereas one study, which assessed satisfaction at 6 weeks postoperatively, showed no significant difference between groups.

Subgroup analysis based on comparator anesthesia type (local vs. regional) showed no statistically significant subgroup interaction.

Adverse events

Adverse events were summarized descriptively because of heterogeneity in outcome definitions and anesthetic techniques. In all included randomized trials, comparator groups involved tourniquet use, whereas the WALANT groups did not.

Overall, adverse events were uncommon. Among trials comparing WALANT with local anesthesia plus tourniquet, only one superficial surgical site infection was reported in the WALANT group (Rellán et al. [24]). In the trial comparing WALANT with IVRA, anesthetic failures occurred only in the IVRA group, while no failures were observed in the WALANT group, indicating higher anesthetic reliability with WALANT. Other reported events were infrequent and generally self-limited. Similarly, in the study comparing WALANT with ultrasound-guided axillary block, systemic and local adverse events were rare and occurred at comparably low rates between groups.

In contrast, the study by Bloc et al., which compared WALANT combined with distal nerve block to distal nerve block with tourniquet use, demonstrated a clear difference in local postoperative complications. At 15 days, hematoma at the surgical site was significantly more frequent in the tourniquet-based group than in the WALANT plus distal block group (57% vs. 20%, p < 0.01); however, these data were obtained via patient-reported telephone follow-up rather than surgeon-confirmed clinical assessment. Persistent paresthesia was common in both groups (73% vs. 63%, p = 0.41).

Functional outcomes

The meta-analysis of functional outcomes included 2 studies with 122 participants, assessed at approximately three months postoperatively using the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ).

For the Functional Status Scale (FSS), the pooled analysis showed a statistically significant difference favoring WALANT (MD: − 1.17, 95% CI: − 1.94 to − 0.39; Fig. 5). No heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I2 = 0%). Subgroup analysis based on comparator anesthesia type showed no statistically significant interaction.

For the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS), the pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between WALANT and comparator groups (MD: − 0.25, 95% CI: − 2.03 to 1.53; Fig. 6). Low to moderate heterogeneity was observed, and subgroup analysis did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between local and regional anesthesia comparators.

Certainty of evidence

Using the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence was rated as low to moderate across outcomes, primarily due to some concerns regarding risk of bias and outcome-specific limitations, including imprecision and heterogeneity. Detailed GRADE assessments are provided in Table S7.

Discussion

Operative time did not differ between WALANT and tourniquet-based anesthetic techniques overall. Subgroup analysis showed that operative time was influenced by surgical approach, with endoscopic procedures generally shorter than open procedures. Notably, operative time was longer in the WALANT group within the open subgroup.

The longer operative time observed with WALANT in open procedures may partially reflect less complete hemostasis without tourniquet use, potentially requiring additional time for visualization and bleeding control. In addition, because patients remain fully awake, occasional intraoperative discomfort may require supplemental local anesthetic administration. Despite longer operative times, no study reported inability to complete the procedure due to bleeding, visualization difficulty, or inadequate pain control.

Surgeon-reported visualization was slightly inferior under WALANT in some studies but remained within acceptable limits and did not preclude completion of the procedure. Subgroup analyses showed no meaningful differences in operative time across epinephrine dilution regimens, suggesting that variations in injectate composition did not result in clinically relevant impairment of operative efficiency.

Intraoperative and early postoperative pain should be interpreted as composite outcomes influenced by multiple factors rather than a single dominant mechanism. Tourniquet exposure may represent one contributing factor. Intraoperative and early postoperative pain may reflect multiple tourniquet-related components, including inflation pain, ischemic discomfort during sustained compression, and rebound pain after release [26, 27]. However, most included RCTs assessed pain using a single overall score without clearly separating intraoperative and early postoperative pain components. This may contribute to heterogeneity in the observed pain outcomes.

Operative duration appears to influence tourniquet-related pain. Prior evidence indicates that tourniquet inflation times shorter than 10 min are associated with significantly less tourniquet-related pain in awake hand surgery [28]. Accordingly, trials involving short operative times, such as those using endoscopic carpal tunnel release, may demonstrate less intraoperative and early postoperative pain regardless of anesthetic strategy, thereby reducing observable differences between WALANT and tourniquet-based techniques.

Findings from Rellán et al. [24], in which tourniquet exposure was minimal (approximately 6 min), are consistent with this interpretation, as comparable intraoperative pain scores were observed between anesthetic techniques under these conditions. Although statistically significant differences were reported, their magnitude did not reach established minimal clinically important difference thresholds (approximately 1.6–1.9 points) [29].

Additional heterogeneity in early postoperative pain outcomes may also reflect differences in study design and pain assessment methods across studies. In the study by Bloc et al. [23], pain assessment explicitly distinguished tourniquet-related discomfort from surgical pain, whereas most trials used a single global pain score. This methodological difference may have influenced how patients attributed pain sources and contributed to between-study variability. In addition, WALANT in the Bloc study was combined with a distal nerve block and applied exclusively to endoscopic procedures, making the anesthetic protocol meaningfully different from trials evaluating WALANT alone and likely contributing to between-study variability.

Differences in local anesthetic techniques between open and endoscopic procedures may also contribute to heterogeneity. In endoscopic procedures, anesthetic infiltration may extend along the median nerve pathway beyond portal sites [30, 31]. Under WALANT, additional epinephrine-containing solution is infiltrated throughout the operative field to achieve hemostasis, which may further narrow the practical difference between groups in endoscopic cases.

The anatomical level of anesthesia may also influence intraoperative and early postoperative pain. Virtos et al. [25] reported no difference in pain scores between WALANT and tourniquet-based anesthesia in an endoscopic cohort using ultrasound-guided axillary block, a proximal technique that may reduce tourniquet-related discomfort. Although not included in quantitative synthesis due to different assessment timing, these findings support the influence of operative duration and anesthetic level on intraoperative and early postoperative pain.

The observed difference in early postoperative pain between open and endoscopic subgroups does not indicate that WALANT is less effective in endoscopic surgery. Rather, it reflects the fact that endoscopic carpal tunnel release is faster, resulting in shorter tourniquet exposure time and therefore less tourniquet-related pain in both groups. When tourniquet time is uniformly short, there is less pain attributable to the tourniquet to eliminate. The smaller differences observed in endoscopic procedures therefore suggest that operative and tourniquet duration influence the magnitude of pain differences between anesthetic strategies. In clinical settings where operative time is longer or unpredictable, such as in open procedures or complex cases, eliminating tourniquet-related pain through WALANT becomes more clinically meaningful.

Postoperative functional outcomes following carpal tunnel release have traditionally been regarded as largely independent of anesthetic technique. Consequently, most randomized trials evaluating WALANT have focused on intraoperative and early postoperative pain and anesthetic-related outcomes, with relatively limited attention to patient-reported hand function. In the present review, functional outcomes assessed by the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ) were reported in only two RCTs.

In both trials, substantial improvement in BCTQ scores from baseline to three months postoperatively was observed regardless of anesthetic technique, consistent with the established effectiveness of carpal tunnel release. Between-group comparisons were inconsistent, with one trial favoring WALANT and the other showing no significant difference.

Despite this discrepancy at the individual trial level, the direction of effect was consistent across studies. When pooled in the present meta-analysis, WALANT was associated with a statistically significant improvement in the BCTQ FSS at three months (MD − 1.17; 95% CI, − 1.94 to − 0.39). This magnitude exceeds the commonly reported minimal clinically important difference for the FSS (approximately 0.74) [32]. However, as only two trials contributed data and the pooled estimate was largely driven by a single study, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory.

The absence of a corresponding between-group difference in the BCTQ Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) is clinically informative rather than contradictory. Prior methodological work on the BCTQ has emphasized that SSS and FSS capture distinct constructs [32, 33]. SSS primarily reflects neurosensory symptoms such as pain, numbness, and nocturnal paresthesia, whereas FSS reflects task-based functional performance and difficulty in daily hand use. Postoperative symptom relief typically occurs more rapidly and intensely than functional recovery, which evolves gradually and is influenced by factors beyond nerve physiology, such as grip strength, endurance, and patient confidence. Therefore, this dissociation between SSS and FSS trajectories is expected and does not imply inconsistent outcomes.

The higher patient satisfaction observed with WALANT aligns with the improvement in BCTQ-FSS scores, together supporting the clinical relevance of WALANT beyond pain reduction alone.

Adverse events were infrequent across included randomized trials and did not differ meaningfully between WALANT and comparator anesthetic techniques. Reported complications were generally minor and self-limited, and no study identified an increased risk of serious anesthetic- or surgery-related adverse events attributable to WALANT. These findings are consistent with prior reports supporting the overall safety of WALANT for carpal tunnel release.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the exclusive inclusion of RCTs and restriction to a single procedure, carpal tunnel release. This reduces confounding from nonrandomized designs and heterogeneous hand conditions seen in prior meta-analyses. Inclusion of recently published RCTs also allowed evaluation of operative duration and tourniquet exposure as modifiers of intraoperative and early postoperative pain, which has not been systematically examined in earlier reviews.

Limitations include the small number of trials and limited sample sizes for several outcomes, particularly postoperative functional measures. Most studies were judged as having some concerns in risk of bias, mainly due to incomplete reporting of the randomization process and outcome measurement. These factors, together with small sample sizes, led to downgrading of evidence certainty in the GRADE assessment. Operative and tourniquet inflation times were not consistently reported across studies. Therefore, surgical approach (open versus endoscopic) was used as an indirect surrogate for operative and tourniquet exposure duration in subgroup analyses. Variation in pain assessment methods and perioperative analgesic strategies further limited comparability across studies. Prespecified subgroup analyses explained part of the heterogeneity, but residual variability remained. Ceiling effects limited interpretation of satisfaction scores, and differences in assessment timing across studies may have further affected comparability.

Conclusion

Based on RCTs, WALANT for carpal tunnel release is associated with consistently lower intraoperative pain compared with tourniquet-based anesthetic techniques, without a significant increase in operative time overall, although operative time was longer in the WALANT group in open procedures.

Early postoperative pain was higher in the tourniquet group, which appears to be associated with tourniquet exposure duration.

In addition to higher overall patient satisfaction, WALANT was associated with better postoperative hand function compared with the control group, as measured by the BCTQ Functional Status Scale. However, available data remain limited, and the finding should be interpreted as exploratory. Future randomized studies should systematically report operative and tourniquet duration and incorporate standardized functional outcome measures to clarify the magnitude and durability of functional benefits associated with WALANT.
