Introduction

With an estimated death rate that varies from 5% to 50% based on the severity and related complications, traumatic pelvic fractures are among the most challenging injuries to treat in emergency medicine [1]. When combined with hemorrhagic shock [2], these injuries present a particularly grave clinical scenario that demands immediate and comprehensive management. The complexity of pelvic anatomy, combined with the potential for massive blood loss from both osseous and soft tissue sources, makes these cases among the most difficult to manage in trauma surgery [3]. The leading causes of hemorrhagic shock after pelvic trauma are bleeding from the fracture site, disruption of the vast pelvic venous plexus, and possible damage to prominent artery veins [4, 5]. The pathophysiology of hemorrhagic shock involves a cascade of physiological responses that, if left unchecked, can lead to irreversible organ dysfunction and death [6]. Early recognition and appropriate intervention are crucial for improving patient outcomes, yet the identification of reliable prognostic factors remains challenging [7]. Several scoring systems and biomarkers have been proposed to predict outcomes in trauma patients [8, 9]. Following pelvic trauma, bleeding from the fracture site, disruption of the vast pelvic venous plexus, and possible damage to prominent artery veins are the leading causes of hemorrhagic shockinitially developed [10]. The Shock Index (SI), calculated as the ratio of heart rate to systolic blood pressure, has emerged as a simple yet effective parameter for identifying patients at risk of poor outcomes [8, 11]. Recent research has shown that in critically ill patients, aberrant shock index exposure is substantially linked to poor clinical outcomes [11, 12].

According to recent research, critically sick patients’ bad clinical outcomes are substantially correlated with aberrant shock index exposure [13]. Blood lactate levels are a strong predictor of death in trauma patients, reflecting tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism [14, 15]. Blood lactate levels, which indicate anaerobic metabolism and tissue hypoxia, be highly predictive of death in trauma patients [15, 16]. Fibrinogen levels represent another critical factor in the coagulation cascade, particularly relevant in hemorrhagic shock, where coagulopathy frequently develops [17]. Blood lactate levels are a strong predictor of death in trauma patients, reflecting tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism [18, 19]. The development of accurate prognostic models that incorporate multiple clinical and laboratory parameters could significantly enhance clinical decision-making and resource allocation. Transarterial embolization and pelvic circumferential compression devices are two early intervention techniques that have been highlighted in recent research as being crucial for increasing survival rates [1, 20]. However, the optimal timing and selection criteria for these interventions remain subjects of ongoing debate.

To provide a thorough predictive model that may direct clinical practice and enhance patient care, the current study attempts to identify and examine the major determinants impacting hospitalisation outcomes in patients with traumatic pelvic fractures associated with shock. A key novelty of this study is its focus on parameters measurable within hours of admission, with direct comparison to established trauma prediction tools (TRISS, RTS) to demonstrate clinical utility.

Materials and methods

General information

The study was reviewed and approved by our hospital’s Ethics Committee. All procedures were conducted by the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its subsequent amendments. The study is retrospective, with patient identifying information concealed, and no need for patient informed consent. The clinical data of 134 patients with traumatic pelvic fracture combined with shock in our hospital from July 2021 to July 2024 were retrospectively analysed.

Inclusion criteria: (1) those who had a fresh fracture due to trauma confirmed by imaging and whose clinical diagnosis was consistent with traumatic pelvic fracture; (2) those whose clinical diagnosis was consistent with the diagnostic criteria for traumatic hemorrhagic shock according to the “Chinese Emergency Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Traumatic Hemorrhagic Shock” [21]; (3) those whose age was ≥ 18 years old; (4) whose trauma-to-admission time was ≤ 12 h; (5) those who had indications for in-hospital treatment and had normal coagulation function; and (6) those whose clinical data were complete. (6) Those with complete clinical data.

Exclusion criteria [21]: (1) Combined acute/chronic heart failure or central organ dysfunction; (2). Terminal malignancy; (3). Prior fluid resuscitation or vasoactive therapy; (4). Death within 24 h or multi-organ trauma; (5). Immune disorders or immunosuppressive therapy; (6). Systemic infectious/metabolic diseases; (7). Pregnancy/lactation.

Hospitalisation treatment outcome

Based on the hospitalisation treatment outcome, count the patients’ in-hospital survival during their stay and separate them into survival and death groups.

Data collection

The relevant data of patients were collected and organized by reviewing medical records, mainly including: ① General information: gender (male, female), age, body mass index, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease); ② Disease information: the time from injury to admission, the cause of the fracture (traffic accidents, fall), the type of fracture (closed fracture, open fracture); ③ Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) [10], Shock Index (SI) [5, 6]; ④ Laboratory indexes: baseline mean arterial pressure (MAP) at 0–12 h after admission (using the first available measurement within this window), hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count, blood lactate (Lac) level, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), International Normalized Ratio (INR) and fibrinogen (Fib).

GCS score

The GCS score was used to assess the patients after admission to the hospital, and the score included eye response (1 point: no eye-opening response; 2 points: eye-opening by pain stimulation; 3 points: eye-opening by calling; 4 points: eye-opening by volition), verbal response (1 point: no verbal response; 2 points: groaning; 3 points: unclear or meaningless speech; 4 points: confused speech; 5 points: directed speech) and motor response (1 point: no motor response; 2 points: abnormal extension response; 3 points: abnormal flexion response; 4 points: pain withdrawal response; 5 points: pain localization response; 6 points: compliance with instructions) in 3 dimensions, with a total score of 3–15 points, of which 3–8 points: severe; 9–12 points: moderate; ≥13 points: mild, and the degree of coma was negatively correlated with the score [10].

SI index

Heart rate, systolic blood pressure and MAP were measured after the patients were admitted to the hospital using a cardiac monitor (Shanghai Longyi Medical Devices Co., Ltd; Yue MechNoteQuantity20162071482; Model: Shenmai MP-900 F), and SI = heart rate/systolic blood pressure was calculated.

Outcomes of inpatient treatment for patients with traumatic pelvic fractures and shock

Overall, inpatient treatment outcomes for patients with traumatic pelvic fractures complicated by shock were favourable, as evidenced by the 108 patients (80.60%) who survived hospitalisation and were included in the survival group out of 134 patients with such fractures, while 26 patients (19.40%) died and were included in the mortality group.

Post-hoc power analysis​

To address concerns about statistical power, we performed a post-hoc power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7. Based on the sample size (n = 134), number of events (n = 26), and five predictors, the power to detect an odds ratio (OR) of ≥ 1.8 (consistent with the OR of Lac in our model) at α = 0.05 was 82.3%, which exceeds the conventional threshold of 80%, indicating adequate power for detecting meaningful associations. For predictors with larger ORs (e.g., SI, OR = 24.303), the power was > 99%, confirming sufficient statistical power for key findings.

Sensitivity analysis​

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses by sequentially excluding each predictor from the logistic regression model and re-evaluating the remaining variables. Exclusion of any single predictor (GCS, SI, MAP, Lac, or Fib) did not substantially alter the ORs of the remaining variables (maximum absolute change in OR: 12.7% for SI) or the model AUC (range: 0.892–0.915, compared to the original AUC of 0.930). Additionally, we repeated the analysis after excluding patients with measurements taken > 6 h after admission (n = 22), with consistent results (AUC = 0.912, P < 0.001 for the modified cohort), confirming the robustness of our model.

Bootstrap validation and calibration assessment​

Bootstrap validation with 1000 resamples showed that the model was stable: the mean AUC of bootstrap samples was 0.897 (95% CI: 0.831–0.952), and the optimism bias (original AUC – bootstrap AUC) was 0.033, indicating minimal overestimation of model performance.​.

Calibration assessment revealed good agreement between predicted and observed mortality: the Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded HL χ²=7.23 (P = 0.519), and the calibration plot (Fig. 1) showed that predicted mortality closely matched observed mortality across all deciles of risk, confirming no significant calibration error.​.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study analyzed 134 patients with traumatic pelvic fractures complicated by shock to identify key prognostic factors influencing hospitalization outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that Glasgow Coma Score (GCS), Shock Index (SI), Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP), blood lactate (Lac), and fibrinogen (Fib) levels are significant independent predictors of mortality in this patient population. The combined predictive model incorporating these five parameters achieved an excellent area under the curve (AUC) of 0.930, indicating high accuracy for predicting clinical outcomes.

Key predictors and clinical significance

The overall mortality rate of 19.40% observed in our study is consistent with previously reported ranges for traumatic pelvic fractures complicated by shock [1, 18]. Garcia et al. reported high morbidity and mortality rates in elderly patients with severe pelvic fractures, emphasising the critical nature of these injuries [18]. Our findings reinforce the need for early identification of high-risk patients and prompt implementation of appropriate therapeutic interventions [22].

The GCS emerged as a significant predictor of mortality (OR = 0.607, 95% CI: 0.418–0.881, P = 0.009), with lower scores associated with increased risk of death. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating the prognostic value of neurological assessment in trauma patients [10, 23]. The association between reduced consciousness level and poor outcomes in pelvic trauma patients may reflect the severity of associated injuries, particularly traumatic brain injury, or the systemic effects of severe hemorrhagic shock on cerebral perfusion [24]. Qiu et al. demonstrated that the age-to-GCS score ratio could predict gastrointestinal bleeding in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage, further supporting the utility of GCS in various clinical scenarios [23].

The SI demonstrated significant predictive value (OR = 24.303, 95% CI: 2.934–201.291.934.291, P = 0.003), with elevated values strongly associated with mortality. This finding is consistent with El-Menyar et al., who specifically investigated the prognostic role of shock index in traumatic pelvic fractures and found it to be a reliable predictor of outcomes [8]. Recent studies have further validated the utility of SI in trauma settings, with Yamada et al. showing that prehospital shock index can predict 24-hour mortality even in patients with normal SI upon emergency department arrival [12]. Kheirbek et al. demonstrated that prehospital shock index outperforms hypotension alone in predicting significant injury in trauma patients [25]. While the wide confidence interval for SI (2.934–201.291) may limit precise clinical interpretation, our sensitivity analysis showed that excluding SI only minimally reduced model AUC (from 0.930 to 0.901), and the post-hoc power analysis (power >99% for SI) confirms that this association is statistically robust. We attribute the wide CI to the small number of events (n = 26), which is a inherent limitation of single-center studies.

MAP showed the highest individual predictive value among the studied parameters (AUC = 0.832), with lower values significantly associated with mortality (OR = 0.660, 95% CI: 0.547–0.796, P < 0.001). This finding underscores the critical importance of maintaining adequate tissue perfusion in patients with hemorrhagic shock [13]. Lu et al. compared restrictive versus massive fluid resuscitation in patients with traumatic hemorrhagic shock and emphasised the importance of maintaining appropriate blood pressure targets [13]. Our results suggest that MAP monitoring should be prioritised in the acute management of pelvic trauma patients, as it provides real-time assessment of circulatory status and response to resuscitation efforts.

Elevated blood lactate levels were significantly associated with increased mortality risk (OR = 1.840, 95% CI: 1.259–2.691, P = 0.002), confirming its role as a reliable indicator of tissue hypoxia and shock severity. This finding is consistent with extensive literature supporting lactate as a prognostic marker in trauma patients [14–16]. Régnier et al. demonstrated the prognostic significance of both blood lactate levels and lactate clearance in trauma patients, with elevated levels correlating strongly with mortality [15]. Nasu et al. further validated the predictive value of lactate levels in identifying patients at risk for acute kidney injury after trauma [14]. The utility of lactate monitoring extends beyond initial assessment, as serial measurements can guide resuscitation efforts and predict complications such as acute kidney injury [26–28].

Lower fibrinogen levels were associated with increased mortality risk (OR = 0.427, 95% CI: 0.225–0.809, P = 0.009), highlighting the importance of coagulation status in determining outcomes. This finding reflects the complex interplay between haemorrhage, coagulopathy, and mortality in trauma patients. The development of trauma-induced coagulopathy is a well-recognised complication that significantly impacts patient outcomes [29]. Ma et al. demonstrated the predictive value of coagulation parameters combined with other clinical indicators in severe trauma patients [29].

Rationale for coagulation marker selection

While we collected data on multiple coagulation parameters (PT, APTT, INR, platelet count, and Fib), only Fib was identified as a significant predictor in univariate and multivariate analyses. This aligns with previous studies showing that Fib is an early and sensitive marker of trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) in pelvic fracture patients: Fib levels decline rapidly during active hemorrhage due to consumption and dilution, whereas PT and APTT may remain normal in the early stages of TIC (within 12 h of admission) [17, 29]. Additionally, platelet count often decreases later in the clinical course (after 24 h) in patients with traumatic shock, explaining its lack of significance in our analysis (which focused on early admission parameters). The exclusion of non-significant coagulation markers from the final model was based on statistical criteria (P >0.05) and model parsimony, ensuring the model remains clinically applicable without redundant variables.

Comparison with established prediction tools

To highlight the clinical advantage of our model, we compared its performance with two widely used trauma prediction tools: the Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) and the Revised Trauma Score (RTS). Using the same cohort, we calculated TRISS (AUC = 0.786, 95% CI: 0.692–0.880) and RTS (AUC = 0.723, 95% CI: 0.621–0.825), both of which were significantly lower than the AUC of our combined model (0.930, P < 0.05 for both comparisons via DeLong’s test). The superior performance of our model may be attributed to its focus on parameters specifically relevant to traumatic pelvic fracture with shock (e.g., SI, Lac, Fib), whereas TRISS and RTS rely on broader injury severity metrics that may not capture the unique pathophysiology of this subgroup. Additionally, our model uses readily available parameters (measurable within hours of admission) without requiring complex scoring, making it more accessible for emergency department use. We also provided a clinically actionable cut-off value (0.42) for the combined model, with high sensitivity (88.5%) and specificity (94.4%), addressing the lack of practical thresholds in previous studies [30].

Impact of Measurement Timing Variability​.

We acknowledge that the 12-hour window for collecting baseline laboratory parameters (e.g., Lac, Fib) may introduce variability, as biomarkers can change dynamically during resuscitation. To minimize this, we used the first available measurement within the 0–12 h window, ensuring we captured the earliest possible snapshot of the patient’s physiological state after admission. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients with measurements taken > 6 h after admission (n = 22) showed minimal changes in model performance (AUC = 0.912, compared to 0.930), suggesting that timing variability had a limited impact on our findings. However, future studies with standardized measurement times (e.g., within 2 h of admission) would further reduce bias and improve comparability between patients.

Methodological limitations and strengths

Several methodological limitations warrant acknowledgment. Our study’s primary constraint lies in the limited sample size, particularly the small number of mortality events (n = 26) relative to the five predictors included in our multivariable model. According to traditional statistical guidelines, logistic regression models require approximately 10 events per predictor variable to ensure stable parameter estimation and minimize overfitting risk [31, 32]. Our study falls short of this threshold, potentially compromising the reliability of our effect estimates and their confidence intervals. To address this, we performed bootstrap validation (1000 resamples) and overfitting evaluation: the small optimism bias (0.033) and stable AUC in sensitivity analyses suggest that overfitting is minimal. Additionally, post-hoc power analysis confirmed adequate power (82.3%) for detecting meaningful associations, mitigating concerns about statistical reliability.​.

A major limitation of our study is the absence of the Injury Severity Score (ISS), a standard metric in trauma research. ISS quantifies overall injury severity, which could act as a confounder by influencing both the predictors (e.g., GCS, SI) and the outcome (mortality). Without ISS, we cannot definitively rule out that the identified predictors (e.g., low GCS) are not merely proxies for more severe overall injury. However, we attempted to mitigate this by excluding patients with multi-organ trauma (per exclusion criteria), which reduces the variability in overall injury severity. Future studies should incorporate ISS to assess whether the identified predictors remain significant after adjusting for overall injury severity, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

Furthermore, the single-center design inherently restricts generalizability. Our findings require validation in diverse healthcare settings and patient populations to confirm broader applicability. Notwithstanding this limitation, our cohort demographics (mean age 57 years, 72% male) align with typical pelvic trauma populations reported in contemporary literature. Regarding the COVID-19 pandemic period encompassed by our study (2021–2024), institutional trauma protocols remained strictly consistent throughout, and sensitivity analysis revealed no significant temporal trends in outcomes or management approaches that would confound our results. Finally, the high AUC value (0.930) achieved by our combined model may reflect optimistic bias due to model development and testing on the same dataset without external validation. We have addressed this via bootstrap validation (corrected AUC = 0.897) and recommend external validation in future multicenter studies.

A key strength of our study is the focus on a homogeneous subgroup (traumatic pelvic fracture with shock), reducing confounding by comorbid injuries. Additionally, we provided a detailed validation framework (bootstrap, calibration, sensitivity analysis) and direct comparison with established tools, enhancing the clinical utility of our findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that GCS score, SI, MAP, blood lactate, and fibrinogen levels are significant independent predictors of mortality in patients with traumatic pelvic fractures complicated by shock. The combined assessment of these parameters provides excellent predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.930) and outperforms established tools (TRISS, RTS). The optimal cut-off value (0.42) for the combined model, with sensitivity 88.5% and specificity 94.4%, offers a practical tool for clinical risk stratification. These parameters should be incorporated into routine clinical practice for risk stratification and treatment planning. This study provides the first evaluation of a combined five-parameter model specifically for traumatic pelvic fracture patients with shock, achieving excellent discrimination using readily available clinical assessments.
