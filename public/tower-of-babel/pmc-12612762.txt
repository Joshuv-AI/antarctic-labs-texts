Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta, time to definitive hemorrhage control, and mortality
Authors: Jan O Jansen
Published: 2025

This edition of Trauma Surgery & Acute Care Open contains an article by Hatchimonji et al,1 which describes a retrospective series of patients who underwent resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA), in the emergency department. The study used Trauma Quality Improvement Project (TQIP) data, and included hypotensive adult patients with penetrating abdominal trauma, who subsequently underwent laparotomy. Patients who had REBOA were propensity score matched to patients who did not. These patients experienced longer times to laparotomy, had increased transfusion volumes, an increased frequency of leg amputations, and increased in-hospital mortality.1

Time to hemorrhage control is known to be critically important in trauma patients—and difficult to reduce in those with non-compressible torso hemorrhage, because a surgeon and an operating room (OR) are required.2 3 This was the “gap” that REBOA was intended to fill, by providing earlier hemorrhage control, albeit temporary, before getting the patient to the OR.4 However, unless REBOA can truly be performed concurrently with other tasks, which is difficult, it will always result in some delay to definitive hemorrhage control. The question is whether this translates into improved mortality.

Hatchimonji et al have, again, shown that this is not the case. Their study is not the first to demonstrate this issue: the UK-REBOA trial, still the only randomized clinical trial of the technique, also showed increased time to definitive hemorrhage control, and increased mortality, in patients who were randomized to REBOA.5 The trial was conducted in a different setting, with different injury epidemiology, but the similarities are hard to ignore.

Similarly, Joseph et al, also drawing on TQIP data, have previously demonstrated higher mortality and amputation rates in patients treated with REBOA; and Norii et al, in a study from Japan, have shown increased mortality.6 7

Retrospective studies, and propensity score matching, are always at risk of confounding, and Hatchimonji’s study is no different. Similarly, some will argue that newer, partial-occlusion REBOA devices have an entirely different risk/benefit profile, and that the results of this study are therefore only of historical interest. (The rapid development and frequent modification of medical devices, before they can be fully evaluated, is a frustrating issue for those seeking to develop the evidence base for such interventions.)

Despite these limitations, Hatchimonji et al’s study provides food for thought, by adding yet more evidence that the use of REBOA, in the emergency department, is associated with increased time to definitive hemorrhage control, and that this delay—despite earlier temporary hemorrhage control—is associated with increased mortality.
