HIVAC (HIV‐associated cardiomyopathy): An overlooked complication of HIV in developing countries
Authors: Manish Kharel, Anukul Subedi, Md Fahad Hossain
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey Clinical MessageEarly initiation of anti‐retroviral treatment and prompt management of heart failure in a AIDS patient is crucial. If resources available endomyocardial biopsy can guide the future management.AbstractHIV‐associated cardiomyopathy (HIVAC) is a serious complication of HIV with poorly understood pathology. Systolic dysfunction was a common characteristic of HIVAC which is now replaced as diastolic dysfunction due to the availability of ART. There are various etiologies of HIVAC depending on the degree of viral replication, immune response, availability of ART, and also the disparity between high and low‐income countries. We present the case report of a 65‐year‐old male with a past medical history of untreated HIV who came to the hospital with hemodynamic instability and was diagnosed with HIVAC with pulmonary TB, where the cause of HIVAC was poor immune response and opportunistic infection. Therefore, early cardiac monitoring is essential in HIV patients to prevent its advancement.Keywords: AIDS, diastolic failure, heart failure, HIV

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of HIV‐associated cardiomyopathy (HIVAC) in the United States is approximately 4% among individuals with HIV. Patients with HIVAC have a significantly higher risk of death, with studies indicating a 6.5–4.0 times greater risk compared to those without this condition. 1 The precise diagnostic criteria for HIVAC have not been firmly established, but echocardiography plays a crucial role in its detection. Another study found that patients with HIVAC have an adjusted hazard ratio for death of 5.86 compared to patients with idiopathic cardiomyopathy. Mortality rates for HIVAC are notably high, reaching 15%–20% in some parts of Sub‐Saharan Africa. 2 HIVAC which is a poor prognostic factor for HIV infection is symptomatic systolic dysfunction with dilated ventricles. 3 HIVAC can present as both systolic and diastolic dysfunction. Earlier, when retroviral treatment was not widely available systolic dysfunction was more common. However, Subclinical diastolic dysfunction is becoming more common than systolic dysfunction in patients with well‐controlled HIV. 4 HIVAC symptoms include new‐onset heart failure in stage IV HIV illness, any systolic impairment or diastolic dysfunction in asymptomatic HIV patients, and symptomatic heart failure with left ventricular dysfunction with or without ventricular dilatation. 5 The degree of viral replication and immunosuppression is mainly responsible for the variable phenotype. The etiology of HIVAC variables which includes insufficient viral suppression and poor immune function are Myocarditis, opportunistic infections, micronutrient deficiencies, and HIV itself, whereas conversely when the disease is under control, the etiology is ART toxicity and cardiac autoimmunity. 6 In our case, opportunistic infections and poor immune response were the etiology for HIVAC. Early cardiac monitoring is crucial in people living with HIV, particularly in those with advanced disease, to detect and manage cardiac complications early. It is uncertain how screening echocardiograms works in populations with HIV infection. Emphasis needs to be placed on designing clear guidelines for screening protocols and diagnostic criteria for HIVAC. 7 The patient is managed with early administration of beta blocker and ACEI. 8

CASE HISTORY

A 65‐year‐old homeless male with a past medical history of untreated HIV infection presented to the emergency department with profound hemodynamic instability, unconsciousness, and respiratory distress. Initial vital signs revealed severe hypotension (50/30 mmHg), bradycardia, and hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 50%). The patient was promptly intubated, and CPR was initiated, resulting in stabilization with improvement in blood pressure (100/60 mmHg) and pulse rate (150 bpm). The patient is shifted to the ICU and placed on a ventilator. Physical examination upon admission revealed a cachectic appearance, bilateral leg swelling up to the knee, and bilateral crepitations in lung fields.

The patient was diagnosed as HIVAC with pulmonary TB. Standard heart failure management was initiated in the ICU with oxygen, diuretics, beta‐blockers, and AECI. Infectious disease consultation was done and ATT was started. ATT included a 2‐month intensive phase with Isoniazid, Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, and Ethambutol, followed by a 4‐month continuation phase with Isoniazid and Rifampin. On, 3rd day of admission, the patient's clinical improvement was noted with successful extubation and weaning of oxygen on day 6 and started on ART. ART was initiated within 2 weeks due to the patient's low CD4 count, using Efavirenz, Tenofovir, and Lamivudine to minimize interactions with Rifampin. 9 Close monitoring for potential adverse effects, including hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), was essential to ensure the successful treatment of both TB and HIV. 10 On 10th, the patient is discharged with advice for regular follow‐up.

OUTCOME AND FOLLOW‐UP

On follow‐up viral load was decreasing gradually and on flow‐up. On day 60, sputum culture was negative and viral load was Negative, CD4 count was increased to 150, and on echocardiography, ejection fraction was increased up to 50 percent.

DISCUSSION

In the past decade, remarkable progress has been made in combating the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) with a decreasing trend of HIV along with a 35% reduction in AIDS‐related deaths during this period. 11 HIV infection is associated with various cardiovascular complications, including HIV‐associated cardiomyopathy (HIVAC), which significantly contributes to morbidity and mortality in affected individuals. Notably, a complication called HIV‐associated cardiomyopathy (HIVAC), commonly symptomatic systolic dysfunction, and a dilated left ventricle has emerged as a poor prognostic factor for individuals with HIV which has a significantly shorter median survival rate of 101 days among HIV patients. 3

Interestingly, the widespread adoption of ART has transformed the profile of HIVAC, from systolic dysfunction to subtle diastolic dysfunction which is revealed by meta‐analysis done in 11 high‐income countries in which 43.4% evidence of diastolic dysfunction. 4 , 12 Therefore, asymptomatic ventricular dysfunction, unusual strain patterns, and increased diastolic dysfunction rates have been observed in HIV‐positive individuals undergoing ART. 13 In our case, the patient was not on art and the patient had poor immune function so severe systolic dysfunction was noted.

Echocardiography remains the primary method for diagnosing HIVAC which identifies ventricular dysfunction, with diastolic dysfunction and abnormal myocardial strain commonly observed as the sole abnormalities in asymptomatic HIV patients undergoing ART. 13 The progression of HIV‐associated cardiomyopathy (HIVAC) in untreated patients is primarily driven by HIV infiltration of the myocardium, triggering an inflammatory response that results in myocardial damage, scarring and systolic dysfunction, the presence of opportunistic infections, particularly tuberculosis (TB), further exacerbates this condition. TB heightened immune response and chronic inflammation, which can directly damage myocardial tissue, accelerating the progression of cardiomyopathy and could cause mortality. These factors underscore the importance of early antiretroviral therapy (ART) to prevent the severe cardiovascular complications associated with untreated HIV. 6 However, the significance of routine echocardiographic screening in HIV‐infected populations remains uncertain. Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) remains another controversial mode of diagnosis that needs further evaluation. 14 Biopsy can also help to choose a treatment protocol. 14 There is evidence that autoimmune myocarditis is a result of viral infection. 14 Those necessitates further evaluation.

The management of HIVAC depends on the early administration of beta‐blockers and ACE inhibitors which prevent progression to severe systolic dysfunction through reducing afterload and modulating sympathoadrenal activity. 8 The role of ART in HIVAC for cardiac outcomes is uncertain. However, WHO guidelines advocate ART initiation regardless of CD4 count. 6 Several other therapies need further evaluation to prevent and treat HIVAC. 15 Studies have found more immune‐mediated cardiomyopathy in HIV‐positive patients. 16 Immunosuppressive therapy in virus‐negative, lymphocytic myocarditis in biopsy can be a good modality of treatment. 17 , 18 Interferon‐ previously showed improved survival in enterovirus‐associated cardiomyopathy. 19 Additional therapies such as carnitine, selenium, and multivitamin supplementation aim to maintain left ventricular function but necessitate further validation. 20

In conclusion, Systolic dysfunction in HIVAC is still a dangerous yet overlooked complication of HIV in developing countries. Appropriate management of acute condition is necessary. The ideal way to prevent the condition is still not developed. There are many modalities of treatment that need to be studied to help manage this condition.
