Impact of nutrition-sensitive agricultural interventions on dietary diversity and selected nutritional outcomes among vulnerable populations in Ethiopia: a systematic review
Authors: Kassahun Ayele, Meron Demisew, Daniel Asfaw Kitessa, Habtamu Fekadu Gemede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition continues to be a significant public health issue in Ethiopia, disproportionately affecting children under five years of age, pregnant and lactating women, and low-income households. Nutrition-sensitive agricultural (NSA) interventions are agricultural strategies specifically designed to enhance nutrition by improving food availability, access, and dietary quality in a population. These interventions have been increasingly advocated to address the root causes of malnutrition. Study selection, data extraction, and reporting were performed according to the PRISMA guidelines.ResultsIn this review, ten studies were analyzed. Background

Malnutrition remains a major public health challenge in Ethiopia, disproportionately affecting children under five years of age, pregnant and lactating women, and low income households [1]. Despite improvements in agricultural production, the country continues to face a high burden of undernutrition, including stunting, wasting, and micronutrient deficiency [2]. These conditions are driven by multiple interrelated factors, such as poverty, limited access to diverse and nutrient-rich foods, environmental constraints, and inadequate nutritional knowledge and practices [3]. Addressing these challenges requires integrated multisectoral approaches that link agriculture, nutrition, health, and social protection systems.

Globally, nutrition-sensitive agriculture (NSA) interventions have been promoted as a key strategy to address the underlying determinants of malnutrition by improving food availability, access, and dietary quality [4, 5]. These interventions integrate agricultural activities with explicit nutrition objectives and often incorporate complementary components, such as nutrition education, behavior change communication, and gender-sensitive approaches. Evidence from various low- and middle-income countries suggests that NSA interventions can improve dietary diversity, although their effects on anthropometric nutritional outcomes are inconsistent [6, 7].

In Ethiopia, various NSA interventions, particularly home gardening, small-scale livestock production, and market-oriented approaches, have been implemented to improve household food and nutrition security [8–10]. Some studies have reported increased consumption of fruits, vegetables, and animal-source foods, contributing to improved dietary diversity [11–13].

However, evidence on nutritional outcomes such as child growth indicators is limited and inconsistent. Moreover, the diversity in intervention design, context of implementation, and methods of measuring outcomes complicates the ability to draw consistent conclusions. The effectiveness of these interventions varies significantly and is shaped by contextual factors, such as household socioeconomic status, market access, climate variability, and the degree to which nutrition education and behavior change communication are incorporated into agricultural programs [14, 15].

Despite the growing number of studies, there is a lack of comprehensive synthesis focusing specifically on the nutritional impact of NSA interventions in Ethiopia. Most existing studies emphasize agricultural productivity or food security outcomes, with less attention paid to direct food and nutrition indicators [16, 17, 20]. This gap highlights the need for a systematic evidence synthesis to better understand the effectiveness of NSA interventions and the factors influencing their impact.

Therefore, this systematic review aimed to synthesize the effects of nutrition-sensitive agricultural interventions on dietary diversity and selected nutritional outcomes among vulnerable populations in Ethiopia. It also examines implementation challenges and contextual factors influencing effectiveness, with the goal of informing future policies and program designs.

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted to identify studies evaluating the effects of nutrition-sensitive agriculture (NSA) interventions on dietary diversity and nutritional outcomes among vulnerable populations in Ethiopia. Four electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar) were searched to identify relevant peer-reviewed literature.

Search terms were developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords, including “nutrition-sensitive agriculture,” “dietary diversity,” “nutritional status,” “Ethiopia,” “agriculture interventions,” “nutrition interventions,” “food security,” and “food insecurity.” Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to refine the search strategy. The search was limited to studies published between January 2015 and March 2025. The final search was conducted on March 15, 2025.

In addition, the reference lists of eligible studies were manually screened to identify further relevant publications. For Google Scholar, records were screened in order of relevance because of database limitations. This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [31].

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into a reference management system, and duplicates were eliminated. Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts based on predefined eligibility criteria [33]. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were assessed for inclusion. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus (Fig. 1).

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted using a standardized form. The extracted variables included study characteristics (author, year, location, and design), population characteristics, intervention type and duration, outcome measures, and key findings related to dietary diversity and nutritional status.

Two reviewers independently extracted the data, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized controlled trials and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies [32]. Studies were categorized as low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on tool-specific criteria, and quality assessments were used to interpret the strength of the evidence.

Sensitivity and subgroup considerations

Given the narrative nature of the synthesis, formal statistical sensitivity and publication bias analyses were not performed. However, the findings were interpreted in relation to study quality, and differences across studies were explored descriptively based on intervention type, geographic setting, and population characteristics to identify patterns influencing effectiveness.

Data synthesis

Owing to the heterogeneity in study design, intervention types, and outcome measures, a meta-analysis was not feasible. Therefore, the findings were synthesized narratively following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidelines. The narrative synthesis followed the established guidance for synthesis without a meta-analysis (SWiM) [34]. A meta-analysis was not feasible because of substantial heterogeneity in study designs, intervention types, outcome measures, and reporting formats across the included studies. The findings were synthesized thematically to summarize the effects of NSA interventions on dietary diversity and nutritional status. A comparative analysis across studies was conducted to identify common patterns, variations in effectiveness, and contextual factors influencing outcomes, including intervention design, duration, and target population.

Results

Characteristics of Included Studies

Ten studies were included in this systematic review, which examined the effects of nutrition-sensitive agriculture (NSA) interventions on dietary diversity and nutritional outcomes among vulnerable populations in Ethiopia (Table 1). Each study was assigned a single primary study design based on its dominant methodological approach to avoid any overlap. The included studies comprised quasi-experimental studies (n = 5), cross-sectional studies (n = 4), pre–post intervention studies (n = 2), mixed-methods studies (n = 2), one cluster-randomized controlled trial, and one systematic review (used for contextual evidence rather than primary quantitative synthesis).

Characteristics of included studies and effects of nutrition-sensitive agricultural interventions on dietary diversity and selected nutritional outcomes

The NSA interventions varied across the studies. The most common were crop diversification and improved crop varieties (n = 5), followed by home gardening and agricultural input support combined with nutrition education (n = 3), integrated agriculture–nutrition programs (n = 3), and livestock-based diversification strategies (n = 2). Most interventions targeted households (n = 5), children under five years (n = 6), and women of reproductive age (n = 4). Geographically, studies were conducted mainly in Oromia (n = 5), Amhara (n = 3), SNNPR (n = 3), and Tigray (n = 2), reflecting diverse agroecological settings in Ethiopia.

Effects of NSA interventions on dietary diversity and selected nutritional outcomes

Overall, nine of the ten included studies reported improvements in dietary diversity outcomes following NSA interventions. These improvements were observed in household dietary diversity, women’s dietary diversity scores, and child minimum dietary diversity indicators. The magnitude of improvement varied across studies but generally ranged from modest to substantial increases in dietary diversity scores or the proportion of households achieving minimum dietary diversity. Home gardening combined with nutrition education showed particularly strong improvements in women’s dietary diversity. Similarly, integrated agriculture–nutrition interventions were consistently associated with increased consumption of multiple food groups, particularly among children under two years. Crop diversification and improved crop varieties have also been associated with higher household dietary diversity in several studies.

In contrast, evidence on nutritional status outcomes, particularly child anthropometric indicators, was inconsistent. Some studies reported reductions in stunting in selected regions (Amhara, Oromia, Tigray, and SNNPR), while others found no statistically significant changes in the growth indicators. Overall, the evidence suggests that NSA interventions have a more consistent effect on dietary diversity than on anthropometric measurements. These findings indicate that improvements in dietary diversity do not always directly translate into improved child growth outcomes, suggesting the importance of complementary health, sanitation, and disease prevention interventions. The heterogeneity of study designs and outcome measures also limits the direct comparability of the studies.

Discussion

This systematic review suggests that nutrition-sensitive agriculture (NSA) interventions in Ethiopia have the potential to improve dietary diversity among vulnerable populations, particularly women and children. Interventions that integrate home gardening with nutrition education are consistently associated with improvements in women’s dietary diversity scores (WDDS). These findings indicate that combining agricultural support with nutritional knowledge may contribute to improved household dietary practices by enhancing food availability and informed food choices. This is consistent with evidence suggesting that interventions promoting both production and nutrition awareness are more likely to be associated with improved dietary outcomes [26].

Community-based nutrition programs are also associated with improved dietary diversity in children. One study reported a substantial increase in the proportion of children meeting the minimum dietary diversity following community-led interventions [24, 27]. These findings suggest that participatory and community-focused approaches may play an important role in improving child feeding practices by increasing access to nutrition information and services at the household level in the long term.

Agricultural interventions, such as crop diversification and improved crop varieties, are generally associated with higher household dietary diversity, particularly in rural areas [19, 28]. These interventions may improve food availability and contribute to a more diversified diet. However, the magnitude of the observed changes varied across studies, depending on the intervention type, implementation context, and household socioeconomic conditions. Interventions combining agricultural inputs with behavior change communication were more consistently associated with improved dietary outcomes than single-component approaches.

Evidence on the nutritional status of children, including stunting and underweight, was inconsistent across studies. Some studies reported reductions in stunting in selected regions, such as Amhara, Oromia, Tigray, and SNNPR, while others found no statistically significant associations [18]. These mixed findings suggest that NSA interventions alone may not be sufficient to improve child growth outcomes and should be complemented by health, sanitation, and disease prevention [29, 30]. This review also highlights the importance of integrating agricultural interventions with health services and behavioral change strategies. Livestock–crop diversification and climate-smart agricultural practices are generally associated with improved dietary diversity, although their effectiveness is constrained by limited infrastructure and access to financial resources [17, 24]. Home gardening combined with nutrition education appears particularly promising for improving women’s dietary diversity, although sociocultural barriers, such as gender norms and food taboos, may limit participation in some contexts.

Behavior change communication and community engagement were important factors influencing the success of the intervention. For example, interventions promoting biofortified crops, such as orange-fleshed sweet potatoes, are associated with improved dietary diversity, although cultural acceptability and limited male involvement sometimes constrain adoption [16]. These findings suggest that culturally sensitive and gender-responsive approaches are important for maximizing the effectiveness of NSA interventions. Finally, the limited availability of longitudinal evidence remains a key gap in the literature on this topic. Future research should focus on long-term evaluations to better understand the sustained impact of NSA interventions on child growth and nutritional outcome. Strengthening the evidence base through rigorous study designs and standardized outcome measurements will support more effective multisectoral nutrition strategies in Ethiopia.

Limitations

The findings of this review are in light of the limitations of the included studies. First, most studies employed observational or quasi-experimental designs, which limited the ability to infer causality. Second, there was substantial heterogeneity in the intervention types, outcome indicators, and measurement approaches, making comparisons across studies challenging. Third, many interventions were of relatively short duration, which may not be sufficient to observe sustained changes in nutritional status, particularly in child anthropometric outcomes. In addition, variations in dietary diversity measurement tools and reporting standards further limit comparability across studies.

Conclusion

This systematic review revealed that nutrition-sensitive agriculture (NSA) interventions in Ethiopia are consistently linked to enhanced dietary diversity among vulnerable groups, particularly women and children. The most compelling evidence was found for interventions that integrated agricultural activities with nutrition education and behavior change communication. Conversely, evidence regarding child anthropometric outcomes remains sparse and inconsistent, with varied findings across regions and intervention types. This indicates that improvements in dietary diversity do not necessarily lead to measurable changes in child growth outcomes within the current evidence.

The effectiveness of NSA interventions is influenced by several contextual and implementation-related factors, including infrastructure constraints, market access and sociocultural barriers such as food taboos and gender norms. Overall, while NSA interventions show promise for improving dietary quality, their impact on nutritional status remains unclear. Strengthening multisectoral collaboration and addressing structural and behavioral barriers are essential for improving the effectiveness of the program. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and methodologically robust studies to better assess the long-term effects of NSA interventions, particularly on children’s growth and development.
