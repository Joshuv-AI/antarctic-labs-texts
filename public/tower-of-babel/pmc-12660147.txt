From biomass waste to CO2 capture: a multi-fidelity machine learning workflow for high-throughput screening of activated carbons
Authors: Faezeh Hajiali, Naoko Ellis, Bhushan Gopaluni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rising atmospheric CO2 levels threaten climate stability, demanding transformative solutions in carbon capture, utilization, and storage. Porous activated carbons (ACs) derived from sustainable waste sources offer a promising route for cost-effective and eco-friendly carbon capture, thanks to their tunable surface chemistry and high surface areas. However, optimizing ACs for peak CO2 uptake is often hindered by complex, resource-intensive experimental workflows and the scarcity of high-quality data. This study presents a machine learning-driven framework that combines a multi-headed one-dimensional convolutional neural network (MH1DCNN) with multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization (MFBO) to efficiently navigate large design spaces by balancing exploration of uncertain regions with exploitation of known high-performing candidates. The MH1DCNN captures nonlinear relationships between physicochemical properties and CO2 uptake, serving as a deployable low-fidelity model. Using 841 literature-reported samples as high-cost, high-fidelity data and MH1DCNN-generated predictions as low-cost, low-fidelity evaluations, MFBO fuses these information sources through a probabilistic surrogate model, enabling rapid and cost-effective optimization. This approach reduces high-fidelity evaluation requirements by over 75% and identifies top-performing candidates using only 13 high-fidelity acquisitions. This scalable, data-driven strategy supports the development of closed-loop experiment-analysis-planning systems for future autonomous laboratories and accelerates sustainable materials discovery.

Introduction

Addressing the escalating climate crisis has become an urgent global priority, as rising atmospheric CO2 levels continue to drive global warming. By November 2024, CO2 concentrations exceeded 423 ppm1, coinciding with persistent increases in global land temperatures. Minimizing CO2 emissions is closely aligned with global sustainable development objectives2. Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) has garnered significant attention as an effective strategy to mitigate the challenges associated with rising CO2 emissions3. Within this framework, porous solid adsorbents have been recognized as promising candidates for CCUS, owing to their high surface areas and tailorable features4. Among these materials, porous carbons-particularly those synthesized from diverse waste sources-offer a compelling solution due to their tunable porosity and outstanding chemical stability, positioning them as strong candidates to advance CCUS systems5.

Taking into account the principles of sustainable waste management, carbon-rich solid waste is considered a cost-effective precursors to produce porous carbons, promoting a circular approach to resource utilization6. With global solid waste generation expected to reach approximately 3.40 billion tonnes annually by 2050, the use of these materials offers both economic and environmental benefits7.

Porous carbons derived from waste sources often have outstanding textural properties and surface basic groups, making them highly effective for CO2 capture8,9. Furthermore, heteroatom doping within the carbon framework has been shown to increase adsorption sites, thereby enhancing the CO2 uptake efficiency10. Nevertheless, understanding the impact of surface chemistry and pore size distributions on CO2 uptake remains a significant challenge. This complexity arises mainly from the limited comprehensive data on pore volumes within specific size ranges and the distribution of functional group compositions, which hinders progress in materials discovery and optimization.

Machine learning (ML) has become a transformative approach for uncovering complex patterns and relationships within datasets, even when direct measurements are incomplete or fragmented11,12. In particular, deep learning techniques, including the convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have garnered attention across engineering disciplines for solving complex inverse problems, where causes are deduced from observed effects13. The architecture of CNNs is often tailored to the dimensionality of input data; for example, one-dimensional CNNs (1DCNNs) have demonstrated superior performance in analyzing datasets consisting of sequential or signal-based information14,15.

While ML models such as 1DCNNs excel at predictive tasks, their integration with optimization strategies can further enhance materials discovery by accelerating the search for optimal candidates. Bayesian Optimization (BO) offers a robust approach for this objective by integrating ML, uncertainty analysis, and decision-making strategies to efficiently direct experimental planning and assessment16,17. BO aims to identify materials with superior properties while reducing the need for extensive and expensive experimental efforts to achieve optimal results18–26.

BO operates through an iterative process that leverages (i) a predefined or dynamically generated pool of material candidates18 and (ii) experimental or simulated evaluations of target properties. By balancing exploration and exploitation, BO strategically selects subsequent candidates for testing, enabling rapid identification of high-performance materials with minimal resource expenditure. This makes BO particularly suitable for applications that require complex property optimizations, such as CO2 capture in porous materials.

The BO workflow, often described as a closed loop experiment-analysis-planning cycle27, involves three repeating steps: (i) performing an experiment to collect new data about the properties of a material, (ii) updating the surrogate model to incorporate the newly acquired information, and (iii) selecting the next material to study by maximizing the acquisition function (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Overview of MH1DCNN-MFBO integration.Integrating a MH1DCNN with MFBO for porous CO2 adsorbents entails a systematic, machine-driven process that includes (i) conducting simulations or experiments, (ii) refining the MFBO surrogate model from the obtained results, and (iii) strategically determining the subsequent step. “Low fidelity” refers to fast, model-predicted CO2 uptake values obtained from MH1DCNN, while “high fidelity” denotes ground-truth experimental uptake values reported in the literature. Overview of MH1DCNN-MFBO integration.

Integrating a MH1DCNN with MFBO for porous CO2 adsorbents entails a systematic, machine-driven process that includes (i) conducting simulations or experiments, (ii) refining the MFBO surrogate model from the obtained results, and (iii) strategically determining the subsequent step. “Low fidelity” refers to fast, model-predicted CO2 uptake values obtained from MH1DCNN, while “high fidelity” denotes ground-truth experimental uptake values reported in the literature. Although both originate from literature, they differ in cost, accuracy, and role within the optimization loop.

While BO workflow effectively optimizes experiments through iterative refinement, it requires balancing the trade-off between experimental accuracy and cost. Evaluating a material’s properties often involves choosing between different experimental methods that vary in both accuracy and cost. Fidelity denotes the accuracy of a method in predicting or measuring a property, whereas affordability refers to its associated costs. For example, computational simulations typically offer cost-effective but lower-fidelity predictions compared to resource-intensive, high-accuracy laboratory experiments.

Multi-fidelity Bayesian Optimization (MFBO) extends the standard BO framework to address these trade-offs by incorporating experimental data from varying fidelity levels28,29. The core objective of MFBO is to minimize resource consumption while identifying materials with optimal properties26. Unlike traditional BO, MFBO integrates both low-fidelity and high-fidelity data into a single surrogate model, enabling low-cost experiments to refine predictions and guide the selection of high-cost, high-fidelity evaluations.

MFBO further enhances the acquisition function to not only choose the subsequent candidate for testing but also determine the appropriate fidelity level. This adaptive strategy efficiently balances exploration of uncharted regions, exploitation of promising candidates, and cost management. Low-fidelity experiments help eliminate less promising candidates early, conserving resources, while high-fidelity evaluations focus on regions most likely to yield optimal results. Despite its potential to accelerate materials discovery, the adoption of MFBO and related approaches remains relatively underexplored in materials science26,30,31.

This work introduces a novel framework for predicting CO2 uptake in porous activated carbons (ACs) by integrating a multi-headed 1DCNN (MH1DCNN) with MFBO. An extensive dataset of 841 AC feature vectors, coupled with experimentally measured CO2 uptake values, is compiled to train and validate the proposed model, while MFBO optimizes the search for ACs with the highest high-fidelity CO2 uptake.

The optimization framework leverages bi-fidelity data to balance predictive accuracy and experimental efficiency32. In this study, we define fidelity not by the origin of the data (which is entirely literature-based), but by its role in the optimization loop. Specifically, the high-fidelity data refers to experimentally measured CO2 uptake values reported in the literature, treated as the ground truth in our surrogate modeling. In contrast, low-fidelity data refers to the MH1DCNN-predicted uptake values derived from the same features. While the MH1DCNN is trained on literature data, its predictions are significantly cheaper to obtain (~7 minutes) compared to reviewing or replicating experiments (~240 minutes), thus serving as a cost-effective, lower-fidelity surrogate. This functional distinction aligns with standard MFBO practices, where the fidelity classification is based on resource cost and accuracy within the modeling workflow rather than experimental provenance.

The surrogate model captures correlations between high-fidelity data and low-fidelity data and a cost-sensitive acquisition function to strategically select the next material and fidelity level for evaluation33. This approach efficiently narrows the search space, identifying ACs with optimal CO2 uptake while minimizing the need for resource-intensive experiments. By leveraging a blend of experimental methods and ML-driven predictions, MFBO demonstrates its potential to expedite the discovery of advanced porous carbons, offering a scalable and cost-effective strategy for materials optimization. Here, “discovery” refers to the identification of optimal property combinations within a known dataset, rather than the proposal of novel synthesis pathways.

Results

To gain an initial understanding of the compiled dataset, a descriptive analysis was conducted. Supplementary Fig. 1 presents the statistical distribution for each variable in the dataset. The specific surface area (SBET) of the porous ACs varies widely, ranging from 133 to 4168 m2/g, with a peak density around 1525 m2/g. The micropore volume (Vmicro) distribution shows a peak near 0.57 cm3/g, suggesting that the majority of ACs exhibit microporous characteristics. These are generally associated with Type I adsorption isotherms, which reflect monolayer adsorption in ACs with a high proportion of micropores34. Some samples also display features consistent with Type IV isotherms, which are indicative of mesoporous structures and are characterized by capillary condensation and hysteresis loops. The prevalence of Type I or mixed Type I/IV behavior underscores the importance of microporosity in enhancing CO2 adsorption.

For mesopores (Vmeso), which provide pathways for CO2 diffusion and facilitate access to micropores, the volume distribution peaks around 0.12 cm3/g. This right-skewed distribution implies that while mesopores are present, they occur in smaller volumes relative to micropores, supporting the role of mesopores as diffusion channels rather than primary adsorption sites. The oxygen content of ACs generally increases with higher decarbonization temperatures, ranging from 2.50 to 64.86 wt%. Nitrogen content, typically introduced intentionally during AC preparation, supports CO2 uptake via acid-base interactions between CO2 molecules and nitrogen-containing functional groups35. The nitrogen concentration varies from 0.20 to 20.40 wt%, with a median value of 3.14 wt%.

The Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) between various physicochemical characteristics of ACs and adsorption operating conditions is analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2, textural properties such as SBET and pore volume exhibit a weak negative correlation with nitrogen and oxygen content. This negative correlation may result from reactions between activating agents (e.g., KOH, H3PO4) and nitrogen- or oxygen-containing functional groups during the activation process. Adsorption capacity correlates positively with SBET, Vtotal, and Vmicro, as reported in prior studies36. In contrast, N and O content show no significant linear correlation with CO2 uptake, suggesting a nonlinear relationship driven by mechanisms like Lewis acid-base interactions and hydrogen bonding37. A large ultramicropore volume, combined with high heteroatom content (e.g., O and N), has been shown to enhance CO2 adsorption, highlighting the importance of synergistic effects for optimizing adsorbent design.

Additionally, we explicitly examined bottom-performing ACs in our analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2), which shows that ACs with low CO2 uptake are associated with combinations of features such as low surface area and micropore volume. These samples are part of our training dataset and are used to inform the surrogate model and the MFBO process.

While literature-reported datasets are often subject to selection bias toward successful ACs, our curated dataset includes both high- and low-performing ACs. No samples were excluded based on CO2 uptake performance. As shown in the violin plots (Supplementary Fig. 1), the dataset spans a wide range of feature values, including BET surface areas from 133 to 4168 m2/g. The radar plot (Supplementary Fig. 2) further illustrates the contrasting feature profiles between the top- and bottom-performing ACs, confirming the presence of low-uptake ACs in our analysis and model training.

It is important to note that all samples in our dataset are activated carbons, which inherently possess a minimum level of porosity, and thus non-zero CO2 uptake, due to their high-temperature synthesis process. During activation, volatile gases such as CO2, H2O, and NH3 are released, forming microporous and mesoporous structures. As a result, samples with completely negligible uptake are rare and unrepresentative of the material class being studied. Nonetheless, we acknowledge the broader issue of “missing failures” in literature-derived datasets and have added this discussion to highlight potential limitations in surrogate modeling and acquisition functions within our MFBO framework. Although our dataset provides sufficient diversity for robust model training, future work could incorporate hypothetical low-performing ACs or expand the scope to include a broader range of carbonaceous adsorbents to enhance the generalizability of virtual screening efforts.

Analyzing the performance of MH1DCNN

We compared the performance of the multi-headed 1D convolutional neural network (MH1DCNN) against three baseline models: Gradient Boosting Trees (GBT), Random Forest (RF), and a single-headed 1D CNN (SH1DCNN), as well as a multilayer perceptron (MLP) serving as a non-convolutional neural network baseline. The developed MH1DCNN was trained on the ACs dataset and fine-tuned by the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0031, selected through hyperparameter optimization. The batch size was fixed at 27 to balance computational efficiency and accuracy. To prevent overfitting, early stopping criteria were implemented, halting training once the validation loss stopped improving over 20 consecutive epochs. The dataset was divided into two subsets, with 80% allocated for training the model and the remaining 20% reserved for evaluation purposes. The dataset was normalized using Min-Max scaling to ensure numerical stability. The MH1DCNN architecture showed strong learning performance, with converging training and validation loss curves and no signs of overfitting. Similar Mean Absolute Error (MAE) trends further confirm the model’s robustness and generalization ability.

Although deep learning models can be prone to overfitting, especially on smaller datasets, several safeguards were put in place to mitigate this risk. The MH1DCNN architecture was kept compact, with regularization strategies such as SpatialDropout1D, L2 penalties, and early stopping integrated into training. The model was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation on a curated dataset with no repeated material-condition pairs, ensuring robust performance without data leakage. To assess the relative merits of different neural architectures, we included MLP as a non-convolutional baseline. While the MLP achieved comparable average performance to RF, both SH1DCNN and MH1DCNN outperformed it across all metrics. SH1DCNN showed an improved prediction accuracy with slightly higher variance, while MH1DCNN consistently delivered the best performance with the lowest MAE and RMSE, and the highest R2, along with reduced variability by folds. GBT, RF, and SH1DCNN models systematically underpredict the highest uptake values, while the MH1DCNN model exhibits only mild underprediction in this regime (CO2 uptake above 7.5 mmol/g), as shown in Fig. 3. This behavior is primarily attributed to dataset imbalance, as high-uptake samples constitute a small fraction of the dataset, making it difficult for the models to robustly learn the distinctive structural or compositional patterns associated with these outliers.

The superior performance of MH1DCNN supports the architectural hypothesis that multiple convolutional heads enhance model expressiveness and generalization. Although the input consists of eight physicochemical variables rather than sequential or image-based data, the convolutional layers still benefit from weight sharing and local connectivity. The multi-head structure enables the network to learn multiple independent feature representations in parallel, capturing complex and diverse relationships across the feature space. This design promotes modular learning, improves robustness, and reduces overfitting through implicit regularization. Our architectural ablation further supports this, showing clear performance gains from MH1DCNN over both SH1DCNN and the MLP baseline. These findings justify the use of multiple convolutional heads even for structured, non-sequential tabular data.

By balancing exploration and exploitation across fidelities, it accelerates the discovery of optimal ACs while minimizing computational cost. As shown in Supplementary Information, low and high fidelity predictions are well aligned, supporting the model’s reliability, though some deviations highlight the need for high fidelity validation. MFBO effectively balances exploration and exploitation. Early iterations broadly sample the search space, while later ones concentrate on high-performing regions. Low-fidelity simulations guide the search efficiently, followed by targeted high fidelity evaluations. The algorithm ultimately identified the top-performing AC, which also ranked first in low-fidelity predictions. While simplified surrogate models, such as noise-perturbed lookup tables, can conceptually illustrate the mechanics of multi-fidelity optimization, they lack generalizability and do not reflect realistic ACs discovery scenarios. In contrast, MH1DCNN was employed as a low-fidelity surrogate to emulate structure property relationships learned from measurable ACs features. This approach aligns with emerging practices in multi-fidelity machine learning, where predictive surrogates serve as scalable, data-driven approximations of experimental or computational evaluations39.

Evaluating MFBO against traditional sequential search approaches

MFBO was evaluated against Single Fidelity Bayesian Optimization (SFBO), which relies solely on high fidelity CO2 uptake values, and Random Search, both evaluated over multiple independent runs. Random Search, constrained to a fixed budget of 800 hours per run, consistently consumed the full budget. While a few runs identified high performing ACs, most plateaued at lower uptake values (8.38–8.64 mmol/g). On average, it took 350.80 hours to locate the top performer, underscoring Random Search’s inefficiency in convergence and resource use. SFBO showed improved performance but remained significantly more resource-intensive than MFBO. It required an average of 492.64 hours per run, with high variability (standard deviation 157.89 hours, maximum 616.0 hours), reflecting inconsistent convergence dependent on initialization (Supplementary Fig. 11).

MFBO outperformed both methods in efficiency and consistency. It achieved an average search time of just 80.17 hours, a maximum of 156.22 hours, and a low standard deviation of 43.24 hours. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12, MFBO runtimes were tightly clustered below 200 hours, demonstrating reliable and fast convergence. Supplementary Fig. 13 further illustrates that MFBO consistently identifies top candidates earlier and with fewer evaluations.

MFBO used only 2.89% of the resources required for a full exhaustive high fidelity search and reduced computational cost by over 75% compared to SFBO and Random Search. It strategically balanced 13 high fidelity and 96 low fidelity evaluations, with 82.3% of the total cost devoted to high fidelity tests. This demonstrates MFBO’s efficiency in using low-fidelity simulations for broad exploration and reserving high-fidelity evaluations for validating top candidates.

MFBO follows a structured search strategy: early iterations explore diverse regions using low-fidelity simulations, while later stages focus on exploiting high-performing areas through targeted high-fidelity evaluations. This transition is reflected in the steep rise of MFBO performance curves, where top ACs are identified early in the search process. By efficiently balancing exploration and exploitation across fidelities, MFBO not only reduces computational cost and resource use but also consistently outperforms random, single-fidelity, and exhaustive search methods.

The correlation disruption study (Supplementary Fig. 14) evaluated the impact of feature structure on MFBO performance by comparing results obtained using the original features versus features that were permuted simultaneously. This global permutation disrupts all feature correlations, allowing us to assess the role of meaningful feature structure in guiding the search process. MFBO with original features converged more rapidly and required fewer high-fidelity evaluations, leveraging the inherent relationships among features. In contrast, the permuted version led to slower convergence and higher computational cost. Nevertheless, MFBO remained robust, occasionally identifying high-performing candidates through stochastic exploration. These results underscore the importance of preserving feature structure and highlight the value of domain-informed features in efficient optimization.

While this study focuses on biomass-derived ACs, the proposed multi-fidelity framework is modular and designed for broader applicability across materials domains. The integration of low-fidelity predictions from MH1DCNN with MFBO enables effective navigation of materials spaces, even when experimental evaluations are limited. However, the generalizability of the framework to materials with sparser or noisier literature coverage may be impacted by dataset quality, and the availability of well-structured features. In such cases, enhanced uncertainty quantification, transfer learning from richer domains, or the inclusion of hypothetical low-performing samples could improve robustness. We also acknowledge the “missing failures” issue inherent in literature-derived datasets, which may skew model training. Despite these limitations, the MFBO approach remains a flexible tool for accelerating discovery in underexplored or data-poor materials domains and can be readily adapted with minimal retraining when new data becomes available.

Discussions

This study presents an integrated framework that combines a multi-headed one-dimensional convolutional neural network (MH1DCNN) with multi-Fidelity Bayesian optimization (MFBO) to identify high-performing porous activated carbons (ACs) for CO2 capture. The MH1DCNN demonstrated strong predictive accuracy across cross-validation, effectively capturing complex, multidimensional relationships between physiochemical features and CO2 uptake. Its automated feature extraction makes it well-suited for future dataset expansion and interpretability tasks. Notably, the model highlights pore structure regulation as an economical and practical strategy for enhancing CO2 adsorption, reducing the need for extensive experimentation.

The MFBO framework efficiently guided the search for optimal ACs by combining low-fidelity predictions from the MH1DCNN with high-fidelity experimental data. It identified the top-performing sample using only 96 low-fidelity and 13 high-fidelity evaluations, reducing computational cost to under 25% of that required by Single-Fidelity Bayesian optimization and Random Search. This demonstrates a scalable, cost-effective strategy for accelerating materials optimization in carbon capture applications. The modular design of the MH1DCNN surrogate and the efficient acquisition strategy of MFBO also support scalability to larger datasets, enabling practical deployment in data-rich materials discovery efforts.

While the model relies on well-established indicators of CO2 uptake from a literature-derived dataset, these features do not directly inform synthesis pathways. This limitation stems from inconsistent reporting of synthesis conditions and variability in biomass feedstock composition. To extend such frameworks toward autonomous material discovery, standardized, synthesis-aware datasets will be essential. Nonetheless, this work offers a practical step in that direction, showing how data-driven surrogates and optimization can significantly accelerate the performance refinement of porous materials for CO2 capture.

Methods

Data collection and statistical analysis

It is widely recognized that extensive, high-quality data is essential for developing accurate and reliable predictive models. To this end, we manually created a dataset of 841 samples on heteroatom-infused, biomass-derived ACs for CO2 capture based on a comprehensive literature review from peer-reviewed journal articles, technical reports, and publicly available databases. Sources were identified through keyword-based searches (e.g., biomass porous carbon, waste-derived carbon, CO2 capture, heteroatom doping, activated carbon adsorption, surface area, micropore, mesopore) in scientific databases (e.g., Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect) and selected based on data completeness and quality. Each entry includes structural, chemical, and operational features relevant to CO2 uptake, along with the corresponding source reference (DOI). Data were manually extracted from peer-reviewed publications, including main text tables, supplementary materials, and publicly available datasets. The full dataset is available in Excel format in the Supplementary Information and online via GitHub.

Previous research has shown that, in addition to temperature and pressure, specific surface area, pore structure, and heteroatom content are critical factors influencing CO2 uptake in activated carbons40. In this study, each AC sample is represented by a feature vector consisting of eight elements: micropore volume (Vmicro, cm3/g), mesopore volume (Vmeso, cm3/g), total pore volume (Vtotal, cm3/g), specific surface area (SBET, m2/g), oxygen content (Ototal, wt%), nitrogen content (Ntotal, wt%), temperature (T, ∘C), and pressure (P, bar). These features capture essential physical, chemical, and operational factors relevant to CO2 adsorption. Although ultramicropores (pore sizes <0.8 nm) are known to enhance uptake, limited experimental data on ultramicropores restrict their inclusion. The target variable used for model training is CO2 uptake (mmol/g).

We acknowledge that incorporating descriptors such as specific surface functionalities, precursor molecular structure, or electrochemical properties like point-of-zero-charge (PZC) could improve model fidelity, particularly for systems where chemisorption plays a significant role. Although synthesis parameters such as activation temperature, precursor identity, and agent loading, are known to directly affect textural and chemical properties, they were excluded from the present model due to inconsistent reporting, vague terminology, and lack of standardization across the literature. For instance, activation temperatures were often reported as broad ranges, or lacked key details such as ramp rates and dwell times. Similarly, precursor identity ranged from general classifications like “biomass” to specific feedstocks such as “tobacco stem,” complicating reliable categorical encoding. Given this variability, we determined that including such heterogeneous variables would likely introduce more noise than signal in a data-driven modeling framework.

We recognize that literature-reported datasets often contain variability due to differences in characterization techniques and reporting practices. To minimize these effects, we applied several quality-control measures. Studies were included only if they reported BET surface area (SBET), total and micropore volumes (Vtotal, Vmicro), heteroatom contents (e.g., N and O), and CO2 uptake under defined temperature and pressure conditions. These properties had to be measured using standard methods (e.g., BET analysis via N2 adsorption, CO2 uptake at specified temperature and pressure). Data were manually extracted from tables, figures, or supplementary materials, digitized with WebPlotDigitizer when necessary, and converted into consistent units. Chemical composition values (e.g., nitrogen and oxygen) were retained only when the measurement techniques (e.g., XPS, elemental analysis) were explicitly reported. Entries with missing, inconsistent, or ambiguous data were excluded, and partially reported pore volumes or heteroatom contents were dropped unless values could be reliably inferred.

Duplicate samples reported across multiple studies were identified by matching synthesis conditions and properties, with only the most complete entry retained. All entries were then standardized into uniform units and manually checked for outliers and transcription errors.

Despite these precautions, we acknowledge that heterogeneity across literature sources arising from different synthesis equipment, adsorbent activation methods, and instrument calibrations cannot be entirely eliminated. Additionally, occasional human or measurement error may be present. We consider this a limitation of our dataset and modeling framework. However, given the size of the data set, the use of regularized models, and the diverse range of ACs included, we believe that the predictive framework remains robust to moderate noise and suitable for high-throughput screening.

As such, we opted to focus on features that were experimentally measured and reported consistently across studies. However, we acknowledge that omitting direct synthesis-controllable parameters limits the applicability of the current framework for autonomous experimentation and the design of inverse ACs. We see this as an important and promising direction for future research. Expanding the descriptor space to include curated synthesis metadata, engineered synthesis descriptors, or text-mined synthesis parameters could significantly improve model generalizability and practical relevance. Moreover, recent advances in natural language processing and generative modeling offer the potential to infer plausible synthesis pathways from the literature and propose new routes conditioned on target ACs properties, facilitating a closed-loop design-to-synthesis workflow.

Multi-headed Convolutional Neural Network (MH1DCNN)

Drawing on insights from previous research, where ensemble methods such as Random Forest (RF)38 have demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for CO2 adsorption capacity in porous adsorbents, RF was used as the foundation for developing our initial predictive model. To complement this, Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBT), a method rooted in boosting theory, was also incorporated. Unlike RF, which constructs trees independently, GBT constructs decision trees in a sequential manner, where the new tree refines the errors of the previous ones to improve predictive performance. This approach emphasizes difficult instances by assigning greater weight to cases that were previously mispredicted, gradually improving the model’s accuracy. The ultimate prediction is obtained as a weighted sum of the outputs from all individual trees41. This iterative refinement process enables GBT to capture intricate patterns in the dataset that RF might overlook, making it a powerful complement to ensemble modeling.

Due to the high-dimensional and non-linear characteristics of the adsorption data, the GBT model is particularly effective in maximizing prediction accuracy42. However, GBT is also more prone to overfitting than RF, as its iterative updates continuously adjust predictions on more challenging instances43. To strike a balance between accuracy and generalization, precise hyperparameter tuning is essential, enabling GBT to improve predictive performance through targeted error correction44.

In addition to these ensemble methods, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a single-head 1D convolutional neural network (SH1DCNN) were implemented as a deep learning baseline to assess the added value of architectural enhancements introduced in the multi-headed variant. The SH1DCNN shares the same underlying convolutional structure as the MH1DCNN but processes the input through a single feature extraction path. This model served as a benchmark to isolate the impact of multi-headed architecture on learning capacity and predictive performance.

In this study, GBT, RF, MLP, SH1DCNN, and MH1DCNN were implemented using the Google Colab platform. An 80:20 train-test split was used, with the training set subjected to five-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning and internal evaluation, while the test set was reserved for external validation45. In MH1DCNN, the input data is processed simultaneously by two parallel branches, each comprising multiple stacked 1DCNN layers46. The two branches are asymmetric, differing in kernel sizes and filter numbers, which allows them to extract unique and complementary features by capturing patterns at varying receptive fields. This parallel design enhances feature extraction compared to traditional sequential CNN architectures by leveraging diverse feature representations, making it particularly effective for regression tasks such as CO2 uptake prediction.

The proposed architecture consists of two parallel branches, each containing two convolutional layers followed by pooling layers. The outputs of these layers, which are multi-dimensional tensors, are then flattened into one-dimensional vectors. This “flattening" process transforms the multi-dimensional data into a single-dimensional format, which is required for merging the outputs of both branches and for subsequent processing in fully connected layers. After flattening, the outputs of both branches are merged and passed through a fully connected dense layer containing 128 neurons. These neurons perform weighted computations to capture complex relationships between the patterns identified by the CNN. Finally, a single neuron in the output layer predicts the continuous target variable, CO2 uptake, using a linear activation function, which is standard for regression tasks.

Multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization

The MBO workflow, illustrated in Fig. 1, follows a systematic three-step process: (i) defining an initial distribution, (ii) acquiring simulation results or experimental measurements, and (iii) refining the initial distribution based on observed data to obtain the posterior distribution16. Low-fidelity predictions, generated by the MH1DCNN model described earlier, offer a computationally efficient solution with an average runtime of 7 minutes. In contrast, high-fidelity experimental measurements, which provide more accurate data, require approximately 240 minutes per test. These runtimes are system-dependent and may vary based on dataset size, computational resources, and experimental protocols. These values provide a clear numerical distinction between fidelity levels and align with the multi-fidelity Gaussian Process framework. This design allows the MFBO framework to balance computational efficiency and predictive accuracy effectively49.
