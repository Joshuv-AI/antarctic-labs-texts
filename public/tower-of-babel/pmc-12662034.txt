Reframing the immunopathogenic role of anti-TPO autoantibodies in chronic spontaneous urticaria: Toward a unified IgE–IgG axis hypothesis☆
Authors: Nav La, Schawanya K. Rattanapitoon, Natthawut Charoenphon, Nathkapach K. Rattanapitoon
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The recent publication by Lopez et al1 represents a significant contribution to our understanding of autoimmune endotypes in chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU). The study's finding that anti-thyroperoxidase (anti-TPO) IgE and IgG define separate clinical clusters while circulating TPO levels fail to predict antibody presence is valuable. Yet, from an immunopathogenic perspective, the data may suggest a deeper unifying mechanism rather than true segregation of IgE and IgG pathways.

From dual to dynamic autoimmunity. Traditionally, CSU has been classified into type I autoallergy (IgE-mediated) and type IIb autoimmunity (IgG-mediated), a framework summarized in recent international guidelines and reviews.2,3 Lopez et al1 correctly report distinct clinical clusters. However, an alternative (and testable) view is that anti-TPO IgE and IgG represent two phases along a continuous autoimmune axis, driven by antigen exposure, epitope spreading and isotype switching. Initial antigen recognition via IgE–FcεRI–dependent mast cell activation could produce local inflammation that promotes antigen presentation and class switch recombination to IgG; over time the dominant isotype (IgE vs IgG) may shift, producing different clinical phenotypes.

Evidence supporting an early role for IgE autoantibodies in CSU comes from seminal reports documenting anti-TPO IgE in subgroups of CSU patients.4 Those observations make it biologically plausible that IgE responses can precede or coexist with IgG autoreactivity, rather than always signifying an entirely separate disease mechanism.

The missing link: FcγR–FcεRI crosstalk. Lopez et al. observed that IgG-positive patients more frequently required omalizumab, suggesting complex relationships between isotype, effector pathways, and treatment response. Mechanistic studies indicate that co-engagement of IgG immune complexes with Fcγ receptors (notably inhibitory FcγRIIb) and FcεRI on mast cells/basophils can modulate IgE-driven activation — sometimes inhibiting and sometimes altering downstream signaling depending on receptor expression and the nature of IgG complexes.5,6 Incorporating FcγR–FcεRI interplay into an IgE–IgG axis model helps reconcile (a) low overlap between high-titer IgE and IgG anti-TPO, and (b) variable responsiveness to anti-IgE therapy across patient subgroups.

Revisiting TPO as a bystander or driver. The paradox reported by Lopez et al (no clear relation between free serum TPO and antibodies) could reflect antigen sequestration in immune complexes; bound TPO would be less detectable in assays for free antigen, yet immune complexes could sustain complement activation, local deposition, or FcR engagement in skin microenvironments. Quantifying TPO in immune complexes and assessing complement-deposition (e.g., C4d) in lesional skin would test whether TPO is actively driving pathology or acting mainly as a shared antigenic marker.7

Implications for CSU endotyping and therapy. If anti-TPO IgE and IgG reflect an immunological trajectory rather than fixed endotypes, clinical practice should move from one-time serotyping to longitudinal biomarker monitoring. Anti-IgE therapy (omalizumab) may be most beneficial in early/IgE-dominant phases, whereas IgG-dominant disease could respond better to B-cell-targeted or FcγR-modulating approaches. Concluding perspective. Lopez et al.1 have provided a robust exploratory foundation. We propose a unified IgE–IgG axis hypothesis that frames anti-TPO reactivity as an evolving autoimmune process with stage-dependent effector mechanisms. Testing this hypothesis will require standardized assays, serial sampling, and functional readouts — an approach that could translate mechanistic insights into stage-specific interventions for CSU.8
