DRP1: At the Crossroads of Dysregulated Mitochondrial Dynamics and Altered Cell Signaling in Cancer Cells
Authors: Ankita Adhikary, Agradeep Mukherjee, Riddhi Banerjee, Shirisha Nagotu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the past decade, compelling evidence has accumulated that highlights the role of various subcellular structures in human disease conditions. Dysregulation of these structures greatly impacts cellular function and, thereby, disease conditions. One such organelle extensively studied for its role in several human diseases, especially cancer, is the mitochondrion. DRP1 is a GTPase that is considered the master regulator of mitochondrial fission and thereby also affects the proper functioning of the organelle. Altered signaling pathways are a distinguished characteristic of cancer cells. In this review, we aim to summarize our current understanding of the interesting crosstalk between the mitochondrial structure–function maintained by DRP1 and the signaling pathways that are affected in cancer cells. We highlight the structural aspects of DRP1, its regulation by various modifications, and the association of the protein with various cellular pathways altered in cancer. A better understanding of this association may help in identifying potential pharmacological targets for novel therapies in cancer.

Introduction

Cancer is a dreadful disease that affects different parts of the body. The disease state is observed when healthy cells evade controlled growth and division processes and exhibit unrestricted growth and abnormal proliferation.1 Further, these cancer cells may detach from their point of origin, invade other tissues, and subsequently transform them in a process called metastasis.1 According to data from the World Health Organization, various cancers together accounted for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020. Lung cancer was number one with about 1.80 million cases, followed by colorectal, liver, stomach, and breast cancers with 935, 830, 769, and 685 thousand deaths, respectively (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cancer). Cancer cells can be distinguished from noncancerous cells by the presence of the core hallmarks of sustained proliferative signaling, evading growth suppressors, activating invasion and metastasis, enabling replicative immortality, inducing angiogenesis, resisting cell death or apoptosis, deregulating cellular energetics, and avoiding immune destruction.2,3 In addition, two characteristics, genome instability and tumor-promoting inflammation, due to which the above hallmarks are acquired by the cancer cells, are also proposed.3 These unique characteristics make cancer cells extraordinarily resilient and resistant to various treatment strategies.

Mitochondria are double membrane-bound organelles essential for energy generation in eukaryotic cells, including cancer cells. The presence of an outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) and an inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) that folds inward multiple times to form cristae results in the formation of two compartments called the intermembrane space and the matrix.4,5 Mitochondria are the house for the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). The IMM houses the electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I–IV, coenzyme Q, cytochrome c, and ATP synthase.6,7 These complexes pump out protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space while regenerating the oxidized forms of NAD+ and FAD. This generates a proton gradient, which powers the ATP synthase and produces ATP in the process.8−11 As the electrons get transferred from one complex to the subsequent one, reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as peroxide (H2O2), superoxide (O2–), and hydroxyl ions (OH•) are generated, commonly referred to as mtROS12−14 (Figure 1A). Accumulation of the produced ROS may cause irreversible damage to DNA and proteins and thereby affect mitochondrial integrity. However, optimum ROS are important as signaling molecules, and this balance is facilitated by the antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SODs), catalase, and glutathione peroxidase present in the cells.13 Mitochondria also play an important role in other crucial cellular processes such as calcium homeostasis and apoptosis.15−17 Apoptotic stimuli such as sustained stress or DNA damage lead to permeabilization of the OMM, causing the release of cytochrome c from intermembrane space to the cytosol.18 Cytochrome c subsequently binds and activates apoptosis-protease activating factor 1 (Apaf1), followed by the maturation and further activation of caspase 9 and caspase 3. This cascade of steps ultimately leads to apoptotic cell death.18,19

Mitochondrial Dynamics in Cancer

Mitochondria are incredibly dynamic organelles and undergo a continuous cycle of fusion and fission to form an active network in response to cellular stimuli.20,21 Fusion results in the formation of an interconnected mitochondrial network that allows the diffusion of matrix contents such as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), ETC components, and nutrient precursors between mitochondria.22,23 Fission, on the other hand, generates smaller and disjoined mitochondria that can be easily transported to regions of higher energy demand and can also aid in the proper inheritance of mitochondria by daughter cells during cell division.24,25 Fission also facilitates mitophagy, a specific autophagic process that clears damaged mitochondria.26,27

The molecular machinery that orchestrates mitochondrial fusion and fission comprises conserved proteins that belong to the family of large GTPases. Mitochondrial fusion can be divided into the OMM fusion mediated by mitofusin-1 (MFN1) and mitofusin-2 (MFN2) and IMM fusion carried out by optic atrophy 1 (OPA1).28−30 MFN1 and MFN2 interact with each other to form homo- and heterodimers and, with the help of their GTPase activity, aid in the fusion of OMMs of adjacent mitochondria.28 OPA1 and MFN1 regulate the IMM fusion in an unknown manner.31 Proteolytic processing results in the long and short isoforms of OPA1. Interestingly, a recent study suggests that the ratio of l-Opa1 and s-Opa1 regulates the IMM fusion.30 On the other hand, mitochondrial fission in mammalian cells requires another protein of this family called the dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1).32 DRP1 predominantly resides in the cytosol and is recruited onto the OMM by the receptor proteins mitochondrial fission protein 1 (FIS1), mitochondrial fission factor (MFF), and mitochondrial division proteins (MID49/51 kDa)33 (Figure 2). An ER-mediated preconstriction of the mitochondrial membrane prior to the recruitment of DRP1 has been reported.34 GTP-bound DRP1 forms a higher-order ring-like structure at these constricted sites on the mitochondria35 (Figure 2). Interestingly, a role for actin polymerization in the oligomerization of DRP1 at the OMM has also been reported.36,37 Actin polymerization precedes DRP1 recruitment to the OMM, and a role for actin-binding proteins such as inverted formin 2 (INF2) and Spire1C in this process has also been reported.37 Negative regulation of DRP1 activity by Cofilin via its role in actin polymerization was also reported.38 Subsequently, hydrolysis of GTP is required for DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission.39,40

A close relationship between mitochondrial dynamics and cancer tumor progression has been reported (Figure 1). Cancer cells often exhibit fragmented mitochondria due to the upregulation of the fission protein DRP1 and the downregulation of fusion proteins MFN1/241,42 (Figure 1C). Increased DRP1 activity is reported in different types of cancers, such as metastatic breast cancer,42 lung cancer,41 colorectal cancer,43 pancreatic cancer,44 glioblastoma,45 neuroblastoma,46 and squamous cell carcinoma.47 Elevated DRP1 activity by cell cycle regulator cyclin B1-CDK1 promotes mitochondrial division during mitosis and cell cycle progression.21,48 This excess mitochondrial fragmentation also blocks calcium-mediated apoptosis by altering intramolecular calcium waves49 (Figure 1H). Increased mitochondrial fragmentation was also reported to enhance the migratory capability of tumorigenic cells.42 Zhao and colleagues showed that invasive breast carcinoma cells utilize DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fission to recruit the organelle to the lamellipodial region to meet the local high energy demand.42 Recent studies reported that impaired mitochondrial fission resulted in impaired asymmetric division of cancer stem cells (CSC). Downregulation of YY2, the transcription factor of DRP1, was observed in liver CSC in this study.50

Under physiological conditions, damaged mitochondria are degraded via mitophagy. The PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway is activated upon depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane in mammalian cells51 (Figure 1G). Alternatively, HIF-1α also activates mitophagy through adaptors BNIP3, NIX, and FUNDC1, independent of the PINK1/Parkin system.52 Dysfunctional mitophagy is closely associated with the occurrence of tumorigenesis, although its role might differ in different stages of cancer.53 Loss of function of Parkin leads to accumulation of damaged mitochondria, suppression of mitophagy, and enhanced ROS, thereby resulting in increased tumorigenesis.53 Further, increased expression of Parkin inhibited tumor growth in different cancers.53 However, increased mitophagy due to abnormal regulation of BNIP3 as an adaptive response for tumor survival under hypoxic conditions was reported.54

DRP1: Structure, Domain, and Isoforms

DRP1 is a large GTPase that belongs to the dynamin superfamily. Unlike the classical dynamins that are required for membrane scission during vesicle formation from the plasma membrane or Golgi complex, DRP1 majorly functions in mitochondrial division.32,55,56 DRP1 is primarily a cytosolic protein, but a significant fraction of it is also found on the OMM.32 DRP1 exhibits an important property of self-assembly that is a requisite for mitochondrial fission.35,39 It is now well-accepted that DRP1 resides in the cytosol as dimers and/or tetramers. However, the different structural forms of DRP1 and their interconversion have to be elucidated in detail.57,58 Further, these dimers/tetramers are directly recruited to the future fission sites on the OMM and form a higher-order ring-like oligomeric structure that encircles it. Additionally, oligomeric DRP1 was also detected in the cytosol and these oligomers are proposed to act as reservoirs for the supply of DRP1 dimers to the mitochondrial membrane.59,60

Similar to the other members of the dynamin superfamily, DRP1 also contains three important domains: an N-terminal GTPase domain, a middle domain (MD), and a C-terminal GTPase effector domain (GED).56,61 In addition to this, DRP1 contains a variable domain (VD) which contains the B-insert62 (Figure 3). The GTPase domain is involved in binding and hydrolysis of GTP. Conformational change due to GTP binding stabilizes the DRP1 oligomeric structure on the OMM.39,35 Upon GTP hydrolysis, a reduction in the diameter of DRP1 spirals that subsequently constrict mitochondria has been proposed.39,35 A role for the MD in the self-assembly of DRP1 into dimers, tetramers, and higher-order oligomers has been reported.63,64 DRP1–DRP1 interaction takes place at a dimerization interface where three helices from the MD and one helix from the GED form a four-helix bundle.63,64 The opposite surface of the dimerization interface may act as a platform where two DRP1 dimers can interact to form tetramers and further higher-order oligomeric structures.64 The C-terminal GED folds back to interact with the GTPase domain and stimulates DRP1 GTPase activity.64−66 This self-activating property of DRP1 is unique and is not dependent on guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) or GTPase activating proteins (GAPs), unlike the RAS GTPase.64 A regulatory role for the VD in controlling self-assembly was reported both in solution and in vitro on the membrane surface.39In vitro studies have demonstrated that, in the absence of the VD, the diameter of the DRP1 spirals was greater than 250 nm on the membrane surface. The presence of the VD without the B-insert results in DRP1 spirals of less than 200 nm in diameter, while DRP1 that contains the B-insert forms spirals of less than 100 nm in diameter. Hence, the B-insert in the VD determines the curvature of the DRP1 spirals, which is necessary for their optimum binding to the membrane, leading to fission.67,68 Furthermore, the majority of the post-translational modification (PTM) sites are also reported in the VD, supporting a regulatory role of this domain.44,69−71

Nine different isoforms of DRP1 are generated due to alternative splicing (Figure 3).56,72 The DRP1L transcript that codes for DRP1 comprises 21 exons, of which splicing occurs primarily in three exons (3, 16, and 17).72 Additionally, the number of amino acids (aa) in the B-insert of the VD is variable in these isoforms.72 Isoform 1 comprises 736 aa and lacks exon 3; isoform 2 has 710 aa and exons 3 and 16 are spliced out; isoform 3 has 699 aa and exons 3, 16, and 17 are spliced out; and isoforms 4 and 5 are comprised of 725 and 710 aa respectively and lack exons 3 and 17. Isoform 6 is comprised of 749 aa and contains all exons. Due to the absence of exons 5–8, isoform 7 has the shortest length (533 aa). It is interesting to note that translation starts at exon 2 rather than exon 1 in isoform 7. Isoform 8 has 738 aa and lacks exon 17, and isoform 9 has 712 aa and lacks exons 16 and 17. Depending on the cell type, the alternative splicing of the DNM1L transcript may have an impact on its localization and function.73 However, the majority of DRP1 isoforms are found on the OMM and in the cytosol. Isoforms that express the third exon but not the second exon exhibit microtubule association.73

DRP1: PTMs That Regulate Protein Activity

Several PTMs that control the activity, localization, and degradation of DRP1 have been reported. In this section, we aim to summarize the role of the PTMs in the regulation of DRP1.

Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is one of the major PTMs that regulates the activity of DRP1. Two important phosphorylation sites, serine at 616 and 637, are present in the GED of the protein (Figure 4). Kinases such as calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase-1 (CAMK-1), ERK1/2, PKC, MAPK, and CDK1 catalyze the phosphorylation of S61644,74−77 (Figure 4). S616 phosphorylation enhances DRP1 activity and results in increased mitochondrial fission.40 On the contrary, phosphorylation at the same site by cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) causes an increase in the monomeric form of the protein and localizes it preferentially to the cytosol, preventing mitochondrial fission.78 Phosphorylation of the S637 residue by cyclic AMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA) decreases the activity of DRP1 by disrupting the intramolecular interaction between the GED and GTPase domain71 (Figure 4). Calcineurin causes dephosphorylation of S637 in response to an increase in cytosolic calcium, resulting in the translocation of DRP1 to OMM and increasing mitochondrial fission.70 Serines at 585 and 693 are also phosphorylated by cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1)/cyclin B and glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), respectively.48,69 Phosphorylation at residue 585 located in the VD increases mitochondrial fission, while at the 693 residue it inhibits GTPase activity and mitochondrial fission.48,69 c-Abl, a tyrosine kinase, also regulates mitochondrial morphology during oxidative stress. Three tyrosine residues at the 266, 368, and 449 positions in DRP1 were reported to be phosphorylated by c-Abl, leading to increased fission and subsequent cell death by apoptosis.79

Ubiquitination

Ubiquitination or addition of ubiquitin is a PTM that signals the cell for proteasome-mediated degradation.80 Two ubiquitin ligases (E3), Parkin and the mitochondrial RING finger E3 ligase (MARCH5), ubiquitinate DRP1 (Figure 4B). Parkin-mediated ubiquitination results in increased degradation of DRP1, resulting in inhibition of fission.81 MARCH5 is a transmembrane ubiquitin ligase that is natively associated with the OMM.82 Apart from ubiquitinating DRP1 for its degradation via the proteasomal pathway, MARCH5 is also needed for the proper recruitment and assembly of DRP1 at the future fission sites on the OMM.83 Abnormal accumulation of DRP1 on the OMM surface and hindered mitochondrial fission were observed in cells expressing mutant MARCH5 or upon its silencing, indicating a pro-fission role of the enzyme.83 Mitochondrial fission is essential during mitosis to supply sufficient healthy mitochondria to the daughter cells. A third ubiquitin ligase called anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome and its coactivator Cdh1 (APC/C) play a pivotal role in the degradation of excess DRP1 when a dividing cell exits mitosis.84 Deubiquitinating enzyme ovarian tumor-associated protease deubiquitinase 6A (OTUD6A) cleaves off ubiquitin residues and increases DRP1 stability in the cell.85

SUMOylation

The attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMO) is an important PTM that regulates the localization and stability of DRP1. Ubc9 (ubiquitin carrier protein 9) and MAPL (mitochondrial-anchored protein ligase) are two important SUMO E3 ligases that SUMOylate DRP1 at eight sites (K532, K535, K558, K568, K594, K597, K606, and K608) in the VD86,87 (Figure 4). This modification increases the localization of DRP1 to the OMM and improves its stability, further increasing mitochondrial fragmentation.86 A role for the SUMOylated DRP1 in stabilizing ER-mitochondria contact sites in response to BAX-BAK-initiated apoptosis and cell death was reported.88,89 On the other hand, SUMO groups are removed by sentrin-specific proteases (SENP5).90 SENP5 catalyzes the removal of SUMO1–SUMO3 groups, rescues the fragmented mitochondrial morphology, and reduces cellular ROS accumulation. However, another deSUMOylating enzyme SENP3 promotes mitochondrial fission.91,92 Removal of SUMO groups by SENP3 resulted in enhanced binding of DRP1 to MFF, subsequently resulting in sustained fission and cytochrome c release followed by apoptosis.91−94

S-Nitrosylation

Nitric oxide (NO), which primarily works as a signaling molecule in a cell, causes damage and cell death when present or generated in excess.95 Downstream of this excess NO production lies increased mitochondrial fission caused by S-nitrosylation of DRP1.96 This PTM happens when an NO group attaches to the cysteine at the 644 position of DRP1 and increases its activity, causing excessive fission, followed by cell death97 (Figure 4).

Crosstalk between DRP1 and Cancer

DRP1: SNPs and Mutations

The gene encoding DRP1 is present on chromosome 12. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are reported in the DRP1 alleles and are linked to defects that may be lethal (single nucleotide polymorphism database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp)). A recent study identified rs879255685 and rs879255689, resulting in DRP1 missense variants G362D and G379K, in lung cancer patient cells.98 These mutations were earlier reported to be associated with developmental delay, refractory epilepsy, and altered function of peroxisomes and mitochondria. Among the two, DRP1 rs879255689 was reported to be associated with an increased risk of developing lung cancer.98

DRP1: Association with the Various Metabolic Pathways Leading to Cancer

In accordance with the diverse nature of cancers, the tumor cells exploit various cellular circuits or pathways to keep themselves alive and dividing. In this section, we aim to summarize the association of DRP1 with various metabolic pathways altered in cancer.

PI3K–Akt Pathway

An interesting inter-regulation between the phosphoinositide-3-kinase (PI3K)–protein kinase B (PKB/Akt) pathway and DRP1 has been reported99,100 (Figure 5A). The PI3K–PKB/Akt pathway is a vital cascade involved in the metabolism, growth, and proliferation of cells.101 Cancer cells exploit this pathway for metastasis and to ensure their survival.102 Regulation of mitochondrial fission via Akt-dependent phosphorylation of DRP1 at S616 has been reported.99 Phosphorylation results in increased translocation of DRP1 to the OMM, thereby increasing fission. An increase in mitochondrial fission subsequently acts as a pro-tumorigenic signal, whereas in noncancerous cells it leads to apoptosis.99 A role for Akt in regulating DRP1 activity was also confirmed when Akt inhibitor CB-124005 was used in neuroblastoma cells. Reduced mitochondrial fission was observed in these cells.99

Activation of DRP1 contributes to increased mitochondrial fission, a characteristic of cancer cells.

On the other hand, DRP1-dependent regulation of PI3K - Akt has also been recently reported.100 Zhao and colleagues showed that the growth and proliferation of hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HCC) are reduced by aerobic exercise. The increased oxygen consumption resulted in reduced DRP1 levels, which resulted in increased network-like mitochondrial morphology. Interestingly, the levels of phosphorylated forms of PI3K and Akt were reported to be reduced in these cells. However, in DRP1 knockout cells, the phosphorylated PI3K and Akt levels remain unchanged, suggesting the influence of DRP1 activation on these proteins and further downstream effects.100

One target of the PI3K–Akt pathway is the salt inducible kinase 2 (SIK2), which is a serine/threonine kinase that plays a role in the metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells. A role for SIK2 in the regulation of the transcription factors SREBP1c and SREBP2 required for fatty acid synthesis in ovarian cancer cells (OCC) was reported.103 It was also reported that SIK2 promotes the Warburg effect by metabolic reprogramming. This, in turn, enhances mitochondrial fission by modulating the function of DRP1 via phosphorylation at S616.104

MAPK/ERK1/2 Pathway

The mitogen-activated protein kinase is a serine/threonine kinase signaling cascade that plays a role in cellular proliferation and survival, and increased activity of this pathway is observed in cancer cells.105 The extracellular-signal regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) pathway is one such MAPK cascade that involves a series of kinases.106 This includes Ras-Raf-MEK (MAPK), which ultimately phosphorylates and activates ERK1/2.107 In turn, ERK1- and ERK2-dependent phosphorylation of DRP1 at S616 has been reported in cancer cells44,108 (Figure 5B). Hence, the inhibition of the MAPK signaling results in inactivation of DRP1.44 ERK-mediated phosphorylation of DRP1 was reported in several cancer cell lines such as T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia cells, pancreatic tumor cells, and breast cancer cells.25,109,110 Activation of MAPK signaling was reported to induce mitochondrial fragmentation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells (PDAC), and on the other hand, knockdown of DRP1 was reported to inhibit PDAC tumor growth.110 A recent study demonstrated that G-protein coupled estrogen receptor activation induced MAPK-mediated DRP1 activation through S616 phosphorylation and thereby increased mitochondrial fission in breast cancer cells (BRC).111 Interestingly, an antiapoptotic effect of ERK1/2 mediated DRP1 phosphorylation was also reported in neuroblastoma cells. Reduced expression of ERK1/2–DRP1 was observed upon increased CDK5 expression in cells exhibiting apoptosis. Upon inhibition of CDK5, restored cell viability and restored levels of ERK1/2 and activated DRP1 were reported.112

mTOR Pathway

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a serine/threonine kinase that is an integral component of numerous signaling pathways and controls many cellular processes, such as cell growth, proliferation, and apoptosis, whose dysregulation leads to cancer.113 The kinase is a part of two distinct protein complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2. mTORC1 integrates signals depending on diverse sources, nutrient availability, and other factors to control cellular growth and metabolism. mTORC2, on the other hand, also receives diverse signals but translates them into pro-survival or cell proliferative outputs.114 mTOR interacts with several pathways such as PI3K/AKT, AMPK, tuberous sclerosis complex subunit 1 (TSC1)/TSC2/Rheb, etc.115 Among these, pathways such as PI3K/Akt and Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK (MAPK) are overexpressed in cancer cells and are also involved in the activation of mTOR.116,117

Interestingly, a role for mTORC1 in regulating mitochondrial metabolism and function has been reported.116 Cancer cells face harsh conditions such as hypoxia or nutrient depletion in the tumor microenvironment at the time of tumor initiation and progression. To survive in these severe conditions, cancer cells need to sense and respond to the hostile extracellular environment, including nutrient availability. One such nutrient sensor responsible for maintaining homeostasis with the extracellular environment is the mTORC1, which drives ATP-consuming cellular processes (anabolic) necessary for cellular proliferation and growth.116 mTOR is reported to be upregulated in glioblastoma, and upon treatment of these cells with rapamycin, the inhibitor of mTOR, reduced survival of the glioblastoma was reported. Interestingly, the authors report a significant reduction in DRP1 levels after treatment of cells with rapamycin.118 mTORC1 selectively promotes the translation of mRNAs, which leads to increased mitochondrial fission.119 The translation of an IMM protein, mitochondrial fission process 1 (MTFP1), was also reported to be regulated by mTORC1. MTFP1 was proposed to stimulate the activity of DRP1 by increasing phosphorylation at S616, causing increased mitochondrial fragmentation.119 Zheng and colleagues reported a role for the mTOR–DRP1 axis in the evasion of immunosurveillance of the natural killer (NK) cells by cancer cells. Increased fragmentation of mitochondria was observed in the NK cells inside the tumor microenvironment. The authors report an activated AKT pathway and consecutively hyperactive mTOR signaling in NK cells. The hypoxic condition in NK cells resulted in an increase in the phosphorylation of DRP1 at S616 and decreased phosphorylation at S637. Inhibiting mTOR using rapamycin or inhibiting DRP1 by mitochondrial division inhibitor-1 (mdivi-1) or by knocking down DRP1, mitochondrial morphology was reverted to a more tubular phenotype and the cell viability and antitumor capacity of NK cells increased significantly.120

AMPK Pathway

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is an energy sensor that responds to the increased AMP accumulation during cellular stress and has a potentially critical role in determining the survival of cells under metabolic stress.121 Earlier studies have reported AMPK-induced apoptosis and autophagy in response to stress.121 DRP1 has been reported as a target of AMPK that, interestingly, can either be activated or downregulated depending on the cell type and physiology122 (Figure 5C). Several studies report the anticancer properties of natural compounds via regulating the AMPK pathway. Baicalein is a flavonoid that inhibits cell proliferation and increases apoptosis in lung cancer cells by modulating the AMPK pathway and thereby DRP1 activity.122 AMPK is activated by phosphorylation of T172, which further phosphorylates and activates DRP1122 (Figure 5C). On the contrary, mangiferin causes a decrease in cellular proliferation rate in neointimal hyperplasic cells by activating AMPK, which causes reduced S616 phosphorylated DRP1, ultimately resulting in decreased mitochondrial fission.123,124 Similarly, resveratrol, an anti-inflammatory compound used to relieve pain in bone cancers, also activates the AMPK pathway by the phosphorylation of T172. Interestingly, the treatment also resulted in reduced levels of S616 phosphorylated DRP1.125 Inhibition of AMPK with dorsomorphin or siRNA-mediated silencing resulted in increased levels of DRP1 in bone cancer cells.125

In brain tumor initiating cells (BTIC), increased activity of DRP1 was reported, and targeting DRP1 by RNAi decreases tumor formation capacity.126 On the other hand, AMPK activation decreased BTIC growth and induced caspase activation. Interestingly, it was reported that DRP1 inhibition resulted in increased phosphorylation of AMPKα and, thereby, upregulation of AMPK activation in these cells.126 Similar regulation of DRP1 by AMPK was also reported in prostate cancer cells.127 Earlier studies also proposed the regulation of DRP1 phosphorylation by AMPK in lung adenocarcinoma cells under hypoxic conditions. Silencing of AMPK resulted in reduced phosphorylation of DRP1 in these cells.128

In order to explain this two-headed fork where activation of an upstream sensor can have two contrasting effects on a downstream target, it can be hypothesized that AMPK acts as an energy sensor based on the current energetic state of the cell. When ATP is scarce, AMPK phosphorylates DRP1 at S616, causing an increase in mitochondrial fission, resulting in increased mitophagy. When fission occurs beyond a certain rate, the AMPK pathway is activated again and phosphorylates S637, resulting in the reduction of DRP1 activity, creating a pro-fusion scenario and producing ATP by natural means.123

Wnt/β-Catenin Pathway

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway plays a crucial role in the embryonic development and homeostasis of adult tissues.129 Dysregulation of the pathway is associated with many human diseases, including cancers such as colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and leukemia.130−132 β-Catenin, the downstream effector of Wnt, controls several transcription factors and regulates gene expression.133 Godoy and colleagues reported a role for the noncanonical Wnt ligand, Wnt-5a, in the regulation of mitochondrial dynamics by altering the S616 and S637 phosphorylation status of DRP1 in rat hippocampal neurons.134

Zhang and colleagues studied the relationship between the tumor suppressor protein LATS2 and DRP1-dependent mitochondrial structure–function maintenance in two HCC cell lines. The authors reported that LATS2 regulates DRP1 levels via the Wnt/β catenin pathway.135 Downregulation of Wnt using the inhibitor DKK1 resulted in increased levels of phosphorylated β-catenin and reduced levels of DRP1.135 Regulation of DRP1 by another tumor suppressor, Ste20-like kinase 1 (Mst1), via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck (SCCHN) was also reported.136 Mst1 overexpression resulted in reduced levels of phosphorylated β-catenin and upregulation of DRP1. Loss of the tumor suppressor PTEN in certain melanomas led to the loss of signaling of the Wnt/β catenin pathway. This further resulted in altered mitochondrial morphology due to reduced levels of MFN1 that created an imbalance between fusion–fission proteins, including DRP1.137 The tumor suppressor secreted frizzled-related protein 2 (SFRP2) regulates the development and metastasis of nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Overexpression of SFRP2 promotes apoptosis in cancer cells via mitochondrial dysfunction. The authors suggest that the observed mitochondrial dysfunction in these cells is due to increased mitochondrial fission mediated by DRP1.138 Wnt signaling-dependent activation of mitochondrial fission was also reported using specific inhibitors of the pathway.138

Cellular metabolism is crucial for tumor growth and metastasis. Asparagine and glutamate availability can regulate the tumor growth and development. A recent study demonstrated that asparagine synthetase (ASNS), an endogenous enzyme responsible for asparagine production, was higher in NSCLC tissue than in healthy lung tissue.139 Overexpression of ASNS influenced lung cancer metastasis by stimulating the Wnt pathway and downregulating DRP1.139 This downregulation of DRP1 was due to increased S637 phosphorylation and reduced S616 phosphorylation, resulting in loss of mitochondrial fission (Figure 5E,G). Fatty acid mediated upregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway via β-catenin acetylation and activation of DRP1 has been reported. Knockdown of DRP1 resulted in disruption of the utilization of fatty acids and thereby reduced acetylation of β-catenin and subsequently resulted in the inactivation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.140

ROCK1/RhoA Pathway

The Ras homologous protein (Rho) is a family of small monomeric GTPases that are involved in cytoskeletal functions and responsible for cell proliferation and invasion in various cancers.141 Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase 1 (ROCK1) is an important downstream effector molecule of Rho GTPases that phosphorylates various cytoskeletal components.141,142 Interestingly, two mechanisms of ROCK1-mediated activation of DRP1 have been reported. Shi and colleagues reported ROCK1-dependent phosphorylation of DRP1 at S616143 (Figure 5D). ROCK1 targets various phosphatases that dephosphorylate DRP1 at S637, and such a role of ROCK1 in the activation of calcineurin, which dephosphorylates DRP1 at S637, was also reported144,145 (Figure 5F). Both phosphorylation of DRP1 at S616 and dephosphorylation at S637 result in the activation of DRP1. Dephosphorylation of DRP1 at S637 mediated by ROCK1 was reported to aid in its translocation to the OMM and result in enhanced mitochondrial fission. This mechanism was observed when cancer cells were treated with arnidiol, a compound known for its apoptotic activities.144 Similar regulation of DRP1 by ROCK1 in Parkinson’s disease that led to the apoptosis of the dopaminergic neurons by excessive mitochondrial fragmentation and cytochrome c release was also reported.145

On the contrary, Yin and colleagues report DRP1 as a key molecule in modulating the proliferation and invasion of cancer in glioma cells. DRP1 was significantly overexpressed in glioma cells. However, upon silencing DRP1, reduced levels of RhoA/ROCK1 were reported, suggesting a direct effect of DRP1 on RhoA/ROCK1.146 As a result, remodeling of F-actin structures leading to reduced invasive and proliferative properties of cells was observed, suggesting that DRP1 may control the activity of RhoA.146

INK4 Pathway

The INK4 family comprises the inhibitors of cyclin-dependent kinases and has four members: p15Ink4a, p16Ink4b, p18Ink4c, and p19Ink4d.147 Distinct expression and control mechanisms of the different members of the family are reported. p16Ink4a is expressed in tissues when oncogenes such as Ras are present or during natural senescence of the cells and in the presence of retinoblastoma susceptibility protein (Rb or p53).148,149 Impairment in the function of INK4 proteins renders an organism more prone to tumor formation and development.150

Taguchi and colleagues showed that cyclin-dependent kinases phosphorylate DRP1 at S616.48 In HeLa cells, during the G1/S phase, mitochondria appear hyperfused due to proteasomal degradation of DRP1 by E3-ubiquitin ligase anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), causing an accumulation of cyclin E in this process. The increased load of cyclin E binds to CDK2, which subsequently inhibits APC/C and thereby restores the cytosolic DRP1 population. In the G2/M phase, the aurora A kinase phosphorylates CDK1/5, which subsequently phosphorylates DRP1 and activates it, resulting in the fragmentation of the mitochondria. CDK4, also known to activate AMPK, further stimulates DRP1, causing mitochondrial fission.151 Cyclin-dependent kinases phosphorylate DRP1 to activate it and upregulate the fission of mitochondria due to increased demand during cell division.

Hippo–Yap Pathway

The hippo signaling pathway has a primary function in tissue proliferation and regeneration. Yes-associated protein (Yap) works downstream of the hippo pathway.152 Yap acts as an oncogenic transcriptional activator in many cancers, such as colorectal, lung, breast, and ovarian cancers, where it assists in the survival and migration of tumor cells.153

In colorectal cancer cells (CRC) the hippo–Yap pathway dephosphorylates c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), which further hampers phosphorylation of DRP1, resulting in its inactivation and blocking mitochondrial fission. Subsequently, the release of high-temperature requirement A2 (HtrA2)/Omi serine protease from the intermembrane space of the mitochondria to the cytoplasm is blocked. This protease cleaves and neutralizes antiapoptotic proteins in the cytosol.154 Apart from the above-mentioned role of Yap in regulating apoptosis, its role in invasiveness in malignant rectal cancer cells has also been reported.154 Upon silencing of Yap, HtrA2/Omi mediated phosphorylation of Cofilin in an unidentified manner, which results in depolymerization of F-actin and subsequently inhibits the lamellipodial-based migratory capacity of cells, was reported.154

KRAS Pathway

Mutations in KRAS are the leading cause of lung cancer.155 Upregulation of the S616 phosphorylated DRP1 in cells with the KRAS mutation has been reported.156 KRAS signals through the MAPK-driven activation of DRP1 and promotes tumor formation, growth, and metastasis of NSCLCs.156 A shift from the regular use of glucose as a primary carbon source to lactate was observed in NSCLCs with a KRAS mutation. This metabolic shift and proliferation were reported to be dependent on DRP1. Inhibition of DRP1 makes the tumor cells more reliant on glycolysis and induces ROS-mediated cell death.156

PDK Pathway

In cancer cells, pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK1) acts as a controlling signal for the metabolic reprogramming of the cells by maintaining the balance between glycolysis and OXPHOS.157 Inhibition of PDK1 promotes the shift of cancer cells from OXPHOS to glycolysis (Warburg effect). Upon PDK1 inhibition in CRCs, reduced phosphorylation of DRP1 at S616, resulting in a decrease in mitochondrial fission, was also reported. Hence, PDK1 is considered an important therapeutic target for modulating epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and regulating metastasis.157

Sirtuins and DRP1

Sirtuins are a family of NAD+-dependent deacetylases that play an important role in numerous cellular processes ranging from deacetylation of histones to cell survival and attenuation of apoptosis.158 This family consists of seven proteins in humans (SIRT1–SIRT7) and are present in different subcellular localizations.159 Sirtuins inhibit cancer cell progression by delaying the cell cycle in response to genomic instability and accumulation of acquired mutations.160 The expression of sirtuins was reported to be variable in different cancer types. For example, downregulation of SIRT1 was reported in colorectal cancer, whereas in breast and prostate cancers it was upregulated.161,162 Studies have reported a regulatory role for sirtuins in the expression and function of DRP1, and variable effects by different sirutins are reported. A role for SIRT1 in the deacetylation of DRP1 was recently reported.163 Reduced SIRT1 expression and increased activation of DRP1 were observed in the spinal cord of bone cancer pain (BCP) model rats.164 Increased mitochondrial fission mediated by DRP1 resulted in increased lipid content in human HCC cells.165 Interestingly, these cells exhibit altered fatty acid metabolism due to suppression of SIRT1.165

SIRT2, the primary cytoplasmic sirtuin, is critically downregulated during cellular reprogramming from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, a phenomenon commonly seen in active cancer cells. This is followed by excessive acetylation and activation of enzymes that promote the glycolytic pathway.166 Upon inhibition of SIRT2 in human dermal fibroblast cells, short and fragmented mitochondria were observed due to an increase in S616 phosphorylation and a reduction in S637 dephosphorylation of DRP1.167 SIRT2 downregulation resulted in hyperacetylated MEK1, further translating into a hyperphosphorylated and activated ERK1/2. A significant hyperacetylation of Akt1 upon SIRT2 knockdown was also reported. SIRT2 inhibition during metabolic reprogramming most likely results in activation of MEK and deactivation of the Akt pathway. SIRT2 inhibition during metabolic reprogramming most likely activates DRP1 through the activation of MEK and Akt, ultimately increasing mitochondrial fission.166 SIRT3, on the other hand, was reported to be downregulated in HCC.168,169

SIRT4, a mitochondrial sirtuin, was also reported to be severely downregulated in lung cancer cells.170 Upon overexpression of SIRT4 in these cells, proliferative and invasive tendencies decreased significantly, suggesting its protective role against carcinogenesis. It was suggested that SIRT4 acts as an antitumor regulator in NSCLC by regulating the expression of S616 phosphorylated DRP1. Reduced levels of active DRP1 were observed upon overexpression of SIRT4, leading to an interruption in DRP1–FIS1 interaction.170 This resulted in hampered mitochondrial fission and inhibition of the metastatic potential of these cells. SIRT5, another mitochondrial sirtuin, was also reported to negatively influence the levels of activated DRP1 in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs).171 In SIRT5 knockout MEFs, increased expression of active DRP1 that resulted in increased mitochondrial fission was reported. However, no direct interaction between SIRT5 and DRP1 has been reported to date in cancer cells. The complex interplay between sirtuins and DRP1 needs to be analyzed in detail in the future.

DRP1 Inhibition and Cancer

Over the past several years, targeted cancer therapies using pharmacological agents created a new dimension in cancer treatment. Targeted cancer therapies specifically interfere with tumorigenesis-associated molecular changes, which primarily involve unique proteins. DRP1 is one such protein that is upregulated in almost all types of tumorigenesis and, hence, can be a potential target for cancer treatment.172 Cassidy-Stone and colleagues first identified a selective, small molecule cell-permeable inhibitor of DRP1 known as mdivi-1through a chemical library screening.173 mdivi-1 is a quinazolinone derivative that selectively blocks DRP1 oligomerization and GTPase activity, subsequently inhibiting mitochondrial fission.126,173 Treatment with mdivi-1 in BTIC resulted in increased apoptosis and tumor growth arrest.126 Furthermore, the metastatic capacity of BRC was suppressed by mdivi-1 mediated DRP1 silencing.46 Studies on a xenograft model showed that mdivi-1 could reduce tumor size and induce cancer cell death.41,174 Reduced growth of HCC tumors in vitro upon treatment with mdivi-1 was also reported.175 Increased apoptosis of HCC cells most likely due to the inhibition of DRP1-mediated mitophagy in the presence of mdivi-1 was reported.175 However, studies reported that mdivi-1 can also inhibit complex I, induce ROS production, and suppress the oxygen consumption rate independent of DRP1.176 Due to this off-target mechanism of mdivi-1, researchers were interested in developing more potent and specific inhibitors of DRP1. Wu and colleagues used in silico screening to identify an ellipticine compound, Drpitor1, as a potent DRP1 inhibitor.177 Drpitor1, along with its congener Drpitor1a, was shown to specifically inhibit DRP1 GTPase activity that ultimately suppressed proliferation and induced apoptosis in five different cancer cell lines (MCF7, SK-MES-1, SW-900, SK-LU-1, and A549).177 Another small molecule inhibitor, called MLN4924, was reported to successfully block mitochondrial translocation of DRP1, which affected the metabolism of BRC cell lines.178 In addition, silibinin showed antitumor activity through a reduction in DRP1 expression. This led to induction of apoptosis and inhibition of migration and invasion in BRC cell lines.179

Conclusion and Future Perspective

Mitochondria are the primary metabolic hubs of most cell types. Their role in several cellular signaling pathways and apoptosis highlights their contribution to cancer incidence and progression. Mitochondrial function is influenced by their shape and morphology, and in turn, the morphology is dependent on the balance between two important events: fission and fusion. The fission of mitochondria is controlled by the master regulator DRP1 and its interacting partners. DRP1 is emerging as an attractive candidate in the context of mitochondrial cancer therapy for various reasons, such as its role in cancer-associated hallmarks such as cell proliferation and apoptosis, increased expression in various types of cancer, associated with the increased metastatic potential of cancer cells, linked to cancer cell metabolism, and inhibition of the protein leading to the rescue of the phenotype. DRP1 has also been reported to be a transcriptional target of p53 and regulated by various oncogenic signaling pathways, linking mitochondrial fission and cancer. A close interdependence between the altered signaling pathways and energy metabolism of cancer cells has also been proposed, and interestingly, DRP1 seems to be connected to both these aspects. Pharmacological inhibition of DRP1 has also been reported to reduce tumorigenesis, migration, survival, and increased apoptosis in various cancer cell lines and models. Future research in this direction needs to evaluate the safety and efficacy of these inhibitors in clinical settings and also synthesize and discover new inhibitors with improved specificity and selectivity. This review provides an overview of the multiple ways by which DRP1 is involved in cancer and spotlights the important roles that could be independent of its principal role in mitochondrial division.
