Abstract
In this contribution, we consider MUltiple SIgnal Classification (MUSIC)-type algorithm for a non-iterative microwave imaging of small and arbitrary shaped extended anomalies located in a homogeneous media from scattering matrix whose elements are scattering parameters measured at dipole antennas. In order to explain the feasibility of MUSIC in microwave imaging, we investigate mathematical structure of MUSIC by establishing a relationship with an infinite series of Bessel function of integer order and antennas setting. This is based on the representation formula of scattering parameters in the presence of small anomalies and the application of Born approximation. Simulation results using real-data at f = 925 f=925 MHz of angular frequency are exhibited to show the feasibility of designed algorithm and to support investigated structure of imaging function.

Following the traditional results, most of non-iterative imaging algorithms were established under some strong assumptions that every elements of so-called multi-static response (MSR) or scattering matrices can be handled, the total number of dipole antennas (in general, transducers and receivers) are sufficiently large enough, and measurement data is not affected by the existence of neighboring antennas. Unfortunately, in many cases, manufacturing microwave systems that can measure scattered field data with the transducer and receiver at the same location (diagonal elements) is inconvenient. Hence, designing an alternative imaging algorithm without consideration of diagonal elements of MSR or scattering matrices is an important and interesting direction for real-world application.

In this research, we apply MUSIC as a non-iterative microwave imaging algorithm when the diagonal elements of the scattering matrix are unknown. In order to show the feasibility of MUSIC in real-world microwave imaging, we carefully explore mathematical structure of imaging function by establishing a relationship with an infinite series of Bessel function of integer order, the total number and location of dipole antennas, and the applied frequency. This is based on the Born approximation in the existence of a small anomaly
[
48

and the physical factorization of the scattering matrix in the presence of an extended anomaly
[

In order to support our theoretical results, simulation results for small and various extended anomalies through the real-data generated by commercial tool and manufactured microwave machine are exhibited.

This research is organized as follows: In Section

Finally, let us emphasize that obtained imaging results via MUSIC seems good but does not guarantee the complete shape of anomalies. Thus, one must apply iteration scheme to retrieve more accurate shape of anomaly (if there remains a discrepancy between the measurement and computed data) once an appropriate cost functional is chosen.

2.1
Forward Problem and Scattering Parameter

In this section, we briefly introduce the scattering parameter and MUSIC algorithm for imaging unknown anomalies from scattering matrix. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that there exists an anomaly
\Gamma
with smooth boundary
\partial\Gamma\in C^{2}
. Throughout this paper, we assume that
\Gamma
is a small anomaly located at
\mathbf{r}_{\star}
and surrounded by the circular array of dipole antennas located at
\mathbf{a}_{n}
,
n=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{N}

\omega=2\pi f
, where
f=c/\lambda
is fixed frequency in microwave band,
c
is the speed of light, and
\lambda
is the wavelength. Hence, we set the value of magnetic permeability of
\Gamma
and background are same and constant at every location
\mathbf{r}
, i.e., we let
\mu(\mathbf{r})=\mu_{\mathrm{b}}=4\pi\times${10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{H}$

\varepsilon_{\star}=\varepsilon_{\rstar}\cdot\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}=\varepsilon_{\rback}\cdot\varepsilon_{0}
as the permittivity of
\Gamma
and the background, respectively and assume that assume that
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\gg\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}/\omega
. Here,
\varepsilon_{0}=$8.854\text{\times}{10}^{-12}\text{\,}\mathrm{F}$
is the vacuum permittivity. Conductivities
\sigma_{\star}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
can be defined analogously. With this, we define the following piecewise permittivity and conductivity as
\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})
and
\sigma(\mathbf{r})

\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varepsilon_{\star}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in\Gamma,\\
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Gamma},\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma(\mathbf{r})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sigma_{\star}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in\Gamma,\\
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Gamma},\end{array}\right.

With this, we introduce the background wavenumber
k
as

k^{2}=\omega^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{b}}\left(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}+i\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega}\right).

Let
\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times 3}
be the incident electric field in a homogeneous medium due to the point current density
\mathbf{J}
at
\mathbf{a}_{n}
. Then, based on the Maxwell’s equation,
\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})
satisfies

\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})=-i\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}{\mathbf{H}_{\inc}}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla\times{\mathbf{H}_{\inc}}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})=(\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}+i\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}})\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r}),

where
{\mathbf{H}_{\inc}}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times 3}
denotes the magnetic field. Analogously, let
\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times 3}
be the total electric field in the existence of
\Gamma
. Then,
\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{n})
satisfies

\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{n})=-i\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}{\mathbf{H}_{\tot}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{n})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla\times{\mathbf{H}_{\tot}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{n})=(\sigma(\mathbf{r})+i\omega\varepsilon(\mathbf{r}))\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{n})

with the transmission condition on
\partial\Gamma
.

Let
\mathrm{S}(m,n)
be the scattering parameter (or
S-
parameter), which is the measurement data through the microwave machine that is defined as

\mathrm{S}(m,n):=\frac{\mathrm{V}_{m}^{-}}{\mathrm{V}_{n}^{+}},

where

\mathrm{V}_{m}^{-}
and
\mathrm{V}_{n}^{+}
denote the output voltage (or reflected wave) at the
m-
th antenna and the input voltage (or incident wave) at
n-
th antenna, respectively. We let
\mathrm{S}_{\tot}(m,n)
and
\mathrm{S}_{\inc}(m,n)
be measured scattering parameters with and without
\Gamma
, respectively. Then, the scattered field
S-
parameter
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(m,n):=\mathrm{S}_{\tot}(m,n)-\mathrm{S}_{\inc}(m,n)
, which is the measurement data in this research

2.2
Introduction to MUSIC Algorithm

Now, we introduce traditional MUSIC algorithm. For a detail, we refer to
[
44
]
. Generally, one assumed that the following scattering matrix can be available

\mathbb{K}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,2)&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,\mathrm{N}-1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,\mathrm{N})\\
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,2)&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,\mathrm{N}-1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,\mathrm{N})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},2)&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{N}-1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{N})\end{array}\right].

(2)

This means that the entries of
\mathbb{K}
are obtained while the other ports are matched. The main purpose of MUSIC is to find locations of
\mathbf{r}_{\star}\in\Gamma
from
\mathbb{K}
without any
a priori
information of
\Gamma
. Based on the representation (
1
), we need the value of total field
\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{m})
at every
\mathbf{r}
. Unfortunately, direct calculation of total field is impossible unless we have complete information of
\Gamma
. Thus, to design the imaging algorithm of MUSIC, we take the next two steps to consider an alternative representation of (
1
).

Remark 2.1

(Linearization)
.

Now, let us assume that

\left(\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{\star}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}}-1\right)r<\frac{\lambda}{4}.

(3)

Then, based on
[

\Gamma
can be regarded as a small anomaly so that by applying the Born approximation

]
and reciprocity property of incident field, (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{S}_{\scat}(m,n)&=\frac{ik^{2}}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r})-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{m})d\mathbf{r}\\
&\approx\frac{ik^{2}}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r})-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{m},\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}.\end{aligned}

(4)

Remark 2.2

(2D approximation)

Notice that the height of microwave machine can be said to be long enough (see

\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{m})

\Gamma

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{S}_{\scat}(m,n)&\approx\frac{ik^{2}}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r})-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{m},\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}\\
&\approx\frac{ik^{2}\area(\Gamma)}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{\star}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma_{\star}-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r}_{\star})\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{m},\mathbf{r}_{\star}).\end{aligned}

(5)

Here,
\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})
is given by

\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})=-\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r}|),

where

denotes the Hankel function of order zero of the first kind (see

58

Based on the representation (

\mathbb{K}
can be determined from the span of

\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r}_{\star})=\bigg[\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{\star}),\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{\star}),\cdots,\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{N}},\mathbf{r}_{\star})\bigg]^{\mathtt{T}}

(6)

corresponding to
\Gamma
. To introduce the MUSIC algorithm, we apply singular value decomposition (SVD) to
\mathbb{K}
, as follows:

\mathbb{K}=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\tau_{n}\mathbf{U}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{*}\approx\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\tau_{n}\mathbf{U}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{*},

where
\tau_{n}
are the singular values that satisfy

\tau_{1}\geq\tau_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\tau_{\mathrm{M}}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{n}\approx 0\quad\text{for}\quad n=\mathrm{M}+1,\mathrm{M}+2,\cdots,\mathrm{N},

and
\mathbf{U}_{n}
and
\mathbf{V}_{n}
are respectively the left- and right-singular vectors of

\mathrm{M}

\left\{\mathbf{U}_{1},\mathbf{U}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{M}}\right\}
provide an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{K}
. With this, we introduce a projection operator onto the noise subspace as

\mathbb{P}_{\noise}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{N}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\mathbf{U}_{n}\mathbf{U}_{n}^{*},

(7)

where
\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{N}}
denotes
\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{N}
identity matrix. Now, let us consider the normalized vector
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r})/|\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r})|
, where
\mathbf{W}
is given by (
6
). Then, based on
[
48
,
60
]
, the following statement holds:

{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r})\in\mbox{Range}(\mathbb{K})\quad\mbox{if and only if}\quad\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_{\star}\in\Gamma.}

This means that,
|\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}))|=0
when
\mathbf{r}\in\Gamma
. Hence, the location of
\Gamma
can be identified by plotting the traditional MUSIC-type imaging function

\mathfrak{F}_{\tm}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}))|},\quad\mathbf{r}\in\Omega.

(8)

The plot of
\mathfrak{F}_{\tm}(\mathbf{r})
is expected to exhibit peaks of large magnitude (in theory
+\infty
) at
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_{\star}\in\Gamma
.

Remark 2.3

(Total number of nonzero singular values)
.

Theoretically, total number of nonzero singular values

of
\mathbb{K}
must be equal to the total number of anomalies

if there exists a small anomaly,

. However, in real-world application,

(Extended anomaly)
.

If the condition (
3

2.3
MUSIC Algorithm Without Diagonal Elements of the Scattering Matrix

Unlike the conventional researches, we cannot measure
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(n,n)
for
n=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{N}
, because each of the
\mathrm{N}
antennas is used for signal transmission and the remaining
\mathrm{N}-1
antennas are used for signal reception (see
[
18
]
for instance) so that in real-world problem, obtained scattering matrix is given by

\mathbb{S}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mbox{unknown}&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,2)&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,\mathrm{N}-1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,\mathrm{N})\\
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,1)&\mbox{unknown}&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,\mathrm{N}-1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,\mathrm{N})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},2)&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{N}-1)&\mbox{unknown}\end{array}\right].

(10)

In this case, it is impossible to determine the range of scattering matrix (
10
) so that we cannot apply traditional MUSIC algorithm for imaging
\Gamma
. Motivated by
[
57
]
, we simply set
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(n,n)=0
for all
n
in the scattering matrix (
10
) and apply MUSIC algorithm, i.e., we consider the following matrix:

\mathbb{D}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,2)&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,\mathrm{N}-1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(1,\mathrm{N})\\
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,1)&0&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,\mathrm{N}-1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(2,\mathrm{N})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},1)&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},2)&\cdots&\mathrm{S}_{\scat}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{N}-1)&0\end{array}\right].

(11)

In this case, determination of the range of (
11
) is still impossible. Nevertheless, we can identify an outline shape of
\Gamma
through the MUSIC algorithm: we apply singular value decomposition (SVD) to
\mathbb{D}
, as follows:

\mathbb{D}=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\tau_{n}\mathbf{U}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{*}\approx\tau_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{V}_{1}^{*}

because there is no multiple scattering effect (see
[
57
]
for instance). With this, we introduce a projection operator onto the noise subspace as

\mathbb{P}_{\noise}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{N}}-\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}^{*},

and corresponding MUSIC-type imaging function without diagonal elements of
\mathbb{D}

\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}))|}.

(12)

Then, it is possible to identify the outline shape of
\Gamma
through the map of
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
. Theoretical reason of applicability including unique determination is discussed in the next section.

3
Analysis of the Imaging Function

Here, we carefully derive mathematical structure of
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
by establishing a relationship with an infinite series of Bessel function of integer order and examine intrinsic properties of MUSIC. For this purpose, we carefully explore mathematical structure of
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})

Theorem 3.1

(Structure of imaging function for single anomaly)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\mathbf{a}_{n}/|\mathbf{a}_{n}|=\mathbf{a}_{n}/R=[\cos\theta_{n},\sin\theta_{n}]^{\mathtt{T}}
and
\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{\star}=|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{\star}|[\cos\phi_{\star},\sin\phi_{\star}]^{\mathtt{T}}
. If
\mathbf{a}_{n}
satisfies
|\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r}|\gg 1/4|k|
for
n=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{N}
,
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
can be represented as follows:

\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})\approx\frac{\mathrm{N}-1}{\mathrm{N}}\left|1-J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)^{2}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star}))\right|^{-1/2},

(13)

where
J_{\nu}
denotes the Bessel function of integer order
\nu
of the first kind and
\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star}))
is the real-part of residual term
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star})=O(1)
, which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star})=

\displaystyle 2J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}i^{\nu}J_{\nu}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\mathrm{e}^{i\nu(\theta_{n}-\phi_{\star})}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}i^{\nu}J_{\nu}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\mathrm{e}^{i\nu(\theta_{n}-\phi_{\star})}\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}(-i)^{\nu}J_{\nu}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\mathrm{e}^{-i\nu(\theta_{n}-\phi_{\star})}\right).

Here,
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integer number and
\mathbb{Z}^{*}=\mathbb{Z}\cup\left\{+\infty,-\infty\right\}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
. Furthermore, for all
n=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{N}
,

\lim_{\mathbf{r}\to\mathbf{a}_{n}}\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})=0.

Based on the identified structure (
13
), we can examine several properties of the imaging function
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
.

Remark 3.1

(Applicability)
.

Since
J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)=1
and
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star})=0
when
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_{\star}\in\Gamma
,
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
will have it maximum at
\mathbf{r}_{\star}\in\Gamma
. Thus, it will be possible to identify location of
\Gamma
via the map of
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
.

Based on the Remark
3.1
, we can obtain the following result of unique determination.

Corollary 3.2

(Unique determination of anomaly)
.

For given angular frequency
\omega
, the location of anomaly can be identified uniquely through the map of
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
.

Remark 3.2

(Appearance of unexpected artifacts and resolution)
.

If
\mathbf{r}\neq\mathbf{r}_{\star}
, the terms
J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)
and
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star})
are the main causative agents of generating unexpected artifacts. The resolution of imaging result is significantly depending on the value of applied frequency
\omega
. If
\omega
is sufficiently large, i.e., if one applies high frequency, one can obtain a good imaging result with high resolution but several artifacts will be contained in the imaging result also. In contrast, if one applies low frequency, appearance of artifacts can be reduced but the resolution will be poor.

Remark 3.3

(Ideal condition for a better imaging performance)
.

Notice that if
N\longrightarrow+\infty
then,
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star})/\mathrm{N}\longrightarrow 0
and correspondingly,
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})\longrightarrow+\infty
because
|\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r}))|\longrightarrow 0
. This means that increasing total number of antennas guarantees the better imaging performance. Unfortunately, increasing total number of antennas is difficult problem due to the mechanical circumstance. If
\omega\longrightarrow+\infty
, one will obtain good result but application of infinite-valued frequency is an ideal condition.

Remark 3.4

(Experimental configuration for a better imaging performance)
.

Based on the structure of imaging function (
13
), it is easy to see that the term
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star})
obstructs imaging performance. Hence, eliminating this term will guarantee good result. Notice that we cannot increase
\mathrm{N}
and control
J_{\nu}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)=0
for all
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega
. This means that we must focus on the elimination of
\mathrm{e}^{i\nu(\theta_{n}-\phi_{\star})}
and
\mathrm{e}^{-i\nu(\theta_{n}-\phi_{\star})}
. Since we have no information of location of target, the value of
\phi_{\star}
is still unknown. Fortunately, based on the following elementary conditions of trigonometric functions

\cos(\theta+\pi)=-\cos\theta\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sin(\theta+\pi)=-\sin\theta,

we can examine that if there exists two antennas located at
\mathbf{a}_{1}=|R|[\cos\theta_{1},\sin\theta_{1}]^{\mathtt{T}}
and
\mathbf{a}_{2}=|R|[\cos\theta_{2},\sin\theta_{2}]^{\mathtt{T}}=|R|[\cos(\theta_{1}+\pi),\sin(\theta_{1}+\pi)]^{\mathtt{T}}=-\mathbf{a}_{1}
then

\sum_{n=1}^{2}\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}i^{\nu}J_{\nu}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\mathrm{e}^{i\nu(\theta_{n}-\phi_{\star})}=0.

This means that to reduce the effect of
\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\star}))
for obtaining a good result, the total number
\mathrm{N}
of the antennas must be even and they must be distributed uniformly on the circle of radius
R
and symmetry from each others.

The mathematical setting and analysis could be extended straightforwardly to multiple anomalies. The derivation is not provided herein; only theoretical result is shown as follows. Of course, unique determination is guaranteed for multiple anomalies.

Corollary 3.3

(Structure of imaging function for multiple anomalies)
.

Assume that there exists multiple anomalies
\Gamma_{s}
with location
\mathbf{r}_{s}
,
s=1,2,\cdots,S
. Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\mathbf{a}_{n}/|\mathbf{a}_{n}|=\mathbf{a}_{n}/R=[\cos\theta_{n},\sin\theta_{n}]^{\mathtt{T}}
and
\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{s}=|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{s}|[\cos\phi_{s},\sin\phi_{s}]^{\mathtt{T}}
. If
\mathbf{a}_{n}
satisfies
|\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r}|\gg 1/4|k|
for
n=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{N}
,
\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})
can be represented as follows: if
\mathbf{r}
is far from
\mathbf{a}_{n}
,

\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})\approx\frac{\mathrm{N}-1}{\mathrm{N}}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(1-J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{s}-\mathbf{r}|)^{2}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{s}))\right)\right|^{-1/2},

(14)

where
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{s})
is introduced in Theorem
3.1
. Furthermore, for all
n=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{N}
,

\lim_{\mathbf{r}\to\mathbf{a}_{n}}\mathfrak{F}_{\dm}(\mathbf{r})=0.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this research, we applied MUSIC algorithm for a non-iterative microwave imaging of unknown anomalies when the diagonal elements of scattering matrix are unknown. To show the feasibility of the MUSIC in real-world application, we explored mathematical structure of imaging function by finding a relationship with infinite series of Bessel function of integer order. Based on explored structures, we confirmed that the shapes of small anomalies can be imaged uniquely and successfully via the MUSIC. Hence, it can be regarded as a good microwave imaging technique.

Based on several works
[
35
,
36
,
62
,
63
]
, it has been shown that MUSIC is effective in a limited-aperture inverse scattering problem. Application of MUSIC in real-world limited-aperture microwave imaging will be the forthcoming work. Furthermore, as we seen, there still exists some limitations on the real-world applications. Improvement of MUSIC for obtaining better results in real-world microwave imaging will be an interesting research subject.

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{N}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}-1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\star}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\bigg)-\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\right\}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}-1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\star}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\bigg)-\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\right\}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\vdots\\
\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{N}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}-1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{N}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\star}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\bigg)-\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{N}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\right\}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{N}}}{(N-1)}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\star}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\bigg)\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\star}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\bigg)\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\vdots\\
\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{N}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{N}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\star}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\bigg)\end{array}\right]

and correspondingly, we arrive

|\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r}))|=\left|\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r}))\cdot\overline{\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r}))}\right|^{1/2}\approx\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{N}}}{\mathrm{N}-1}\left|\mathrm{N}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}(\Phi_{1}+\overline{\Phi}_{1})+\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi_{2}\overline{\Phi}_{2}\right|^{1/2},

where

\Phi_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r})}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\bigg)\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\star}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\bigg).

First, applying (
16
) again, we can see that

\mathrm{e}^{ik\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r})}=J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}i^{\nu}J_{\nu}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\mathrm{e}^{i\nu(\theta_{m}-\phi_{\star})}=J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{m}).

With this, performing an elementary calculus yields

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{m})\right)\left(\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{N}^{2}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\bigg)+\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\right).

Hence, we can obtain

\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}(\Phi_{1}+\overline{\Phi}_{1})=2\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)^{2}+4J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\text{Re}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\\
+\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\text{Re}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\right).

Next, by performing an elementary calculus,

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}\overline{\Phi}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\right)\left(\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{N}^{2}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\bigg(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\bigg)+\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\right).

Hence, we can derive

\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\Phi_{2}\overline{\Phi}_{2}=\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)+2J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\text{Re}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})+\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\text{Re}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\right).

(18)

|\mathbb{P}_{\noise}(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r}))|=\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{N}}}{\mathrm{N}-1}\left|\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)^{2}+2J_{0}(k|\mathbf{r}_{\star}-\mathbf{r}|)\text{Re}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\right.\\
+\left.\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}\text{Re}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}_{\star},\mathbf{a}_{n})}\right)\right|^{1/2}
