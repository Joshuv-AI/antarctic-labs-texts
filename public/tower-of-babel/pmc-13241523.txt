Review of breast arterial calcifications for cardiovascular disease risk prediction
Authors: Brandy Ndirangu, Judy Gichoya, Theo Dapamede, Frank Li, Mohammadreza Chavoshi, Beatrice Brown-Mulry, Rohan Isaac, Aawez Mansuri, Chiratidzo Sanyika, Puja Mehta, W. Charles O’Neill, Hari Trivedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breast arterial calcification (BAC), frequently visible on screening mammography, is a potent biomarker for cardiovascular disease risk in women. Unlike atherosclerosis, BAC represents medial arterial calcification characterized by arterial stiffness rather than narrowing. BAC has been linked to myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure and may offer a scalable risk stratification tool through AI-driven quantification. Standardizing BAC measurement and reporting is essential to improving long-term clinical outcomes for women.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among women globally, surpassing all forms of cancer combined1,2. Established risk prediction models such as the Framingham Risk Score, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk estimator, and the Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT) calculator often underestimate risk in women, particularly those with atypical symptoms or nontraditional risk factors3–5. This gap underscores the need for improved approaches to cardiovascular risk assessment for women.

Breast arterial calcification (BAC), a common finding on routine mammography, has been studied as a potential biomarker for CVD risk stratification with an estimated prevalence of 10-13% in women undergoing screening mammography6,7. Unlike coronary artery calcification, which reflects atherosclerotic plaque burden, BAC represents medial arterial calcification (MAC)—a distinct vascular process with unique pathophysiology and clinical implications8. While MAC shares several risk factors with atherosclerosis9–12, it has been studied extensively as an independent prognosticator of CVD for more than two decades13,14, and as the most readily imaged and measurable form of medial calcification, it offers an opportunity to assess CVD risk using routine mammography without additional testing6,15–22.

Until recently, BAC was measured and quantified manually on mammograms23,24, limiting its applicability in large populations. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning algorithms, have enabled automated detection and quantification of BAC, allowing its study as a biomarker at scale25. If deployed into routine clinical care, BAC could provide an opportunity to screen women in the general population for cardiovascular risk without additional cost or radiation. This paper reviews the current evidence supporting BAC as a predictive tool for CVD risk. We first describe the biology and pathophysiology of medial arterial calcification, then examine clinical associations between BAC and cardiovascular outcomes. We contextualize BAC within the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) framework, review AI innovations enabling automated detection, and discuss barriers to clinical adoption. Overview of breast arterial calcifications in cardiovascular risk prediction.

Breast arterial calcification (BAC) is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) that is detectable on routine screening mammography. BAC is a type of medial arterial calcification that is present in 10–13% of women. It causes arterial stiffness and impaired perfusion, and is distinct pathophysiologically from atherosclerosis as seen in coronary artery disease. BAC is strongly associated with major adverse cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, heart failure, and death, as well as peripheral artery disease (PAD) and cardio-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome. Deep learning models can automatically segment and quantify BAC, providing standardized clinical tools to trigger notifications for patients and providers.

Biology and pathophysiology of medial arterial calcification (MAC) and BAC

Breast arterial calcification (BAC) is a form of medial arterial calcification (MAC) that occurs in the tunica media of arterial walls, distinct from the intimal calcification characteristic of atherosclerosis8. While atherosclerotic calcification develops within lipid-laden plaques and causes luminal narrowing, MAC involves calcium-phosphate deposition in the medial smooth muscle layer, leading to vascular stiffness and impaired arterial compliance rather than stenosis15,26.

The pathophysiology of MAC involves several interconnected mechanisms. Lanzer et al. identified key molecular drivers including phosphate dysregulation, oxidative stress, and inhibition of matrix Gla protein (MGP), a vitamin K-dependent calcification inhibitor15. Under pathological conditions, vascular smooth muscle cells acquire osteoblast-like characteristics and actively deposit hydroxyapatite crystite in the arterial media15,27.

MAC and atherosclerosis share several risk factors, including age, diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease10–12,15. However, MAC has distinct associations that differentiate it pathophysiologically. Diabetes mellitus is particularly strongly linked to MAC; Yahagi et al. demonstrated that diabetes promotes extensive medial calcification through mechanisms related to advanced glycation end-products and prolonged hyperglycemia28. The cumulative effect of glycemic exposure, often reflected by elevated HbA1c, appears to drive calcification progression, though direct clinical studies linking HbA1c to BAC specifically remain limited29,30.

The hemodynamic consequences of MAC also differ from atherosclerosis. Rather than causing ischemia through luminal obstruction, MAC increases arterial stiffness, elevates systolic blood pressure, widens pulse pressure, and impairs coronary perfusion during diastole15,26. This explains the strong association between BAC and hypertension observed in longitudinal studies, where hypertension emerged as the primary distinguishing cardiovascular risk factor in BAC-positive women over time31.

Calcification of arteries in the distal extremities, particularly below the knee, is almost exclusively medial in nature32. Consequently, BAC correlates strongly with peripheral arterial calcification, particularly in patients with ESRD33, and serves as a readily imageable surrogate for systemic MAC burden.

Clinical associations of BAC with cardiovascular outcomes

A growing body of evidence supports BAC as a robust, independent marker of cardiovascular risk in women16,34,35. Reported BAC prevalence ranges from 10% to 12% with earlier studies likely underestimating prevalence because of limitations in older imaging technology8. Multiple studies have demonstrated consistent associations between BAC and clinical outcomes, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality26. Some studies also indicate improved risk stratification when BAC is incorporated alongside established risk prediction tools20,36. A. Composite cardiovascular outcomes and mortality

Several large studies have established BAC as an independent predictor of adverse cardiovascular events. Iribarren et al. studied 5059 postmenopausal women aged 60–79 years and found that BAC presence was associated with a 51% higher hazard of incident ASCVD (heart attack, stroke, or cardiovascular death; HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.08–2.11) and a 23% higher hazard of incident global CVD (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.002–1.52) over a 6.5-year follow-up16.

In a 23-year retrospective cohort study, Galiano et al. demonstrated the durability of BAC as a risk marker31. BAC-positive women had significantly higher relative risks across multiple cardiovascular conditions: ischemic heart disease (RR 3.25), hypertensive heart disease (RR 2.85), valvular heart disease (RR 2.19), heart failure (RR 2.06), peripheral vascular disease (RR 2.80), atrial fibrillation (RR 1.83), and lacunar infarction (RR 2.23). BAC was independently associated with reduced cardiovascular event-free survival (HR 1.94) and markedly decreased cardiovascular-specific survival (HR 6.60), even after adjustment for traditional risk factors31. The predictive value of BAC remained strong throughout the 23-year follow-up, supporting its role as a long-term cardiovascular risk marker.

Rotter et al. similarly demonstrated that women with BAC had significantly higher occurrence of ASCVD events, including angina, previous myocardial infarction, abnormal angiography, stroke, and coronary artery bypass grafting (p < 0.001)37. BAC presence remained a strong independent predictor of ASCVD with an odds ratio of 2.29, even after accounting for coronary artery disease risk factors37.

Finally, in a historical cohort of 12,761 women aged 40–79 years, Iribarren et al. found that women with BAC had a 1.32-fold increased risk of coronary heart disease (CHD), a 1.41-fold increased risk of ischemic stroke, and a 1.52-fold increased risk of heart failure35.

B. Peripheral arterial disease

Several studies have demonstrated a significant association between BAC and peripheral arterial disease (PAD). Iribarren et al. found that BAC was linked to an ankle-brachial index (ABI) < 0.90, indicative of PAD, with an odds ratio of 1.37 (p = 0.04) after adjustment for cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors38. Abou-Hassan et al. reported that BAC presence was associated with markedly higher odds of PAD (OR 4.56, p < 0.0002), which persisted after accounting for other factors39. Additionally, Hendriks et al. found that severe BAC was associated with a higher hazard of PAD (HR 3.24, 95% CI 1.16–9.00; p = 0.025) in a model adjusted for traditional cardiovascular risk factors, physical activity, alcohol intake, reproductive history, and menopausal status40.

C. Association with coronary artery calcium

Many studies have demonstrated a relationship between BAC and coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores19,31,41. Saenger et al. reported that BAC was significantly more prevalent (91%) in patients with coronary artery calcification (p < 0.001), and that CAC severity increased with rising BAC scores, indicating that higher BAC grades were associated with greater coronary calcium burden41.

However, not all studies have found a strong correlation. Deeg et al. reported that in women aged ≤55 years, the absence of BAC was strongly associated with a very low prevalence of severe CAC (>300 AU)42. In women over 55, however, zero BAC did not reliably exclude severe CAC or obstructive coronary artery disease (>50% stenosis), which was present in up to 29.9% of symptomatic women over 65. Overall, BAC showed weak-to-moderate correlation with CAC and stenosis severity, and poor discriminatory power for identifying obstructive CAD42. These findings suggest that while BAC and CAC share some underlying risk factors, they represent distinct calcification processes with different clinical implications.

D. Association with Traditional Risk Factors

In addition to its prognostic value, BAC is closely tied to underlying risk factors for CVD. There is a significant association between BAC presence and major CAD risk factors, including older age, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes mellitus, and menopausal status (p < 0.001)37. Meta-analytic data identify strong determinants of BAC, including older age (pooled OR 2.98 per decade), diabetes (pooled OR 1.88), and parity (pooled OR 3.43)6.

In a primary care setting, Gennarelli et al. found that BAC was present in women who were significantly older (mean age 60 vs. 52, p < 0.001), had higher 10-year cardiac risk scores (11% vs. 6%; p < 0.001), higher rates of hypertension (65% vs. 40%; p < 0.001), and higher rates of coronary artery disease (10% vs. 2%; p = 0.0041)43.

Interestingly, the Galiano et al. study found that hypertension emerged as the main cardiovascular risk factor distinguishing women with and without BAC over time31. While hypertension prevalence was similar between groups at baseline (29.7% vs 24.2%, p = 0.398), a significantly higher proportion of women with BAC had hypertension at the end of follow-up compared with those without BAC (78.9% vs 67.2%, p = 0.049). In contrast, no significant between-group differences were observed for diabetes, dyslipidemia, or obesity.

Paradoxically, smoking has been associated with lower BAC prevalence in multiple studies (pooled OR 0.48) for unclear reasons6,44, whereas other studies found no association16. This inverse relationship remains unexplained and warrants further investigation.

Association between BAC and cardiovascular-metabolic-kidney (CKM) syndrome

The strong mechanistic links between medial arterial calcification, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (described in Section I) have clinical implications for understanding BAC within the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) framework. Multiple studies have demonstrated significant associations between BAC and these metabolic conditions, supporting BAC as a marker of systemic vascular health in high-risk populations39,45. This is likely because vascular calcification increases the risk of both CVD and CKD39,46.

BAC prevalence varies substantially by metabolic status. Cetin et al. found BAC was nearly four times more common in diabetic patients and three times more common in those with hypertension compared to non-diabetic/non-hypertensive groups47. BAC prevalence increased with age and was highest among patients with diabetes (25.4%), followed by those with hypertension (17.6%), and lowest in the non-diabetic/non-hypertensive group (7.3%)47.

In diabetic populations specifically, Selva et al. reported a 40% prevalence of BAC, with affected patients having longer diabetes duration and a higher burden of microvascular complications47. These findings suggest that cumulative glycemic exposure promotes BAC progression, consistent with studies correlating glycemic control with coronary artery calcification29,30.

The relationship between BAC and renal function follows a dose-response pattern. Manzoor et al. demonstrated that BAC progression rates increased stepwise across CKD severity: 3.9 mm/breast/year in controls, 3.3–4.2 mm/breast/year in mild-to-moderate CKD, and 8.1 mm/breast/year in those with eGFR <40 mL/min/1.73 m² 48. Patients with end-stage renal disease showed the highest progression at approximately 20 mm/breast/year48. Importantly, kidney transplantation halted MAC progression, returning rates to levels observed in control subjects (median 3.9 mm/breast/year; p = 0.006), suggesting that restoring metabolic homeostasis can arrest vascular calcification48. Finally, diabetes significantly augments BAC progression in the setting of CKD and ESRD, creating a synergistic effect on vascular calcification burden48. This interaction underscores the interconnected nature of CKM syndrome and positions BAC as a potential integrative marker reflecting cumulative metabolic and renal injury.

Artificial intelligence for BAC detection and quantification

To date, routine clinical use of BAC has been limited by the need for manual detection and quantification. More recently, AI-based methods for BAC detection and quantification offer the ability to measure BAC at scale and identify high-risk women through routine screening mammography.

A. Detection and segmentation models

Several deep learning architectures have demonstrated strong performance for BAC detection and segmentation on mammograms. (Table 2) Guo et al. introduced SCU-Net, a deep learning method designed for segmentation and quantification of BAC on mammograms49. They confirmed the model’s reliability in assessing BAC, achieving over 95% correlation with ground truth measurements from mammograms49. AlJabri et al. introduced a recurrent attention U-Net model, which achieved an accuracy of 99.89% in detecting BAC on synthesized 2D mammograms50. Similarly, Mobini et al. explored deep learning using a VGG16-based convolutional neural network (CNN), achieving an AUC-ROC of 0.94 in BAC detection and a strong correlation with manual BAC measurements (Spearman ρ = 0.88, p < 0.001)51. B. Extension to digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT)

While traditional digital mammography creates a 2D projection of the compressed breast, digital breast tomosynthesis uses an X-ray tube that moves in an arc to acquire low-dose images from multiple angles, which are reconstructed into thin slices53,54. As approximately half of screening mammograms in the country are now performed using DBT55,56, validating AI performance across both modalities is essential for widespread clinical deployment. Watanabe et al. conducted a retrospective analysis evaluating AI software for BAC detection on both digital mammography (DM) and digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT), achieving an AUC of 0.96 for DM and 0.95 for DBT, with high sensitivity and specificity57.

C. Clinical outcome validation

While technical performance metrics are important, the clinical utility of AI-based BAC detection depends on its association with meaningful patient outcomes. Two large-scale studies have directly linked automated BAC detection to cardiovascular events. Dapamede et al. published the first multi-site validated model for BAC area quantification, demonstrating in a cohort of 123,762 women that BAC added prognostic value to the PREVENT score in predicting MACE and mortality. Mild (BAC area >0–10 mm2), moderate (>10–25 mm2) and severe BAC (>25 mm2) showed clear dose-response in PREVENT adjusted models. Additionally, this study showed that the risk of MACE increased by 2–3% for each 1 mm2 increment in total BAC area58.

Allen et al. also demonstrated in a retrospective cohort of 18,092 women that there was a graded, dose-response association between BAC severity and adverse outcomes59. Over a median 6-year follow-up, mortality was significantly higher in women with BAC (7.8% vs. 2.3%), and the composite outcome occurred in 12.4% of women with BAC versus 4.3% without. Each 10-point increase in BAC score was linked to an 8% higher risk of adverse outcomes59. These findings validate that AI-quantified BAC severity has meaningful prognostic value for cardiovascular risk stratification.

Reporting practices, patient perspectives, and standardization needs

Despite evidence supporting BAC as a cardiovascular risk marker, its clinical utility has been hindered by inconsistent reporting practices and the absence of standardized guidelines6,16,60.

A. Current reporting practices

Brown et al. surveyed 598 radiologists and found that while 87% include BAC findings in at least some mammogram reports, only 41% consistently report BAC presence61. Factors influencing BAC reporting included years of practice (OR 1.10, 95% CI, 1.0–1.20; p = 0.023) and fellowship training (OR 0.63, 95% CI, 0.42–0.94; p = 0.024) with more experienced radiologists more likely to report BAC61.

Among those who report BAC, reporting style varied considerably. Sixty-nine percent of respondents simply indicate presence, 23% provide a subjective burden grading, and only 1% calculate a quantitative BAC score61. Follow-up recommendations were equally inconsistent. Fifty-eight percent made no specific follow-up recommendations, while others suggest primary care evaluation (39%), cardiology consultation (13%), and coronary calcium scoring (CAC) CT (11%)61. Notably, 66% of respondents acknowledged BAC as a cardiovascular risk factor, yet there was no consensus on standardized reporting nor on whether patients should be routinely informed61.

Trimboli et al. surveyed 1084 members of the European Society of Breast Imaging (EUSOBI) with 378 responses62. Of these, 305 (80.7%) reported being aware of the cardiovascular implications of BAC, and 234 (61.9%) stated that they routinely included BAC in mammogram reports when detected. However, documentation methods varied: 64.8% reported documenting BAC presence with a simple annotation, 25.3% distinguished between low and extensive burden, 9.5% used an ordinal scale, and only 1 0.4% applied a cardinal scale. Among the 233 radiologists who reported BAC, 45.5% stated that they orally informed patients, 44.2% investigated cardiovascular history in cases of severe burden, and 39.5% referred patients to a cardiologist.

B. Patient perspectives

Studies show that patient preferences strongly favor BAC disclosure. Margolies et al. conducted a cross-sectional survey to assess women’s views on the reporting of BAC in mammography and how it might influence their healthcare decisions63. Of 419 respondents, 95.8% preferred BAC information to be included in their mammogram results. All respondents without known heart disease wanted to be notified of BAC findings, emphasizing its value in raising cardiovascular risk awareness. Most respondents preferred notification via letter or phone call, highlighting the need for clear communication from radiology teams. Additionally, 87.4% of respondents expressed willingness to undergo coronary artery CT if BAC was detected. These findings demonstrate that patients value BAC information and are willing to pursue additional evaluation when indicated.

C. The case for standardization

The disconnect between radiologist awareness (66–81%), inconsistent reporting (41–62% report routinely), and strong patient preference for disclosure (96%) highlights an important gap requiring attention. Several barriers contribute to this inconsistency, including the lack of standardized scoring systems, unclear clinical pathways, and ambiguity about whether radiologists, primary care providers, or cardiologists should lead follow-up. Most radiologists currently use binary (present/absent) or subjective grading rather than validated quantitative scales, and without guidelines specifying thresholds for action, many are uncertain what recommendations to make.

Standardized BAC reporting—analogous to BI-RADS for breast lesions—would require consensus on several elements: a validated, reproducible grading system (such as none/mild/moderate/severe categories or quantitative calcium mass); clear language for inclusion in radiology reports; recommended follow-up actions based on BAC severity; and patient communication standards. AI-based quantification, as described in Section IV, could facilitate standardization by providing objective, reproducible BAC scores that remove inter-reader variability and enable consistent risk stratification across institutions.

Expanding the scope: BAC beyond CVD

Because BAC is a marker of vascular disease38, its utility may extend beyond CVD risk prediction. In a retrospective study of 405 postmenopausal women, Saá et al. explored the relationship between BAC on screening mammograms and age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a leading cause of vision loss in older adults64. Of the 121 participants, BAC was present in 71, and 76 had AMD, with the prevalence of AMD being higher in women with BAC (58/71) compared to those without BAC (18/36) (p < 0.001). The study suggests that BAC may reflect systemic microvascular pathology and could serve as a surrogate marker of vascular aging, with implications for both cardiovascular and ocular risk stratification. These studies show that development and implementation of AI models to detect BAC to promote early detection and treatment of early treatment.

Iribarren et al. investigated whether BAC is associated with mild cognitive impairment or incident all-cause dementia among postmenopausal women, using data from the MINERVA Study65. Since vascular risk factors are implicated in cognitive decline, and BAC is linked to systemic vascular disease, they hypothesized that BAC presence might contribute to cognitive impairment or dementia risk65. However, after analyzing a multiethnic cohort of 3913 asymptomatic women aged 60–79 years, researchers found no significant association between BAC and cognitive decline.

These studies highlight both the potential and the limitations of BAC as a marker beyond CVD. The positive association with AMD supports the hypothesis that BAC reflects broader vascular aging, while the null association with dementia suggests disease-specific boundaries to its predictive value. Additional multicenter studies are needed to further delineate BAC associations with other vascular and age-related diseases, and to determine whether BAC-based interventions could impact outcomes beyond cardiovascular events.

Clinical adoption of BAC detection and management

Despite strong evidence linking BAC to cardiovascular risk, integration into routine clinical care remains limited. The main barriers include inconsistent radiologist reporting, lack of standardized scoring systems, limited clinician awareness, and absence of formal management guidelines. To translate BAC from an incidental mammographic finding into a clinical biomarker, several steps are needed across detection, reporting, and downstream patient care. 1. Automated BAC detection and reporting

AI-based tools have the potential to close the gap between evidence and practice by enabling consistent, scalable, and objective BAC assessment. Similar to how retinal imaging AI tools (e.g., Reti-CVD)66,67 opportunistically screen for cardiovascular disease, automated BAC detection can transform routine mammography into a dual-purpose exam—simultaneously screening for breast cancer and cardiovascular disease risk.

For clinical implementation, an ideal system should: (1) Automatically detect and quantify BAC across all mammography modalities (FFDM and DBT), (2) Integrate seamlessly into PACS and structured radiology reports, (3), Provide standardized severity categories (e.g., none, mild, moderate, severe), and (4) Export BAC scores into the electronic health record (EHR) to support coordinated care.

Such automation would enable population-level risk stratification without adding workload for radiologists or requiring additional imaging, radiation exposure, or cost.

2. Management of patients with BAC: current gaps and future needs

There are currently no formal guidelines for the clinical management of BAC, largely because large-scale prospective studies are lacking. However, its robust association with hypertension, diabetes, CKD, CVD events, and mortality suggests that BAC should prompt a structured cardiovascular evaluation.

A proposed initial approach could include: (1) Primary care referral for cardiovascular risk assessment focused on existing guidelines68,69, (2) CVD risk estimation using existing calculators such as ASCVD69 and PREVENT70 with consideration of how BAC may up-classify women whose traditional risk scores underestimate risk, (3) Cardiology referral for women with extensive BAC or multiple high-risk comorbidities, and (4) Lifestyle and pharmacologic optimization, including intensified control of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia.

Future guidelines should specify BAC thresholds that warrant action, similar to how coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores guide statin use. This will require harmonization of BAC scoring systems and large prospective outcome trials.

3. The current commercial landscape

At present, only one FDA-cleared AI tool exists for automated BAC detection across both FFDM and DBT, however, it only detects the presence of BAC and does not quantify BAC burden23,24,71. While clinically deployed in some centers, current implementations are limited. For example, patients may receive BAC results directly, sometimes as an add-on paid service, and without any clear recommendations or next steps. In addition, primary care providers are often not notified, which can result in a disconnect between patients and providers.

This mismatch between detection and actionable information underscores the need for validated quantitative models, standardized reporting, and coordinated clinical pathways.

Limitations

Despite growing evidence linking BAC to cardiovascular disease (CVD) and coronary artery calcium (CAC), several limitations across existing studies highlight the need for more rigorous investigation. Many studies were small, retrospective, or cross-sectional, with selective recruitment of high-risk individuals, limiting generalizability. Most cohorts lacked racial and ethnic diversity, and some relied on outdated imaging techniques, potentially underestimating BAC prevalence. Additionally, BAC was measured using non-standardized methods, without a uniform grading system, making cross-study comparisons difficult. Several studies relied on self-reported cardiovascular risk factors, introducing potential bias. While BAC has traditionally been viewed as a benign, age-related medial calcification, its consistent association with ASCVD and CAC suggests clinical relevance that warrants further investigation. Unexplained findings, such as an inverse relationship with smoking, further emphasize the need for pathophysiologic understanding. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for large, prospective, multiethnic studies using standardized BAC scoring and contemporary imaging to determine BAC’s true value in long-term cardiovascular risk prediction and prevention.

Conclusion

Breast arterial calcification, a common and incidental finding on routine mammograms, is a powerful yet underutilized biomarker for cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in women. Unlike traditional atherosclerotic markers, BAC reflects medial arterial calcification and has been independently associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including heart attack, stroke, heart failure, and death, even after adjustment for conventional risk factors. Despite strong supporting evidence, BAC remains underreported due to lack of standardized guidelines and limited provider awareness.

Recent advances in AI allow accurate automated detection and quantification of BAC, creating an opportunity for opportunistic cardiovascular disease screening integrated into routine breast imaging. AI-based BAC assessment into clinical practice could significantly enhance early risk detection, particularly in women whose risk is often underestimated by standard tools such as the Framingham or ASCVD risk scores. We emphasize the need for standardized BAC measurement, reporting, provider education, and clinical integration to unlock its full potential in improving cardiovascular outcomes in women.
