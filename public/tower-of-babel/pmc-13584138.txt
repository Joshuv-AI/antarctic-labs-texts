An integrated One Health surveillance on cyclosporiasis outbreaks in the United States
Authors: Abdullahi Tunde Aborode, Mariam Oladayo Allison, Fredrick Olusegun Akinyosoye, Akeem A Aliyu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The recent increase in cyclosporiasis cases across the United States has exposed a critical weakness in the country's foodborne disease surveillance system, the inability to rapidly identify contaminated food sources before infections become geographically widespread [1]. Although cyclosporiasis has been a nationally notifiable disease for many years and follows a well-recognized seasonal pattern, recurring outbreaks continue to challenge public health authorities because the source of contamination is often identified only after hundreds of people have already been exposed. As food production and distribution networks become increasingly complex, there is an urgent need to move beyond reactive surveillance toward an integrated One Health approach that simultaneously monitors human health, agricultural production, environmental contamination, food supply chains, and molecular epidemiology to enable earlier detection and prediction of outbreaks as seen in Fig. 1 below.

The scale of the 2026 cyclosporiasis season has brought these public-health challenges into sharper focus. According to CDC surveillance data updated on September 1, 2026, 18,445 laboratory-confirmed domestically acquired cases were reported across 49 states and the District of Columbia between May 1 and August 31 [2]. These infections resulted in 990 hospitalizations and two deaths, a dramatic increase from the 1180 cases recorded during the comparable period in 2025 [2]. The affected individuals reported no international travel during the 14 days preceding illness, supporting domestic foodborne exposure as the source of infection. As of September 3, 2026, this outbreak alone had caused 11,458 confirmed illnesses, 495 hospitalizations, and two deaths across 20 states. Nevertheless, it does not explain the entire national increase [2]. The CDC and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration are investigating additional clusters for which contaminated food sources have not yet been identified [2].

One of the major challenges in controlling cyclosporiasis lies in the unique biology of Cyclospora cayetanensis. Unlike many bacterial foodborne pathogens, Cyclospora is not transmitted directly from one infected individual to another because the parasite requires several days to weeks in the environment to sporulate before becoming infectious [3]. Human infections therefore occur almost exclusively through ingestion of food or water contaminated with mature oocysts originating from human fecal waste. Contamination frequently occurs during agricultural production through inadequately treated irrigation water, contaminated wash water, poor sanitation facilities for farm workers, or improper handling during harvesting, packaging, and processing.

Over the past decade, the CDC, in collaboration with state health departments and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), has significantly strengthened national cyclosporiasis surveillance through mandatory disease reporting, improved laboratory diagnostics, enhanced molecular epidemiology, and coordinated multistate outbreak investigations [4]. These improvements have increased the capacity to detect outbreaks and monitor seasonal trends. Nevertheless, the current surveillance framework remains predominantly reactive. Public health action generally begins only after infected individuals develop symptoms, seek healthcare, receive laboratory confirmation, and are linked through epidemiological investigations. Although these approaches are essential for outbreak response, they provide limited opportunity to prevent transmission before contaminated food reaches consumers. Consequently, interventions often occur after substantial community exposure has already taken place.

Food supply chain surveillance is an important integrated strategy. Modern food distribution systems transport fresh produce across multiple states within a matter of days, making rapid traceback investigations essential during outbreaks. Emerging technologies such as blockchain-enabled traceability, digital supply chain management platforms, radio-frequency identification systems, and electronic shipment records now provide unprecedented opportunities to monitor the movement of produce from farms to processing facilities, distribution centers, retailers, restaurants, and ultimately consumers. Integrating these traceability systems with epidemiological surveillance databases would enable investigators to reconstruct distribution pathways within hours rather than weeks, substantially reducing the time required to identify contaminated food sources and implement recalls.

Clinical surveillance requires modernization to improve the speed and sensitivity of outbreak detection. Electronic laboratory reporting should be seamlessly integrated with hospital electronic health records and state surveillance systems to facilitate immediate notification of confirmed cyclosporiasis cases [5]. Syndromic surveillance systems capable of monitoring increases in acute diarrheal illness could provide additional early warning signals before laboratory confirmation becomes available. Coupling these systems with automated statistical algorithms would enable public health authorities to detect unusual disease clusters earlier and initiate investigations before outbreaks expand across multiple jurisdictions. Molecular epidemiology should become a central component of future cyclosporiasis surveillance. Although genetic characterization of Cyclospora cayetanensis has historically been constrained by technical limitations and relatively low genetic diversity, recent advances in molecular biology provide new opportunities for outbreak investigation. Whole-genome sequencing, multilocus sequence typing, targeted amplicon sequencing, and metagenomic approaches have the potential to distinguish outbreak-associated strains from unrelated sporadic infections with increasing precision.

Effective implementation of a One Health surveillance framework will also require unprecedented collaboration among clinicians, epidemiologists, microbiologists, environmental scientists, agricultural specialists, food safety regulators, veterinarians, and policymakers. One of the greatest barriers to effective outbreak response remains the fragmentation of surveillance data across multiple agencies with limited interoperability. Establishing standardized national data-sharing platforms capable of integrating clinical reports, laboratory findings, environmental monitoring results, genomic sequencing data, and food traceability information would significantly improve coordination among federal, state, and local agencies while accelerating outbreak investigations and public health decision-making.
