Cutaneous fistula of the breast: A complication of cosmetic autologous fat transfer
Authors: Anna H Maki, Millicent Gentry, Christi S Bourne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutologous fat transfer is a growing cosmetic procedure which is most often well tolerated with generally low rate of complications, the majority of which occur in the immediate post-treatment period. The procedure has been more recently applied to the breast as an alternative to traditional prosthetic augmentation. We present a case of a delayed cutaneous fistula, which occurred 18 months following fat transfer procedure, in a 34-year-old female. The patient presented with increasing pain and dermal erythema. Mammographic appearance evolved from a typical thin-walled liponecrotic cyst to a dense mass with an obscured margin. Sonography showed complex cystic and solid components, with an elongated tract extending to the draining fistula at the dermis. The fistula and symptoms ultimately resolved following antimicrobial therapy and conservative treatment. Physicians and patients should be aware of this uncommon potential complication.Keywords: Breast augmentation, Fat transfer, Cutaneous fistula

Introduction

Autologous fat transfer (AFT) is an increasingly utilized cosmetic procedure in which adipose cells are harvested from a distant donor site, such as abdominal wall fat, variably processed ex vivo, and subsequently reinjected into a desired target site for aesthetic purposes. Over the past decade, the procedure has become quite popular for application to the gluteal region, or so-called Brazilian Butt lift [1]. In addition, the technique has for many years had been applied to the post-mastectomy breast, in attempt to ameliorate inconsistencies in aesthetics or texture of the reconstructed breast [2,3].

More recently, many aesthetic practices have applied the technology to the native breast as a “minimally invasive nonsurgical” (percutaneous only) means of breast augmentation. The procedure has been reported as yielding relatively high volume-retention of injected fat long term, with purportedly high patient satisfaction rates, while carrying low risk of complications (17.2%), most commonly painful fat necrosis (oil cysts), hematomas, or mammographic imaging abnormalities [4,5]. The dreaded, potentially fatal, fat embolus remains perhaps the greatest risk of AFT but is fortunately extraordinarily rare.

In this report, we describe a patient who underwent AFT for cosmetic breast augmentation and presented 18 months later with development of a spontaneous fistula to the skin. This case highlights an unusual potential complication of AFT, which breast radiologists and primary care providers might encounter. This complication has not been, to our knowledge, previously described in the radiology literature, in this setting.

The case underscores a delayed complication of the AFT procedure perhaps not likely commonly discussed with or considered by patients.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old female presented to a local cosmetic medical clinic in search of less invasive and more economical means of breast augmentation, as compared to traditional lift and implantation of breast prostheses. She had no significant past medical history or underlying medical problems.

The clinic was directed by single physician, who employed additional physician assistants to help perform various in-office percutaneous AFT procedures, including buttock and breast augmentation.

The patient underwent harvesting of mid abdominal fat with subsequent reimplantation of harvested cells into the upper central breast bilaterally.

Six months following the procedure, she underwent mammography, to establish an imaging baseline and to evaluate retention of transplanted tissue. Digital 2D/3D tomographic mammography was performed showing breast density to be scattered areas of fibro glandular density. Mammography demonstrated a single round 3 cm fat-density mass in each breast, in the upper central 12:00 position, characteristic of benign fat necrosis (liponecrotic cyst) (Fig. 1).

Right breast mammographic MLO projection, performed 6 months after AFT, showing an asymptomatic 3.0 cm thin-walled mass containing central lucent fat, compatible with benign fat necrosis (liponecrotic oil cyst).

One year later, the patient experienced developing pain in both breasts, much worse on the right than left. On physical exam, there was visible unilateral erythema and mild skin thickening on the superior right breast. A firm palpable lump was present on physical examination, tender to palpation. There was no fever or tachycardia.

A week later, an open wound developed spontaneously along the 12:00 axis of her right breast, from which cloudy straw-colored fluid wept spontaneously, leading her to seek medical evaluation.

Diagnostic digital mammography was repeated, showing transformation of the previous thin-walled liponecrotic oil cyst in the right breast into a more complex process. While the upper portion of the mass remained fat density and thin walled, the lower portion now demonstrated equal to high density aspects within it, with indistinct and obscured margins. No abnormal calcifications were seen (Fig. 2).

Right breast mammographic MLO projection, performed 18 months following AFT, showing complex transformation of the prior liponecrotic cyst. There is now equal to high density components within the lower aspect of the mass, with indistinct and obscured margins. In addition, a linear tract of radiodensity is now seen extending to the skin surface (arrows), corresponding to newly developed clinical fistula.

Ultrasound revealed a correlating 3.4cm round mass in the central right breast, with not-circumscribed margins, containing both anechoic and hyperechoic components, suggesting a mixed cystic and solid nature. There was no posterior shadowing or increased vascularity on doppler analysis. An elongated tract was also evident, connecting the mass to the overlying skin surface. This fistulous tract, containing centrally hyperechoic tissue and peripheral anechoic cystic components, (Figs. 3 and 4), appeared to communicate with the clinically visible draining wound on the skin.

Static axial image from right breast ultrasound performed at 18 months, demonstrating round mass in the central right breast, with not-circumscribed margins, containing both anechoic and hyperechoic components, suggesting a mixed cystic and solid nature. There was no posterior shadowing or increased vascularity on doppler analysis. An elongated tract was also evident, connecting the mass to the overlying skin surface.

Static axial image from right breast ultrasound at 18 months, showing elongated extension, or tract (green arrows), extending from the mass to skin surface, correlating with clinical fistula. This tract is distended with both echogenic tissue (red arrow), as well as trace anechoic fluid along the periphery of the solid tissue (blue arrows).

Although the clinical presentation favored infectious etiology, an occult malignancy, perhaps contiguous with, or partly obscured by the post-procedure fat necrosis, was felt difficult to exclude. Due to the escalating clinical changes and evolving mammographic and sonographic findings, her workup was categorized as BIRADS-4. A percutaneous aspiration of the dominant 12:00 position right breast cystic component was performed under ultrasound guidance. The aspirate appeared turbid and straw-colored, with oily consistency, but without frank blood. Gram stain and cultures of aspirates from the revealed presence and growth of Streptococcus sanguinis and Cutibacterium avidum, both species showing sensitivity to doxycycline.

Since the elongated tract-like component appeared distended with echogenic solid tissue, a14-gauge spring-guided core biopsy of this solid component was also performed. Pathology from the core biopsy revealed benign fat necrosis, without atypical cells or malignancy, but evidence of both acute and chronic inflammation.

The patient was referred to a breast surgeon and placed on oral Doxycycline treatment along with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medication for pain relief. After 4 weeks of treatment, the fistula closed spontaneously and she returned to her asymptomatic baseline.

Discussion

Although most AFT procedures occur uneventfully, and with relatively low published incidence of significant complications [4], the growing utilization of the procedure will undoubtably lead to greater likelihood that breast radiologists and primary care providers may encounter patients with occasional post procedural problems. The most common complications, including pain, hematomas, and symptomatic fat necrosis, are generally self-limited and benign. The potential for low grade indolent infection, or even delayed cutaneous fistula, many months following procedure however, are adverse outcomes that patients and clinicians may not consider.

In this case, the patient’s aspirates returned Cutibacterium avidum, an opportunistic pathogen known to cause sporadic chronic postoperative infections, months or years following surgery, particularly if seeded to an iatrogenic implanted device, such as joint prostheses [5].

Aspirates also grew Streptococcus sanguinis, an opportunistic member of the Viridans group, with well documented rare potential for both delayed postoperative infection and chronic endocarditis. Delayed infections involving breast implants, even years following implantation, have been reported with this species, although it is often not clear if such infections are due to surgery, or rather are later introduced via the bloodstream during aggressive dental work or systemic bacteremia [6].

It is similarly unclear, in this case, if the fistula was directly caused by an initial infection, or rather, if intense inflammation from fat necrosis led to the development of a sterile fistula, which subsequently allowed a pathway for introduction of secondary infection. Since the fistula closed without surgical intervention following antibiotics alone, it would seem more likely that fistula was directly due to an initial infection, rather than the converse.

Postoperative infection with nontuberculous mycobacteria, following AFT to the breast, has previously been described in the literature, but is reportedly less indolent, requiring surgical debridement of grafted fat, and long-term treatment with antimicrobials [7]. In our case, the fistula and symptoms cleared with antimicrobial therapy alone, not requiring surgical intervention.

Ironically, AFT has recently been proposed, and increasingly utilized, as treatment for non-enteric cutaneous fistulas [8]. Injection of harvested fat cells into nonhealing fistulas has had reported success in promoting healing and closure in many cases. Thus, while complications of AFT led to formation of the fistula in this case, the same AFT procedure may soon be intermittently performed for its treatment.

In summary, radiologists should be aware that evolution of a previously thin-walled liponecrotic cyst following AFT, particularly in a symptomatic patient, could indicate development of active inflammation or even infection of the transplanted fatty tissue.

It is also important for medical providers to be aware that unusual complications from AFT can develop, even months or years following the procedure. This technique is being utilized with increasing frequency, and by providers with variable degrees of training and experience. It may also be intermittently performed in office settings with lesser degrees aseptic environment than traditional operating rooms. Therefore, delayed complications, such as described in this report, may be seen more commonly in the future.
