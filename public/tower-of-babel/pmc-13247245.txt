Unveiling the roles of device structure and driving frequency in mitigating crosstalk effects in nanopixel light-emitting displays
Authors: Wenhao Li, Shuqian Zhang, Xiongtu Zhou, Yongai Zhang, Tailiang Guo, Chaoxing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe noncarrier injection (NCI) mode holds promise for application in nanopixel light-emitting display to enhance the competitiveness of display technology. However, conventional approaches face challenges in fabricating NCI mode light-emitting arrays that simultaneously achieve pixel-level precision and high emission quality. In this work, the effect of electrical crosstalk in NCI mode array is revealed by the finite element simulation and the effective suppression of crosstalk is proved by experimental tests. The mechanism of crosstalk is revealed by the dynamic variation of carrier concentration and circuit model analysis. It is demonstrated that adjacent nLEDs show different electrical crosstalk phenomena in changing voltage amplitude, voltage frequency, insulation thickness and the distance between adjacent nLEDs. According to the analysis, we propose a crosstalk suppression solution, that is reducing the etching depth of the nLED array. Finally, by comparing the difference in the working characteristics of the array in injection mode and NCI mode, it is demonstrated the crosstalk suppression capability of the NCI mode. This work provides valuable theories for understanding the NCI mode and opens a new perspective for the display technology related to the nLED array. Subject terms: Optical materials and structures, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

The display technology, as a medium of information transmission, is a key to dealing with the explosive growth of information for the rapid development of the Internet1–5. The demand for transforming digital information into more realistic visual forms pushes the reduction of pixel size to a submicron/nanometer scale6–10. Because of this, nanopixel light-emitting display technology is expected to construct nanoscale spatial light fields based on independent nanoscale electroluminescent devices to achieve high-quality display information presentation11–13. For the choice of light source of nanopixel light-emitting display technology, the gallium nitride (GaN)-based light-emitting diode with the benefits such as high brightness14, long life15 and fast response speed16 is the potential candidate for making light-emitting arrays with high frequency, high power and high-density integration. However, at the nanometer-scale dimensions relevant to the size of GaN-LED, the electrical crosstalk between pixels becomes increasingly more obvious and complex17,18. Generally, the electrical crosstalk phenomenon in adjacent nanoscale LED (nLED) refers to disrupting the original transport process of electrons and holes, affecting the performance of nLED array.

To improve the performance and stability of the LED array, several methods for weakening electrical crosstalk are proposed. For example, a gap-free LED array can effectively prevent the diffusion of lateral current to ensure uniform current distribution. Noor Elahi and Xu utilized the ion implantation method to create an isolation barrier between the adjacent LEDs17, that is, increasing the concentration of deep-level traps, to impede the lateral current flow between the pixels. S.C. Huang et al. adopted the method of filling the oxide layer between Micro-LED to suppress the diffusion of lateral current19. In addition, the electrode structure is also one of the key factors that cause the electrical crosstalk between pixels, which affects whether the electric field lines are vertically distributed or diverged. H. Kang et al. divided the common ground electrode into independent electrodes matched with pixels20, which increased the electric field intensity in each pixel. The design of conical electrodes21 and resistive-switching electrodes22 can also make the electric field lines more concentrated on the target pixel. To realize the suppression of crosstalk between signals in multi-channel, signal transmission is also important in the design of driving circuits. Both methods of using a graphene shielding layer in the driver circuit23 proposed by S.G. Hamedani et al. and choosing common-layer materials with suitable lateral conductivity between pixels24 proposed by M. Diethelm et al. can effectively reduce interference in electric signal transmission.

Although some progress has been made, it seems that these methods are challenging to prevent electrical crosstalk for nLED array resulting from complex interaction of conduction current and displacement current. Moreover, the optical characteristics in the nanoscale space are also susceptible to electrical crosstalk, leading to optical crosstalk and degradation in display quality25–27. Recently, a driving mode for nLED, called noncarrier injection (NCI) mode28–33, has been proposed to respond challenges of precise band matching between materials and good ohmic contacts between electrodes and light-emitting devices. The basic structure of the NCI-nLED for electroluminescence (EL) is the insulator/LED chip/ insulator34–39. Given the existence of insulating layers, the EL pulse is the result of the periodic oscillation of carriers inside the LED driven by the alternating current (AC) electric field40,41. Although the high pixel density of the NCI mode is easier to realize than the injection mode, the influence of electrical crosstalk on array performance cannot be ignored.

In this work, we used finite element simulations to explore the effect of electrical crosstalk inside NCI-nLED array and experimentally validated the efficacy of NCI mode in suppressing crosstalk. Firstly, the mutual conversion relationship between displacement current and conduction current in NCI-nLED is revealed. We studied the numerical variation in the light emission rate of adjacent nLEDs by changing voltage amplitude, voltage frequency, insulation thickness and the distance between adjacent nLEDs. We found that the electrical crosstalk phenomena exhibited by the nLED array are diversified due to the sensitivity of NCI mode to driving frequency. The equivalent circuit model is established to reveal the generation process of electrical crosstalk. Moreover, we propose a method to further suppress electrical crosstalk by adjusting the etching depth of nLED array. Importantly, the NCI mode array exhibits a significant crosstalk suppression compared to conventional injection mode by experimental comparison. The results of this work provide valuable theories for understanding the NCI mode and opens a new perspective for the display technology related to nLED array.

Results and discussion

Model details of the NCI-LED

As shown in Fig. 1a, the NCI-nLED model with the structure of the aluminum oxide (Al2O3) layer/nLED/ sapphire layer sandwiched between two electrodes is demonstrated. External carriers are blocked by the insulating layers, causing the internal carriers within the device to achieve EL under AC electric field. Previous studies have shown that the carriers involved in radiation recombination primarily originate from free carriers contributed by the doping concentration of the device, as well as additional free carriers generated by surface defect34, interband tunneling35, and avalanche multiplication36 effects resulting from a significant increase in the reverse barrier. In addition, the number of carriers generated by these effects can be modulated by the amplitude of the AC electric field and the electric field strength of the potential barrier. A detailed structure for the NCI-nLED used for our simulations is presented in Fig. S1, Tables S1 and S2. A detailed introduction to the model is presented in the Texts S1, S2 and S3. Obviously, the device is equivalent to a series parallel resistor-capacitor combined circuit42–44, as shown in Fig. 1b. In this circuit, C1 and C2 are equivalent capacitors associated with Al2O3 layer and the sapphire layer, respectively. RLED and CLED are equivalent resistance and equivalent capacitors related to PN junction, respectively. Figure 1c shows the time-resolved characteristics among applied voltage, external current and emission rate (ER). It can be seen that the maximum value of external current occurs at the moment when the voltage change rate is maximum and only one EL pulse can be obtained in a single voltage cycle.

On the top Al2O3/p-GaN interface of the device (Fig. 1d), the ICC has a value of zero while the IMDC has the same value as the IExternal. Due to the insulation layer covering, external carriers are prevented from injecting into the device and also the leakage of charge carriers is prevented, proving the principle of electro-optical conversion in NCI-nLED is achieved based on electrostatic induction. In the p-GaN region (Fig. 1e), the value of ICC and IExternal are the same, and the IMDC has a value of zero. In the multiquantum wells (MQWs) region (Fig. 1f), the blue area (ICC ≠ 0) represents the duration of EL, while the non-blue area (ICC = 0) represents the time period during which the device is in a non-conductive state. Specifically, the EL process can be divided into two parts: the increase in ICC due to the entry of carriers into the MQWs, and the decrease in ICC due to the participation of carriers in radiative recombination.

The internal current conversion in NCI-nLED demonstrates that, compared to injection mode, NCI mode can prevent carrier path diversification caused by poor ohmic contacts while simplifying the complex interactions between conduction current and displacement current. Thus, the preparation of luminescent arrays by NCI-nLED (Fig. 1g) can simplify the preparation process and increase the performance uniformity. However, due to the radiation-divergent propagation characteristics of MDC in space, the nLEDs in the array would mutually influence with each other, that is, the electrical crosstalk phenomenon (Fig. 1h). Inside the nLEDs, in addition to the carrier driven by the corresponding electrical signal (signal photons), the carrier driven by the adjacent LED participates in the radiation recombination (crosstalk photons). Moreover, in complex signal overlap scenarios, the number of carriers participating in radiation recombination inside the device can vary, leading to the obvious optical crosstalk phenomena. Thus, the exploration of the electrical crosstalk mechanism is essential for understanding NCI mode and realizing a high-quality array.

Electrical characteristics and carrier concentration distribution

As the intensity of the electric field decreases with increasing distance, we studied the operational characteristics of two adjacent nLEDs to explore the impact of electrical crosstalk on the performance of the light-emitting array, as shown in Fig. 2a. In this model, an electric field is only applied to the nLED-1. A detailed structure for the NCI-nLED array used for our simulations is presented in Fig. S2. Fig. 2b presents the distribution of the electric field intensity for both forward peak voltage and reverse peak voltage. The electric field intensity of MQWs in the nLED-2 is weaker. Moreover, the dynamic variation of the barrier potential in the MQWs region (ΔEMQW) in a voltage cycle is presented in Fig. 2c. During the electrical signal transmission of the electrode corresponding to nLED-1, the variation trend of the barrier is consistent with that of the electric field intensity, and the nLED-2 can exhibit an on-state and generate crosstalk photons inside it. Quantitatively, the emission rate (ER) of signal photons generated by nLED-1 is 6.7 × 1011/s, while the ER of crosstalk photons generated by nLED-2 is 3.4 × 1011/s (Fig. 2d). It should be noted that the change in the potential barrier of the device under a reverse electric field is more significant than that under a forward electric field, and this difference is primarily determined by the voltage distribution between the LED and the insulating layer. Under the forward electric field, the applied voltage is mostly distributed on the insulating layers due to the relatively small forward conduction resistance of the nLED. Based on the unidirectional conduction characteristics, nLEDs exhibit a high-impedance state under the reverse electric field. Thus, the voltage applied to the nLEDs increases significantly, leading to a significant change in the barrier, which is different from injection nLEDs.

Thus, the working state of nLED-2 is affected by the electric signal transmitted by the electrode of nLED-1. Although the photon generation inside nLED-2 is increased, the crosstalk signal would affect the trajectory of its internal carriers. For a further description of the influence of electrical crosstalk, the distributions of electron concentration for both forward peak voltage and reverse peak voltage are demonstrated in Fig. 2e. In the positive half cycle, the carriers inside nLED enter MQWs region to participate in radiation recombination. Due to the existence of the insulating layers, external carriers cannot be injected into the NCI-nLED, so only the inherent carriers contribute to radiation recombination. Because the p/n-GaN terminals are closer to the electrodes, the change in carrier concentration at these terminals is the most significant. Therefore, the movements of carriers in the p/n-GaN form depletion regions at the Al2O3/GaN interface and generate an induced electric field opposite in direction to that of the external electric field, which is different from injection nLED45–47. In the negative half cycle, the reverse electric field drives the carriers that have entered the device interior to move back toward their original positions, gradually filling the depletion region and forming a carrier accumulation process. Thus, the operation mechanism of NCI-LEDs is essentially a periodic dynamic process of depletion, filling and accumulation of inherent carriers in the device. It is important to note that the distribution of electrons in the MQWs of nLED-2 is non-uniform, especially with these electrons accumulating at the boundaries along the electric field lines. Although the lateral movement of carriers is inevitable, the electrical crosstalk exacerbates the extent of this movement within adjacent nLEDs, resulting in non-uniform current distribution and increased surface non-radiative recombination. The electron concentration distribution of the left boundary and the right boundary of MQWs in nLED-1 and nLED-2 within a cycle is demonstrated quantitatively in Fig. 2f and g. Compared with nLED-1, the lateral diffusion in nLED-2 is obviously enhanced and the diffusion degree is increased by 6 times.

Optoelectronic characterization for electrical crosstalk effect

As a result, the impact of electrical crosstalk on the performance of nLED array cannot be ignored; specifically, the uneven light emission results from the non-uniform distribution of carriers inside the device along the direction perpendicular to the MQWs. Due to the existence of the insulating layer, changes in external driving parameters and diverse array structures would both lead to changes in the parameters of the equivalent circuit. The mechanism of crosstalk generated during the operation of NCI-nLED revealed by equivalent circuit model analysis is presented in Fig. 3a. Each NCI-nLED in the array can be equivalent to a series parallel resistor-capacitor combined circuit, and they are connected in parallel to transmit electrical signals, as shown in Fig. 3a-I. C1 and C2 are equivalent capacitances of insulation layers on both sides of nLED-1. C3 and C4 are equivalent capacitances of insulation layers on both sides of nLED-2. C5 is the equivalent capacitance formed by the insulation layer between nLED-1 and nLED-2, that is, the performance of nLED-2 is affected by crosstalk depends on the ability of C5 to induce charges. With the increase of the forward electric field (Fig. 3a-Ⅱ), C1 ~ C4 begin to charge and drive the carriers inside nLED-1 and nLED-2 to enter the MQWs for radiative recombination. Due to the higher impedance, the voltage applied to nLED-2 is less than the voltage applied to nLED-1. Thus, the number of photons generated by nLED-2 is less than that generated by nLED-1. Note that the degree of depletion at GaN/insulator interface reaches its maximum at peak voltage (1/4 T0). With the decrease of the forward electric field (Fig. 3a-Ⅲ), C1 ~ C4 begin to discharge and the voltage applied to nLED-1 and nLED-2 gradually decreases and the carriers inside the devices begin to return to their original positions. During the negative half cycle (Fig. 3a-Ⅳ), C1~C4 also undergo the process of charging and discharging. The holes and electrons continue to move towards the p-GaN and n-GaN, respectively, causing the state at GaN/insulator interface to gradually change from depletion to accumulation.

In addition, the spacing between nLED is also an important factor affecting the changes in equivalent capacitance parameters. Analysis of crosstalk suppression capability in unetched n-GaN layer

Although proper optimization of the external electric field and array structure may weaken electrical crosstalk, it must be done with the premise of further increasing power consumption or further reducing pixel density. In response to this, we propose a method to effectively suppress electrical crosstalk, that is, the n-GaN layer is not completely etched to the sapphire layer, as shown in Fig. 4a. We define the structure that is etched completely to the sapphire layer as Structure Ⅰ, and the structure that is not completely etched to the sapphire layer as Structure Ⅱ. Figure 4b presents the mechanism underlying the further suppression of electrical crosstalk inside NCI-nLED array. According to the decay characteristics of electric fields in space, for structure Ⅰ with full etching, the electrical potential at the bottom of the n-GaN layer in nLED-1 is significantly higher than that in nLED-2, that is, the electrical potential at point 1 is higher than the electrical potential at point 2. When an unetched n-GaN layer exists in the array as a shared structure between pixels (Structure II), the related voltage-dividing resistor (Rdiv) is connected between point 1 and point 2. The electrical potential at the bottom of the n-GaN layer in nLED-1 decreases accordingly to balance the electrical potential of this layer, while that at the bottom of the n-GaN layer in nLED-2 increases correspondingly; that is, the electrical potential at point 1 drops, and the electrical potential at point 2 rises until the potentials at the two points are equal. Due to the presence of an insulating layer between the p-GaN and the electrode in the nLED, and the fact that the electrical potential decays linearly with distance within a uniform insulating layer, the electrical potentials at the top of the p-GaN in nLED-1 and nLED-2 in Structure II are the same as those in Structure I. Thus, in Structure II, the voltage applied to nLED-1 is increased, while that applied to nLED-2 is reduced; that is, V31 increases and V42 decreases.

a Schematic diagram of device structure with n-GaN layer partially etched. b Schematic showing the equivalent circuit model of crosstalk suppression. Structure II works on the principle that the remaining n-GaN layer acts as a voltage-dividing resistor to reduce crosstalk. As is well known, the accumulation of heat due to the Joule heating effect is inevitable during the operation of resistive components. However, compared with the Joule heat generated from the MQWs regions, the Joule heat generated from the unetched n-GaN layer can be ignored (Fig. S4). The main reasons are as follows: (1) Due to the high electron mobility of n-GaN, the movement of electrons encounters less resistance from lattice scattering. (2) n-GaN is prone to forming a high-doping state, enabling excellent electrical conductivity. Thus, in high-density arrays, the heat accumulation in the unetched n-GaN layer has a negligible impact on the device lifetime or efficiency.

The potential difference between point 1 and point 2 in the two structures are shown in Fig. 4c. It can be seen that the potential at point 1 is higher than the potential at point 2 in Structure Ⅰ, while the potentials at point 1 and point 2 are equal in Structure Ⅱ. In addition, the potential barrier in the nLED-1 and nLED-2 also changes as expected in the positive half cycle (Fig. 4d, e). The potential barrier of nLED-1 in Structure Ⅱ is lower than that of nLED-1 in Structure Ⅰ, while the potential barrier of nLED-2 in Structure Ⅱ is higher than that of nLED-2 in Structure Ⅰ. In other words, the unetched n-GaN layer leads to an increase in voltage applied to nLED-1 and a decrease in voltage applied to nLED-2, effectively suppressing the electrical crosstalk of adjacent nLEDs.

The distribution of carriers inside the device is influenced by the MDC with divergent characteristic, as reflected by the irregular current waveform inside the nLED is presented in Fig. 4f, g. Compared with the current waveform of a single NCI-nLED (Fig. 1d), the irregular current waveform is the result of mutual influence between adjacent nLEDs, i.e., the diversification of carrier moving paths. Moreover, the change in total charge (Nc) inside each nLED can also quantitatively reflect the results of crosstalk suppression. The Nc of the nLED-1 in Structure Ⅰ and the nLED-1 in Structure Ⅱ are 7.1 × 10−16 C and 8.1 × 10−16 C, respectively. The Nc of the nLED-2 in Structure Ⅰ and the nLED-2 in Structure Ⅱ are 4.1 × 10−16 C and 3.8 × 10−16 C, respectively. Therefore, the Rdiv is a crucial factor that affects the voltage distribution within the array. As shown in Fig. 4h, when the voltage amplitude is 100 V, the frequency is 50 MHz and the spacing between nLEDs is 500 nm, the MER and σ of nLED-1 and nLED-2 at unetched n-GaN layers with different heights are presented. With the increase of the height of the unetched n-GaN layer, the MER of nLED-1 remains almost unchanged, while the MER of nLED-2 continues to decrease. Obviously, the σ is also further reduced; especially, the σ can be reduced from 3.20% to 0.08% compared to an array where the n-GaN layer is completely etched.

To further reveal the effectiveness of Structure Ⅱ in suppressing electrical crosstalk, we explore the MER and σ contrast of nLED-1 and nLED-2 between Structure Ⅰ/Ⅱ under different voltage amplitudes, frequencies, insulation layer thicknesses, and spaces between the nLEDs, as shown in Fig. 5a–d. By changing these four parameters, the variation trend of MER and σ in Structure Ⅱ is consistent with that in Structure Ⅰ (Fig. 3b–e). We define the MER’s growth multiple is the ratio of MER of nLED in Structure Ⅱ to MER of nLED in Structure Ⅰ. The σ’s growth multiple is defined as the ratio of σ in Structure Ⅱ to σ in Structure Ⅰ. It should be noted the growth multiple greater than 1 represents enhancement.

As shown in Fig. 5a, with the increase of voltage amplitude, the growth multiples of MER show a gradually decreasing trend before stabilizing, while the growth multiples of σ show a gradually increasing trend before stabilizing. The change in MER is not apparent from 250 to 1000 V because all nLEDs are in the on state due to the excessively high external voltage. With the increase of driving frequency, the growth multiples of MER show a gradually increasing, while the growth multiples of σ show a gradually decreasing, as shown in Fig. 5b. In the high frequency range of 50 to 1000 MHz, the electrical crosstalk is reduced by 96.5%, demonstrating interesting frequency-selective luminescence characteristics different from the injection mode48–50. Normally, the performance of NCI-nLED array needs to be improved by increasing the applied voltage. Thus, the inevitable enhancement of electrical crosstalk under large applied electric field can be alleviated by regulating the working frequency.

As shown in Fig. 5c, when the thickness of the insulating layer on the p-GaN side is increased from 100 to 2000 nm, the MER’s growth multiples of nLED-1 increased by 50%, and the MER’s growth multiples of nLED-2 decreased by 50%. Moreover, the increase in the thickness of the insulation layer on the p-GaN side leads to an increase in crosstalk, with the growth multiple of σ increasing from 0.0% at 100 nm to 41.1% at 2000 nm. Different from the change of insulation layer thickness, the increase in spacing between nLEDs causes the σ to reduce to ~0%, which is result from the impedance of C5 increases gradually, as shown in Fig. 5d. In addition, the applied electric field mainly acts on the nLED-1, leading to a significant improvement in the performance.

Adjusting the etching depth of the array can not only improve the performance of the target nLED, but also increase the energy conversion efficiency. The increase in the voltage magnitude makes the power efficiency initially increase and then decrease (Fig. 5e), and so does the increase in frequency (Fig. 5f). This is because the ability of each equivalent capacitor to induce charge has an upper limit. In the case that the device structure changes, the ability of partial equivalent capacitors to induce charges would also change. As shown in Fig. 5g, the increase of insulation thickness makes the equivalent impedance increase, leading to the power efficiency of target nLED-1 gradually decreasing. But the presence of the Rdiv slows down the degree of decrease compared to structure Ⅰ. In addition, as the spacing between nLEDs increases (Fig. 5h), the power efficiency gradually improves; conversely, the growth multiple of power efficiency gradually decreases. This means that the variation in parameters of insulation layer has a more significant impact on the target nLED-1 compared to the Rdiv.

Demonstration of crosstalk suppression effectiveness within the array

We simulated 5 × 5 NCI-nLED array with upper insulation thickness of 100 nm, 500 nm and 1000 nm to further verify the effectiveness of electrical crosstalk suppression, as shown in Fig. 6a. In order to simplify the description, only the related simulation result of nLED in the red box are discussed, and the nLEDs from left to right are named nLED-1 to nLED-5. An electric field is only applied to the nLED-3 in the NCI-nLED array. Figure 6b presents the MER of target nLED-3 in Structure Ⅰ/Ⅱ. It can be seen that the performance of the target nLED-3 is improved due to the existence of the incompletely etched n-GaN layer. When the thickness of the upper insulation is 100 nm, 500 nm and 1000 nm, the MER’s growth multiples of n-LED-3 are 1.72, 1.73 and 2.22, respectively. Moreover, the energy conversion efficiency is also increased accordingly (Fig. S5). Although the performance of the target pixels is improved, compared with injection LEDs, the power efficiency of NCI-LEDs is relatively lower. The reasons are as follows: (1) Given that no carriers are injected from the external electrode, the carriers involved in the radiative recombination process mainly originate from the inherent carriers of the device itself, and their quantity is significantly lower than that in injection LEDs. (2) Under the forward electric field, most of the voltage is applied to the insulating layers and only a small portion of the voltage is applied to the nLED itself.

The crosstalk rate of each nLED is shown in Fig. 6c. The EL intensity of nLED around nLED-3 in the Structure Ⅱ is lower than that in the Structure Ⅰ (Fig. 6d–f). As the thickness of the upper insulation decreases, the suppression of crosstalk effect becomes more significant. Numerically, when the insulation layer thickness is 100 nm, the crosstalk rates of nLED-2 and nLED-4 are 3.29 × 10−15, and the crosstalk rates of nLED-1 and nLED-5 are 5.38 × 10−21. In addition, with the condition of operating at the optimal luminance frequency point, we calculate the sensitivity index of luminance non-uniformity (SU) and crosstalk suppression (SC) corresponding to the 5 × 5 array (Fig. S6). An increase in etching depth leads to a slight enhancement in the crosstalk effect (Fig. 4h). But the change values of SU and SC are relatively small under the regulation of the driving frequency. Therefore, within the driving frequency range effective for suppressing electrical crosstalk, even if etching errors occur in mass production, the system’s ability to suppress electrical crosstalk remains largely unaffected, which provides an important guarantee for the stable work of the display array. Therefore, the incompletely etched n-GaN layer as a voltage divider not only suppresses crosstalk effects but also improves the performance of the target nLED.

In order to validate the simulation results and demonstrate the crosstalk suppression capability of the NCI mode, we fabricate a LED array with the pixel diameters of 20.0 µm to demonstrate the difference in the working characteristics of this array in injection mode and NCI mode. Fig. 7a presents the EL microscope photographs of the injection and NCI mode arrays under different voltages. With the increase in voltage amplitude, the light emission from each pixel in the NCI mode array remains almost entirely independent. Moreover, there is a distinct brightness contrast between the pixels and their inter-pixel gaps, while the pixel edges exhibit excellent sharpness. For the injection mode array, the light emission from individual pixels progressively overlaps, resulting in the boundaries between pixel gaps and active pixel areas indistinguishable. As shown in Fig. 7b, the EL intensity of the NCI-LED exhibits an increasing trend with the increase of voltage amplitude, which is similar to the injection LEDs. Fig. 7c presents the EL microscope photographs of NCI mode arrays under different driving frequencies. With the increase in driving frequencies, each pixel maintains excellent visibility, and the EL intensity increases first and then decreases with an optimal frequency point (Fig. 7d). Thus, the periodic oscillation of internal carriers at high frequencies effectively suppresses mutual interference between adjacent pixels.

On the basis of the above simulation results and experimental results, the NCI mode based on electrostatic induction demonstrates the capability in suppressing electrical crosstalk within light-emitting arrays through high-frequency field modulation. Specifically, this mode can maximize the utilization of the existing light-emitting array, requiring only minor adjustments on the original structure without the need for redesigning and manufacturing the entire optical structure. Although we only conducted experimental verification on the NCI array with a pixel size of 20 μm, the underlying mechanism of the proposed electrical crosstalk suppression scheme holds universal applicability for GaN-LED arrays. The variation trend of the experimental results is consistent with carrier concentration, potential barrier, and photoelectric characteristics observed in the simulation results, proving the feasibility of the proposed electrical crosstalk suppression scheme. In addition, previous studies have shown that for LEDs operating in the NCI mode, the surface defect capture process under a positive electric field and the surface defect excitation process under a reverse electric field would form a stable dynamic equilibrium process34. In other words, the number of carriers captured by surface defects is the same as the number of carriers generated by surface defect excitation, which significantly reduces the impact of the sharply enhanced surface effect and lateral diffusion effect on the device performance due to the increase in the surface-volume ratio. Thus, the mutual confirmation between simulation and experimental results not only validates the reliability of the theoretical model but also provides direct evidence for the applicability of this scheme at the nanoscale.

For the final display system, the feasibility of the system depends on whether the IC bandwidth matches the theoretically optimal operating frequency of the NCI-LEDs. The key characteristic of NCI-LED lies in the fact that its optoelectronic performance is governed by the charge-induction capability of the equivalent capacitance of the insulating layer (Fig. S7). The thicker the insulating layer, the weaker the charge induction capability of its equivalent capacitance, resulting in a lower frequency threshold required for the induced charge to reach saturation. But this result is accompanied by a decline in optoelectronic performance. Therefore, for the structurally fixed NCI-LEDs, their inherent characteristics determine the theoretical upper limit of the operating frequency. In addition, the optimal frequency remains almost constant with variations in pixel size, indicating no significant correlation with this parameter (Fig. S8). This characteristic demonstrates that the NCI mode can effectively avoid performance non-uniformity caused by dimensional variations, thereby enhancing the operational stability of multi-scale device integration. Another important role of our work lies in providing theoretical guidance for the driver integration of the NCI mode. Specifically, the optimal device structural parameters and the corresponding IC bandwidth design can be determined based on the requirements of the display function.

Conclusion

In summary, the working mechanism of the electrical crosstalk induced by MDC inside NCI-nLED array is revealed by the finite element simulations and the effectiveness of crosstalk suppression in NCI mode array is proved by experimental tests. Because the performance of the NCI-nLED is highly sensitive to the applied AC electric field, the crosstalk behavior is more diversified than that of the injection nLED. This crosstalk behavior varies with voltage amplitude, frequency, insulation thickness, and spacing between nLED. By analyzing an equivalent circuit model, the generation process of electrical crosstalk is elucidated. Moreover, the study demonstrates that adjusting the etching depth of the nLED array, that is, the unetched n-GaN layer as a voltage dividing resistor, can effectively further suppress crosstalk, and the crosstalk ratio can be reduced to a minimum of 0.08%. Finally, through the fabrication and testing of injection mode and NCI mode arrays, we verified that the high-frequency oscillation of the internal carrier in NCI mode can effectively suppress crosstalk. This work offers valuable insights into the NCI mode and provides a new perspective on display technology related to the nLED array.
