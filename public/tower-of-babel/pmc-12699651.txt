Multifrequency MR elastography for grading inflammation in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: a pilot study
Authors: Amirhosein Baradaran Najar, Guillaume Gilbert, Anton Volniansky, Elige Karam, Audrey Fohlen, Maxime Barat, Emmanuel Montagnon, Hélène Castel, Jeanne-Marie Giard, Marie-Pierre Sylvestre, Bich N Nguyen, Guy Cloutier, Elijah Van Houten, An Tang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNoninvasive grading of liver inflammation in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) remains an unmet clinical need.PurposeTo evaluate the diagnostic performance of multifrequency MR elastography (MMRE) for grading liver inflammation and diagnosing metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).Materials and methodsIn this prospective, single-site study, participants underwent MMRE at 30, 40, and 60 Hz on a 3T system. Multifrequency dispersion coefficient (α), storage modulus (G'), loss modulus (G”), and magnitude of the shear modulus (|G*|) were computed. The reference standard was histopathological analysis of needle biopsy specimens. The MASLD activity score was computed to assign MASH status. Univariate and multivariable correlation and areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs) were assessed.ResultsOf the 72 participants enrolled, 60 (12 healthy, 48 MASLD) were analyzable. Introduction

Chronic liver disease arises from various etiologies and represents a major global burden.1 Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is prevalent and associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases.2 MASLD is characterized by steatosis and may be accompanied by inflammation, leading to fibrosis and cirrhosis.3 Lobular inflammation and ballooning represent key indicators of disease activity in advanced metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).4,5 Differentiating simple steatosis from MASH currently requires liver biopsy. Clinically validated tests, such as MRI proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) for steatosis grading and MR elastography (MRE)-determined stiffness for fibrosis staging, are available. However, no widely accepted or clinically validated diagnostic exam currently exists for the noninvasive assessment of liver inflammation. This study evaluates multifrequency MRE parameters for grading inflammation and diagnosing MASH.

MRE is a noninvasive technique primarily used to estimate hepatic shear stiffness for fibrosis staging.6,7 The viscoelastic, frequency-dependent behavior of tissue requires measuring elasticity, viscosity, and how these properties disperse with frequency.8–10 Multifrequency MRE (MMRE) applies vibrations at discrete frequencies and estimates the shear modulus as a function of frequency using rheologic models.11–13 From these data, the frequency dispersion coefficient summarizes the rate of change in the shear modulus across the applied band, reflecting the tissue’s fluid-solid balance.10 Inflammation alters liver microstructure and viscoelastic behavior; accordingly, MMRE parameters may provide noninvasive information about lobular inflammation and ballooning across the spectrum from normal liver to MASLD and MASH.10,14

We prospectively evaluated whether MMRE parameters, especially the dispersion coefficient α, are associated with lobular inflammation and MASH on biopsy.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a prospective, single-site cross-sectional ancillary study to a diagnostic clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier no: NCT03572465). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the participating institution, and all participants provided written informed consent. The main study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of investigational ultrasound and MRI techniques in healthy volunteers and MASLD patients, using histopathology as the reference standard in patients.

Participant selection

From January 2020 to June 2024, both healthy volunteers and participants were consecutively enrolled to capture a representative range of diseases. Non-MASLD volunteers were adults with no risk factors for liver steatosis, such as type 2 diabetes, alcohol consumption over 60 g/day, lipogenic medication use, or a BMI (body mass index) over 25 kg/m2, and no liver steatosis as determined by MRI-PDFF <5%. MRI-PDFF was obtained for research purposes in all healthy volunteers and participants; the volunteer cohort had a mean PDFF of 2.5 ± 1.6% (range 0.8%-5.9%). MASLD participants were adults with suspected or confirmed MASLD or MASH who required a liver biopsy as part of their clinical standard of care. Participants were excluded if they had other causes of chronic liver disease or prior liver transplantation.

Multifrequency MR elastography

MRI sequences were acquired on an Achieva TX 3T MRI (Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands) from January 2020 to November 2022 and then on an upgraded Achieva dStream 3T MRI (Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands) after November 2022. Both MRI configurations used a 2-channel body coil for signal transmission. For signal reception, Achieva TX had a 16-channel surface array coil, and Achieva dStream had a combination of a 12-channel posterior array and a 16-channel anterior array. No harmonization analysis between platforms was performed, considering that the sequence parameters remained exactly the same before and after the upgrade. Synchronized mechanical vibrations of 30 Hz, 40 Hz, and 60 Hz were performed using the eXpresso sequence15 with 3-dimensional motion-encoding and a commercially available active transducer (Resoundant, Rochester, MN, United States). Key sequence parameters for the extrinsic MRE sequences are provided in Table S1. Preprocessing, blinding, and segmentation protocols are detailed in the Supplemental Materials. No harmonization analysis between platforms was performed, as sequence parameters and reconstruction pipeline were identical before and after the upgrade.

MRE post-processing

Nonlinear inversion (NLI) jointly reconstructed viscoelastic properties from displacement fields at 30, 40, and 60 Hz.16,17 A fractional power-law model fitted across frequencies, yielding 2 global parameters (an amplitude term and the dispersion coefficient α), from which frequency-specific storage (G′), loss (G″), and magnitude (|G*|) shear moduli were computed.18 G′ and G″ quantify elastic and viscous behavior, respectively, and |G*| combines both; moduli are reported at 60 Hz. The coefficient α summarizes frequency dispersion without assigning a mechanism and was a primary parameter of interest.10,19–21 All metrics derive from a single multifrequency reconstruction; implementation details are provided in the Supplemental Materials. Access to the NLI reconstruction pipeline and pre- and post-processing scripts is available upon reasonable request. In a subgroup of 20 participants, 2 abdominal imaging fellows (radiologists, each with 1 year of abdominal imaging experience) independently performed whole-liver segmentations.

Reference standard

For participants, liver biopsies were obtained with 16- or 18-gauge needles in the right liver lobe using an intercostal approach. An expert hepatopathologist (27 years of experience) analyzed histology slides and applied the MASH Clinical Research Network scoring system (5). Steatosis was graded from 0 to 3 (S0 to S3), lobular inflammation was graded from 0 to 3 (A0 to A3), hepatocellular ballooning was graded from 0 to 2 (B0 to B2), and fibrosis was staged from 0 to 4 (F0 to F4). MASH diagnosis was based on MASLD activity score, which was computed as the unweighted sum of scores for steatosis, lobular inflammation, and ballooning (5). Scores of 0 to 2 were classified as non-MASH, 3 to 4 as borderline, and 5 to 8 as MASH. As it would have been unethical to perform liver biopsies on healthy volunteers, they were presumed to have no steatosis, fibrosis, inflammation, or ballooning based on inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Statistical analysis

Primary analyses were prespecified as correlations and ordinal discrimination for lobular inflammation; subgroup analyses were exploratory and not powered. Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 3.4.3; R Foundation), and details of packages are provided in the Supplemental Materials. 95% CIs were generated by non-parametric bootstrap resampling using the boot package. A 2-sided P < .05 defined statistical significance. Normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± SD; non-normal continuous variables as median (IQR). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test, with P-values reported in Table S2. The impact of the time interval between liver biopsy and MRE was evaluated by sensitivity analysis. Associations between steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning, fibrosis, and viscoelastic parameters were quantified with Spearman rank correlations. A multiple regression analysis was conducted to determine the relationship between histopathological features, including steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning, and fibrosis, and parameters α, G', G”, and |G*| using backward least-squares regression with Akaike information criterion (AIC) stopping. Group differences in viscoelastic parameters across histologic grades and stages were tested with Kruskal-Wallis; when significant, pairwise contrasts used Dunn’s test with Bonferroni adjustment. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for each multivariable model to document the absence of problematic multicollinearity.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was applied to each viscoelastic parameter. ROC curves were generated with the empirical non-parametric method, and areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) with 95% CIs were obtained. For every dichotomous comparison, the threshold that maximized Youden’s index was selected; at that threshold, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the corresponding confusion-matrix counts (true positive, true negative, false positive, false negative) were reported. High diagnostic accuracy was defined a priori as AUC >.80. Pairwise AUCs were compared with DeLong’s test.22 Overall ordinal accuracy of each viscoelastic parameter across the full histology-determined grading scales was quantified with the non-parametric Obuchowski score.23 Subgroup comparisons between histopathological grades were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Agreement between readers for viscoelastic parameters was assessed with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman analysis; agreement was categorized as poor (ICC < 0.50), good (0.50-0.79), very good (0.80-0.89), or excellent (≥0.90).

Diagnostic performance

For grading lobular inflammation, the multifrequency dispersion coefficient α had AUCs of 0.85 (0.75-0.96), 0.96 (0.89-1.00), and 0.94 (0.84-0.98) for A0 vs A1-A3, A0-A1 vs A2-A3, and A0-A2 vs A3, respectively (Figure 4). The diagnostic performance of mechanical parameters calculated using MMRE is summarized in Table 4, all metrics for all dichotomizations in Table S7, and confusion matrix at the optimal thresholds in Table S8. Across these contrasts, AUCs of α were significantly higher than those of G', G”, and |G*|. For the highest dichotomization (ie, A0-A2 vs A3), the Youden threshold of 0.98 yielded sensitivity of 100% (92-100) and PPV of 53% (27-79). When the full ordinal scales were considered, α yielded Obuchowski scores of 0.83 (0.74-0.93), 0.95 (0.87-1.00), 0.88 (0.72-1.00), and 0.80 (0.67-0.89) for steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning, and fibrosis, respectively.

Inter-reader variability

Inter-reader agreement was excellent for α (ICC = 93), excellent for G' (ICC = 0.95), good for G” (ICC = 0.77), and excellent for |G*| (ICC = 0.93). The Bland-Altman analyses show that the Mean bias and 95% limits of agreement for the inter-reader variability were −0.01 ± 0.06 for α, 0.02 ± 0.54 for G', 0.08 ± 0.86 for G”, and 0.06 ± 0.84 for |G*| (Figure S3).

Discussion

This prospective single-center pilot study evaluated the diagnostic performance of MMRE viscoelastic parameters for assessing inflammation using biopsy as the reference, including participants with MASLD, MASH, and healthy volunteers. The α parameter moderately correlated with lobular inflammation after adjusting for steatosis, ballooning, and fibrosis, and showed high apparent AUCs for differentiating inflammation grades and moderate AUCs for MASH, in a single-center cohort. Additionally, α varied significantly across inflammation grades within each fibrosis stage. Inter-reader agreement was excellent for the multifrequency dispersion coefficient, storage modulus, and shear modulus, and good for the loss modulus.

In this cohort, α achieved AUCs of 0.85 to 0.96 for grading lobular inflammation and AUCs of 0.81 to 0.85 for MASH diagnosis. This classification performance is comparable to that of other tests proposed such as ultrasound-based 2D-shear wave elastography dispersion for grading lobular inflammation in MASLD (AUC of 0.85-0.89), FibroScan aspartate aminotransferase (FAST) for diagnosis of active fibrotic MASH (AUC of 0.85), MRI-based corrected T1 (cT1) for diagnosis of MASH (AUC of 0.78) or corrected T1 aspartate transaminase fasting glucose (cTAG) for diagnosis of MASH with significant fibrosis (AUC of 0.90).24–27 Also, the results are similar to those of an MMRE dispersion parameter that achieved an AUC of 0.90 for grading inflammation in viral hepatitis.28

We found that α decreased with higher lobular inflammation grade and with ballooning. This is consistent with the findings of other elastography studies. In an animal model of pancreatic inflammation, Rebours V et al reported that the shear wave dispersion coefficient decreased with higher inflammation grades.29 In the human liver, Garteiser et al found that the dispersion coefficient decreased with higher necro-inflammatory grades in chronic viral hepatitis.10 Together, these findings suggest that inflammatory changes are associated with a decrease in the dispersion coefficients.

The high classification performance of α achieved in this study may be attributed to the use of NLI-MMRE. This inversion method employs a nonlinear optimization process to simultaneously estimate the viscoelastic parameters across all 3 measurement frequencies based on the power-law formulation, such that frequency dispersion is included directly within the image reconstruction process. This contrasts with other studies that first reconstructed |G*|, G′, and G″ at each drive frequency and then fit a power-law model.10,11 The proposed NLI approach eliminates potential inaccuracies due to separately fitting models post-reconstruction, resulting in more precise parameter estimation and higher classification performance.

In this cohort, we identified α thresholds of 1.11 for detecting the presence of lobular inflammation, 1.05 for detecting lobular inflammation grades 2 or 3, and 0.98 for identifying lobular inflammation grade 3. Given its reproducibility, α warrants further validation in multicenter settings. α could, if validated, complement PDFF and stiffness as part of a noninvasive panel for MASH activity monitoring.

The mean loss modulus reported in this study was higher than some published values, possibly because of dispersive behavior.30,31 Both the elastic and viscous components of the shear modulus increase with drive frequency, modulated by the frequency dispersion coefficient. In addition, the current cohort focuses on MASLD and MASH, a disease known to elevate the loss modulus. The mean loss modulus of 1.3 kPa reported here is within the range of values reported by Darwish et al32 (1.2 kPa at 60 Hz in MASLD) and by Garteiser et al10 (1.4 kPa at 56 Hz in chronic viral hepatitis).

This pilot study was limited by a modest single-center sample size, incomplete representation of fibrosis-inflammation combinations, and the absence of external validation. The median biopsy-to-MMRE interval was 42 days, which may introduce temporal bias due to potential changes in inflammation activity over a time scale of weeks. A platform change occurred without a harmonization analysis. The overall rate of technical failure was 15%. The current implementation of MMRE requires a complete dataset, including all frequencies, to compute the viscoelastic parameters. Hence, if any frequency data are missing for a given participant, for example, due to exam interruption by a participant or a technical problem during acquisition, the calculation of viscoelastic parameters is impaired. This indicates the need for prospective sequence monitoring and reacquisition if data from any single frequency are missing. Reliance on needle biopsy read by a single expert hepatopathologist introduces reference-standard limitations, including sampling error, absence of interobserver reproducibility estimates, and potential staging variability; such errors would generally bias imaging-histology agreement toward the null. Detailed comorbidity data (alcohol intake, metabolic medications, diabetes, and hypertension) were not collected for all MASLD participants, precluding adjustment for these confounders. The cross-sectional design, single 3T platform, restricted frequency band (30-60 Hz), and 2-reader inter-reader agreement sub-study further limit generalizability; larger, multicenter longitudinal studies are warranted.

In conclusion, this prospective single-center, pilot study, involving both healthy volunteers and MASLD participants, found that the multifrequency dispersion coefficient (α) was most strongly associated with inflammation, supporting its potential as a noninvasive biomarker pending external validation.
