WaveletInception Networks for Drive-by Vibration-Based Infrastructure Health Monitoring Journal: Engineering Applications of Artificial Intelligence
Reza Riahi Samani, Alfredo Núñez, Bart De Schutter

Abstract
This paper presents a novel deep learning-based framework for infrastructure health monitoring using drive-by vibration response signals. Recognizing the importance of spectral and temporal information, we introduce the WaveletInception-BiLSTM network. The WaveletInception feature extractor utilizes a Learnable Wavelet Packet Transform (LWPT) as the stem for extracting vibration signal features, incorporating spectral information in the early network layers. This is followed by 1D Inception networks that extract multi-scale, high-level features at deeper layers. The extracted vibration signal features are then integrated with operational conditions via a Long Short-term Memory (LSTM) layer. The resulting feature extraction network effectively analyzes drive-by vibration signals across various measurement speeds without preprocessing and uses LSTM to capture interrelated temporal dependencies among different modes of information and to create feature vectors for health condition estimation. The estimator head is designed with a sequential modeling architecture using bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) networks, capturing bi-directional temporal relationships from drive-by measurements. This architecture allows for a high-resolution, beam-level assessment of infrastructure health conditions. A case study focusing on railway track stiffness estimation with simulated drive-by vibration signals shows that the model significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods in estimating railway ballast and railpad stiffness parameters. Results underscore the potential of this approach for accurate, localized, and fully automated drive-by infrastructure health monitoring.

Introduction

Efficient maintenance is fundamental to the sustainability, safety, and cost-effectiveness of transportation infrastructure. In 2020, for example, maintenance and renewal for railway infrastructure in Europe amounted to €10.47 billion and €11.24 billion, respectively, accounting for 52% of total infrastructure-related expenditures
[
1

. An effective maintenance strategy heavily relies on its underlying monitoring techniques. Early and accurate fault detection is crucial for minimizing downtime, extending service life, and reducing overall lifecycle and maintenance costs. Traditionally, railway infrastructure health monitoring has relied on expert visual inspections or deploying sensing devices in strategic locations. However, these methods are often time-consuming, labor-intensive, and limited in scale. By overcoming such challenges, vehicle-based monitoring tools have gained significant attention

Drive-by vibration response analysis is a powerful and widely adopted vehicle-based monitoring technique that enables cost-effective and frequent health assessments across the entire infrastructure network under operational conditions. In particular, in the context of railway track and bridge infrastructures, vibration signals collected by accelerometers mounted on operational vehicles offer a cost-effective and scalable means of assessing infrastructure conditions

Several approaches have been reported in the literature for the analysis of drive-by vibration response signals

[

,

. While these approaches offer physical interpretations, they are often limited by the complexity of selecting the most relevant features and requiring experts’ experience in defining handcrafted features, which can hinder an automated process with high performance.

In recent years, deep learning methods for analyzing drive-by vibration responses have gained significant attention. However, they still encounter key challenges. Most notably, existing approaches often struggle to effectively capture the multi-scale, non-stationary characteristics of high-frequency drive-by vibration signals. Wavelet-inspired neural networks can decompose signals into time-frequency components, presenting a promising solution for extracting meaningful multi-resolution features from this data. Nonetheless, there is currently no fully automated, supervised learning framework that integrates these capabilities. Additionally, current methods typically have difficulty accommodating varying train speeds, which limits their robustness in real-world applications under diverse operational conditions. Furthermore, existing deep learning frameworks overlook the incorporation of operational factors—–such as measurement speed, rail profile, or track alignment–—when analyzing drive-by vibration data, failing to account for the influence of these factors. To address these research gaps, this paper introduces a novel deep learning methodology for monitoring the health of railway infrastructure using drive-by vibration responses. The key contributions of this work include:

1.

A WaveletInception network is proposed for feature extraction from vibration signals. This network combines a learnable wavelet packet transform as its initial module with subsequent Inception blocks. The stem module directly incorporates spectral information into the deep learning framework and decomposes drive-by vibration signals into various time-frequency scales. This design allows the model to learn meaningful kernels using the learnable wavelet transform filters in the early stages of the architecture while capturing additional multi-scale features in the deeper layers through Inception blocks that utilize multi-scale filter banks. Comparison of our proposed WaveletInception feature extraction netwrok to state-of-the-art feature extraction methods shows significant improvements in both estimation accuracy and computational efficiency.

2.

A feature fusion approach is designed to incorporate operational conditions—such as measurement speed, rail profile, or track alignment,—into the vibration signals analysis. These measurement conditions can significantly affect the drive-by vibration signal analysis. For example, measurement speed directly alters the excitation levels and the spatial frequency of the drive-by vibration response measurements. Therefore, integrating measurement conditions, such as the measurement speed, into the analysis improves estimation performance by better characterizing the excitation levels and effectively accounting for the underlying factor for variations in the signal lengths. To achieve this, we propose employing an LSTM layer in the feature extraction network to fuse multiple features and to capture interrelated temporal features between different mode of inputs.

3.

The proposed model architecture processes signals in the time domain and effectively handles signals with different measurement speeds and input lengths without requiring any preprocessing. This design broadens the model applicability to a wider range of drive-by measurements under varying measurement speeds and enables a fully automated infrastructure monitoring process.

4.

A bidirectional long-short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) module is proposed for the estimation head of the network. Since the health condition of one sequence is also reflected in the drive-by measurements obtained over the neighboring sequences, the Bi-LSTM network incorporates information from both forward and backward paths of drive-by measurements. This integration notably improves the performance of drive-by health condition estimation. Furthermore, this approach enhances the resolution of infrastructure health assessments at the component or beam level.

5.

Our proposed WaveletInception-BiLSTM network demonstrates notable improvements in estimation accuracy and computational efficiency compared to state-of-the-art deep learning models for drive-by vibration signal analysis.

Preliminaries

3.1
Discrete Wavelet Transform

The wavelet transform is a powerful mathematical tool for analyzing signals in the time and the frequency domain
[
31
]
. Unlike the Fourier transform, which provides only a frequency-domain representation, the wavelet transform offers a multi-resolution analysis by decomposing signals into components at various scales. This capability makes it particularly effective for analyzing non-stationary signals, such as drive-by vibration signals.

The Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) is a computationally efficient version of the wavelet transform, widely used for signal and image processing. The basic operation of DWT is the convolution operation of a low-pass and a high-pass filters, followed by a downsampling by a factor of two, which can be denoted as

\begin{cases}(x^{\mathrm{l}})_{n}=(f^{\mathrm{l}}*x)_{2n}\\
(x^{\mathrm{h}})_{n}=(f^{\mathrm{h}}*x)_{2n}\end{cases}

(1)

where
x^{\mathrm{l}}
and
x^{\mathrm{h}}
correspond to the low-pass (approximation) and high-pass (details) filtered input data with a downsampling rate of 2,
f^{\mathrm{l}}
and
f^{\mathrm{h}}
are low-pass and high-pass filters,
*
is the convolution operation,
x
is the input signal with the signal length of
2n
. The perfect reconstruction and orthogonality property of the DWT ensure that all information is retained in its coefficients despite the downsampling
[
26
,
31
]
. Taking the Haar filter as a basic and simple DWT example, the low-pass and high-pass filters are defined as

\begin{cases}f^{\mathrm{l}}=\,\left[1,\,1\right]\\
f^{\mathrm{h}}=\left[1,-1\right]\end{cases}

(2)

where its binary step structure allows for a clear separation between high-frequency and low-frequency components, making it suitable for applications like edge detection and vibration signal analysis
[
26
]
.

3.2
Discrete Wavelet Packet Transform and LWPT

The discrete Wavelet Packet Transform (WPT) decomposes a signal into uniform frequency bands of a specified size
[
31
]
. With its multi-level hierarchical structure, the WPT performs multi-resolution analysis, where the output at each level serves as the input for the next level. The basic block of WPT is the convolution operation of the selected DWT as described in Eq.
1
. This convolution operation enhances the frequency resolution, as each wavelet coefficient represents half of the original frequency spectrum, but reduces the time resolution. Repeating this process further divides the frequency spectrum into four equal bands, progressively refining the frequency analysis. In fact, the WPT operates through a tree-like structure defined by
L
layers, as demonstrated in Figure
1
, where each layer represents an iteration of the transformation applied to the outputs from the previous layer
[
31
]
.

3.3
1DCNN

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is a deep learning architecture designed to extract spatial and temporal features from input data using convolutional operations with filter banks
[
32
]
. While CNNs are commonly associated with image data, their 1D variants are particularly effective for processing sequential or time-series data, such as vibration and audio signals. A 1DCNN operates on one-dimensional signals, where convolutional kernels in a filter bank slide along the temporal dimension. These networks are designed to capture local patterns or dependencies in the input signal. This process generates feature maps that capture abstract representations of the input and highlight different aspects of the input data. The formula for a typical convolutional layer is

y_{f}=\mathrm{conv1D}(W_{f},x)+b_{f}

where

Long Short-term Memory (LSTM) Cell

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are a type of Recurrent Neural Networks designed to capture temporal dependencies and address the vanishing gradient problem common in traditional Recurrent Neural Networks

33

34
]
. Each LSTM cell contains three gates—input, forget, and output—that regulate the flow of information, along with a memory cell that retains relevant long-term context, as illustrated in Figure

Given a sequence input
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
, the LSTM updates its hidden state
h_{t}
and cell state

at each time step

x_{t}

While a standard LSTM processes sequences in a single forward direction, a Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) enhances this by incorporating both forward and backward passes. This allows the network to capture past and future context simultaneously, improving performance in tasks where context from both directions is valuable, such as drive-by vibration response analysis.

3.5
Multimodal data fusion

Multimodal data fusion involves combining different types of data, such as vibration signals, LiDAR, audio, or speed measurements, each of which provides complementary insights into the same phenomenon. Integrating multiple data sources can result in more robust, accurate, and generalizable models
[
35
]
. Existing multimodal fusion methods can be categorized into three main types: feature-level fusion, decision-level fusion, and hybrid fusion
[
35
]
. Feature-level fusion entails extracting features from each modality and merging them into a unified representation. In contrast, decision-level fusion combines the outputs of separate models trained on individual modalities. Hybrid fusion integrates both feature and decision levels, taking advantage of the strengths of each approach
[
35
]
.

Feature fusion is widely used across various domains to exploit correlations among multiple input modalities, sensors, or extracted features. This method leverages inherent correlations between features from different modalities, thereby enhancing the training process. However, it does face challenges related to temporal synchronization, as features from closely related modalities may be sampled at different time instants
[
36
]
. For instance, in
[
37
]
, a hybrid 1D CNN-LSTM network is proposed that combines autoregressive features, discrete wavelet features, and empirical mode decomposition features from multiple sensors into a unified representation for model training. Similarly,
[
38
]
utilized an attention mechanism to integrate features extracted by a CNN-LSTM network from three distinct input sources: environmental data, vehicle data, and driver eye data.

In the current paper, we propose a feature fusion approach that concatenates features obtained from vibration response signals and operational conditions. We use an LSTM layer to capture interrelated and temporal dependencies within the feature extraction network.

4.1
Problem statement

Infrastructure health monitoring using vibration response signal analysis requires extracting features across multiple resolutions and scales. Features identified at different frequencies and resolutions can provide insights into the health conditions of various infrastructure components
[
7
,
8
]
. Previous studies
[
14
,
7
,
8
]
have emphasized the importance of spectral features derived from the frequency domain of drive-by vibration response signals for structural health monitoring. Therefore, our current paper develops a deep learning architecture incorporating spectral information into the feature extraction process for vibration signals. While CNNs are widely recognized as universal approximators, their effectiveness in performing spectral analysis is still not well understood
[
28
]
. Therefore, instead of relying solely on CNN filters, we integrate wavelet-inspired networks into our feature extraction process to ensure that spectral information is effectively included.

One of the challenges in processing drive-by vibration signals is dealing with variations in the measurement speed, which lead to different signal lengths and excitation levels. Signals collected at varying speeds display different spatial frequencies and, consequently, have different lengths. Most deep learning models, especially those that rely on CNNs, need fixed input sizes. This requirement often necessitates specific preprocessing techniques, such as resampling
[
21
]
or resizing
[
20
]
the wavelet transforms of the input signals to ensure a consistent length suitable for deep learning networks. However, such preprocessing can hinder fully automated drive-by signal processing and may not address the core reasons behind the variations in signal length and excitation levels. Therefore, it is essential to develop techniques that can manage variable input lengths without depending on preprocessing, while also taking into account the underlying causes of these variations.

Previous research on infrastructure health monitoring has primarily focused on developing aggregated health condition indices for segments of 30
[
9
]
or 200 meters
[
39
]
. However, performing a detailed health condition assessment and defect localization at the component level can provide more valuable insights into infrastructure monitoring. Gaining a deeper understanding at the component level can significantly improve maintenance planning and decision making. Therefore, it is crucial for this paper to develop a methodology that estimates health conditions with greater resolution at the component level.

Our proposed WaveletInception-BiLSTM Networks address these challenges by combining several key components: a WaveletInception module for extracting features from vibration signals, a feature fusion technique to integrate operational conditions into the analysis, and a BiLSTM sequential modeling framework for estimating health conditions.

The WaveletInception feature extractor includes an LWPT stem module, which ensures that spectral information is incorporated during network training while significantly reducing the computational effort needed to analyze high-frequency signals. The inception blocks utilized in this architecture are designed to capture multi-scale, high-level, and more abstract features in the deeper layers. Our feature fusion method effectively integrates operational conditions and captures temporal dependencies within the feature extraction networks. This approach addresses the underlying causes of signal length variation, specifically, the measurement speed, and incorporates them into the analysis. Finally, the BiLSTM networks leverage both forward and backward information from the drive-by vibration response signal. This allows for a more accurate estimation of health conditions using a sequential approach. The proposed sequential technique significantly improves the resolution of estimations, allowing for analysis at the level of individual sequences or beams. The proposed methodology is outlined in detail in the following sections.

4.2
Feature Extraction Networks

Our proposed feature extraction network is a hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture, specifically designed for robustly analyzing drive-by vibration signals. Drive-by vibration signals collected at different measurement speeds are zero-padded to match the maximum length of each batch. These inputs are then fed into the WaveletInception vibration feature extraction networks. Additionally, the LSTM layer incorporates operational conditions and captures further temporal dependencies, modeling the interactions between measurement speed, signal length, and excitation levels. This approach allows the network to learn interrelated temporal relationships, thereby enhancing its ability to analyse vibration signals under varying operational conditions. A detailed discussion of the feature extraction networks follows.

4.2.1
WaveletInception Vibration Signal Feature Extraction Networks

The WaveletInception network (see Figure
4
) forms the core of the proposed framework by extracting features from vibration signals. It begins with an LWPT stem with
L
levels, which decomposes the input signal into high and low frequencies while downsampling the temporal dimension. The LWPT filters are initialized using discrete wavelet transform (DWT) bases, such as Haar and Daubechies’ db4
[
25
]
, and further refined through backpropagation. Haar DWT is the basic form of DWT, using step functions that ensure computational efficiency and excel at detecting abrupt changes within the signal. The db4 filter offers a finer frequency discrimination and captures more nuanced, smooth variations. These DWT-based initializations provide physically meaningful layers that enable effective time-frequency decomposition during early training. The LWPT also supports perfect reconstruction due to its orthogonality, allowing high-frequency signals to be downsampled without information loss
[
24
]
. This preserves key signal characteristics and enhances the quality of features passed to the subsequent 1D Inception blocks.

The 1D Inception blocks employed in the deeper layers take the physically informative outcome of the LWPT as input. As illustrated in Figure
3
, the 1D Inception blocks perform convolutions exclusively in the temporal dimension. The 1D Inception blocks extract high-level abstract features with their multi-scale filters. By leveraging filters of varying sizes, the Inception blocks efficiently capture multi-scale features relevant to vibration signal analysis. Therefore, they further extract more abstract and high-level features from the LWPT spectral information earlier in the network.

This architecture effectively integrates spectral information into the deep learning framework. The hyperparameters of the WaveletInception networks consist of the number of levels
L
for the LWPT decomposition and the number of Inception blocks that follow the LWPT. Figure
4
illustrates the fine-tuned WaveletInception networks after hyperparameter optimization for the case study conducted.

4.2.2
Feature Fusion and Temporal Features

To incorporate the operational conditions of vibration signal measurement, we propose using an LSTM layer that integrates features from different sources. The operational conditions, such as measurement speed, are represented in a feature vector through an embedding layer. This embedding is then concatenated with the extracted vibration features and passed to the LSTM layer. To ensure compatibility between the vibration signal features and the operational condition data, we align their temporal dimensions. This is accomplished by resampling the temporal dimension of operational condition inputs to match the sampling rate of the vibration signal features. As a result, the embedding layer for the operational conditions aligns with the temporal domain of the vibration signal features, allowing them to be concatenated into a single feature layer.

The concatenated features of the vibration signal and operational conditions are fed into the LSTM layer. This layer has several functions: it integrates temporal dependencies into the feature extraction process and captures interrelated factors between the vibration signal and operational conditions such as signal length variation, excitation level. Incorporating temporal features during feature extraction can significantly enhance the analysis of drive-by vibration signals
[
23
]
. Additionally, the LSTM layer considers the relationship between measurement speed, signal length, and padding length, which further refines the features it has learned.

The LSTM layer processes a sequence of concatenated features that integrate vibration signal features with corresponding operational conditions. This sequence spans the downsampled temporal dimension of the input signal, and at each time step, the LSTM outputs a feature vector capturing the learned temporal patterns. For beam-level health condition estimation, the downstream networks require
N
feature vectors — one for each beam within the targeted infrastructure segment. The value of
N
is determined by the physical layout of the structure, reflecting the number of beams present in the segment under analysis. Although LSTMs are capable of modeling long-term dependencies, meaning that the entire input sequence could theoretically be summarized using the last hidden state or any subset of final outputs, we propose selecting
N
LSTM outputs at uniformly spaced time steps. This selection strategy is designed to generate the required
N
inputs for beam-level health estimation, establishing a one-to-one correspondence between the selected feature vectors and the physical layout of the beams. While this spatial alignment is not strictly necessary due to the LSTM’s ability to capture long-term dependencies, our empirical results show that maintaining this correspondence leads to a slight improvement in the estimation accuracy.

The selected feature vectors are fed into a dropout layer to improve the robustness and generalization of these vectors. The hyperparameters of this network include the size of the embedding layer for operational conditions, the number of LSTM units in the LSTM layer, and the dropout ratio. Figure
4
illustrates the fine-tuned feature fusion networks used in the case study.

4.3
BiLSTM Structure Health Conditions Estimator

We propose a BiLSTM sequential modeling approach as the estimation head for infrastructure health condition monitoring using drive-by vibration signals (see Figure
4
). In this context, sequential modeling involves capturing spatial dependencies across a sequence of beams, where each time step corresponds to a single beam along the structure. By processing input feature sequences in both forward and backward directions, the Bi-LSTM networks combine hidden states in both directions, which allows the model to learn not only local interactions but also relationships between adjacent and distant beams. This bi-directional analysis enhances the model’s ability to capture interactions between consecutive beams, thereby improving the accuracy of health condition estimation.

Our approach is based on the observation that the health of an infrastructure component is reflected not only in its local vibration signals but also in the signals obtained from neighboring beams. For example, in railway tracks, considering the sleepers as the beams of the superstructure, the vibration signal over a particular sleeper is not solely indicative of that sleeper’s condition; it is also influenced by the conditions of adjacent sleepers. Therefore, estimating the health condition for individual sleepers is enhanced by incorporating features from both preceding and succeeding sleepers.

The feature extraction network, detailed in Section
4.2
, processes drive-by vibration response signals collected under varying operational conditions. It outputs a sequence of
N
feature vectors, where each vector corresponds to one of the
N
beams along the targeted infrastructure segment. These feature vectors serve as inputs—one for each time step—to the proposed BiLSTM-based sequential modeling framework.

BiLSTM networks process inputs for each time step and pass the outputs through fully connected layers. These layers map the learned representations to specific health condition indicators for each beam. This sequential modeling allows for detailed predictions at the beam level, which corresponds to the physical layout of the structure. Consequently, this method improves the resolution of health condition estimations and facilitates comprehensive assessments of individual components, providing valuable insights for maintenance planning.

The key hyperparameters in architecture include the number of BiLSTM layers, the number of LSTM cells per layer, and the size of the dense layers, all of which are tuned during the model validation step.

5
Illustrative case study of railway track health monitoring

Railway track stiffness serves as a key indicator of the health condition of railway infrastructure. It is primarily determined by the stiffness of the ballast substructure and the fastening components, such as bolts, clamps, and railpads. Estimating the stiffness parameters of these track components provides valuable insights into the condition of the railway track infrastructure. For instance, defects in railway sleepers, such as hanging sleepers or severe ballast crushing, have been shown to reduce track stiffness directly
[
40
]
. In the current case study, we demonstrate the estimation of track component stiffness using Axle-Box Acceleration (ABA) measurements. The ABA measurement system is a cost-effective drive-by inspection technique installed on operational trains. It captures vibration signals under operational conditions using accelerometer sensors mounted on the axle box of a moving vehicle
[
9
]
. With the availability of ABA systems, developing a fully automated framework to analyze the ABA drive-by vibration response can significantly enhance the monitoring capabilities of railway infrastructure over both time and space.

To demonstrate the application of our proposed methodology, we use simulated ABA vibration responses to estimate track stiffness parameters. In this case study, the track stiffness is characterized by two parameters: railpad stiffness and ballast stiffness, both estimated from the ABA signals. The track stiffness is represented as the vector
k=[k_{\mathrm{p}},k_{\mathrm{b}}]^{\intercal}
, where
k_{\mathrm{p}}
denotes the railpad stiffness and
k_{\mathrm{b}}
denotes the ballast stiffness.

5.1
Data

We simulate the Vehicle-track interactions for segments of 10 sleepers, obtained for four measurement speeds of 35 km/h, 50 km/h, 55 km/h, and 65 km/h, under four stiffness variation scenarios. Data for this case study is generated using the finite-element model described in
[
7
]
and is publicly available online
[
41
]
. This model accurately represents the physics of the vehicle-track interaction and simulates ABA measurements at different operational speeds under different stiffness variation scenarios. Figure
5
illustrates the schematic view of a 10-sleeper track segment and the corresponding ABA measurements collected over the sleepers.

The four simulated stiffness scenarios include uniform track stiffness, local stiffness drops, and stiffness variations in transition zones.

(I) Uniform track stiffness represents normal track conditions, where no stiffness variation occurs along the track. The ballast and railpad stiffness values are randomly sampled using a uniform distribution from the nominal (R1) or moderate (R2) stiffness ranges in Table
1
.

(II) Stiffness reduction in one sleeper simulates a defect such as a hanging sleeper, where a single unsupported sleeper leads to a localized reduction in stiffness. The stiffness range set R3 can represent multiple faults with varying severities, including degraded or missing railpads or local reductions in ballast stiffness, indicating potential component degradation.

(III) Stiffness reduction in three sleepers models a scenario where the substructure support is reduced over a wider length, causing decreased stiffness across three sleepers. In scenarios (II) and (III), stiffness values for railpads and ballast are simultaneously reduced using a uniform distribution from R1 to R2 or R3, or from R2 to R3. Moreover, the locations of stiffness reductions are randomized within the 10-sleeper segments to prevent spatial bias.

(IV) Transition zones represent stiffness variations between the first and second halves of track segments, mimicking conditions near railway bridges or viaducts. Stiffness values are selected randomly with a uniform distribution from R1 and R2 for each half of the segment to simulate the transition zones.

Training and validation

To evaluate the performance of our proposed model and to compare it with existing architectures, we trained, validated, and tested all models using fixed datasets for each phase. The training process utilized gradient descent with backpropagation, minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) loss function using the Adam optimizer

Hyperparameter tuning was conducted during the validation phase to enhance model performance. This process involved selecting the optimal configuration of parameters, including the number of layers, the number of units, dropout rates, and learning rates. We employed Bayesian optimization, supported by the Weights&Biases tool
[

]
, to efficiently search the hyperparameter space and track experiments. Each model was fine-tuned using 40 different hyperparameter configurations, with the objective of minimizing the validation loss. Figure

5.4
Discussion

The illustrative case study demonstrates that the proposed WaveletInception-BiLSTM model effectively estimates infrastructure stiffness using drive-by vibration signals. WaveletInception-BiLSTM outperforms the four baseline models, achieving a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 4.84% for railpad stiffness and 1.98% for ballast stiffness estimation (see Table
2
), while also demonstrating notable computational efficiency (see Table
3
). The proposed model architecture effectively estimates the track stiffness parameter in different simulated scenarios of track stiffness reduction (see Table
4
). Furthermore, the proposed WaveletInception networks outperform baseline vibration signal feature extraction methods (see Table
5
).

At the core of the proposed architecture is the WaveletInception network, which integrates an LWPT stem with 1D Inception blocks. The LWPT stem decomposes the input vibration signal into multi-resolution components while retaining all the original signal information due to its orthogonal properties. This decomposition not only improves the quality of the features extracted by subsequent layers but also allows for efficient down-sampling without any loss of information. As a result, this approach significantly reduces the computational burden of analyzing high-frequency vibration signals.

The WaveletInception network integrates spectral information directly into the deep learning architecture, aligning with previous studies that highlight the significance of frequency-domain features in structural health monitoring
[
7
,
14
,
8
]
. Its Inception blocks utilize filters of varying sizes to extract multi-scale, high-level features from the outputs of the stem module. By employing wavelet-based multi-resolution analysis, the network effectively distinguishes track components that affect different frequency ranges. As shown in Figure
10
, the LWPT module maintains the essential characteristics of the wavelet coefficients, even after fine-tuning, confirming that spectral information is preserved and integrated into the deep learning architecture.

Our proposed vibration signal feature extractors, db4-WaveletInception and Haar-WaveletInception, demonstrate significantly better performance compared to the baseline models (see Table
5
). While the db4 variant slightly outperforms the Haar variant in its learnable form, the trend reverses for the non-learnable stem versions, where Haar-WaveletInception performs slightly better. This may be attributed to the higher parameter count in db4-WaveletInception, which facilitates more effective learning. These findings underscore the necessity for future research to further investigate optimal configurations for the LWPT stem and its integration into the deep learning framework.

The proposed models also demonstrate a high degree of computational efficiency. Compared to baseline approaches, WI-LSTM and WI-BiLSTM require significantly fewer parameters and achieve faster deployment times (see Table
3
). Among them, WI-LSTM stands out as the most computationally efficient approach, offering both the lowest parameter count and the fastest deployment time. WI-BiLSTM, on the other hand, achieves the highest accuracy while still maintaining superior computational efficiency compared to the baseline approaches. This efficiency is primarily due to the LWPT stem module and the use of 1D inception blocks. The LWPT not only provides valuable, physically informative features in the early stages of the network but also significantly reduces computational requirements by downsampling high-frequency vibration signals.

Our proposed model architectures utilize 1D convolutional filters to analyze the vibration signal, which are computationally more efficient than 2D convolutional networks. Unlike methods that employ 2D convolutional networks on time-frequency representations, such as the continuous wavelet transform (CWT)
[
20
]
, our approach eliminates the extra computational load associated with processing the frequency dimension. Additionally, features extracted by 1D networks are often more physically interpretable, as they are derived from the time-domain signal. In 2D convolutional networks, where filters operate across both time and frequency, the resulting features may be more abstract and difficult to relate to specific physical properties of the signal. While 2D networks may capture more complex time-frequency patterns with abstract features, this complexity comes at the cost of higher computational demands and reduced interpretability.

The proposed drive-by vibration response feature extraction networks process the input signal directly in the time domain. It effectively processes signals collected at varying measurement speeds and lengths. By eliminating the need for data preprocessing or manual feature extraction, it enables a fully automated health condition monitoring system. Furthermore, it addresses the challenges posed by measurement speed variations without requiring resampling or any preprocessing of the vibration signals.

The feature extraction network architecture utilizes an LSTM layer to incorporate temporal dependencies during feature extraction. This approach allows for the identification of interrelated temporal dependencies between measurement speed information and vibration signal features, including signal length and excitation levels, as well as the inherent temporal characteristics of each input mode. The LSTM layer integrates information from the two modal inputs and generates feature vectors that are used for the subsequent health condition estimation.

From the LSTM outputs of the feature extraction network,
N
feature vectors are selected for the
N
beams in the infrastructure. While the last
N
LSTM outputs could be used directly, we propose a uniform selection strategy that aligns with the spatially uniform distribution of beams in the case study. This approach better reflects the physical layout of structures like railway tracks and bridges, where beams are evenly spaced. It can also be extended to non-uniform cases, as LSTMs effectively capture temporal dependencies. This selection strategy eliminates the framing approach proposed in our previous work
[
23
]
, which required precise knowledge of the signal’s spatial position to segment it over individual beams. This feature is particularly advantageous when measurement speed varies, making precise positioning challenging, while determining the number of beams over a curtain infrastructure segment remains straightforward. As a result, our method effectively generates the required
N
feature vectors for the downstream health condition estimation networks, considering the number of beams in the segment being analyzed.

The proposed sequential model for estimating health conditions shows excellent performance with both the WI-LSTM and WI-BiLSTM models. This approach is motivated by the long-term dependencies present in the drive-by vibration signals along the beam’s length. As demonstrated in Figure
7
, the average power of these vibration signals at specific locations is influenced by stiffness variations in adjacent beams. To enhance the health condition estimation of individual beams, the sequential modeling approach incorporates drive-by measurement features from neighboring sequences. This method also increases the resolution to the level of individual beams and components. Notably, the WI-BiLSTM model outperformed its WI-LSTM counterparts and other baseline models, as shown in Table
2
. This highlights the importance of including bidirectional information to improve infrastructure health monitoring algorithms. By utilizing data from both the forward and backward paths of drive-by vibration responses, the estimation accuracy of drive-by measurements is significantly enhanced. Overall, this sequential modeling approach effectively estimates the stiffness parameters of railpad and ballast for individual beams and components.

The illustrative case study considers track stiffness estimation in four stiffness scenarios: uniform stiffness, stiffness drops in one or three sleepers, and stiffness variations in transition zones (see Table
4
). The model achieves an overall MAPE of
2.81\%
for railway track stiffness estimation in transition zones, demonstrating its potential for structural health monitoring in critical areas such as bridge and viaduct approaches, where the stiffness changes due to structural transitions. Additionally, the model successfully identifies stiffness drops in one or three sleepers, effectively localizing conditions such as a hanging sleeper or ballast structural deformation. This capability makes it particularly applicable for monitoring railway tracks under normal uniform conditions and detecting localized structural defects. Furthermore, ballast stiffness estimation proves more accurate than railpad stiffness estimation. This may be due to the ballast’s greater influence on the overall track stiffness and vibration response, thereby allowing the model to detect the ballast stiffness variations more distinctly.

The dataset used in this study is based on the vehicle-track interaction finite-element simulation developed in
[
7
]
. While this case study effectively demonstrates the proposed framework, simulated drive-by signals may not fully capture the range of environmental and operational factors present in real-world conditions. For example, the simulation reflects a single vehicle-track configuration, whereas actual railway networks vary in rail types, fastening systems, sleeper designs, and spacing,—factors that are not fully represented in the current model
[
7
]
. Consequently, the current paper highlights the need for future research to validate the proposed WI-BiLSTM architecture using real-world drive-by vibration measurements for infrastructures such as bridges with a similar configuration of beams, where the operational conditions, such as speed, influence the vibrational data
[
21
,
42
]
.

The proposed WaveletInception-BiLSTM model relies solely on the learning process of neural network parameters to address noise. However, incorporating explicit denoising techniques during preprocessing can improve the noise robustness of the deep learning model
[
46
,
25
]
. Therefore, future research should investigate deep learning methods with explicit denoising techniques, particularly important for real-world applications, where dealing with noisy data is a common challenge.

6
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we have proposed a novel deep learning framework, namely WaveletInception-BiLSTM, for estimating infrastructure health conditions using drive-by vibration signals. The core of our methodology is a new feature extraction network, termed WaveletInception, which integrates Learnable Wavelet Packet Transform (LWPT) and 1D inception blocks to extract multi-scale, high-level representations from vibration signals effectively. Furthermore, the feature extraction network incorporates operational conditions—specifically, the measurement speed—through feature-level fusion using a Long Short-term Memory (LSTM) layer. This enables the model to learn interrelated features across different measurement conditions. For the health condition estimation head, we propose a sequential model based on BiLSTM networks. The model leverages temporal information from both the forward and backward directions of drive-by measurements. Additionally, the sequential modeling approach enables a more detailed resolution of estimations at the beam or component level, allowing for comprehensive assessments of different segments of infrastructure.

Future research will focus on three main directions. First, the current WaveletInception vibration signal feature extractor shows promise in integrating LWPT and 1D Inception blocks. Future research can explore adding residual skip connections
[
47
]
and/or transfer learning techniques
[
20
]
to enhance our WaveletInception architecture or similar models. Second, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the current paper is one of the first to incorporate operational conditions of drive-by vibration response into a deep learning model architecture. While we propose an LSTM layer to capture temporal dependencies during feature extraction and to fuse features from both the drive-by vibration signal and operational conditions, future research can investigate other temporal feature encoders, such as Gated Recurrent Units
[
48
]
and attention mechanism
[
49
,
50
]
, to further integrate temporal dependencies into the feature extraction network. Third, while the current paper demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed model using simulated data, future research should focus on validating its performance in real-world conditions through field measurements.
