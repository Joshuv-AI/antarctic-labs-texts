Professional self-efficacy and identity as predictors of spiritual care competence in nursing and midwifery students
Authors: Nila Norouzi, Mehrnaz Ahmadi, Solmaz Mohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpiritual care is a vital component of holistic nursing and midwifery. This study aimed to assess nursing and midwifery students’ perceived competence in spiritual care and to examine the predictive roles of two key constructs: professional self-efficacy (encompassing care, support, and professional situations) and professional identity (self-image, social modeling, retention benefits and turnover risks, social comparison and self-reflection, and independence of career choice).MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2024 to January 2025 among 201 undergraduate nursing and midwifery students who had completed at least two semesters and had begun clinical internships, ensuring exposure to both theoretical education and practical training. Participants completed a demographic questionnaire that included personal and educational characteristics, as well as selected items such as clinical work experience and previous training in spiritual care. They also completed the Iranian Scale for Assessment of Nurses’ Competencies in Spiritual Care, the Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale, and the Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students. Data were analyzed using t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression.ResultsA significant positive correlation was observed between spiritual care competence and both self-efficacy (r = 0.547, p < 0.001) and professional identity (r = 0.369, p < 0.001). Regression analysis revealed that professional self-efficacy (β = 0.475, p < 0.001), professional identity (β = 0.148, p = 0.022), prior training in spiritual care (β = 0.145, p = 0.017), and independence of career choice (β = 0.124, p = 0.037) were key predictors, explaining 37% of the variance.ConclusionThe findings suggest that students’ self-perceived ability to provide spiritual care is associated with their confidence, professional identity, and prior training. Incorporating comprehensive spiritual care training into curricula can enhance students’ confidence and skills, thereby better preparing them for holistic clinical practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Spiritual care competency, Professional self-efficacy, Professional identity, Nursing students, Midwifery students

Introduction

Spiritual care is a fundamental component of holistic nursing and midwifery, contributing to patients’ psychological well-being, resilience, and quality of communication during illness and crisis [1–3]. Recognized by the World Health Organization as the “fourth dimension of health,” spirituality has gained increasing attention in healthcare education and practice [4]. Despite its acknowledged importance, the integration of spiritual care into nursing education and practice remains inconsistent, and many nursing and midwifery students report inadequate preparation to deliver spiritual care effectively [5].

In Iran, the undergraduate nursing program is a four-year curriculum that integrates both theoretical and clinical education. Students begin their clinical placements from the second semester, allowing them to apply theoretical knowledge in real patient care settings at an early stage. By the third semester, they are exposed to both theory and practice, making them eligible for studies on competencies such as spiritual care [6, 7]. However, early clinical exposure does not necessarily translate into adequate preparedness.

Evidence from both Iranian and international studies highlights that nursing students continue to face challenges in developing competence in spiritual care. In Iran, several studies have reported that although students acknowledge the importance of spirituality in patient care, they often receive insufficient training and lack confidence in providing such care [8, 9]. More recently, Rabiei Vaziri et al. (2025) found that Iranian nursing students demonstrated moderate levels of spiritual care competence and sensitivity, underscoring the need for targeted educational interventions [10]. Similar findings have been reported internationally: a cross-sectional study in Turkey showed that students’ religious attachment was significantly associated with their spiritual care competence [11], while a systematic review of nursing education in the Middle East confirmed that current programs remain inadequate in preparing students for this aspect of care [12]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the gap between the recognized importance of spiritual care and students’ actual competence is a widespread issue.

Although the importance of spiritual care in nursing education has been widely acknowledged, most existing studies have primarily focused on describing students’ perceptions, attitudes, or general levels of competence [9, 10]. Far fewer investigations have examined the underlying predictors that may shape students’ ability to provide spiritual care effectively. In particular, the roles of professional self-efficacy and professional identity—two critical constructs that influence students’ confidence, motivation, and integration of professional values—have received limited empirical attention [13–15].

Given that nursing and midwifery are not only skill-based but also value-driven professions, understanding how students’ belief in their professional abilities and their sense of professional identity shape their readiness to provide spiritual care is essential. This study aims to address this gap by investigating the extent to which professional self-efficacy and professional identity predict spiritual care competence among nursing and midwifery students.

Professional self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to fulfill professional responsibilities and plays a crucial role in building the confidence needed to navigate complex situations, such as providing spiritual care [16]. This attribute is recognized as the foundation of competence and optimal performance in nursing and midwifery, and strengthening it is crucial for enhancing service quality and empowering human resources [17]. This study focuses on professional self-efficacy, which differs from clinical self-efficacy. Clinical self-efficacy pertains to an individual’s ability to perform specific technical tasks, such as administering injections, assessing vital signs, or managing pain [18]. In contrast, professional self-efficacy encompasses a broader range of skills, including confidence in fulfilling professional roles, making ethical decisions, engaging in interpersonal communication, and managing complex situations like spiritual care [19]. While most previous studies have focused on clinical self-efficacy and its relationship with competence in spiritual care among nurses [20] and nursing students [13], the relationship between professional self-efficacy and the commitment of nurses and midwives, particularly students, to comprehensive care remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

On the other hand, professional identity, which encompasses an individual’s understanding of their professional role, values, and commitments, can significantly influence motivation, responsibility, and the quality of clinical practice [21, 22]. Previous research has shown that a stronger professional identity not only enhances resilience and initiative when tackling the challenges of spiritual care but also plays a vital role in improving the quality of comprehensive care [23]. This concept has a bidirectional relationship with professional self-efficacy [19]. Professional identity encourages a commitment to human values and motivation for comprehensive care [24], while professional self-efficacy provides the confidence needed to handle complex situations such as spiritual care [13]. The interaction between these two variables can strengthen a sense of responsibility, empathy, and focus on the spiritual dimensions of care [15].

Although some studies have explored professional identity and self-efficacy among nursing and midwifery students, comprehensive research examining the relationships between these variables and spiritual care competence, particularly within the Iranian academic context, remains limited. A simultaneous investigation of professional identity and self-efficacy may offer deeper insights into the determinants of spiritual care competence, helping to inform strategies that enhance clinical and ethical education in nursing and midwifery. Accordingly, the present study was conducted to assess spiritual care competence and to examine the predictive roles of professional identity and professional self-efficacy among nursing and midwifery students.

Method

Design and sample

This descriptive-correlational study was conducted at the Faculty of Nursing, Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences in Iran, between September 2024 and January 2025. A convenience sampling approach was used. The survey link was distributed by class representatives in the official class groups on WhatsApp or Telegram. As all students in each class were members of their respective class group, the entire population of students who met the inclusion criteria had access to the survey link. The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula, targeting 191 participants from a population of 380 students, with a 95% confidence interval and 80% statistical power. To account for potential non-responses and ensure adequate sampling coverage, the survey was sent to 230 students, of whom 201 completed it, resulting in an 87% response rate. To ensure data quality, the survey was designed to prevent incomplete responses and incorporated basic checks to verify participant eligibility. The inclusion criteria required participants to be undergraduate nursing or midwifery students in their 2nd to 4th years of study (i.e., those who had completed at least two semesters, ensuring they had acquired basic care concepts and begun hospital internships), and to have provided informed consent to participate in the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, and informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants before data collection.

Measurements

Three validated instruments were used in this study. The Spiritual Care Competency Scale was originally developed and psychometrically validated in Iran. The other two instruments: the Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale and the Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students, were adopted in their validated Persian versions, which had previously undergone translation and psychometric testing in Iranian populations. We did not adapt or further develop any of the instruments in the present study; therefore, no additional CVI or IOC assessments were conducted.

The Iranian scale for assessment of nurses’ competencies in spiritual care (SANCSC)

The Iranian Scale for Assessment of Nurses’ Competencies in Spiritual Care (SANCSC) was developed by Adib-Hajbaghery et al. in 2016 as a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating professional competence in spiritual care [25]. The scale consists of 32 items distributed across five domains: knowledge (4 items), assessment and implementation of spiritual care (17 items), human values (6 items), attitudes (3 items), and self-recognition (2 items). Respondents rate each item using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), resulting in a total score between 32 and 160. According to the scoring guidelines, a total score of 118 or higher indicates high competence, scores between 74 and 117 reflect moderate competence, and scores of 73 or below signifies low competence in spiritual care. The psychometric properties of the SANCSC have been well-established, with internal consistency reliability coefficients (Cronbach’s alpha) reported to range from 0.75 to 0.93 across the entire scale and its subdomains [25, 26]. In the current study, the internal consistency of the SANCSC was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.951.

Nursing profession self-efficacy scale (NPSES)

The Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale (NPSES), originally developed by Caruso et al. (2016), is a specialized instrument designed to assess perceived professional self-efficacy among nurses [27]. The Persian version, validated by Lazemi and Barkhordari-Sharifabad (2023), comprises 19 items distributed across three subscales: professional situation (8 items), care situation (6 items), and support situation (5 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“not at all confident”) to 5 (“completely confident”), yielding a raw total score between 19 and 95. To enhance interpretability and comparability, total and subscale scores are standardized to a 0–100 scale. Based on this transformation, self-efficacy levels are categorized as follows: low (0–33.3), moderate (33.4–66.6), and high (66.7–100). The Persian version demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86; ICC = 0.83), confirming its reliability for evaluating professional self-efficacy in Iranian nursing populations [28]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was re-evaluated and confirmed, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.918, which indicates excellent reliability.

The professional identity scale for nursing students (PISNS)

The Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students (PISNS), originally developed by Hao et al., was utilized to assess the level of professional identity among nursing students. This scale comprises 17 items distributed across five dimensions: professional self-image (6 items), benefits of retention and risks of turnover (4 items), social comparison and self-reflection (3 items), independence of career choice (2 items), and social modeling (2 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), resulting in a total score that ranges from 17 to 85, with higher scores indicating a stronger professional identity [29]. In the study conducted by Haghighat et al., the Persian version of the questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.787) and a reported validity coefficient of 89% [30]. In this study, the internal consistency of the scale was further evaluated and confirmed, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.887.

Demographic information questionnaire

The questionnaire collected data on participants’ demographic characteristics, including sex, age, marital status, academic year, field of study, type of residence, grade point average (GPA), prior training in spiritual care (yes/no), clinical work experience (excluding internships) (yes/no), and expressed interest in the field of nursing or midwifery (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, were used to summarize demographic and study variables. To assess the normality of data distribution, the Shapiro–Wilk test was applied due to its higher statistical power for moderate sample sizes (n = 201). The results indicated p-values below 0.05 for most variables, suggesting deviations from normality. However, considering the relatively large sample size and the robustness of parametric tests to moderate non-normality—as supported by the Central Limit Theorem—parametric analyses were retained. In addition to statistical testing, visual inspections (histograms and Q–Q plots) and distribution indices were examined. Notably, the skewness and kurtosis values for all key variables fell within the acceptable range of -1 to + 1, indicating approximately normal distributions and further supporting the use of parametric methods. Accordingly, independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to compare mean scores of SANCSC, NPSES, and PISNS across various demographic groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied to examine relationships among the SANCSC, NPSES, and PISNS. Stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify significant predictors of competencies in spiritual care among nursing and midwifery students.

Results

Normality assessment

The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed for the three main variables. The professional identity score showed a non-significant result (p = 0.425), indicating normal distribution, while spiritual care competence (p = 0.019) and professional self-efficacy (p = 0.002) showed significant deviations from normality. However, skewness and kurtosis values for all variables were within the acceptable range of − 1 to + 1: professional identity (skewness = − 0.096, kurtosis = − 0.414), spiritual care competence (skewness = 0.059, kurtosis = − 0.562), and professional self-efficacy (skewness = − 0.270, kurtosis = 0.223). Taken together, these indicators suggest approximately normal distributions and justify the use of parametric analyses.

Descriptive statistics

A total of 201 undergraduate nursing and midwifery students participated in the study, all of whom were in their 2nd, 3rd, or 4th years of study, consistent with the stated inclusion criteria. The mean age of the participants was 22.92 ± 2.30 years. The majority were female (71.6%), and most (92.5%) were single. Of the total participants, 55.7% were enrolled in nursing programs, while the remainder were midwifery students. Notably, only 30.3% reported having received any training in spiritual care (see Table 1).

Analytical statistics

Group comparisons

The demographic characteristics of the students, categorized by their mean scores in competence in spiritual care, professional self-efficacy, and professional identity, are presented in Table 1. Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA indicated no significant differences in the total scores for competence in spiritual care, professional self-efficacy, and professional identity with respect to age, GPA, academic year, clinical work experience, or type of residence. However, significant differences were found based on marital status and prior training in spiritual care. Additionally, the field of study was associated with significant differences in the mean scores for competence in spiritual care and professional self-efficacy. A notable difference in professional self-efficacy was also observed between male and female students, while professional identity scores varied significantly based on students’ interest in their respective fields of study (nursing or midwifery) (see Table 1).

Regression analysis results

Independent variables that demonstrated significant associations with competence in spiritual care outcomes were incorporated into the regression model. A stepwise multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to assess the extent to which professional self-efficacy, professional identity, and selected demographic variables could predict variance in competence in spiritual care. The results indicated that professional self-efficacy (β = 0.475, p < 0.001), professional identity (β = 0.148, p = 0.022), prior training in spiritual care (β = 0.145, p = 0.017), and independence of career choice (β = 0.124, p = 0.037) were significant predictors of competence in spiritual care. These variables accounted for 37% of the total variance in competence in spiritual care (F = 30.680, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the model suggested that increases in professional self-efficacy, professional identity, and independence of career choice were associated with corresponding increases in competence in spiritual care scores of approximately 0.66, 0.26, and 1.54 points, respectively. Additionally, students who received spiritual care training scored on average 6.01 points higher in competence compared to those without training (see Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess spiritual care competence and to examine the associations between this competence and professional identity and professional self-efficacy among nursing and midwifery students at Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences.

In the present study, nursing and midwifery students reported high levels of competence in providing spiritual care, consistent with the findings of several previous studies involving nursing students [9, 10, 13]. In contrast, evidence from studies of practicing nurses indicates that they typically rate their competence in this area as moderate to low [31–33]. This discrepancy may seem unexpected, given the limited clinical exposure of students. One possible explanation is that the concepts of holistic and spiritual care have recently been integrated into nursing and midwifery curricula, which may have enhanced students’ awareness and mental preparedness [34, 35]. Furthermore, unlike practicing nurses and midwives who face heavy workloads, students are generally responsible for a limited number of patients under the supervision of clinical instructors. This environment provides more opportunities for effective communication, patient education, and active listening, which can contribute to improved perceived competence in spiritual care. These observations not only highlight current educational strengths but also invite deeper reflection on the broader role of nursing students in delivering spiritual care and the significance of their competence levels. As future frontline caregivers, nursing students play a critical role in addressing patients’ spiritual needs [36]. High competence in this area not only enhances holistic care but also supports students’ empathy, ethical sensitivity, and professional development [14]. On the other hand, low competence may result in missed opportunities for emotional support, reduced patient satisfaction, and increased stress or uncertainty among nurses [8, 37]. Therefore, evaluating and strengthening spiritual care competence during training is essential for both patient outcomes and the well-being of future nurses [9, 12].

In addition, most participants in the present study reported high levels of professional self-efficacy and a strong sense of professional identity. This finding aligns with several previous studies [38–41]. However, a study conducted in China among 1,051 freshman nursing students found that 15.6% belonged to the lowest self-efficacy group and 45% to the medium-low group, indicating that over 60% of participants reported low to moderate levels of professional self-efficacy [15]. Similarly, research from Uganda revealed that nursing and midwifery students face multiple barriers to professional identity formation, including a lack of effective mentorship, unclear role definitions, and low professional esteem [42]. These discrepancies may arise from contextual factors such as the quality of educational programs, institutional culture, societal perceptions of the profession, and access to academic support [43, 44]. Methodological differences, such as the use of alternative measurement instruments, may also account for these contrasting results. Therefore, while the current study reflects generally positive self-perceptions among participants, these findings should be interpreted in light of the educational context, cultural influences, and the potential for self-report bias.

The findings of this study indicate that students who received prior training in spiritual care reported higher competence in this area. Regression analysis confirmed that educational experience is significantly associated with competence. spiritual care competence. This supports previous studies that found participation in ethics and spirituality workshops improves students’ spiritual care skills [10, 14]. Additionally, a recent systematic review by Dewi et al. (2025) found that spiritual care training programs in Asia and the Middle East effectively boost students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practical abilities [12]. However, some research points out limitations of short-term training, especially when such programs lack clinical continuity or instructor support [45]. Therefore, an important conclusion is that theoretical training alone is insufficient; structured design, clinical integration, and reflective learning, embedded within comprehensive, evidence-based curricula, are essential for effectively developing spiritual competence and preparing students to address the complex spiritual needs of patients [10, 12–14].

The findings of the present study indicated that married students reported higher levels of professional self-efficacy, professional identity, and competence in spiritual care. These results align with previous research [46–48] and may reflect broader life experiences associated with marriage, such as emotional support or increased responsibility [49]. While it is plausible that married students develop interpersonal skills like communication and conflict resolution, this interpretation remains speculative and warrants further empirical investigation. Nevertheless, some studies have highlighted the potential challenges posed by family responsibilities, which may adversely affect students’ academic performance [50, 51]. Therefore, the influence of marital status on professional development is not uniform and may vary depending on individual circumstances and educational contexts. Consequently, a deeper understanding of students’ personal contexts, including their marital status, can inform the design of more effective support and counseling programs that complement formal education.

This study’s findings show that midwifery students feel more confident in providing spiritual care. This matches earlier research suggesting that midwifery students see themselves as more capable of offering spiritual support than nursing students [52]. These differences might stem from variations in curriculum focus, as midwifery programs often place greater emphasis on holistic and patient-centered approaches, including spiritual aspects, than general nursing programs [53]. Additionally, the closer and ongoing interactions with mothers in midwifery practice may boost students’ confidence and skills in offering spiritual support [54]. However, it is still unclear whether these differences are consistent across different educational settings or specific to our faculty. Future research could examine how institutional structures and curriculum priorities influence students’ perceived ability to deliver spiritual care across disciplines. Nonetheless, these findings highlight the importance of incorporating thorough spiritual care training into both nursing and midwifery programs.

The study’s findings revealed a significant relationship between students’ perceived competence in spiritual care and their levels of professional self-efficacy and professional identity. Regression analysis showed that both constructs, particularly the dimension of “autonomy in career choice” within professional identity, were significantly associated with higher levels of competence in spiritual care. These results indicate that students who consciously choose their careers, possess a strong professional identity, and are confident in their abilities are better prepared to provide sensitive and effective spiritual care. This pattern highlights the role of intrinsic motivation and self-belief in developing readiness for comprehensive care [55]. The integration of Bandura’s social-cognitive theory [56], Erikson’s psychosocial development framework [57], and Deci and Ryan’s self-determination theory [58] offers a coherent foundation for understanding how nursing and midwifery students develop competence in spiritual care. Bandura emphasizes the role of self-efficacy in shaping behavior and performance, which aligns with students’ confidence in managing complex care situations [56]. Erikson’s framework highlights identity formation as a key developmental task, supporting the idea that a strong professional identity enhances motivation and responsibility [57]. Deci and Ryan’s theory complements these perspectives by underscoring the importance of autonomy and intrinsic motivation—particularly relevant for students who independently choose their career paths [58]. Together, these theories suggest that competence in spiritual care emerges from the dynamic interplay of confidence, identity, and autonomous motivation within professional development. Within the framework of these theoretical perspectives, previous studies have emphasized the importance of these two constructs. For example, research by Chen et al. (2024) has highlighted the role of professional identity in addressing spiritual challenges [23]. Additionally, Sahebi (2023) [13], Cheng et al. (2021) [20], and Yang et al. (2024) [59] have identified clinical self-efficacy as a key factor associated with competence in spiritual care. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of strengthening professional identity and self-confidence in nursing and midwifery students prior to their entry into the clinical field.

These findings suggest that nursing education programs should not only include theoretical content on spiritual care but also provide experiential learning opportunities such as reflective practice, mentorship, and supervised clinical exposure. Embedding spiritual care into routine clinical training can help normalize it as a core component of nursing practice, thereby improving both student preparedness and patient-centered outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights that students’ professional self-efficacy, professional identity, independence of career choice, and prior training in spiritual care are significantly associated with their perceived competency in this area. As this was a cross-sectional study, these findings reflect associations rather than causal relationships. To effectively prepare nursing and midwifery students to deliver holistic and confident spiritual care, educational programs should incorporate structured training alongside mentorship and role modeling. When students observe experienced professionals providing compassionate care, they gain practical insights and begin to develop their own professional identity. Furthermore, offering opportunities for guided clinical practice, reflective dialogue, and learning from role models can enhance students’ self-efficacy and readiness. Students who had prior exposure to spiritual care training reported higher competence. Therefore, incorporating structured educational experiences—such as workshops, coursework, and clinical simulations—may contribute to enhancing students’ confidence and competence in addressing patients’ spiritual needs. Future research is needed to build on these findings. Longitudinal studies could track how students’ competence, self-efficacy, and professional identity evolve over the course of their education. Intervention studies may help determine whether targeted programs aimed at enhancing self-efficacy or professional identity lead to measurable improvements in spiritual care competence. Additionally, cross-cultural comparisons—such as between Iranian students and those in other regions—could offer insights into how cultural and institutional contexts shape students’ perceptions and preparedness for spiritual care. These directions can inform both curriculum design and broader educational strategies to support holistic care across diverse healthcare systems.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its focus on simultaneously examining the relationships among spiritual care competence, professional identity, and self-efficacy—variables that have rarely been studied together in this context. Furthermore, the use of a culturally adapted, native questionnaire facilitated the collection of relevant data that aligns with the context of the study population, thereby enabling more accurate and meaningful assessments. Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. Since all three main variables—spiritual care competence, professional self-efficacy, and professional identity—were assessed through self-report questionnaires, students may have overestimated their abilities, especially in early stages of training. Implementing observational research could provide a more objective and reliable method for evaluating actual proficiency in delivering spiritual care. Moreover, since the study was conducted at a single university in Iran, the findings may not be fully generalizable to students in other institutions or cultural contexts. Additionally, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships. Finally, the regression model explained only 37% of the variance in spiritual care competence, leaving 63% unexplained. This suggests that additional unmeasured factors—such as personal experiences, communication skills, and cultural background—may also play a role. Future studies could explore these dimensions to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of spiritual care competence.
