Associations of changes in frailty with multiple chronic diseases in middle aged and elderly adults
Authors: Tianci Yao, Chengyun Liu, Kun Wang, Guangyu Gao, Xiaofang Zhao, Haohui Fan, Bei Song, Ting Liu, Xinyue Zhang, Xueke Guang, Hao Zhang, Weilin Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the impact of baseline frailty status on chronic disease risks has been well-established, evidence on the associations between changes in frailty status and the incidence of new-onset chronic diseases remains limited.MethodsA retrospective study of prospectively collected data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) including 7 021 adults aged 45 years or older. Frailty status was evaluated by the Rockwood frailty index and classified as robust, pre-frail, or frail. Changes in frailty status were assessed by frailty status at baseline (2011) and the second survey (2013). All chronic diseases were identified by self-reported physician diagnosis. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs.ResultsCompared to stable robust participants, robust participants who progressed to pre-frail/frail status had significantly elevated risks of all chronic diseases studied (HRs: 1.38–2.73), except for cancer (HR: 1.32; 95% CI: 0.83–2.12). Pre-frail participants who progressed to frail status showed only significantly elevated risks of hypertension (HR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.01–1.77) and memory disease (HR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.11–2.39) compared to stable pre-frail participants. In contrast, pre-frail participants who recovered to robust status demonstrated decreased risks of CVD, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive disease, psychiatric disease, and memory disease than stable pre-frail participants (HRs: 0.39–0.66). Similarly, frail participants who recovered to robust/pre-frail status also showed decreased risks of CVD, stroke, diabetes, psychiatric disease, and memory disease compared to stable frail participants (HRs: 0.39–0.73).ConclusionsDifferent changes in frailty status are associated with different risks of new-onset chronic diseases. Progression of frailty status increases risks of almost all chronic diseases, while recovery of frailty status decreases risks of approximately half of all chronic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25872-6.

Introduction

Frailty has emerged as a significant challenge for healthcare systems globally, particularly in the context of an aging population [1]. Defined as a state of increased vulnerability to physiological decompensation in response to stressors [2], frailty is associated with a wide range of adverse health outcomes, including falls, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), functional decline, hospital admission, and mortality [1, 3]. With the rapid growth of the aging population, understanding the impact of frailty on health outcomes and developing strategies to prevent, delay, or reverse frailty have become critical priorities.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that baseline frailty and pre-frailty are associated with increased risks of multiple age-related chronic diseases [4–8]. In contrast, evidence on the associations between changes in frailty status and the incidence of new-onset chronic diseases remains limited. This knowledge is particularly relevant given the growing recognition that frailty is a modifiable state, with interventions showing promise in preventing, delaying, or even reversing frailty at an individual level [9, 10]. By considering not only the baseline frailty status but also the transitions between different frailty states, we can gain insights into the potential impact of frailty progression, stability, or reversal on the development of chronic diseases. Investigating the risks of age-related chronic diseases in individuals who recover from frailty can provide crucial evidence whether to support the implementation of frailty interventions.

Therefore, the current study aims to address this gap by examining the associations of baseline frailty status and the dynamic nature of frailty (including changes in frailty status, total frailty index, and changes in frailty index) with the incidence of chronic diseases using longitudinal data from a large, nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older adults.

Methods

Study design and population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a high-quality public database designed to investigate families and individuals aged 45 years and older, covering 450 communities (villages) in 150 counties and districts of 28 provinces across the country [11]. All participants underwent face-to-face interviews conducted by trained staff using a standardized questionnaire. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011 (wave1), and follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013 (wave 2), 2015 (wave 3), 2018 (wave 4), and 2020 (wave 5). CHARLS obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Peking University Health Science Center, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Although a small number of participants younger than 45 years were surveyed (mainly younger spouses of eligible participants), only individuals aged 45 years or older were included in the present analysis to ensure consistency with the study design and comparability across participants, in line with previous CHARLS-based studies [12, 13]. Among the 17 708 participants, we excluded those aged < 45 years (n = 777), those with missing data on the frailty index (FI) calculation at baseline (n = 3476), and those lost to follow-up during 2011–2020 (n = 4090), leaving 9365 participants in baseline frailty status analysis. Additionally, participants with a history of corresponding diseases at baseline were excluded (Supplementary Figure S1A). For changes in frailty status analyses, participants with missing data on the FI calculation at the second survey were further excluded, resulting in a final sample of 7,021 participants (Supplementary Figure S1B).

Assessment of frailty

In this study, 28 items of age-related health deficits were selected to construct the FI assessment scale according to previously described criteria, involving disease, symptoms, disability, physical function, depression, and cognition (Supplementary Table S1) [14–16]. Items 1–27 were dichotomized as either 1 (presence of a health deficit) or 0 (absence of a health deficit). The 28th item, cognitive score, was a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1. FI was calculated by the sum of the present health deficits divided by 28, which ranged from 0 to 1, with larger values indicating more weakness. To assess the potential impact of missing data, we recalculated the FI for participants with ≤ 5 missing items by dividing the deficit sum by the number of items with valid data. In accordance with previous studies, frailty status was categorized into robust (FI ≤ 0.10), pre-frail (0.10 < FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25) [14, 15]. Because the FI cut-off values for defining frailty status were controversial [17], we used another two common cut-off values to define frailty status: Threshold Set 1 (robust: FI ≤ 0.10; pre-frail: 0.10 < FI ≤ 0.21; frail: FI >0.21) and Threshold Set 2 (robust: FI ≤ 0.08; pre-frail: 0.08 < FI < 0.25; frail: FI ≥ 0.25) [15].

Changes in frailty status were evaluated by comparing frailty status at baseline (2011) and second survey (2013), resulting in nine potential conversion patterns of frailty status. To simplify the analysis and improve interpretability, transitions with similar clinical implications were combined. “Robust to pre-frail” and “Robust to frail” were merged as “Robust to worsened status,” and “Frail to robust” and “Frail to pre-frail” were merged as “Frail to improved status.” We also used total FI and change in FI to assess the dynamic nature of frailty status. Total FI was calculated by summing the FI at baseline and second survey, providing an overall assessment of an individual’s cumulative frailty burden over the two-year period. Change in FI was calculated by subtracting the FI at baseline from the FI at second survey, reflecting the direction and magnitude of change in individual’s frailty status.

Covariates

The covariates were obtained through questionnaires, including age, sex, marital status, education, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity. Body mass index (BMI) and systolic blood pressure (SBP) were measured at baseline. Procedures for collecting and processing baseline blood samples have been previously described [11], including measurement of High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Marital status was categorized into married/partnered and other (separated, divorced, widowed, or never married). Education was categorized as below high school, high school, and college or above. Participants reported their history of tobacco use (current vs. former vs. never), alcohol consumption (ever vs. never), and physical activity (vigorous vs. moderate vs. light).

Ascertainment of outcomes

Health outcomes were assessed by self-reported physician diagnosis: “Have you been diagnosed with… by a doctor?” In the analyses of baseline frailty status and changes in frailty status, follow-up was initiated from wave 1 (2011) and wave 2 (2013) of CHARLS, respectively. The endpoint of follow-up was at the first occurrence of health outcomes, death, or the end of follow-up date, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were described across baseline frailty status and changes in frailty status. The adjusted incidence rates of outcomes across groups of changes in frailty status were estimated and reported as the number of events per 100 person-years.

We assessed associations of baseline frailty status with risk of outcomes using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, marital status, education, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI, SBP, HDL-C, HbA1c, and CRP. To account for the competing risk between chronic diseases and mortality, we repeated the analyses using the Fine-Gray model. We used the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations method (10 imputed datasets) to handle missing covariates. Moreover, the dose-response associations of baseline FI with outcomes were examined using restricted cubic spline regression.

Adjusted survival curves, based on Cox proportional hazard models, were plotted to depict the cumulative risk across different groups of changes in frailty status. We also used Cox proportional hazard regression models and Fine-Gray models to estimate the hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals of outcomes associated with changes in frailty status, total FI, and change in FI. Additionally, baseline FI was further adjusted in the analyses of change in FI. Total FI and changes in FI were divided into tertiles, with the lower tertile (representing the least frail individuals or those with the smallest increase in frailty) as the reference. Testing for linear trends was by assigning a median value to each category as a continuous variable. Proportional hazards assumptions were tested by using likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without time-dependent exposure, and we found no significant deviation from the assumption.

Stratified analyses were also performed according to age (< 65 and ≥ 65), and sex (males and females) to assess potential modification effects. We did several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of results. First, we repeated analyses of changes in frailty status by further adjusting for baseline FI. Second, we repeated analyses of changes in frailty status by separating “Robust to pre-frail/frail” and “Frail to robust/pre-frail” groups. Third, given that FI thresholds for defining frailty status are unclear and controversial [17], we tested additional thresholds. For thresholds 1, frail, pre-frail, and robust were defined as FI >0.21, 0.10 < FI ≤ 0.21, and FI ≤ 0.10, respectively. For thresholds 2, frail, pre-frail, and robust were defined as FI ≥ 0.25, 0.08 < FI < 0.25, and FI ≤ 0.08, respectively [15]. Fourth, to account for the competing risk between chronic diseases and mortality, we repeated all analyses using the Fine-Gray model. Finally, we also attempted to include participants with fewer missing FI items (≤ 5 items). FI was calculated by summing the present health deficits and dividing by the number of complete items.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). The main packages employed included “survival” (for Cox models), “cmprsk” (for competing risk analyses), and “forestploter” and “ggplot2” (for data visualization). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the study population according to baseline frailty status were shown in Table 1. Among 9 365 participants (mean age 58.2 years, 47.3% men), 5 078 (54.2%) were of robust, 2 455 (26.2%) of pre-frail, and 1 832 (19.6%) of frail. Frail participants were more likely to be older, women, not married, and less educated. Moreover, frail participants had lower levels of physical activity and higher levels of SBP, HbA1c, and CRP compared to robust participants. Self-reported comorbidities were more prevalent among frail participants. Among 7 021 participants (mean age 59.8 years, 47.3% men) were included in the analyses of changes in frailty status, similar baseline characteristics were observed (Supplementary Table S2). Compared with participants retained in the longitudinal analyses, those who were excluded were generally older and had a higher burden of comorbidities (Supplementary Table S3). This pattern is consistent with the natural attrition commonly observed in aging cohorts.

Association of baseline frailty status with multiple chronic diseases

Compared to robust participants, prefrail and frail individuals had significantly higher risks of developing 13 out of 14 chronic diseases studied, except for cancer (Fig. 1). Frail participants exhibited a 1.38 to 4.83-fold higher risk of developing these chronic conditions compared to their robust counterparts. In addition, nonlinear relationships were observed for all outcomes except for cancer after multivariable adjustment (all P nonlinearity < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S2). The risks of 11 chronic diseases, including CVD, heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, arthritis/rheumatism, and asthma, demonstrated a leveling-off pattern at higher levels of frailty (Supplementary Figure S2).

Association of changes in frailty status with multiple chronic diseases

Among baseline robust participants, 1004 (24.9%) progressed to pre-frail or frail status. Among baseline frail participants, 356 (28.1%) recovered to pre-frail or robust status. In addition, among baseline pre-frail participants, 615 (35.6%) recovered to robust status, and 436 (25.2%) progressed to frail status (Fig. 2). Adjusted incidence rates for all chronic diseases studied were higher in individuals who progressed from robust to pre-frail/frail status compared to stable robust participants (Supplementary Figure S3). Similar trends were observed in those who progressed from pre-frail to frail status compared to stable pre-frail participants. Conversely, adjusted incidence rates for all chronic diseases studied, except for hypertension and cancer, decreased in individuals who recovered from pre-frail to robust status compared to stable pre-frail participants. Additionally, compared with stable frail participants, those who recovered from frail to robust/pre-frail status also showed decreasing adjusted incidence rates for all chronic diseases (Supplementary Figure S3).

Adjusted survival curves, categorized by changes in frailty status, are displayed in Supplementary Figure S4. Stable robust participants had the lowest risks of almost all chronic diseases, followed by those who recovered from pre-frail to robust status. The highest risks of chronic diseases were observed in stable frail participants, followed by those who progressed from pre-frail to frail status. Compared with stable robust participants, those who progressed from robust to pre-frail/frail status had significantly elevated risks of all chronic diseases studied (HRs: 1.38–2.73) except for cancer (HR: 1.32; 95% CI: 0.83–2.12) (Fig. 3). In contrast, frail participants who recovered to robust/pre-frail status demonstrated significantly decreased risks of CVD (HR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.56–0.95), stroke (HR: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.33–0.73), diabetes (HR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.49–0.99), psychiatric disease (HR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.22–0.68), and memory disease (HR: 0.50; 95% CI: 0.34–0.76) compared to stable frail participants (Fig. 3). In comparison with stable pre-frail participants, those who recovered from pre-frail to robust status showed significantly decreased risks of CVD (HR: 0.51; 95% CI: 0.39–0.66), heart disease (HR: 0.57; 95% CI: 0.43–0.75), stroke (HR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.38–0.91), kidney disease (HR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.45–0.97), digestive disease (HR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.47–0.84), psychiatric disease (HR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.19–0.79), and memory disease (HR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.34–0.85), whereas those who progressed from pre-frail to frail status had significantly elevated risks of hypertension (HR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.01–1.77) and memory disease (HR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.11–2.39) (Fig. 3).

Associations of total frailty index and change in frailty index with multiple chronic diseases

Participants in the upper tertile of the total FI showed significantly higher risks for all chronic diseases studied (HRs: 1.52–5.58), except for cancer (HR: 1.23; 95% CI: 0.81–1.85), compared to those in the lower tertile. Moreover, participants in the middle tertile of the total FI also demonstrated significantly elevated risks of all chronic diseases studied (HRs: 1.24–2.34), except for cancer (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.69–1.53). Furthermore, upward trends in the risks of all chronic diseases studied, except for cancer, were detected as total FI increased (all P for trend < 0.001) (Fig. 4A).

After adjusting for the aforementioned confounding factors and baseline FI, participants in the upper tertile of change in FI also showed significantly higher risks for all chronic diseases studied (HRs: 1.02–2.40), except for cancer (HR: 1.19; 95% CI: 0.81–1.74) and hypertension (HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 0.98–1.28), compared to those in the lower tertile. However, participants in the middle tertile of change in FI did not exhibit significantly elevated risks of chronic diseases. Similarly, upward trends in the risks of all chronic diseases studied, except for cancer and hypertension, were detected as change in FI increased (all P for trend < 0.05) (Fig. 4B).

Sensitivity and stratified analyses

When further adjusting for the baseline FI, the magnitude of the associations between changes in frailty status and chronic diseases was slightly attenuated (Supplementary Figure S5). The results were generally consistent when separating “Robust to pre-frail/frail” and “Frail to robust/pre-frail” groups. Robust participants who progressed to pre-frail or frail status showed significantly elevated risks of 12 chronic diseases. Conversely, frail participants who recovered to pre-frail or robust status showed decreased risks of all chronic diseases studied, although only the associations with CVD, stroke, psychiatric disease, and memory disease reached statistical significance (Supplementary Figure S6). Repeating the analyses using two additional thresholds for defining frailty status, the results were not substantially altered (Supplementary Figure S7-S8). When accounting for the competing risk between chronic diseases and mortality, the results were consistent with the main analyses (Supplementary Figure S9-S12). Finally, no appreciable differences were observed when individuals with fewer missing FI items (≤ 5 items) were included in the analyses (Supplementary Figure S13-S16). Although several statistically significant interactions were found in the stratified analyses, the results were generally similar across gender and age groups (Supplementary Figure S17-S24).

Discussion

This individual participant data analysis from a large prospective cohort study provides compelling evidence for the significant associations between frailty status, changes in frailty status, and the risks of multiple chronic diseases in middle-aged and elderly population. Prefrail and frail individuals had significantly higher risks of all chronic diseases studied except cancer than robust participants. More importantly, progression of frailty status increases risks of almost all chronic diseases except cancer, whereas recovery of frailty status decreases risks of approximately half of all chronic diseases. Furthermore, higher total FI and change in FI were associated with elevated risks of all chronic diseases except cancer and hypertension.

Previous studies have demonstrated that frailty and pre-frailty are associated with increased risks of morbidity and mortality of various diseases [4–8]. The current study confirms these associations, showing that pre-frail and frail participants had elevated risks of multiple age-related chronic diseases, with the exception of cancer. The lack of significant association between frailty and cancer risk is noteworthy, which may be attributed to strong influence of genetic predisposition overshadowing frailty’s effects [18], as well as limited statistical power. Additionally, the observed dose-response relationships between frailty severity and chronic disease risks underscores the importance of early identification and management of frailty to prevent or delay the onset of chronic conditions.

Previous evidence on the associations of changes in frailty status with age-related chronic diseases is sparse. A study using data from three prospective cohorts found that progression of frailty status increased incident CVD risks, while recovery of frailty status decreased incident CVD risks [15]. Sun et al. used data from the UK Biobank and found that long-term changes in frailty were associated with risk of type 2 diabetes [7]. Wang et al. also reported that sustained frailty remission was associated with reduced risk of developing dementia [8]. The current study expands on these previous findings by examining the associations of changes in frailty status with a wide range of age-related chronic diseases. Our findings highlighted the detrimental impact of frailty progression on chronic disease risk, with the progression from pre-frail to frail having a lesser impact than the progression from robust to pre-frail or frail, possibly due to already increased vulnerability of pre-frail individuals.

In our data, pre-frail participants who improved to robust status and frail participants who improved to robust or pre-frail status exhibited lower risks for approximately half of the chronic diseases assessed compared with their counterparts who remained pre-frail or frail. These study-specific findings support the notion that interventions targeting frailty reversal may confer broader health benefits. Consistent with prior reports that frailty is dynamic and potentially modifiable [9, 10], and that improvements in frailty status are associated with better clinical outcomes [7, 8, 15], our results suggest that enhancing frailty status could help reduce the burden of multiple chronic diseases. Furthermore, we demonstrated that higher total FI and change in FI were associated with elevated risks of chronic diseases, except for cancer and hypertension. These findings further corroborate results based on changes in frailty status and provide a more nuanced understanding of the relationships between frailty and chronic diseases risks.

Although mechanisms underlying the complex association between frailty and risks of age-related chronic diseases are not definitively clear, there are several possible explanations. Frailty is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation [19] and increased oxidative stress [20], contributing to the development of age-related chronic diseases [21, 22]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, a hallmark of aging linked to frailty [23], can lead to reduced energy production, increased oxidative stress, and cellular damage, promoting age-related chronic diseases [24]. Accumulating evidence also suggests that frailty is associated with alterations in hormonal and metabolic pathways, such as insulin resistance and reduced insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels [25], which can contribute to the development of chronic diseases [26]. Additionally, changes in physical activity have been proposed as a potential pathway linking frailty and chronic diseases because frailty is often accompanied by reduced physical activity and increased sedentary behavior [1].

The strengths of this study include the prospective cohort design and comprehensive assessment of multiple chronic diseases. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate the associations between changes in frailty status and risks of new-onset chronic diseases. Furthermore, the consistent results in sensitivity analyses demonstrated the robustness of study findings.

Our study also has some potential limitations. First, the study relied on self-reported data for the assessment of chronic diseases, which may be subject to misclassification bias. Second, optimal FI thresholds for defining frailty status remain unclear. However, findings were generally consistent across multiple thresholds. Third, the exclusion of 10 687 participants may have introduced selection bias. A comparison of the baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants suggests the presence of healthy participant bias (Supplementary Table S3). Fourth, the present study was conducted in a Chinese cohort, and whether these conclusions can be applied to other ethnic groups needs to be further confirmed. Fifth, due to the nature of the observational studies, residuals confounding could not be excluded despite efforts to adjust for potential confounding factors.

Conclusion

Different changes in frailty status are associated with different risks of new-onset chronic diseases. Progression of frailty status increases risks of almost all chronic diseases, while recovery of frailty status decreases risks of approximately half of all chronic diseases. These findings underscore the importance of early detection and intervention for frailty. By targeting frailty prevention and reversal, it may be possible to reduce the burden of chronic diseases and promote healthy aging.
