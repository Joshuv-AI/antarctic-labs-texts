by a torus. We denote

. In this article we propose the following Conjecture, which is the analog of

to the quasi-projective setting.

Conjecture 1.4
.

(see
section 4
) Let
U
be a smooth quasi-projective surface over a finite extension
k

-adic field

.
Then the kernel

of the generalized Albanese map

admits a decomposition

, where
D
is a divisible group and

a finite group.

Our main evidence for this conjecture is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

(see
section 4
,
section 4
)

The main technical step is to show that if

p

2.1.
Generalized Albanese Variety

For a smooth projective variety
X
over a field
k
such that
X(k)\neq\emptyset
, the usual Albanese variety
Alb_{X}
is an abelian variety such that for every
x_{0}\in X(k)
there is a morphism
\phi_{x_{0}}:X\to Alb_{X}
sending
x_{0}
to the zero element of
Alb_{X}
, and if
f:X\to B
is any morphism to an abelian variety
B
with
f(x_{0})=0_{B}
, then
f
factors uniquely through a homomorphism
Alb_{X}\to B
. We recall that
Alb_{X}
is the dual abelian variety to the Picard variety
{\rm Pic}^{0}(X)
.

Serre in his éxposé (
[
Se58
]
) constructed an analog for an open subvariety
U
of a smooth projective variety
X
. In what follows assume that
U(k)\neq\emptyset
. The Albanese variety
Alb_{U}
is an extension of
Alb_{X}
by a torus
T
,

0\to T\to Alb_{U}\to Alb_{X}\to 0.

It is thus a semi-abelian variety, which is universal for morphisms from
U
to semi-abelian varieties. When the complement
X-U
is of codimension at least
2
, then
T=0
.
Next suppose that
X-U
is the support of a divisor
D
on
X
. We recall how the torus
T
is obtained. In what follows we make the simplifying assumption that the geometric Néron-Severi group
{\operatorname{NS}}(X_{\overline{k}})
has trivial
{\rm Gal}(\overline{k}/k)
-action, and hence
{\operatorname{NS}}(X)={\operatorname{NS}}(X_{\overline{k}})
. Write
D=D_{1}+\ldots+D_{r}
, where each
D_{i}
is a prime divisor and let
\displaystyle I={\mathbb{Z}}^{r}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}{\mathbb{Z}}(D_{i})
be the subgroup of
{\rm Div}(X)
consisting of divisors supported in
D
. Let
J
be the kernel of the composition

I\hookrightarrow{\rm Div}(X)\to{\rm Pic}(X)\to{\operatorname{NS}}(X).

Then
T
is a torus of dimension equal to the rank of
J
as a free abelian group. Thus, we may write
\text{\rm dim}(T)=r-n
, for some
n\leq r
. It is clear from the definition that
n
is the size of a maximal
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linearly independent subset of
\{[D_{1}],\ldots,[D_{r}]\}
when considered as a subgroup of
{\operatorname{NS}}(X)
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
C\hookrightarrow X
be a smooth projective connected curve and let
U=X-C
. Since
[C]
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linearly independent element of
{\operatorname{NS}}(X)
, it follows that
r=n=1
, and hence
Alb_{X}=Alb_{U}
. This observation will be used in
subsection 5.3
.

2.2.
Suslin’s Homology

Let
U
be a smooth quasi-projective variety over a field
k
. In
[
Sus96
]
Suslin and Voevodsky defined a class group
H_{0}^{{\operatorname{sus}}}(U)
of zero-cycles on
U
, which coincides with the Chow group
{\rm CH}_{0}(U)
when
U
is proper, but it is larger otherwise. We recall the definition below.

Definition 2.2
.

(
[
Sus96
]
)
Let
\displaystyle Z_{0}(U)=\bigoplus_{x\in U_{(0)}}{\mathbb{Z}}(x)
be the free abelian group on all closed points of
U
. Then
H_{0}^{{\operatorname{sus}}}(U)
is the quotient of
Z_{0}(U)
by the subgroup
R
generated by
i_{0}^{\star}(Z)-i_{1}^{\star}(Z)
, where
i_{\nu}:U\to U\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
stands for the inclusion
x\mapsto(x,\nu)
for
\nu=0,1
, and
Z
runs through all closed integral subschemes of
U\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
such that the projection
Z\to{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
is finite and surjective.

This group is often referred to in the literature as
Suslin’s singular homology
.

Relation to Wiesend’s Class group

When the base field
k
is perfect, the group
H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(U)
coincides with another class group,
C_{0}(U)
, called
Wiesend’s
0
-th ideal class group
. The name reflects that the definition was inspired by a similar group introduced by G. Wiesend (
[
Wie07
]
) for arithmetic schemes (also see
[
Yam13
]
). We recall the set-up below.

Notation 2.3
.

Let
y\in U_{(1)}
. We will denote by
C(y)
the closure of the point
y
in
U
(which is a closed integral curve in
U
),
\widetilde{C}(y)\twoheadrightarrow C(y)
its normalization,
\widetilde{C}(y)\hookrightarrow\overline{C}(y)
its smooth completion, and
C_{\infty}(y)=\overline{C}(y)-\widetilde{C}(y)
.

Definition 2.4
.

(cf.
[
Yam13
, Definition 1.1]
)
Wiesend’s
0
-th ideal class group is defined to be the quotient of
Z_{0}(U)
by the subgroup
R^{\prime}
generated by divisors of functions
{\rm div}(f)
, where
f\in k(y)^{\times}
for some
y\in U_{(1)}
satisfying
f=1
on
C_{\infty}(y)
.

It is a theorem of A. Schmidt (
[
Sch07
, Theorem 5.1]
) that when
k
is perfect there is an isomorphism

H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(U)\cong C_{0}(U).

In what follows we will be mostly using Suslin’s homology, but we won’t be distinguishing between these two groups.

The group
H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(U)
enjoys similar properties as the Chow group of zero-cycles
{\rm CH}_{0}(X)
for
X
projective. Namely, there is a well-defined degree map,

\deg:H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(U)\to{\mathbb{Z}},\;\;[x]\mapsto[k(x):k].

We will denote by
F^{1}(U)
the subgroup of degree
0
elements. That is,
F^{1}(U):=\ker(\deg)
.
From now on assume that
U(k)\neq\emptyset
. Then the Albanese map
U\to Alb_{U}
considered in
subsection 2.1
induces a homomorphism

{\rm alb}_{U}:F^{1}(U)\to Alb_{U}(k)

that does not depend on the choice of base point
x_{0}\in U(k)
.
A proof of why
{\rm alb}_{U}
is well-defined using Suslin’s definition can be found in
[
SpSz03
, Section 3]
.

Definition 2.5
.

Suppose
U(k)\neq\emptyset
.
We define
F^{2}(U):=\ker[F^{1}(U)\xrightarrow{{\rm alb}_{U}}Alb_{U}(k)]
to be the kernel of the generalized Albanese map. We thus have a filtration,

H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(U)\supset F^{1}(U)\supset F^{2}(U)\supset 0.

2.2.1.
Relation to Generalized Jacobians

Wiesend’s definition is often easier to use in applications. For example, it follows directly that when
U=\overline{C}-C_{\infty}
, where
\overline{C}
is a smooth projective curve over a perfect field
k
and
C_{\infty}
is a reduced closed subvariety, then
C_{0}(U)
is the group of classes of divisors on
\overline{C}
prime to
C_{\infty}
modulo
C_{\infty}
-equivalence defined by Serre in
[
Se59
, Chapter V.2]
. Its degree
0
subgroup,
F^{1}(U)
, coincides with the generalized Jacobian
J_{\mathfrak{m}}
of
\overline{C}
corresponding to the modulus
\mathfrak{m}=\sum_{x\in C_{\infty}}x
.
Using this observation, one can immediately prove that for an arbitrary smooth quasi-projective variety

the Albanese map
{\rm alb}_{U}:F^{1}(U)\to Alb_{U}(k)
is well-defined using the universal property of generalized Jacobians.

In a similar spirit as Serre, H. Russell (
[
Rus13
]
) defined for an effective divisor
D
on a smooth projective variety

over a perfect field
k
the notion of an Albanese variety
Alb(X;D)
with modulus
D
. When
D
is reduced, this group coincides with
Alb_{U}
as defined in
subsection 2.1
, where
U=X-{\rm supp}\,(D)
. When
k=\overline{k}
, Russell proved that the group
Alb_{U}
is the
universal quotient
of
F^{1}(U)
. That is, if
\phi:F^{1}(U)\to G
is a homomorphism to a semi-abelian variety

, then
\phi
factors uniquely through
Alb_{U}
(cf.
[
Rus13
, Theorem 3.29]
). This in particular implies that over
\overline{k}
the Albanese map is surjective. These facts will be used in the proof of
section 1
.

(also

For a given Noetherian

2.4.

-theory for Curves over

In this section we recall some necessary facts from the Class field theory for curves over
p
-adic fields as developed by S. Bloch (
[

]
) and S. Saito (
[
Sai85
]

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve over a field

-group

SK_{1}(C):={\operatorname{coker}}\left[\bigoplus_{x\in C_{(0)}}\partial_{x}:K_{2}(k(C))\to\bigoplus_{x\in C_{(0)}}k(x)^{\times}\right],

where for each closed point

\partial_{x}:K_{2}(k(C))\to k(x)^{\times}
is the boundary map in
K
-theory. The pointwise norm maps
N_{k(x)/k}:k(x)^{\times}\to k^{\times}

induce a
norm map

N:SK_{1}(C)\to k^{\times},

whose kernel is traditionally denoted by
V(C)
, yielding an exact sequence

0\to V(C)\to SK_{1}(C)\xrightarrow{N}k^{\times}.

Moreover, when
C
has a
k
-rational point
x_{0}\in C(k)
, the closed immersion
\iota_{x_{0}}:{\rm Spec\,}(k)\hookrightarrow C
gives a splitting to the norm map, and hence we have a decomposition

(3)

SK_{1}(C)\cong V(C)\oplus k^{\times}.

From now on, we assume that
k
is a finite extension of the
p
-adic field
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
. In this case, Bloch (
[
Bl81
]
) and Saito (
[
Sai85
]
) constructed
reciprocity maps

\sigma:SK_{1}(C)\to\pi_{1}^{\rm ab}(C)\;\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\;\tau:V(C)\to T_{G_{k}},

where
\pi_{1}^{\rm ab}(C)
is the abelian étale fundamental group of
C
,
T
is the Tate module associated to the Jacobian
J_{C}
of
C
,
G_{k}={\rm Gal}(\overline{k}/k)
, and
T_{G_{k}}
are the
G_{k}
-coinvariants. Bloch and Saito studied the kernel and the image of these maps when the Jacobian
J_{C}
has respectively good and bad reduction. The following theorem summarizes some of the main results in the class field theory of smooth projective curves.

Theorem 2.9
.

(
[
Bl81
, Theorem 2.9]
,
[
Sai85
, Chap. II, Theorems 5.1 & 4.1]
). Let

be a smooth projective geometrically connected curve over a
p
-adic field

. Then the following are true:

(i)

The kernel of
\sigma
(resp.
\tau
) is the maximal divisible subgroup of
SK_{1}(C)
(resp.
V(C)
).

(ii)

The image of
\tau
is finite.

Note that since
k
is
p
-adic, the multiplicative group

has no nonzero divisible elements, and hence the maximal divisible subgroups of

and

coincide. Thus, the above theorem yields the following Corollary.

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective connected curve over a
p
-adic field

with

. Then we have a decomposition

SK_{1}(C)\simeq SK_{1}(C)_{{\rm div}}\oplus F\oplus k^{\times},

where

In what follows we will only make use of
subsection 2.4

subsection 2.4

3.1.
Torsion-by-divisible

Let
A
be an abelian group and
A_{{\rm div}}
its maximal divisible subgroup. Then the short exact sequence

0\to A_{{\rm div}}\to A\to A/A_{{\rm div}}\to 0

splits, since divisible groups are injective

-modules. If the group

is torsion of finite exponent, we will say that

is
torsion of finite exponent by divisible
.

The following lemma is probably well-known to the experts.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C\to 0
be an exact sequence of abelian groups. If the groups
A,C
are torsion of finite exponent by divisible, then the same is true for
B
.

Proof.

Consider the short exact sequence
0\rightarrow A^{\prime}\xrightarrow{i}B\xrightarrow{g}C\to 0
, where
A^{\prime}
is the image of
f
. Since
A\cong A_{{\rm div}}\oplus A/A_{{\rm div}}
, where
A/A_{{\rm div}}
is torsion of finite exponent, we have
A^{\prime}=f(A_{{\rm div}})+f(A/A_{{\rm div}})
with
D=f(A_{{\rm div}})
divisible and

torsion of finite exponent. Therefore,
\displaystyle A^{\prime}=D+T\cong D\oplus\frac{T}{T\cap D}
. So, without loss of generality, one can assume that
f
is injective.

Note that for any abelian group
G
, we have

G_{{\rm div}}={\rm Im}\left[\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Q},G)\xrightarrow{i_{G}^{\ast}}\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},G)=G\right],

where the later map is induced from the inclusion
\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}
. Applying
\operatorname{Hom}(-,A)
to the short exact sequence
0\to{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Q}}\to{\mathbb{Q}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\to 0
and using that

is torsion of finite exponent, we get that
\operatorname{Ext^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{Q},A)
is torsion of finite exponent. To finish the proof, we now consider the following commutative diagram with exact rows:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Q},A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Q},B)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Q},C)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ext^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{Q},A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},B)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z},C)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\star}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{A}^{\ast}}

The following proposition is a key ingredient in the proof of

Proposition 3.2
.

Consider the set-up of the previous paragraph. Then
\iota^{\star}({\operatorname{NS}}(X))
is a subgroup of

of rank

Proof.

By assumption the elements

are

Combining this with equation (

\displaystyle C\cdot D_{n}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i,C}(S_{i}\cdot D_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i,C}\sum_{j<n}\lambda_{j}(S_{i}\cdot D_{j})

Next consider the map

) is the inclusion (resp. projection) into the second factor. Since the group

admits no nontrivial continuous group homomorphism into

, it follows that the map

induces an isomorphism between

and

. Therefore, the claim is equivalent to showing finiteness of the group

.

Since

is finite, so is
{\operatorname{coker}}(\pi_{2}\circ f)
, which implies that the group
{\operatorname{coker}}(\pi_{2}\circ f\circ i_{2})
is finitely generated. Moreover, the map
\pi_{2}\circ f\circ i_{2}
is a continuous group homomorphism between profinite groups, and thus
{\operatorname{coker}}(\pi_{2}\circ f\circ i_{2})
is a profinite group with the quotient topology. This implies that
{\operatorname{coker}}(\pi_{2}\circ f\circ i_{2})
is a profinite group that is also finitely generated abelian group, and hence must be finite (see
[
RZ10
, Propositon 2.3.1]
). Therefore, we are reduced to showing the following claim.

Claim
. Let
\theta:G\rightarrow G
be a continuous group homomorphism with finite cokernel. Then
\ker(\theta)
is finite.

Proof
. As
G=(\mathcal{O}_{k}^{\times})^{r}\cong F\times(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{m}
(see
[
Neu99
, Proposition II. 5.7]
) for some finite group
F
and some integer

, consider the following composite map
\pi_{2}\circ\theta\circ i_{2}
:

\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m}\xrightarrow{i_{2}}F\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m}\xrightarrow{\theta}F\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m}\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m}
,

where
i_{2}
(resp.
\pi_{2}
) is the inclusion (resp. projection) into the second factor. It is easy to see that the
\ker(\pi_{2}\circ\theta\circ i_{2})
is finite if and only if
\ker(\theta)
is finite. Moreover, since
{\operatorname{coker}}(\theta)
is finite, it follows that
{\operatorname{coker}}(\pi_{2}\circ\theta\circ i_{2})
is also finite. Hence, without loss of generality, we may assume

. In this case, we will show that
\ker(\theta)=0
. Consider the image of the map
\theta
, say Im(
\theta
), which is a closed subgroup of
G
of finite index. It follows that Im(
\theta
) is an open subgroup of
G
, and hence a topologically finitely generated profinite group (see
[
RZ10
, Proposition 2.5.5]
). By the structure theorem (see
[
RZ10
, Theorem 4.3.5]
), we have Im

for some integer
m^{\prime}\leq m
. Since Im(
\theta
) has finite index in
G
, it follows that

. We now have a continuous epimorphism
\theta:G=\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m}
. Such

is an isomorphism (see
[
RZ10
, Proposition 2.5.2]
), which implies the

. This completes the proof.

∎

The finiteness of
{\operatorname{coker}}(\varepsilon)
together with the exact sequence (
8
) imply that we have an exact sequence

(9)

\ker(\varepsilon)\to F^{2}(U)\xrightarrow{\alpha}F^{2}(X)\to F\to 0,

where
F
is a finite group.

Now suppose that the group
F^{2}(U)/F^{2}(U)_{{\rm div}}
is finite. It then follows by
subsection 3.1
that the group
F^{2}(X)/F^{2}(X)_{{\rm div}}
is torsion of finite exponent, and hence finite. This completes the proof of the converse direction of our theorem.

We next focus on the forward direction, where we assume that
F^{2}(X)/F^{2}(X)_{{\rm div}}
is a finite group. The map
\varepsilon
factors through
{\rm Im}(f)
, which by exactness is isomorphic to
SK_{1}(D)/{\rm Im}(g)
. Denote by
\overline{\varepsilon}:SK_{1}(D)/{\rm Im}(g)\to(k^{\times})^{s}
the induced map with kernel
\ker(\overline{\varepsilon})\cong\ker(\varepsilon)/{\rm Im}(g)\cap\ker(\varepsilon)
. Moreover, let
\pi:SK_{1}(D)\twoheadrightarrow SK_{1}(D)/{\rm Im}(g)
be the projection. The sequence (
9
) can be rewritten as

(10)

0\to\ker(\overline{\varepsilon})\to F^{2}(U)\to F^{2}(X)\to F\to 0.

Thus, using
subsection 3.1
finiteness of
F^{2}(U)/F^{2}(U)_{{\rm div}}
can be reduced to proving:

Claim 2:
The group
\ker(\overline{\varepsilon})
is torsion of finite exponent by divisible.

Proof.

Consider the short exact sequence

0\to F_{2}\to H\to F_{1}\oplus(k^{\times})^{r}\to 0,

where
H=SK_{1}(D)_{{\rm div}}/SK_{1}(D)
.
Here we recall that
F_{1},F_{2}
are finite groups and
r
is the number of irreducible components of the divisor
D
.
Since quotients of divisible groups are divisible and the field
k
is
p
-adic, it follows that
\pi(SK_{1}(D)_{{\rm div}})\subset\ker(\overline{\varepsilon})
is a divisible subgroup of
\ker(\overline{\varepsilon})
.

It remains to show that the kernel of the induced map

\overline{\varepsilon}:H/{\rm Im}(g)\cap H\to(k^{\times})^{s}

is torsion of finite exponent. This will clearly follow if we show that
n\ker(\overline{\varepsilon})=\ker(n\circ\overline{\varepsilon})
is torsion of finite exponent for some
n\geq 1
. By multiplying with the orders of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
if necessary, we may assume that the map
\varepsilon:H\to(k^{\times})^{s}
factors through the toric piece,
(k^{\times})^{r}
, of
H
.

Recall from
subsection 2.1
that
s=r-n
, where
n
is the size of a maximal
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linearly independent subset of
\{[D_{1}],\ldots,[D_{r}]\}
when considered as a subgroup of
{\operatorname{NS}}(X)
.
Let us assume for simplicity that the Néron-Severi group
{\operatorname{NS}}(X_{\overline{k}})={\operatorname{NS}}(X)
is torsion-free. Consider the homomorphism
{\rm Pic}(X)\otimes k^{\times}\to{\rm CH}^{2}(X,1)
. Since
{\operatorname{NS}}(X)
is free we have a decomposition

{\rm Pic}(X)\simeq{\rm Pic}^{0}(X)\oplus{\operatorname{NS}}(X)
, and hence an induced homomorphism
{\operatorname{NS}}(X)\otimes k^{\times}\to{\rm CH}^{2}(X,1)
. We consider the composition

\rho:{\operatorname{NS}}(X)\otimes k^{\times}\to{\rm CH}^{2}(X,1)\xrightarrow{g}SK_{1}(D)\twoheadrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}(V(D_{i})\oplus k^{\times})\twoheadrightarrow(k^{\times})^{r}.

Then this is precisely the map obtained by applying
\otimes k^{\times}
to the pullback

\iota^{\star}:{\operatorname{NS}}(X)\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}{\operatorname{NS}}(D_{i})

induced by the closed immersion
\iota:D\hookrightarrow X
. It follows from
subsection 3.2
that
{\rm Im}(\iota^{\star})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, and hence the map

\overline{\varepsilon}:H/{\rm Im}(g)\cap H\to(k^{\times})^{s}
factors through an
r-n=s
dimensional torus.
Since we already know from Claim 1 that
{\operatorname{coker}}(\overline{\varepsilon})
is finite, Claim 2 follows from
subsection 3.3
.

If
{\operatorname{NS}}(X_{\overline{k}})={\operatorname{NS}}(X)
is not torsion-free, then we can imitate the above argument by considering the surjection
{\rm Pic}(X)\to{\operatorname{NS}}(X)/{\operatorname{NS}}(X)_{{\rm tor}}\to 0
, which splits, and using
subsection 3.2
.

∎

For a smooth projective variety
X
over a
p
-adic field
k
, S. Saito and Sujatha (
[
SSu95
, p. 409]
) showed that the cokernel of the Albanese map
F^{1}(X)\xrightarrow{{\rm alb}_{X}}Alb_{X}(k)
is finite. As a byproduct of the proof of
section 4
we obtain an analog of this for quasi-projective surfaces under some assumptions.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective surface over
k
and
D
a simple normal crossing divisor on
X
. Let
U=X-{\rm supp}\,(D)
. Suppose that
U(k)\neq\emptyset
and that the maximal subtorus of
Alb_{U}
is split. Then the Albanese map
{\rm alb}_{U}:F^{1}(U)\to Alb_{U}(k)
has finite cokernel.

Proof.

Consider the exact sequence (
8
). It follows by Claim 1 in the proof of
section 4
that under these assumptions
{\operatorname{coker}}(\varepsilon)
is finite. Moreover, the same is true for
{\operatorname{coker}}({\rm alb}_{X})
(
[
SSu95
, p. 409]
). The result then follows by exactness.

∎

Remark 4.6
.

We expect that the assumptions of
section 4
can potentially be removed. One could go further and explore quasi-projective analogs of the main result of
[
Kai16
]
. In this article, Kai proved that for a smooth projective variety
X
over a
p
-adic field
k
admitting a smooth projective model
\mathcal{X}
over
{\rm Spec\,}(\mathcal{O}_{k})
whose Picard scheme
{\rm Pic}_{\mathcal{X}/\mathcal{O}_{k}}
is smooth, the cokernel of
{\rm alb}_{X}
is Pontryagin dual to the cokernel of
{\rm Pic}(X_{\overline{k}})\to{\operatorname{NS}}(X_{\overline{k}})
. The authors hope to explore this in a future paper.

section 4
suggests that an analogue of Colliot-Thélène’s conjecture must hold for smooth quasi-projective surfaces over
p
-adic fields.
This urges us to propose the following:

Conjecture 4.7
.

Let
U
be a smooth quasi-projective surface over a finite extension
k
of the
p
-adic field
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
. Then the kernel
F^{2}(U)
of the generalized Albanese map admits a decomposition
F^{2}(U)\cong F\oplus D
, where
F
is a finite group and
D
is a divisible group.

It is clear that if
section 4
holds, then
section 1
is true for smooth projective surfaces. As a consequence of
section 4
, we will now show that
section 1
for smooth projective surfaces also implies
section 4
.

Corollary 4.8
.

section 1
holds for smooth projective surfaces if and only if
section 4
holds.

Proof.

As mentioned above, we only need to prove the “only if” part. Assume that
section 1
holds. Let
U
be any smooth quasi-projective surface over
k
, and let
Y
be a projective variety such that
U
is an open subvariety of
Y
. By resolution of singularities, there exists a proper birational morphism
X\rightarrow Y
, where
X
is smooth. In fact, since
Y
is projective, the map
X\rightarrow Y
can be taken to be projective (see
[
Kol07
, Corollary 3.22]
). Since
U
is smooth, it can be considered as an open subvariety of
X
, where
X
is a smooth projective surface over
k
. Furthermore, by performing suitable blow-ups, we may assume that
X-U
is a simple normal crossing divisor. More precisely, suppose
X-U
is the support of a divisor containing a singular irreducible component
C
. Let
X_{1}
be the blow-up of
X
at the singular points of
C
,
\rho:X_{1}\to X
the birational morphism and
U_{1}=\rho^{-1}(U)
. Since
C
is a curve and
X
is smooth, its strict transform
C_{1}
is a simple normal crossing divisor on
X_{1}
. Since the blow-up happens at the complement of
U
,
\rho:U_{1}\to U
is an isomorphism. By considering a chain of such operations, we obtain a variety
\tilde{X}
birational to
X
and an open
\tilde{U}
in
\tilde{X}
such that
\tilde{X}-\tilde{U}
is simple normal crossing divisor. Replacing (
\tilde{X}
,
\tilde{U}
) by
(X
,
U)
, we may assume that
X-U
is a snc divisor on
X
. Furthermore, similarly to the proof of
section 4
, by base change to a finite extension
L/k
, we may assume that
U(L)\neq\emptyset
, each irreducible component of
X-U
has a
L
-rational point, the maximal subtorus
T
of the semi-abelian variety
Alb_{U}
is split and the Galois group
{\rm Gal}(\overline{L}/L)
acts trivially on the Néron-Severi group
{\operatorname{NS}}(X_{\overline{L}})
.
Now applying
section 4
to the pair (
U_{L},X_{L}
), the group

F^{2}(X_{L})/F^{2}(X_{L})_{{\rm div}}
is finite if and only if
F^{2}(U_{L})/F^{2}(U_{L})_{{\rm div}}
is finite. Since
F^{2}(X_{L})/F^{2}(X_{L})_{{\rm div}}
is finite by assumption, it follows that
F^{2}(U_{L})/F^{2}(U_{L})_{{\rm div}}
is also finite. We now apply a standard norm argument (see for example
[
Gaz24
, Lemma 3.3]
) to conclude that
F^{2}(U)/F^{2}(U)_{{\rm div}}
is torsion of finite exponent. Finally, using
section 4
, we conclude that
F^{2}(U)/F^{2}(U)_{{\rm div}}
is finite, which concludes the proof.

∎

5.
More cases of Colliot-Thélène’s Conjecture

5.1.
Proof of

We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

be smooth projective varieties over a perfect field

X(k),Y(k)\neq\emptyset

Let
\phi_{\star}:{\rm CH}_{0}(X)\to{\rm CH}_{0}(Y)

\phi_{\star}:F^{1}(X)\to F^{1}(Y)
. It follows by
[
CT11
, Proposition 6.3]
that
F^{1}(X)\simeq F^{1}(Y)
. Moreover, the Albanese variety of a smooth projective variety is a birational invariant. Thus, we have a commutative diagram with exact rows,

We are now ready to prove
section 1
, which we restate here.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
X,Y
be smooth projective surfaces over a
p
-adic field
k
. Suppose there is a generically finite rational map
\pi:X\dashrightarrow Y
. If
section 1
is true for
X_{L}
for every finite extension
L/k
, then it is true for
Y
. The same result holds true if the map
\pi
is defined over a finite extension of the base field.

Proof.

Let
U\subset X,V\subset Y
be dense open subsets such that
\pi:U\to V
is a finite morphism of degree
n
. We first want to reduce to the case when the complements
X-U,Y-V
are either
0
-dimensional or simple normal crossing divisors. By using a sequence of blow-ups (see
section 4
), we obtain a variety
\tilde{Y}
birational to
Y
and an open
\tilde{V}
in
\tilde{Y}
such that
\tilde{Y}-\tilde{V}
is snc and
U\xrightarrow{\pi}\tilde{V}
is a finite map. Repeating the argument for the pair
X,U
we obtain the desired reduction.

Similarly to the proof of
section 4
, since
X,Y
are smooth surfaces over
k
, it is enough to prove the theorem after we base change to a finite extension. Thus, we may assume
U(k),V(k)\neq\emptyset
, and that the rational map
\pi
is defined over
k
.
Since the group
F^{2}(X)/F^{2}(X)_{{\rm div}}

that so is the group
F^{2}(U)/F^{2}(U)_{{\rm div}}
. We will show that
F^{2}(V)/F^{2}(V)_{{\rm div}}
is torsion of finite exponent (and hence finite by

). Then, the finiteness of
F^{2}(Y)/F^{2}(Y)_{{\rm div}}
will follow from the forward direction of

.
We will prove that
F^{2}(V)/F^{2}(V)_{{\rm div}}

.

Since
\pi:U\to V

\pi_{\star}:H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(U)\to H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(V),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\pi^{\star}:H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(V)\to H_{0}^{\operatorname{sus}}(U),

.

).

. This gives the map

. For the pullback we can argue similarly by extending to the algebraic closure

In this section we use

Definition 5.3
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective surface over a
p
-adic field
k

X
is geometrically dominated by a product of curves
C_{1}\times C_{2}

\overline{k}

C_{1}\times C_{2}\dashrightarrow X_{\overline{k}}

Definition 5.4
.

Let
A
be an abelian variety over a
p
-adic field
k

\mathcal{A}
be the Néron model of
A
and
\mathcal{A}_{s}:=\mathcal{A}\times_{{\rm Spec\,}({\mathcal{O}}_{k})}{\rm Spec\,}(\kappa)

A

\mathcal{A}_{s}^{0}
containing the identity element of
\mathcal{A}_{s}
is a semi-abelian variety over the finite residue field

0\to T\to\mathcal{A}_{s}^{0}\to B\to 0,

where
B
is an ordinary abelian variety and

5.2.2.
Isotrivial Fibrations

We recall the following definition.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
Y\xrightarrow{\pi}C
be a fibration over a smooth projective curve
C
of genus
g\geq 0
. We say that
Y
is isotrivial if all smooth fibers of
\pi
are isomorphic.

It is well-known (
[
Ser96
, (1.1)]
) that over
\overline{k}
every isotrivial fibration is birational to a product-quotient surface
(C_{1}\times C_{2})/G
of unmixed type. This is a very wide class of surfaces. We next focus in particular to the
K3
surfaces that happen to have a structure of an isotrivial fibration. For isotrivial surfaces with
p_{g}=q=1
(resp.
p_{g}=q=2
) we refer to
[
Pol09
,
MP10
]
(resp.
[
CP20
]
).

Isotrivial
K3
surfaces

A classical example of an isotrivial
K3
surface is the Kummer surface
X={\rm Kum}(A)
associated to an abelian surface
A
. It is now known that the class of isotrivial
K3
’s goes much beyond the Kummer surfaces.

We recall that an automorphism
g
of a
K3
surface
X
is called
non-symplectic
, if it acts nontrivially on the nowhere vanishing holomorphic
2
-form
\omega
. In fact, if
g
has order
p
, a prime number, then
g(\omega)=\zeta_{p}\omega
, where
\zeta_{p}
is a primitive
p
-th root of unity. In
[
GP15
]
Garbagnati and Penegini proved that most
K3
surfaces admitting a non-symplectic automorphism of order
p
can be realized as the mimimal resolution of singularities of a product
C_{1}\times C_{2}
by a diagonal action of
{\mathbb{Z}}/p{\mathbb{Z}}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}/2p{\mathbb{Z}}
2
2

2

In fact a maximal irreducible component of the moduli space of
K3
surfaces with a nonsymplectic automorphism has such a structure.
. Moreover, they show that the curve
C_{2}
can be taken to be the hyperelliptic curve
D_{p}:y^{2}=u^{p}-1
. This curve has an automorphism
\delta_{p}:(x,y)\mapsto(\zeta_{p}x,y)
of order
p
, and an automorphism
\tau_{p}
of order
2p
given by the composition of
\delta_{p}
with the hyperelliptic involution. More precisely they prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.9
.

(
[
GP15
, Theorem 1.1]
)
Let
S
be a
K3
surface admitting a non-symplectic automorphism of order
p=3
(resp.
p=5,7,11,13
,
p=17,19
) whose fixed locus contains at least
2
(resp.
0,1
) curves. Then
S
is the minimal resolution of singularities of the quotient
(C_{1}\times D_{p})/(g_{1}\times\tau_{p})
, where
g_{1}
is an automorphism of
C_{1}
of order
2p
. The non-symplectic automorphism of order
p
on
S
is induced by the automorphism
id\times\delta_{p}
.

Using this recipe, they gave explicit examples (including equations) of
K3
surfaces for each one of the primes
p=3,5,7,11
(see
[
GP15
, Section 7]
). We note that the surfaces corresponding to
p=3
are elliptic, but most of the others are not.

5.2.3.
Symmetric Squares and geometrically simple Abelian Surfaces

The simplest example of a product-quotient surface of mixed type is the symmetric square
{\operatorname{\rm Sym}}^{2}(C)=(C\times C)/\langle\sigma\rangle
of a smooth projective curve
C
over
k
, where
\sigma
is the automorphism permuting the two factors. Note that since the fixed locus of
\sigma
is
1
-dimensional, the surface
{\operatorname{\rm Sym}}^{2}(C)
is smooth. We obtain the following Corollary.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective connected curve over
k
. Suppose that the Jacobian
J_{C}
of
C
has a mixture of potentially good ordinary and multiplicative reduction. Then
section 1
is true for
{\operatorname{\rm Sym}}^{2}(C)
. If
C
has genus
2
, then the same is true for
J_{C}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
C(k)\neq\emptyset
.
Consider the composition,

\sigma:C\times C\xrightarrow{\pi}{\operatorname{\rm Sym}}^{2}(C)\xrightarrow[\sim]{f}{\rm Pic}^{2}(C)\simeq{\rm Pic}^{0}(C)=J_{C}.

Here
\pi
is the degree
2
quotient map,
f
is the map that sends a divisor of degree
2
to its class in
{\rm Pic}^{2}(C)
, and the isomorphism
{\rm Pic}^{2}(C)\simeq{\rm Pic}^{0}(C)
is defined using a basepoint
P_{0}\in C(k)
. The map
f
is birational (
[
Mil08
, III.5, Theorem 5.1]
). Thus, this composition is a generically finite morphism of degree
2
and the result follows from
subsection 5.1
.

∎

In
[
Gaz19
]
the first author showed that for an abelian variety
A
over
k
with good ordinary reduction, the group
F^{2}(A)/F^{2}(A)_{{\rm div}}
is torsion, but no more information was given. The above Corollary allows us to improve this result for abelian surfaces.

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
A
be an abelian surface over
k
with a mixture of potentially good ordinary and multiplicative reduction. Then
section 1
is true for
A
and for the Kummer
K3
surface
{\rm Kum}(A)
associated to
A
.

Proof.

Since we have a generically finite rational map
\pi:A\dashrightarrow{\rm Kum}(A)
, the claim for the Kummer surface will follow from the claim for
A
.
Since we are allowed to extend to a finite extension, we may assume that
A
is isogenous to a principally polarized abelian surface
B
.
The case when
B=E_{1}\times E_{2}
is a product of elliptic curves is already proved in
[
GL24
, Theorem 1.2]
. The case when
B=J_{C}
follows from
subsubsection 5.2.3
. The only thing we need to verify is that isogenous abelian surfaces have the same reduction type.

It follows easily from the Néron-Ogg-Shafarevich criterion that
A
has good reduction if and only if the same is true for
B
(
[
Sil85
, VII, Corollary 7.2]
). Suppose first that both surfaces have good reduction. In this case if
\phi:A\to B
is an isogeny, then
\phi
induces an isogeny
\overline{\phi}:\overline{A}\to\overline{B}
between the reductions of
A,B
. Since
\ker(\overline{\phi})
is finite,
B
must have the same
p^{r}
-torsion as
A
for every
r\geq 1
.

Next assume that
A,B
have bad reduction. By extending the base field if necessary, we may assume that
A,B
have a mixture of good ordinary and split multiplicative reduction (see
subsection 5.2
). Then the connected component
\mathcal{A}_{s}^{0}
of the zero element of the special fiber of the Néron model
\mathcal{A}
of
A
fits into a short exact sequence

0\to T\to\mathcal{A}_{s}^{0}\to X\to 0,

where
T
is a torus and
X
is an ordinary abelian variety over the finite field
\kappa
and the same holds for
\mathcal{B}_{s}^{0}
. The reduction morphism
\overline{\phi}:\mathcal{A}_{s}^{0}\to\mathcal{B}_{s}^{0}
must preserve the toric pieces and since
\phi
is a finite surjective map, the dimensions of the tori and the abelian quotients must be the same. To show that both abelian quotients are ordinary one can argue similarly to the good reduction case.

∎

5.2.4.
Fermat Surfaces

We close this subsection by considering the diagonal hypersurfaces of degree
m
in
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{3}

X_{m}=\{a_{0}x_{0}^{m}+a_{1}x_{1}^{m}+a_{2}x_{2}^{m}+a_{3}x_{3}^{m}=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}^{3},

where
m\geq 4
, and
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in k^{\times}
.
Over the extension
L=k(\mu_{m},\sqrt[m]{a_{0}},\ldots,\sqrt[m]{a_{3}})
the surface
X_{m}
becomes isomorphic to the Fermat surface

F_{m}=\{x_{0}^{m}+x_{1}^{m}+x_{2}^{m}+x_{3}^{m}=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}^{3}.

Moreover, over the extension
L(\mu_{2m})
, there is a dominant rational map

\phi:C_{m}\times C_{m}\dashrightarrow F_{m},

constructed by Shioda (
[
Shi78
, (2.1)]
), where
C_{m}
is the Fermat curve of degree
m
,
C_{m}=\{x_{0}^{m}+x_{1}^{m}+x_{2}^{m}=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}^{2}
. The map
\phi
is defined as follows,

((x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}),(y_{0}:y_{1},y_{2}))\mapsto(x_{0}y_{2}:x_{1}y_{2}:\zeta_{2m}x_{2}y_{0}:\zeta_{2m}x_{2}y_{1}),

where
\zeta_{2m}
is a primitive
2m
-th root of unity.
When
m=4
,
X_{m}
is a
K3
surface, which is geometrically isomorphic to a Kummer surface
{\rm Kum}(A)
associated to an abelian surface
A
isogenous to a self-product
E\times E
of a CM elliptic curve (see
[
GL24
, Subsection 2.4]
). For every
m\geq 5
,
X_{m}
is a surface of general type.

The structure of the Jacobian variety
J_{\tilde{C}_{m}}
of the isomorphic Fermat curve
\tilde{C}_{m}=\{x_{0}^{m}+x_{1}^{m}=x_{2}^{m}\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}^{2}
over a finite field is well-known (
[
Yui80
]
). In fact it follows that for every prime
p\equiv 1\mod m
,
J_{\tilde{C}_{m}}
has good ordinary reduction (
[
Yui80
, Proposition 4.1, Theorem 4.2]
). Thus, we obtain the following Corollary.

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
X_{m}
be a diagonal hypersurface of degree
m
in
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{3}
where
k
is a
p
-adic field with
p\equiv 1\mod m
. Then
section 1
is true for
X_{m}
.

5.3.
Some computations of Suslin’s Homology

We close this article by giving some examples illustrating that our methods have the potential to give explicit information on Suslin’s homology.

Let
X
be a smooth projective surface over a
p
-adic field
k
and
j:U\hookrightarrow X
a dense open immersion.
As we saw in the proof of
section 4
, when
X-U
is
0
-dimensional, we have an equality
F^{2}(U)=F^{2}(X)
.
On the other hand, if
X-U
is a divisor, it is very often the case that
F^{2}(U)
is much larger than
F^{2}(X)
. This can already be seen in the following classical examples.

Example 5.13
.

Let
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
and
U={\mathbb{G}}_{m}\times{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
. Since
X
is a rational surface,
F^{2}(X)=0
. On the other hand, there is an isomorphism
F^{2}(U)\simeq K_{2}^{M}(k)
. The
K
-group
K_{2}^{M}(k)
is the direct sum of a finite group and an uncountable uniquely divisible group.

Example 5.14
.

Consider
X=C\times\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
, where
C
is a smooth projective connected curve over
k
with
C(k)\neq\emptyset
. Since
X
is a ruled surface, we again have
F^{2}(X)=0
. Consider the open subvariety
U=C\times{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
. Then there is an isomorphism
F^{2}(U)\cong V(C)
(see
[
KY13
, 1.7]
and
[
RS00
, 2.4.4]
), which is the direct sum of a finite group and a divisible group.
Assume further that genus of curve,
g(C)\geq 1
, then
V(C)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
is an infinite-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space (
[
Sz00
, Page 210]
). Furthermore, if
C
is a Mumford curve, then
V(C)_{\mathrm{tor}}
is infinite (see
[
AS07
, Page 178]
). Hence
V(C)_{{\rm tor}}
, the torsion subgroup and
V(C)/V(C)_{{\rm tor}}
, the uniquely divisible subgroup of
V(C)
(=
F^{2}(U)
) are both infinite.

One thing that these two examples have in common is that their Albanese variety
Alb_{U}
has nontrivial toric piece. Generalizing these examples, B. Kahn and T. Yamazaki
[
KY13
]
gave a
K
-theoretic description of
F^{2}(U)
for

U=C_{1}\times C_{2}
a product of two smooth quasi-projective curves over
k
, generalizing the work of Raskind and Spiess (
[
RS00
]
) on products of projective curves. Let
\overline{C_{i}}
be the smooth compactification of
C_{i}
and
X=\overline{C_{1}}\times\overline{C_{2}}
so that we have an open immersion
j:U\hookrightarrow X
. Suppose that
\overline{C_{i}}-C_{i}
is the support of a reduced divisor
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
. They showed an isomorphism

(11)

F^{2}(U)\simeq K(k;J_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}},J_{\mathfrak{m}_{2}})

(
[
KY13
, 1.7]
),
where
J_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is the generalized Jacobian of
\overline{C_{i}}
corresponding to the modulus
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
(see section
2.2.1
) and
K(k;J_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}},J_{\mathfrak{m}_{2}})
is the Somekawa
K
-group attached to the semi-abelian varieties
J_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}},J_{\mathfrak{m}_{2}}
(see
[
Som90
]
).
We can deduce from this that an equality
F^{2}(X)=F^{2}(U)
holds if and only if
J_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
coincides with the usual Jacobian of
\overline{C_{i}}
for
i=1,2
, if and only if
Alb_{X}=Alb_{U}
. Recalling the dimension of the maximal subtorus of the generalized Albanese variety from
subsection 2.1
we see that this happens if and only if the support of each
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
is at most one rational point.

One can thus pose the question: how large can
F^{2}(U)
be when
Alb_{X}=Alb_{U}
? In what follows we focus on the simplest case that this happens, namely when
U=X-C
for some projective smooth connected curve
C\hookrightarrow X
(see Example
2.1
). We show that even in this case the structure of
F^{2}(U)
can really vary.

Example 5.15
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective connected curve contained in
X=\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
with
C(k)\neq\emptyset
, and let
U=\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}-C
. Then we have
F^{1}(X)=F^{2}(X)=0
. Moreover, since
Alb_{U}=Alb_{X}=0
(see Example
2.1
), we have
F^{1}(U)=F^{2}(U)
. Therefore,

F^{2}(U)\cong\operatorname{coker}\left({\rm CH}^{2}(X,1)\xrightarrow{g}SK_{1}(C)\right)

(see
4
). The group
SK_{1}(C)\cong k^{\times}\oplus V(C)
. By the projective bundle formula (see
[
Bl84
]
), we have
{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k},1)\cong{\rm CH}^{0}(k,1)\oplus{\rm CH}^{1}(k,1)\oplus{\rm CH}^{2}(k,1)
. The group
{\rm CH}^{2}(k,1)
vanishes by a dimension argument, and
{\rm CH}^{0}(k,1)
vanishes by
[
MVW06
, Corollary 4.2]
. Hence,

{\rm CH}^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k},1)\cong{\rm CH}^{1}(k,1)\cong k^{\times}
. Also, the composite map

{\rm CH}^{2}(X,1)\xrightarrow{g}SK_{1}(C)\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}}k^{\times}

has finite cokernel (see Proposition
5.3
). Therefore, we have
F^{2}(U)\cong V(C)
(modulo finite kernel and cokernel).
In particular, if
V(C)_{\mathrm{tor}}
is infinite, then so is
F^{2}(U)_{\mathrm{tor}}
.
Furthermore, if the genus of
C
i.e.,
g(C)\geq 1
, then
V(C)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
is an infinite-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space (
[
Sz00
, Page 210]
). It then follows from the above discussion that
F^{2}(U)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
is also an infinite-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space.

Remark 5.16
.

This example can be further extended to surfaces which are
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-bundle. These examples show that
F^{2}(U)
can be too large. In fact, they suggest that the torsion divisible subgroup
(F^{2}(U)_{\mathrm{div}})_{\mathrm{tor}}
, the uniquely divisible quotient
F^{2}(U)_{\mathrm{div}}/(F^{2}(U)_{\mathrm{div}})_{\mathrm{tor}}

and the finite summand of
F^{2}(U)
can all be arbitrarily large, even when
F^{2}(X)=0
.

On the other extreme, generalizing the example of Kahn-Yamazaki, the next proposition shows that when the embedding
C\hookrightarrow X
is “special”, then an equality
F^{2}(X)=F^{2}(U)
can happen.

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
\pi:X\to C
be a smooth fibration, where
C
is a smooth projective curve over
k
with
C(k)\neq\emptyset
. Let
s:C\hookrightarrow X
be a section of the fibration and
j:U\hookrightarrow X
be the complement of the section. Then
F^{2}(U)=F^{2}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
J_{C}
be the Jacobian of
C
, and
P_{0}\in C(k)
a fixed rational point.
Keeping the same notation as in the proof of
section 4
, we have an exact sequence

{\rm CH}^{2}(X,1)\xrightarrow{g}SK_{1}(D)\xrightarrow{f}F^{1}(U)\xrightarrow{\beta}F^{1}(X)\to 0.

Since
D=s(C)\cong C
is a smooth curve, it follows that
Alb_{U}=Alb_{X}
(see Example
2.1
). Thus, the conclusion of the proposition will follow from the following.

Claim:
The map
{\rm CH}^{2}(X,1)\xrightarrow{g}SK_{1}(s(C))
is surjective.

As in the previous example, we have a decomposition
SK_{1}(s(C))\cong SK_{1}(C)\cong V(C)\oplus k^{\times}
, where
V(C)=\ker(SK_{1}(C)\xrightarrow{N}k^{\times})
is the kernel of the norm map. Note first that the pullback map
s^{\star}:{\operatorname{NS}}(X)\to{\operatorname{NS}}(s(C))\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}
is surjective. To see this, consider the fiber
X_{P_{0}}
above
P_{0}
. Then

s^{\star}([X_{P_{0}}])=[X_{P_{0}}]\cdot[s(C)]=1.

It remains to show that
V(C)
is contained in the image of
g
. It is well-known (
[
Som90
, Theorem 2.1]
) that there is an isomorphism
V(C)\simeq K(k;J_{C},{\mathbb{G}}_{m})
, with the Somekawa
K
-group attached to
J_{C}
of
C
and
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
. This group admits a surjection

\bigoplus_{L/k\text{ finite}}J_{C}(L)\times L^{\times}\twoheadrightarrow K(k;J_{C},{\mathbb{G}}_{m}).

In particular, the group
SK_{1}(C)\simeq{\rm CH}^{2}(C,1)
is generated by decomposable
(2,1)
-cycles. We have a commutative square,

Example 5.18
.

Generalizing the result of Kahn and Yamazaki, let
X=C\times C
be the self-product of a smooth projective curve over
k
with
C(k)\neq\emptyset
. Consider the diagonal embedding
\Delta:C\hookrightarrow X
and let
U=X-\Delta
. Then
F^{2}(U)=F^{2}(X)
.

Example 5.19
.

Let
A=E_{1}\times E_{2}
be a product of elliptic curves over
k
. Let
G
be a finite group acting on
E_{1}
by translations by a fixed torsion point
P_{0}\in E_{1}(k)
and on
E_{2}
by automorphisms such that
E_{2}/G\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
. Consider the diagonal action of
G
on
A
. This is a fixed point free action, and hence the quotient surface
X=A/G
is smooth. Such surfaces are called
bielliptic
, or
hyperelliptic
. The Albanese variety of
X
is
Alb_{X}=E_{1}^{\prime}
, where
E_{1}^{\prime}
is the elliptic curve
E_{1}/\langle P_{0}\rangle
, and the Albanese map
{\rm alb}_{X}:X\to E_{1}^{\prime}
is an elliptic fibration. The surface
X
has Kodaira dimension
0
and geometric genus
p_{g}=0
. Thus, it follows by
[
CTR91
, Théorème D]
and the main result of
[
BKL76
]
that
F^{2}(X)
is finite. It follows from
subsection 5.3
that for every section
s:E_{1}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow X
of the fibration,
F^{2}(X-s(E_{1}^{\prime}))=F^{2}(X)
. In particular, it is finite.

Remark 5.20
.

The above examples suggest that the structure of
F^{2}(U)
in the case when
U=X-C
depends on
V(C)
, the cycle-theoretic invariant of
C
. At the same time, it also depends on the embedding of the curve, as shown in
subsection 5.3
.
