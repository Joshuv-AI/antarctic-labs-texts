Gastroschisis‐Like Defect in a Crossbred Bovine Calf: Anatomical, Clinical and Surgical Perspectives
Authors: Mohammad Raguib Munif, Tanjin Tamanna Mumu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCongenital abdominal wall defects with evisceration are relatively uncommon in bovine neonates. This report describes a gastroschisis‐like anomaly in a one‐day‐old female crossbred calf, suspected to result from the prenatal rupture of an omphalocele. The condition was characterised by evisceration of the pyloric abomasum and a portion of the small intestine through a ventral abdominal wall defect (involving the umbilical ring) without a protective sac. Clinically, the calf presented with generalised weakness, lateral recumbency and an inability to suckle. Physical examination revealed hypothermia and mild cardiorespiratory depression, while the eviscerated organs appeared viable. Hematobiochemical evaluation demonstrated hyperlactatemia, mild hemoconcentration, borderline hypoproteinemia, hypoglycemia and electrolyte imbalances, alongside elevated renal biomarkers. Radiographic examination revealed no additional abnormalities. Following stabilisation with fluid therapy and active warming, surgical management involved the reduction of eviscerated contents and closure of the abdominal defect. Intraoperative findings of membranous sac remnants supported a diagnosis of prenatal sac rupture. The calf recovered uneventfully with postoperative supportive care, achieving complete healing by Day 29. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first documented case of a gastroschisis‐like defect with abomasal evisceration in a bovine calf. This condition represents a clinical emergency and should be included in the differential diagnosis for neonatal calves with ventral abdominal wall defects.

Introduction

Developmental anomalies of the ventral abdominal wall in bovine neonates represent an occasional yet clinically important subset of congenital malformations (Baird 1993; Prasad et al. 2017; Boripun et al. 2022). In cattle, the available literature predominantly addresses umbilical hernia and omphalocele, wherein abdominal viscera herniate through the umbilical ring and are typically covered by the skin, peritoneum, or a membranous sac (Singh et al. 2018; Abbas et al. 2021; Spadola et al. 2022; Munif et al. 2024). Although exposure of abdominal viscera has been described in calves, most cases represent traumatic or postnatal evisceration secondary to umbilical rupture, persistent umbilical ring, sepsis, or external injury rather than true congenital malformations (Dar et al. 2015; Singh et al. 2018; Koebel et al. 2023).

From an embryologic perspective, ventral abdominal wall defects (Figure 1A−C) arise from disruption of lateral body wall folding and fusion, as well as incomplete ventral midline closure, during early gestation, resulting in variable patterns of visceral exposure depending on the timing and extent of developmental failure (Sadler 2010; Boripun et al. 2022). In this context, gastroschisis represents a distinct entity from other umbilical anomalies. It is defined as a congenital, full‐thickness, paraumbilical abdominal wall defect through which abdominal viscera herniate in utero and, in the absence of a protective membrane covering, become directly exposed to the extrauterine environment after birth (Bergholz et al. 2012; Martín‐Alguacil and Avedillo 2020). In human neonatology, gastroschisis is a well‐characterised entity associated with severe fluid and heat loss, electrolyte derangements, serosal inflammation and a high risk of septic complications, rendering it a surgical emergency (Jaczyńska et al. 2023; Nassif et al. 2026). In contrast, veterinary literature, particularly in food animals, contains only isolated and indirect references to comparable defects, which are frequently misclassified. This limitation in diagnostic precision obscures the true spectrum of congenital ventral body wall defects and constrains evidence‐based clinical decision‐making in neonatal calves presenting with exposed abdominal viscera.

Furthermore, there is a paucity of veterinary literature concerning the involvement of the abomasum (Figure 1D) in congenital abdominal wall defects. While abomasal disorders in neonatal calves have been well described in the context of displacement, volvulus and motility dysfunction using clinical and ultrasonographic assessment (Ismail and Omoush 2019; Braun et al. 2022), reports of congenital abomasal evisceration remain scarce and are largely confined to cases of abomasal incarceration within umbilical hernias (Field 1988; Plüss et al. 2024). Additionally, occasional involvement of the pyloric end of the abomasum in omphalocele has been reported (Mee 1994). However, abomasal evisceration (partial or complete) associated with gastroschisis (atypical or typical) and concurrent intestinal protrusion has not been documented in bovine neonates to date. This distinction has important clinical implications, as exposed abomasal tissues are likely more susceptible to vascular compromise, metabolic alterations and delayed postoperative functional recovery (Fubini et al. 1991), which differ fundamentally from defects involving the intestine(s) alone. In such cases, prompt and accurate diagnosis, followed by timely interventions, is recommended for the survival of the newborn animals.

The current report outlines a case of atypical gastroschisis in a neonatal calf, presenting evisceration of the pyloric abomasum and part of the intestine, with membranous remnants at the margin of the body wall defect. The interpretation was supported by clinical assessment, diagnostic workup and intraoperative findings, with postoperative outcomes documented. The case is discussed in relation to the existing spectrum of congenital ventral abdominal wall defects in bovine calves, highlighting the challenges in distinguishing atypical gastroschisis from other umbilical or paraumbilical anomalies. The report provides evidence to inform clinical diagnosis and management of such uncommon presentations in large animal practice.

Case Presentation

Case History

A one‐day‐old female, farm‐reared, crossbred bovine calf (Sahiwal × Holstein‐Friesian), weighing 28.69 kg, was referred to a local veterinary hospital with a complaint of abdominal evisceration through the umbilical region. The calf was presented with this defect approximately five hours postpartum. On admission, the calf had a VIGOR score of 18, indicating marginal vitality (Table 1). The evisceration was observed immediately after birth, as the owner was present during calving of the dam (Sahiwal cow). Initial first‐aid measures included securing the exposed abdominal viscera by covering them with a clean cloth, based on advice from local non‐professional personnel before referral to the veterinary hospital. The dam was clinically healthy, maintained under standard feeding and housing conditions and was reportedly up to date on vaccination and deworming. Breeding records indicated an uneventful first parity following natural service with a bull of the same breed. In contrast, the present calf was born during the dam's second parity following artificial insemination using semen from a Holstein‐Friesian bull. The parturition was uncomplicated with an unassisted vaginal delivery. Postnatally, the calf demonstrated spontaneous cardiorespiratory activity but generalised weakness, lateral recumbency, reduced alertness and failure to initiate effective suckling from the dam, with intermittent yet unsuccessful attempts to stand. The owner was able to administer a limited volume of freshly harvested colostrum on two occasions via bottle feeding. No defecation or urination was observed prior to presentation.

Investigations

Physical examination of the ventral abdominal evisceration identified the exposed viscera as the pyloric antrum of the abomasum with an associated segment of the small intestine, without a membranous sac encapsulation (Figure 2A,B). The exposed organs exhibited mild serosal haemorrhage but were non‐exudative, non‐traumatised, minimally contaminated and clinically viable in appearance. The viscera were observed to protrude through a ventral abdominal wall defect measuring 4.61 cm along its longest axis, which was contiguous with the umbilical ring. The eviscerated organs maintained internal continuity with the abdominal cavity, via cranial and caudal visceral attachments, as part of the gastrointestinal tract. Anatomically, the majority of the wall defect was located slightly to the right of the ventral midline. However, involvement of the midline umbilical ring precluded classification as a purely right paraumbilical defect, instead indicating a broadly defined umbilical defect.

The calf presenting with the congenital anomaly: (A, B) full‐thickness ventral abdominal wall defect (black arrow) with exposure of the uncovered pyloric abomasum (white arrow) and small intestine (yellow arrow).

Apart from the presenting anomaly, the calf showed normal external conformation, including the head, limbs, trunk, tail, perineum, external genitalia and anus. Four anatomically normal rudimentary teats were present. The mucous membranes were light pink, with a capillary refill time of 1−2 s at the gingival margins. The external ear pinnae were cool to the touch. No abnormal discharges (oculo‐nasal, aural, vulvovaginal, or anal secretions) were noted.

Clinically, the calf was weak, non‐vocal and remained in lateral recumbency with mild depression. Approximately 7% dehydration was estimated using a cervical skin‐fold test (skin‐fold return time: ∼3 s) at the dorsal neck. The calf appeared moderately emaciated, without evidence of muscle fasciculations. The suckling reflex was weak and was determined manually using clean, gloved fingers placed over the tongue within the oral cavity. Clinical assessment during triage (Table 2) indicated moderate hypothermia accompanied by mild bradycardia and bradypnoea, while peripheral oxygen saturation remained normal. No additional cardiorespiratory abnormalities were detected on thoracic auscultation. Point‐of‐care hematobiochemical evaluation (Table 2) revealed a mild increase in circulating cellular components (red blood cells), hyperlactatemia, marginal decreases in blood pH, total protein, glucose, sodium and chloride concentrations, along with mild elevations in potassium, blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels.

Right lateral thoracoabdominal radiography, encompassing the ventral abdominal wall defect, was performed using a high‐frequency portable X‐ray unit (ORANGE 1060HF; ECORAY Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) with exposure factors of 66 kV and 10 mAs at a focal‐film distance of 75 cm. The image was acquired using a computed radiography system (FCR PRIMA Console; FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with tight beam collimation to the region of interest. The radiographic examination did not reveal additional congenital anomalies or evidence of cavitary effusion (image not shown).

Collectively, these observations supported a presumptive diagnosis of gastroschisis. No further imaging or immunological assays were performed.

Emergency Care and Monitoring

Based on initial assessments, the calf underwent immediate stabilisation with an intravenous (IV) bolus of 500 mL of lukewarm Hartmann's solution supplemented with 5% dextrose (Hartsol Plus, Libra Infusions Ltd.), administered over 30 min using a macrodrip set (15 gtt/mL). Concurrent active warming was instituted using overhead heat lamps, with a warming blanket placed beneath the calf. Intramuscular (IM) ceftiofur sodium at 2.2 mg/kg (Tifur Vet, ACME Laboratories Ltd.) was used as prophylactic antimicrobial coverage. Following stabilisation, the calf's VIGOR score improved to 22, indicating fair vitality with a moderate suckling reflex (Table 1). Subsequent monitoring demonstrated restoration of hemodynamic stability (Table 2). Accordingly, a decision was made to proceed with surgical correction following informed consent from the owner.

Surgical Intervention

The calf was premedicated with IM atropine sulfate at 0.04 mg/kg (Atrovet, Techno Drugs Ltd.) and sedated subsequently with IV diazepam at 0.1 mg/kg (Easium, Opsonin Pharma Ltd.). Under sedation, the surgical field encompassing the ventral abdominal wall defect was prepared aseptically by clipping superficial hair, cleansing the skin with 70% ethanol and scrubbing with 2% chlorhexidine gluconate, while avoiding contact with the eviscerated tissues. Then the exposed viscera were gently and thoroughly lavaged using lukewarm normal saline (0.9% NaCl) to remove external contamination and adherent debris, including blood clots (Figure 3A). Intraoperatively, IV fluid therapy was maintained using 5% dextrose in normal saline at 4 mL/kg/h. Close inspection revealed that the herniated contents comprised the pars pylorica of the abomasum, extending cranially to the incisura angularis (with continuity toward the corpus abomasi within the abdominal cavity) and, caudally, a segment of the small intestine, specifically the majority of the jejunum and the entirety of the duodenum, which remained cranially continuous with the pylorus. Interestingly, remnants of a membranous sac, resembling parts of the foetal membrane (e.g., amnion) and peritoneum, were identified along the margin of the ventral abdominal rent. These findings supported the notion of a prenatally ruptured omphalocele, leading to a revision of the presumptive diagnosis of true gastroschisis. A final diagnosis of gastroschisis‐like defect, involving the pyloric abomasum and small intestine, was subsequently established.

Intraoperative management of eviscerated organs: (A) gentle lavage of the exposed viscera with sterile isotonic saline and (B) surgical repair of a jejunal–mesenteric marginal tear (white arrow).

The intestine was gently detorsed and further exploration revealed a small tear at the mesenteric attachment to the jejunum, which was repaired by suturing (Figure 3B) with 3–0 chromic catgut (Trugut, Healthium Medtech Pvt. Ltd.). No concurrent abnormalities (e.g., patent urachus, engorged umbilical vessels, adhesion, rupture, haemorrhage, or local infection or inflammation) were observed. The reduction was performed in two phases with the calf positioned in dorsal recumbency using sterile surgical lubricant (Surgilube, HR HealthCare). First, the eviscerated intestinal loops were sequentially reduced manually into the abdominal cavity using gentle digital pressure (Figure 4A) through the existing orifice (i.e., the ventral abdominal wall defect located along the midline). Second, the pyloric chamber of the abomasum was returned; however, initial attempts were unsuccessful because its size exceeded the diameter of the orifice. Consequently, a small, controlled incision was made with straight scissors to enlarge the orifice (Figure 4B) following local infiltration with 2% lidocaine HCl (Jasocaine, Jayson Pharmaceuticals Ltd.), which permitted reduction of the pyloric abomasum into the abdominal cavity (Figure 4C,D). This manoeuvre also enabled inspection of the adjacent intracavitary organs, with the remaining abomasum cranially and the apex of the urinary bladder caudally appearing grossly normal. No further abdominal lavage was performed; however, 40 mL of lukewarm sterile normal saline was instilled intraperitoneally over the reduced viscera (Figure 5A) to maintain serosal hydration and minimise evaporative loss. The abdominal wall defect was subsequently closed using size‐1 polyglactin 910 (Vicryl, Ethicon, J & J Medical Devices), following gentle and cautious debridement (mechanical scraping) of the margins of the abdominal wall rent to expose a fresh raw surface and facilitate adhesion and healing. The peritoneum and abdominal musculature were approximated using a simple interrupted suture pattern (Figure 5B), followed by a second‐layer closure of the fascia and subcuticular tissues in a simple continuous pattern (Figure 5C). The second‐layer closure was undertaken after excision of remnants of the membranous sac. The irregular skin edges were then trimmed to create fresh viable margins and improve apposition, and skin closure (Figure 5D,E) was achieved using simple interrupted sutures with 2−0 black monofilament nylon (Ethilon, Ethicon, J & J Medical Devices). Finally, the surgical site was dressed using sterile gauze impregnated with 10% povidone‐iodine (Povisep 10% solution, Jayson Pharmaceuticals Ltd.) to minimise the risk of local contamination.

Postoperative Care and Follow‐up

Immediately after surgery, nursing care included moderate warming therapy using the aforementioned method. In addition, 5% dextrose in normal saline was delivered as a tapered IV infusion at 154 mL/h per animal for 4 h, repeated on four occasions over 48 h based on periodic reassessment. Supportive medication included the continuation of the same IM antibiotic (Tifur Vet) once daily at an identical dosage for five consecutive days, along with IV flunixin meglumine at 1.1 mg/kg (Lega VetACME Laboratories Ltd.) once daily for three successive days. Harvested colostrum from the dam and a commercial calf milk replacer at 10% of the calf's body weight were provided via bottle feeding or oesophageal tube (as appropriate), five times daily for six postoperative days until the calf resumed active suckling. Normal defecation (passage of meconium) and urination were observed within 12 h post‐surgery. A fly‐repellent spray (mixture of essential oils and neem extracts) was routinely used in the hospital recovery pen and the surgical site was dressed twice daily using the same antiseptic (Povisep 10% solution) for five days postoperatively. Furthermore, oral sucralfate was prescribed at 1.5 g per animal every eight hours for seven postoperative days [Gastalfet oral suspension (1 g/5 mL), Beximco Pharmaceuticals Ltd.]. Following the 3rd postoperative day, a supplement comprising amino acids, multivitamins, dextrose and electrolytes was administered subcutaneously at 1 mL/10 kg on alternate days for three injections [Aminovit Plus Vet, Popular Pharmaceuticals Ltd.; each 100 mL contains dextrose 5, sodium acetate 0.75, KCl 0.2, CaCl2 0.2, MgSO4 0.2, L‐leucine 0.06, L‐lysine HCl 0.0525, L‐isoleucine 0.0525, L‐valine 0.0525, L‐methionine 0.0525, L‐arginine 0.1425, L‐phenylalanine 0.035, L‐threonine 0.035, L‐tryptophan 0.0175, L‐histidine 0.002, L‐cystine 0.002, monosodium glutamate 0.008, nicotinamide (vitamin B3) 0.3, D‐pantothenol (vitamin B5) 0.01, thiamine HCl (vitamin B1) 0.01, pyridoxine HCl (vitamin B6) 0.01 and riboflavin (vitamin B2) 0.005 g]. The calf demonstrated progressive improvement in physical strength and vocalisation, attaining a stable standing posture and normal neonatal gait 96 h post‐surgery. Patient discharge was undertaken on the 5th postoperative day, contingent upon the owner's ability to provide equivalent care in the farm maternity pen.

The skin sutures were removed 13 days after surgery and the surgical site exhibited satisfactory healing. A follow‐up clinical evaluation (29 days postoperatively) confirmed recovery, with marked improvement in vitality (Table 1) and hematobiochemical indices (Table 2). No further diagnostic investigations were deemed necessary and the animal was subsequently discharged. Thereafter, weekly follow‐up consultations were conducted via mobile communication for six months, with a pictorial update obtained ∼2.5 months postoperatively (Figure 6). During these sessions, no complications were reported by the owner.

The recovered calf with normal growth, 73 days postoperatively.

Discussion

Development of the bovine abdominal cavity during embryogenesis is inherently complex, with congenital abdominal wall deformities predominantly localised to the umbilical and paraumbilical region (Boripun et al. 2022). The present case is distinct from other congenital conditions affecting the ventral abdominal wall in calves. Although the defect involved the umbilical ring, its slightly paraumbilical location, combined with the absence of skin or membranous coverage over the exposed viscera, clearly differentiates it from umbilical hernia and omphalocele (Wieland et al. 2014; Fazili et al. 2016; Munif et al. 2024). The condition is further distinguished from lethal schistosomus reflexus and perosomus elumbis. Schistosomus reflexus typically presents with a severe ventral body wall defect accompanied by spinal inversion, limb ankylosis and marked visceral malposition (Munif et al. 2023), while perosomus elumbis is characterised by agenesis or hypoplasia of the caudal parts of the vertebral column (lumbar, sacral and coccygeal segments), resulting in hindquarter musculoskeletal deformities, with or without visceral exposure (Helms et al. 2020). None of these axial or appendicular skeletal abnormalities were observed in the present case. Moreover, patent urachus was ruled out due to the absence of a persistent urachal remnant and lack of urine leakage from the umbilicus (Ha et al. 2018).

The precise pathogenesis of bovine gastroschisis remains incompletely understood; nevertheless, embryological evidence suggests that the defect likely arises during early gestation, coinciding with physiological umbilical herniation and ventral body wall formation (Hyttel 2010; Oluwafemi et al. 2020). In mammals, the conserved sequence of physiological umbilical herniation, intestinal looping or coiling and subsequent deherniation is essential for orderly intestinal positioning and the prevention of torsion or compression (Hyttel 2010; Boripun et al. 2022). Disruption of this sequence during prenatal development may predispose to congenital abdominal wall anomalies. The proposed pathogenic mechanisms for bovine gastroschisis include incomplete closure of the ventral abdominal wall due to delayed or defective myoblast migration and differentiation, localised vascular disruption associated with abnormal regression of the right umbilical vein and rupture of a pre‐existing omphalocele (Boripun et al. 2022). In human neonatology, however, gastroschisis is now considered to arise from rupture of the physiological hernia during intrauterine life, superseding earlier controversial theories that strictly defined gastroschisis as a paraumbilical defect unrelated to the umbilical cord (Beaudoin 2018). In light of this paradigm, it is plausible that prenatal rupture of an omphalocele may result in partial or complete loss of the protective membranous sac, exposing abdominal viscera and potentially leading to clinical misclassification as unequivocal true gastroschisis. In the present case, the slight paraumbilical location of the defect—despite involvement of the umbilical ring—together with non‐encapsulated evisceration and the presence of marginal membranous remnants, favours interpretation as a gastroschisis‐like defect rather than classic gastroschisis. While classic gastroschisis typically lacks any residual sac structures, the presence of membranous remnants in this case plausibly indicates a periparturient or late‐gestation rupture of an omphalocele sac. Furthermore, the necessity to surgically enlarge the defect to allow reduction of the pyloric abomasum supports the thought that the evisceration developed prenatally, progressed during foetal growth and attained a substantial size before birth. This reinforces a congenital intrauterine origin, in contrast to secondary development during parturition or as a consequence of postnatal trauma. This interpretation is further sustained by the owner's account of the periparturient events.

The coexistence of membranous remnants and umbilical involvement in this case may suggest a convergent developmental pathway where abnormal physiological herniation and umbilical cord formation overlap. Accordingly, this case underscores the diagnostic continuum between gastroschisis and omphalocele and highlights the need for cautious interpretation when residual membranes are identified (Supplementary File 1). Recognising this distinction is important both clinically and academically, as it reflects the underlying embryopathogenesis, informs case classification and contributes to the evolving understanding of heterogeneity in abdominal wall defects in bovine neonates.

Given the nature of the present work as a single case report, a comprehensive etiological investigation of the suspected congenital factors was not feasible. Existing literature suggests that genetic and epigenetic influences, environmental determinants (including exogenous or endogenous toxins), disruption of fibroblast growth factor–mediated ventral body wall closure and maternal immune milieu may contribute to this condition (Beaudoin 2018; Van Dorp et al. 2010; Boylan et al. 2020). Breed predisposition has also been documented, with Friesian sires historically associated with foetuses exhibiting congenital ventral abdominal wall defects, including omphalocele and abomasocele—entities closely related to gastroschisis (Mee et al. 1994). Such anomalies have also been reported in neonates of Holstein, Sahiwal and crossbred (Dar et al. 2015; Fazili et al. 2016; Wieland et al. 2014; Abbas et al. 2021; Koebel et al. 2023). Additionally, a female predominance, with a female‐to‐male ratio of approximately 2:1, has been suggested for ventral abdominal wall defects in calves (Boripun et al. 2022). The breed, sex and ancestral background described in the present case are consistent with these reported observations.

The clinical relevance of the present case is largely attributable to the physiological consequences of visceral exposure rather than the anatomical defect alone. Neonatal calves possess limited thermoregulatory capacity, immature renal concentrating ability and reduced metabolic buffering reserves (Robinson and Young 1988; Meyer 2025), which increase their vulnerability to conditions that disrupt abdominal integrity. The limited adaptive capacity of such neonates may further predispose them to rapid systemic destabilisation. In the present case, the onset of hypothermia, lethargy and metabolic derangements within hours postpartum may reflect a pathophysiological cascade involving fluid loss, hypovolemia and impaired tissue perfusion (Berchtold 2009; Koebel et al. 2023). These early systemic effects indicate that congenital abdominal wall defects with visceral exposure should be considered metabolic emergencies in addition to surgical conditions, necessitating prompt physiological stabilisation before definitive correction. Accurate diagnosis of this entity is clinically important, as misclassification may delay appropriate intervention and worsen prognosis.

The hematobiochemical profile in the calf supports a primarily perfusion‐related and dehydration‐driven process rather than irreversible metabolic decompensation (Trefz et al. 2012; Trefz et al. 2013; Trefz et al. 2017). The observed hyperlactatemia in the presence of near‐normal bicarbonate concentration and marginal acidaemia may suggest an early increase in anaerobic glycolysis due to reduced effective circulating volume and tissue hypoxia, without established metabolic acidosis. The mild elevations in blood urea nitrogen and creatinine observed in the present case are likewise consistent with prerenal azotemia secondary to dehydration and impaired renal perfusion (Sommardahl et al. 1997; Aydoğdu et al. 2023; Yanar et al. 2024). The electrolyte alterations in the calf followed a pattern well described in neonatal bovine stress states. Mild hyponatremia and hypochloremia with transient hemoconcentration identified herein may be attributed to extracellular fluid loss, redistribution and limited intake (Tsukano et al. 2024), while mild hyperkalemia may be explained by acidosis‐associated transcellular shifts and reduced renal excretion under dehydration (Trefz et al. 2013). The observed marginal hypoglycemia may reflect inadequate colostrum intake, alongside increased metabolic demand and immature hepatic gluconeogenesis (Trefz et al. 2016). Importantly, normalisation of these parameters following therapeutic interventions supports their functional and reversible nature. This was reflected in their progressive improvement during the postoperative period, indicating physiological restoration and resolution of the acute stress response.

A notable feature of the present case is the involvement of the pyloric abomasum, a comparatively rare and clinically consequential variation of congenital abdominal wall defects in calves. Direct exposure of abomasal tissue, as observed here, confers additional risk owing to the organ's vascular supply, secretory function and essential role in neonatal digestion. Ischemic or inflammatory compromise of the bovine abomasum may delay abomasal emptying, resulting in altered gastrointestinal dynamics and impaired growth (Burgstaller et al. 2017; Guarnieri et al. 2020). Nevertheless, abomasal viability was preserved in the present case, most likely due to the short interval between birth and surgical correction. Furthermore, the absence of inflammatory peel formation, serosal thickening (edema) and smooth muscle injury—changes typical for advanced gastroschisis (Nassif et al. 2026)—together with no volvulus‐like abnormalities and the meticulous intraoperative technique, contributed to the preservation of abomasal integrity.

The favourable outcome observed in this case underscores the importance of early detection, prompt stabilisation and timely surgical correction. The surgical repair resembling herniorrhaphy aligns with the procedure described elsewhere (Munif et al. 2024). Pre‐sedation administration of atropine was considered, given its cardiac stimulatory effects in clinically depressed animals (Short et al. 1986), thereby offering a balanced and reversible regimen. Gentle handling of exposed viscera, avoidance of excessive manipulation during reduction and controlled enlargement of the defect minimised the risk of iatrogenic injury, tissue damage and vascular compromise, while permitting repositioning of the viscera and associated mesentery without kinking or torsion. Layered closure of the abdominal wall provided adequate support while reducing tension and minimising the risk of seroma formation. The decision to prioritise reduction and primary closure rather than staged repair was justified by the size of the overt defect and the viability of the viscera.

The postoperative risks of infection and inflammation were mitigated through supportive medical management, including administration of the antibiotic (ceftiofur sodium) and anti‐inflammatory drug (flunixin meglumine). Perioperative fluid therapy was instituted to correct fluid deficits. Nutritional support included the provision of calf milk replacer; however, feeding of harvested raw colostrum was considered important to ensure transfer of maternal immunoglobulins (IgGs). Concurrent maternal plasma or serum infusion was not performed as the calf was stabilised with oral colostrum alone. Although evaluation of passive immunity using a bovine IgG assay would have provided additional insight, this was not undertaken due to unavailability at the point of care. The uneventful recovery, however, suggests that adequate passive transfer was likely achieved via the harvested colostrum. Oral administration of sucralfate was recommended to reduce the risk of mucosal irritation and ulceration, given its gastroprotective properties (Rees 1991), as the abomasum is the functional equivalent of the simple stomach. The fly‐repellent spray was applied to prevent myiasis. The parenteral (subcutaneous) supplement containing essential amino acids and multivitamins was administered to address hypoproteinemia and convalescent weakness and to support growth.

Although no postoperative complications were reported during the follow‐up period, the long‐term prognosis of such cases is largely determined by gastrointestinal function, risk of strictures or adhesions and subsequent growth performance. In general, restoration of abdominal wall integrity without bowel resection is associated with favourable gastrointestinal function, provided that their viability and motility are preserved at the time of surgical correction (Desrochers and Anderson 2016). However, intra‐abdominal adhesions are a recognised sequela of visceral exposure and surgical manipulation (Alonso et al. 2014), with the potential to result in delayed intestinal obstruction or digestive disturbances. In the present case, the absence of abdominal distension, colic, or impaired defecation during follow‐up suggests a low likelihood of clinically significant adhesions or strictures. Calves recovering without these complications are generally expected to achieve satisfactory growth and productivity, although mild growth retardation may occur following early‐life metabolic or surgical stress. The sustained improvement in vitality (Table 1) and steady growth and weight gain over six months postoperatively (as reported by the owner) therefore indicate a favourable long‐term prognosis. Collectively, these findings underscore that primary closure of the abdominal wall defect after visceral reduction, when appropriate, can result in a satisfactory outcome, even in cases involving abomasal protrusion.

Conclusion

This report documents a rare gastroschisis‐like defect with abomasal–intestinal evisceration in a bovine calf, emphasising the critical impact of exposed viscera on systemic physiology. The case highlights the necessity of prompt identification and management of neonatal abdominal wall defects to prevent secondary complications. Timely surgical correction following primary stabilisation enabled gradual recovery, underscoring the condition as a dual metabolic and surgical emergency. Future prospective studies or genomic investigations are warranted to clarify the embryological mechanisms and congenital risk factors underlying such anomalies in cattle.
