Incidental Isolated Bilateral Clavicular Agenesis Discovered During Preoperative Hernia Assessment of a 78-Year-Old Male in Rural Uganda: A Case Report
Authors: Bakwanamaha Maha Alain, Arthur Ajwang, Mugarura Anwar Biraali, Ojangole Albert, Micheal Mugenyi, Mumbere Vahwere Bienfait
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBilateral agenesis of the clavicles is an extremely rare congenital anomaly. Introduction

The clavicle is the first bone in the human body to begin the process of ossification during embryogenesis (around the 5th to 6th week of gestation) and the last to complete it.1 Congenital anomalies of the clavicle range from partial hypoplasia to complete agenesis. Complete bilateral agenesis of the clavicles is a remarkably rare clinical entity, most frequently encountered as a hallmark of Cleidocranial Dysplasia.2

While the prevalence of cleidocranial dysplasia (CCD) is estimated at 1:1,000,000, no reliable prevalence or incidence data currently exists specifically for isolated bilateral clavicular agenesis due to its rarity in literature.3,4 The cleidocranial dysplasia is primarily caused by mutations in the RUNX2 (also known as CBFA1) gene located on chromosome 6p21, which encodes a transcription factor essential for osteoblast differentiation and skeletal morphogenesis.3 Patients usually present in early childhood with delayed closure of fontanelles, midface hypoplasia, multiple supernumerary or unerupted teeth prominent phenotypic features that typically mandate early pediatric or orthodontic investigation, and an abnormal ability to appose their shoulders anteriorly due to clavicular defects.5

Because the phenotypic manifestations in the skull and dentition are usually prominent, diagnosis is rarely delayed into late adulthood. Isolated bilateral agenesis of the clavicle, without the accompanying cranial or dental features of Cleidocranial dysplasia, is even rarer.6 We present a highly unusual case of a 78-year-old male who lived an active, asymptomatic life, only to be incidentally diagnosed with complete bilateral clavicular agenesis during a preoperative workup for a recurrent inguinal hernia.

Case Presentation

A 78-year-old male from rural Uganda presented with a reducible swelling in the left groin that had been progressively increasing in size over the past two years. The patient reported mild, dull, non-radiating pain over the mass but denied any symptoms of bowel obstruction, vomiting, constipation, or lower urinary tract symptoms.

His past medical history was notable for well-controlled essential hypertension, managed with oral amlodipine and losartan for more than 30 years. Ten years before this presentation, he had undergone a left-sided inguinal hernioplasty at another facility. He had no history of diabetes mellitus, transfusions, or known allergies. He reported no history of tobacco use or alcohol consumption.

On physical examination, the patient was conscious, alert, oriented, and in no acute distress. Vital signs revealed a blood pressure of 138/79 mmHg and a pulse rate of 90 beats per minute. An examination of the head and neck demonstrated normal facial symmetry without midface hypoplasia, hypertelorism, or persistent cranial suture defects. Basic intraoral evaluation showed adult dentition without obvious gross retention or severe malocclusion. Abdominal examination revealed fullness in the left iliac fossa and a reducible left inguinal mass with a positive cough impulse, confirming the clinical diagnosis of a recurrent left inguinal hernia. On chest auscultation, cardiovascular and respiratory system examinations were unremarkable, with normal S1 and S2 heart sounds and clear, equal bilateral air entry.

Incidental to the abdominal findings, exposure of the patient’s chest and shoulders revealed a notable anatomical abnormality. The patient exhibited distinct drooping of both shoulders (Figure 1). Upon functional assessment, there was marked hypermobility of the shoulder girdle, allowing the patient to nearly appose his shoulders in the anterior midline. There were superficial skin changes consistent with Tinea versicolor on the anterior chest, but no surgical scars in the clavicular region. The patient reported that his shoulders had “always been this way”, and he had worked a manual labor job most of his life without any physical limitations, pain, or weakness in his upper extremities.

Clinical photograph showing drooping shoulders.

Preoperative laboratory investigations were within normal limits: Hemoglobin 16.4 g/dL, Hematocrit 48.8%, White Blood Cell count 5.15 x 109/L, and Platelets 263 x 109/L. His blood group was O positive.

As part of the standard preoperative assessment for elderly patients, a standard anterior-posterior (AP) chest radiograph was ordered. The radiograph revealed a completely normal cardiac silhouette and clear lung fields; however, it dramatically demonstrated the complete bilateral absence of the clavicles (agenesis). The thorax had a somewhat bell-shaped contour, but the rib cage and remaining visible skeletal structures appeared intact (Figure 2).

AP chest radiograph showing complete bilateral clavicular agenesis.

The patient was counseled regarding the diagnosis of the recurrent hernia and the incidental finding of his absent clavicles. Informed consent was obtained for an open left inguinal hernioplasty. The patient was admitted to the surgical ward, started on intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g) as a single preoperative dose for surgical prophylaxis, and successfully underwent the hernia repair under spinal anesthesia.

The surgery was uncomplicated, and no respiratory or airway difficulties were encountered during anesthesia administration, a factor occasionally complicated by undiagnosed anatomical anomalies. Postoperatively, the patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged. No orthopedic or genetic interventions were deemed necessary for the clavicular agenesis given the patient’s advanced age, complete lack of symptoms, and excellent functional adaptation.

Discussion

Biomechanical and Developmental Perspectives of the Clavicle

As the sole bony strut connecting the axial and upper appendicular skeleton, the clavicle positions the shoulder girdle and protects underlying neurovascular structures.7,8 Its complex intramembranous and endochondral ossification renders it uniquely vulnerable to RUNX2-mediated genetic dysregulation.9

Differential diagnoses for absent or hypoplastic clavicles include Cleidocranial Dysplasia (CCD), Pycnodysostosis, Crane-Hecht syndrome, and post-traumatic or osteolytic resorption.2 In this patient, the absence of short stature, dense fragile bones, dysmorphic facial features, or cranial abnormalities helps clinically differentiate this presentation as an isolated, non-syndromic congenital anomaly.6,7

Cleidocranial Dysplasia and Phenotypic Variability

Bilateral agenesis of the clavicles is most frequently identified as a pathognomonic feature of Cleidocranial Dysplasia (CCD), an autosomal dominant skeletal dysplasia with a prevalence of approximately 1:1,000,000.2,4 CCD typically presents with a constellation of findings: persistent fontanelles, delayed closure of cranial sutures, wormian bones, and dental abnormalities such as supernumerary teeth and delayed eruption of permanent dentition.2

However, the degree of clavicular involvement in CCD is highly variable, ranging from mild hypoplasia to complete aplasia, as seen in this 78-year-old patient. The absence of associated craniofacial or dental stigmata in this case suggests either a highly localized phenotypic expression of a RUNX2 mutation or an exceptionally rare instance of isolated bilateral clavicular agenesis. The delayed diagnosis into the eighth decade of life is statistically rare, as the characteristic shoulder hypermobility permitting the anterior apposition of the shoulders usually prompts pediatric investigation.4,7

Functional Adaptation and Compensatory Mechanisms

An important observation in this case is the preservation of full functional capacity for nearly 80 years despite the absence of a primary skeletal support structure. The patient’s history of manual labor in a rural setting without significant musculoskeletal morbidity highlights the adaptability of the shoulder girdle’s dynamic stabilizers. In the absence of the clavicular strut, the serratus anterior, sternocleidomastoid, trapezius, and pectoralis major muscles undergo functional remodeling to provide the necessary scapulothoracic stability required for upper limb elevation and load-bearing.8 This case supports the hypothesis that while the clavicle optimizes biomechanical leverage, it is not strictly essential for the activities of daily living or rigorous manual tasks, provided the muscular compensatory mechanisms remain intact.10 Previous literature suggests that while the clavicles are functionally important, their absence is surprisingly well-compensated by the musculature of the shoulder girdle, specifically the trapezius, serratus anterior, sternocleidomastoid, and pectoralis major muscles.8,10

Global Health and Diagnostic Constraints in Rural Settings

The management of this case underscores the challenges inherent in global health, particularly in resource-limited rural environments. In high-resource settings, the diagnostic protocol for suspected skeletal dysplasia involves molecular genetic analysis to identify mutations in RUNX2 on chromosome 6p21.9,11 Such tests are critical for assessing associated risks, such as bone mineral density loss or hearing impairment.

In rural Uganda, however, the lack of genomic infrastructure and the high cost of molecular diagnostics often exceeding the patient’s annual income preclude such investigations.12,13 Consequently, clinical acumen and conventional radiography remain the primary diagnostic modalities. This case illustrates the “natural history” of a congenital anomaly in an environment where healthcare access is scarce. It emphasizes the need for a pragmatic approach to medicine that prioritizes functional outcomes over molecular confirmation when the latter is financially inaccessible and would not alter the immediate surgical management.13

Preoperative Implications and Clinical Restraint

The incidental discovery of clavicular agenesis during a preoperative evaluation for an inguinal hernia serves as a reminder of the necessity of a systematic physical examination. While the hernia was the primary surgical concern, the anatomical variation of the shoulder girdle holds significant implications for perioperative care. The absence of the clavicle alters the anatomical landmarks essential for central venous catheterization, potentially increasing the risk of iatrogenic pneumothorax or vascular puncture.14 Furthermore, CCD-associated skeletal variations can include narrow upper airways or vertebral anomalies that may complicate anesthetic induction and patient positioning.11

Finally, this case offers a lesson in clinical restraint. The detection of a dramatic anatomical anomaly in an elderly, asymptomatic patient often triggers a “cascade of intervention”. However, given the patient’s successful lifelong adaptation, the decision to proceed with the hernia repair without pursuing exhaustive and invasive orthopedic workups was justified. The preservation of homeostasis over nearly eight decades suggests that in such instances, the radiograph should be treated as an incidental finding rather than a pathology requiring correction.

Limitations

A notable limitation of this report is the unavailability of genetic sequencing and advanced craniofacial CT in our rural setting. Furthermore, a specialized dental consultation and orthopantomogram (OPG) were not obtained. Consequently, while clinical exam revealed no classical extra-oral or dental features of CCD, a localized genetic variant or subclinical dental manifestation cannot be definitively excluded.

Take-Home Message for Global Clinicians

For global clinicians, this case underscores that major skeletal anomalies like bilateral clavicular agenesis can remain entirely asymptomatic and functionally compensated into advanced age, even permitting demanding manual labor. Furthermore, it highlights the critical value of routine baseline preoperative radiography in resource-limited settings for uncovering occult, lifelong congenital conditions. Finally, medical practitioners must recognize that classic genetic syndromes can manifest as isolated, benign phenotypic variations, completely lacking the expected systemic or craniofacial stigmata often described.

Conclusion

We report an unusual case of complete bilateral clavicular agenesis incidentally discovered in a 78-year-old male preparing for a recurrent inguinal hernia repair. This case illustrates the rarity of reaching advanced age without a formal diagnosis of such a congenital skeletal variation. It underscores the remarkable compensatory capabilities of the human musculoskeletal system and serves as a reminder that routine preoperative assessments can unmask lifelong anatomical secrets that require no clinical intervention.
