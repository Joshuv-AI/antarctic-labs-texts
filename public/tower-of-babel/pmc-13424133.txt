Financial incentives increase uptake and perceived effectiveness of autonomous medical AI, yet patients still seek human reconfirmation
Authors: Haiyang Yang, Tinglong Dai, Risa M. Wolf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly used for diagnostic screening. In diabetic retinopathy screening, autonomous AI systems can identify disease without a specialist. However, patient trust in AI remains a challenge, and it remains unknown whether and how financial incentives—commonly used to promote uptake of medical services—can increase patient acceptance of AI-based care. We examined, in a randomized vignette experiment, whether eliminating a copay would increase patients’ willingness to choose AI over an eye care professional’s (ECP’s) exam, and how patients would respond to an AI diagnosis. We conducted a randomized vignette experiment with 248 U.S. adults with type 1 diabetes, recruited online between August and December 2024. Participants were presented with a primary-care vignette in which they were due for annual diabetic eye screening. An autonomous AI tool was available as an alternative to ECP referral. Participants were randomized in a 2×2 design: (1) no copay vs. $50 copay for AI screening; (2) copay waiver sponsored by insurer vs. AI developer. Participants chose between AI or ECP screening, and rated AI effectiveness. Those choosing AI or ECP indicated their willingness to seek a second opinion (from ECP or AI, respectively) after receiving a normal vs. abnormal result. Waiving the copay significantly increased AI selection (81% vs. 43%, p < 0.001); the source of the incentive did not affect choice. No copay also improved perceived AI effectiveness (mean score 3.67 vs. 3.24, p = 0.02). Patients choosing AI (vs. ECP) were far more likely to seek reconfirmation after abnormal (mean 6.69 vs. 2.41) or even normal (3.43 vs. 2.47) results (both p < 0.001). Eliminating cost barriers increases participants’ willingness to opt for AI-based screening, but they still seek human reconfirmation. Incentives can promote AI adoption, but integration strategies must address persistent preferences for ECP reconfirmation—even when AI results are normal.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being integrated into diagnostic medicine1,2. In ophthalmology, autonomous AI systems can now detect diabetic retinopathy from retinal photographs without the need for a specialist, enabling screening to take place during routine primary care visits3,4. By lowering logistical and access barriers, these technologies have the potential to increase screening rates and enable earlier detection of preventable vision loss. Real-world deployments have shown encouraging results5–7. For example, a recent implementation study in a large U.S. health system found that primary care clinics using an autonomous AI tool achieved greater improvements in diabetic eye screening adherence than clinics relying on usual care pathways, with particularly pronounced gains in underserved populations8. These findings suggest that AI-based screening may contribute to greater equity in chronic disease prevention. In addition, patients who receive abnormal results from AI-based eye screening are more likely to complete follow-up with an eye-care provider7,9.

Yet the promise of AI in medicine cannot be realized without patient acceptance10. Although patients are central to the uptake of any health innovation, much of the existing discourse around AI implementation has focused on technical performance, clinician adoption, and regulatory oversight8,11–13. Far less attention has been paid to how patients actually engage with autonomous AI tools when these systems are introduced into clinical workflows14. Emerging evidence points to a persistent trust gap: surveys indicate that a majority of U.S. adults are uncomfortable with AI-generated diagnoses in their own care and express low trust in health systems’ ability to use AI responsibly15. Prior work also suggests that patient acceptance is shaped by trust in health systems and concern about how AI may affect the patient-provider relationship 15. Conversely, when AI systems are perceived as opaque or unfamiliar, they may elicit hesitation or resistance, potentially limiting their effectiveness even when technical performance is strong.

Incentives are commonly used in healthcare to encourage preventive care and treatment adherence, and patients often view such incentives as supportive rather than coercive16,17. Whether similar strategies might promote acceptance of AI-based diagnostics remains largely unexplored. It is also unclear whether the source of the incentive matters—whether patients respond differently to an AI tool offered free of charge by their insurer versus one subsidized by the technology’s developer. The former might signal clinical endorsement and system-level integration; the latter might elicit suspicion about the motives behind the offer.

Another important but understudied question is how patients behave after receiving results from an AI tool. Autonomous AI systems are designed to make diagnoses on par with (and sometimes more accurately than) clinicians, yet little is known about whether patients trust AI diagnoses, especially when the results are “normal.” If patients routinely seek confirmatory testing after a normal AI result, the potential efficiency gains of such tools may be reduced or negated. Conversely, if patients readily follow up after an “abnormal” AI finding, the technology may still serve as an effective entry point into care. Understanding these behavioral dynamics is important for designing workflows that improve both trust and efficiency.

In this study, we examined whether financial incentives affect patients’ willingness to use an AI-based diabetic retinopathy screening tool and their perception of AI effectiveness, whether the identity of the incentive sponsor alters that choice, and how patients respond to screening results when AI replaces the eye care professional (ECP) at the initial point of care. Our aim was to generate empirical insight into patient decision-making at the intersection of AI, trust, and behavioral economics. Although focused on one diagnostic context, the findings have broader implications for how autonomous AI systems are introduced to patients and integrated into frontline care. Conceptually, financial incentives may act as an economic signal that shapes patients’ perceptions of an AI tool’s credibility, which in turn influences their trust and behavior18,19. For instance, covering the out-of-pocket cost of AI screening could implicitly signal clinical endorsement or high quality, raising patients’ confidence in the AI’s effectiveness and their willingness to use it. We define adoption as the initial choice of the AI tool (over a human provider) and reliance as the extent to which patients trust and act on the AI’s result without seeking additional confirmation. Crucially, initial adoption does not guarantee full trust. Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material illustrates this framework: covering the copay may enhance the perceived effectiveness of AI, leading more patients to choose AI screening, but a residual trust gap can still drive many to seek a human second opinion even after an AI exam.

Participant characteristics

Initial uptake of AI vs. ECP

Overall, 62.5% (155/248) chose the AI over the ECP exam, but uptake was significantly higher with no copay (Fig. 1a). Specifically, among those who were informed that the AI screening would be without any copay, 81% opted for AI, compared with 43% of those who were told that the AI screening would entail a $50 copay. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001), corresponding to an odds ratio (OR) of 5.47 (95% CI for OR: [2.54–11.77]) for choosing AI when no copay was required. The identity of the copay sponsor had no detectable impact on uptake (p = 0.54, OR = 1.25, 95% CI for OR: [0.61, 2.57]), and there was no significant interaction between incentive and sponsor (p = 0.96, OR = 1.03, 95% CI for OR: [0.33, 3.27]). Thus, the presence of a financial incentive (waiving the copay) drove uptake, with no detectable difference based on who covered the cost. Perceived comparative effectiveness of AI

Compared with those who faced a copay, participants who were offered free AI screening tended to view the AI tool as more effective relative to an ECP. On the 7-point comparative effectiveness scale (with higher values meaning greater perceived AI effectiveness), the no-copay group’s mean was significantly higher at 3.67 (standard deviation [SD] 1.64) compared to 3.24 (SD 1.59) in the copay group (β = 0.64, p = 0.02). The incentive source factor did not significantly influence this perception. In other words, waiving the cost modestly improved participants’ confidence in the AI’s ability. An ordinal logistic regression on responses to each of the two scale items of the comparative effectiveness measure also showed a significant incentive effect (item 1: β = 0.74, p = 0.02; item 2: β = 0.74, p = 0.02), indicating robustness to modeling assumptions. A mediation analysis (PROCESS Model 4; 5000 bootstrap resamples) indicated that the no-copay incentive’s impact on AI uptake was partly associated with increased perceived effectiveness. The indirect effect of the incentive (via perceived effectiveness) was estimated at 0.38 (95% CI: [0.03, 0.84]), indicating that the incentive’s impact on uptake was only partly explained by improved AI perceptions—the majority of the uptake gain came directly from removing the cost barrier.

Follow-up intentions (second opinion)

We next examined participants’ follow-up intentions after receiving the results (note: because initial screening choice was self-selected rather than randomized, these analyses are associational), focusing on whether they would seek a follow-up second examination using the alternative method.

For an “abnormal” result (i.e., the screening indicated signs of diabetic retinopathy [DR]): participants who had used the AI were almost universally likely to seek a second opinion from an ECP (Fig. 1b). The AI-first group’s average likelihood rating was 6.69 out of 7 (SD 0.98), indicating a near “very likely” intent to get a follow-up exam with an ECP. In fact, 85.2% of those in the AI-first group responded with the maximum 7 (“very likely”) when asked about seeing an ECP if the AI found abnormalities. In contrast, participants who initially went to an ECP for screening were far less inclined to get a second screening by the AI if the ECP found an abnormality. The ECP-first group’s mean likelihood was only 2.41 (SD 1.97) on a 7-point scale, meaning most were “unlikely” to seek an AI second opinion after their ECP had already detected disease. The difference between the AI-first and ECP-first groups in the abnormal result scenario was highly significant (β = 4.28, p < 0.001). This gap remained significant after controlling for incentive group, incentive source, and demographic variables (β = 4.29, p < 0.001).

For a “normal” result (i.e., the screening found no signs of DR): a more revealing pattern emerged (Fig. 1c). If the initial exam was done by the AI and it found nothing abnormal, participants showed some hesitation about relying on that result alone. The AI-first group’s average likelihood of seeking a follow-up second exam with an ECP was 3.43 (SD 2.14) on a 7-point scale. This indicates that, on average, they were relatively neutral about getting a confirmatory ECP visit when the AI gave an all-clear—though a subset were likely to do so. By contrast, those who had initially gone to an ECP and received a normal result were far less inclined to seek any further testing by AI: the ECP-first group’s mean likelihood of seeking an AI screening after a normal ECP exam was 2.47 (SD 2.01), indicating most felt no need for additional confirmation. That is, when the result is normal, participants in the AI-first group were significantly more likely to seek a second examination than those in the ECP-first group (β = 0.95, p < 0.001). This gap also remained significant after covariate adjustment (β = 0.94, p = 0.002). Notably, this tendency toward extra confirmation occurred despite explicit information in the scenario that “when nothing is detected by the AI system, your exam results are considered normal. You will NOT need to see a specialist for further examination.” Thus, even with explicit reassurance, a proportion of patients who chose AI initially would still seek a follow-up second examination with an ECP after a normal AI result.

Discussion

This research explored the impact of incentivization on patients’ decisions to use AI, as well as the associated mechanisms and implications. Even a relatively small financial barrier had a major impact on uptake: eliminating a modest out-of-pocket cost had a powerful effect—with free autonomous AI screening, a large majority opted for AI, whereas most patients facing a $50 copay for AI preferred the ECP exam. Importantly, we did not find any evidence that the source of the incentive mattered. Patients responded similarly whether the free screening was presented as a benefit from their insurer or as a courtesy from the AI developer. This suggests patients were not more distrustful of an incentive coming from a technology company versus their insurer, contrary to the notion that a “free” offer from an AI developer evokes patient suspicions.

Covering the out-of-pocket cost also slightly increased patients’ confidence in AI. Participants who faced no cost rated the AI as more effective than those who had to pay, indicating that removing a cost barrier not only reduces financial disincentives but may also signal the capability of the AI option and hence increase patients’ confidence in it. Our mediation analysis indeed suggests that improved perceived effectiveness of the AI accounted for part of the incentive’s impact on uptake.

However, patients’ trust in the autonomous AI’s results appeared to lag behind their initial willingness to use it. Consistent with recent research findings15,20, patients appear less willing to rely on AI than on human clinicians. In the current study, many participants who opted for AI screening still sought reconfirmations from eye care professionals (ECPs), even when the AI result was normal. This occurred despite clear guidance in the scenario that no follow-up was necessary. In contrast, participants who saw an ECP rarely indicated interest in a follow-up second AI exam when the ECP found no abnormalities. These findings suggest that patients place greater confidence in an ECP’s “all-clear” than in an AI-generated one, reflecting a persistent aversion to relying solely on automated tools. If such preferences lead to unnecessary follow-up visits after normal AI findings, the potential efficiency gains of AI-based screening may be undermined.

From a clinical and policy perspective, our findings suggest a dual strategy when deploying autonomous AI diagnostic tools. First, removing financial obstacles can be a highly effective way to promote initial uptake of AI-based services. In contexts like diabetic retinopathy screening, where adherence is suboptimal, making AI screenings free to patients could substantially increase screening rates and potentially lead to earlier disease detection. For example, an autonomous AI deployed in primary care yielded a 7.6% higher increase in diabetic eye screening rates compared to usual care, with particularly large gains in underserved populations8. Our results suggest that removing patient copays could further boost usage of such AI tools. This strategy may also improve the overall cost-effectiveness of AI screening programs—prior modeling studies indicate that autonomous AI-based DR screening can be cost-saving by preventing vision loss and reducing unnecessary referrals11,21.

Second, it is important to address the trust gap surrounding AI-produced results. Patients may need education and reassurance to feel comfortable with AI-only results22,23. Incorporating clinician endorsement of AI findings and providing clear follow-up guidelines (for instance, emphasizing that a normal AI result is sufficient and scheduling routine re-screening in a year) could help reduce unnecessary second-opinion visits. Over time, as AI tools become more familiar to patients and build a proven track record, patient trust may improve. Clear communication and support can help patients gain confidence in AI’s role, so they do not feel a need to “double-check” when it is unnecessary24.

Finally, the use of financial incentives to influence patient screening choices warrants ethical consideration. On one hand, waiving costs is a well-established strategy to promote preventive care and is often viewed by patients as a helpful nudge rather than undue coercion18,19. On the other hand, selectively subsidizing services can raise concerns about autonomy and justice25. In our context, eliminating the copay simply removed a financial barrier to access (improving equity) rather than directly paying patients to choose AI. This approach likely poses fewer ethical pitfalls, but transparency about the incentive’s source and intent remains important. Stakeholders should weigh these considerations—ensuring that incentives empower patient choice without eroding trust in the care process—when designing AI deployment programs. Moreover, if a large portion of patients prefer to seek a second opinion from a human provider after receiving the AI screening, the overall cost may in fact be higher due to the costs associated with the second opinion; improving trust in AI is therefore critical to cost-effectiveness.

This study has limitations. It was based on a hypothetical scenario and self-reported intentions, which may not perfectly translate to real-world behavior. Our participants, drawn from an online type 1 diabetes community (with self-reported diagnoses) and mostly insured, highly educated, and actively engaged in their care, may not represent all patients with diabetes. One might expect even greater AI hesitancy among patients less familiar with technology or less connected to online communities. We focused on individuals with type 1 diabetes; results might differ in other populations (e.g., type 2 diabetes) or with different incentive structures. Moreover, because the mediator and outcome were assessed in the same survey, our mediation findings reflect a statistical association rather than a confirmed causal pathway. Finally, propensity to seek follow-up second examination was self-reported; future research should examine actual follow-up behavior when AI screening is implemented.

Despite these limitations, this study offers novel insights into patient behavior at the point of AI-enabled diagnosis. We show that financial incentives can substantially increase patients’ willingness to select an autonomous AI diagnostic option. At the same time, some still seek ECP reconfirmation, indicating a reluctance to rely on AI-only screening pathways. Together, these findings underscore that while cost incentives may accelerate initial adoption of AI, they do not guarantee that patients will fully trust or rely on the AI’s findings. In our study, many participants who chose AI still wanted ECP reconfirmation, indicating a gap between adoption and reliance. Pairing incentives with measures to improve trust calibration (e.g., education, clinician endorsement) will be critical for realizing the efficiency and access gains promised by autonomous AI in clinical care.

In conclusion, waiving even a modest patient copay dramatically increased participants’ stated willingness to opt for an AI-based diabetic retinopathy screening. Cost appears to be a substantive barrier to patient adoption of medical AI. At the same time, patients’ hesitancy to trust AI results, as evidenced by their desire for human reconfirmation, indicates that removing financial barriers alone is not sufficient to ensure seamless integration of AI into care. Both affordability and trust must be addressed. Stakeholders implementing AI screening should pair cost incentives with strategies to bolster patient confidence in AI, thereby maximizing the technology’s benefits for early detection, increasing health system efficiency, and improving health outcomes on a larger scale.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional, randomized experiment administered through Qualtrics, an online survey platform. Participants were adults (aged ≥18 years) with a self-reported diagnosis of type 1 diabetes (T1D), residing in the United States. Recruitment was carried out between August 20 and December 31, 2024, via the T1D Exchange Online Community, a large, U.S.-based patient registry and digital forum comprising more than 25,000 members26. Invitations to participate were distributed through a single community-wide email, along with one post each on the organization’s Twitter/X, Facebook, and Instagram accounts. Interested individuals accessed the study via a secure link, reviewed an informed consent statement outlining the study’s objectives and participant rights, and provided electronic consent prior to enrolment. Summary statistics for the study population are presented in Table 1. Ethical approval was obtained from the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Review Board (IRB# HIRB00015324).

Clinical scenario

After consent, participants were presented with a clinical scenario of their assigned condition. They were asked to imagine that they were at a routine primary care visit and due for their annual diabetic eye exam (screening for diabetic retinopathy [DR], a diabetes complication that can cause blindness if untreated). In the scenario, an AI-based screening system was offered as an alternative to the usual referral to an eye care professional (ECP). The scenario described a choice between an autonomous AI retinal exam vs. the standard ECP exam. Participants were informed that a “normal” AI result would mean no ECP visit needed, whereas an “abnormal” AI result would require follow-up with an ECP. The questionnaire and scenario were developed collaboratively with practicing clinicians and pretested to ensure realism, clarity, and relevance to patient clinical experience (see Study Instrument below for details).

Participants were randomly assigned (via the survey platform’s randomizer) to one of the four conditions of a 2×2 factorial design: Incentive (no copay vs. $50 copay) and Incentive Source (insurer vs. tech company covers copay). In the No Incentive condition, the AI screening required a $50 copay (similar to a typical specialist copay). In the Incentive condition, this copay was waived (AI screening without any copay). The copay waiver was either attributed to the patient’s health insurance or to the AI developer, depending on assignment. In all cases, the alternative to AI screening was the standard referral to an ECP with usual costs.

After reading their assigned scenario, participants indicated which option they would choose for their DR screening: the AI-based exam or an exam by an ECP. This binary choice (AI vs. ECP) was the primary outcome measure for initial uptake of AI.

Outcome measures

We measured several endpoints after the choice. For perceived comparative effectiveness, participants rated two 7-point scale items comparing the AI’s effectiveness at detecting DR to an ECP’s, which we coded and averaged into a single score (α = 0.90, with 1 = AI is much less effective, 7 = AI is much more effective than ECP). For preference for second opinions, participants indicated how likely they would be to seek a follow-up exam from the other provider if the initial screening result was abnormal or if it was normal (1 = not at all likely, 7 = very likely), tailored to whether they initially chose AI vs. ECP. Demographic information was also collected.

Statistical analysis

We first calculated summary statistics to describe participant demographics. To assess the effect of incentives on AI uptake, we used a binary logistic regression with three predictors: a no copay indicator (1 = no copay, 0 = $50 copay), an incentive source indicator (1 = insurer, 0 = AI developer), and their interaction. The outcome variable was choice of AI screening (1 = AI, 0 = ECP). The primary model is specified as: log(p/(1−p)) = β₀ + β₁·NoCopay + β₂·InsurerSponsor + β₃·(NoCopay × InsurerSponsor), where p is the probability of choosing AI screening. The OLS model for perceived comparative effectiveness is specified as: Effectiveness = β₀ + β₁·NoCopay + β₂·InsurerSponsor + β₃·(NoCopay × InsurerSponsor). For follow-up intentions, we used: FollowUp = β₀ + β₁·AIFirst. The ordinal logistic regression for sensitivity analysis is: log(P(Y ≤ j)/P(Y > j)) = αj − (β₁·NoCopay + β₂·InsurerSponsor + β₃·Interaction). We also ran a covariate-adjusted logistic regression model: log(p/(1−p)) = β₀ + β₁·NoCopay + β₂·InsurerSponsor + β₃·Interaction + β₄·Age + β₅·Gender + β₆·Education + β₇·Insurance, including age, gender, education, and insurance status as covariates to ensure that results are robust to potential confounders. Furthermore, we confirmed that randomization yielded comparable groups by examining baseline characteristics by arm (Supplementary Table S1).

To test whether perceived effectiveness mediated the effect of the incentive on AI uptake, we conducted a bootstrap mediation analysis (5000 resamples), with incentive condition as the predictor, perceived effectiveness as the mediator, and AI uptake as the dependent variable, adjusting for incentive source.

To evaluate follow-up intentions (note: because initial screening choice was self-selected, these analyses are associational), we ran OLS regressions for each result scenario (normal vs. abnormal). The predictor was initial screening choice (1 = AI first, 0 = ECP first), and the outcome was likelihood of seeking a follow-up second exam. All tests were two-tailed with significance set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed in SPSS version 28.

Study instrument

The clinical scenario presented to participants read as follows: “You are at a clinic for a checkup with your primary care doctor. Following the recommended diabetes screening guidelines, your doctor tells you that you are due for a diabetic eye exam. This exam screens for diabetic retinopathy, a diabetes complication that may cause blindness if not treated. Your doctor informs you that a newly developed artificial intelligence (AI) system can be used to screen your eyes for potential diabetic retinopathy. If you do not wish to be screened by the AI system, your primary care doctor will provide you with a referral so that you can see another doctor, an eye care specialist, for this examination. If you agree to be screened by the AI system, there can be two outcomes: When nothing is detected by the AI system, your exam results are considered normal. You will NOT need to see a specialist for further examination. When the system detects potential diabetic retinopathy, your primary care doctor will refer you to an eye care specialist for further diagnosis and confirmation.”

The screening choice item asked: “Which diabetic retinopathy screening option would you choose?” (1 = the AI system, 0 = an eye care specialist). Perceived comparative effectiveness was measured with two 7-point Likert scale items: “How do you think the AI system might compare to an actual eye care specialist at detecting diabetic retinopathy?” (1 = the AI system is much more effective/better, 7 = an eye care specialist is much more effective/better; responses reverse-coded so that higher values indicate higher perceived AI effectiveness). Follow-up intentions were measured with two 7-point Likert items (1 = not at all likely, 7 = very likely): “If [the AI system/eye care specialist] indicates that your eyes are abnormal, how likely is it that you will [see an eye care specialist/use the AI system] for a second eye examination to confirm the result?” and “If [the AI system/eye care specialist] indicates that your eyes are normal, how likely is it that you will [see an eye care specialist/use the AI system] for a second eye examination to confirm the result?”
