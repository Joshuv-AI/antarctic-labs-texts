Enhancement of airflow uniformity in multi-tray dryers through a CFD-integrated sequential linear programming framework
Authors: Dawit Andualem Asrate, Fekerte Berhanu Tadle, Yordanos Derseh Mihretie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrying is a widely employed preservation method in food processing, aimed at extending shelf life by reducing moisture content, thereby minimizing storage and transportation costs. Despite their widespread use, cabinet-type multi-tray dryers often exhibit non-uniform airflow distribution, leading to inconsistent airflow uniformity. This study presents an optimization framework to enhance airflow uniformity by strategically repositioning supply fans, integrating Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations with Sequential Linear Programming (SLP). An L25 orthogonal array guided the CFD experiment design, and a second-order polynomial regression model was developed to correlate the airflow uniformity index (Ui) with fan positions (P1, P2, P3). The optimization, constrained by fixed geometric and operational parameters including a 78.80 mm supply gap, 45° baffle angle, 2.74 m/s Inlet airflow speed and 315 mm fan diameter was conducted for a 32-tray dryer (dimensions: 1420 × 650 × 1600 mm). The regression model achieved R² = 0.9247. Incorporating trust-region techniques into the SLP framework significantly improved convergence efficiency: static and dynamic trust-region approaches reduced iterations to 15 and 8, respectively, compared to 661 without trust-region control. These methods adaptively managed step sizes, promoting faster and more stable convergence without compromising accuracy. The MATLAB-based optimization yielded optimal fan positions of P1 = 359.05 mm, P2 = 395 mm, and P3 = 395 mm, achieving a maximum uniformity of 76.5% with 0.2% prediction error. This CFD–SLP integrated approach offers a reliable, scalable, and cost-effective solution for enhancing airflow uniformity in industrial dryers and holds promise for broader applications such as thermal or fluid process optimization tasks across food, pharmaceutical, and chemical industries.Keywords: Multi-tray cabinet dryer, Airflow uniformity, Computational fluid dynamics, Sequential linear programming, Trust-region methods, Fan positioning optimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

There is an increasing trend in the global food market industries to improve the food products value chains through different techniques1. Drying is one of the most common food preservation techniques2. Drying can minimize the storage and transportation costs, and makes handling easier by reducing size and weight. It is the process of moisture removal due to the simultaneous heat and mass transfer under controlled conditions.

Efforts have been made recently to develop dried foods with high quality by using innovative technologies. The drying technologies can reduce the water activity to a level with no microbiological activity and biochemical reactions. Most commonly, the efficiency of a dryer is dependent on air flow speed and drying air temperature3,4. Based on a most recent comprehensive review5, Increasing the drying temperature and airflow speed significantly accelerates moisture evaporation and reduces drying time, via enhancing both heat and mass transfer.

Advancement on sun drying leads to solar drying. The artificial method of drying technologies such as cabinet, tunnel, conveyor, fluidized bed, drum and vacuum shelf. During drying, water from the surface evaporates by mass and heat transfer mechanisms resulting in a reduction of the water activity of the product5–9. In a cabinet type multi tray dryer, more products can be loaded as the trays are arranged at different levels10. The key to a quality operation of those dryers is uniformity of hot airflow distribution over trays. However, multi-tray dryers (large size industrial dryers), encounter poor airflow speed uniformity. This study focused on optimizing the positions of the supply fans to enhance airflow distribution, while other design elements such as airflow-guiding baffles and the supply zone gap beneath the wire mesh trays were kept fixed5,11.

This research specifically focused on optimizing the position of the supply fans under a fixed supply zone gap (78.80 mm) and baffle angles (45°, downward)12–14. As illustrated in Fig. 2, three supply fans were positioned centrally on each side along the height of the dryer. A fan with a diameter of ∅315 mm and six impellers, each set at a 10° impeller angle (AXC 315-6/12°-4-PV axial fan, https://www.systemair.com), was selected based on the calculated static pressure and volume flow rate requirement (15.56 pa and 0.252 m³/s). Sequential Linear Programming (SLP), also known as Successive Linear Programming, is an iterative method used to solve nonlinear constrained optimization problems by converting them into a sequence of linear programming problems19. This approach is based on successive first-order linearizations of the objective and constraint functions. SLP has been successfully applied to optimize complex nonlinear problems in various fields, including heat distribution in living spaces20, optimal selection of auxiliary fans21, hybrid renewable energy systems22, and pick-and-place task optimization23, resulting in significant airflow uniformity improvements.

Optimization of the fan positions then followed, employing SLP with static and dynamic trust-region strategies, further guided by insights from literatures on drying chamber design modifications7,11,15,24.

For each experiment design a fluid model was created by subtracting the solid structures of the dryer, such as the body, baffles, wire mesh trays, and trolley from a 2D model (Fig. 2b). The resulting geometry was saved in .igs format and imported into Design Modeler in ANSYS Fluent for each simulation run. Figure 3a illustrates the 3D fluid model of the dryer.

Model selection and boundary conditions setup

The inlet airflow speed was set to 2.74 m/s based on prior research, with a turbulence intensity of 5% and a turbulence length scale of 0.07 × the inlet diameter (315 mm). The outlet was specified as a pressure outlet at 0 MPa (gauge). All walls were assumed smooth, with negligible roughness effects. Baffles were represented as solid internal surfaces, with angles fixed according to previous studies. Since this study focuses on airflow rather than heat distribution, the energy equations were not considered. Air was treated as an incompressible working fluid with default thermophysical properties in ANSYS Fluent. Buoyancy effects were neglected because the flow was dominated by forced convection, and the maximum velocity corresponded to a Mach number far below 0.3.

To achieve accurate airflow predictions, selecting an appropriate turbulence model is essential. As this study does not focus on heat distribution, the energy equations were not considered. The standard k-ε model performs well for turbulent shear flows but is less accurate for flows with adverse pressure gradients, rotation, or streamline curvature. In the drying process, the presence of baffles (air diffusers), heating coils, and wire mesh trays generates strain flows and static pressure differences, where the k-ε model may not predict airflow circulation accurately. The k-ω model is more effective for boundary layers with adverse pressure gradients and separated flows, and it can also handle turbulent flows in porous media10,25. An improved version, the SST k-ω model, combines the advantages of k-ε and k-ω models across the domain. Therefore, the SST k-ω model was selected to accurately predict airflow over the walls and wire mesh trays.

Surface plotting and fitting

Based on the Ui results corresponding to each DOE point, surface plots of Ui versus P1, P2 and P3 were generated with origin 2018 software to gain insight into the input-output relationships. A 4D surface plot was constructed. Subsequently, polynomial nonlinear surface fitting was performed, and a second-order polynomial regression model with interaction terms for P1, P2 and P3 was developed using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) technique.

Sequential linear programming-based optimization and validation

This study employs a Sequential Linear Programming (SLP) in MATLAB R2024b to optimize the positions of supply fans in a multi-tray cabinet dryer, with the objective of maximizing the airflow uniformity index (Ui). The optimization framework was implemented in MATLAB, beginning with an initial guess for the design variables (P1, P2, P3). The design variables were iteratively updated based on a small step size (α = 0.01), which ensured stability and reduced the risk of overshooting the optimum solution. Convergence was determined when the norm of the design variable update between successive iterations fell below a tolerance threshold (tol = 1e− 6), with a maximum of 1000 iterations allowed to facilitate sufficient exploration of the design space.

In addition to this baseline SLP configuration, an enhanced SLP framework incorporating trust-region strategies was established to further regulate the step size and directionality of the search process. This method constrains the updates within a trust region defined by a radius vector Δ, ensuring that steps remain within a bounded local neighbourhood. The optimization incorporated an exponentially decaying step size mechanism, where the step size α was reduced over iterations according to a decay rate (α₀ = 1, decay rate = 0.95), with a lower bound (αmin = 0.01) to prevent stagnation. This adaptive strategy enables large, exploratory steps during the early iterations and progressively finer updates as convergence nears, facilitating both rapid progress and precise refinement. Two distinct trust-region schemes were implemented to guide the optimization process. In the Static Trust Region approach, the trust region radius decays progressively over iterations. A step is accepted if the improvement ratio (ρ) defined as the ratio of actual to predicted improvement is positive (ρ > 0). If the step is rejected (ρ ≤ 0), the trust region radius is reduced by 5%, with a lower bound set at 0.01 to maintain sufficient search flexibility. In contrast, the Dynamic Trust Region method adaptively adjusts the radius based on model prediction accuracy. When ρ > 0.75, indicating a high degree of agreement between predicted and actual improvements, the trust region is expanded to allow broader exploration. Conversely, if ρ < 0.25, suggesting poor model fidelity, the region is contracted to enforce more conservative steps. This adaptive mechanism allows the optimizer to respond to the local landscape of the objective function, promoting stability and ensuring tighter convergence near the optimum.

Importantly, the decaying step size and trust region mechanisms operate independently, with the former enforcing global control over the step magnitude, and the latter enforcing local directional constraints.

At each iteration, the nonlinear objective function (Ui) is locally approximated using a linear model. The gradient of the objective function is computed using central finite differences, and the resulting linear programming subproblem is solved using MATLAB’s linprog function. The solver minimizes the negative gradient while respecting the bounds on the design variables and the trust region constraints.

Finally, to evaluate the fidelity of the SLP model, predicted Ui values were compared against the actual values computed via CFD simulations at the optimal fan positions by using the percentage error metric defined in Eq. (1). Additionally, a parity plot comparing the predicted and CFD-obtained airflow speed uniformity index (Ui) confirms the reliability and practical applicability of the proposed SLP-based optimization framework for enhancing airflow uniformity in drying systems.

Optimization

Sequential linear programming was employed to determine the optimum positions of the supply fans, considering the airflow uniformity index (Ui) as the objective function and P1, P2 and P3 as design constraints.

As a result of using SLP with and without trust region strategies, the optimal positions of the supply fans in the multi-tray cabinet dryer were successfully identified. By considering a strict convergence tolerance (tol = 1e− 6), the nonlinear objective function (Ui) was efficiently maximized. Compared to the SLP method without trust region, which required approximately 650 iterations to converge (Fig. 7c), SLP with static trust region approach together with adaptive step size (decreasing in every iteration) decayed the region size at each iteration and prevents getting stuck in small updates, Fig. 7e. While, the dynamic approach has adaptively expanded or contracted the trust region based on the step acceptance criteria. As illustrated in Fig. 7g, This approach ensured convergence in fewer iterations and provided a straightforward design path. Both trust-region methods demonstrated substantially faster convergence compared to the standard SLP approach without trust-region control, which required approximately 661 iterations to converge (Fig. 7c), following a complex zigzagging path (Fig. 7d). In contrast, the trust-region strategies facilitated a more stable and directed optimization process with minimal iterations. A steady increase in the Ui value was observed, characterized by rapid improvements during early iterations and gradual stabilization as the algorithm approached the optimum. This behavior is attributed to the use of a small step size, which played a crucial role in regulating search direction, minimizing the risk of overshooting, and preventing oscillations around local optima. The carefully chosen parameters ensured an effective balance between convergence speed and solution quality, enabling the algorithm to closely approximate the global maximum. Among trust region-based methods, the lowest prediction error (0.201%) was achieved, indicating high accuracy of the optimization outcome within the defined design bounds.

Validation

To validate the model’s predictive capability beyond the optimized region, additional DOE points were evaluated by randomly selecting P1, P2, and P3 within the range of 207–395 mm. Figure 8 presents a parity plot comparing the predicted and CFD-obtained airflow speed uniformity index (Ui), at 95% confidence intervals. The scatter points closely follow the red dashed line representing the ideal fit, indicating strong agreement between the surrogate model and CFD results. This confirms good generalization performance, with R² = 0.9315 and RMSE = 0.0021.

Parity plot comparing predicted and CFD-obtained airflow speed uniformity index (Ui).

Although the original objective function (Ui) is nonlinear and derived from CFD-based approximations, the Sequential Linear Programming (SLP) method, which employs linear approximations using gradient information at each iteration successfully approximated the optimal solution. As shown in Fig. 8, the parity plot demonstrates close agreement between the predicted and actual Ui values, while Table 5 indicates a very small prediction error. The SLP optimization achieved near-optimal supply fan positions (P1, P2, and P3) without overshooting or instability. Notably, the SLP approach without trust-region strategies yielded a minimum error of 0.201%. and required 1.069 s of CPU time. Introducing a static trust region reduced the error to 0.356% with a CPU time of 0.0858 s, while the dynamic trust region approach achieved an error of 0.241% in only 0.0757 s. These results demonstrate that trust-region strategies, particularly dynamic adjustment, not only maintain accuracy but also substantially reduce computational cost. The consistently low errors across all approaches further confirm the reliability of the regression-based Ui function employed in the optimization process.

As shown in Fig. 9 (a), high-velocity regions remain concentrated near the outlet walls, while the central zones experience reduced speeds due to flow dissipation and recirculation. The upper and lower trays are more exposed to velocity variations, with localized zones of both high-speed jets and low-speed recirculation, which diminish overall uniformity. In contrast, moving farther from the center of the supply fans along the z-axis (Fig. 9 (b)) results in a more balanced distribution, with airflow across the 32 trays (16 on each side) becoming relatively uniform. This configuration produces a consistent velocity range of 0.82–1.9 m/s, providing favourable drying conditions and marking a significant improvement over recent studies4,26–29.

Nevertheless, residual non-uniformities persist. Low-speed recirculation near the lower trays can lead to under-drying, while high-speed jet impingement near the upper trays may cause localized over-drying. These effects highlight the practical limitations of the current optimization: even with careful fan placement, achieving full uniformity is constrained by the inherent flow physics, including boundary layer effects, momentum dissipation, and geometric asymmetries within the cabinet12,14,30.

To further mitigate these residual non-uniformities, future geometric modifications could be explored beyond fan positioning. Potential strategies including fine-tuning baffle angles and configurations to guide airflow more evenly, introducing flow straighteners or perforated panels to reduce jet impingement, and adjusting tray spacing or inlet/outlet shapes to minimize recirculation zones. Implementing such modifications has been shown to improve both airflow speed and temperature uniformity7,15,16,31, bringing multi-tray cabinet dryers closer to ideal drying conditions. Conclusion

This study presented an integrated Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and Sequential Linear Programming (SLP) approach to optimize airflow distribution in a cabinet-type multi-tray dryer. Using orthogonal experimental design and polynomial regression modelling, the influence of supply fan positions on airflow uniformity was systematically evaluated. The regression model exhibited high predictive accuracy with a coefficient of determination (R² = 0.9247) and RMSE of 0.0037, validating the robustness of the model. The optimization processes achieved a notable enhancement in the airflow uniformity index (Ui), reaching a maximum value of 76.6% at optimal fan positions. This study achieved a uniform airflow range of 0.82–1.9 m/s, confirming the effectiveness of CFD-based optimization in enhancing drying uniformity. These findings underscore the significance of fans positioning in enhancing the airflow uniformity of multi-tray cabinet dryers. When compared to recent studies, a significant improvement in airflow uniformity has achieved.

To improve the optimization’s convergence behavior, trust-region strategies were introduced. Compared to the SLP method without trust regions, which required approximately 661 iterations, the use of static and dynamic trust-region mechanisms significantly accelerated convergence, reducing the iteration count to 15 and 8, respectively without substantially altering the optimal result. Notably, the dynamic trust-region method demonstrated the fastest convergence rate with minimal zigzagging, offering more efficient navigation of the solution space.

The trust-region approach dynamically controls the step size, eliminating the need for manually enforced small steps and contributing to improved stability and speed. This integrated CFD-SLP-trust region framework provides a scalable and computationally efficient tool for optimizing airflow in drying systems. Its versatility also opens potential for application in other thermal or fluid process optimization tasks across food, pharmaceutical, and chemical industries.

Overall, these findings underscore the critical influence of supply fan positioning on airflow uniformity in multi-tray cabinet dryers. While the CFD-SLP approach provides valuable insights applicable to full-scale operations, the absence of experimental validation and thermal coupling remains a key limitation of this study. The optimum airflow uniformity represents a significant improvement in flow distribution. However, it does not necessarily ensure uniform drying, as temperature uniformity may become the next limiting factor. Therefore, future research should integrate thermal uniformity and perform comprehensive experimental validation under practical conditions to establish a complete and reliable optimization framework for multi-tray drying systems.
