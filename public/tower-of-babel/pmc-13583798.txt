Maintaining quality out-of-hours in an acute cardiology service in a UK primary PCI centre
Authors: Cullum Shaw, Karen Roberts, Karen McDonagh, Adam Frosdick, Melody Thomas, Faye Robson, Lissy Jose, Kerry Moore, David Williams, Simon Hetherington, Javed Ehtisham, Mohammed El-Din, Prashanth Raju, Mohsin Farooq, Ayman Helal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany district general hospitals (DGHs) in the UK do not have resident on-call cardiology registrars overnight; in these situations, it is becoming more common for cardiac outreach practitioners (COPs) to provide specialist input for patients presenting with suspected cardiac events. The aim of this study is to evaluate the exact diagnostic concordance between the initial working diagnoses made independently by COPs and subsequent final discharge diagnoses in patients assessed out of hours and to assess the accuracy of identifying acute coronary syndrome (ACS).MethodsA retrospective, single-centre service evaluation was conducted at a UK DGH between January and June 2025. Initial COP diagnoses were compared with final discharge diagnoses, with exact diagnostic concordance reported as percentage agreement. Diagnostic performance for identifying ACS was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, overall accuracy and Cohen’s kappa (κ).ResultsA total of 779 cases were screened, with 233 patients meeting inclusion criteria following staged exclusions. The overall exact diagnostic concordance was 92.7% (216/233). The 216 exact concordant cases comprised 125 concordant cardiac diagnoses and 91 concordant non-cardiac diagnoses. 17 cases had discordant diagnoses, but none were associated with delayed escalation, investigation or management. COP sensitivity for identifying ACS was 96.0%, specificity was 92.4%, positive predictive value was 85.9%, negative predictive value was 98.0% and overall classification accuracy was 93.6%. Cohen’s κ was 0.858 (95% CI 0.789 to 0.927), indicating almost-perfect agreement.ConclusionCOPs demonstrated a high level of exact diagnostic concordance when compared with discharge diagnosis made by consultant cardiologist. This new evidence suggests that COP-led assessments are reliable when working within a clinical pathway.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the UK, with acute events placing substantial pressure on NHS services. The British Heart Foundation estimates that 7.6 million people are living with cardiovascular disease in the UK, with acute coronary syndromes (ACSs), arrhythmias and heart failure accounting for a significant proportion of emergency department (ED) presentations. Timely recognition and management of acute cardiac presentations is vital, as delays in diagnosis are associated with increased mortality, avoidable complications and prolonged hospital admission, increasing strain on the NHS.1

Out-of-hours cardiology provision presents a challenge for many hospitals with overnight specialist staffing being reduced, and non-resident on-call services providing remote advice rather than clinical review. Consequently, initial patient assessment is commonly undertaken by acute medical or emergency medicine teams whose experience and clinical acumen in cardiology may vary considerably. Due to the ongoing increase in service demand and workforce pressures, many NHS organisations have expanded the role of advanced clinical practitioners (ACPs) within acute specialties to ensure service provision is met.2

The Cardiac Outreach Practitioner (COP) service evaluated in this study was designed to provide a 24/7 cardiology presence in the ED and facilitate early identification and assessment of high-risk ACS presentations. Over the last decade, this service has evolved to include assessment of a range of other emergency cardiology presentations. The service is made up of experienced nurses and paramedics with extensive cardiology experience supplemented with further clinical and academic training to master’s level advanced practice as set out in NHS England’s Multi-Professional framework for advanced practice. In hours, they work alongside cardiology registrars and out-of-hours they work alongside the ED team and on-call medical registrars who provide support.

The COPs scope of practice is set out in its operational policy; its primary role is to support the operational running of the primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) pathway in the trust’s role as a Heart Attack Centre via initial assessment of suspected or confirmed ST elevation myocardial infarctions (STEMI) referred to them by the ED, the ambulance services, other hospital trusts or urgent care centres. Also, COPs routinely review referrals from the ED and same-day emergency care (SDEC) where there is suspicion of a primary presentation of a cardiac pathology; in this setting its scope is the assessment and management of non-ST elevation ACS and arrhythmias. It does not routinely review patients with syncope, where there is no clear cardiac pathology or decompensated heart failure which are managed by the acute medical team, although it will offer informal advice if approached.

In their role, the COPs clinical responsibilities include patient history taking and physical assessment, ECG interpretation, requesting and interpreting investigations, initiating treatment and escalating care when required. Between 22:00 and 08:30, COPs are the first point of cardiology input for patients presenting with suspected cardiac conditions in the ED, SDEC and various inpatient wards. Consultant cardiologists and cardiology registrars remain available for life-threatening emergencies and escalation advice.

The expansion of ACP roles has occurred alongside growing evidence highlighting their role in providing safe and effective care to patients. However, there is relatively little literature on the performance of ACPs working in specialties such as cardiology, and even less on their performance in out-of-hours settings. Existing evidence on ACPs working in more generalist roles such as in emergency medicine and general practice suggests that experienced ACPs demonstrate capability comparable with senior resident physicians, particularly when working within robust clinical frameworks. Nevertheless, evidence to support ACP practice within cardiology remains limited.3

Early diagnosis in cardiology can be challenging, with cardiology ACPs frequently seeing patients who present with non-specific symptoms such as chest pain, dyspnoea, palpitations or syncope, many of which can reflect a wide range of pathology. This diagnostic uncertainty is often amplified overnight due to limited access to echocardiography, advanced imaging and senior specialist input. Thus, evaluating the reliability of COP-led initial investigations/management is important for patient safety and identifying potential for future workforce development.4

This service evaluation aimed to assess diagnostic concordance between COPs’ initial assessments and subsequent consultant-led discharge diagnoses in patients independently reviewed out-of-hours. Secondary aims included characterising diagnostic discrepancies and evaluating the nature of discordant cases.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective service evaluation was conducted within Kettering General Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, a UK district general hospital providing 24-hour acute cardiology services, including PPCI. The evaluation reviewed all out-of-hours cardiac outreach activity between January and June 2025. The project was undertaken as part of the Trust’s quality improvement and clinical governance programme and was formally classified as a service evaluation. The project was registered through the Trust audit and quality improvement department. All data were anonymised prior to analysis.

Cardiac outreach service structure

COPs provide a 24/7 cardiology presence across the urgent care division, that is, the ED and SDEC. Out-of-hours, they also provide advice and support to other specialties if an acute cardiac event occurs; and to paramedics in ambulance services and other hospitals seeking input from the PPCI service. During daytime hours, COPs work alongside cardiology registrars and consultants. While overnight (22:00–08:30), they provide semi-autonomous specialist assessment and management, primarily with the support of the medical registrar, but with senior specialist input and review available as required. COPs’ responsibilities include managing suspected ACS and confirmed cardiac arrhythmias, but there are a range of other conditions they provide input into. COPs are responsible for clinical assessment, ECG interpretation, requesting and interpreting investigations, initiating treatment and determining whether escalation is required.

Inclusion criteria

All cases documented within the cardiac outreach logbook during night shifts over the 6-month study period were included.

Exclusion criteria

A staged exclusion process was undertaken to isolate independently managed out-of-hours COP assessments. Excluded cases included: (1) patients reviewed during registrar working hours (19:00–22:00) or jointly assessed with cardiology registrars or consultants. (2) Inpatient consultations. (3) PPCI activations, including declined PPCI calls. (4) Cases considered outside the cardiac outreach service scope. (5) Cases without available electronic clinical documentation.

Data collection

Data were collected retrospectively from multiple electronic sources, including the COPs logbook, electronic document management system and electronic referral system. For each patient, diagnoses were identified at three stages of the clinical pathway: (1) initial COP diagnosis; (2) post-take consultant diagnosis and (3) final discharge diagnosis. Other data collected included gender, age, sex and presenting complaint. This collection was undertaken independently by the first author.

Diagnostic classification

Diagnoses were categorised as ACS or non-ACS for the binary classification analysis. This categorisation was applied independently to both the initial COP working diagnosis and the final discharge diagnosis. The ACS category included STEMI, non-STEMI and unstable angina. The non-ACS category included all other cardiac diagnoses, including stable angina, tachyarrhythmias, bradyarrhythmias, heart failure, cardiac syncope, myocarditis or pericarditis, as well as non-cardiac diagnoses such as musculoskeletal chest pain, gastrointestinal pathology, lower respiratory tract infection, pulmonary embolism, anxiety-related symptoms, non-cardiac syncope, hypertension without acute cardiac pathology and cases in which no abnormality was detected.

Diagnostic concordance and statistical analysis

Two complementary analyses were undertaken. First, exact diagnostic concordance was assessed by comparing the initial COP working diagnosis with the final discharge diagnosis. Exact concordance was defined as agreement between the specific diagnostic labels recorded at the two assessment points.

Second, the ACS/non-ACS categorisations were compared using a 2×2 contingency table, with the final discharge diagnosis used as the reference standard. Cases were classified as true positive, true negative, false positive or false negative. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value and overall classification accuracy were calculated. Agreement beyond chance was assessed using Cohen’s κ with a 95% CI. Cases without exact diagnostic concordance were reviewed individually. Discordances were classified as diagnostic refinement within the same ACS/non-ACS category or as reclassification across the ACS/non-ACS boundary. The clinical records were also reviewed to determine whether any discrepancy resulted in delayed escalation, inappropriate treatment or inappropriate discharge.

Primary endpoint

The primary outcome measure was exact diagnostic concordance between the COP working diagnosis and final discharge diagnosis.

Secondary endpoints

Secondary outcomes included: concordance between COP and post-take diagnoses, characterisation of discordant cases and classification of discrepancies as major or minor. Following assessment of diagnoses, discrepancies between COP and discharge diagnosis were classified as either a major or minor divergence. Major discrepancies were defined as differences with potential to alter acute clinical management. Minor discrepancies were defined as diagnostic refinement without impact on management.

Classification of discordant cases was undertaken retrospectively according to these predefined study definitions and was not performed under blinded conditions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Google Sheets (Google, USA). Exact diagnostic concordance was assessed using percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ). Kappa values were interpreted according to the Landis and Koch classification: <0.20: poor agreement, 0.21–0.40: fair agreement, 0.41–0.60: moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80: substantial agreement, 0.81–1.00: almost perfect agreement.5 95% CIs were calculated using SE methodology.

Results

Study population

A total of 779 cases seen by COPS were identified during the study’s 6-month period. Following a staged exclusion process, 233 patients met criteria for inclusion and analysis (tables 1 and 2). The median age of the cohort was 70.5 years (IQR 58–80 years; range 19–98 years). And 47.6% (n=111) patients were male. Presenting complaints included chest pain 62.2% (n=145), palpitations 19.3% (n=45), dyspnoea 8.6% (n=20), syncope 4.3% (n=10) and other miscellaneous symptoms 5.6% (n=13). Of the 233 cases, 57.9% (n=135) received a final diagnosis of cardiac and 42.1% (n=98) a non-cardiac diagnosis.

Diagnostic concordance and cardiac classification accuracy

Two complementary measures of diagnostic performance were evaluated. First, exact diagnostic concordance between the COP’s initial working diagnosis and the final consultant cardiologist discharge diagnosis was assessed. Concordance was defined by the authors as agreement between the initial COP diagnosis and the final consultant discharge diagnosis. Overall, exact diagnostic concordance was achieved in 216 of 233 patients (92.7%) (figure 1).

In addition to exact diagnostic concordance, a second analysis was undertaken to evaluate the COPs diagnostic performance in identifying ACS (figure 2). 15 of the 17 cases without exact diagnostic concordance also crossed the ACS/non-ACS classification boundary. 12 patients initially assessed as having ACS received a final non-ACS diagnosis, while three patients initially assessed as having non-ACS pathology received a final diagnosis of ACS. Review of the clinical records found no documented delayed escalation, inappropriate treatment or inappropriate discharge associated with these discrepancies. The remaining two cases represented refinement of the specific diagnosis without a change in ACS classification.

Overall, the findings demonstrate a high degree of agreement between independently performed COP assessments and consultant-led discharge diagnoses. Exact diagnostic concordance exceeded 90%, while binary ACS/non-ACS classification demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance. The small number of discordant cases predominantly reflected expected diagnostic refinement following definitive inpatient investigation rather than clinically significant diagnostic error, and none resulted in inappropriate escalation, treatment or discharge.

Discussion

This service evaluation demonstrates a high level of diagnostic concordance between independently performed COP assessments and final discharge diagnoses within an out-of-hours cardiology service.

This service evaluation demonstrated a high level of agreement between independently performed COP assessments and final discharge diagnoses within an out-of-hours cardiology service. Exact diagnostic concordance was achieved in 92.7% of cases. In the separate ACS versus non-ACS classification analysis, COP assessment demonstrated a high sensitivity and specificity, with Cohen’s κ of 0.858, indicating almost-perfect agreement. These findings suggest that specialist cardiac ACPs can provide a clinically reliable assessment service while practising semi-autonomously within a robust clinical governance framework.

Importantly, review of the discordant cases found no documented evidence of delayed escalation, inappropriate treatment, inappropriate discharge or other clinically significant harm. 15 of the 17 cases without exact diagnostic concordance also crossed the ACS/non-ACS classification boundary, but reassuringly these were predominantly re-classified from ACS to non-ACS.

These findings indicate that diagnostic discrepancies were uncommon and were not associated with documented safety concerns. Several discrepancies involved unstable angina, a diagnosis that can be challenging during the initial assessment because it relies heavily on clinical symptoms and may be clarified only after serial investigations and senior review. The findings therefore provide reassurance regarding the performance of the COP service within its established governance and escalation framework. This is not only reassuring for the service which undertook this self-assessment, but relevant to the wider cardiology setting in the context of increasing service demands and workforce pressures. With increasing demand for early specialist assessment and management in the form of increasing ED attendances and the evolving national requirements (most recently set out in NHS England Urgent and Emergency Care Plan 2025/2026), there is space in the status quo to consider further creation of or expansion of specialist ACP service to provide early specialist input and continuity of care in the out-of-hours setting.6

In our hospital, the importance of dedicated out-of-hours cardiology support was particularly evident during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the NHS experienced unprecedented strain, with disruption to cardiology services being common due to unexpected challenges and staffing pressures associated with illness and shielding. In this crisis, COPs played a critical role in ensuring continuity of care, rapid triage, initiating management and facilitating admission avoidance to reduce pressure on EDs where possible. It is well known and demonstrated in the literature that delays in diagnosis and treatment of ACS are associated with worse outcomes for patients. This knowledge in combination with the flexibility of COPs to provide service provision as witnessed during the pandemic highlights the importance and ability of COPs to provide accessible specialist input during times of unprecedented demand.7

The high levels of concordance identified in this study likely reflect several factors. First, the COPs are experienced practitioners with established cardiology backgrounds, with advanced levels of clinical training and ongoing multidisciplinary feedback and informal mentorship go a long way in further refining their practice. Second, the service operated within clearly defined governance and escalation structures, enabling remote consultant or registrar input for clinically complex cases. Finally, most presentations involved common acute cardiology syndromes for which structured diagnostic pathways and standardised investigations are well established. The diagnostic performance metrics identified in this evaluation are notable. The high sensitivity and negative predictive value suggest that COPs were particularly effective at identifying patients with ACS and at correctly classifying patients whose final diagnosis was non-ACS. This is clinically important because failure to recognise ACS may result in delayed investigation, treatment and escalation. This is especially important in the assessment of cardiac pathologies, where failure to recognise the correct pathology or follow the correct guidelines may result in significant morbidity or unfortunately, mortality.

Existing literature evaluating ACPs diagnostic performance within cardiology remains limited. However, studies from emergency medicine and specialist nursing environments have demonstrated that experienced ACPs may achieve diagnostic agreement comparable to physician-led assessment in selected clinical scenarios. Hoang et al demonstrated comparable ECG interpretation performance between experienced ACPs and emergency physicians in emergency department settings, although this should be taken in context of emergency medicine clinicians’ confidence in interpreting ECGs when compared with their cardiology counterparts, a cardiology ACP would be expected to have greater exposure to ECG interpretation and acute cardiac presentations than non-specialist practitioners.8 Similarly, Ezra et al reported that emergency nurse practitioners demonstrated greater diagnostic accuracy than junior medical staff when assessing ophthalmic emergencies.9 Although these studies are not directly equivalent to acute cardiology assessment, they support the broader principle that experienced ACPs can safely contribute to acute diagnostic decision-making.

The findings also highlight the complexity of distinguishing ACS from alternative cardiac and non-cardiac diagnoses during the initial presentation. Myocarditis, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, tachyarrhythmia-related myocardial injury and other causes of chest pain or troponin elevation may initially resemble ACS and may only become distinguishable following serial biomarkers, cardiac imaging, angiography or specialist review.10

Importantly, the out-of-hours environment introduces additional diagnostic challenges. Overnight, most hospitals have limited access to echocardiography, CT, MRI, angiography and consultant review outside of high-risk or life-threatening conditions. This may provide some reasoning as to why the major discrepancies occurred, with COPs erring on the side of caution and making a working diagnosis of unstable angina rather than non-cardiac chest pain, thus ensuring the patient receives a senior cardiology review in-hours rather than relying on non-cardiac specialists to review out-of-hours.

Despite these constraints, the COPs maintained a high level of diagnostic concordance with their senior colleagues, this suggests that when used within a structured role, the ACPs have the capability to mitigate some of the risks associated with reduced out-of-hours service provision. Our evidence supports the system benefits of cardiology ACP services in improving patient flow and reducing delays in assessment within the emergency department in a safe manner. Cardiology ACPs can also reduce the workload of cardiology physicians by providing care for routine cases, allowing cardiology physicians to prioritise higher-acuity interventions and reduce workload for the acute medical team by reducing dependence on them by taking ownership of cardiology presentations.

Our findings are also consistent with wider service development experiences within our centre demonstrating the safe expansion of ACP-led cardiology pathways. In a recent prospective quality improvement project undertaken in our trust, we evaluated a chair-based ambulatory implantable loop recorder explant service. ACPs successfully delivered a streamlined outpatient pathway with excellent safety and patient satisfaction outcomes. Across 201 patients, the pathway eliminated procedure cancellations related to catheter laboratory pressures, reduced bed occupancy and optimised cardiologist utilisation without any increase in complications; with no occurrence of major adverse events, infections or bleeding complications observed, supporting the ability of appropriately trained ACPs to safely deliver autonomous care within structured frameworks.11

The present findings also align with other quality improvement initiatives within our institution aimed at enhancing continuity and quality of cardiovascular care beyond the acute inpatient setting. In a previously reported hybrid post-myocardial infarction clinic, early face-to-face follow-up provided by cardiology ACPs demonstrated significant opportunities for medication up-titration, lipid management, diabetes optimisation and patient education following ACS.12

Together with this current evaluation, these experiences highlight the broader opportunities for the continued development of cardiology ACP services in improving patient pathways across both acute and outpatient care, while maintaining high clinical standards. They also reinforce the importance of flexible and innovative service provision within acute trusts in providing high-quality cardiology care in the context of increasing healthcare demand and workforce limitations.

This study identified several potential areas for future service development. One opportunity involves implementation of structured low-risk chest pain pathways led by COPs. Given the high negative predictive value observed, appropriately governed protocols incorporating validated risk stratification tools such as the HEART score and serial troponin pathways may facilitate safe discharge of selected low-risk patients, potentially reducing avoidable admissions. The evaluation additionally identified the potential value of expanding COPs ability to organise outpatient follow-up and provision of out-of-hours diagnostic resources. Allowing COPs to organise follow-up with outpatient investigations (echocardiography, ambulatory rhythm monitoring or functional testing) under consultant oversight would likely allow for early discharge from the ED and improve patient flow and experience, via reduced length of stay in the hospital. With the need for increased access to echocardiography or advanced imaging out-of-hours obvious in several of the discordant cases reflected on, increasing capacity for focused echocardiography training, portable imaging, or rapid-access diagnostic pathways may further improve diagnostic precision and increase the diagnostic accuracy of COPs.

Strengths and limitations

While the findings are encouraging, they should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-centre service evaluation conducted within a UK primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) centre, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to hospitals with different COP models. Second, data extraction was performed retrospectively from routinely collected clinical records by the first author, who is also a COP within the service, these assessments were not performed under blinded conditions, and observer bias cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, this evaluation focused specifically on diagnostic agreement and was not designed to assess patient-centred outcomes. The authors deliberately evaluated diagnostic concordance to assess the performance of COP.

Despite these limitations, this study has several important strengths. It represents one of the first evaluations of independently practising COP undertaking specialist out-of-hours assessment within a UK primary PCI centre. It included consecutive real-world patients managed within routine clinical practice and compared initial COP assessments with consultant cardiologist discharge diagnoses. Consequently, this evaluation provides important real-world evidence supporting the reliability of COP-led out-of-hours cardiology assessment within a structured governance framework.

Conclusion

This service evaluation demonstrates that COPs/cardiology ACPs working within a clear framework have a high level of diagnostic concordance when compared against the discharge diagnosis of their consultant cardiologist colleagues. These findings support the reliability of cardiology ACP led assessment within acute cardiology pathways and highlight the value of COPs in maintaining specialist cardiology provision out-of-hours. Diagnostic discrepancies were primarily related to diagnostic clarification following advanced investigations, or a tendency to over-diagnose unstable angina, a notoriously subjective diagnosis when assessed by symptoms alone, rather than clinically unsafe errors. As demand for acute cardiology services continues to increase, cardiology ACP models may provide an important strategy for improving service provision, supporting patient flow, and maintaining continuity of specialist care in a safe manner. Further prospective multicentre research is required to evaluate patient outcomes, treatment appropriateness, healthcare utilisation, and long-term safety associated with ACP-led cardiology services.
