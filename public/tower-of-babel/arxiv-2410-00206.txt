A Unified FPT Framework for Crossing Number Problems
Éric Colin de Verdière, Petr Hliněný

Abstract
The basic (and traditional) crossing number problem is to determine the minimum number of crossings in a topological drawing of an input graph in the plane. We develop a unified framework yielding fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) algorithms for many generalized crossing number problems. Our framework takes the following form. We fix a surface 𝒮 \mathscr{S} and a class 𝒟 {\mathscr{D}} of “allowed” topological drawings of graphs in 𝒮 \mathscr{S} (e.g., some class of drawings with at most t t crossings). We assume that testing membership in 𝒟 {\mathscr{D}} can be done algorithmically, and that restricting a drawing in 𝒟 {\mathscr{D}} , extending it without adding any crossing, or transforming it with a self-homeomorphism of 𝒮 \mathscr{S} yields a drawing that is also in 𝒟 {\mathscr{D}} . Then deciding whether an input graph G G has a drawing in 𝒟 {\mathscr{D}} , and computing one if it is the case, is fixed-parameter tractable in (essentially) the genus of 𝒮 \mathscr{S} and the maximum number of crossings in a drawing in 𝒟 {\mathscr{D}} . More generally, we may take as input an edge-colored graph and distinguish crossings by the colors of the involved edges; and we may allow a bounded number of edge removals and vertex splits on G G before drawing it. The proof is a reduction to the embeddability of a graph on a two-dimensional simplicial complex. This implies, in a unified way, linear or quadratic FPT algorithms for many topological crossing number variants established in the graph drawing community. Some of these variants already had previously published FPT algorithms, mostly relying on Courcelle’s metatheorem, but our algorithms have a better runtime. Moreover, our framework extends to these crossing number variants in any fixed surface, and also allows us to fix the rotation system of the drawing of a graph in some variants.

Introduction

The (traditional)
crossing number
problem, minimizing the number of pairwise edge crossings in topological drawings of an input graph in the plane,
is a long-standing and central task in graph drawing and visualization that comes in many established flavors; see the extensive dynamic survey by Schaefer
[
48
]
.
In this paper, we give a framework that smoothly captures many crossing numbers variants and provides, in a unified way, efficient algorithms when parameterized by the solution value (i.e., the respective crossing number).

Many flavors of crossing numbers.

There is currently a surge of crossing number variants. A motivation for such variations is that, in practical drawing applications, not every crossing or crossing pattern of edges may be “equal” to other ones.
One may, e.g., want to avoid mutual crossings of important edges. Or, to allow crossings only within specific parts of a graph, and not between unrelated parts. Or, to exclude crossings of edges of some type. Or, to allow only certain “comprehensible” crossing patterns in order to help visualize the graph.
This is the main focus of the recent research direction called “beyond planarity”
[
15
]
.

Paraphrasing Schaefer
[
48
, Chapter 2]
, a crossing number variant minimizes, over all
allowed drawings

D
of an input graph
G
in some specified
host surface
, an
objective function
related to the crossings in
D
. The
allowed drawings
may, e.g., restrict the crossings on one edge or in a local pattern of crossing edges, or prescribe or forbid certain local properties of the drawing (also depending on types or colors of edges). The
host surface
is usually the plane, but can be any fixed surface, orientable or not. The
objective function
is often the number of crossings between edges, but other possibilities include, e.g., the number of edges involved in crossings, or the number of pairs of edges that cross an odd number of times.

Existing algorithms for the traditional version.

One usually considers the decision version of the (traditional) crossing number problem: Given an input graph
G
of size
n
, and an integer
t
, does
G
have crossing number at most
t
?
This problem is already
\mathsf{NP}
-hard in the plane, as proved by Garey and Johnson in 1983
[
18
]
,
and even in very specific cases
[
27
,
29
,
8
]
. Moreover, the problem is
\mathsf{APX}
-hard
[
7
]
, and the best known polynomial-time approximation algorithm
[
10
]
gives an approximation factor that is subpolynomial in
n
only for bounded-degree graphs.

Thus, the main current focus is on algorithms that are fixed-parameter tractable (
\mathsf{FPT}
) in
t
— the runtime has the form
f(t)\cdot{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
, where
f
is a computable function of the
parameter

t
. For fixed
t
, Grohe
[
22
]
has described an
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
-time algorithm, and Kawarabayashi and Reed
[
35
]
have announced an
{\mathcal{O}}(n)
-time algorithm. Both rely on Courcelle’s metatheorem
[
14
]
, which automatically entails a huge dependency in the parameter
t
.

Two recent approaches avoid resorting to Courcelle’s theorem. First, Colin de Verdière, Magnard, and Mohar
[
13
]
have studied the problem of embedding a graph in a
two-dimensional simplicial complex
(2-complex for short), a topological space obtained from a surface by adding isolated edges and identifying vertices. Among other motivations for this problem, they observe
[
13
, Introduction]
that the crossing number problem reduces to the embeddability of the input graph in a certain 2-complex depending only on
t
. Then Colin de Verdière and Magnard
[
12
]
have shown that the embeddability of a graph of size
n
in a 2-complex of size
C
can be tested in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(C)}\cdot n^{2}
, or in time
f(C)\cdot n
for some function
f
. Hence, this results in
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(t)}\cdot n^{2}
-time and
f(t)\cdot n
-time algorithms for the crossing number problem, which, moreover, extend to any fixed surface.

Second, even more recently, Lokshtanov, Panolan, Saurabh, Sharma, Xue, and Zehavi
[
38
]
have given a
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(t\log t)}\cdot n\,
-time algorithm for the traditional crossing number problem in the plane, by a reduction to bounded treewidth and dynamic programming,
all in time linear in
n
.

Existing algorithms for other variants.

For other flavors of crossing numbers, which are (typically) also
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, the literature on FPT algorithms is scarce; besides Pelsmajer, Schaefer, and Štefankovič
[
44
]
giving
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms for the odd and pair crossing numbers, we are only aware of two very recent papers, which bring a general approach to multiple crossing number flavors and which we now detail. (The various flavors of crossing numbers are defined in
Section

5
.)

First, Münch and Rutter
[
40
]
, extending Grohe’s approach
[
22
]
, provide a framework for quadratic
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms for the crossing number of several types of beyond-planar drawings of graphs in the plane, characterized by forbidden combinatorial crossing patterns. This includes the crossing number of
k
-planar,
k
-quasi-planar, min-
k
-planar, fan-crossing, and fan-crossing free drawings of a graph for any constant
k
.

Second, Hamm, Klute, and Parada
[
24
]
, in a subsequent recent preprint, have announced a generalization of the previous framework
[
40
]
, also handling forbidden topological crossing patterns in drawing styles in the plane and, more importantly, bringing the possibility of handling predrawn parts of the input graph (as already known for the traditional crossing number
[
23
]
). The dependency in the size
n
of the input graph is an unspecified polynomial.

The above results all rely on Courcelle’s theorem. Apart from that, we are only aware of sporadic results for isolated variants. Kawarabayashi and Reed
[
35
]
and Jansen, Lokshtanov, and Saurabh
[
33
]
both claim, without details, a linear
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm to compute the skewness of a graph, and Nöllenburg, Sorge, Terziadis, Villedieu, Wu, and Wulms
[
42
]
give a non-uniform
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm for the splitting number in surfaces.
All mentioned papers except
Nöllenburg et al. are restricted to the plane
.

New contributions.

Our primary contribution lies in developing a general and easily describable framework that unifies a large part of the many existing variants of the crossing number problem.
Our framework takes the following form. We fix a surface
\mathscr{S}
and a class
{\mathscr{D}}
of (possibly edge-colored) topological drawings of graphs on
\mathscr{S}
, corresponding to the class of “allowed” drawings.
We assume that testing membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
can be done algorithmically and that restricting a drawing in
{\mathscr{D}}
, extending it without adding any crossing, or transforming it with a self-homeomorphism of
\mathscr{S}
yields a drawing that is also in
{\mathscr{D}}
. For example,
{\mathscr{D}}
could be the class of drawings of a given drawing style with at most
t
crossings, for some fixed integer
t
.

We prove that deciding whether an input (possibly edge-colored) graph
G
has a drawing in
{\mathscr{D}}
, and computing one if it is the case, reduces to (several) tests of embeddability of graphs on 2-complexes, discussed above
[
12
]
. As a consequence, it can be done in quadratic time in the size
n
of
G
, and exponential in a polynomial in the other parameters, namely the genus of
\mathscr{S}
, the number of colors, and the maximum number
r
of crossings in a drawing in
{\mathscr{D}}
; alternatively, the decision problem can be solved in time linear in
n
if the other parameters are fixed. All of this remains true if one allows a bounded number of (possibly color-restricted) edge removals and vertex splits to
G
before drawing it; these numbers are also parameters.

We deduce linear
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms for many established crossing number variants; we now survey some of them.
First, we can restrict ourselves to many established drawing styles, such as
k
-planar,
k
-quasi-planar, min-
k
-planar,
k
-gap, fan-crossing, weakly fan-planar, strongly fan-planar,
k
-cover planar,
k
-matching planar, and fan-crossing free drawings. In this setting,
k
is formally an additional fixed parameter, but it does not affect the runtime since we can always assume
k\leq t
.
For the cases of the
k
-gap,
k
-cover planar, and
k
-matching planar crossing numbers, no
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms were known.
Second, we may assign colors to the edges of the input graph and count the crossings differently depending on the colors of the edges involved, leading to the first
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms for the joint crossing number on surfaces and its generalizations.
In particular, color-based restrictions on the crossings can be used to fix the rotation system of a graph (the cyclic ordering of the edges around each vertex), a property that is not yet addressed by any of the existing
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms.
Third, assuming suitable additional properties, we can handle problems that do not count the crossings, but rather the number of edges, or pairs of edges, involved in crossings, such as the edge, pair, and odd crossing numbers (no
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm was known for the edge crossing number).
And fourth, since we allow for prior edge removals and vertex splits, our framework encompasses, e.g., the skewness, for which
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms were only sketched
[
35
,
33
]
in the plane, and the splitting number, which was only known to admit a nonuniform
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm
[
42
]
.

Last but not least, all these four aspects that define flavors of crossing numbers can be combined arbitrarily, and our main results are valid not only in the plane, but for arbitrary surfaces.
As a side note, we allow non-orientable surfaces in our framework, homeomorphic to a sphere with a number of “crosscaps”. Placing
k
crosscaps in the plane amounts to choosing
k
specific points in the plane that can be traversed by an arbitrary number of pairwise crossing edges of the planar drawing “for free”, without accounting for the induced crossings at these points, which seems relevant from a graph drawing perspective.
Also, we allow surfaces with boundary, which are needed for some subtle variations of the fan-planar crossing number.

For most flavors of crossing number, the arguments are direct, though they really differ according to the flavor; they essentially boil down to checking that the class
{\mathscr{D}}
of drawings satisfies the desired conditions, and to giving an algorithm to test membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
.

Comparison with the state of the art.

Our main contribution is a convenient, versatile, and unified framework capturing a very general class of crossing number variants while giving
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms. In particular, as listed above, we obtain new
\mathsf{FPT}
results for several established variants and their generalizations (to surfaces, or by combining the features of multiple variants).
It is conceivable that some of the variants newly covered by our framework could be proved
\mathsf{FPT}
using other techniques, perhaps Courcelle’s powerful metatheorem, as already used in
[
22
,
23
,
40
,
24
]
. However, such developments are extremely delicate even in isolated variants and lead to hard-to-describe algorithms. Indeed, first, checking a given drawing and counting its crossings must be formalized in the MSO
2
logic of graphs, which usually needs heavy tricks tailored to the specific case; second, there remains the specific and often highly nontrivial treewidth reduction step to be done. So far, such approaches have been successful only in the case of the plane.

Moreover, all approaches using Courcelle’s theorem inherently come with a high computational cost. Indeed, they result in algorithms with a huge multi-level exponential dependency in the parameter
r
; for instance, it is an exponential tower of height at least four in the case of
[
22
]
and three in the case of
[
35
]
, see the discussion in
[
38
]
. Also, while Courcelle’s theorem itself runs in linear time in the size
n
of the input graph, the necessary treewidth reduction step requires quadratic time, if not more, in the known approaches for the non-traditional crossing numbers. In contrast, we obtain quadratic
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms with a singly exponential dependency in the parameters, and alternative linear
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms with unspecified dependency in the parameters.
Furthermore, any improved algorithm for the embeddability problem
[
12
]
(or to the irrelevant vertex algorithm by Golovach, Kolliopoulos, Stamoulis, and Thilikos
[
20
]
, used in the linear-time algorithm) would automatically speed up our algorithms.

The frameworks used in the two very recent works mentioned above
[
40
,
24
]
, which both use treewidth reduction and Courcelle’s theorem, can handle some of the drawing styles that we encompass, but different counting functions, vertex splits, and nonplanar surfaces are out of their reach.
However, it should not be difficult to extend their framework to handle edge-colored graphs, thanks to the MSO
2
logic being able to handle arbitrary sets of edges. On the other hand, the recent breakthrough of Lokshtanov et al.
[
38
]
for the traditional crossing number problem may fuel hope for more
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(t\log t)}\cdot n
-time algorithms, but tweaking all ingredients of that highly technical paper seems hard, even for isolated crossing number variants, and moreover this approach is inapplicable to surfaces other than the plane, due to the use of several inherently planar techniques (3-connectivity arguments and dependency on
[
33
]
).

Organization of the paper.

Starting with the preliminaries (
Section

2
), we describe our general framework and state our main result (
Section

3
), which is then proved (
Section

4
). After that, we instantiate this framework to many crossing number variants, and list the resulting FPT algorithms (
Section

5

Section

).

Preliminaries

Graphs and surfaces.

In this paper, graphs are finite and undirected, but not necessarily simple unless specifically noted. The
size
of a graph
G
is the number of vertices plus the number of edges of
G
. A
vertex split
of a graph
G
at vertex
v
creates a new vertex
v^{\prime}
and replaces some of the edges incident to
v
by making them incident to
v^{\prime}
instead of
v
.

We follow
[
2
,
39
]
for surface topology. A
surface

\mathscr{S}
is a topological space obtained from finitely many disjoint solid, two-dimensional triangles by identifying some of their edges in pairs. Surfaces are not necessarily connected. The
boundary
of
\mathscr{S}
is the closure of the union of the unidentified edges. The
(Euler) genus
of a connected surface is twice its number of “handles”, if it is orientable, and is its number of “crosscaps”, otherwise.
Up to homeomorphism, each
connected
surface
\mathscr{S}
is specified by whether it is orientable or not, by its (Euler) genus, and by its number of boundary components. In this paper, by ‘genus’ we always mean the Euler genus.
The
topological size
of
\mathscr{S}
equals
s:=d+g+b
, where
d
is the number of connected components of
\mathscr{S}
,
g
is the sum of the (Euler) genera of its connected components, and
b
is the number of boundary components. Algorithmically, a surface (up to a homeomorphism) can be specified by the genus, number of boundary components, and orientability of each connected component. The plane is not a surface according to our definition, but in this paper it can always be substituted with a disk, which is (homeomorphic to) a surface. A
self-homeomorphism
of
\mathscr{S}
is just a homeomorphism from
\mathscr{S}
to
\mathscr{S}
.

(Topological) drawings of graphs.

In this paper, any drawing of any graph in any topological space maps distinct vertices to distinct points, and has finitely many intersection points, each avoiding the images of the vertices; however, an intersection point may involve two or more pieces of edges. In more formal terms, and introducing more terminology, the previous sentence means the following.
A
curve

c
in a topological space
\cal X
is a continuous map from the closed interval
[0,1]
into
\cal X
. The
relative interior
of
c
is the image, under
c
, of the open interval
(0,1)
. In a
drawing

D
of a graph
G
in a topological space
\cal X
, vertices are represented by points of
\cal X
and edges by curves in
\cal X
such that the
endpoints

c(0)
and
c(1)
of a curve
c
are the images of the end vertices of the corresponding edge. Moreover, we assume that distinct vertices are mapped to distinct points and that the relative interiors of the edges avoid the images of the vertices. Given a point
x\in\cal X
, its
multiplicity
in
D
is the number of pairs
(c,t)
such that
c(t)=x
, where
c
is a curve representing an edge of
G
in
D
, and
t\in(0,1)
. An
intersection point
(shortly an
intersection
) of
D
is a point with multiplicity at least two. We only consider drawings
D
with finitely many intersection points.

When considering a drawing
D
of a graph
G
on a
surface

\mathscr{S}
, we
always
implicitly assume that
G
has no isolated vertex and that the image of
D
avoids the boundary of
\mathscr{S}
; these conditions are actually benign in a graph drawing context. In
D
, each edge
e
is subdivided into (finitely many)
pieces
by the intersection points along
e
. A
crossing
is an intersection of multiplicity two that cannot be removed by a local perturbation of the curves. The other intersections of multiplicity two are
tangencies
. The drawing
D
is
normal

[
47
]
if every intersection point is a crossing (in particular, it has multiplicity two). Moreover,
D
is
simple
if it is normal, no edge self-crosses, no two adjacent edges cross, and no two edges cross more than once.

Note that many authors do not consider a drawing
D

simple
if
D
contains two parallel edges (even uncrossed), as two parallel edges necessarily intersect at their two endpoints. Likewise, uncrossed loops are usually not allowed in simple drawings. We do not make these additional restrictions here, for reasons that will be apparent in the proof of
Proposition

.

The (traditional)
crossing number
of a graph
G
in a surface
\mathscr{S}
is the least number of crossings over all normal drawings of
G
in
\mathscr{S}
. Many variations exist
[
48
]
, see
Section

5
.

Storing drawings on surfaces.

We need to represent drawings of graphs on a surface
\mathscr{S}
combinatorially, up to self-homeomorphisms of
\mathscr{S}
. Cellular embeddings can be represented by combinatorial maps
[
16
]
, but we need to allow intersection points, and moreover faces need not be disks. We can achieve this, ultimately relying on combinatorial maps.
Here are the details.

We represent drawings on surfaces as subsets of the vertex-edge graph of some triangulation of
\mathscr{S}
. (An alternative possibility would be through
extended combinatorial maps

[
11
, Section 2.2]
, but this appears to be unnecessarily complicated for our purposes.)

Specifically, a
triangulation
of a surface
\mathscr{S}
is a graph
T
embedded on
\mathscr{S}
such that each face of
T
(each connected component of the complement of the image of
T
) is homeomorphic to a disk bounded by three edges, and all three incident vertices and edges are pairwise distinct. In particular, a triangulation is a 2-complex. Let
T^{\prime}
be the graph made of the vertices and edges of
T
that lie in the interior of
\mathscr{S}
. Let
W=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})
be a set of
k
walks in
T^{\prime}
, such that each edge of
T^{\prime}
is used at most once by the union of these walks. This set
W
naturally gives rise to a drawing
D
of a graph
G
on
\mathscr{S}
: The graph
G
has
k
edges, each drawn as
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
on
\mathscr{S}
; the vertices of
G
are the at most
2k
endpoints of the walks in
W
. We say that the pair
(T,W)
is a
representation
of
D
.

A representation of a colored drawing is a representation
(T,W)
of the corresponding uncolored drawing, together with the data of a color for each walk in
W
. Its
size
is the size of a data structure to represent it, which is
{\mathcal{O}}(tc)
, where
t
is the number of triangles and
c
is the number of colors (assuming we store the colors in unary). Conversely (as proved in Lemma
6
below), every drawing
D
on
\mathscr{S}
can be represented by such a pair
(T,W)
in which
T
is made of
{\mathcal{O}}(s+u)
triangles, where
s
is the topological size of
\mathscr{S}
, and the intersection points of
D
subdivide the edges of
D
into
u
pieces in total.

2-complexes.

Figure 2.1:
(a): An example of a two-dimensional simplicial complex, or 2-complex for short. (b): An embedding of some graph with four vertices and three edges on the same 2-complex.

In this paper, a
2
-complex

\mathscr{C}
(or two-dimensional simplicial complex) is a topological space obtained from a simple graph (without loops or multiple edges) by attaching solid, two-dimensional triangles to some of its cycles of length three; see
Figure

2.1
.
2
2

2

Our definition of 2-complex slightly departs from the standard one; it is the same as a geometric simplicial complex of dimension at most two, realized in some ambient space of dimension large enough.
The
simplices
of
\mathscr{C}
are its vertices, edges, and triangles.
We can easily represent 2-complexes algorithmically, by storing the vertices, edges, and triangles, and the incidences between them. In general, many such representations correspond to the same topological space, and the choice of the representation only impacts the complexity analysis of our algorithms. The class of 2-complexes is quite general; it contains all graphs, all surfaces, all
k
-books (although in this paper, we only need 2-complexes in which every edge is incident to at most two triangles), and any space obtained from a surface by identifying finitely many finite subsets of points and by adding finitely many edges between any two points.

An
isolated edge
of a 2-complex
\mathscr{C}
is an edge incident to no triangle. The
surface part
of
\mathscr{C}
is the union of all its triangles, together with their incident vertices and edges. A
singular point
of
\mathscr{C}
is a point that has no open neighborhood homeomorphic to an open disk, a closed half-disk, or an open segment.

Our above definition of drawings in topological spaces applies to drawings in 2-complexes. In particular, vertices of
G
may lie anywhere on
\mathscr{C}
, and edges of
G
as curves may traverse several vertices, edges, and triangles of
\mathscr{C}
. An
embedding
of
G
into
\mathscr{C}
is a drawing without any intersection point.

Embeddability of graphs on 2-complexes.

The
embeddability problem
takes as input a graph
G
and a 2-complex
\mathscr{C}
, and the task is to decide whether
G
has an embedding in
\mathscr{C}
. This problem is fixed-parameter tractable in the size of the input 2-complex:

Theorem 1

(Colin de Verdière and Magnard
[
12
, Theorems 1.1 and 11.1]
)
.

One can solve the embeddability problem (of graphs in 2-complexes) in
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(C)}\cdot n^{2}
time or in
f(C)\cdot n
time, where
C
is the number of simplices of the input
2
-complex,
n
is the size of the input graph, and
f
is some function.

The framework: description and main result

Let
c
be a positive integer and
\mathscr{S}
a surface. We consider
colored
graphs, in which each
edge
is labeled with an integer in
\{1,\ldots,c\}
. A
colored drawing
is a drawing
D
of a colored graph
G
in
\mathscr{S}
in which each edge of
D
inherits the color of the corresponding edge of
G
.

Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be a class of colored drawings on
\mathscr{S}
. (Thus
{\mathscr{D}}
implicitly depends on
c
and
\mathscr{S}
.) We say that
{\mathscr{D}}
is
stable
if, for any
D\in{\mathscr{D}}
, any drawing obtained from
D
by any of the following operations is also in
{\mathscr{D}}
:

[(a)]

1.

the removal of a vertex or an edge;

2.

the addition of a vertex whose image is disjoint from the image of
D
(and from the boundary of
\mathscr{S}
);

3.

the addition of an edge, of an arbitrary color, connecting two vertices in
D
, and otherwise disjoint from the image of
D
(and from the boundary of
\mathscr{S}
);

4.

a self-homeomorphism of
\mathscr{S}
.

For example, for any integer

t
crossings is stable.

For a stable class
{\mathscr{D}}
of colored drawings, we introduce the following problem:

{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem

Input:
a positive integer
c
; a colored graph
G
with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}

Question:
Is there a colored drawing
D\in{\mathscr{D}}
of the graph
G
?

Before proceeding further, we remark that the traditional crossing number problem is an instance of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem
:

Observation 2
.

For every integer
t
, the problem whether a graph
G
has crossing number at most
t
on a surface
\mathscr{S}
is a
{\mathscr{D}}_{t}
-Crossing
instance for
c=1
(a single color) and the stable class
{\mathscr{D}}_{t}
of all normal drawings with at most
t
crossings on
\mathscr{S}
.

Our framework is actually much richer than formulated above; for any stable class
{\mathscr{D}}
of drawings, we introduce the following generalization of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem
:

{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Vertex Splits and Edge Removals

Input:
a positive integer
c
; non-negative integers
p
,
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{c}
; a colored graph
G
with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}

Question:
Is there a colored drawing
D^{\prime}\in{\mathscr{D}}
of a colored graph
G^{\prime}
obtained from
G
by performing at most
p
successive vertex splits and by removing, for
i=1,\ldots,c
, at most
q_{i}
edges of color
i
?

To successfully tackle these problems in the parameterized setting, we need a suitable parameter bounding the “richness of crossings” in a stable class
{\mathscr{D}}
. The
intersecting size
of a drawing is the sum of the multiplicities of its intersection points. The
intersecting size
of
{\mathscr{D}}
is the maximum intersecting size of a drawing in
{\mathscr{D}}
. For example, if as above
{\mathscr{D}}_{t}
is the class of normal drawings with at most
t
crossings, then the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}_{t}
is at most
2t
, because by definition each intersection point in a normal drawing is a crossing, which has multiplicity two.

Our main result is that, assuming an algorithm to decide membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
and an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
, the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Vertex Splits and Edge Removals
is fixed-parameter tractable (and, as a special case, also the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem
):

Theorem 3
.

Let
c
be a positive integer. Let
\mathscr{S}
be a surface of topological size
s
. Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be a stable class of drawings with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}
on
\mathscr{S}
, specified by an integer
r
that is an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
, and by an algorithm that, in time
{\mathcal{O}}(\delta(j))
, decides membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
of a representation of a colored drawing of size at most
j
.

Then, the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Vertex Splits and Edge Removals
can be solved in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(c+s+r+p+q)}\cdot\delta({\mathcal{O}}((s+r)c))\cdot n^{2}
, or in
f(c+s+r+p+q)\cdot n
time for some function
f
, where
n
is the size of
G
and

.
In particular, the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem
can be solved in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(c+s+r)}\cdot\delta({\mathcal{O}}((s+r)c))\cdot n^{2}
, or in time
f(c+s+r)\cdot n
.

A few notes on
Theorem

3
are in order:

1.

More precisely, the proof of
Theorem

3
shows that an instance of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Vertex Splits and Edge Removals
can be solved by:

•

(c+s+r)^{{\mathcal{O}}(s+r)}
calls to an oracle that decides membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
, for drawing representations of size
{\mathcal{O}}((s+r)c)
, and

•

(c+s+r)^{{\mathcal{O}}(s+r)}\cdot(s+r+p+q)^{{\mathcal{O}}(p+q)}
tests of embeddability of a graph of size

into a 2-complex of size
{\mathcal{O}}(s+(c+r+p+q)(r+q))
.

2.

The upper bound

on the intersecting size of

shows up in the runtime, but is also used by the algorithm and must be provided on the input. If
{\mathscr{D}}
contains only normal drawings, then we can take

to be twice the maximum number of crossings in a drawing in

, as explained above.

3.

In all our applications, the function

will be at most exponential, and so the corresponding factor
\delta({\mathcal{O}}((s+r)c))
(which is independent of the input graph size

) in the runtime can be ignored.

4.

Our algorithms are uniform, in the sense that there is a single quadratic (respectively, linear) algorithm valid for all choices of

,
\mathscr{S}
,
{\mathscr{D}}
,

,

\mathscr{S}
, and

.

5.

For positive instances of the

4.1
Cutting a surface along a drawing

Let

be a drawing of a graph

on
\mathscr{S}
. We first need to define what we mean by

\mathscr{S}
along (the relative interior of the edges of)

(see Figure
4.1
(a–b)). Intuitively, we cut
\mathscr{S}
along the relative interior of edges of

, resulting in a topological space that is actually homeomorphic to a 2-complex. Note that each point of
\mathscr{S}
that is an endpoint of some edge of

still corresponds to a single point on the 2-complex obtained by cutting along

Section

that

is represented by a pair

where

is a triangulation of

and

is a set of walks in the triangulation and in the interior of the surface, such that each edge of

is used at most once. The triangulation

is obtained from a collection of initially disjoint triangles by gluing some vertices and edges together. Cutting

along

is best described by starting with the same collection of disjoint triangles and specifying a
subset
of these gluing operations:

•

whenever two directed edges

and

of triangles are identified to a single edge in
T
, and that edge is not used by any walk in
W
, we identify
e
and
e^{\prime}
. Note that this process automatically identifies the source endpoints of

and
e^{\prime}
, and similarly for their target endpoints;

•

whenever two vertices of the resulting triangulation correspond to the same vertex of
T
, and that vertex is an endpoint of at least one walk in
W
, we identify these two vertices.

Since we only perform a subset of the identifications of vertices and edges in

, the resulting space is naturally a 2-complex.

4.2
Overview of the reduction

The proof of

crossing number be the minimum number of crossings over all
k
-cover-planar drawings, and similarly let the
k
-matching-planar
crossing number be the minimum number of crossings over all
k
-matching-planar drawings.

Also, a normal drawing is
fan-crossing free
if no two edges crossing the same edge share a vertex, and the
fan-crossing free crossing number
is the minimum number of crossings over all fan-crossing free drawings.

\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms, all using Courcelle’s theorem, exist for the fan-crossing and fan-crossing free crossing numbers, by Münch and Rutter
[
40
]
, and for the weakly fan-planar crossing number, by Hamm, Klute, and Parada
[
24
]
, and it seems that with further adjustments the latter authors could also handle the strongly fan-planar version.
We solve all these variants, again with a better runtime. For the strongly fan-planar version, we use the fact that our framework allows for surfaces with boundary, and thus can easily capture the “infinite face” of the drawing; indeed, strong fan-planarity is invariant under self-homeomorphisms of the plane, but not of the sphere (e.g., the drawings in
Figure

5.2
(a) and (b) are homeomorphic when seen on the sphere).

Proposition 10
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
t\geq 0
be integers and let
G
be a graph of size
n
. Deciding whether
G
has strongly fan-planar, weakly fan-planar, fan-crossing,
k
-matching-planar,
k
-cover-planar, or fan-crossing free crossing number at most
t
in the plane can be done in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(t)}\cdot n^{2}
, or in time
f(t)\cdot n
for some function
f
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to that of
Proposition

9
, specialized to the plane.
We need to be able to recognize strongly fan-planar, weakly fan-planar, fan-crossing, or fan-crossing free drawings in polynomial time, which is not difficult from their definitions.
To recognize
k
-matching-planar drawings, we employ a standard maximum matching algorithm for every edge; as before, it suffices to do this in polynomial time. The case of recognizing
k
-cover-planar drawings is more interesting since no fully polynomial recognition algorithm is known; the problem is equivalent to solving
k
-vertex-cover, which can easily be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(2^{k}t)
for each of the at most
2t
crossed edges since no more than
t
edges cross a single edge. However, this runtime does not impair the overall runtime of
Theorem

3
.
∎

Extensions of
Proposition

10
to arbitrary surfaces are obviously possible.

Drawings that may not be normal.

To illustrate the applicability of our framework to non-normal drawings, we introduce the following example. For an integer
k\geq 2
, we define the
k
-intersecting crossing number
of a graph
G
to be the minimum value
t
such that there is a drawing of
G
with at most
t
intersection points in which the multiplicity of every intersection point is at most
k
. For every graph
G
, the traditional crossing number of
G
is at most
k\choose 2
times its
k
-intersecting crossing number, and this bound can be tight. We prove:

Proposition 11
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be a surface with topological size
s
, let
k\geq 2
and
t\geq 0
be integers, and let
G
be a graph of size
n
. Deciding whether
G
has
k
-intersecting crossing number at most
t
in
\mathscr{S}
can be done in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(s+k+t)}\cdot n^{2}
, or in time
f(s+k+t)\cdot n
for some function
f
.

Proof.

The proof is again very similar to the proof of
Proposition

9
. Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be the class of (possibly non-normal) drawings on
\mathscr{S}
with at most
t
intersection points, all of multiplicity at most
k
;
{\mathscr{D}}
is clearly stable, and testing membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
can be done in polynomial time. Moreover,
r:=kt
is an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
. Hence, our conclusion follows again by applying
Theorem

3
.
∎

Note that our definition allows for tangential intersections of edges. However, the same argument would work in the variant where we forbid tangential intersections.

We refer to
Section

5.3
for more examples of different methods for counting crossings.

5.2
Crossing problems with colored edges: Joint crossing number and generalizations

We now turn our attention to problems in which the colors of intersecting edges play a role. As a natural example, we choose the
joint crossing number
problem introduced a while ago by Negami
[
41
]
. In this problem, the goal is to embed two input graphs simultaneously on the same surface while minimizing the number of crossings between them. It is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for any fixed Euler genus
g\geq 6

[
31
,
28
]
, and the complexity parameterized by the solution value has not been studied prior to our work. We actually solve the following new problem generalizing the joint crossing number:

Color-Constrained Crossing Problem

Input:
A surface
\mathscr{S}
; a symmetric matrix
M
of size
c\times c
with nonnegative integer values; a colored graph
G
with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}

Question:
Is there a normal colored drawing
D
of
G
in
\mathscr{S}
such that, for each pair
i,j\!\in\!\{1,\ldots,c\}
, the number of crossings involving two edges, one colored
i
and the other
j
, is at most
M_{i,j}
?

Proposition 12
.

The
Color-Constrained Crossing Problem
can be solved in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(s+t)}\cdot n^{2}
, where
s
is the topological size of
\mathscr{S}
,
t=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq c}M_{i,j}
, and
n
is the size of
G
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
c\leq t+1
, since merging the colors corresponding to empty rows of
M
will not change the problem. Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be the class of normal drawings on
\mathscr{S}
with at most
M_{i,j}
crossings involving an edge of color
i
and an edge of color
j
. Clearly
{\mathscr{D}}
is stable, and one can decide whether a drawing is in
{\mathscr{D}}
in time polynomial in the representation of the drawing. Moreover,
r:=2t
is an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
.
Theorem

3
yields the result.
∎

Corollary 13
.

One can decide whether the joint crossing number of graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, each embeddable in a surface
\mathscr{S}
, is at most
t
in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(s+t)}\cdot n_{0}^{2}
or in time
f(s+t)\cdot n_{0}
for some function
f
, where
s
is the topological size of
\mathscr{S}
and
n_{0}
is the size of
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
.

Proof.

We apply
Proposition

12
as follows. For
i=1,2
, we use color
i
for
G_{i}
, define
G
to be the disjoint union of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, let
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&t\\
t&0\end{pmatrix}
.
∎

Many variants of the above problem exist in which one also prescribes the rotation system of the input graph. We defer the discussion on how we handle such problems to
Section

6
.

5.3
Non-traditional methods of counting crossings

We now look at different problems that do not count the crossings simply “one by one”.

Edge crossing number.

Perhaps the most natural problem that fits into this category is the edge crossing number. The
edge crossing number
of a graph
G
is the smallest integer
t
such that
G
has a normal drawing having
t
edges with at least one crossing.
Computing the edge crossing number is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
4
]
, and according to Schaefer
[
48
, Section 3.2: Edge crossing number]
, no
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm parameterized by the solution size
t
is known even in the plane.

Proposition 14
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be a surface with topological size
s
, let
t\geq 0
be an integer, and let
G
be a graph of size
n
. Deciding whether
G
has edge crossing number at most
t
in
\mathscr{S}
can be done in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(s+t)}\cdot n^{2}
, or in time
f(s+t)\cdot n
for some function
f
.

Proof.

It is well known, see, e.g., Schaefer
[
47
]
, that any drawing
D
of a graph
G
with at most
t
edges carrying an intersection point can be turned into a normal drawing
D
in which no two edges cross twice, without introducing new crossed edges. After removing the possible self-crossings of edges, we obtain a drawing of
G
with at most
t
edges carrying an intersection point and, moreover, with at most
t\choose 2
intersection points.

Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be the class of normal drawings on
\mathscr{S}
such that at most
t
edges carry an intersection point, and with at most
t\choose 2
intersection points. By the previous paragraph,
G
has edge crossing number at most
t
if and only if
{\mathscr{D}}
contains a drawing of
G
. Moreover,
{\mathscr{D}}
is stable, and membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
can be tested in polynomial time. Also,
r:=2{t\choose 2}
is an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
.
Theorem

3
concludes.
∎

A natural edge-colored generalization can be solved by a straightforward combination of the ideas in the proof of
Proposition

12
and of the previous proof.

Odd and pair crossing numbers.

The
pair (resp. odd) crossing number
of a graph
G
is the minimum
t
such that there exists a normal drawing of
G
in which at most
t
pairs of edges mutually cross (resp. cross an odd number of times).
While the question whether the pair crossing number coincides with the traditional crossing number is one of the biggest open problems in crossing numbers, Pelsmajer, Schaefer, and Štefankovič
[
45
]
proved that the odd crossing number can be lower than the traditional crossing number.

\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms for the pair and odd crossing numbers were given again by Pelsmajer, Schaefer, and Štefankovič
[
44
]
, with an unspecified dependency on
t
; however, because these algorithms are based on an adaptation of Grohe’s algorithm
[
22
]
, they are quadratic in the size
n
of the input and the dependency on
t
is at least an exponential tower of height four. We get faster algorithms:

Proposition 15
.

Let
\Pi\in\{
‘pair’, ‘odd’
\}
, let
t\geq 0
be an integer, and let
G
be a graph of size
n
. Deciding whether
G
has
\Pi
-crossing number at most
t
in the
plane
can be done in time
2^{2^{{\mathcal{O}}(t)}}\!\!\cdot n^{2}
, or in time
f(t)\cdot n
for some function
f
.

Proof.

The approach is analogous to the proof of
Proposition

14
, with the additional ingredient given by the bounds by Pelsmajer et al.
[
44
]
: in the case of the plane, if
G
is of pair (respectively, odd) crossing number
t
, then there exists a normal drawing
D
of
G
achieving this optimum such that the number of crossings in
D
is at most
t2^{t}
(respectively,
9^{t}
).

Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be the class of normal drawings in the plane (or sphere) with
\Pi
-crossing number at most
t
and with at most
\max(t2^{t},9^{t})
intersection points. By the previous paragraph,
G
has
\Pi
-crossing number at most
t
if and only if
{\mathscr{D}}
contains a drawing of
G
. Moreover,
{\mathscr{D}}
is stable, and membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
can be tested in polynomial time. Also,
r:=2\max(t2^{t},9^{t})
is an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
.
Theorem

3
concludes.
∎

We remark that the same approach would extend to surfaces, assuming some bounds analogous to those given by Pelsmajer et al.
[
44
]
for the plane.

5.4
Allowing edge removals and vertex splits before drawing

Finally, we consider crossing number variants that allow graph simplifications before drawing.

Skewness.

The
skewness
of a graph
G
is the smallest number of edges whose removal from
G
leaves a planar graph. Deciding whether the skewness of
G
is at most
q
is NP-complete
[
37
]
, and linear-time FPT algorithms are claimed, without details, by Kawarabayashi and Reed
[
35
]
and Jansen, Lokshtanov, and Saurabh
[
33
]
. We generalize the problem as follows.

Color-Constrained Skewness with Crossings

Input:
A surface
\mathscr{S}
; non-negative integers
t
and
q_{1},\ldots,q_{c}
; a colored graph
G
with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}
.

Question:
Can we remove, for each
i=1,\ldots,c
, at most
q_{i}
edges of color
i
from
G
, such that the resulting graph has crossing number at most
t
in
\mathscr{S}
?

Proposition 16
.

The
Color-Constrained Skewness with Crossings
problem can be solved in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(s+t+q)}\cdot n^{2}
or in time
f(s+t+q)\cdot n
for some function
f
, where
s
is the topological size of
\mathscr{S}
,
q=\sum_{i}q_{i}
, and
n
is the size of
G
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
c\leq q+1
, since the colors
i
with
q_{i}=0
can be merged into one. Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be the class of normal drawings on
\mathscr{S}
with at most
t
crossings. Then
{\mathscr{D}}
is a stable class, membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
can be tested in polynomial time, and
r:=2t
is an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
. There remains to apply
Theorem

3
with
p=0
and with the specified values of
q_{1},\ldots,q_{c}
.
∎

Splitting number.

The smallest integer
p
such that a graph obtained from the given graph

by
p
successive vertex splits is embeddable in

is called the
splitting number
of

in
\mathscr{S}

[
32
,
25
]
. This problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
17
]
, already in the plane.
Nöllenburg, Sorge, Terziadis, Villedieu, Wu, and Wulms
[

proved that the property of having splitting number at most

is minor-monotone in any fixed surface, and so the

-splitting number has a nonuniform

algorithm parameterized by

using the theory of graph minors. We generalize and improve the latter result to a uniform
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm:

Proposition 17
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be a surface with topological size

, let
t,p\geq 0
be integers, and let

be a graph of size
n
. Deciding whether

has, after at most

can be done in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(s+t+p)}\cdot n^{2}
, or in time
f(s+t+p)\cdot n
for some function
f
.

Proof.

We use the same definitions of
{\mathscr{D}}
and

as in the proof of
Proposition

16
, and apply
Theorem

3
with
q_{1}=\ldots=q_{c}=0
and the specified value of

.
∎

6
Fixing the rotation system: framework and applications

A natural restriction, when minimizing the number of crossings of an input graph, is to prescribe the clockwise cyclic order of edges around each vertex in the drawing—the
rotation system
. It turns out that
Theorem

3
also allows us to capture such constraints, at the cost of a mild additional condition on the class
{\mathscr{D}}
. In order to make our framework more directly applicable, we first show a quite general consequence of
Theorem

3
, which can then be instantiated to obtain FPT algorithms for several well-studied problems with fixed rotation system. In this section, we only consider orientable surfaces.

6.1
Statement of the general result

In our general result, we consider an input graph
G
together with a prescribed rotation system, and we consider drawings of
G
in which the input rotation system may be flipped independently for each connected component of
G
. We first need some terminology.

Given a graph
G
, a
rotation system
is a family of cyclic permutations
\pi=\big(\pi_{v}:v\in V(G)\big)
, one for each vertex of
G
, such that the domain of the permutation
\pi_{v}
is the set of edges incident to
v
in
G
. (If a loop is incident to
v
, it should appear twice in that cyclic permutation—or more formally, we should consider a cyclic permutation of the
half-edges
of
G
incident to
v
.) Let
\pi_{v}^{-1}
be the inverse of
\pi_{v}
; also, let
\pi^{-1}:=\big(\pi^{-1}_{v}:v\in V(G)\big)
. Now, let
D
be a drawing of
G
in an oriented surface
\mathscr{S}
. The
rotation system
of
D
is obtained by recording the clockwise ordering of the edges around each vertex in
D
. Let
G_{0}
be a connected component of
G
. We say that the rotation system of
G_{0}
in
D
is
induced
by
\pi
if, for each vertex
v
of
G_{0}
, the cyclic ordering of the edges around
v
in
D
equals
\pi_{v}
.

In this section, we study the following variant of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem
:

{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Flippable Rotation System

Input:
a positive integer
c
; a colored graph
G
with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}
; a rotation system of
G

Question:
Is there a colored drawing
D\in{\mathscr{D}}
of the colored graph
G
such that, for each connected component
G_{0}
of
G
, the rotation system of
G_{0}
in
D
is induced either by
\pi
or by
\pi^{-1}
?

In order to obtain an FPT algorithm for this problem, we need to strengthen the stability condition for
{\mathscr{D}}
. Let
D
be a drawing of a graph
G
. Let
G^{\prime}
be a matching with the same edge set as
G
. We define a drawing
D^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
as follows. In
D
, each edge
e
of
G
is drawn as a curve
c
, and in
D^{\prime}
the corresponding edge of
G^{\prime}
is drawn as a subcurve
c^{\prime}
of
c
obtained by removing from
c
small initial and final parts of
c
; in particular,
c^{\prime}
contains all intersection points of
c
. The drawing
D^{\prime}
is unique up to homeomorphism, and we call it the
detachment
of
D
. A class
{\mathscr{D}}
of drawings is
detachably stable
if (1)
{\mathscr{D}}
is stable and (2) a drawing
D
lies in
{\mathscr{D}}

if and only if
its detachment lies in
{\mathscr{D}}
.

We obtain the following result:

Theorem 18
.

Let
c
be a positive integer and
\mathscr{S}
be an orientable surface of topological size
s
. Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be a detachably stable class of drawings with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}
on
\mathscr{S}
, specified by an integer
r
that is an upper bound on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
, and by an algorithm that, in time
{\mathcal{O}}(\delta(j))
, decides membership in
{\mathscr{D}}
of a representation of a colored drawing of size at most
j
.

Then, the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Flippable Rotation System
can be solved in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(c+s+r)}\cdot\delta({\mathcal{O}}(cr^{2}s^{2}))\cdot n^{2}
, or in
f(c+s+r)\cdot n
time for some function
f
, where
n
is the size of
G
.

The proof of
Theorem

18
is a reduction from the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem
, and in particular the remarks listed after
Theorem

3
are also valid for
Theorem

18
.

6.2
Proof of
Theorem

18

We now turn to the proof of
Theorem

18
. Given the input
(G,\pi)
to the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem
, we thicken
G
using grids in order to make it rigid according to the input rotation system
\pi
.

We start with some preliminaries. Let
m,n\geq 2
be two integers. The
grid with
m
rows and
n
columns
is the Cartesian product of two paths on
m
and
n
vertices. That is, its vertex set is
\{(i,j):1\leq i\leq m,\,1\leq j\leq n\}
, and vertices
(i,j)
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if
|i-i^{\prime}|+|j-j^{\prime}|=1
. A
cell
is a
directed
cycle of the grid of the form
(i,j),(i+1,j),(i+1,j+1),(i,j+1)
. The
k
-th
vertical path
is induced by the vertices
(i,k)
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, while the
k
-th
horizontal path
is induced by the vertices
(k,j)
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. The
top path
of the grid is the first horizontal path. A
horizontal (respectively, vertical) strip
is induced by the vertices of two consecutive horizontal (respectively, vertical) paths. A cell of a grid embedded in
\mathscr{S}
is
flat
if it bounds a face of the grid that is homeomorphic to a disk in
\mathscr{S}
. A strip is
flat
if all its cells are. The following lemma is a simple consequence of a lemma by Geelen, Richter, and Salazar
[
19
]
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
G
be the (
m\times n
)-grid, where
m,n\geq 3
, embedded in a (possibly non-orientable) surface
\mathscr{S}
with topological size
s
. Then, the number of cells of
G
that are not flat is at most
9s
.

Proof.

Geelen, Richter, and Salazar
[
19
, Lemma 1]
proved that, under the hypotheses of the lemma but assuming, in addition, that
\mathscr{S}
has no boundary, the number of noncontractible cells in
G
is at most
9g
, where
g
is the Euler genus of
\mathscr{S}
. (Actually, they only consider square grids, but it is immediate from their proof that the condition
m,n\geq 3
is sufficient for the result to hold.)

Since
G
is connected, we may without loss of generality assume
\mathscr{S}
connected. Let
g
and
b
be the genus and number of boundary components of
\mathscr{S}
, respectively, so that
s=1+g+b
. Let
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
be obtained from
\mathscr{S}
by attaching a crosscap (a Möbius strip) to each boundary component of
\mathscr{S}
; thus
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
has no boundary, and its Euler genus is
s-1
. By the above lemma by Geelen, Richter, and Salazar, at most
9(s-1)
cells of
G
are noncontractible in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
. Each remaining cell
C
is contractible in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
, hence separating in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
, hence bounds a face of
G
in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
, because
G-C
is connected.

So, each cell of
G
, with at most
9(s-1)
exceptions, is contractible and bounds a face in
\mathscr{S}
. If every such cell bounds a face that is homeomorphic to a disk, then we are done. Otherwise, there is a cell
C
that splits
\mathscr{S}
into a disk and another surface, and all the remaining part of
G
lies inside the disk. Since
G
is 3-connected, it has a unique combinatorial embedding in the sphere, so all cells of
G
except
C
bound a face homeomorphic to a disk in
\mathscr{S}
. The result follows since
s\geq 1
.
∎

Let
(G,\pi)
be an instance of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Flippable Rotation System
. We define a class
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
and a graph
G^{\prime\prime}
such that
(G,\pi)
is a positive instance of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Flippable Rotation System
if and only if

is a positive instance of the
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
-Crossing

Problem
.

Figure 6.1:

and

for the proof of

22

and

Proof of
Theorem

18
.

The construction of
G^{\prime\prime}
clearly takes
{\mathcal{O}}(k^{2}rn)
time. Using the fact that
{\mathscr{D}}
is stable, it is routine to check that
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
is stable. Moreover, given a representation of a colored drawing of size at most
j
, one can decide whether it belongs to
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
in time
{\mathcal{O}}(\delta(j)+j)
.

We now bound the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
. A drawing

in
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
has two types of intersection points: (1) the intersection points involving only edges with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}
, whose multiplicities sum up to at most
r
, the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
; (2) the crossing points between a green edge and a blue edge, and there are at most
(k+1)^{2}r^{2}
such crossings, whose multiplicities sup up to at most
2(k+1)^{2}r^{2}
. So the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
is at most
r^{\prime\prime}:=r+2(k+1)^{2}r^{2}={\mathcal{O}}(k^{2}r^{2})
.

Finally, we can apply
Theorem

3
to the class
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
and the graph
G^{\prime\prime}
, noting that

. Using the quadratic-time algorithm, the resulting runtime is
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(c+s+r)}\cdot\big(\delta({\mathcal{O}}(cs^{2}r^{2}))+{\mathcal{O}}(cs^{2}r^{2})\big)\cdot n^{2}
, which is bounded from above by
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(c+s+r)}\cdot\delta({\mathcal{O}}(cr^{2}s^{2}))\cdot n^{2}
. Alternatively, the linear-time algorithm runs in
f(c+s+r)\cdot n
time for some function
f
. This allows us to decide whether
G^{\prime\prime}
has a colored drawing in
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}
. By Lemmas
20
and
21
, this is equivalent to the fact that
(G,\pi)
is a positive instance of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Flippable Rotation System
.
∎

We remark that the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Flippable Rotation System
admits a formulation for non-orientable surfaces. In this realm, an instance of this problem is given not only by a colored graph

and cyclic permutations

for each vertex

, but also by a

, which prescribes whether traversing an edge “reverses the orientation”; see Mohar and Thomassen

for non-orientable surfaces; we omit the details.

6.3
Applications of
Theorem

18

We now consider applications of
Theorem

18
to well-studied crossing number problems with fixed rotation system. The traditional crossing number with a fixed rotation system in the plane is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
46
]
, and its parameterized complexity has not been considered so far.

A crossing number variant that uses a similar restriction is the
homeomorphic joint crossing number
problem, which is similar to the joint crossing number problem, but with constraints on the rotation systems. More precisely, in that problem, two input graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are cellularly embedded in an orientable surface
\mathscr{S}
without boundary (every face is homeomorphic to a disk) and the solution must be composed of embeddings of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
that are each homeomorphic to the input one (the rotation systems must be the same, possibly up to reversal). Given an integer
t\geq 0
, the goal is to determine whether there is a way to achieve this with at most
t
crossings between
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. The problem is also
\mathsf{NP}
-hard if the Euler genus of
\mathscr{S}
is at least 12
[
31
]
. The
orientation-preserving joint crossing number
is similar, except that we restrict ourselves to orientable self-homeomorphisms (the rotation systems of both graphs cannot be flipped).

To capture these problems, we introduce a variant of the
Color-Constrained Crossing Problem
where the input also prescribes the rotation system, but as before the input rotation system may be flipped independently for each connected component of
G
. Formally:

Input:
A surface
\mathscr{S}
; a symmetric matrix
M
of size
c\times c
with nonnegative integer values; a colored graph
G
with colors in
\{1,\ldots,c\}
; a rotation system of

Question:
Is there a normal colored drawing

of
G
in
\mathscr{S}
such that:

•

for each pair

, the number of crossings in
D
involving two edges, one colored
i
and the other
j
, is at most
M_{i,j}
, and

•

for each connected component
G_{0}\subseteq G
, the rotation system of
G_{0}
in
D
is induced either by
\pi
or by
\pi^{-1}
?

We have:

Proposition 22
.

The
Color-Constrained Crossing Problem

with Flippable Rotation System
can be solved in time
2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(s+t)}\!\cdot n^{2}
or in time

for some function

, where

is the topological size of
\mathscr{S}
,

, and

is the size of

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of
Proposition

12
, with
Theorem

18
applied instead of

3
. (Note that the class

defined in the proof is also detachably stable.)
∎

We apply the algorithm of

for

Otherwise, we obtain a drawing of

with at most

7
Computing drawings for positive instances

In this section, we prove the following result.

Theorem 26
.

For every positive instance of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Vertex Splits and Edge Removals
problem, we can compute a representation of the corresponding drawing, of size the size
n
of the input graph times a polynomial in

in time

(i.e., without overhead compared to the runtime of the quadratic-time algorithm in

3

We remark that all FPT algorithms for crossing number variants in this paper are reductions to the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing Problem with Vertex Splits and Edge Removals
problem. So, for any positive instance of such a variant,
Theorem

26
allows us to compute a corresponding drawing.

We need some preliminaries. A graph embedding on a surface is
cellular
if all its faces are homeomorphic to open disks. Cellular graph embeddings can be manipulated through their

The idea of the proof of Theorem

Some comments on this theorem are in order. First, the requirements on the 2-complex

8
Conclusions

More applications.

Theorem

3
allows for almost endless combinations for crossing number variants, in terms of drawing styles, ways to count crossings, allowed vertex splits and edge removals, all possibly taking edge colors into account, all on an arbitrary surface.

Theorem

18
additionally makes it possible to restrict the rotation system in a rich subset of the variants.
This shows the versatility of our approach. Altogether, our general framework encompasses most existing crossing number variations, and even more general ones, implying, in a unified way, fixed-parameter tractable algorithms for these crossing number problems in any surface.

Limitations.

Nonetheless, our framework requires an upper bound
r
on the intersecting size of
{\mathscr{D}}
, and
{\mathscr{D}}
must be preserved by the removal of any vertex or edge. The first requirement immediately excludes, e.g., the local crossing number (the minimum
t
such that a given graph has a
t
-planar drawing, which is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete to compute already for
t=1

[
8
,
21
]
), and the second requirement excludes some recently introduced drawing styles such as, for instance,
1^{+}
and
2^{+}
-real face drawings
[
5
]
. It is conceivable that Courcelle-based approaches would be still able to handle
1^{+}
and
2^{+}
-real face drawings, albeit not easily.

Another weakness, in particular compared to the recent preprint by Hamm, Klute, and Parada
[
24
]
, is our quite restricted ability to handle predrawn parts of the input graph, essentially limited to fixing
uncrossable
parts of the graph via rigid subembeddings, and to special restrictions like the fixed rotation system in
Theorem

18
. In contrast,
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
are very general in this respect and, in particular, allow crossings of fixed parts with the unfixed rest of the graph.

Remarks and possible extensions.

Beyond their use for strongly fan-planar drawings, surfaces with boundaries can be useful for other problems, because they allow to pinpoint specific regions of the surface. One could actually consider a version with colored boundaries, reinforcing the definition of a stable class by requiring that it is preserved by color-preserving self-homeomorphisms. Our arguments carry through; this only affects the running time by a
b!
factor, where
b
is the number of boundary components (see the proof of Lemma
7
).

Finally, a possible extension of the
{\mathscr{D}}
-Crossing
problem would be to replace the surface
\mathscr{S}
with an arbitrary 2-complex. We leave open whether such an extension is possible, and also potential applications.
