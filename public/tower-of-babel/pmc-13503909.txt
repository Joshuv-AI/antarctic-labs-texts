Vision based feedback control of weatherstrip coextrusion with predictive dimension modeling
Authors: Donguk Lee, Thong Phi Nguyen, Hye-Jin Lee, Hyo-Kyung Kim, Sang-Min Lee, Gwi-Young Park, Jongseok Lee, Chanhee Won
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving geometric accuracy in weatherstrip co-extrusion is inherently challenging due to the complex nonlinear interactions among process variables and the significant time delay caused by the long vulcanization line. However, conventional approaches primarily focus on managing process parameters or fail to integrate dimensional inspection into closed-loop feedback systems. Therefore, an integrated control framework is required to regulate product dimensions while simultaneously accounting for complex process variables. This study proposes a data-driven control strategy comprising two models to manage both process initialization and steady-state production. For the startup phase, a Random Forest-based prediction model was developed to determine optimal initial screw speeds, effectively minimizing trial-and-error adjustments. For steady-state operation, a smart vision-based feedback framework utilizing an artificial neural network was implemented to proactively compensate for dimensional deviations despite transport delays. Field validation in a mass-production environment confirmed that the system successfully maintained dimensional errors within a strict tolerance range of − 0.1 to + 0.2 mm. These results demonstrate the practical feasibility of this integrated approach for autonomous quality management in industrial weatherstrip co-extrusion.

Introduction

With the growing emphasis on sustainable mobility and energy efficiency, the global automotive industry is rapidly transitioning toward electric vehicles (EVs) and hybrid vehicles. The absence of engine noise in EVs has accentuated noise, vibration, and harshness (NVH) issues, which were previously masked by internal combustion engines1. To address these challenges, the role of weatherstrips, which seal the gaps between the vehicle body and doors to block noise, vibrations, rain and dust, has gained significant attention. Beyond their conventional sealing functions, enhanced acoustic insulation and impact absorption have become essential requirements. Consequently, product specifications have become increasingly demanding, involving complex geometries and composite materials2–4.

Rubber weatherstrips are manufactured by co-extrusion, as different regions of the component require distinct material properties. The co-extrusion process involves melting and conveying multiple rubber compounds through separate extruders, which are subsequently merged and shaped by a die. The extrudate then undergoes vulcanization in a line extending over 100 m, followed by cooling to stabilize the final geometry5. However, the inherent complexity of this process, where numerous factors interact, makes achieving geometric accuracy through parameter control challenging. The state of each compound affects the others; the processing conditions cannot be set for each compound independently6. Furthermore, phenomena such as die swell and deformation during the vulcanization further complicate the prediction of final geometry dimension. A key challenge is that the final geometry is decisively influenced by the screw speed, a parameter set at the start of the extrusion7. Consequently, the effect of any adjustment can only be verified through dimensional inspection after a significant time delay of 10 to 15 min, once the rubber compound passes through the entire vulcanization line8.

Various methods have been studied to control the extrusion process and ensure the quality of the final product. Yang and Lee9 demonstrated that product geometry and dimensions could be managed by adjusting process variables such as die temperature and puller speed. However, control strategies based on linear assumptions are often insufficient for the highly nonlinear co-extrusion process. Previdi et al.10 and Nguyen et al.11 developed feedback control systems to stabilize key properties, including volumetric flow and melt flow index, by adjusting variables such as temperature, pressure, and screw speed. While these approaches are effective in controlling die swell, these methods are unable to compensate for the dimensional variations that arise during the subsequent vulcanization stage.

The complex, highly coupled, and time-varying nature of co-extrusion processes, combined with variability in rubber material properties, makes accurate process modeling challenging, leading to the increasing use of Machine Learning (ML) techniques for predicting optimal process conditions. Akram et al.12 and Manohar et al.13 demonstrated that ML models, such as gradient boosting regressors, can predict key process outcomes with high accuracy. However, these studies had significant practical limitations, either lacking feedback control12or relying on small, simulated datasets13. This often hinders their robustness and applicability in real-world mass-production environments.

Among various ML algorithms, artificial neural networks (ANNs) have emerged as a particularly effective solution due to their robust capability to capture complex, nonlinear dynamics. Adesanya et al.14 developed an ANN model to predict initial process conditions based on raw material properties, thereby reducing both time and costs. However, their model was restricted to providing initial setpoints and did not achieve real-time control by incorporating dynamically changing variables. Kugler et al.15 utilized an ANN-based soft sensor to predict melt viscosity in real-time, enabling quality monitoring by accounting for batch-to-batch variations. Nevertheless, its application was limited to predicting a process indicator and was not extended to a closed-loop feedback system for quality control through process adjustments. Similarly, Lukas et al.16 implemented an ANN-based real-time temperature control system, achieving control with higher precision and faster response compared to conventional proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control. However, their study focused exclusively on predicting melt temperature and did not demonstrate direct control over the final product’s geometric quality.

In practice, maintaining dimensional tolerance is essential, however, conventional quality prediction based on variables such as melt temperature and pressure has inherent limitations. Process drifts during the vulcanization stage often cause deviations from the actual product quality, making direct dimensional inspection indispensable. García et al.17 investigated regression models to predict diameters in polymer extrusion using process variables like temperature and screw speed. However, their study was constrained by a lack of measurement reliability due to manual inspections by operators and an insufficient volume of training data. Jiang et al.18 employed Generalized Predictive Control (GPC) to reduce diameter deviations by up to 70%. However, the structural complexity of the controller design and its high computational demand make it difficult to apply to complex co-extrusion processes. Aschemann et al.19 implemented an AI-based process control system utilizing real-time data from multiple sensors. Although theses sensors also acquired quality-related data for monitoring, this geometric information was not directly integrated into the ANN-based model used for feedback control.

This study mainly proposes a comprehensive approach to address the inherent challenges of controlling the weatherstrip co-extrusion process, which is complicated by the interaction effects among process variables and the time delay between control inputs and the final geometry due to the long vulcanization stage. To achieve this, two AI-based models were developed: a Random Forest-based prediction model to optimize initial screw speeds during process startup, and an ANN-driven predictive model for closed-loop feedback control during steady-state operation. The overall framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. These models are trained on a comprehensive dataset that includes extruder temperatures and pressures, vulcanization zone temperatures, screw and puller speed, and vision-based measurements of cross-sectional dimensions. Finally, the predictive accuracy of the models was evaluated using statistical performance metrics, and the practical feasibility of the proposed system was validated through implementation on an industrial mass-production line.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the proposed AI-based control framework for the weatherstrip co-extrusion process. Experimental condition

The target weatherstrip

Weatherstrips are manufactured via a co-extrusion process utilizing ethylene propylene diene monomer (EPDM) rubber. The raw materials are several distinct EPDM compounds, each prepared with a tailored formulation of additives (e.g., curing agents, fillers) to achieve specific mechanical properties required for different sections of the weatherstrip component. During the co-extrusion process, these distinct compounds are fed into separate extruders to simultaneously form the various segments of the profile20. Specifically, a Ø120 extruder is utilized for the compound that forms the waterproof lip and molding, a Ø90 extruder for the cover and inner lip, and a Ø70 extruder for the upper lip as illustrated in Fig. 2. The individual compound streams are merged within a common die to produce a single, integrated profile designed for effective sealing against water, dust, and noise. The profile undergoes a vulcanization process to achieve its final elastic characteristics.Fig. 2Schematic of the cross-section of weatherstrip and its key geometric features.

Experimental system

The proposed system establishes an integrated control architecture for the entire extrusion process, managing a data-driven workflow from real-time acquisition to screw speed control, as illustrated in Fig. 3. During the extrusion stage, rubber compounds are introduced via hoppers and conveyed by the screw within the barrel. Since the rheological properties of the polymer melt, such as shear-thinning behavior, are highly temperature-dependent, the temperatures of the feeding zone, hopper, screw, and barrel are precisely monitored21.Fig. 3Configuration of the proposed AI-integrated co-extrusion system. (a) Schematic of an extrusion line, (b) Vision inspection system configuration, (c) On-site implementation of the SVF control system at the commercial production line.

Configuration of the proposed AI-integrated co-extrusion system. (a) Schematic of an extrusion line, (b) Vision inspection system configuration, (c) On-site implementation of the SVF control system at the commercial production line.

The extrudate passing through a die undergoes die swell due to the pressure drop. consequently, the temperature and pressure at the die exit are continuously monitored as key process indicators. The unvulcanized extrudate then proceeds to the vulcanization stage, where crosslinking is initiated by thermal energy supplied via ultra-high frequency (UHF) and hot air vulcanization (HAV) systems. In this stage, the UHF system utilizes microwaves for rapid internal heating of the rubber, while the HAV system applies hot air to ensure comprehensive curing through the surface. This sequential heating mechanism ensures uniform crosslinking throughout the profile’s cross-section, leading to consistent mechanical properties.

Following vulcanization and subsequent cooling, the extrudate is inspected by four vision cameras (Ruler3010, SICK CO., LTD.) to measure the coordinates and dimensions of each segment. Each camera projects a laser onto the surface to acquire 3D geometry data, from which distances between specific feature points are calculated to determine the range and dimensions of geometric features as depicted in Fig. 2. These measurement data, integrated with operator-input product information (e.g., product code and work order number), are transmitted from the Manufacturing Execution System (MES) to the proposed Smart Vision-based Feedback (SVF) control program through TCP/IP socket communication. The control cycle is performed every 15 min, which corresponds to the transit time required for the extrudate to reach the vision system. This synchronization enables the system to proactively determine optimal process conditions, rather than adjusting the screw speed only after dimensional deviations or errors are detected.

The generated control signals are transmitted to the programmable logic controller (PLC) via the MES, allowing the extruder screw speed to be precisely controlled. Fig. 3c depicts the on-site configuration of the proposed co-extrusion control system at the mass-production line. By integrating the ANN-based closed-loop feedback mechanism, precise regulation of the extruder screw speed without manual intervention. Consequently, the operator’s role has shifted from manual process tuning to supervisory monitoring of process stability, facilitated by real-time visualization of dimensional errors derived from the vision inspection system.

The dataset was acquired from a commercial production line at Hwaseung Corp. (Yangsan, Korea) over a seven-month period from April to October 2025. It comprises a total of 72,732 samples collected from 34 production batches. The input variables for the model consist of two main types: 41 process parameters acquired throughout the entire process and 7 vision measurement data points. In the extrusion stage, three extruders with barrel diameters of Ø120, Ø90, and Ø70 mm were operated simultaneously. For each extruder, a suite of process variables was monitored, including feeding zone temperature, hopper temperature, screw temperature, barrel temperature, discharge pressure, screw speed, and discharge temperature. During the subsequent vulcanization stage, data regarding the temperature and power output of the UHF equipment, HAV temperatures, and puller speed were included.

The vision inspection variables consist of the geometric dimensions of the final product. These consist of the designated points on each section and the length and height values of key sections, as measured by a vision system after the vulcanization process. Specifically, the seven vision parameters are the Ø120 waterproof lip length, Ø120 molding width, two Ø120 molding spaces, the Ø90 cover lip length, the Ø90 inner lip length, and the Ø70 upper lip length.

Method

Overall model configuration

The proposed control framework consists of two models, as illustrated in Fig. 4: (a) the Initial Screw Speed (ISS) model, which determines optimal settings for process startup, and (b) the SVF model, which predicts screw speed adjustments during steady-state production. The ISS model estimates the optimal screw speed at the onset of production. By utilizing data from MES, this model predicts screw speeds that ensure the initial extrudate conforms to the target dimensional specifications. This initial prediction minimizes the material waste and energy consumption typically associated with iterative manual adjustments during the startup phase. Once the process reaches a steady state, the transition to the SVF model-based control is manually initiated by the operator. The operator verifies that key process variables, such as pressure and temperature, have stabilized before activating the control loop. This human-in-the-loop approach provides an additional safety layer, allowing transient startup anomalies to be addressed prior to closed-loop operation. Control inputs are periodically adjusted based on the model predictions, using real-time process variables collected from the extrusion line sensors and dimensional data acquired from the vision inspection system. A control signal is generated at 15-min intervals, corresponding to the transit time required for the extruded material to travel through the vulcanization line and reach the inspection cameras. By analyzing the deviation between measured and target dimensions, the model predicts the required screw speed correction, and the control input is adjusted accordingly. To select appropriate algorithms for the ISS and SVF models, three representative model structures were considered: RF and XGBoost as tree-based ensemble methods, and ANN as a neural network-based approach. Tree-based models were selected for their ability to handle nonlinear relationships and their robustness with relatively small industrial datasets22, while ANN was included for its flexibility in modeling complex continuous relationships among process variables23. Notably, vision-based architectures such as convolution neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers are typically designed for image-based tasks, whereas the inputs in this study consist of structured, low-dimensional numerical features extracted by the vision system rather than raw image data. Therefore, these architectures were not considered in this study. The proposed models were developed in Python (v3.12.12) using Scikit-learn (v1.6.1) and XGBoost (v2.1.1). Training and evaluation were conducted on a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen 5 5600X CPU, 16 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU.Fig. 4Schematic diagrams of the proposed models. ISS model

Model training

The ISS model was developed using data acquired from the commercial extrusion line. To accurately capture the specific conditions of the startup phase, the input dataset was constructed using the first 10% of the data recorded from each production batch. Feature selection was subsequently performed based on random forest (RF) feature importance to identify the most critical process parameters affecting the screw speeds24. To mitigate potential sensor instability or measurement errors inherent in the early stages, only the top 30% of features with the highest relative influence were retained to ensure robust prediction25.

Furthermore, to prevent data leakage between training and validation sets, a group-based splitting strategy was implemented whereby data were divided at the production batch level. This ensured that samples from the same batch were not split across different sets26. Model operation

Upon the initiation of the production process, all controllable process variables, excluding the screw speeds, are first stabilized at their predefined setpoints. To determine the optimal screw speeds corresponding to these specific process conditions, the control system connects to the MES via a TCP/IP socket to acquire real-time process data as a JSON object. This data packet contains the product code and the real-time values of essential parameters, such as extruder temperatures and pressures. The system processes these inputs using the identical pre-processing pipeline defined during training: numerical values are standardized, and only the key features identified as significant during the feature selection phase are retained. These processed inputs are then fed into the trained RF model to generate scaled predictions. Finally, an inverse transformation converts these values back into rpm units, yielding the optimal screw speed setpoints for the three extruders.

SVF model

Model training

A fundamental challenge in the weatherstrip co-extrusion is the significant physical time delay between screw speed adjustments and the resulting dimensional changes observed by the vision system. To mitigate this latency, we adopted an inverse modeling approach rather than a forward model. Although forward models are inherently consistent with physical causality, our primary objective is to minimize dimensional deviations in real time. By directly mapping observed errors and current process states to the required screw speed adjustments, the inverse model is specifically optimized for active compensation within the feedback loop, offering a more practical solution for precise and goal-oriented control in co-extrusion process27–29. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 5, the model inputs consist of a composite vector containing the current process variables, the variation in process variables between t − gap and t, and the dimensional error relative to the target specifications. Here, the gap denotes the feedback control interval configured to account for the physical transport delay of the vulcanization line.Fig. 5Schematic diagram of ANN architecture for SVF model.

To enhance predictive performance with this heterogeneous data, a dual-path feature engineering strategy was employed. Process variables, such as temperaturesand pressures, which exhibit high dimensionality and multicollinearity, were compressed using standardization followed by principal component analysis (PCA) to retain 99% of the variance. This approach enhances model robustness by filtering noise and redundancy while maintaining the essential environmental context. In contrast, the dimensional error features were standardized and utilized without dimensionality reduction to preserve precise geometric information essential for quality control. Finally, these processed features are concatenated to serve as the input for the ANN, which is designed to capture the complex nonlinear dynamics of the co-extrusion process. A grid search was conducted to determine the optimal hyperparameters, and fivefold cross-validation was applied to evaluate model performance. Model operation

During the operation phase, the SVF control system is executed through an integrated interface connected with the MES. Process data is acquired as a JSON-formatted object via a TCP/IP socket connection. These datasets include the live dimensional measurements from the vision system, target specifications (master values), and real-time extrusion process variables, such as temperatures and pressures.

The system utilizes the identical feature engineering pipeline established during training. First, the dimensional error is calculated as the difference between the live vision measurements and the target master values. Input features are then processed according to their categories: the dimensional error features are standardized using the preloaded scaler and retained in their raw form, whereas extrusion process variables are standardized and projected onto a lower-dimensional latent space using the pre-trained PCA model. These processed features are concatenated and utilized as input for the trained ANN model. The model outputs a scaled prediction of the required screw speed adjustments, which is converted into rpm units via an inverse transformation. Subsequently, this predicted value is translated into a directional control signal, such as up, down, or maintain, based on the required adjustment direction. Finally, this signal is transmitted back to the MES to regulate the extruder settings, forming a closed-loop control system that continuously minimizes dimensional error. To ensure operational safety, a safety interlock mechanism with a dead-band threshold of ± 0.3 mm is incorporated, based on the standard tolerance limits of weatherstrip. When the vision-based feedback detects a dimensional error exceeding this threshold, an alarm is triggered and automated control is suspended. In such cases, the process reverts to manual control, allowing the operator to stabilize the system and investigate potential anomalies.

Discussions

This study proposes a robust control framework designed to address the inherent complexities of weatherstrip co-extrusion, where achieving geometric precision is challenging due to the multifaceted interaction among process variables and the significant time delay caused by the vulcanization stage. Previous control approaches have shown limitations in two primary aspects. Early methodologies focused on regulating indirect quality indicators, such as the melt flow index, rather than directly controlling the final geometry10,11. While subsequent studies introduced ANN to capture process nonlinearities, they were often limited to process monitoring or parameter prediction without establishing a fully integrated closed-loop feedback mechanism14,15. To overcome these limitations, the proposed system combines two AI models: an ISS model and an SVF model. By utilizing an extensive dataset acquired from an industrial mass-production line, this research demonstrates a practical data-driven approach that addresses the distinct production stages, spanning from process startup to continuous production.

In conventional practice, the initial screw speed is determined based on operator experience and iterative trial-and-error, often resulting in variability and material waste during startup. By formulating ISS prediction as a data-driven task, the proposed approach enables more reliable and consistent initialization of process conditions, reducing dependency on operator intuition. Regarding the ISS model, the RF algorithm demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy. Given that process conditions vary significantly across production batches, data was partitioned at the batch level during both dataset splitting and cross-validation to prevent data leakage and ensure validation. The superiority of the RF model is attributed to its ensemble nature, which effectively handles the high variance and noise inherent in the startup data. However, intrinsic limitations remain. Despite the model’s robustness, inevitable prediction residuals persist, primarily because the optimal screw speed can fluctuate even under similar observable conditions. These variations arise from unmeasured factors not captured in the current dataset, such as batch-to-batch variations in raw material rheology (e.g., viscosity changes in rubber compounds) or the maintenance status of the extrusion equipment30. Since the model relies solely on recorded process parameters, it cannot fully account for these latent physical disturbances, resulting in inevitable prediction residuals.

For continuous quality control, the SVF model was developed and implemented via TCP/IP socket connection with the MES. The ANN architecture effectively captured the nonlinear multivariate relationships required to map dimensional errors and process states to precise control signals. Field demonstrations confirmed that the system successfully maintained dimensional errors within the target tolerance range (− 0.1 to + 0.2 mm). This demonstrates the practical feasibility of deploying a data-driven feedback control system in a real production environment. Compared to conventional operator-driven adjustments, the proposed approach enables consistent and adaptive quality control while reducing reliance on manual intervention. However, certain limitations exist. First, the control interval is constrained by the physical transport time (approximately 15 min) through the vulcanization line, preventing the system from instantly compensating for rapid fluctuations occurring within this dead time, which remains an inherent limitation of the process. Second, the ground truth labels for training were derived from historical adjustments made by skilled operators, reflecting the decision patterns embedded in expert operational data. Nevertheless, the proposed approach leverages expert knowledge inherent in the operational data, which has been shown to enhance robustness and generalization in data-driven models34,35, while enabling more consistent and standardized control beyond individual operator performance. The proposed SVF model demonstrated practical utility as a robust solution for minimizing dimensional deviation in the actual production environment. Furthermore, in practical production environments, system characteristics can shift over time due to external factors such as material variability and environmental changes. To address this, the models are retrained on a weekly basis. As production is carried out according to a weekly schedule, sufficient data is accumulated each week from the MES to support regular model updates. By combining previously accumulated data with newly acquired data, the models can continuously adapt to evolving process characteristics.

Future work will explore the integration of domain knowledge and material-specific properties into the learning framework to further improve model robustness and adaptability across varying production conditions.

Conclusion

This research developed and validated a dual-stage AI control system using real-world data from a mass-production line. The ISS model, driven by the RF algorithm, successfully predicts optimal startup setpoints, allowing the process to rapidly establish stable operating conditions. Subsequently, the SVF model, utilizing an ANN, predicts the required screw speed adjustments for precise feedback control, effectively managing the complex interactions of the co-extrusion process. By minimizing the reliance on manual adjustments, this system ensures stable production quality and offers a practical pathway for autonomous manufacturing in weatherstrip co-extrusion.
