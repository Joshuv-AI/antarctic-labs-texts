Triglyceride to High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Ratio and Sensorineural Hearing Loss in Community-Dwelling Adults: an NHANES Analysis
Authors: Yang Yu, Zhi-Chao Yang, Li-Xin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeSensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) is prevalent among older adults in the United States. Recent studies suggest the triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio as a potential marker for metabolic and cardiovascular conditions. Our research investigates the association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL using a comprehensive national dataset.Materials and MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data cycle 1999–2000, 2001–2002, 2003–2004, 2011–2012, and 2015–2016. Participants aged 50–69 years with complete audiometry and TG/HDL-C data were included. The outcome was the presence of SNHL, defined as an average hearing threshold >25 dB in the better ear. We employed multivariate logistic regression adjusted for demographics, smoking, noise exposure, and comorbidities to explore the association.ResultsA total of 1148 participants constituted the analytic sample, and 31.4% had SNHL. Compared to no SNHL, those with SNHL exhibited higher TG/HDL-C ratios (3.5 vs. 2.7). Elevated TG/HDL-C tertiles correlated with increased SNHL odds [tertile 2: adjusted odds ratio (aOR)=1.50, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.97–2.32, p=0.069; tertile 3: aOR=1.64, 95% CI: 1.03–2.63, p=0.039]. The link was stronger in participants without diabetes or obesity, with significant predictive values for SNHL presence (area under the ROC curve=0.716 and 0.753, respectively).ConclusionA higher TG/HDL-C ratio was significantly associated with SNHL in US adults aged 50–69 years, especially in those free from diabetes or obesity. These findings support considering TG/HDL-C as a useful marker for SNHL risk, highlighting the importance of combined metabolic and auditory health assessments.

INTRODUCTION

Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), caused by damage to the inner ear’s hair cells, is the most common hearing loss type, affecting 5 to 27 per 100000 people annually and resulting in about 66000 new cases each year in the United States (US).12 Hearing loss prevalence doubles every decade from the second to the seventh, with 80% of those over the age of 80 experiencing SNHL.2 About 23% of the US population experiences mild to severe hearing loss, primarily affecting older adults.3 SNHL is typically permanent and progressive. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSHL) is a sudden, unexplained, unilateral deafness that typically occurs in adults aged 40–50.4 SNHL’s etiology includes congenital factors, infections, aging, noise exposure, ototoxic drugs, and genetic predisposition.1 Progressive hearing loss is linked to social isolation, depression, falls, cognitive decline, and dementia.56

Risk factors for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are believed to be associated with age-associated hearing loss.7 Furthermore, the five components of metabolic syndrome (MetS) align with risk factors for SNHL, including hypertension, obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and low levels of high-density lipoprotein.8 This trend suggests that cardiometabolic indexes, which can predict the presence and severity of metabolic diseases, may also be associated with SNHL.

The triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio, which represents the proportion of triglycerides to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, serves as a surrogate measure of insulin resistance.910 In addition, this ratio has emerged as a significant predictor of MetS and CVD.1112 Literature indicates that individuals experiencing SSHL frequently present with heightened triglyceride concentrations.13 Additionally, the HDL-C level was inversely associated with high-frequency hearing loss, suggesting that lower HDL-C levels might be a risk factor for SNHL.14 Therefore, it could be speculated that the combination of these two parameters, namely the TG/HDL-C ratio, may more accurately assess the relationship between lipid metabolism and hearing loss.

Motivated by the above observations, we sought to investigate the potential connection between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL. Evaluating the potential link between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL may enhance our understanding of SNHL pathophysiology and provide evidence for incorporating metabolic factors into the assessment and management of hearing loss. We hypothesized that individuals with an increased TG/HDL-C ratio would be more likely to have SNHL compared to those without. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the associations between TG/HDL-C and SNHL in US adults, utilizing data from the publicly accessible National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

This cross-sectional study analyzed secondary data from the NHANES database. NHANES is a program of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which is part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the United States (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). The ongoing series of annual surveys combines interviews and examinations to evaluate the health and nutritional status of adults and children. The NHANES uses a complex, multistage design to collect and analyze data representative of the national, non-institutionalized population of the US. The data are released for research purposes, and permission to use the data is granted to researchers by the NCHS. Participants in the NHANES complete a household interview and are invited to undergo an extensive examination in a mobile examination center (MEC), including a physical exam, specialized measurements, and laboratory tests. Consequently, evaluating subjects within the NHANES database is reliable and multidimensional and is equivalent to a population-level assessment.15

In this study, data were extracted from the five released cycles of the NHANES database that included audiometry test results: 1999–2000, 2001–2002, 2003–2004, 2011–2012, and 2015–2016.

Ethics considerations

The NCHS Research Ethics Review Board reviewed and approved the NHANES program, and all survey participants provided signed informed consent. Therefore, no further ethical approval or informed consent was required to analyze secondary data reported in this manuscript. Please check the NHANES website for NCHS Research Ethics Review Board Approval (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/irba98.htm). Additionally, all NHANES data released by the NCHS are de-identified and remain anonymous during data analysis.

Study population

Participants aged between 50 and 69 years who had complete audiometry data and the necessary measures for calculating the TG/HDL-C were eligible. This specific participant age range was selected for data completeness, as it corresponds to the age range in which the NHANES database conducts audiometry. Pregnant women, as well as participants who had an ear tube (tympanostomy) installation or were diagnosed with impacted cerumen or collapsed ear canals, were excluded. Additionally, to ensure the validity of tympanometry measurements, participants with peak pressure ≤-150 daPa or compliance ≤0.3 mL, or more than a 10-dB difference between test and retest thresholds at 1 kHz, were excluded. The NHANES survey does not include assessments for congenital/genetic or SSHL. Accordingly, participants who exhibited low-frequency pure tone averages (LFPTAs) or high-frequency pure tone averages (HFPTAs) more than 3 standard deviations above the mean for their respective age group, or a difference of more than 15 dB between ears in either LFPTA or HFPTA, were also excluded from further analysis.

Study variables

SNHL measurement

The NHANES audiometry examination consisted of several components conducted in a specialized, acoustically isolated room within the NHANES MEC. These components were: an audiometric questionnaire aimed at identifying any factors that could affect the audiometric testing process or the interpretation of its results; a concise otoscopic screening examination of the ear canals and eardrums, which aimed to identify any abnormalities or conditions that would necessitate alternative audiometric procedures or referral to medical professionals; tympanometry aiming to identify any pathologies in the middle ear that might contribute to hearing loss; and pure tone air conduction audiometry measuring the participants’ hearing thresholds across various frequencies within the range of human hearing (500, 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 6000, and 8000 Hz). Details of the protocols and procedures for the audiometry exam can be found on the NHANES website: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2015-2016/AUX_I.htm.

In pure tone air conduction audiometry, the thresholds for air conduction pure tones were obtained at frequencies of 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz, and these thresholds were averaged to determine the LFPTA. Additionally, the thresholds at frequencies of 3, 4, 6, and 8 kHz were averaged to determine the HFPTA. A person with SNHL, in this analysis, was defined as having an HFPTA or LFPTA threshold of more than 25 dB in the better ear, in accordance with a previous study using the same dataset.16

TG/HDL-C ratio measurement

Serum TG (mg/Dl) and HDL-C (mg/Dl) concentration data were retrieved from the laboratory profiles from the NHANES dataset. The TG/HDL-C ratios were calculated by dividing TG by HDL-C. Subsequently, we categorized the TG/HDL-C ratios into three levels based on tertiles. The cutoff values for the 33rd percentile and the 66th percentile were 1.48 and 2.80, respectively.

Covariates

Demographic data, including age, sex, race, and education level, were obtained through in-person interviews conducted by trained the NHANES interviewers using the Family and Sample Person Demographics questionnaires and the Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing system (Confirmit Corp., New York, NY, USA). Collected data were weighted according to the NHANES protocol.

Body mass index (BMI) value was obtained from the NHANES examination measurements, calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height (m2). Body weight was measured using an electronic load cell scale, and standing height was measured using a fixed stadiometer.

Hypertension was defined as responding “yes” to the questions: “Were you told on two or more different doctor/clinic visits that you had hypertension, also called high blood pressure?” or “Because of your (high blood pressure/hypertension), have you ever been told to take prescribed medicine?” or having an average of three consecutive measures of systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or an average of three successive measurements of diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg.

CVDs, including coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, and stroke, were defined by the response to the question: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have (name of disease)?”

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was estimated using the four-variable Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) Study equation from re-calibrated serum creatinine. In this study, we utilized the Isotope Dilution Mass Spectrometry-traceable MDRD Study equation that uses standardized creatinine: GFR=175×(standardized serum creatinine)-1.154×(age)-0.203×0.742 (for female participants)×1.212 (for black participants). Estimated GFR was reported in mL/min/1.73 m2. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined by an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Participants’ smoking status was classified as non-smoker, former smoker, or current smoker, defined as follows: non-smoker, lifetime smoking of fewer than 100 cigarettes; former smoker, lifetime smoking >100 cigarettes but not currently a smoker; current smoker, lifetime smoking >100 cigarettes and responding “yes” to the question: “Do you smoke now?”

Alcohol consumption was classified based on responses to survey questions defining alcohol consumption. Heavy drinker was defined as answering ≥4 times/week to the following question: “In the past 12 months, how often did you drink any alcoholic beverage?”

Diabetes was primarily determined through the criteria set forth by the American Diabetes Association, with participants meeting any one of the following criteria: self-reported as having been diagnosed with diabetes by a doctor; now using anti-diabetic drugs; with hemoglobin A1c level ≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol); and with fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L).17

Chronic respiratory tract disease was determined if the participants reported having emphysema, chronic bronchitis, or asthma.

Noise exposure was separated into three categories: exposure to loud noise/music in the last 24 hours, exposure to firearm noise, and exposure to occupational noise. Occupational noise was defined as noise so loud that participants had to raise their voices to be heard at their workplace.

Information on the usage of lipid-lowering medications, including statins (Atorvastatin, Rosuvastatin, Simvastatin, Pravastatin, Lovastatin, Fluvastatin, Pitavastatin), cholesterol absorption inhibitors (Ezetimibe), bile acid sequestrants (Cholestyramine, Colesevelam, Colestipol), and fibrates (Fenofibrate, Gemfibrozil), were extracted from the NHANES’s prescription medication data.

Statistical analysis

The NHANES uses a complex, multistage, probability sampling design to assure national representation, wherein sampling weights (WTSAF2YR), pseudo-stratum (SDMVSTRA), and pseudo-cluster (SDMVPSU) provided by the NHANES were applied in all analyses as guided by the NCHS. The weighted mean and standard error are shown for continuous variables, and unweighted numbers and weighted proportions are shown for categorical variables. The p-values for comparisons between the groups were calculated using the PROC SURVEYFREQ and SURVEYREG for categorical and continuous data, respectively. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using the PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC statement to determine the associations between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL. We developed the three models using a stepwise adjustment approach based on the significance of variables in univariate analysis. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, which were the fundamental demographic factors. Model 2 added lifestyle and environmental factors (race, smoking, firearm noise, and occupational noise). Model 3 further included clinical variables (hypertension, diabetes, CVD, CKD, and lipid-lowering medications) to account for underlying health conditions. This approach allowed us to assess the impact of adding covariates incrementally in the multivariable regression analysis.

In patients without diabetes and without obesity, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated for the diagnostic performance of the TG/HDL-C ratio in the presence of SNHL. We also used the DeLong test to determine the p-values among the models. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software (version 9.4, SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Study population

The study population selection process is depicted in Fig. 1. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 1148 participants comprised the study population for the subsequent analyses. This sample was estimated to represent 23285462 US residents using the weighting method provided by NHANES (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the study population

The demographics and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Among all participants, 360 (31.4%) had SNHL, and 788 (68.6%) did not. The mean age of the study population was 57.9±0.2 years; most were female (64.8%) and non-Hispanic White (72.9%). Other races included non-Hispanic Black (12.0%), Hispanic (4.6%), and others (10.5%). Individuals who had never smoked represented 48.3% of the study population, and the most common comorbidity was hypertension (48.8%). A significantly higher TG/HDL-C ratio was observed in participants with SNHL compared to those without SNHL (3.5 vs 2.7) (Table 1).

Associations between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL

Table 2 shows the associations between the TG/HDL-C ratio and the presence of SNHL. Multivariate model 1 showed that every 1-unit increase of the TG/HDL-C ratio was not significantly associated with the odds of SNHL (p>0.05). Similar associations were shown in model 2 and model 3 (p>0.05). In addition, tertile 2 (vs. tertile 1) of the TG/HDL-C ratio was found to be associated with significantly higher odds of SNHL in model 1 [adjusted odds ratio (Aor)=1.68, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.09–2.57, p=0.018] and model 2 (Aor=1.57, 95% CI: 1.03–2.40, p=0.036), respectively. Tertile 3 (vs. tertile 1) of the TG/HDL-C ratio was significantly associated with higher odds of SNHL in model 1 (Aor=2.08, 95% CI: 1.28–3.39, p=0.004), model 2 (Aor=1.89, 95% CI: 1.18–3.02, p=0.008), and model 3 (Aor=1.64, 95% CI: 1.03–2.63, p=0.039), respectively (Table 2).

Associations between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL stratified by sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and obesity status

Table 3 shows the associations between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL, stratified by sex, diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, and obesity status. For males, tertile 3 of the TG/HDL-C ratio was associated with higher odds of SNHL in model 1 (aOR=2.51, 95% CI: 1.15–5.48, p=0.021). Among those without DM, tertile 3 was associated with higher odds of SNHL across all models (model 1: aOR=2.06, 95% CI: 1.14–3.72, p=0.018; model 2: aOR=1.88, 95% CI: 1.06–3.31, p=0.030; model 3: aOR=1.83, 95% CI: 1.05–3.16, p=0.032). For subjects with hypertension, tertile 2 was significantly associated with SNHL in models 1 and 2 (model 1: aOR=1.86, 95% CI: 1.02–3.39, p=0.045; model 2: aOR=1.87, 95% CI: 1.01–3.47, p=0.046). Among those without hypertension, tertile 3 was associated with higher odds in model 1 (aOR=2.53, 95% CI: 1.15–5.59, p=0.022). For subjects without obesity, a 1-unit increase in TG/HDL-C ratio was associated with higher odds of SNHL in models 2 and 3, with significant associations for tertiles 2 and 3 in all models.

Predictive performance of the TG/HDL-C ratio on SNHL

The discriminative ability of the TG/HDL-C ratio in the presence of SNHL is documented in Tables 4 and 5 and Figs. 2 and 3. In patients without diabetes, the AUC for predicting SNHL using the TG/HDL-C ratio alone was 0.581 (95% CI: 0.539–0.623). This improved to 0.716 (95% CI: 0.679–0.752) when adjusted for covariates in model 3 (all models demonstrated significantly improved AUCs compared to the TG/HDL-C ratio alone, p<0.001) (Table 4, Fig. 2). Similarly, in patients without obesity, the AUC was 0.621 (95% CI: 0.575–0.666) using the TG/HDL-C ratio alone, which increased to 0.753 (95% CI: 0.716–0.790) after adjustment for covariates in model 3 (all models demonstrated significantly improved AUCs compared to the TG/HDL-C ratio alone, p<0.001) (Table 5, Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Our findings indicated a significant association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL among US adults. Participants with higher TG/HDL-C ratios, especially in the upper tertile, demonstrated an increased likelihood of having SNHL. This association was particularly evident in subgroups without diabetes or obesity. Furthermore, the TG/HDL-C ratio showed a good predictive performance for SNHL when combined with demographic and other clinical variables. These results suggest that the TG/HDL-C ratio could be a valuable indicator in identifying individuals at higher risk for SNHL in adults aged 50–69 years, emphasizing the need for integrated metabolic and hearing health assessments.

The present study focused on individuals aged just short of 70 years (50–69 years). Hypertension, one of the components of MetS, was the most commonly observed comorbidity among these participants. Irreversible hearing loss, SNHL in particular, was most prevalent among individuals aged 70–80 years and older 3. Historically, this age group also has an increased risk of vascular events and comorbid cardiovascular illnesses,18 and those with cardiovascular illnesses are known to be more likely to experience hearing loss.19 Cardiovascular risk factors are associated with age-related SNHL.7 In a previous study, hypertension was shown to interact with chronic diseases, such as diabetes, and was therefore suggested to be associated with the pathogenesis of SNHL.20 A cross-sectional study of 11000 participants showed that the risk of having SNHL was 1.4% in individuals with MetS compared to those without MetS, increasing to 1.48% if four components were present and to 1.78% when five components were present. This demonstrated that MetS itself is a risk factor for SNHL, and that the risk increases as the number of MetS components increases.8 Although the present study did not focus on people with “sudden” SNHL, a specific type of SNHL with a rapid onset, a previous study investigated people with this condition and found a slower recovery in patients with MetS than in those without MetS, and the prognosis was poorer among those who had at least four of five MetS components.21

Insulin resistance is characterized by abnormal uptake and oxidation of glucose, decreased glycogen synthesis, and its normal ability to suppress lipids oxidation.

As such, it is an important part of alterations in the metabolic pathway that lead to both CVD and diabetes.2223 The development of insulin resistance is thought to stem from possible undernutrition in early life combined with unhealthy, obesity-promoting diets and sedentary lifestyles in adulthood, which together lead to increased risk of insulin resistance, inflammatory processes, and chronic diseases, including diabetes and CVD.2425 The TG/HDL-C ratio is known to help identify those at the highest risk of CVD and diabetes in populations vulnerable to insulin resistance.26 Our results indicated that the TG/HDL-C ratio is associated with SNHL. Although causal inference cannot be strictly made due to the cross-sectional design of this study, the findings suggest a potential link between metabolic disturbances associated with insulin resistance and an increased risk of developing SNHL.

Interestingly, our results revealed that the association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL is modified by the participants’ diabetes and obesity status. However, this needs further explanation. The associations between a high TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL are observed only in individuals without diabetes. This may indicate that the underlying insulin resistance, as suggested by the TG/HDL-C ratio, could directly link to microvascular damage, including in the auditory system, among relatively healthy participants.1127 In contrast, individuals who already have diabetes might possess pre-existing microvascular complications that overshadow the specific impact of high insulin resistance on hearing loss. Moreover, the cumulative effects of chronic metabolic disturbances and medical interventions in diabetic patients may diminish the detectable impact of the TG/HDL-C ratio on hearing loss. Similarly, the TG/HDL-C ratio can predict SNHL among individuals without obesity but not among those with obesity. This suggests that obesity, which itself contributes to widespread vascular damage, may mask the specific effects of dyslipidemia on hearing health.28 The compounded vascular damage in obese individuals may make it harder to isolate the impact of the TG/HDL-C ratio alone on SNHL.

Overall, the TG/HDL-C ratio can be easily measured and calculated from routine laboratory tests, as done in the present study. These features support its suitability for routine daily practice, which may help identify those at the highest risk for SNHL, an important step toward earlier treatment.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study in the medical literature to evaluate the associations between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL. A significant strength of this research is its use of data from the NHANES, which includes a large and diverse participant sample representative of the entire US population. This extensive and comprehensive dataset enhanced the reliability of our findings. Additionally, SNHL was objectively determined through audiometry using standard diagnostic criteria. We also examined the relationship between the TG/HDL-C ratio and SNHL across different subgroups. However, this analysis had certain limitations. Most importantly, the study’s cross-sectional design allowed us to establish an association but not a cause-and-effect relationship between the TG/HDL-C ratio and the development of SNHL. Additionally, since this was a cross-sectional study, temporal changes, such as variations in BMI or blood levels, were not considered in the analysis. The retrospective nature of the data limits the generalizability of the results to other populations and does not eliminate selection bias. Moreover, we focused on individuals aged 50–69 years, as no subjects between the ages of 20 and 49 years met the criteria for SNHL, potentially biasing the data weights due to the restricted age range. Additionally, other risk factors not captured by the NHANES may confound the study results. Future longitudinal studies are needed to obtain more evidence and confirm our findings.

In conclusion, this study showed a strong association between high TG/HDL-C ratios and SNHL in older US adults. Elevated TG/HDL-C ratios, particularly in the higher tertiles, were associated with a greater risk of SNHL. This association was stronger in individuals without diabetes or obesity. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and explore underlying mechanisms.
