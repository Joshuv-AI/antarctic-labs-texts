A qualitative study on the management system for investigator-initiated studies in healthcare institutions in Beijing, China
Authors: Xiayan Chen, Jiaxue Meng, Hongling Chu, Yangfeng Wu, Huijuan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Measures for Management of Investigator-Initiated Studies (IISs) Conducted by Healthcare Institutions (trial version) (referred to as Management Measures) has been piloted successively in 12 provincial regions across China since 1 October 2021 and took effect nationwide on 1 October 2024. This study aimed to examine the perspectives, attitudes and challenges of administrators and investigators within healthcare institutions in Beijing regarding IIS management to provide further strategic guidance in China.MethodsThis descriptive qualitative study included 13 focus group discussions among 74 participants from 25 healthcare institutions stratified selected in Beijing, including 13 executive-level administrators, 41 functional administrators and 20 investigator representatives. The focus groups were conducted from November to December 2021. The discussion topics focussed on current status of IIS management in healthcare institutions and the participants’ attitudes and challenges in implementation of the Management Measures, mainly on the specific management system establishment, independent scientific review, management by study type and whole-process supervision.ResultsOpinions varied among institution presidents/directors regarding the establishment of clinical research management committees and offices, with significant challenges identified in insufficient human resources and difficulties in integrating with existing management models. While scientific review was seen as essential, there was disagreement on how to implement it. Interviewees raised concerns about redundancy for grant-supported projects, unclear distinctions between scientific and ethical reviews, efficiency delays, reviewer qualifications and intellectual property risks. Although management by study type might help control risks, the qualification restrictions on the leading sites and investigators were viewed as excessively stringent. No institution has achieved whole-process supervision of all IISs, primarily due to insufficient human resources, ineffective supervision and management systems, and insufficiently trained professionals.ConclusionsThis study reveals that the current management of IISs is generally weak in Beijing and should be strengthened by implementing Management Measures. However, major challenges existed for the implementation, mainly including: a lack of clear and feasible management models to follow, insufficient human resources for both scientific review and whole-process supervision, and worries about restricting research initiatives. It takes time to overcome these challenges, which may be achieved through building management capacity at national, regional and institutional levels, learning successful management models that were piloted in various regions and further policy research to develop specific guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-024-01264-x.Keywords: Investigator-initiated studies, Healthcare institutions, Research management system, Qualitative interview, Beijing, China

Background

Investigator-initiated studies (IISs) are clinical research initiated and managed by investigators or institutions rather than by pharmaceutical, biotech or medical device companies [1]. IISs go by various terms, including investigator-initiated trials, investigator-sponsored trials, noncommercial trials, academic clinical trials, physician-led studies, and investigator-driven clinical trials. Additionally, the word “investigator” can be replaced with “academic” or “physician”, and “study” can be replaced with “trial” or “research” [1, 2]. IISs are characterized by diverse objectives, innovative designs and close alignment with clinical diagnosis and treatment needs, often addressing gaps not covered by industry-sponsored studies [3–5]. IISs can vary widely, ranging from interventional to observational studies, from small single-centre studies to large multi-centre studies, involving drugs, medical devices and other medical technologies, covering different phases of clinical research [6]. Conducting these studies is highly rewarding for investigators and has the potential to significantly contribute to the generation of evidence and influence policy decisions, while also carefully managing and avoiding commercial interests [7–9]. However, limited financial resources, time constraints, and a lack of capacity and training often present significant challenges [9–12].

In recent years, the number of IISs has increased globally, with particularly notable growth in China [13–16]. Data from ClinicalTrials.gov show that the annual number of registered IISs doubled globally, from 15 611 in 2013 to 33 735 in 2023 [17]. In fact, the total number of IISs in China may exceed 100 000 according to other sources of information such as Chictr.org.cn [18]. Globally, the management of IISs is influenced by diverse regulatory frameworks, with some countries such as the United States adopting a flexible approach by regulating only investigational new drug IISs, while the European Union adopts stringent regulations for all interventional studies regardless of the sponsor [19, 20]. International guidelines, such as the Declaration of Helsinki and those from organizations such as the International Council for Harmonization, aim to standardize practices, but variations in implementation persist across countries [21, 22].

Poor management of IISs can compromise their scientific integrity, expose participants to risks, lead to the discontinuation of research and result in the wasteful use of research resources [23–25]. For example, the study of editing babies’ genes for prevention of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) sparked widespread ethical outrage [26]. The more than a thousand unnecessary randomized trials on statins’ lipid-lowering effect, reported by British Medical Journal, has harmed China’s global research reputation [27]. To standardize the management of IISs, the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China issued the Measures for Management of Investigator-Initiated Studies Conducted by Healthcare Institutions (trial version) (referred to as Management Measures) in September 2021 (detailed information about Management Measures can be found in Additional file 1) [28]. It was initially piloted in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangdong and Hainan in China, with eight provincial regions subsequently added in April 2022 [28, 29]. In September 2024, the National Health Commission, together with the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine and the National Disease Control and Prevention Administration, issued the finalized Management Measures, which took effect nationwide in October 2024 [30]. Management Measures requires healthcare institutions to enhance their internal management and support services for clinical research, implement management on the basis of the types and potential risks of IISs and ensure comprehensive whole-process supervision. These measures aim to standardize clinical research management, improve research quality, promote the healthy development of clinical research and enhance the capacity of healthcare institutions.

Beijing, a key player in global clinical research efforts, is home to many prominent healthcare institutions and research centres. From 2020 to 2024, over 20 000 clinical research studies were registered by 145 healthcare institutions in Beijing using the Medical Research Registry and Recordkeeping System (www.medicalresearch.org.cn), a system that mandates the registration of all biomedical research involving human subjects conducted by healthcare institutions in China [31]. Amongst the 145 healthcare institutions involved, 94 are grade 3 hospitals (64.8%), 23 are grade 2 hospitals (15.9%), 10 are grade 1 hospitals (6.9%), 8 are maternal and child healthcare institutions (5.5%), 2 are centres for disease control and prevention (CDCs) (1.4%), 1 is a blood bank (0.7%) and 7 are other types of institutions (4.8%). In China, grade 3 hospitals, equipped with advanced resources, address complex cases and support high-level research, while grade 2 hospitals typically serve county or district-level populations and grade 1 hospitals function as community or rural facilities, providing broader, generalizable data from standard clinical settings. Specialized institutions, including maternal and child healthcare centres, CDCs, and blood banks, offer insights specific to their areas of expertise, enriching clinical and research perspectives across diverse healthcare needs.

Management Measures offer crucial guidance for healthcare institutions managing IISs; however, their implementation might face numerous challenges. Peking University Clinical Research Institute (PUCRI) serves as the monitoring, evaluation and technical guidance centre for clinical research management in Beijing. PUCRI designed and conducted this study to understand the perspectives, attitudes and challenges of administrators and investigators within healthcare institutions in Beijing regarding IIS management to provide further strategic guidance in China.

Methods

Study design and team

Given the variability and complexity of organizational structures and management methods, which are difficult to quantify, a qualitative approach aligns well with our study objectives of understanding their current status. It also allows for an in-depth exploration of participants’ experiences, emotions and meanings, enabling us to capture attitudes towards the Management Measures and uncover implementation challenges that quantitative methods might overlook. Focus group discussions were held to collect data in this descriptive qualitative study, as they address non-private topics, facilitate rich interactions among participants, capture diverse perspectives, are time-efficient, stimulate creative discussions and provide insights into social dynamics, leading to deeper understanding of the research topic. The research team consists of members with solid professional backgrounds. The core team members included W.Y.F. (male, MD, with more than 40 years of clinical research experience and more than 20 years in clinical research management); L.H.J. (female, PhD, with more than 20 years of experience in clinical research management); C.H.L. (female, PhD, with more than 10 years of experience in qualitative research design and implementation); M.J.X. (female, Master, with more than 5 years of experience in clinical research management); and C.X.Y. (female, Master, with more than 5 years of experience in clinical research management). The focus group discussions were coordinated and conducted by W.Y.F. and L.H.J., while M.J.X., C.H.L. and C.X.Y. were responsible for the data analysis.

Participants and recruitment

The sampling process comprised two stages: institution selection followed by designation of representatives by each institution. Using a list of healthcare institution provided by the Beijing Municipal Health Commission, a stratified random sampling approach was employed on the basis of the types of healthcare institutions within Beijing’s administrative regions, resulting in the selection of 25 institutions. The Beijing Municipal Health Commission issued an official notice detailing the study objectives and requesting that each institution designate relevant personnel to participate in qualitative interviews. Each selected institution was required to appoint one executive-level administrator (typically fixed personnel), one or two functional administrators (typically fixed personnel) and one investigator representative (purposively sampled on the basis of the institution’s recommendations). Executive-level administrators could provide strategic insights into institutional priorities, functional administrators focus on operational implementation and investigator representatives offer a frontline perspective on research practices, ensuring a comprehensive understanding across different levels. Participants were not aware of the study prior to being invited to participate. L.H.J. contacted them via phone, email and other methods to explain the research objectives, methods and other details. All participants provided oral informed consent, and written consent was not needed. This study was approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-21112) on 12 November 2021.

Data collection

A semi-structured interview guide was created by the research team (detailed information about the interview guide can be found in Additional file 2). The discussion topics focussed on the current status of IISs management in healthcare institutions and the participants’ attitudes and challenges in implementation of the Management Measures, mainly on the specific management system establishment, independent scientific review, management by study type and whole-process supervision.

From November to December 2021, 13 focus group discussions were organized online via the Tencent Meeting. Focus group discussions were distributed on the basis of the number and types of institutions conducting IISs in Beijing. This included seven for public grade 3 hospitals, three for public grade 1 and 2 hospitals and private grade hospitals, one for CDCs, one for blood banks, and one for the maternal and child healthcare institution. For hospitals, separate discussions were held for executive-level administrators, functional administrators and investigators to minimize hierarchical dominance and encourage open discussion. Each discussion was initiated by leaders from the Beijing Municipal Health Commission and included a 10-min introduction of Management Measures by L.H.J. Following this, the interview team guided the discussion using a predesigned semi-structured interview guide, and each group discussion lasted 2–3 h and involved 3–8 participants. The interviews continued until data saturation was achieved, meaning that no new information was being uncovered. All the interviews were conducted and recorded with the participants’ oral consent.

Data analysis

The interview recordings were transcribed into text documents by C.L.N. (female, shorthand typist). The private information of the interviewees was stored in a coded table. After the data were transcribed, they were labelled with codes, so that analysts cannot identify the respondents’ specific private information. The data were analysed using a thematic analysis approach with the assistance of QSR NVivo 11.0 software. Two researchers, M.J.X. and C.H.L., independently coded and analysed the transcripts. This process involved selecting units of analysis, interpreting the transcribed data, developing codes, categorizing the data and abstracting the main themes. After the initial review, two researchers, in collaboration with core team members, discussed and agreed on the code and related instructions for each code (see Table 1).

Results

Characteristics of institutions and participants

In 25 selected healthcare institutions, excluding IISs where these institutions only serve as participating sites, public grade 3 hospitals lead with an average of 102.6 ongoing IISs, followed by municipal maternal and child healthcare institutions (70.0) and municipal CDCs (25.0), while other institutions have significantly fewer (see Table 2). A total of 90 individuals from the selected 25 healthcare institutions were invited to participate in the interviews, and 16 were unable to participate for business or personal reasons, including 9 executive-level administrators and 7 functional administrators. A total of 74 interviewees, consisting of 13 executive-level administrators, 41 functional administrators and 20 investigator representatives, were included in the study (detailed participant information can be found in Additional file 3).

Structured interview findings

Clinical research management committee

Article 15 of Management Measures requires that “healthcare institutions conducting clinical research should set up a clinical research management committee (referred to as management committee)”.

Approximately half of healthcare institutions have established or are in the process of establishing a management committee, but only a few public grade 3 hospitals have developed independent management committees in full compliance with the Management Measures. Opinions on the position of the management committee vary. Some believe it should be an independent office directly under the institution, parallel to the scientific research office. Others suggest that it should oversee the scientific research office to guide its management activities. In terms of the role of the committee, most institutions mentioned the integration of existing resources and management models. This integration can take several forms: (1) the management committee coordinates with other committees for overall clinical research management; (2) the management committee manages only IISs, while the Good Clinical Practice Office (an office mandated by Chinese regulations for clinical trial institutions to oversee for new drug applications) handles clinical trials for these applications; and (3) no new management committee is needed, and an optimized academic committee or scientific research office can fulfil its functions.

The attitudes towards establishing the management committee differ significantly. Some investigator interviewees emphasized its necessity for decision-making, arbitration and professional management.

“It would function as an arbitration board, providing valuable support to investigators when presenting their research opinions or suggestions to the committee” (Investigator from a public grade 3 hospital, no. 34).

Many executive-level and functional administrators felt that establishing a clinical trial management committee was unnecessary, as similar responsibilities were already being handled by the scientific research office, academic committee and other committees or offices before the implementation of the Management Measures.

“The functions of […] may overlap, potentially leading to inefficiencies when establishing institutions with similar functions”

(Investigator of a public grade 3 hospital, no. 40; presidents of public grade 2 hospitals, no. 42, 43, 44; and directors of CDCs, no. 62, 65).

Institutions with limited clinical research activities question the necessity of establishing such committees, believing that the need for such a committee depends on the amount of clinical research.

“In hospitals with little clinical research, it may not be meaningful to establish such a committee. However, larger healthcare institutions with extensive clinical research could be necessary”

(President of a public grade 3 hospital, no. 10; investigator from a blood bank, no. 61).

The majority of division heads identified the lack of human resource support as a significant obstacle to establishing these committees. In Chinese hospitals, administrative staffing is typically fixed and stable due to strict staffing quotas, leaving insufficient capacity to manage an increased number of committees.

Clinical research management office

Article 15 of Management Measures requires that healthcare institutions “clearly identify a specialized office responsible for clinical research management. Healthcare institutions should provide necessary supporting administrators and conditions for clinical research management”.

In most healthcare institutions, the scientific research office primarily handles the management of clinical research. However, it also frequently handles additional responsibilities, including discipline development, medical education, applications for research platforms, paper publication management, and other general management tasks. With the increasing scale and complexity of clinical research, some institutions have started to establish specialized offices for clinical research management, such as a clinical research quality management office. This office effectively integrates the roles of scientific and ethical reviews by coordinating with academic and ethics committees. Although a specialized office theoretically improves management efficiency and research quality in Management Measures, many interviewees highlighted the practical challenges in implementing such offices. Investigator interviewees voiced concerns about the need for additional approval and management by another office, while administrators expressed issues with human resources and overlapping responsibilities.

“While such an office is necessary, the responsibility will still likely fall on our shoulders (the scientific research office), and our available human resources are limited”

(Division head of a maternal and child healthcare institution, no. 73).

Initiation management

Scientific review

Article 18 of Management Measures requires healthcare institutions to “formulate management systems, detailed rules, and working procedures for the scientific review of IISs, and organize scientific reviews”.

Most of the institutions interviewed, particularly public grade 3 hospitals, had already begun conducting scientific reviews of IISs prior to the implementation of Management Measures, with some starting as early as 2012. There are four types of organizations responsible for scientific reviews: (1) independent scientific review organizations or committees; (2) ethics committees; (3) scientific research offices; and (4) clinical departments. The scientific review committees are typically composed of diverse members. Institutions with strong research foundations can generally meet Management Measures’ requirements by including senior experts from relevant fields, as well as methodological and statistical experts, on the committees.

Almost all the interviewees supported the concept of scientific review, especially those investigators with extensive experience in clinical research implementation. They emphasized its importance for ensuring the scientific integrity of clinical research implementation.

“For investigators, scientific review significantly contributes to the successful implementation of clinical research. A comprehensive review conducted by a diverse team of experts […] ensures fairness and comprehensiveness”

(Investigator from a public grade 3 hospital, no. 41).

However, some interviewees highlighted challenges, such as doubts about the necessity of additional scientific review for grant-supported projects and potential delays caused by the review process.

“IISs involved in grant-supported projects have been approved by experts […], scientific review is deemed unnecessary”

(Division heads of public grade 3 hospitals, no. 8, 17, 24; investigators of public grade 3 hospitals, no. 37, 40).

Division heads and investigators from public grade 3 hospitals pointed to unclear boundaries between scientific and ethical reviews, while interviewees from other research institutions raised issues with the qualifications, ability, and availability of review experts and risks of intellectual property leakage when inviting external experts.

“Hospitals face challenges in accessing a large pool of experts, particularly those specializing in methodology”

(President of a maternal and child healthcare institution, no. 70).

Implementation management

Management by study type

Article 5 of Management Measures requires that “healthcare institutions should assess the risk of IISs based on their specific circumstances and implement management according to study types and intervention measures”. In addition, Article 11 of Management Measures requires that “interventional studies should generally be led by grade 3 hospitals or higher-level municipal institutions. If the research involves clinical intervention measures, a multidisciplinary team of licensed physicians should be established”.

Most institutions have developed their own criteria of study types for IISs, including factors such as financial support, whether the institution is the leading site and whether the study is commercial or grant supported. Regarding the classification of interventional and observational studies, approximately half of the institutions have implemented management by this classification. Most administrator interviewees support the management by interventional and observational studies, frequently mentioning “risk” as a key consideration. They believe that it is an effective way to control the risks associated with clinical research, while many investigator interviewees from public grade 3 hospitals observed that investigators and academic committee members often define interventional and observational studies differently. One investigator interviewee also noted that the classification of studies as interventional or observational is insufficient to define the associated risk, as interventional studies do not always carry a higher risk than observational studies.

In addition, interviewees from healthcare institutions other than grade 3 hospitals reflected that the qualification requirements for institutions leading interventional studies are too strict; institutions such as grade 1 and 2 hospitals, maternal and child healthcare institutions, CDCs and blood banks also have a need to lead interventional clinical trials, and such regulations would impose significant limitations.

“With multidisciplinary team and qualified investigators, I am curious whether highly specialized grade 2 hospitals such as ours can lead interventional studies”

(Division head of a blood bank, no. 68).

Interviewees from CDCs pointed out that the qualification requirements for investigators conducting research involving clinical interventions are overly strict. In China, many researchers in healthcare institutions are not licensed physicians, such as licensed nurses, licensed pharmacists, laboratory technicians, public health physicians and dedicated research personnel. CDC interviewees suggested that investigators should only be required to possess qualifications and capacities specifically relevant to the interventional studies they conduct.

“CDC encompass various qualifications, including licensed physicians, public health physicians, technicians, and laboratorians. Relying solely on licensed physician for interventional study eligibility is not practical”

(Division head of a CDC, no. 62).

Investigator interviewees from public grade 3 hospital added that this requirement does not adequately account for studies involving health education, behavioural intervention, nutrition, psychological intervention, and health management.

“Restricting epidemiology Medical Doctors (MDs) from conducting interventional studies on nutrition and lifestyle could hinder the field's development”

(Investigator of a public grade 3 hospital, no. 29).

Whole-process supervision

Article 35 of Management Measures requires that “healthcare institutions should supervise the entire process of IISs and organize routine inspection. Principal investigators should regularly review the clinical research they are responsible for to ensure smooth progress”.

Currently, no institution has fully implemented whole-process supervision for all IISs. Only one institution among those interviewed has implemented risk-based, whole-process supervision. This approach includes departmental and institutional quality control, as well as third-party inspections at various stages: before initiation, during the enrolment of the first participant, during the mid-term, at project conclusion and during the filing process.

Most healthcare institutions manage their IISs through initial, mid-term and concluding meetings. This approach generally meets the requirements for managing grant-supported projects. For institutions with numerous IISs, annual random inspections are commonly used. In these inspections, investigators either present their progress or submit self-inspection reports.

Many investigators support whole-process supervision for its role in ensuring research quality and protecting participant safety, while a few have concerns about the potential increase in workload. In contrast, nearly all administrator interviewees from all types of healthcare institutions acknowledged that whole-process supervision for all IISs is challenging. Public grade 3 hospitals, which typically handle a large number of IISs, often lack the resources needed for whole-process supervision and routine inspection. Administrator interviewees highlighted shortages in personnel, time and expertise as significant challenges. For example, a four-member specialist team in a grade 3 hospital can only review about 10 studies annually alongside their regular duties.

“The primary challenges are staffing shortages and heavy workloads”

(President of a public grade 3 hospital, no. 2).

“The scientific research office mainly comprises medical undergraduates from nonclinical specialities who lack clinical experience. It is unrealistic to expect them to deeply engage in clinical research management from an expert and manpower standpoint”

(Division head of a public grade 3 hospital, no. 26).

Many interviewees from public grade 3 hospital suggested focussing primarily on random inspections. For other types of institutions, they face additional challenges such as inadequate supervision and management systems, as there is no dedicated office to handle these tasks.

Discussion

This study highlights the status and challenges of implementing management systems for IISs in Beijing. To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study to examine the perspectives, attitudes and challenges of key stakeholders regarding the pilot implementation of Management Measures in China.

Our study reveals that the current management of IISs is generally weak in Beijing and should be strengthened by implementing Management Measures. Currently, most healthcare institutions primarily manage clinical research through their scientific research offices, while only a few have already established independent management committees and offices for IISs in accordance with Management Measures [32–34]. In Shandong Province, China, a survey revealed that 40.30% of healthcare institutions manage IISs through their existing research management offices, while 29.39% have established independent management offices [35]. Most of the interviewed institutions had already begun conducting scientific reviews of IISs and approximately half have implemented management by study type prior to the implementation of Management Measures, but no interviewed institution in Beijing has fully implemented whole-process supervision for all IISs.

Regarding Management Measures, many respondents highlighted implementation challenges, primarily due to the policy’s lack of feasibility and unclear management details. Several issues with Management Measures were identified through the interviews: (1) unclear division of responsibilities between management committees and the existing committees; (2) redundancy in of additional scientific review for grant-supported projects; (3) unclear boundaries between scientific and ethical reviews; (4) overly stringent requirements for institutions and investigators to lead interventional studies; and (5) lack of feasibility of whole-process supervision for all IISs. These issues have also been highlighted in other studies [36, 37], which can result in significant administrative burdens and resource constraints, especially in institutions with limited clinical research activities, and may potentially restrict research initiatives.

Subsequent to our study, the Management Measures underwent two major revisions during their transition from the trial to the the finalized version in 2024: (1) the requirement for scientific review of observational studies was no longer mandatory; and (2) the requirement that investigators be licensed physicians was removed. Given that our study’s findings were submitted to the Beijing Municipal Health Commission as a reference report in March 2022, it is possible that insights from our research contributed to these adjustments.

Compared with the global regulatory framework, IIS management in China is primarily handled by healthcare institutions, which establish dedicated clinical research management committees and offices to oversee the entire research lifecycle. This approach aligns with the high volume of IISs in China, enabling institutions to tailor management practices to their specific needs and capacities. However, it also presents challenges, such as inconsistencies in standards and significant administrative burdens. For instance, a multi-centre trial may require multiple scientific reviews at different institutions, leading to duplicated efforts and delays in trial initiation. In the European Union, the review process for interventional studies is centralized through the Clinical Trials Information System, where applications are submitted directly to regulatory authorities. This centralized system promotes innovation and transparency by streamlining approvals and enabling larger, multi-country clinical trials [19, 38]. In contrast, China’s Medical Research Registry and Recordkeeping System is not publicly accessible, limiting external scrutiny and transparency. In addition, differing from the administrative emphasis in China, international clinical trial units or offices typically function as technical service providers, offering support in protocol design, data management, statistical analysis and regulatory consulting [39, 40]. For example, the Mayo Clinic Department of Medicine uses a “shared-resource” model to connect investigators with experienced staff, reduce costs and streamline trial operations, enabling compliance with Good Clinical Practice [39]. This service-oriented approach allows researchers to focus on the scientific and clinical aspects of their studies while leveraging specialized expertise for technical and administrative tasks.

To further address the issues in our findings, at the national level it is crucial to engage with the National Health Commission to provide policy flexibility, develop detailed implementation guidelines and clarify operational procedures and specific requirements. At the institutional level, healthcare institutions should consider their own research scale and needs comprehensively, rationally allocate resources and integrate management models to ensure efficient management and smooth scientific research activities. Institutions might also benefit from expanding their supervision teams or collaborating with specialized technical platforms from enterprises or universities to enhance support and training for administrators and investigators. Furthermore, learning from successful management models that have been piloted in various regions can provide valuable insights into addressing current challenges and improving efficiency. For instance, a grade 3 hospital in Beijing developed a clinical research integration platform in 2022 that enhances the standardization, management quality and efficiency of IISs [41]. It is also essential to explore innovative management models, such as centralized integration management and investigator responsibility systems, and to leverage information-based tools and platforms [42–45].

Limitations

This study has limitations: (1) the interviews were conducted from November to December 2021, making the findings potentially time-sensitive; however, as there were only a few major changes between the trial and finalized versions of Management Measures, the findings of this study remain relevant and valuable for reference; and (2) although the samples cover different types of healthcare institutions, all located in Beijing, potentially limiting the study’s generalizability.

Conclusions

This study examines the perspectives, attitudes and challenges of administrators and investigators within healthcare institutions in Beijing regarding the pilot implementation of Management Measures. The interviews reveal key challenges, particularly concerning the feasibility and clarity of the Management Measures. These findings highlight the need for greater policy flexibility and detailed implementation guidelines.
