Transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve implantation for residual prosthetic valve dysfunction and paravalvular leak after healed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis: a case report

Introduction

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is a severe complication after valve replacement and is frequently associated with heart failure, periannular destruction, and high mortality.1,2 Although surgery is recommended for complicated PVE, some patients remain unsuitable for redo surgery because of haemodynamic instability, organ dysfunction, or excessive operative risk.1

Even after microbiological cure, residual prosthetic valve dysfunction and paravalvular leak (PVL) may persist and lead to progressive heart failure. While transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is generally contraindicated in active infective endocarditis, emerging evidence suggests that valve-in-valve implantation may be a rescue option in carefully selected patients with successfully treated (healed) infection and prohibitive surgical risk.3,4

Herein, we report a high-risk patient with severe residual prosthetic valve dysfunction and paravalvular leak following Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis with controlled infection, who underwent transfemoral valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve implantation after multidisciplinary Heart Team evaluation.

Case presentation

A 64-year-old man was transferred to our centre because of progressive exertional dyspnoea and peripheral oedema for 10 days. Three years earlier, he had undergone surgical repair of a ventricular septal defect complicated by aortic valve prolapse with severe aortic regurgitation, together with bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement using a 23-mm Mosaic® valve (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). He had also undergone left knee replacement 1 year earlier and had hypertension, prior cerebral infarction, chronic kidney disease, and thrombocytopenia.

Eight months before referral, the patient developed fever (39.0°C) and positive blood cultures for Enterococcus faecalis and was diagnosed with prosthetic valve endocarditis at the referring institution. Because the original echocardiographic images and detailed reports from that hospitalization were unavailable, specific structural findings, including vegetation, periannular abscess formation, pseudoaneurysm, or leaflet destruction, could not be retrospectively confirmed.

At that time, there was no available evidence suggesting uncontrolled infection, refractory heart failure, periannular abscess, or recurrent embolic events requiring urgent surgical intervention. Given the patient’s chronic kidney disease, thrombocytopenia, previous cerebral infarction, and elevated operative risk, the multidisciplinary team initially selected targeted antimicrobial therapy with close follow-up.

The patient completed an 8-week course of targeted intravenous antimicrobial therapy with ampicillin and daptomycin. Follow-up blood cultures remained negative, body temperature normalized, and inflammatory markers improved substantially. These findings indicated microbiological control of the infection before subsequent transcatheter intervention.

However, severe residual prosthetic valve dysfunction persisted, including severe prosthetic stenosis, severe central transprosthetic regurgitation, and severe paravalvular leak. On admission to our centre, his heart rate was 110 beats/min, blood pressure was 90/52 mmHg while receiving norepinephrine 0.05 μg/kg/min, respiratory rate was 18 breaths/min, oxygen saturation was 95% on 3 L/min nasal cannula oxygen, and temperature was 37.2°C. Cardiac examination revealed systolic ejection and early diastolic murmurs in the aortic area. Bilateral crackles, mild limb oedema, and haemorrhagic skin spots were present.

The patient was considered to have cardiogenic shock secondary to severe prosthetic stenosis, severe central transprosthetic regurgitation, and severe paravalvular leak, resulting in low forward cardiac output and acute decompensated heart failure. Initial management included norepinephrine support, intravenous diuretics, oxygen supplementation, blood transfusion, correction of electrolyte imbalance, and close haemodynamic monitoring.

Laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (11.9 × 109/L), severe anaemia (haemoglobin 58 g/L), thrombocytopenia (52 × 109/L), hyponatraemia (128.3 mmol/L), renal dysfunction (creatinine 1.96 mg/dL), hypoalbuminaemia (23.3 g/L), elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (1483 pg/mL), and elevated inflammatory markers.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 43%, severe prosthetic aortic stenosis with peak/mean gradients of 74/47 mmHg, severe central transprosthetic regurgitation, and severe PVL arising from the non-coronary sinus of Valsalva (Figure 1). Cardiac-gated computed tomography and a patient-specific three-dimensional printed model confirmed the site and extent of the leak and supported procedural planning (Figure 2).

The patient’s deterioration was attributed to acute decompensated heart failure secondary to residual prosthetic valve dysfunction after prosthetic valve endocarditis with microbiological control following targeted antimicrobial therapy.

The Heart Team considered redo surgical aortic valve replacement with repair of the periannular defect as the conventional treatment strategy. However, given the patient’s cardiogenic shock, severe anaemia, thrombocytopenia, chronic kidney disease, and markedly elevated operative risk (EuroSCORE II 32.22%; STS predicted risk of mortality 12.9%), redo surgery was considered unsuitable.

After multidisciplinary discussion of risks and benefits, transfemoral ViV TAVI was selected as a rescue treatment strategy. A 21-mm VitaFlow self-expanding transcatheter heart valve (MicroPort CardioFlow Medtech Corporation, Shanghai, China) was implanted via the transfemoral approach under general anaesthesia.

Post-implantation transoesophageal echocardiography demonstrated improved transvalvular gradients with only mild paravalvular regurgitation (see Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2). Final aortic root angiography confirmed mild residual regurgitation (Figure 3), and postprocedural electrocardiography showed no new conduction abnormalities (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).

Periprocedural imaging before and after valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve implantation. (A) Transoesophageal echocardiography before intervention demonstrating severe prosthetic valve dysfunction with severe regurgitation. (B) Aortic root angiography before implantation. (C) Post-implantation transoesophageal echocardiography demonstrating improved prosthetic valve haemodynamics with only mild residual paravalvular regurgitation. (D) Aortic root angiography after implantation confirming a reduction in regurgitation.

Following ViV TAVI, additional oral antimicrobial therapy with amoxicillin was administered for 4 weeks under infectious disease specialist supervision because of the history of prosthetic valve endocarditis and residual periannular involvement.

The postoperative course was uncomplicated. At 12-week follow-up, transthoracic echocardiography showed a well-functioning transcatheter valve with peak and mean transvalvular gradients of 16 and 9 mmHg, respectively, and trace paravalvular regurgitation (Figure 4). Laboratory evaluation demonstrated improvement of the previous thrombocytopenia and anaemia, with the platelet count increasing to 133 × 109/L and haemoglobin level rising to 92 g/L.

Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography at 12 weeks after valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve implantation. Doppler assessment (left) demonstrates improved transvalvular gradients, and two-dimensional colour Doppler echocardiography (right) demonstrates stable transcatheter valve position and trace residual paravalvular regurgitation.

At 1-year follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable without significant recurrent paravalvular regurgitation or evidence of recurrent infection.

Discussion

This case highlights a difficult boundary between guideline-based surgical management of complicated PVE and individualized rescue therapy in a patient with prohibitive operative risk. The 2023 ESC endocarditis guideline emphasizes management by an Endocarditis Team and recommends surgery for complicated PVE when there is severe valve dysfunction, heart failure, or uncontrolled infection.1 In the present case, redo surgery was considered but judged excessively high risk because of shock, severe cytopenias, renal dysfunction, and bleeding risk.4

TAVI is generally contraindicated in patients with active infective endocarditis because implantation of prosthetic material into an infected field may increase the risk of persistent infection or relapse. The key consideration in this patient was that the previous Enterococcus faecalis PVE had achieved microbiological control after targeted antimicrobial therapy, while the predominant clinical problem was residual bioprosthetic valve dysfunction characterized by severe stenosis, severe central transprosthetic regurgitation, and severe PVL. Previous reports and registry data suggest that transcatheter intervention after controlled infective endocarditis may be feasible in carefully selected high-risk patients, although current evidence remains limited and individualized assessment remains essential.5

This case also emphasizes the importance of multimodality imaging and careful procedural planning. Echocardiography defined the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction and haemodynamic consequences, whereas computed tomography and three-dimensional modelling provided detailed assessment of root anatomy, coronary height, and the spatial relationship between the paravalvular leak, prosthesis, and surrounding structures. Such imaging-based evaluation is particularly important for ViV TAVI, where coronary obstruction, residual gradients, paravalvular leak, embolic risk, and prosthesis–patient mismatch should be carefully considered.6–8 In this patient, comprehensive imaging assessment contributed to procedural feasibility evaluation and supported individualized treatment planning.

An important limitation of this case is the absence of detailed echocardiographic findings immediately after completion of antimicrobial therapy ∼8 months before the current admission. Because the patient was referred from another institution, complete imaging data during that period were unavailable, and the progression of prosthetic valve dysfunction between microbiological control of infection and clinical deterioration could not be fully assessed.

At the time of completion of antimicrobial therapy, microbiological control had been achieved, with negative follow-up blood cultures and no documented evidence of uncontrolled infection, periannular abscess, or other urgent surgical indications based on the available information. Considering the patient’s substantial comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease, thrombocytopenia, previous cerebral infarction, and elevated operative risk, a conservative follow-up strategy was initially adopted.

Nevertheless, the absence of comprehensive interval imaging represents an important limitation. Progressive deterioration of prosthetic valve function may have occurred during this period and contributed to the subsequent presentation with cardiogenic shock. Earlier reassessment with multimodality imaging and multidisciplinary Heart Team evaluation might have allowed earlier recognition of disease progression and consideration of intervention before haemodynamic compromise developed.

Several practical considerations emerge from this case. First, microbiological status should be rigorously assessed before considering transcatheter intervention after PVE to ensure that active infection has been adequately controlled. Second, ViV TAVI in this setting should be regarded as a rescue strategy for selected patients who are unsuitable candidates for redo surgery rather than a routine alternative to surgical management. Third, multidisciplinary Heart Team evaluation remains essential to balance procedural feasibility, surgical risk, and long-term outcomes. The favourable 1-year outcome observed in this patient supports the feasibility of this approach but does not establish its generalizability; longer-term follow-up is required to assess recurrent infection, valve durability, residual paravalvular leak, and clinical outcomes.
