ApolipoproteinE+ Kupffer cells maintain immune homeostasis following liver transplantation by inducing CD8+ T cell exhaustion
Authors: Zhuoyu Jia, Xinqiang Li, Xueteng Wang, Xin Zhou, Hailun Cai, Peng Jiang, Ruidong Ding, Huan Liu, Ye Wang, Shipeng Li, Jinzhen Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryKupffer cells (KCs) are key drivers of immune homeostasis following liver transplantation (LT). We identified a specialized APOE+ KC subset enriched in stable and tolerant grafts by integrating human single-cell RNA sequencing with murine orthotopic LT models. These APOE+ KCs function as a central signaling hub, communicating with CD8+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells via the CXCL16-CXCR6 axis. Using APOE-knockout models, we demonstrated that donor-derived APOE was essential for KC suppressive capacity. APOE deficiency exacerbates acute rejection by preventing CD8+ TRM cells from upregulating inhibitory checkpoints (PD-1 and TIGIT) and from transitioning into an exhausted state, which is necessary to resolve alloreactivity. Clinically, elevated donor serum APOE inversely correlates with recipient model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) scores and liver injury. Ultimately, APOE+ KCs promote graft tolerance by driving CD8+ TRM exhaustion, establishing donor APOE as a critical prognostic biomarker and dual therapeutic target for optimizing transplant survival.

Introduction

Organ transplantation, particularly liver transplantation (LT), is a critical treatment for end-stage organ failure.1 Despite the transformative impact of modern immunosuppressive regimens on graft survival, the inevitable major histocompatibility complex (MHC) mismatch between donor and recipient necessitates the prolonged administration of these agents. This lifelong reliance is inextricably linked to severe systemic complications, including opportunistic infections, malignant tumors, cardiovascular morbidity, and chronic renal failure.2 Consequently, optimizing the post-transplant immune microenvironment to facilitate spontaneous immune tolerance and mitigate rejection remains a paramount objective in transplantation immunology.3

Recent advances have highlighted the critical role of macrophages in hepatic immune regulation. Specifically, the mononuclear phagocyte system has emerged as a focal point of transplantation immunology.4 Within this system, Kupffer cells (KCs) constitute the largest population of tissue-resident macrophages and are central to maintaining liver homeostasis. Far from being mere phagocytes, KCs exert a sophisticated dual influence on the post-transplant immune response.5 They can act as potent antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines to exacerbate inflammatory damage and also possess the capacity to dampen immune responses by inducing T cell apoptosis or by promoting regulatory T cell differentiation.6,7 This functional plasticity suggests that specific KC subpopulations may be the primary architects of post-transplant immune homeostasis. However, the precise identities and regulatory mechanisms of these subsets remain incompletely understood.8,9

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have unveiled the profound heterogeneity of intrahepatic myeloid cells.10,11 Previously, we constructed a single-cell immune landscape of the human liver, highlighting the core role of macrophages in LT homeostasis.12 Our preliminary investigations using scRNA-seq identified a distinct subpopulation of KCs characterized by high expression of APOE within the transplanted graft. While APOE is traditionally recognized for its role in lipid metabolism,13,14 emerging evidence highlights its pivotal immunomodulatory functions, particularly in regulating immune checkpoint responses in the tumor microenvironment.15,16,17 In addition, studies have shown that APOE plays a key role in promoting tumorigenesis and becomes a key part of immune regulation in the tumor immune microenvironment, suggesting that APOE is a new target for potential treatment.18,19 However, the specific contribution of APOE+ KCs to the maintenance of immune tolerance following LT has not been systematically explored.

Tissue-resident memory (TRM) cells play an important role in organ transplantation.20,21 Unlike circulating memory T cells, TRM cells reside permanently within the graft, providing site-specific immune responses.22,23 We underscored the critical role of CD8+ TRM in mediating localized liver allograft rejection.24 Further cellular communication analysis highlighted robust interactions between intrahepatic KCs and CD8+ TRM cells during rejection episodes. This leads us to hypothesize that APOE+ KCs may serve as a regulatory “brake” on CD8+ TRM-mediated rejection, potentially damping the expansion of the bona fide hepatic TRM phenotype (specifically the CD69+CD103− subset) and driving these cells toward a PD-1+/TIGIT+ exhausted state to preserve graft stability.

To definitively test this hypothesis, we employed scRNA-seq, flow cytometry, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC), and a technically demanding murine orthotopic LT model to delineate the spatiotemporal dynamics of APOE+ KCs. We characterized the longitudinal shifts of this subpopulation during the transition from acute rejection to immune tolerance. Notably, utilizing APOE-knockout and APOE-overexpressing transgenic models both in vivo and in vitro, we systematically established the causal role of APOE in dictating graft outcomes. Furthermore, we evaluated the clinical relevance of donor serum APOE levels as a potential predictive biomarker for early post-transplant liver injury. By elucidating the mechanistic axis where APOE+ KCs restrict CD8+ TRM expansion and enforce phenotypic exhaustion, this study provides insights into the induction of transplant tolerance and identifies promising therapeutic targets for optimizing long-term allograft survival.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals APOE+ KCs as a distinct subpopulation mediating robust T cell crosstalk

To comprehensively elucidate the cellular heterogeneity and identify potential immunomodulatory macrophage populations within the liver transplant microenvironment, we analyzed scRNA-seq data derived from recipient liver grafts and peripheral blood. Initial unsupervised clustering and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) dimensionality reduction of the total viable cells successfully distinguished major hepatic cell lineages, including T cells, B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, hepatocytes, and a broad myeloid cell compartment (Figure S1A). The identities of these primary clusters were rigorously defined using canonical lineage-specific marker genes (e.g., CD3D/E/G for T cells, CD19/MS4A1 for B cells, CD68/CD163 for myeloid cells, and ALB/HNF4A for hepatocytes; as detailed in the dot plot in Figure S1B).

To specifically address the heterogeneity within the myeloid compartment, we isolated and re-clustered the total myeloid cells (Figure S1C). Utilizing established marker profiles (CD1C/CLEC9A for dendritic cells, S100A8/S100A9 for myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and CD68/CD163/MRC1 for macrophages), we clearly distinguished the macrophage populations from dendritic and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (Figure S1D). Subsequent high-resolution subclustering of the macrophage population revealed diverse subsets of monocytes and macrophages (Figure 1A). Based on tissue distribution analysis (blood versus liver) (Figure 1C) and the robust expression of canonical KC markers, specifically CD5L, VSIG4, and MARCO, we precisely delineated the liver-resident KC populations from infiltrating the monocyte-derived macrophages. To infer the biological functions of these APOE+ KCs, we performed the Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis. This specific subset was significantly enriched in pathways related to “receptor-mediated endocytosis,” “antigen processing and presentation,” “efferocytosis,” and “lipid and atherosclerosis” (Figures 1D and 1E). This transcriptomic profile strongly suggests that the APOE+ KCs possess highly active phagocytic, scavenging, and immunomodulatory capacities within the graft.

Given their potent immunomodulatory signatures, we further utilized CellChat to infer intercellular communication networks between these diverse macrophage subsets and distinct lymphocyte populations across different transplant outcomes (non-rejection vs. rejection). We observed striking differences in both the overall number of inferred interactions (Figure 1F) and the relative information flow of key signaling pathways (such as MHC-I, CD86, and GALECTIN) between the rejection and non-rejection states (Figure 1H). Heatmaps analyzing differential interactions revealed that the APOE+ KCs, acting as major signal senders, established dense communication networks with various T cell subsets, including CD8+ TRM, exhausted T cells (TEX), and CD4+ regulatory T cells (Figure 1G).

A detailed analysis of specific ligand-receptor pairs further delineated the molecular basis of the crosstalk between the APOE+ KCs and T cells. Notably, the APOE+ KCs were found to robustly interact with the T cells (particularly TRM and TEX subsets) via multiple critical co-inhibitory and co-stimulatory axes, including CD86-CTLA4, CD86-CD28, LGALS9-HAVCR2 (TIM-3), and NECTIN2-TIGIT (Figure 1I). The distinct enrichment of these specific communication patterns between the non-rejection and rejection groups strongly indicates that the APOE+ KCs actively orchestrate the T cell functional states—especially the regulation of the CD8+ TRM—through these receptor-ligand interactions, thereby playing a pivotal role in determining the fate of the liver allograft.

The mIHC (Figures 1J and 1K) of the different immune rejection states of the patient’s transplanted liver showed that with the occurrence of rejection, there was an increase in the count of the CD8+ T cells, augmentation of the KCs, and a significant alteration in the proportion of APOE+ KCs compared to the non-rejection graft samples. These data suggest that the APOE+ KCs may influence the stability of the immune microenvironment within the graft.

Spatiotemporal tracking reveals localized accumulation of the APOE+ KCs during the transition to spontaneous allograft tolerance

To systematically map the dynamic immune alterations from acute rejection to spontaneous tolerance, an allogeneic (C57BL/6 to C3H/He) murine orthotopic LT model was established. The experimental workflow integrated multi-organ sampling (liver, spleen, blood, lymph node (LN), and bone marrow (BM)) across four distinct post-operative time points (LT1W to LT4W) alongside comprehensive downstream evaluations (Figures 2A and 2B). Histological assessment of the allografts via H&E staining revealed severe structural damage and dense inflammatory infiltration at 1 week post-transplantation, which progressively resolved by week 4. During the acute rejection phase (LT1W), a profound systemic surge of macrophages/monocytes was observed, marked by significantly elevated frequencies within the liver allograft as well as across the spleen, peripheral blood, and BM (Figures 3A, 3C, 3D, and 3E). As the rejection phase subsided, this systemic expansion contracted. Conversely, high-dimensional mIHC and quantitative analyses revealed a distinct phenotypic evolution within the local graft microenvironment. While the overall KC frequency decreased post-rejection, the specific proportion of the APOE-expressing KCs (APOE+F4/80+) steadily increased, peaking during the established tolerance phase at LT4W (Figure 3B). Systemic accumulation of the APOE+ macrophages aligns with a CD206+ immunoregulatory phenotype during tolerance induction

To further elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of the APOE expression within the myeloid compartment, we tracked the APOE+ macrophages across multiple organs post-transplantation. A flow cytometric analysis, validated by strict FMO controls, revealed a striking biphasic systemic response. During the acute rejection phase at 1 week post-transplantation (LT1W), the frequency of the APOE+ KCs within the liver graft precipitously reduced compared with that of the normal C57 controls (Figure 4A). However, as the allograft spontaneously transitioned toward immune tolerance (LT2W to LT4W), the proportion of the APOE+ KCs underwent a robust, progressive expansion, reaching peak levels at LT4W (Figure 4A). Notably, this progressive accumulation and increased expression post-transplantation was not restricted to the local hepatic microenvironment. Given the critical role of M2-like macrophages in resolving inflammation, we next investigated the phenotypic association between APOE and the classical immunoregulatory marker CD206. Mirroring the overall APOE dynamics, the frequency of the CD206+ KCs dropped significantly during acute rejection (LT1W) but underwent substantial recovery and expansion through LT4W (Figures S2A, S2D, and S2E). Notably, specific evaluation of the APOE+ compartment within these CD206+ macrophages revealed a highly synchronized enrichment; as tolerance was established, the vast majority of the expanded CD206+ macrophages robustly co-expressed APOE (Figures S2B, S2F, and S2G). Consequently, the overarching frequency of the double-positive APOE+CD206+ KCs within the total macrophage pool peaked during the LT4W tolerance phase (Figures S2C, S2H, and S2I). This profound spatiotemporal phenotypic shift was visually corroborated by high-dimensional mIHC, which demonstrated extensive in situ co-localization of F4/80, CD206, and APOE exclusively within the tolerated liver allografts (Figure S2J). Together, these data indicate that the progressively accumulating APOE+ KCs predominantly adopt a CD206+ alternative activation phenotype, structurally supporting the maintenance of hepatic allograft tolerance.

Acute rejection drives CD8+ TRM cell expansion, whereas tolerance induction triggers their progressive contraction and exhaustion

To delineate the spatiotemporal dynamics of cellular immunity following LT, we evaluated the infiltration and composition of the hepatic T cells. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed a massive infiltration of CD8+ T cells within the allograft at 1 week post-transplantation (LT1W), which gradually contracted over the subsequent observation period (LT2W–LT4W) (Figure 5A). To precisely characterize these functional subsets, we established a rigorous sequential flow cytometry gating strategy (Figure 5B). Quantitative flow cytometric analysis corroborated the histological findings, demonstrating that the relative proportion of the CD8+ T cells surged during the acute rejection phase (LT1W) (Figure 5C). APOE deficiency exacerbates allograft rejection by driving CD8+ TRM expansion and abrogating their phenotypic exhaustion

To definitively establish the mechanistic causality of APOE in modulating graft outcomes, we utilized a transgenic orthotopic LT model featuring APOE-knockout (APOE−/−) donors (Figure 7A). By specifically depleting APOE within the donor graft, we could isolate the immunoregulatory contribution of graft-resident cells, predominantly KCs, to the local immune microenvironment. Strikingly, the loss of donor-derived APOE profoundly exacerbated early acute allograft rejection. Histopathological evaluation at 1 week post-transplantation revealed severe tissue architecture damage characterized by dense inflammatory infiltrates and extensive structural disruption, yielding markedly elevated Banff RAI scores in the APOE−/− allografts compared with those of wild-type controls (Figure 7B). This exacerbated histological rejection translated directly into profound clinical liver injury, as evidenced by a dramatic surge in the serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in the APOE-deficient group at 1 week (Figure 7D). Crucially, the tolerogenic microenvironment in the wild-type allografts successfully initiated the progressive exhaustion of these expanded TRM cells, a necessary checkpoint to limit immune-mediated tissue damage; however, the absence of APOE effectively dismantled this critical regulatory transition. Quantitative temporal analysis demonstrated a marked, statistically significant reduction in the expression of the essential inhibitory checkpoints PD-1 (Figures 8C and 8D) and TIGIT (Figures 8E and 8F) on the CD8+ TRM cells within the APOE-deficient allografts. Taken together, these in vivo data provide compelling causal evidence that APOE acts as a pivotal molecular brake against severe hepatic alloreactivity. It is strictly required not only to restrain pathogenic CD8+ TRM cell expansion but also to enforce their phenotypic exhaustion, thereby facilitating the establishment of allograft tolerance.

In vitro gain- and loss-of-function models validate APOE as a critical molecular brake on CD8+ T cell proliferation

To rigorously validate the direct immunomodulatory role of APOE observed in vivo, we established an in vitro co-culture system pairing the primary KCs—harvested from syngeneic, wild-type allogeneic, APOE-overexpressing (OE), and APOE-knockout (KO) mice—with responder splenic T cells (Figure S3A). We first tracked the temporal dynamics of the APOE expression in the KCs during the T cell interaction. While the syngeneic co-cultures maintained relatively low, baseline APOE levels, wild-type allogeneic stimulation triggered a significant and progressive upregulation of APOE in the KCs over a 72-h observation period (Figures S3B and S3C). This result indicates that alloreactive stimulation inherently induces APOE expression as a responsive regulatory mechanism. As intended by the model design, this APOE expression was vastly amplified in the APOE-OE group, whereas it was completely abrogated in the APOE-KO group (Figures S3B and S3C).

Next, we evaluated the direct consequence of this APOE modulation on T cell proliferation. We utilized a precise sequential flow cytometric gating strategy to identify CD3+CD8+ T cells (Figure S4A) and thus monitor their temporal expansion. Consistent with our in vivo findings, the wild-type allogeneic co-culture drove a marked expansion of the CD8+ T cells from 0 to 72 h compared to the syngeneic control (Figures S4B and S4C). Strikingly, manipulating APOE levels in the co-cultured KCs profoundly altered this alloreactive trajectory. The genetic ablation of APOE (APOE-KO) significantly accelerated and exacerbated the CD8+ T cell expansion, mirroring the unbridled alloreactivity observed in our murine transplant model. Conversely, the forced overexpression of APOE (APOE-OE) effectively blunted this pathogenic proliferation, remarkably constraining the CD8+ T cell frequencies to levels comparable to the syngeneic baseline (Figures S4B and S4C). Collectively, these in vitro gain- and loss-of-function data provide direct causal evidence that KC-derived APOE acts as a critical suppressor of alloreactive CD8+ T cell expansion.

Preoperative donor serum APOE levels predict longitudinal clinical recovery and negatively correlate with systemic inflammation

To further evaluate the translational and prognostic value of APOE in clinical settings, we analyzed the preoperative serum APOE levels of 31 liver transplant donors and correlated them with the post-operative clinical trajectories of the recipients (Figure S5). Baseline analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between the donor APOE levels and the model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) scores (r = −0.4488, p = 0.0113) of the recipients. Furthermore, higher donor APOE levels were associated with an attenuated systemic immune response in the recipients, which was evidenced by a significantly lower post-operative monocyte count (r = −0.4066, p = 0.0232) and a marginal negative correlation with the total white blood cell (WBC) count (p = 0.0545).

Crucially, longitudinal tracking of liver function biomarkers across the early post-operative days (POD1, POD3, and POD5) demonstrated a robust hepatoprotective association. The donor serum APOE levels exhibited a consistent and significant negative correlation with the total bilirubin (TB) and ALT levels of the recipients throughout the 5-day observation window (Figure S5). While the AST levels displayed a strong inverse correlation at POD1 (p = 0.0034), this statistical significance gradually waned by POD5 (p = 0.2314), likely reflecting the rapid, physiological short half-life of AST in the serum following acute injury. Collectively, these longitudinal clinical data robustly reinforce our in vivo and in vitro mechanistic findings, underscoring donor-derived APOE as a potent predictive biomarker for mitigated early allograft injury and accelerated post-operative functional recovery.

Discussion

The induction of spontaneous immune tolerance remains the goal in solid organ transplantation. While the widespread application of systemic immunosuppressive agents—such as calcineurin inhibitors—has significantly reduced acute rejection rates, their lifelong administration is inextricably linked to severe toxicities, opportunistic infections, and metabolic derangements.25,26 Consequently, graft-intrinsic immunoregulatory pathways are imperative for developing targeted therapies that spare systemic immunity. Following orthotopic LT, the allograft establishes a highly dynamic and heterogeneous microenvironment.27,28 Within this unique immunological niche, KCs act as central orchestrators, exhibiting a profound functional plasticity to either exacerbate alloreactivity or foster immune tolerance.29 In this study, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic mapping, murine LT models, histological staining, flow cytometry, in vivo validations, and in vitro cell co-culture assays to delineate a novel mechanistic axis: a phenotypically distinct APOE+ KC subpopulation serves as a critical molecular brake. This specific subset dictates allograft outcomes by actively suppressing pathogenic CD8+ TRM cells and enforcing their progressive phenotypic exhaustion. This is the first study that systematically elucidates the critical role of APOE+ KC subpopulation in inducing immune tolerance.

To explore the shared cell types, transcriptional programs, and signaling pathways between immune cells and KCs during post-transplant rejection, our team previously constructed a detailed single-cell atlas and analyzed the heterogeneous distribution and gene expression of macrophages in human liver infiltrates and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs).12 A primary challenge in post-transplant immunology is distinguishing the specific regulatory roles of recruited monocyte-derived KCs (MoKCs) from endogenous tissue-resident KCs (ResKCs) during acute inflammatory responses.30 To overcome this challenge, we confirmed that the APOE+ KCs expressed core resident signatures (VSIG4, MARCO).31 Utilizing spatiotemporal multi-omics and strict flow cytometric gating (employing the stable residency marker TIM4),32 we definitively identified the immunoregulatory APOE+ subset as bona fide ResKCs. Our longitudinal in vivo tracking revealed a striking biphasic dynamic: the localized proportion of the APOE+ KCs precipitously reduced during the severe acute rejection phase (LT1W), likely reflecting ischemia-reperfusion injury, oxidative stress, and profound cellular apoptosis. However, a robust, progressive accumulation of these cells was observed as the allograft spontaneously transitioned into immune tolerance (LT3W-LT4W). Notably, this specific expansion paralleled a phenotypic shift toward a CD206+ alternative activation (M2-like) state.33,34 Biologically, this shift suggests an adaptive transition; the intense clearance of apoptotic cellular debris (efferocytosis) following acute rejection may engage intracellular lipid metabolic pathways, inherently upregulating APOE expression and structurally anchoring the hepatic microenvironment in a resolving, tolerogenic state.

While our scRNA-seq analysis effectively delineated the APOE+ KC cluster within the hepatic immune landscape, it is well-established that mRNA transcript abundance does not always strictly correlate with actual protein levels. This biological discordance can arise from complex post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, variable translational efficiencies, and dynamic protein turnover rates, which are particularly relevant for heavily processed and secreted proteins like APOE. Acknowledging this transcriptomic-to-proteomic divergence, we deliberately anchored our mechanistic and functional validations on protein-level expression. By extensively utilizing flow cytometry and mIHC to confirm the specific enrichment of the APOE+ KCs during the tolerogenic phase, we ensure that our conclusions reflect the functionally active protein state rather than mere transcriptional potential. Thus, the synergy between our transcriptomic mapping and targeted protein-level validation robustly substantiates the immunomodulatory role of the APOE+ KCs in preserving allograft homeostasis.

The fate of the liver allograft is heavily dictated by localized cellular immunity, which is primarily driven by CD8+ TRM cells.35 Operating as site-specific, rapid-response effectors poised at the tissue frontline, their unrestrained accumulation and activation directly correlate with the severity of parenchymal destruction. By integrating in vivo APOE-knockout transgenic models with in vitro co-culture assays, we established absolute causality in this regulatory axis: APOE deficiency unleashes the proliferation of the bona fide CD69+CD103− CD8+ TRM subpopulation, thereby severely exacerbating histopathological damage and clinical liver injury. Conversely, APOE sufficiency is strictly required to induce the progressive upregulation of critical inhibitory immune checkpoints, namely PD-1 and TIGIT, on these graft-infiltrating TRM cells. The immune system maintains homeostasis through a delicate equilibrium of co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory signals.36,37 By driving the potent, alloreactive CD8+ TRM cells into a hyporesponsive, exhausted state, the APOE+ KCs effectively dismantle localized alloimmunity without necessitating the systemic depletion of T cells, representing an elegant evolutionary adaptation to preserve organ integrity against chronic allo-attack.

Mechanistically, our findings strongly advocate for a sophisticated paracrine regulatory model orchestrated by APOE+ KCs. Previous pan-cancer scRNA-seq studies have highlighted interactions between APOE+ macrophages and exhausted CD8+ T cells in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, functioning as an impediment to immunotherapy. Reversing this paradigm in the context of transplantation, our CellChat analysis identified enriched signaling via the CXCL16-CXCR6 and CXCL12-CXCR4 chemokine axes. These pathways likely facilitate the spatial recruitment and retention of CD8+ TRM cells in close physical proximity to the KC network, establishing a localized regulatory niche. Crucially, our Transwell co-culture experiments, which physically segregated the cell populations, demonstrated that the APOE+ KCs maintain their profound suppressive and exhaustion-inducing effects on the T cells without requiring direct cell-to-cell contact. This pivotal finding implies that APOE primarily acts as a secreted immunomodulator within the local hepatic niche, continuously bathing neighboring TRM cells in co-inhibitory signals, potentially by engaging specific downstream lipid or immune receptors on the T cell surface (such as LRP1 or other members of the LDLR family), a hypothesis that warrants intense future investigations.

Beyond elucidating the local microenvironmental circuitry, our study highlights the compelling translational and prognostic utility of systemic APOE. We identified a robust inverse correlation between pre-operative donor serum APOE levels and early post-transplant recipient liver injury markers (MELD score, ALT, and AST). While systemic circulating APOE is predominantly hepatocyte-derived, we propose that a high baseline donor APOE concentration reflects an intrinsically tolerogenic hepatic reserve. Clinically, grafts with elevated initial APOE levels may be inherently predisposed to a more robust immunosuppressive recovery, facilitating the rapid differentiation of resident KCs toward the protective APOE+ phenotype following surgical trauma. This positions donor serum APOE as a valuable pre-transplant stratification tool, potentially guiding the selection of marginal donors or informing the safe, early weaning of immunosuppressive regimens in select recipients.

In conclusion, this study systematically delineates APOE+ KCs as indispensable guardians of hepatic transplant tolerance. Acting as a critical molecular brake, this distinct macrophage subpopulation actively restrains the pathogenic expansion of localized CD8+ TRM cells and strictly enforces their progressive phenotypic exhaustion, thereby dismantling acute alloimmunity. Furthermore, our clinical data establish pre-operative donor serum APOE as a robust, non-invasive prognostic biomarker capable of predicting early allograft vulnerability. Ultimately, targeting the APOE regulatory axis offers a highly promising therapeutic and diagnostic framework to mitigate acute rejection and optimize long-term, immunosuppression-free allograft survival.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge certain limitations in our current study that provide fertile ground for future research. First, while our single-cell data identify the APOE+ KC cluster transcriptionally, mRNA abundance frequently diverges from functionally active protein levels owing to post-transcriptional processing and rapid secretion. We deliberately mitigated this discrepancy by anchoring our core mechanistic conclusions on rigorous protein-level evaluations (mIHC and flow cytometry). Second, while PD-1 and TIGIT upregulation robustly indicates phenotypic exhaustion, future investigations incorporating specific functional cytokine profiling will be required to comprehensively map the abrogation of TRM effector functions. Finally, identifying the exact downstream receptor on CD8+ TRM cells that directly bind KC-derived APOE is the critical next step in mapping this signaling cascade.

Method details

Single-cell data processing and clustering analysis

Single-cell transcriptome data analysis was performed using the Seurat R package (version 4.3.0).38 To ensure high-quality downstream analyses, cells with fewer than 501 detected genes or greater than 25% mitochondrial transcripts were rigorously filtered. After standard log-normalization and scaling, highly variable genes were identified to drive Principal Component Analysis (PCA). For high-resolution cell clustering, we constructed a K-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph utilizing the top 10 principal components and applied the Louvain algorithm (resolution = 0.5), with t-SNE employed for non-linear dimensionality reduction. To accurately define our target populations, a stepwise hierarchical clustering strategy was applied. Viable cells were initially classified into major hepatic lineages using canonical markers. The myeloid compartment was then computationally extracted to precisely distinguish macrophages from dendritic cells (DCs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Ultimately, the macrophage population underwent fine sub-clustering, allowing the robust identification of APOE+ Kupffer cells based on specific marker profiles, including CD5L, VSIG4, MARCO, and APOE.

Functional enrichment and pathway analysis

Following cell type identification, we characterized the biological functions of distinct clusters by performing enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. All analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (version 3.17.0)39 in conjunction with the org.Hs.e.g.,.db annotation database (version 3.11.4). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied to determine statistically significant enrichment. To provide a clear visualization of the functional landscape, the top ten enriched terms or pathways for each cell subset were displayed as bar plots or dot plots.

Inference of intercellular communication networks

To characterize the complex intercellular signaling landscapes and evaluate their dynamic shifts across different clinical states (Non-rejection vs. Rejection), we utilized the CellChat algorithm (version 1.6.1), leveraging a comprehensive database of ligand-receptor pairs derived from KEGG signaling pathways and validated experimental literature. The analytical workflow proceeded through key stages: initially, we identified overexpressed signaling ligands and receptors to pinpoint significant transcriptional variations. Subsequently, the ensemble average expression was computed to robustly capture expression patterns across cell types. We then modeled the communication probability to infer the strength and topology of the signaling networks. Crucially, we performed differential interaction analysis to compare the overall information flow between the Rejection and Non-rejection groups. Furthermore, we conducted a targeted ligand-receptor pair analysis to delineate the specific communication axes—particularly inhibitory immune checkpoint pathways (e.g., NECTIN2-TIGIT, PDCD1LG2-PDCD1, and CD86-CTLA4)—mediating the crosstalk between APOE+ KCs and distinct T cell subsets, with a primary focus on TRM cells.

Histological and immunological evaluation

Post-transplantation graft injury and systemic immune responses were evaluated using a comprehensive suite of analytical techniques. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) stained histological sections were independently blindly evaluated by experienced pathologists utilizing the Banff Rejection Activity Index (RAI) criteria to define the states of rejection and tolerance. Additionally, immunohistochemistry (IHC), multiplex IHC (mIHC), and serum biochemistry (ALT/AST levels) were performed. Crucially, high-dimensional flow cytometry, incorporating strict Fluorescence Minus One (FMO) controls, was utilized to precisely quantify the dynamic shifts of APOE+ Kupffer cells and meticulously track the expansion and progressive exhaustion (via PD-1 and TIGIT expression) of the bona fide CD69+CD103-CD8+ TRM cell phenotype.

Tissue dissection and cell suspension

Liver tissues were obtained at designated time points post-transplantation. A portion of the liver was sliced and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and IHC sample preparation. The liver of the transplanted mice was processed using the automated tissue dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, gentleMACS Octo) in conjunction with the liver dissociation kit (Liver Dissociation Kit, mouse #130-105-807) to prepare a single-cell suspension. The resulting suspension was filtered through a MACS® SmartStrainer (100 μm), followed by centrifugation at 300g for 10 min using a centrifuge with a 15 cm radius to remove the supernatant. The cells were then resuspended to obtain a single-cell suspension. Blood from the transplanted mice was collected via retro-orbital blood collection prior to liver harvesting, anticoagulated with EDTA (Thermo Fisher), and combined with whole blood and tissue dilution buffer (Thermo Fisher), as well as red blood cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) to prepare the single-cell suspension. The spleen, mesenteric lymph nodes, and liver were harvested simultaneously, and single-cell suspensions from the spleen and lymph nodes were obtained through a grinding and filtration method.

Isolation and coculture of KCs and T cells

Following the previously published protocol,40 KCs were extracted from normal and transplanted mice, with KCs accounting for approximately 85%–95% of the isolated cells, and the viability of the cells being around 90%–95%. The spleens of C57 and C3H mice were processed using the aforementioned grinding method to prepare a single-cell suspension, and T cells were isolated using magnetic bead sorting (Miltenyi Biotec, PanT Cell Isolation Kit II, mouse), with T cells comprising approximately 90%–95% of the isolated cells and a viability of around 95%. The isolated KCs and T cells were then used in co-culture experiments, with 2 mL of RPMI 1640 (HyCyte, China) medium containing 1∗106 T cells placed in the lower compartment of a 6-well cell culture chamber (LABSELECT #14112), and 1.5 mL of RPMI 1640 (HyCyte, China) medium containing 1∗106 KCs placed in the upper compartment. The co-culture experiments were divided into four groups: group 1: KCs from C57 liver and T cells from C57 spleen; group 2: KCs overexpressing APOE from C57 liver (Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 8000 (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s instructions.) and T cells from C3H spleen, group 3: KCs from C57 liver and T cells from C3H spleen, group 4: KCs with APOE gene knockout from C57 liver and T cells from C3H spleen. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted at 0h, 24h, 48h, and 72h post-co-culture.

ELISA and flow cytometry

Serum samples from donors (n = 31) preoperativelywere stored at −80°C until analysis. The concentration of APOE in the serum was determined using the Human Apoprotein-E ELISA Kit (Bioswamp, HM10860), following the manufacturer’s instructions for procedure.

The prepared single-cell suspensions of various tissues were subjected to cell identification and classification through flow cytometry. The cells were stained with various fluorescently conjugated antibodies, including those used to define KCs: anti-CD45, anti-CD11b, anti-F4/80, anti-Tim4, anti-CD86, anti-CD206, anti-APOE (Proteintech, CatNo. 66830-1-Ig), as well as those used to define T cells: anti-CD3, anti-CD4, anti-CD8, anti-CD69, anti-CD103, anti-PD1, anti-TIGIT. All other antibodies were obtained from BioLegend. The staining protocol was strictly followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After staining, the samples were analyzed on a flow cytometer (URIT BF-730 Flow Cytometer, URIT Medical Electronic Co.,Ltd). The staining results were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations in this study were conducted using R (version 4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism 9, with data presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were assessed with an unpaired Student’s t test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using Kruskal-Wallis H test. Statistical significance was indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
