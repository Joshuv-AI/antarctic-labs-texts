Dementia in older adults with schizophrenia: a 12-year analysis of prevalence, incidence, and treatment patterns in South Korea
Authors: Jung Su Park, Sangwan Kim, Donghoon Jeong, Jeong Pil Choi, Hyunsu Jeong, Alan Jung Park, Yong Sik Kim, Joongyub Lee, Se Hyun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlder adults with schizophrenia face a significantly elevated risk of dementia. However, recent trends remain unclear within South Korea’s rapidly aging schizophrenia population. This study aimed to quantify the burden of dementia in older schizophrenia patients by examining prevalence, incidence, dementia subtypes, pharmacologic treatment, and healthcare utilization. Using nationwide Health Insurance Review and Assessment (HIRA) data from 2010 to 2021, we identified 220,378 individuals aged ≥50 with schizophrenia. Dementia was diagnosed in 14.6% of patients, with 20.7% of cases occurring before age 60. Patients with dementia were more likely to be female and to have a higher comorbidity burden. Age-standardized all-cause dementia prevalence rose from 11.8% (2010) to 15.8% (2021) in those aged ≥50, and from 24.2% to 32.8% in those aged ≥65—exceeding estimates in the general population. In contrast, incidence declined from 3.4% to 1.7% over the same period. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) was the most prevalent subtype (12.2%), followed by vascular dementia (VD, 2.4%), and frontotemporal dementia (0.5%). A lower AD/VD prevalence ratio in schizophrenia (5.3 vs. 8.6 in the general population) suggests a relatively higher burden of vascular pathology. Cognitive enhancer prescriptions increased from 62% to 77% in patients with dementia, compared to 4% to 10% in those without dementia. In 2021, those with dementia were more likely to live in nursing homes (68% vs. 56%) and had greater annual increases in psychiatric hospitalization duration (+2.30 vs. +1.11 days/year). These findings underscore growing care demands and need for integrated treatment strategies for aging schizophrenia patients with dementia.Subject terms: Schizophrenia, Schizophrenia

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a chronic mental disorder that affects approximately 1% of the global population1. It is characterized by a wide range of symptoms, including positive symptoms (e.g., delusions and hallucinations), negative symptoms (e.g., anhedonia and social withdrawal), and cognitive dysfunction2. Cognitive deficits that affect memory, attention, and executive function can be particularly concerning, because these symptoms occur in up to 75% of those with schizophrenia3.

Emerging evidence suggests that individuals with schizophrenia exhibit accelerated aging processes, including earlier onset of cognitive decline compared to the general population4,5. In a comparative analysis, individuals with schizophrenia performed significantly worse on cognitive performance across domains (including working and episodic memory, executive function, and psychomotor speed) than psychiatrically healthy adults who were older on average; notably, even healthy adults over 70 outperformed the youngest schizophrenia patients (aged 40–49)6. This pattern is further supported by neuroimaging studies, which reveal accelerated brain aging patterns in schizophrenia patients relative to the general population7–9.

Recent studies have found that individuals with schizophrenia have a significantly higher risk of developing dementia than individuals in the general population. A meta-analysis of six studies from various countries noted this increased risk of dementia among people with schizophrenia, emphasizing the need for further relevant research10. Furthermore, a recent large-scale study that analyzed data from nine countries and over 13 million participants revealed that individuals with schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders have a 2.5-fold greater risk of developing dementia11.

Previously, Ku et al.12 reported a higher prevalence of dementia among patients with schizophrenia compared to the general population, based on National Health Insurance claims data. Building upon their findings, the present study extends the scope by analyzing nationwide claims data over a recent 12-year period (2010–2021), taking into account the recent growth in the older schizophrenia population13. In addition to identifying temporal trends in dementia prevalence and incidence among older adults with schizophrenia, this study also explores pharmacologic treatment patterns and healthcare utilization in this population. These additional outcomes are particularly important given previous findings that older schizophrenia patients may underutilize tertiary care and receive inadequate dementia treatment13.

This study addresses important gaps in the existing literature by providing updated data on the aging population with schizophrenia in South Korea, capturing recent demographic changes that have not been fully documented. It further extends the analysis to include pharmacologic treatment patterns and healthcare utilization—areas of particular concern given prior findings that older adults with schizophrenia may receive insufficient care. By integrating these aspects, the study offers a more comprehensive understanding of the evolving burden of dementia in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

In South Korea, the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) serves as the single public insurer, managing health insurance enrollment, premium collection, and benefit administration for nearly the entire population14. In parallel, the Health Insurance Review and Assessment (HIRA) Service is responsible for reviewing and evaluating the adequacy of healthcare services and associated costs14.

This study utilized data from the HIRA database, which comprises nationwide claims submitted by healthcare providers for reimbursement under South Korea’s National Health Insurance (NHI) program and the medical aid program. The NHI covers approximately 97% of the population, while the medical aid program covers most of the remaining 3%, together encompassing nearly the entire South Korean population14. The claims data include detailed information on healthcare utilization, including primary and secondary diagnoses (coded using the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases, ICD-10), prescription records, procedure codes, and laboratory or diagnostic tests. Additional variables include the type of care (inpatient or outpatient) and the type of healthcare institution (e.g., tertiary hospital, general hospital). All data were anonymized, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital, which confirmed that further review and informed consent were not required (IRB document 2411-108-1588).

Data were collected from 1 January 2007 to 31 December 2021, which corresponds to the recruitment period for this retrospective observational study. However, the actual study period was defined as 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2021, with a 3-year look-back period (2007–2009). The look-back period was included to ensure the eligibility of cohort patients by allowing sufficient time for diagnosis.

Participants

Study participants were defined annually from 2010 to 2021. For each year, individuals aged 50 years or older with a diagnosis of schizophrenia (ICD-10 code F20) were included in the study population. Schizophrenia diagnoses were identified based on having at least two outpatient visits or one inpatient admission with the diagnosis recorded as a primary or secondary condition to enhance diagnostic accuracy15,16, and at least one antipsychotic prescription, consistent with the criteria used in our previous study13. Diagnoses were limited to individuals whose initial diagnosis occurred between 15 and 99 years of age to minimize misclassification related to early-onset schizophrenia17.

Exclusion criteria included any prescription for cognitive enhancers before the first antipsychotic prescription, as well as any diagnosis of dementia prior to the first schizophrenia diagnosis. These criteria were implemented to reduce the potential for misdiagnosing dementia as schizophrenia. The medication codes for antipsychotics and cognitive enhancers are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Baseline characteristics

We investigated baseline characteristics—including age, sex, type of insurance, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score—at the time of each participant’s initial schizophrenia diagnosis. Although the inclusion criteria required all participants to be aged 50 years or older in each observation year, many individuals were initially diagnosed with schizophrenia before reaching 50 years of age. Accordingly, baseline characteristics were assessed at the time of initial diagnosis, which may have preceded cohort entry for some participants. Type of insurance was classified as health insurance, medical aid, or veteran status. CCI scores were calculated using the ICD-10 coding algorithm developed by Quan et al.18 to assess the burden of physical comorbidity.

Dementia defined

Dementia was defined as including AD, VD, FTD, LBD, dementia in other diseases classified elsewhere (Others), and NOS dementia. This comprehensive definition of dementia was based on a government-funded report by Kim et al.19, published by the National Institute of Dementia in collaboration with the Board of Audit and Inspection of Korea, which aimed to improve the national dementia management system in South Korea. The corresponding ICD-10 codes for these dementia subtypes are provided in Table 1.

Participants were defined as having dementia if they had this recorded as a primary or secondary diagnosis in at least three outpatient visits or one inpatient admission. The diagnostic criterion is based on a previous Korean nationwide epidemiological study using the NHIS database20. This requirement aims to increase diagnostic accuracy by reducing the risk of misclassification from isolated or tentative diagnoses in claims data.

While previous studies we cited have employed dementia medication prescriptions as part of the diagnostic criteria12,20, we intentionally excluded this from our case identification. This decision was made because the use of cognitive enhancers is a valuable outcome variable in itself, and including medication exposure in the case definition would confound this analysis.

Dementia prevalence and incidence

We calculated the crude annual prevalence and incidence of dementia among older adults with schizophrenia, and derived corresponding age-standardized rates to facilitate temporal and population-level comparisons. The detailed methodology for these calculations is presented in the Statistical analysis section.

For the main analyses (presented in Table 4), older adults were defined as individuals aged 50 years or older. Additionally, to facilitate comparison with dementia reports on the general population by aligning the age criteria, subgroup analyses were conducted restricted to individuals aged 65 years or older (presented in Table 5), providing a more comparable framework with national prevalence data and allowing exploration of age-related differences in dementia epidemiology among patients with schizophrenia.

For comparison, we obtained data on dementia prevalence in the general population aged 65 years or older from the National Institute of Dementia’s reports21–27. Specifically, all-cause dementia prevalence data were available for the entire 2010–2021 period. However, data on prevalence by dementia subtype were limited to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and vascular dementia (VD), and reported only for the 2016–2020 period; therefore, we present subtype distribution comparisons exclusively for these years. These prevalence estimates were derived from nationwide epidemiological surveys employing age-, sex-, and region-stratified sampling28. Dementia diagnoses in the general population were established through a two-stage process involving standardized cognitive screening with the MMSE-DS, followed by clinical assessments and confirmatory testing in selected participants. Because this survey-based, clinically confirmed diagnostic approach differs substantially from our claims-based definition relying on diagnostic codes, direct statistical comparisons were not performed; these data are presented for descriptive purposes only.

For all-cause dementia prevalence, each individual was counted as a single case regardless of the presence of multiple subtypes. By contrast, when calculating subtype-specific prevalence, individuals with multiple subtypes were counted separately for each applicable subtype. Similarly, the incidence of each subtype was counted as a new case even if the participant had previously been diagnosed with another subtype. This approach facilitates a comprehensive assessment of overlapping dementia subtypes within the study population.

Cognitive enhancer prescription rates

We evaluated the annual rates of cognitive enhancer prescriptions among participants, stratified by dementia status. A participant was considered to have received a cognitive enhancer prescription if they had one or more prescriptions for cognitive enhancers during the observation year. The list of drug codes for cognitive enhancers was identical to that used in the exclusion criteria for defining study participants and the relevant information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Durations of hospitalization and healthcare utilization patterns

We assessed the annual durations of psychiatric hospitalization and the types of medical facilities where participants were admitted, stratified by dementia status. Psychiatric hospitalization was defined as any hospitalization with a mental disorder (ICD-10 codes starting with F) listed as either the primary or secondary diagnosis. The annual duration of hospitalization was calculated as the total number of days in hospital within each year.

Healthcare facilities were categorized according to their capacities based on classifications from the Korean National Health Insurance system: tertiary/general hospitals (>99 beds), hospitals (30–99 beds) and nursing homes, and clinics (<30 beds). Tertiary hospital status is designated by the Ministry of Health and Welfare when a general hospital meets specific criteria. The annual utilization rate for each facility type was calculated as the proportion of subjects hospitalized at least once in that type of facility out of all subjects hospitalized during the year.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics for dementia and non-dementia groups were compared using appropriate statistical tests: categorical variables were analyzed with chi-square tests, and continuous variables were examined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, owing to non-normal distribution confirmed by normality tests (see Supplementary Table 2).

Crude prevalence was calculated as the number of older adults with schizophrenia who had been diagnosed with dementia before December 31 of the given year, divided by the total number of older adults with schizophrenia identified in that year.

Age-standardized prevalence was calculated by applying the direct standardization method using the 2020 Resident Registration Central Population from Statistics Korea29 as the standard population. This dataset includes population counts in 5-year age intervals, enabling accurate standardization. The formula is as follows:

Ei: Expected number of cases in age group i in the standard population, based on the age-specific rates observed in the schizophrenia population.ei: Number of observed dementia cases in age group i among older adults with schizophrenia aged 50 or olderpi: Population of older adults with schizophrenia in age group iPi: Number of individuals in age group i in the standard population

Similarly, standardized incidence was calculated using the same approach, with incident dementia cases as the numerator, where applicable.

To analyze changes over time within each subgroup (dementia and non-dementia), we conducted separate regression analyses for continuous and binary outcome variables. The independent variable in all models was year, treated as a continuous variable representing the calendar year from 2010 to 2021. For continuous outcomes, such as the annual duration of psychiatric hospitalization (measured in days), the dependent variable represented the specific annual measure for each subgroup. For binary outcomes, including cognitive enhancer prescriptions (yes/no) and healthcare facility utilization (yes/no) within the year, the dependent variables were binary indicators denoting the presence or absence of the outcome.

To account for repeated measurements (2010–2021) and clustering by dementia status, we employed mixed effects models. For continuous outcomes, such as the annual duration of psychiatric hospitalization (measured in days), we used linear mixed models (LMMs) with an autoregressive covariance structure to evaluate both temporal trends and subgroup differences. In these analyses, the primary independent variables included year (as a continuous variable), dementia status (as a categorical variable: dementia vs. non-dementia), and their interaction term (year*dementia status). This allowed us to assess (1) overall change in annual hospitalization duration over time (temporal trend), (2) differences between dementia and non-dementia groups (subgroup difference), and (3) whether the temporal trend differed by dementia status (group-by-time interaction). For binary outcomes, such as prescription of cognitive enhancers and healthcare facility utilization, mixed effects logistic regression models were applied analogously.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise software (ver. 7.1; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics

Table 2 shows the baseline characteristics of the study population. During the period from 2010 to 2021, there were 220,378 older adults with schizophrenia in the study population. Among these, 32,071 (14.6%) were diagnosed with dementia, whereas 188,307 (85.4%) were not. Adults in the dementia group were older on average than those in the non-dementia group (62.3 vs. 50.7 years, p < 0.001). The proportion of females was higher in the dementia group (55.8% vs. 51.9%, p < 0.001). Adults with dementia were less likely to have health insurance (57.1% vs. 62.7%) and more likely to receive medical aid (42.5% vs. 37.2%, p < 0.001). The CCI scores were significantly higher in the dementia group (mean: 2.1 vs. 1.5, p < 0.001), with a greater proportion of adults having a CCI score ≥ 4 (20.3% vs. 12.5%). Given the large sample size, statistically significant differences can arise even for relatively minor differences between groups. Accordingly, interpretations should consider clinical relevance in addition to statistical significance when evaluating these findings.

Age at dementia diagnosis and distribution of dementia subtypes

Table 3 shows the age distribution at the time of first dementia diagnosis among older adults with schizophrenia. Notably, 20.7% of dementia diagnoses occurred before the age of 60 years, with 3.0% diagnosed before 50 years of age and 17.7% between the ages of 50 and 59 years. The majority of diagnoses were made for individuals between the ages of 60 and 79 years, with 36.3% aged 60–69 years and 30.9% aged 70–79 years. A smaller proportion of adults were diagnosed at older ages, with 10.8% aged 80–89 years, 1.3% aged 90–99 years, and < 0.1% over 100 years of age.

Table 4 shows the distribution of dementia subtypes among the older adults. AD was the most common subtype (85.5% of cases), followed by VD (15.7%). Less frequent subtypes included FTD (4.0%) and Parkinson’s disease-related dementia (2.9%). LBD was observed in only 0.7% of cases. Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, Huntington’s disease, and human immunodeficiency virus-related dementia were very rare subtypes that were observed in only a few cases. In addition, 8.9% of adults were classified as having unspecified dementia.

Prevalence and incidence of all-cause dementia

Table 5 shows the annual prevalence and incidence of all-cause dementia among older adults with schizophrenia in South Korea from 2010 to 2021. The crude prevalence of dementia in adults with schizophrenia increased steadily from 7.0% in 2010 to 12.0% in 2021 (p < 0.001). Similarly, the standardized prevalence, adjusted for age, showed a significant increase from 11.8% to 15.8% over the same period (p < 0.001). The crude incidence of dementia among older adults with schizophrenia decreased slightly from 2.2% in 2010 to 1.5% in 2021 (p < 0.001), with a corresponding decline in standardized incidence from 3.4% to 1.7% (p < 0.001).

Table 6 compares the prevalence of dementia in older adults with schizophrenia aged 65 years or older with that in the general population aged 65 years or older. The standardized prevalence of dementia among adults with schizophrenia in this age group significantly increased from 24.2% in 2010 to 32.8% in 2021 (p < 0.001), whereas the estimated prevalence of dementia in the general population aged 65 years or older increased from 8.8% to 10.3% during the same period (p < 0.001). Across the entire study period from 2010 to 2021, the pooled standardized prevalence of all-cause dementia among older adults with schizophrenia was 31.2%, whereas the pooled estimated prevalence among the general South Korean population aged 65 years or older was 9.8%.

Discussion

In this nationwide, population-based study spanning 12 years, we comprehensively investigated the prevalence, incidence, and treatment patterns of dementia among older adults with schizophrenia in South Korea. Our analysis demonstrated a considerable and sustained increase in dementia prevalence within this population over the study period. In contrast, the crude and age-standardized incidence rates of dementia showed a modest but statistically significant decline. We also observed a distinctive distribution of dementia subtypes, characterized by a markedly lower AD/VD ratio, suggesting a relatively greater burden of VD among individuals with schizophrenia. These trends were paralleled by increased use of cognitive enhancers and greater reliance on long-term care facilities. Collectively, these findings emphasize the unique and evolving clinical challenges faced by older individuals with schizophrenia and highlight the urgent need for tailored, integrated care strategies.

Approximately one-fifth (20.7%) of dementia cases among individuals with schizophrenia were first diagnosed before 60 years of age. This is disproportionately high compared to the general population in South Korea, in which only 3.4% of dementia cases occur in individuals under 60 years and 8.3% in those under 65 years of age as reported in 202127. This indicates that the prevalence of early-onset dementia diagnoses is up to six-fold higher in adults with schizophrenia than in the general population. This disparity is consistent with emerging evidence of accelerated aging characterized by premature cognitive decline among individuals with schizophrenia4,5. Neurobiological studies further support this conclusion, reporting poorer cognitive test performance6 and more neuroimaging markers of accelerated brain aging7–9 in older adults with schizophrenia compared to age-matched controls.

The dementia group included a higher proportion of females (55.8% vs. 51.9%), which is consistent with previous studies10,12. Women with schizophrenia have a longer life expectancy than men30,31: this may result in prolonged exposure to dementia risk factors and a greater cumulative risk of dementia. In addition, adults with schizophrenia and dementia had more comorbidities than those without dementia, as reflected by a higher mean CCI score (2.1 vs. 1.5). Higher CCI scores32 and specific multimorbidity clusters (e.g., cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, and neuropsychiatric conditions)33 are independently associated with an increased incidence of dementia. Therefore, women and adults with physical comorbidities may be more at risk.

Over the study period, the standardized prevalence of all-cause dementia increased from 11.8% in 2010 to 15.8% in 2021, whereas the standardized incidence decreased from 3.4% to 1.7%. Among dementia subtypes, AD consistently showed the highest prevalence (12.2%), followed by VD (2.4%). The prevalence of AD, VD, FTD, and LBD increased over time, whereas the incidence of these subtypes remained relatively stable or showed a declining trend.

Although the prevalence of dementia among adults with schizophrenia has continued to rise, probably reflecting both increased survival and the cumulative effects of aging34,35, the incidence of new dementia cases has decreased over time. This encouraging trend suggests that, despite the growing number of older adults living with both schizophrenia and dementia, the risk of developing dementia among older adults with schizophrenia may be decreasing. Such a pattern is consistent with observations in other high-income countries, where improvements in education, healthcare access, and vascular risk management have contributed to declining dementia incidence, despite overall increases in prevalence due to older populations and those with the disease living longer36,37. Furthermore, it is possible that advances specific to the care of individuals with schizophrenia—such as the greater use of second-generation antipsychotics38 and the expansion of community-based treatment options39,40—may have contributed to the declining incidence of dementia in this population. These findings offer grounds for cautious optimism that ongoing public health efforts and better management of modifiable risk factors may be reducing the burden of new dementia cases among older adults with schizophrenia.

We also compared the prevalence of dementia in this group with that of the South Korean general population by restricting our analysis to individuals aged 65 years or older. Over the entire study period (2010–2021), the pooled standardized prevalence of all-cause dementia in adults with schizophrenia aged 65 years or older was 31.2%, far exceeding the estimated prevalence of 9.8% in the general population of equivalent age. Regarding dementia subtypes, we compared the prevalence of AD and VD between adults with schizophrenia and the general population from 2016 to 2020. AD prevalence was much higher in adults with schizophrenia (28.6%) than in the general population (7.6%). Similarly, VD prevalence was much higher in adults with schizophrenia (5.4%) than in the general population (0.9%).

The rapidly aging South Korean population and nationwide public health programs have probably contributed to the increase in the AD/VD ratio in the general population41. In our study, the overall decline in dementia incidence among adults with schizophrenia may reflect improvements in life expectancy and management of vascular risk factors in this population. However, the significantly lower AD/VD ratio observed in adults with schizophrenia relative to the general population indicates that these benefits may still be insufficient. Adults with schizophrenia continue to experience shorter life expectancies and suboptimal control of vascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension and diabetes) compared to the general population42,43. These observations highlight the need for further research to investigate the interplay between vascular risk profiles, premature mortality, and dementia subtypes in adults with schizophrenia. Although prevalence values from the general population and schizophrenia groups are presented side by side, direct statistical comparisons were not performed, and caution should be exercised in interpreting these findings beyond descriptive purposes.

Beyond epidemiological trends, our study also examined treatment and care patterns. The prescription rate of cognitive enhancers significantly increased for both dementia and non-dementia groups during the study period. Among adults with schizophrenia and dementia, cognitive enhancer prescription rates increased from 62% in 2010 to 77% in 2021, whereas prescription rates for those without dementia increased from 4% to 10% over the same period. Adults with schizophrenia and dementia were consistently linked to higher prescription rates than those without dementia, reflecting active pharmacological management for cognitive symptoms.

Data on the proportion of adults with dementia in the general South Korean population being prescribed cognitive enhancers remain limited. However, a study utilizing the National Health Insurance Service Database reported that 108,596 of 118,439 adults newly diagnosed with dementia between 2003 and 2018 had a history of anti-dementia drug prescriptions44. This suggests that approximately 92% of diagnosed adults with dementia in South Korea received pharmacological treatment during this period. Therefore, although adults with schizophrenia and dementia do receive active pharmacological treatment, their prescription rates remain lower than those of individuals with dementia but not schizophrenia in the general population. This finding highlights the need for further efforts to ensure that individuals with schizophrenia and dementia have equitable access to optimal dementia care.

Increasingly, cognitive enhancers have been used to treat adults with schizophrenia but not dementia. However, several small studies have failed to show that individuals with schizophrenia experience beneficial cognitive effects after treatment with donepezil, galantamine, or memantine45–51. On the other hand, the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist memantine may alleviate symptoms of schizophrenia when used as an adjunct to antipsychotics49,52,53. Large-scale studies with validated cognitive measures are warranted, and cognitive enhancers could be used to augment antipsychotics.

The duration of psychiatric hospitalization also showed notable trends. Although adults with schizophrenia and dementia initially spent less time in hospital than those without dementia (217.7 days vs. 235.1 days in 2010), their time spent in hospital had increased sharply, reaching 252.0 days in the dementia group and 254.6 days in the non-dementia group, by 2021. This highlights the growing care demands of adults with schizophrenia and dementia as these conditions progress.

In terms of healthcare utilization patterns, both groups showed a decline in hospital (mainly psychiatric hospital) utilization rates and an increase in nursing home admissions over time. For adults with schizophrenia and dementia, nursing home utilization increased from 39% in 2010 to 68% in 2021, whereas hospital utilization decreased from 42% to 22%. Similar trends were observed among those without dementia, but at different magnitudes. These trends reflect a transition toward long-term care settings for managing adults with schizophrenia as they age and develop comorbid conditions such as dementia.

The findings of this study align with previous research from various countries, demonstrating an elevated risk of dementia among individuals with schizophrenia. A large-scale Medicare study conducted in the United States showed that by the age of 66 years, the prevalence of diagnosed dementia in adults with schizophrenia was 27.9%, compared to 1.3% in those with no serious mental illness54. In Denmark, a population-based cohort study revealed that adults with schizophrenia had approximately twice the risk of developing dementia compared to adults in the general population55. Similarly, a Taiwanese cohort study found a two-fold increase in the risk of dementia when individuals already had schizophrenia56.

The increased risk of dementia among adults with schizophrenia may be attributable to various mechanisms. First, cognitive reserve, which describes the brain’s resilience to neuropathology, may be considerably decreased by schizophrenia due to early-life cognitive deficits and accelerated brain aging57. Second, lifestyle factors that are frequently associated with schizophrenia, such as smoking, sedentary behavior, and poor cardiovascular health, can exacerbate systemic inflammation and vascular risks, increasing the likelihood of dementia58,59. Third, antipsychotic medications may directly or indirectly influence dementia risk via long-term metabolic side effects (e.g., diabetes and dyslipidemia) or anticholinergic activity, which impairs cognitive function60,61.

Dementia in adults with schizophrenia may be caused by independent neurodegenerative processes or it may stem from the natural progression of schizophrenia itself. A recent meta-analysis found no elevated AD-related pathologies, such as amyloid-β plaques or neurofibrillary tangles, in cognitively impaired adults with schizophrenia compared to controls62. Conversely, neuroimaging studies have demonstrated accelerated hippocampal atrophy and cerebral white matter deficits in adults with schizophrenia as they get older, and these structural changes correlated with cognitive decline8,9. These findings suggest that dementia in individuals with schizophrenia probably has a unique etiology that is distinct from typical neurodegenerative pathways, highlighting the need for tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for this condition.

This study had several strengths. First, it builds on previous South Korean research on dementia risk among adults with schizophrenia by taking the impact of ongoing population aging into account12,13. The extensive 12-year observation period (2010–2021) enabled us to capture both long-term trends and recent developments, providing up-to-date insights into the status of dementia in older adults with schizophrenia. By classifying dementia into six distinct subtypes, our analysis provides a comprehensive assessment of the epidemiological patterns associated with each subtype. Furthermore, we examined how treatment patterns, including cognitive enhancers and healthcare utilization, differ according to dementia status, offering valuable insights into clinical management. These comprehensive and up-to-date observations provide a basis for developing tailored and effective treatment strategies.

Despite these strengths, this study had several limitations. First, diagnostic accuracy may be somewhat limited, because claims data are collected primarily for administrative rather than research purposes. To counteract this problem, we improved diagnostic validity by requiring multiple medical visits and medication prescriptions, and by excluding individuals with dementia diagnoses preceding the onset of schizophrenia. Second, our study cohort included only adults with schizophrenia, which precluded direct comparison with a general population control group. However, we estimated the prevalence of dementia in the general population using data reported by the National Institute of Dementia and compared the relevant rates with those observed in our schizophrenia cohort. Third, our regression models did not account for potential confounding factors such as specific medication use, physical health conditions, and socioeconomic status, which may have influenced the observed associations. Future studies should incorporate a general population control group and adjust for a broader range of confounding variables to clarify further the relationship between schizophrenia and dementia risk. Moreover, the large sample size increases the likelihood of detecting statistically significant differences, which may not always translate into clinically meaningful effects. Therefore, careful consideration of clinical significance is warranted when interpreting our results.

To address these growing and complex care needs, we recommend implementing integrated, multidisciplinary care models that involve psychiatry, geriatrics, and social work. Routine cognitive and psychiatric assessments, personalized pharmacologic treatment with careful monitoring of antipsychotic side effects, and the development of specialized cognitive rehabilitation programs are crucial. Strengthening caregiver education and enhancing community-based support and care transitions are also important for optimizing outcomes in this population. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of these comprehensive approaches.

Conclusions

We investigated dementia prevalence, incidence, and treatment among older adults with schizophrenia over a 12-year period in South Korea. Dementia prevalence increased steadily, with AD and VD being the most common subtypes; conversely, the incidence of new dementia cases showed a modest decline over time. The prevalence of dementia in adults with schizophrenia was much higher than in the general population, reflecting the increased vulnerability and accelerated aging processes associated with this group. During the study period, prescription rates for cognitive enhancers significantly increased and healthcare utilization shifted toward long-term care settings, such as nursing homes. These findings highlight a pressing need for tailored interventions to address the unique challenges faced by this high-risk group. Future studies should focus on improving diagnostic accuracy and developing effective strategies to manage dementia in older adults with schizophrenia, thereby leading to better care outcomes.
