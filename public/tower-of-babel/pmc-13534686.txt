Correlation of thalamic functional organization disturbances and genetic architecture in motor subtypes of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Shuting Bu, Huize Pang, Xiaolu Li, Yu Liu, Mengwan Zhao, Juzhou Wang, Lina Huang, Qin Niu, Le Liang, Hongmei Yu, Guoguang Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe thalamus is a critical relay center in motor circuit and plays a key role in the pathophysiology of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Hierarchical organization is a fundamental principle underlying human brain networks. However, how thalamic functional gradient relates to PD motor symptoms and their genetic modulation remains unclear. Here, we employed dimensional reduction techniques on thalamocortical functional connectome in two independent cohorts and derived lateral-medial and anterior-posterior thalamic functional axes. Dataset 1 included 46 healthy controls (HC), 72 postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) patients, and 71 tremor-dominant (TD) patients. Dataset 2 comprised 118 HC, 99 PIGD patients, and 101 TD patients. We observed expansions in TD subtype and contractions in PIGD subtype along lateral/medial principal axis, as well as contractions along anterior/posterior secondary axis in both two subtypes, reflecting potentially altered thalamocortical interactions across unimodal and transmodal networks. Thalamic network gradient analysis revealed widespread network-level gradient alterations in both PIGD and TD subtypes. Notably, ventral attention network gradients within the first two gradients were negatively correlated with motor impairment in both subtypes. Transcription-neuroimaging association analyses using Allen Human Brain Atlas data identified subtype-specific genes associated with thalamic gradient alterations, which were enriched in distinct biological processes and overexpressed in developmental periods as early as “early fetal” stage. These findings characterize alterations in thalamocortical functional organization in PD motor subtypes and provide a multiscale description linking thalamic functional gradients with gene expression patterns.Subject terms: Parkinson's disease, Neural decoding, Motor control

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, characterized by bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor1. Based on motor symptoms, PD can be divided into three categories: tremor-dominant (TD), postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD), and mixed (MIX) subtype2. Notably, the PIGD subtype is associated with a poorer prognosis, faster disease progression, and a higher incidence of non-motor symptoms, including dementia, depression, and olfactory dysfunction3. Despite the distinct prognostic differences among these subtypes, the underlying pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. As a vital relay hub in the motor circuitry of the brain, the thalamus is thought to play a key role in the pathophysiology of PD4.

The thalamus, a paired diencephalic structure, is fundamental to cortical function. It transmits sensory information from peripheral organs to the cortex and regulates various sensory information processes, including somatosensory, visual, and auditory. Moreover, the thalamus as a diverse hub is involved in a wide range of executive functions such as attention, behavioral flexibility, and goal-directed behavior5–8. A well-established model9 of classic PD tremor consists of two components: a tremor trigger originating from the basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuit and a tremor-driving force stemming from the cerebellothalamocortical network. As the central hub connecting these two circuits, the thalamus has been a primary target for deep brain stimulation and surgical interventions aimed at alleviating tremor symptoms in PD10. Beyond tremor regulation, the thalamus also plays a pivotal role in gait control. Disruptions in thalamic neural activity and functional connections have been implicated in the development of freezing of gait (FOG)11. These studies underscore the involvement of thalamus in the pathophysiology of PD and revive interest in the role of the thalamus in PD.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a fast and non-invasive imaging technique that allows for the investigation of large-scale functional networks across the entire brain. Given the hypothesis that PD motor symptoms arise from distinct neuronal mechanisms, several fMRI studies have explored thalamic functional alterations in PD motor subtypes with dividing thalamus into nuclei according to specific atlas. Chen et al12 found that TD patients exhibited higher functional connectivity (FC) between thalamic subregions and several cortical areas, including the left middle temporal gyrus (MTG), the right dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, the left middle occipital gyrus, and the right superior temporal gyrus, compared to the PIGD subtype. And altered FC between the thalamus and the MTG was identified as a potential biomarker for distinguishing PD motor subtypes. Similarly, Bu et al.13 set the bilateral premotor thalamus (mPMtha) as seed regions, and found that the TD subtype exhibited increased FC between the bilateral mPMtha and the left middle occipital gyrus as well as left inferior parietal lobule (IPL). In contrast, the PIGD subtype demonstrated decreased FC between the right mPMtha and precentral gyrus, supramarginal, IPL, and superior parietal lobule. Moreover, the FC between the bilateral mPMtha and these identified regions were significantly correlated with motor performance scores in PD patients. However, parcellation-based studies mentioned above need to define discrete cortical networks or delineate rapidly changing thalamic boundaries; despite substantial overlap in cortical innervation within the thalamus14. Furthermore, thalamic nuclei are projected to multiple cortical regions15, and cytoarchitectural variations within/between nuclei suggests that conventional parcellation methods may provide an incomplete understanding of its role in thalamocortical organization.

Recently, dimension reduction techniques have emerged as a promising approach for holistic representations of brain connectivity. These advanced data-driven methods decompose the high-dimensional connectome into a series of low dimensional axes, capturing spatial gradients of connectivity variations16,17. To date, only a few researches have employed such techniques to investigate cerebral hierarchy of PD18–20, and no research has specifically examined thalamic gradient alterations in PD. Given the potential involvement of the thalamus in PD motor syndromes, exploring alterations in thalamic functional hierarchies may provide novel insights into disrupted thalamocortical organization in PD. Additionally, genetic factors are increasingly recognized as one of the most critical contributors in understanding the pathological mechanism and pathogenesis of PD21–23. In this context, transcriptome-connectome association analysis has emerged as a powerful approach to link brain connectome and transcriptome24. This method has been widely applied to explore the potential molecular and genetic mechanisms underlying MRI-derived connectome alterations in various neuropsychiatric disorders, including unipolar disorder and bipolar disorder25, major depression26, and schizophrenia27.

In this study, we utilized a local cohort of individuals with different PD motor subtypes to examine whether macroscale thalamic functional organization is disrupted in PD. Additionally, we employed a cohort from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) database to validate our findings. Finally, we applied transcriptome-connectome association analysis to explore genes associated with thalamic gradient alterations in PD motor subtypes. Results

Demographics and clinical data

A total of 189 participants were included in dataset 1 following the exclusion of cases with MIX subtype, excessive head motion artifacts, and incomplete clinical data, which included 46 healthy controls (HC) and 143 PD patients (72 PIGD subtype and 71 TD subtype). No significant differences were observed in age and gender among the PIGD, TD, and HC groups. Furthermore, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) scores did not differ between the PIGD and TD subtypes. However, years of education were significantly lower in the TD subtype compared to the HC and PIGD groups, whereas no significant differences were found between the HC and PIGD groups. Additionally, Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD), disease duration, H&Y stage, Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) II scores, UPDRS III scores, and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores were significantly lower in the TD subtype compared to the PIGD subtype (Table 1). Macroscale Thalamic Gradients in PD motor subtypes

The principal gradient (G1) revealed a L-M axis (Fig. 2A), while the second gradient (G2) described an A-P axis (Fig. 2B). These gradients exhibited a high degree of similarity across both the discovery and validation datasets (Fig. S1). To enhance interpretability, we focused on the first two gradients, in line with prior studies16,28,29, as they accounted for over 10% of the variance in the overall FC data (Fig. S2) for a good trade-off between few components whilst retaining a large amount of variance. Compared to HC, TD subtype patients exhibited an expansion in G1, whereas PIGD subtype patients demonstrated a contraction (Fig. 2A), these findings were consistently replicated in the validation dataset (Fig. S3). We also observed contractions at both anchors of G2 in both TD and PIGD subtype patients, with PIGD patients exhibiting more pronounced shrinkage (Fig. 2B). Of note, no significant differences were found in the range, variability, or explanatory rate of G1 and G2 among the three groups (GLM analysis controlling for gender, age, years of education, and LEDD, with Bonferroni correction) (Table S3). By integrating G1 and G2 axes, we calculated an eccentricity score, defined as the square root of the Euclidean distance from each thalamic voxel to the center of mass within the two-dimensional gradient space30. However, no significantly differences in global eccentricity were observed among the three groups (Fig. 2C). In line with thalamocortical gradient results in dataset 1, dataset 2 also showed no significant differences in global eccentricity (Fig. S3). Of note, the first two gradients exhibited highly consistency across different thresholds in both two datasets, see Table S4 and S5.

Functional relevance

To investigate functional alterations in the thalamus across different PD motor subtypes, we employed a combination of the winner-take-all method to distribute thalamic voxels into six large-scale functional networks. Group comparisons using GLM revealed significant differences in multiple networks for both G1 and G2. Specifically, for G1, the visual (VN), sensorimotor (SMN), dorsal attention (DAN), ventral attention (VAN), and default mode (DMN) networks exhibited significant differences among the TD, PIGD, and HC groups. Similarly, for G2, the SMN, DAN, VAN, frontoparietal network (FPN) and DMN demonstrated significant group differences (Fig. 3, Bonferroni correction, p < 0.05/3). Group comparisons using GLM revealed significant differences in multiple networks for both G1 and G2. Specifically, for G1, the VN, SMN, DAN, VAN, and DMN networks exhibited significant differences among the TD, PIGD, and HC groups (p = 0.012, F = 4.510 for the VN; p < 0.001, F = 24.562 for the SMN; p < 0.001, F = 31.540 for the DAN; p < 0.001, F = 123.936 for the VAN; p < 0.001, F = 68.024 for the DMN; Bonferroni’s correction, respectively). Similarly, for G2, the SMN, DAN, VAN, frontoparietal network (FPN) and DMN demonstrated significant group differences (p < 0.001, F = 15.816 for the SMN; p < 0.001, F = 9.153 for the DAN; p < 0.001, F = 16.943 for the VAN; p < 0.001, F = 12.012 for the FPN; p = 0.04, F = 5.145 for the DMN; Fig. 3, Bonferroni’s correction, respectively). As illustrated in Fig. 3, within G1, the DMN gradient values were markedly reduced in both PIGD and TD subtypes compared to HC (p < 0.001; p < 0.001; Bonferroni’s correction, respectively), whereas the VAN gradient was significantly elevated in both PD subtypes (p < 0.001, p < 0.001; Bonferroni’s correction, respectively). Additionally, the TD subtype exhibited significantly higher VN and SMN gradient values (p = 0.013, p < 0.001; Bonferroni’s correction) compared to the PIGD group, whereas the PIGD subtype showed a significantly higher DAN gradient compared to the TD group (p < 0.001; Bonferroni’s correction).

Genes associated with thalamic gradient alterations

We conducted a spatial correlation analysis to examine the relationship between alterations in thalamic gradients across PD motor subtypes and gene expression using transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA). Partial least squares (PLS) regression was employed to elucidate the spatial correspondence between hierarchical thalamic alterations and transcriptional activity. In the PLS analysis, the first component (PLS1) represents the spatial map that accounted for the largest (13.16% in G1 and 12.55% in G2 of PIGD group; 12.71% in G1 and 13.20% in G2 of TD group) proportion of gene expression variance across cortical areas (Pspin<0.0001). Notably, the PLS1 weighted gene expression map showed a significant spatial correlation with the case-control t-map (all Pearson’s r ≥ 0.15; Pspin≤0.005). In the comparison between HC and PIGD subtype, we identified 121 PLS1+ and 178 PLS1- genes in G1, 42 PLS1+ and 403 PLS1- genes in G2, respectively. For the HC versus TD subtype comparison, 427 PLS1+ and 270 PLS1- genes were detected in G1, whereas 87 PLS1+ and 40 PLS1- genes were identified in G2. In the direct comparison between the PIGD and TD subtypes, we observed 87 PLS1+ and 285 PLS1- genes in G1, 174 PLS1+ and 164 PLS1- genes in G2, respectively (PLS1 + , Z>5; PLS1-, Z < -5; all pFDR <0.005) (Fig. 4). Detailed results of transcription-neuroimaging association analysis in dataset 2 were shown in Supplementary materials (Fig. S7).

Enrichment analyses

We conducted enrichment analyses to delineate the biological functions of genes robustly associated with thalamic gradient alterations across different PD motor subtypes. For the comparison between HC and PIGD groups, genes negatively associated with G1 alterations were enriched in oxidative stress responses pathways. Conversely, positively associated genes were enriched in ion transport. Regarding thalamic G2 alterations, negatively related genes were primarily involved in mitochondrial function, whereas positively related genes were linked to genomic stability. For the comparison between HC and TD groups, genes negatively correlated with G1 alterations were significantly enriched in synaptic transmission pathways. In contrast, positively related genes were enriched in vesicular transport and protein processing pathways. Similarly, genes negatively associated with G2 alterations were enriched in extracellular matrix-related pathways. Meanwhile, positively related genes were enriched in mitochondrial stress responses and neuronal differentiation. In the comparison between PIGD and TD subtypes, genes negatively associated with G1 alterations were enriched in “amide metabolic process,” while positively related genes were linked to synaptic organization. For G2, negatively related genes were enriched in vascular development pathways. In contrast, positively associated genes were enriched in synaptic vesicle transport (the biological processes of PLS1- genes were presented in Fig. 5, while those of PLS1+ genes were showed Fig. S8; p < 0.05; FDR-BH corrected).

Clinical relevance

In PIGD group, PIGD scores were negatively correlated with the VAN gradient value of G1 (r = –0.258, p = 0.034, FDR correction). Additionally, VN gradient values of G1 were negatively correlated with HY stage (r = –0.308, p = 0.011, FDR correction). In the TD subtype group, years of disease duration demonstrated a significant positive correlation with DAN gradient values of G2 (r = 0.450, p < 0.001; FDR correction), whereas UPDRS3 scores were negatively correlated with VAN gradient values of G2 (r = –0.250, p = 0.041, FDR correction) (Fig. S10).

Discussion

In this multi-center systematic investigation of functional specialization along the thalamus axis in PD with distinct motor symptoms, we employed dimensionality reduction techniques to quantitatively characterize thalamic functional organization in a data-driven manner. Our study found contractions in PIGD subtype and expansions in TD subtype along L-M principal axis, as well as contractions along A-P secondary axis of thalamic hierarchies in both subtypes. These findings suggest that connectivity profiles between both anchors showed higher similarities in PIGD patients compared to TD subtype. Network-level analyses revealed distinct thalamic gradient patterns associated with different PD motor symptoms. Furthermore, transcriptional analyses linked to thalamic gradient variations identified subtype-specific gene expression patterns, enriched in distinct biological processes and developmental periods. Collectively, these findings provide a systematic characterization of thalamic functional organization in PD motor subtypes and its associated gene expression profiles.

Examination of whole-brain cortical projection patterns demonstrated that the principal thalamic gradients observed in our study closely recapitulated those reported in prior work31–33, underscoring the robustness of thalamic macroscale organization. Specifically, the G1 delineated a continuous L-M transition extending from the ventral lateral nuclei toward the anterior and pulvinar groups, whereas the G2 captured an A-P axis spanning the anterior nuclei and the pulvinar. These organizational axes are not arbitrary but reflect fundamental anatomical constraints. The L-M axis has been linked to the spatial distribution of gray matter morphology, reflecting variations in neural mass density, while the A-P axis is more strongly governed by the intrinsic geometric architecture of the thalamus33. Within this organizational framework, PD motor subtypes exhibited a directional reconfiguration of thalamic gradients, with opposing shifts along the L-M axis and a shared contraction along the A-P axis. These functional gradient alterations are highly consistent with previous structural imaging findings, which have reported increased gray matter volume along the lateral thalamic border in TD patients34 and greater vulnerability to thalamic microstructural damage in PIGD patients35. The concordance between structural and functional observations suggests a close coupling between thalamic structure and functional hierarchy, whereby subtype-specific structural substrates may bias the distribution of functional states along the L-M axis. As a critical integrative hub, the human thalamus connects extensively with cortical networks, playing a key role in both cognitive and motor functions. Cortical projections pass through thalamic subfields before reaching their targets, where they form synapses and cluster with cortical-projecting neurons36. Their neurons were relatively homogenously organized within the thalamus37, which may account for the gradual transitions observed in functional gradients. This organizational principle likely underpins the gradual functional gradients patterns observed here and parallels the unimodal to transmodal hierarchy described in the cortex19,38. Notably, the preservation of overall gradient architecture in PD, as evidenced by comparable gradient range, variability, and eccentricity across groups, suggests that thalamic functional hierarchies remain largely intact, despite subtype-specific reweighting of connectivity along principal and secondary organizational axes.

Unimodal and transmodal networks in our study were inherited from the cortical terminology by mapping the cortical 6-network parcellation into the thalamus. Network-level analyses further demonstrated that PIGD patients exhibited less dissociation in the VN, SMN and VAN within G1 compared to TD patients. A decreased separation along the gradient implies increased similarity in connectivity profiles and greater integration between regions, potentially indicating abnormal interactions between them. The VAN detects unexpected stimuli and facilitates attentional shifts39. Impaired VAN function may deprive the basal ganglia of critical sensory information, further exacerbating dopamine deficiencies40,41. This dysfunction could weaken basal ganglia inhibitory control, increasing reliance on external “drives”, ultimately contributing to freezing behavior42. This mechanism is particularly relevant to the PIGD subtype, which is predominantly associated with dysfunction of the striato-thalamo-cortical (STC) circuit34 and is characterized by more severe motor impairment than TD subtype2. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed a significant negative association between VAN gradient values of G1 and PIGD scores, suggesting that progressive attenuation of VAN differentiation is linked to worsening postural and gait disturbances. In contrast, the TD subtype is thought to rely more heavily on cortico-thalamo-cortical (CTC) circuit34, which may explain the relative absence of PIGD symptoms associated with BG degeneration43.

Beyond attentional networks, dysfunction of VN has also been known as one of the mechanisms underlying the occurrence of FOG. PD-FOG patients can overcome freezing by stepping over visual cues like transverse lines44,45. In our study, the gradient values of VN and SMN in G1 were lower in the PIGD group compared to the TD group, indicating decreased interactions between them. Researchers have pointed out the importance of visual-sensorimotor interactions in motor control and learning, through which visual information can be integrated into the formation of motion46,47. Supporting a disease-progressive effect, the principal gradient value of VN was negatively correlated with HY stage, suggesting a gradual breakdown of visuomotor integration with advancing disease severity. Alterations in attentional control networks further distinguished the two motor subtypes. The DAN, which sustains goal-directed attention during locomotion, showed aberrant dissociation in PIGD patients, potentially contributing to impaired visuomotor control during walking48,49. In contrast, TD patients showed a positive association between the secondary gradient value of DAN and disease duration, which may reflect compensatory engagement of higher-order attentional systems as disease progresses. Moreover, we observed a decreased principal gradient in the DMN, which was consistent with prior research18. From a pathophysiological perspective, the PIGD subtype is increasingly recognized as reflecting substantial non-dopaminergic involvement50, accompanied by more severe motor and non-motor manifestations, especially cognitive impairment. By contrast, the TD subtype is more closely associated with dopaminergic dysfunction and generally exhibits relatively preserved motor function51. As a higher-order cognitive network, DMN supports gait control through cognitive strategies52. Increased DMN integration might impair motor mental imagery and movement planning, ultimately exacerbating motor symptoms in the PIGD group. Taken together, these network-level alterations indicate that motor and non-motor symptoms in PD are tightly interconnected rather than representing independent clinical domains53. This dynamic reconfiguration likely reflects the increasing involvement of non-motor systems (particularly cognitive and visual networks) in shaping motor manifestations, providing a potential systems-level explanation for the progressive heterogeneity observed across PD motor subtypes. From this perspective, future studies that explicitly model interactions between motor and non-motor symptoms may enable a more refined subtype stratification and ultimately facilitate the development of more personalized therapeutic strategies.

PD is a multifactorial disorder driven by aging, genetics, and environmental factors. Using neuroimaging-transcription association analysis, we identified gene expression patterns linked to alterations in the first two thalamic gradients. Negatively weighted genes were enriched in oxidative stress and mitochondrial function pathways, both implicated in PD pathogenesis54. Postmortem studies have reported increased lipid peroxidation products such as 4­hydroxynonenal55 and protein carbonyls56 and reduced glutathione levels57 in PD patients, highlighting oxidative stress as a key contributor. Given that oxidative phosphorylation generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrion vulnerability to dysregulation is further exacerbated58. Positively weighted genes were enriched in ion transport, with Fe2 + , Ca2 + , and K+ playing critical roles in the pathogenesis of PD59,60. Brain iron deposition correlates with motor and cognitive impairments61, while ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death mechanism, is a major pathological process in PD62. Clinical studies suggest that iron chelator may slow motor progression and reduce substantia nigra iron deposition, underscoring their therapeutic potential63. These findings provide insights into PD’s molecular mechanisms and highlight potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Regarding developmental influences, our analysis revealed that genes linked to thalamic gradient alterations in PD motor subtypes were preferentially expressed as early as the “early fetal” stage. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics study indicated that human thalamic neuronal subtypes begin to emerge during the first trimester of development and start to organize into spatially distinct nuclei during the second trimester64. These findings suggest that developmental gene expression patterns may contribute to the large-scale organization of thalamocortical systems. However, their specific role in PD remains to be further investigated.

This study has several limitations. First, although the first two gradients captured the largest proportion of variance and showed consistent patterns across different sparsity thresholds, their selection remains partly heuristic. Future studies incorporating multiple embedding strategies and test-retest assessments would further strengthen the robustness of these findings. Second, most PD patients in our study were on levodopa. Although we controlled for LEDD, medication effects on thalamic gradients cannot be fully excluded. Future research should include drug-naïve patients to differentiate disease-related changes from pharmacological influences. Third, our data are cross-sectional in nature, although an independent external dataset was used to validate the robustness of our findings. Given that PD motor subtypes may evolve with disease progression65, large-scale longitudinal studies are needed to characterize subtype-specific trajectories of thalamic functional gradients and to clarify their relationships with longitudinal changes in motor subtype classification. Fourth, this study is based on correlational analyses and provides a preliminary exploration of thalamic functional gradient alterations across different motor subtypes, along with their associated genes. Further animal studies are needed to validate the molecular biological mechanisms underlying these thalamic gradient changes in PD motor subtypes.

In summary, this study is the first to characterize thalamic functional organization abnormalities across PD motor subtypes and their associated gene expression profiles. We identified thalamic gradient alterations linked to gene expression and motor symptoms. Notably, genes associated with these alterations were enriched in key biological processes, including lipid hydroperoxide response, electron transport, ion transport, and DNA recombination, with expression detected as early as “early fetal” stage. This study provides a multilevel description of thalamic functional organization in PD and its relationship with transcriptional patterns, offering a framework for future investigations into the biological basis of these alterations.

Methods

Subjects

The current study included a discovery dataset and an independent cross-race, cross-scanner validation dataset. The discovery dataset (dataset 1) comprised 152 PD patients (78 PIGD patients including 36 males and 42 females, 74 TD patients including 34 males and 40 females) recruited from the Department of Neurology at the First hospital of China Medical University between September 2016 and June 2024. Additionally, 48 age-matched HC (20 males, 28 females) were recruited from the local community through poster advertisements. The validation dataset (dataset 2) was obtained from the PPMI database (www.ppmi-info.org/data), which included 205 PD patients (100 PIGD patients with 53 males and 47 females, 105 TD patients with 66 males and 39 females) along with 118 HC (59 males, 59 females). PD motor subtypes were classified based on the ratio of the mean UPDRS tremor scores (11 items) to the mean PIGD scores (5 items): TD patients (ratio ≥1.15), PIGD patients (ratio ≤0.90), and MIX patients (ratios >0.9 and <1.15)66. Inclusion criteria for both PD patients and HC were as follows: age between 40 and 75 years, right-handedness. Participants included in discovery dataset were all Han ethnicity. Exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of neurologic surgery or psychological disorders, (2) any contradictions for MRI scanning, and (3) classification as the MIX subtype. Demographic data, including age, sex, and years of education, were collected from all participants. Additionally, disease duration and LEDD were recorded. Clinical assessments encompassed cognitive evaluations such as the MoCA and motor severity assessments, including the UPDRS (Parts II and III), the H&Y stage, tremor scores, and PIGD scores for both datasets. Besides, we also collected MMSE, HAMD and HAMA scores to assess the cognitive and psychiatric functioning of patients in dataset 1. It is noteworthy that all participants in the discovery dataset provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China Medical University (IRB NO.2020-52). PPMI is an open public database, so informed consent and ethical approval were not required for access to data from this database.

MRI acquisition

All subjects were scanned on a 3.0 T MRI scanner (Siemens, Magnetom Verio) with a 32-channel head coil. Participants were required to relax and close their eyes. The resting-state functional images were collected using gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence: time repetition (TR), 2500 ms; time echo (TE), 30 ms; flip angle, 90°; slice number, 43; slice thickness, 3.5 mm without slice gap; field of view (FOV), 224 × 224 mm ; matrix size, 64 × 64; and voxel size, 3.5 × 3.5 × 3.5 mm. The magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence was required to collect high-resolution T1-weighted images: TR, 5000 ms; TE, 2960 ms; flip angle, 12°; slice number, 176; slice thickness, 1 mm; FOV, 256 × 256 mm ; matrix size, 256 × 256; and voxel size, 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm. The parameters of MRI in validation dataset can be seen at www.ppmi-info.org/data.

MRI preprocessing

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI, http://rfmri.org/dpabi)67. The preprocessing pipeline included the following steps: (1) removal of the first 10 time points, (2) slice-timing correction, (3) head motion correction, (4) spatial normalization to individual T1-weighted images, (5) linear drift removal, (6) regression of nuisance covariates (including 24 head motion parameters, white matter signals, and cerebrospinal fluid signals), (7) band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz), and (8) spatial smoothing with a 6-mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel. Finally, each filtered functional volume was spatially normalized to the MNI space using the deformation parameters estimated during the previous steps and resampled into 3-mm isotropic voxels. In the discovery dataset, 9 PD patients (6 TD, 3 PIGD patients) and 2 HC of discovery dataset were excluded due to excessive head motion (>2 mm translation or >2° rotation). As a result, 143 PD patients (including 72 PIGD and 71 TD patients), as well as 46 HC from the discovery dataset were included in the final analysis. Similarly, 1 PIGD patient and 4 TD patients were excluded in the validation dataset, leaving a final sample of 200 PD patients (99 PIGD and 101 TD patients) and 118 HC for further analysis.

Macroscale thalamocortical gradient identification

Gradients of thalamocortical functional connectivity (FC) were constructed to characterize thalamic functional organization using the diffusion embedding algorithm in BrainSpace Toolbox68. For each subject, thalamocortical resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) was first computed by calculating Pearson’s correlations between the time series of each thalamic voxel and all voxels across the cerebrum16,69, followed by transformation into cosine similarity matrices16,17. The BrainNet246 Atlas was utilized to define the thalamus (958 voxels), including medial pre-frontal thalamus (mPFtha), pre-motor thalamus (mPMtha), sensory thalamus (Stha), rostral temporal thalamus (rTtha), posterior parietal thalamus (PPtha), occipital thalamus (Otha), caudal temporal thalamus (cTtha) and lateral pre-frontal thalamus (lPFtha). This procedure yielded a subject-specific thalamus-to-cerebrum rsFC matrix (958 × 49268). Fisher’s Z-transformation was applied to correlation coefficients to improve normality. Prior to FC computation, the time series were standardized using z-scores within each subject. Each subject-specific rsFC matrix was subsequently thresholded by retaining the top 10% of strongest connections per row while setting all other values to zero16,70–73.To assess the robustness of this thresholding strategy, additional analyses were performed using alternative sparsity levels (top 5% and 20%). Next, cosine similarity was computed between rows of the thresholded rsFC matrix to generate an affinity matrix, reflecting the similarity of connectivity profiles between pairs of thalamic voxels. Diffusion map embedding was then applied to each subject’s affinity matrix to derive individual-level connectome gradients74. The relative positioning of thalamic voxels along each gradient axis reflects the similarity of their functional connectivity profiles. To enable group-level comparisons, individual gradients were aligned using Procrustes alignment, and group-average gradients were calculated for visualization. To quantify the spatial dispersion of thalamic voxels in the gradient space, eccentricity was computed as the Euclidean distance from each voxel to the center of mass30. Additionally, we examined cortical-thalamic gradients by generating cortical similarity matrices based on cortical-thalamic connectivity profiles.

Thalamic functional network division

To characterize the functional implications of thalamic gradients, we evaluated their associations with canonical functional networks based on the seven-network parcellation75. A thalamic functional atlas was constructed using a custom winner-take-all parcellation approach31. That is, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed between the BOLD time course of each thalamic voxel and the average BOLD time course of each functional network. Each thalamic voxel was then assigned to the network with which it exhibited the highest correlation. Repeating this procedure for all thalamic voxels yielded a thalamic functional atlas including six functional subdivisions corresponding to six canonical functional networks, including the VN, SMN, DAN, VAN, FPN, and DMN. Considering susceptibility artifacts in these limbic areas, the limbic network was excluded in the process of winner-taken-all parcellation for dataset 1. Finally, we extracted the gradient values of voxels within these thalamic functional subdivisions and sorted them by the median. Additionally, to assess the cortical correspondences of thalamic gradients, we projected thalamic gradients onto the cerebral cortex. For each cortical vertex, gradient projection was determined by correlating its cortical-thalamic connectivity profile with thalamic gradient values. These cortical maps were subsequently downsampled into 400 cortical parcels and grouped into functional networks76 to further validate thalamic functional network division.

Group comparisons of functional gradients

A general linear model (GLM) was utilized to evaluate differences in functional gradient networks among the three groups, controlling for age, gender, years of education, and LEDD as covariates. For each gradient (G1 and G2), separate GLMs were performed at the network level. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with post hoc pairwise comparisons (HC vs. PIGD, HC vs. TD, PIGD vs. TD) adjusted using Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing. Effect sizes were reported for both overall group effects (partial η²) and pairwise contrasts (Cohen’s d) to facilitate interpretation of the magnitude of group differences. To examine potential differences in the associations between clinical variables and network gradient values across the two patient groups, partial correlation analyses were conducted, and only significant interactions (p < 0.05) were reported.

Gene expression data preprocessing

The Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA; http://human.brain-map.org) provides normalized microarray expression data from six postmortem human brains, each without a history of neuropsychiatric or neuropathological conditions. This dataset includes expression profiles of over 20,000 genes across 3702 brain tissue samples77. Demographic details of each donor are provided in Table S1. Gene expression data were preprocessed using the abagen toolbox (https://www.github.com/netneurolab/abagen)78 following the methodology of Arnatkevičiūtė et al79.. This process involved several steps: (1) verifying probe-to-gene annotations using the reannotator toolkit; (2) filtering out probes that did not exceed background noise levels; (3) selecting representative probes to index gene expression80; (4) mapping the tissue samples from the AHBA to the Schaefer 400 atlas; (5) normalizing expression measures using a scaled robust sigmoid transformation across participants; (6) filtering out inconsistently expressed genes. Notably, while all donors provided left-hemispheric expression data, only two donors had right-hemispheric expression data. To address this asymmetry, we employed a mirror-based approximation method (“leftright”) for the similarities of gene expression between hemisphere81. Only genes with consistent expression across donors (i.e., average inter-donor correlation ≥0.5) were retained. Scaled robust sigmoid (SRS) normalization was applied to correct for donor-specific effects, ensuring comparability of expression values. The final processed dataset consisted of a 316 × 15,677 matrix (samples × genes).

Transcription-neuroimaging association analysis

Based on the AHBA gene expression data and case-control thalamic gradient difference T-maps, we employed PLS regression82 to investigate spatial correlations between gene expression and gradient alterations in PIGD and TD subtypes. Gene expression data served as predictor variables for alterations in gradient within the PLS regression. The first component of the PLS (PLS1) represented the linear combination of gene expression values most strongly correlated with gradient changes. To test the null hypothesis that PLS1 accounted for no more covariance between the gradient map and whole-genome expression than expected by chance, we applied permutation testing with spherical rotations (5000 times)83 to control for spatial autocorrelation. Bootstrapping was utilized to estimate the variability of each gene’s PLS1 weight, and Z scores were calculated as the ratio of each gene’s weight to its bootstrap standard error, thereby ranking the genes based on their contributions to PLS184. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5‰, were categorized as PLS1+ or PLS1- in relation to gradient alterations. This analysis was conducted separately for the PIGD and TD subtypes using thalamic gradient difference maps from both dataset 1 and dataset 2.

Validation

Our main analyses were conducted in the discovery dataset. To exclude the influence of samples, we also carried out the above-mentioned analyses in an independent cross-race, cross-scanner validation dataset from PPMI.

Functional annotation

In order to enhance the reliability of the results, we picked robust thalamic gradient associated genes (genes statistically significant in both dataset 1 and dataset 2) for further analyses. To investigate the biological functions of genes associated with thalamic gradient alterations in different PD motor subtypes, we utilized G:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost)85 for Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment analysis86. Additionally, we performed specific expression analysis across developmental stages using the Specific Expression Analysis (SEA) tool (http://doughertytools.wustl.edu/CSEAtool.html)87. This analysis aimed to identify the particular developmental windows in which the identified genes were overexpressed. For all enrichment analyses, statistical significance was determined using the Benjamini and Hochberg method for false discovery rate correction (FDR-BH), with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.
