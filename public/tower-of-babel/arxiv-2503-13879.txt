Bridging Social Psychology and LLM Reasoning: Conflict-Aware Meta-Review Generation via Cognitive Alignment
Wei Chen, Han Ding, Meng Yuan, Zhao Zhang, Deqing Wang, Fuzhen Zhuang

Abstract
The rapid growth of scholarly submissions has overwhelmed traditional peer review systems, driving the need for intelligent automation to preserve scientific rigor. While large language models (LLMs) show promise in automating manuscript critiques, their ability to synthesize high-stakes meta-reviews, which require conflict-aware reasoning and consensus derivation, remains underdeveloped. Existing methods fail to effectively handle conflicting viewpoints within differing opinions, and often introduce additional cognitive biases, such as anchoring effects and conformity bias. To overcome these limitations, we propose the Cognitive Alignment Framework (CAF), a dual-process architecture that transforms LLMs into adaptive scientific arbitrators. By operationalizing Kahneman’s dual-process theory, CAF introduces a three-step cognitive pipeline: review initialization, incremental integration, and cognitive alignment. Empirical validation shows that CAF outperforms existing LLM-based methods, with sentiment consistency gains reaching up to 19.47% and content consistency improving by as much as 12.95%.

Introduction

Academic peer review is fundamental to ensuring scientific rigor, with experts assessing research validity through structured evaluation protocols
Chubin et al. (1990)
;
Rowland (2002)
;
Alberts et al. (2008)
. However, the rapid rise in scholarly submissions has created challenges in maintaining these standards, leading to imbalances between reviewer workload and quality assurance
Lee et al. (2013)
;
Fox et al. (2017)
. This scalability issue has driven a shift towards intelligent automation, with large language models (LLMs) showing great promise in enhancing peer review through multimodal reasoning and domain-specific text generation
Tyser et al. (2024)
;
Jin et al. (2024)
;
Zhou et al. (2024)
.

Figure 1:

Illustrating the “Fast Thinking” and “Slow Thinking” processes in human meta-review.

While current LLM-driven review automation mainly targets the peer review process, particularly the generation of detailed comments for individual manuscripts, a crucial yet underexplored aspect is meta-review generation
Bhatia et al. (2020)
;
Santu et al. (2024)
. This higher-order task involves integrating conflicting evaluations, and deriving consensus through evidence-based reasoning
Li et al. (2023)
;
Jin et al. (2024)
. Unlike standard summarization, effective meta-review generation requires conflict-aware reasoning that mirrors human decision-making, balancing psychological heuristics and logical deduction
Katsikopoulos (2011)
.

Recently, a few studies
Li et al. (2024)
;
Zeng et al. (2024)
have explored simulating human deliberation in meta-review generation, primarily leveraging the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning framework
Wei et al. (2022)
. These approaches decompose the meta-review process into sequential steps: extracting evidence-based opinions, summarizing areas of consensus and controversy, and composing an AC/REJ meta-review.
However, the absence of cognitive conflict modeling makes these systems prone to biased decision-making, especially when dealing with opposing opinions in peer reviews.
Additionally, we observe that current LLM-based methods unintentionally inherit, and at times amplify, fundamental cognitive biases from human decision-making. These patterns are specifically reflected in:
❶ Anchoring Effect
Wilson et al. (1996)
:
Initial reviewer comments exhibit a stronger anchoring effect on final decisions, with LLMs exhibiting an anchoring coefficient of 0.255, higher than the 0.193 observed in humans.
❷ Conformity Bias
Allen and Levine (1968)
: Majority opinions tend to suppress minority perspectives through pseudo-consensus reinforcement.
The conformity coefficient for LLMs is 0.25, significantly lower than that for humans (1.00), indicating that LLMs are more susceptible to conformity bias.

To bridge this gap, we draw inspiration from dual-process theory
Groves and Thompson (1970)
;
Chaiken (1999)
, which posits two distinct modes of cognitive processing: “Fast Thinking” and “Slow Thinking”. As illustrated in Figure
1
, the “Fast Think” quickly handles low-conflict cases with immediate responses, while the “Slow Thinking” is engaged for high-conflict decisions, requiring deeper analysis and reflection.
Building on this idea, we propose the Cognitive Alignment Framework (CAF) for meta-review generation, which reimagines the role of LLMs in managing multi-perspective discourse in peer review. Specifically, the CAF architecture involves three key phases:
1) Initialization Phase, where the model extracts key points from each comment;
2) Incremental Integration Phase, which progressively incorporates subsequent reviews while maintaining a conflict map; and
3) Cognitive Alignment Phase, which employs dual-process reasoning to balance heuristic pattern recognition with analytical evaluation.
By introducing this adaptive arbitration mechanism, CAF extends the capabilities of LLMs beyond traditional summarization, enabling them to systematically reconcile conflicting scientific opinions.

In summary, our contributions are threefold:

•

We uncover inherent cognitive biases, such as the anchoring effect and conformity bias, within existing LLM-driven meta-review systems. To our knowledge, this is the first instance in which these biases have been identified in this task.

•

We propose a novel three-phase framework that integrates dual-process theory into LLMs. This framework combines iterative conflict mapping and dual reasoning pathways to mitigate biases and resolve scientific disputes.

•

Experiments demonstrate that CAF outperforms conventional methods, with sentiment consistency gains reaching up to 19.47% and content consistency improving by as much as 12.95%.

Cognitive Biases in LLM-based Meta-Review Generation

In this section, we will further analyze the existing challenges in current LLM-based meta-review generation systems, particularly focusing on how they may unintentionally introduce human cognitive biases: anchoring effect and conformity bias.

3.1
Anchoring Effect in Sequential Processing

The anchoring effect
Mussweiler (2002)
refers to the cognitive bias where individuals’ judgments are disproportionately influenced by an initial reference point, or “anchor” during decision-making processes. In meta-review generation, LLMs may exhibit this bias when they first process an initial set of comments or ratings, which can then disproportionately affect their final assessment.

Measure.

To assess the impact of different reviewers’ opinions on final decision, we model this process with logistic regression. Formally, the final score
\mathcal{E}
is modeled as a function of a weighted sum of the individual reviewer scores
s_{i}
, passed through a Sigmoid function to produce a probability:

P(\mathcal{E}=1\mid s_{1},\dots,s_{N})=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}\cdot s_{i}\right),

(1)

where
\xi_{i}
represents the anchoring coefficient for the
i
-th review,
\sigma
represents the sigmoid function, and
s_{i}
is the score provided by reviewer
i
. Ideally, the coefficients
\xi_{i}
should be uniform to ensure equal representation of all reviews. However, due to the anchoring effect, the coefficient
\xi_{1}
(for the first review) is often disproportionately emphasized, particularly in CoT methods. By comparing the
\xi_{1}
values across experiments, we can gauge the strength of the anchoring bias in LLM-based meta-review generation. A higher
\xi_{1}
value indicates a stronger bias, meaning the first review has had a greater impact on the final outcome.

Observation Results.

We selected 50 peer-reviewed papers from PeerSum benchmark and applied least squares regression to calculate the anchoring coefficients for the different peer review opinions, as shown in Figure
2
. The results indicate that the recommendations of the first reviewer have a significant influence on the final decision. Specifically, the anchoring coefficient for the first reviewer is 0.255 for GPT-4o, which is substantially higher than the human committee baseline (
\xi_{1}=0.193
). This bias persists even when subsequent reviews provide contradictory evidence, as models tend to overemphasize earlier tokens in their self-attention layers. This demonstrates that LLMs are particularly sensitive to the first review, and their reliance on earlier inputs can significantly distort the final decision, even in the face of later evidence.

3.2
Conformity Bias via Pseudo-Consensus

Conformity bias, or groupthink
Moscovici and Faucheux (1972)
, occurs when individuals align their judgments with the majority, even when those judgments differ from their own independent assessments. In LLM-based meta-review generation, this bias arises when the model’s output is disproportionately influenced by the majority of prior reviews, rather than synthesizing feedback in an unbiased manner. As a result, the meta-review reflects the dominant opinions, even when they may not be the most accurate or fair assessment.

Note that a higher value of
\kappa

This indicator proved effective in quantifying this bias, and future work will focus on strategies to mitigate it.

4
Methodology

Existing methods fail to effectively handle conflicting viewpoints within differing opinions, and often introduce additional cognitive biases, such as anchoring effects and conformity bias.
To overcome these limitations, we propose a novel framework CAF in Figure
3
. It implements a three-phase cognitive architecture for meta-review synthesis, inspired by dual-process cognitive theory.

4.1
Key Information Extraction

For a target paper with
N
reviews
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{N}\}
, we establish the foundational context
\mathcal{S}
through structured knowledge extraction from all initial reviews. Since review texts can be lengthy and redundant, processing them in full may lead to inefficiencies. To mitigate this, we first extract key elements from each individual review:

\mathcal{S}_{1\sim N}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{F}_{\text{LLM}}(R_{i}),

(4)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\text{LLM}}
is a LLM that identifies and summarizes essential aspects from each review. By extracting these critical points, the synthesis process is streamlined, avoiding information overload while retaining relevant insights. The extracted elements are then organized into a preliminary structure to facilitate iterative refinement. The initial prompt used is provided in Appendix
D.4.1
.

4.2
Conflict-Aware Iterative Integration

After initializing the review pool, the framework incrementally synthesizes reviews while simultaneously identifying inconsistencies. Traditional LLM-based methods
Zeng et al. (2024)
;
Li et al. (2024)
, which process all reviews at once, can exacerbate issues like anchoring effects and conformity bias. Specifically, when all reviews are considered together, early reviews often serve as anchors, disproportionately influencing the synthesis process and leading to biased conclusions. Similarly, the presence of multiple reviews at once can cause the model to align too closely with the majority opinion, reinforcing conformity bias. Our approach mitigates these biases by incorporating reviews progressively, allowing the system to adjust its synthesis in response to each new input.

Initially, when the first reviewer’s input
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is received, the initial cognitive representation
\mathcal{C}_{1}
of the meta-review is fully derived from the first review.
Subsequently, when the second reviewer’s input
\mathcal{S}_{2}
arrives, the framework updates the existing cognitive iteratively. Given the current cognitive context
\mathcal{C}_{i-1}
and the incoming review
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, a conflict detection model
\mathcal{D}
assesses potential inconsistencies:

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C}_{i-1},\mathcal{S}_{i})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if a conflict exists,}\\
0,&\text{if no conflict exists.}\end{cases}

(5)

This module identifies inconsistencies based on three key factors:
❶ Propositional Disagreements: Contradictions in claims or assessments of the paper’s methodology and contributions,
❷ Sentiment Divergences: Variations in sentiment across reviewers, such as one being highly positive while another is critical,
❸ Evidence Conflicts: Differences in how reviewers interpret and weigh empirical evidence.
This conflict-aware integration process ensures that all perspectives are carefully considered, progressively refining the meta-review while addressing inconsistencies systematically.

This continues until all reviews (i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{3}
, …,
\mathcal{S}_{N}
) have been integrated into the model.

4.3
Dual-Process Cognitive Alignment

In each conflict detection process, the CAF performs a cognitive alignment procedure, balancing rapid heuristic processing with analytical reasoning. If no conflict is detected, the system directly integrates
\mathcal{S}_{i}
into the current cognitive framework. However, if a conflict is identified, a structured conflict resolution mechanism is triggered to maintain coherence and consistency within the meta-review. This cycle of conflict detection continues until no conflict remains or the predefined maximum number
Q
of iterations is reached. During this phase, the model engages in cognitive reconstruction, generating a refined set of essential arguments, known as the
key-point definition

\mathcal{K}_{i}
.
This two processes (i.e., fast thinking and slow thinking) aims to integrate conflicting points into a unified position and are formally expressed as follows:

\mathcal{K}_{i}=\mathcal{G}_{\text{Fast}}(\mathcal{C}_{i-1},\mathcal{S}_{i})\oplus\mathcal{G}_{\text{Slow}}(\mathcal{C}_{i-1},\mathcal{S}_{i}),

(6)

where “
\oplus
”denotes concatenation fusion. Here,
\mathcal{G}_{\text{Fast}}
facilitates the rapid identification of argument structures and sentiment trends, while
\mathcal{G}_{\text{Slow}}
serves as a reconciliation function, guiding the model to rethink and generate a cohesive set of key points. This process helps minimize contradictions and enhances the overall meta-review synthesis.
The refined cognitive is then updated as follows:

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{C}_{i-1},\mathcal{K}_{i},\mathcal{S}_{i}),

(7)

where
\mathcal{U}
integrates the resolved key-points into the current cognitive.
After all reviews have been processed and conflicts resolved, the final meta-review is given by
\mathcal{M}_{\text{final}}=f(\mathcal{C}_{N})
, where
f
is a synthesis function based on the current cognitive knowledge
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. This structured meta-review provides a balanced, evidence-supported summary, ensuring that disagreements are systematically addressed while preserving key insights from all peer reviews.

5.2
Main Experimental Results (RQ1)

To validate the effectiveness of CAF, we conducted experiments across multiple models in Table
2
.

The results shows that our proposed framework excels in both sentiment consistency and text quality when generating meta-reviews. Specifically, on GPT-3.5 and Llama3-8B, our generated meta-reviews significantly outperform other baseline methods in terms of sentiment consistency and ROUGE scores. By integrating the “Fast-Slow Thinking” theory, CAF effectively balances intuitive and deliberative strategies,
resulting in meta-reviews that are fluent, professional, and closely aligned with real-world meta-review standards.

However, on GPT-4o and Qwen2.5-7B, our method does not surpass the Prompt-SCF approach in sentiment consistency. We hypothesize that this is due to GPT-4o’s inherent capabilities, which allow it to achieve high sentiment consistency with relatively simple prompts. Additionally, since SCF explicitly optimizes for sentiment consistency, it performs better in this aspect on GPT-4o.
Overall, despite the observed limitations in certain cases, our method demonstrates strong generation capabilities across most models, particularly in enhancing sentiment and content consistency. These findings validate the effectiveness of CAF in automatically generating high-quality meta-reviews.

5.3
Further Analysis (RQ2)

To explore under what circumstances our model engages in cognitive reconstruction, we analyzed the relationship between the score differences in comments among reviewers and the probability of reflection for each paper, as shown in Figure
4

The results show that as the score difference in opinions between reviewers increases, the ratio of reflection rises, peaking at a difference of 5. This suggests that when there is moderate to large divergence in reviews, more reflection is needed to reconcile conflicting viewpoints and ensure consistency in the final meta-review.
The gradual increase in reflection up to the peak reflects the cognitive process of resolving discrepancies. The subsequent decline indicates that for very small or large differences, less reflection is required. In cases of small differences, meta-reviewers can quickly align their opinions, while for large differences, reflection may decrease as reviewers focus on clear-cut arguments or rely on existing consensus.

5.4
Cognitive Bias Mitigation (RQ3)

In §
3
, we discussed how existing methods can lead to the anchoring effect and conformity bias. To evaluate whether CAF mitigates these biases, we conducted further experiments in this section.

Primacy Effect.

As shown in
5
, the GPT-Naive model exhibits a higher primacy effect (0.255) compared to GPT-Ours (0.221), with a difference of 0.034. This indicates that our approach effectively reduces the anchoring effect helping overcome the bias toward earlier information. LLMs are inherently sensitive to earlier inputs due to their self-attention mechanisms, and previous methods process all reviews at once, exacerbating this bias. In contrast, our incremental input approach allows the model to reason with each new review sequentially, reducing the influence of earlier reviews.

Conformity Bias.

Table
3
shows that CAF improves evaluation consistency across models. For GPT-3.5, the improvement is 0.3194 (from 0.1250 to 0.4444), and for Qwen2.5-7B, it is 0.25 (from 0.25 to 0.5). These results demonstrate that our method reduces conformity bias, resulting in more independent and diverse outputs.
Existing methods process all reviews at once, making the model more susceptible to conformity bias as it aligns with the majority opinion. In contrast, our incremental input approach, combined with fast-slow thinking for reasoning, allows the model to reprocess each review iteratively, making it less likely to be swayed by dominant viewpoints.

Case Study (RQ4)

To better illustrate the effectiveness of CAF, we conducted a case study shown in Figure

. The complete case study can be found in Appendix

In this case, both the first and second reviewers praised the paper. However, the third reviewer raised some concerns regarding the methodology. Upon detecting this conflict, CAF engaged in a “Slow Thinking” process, analyzing the feedback and identifying four key issues: Planckian Jitter, Test Dataset Manipulation, Image Resolution, and Gamma Correction. After reflecting on these issues, the conflict was resolved, leading to the paper’s rejection due to unresolved issues and lack of demonstrated validity. . A major area of focus has been the automation of individual reviews to enhance the quality of academic assessments. For example,
Liu and Shah (2023)
and
Liang et al. (2024)
have developed custom prompts for guiding GPT-4 in generating specific scientific feedback. Meanwhile,
Wei et al. (2023)
have explored the use of continuous pretraining to better align LLMs with human-like review behaviors, and
Jin et al. (2024)
have leveraged LLMs to simulate the entire review process, examining how different factors impact academic evaluation.
However, these studies mainly focus on the peer review itself and overlook the crucial aspect of meta-reviewing, which involves synthesizing feedback from multiple reviewers
Li et al. (2023)
;
Li et al. (2024)
.

Applications of Dual-process Theory.

Dual-process theory
Groves and Thompson (1970)
;
Chaiken (1999)
provides a psychological framework for understanding human thinking and decision-making, proposing two distinct modes of cognitive processing: fast, automatic thinking and slow, deliberate reasoning. This theory has been effectively applied to machine learning approaches. For example,
Mittal et al. (2017)
conceptualizes the vector space model as representing fast thinking and knowledge graph reasoning as slow thinking.
Bengio (2017)
highlights the connection between reflective thinking and consciousness, while
Chen et al. (2019)
introduces an end-to-end framework integrating a generative decoder for fast thinking with a reasoning module for slow thinking to tackle complex tasks. Building on these ideas,
Liu et al. (2022)
combines neural networks and symbolic reasoning to solve question-answering challenges, and
He et al. (2024)
designs a dialogue planning framework using a fast policy language model and a slow Monte Carlo tree search planner.
Inspired by these works, we apply the dual-process theory to simulate the human peer review process.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose the Cognitive Alignment Framework (CAF), which leverages dual-process cognitive modeling to enhance meta-review synthesis in LLM-based systems. By incorporating a three-phase processing pipeline, CAF effectively manages conflicting viewpoints, enabling more balanced and coherent meta-reviews.
The experimental results validates the effectiveness of our proposed CAF across multiple LLM-based models.

Limitations

CAF is designed to assist authors in understanding review comments during the peer review process, particularly in the meta-review stage. However, there are several limitations to this approach, as well as opportunities for future exploration.

Data Scope.
The dataset used in this study is primarily sourced from the PeerSum benchmark, which focuses on machine learning papers. As a result, most of the meta-reviews in the dataset are specific to this domain. This limits the generalizability of the findings, as the conclusions drawn may not apply to other academic fields, and the framework’s effectiveness across diverse domains remains untested.

Rebuttal Exclusion.
For simplicity, the current framework does not incorporate author rebuttals during meta-review generation. Including rebuttal content could enhance the synthesis by considering author responses and alternative perspectives, which may influence the final meta-review. The exclusion of this element may restrict the depth and nuance captured in the generated reviews.

Ethics Statement

Although large language models (LLMs) have shown promise in generating useful meta-reviews, it is crucial to note that their results should not be relied upon without human oversight. LLMs may occasionally produce inaccurate content or reflect biases present in their training data. We have made efforts to mitigate bias and ensure fairness, though some unintended biases may still occur. Furthermore, we have prioritized data privacy and confidentiality, complying with relevant data protection regulations. Our objective is not to replace human reviewers, but to demonstrate that LLMs can be valuable tools to assist and streamline the meta-review process, enhancing both efficiency and effectiveness when used alongside expert judgment.

Following prior work

This comprehensive dataset, constructed by crawling peer reviews from OpenReview, aggregates 14,993 submission instances from two major machine learning conferences: NeurIPS (2021-2022) and ICLR (2018-2022). We conducted experiments with two open-source LLMs: Qwen2.5-7B

\lx@sectionsign

A.4
Evaluation

We assess the generated meta-review from two main aspects: sentiment consistency and content consistency.
❶ For sentiment consistency, we use
FacetEval
from SCF to evaluate the quality and sentiment alignment of the LLM-generated meta-review. To label the sentiment of the ground-truth meta-review, we employ GPT-4 and classify it into five sentiment categories: strongly positive, positive, neutral, negative, and strongly negative. To quantify emotional consistency, we utilize one-hot encoding to map the sentiment of each aspect to a vector space, and then concatenate these aspect-level vectors. Subsequently, we compute the cosine similarity between the generated emotional vector and the ground-truth emotional vector to derive the emotional consistency score.
❷ For content consistency, we use the ROUGE metric, which includes
ROUGE-1
,
ROUGE-2
, and
ROUGE-L
, to evaluate text consistency. ROUGE is a widely adopted automatic evaluation metric primarily used to assess the quality of text in natural language generation tasks. It calculates the similarity by comparing the overlap between the generated content and the ground-truth content.
