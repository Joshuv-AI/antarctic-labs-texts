Adjoint-based A Posteriori Error Analysis for Semi-explicit Index-1 and Hessenberg Index-2 Differential-Algebraic Equations
Jehanzeb Chaudhary, Owen Lewis, Md Al Amin Molla

Abstract
In this work we develop and analyze adjoint-based analyses for a posteriori error estimation for the temporal discretization of differential-algebraic equations (DAEs) of special type: semi-explicit index-1 and Hessenberg index-2. Our technique quantifies the error in a Quantity of Interest (QoI), which is defined as a bounded linear functional of the solution of a DAE. We derive representations for errors various trypes of QoIs (depending on the entire time interval, final time, algebraic variables, differential variables etc.). We form two analyses: one that defines the adjoint to the DAE system, and one that first converts the DAE to an ODE system and then applies classical a posteriori analysis techniques. A number of examples are presented, including nonlinear and non-autonomous DAEs, as well as discretized partial differential-algebraic equations (PDAEs). Numerical results indicate a high degree of accuracy in the error estimation.

Introduction

Differential-algebraic equations (DAEs) are a broad class of mathematical models that arise in a wide range of scientific and engineering applications where constraints are intrinsic to the system under consideration. Such systems arise in optimization, model simplification, optimal control, uncertainty quantification, and many other fields to investigate physical phenomena
Cao
et al. 2003
;
Brenan et al. 1995
;
Hairer and
Wanner 2010
.
DAEs are a generalization of ordinary differential equations (ODEs).
Unlike ODEs, DAEs combine both differential dynamics and algebraic constraints, which introduce unique challenges for their analysis and numerical simulation.
In this article we focus on DAEs containing only ordinary derivatives, as opposed to partial derivatives.
However, discretization of PDEs with constrains often leads to DAEs
Brenan et al. 1995
.

Numerical techniques for solving DAEs must handle complexities like stiffness and nonlinearity inherent to ODEs, but also the additional difficulties introduced by the algebraic structure, index, hidden constraints and consistent initial conditions. The index of a DAE, which represents the complexity of its constraint structure, plays an important role in determining the appropriate numerical method.
There are a number of techniques for the numerical solution of DAEs (see
section

2.3
and the references therein).

Solving DAEs numerically invariably involves error in the computed solution. This error in the computed solution must be quantified if DAEs are to be used in a robust and reliable manner in science and engineering applications.

There are many cases where scientists and engineers are interested in quantifying the error in a bounded linear functional of the solution to DAEs, the so called quantity of interest (QoI). Adjoint based
a posteriori
error estimation plays a key role in accurate estimation of the error in the computed value of a QoI, and is the focus of this article. Adjoint based methods and analyses are used in numerous applications, e.g., sensitivity analysis

In this article we carry out a thorough adjoint based analysis for semi-explicit index-1 and Hessenberg index-2 DAEs. In particular, this involves defining the associated adjoint DAEs with consistent initialization depending on the structure of the QoI, and using the adjoint solutions to quantify the error in the computed QoI. The theoretical results in this article derive accurate error estimates for various QoIs ( e.g. depending on final time, average over time intervals, involving either differential or algebraic variables, or both).
We develop two error analyses in this article. The first approach is based on defining the adjoint directly to the DAE system, and requires careful setting of the adjoint initial conditions. The second approach defines an adjoint problem to the ODE corresponding to the DAE, which in turn allows the use of classical error analyses techniques. The accuracy of the resulting error estimates is demonstrated through a number of numerical examples including nonlinear, non-autonomous DAEs as well as a semi-discretized PDE.

2
Quantities of Interest in Differential Algebraic Equations

This sections gives a brief background on Differential-Algebraic Equations (DAEs), their numerical approximation, and introduces two quantities of interest (QoIs) that are the focus of error estimation.

2.1
Differential-Algebraic Equations

The general form of an implicit DAE is

\displaystyle F(\dot{x}(t),x(t),t)=0.

(1)

Here
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
for some positive integers
n
,
m
, and
\dot{x}(t)=\frac{dx(t)}{dt}
.
If the Jacobian matrix
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\dot{x}}
is invertible for all values of the arguments within a suitable domain
Ascher and
Petzold 1998
then we can solve
eq.

1
for
\dot{x}
to get an
explicit
ODE of the form

\displaystyle\dot{x}=f(x,t),

(2)

and
eq.

1
represents an implicit ODE (or a so-called index-0 DAE). If the Jacobian matrix
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\dot{x}}
is singular then
eq.

1
is a DAE with a particular index greater than zero.
In this case,
x(t)=[y(t),z(t)]^{T}
, for
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
z(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and we have an ODE with constraints, resulting in a semi-explicit DAE,

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=f(y,z,t),

(3a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=g(y,z,t).

(3b)

Here,
y(t)
and
z(t)
are called the differential and algebraic variables respectively.

2.2
Index and Semi-explicit DAEs

The index of a DAE is an important property of the problem that plays a role in classification and has many consequences for the behavior of solutions
Brenan et al. 1995
.
There are, in fact, several distinct, but related, definitions of the index of a DAE.
Here we restrict ourselves to the discussion of the “differential index.”
For a DAE of the form given by
eq.

1
, the differential index
k
is the minimum number of differentiations required such that the
k
-th total derivative

\frac{d^{k}F}{dt^{k}}=0,

uniquely determines the variable
\dot{x}
as a continuous function of
x(t)
and
t

Brenan et al. 1995
.

2.2.1
Semi-explicit index-1 DAE

Consider a semi-explicit DAE of the form
eq.

3
. Differentiating the constraint
eq.

3b
with respect to
t
and formally solving for
\dot{z}
gives

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=-[g_{z}]^{-1}\left[g_{y}f+g_{t}\right],

(4)

where
g_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the Jacobian matrix of the function
g
with respect to
y
and the
ij
-th component of
g_{y}
is:
(g_{y})_{ij}=\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}
for
i=1,2,\dots,m
, and
j=1,2,\dots,n
. Similarly,
g_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the Jacobian of
g
with respect to
z
and
g_{t}=\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}
.
It follows from
eq.

4
that the invertibility of the Jacobian matrix
g_{z}
in a neighborhood of the solution of
eq.

3
is required to yield explicit differential equations corresponding to the algebraic variables
z(t)

Hairer and
Wanner 2010
.
Indeed, the DAE
eq.

3
is index-1 if and only if
g_{z}
is invertible
Brenan et al. 1995
.

2.2.2
Hessenberg (Pure) index-2 DAE

We now consider a common class of DAEs for which the index is greater than one.
Consider the semi-explicit DAE in case that the algebraic constraint
eq.

3b
does not explicitly depend on the algebraic variable
z
. In this case, the DAE may be written,

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=f(y,z,t),

(5a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=g(y,t).

(5b)

Taking the derivative of
eq.

5b
with respect to
t
yields the so-called hidden constraint,

\displaystyle 0=g_{y}f+g_{t}.

(6)

Now, in the event that
g_{y}f_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is invertible, the ODE
eq.

5a
together with the hidden constraint
eq.

6
form a index-1 DAE
Hairer and
Wanner 2010
.
To see this, we differentiate the hidden constraint
eq.

6
with respect to time and formally solve for
\dot{z}
to yield the differential equations corresponding to the algebraic variables,

\displaystyle\dot{z}=-\left(g_{y}f_{z}\right)^{-1}\displaystyle\left(f^{T}g_{yy}f+g_{y}f_{y}f+2g_{yt}f+g_{y}f_{t}+g_{tt}\right),

(7)

here,
g_{yy}
is the derivative of the Jacobian matrix
g_{y}
with respect to
y
, a third order tensor of dimension
m\times n\times n
with components given by

\displaystyle\left(g_{yy}\right)_{ijk}=\frac{\partial^{2}g_{i}}{\partial y_{j}\partial y_{k}},\quad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,m,\,j=1,2,\dots,n,\text{ and }k=1,2,\dots,n.

For a fixed
i
, each slice of this tensor represents a Hessian matrix.
For
f\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
f^{T}g_{yy}f
has components

\left(f^{T}g_{yy}f\right)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}g_{i}}{\partial y_{j}\partial y_{k}}f_{j}f_{k},\quad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,m.

The DAE
eq.

5
with
g_{y}f_{z}
nonsingular in a neighborhood of the solution is called Hessenberg (Pure) index-2 DAE
Brenan et al. 1995
.
This is called a pure index-2 because all the algebraic variables (components of
z
) are of index-2. The initial conditions (IC):
(y_{0},z_{0},t_{0})
must satisfy

0=g(y_{0}),\quad\text{ and }\quad 0=g_{y}(y_{0},t_{0})f(y_{0},z_{0})+g_{t}(y_{0},t_{0}),

for the consistency of IC
Hairer and
Wanner 2010
.

2.3
Numerical Solution of DAEs

There are two main ways to solve DAEs numerically.
The first is to directly discretize the DAE as written.
The second is to reformulate the DAE as an ODE through a process knowns as index reduction.
Direct discretization is often preferred due to the cost of index reduction and to preserve the constraint
eq.

3b
exactly for the numerical solution.
The well studied class of numerical methods for ODEs called backward differentiation formula (BDF) methods were applied first in 1971 by Gear
Gear 1971
to solve DAEs numerically.
These methods serve as the basis for the DASSL code
Brenan et al. 1995
;
Petzold 1982
.
It is known that the
p
-step BDF method is accurate to order
p
for
p\leq 6
and effective for solving index-1 as well as Hessenberg index-2 DAEs numerically
Atkinson et al. 2009
;
Brenan et al. 1995
.

We denote the approximate solution to
eq.

3
or
eq.

5
as

X(t)=[Y(t),Z(t)]^{T},

(8)

where
X(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m},Y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Z(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
In this work, we use the simplest first order BDF method, also known as the Implicit Euler Method, for the numerical solution of index-1
eq.

3
as well as Hessenberg index-2 DAEs
eq.

5
. This method is first order accurate, stable and convergent for semi-explicit index-1 and Hessenberg index-2 DAEs
Atkinson et al. 2009
.
The approximate solution is calculated at a discrete set of nodes,

\displaystyle 0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{N}=T.

(9)

We take these nodes to be evenly spaced,
t_{n}=t_{0}+n\Delta t
,
n=0,1,2,\dots,N
, where
\Delta t=T/N
.
Applying the BDF-1 method to the DAE
eq.

3
yields,

\left.\begin{aligned} Y_{n+1}&=Y_{n}+\Delta tf(Y_{n+1},Z_{n+1},t_{n+1}),\\
0&=g(Y_{n+1},Z_{n+1},t_{n+1}),\end{aligned}\right\}

(10)

for
n=0,1,\dots,N-1
.
In the case of Hessenberg (Pure) index-2 DAE
eq.

5
, the constraint equation does not explicitly depend on the algebraic variable and the first order BDF method becomes

\left.\begin{aligned} Y_{n+1}&=Y_{n}+\Delta tf(Y_{n+1},Z_{n+1},t_{n+1}),\\
0&=g(Y_{n+1},t_{n+1}),\end{aligned}\right\}

(11)

for
n=0,1,\dots,N-1
, where
Y(t_{n})=Y_{n}
, and
Z(t_{n})=Z_{n}
for
n=0,1,\dots,N
.
This discretization is implicit in time and yields a system of
n+m
equations in
n+m
unknowns.
Given values for
Y_{n}
, and
Z_{n}
, one must solve this nonlinear system for
Y_{n+1}
, and
Z_{n+1}
.
The numerically computed solution to the index-1 DAEs
eq.

3
and index-2 DAEs
eq.

5
are naturally calculated at the discrete time points
t_{n}
.
Whenever we must evaluate the numerical solution any
other
time point
t\in(0,T]
, we do so using linear interpolation between the two closet time points such that
t\in(t_{n},t_{n+1})
.

2.4
Quantity of Interest (QoI)

In this section we formalize the notion of a Quantity of Interest or QoI.
In many applications, the approximate solution of an DAE is used to calculate some measurable quantity, called the QoI, and thus predict behavior of the underlying system.
In this work we model QoIs as measurable quantities that can be expressed as a linear functional acting on the solution of the DAE. To describe the QoI considered, we first introduce some notation. For any two time-dependent functions
a,b:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\displaystyle\langle a,b\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(a(t),b(t)\right)\,\,dt,

(12)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the usual Euclidean inner-product.

We consider two types of QoI in this work, one is cumulative over the interval
\left[0,T\right]
and defined as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\left[0,T\right]}(x)=\langle x,\psi\rangle,\quad\quad\psi\in[\mathcal{L}^{2}(0,T]]^{n+m}.

(13)

The other type of QoI depends only on the solution at the terminal time
T
, and is defined as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\left[T\right]}\left(x\right)=\left(x(T),\zeta\right),\quad\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}.

(14)

2.5
Error in QoI

Now, given an approximate solution to a DAE (or ODE), our main goal is to calculate
the error in the computed value of the QoIs:
\mathcal{Q}_{\left[0,T\right]}(X)
, and
\mathcal{Q}_{\left[T\right]}\left(X\right)
(see
eqs.

13
and
14
for the definitions of the QoIs).
Recalling that
x
is the
true
solution of
eq.

3
and
X
the numerically computed solution, we denote the error in the solution by
e^{(x)}=x-X
. The error in the two QoIs is given below.

•

The error in the QoI
\mathcal{Q}_{\left[0,T\right]}(X)
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\left[0,T\right]}(x)-\mathcal{Q}_{\left[0,T\right]}(X)

\displaystyle=\langle x,\psi\rangle-\langle X,\psi\rangle=\langle e^{(x)},\psi\rangle=\mathcal{Q}_{\left[0,T\right]}\left(e^{(x)}\right).

(15)

•

Similarly, the error in the QoI
\mathcal{Q}_{\left[T\right]}\left(X\right)
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{[T]}\left(x\right)-\mathcal{Q}_{[T]}\left(X\right)=\left(e^{(x)}(T),\zeta\right)=\mathcal{Q}_{[T]}\left(e^{(x)}\right).

(16)

For notational clarity, it is often convenient to break vector quantities into their components corresponding to the differential and algebraic variables of the given DAE.
Thus, we write
x=[y,z]^{T}
,
\psi=[\psi^{y},\psi^{z}]^{T}
,
\zeta=[\zeta^{y},\zeta^{z}]^{T}
, and
e^{(x)}=[e^{(y)},e^{(z)}]^{T}
where
y(t),\psi^{y}(t),\zeta^{y},e^{(y)}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
z(t),\psi^{z}(t),\zeta^{z},e^{(z)}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Note that

e^{(y)}=(y-Y)
and
e^{(z)}=(z-Z)
.
Based on this notation
eq.

15
and
eq.

16
become,

\mathcal{Q}_{\left[0,T\right]}\left(e^{(x)}\right)=\langle e^{(y)},\psi^{y}\rangle+\langle e^{(z)},\psi^{z}\rangle,

(17)

and

\mathcal{Q}_{[T]}\left(e^{(x)}\right)=\left(e^{(y)}(T),\zeta^{y}\right)+\left(e^{(z)}(T),\zeta^{z}\right).

(18)

Quantifying this error is the focus of the next two sections. In
section

3
, the analysis is carried out by defining an adjoint problem to the DAE system
eq.

3
. Later, in
section

4
, index reduction is used to convert the DAE to an ODE, and then classical analysis is employed to derive error estimates.

3
Error Analysis of DAEs by Adjoint to the DAE system

The error analysis in this section, called Adjoint DAE analysis, is carried out by defining an adjoint problem to the DAE system
eq.

3
, and by a careful choice of the initial conditions for the adjoint problem.

3.1
Adjoint System

We introduce adjoint differential variables
\phi^{(y)}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and adjoint algebraic variables
\phi^{(z)}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and define the corresponding adjoint problem for a system of semi-explicit DAE
eq.

3
as follows,

\displaystyle-\dot{\phi}^{(y)}

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{y}^{T}\phi^{(y)}+\bar{g}_{y}^{T}\phi^{(z)}+\psi^{y},\quad t\in[0,T),

(19a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{z}^{T}\phi^{(y)}+\bar{g}_{z}^{T}\phi^{(z)}+\psi^{z},\quad t\in[0,T),

(19b)

where the linearized operators are

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{y}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f(\tilde{y},\tilde{z})}{\partial y}ds,\quad\quad\displaystyle\bar{f}_{z}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f(\tilde{y},\tilde{z})}{\partial z}ds,

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{y}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial g(\tilde{y},\tilde{z})}{\partial y}ds,\quad\quad\displaystyle\bar{g}_{z}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial g(\tilde{y},\tilde{z})}{\partial z}ds,

for
\tilde{y}=sy+(1-s)Y
, and
\tilde{z}=sz+(1-s)Z
.
Here, we leave the initial conditions
\phi^{(y)}(T)
and
\phi^{(z)}(T)
unspecified for the moment. Defining the correct initial conditions is a key component of analysis. Our choice of the initial conditions will be motivated from the work on adjoint based sensitivity analysis in
Cao
et al. 2003
. For the adjoint DAE
eq.

19
, the initial conditions must be set in a manner determined by the index of the original DAE as well as the dependency of QoIs over the interval
[0,T]
, and at the terminal time
T
as we show in later sections. For now, we note some properties of the linearized operators. By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\displaystyle f(y,z)-f(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\left[f(sy+(1-s)Y,sz+(1-s)Z)\right]\,ds,

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}ds\,(y-Y)+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}ds\,(z-Z),

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{y}(y-Y)+\bar{f}_{z}(z-Z),

so, we have

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{y}(y-Y)+\bar{f}_{z}(z-Z)=f(y,z)-f(Y,Z),

(20)

similarly,

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{y}(y-Y)+\bar{g}_{z}(z-Z)

\displaystyle=g(y,z)-g(Y,Z).

(21)

Lemma 3.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\langle e^{(y)},\psi^{y}\rangle+\langle e^{(z)},\psi^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\phi^{(y)}(0),e^{(y)}(0)\right)-\left(\phi^{(y)}(T),y(T)-Y(T)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\langle\phi^{(y)},f(Y,Z)-\dot{Y}\rangle+\langle\phi^{(z)},g(Y,Z)\rangle.

(22)

The proof is quite similar to standard proofs for error analysis of ODEs, however, for completeness we include it in the appendix, see
section

7
.

5.4
Electro-Neutral Nernst-Planck Equation (ENNPE) of Ion Transport

Example 5.5
.

We consider a system describing the evolution of the concentrations of two monovalent ions. This system is sometimes called the Electro-Neutral Nernst-Planck Equations
Lewis et al. 2017
;
Sircar et al. 2013
.

\left.\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial c}{\partial t}&=D_{c}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}c}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(c\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)\right),\\
\frac{\partial a}{\partial t}&=D_{a}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}a}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(a\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)\right),\\
c-a&=0,\end{aligned}\right\}

(52)

where
c(x,t)
and
a(x,t)
denote the concentration of Hydrogen ion (cation) and Chloride ion (anion) respectively, and
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}(x,t)
is the electric potential gradient.
No flux (homogeneous Robin) boundary conditions are given by

\left.\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial c}{\partial x}+c\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr|_{x=0}&=\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}+c\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr|_{x=1}&=0,\\
\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}-a\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr|_{x=0}&=\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}-a\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr|_{x=1}&=0.\\
\end{aligned}\right\}

(53)

Initial conditions are
c(x,0)=a(x,0)=2+cos(\pi x)
and
x\in[0,1],t\in(0,T]
, where
D_{c},D_{a}
are the diffusion coefficients of the cation and anion ions respectively.

Now, using the method of separation of variables we have the analytic solution

\displaystyle c(x,t)

\displaystyle=a(x,t)=2+e^{-\pi^{2}D_{eff}t}\cos(\pi x),

(54)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D_{a}-D_{c}}{D_{a}+D_{c}}\right)\frac{-\pi e^{-\pi^{2}D_{eff}t}\sin(\pi x)}{2+e^{-\pi^{2}D_{eff}t}\cos(\pi x)},

(55)

where

D_{eff}=\frac{2D_{c}D_{a}}{D_{c}+D_{a}},\text{ and }w=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}.

Notice that the electric potential
\Psi
is only defined up to an additive constant.
Therefore we utilize
w=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}
as our state variable.

5.4.1
Spatial discretization: Staggered Grid Approach

We use a staggered grid to discretize the spatial derivatives.
This technique has been used in many numerical methods (particularly in computational fluid dynamics) since the development of the Marker and Cell (MAC) method
McKee et al. 2008
.
Consider a set of
N_{s}-1
uniformly spaced grid points in the spatial domain with spacing
\Delta x
.
For the unit interval,

\tilde{x}_{i}=i\Delta x,\quad\quad i=1,2,\dots,N_{s}-1,\quad\quad\Delta x=\frac{1}{N_{s}}.

These points are often called “cell edges” and are the locations where we represent vector valued quantities such as the electric potential gradient
w(\tilde{x}_{i},t)
.
We also introduce a second spatial grid at the so-called “cell centers”

x_{j}=(2j-1)\Delta x/2,\quad j=1,2,\dots,N_{s}.

These points will be used to represent the values of scalar valued quantities such as the ion concentrations (
c(x_{j},t)
and
a(x_{j},t)
).
This staggered grid facilitates the use of centered finite difference approximations to the various spatial derivatives in
eq.

52
and representation of the no flux boundary conditions
eq.

53
.
This leads to the approximation:
C_{j}(t)\approx c(x_{j},t)
,
A_{j}(t)\approx a(x_{j},t)
, and
W_{i}(t)\approx w(\tilde{x}_{i},t)
.
Details are given in
8
Staggered Grid
.
Finally the
ENNPE
becomes a Hessenberg (Pure) index-2 DAE as below,

\left.\begin{aligned} \dot{C}&=D_{c}MC+D_{c}B_{C},\\
\dot{A}&=D_{a}MA-D_{c}B_{A},\\
0&=\Pi(C)-\Pi(A),\end{aligned}\right\}

(56)

where,

M=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&-2&1&\cdots&0\\
0&1&-2&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&-1\end{pmatrix},B_{C}=\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\begin{pmatrix}(C_{1}+C_{2})W_{1}\\
(C_{2}+C_{3})W_{2}-(C_{1}+C_{2})W_{1}\\
(C_{3}+C_{4})W_{3}-(C_{2}+C_{3})W_{2}\\
\vdots\\
-(C_{N_{s}-1}+C_{N_{s}})W_{N_{s}-1}\end{pmatrix},

B_{A}=\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\begin{pmatrix}(A_{1}+A_{2})W_{1}\\
(A_{2}+A_{3})W_{2}-(A_{1}+A_{2})W_{1}\\
(A_{3}+A_{4})W_{3}-(A_{2}+A_{3})W_{2}\\
\vdots\\
-(A_{N_{s}-1}+C_{N_{s}})W_{N_{s}-1}\end{pmatrix},C=\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}\\
C_{2}\\
\vdots\\
C_{N_{s}}\end{pmatrix},A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\\
A_{2}\\
\vdots\\
A_{N_{s}}\end{pmatrix},

and

W=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}\\
W_{2}\\
\vdots\\
W_{N_{s}-1}\end{pmatrix},\Pi(v)=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{N_{s}-1}\end{pmatrix}\text{ for }v=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{N_{s}}\end{pmatrix},

here
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{s}}
, and
B_{C},B_{A},C(t),A(t),v(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
, and
W(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}-1}
.

Initial conditions corresponding to the differential variables are given by:

C_{j}(0)=A_{j}(0)=2+\cos{(\pi x_{j})},\quad\text{ for }j=1,2,3,\dots,N_{s}.

There is a subtle point to be made with regards to the initial condition for the electric potential gradient (the algebraic variable).
For the PDE system
eq.

52
, one can obtain the initial condition by differentiating the constraint equation, substituting the transport equations to eliminate
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}
and
\frac{\partial a}{\partial t}
, solving for
w(x,t)
(this is equivalent to deriving
eq.

55
) and evaluating at
t=0
to yield

w(x,0)=\left.\frac{D_{c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}-D_{a}\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}}{D_{c}c+D_{a}a}\right|_{t=0}.

(57)

This expression can then be evaluated at the points
\tilde{x}_{i}
to derive what we call the “analytic initial condition” for the algebraic variables
W_{i}
.

Alternately, one can perform a similar calculation on the spatially discretized DAE system
eq.

56
: differentiate the constraint equation, eliminate
\dot{C}
and
\dot{A}
, solve for
W
and set
t=0
.
Doing so leads to what we call the “discrete initial condition” corresponding to the algebraic variables
W_{i}
,

\displaystyle W_{l}(0)=\frac{D_{c}\left(\frac{C_{l}-C_{l+1}}{\Delta x}\right)-D_{a}\left(\frac{A_{l}-A_{l+1}}{\Delta x}\right)}{D_{c}\left(\frac{C_{l}+C_{l+1}}{2}\right)+D_{a}\left(\frac{A_{l}+A_{l+1}}{2}\right)}\Bigr|_{t=0},\quad\text{for }l=1,2,3,\cdots,N_{s}-1.

(58)

Notice that
eq.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a novel technique for
a posteriori
error estimates for numerical solution of DAEs.
In particular, our technique may be used to assess error for QoIs involving the differential
or
algebraic state variables, and that are cumulative in time, or evaluated at the terminal time.
Furthermore, our methodology may be applied to the semi-explicit index-1 or Hessenberg index-2 DAEs.
Our technique is based on the formulation and solution of an adjoint DAE, that is a linear problem, making it relatively cost effective to implement.
We also present a second technique which is based on adjoint ODE. This second technique, while being conceptually simpler, is more computationally more expensive. We tested these two techniques experimentally for various types of index-1 and Hessenberg index-2 DAE problems: linear, nonlinear, autonomous, non-autonomous, and PDAE (Electro-Neutral Nernst-Planck Equation). In both techniques we have the accurate error estimate and the effectivity ratio close to one, unless there was catastrophic cancellation resulting in both the computed and exact error being extremely small.
Adjoint ODE approach is too costly for a large scale problem like PDAEs but adjoint DAE works well in this case.

There are a number of future directions which arise from this work. One such direction is extending the analysis to partial differential algebraic equations in such a way that the estimates
identify the error contribution due to the spatial and temporal discretizations, as was done for a PDE in
Chaudhry
et al. 2019
. Another direction is deriving adaptive algorithms based on the
a posteriori
estimates. This requires a careful analysis of the numerical scheme under consideration to quantify the effects of different quadratures, time-stepping choices, projection operators etc. Such analysis for ODEs and PDEs have been carried out in
Chaudhry
et al. 2015
;
Chaudhry
et al. 2017
;
Collins et al. 2015
;
Collins et al. 2014
;
Chaudhry and Collins 2021
, and in future we aim to utilize these ideas to extend the analysis in this work.

8
Staggered Grid

We use relatively standard central difference approximations for the spatial derivative of
c_{x},c,a_{x},
and
a
.
The nonlinear terms
cw
, and
aw
, require multiplication of quantities which “live” at cell centers and those that live at cell edges.
In this case, we average the two cell centered quantities (
c
or
a
) to the cell edge between.
We explicitly show the details of the spatial discretization for
c
below.
The discretization of
a
is completely analogous.

The partial derivative of
c
with respect to
x
at
\tilde{x}_{j}
is approximated as follows:

c_{x}(x_{j})\approx\frac{c(\tilde{x}_{j}+\Delta x/2)-c(\tilde{x}_{j}-\Delta x/2)}{\Delta x}=\frac{c(x_{j+1})-c(x_{j})}{\Delta x}.

That is to say that first derivatives (which naturally live at cell edges) are calculated by applying centered differences to quantities defined at cell centers.
For the nonlinear terms, we average concentrations to the cell edges to yield

c(\tilde{x}_{j})w(\tilde{x_{j}})\approx\frac{c(\tilde{x}_{j}+\Delta x/2)+c(\tilde{x}_{j}-\Delta x/2)}{2}w(\tilde{x}_{j})=\frac{c(x_{j+1})+c(x_{j})}{2}w(\tilde{x}_{j})

(60)

We can now apply centered differences to the above expressions to yield the following expressions for
j=2,3,\dots,N_{s}-1
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(c_{x}+cw\right)\right|_{x_{j}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(c_{x}(x_{j}+\Delta x/2)+c(x_{j}+\Delta x/2)w(x_{j}+\Delta x/2)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(c_{x}(x_{j}-\Delta x/2)+c(x_{j}-\Delta x/2)w(x_{j}-\Delta x/2)\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(c_{x}(\tilde{x}_{j})+c(\tilde{x})w(\tilde{x}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(c_{x}(\tilde{x}_{j-1})+c(\tilde{x}_{j-1})w(\tilde{x}_{j-1})\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{c(x_{j+1})-c(x_{j})}{\Delta x}+\frac{c(x_{j+1})+c(x_{j})}{2}w(\tilde{x}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{c(x_{j})-c(x_{j-1})}{\Delta x}+\frac{c(x_{j})+c(x_{j-1})}{2}w(\tilde{x}_{j-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\left(c(x_{j+1})-2c(x_{j})+c(x_{j-1})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\Big(\left(c(x_{j})+c(x_{j+1})\right)w(\tilde{x}_{j})-\left(c(x_{j})+c(x_{j-1})\right)w(\tilde{x}_{j-1})\Big).

For
j=1
and
j=N_{s}
, we need to utilize the no flux (homogeneous Robin) boundary conditions
eq.

53
.
These can be expressed as

\left.c_{x}+cw\right|_{x=0}=\left.c_{x}+cw\right|_{\tilde{x}_{0}}=0,

and

\left.c_{x}+cw\right|_{x=1}=\left.c_{x}+cw\right|_{\tilde{x}_{N_{s}}}=0.

Now, for
j=1
, i.e., the scheme at the left boundary of the modified domain is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(c_{x}+cw\right)\right|_{x_{1}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(c_{x}(\tilde{x}_{1})+c(\tilde{x})w(\tilde{x}_{1})\right)-0,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{c(x_{2})-c(x_{1})}{\Delta x}+\frac{c(x_{2})+c(x_{1})}{2}w(\tilde{x}_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\left(c(x_{2})-c(x_{1}))\right)+\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\Big(\left(c(x_{1})+c(x_{2})\right)w(\tilde{x}_{1})\Big).

Similarly, for
j=N_{s}
, i.e., the scheme at the right boundary of the modified domain is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(c_{x}+cw\right)\right|_{x_{N_{s}}}

\displaystyle\approx 0-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(c_{x}(\tilde{x}_{N_{s}-1})+c(\tilde{x}_{N_{s}-1})w(\tilde{x}_{N_{s}-1})\right),

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{c(x_{N_{s}})-c(x_{N_{s}-1})}{\Delta x}+\frac{c(x_{N_{s}})+c(x_{N_{s}-1})}{2}w(\tilde{x}_{N_{s}-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\left(-c(x_{N_{s}})+c(x_{N_{s}-1})\right)+\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\Big(-\left(c(x_{N_{s}})+c(x_{N_{s}-1})\right)w(\tilde{x}_{N_{s}-1})\Big).

A completely analogous discretization is used for the the anion
a
.
These finite difference discretizations are used to construct the right hand side of the differential equation for the differential variables in
eq.

56
.

In order to construct the algebraic constraint for
eq.

56
we notice that the above finite difference equations are conservative.
This implies that

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}c(x_{j},t)\text{ and }\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}a(x_{j},t),

are both constants independent of time.
This, in turn, means that if
c(x_{j},0)-a(x_{j},0)=0
at the initial time, and
c(x_{j})-a(x_{j})=0
for
j=1,2,\ldots N_{s}-1
, then it must be true that
c(x_{N_{s}})-a(x_{N_{s}})=0
.
Put another way, if we use a conservative discretization and use initial conditions which satisfy the constraint at all spatial locations, then we do not need to explicitly enforce the constraint at all locations.
We need only enforce it in the first
N_{s}-1
grid cells, and we are guranteed that it will be satisfied in the final grid cell “for free”.
Thus, we do not explicitly require that
C-A=0
in
eq.

56
.
Rather, we require that
\Pi(C-A)=0
, where
\Pi
is the linear operator which simply evaluates a variable at all but the final cell center.
