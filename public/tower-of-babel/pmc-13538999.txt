Understanding Patient Navigation Functions: A Qualitative Exploration of Navigators’ Perspectives in Alberta, Canada
Authors: Sandra Berzins, Fedir Razumenko, Christine A Walsh, Karen L Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient navigation (PN) exemplifies a complex health intervention, involving multiple interacting components yet tailored to individual patient contexts and needs. Identifying functions and processes of complex interventions like PN enables a better understanding of how they are meant to work, moving beyond descriptions of program components such as tasks and services. With broad variation across programs, the navigator role can look quite different depending on context.ObjectiveTo explore the functions of PN from the perspective of patient navigators in Alberta, Canada.DesignQualitative descriptive approach.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with patient navigators working in a range of patient-facing program types and settings across Alberta. Interviews were recorded and transcribed, then coded using NVivo 12TM software. A thematic content analysis approach guided data analysis.ResultsWe interviewed 29 navigators from a range of PN programs including cancer care, primary care networks, community agencies, and youth to adult transition. Nursing was the most common educational background of participants (n=12), followed by social work (n=9). Themes generated through thematic analysis highlighted three core functions of PN that collectively define its role as a complex, adaptive intervention: 1) creating a safety net for patients (theme 1), by addressing health and psychosocial needs (theme 1a) or by reducing barriers to care (theme 1b), 2) using advocacy and skill-building to enable patients to become more active participants in managing their health (theme 2), and 3) building relationships to strengthen patient trust and engagement (theme 3).ConclusionsPN is a complex, multi-functional intervention that enhances patient care through relational, practical, and systemic support. Defining and understanding these key functions provides clarity and is critical for effective implementation and integration of PN into health systems and to inform future implementation and evaluation of PN programs in Canada.Keywords: patient navigation, health services research, complex health interventions, thematic analysis

Introduction

Patient navigation (PN) exemplifies a complex health intervention, involving multiple interacting components yet tailored to individual patient contexts and needs. The PN construct developed from an intervention in the United States that aimed to address racial and economic disparities in breast cancer diagnosis. 1 Individuals called “patient navigators” worked with patients to identify barriers to screening, then connected them with resources to address these barriers. 2 The navigation field has rapidly expanded and evolved across North America with programs in a variety of clinical fields ranging from, for example, cancer screening 3 to obstetrics care, 4 across a wide range of geographic settings, including rural communities to urban hospitals, and delivered by navigators of different backgrounds, including nursing, 5 social work, 6 and non-health professional peer supports. 7 With the broad variation across PN programs, the navigator role can look quite different depending on context.

Despite this variation, the research literature has identified several recurring domains of PN including care coordination, barrier mitigation, patient education, and psychosocial support,8,9 though these elements are often described as discrete tasks or services rather than higher order functions that explain how navigation works. 10 However, complexity theory suggests that understanding a complex system requires attention to how components interact and how those interactions are shaped by context. 11 Recognizing PN as a complex intervention therefore requires clarification of their functions and mechanisms, rather than just their components. 12 Core functions are defined as “theory and evidence-driven purposes of an intervention” 13 ; they often require multiple, interacting tasks to enact and may span time, settings and stakeholders. 14 Form, in comparison, refers to how functions are implemented and the specific activities and structures through which core purposes are enacted. 13 Identifying core functions is necessary to understand and articulate how complex interventions like PN are meant to work, 13 moving beyond descriptions of program form and components such as tasks, services, and target populations. 15 This understanding enables tailoring of navigation programs to different contexts while maintaining fidelity to their core functions. 16

While the PN literature is replete with studies that describe navigation models, services, purpose, program implementation, and navigator training, these tend to be limited to specific settings or areas of clinical care,5,6,10,17-27 lacking a unifying conceptualization of core functions of PN that can be applied across different settings and contexts. To address this gap, we conducted a qualitative study of navigators working across diverse programs and settings in Alberta, Canada. The significance of our study is two-fold. First, we aim to move beyond describing navigation tasks or roles to identify core functions. Such an understanding is critical to understand, evaluate and compare PN programs, which may appear quite different on the surface (e.g., with different navigator training backgrounds, and different clinical areas served) yet share a similar purpose. Second, by examining navigator perspectives across a deliberately broad range of programs that target different settings, clinical areas, and populations, we aim to generate a conceptualization of PN that clarifies how it works as a complex intervention that can be applied across contexts.

Methods

Study Design

We used a qualitative descriptive approach 28 to explore different navigators’ perceptions of the functions and scope of PN. Semi-structured interviews were used to collect information, and data were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach. 29

Study Recruitment and Participants

Patient navigator participants were recruited through several sources. An initial program contact list was shared by the research team of a related study that focused on patient experience of PN programs in Alberta. 30 This list included a range of program types, settings, and locations around the province, including youth to adult transition programs, Cancer Care, Primary Care Networks (PCN), and community agencies. Community agencies included a variety of not-for-profit organizations within the health and social services sectors, as well public agencies such as police services. We verified with these agencies that they had health-related patient navigation programs prior to recruitment. Navigators from PN programs were contacted by phone and/or by email using a standard script. This recruitment strategy was expanded using snowball sampling; the interviewer asked participants to refer other navigators for our study, from either the PN program in which they work or other PN programs across Alberta. We additionally conducted a Google search for PN programs in Alberta and contacted these programs using publicly available emails and/or phone numbers available on their websites. Each participant was provided with a $25 CAD gift card honorarium. Recruitment continued until information power was achieved 31 in relation to generating distinct themes relevant to our research objective. Information power was selected over saturation on theoretical grounds. In reflexive thematic analysis, the assumption is that meaning is not inherent in data but constructed through interpretative engagement with the data. 32 Saturation, which assumes a fixed point at which no new themes emerge, is therefore difficult to reconcile with an approach in which meaning is contingent on interpretation rather than discoverable through coding alone. We acknowledge that information power does not eliminate the subjectivity inherent in determining when sufficient information has been reached, as this judgment remains dependent on our assessment of data quality and analytical depth.31,33

Criteria for study inclusion were that participants must: 1) currently or have worked in a “navigator” position for an Alberta-based, health-related program for adults 18 years or older (or older youth transitioning to adult care); and 2) have been in a navigator position for a minimum of one year. Exclusion criteria included navigators whose roles were not patient-facing (e.g. only managed referrals for physicians without contact with patients), and navigators from private, for-profit organizations, as these services would not be insured by the universal health coverage offered in Canada and therefore not widely accessible. In addition, we did not include navigators who worked for programs serving Indigenous communities exclusively. Indigenous navigation operates within a distinct cultural and epistemological framework, including relational and holistic ways of knowing, that requires meaningful community engagement and the incorporation of Indigenous worldviews from the outset of study design. Our study did not incorporate an Indigenous worldview 34 or Indigenous ways of knowing, nor did we engage with the Indigenous community in advance to address potential ethical issues related to the Indigenous context. The cultural considerations of these programs, and the ways by which they address the impacts of colonialism in Western healthcare systems, require a separate, dedicated study that involves Indigenous community partners. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Calgary’s Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board.

Data Collection and Analysis

A semi-structured interview guide was developed to explore participant understanding of and experiences as a navigator, services they provided, as well as perspectives about the role and influence of navigation on patients, providers, and health care systems (Supplemental File 1). Themes generated from a related but separate study of patient perspectives on patient navigation 30 were used to inform the interview guide questions and probes. Interviews were conducted by FR (male health services researcher), SB (female health services researcher) or KT (female clinician and health services researcher) using the video-conferencing platforms Zoom or MS-Teams, with each being no more than one hour in duration. Interviews were audio and video recorded with consent of the participant. None of the research team members had worked with any of the participants previously. Demographic data for each participant were obtained at the end of each interview – these included: age, gender, ethnicity, years of experience in the PN role, professional background and training especially relating to PN. Interview audio recordings were transcribed using either the videoconferencing platform’s transcription function, or a web-based transcription service (https://www.rev.com/). Each transcript was reviewed for accuracy by at least one study team member (FR or SB), and personal identifiers were removed from transcripts. Transcripts were uploaded into NVivo 12TM to facilitate coding.

A thematic content analysis approach 29 was chosen to guide data analysis. To begin, two members of the study team (SB,FR) read the interview transcripts, in duplicate, to familiarize data, noting potential patterns. After reviewing the first three transcripts, an initial coding framework was developed relevant to the research objectives. Each transcript was then independently coded by two researchers (SB,FR) and reviewed by a third investigator (KT). Coding was reviewed and revised by the research team (SB,FR,KT) after the first three interviews were coded, then again at regular intervals as coding progressed. If new codes were added, previously analyzed transcripts were re-reviewed to look for information that fit under these new codes using constant comparison. When coding of all interview transcripts was complete, codes were organized into broader categories to identify themes related to PN functions. The research team (SB,FR,KT) met regularly to discuss, refine, and modify these themes, and to determine how they fit together within a broader narrative structure. Each theme was named and key examples from the transcripts were extracted to illustrate the themes. Finally, all participants were asked to review the themes as well as their included quotes for member checking, 35 to ensure validity of our interpretation of results and themes developed. We used the COREQ reporting checklist 36 to draft this manuscript (Supplemental File 2).

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 29 navigators from across Alberta were interviewed between March 2024- March 2025. Most participants were female; only one participant was male. No other potential male participants were identified through our recruitment strategy. The majority were between 30-49 years old (n=17, 59%), while 21% (n=6) were between 50-59 years of age (Table 1). PN programs were grouped by organizational setting: non-profit community organizations (n=4), provincial health programs (i.e., Cancer Care Alberta, n=10), and PCNs (n=8). PCNs are multidisciplinary teams that deliver primary health care services in Alberta. 37 Other programs included those that supported transitions of care, such as from hospital to home or from youth to adult care (n=5), and health navigation programs within police services (n=2).

Thematic Analysis

Themes generated from the interview data highlight three core functions by which navigation influences patient well-being and outcomes from the navigators’ perspectives. One of the core functions of PN is perceived to be the creation of a safety net for patients, to prevent them from falling through the cracks (theme 1). This is achieved by addressing the patient’s needs and barriers (theme 1a) as well as building bridges within and across systems to allow access to timely and tailored care appropriate to patient needs (theme 1b). In addition to safety net creation, two other core functions of PN involve using advocacy and skill-building to empower patients to manage their own health (theme 2) and building relationships to strengthen patient trust and engagement (theme 3). Supporting quotations for each theme can be found in Table 2.

Themes and Exemplar Participant Quotations

Theme 1: By Addressing Health and Psychosocial Needs and Barriers to Care, Patient Navigation Creates a Safety Net

A primary function of PN (theme 1) across the different settings, programs, and target populations is the creation of a safety net around the patient to prevent them from falling through the cracks in the care system. Navigators noted that a safety net is required due to the lack of another provider in the system who can proactively ensure that the patient has the capacity to follow through with their recommended care and assess if the needed care is progressing as planned:

“The way things are with… people not having family doctors, losing family doctors, not knowing where to turn, long waits, all these things. Some people … need that extra… safety net behind them to help and catch them for directing things. If a person isn’t advocating strongly for themselves in their health journey, they can very easily fall through the cracks. So, to have that … a navigator there as an extra support for guiding them through that time of their life or that diagnosis or whatever it is. I think that's really important for people nowadays.” (Int_23, 40-49 yrs)

There is a need to create a safety net for patients, given the lack of any key contact person otherwise available for patients in the traditional health care system if patients have questions, concerns, or need guidance or direction. Patient navigators fill this gap - being described as the ‘go-to person’ in the system:

“We’re kind of the go-to or go-between person for some of those services because we don’t have them locally.” (Int_25, 30-39 yrs)

Another reason a safety net is required is that some patients do not have the capacity, means, motivation, and/or opportunity to prioritize and manage their health. Healthcare providers (HCPs) often provide patients with recommendations, resources, or education. By building a safety net around the patient, the navigator ensures that the patient can follow through with these by addressing patient needs and barriers (either by directly meeting these needs or by building bridges to connections and resources), skill building and empowerment, and by building relationships to increase trust and engagement:

“Honestly, humanising the experience is so crucial and so key. Because sometimes you know it is in these areas, it is very clinical, it is very dry and a lot of times the information that is coming to them is not really comprehended. And you’ll get that person that will just nod just because they’re so uncomfortable and they’re just ready for the conversation to be over.” (Int_12, 30-39 yrs)

The ways by which PN creates a safety net are reflected in subthemes 1a and 1b. Subtheme 1a is that PN creates a safety net through meeting patient health and psychosocial needs, thereby addressing critical barriers to care. Navigators engage with the patient to identify and remove competing practical and logistical needs, so that the patient can focus on their health and wellbeing. These needs are often due, at least in part, to non-clinical or non-health related issues. The scope of PN extends beyond the clinical sphere; that is, navigators must identify and address not only biologic but also psychosocial needs in their needs assessments:

“So, intervening at that time as quickly as possible, assessing for prescription coverage, time off work, because often they can’t focus on their diagnosis when they’re worried about how they’re going to pay their bills. So, it’s kind of that holistic approach.” (Int_9,40-49 yrs)

That is, patient navigators assess and work with their patients from the perspective that they are “whole persons” with multiple needs. This holistic approach differs from the typical medical focus on a single health condition or disease that requires treatment; it includes a broad focus on social determinants of health and basic needs:

“We tend to do a lot of everything with our patients. You know, it varies from navigating the healthcare system with them, to navigating the bus, public transit, to helping them find resources in the community, whether it be food resources, financial resources, it can be for some patients helping them find childcare, work related resources.” (Int_28, missing age category)

Because of the broad nature of their scope of the work, there is a need for navigators to be flexible, tailoring their services to meet the unique needs of individual patients. Navigators also described the importance of troubleshooting as problems arise, as there was no single approach to meet the many varied types of patient needs:

“What don't I do? […] I think it's one of the things about navigation that's good- every day is kind of a little different because every family is a little unique and what their needs are is unique. So I get to connect with all kinds of people in the community, from housing to income support, to caregiving support, to primary care, to counseling, mental health, etc.” (Int_17, 60-69 yrs)

For some navigation programs - particularly those that were that were community based or part of a primary care network, the services provided were based on care goals that were co-developed with patients. Across all programs, navigation was described as a self-limiting intervention that ended either when patient goals were met, or when their treatment/transition program was complete.

Another way by which PN creates a safety net is building bridges within and across systems to allow access to timely and tailored care appropriate to patient needs (subtheme 1b). A navigator, after identifying the patient’s needs and barriers, can work to directly meet and address them (subtheme 1a), or alternatively, they may connect patients to resources, organizations, and/or people who may be better able to meet these needs (subtheme 1b). Patient navigators therefore note the importance of being familiar with the resources available and linking patients to the appropriate resources:

“Patients are really happy to hear that they have someone who can be that middle person and connect them to the resources that they didn't even knew existed for them” (Int_15,30-39 yrs)

This bridging function helps fill gaps in the safety net. Bridges built may be both within the healthcare system, as well as across multiple systems (e.g. primary to tertiary health care, social, and community organizations and services), in recognition that patient needs tend not to be exclusively health related:

“We’re continually communicating with referring providers, diagnostic imaging services, what support groups are out there. I’m always just trying to network and reach out and try and keep abreast of what’s available and what’s out there. And so just a real pivot point between the patient and what’s available, I think.” (Int_22, 50-59 yrs)

Navigators noted that due to the unique needs of patients, bridge building needed to be tailored to the individual patients. The navigator must identify the connections for specific services that need to be made, and these vary from one patient to the next:

“So, as a navigator here, I provide that ear to listen, to actually understand what our patient is looking for. Again, then I also offer to direct them to any services that we have here at the PCN.”(Int_14, 30-39 yrs)

Theme 2: Using Skill-Building and Advocacy to Empower Patients to Manage Their Own Health

Beyond meeting competing needs and addressing competing priorities, as well as building intra- and inter- system bridges, navigators support patients to become more active participants in their care through skill-building and advocacy. These two key functions work together to empower patients and enhance their ability to navigate the healthcare system effectively. For many navigators, their role in empowering patients lies in skill building to enhance patient autonomy, confidence, and ability to self-manage their health. Skill-building methods used range from providing workshops, sharing information, to coaching, role-modelling, and one on one instruction. Topics focused on range from health literacy, self-management, and communication skills to how to complete government forms:

“We do workshops for teens to help them develop medical self-management and self-advocacy skills and know what they need to know as they move to adult healthcare. And then on the other side, it's preparing the parents for their role of being a support person rather than the primary caregiver. It's helping one pick up the reins and helping one learn to hand them over.” (Int_17, 60-69 yrs)

Participants emphasized that navigation empowered patients not only through skill-building but also through enhancing the patient’s confidence, motivation, and capacity to advocate for themselves. Advocacy helps patients feel heard and respected, which is critical for empowerment and in turn promotes patient centred care. They noted that an important facet of their work was to “help patients have a voice” (Int_11, 40-49 yrs) - such as by asking questions or by expressing themselves if they disagreed with a HCP’s recommendations:

“Okay, we help the patient make their goals and we'll reach out, we'll be the advocate or, to advocate for the patient with the physician. So, if the patient's saying, you know, I'm not being heard by the physician or there’s only a limited amount of time, it's a five-minute appointment, I have all these things. So, you’re working with them, you are supporting them to maybe articulate their concerns better and more efficiently in the five minutes they have. I would call that advocacy and helping the patient build self-efficacy and self-empowerment. And ultimately building self-management of their condition.” (Int_3, 20-29 yrs)

The focus of this advocacy work is different for each patient, reflecting their individual needs and goals. Particularly in the community PN programs, a focus of the work often moves to increasing patients’ self-efficacy as ‘holes’ in the safety net are filled in and their care goals are met. Navigation, at this point, is to facilitate independence for patients to be active participants in the management of their own health and well-being:

“For me, advocating for, and or with is going to someone else on behalf of, or with that patient and family to help the other person understand what their needs are and help facilitate them getting the support that they need. Coaching is helping them tap into skills they already have. It's just kind of being their cheerleader and reminding them of the skills that they have, whereas, when you're advocating for, you’re, doing it on their behalf, and also then helping them develop those skills that they maybe don't already have, to speak up on behalf of themselves and do it in a way that’s going to facilitate the other person hearing them. And then wanting to help meet their needs.” (Int_17, 60-69 yrs)

By combining skill-building and advocacy, patients can become more active participants in their care, and empowerment becomes more sustainable as they are able to advocate for themselves.

Theme 3: Relationship Building to Develop and Improve Trust and Engagement

This theme illustrates how building trust through relationship building is foundational to safety net creation as well as to empowering patients to manage their own health and reach their care goals. The engagement that comes from creating connection and trust is a foundational contribution to the safety net development.

“I think that the relationship is a huge part of how the work gets done, particularly for families that are struggling. I mean that all depends on engagement. I have patients who are in that category who are just not interested yet, but I've also had success in telling those patients, like, ’you come back whenever you want, I'm not mad at you that you didn't come see me’.” (Int_20,40-49 yrs)

Personal connection is a key building block in the development of a trusting relationship between the navigator and patient. This connection helps to build rapport, which in turn can facilitate engagement, support behavior change, and facilitate progress towards patient care goals.

“And so essentially, you're just [there to] develop rapport, build relationships, and personalize their care plan on where they are in that moment 'cause today might be different than tomorrow and it might be different in three months and it might be different in six months, right?” (Int_2, 40-49 yrs)

Relationship building between navigators and patients is not only foundational for the first two functions of safety net creation and advocacy/skill building, but also an important function of how navigation work is achieved; it improves patient willingness and motivation to engage with the healthcare system and its providers. Establishing this relationship facilitates the building of trust between the patient and the navigator, as well as between the patient, broader healthcare system, and its providers. Navigators noted that relationship-building, regardless of how it was operationalized, worked to improve overall patient interactions with their broader healthcare team. As noted, this was in part due to the building of trust between the patient and the navigator, who worked within, and in some ways represented, the broader healthcare system:

“The whole initial point is being engagement, to connect them so that you can use that connection to sort of move with them through the system or wherever they need to go.” (Int_20,40-49 yrs)

The improvement in patient interaction and communication with other HCPs was also due though to the navigator making the healthcare journey a smoother and calmer one, through the provision of emotional support. Navigation is noted to reduce anxiety by being “like a virtual handhold” (Int_9,40-49 yrs). Navigators described different manifestations of what this patient-navigator relationship may entail. For some, relationship building hinged simply on accessibility – being a safe person to call to “provide relatability and compassion and empathy” (Int_5, 20-29 yrs). For others, navigators were much more than a reliable, accessible listening ear. Rather, they described the navigator as being much more intensive and supportive, having the role of a companion, to ‘walk alongside’ (Int_3, 20-29 yrs) their patients/clients as they move through their healthcare journey. By being present, for example at medical appointments, this provides opportunities for the navigator to monitor patient care and provide additional support if needed:

“But I think people just feel more supported when they have a navigator with them because they know that they can reach out and that they're going to be there to help them get through the situation.” (Int_18, 20-29 yrs)

Secondary Functions of PN

While participants outlined that the three main functions of PN are to build a safety net, skill-building and empowerment, and relationship-building to improve trust and engagement of patients, other secondary functions of PN were noted. These, however, were not felt to represent core functions of PN nor their primary purpose; rather, these were noted to be complementary and collateral potential benefits.

First at a health systems level, participants felt that navigation, by reducing barriers to care, may reduce overall burden to the healthcare system by helping patients receive holistic, timely, appropriate and necessary care – thereby reducing unnecessary acute care visits, preventing delays in care, as well as deterioration in health that could lead to preventable hospital admissions and readmissions. Barriers to obtain needed supports in the community for patients are reduced concurrently with the health care system interactions:

“The idea is to connect them with the appropriate place at the right time. So, it's a less burden on the healthcare system in the sense of they're not accessing all these different places that they never should have.” (Int_16, 30-39 yrs)

As a result of reducing burden on the healthcare system, navigators perceived there to be acute care resources that are then “[freed] up for those who actually need them”, thereby improving access and care for other patients. (Int_17, 60-69 yrs). Second, some navigators, particularly those within provincial health programs, described that PN improves patient care by broadening the competencies of other HCPs and enable them to better meet the needs of patients. With navigators supporting HCPs and staff to learn about the additional non-medical needs of patients (mainly related to social determinants of health) and types of resources available, they become better equipped to provide the services needed by their patients even when patient navigators are not involved:

“My goal is to provide that [consultation] to healthcare providers and build their capacity to provide more information to patients and families who have the ability to navigate on their own.” (Int_17, 60-69 yrs)

In these ways, navigators perceived that PN had secondary functions in improving the healthcare system as a whole, benefitting the broader population beyond those served directly by PN.

Discussion

This study was guided by the conceptual distinction between core functions and forms of complex interventions, where core functions represent the underlying purposes or mechanisms of action and forms refer to the specific activities used to operationalize them in local contexts.13,14 Consistent with our research objective and study design, we have focused specifically on identifying core functions, stopping short of mapping the specific PN activities (i.e., forms) used to enact them. Our thematic analysis of the navigator interview responses highlights three core functions by which PN influences patient well-being and outcomes. Foundationally, PN creates a “safety net” around patients by addressing health and psychosocial needs and barriers to care, as well as by building bridges to connect patients to providers and services within and across systems. Second, advocacy and skill-building represent an empowerment function that supports patients to become more active participants in managing their own health. Third, relationship-building represents a relational engagement function, that strengthens patient trust and engagement with healthcare providers and systems. These three core functions interact dynamically to facilitate timely, appropriate and necessary care.18,38 They operate as mechanisms of change that are enacted through diverse and context-specific forms across navigation programs.

Much of the research in PN to date has focused on understanding the navigator role,39,40 skills needed 41 and categorizing services or tasks.42,43 Within a function-form framework, these elements can be understood as forms; that is, they are the observable activities through which navigation is delivered. 13 There is often some conflation of identity or task checklists with core navigation functions reported.44,45 One function may involve multiple tasks (for example, the creation of a safety net, as a core function, may involve dozens of different tasks depending on the case) and skills needed, while a single task may relate to several functions. There is however an emerging body of literature, using predominantly qualitative methods, that aims to explore PN functions beyond the tasks that are completed.46-50 For example, studies in chronic pain and in breast cancer have noted common key aspects of navigation including the provision of person centred and individualized care; they also describe the importance of navigators in coordinating care, providing empathetic and compassionate support, and in fostering relationships.46,48 Funk and Hounslow similarly note the importance of PN in individual empowerment and education, with less emphasis on advocacy. 47 Despite similarities across these qualitative studies, an integrative review of navigation for people experiencing long term physical disabilities reported that different mechanisms may be in play for different types of navigation goals or outcomes. 50 For example, educating and managing are important if navigation focuses on health conditions, while motivating and coordinating are important in tailoring support for goal-related outcomes; relationship building and advocacy are key if the focus is on more holistic health outcomes. 50

The current literature on PN functions tends to be specific to certain areas of clinical care, 49 and it is therefore unclear to what extent the reported functions in the literature span the different clinical care areas. There have also been different findings across these qualitative studies – potentially because of different contexts such as navigator training backgrounds and professional experience (e.g. nurse navigation vs lay navigation). For example, Funk et al. suggests that the primary role of navigators working with older adults is empowerment, 47 whereas Miles et al. conclude that a key role of community-based navigators is a community resource advocate and care consensus builder. The value of our study is therefore in synthesizing overarching, high level PN functions, from the perspectives of navigators, that span different contexts and settings such that the functions identified are not specific to any single setting but may be applicable to multiple contexts. That is, the distilling of PN into its essential elements is a necessary foundational step by which to adapt and tailor programs across different populations settings (form), while staying true to overarching principles and functions. The navigator participants in our study worked in a range of clinical areas and settings in Alberta, Canada, from community nonprofit organizations to provincial cancer care programs, and represented a wide variety of educational backgrounds. Despite these varied and diverse backgrounds and programs, how PN worked to address patient needs and improve access to care remained very similar.

The core functions identified in this current study show how PN is a cohesive intervention, despite its complexity and the different ways by which it is operationalized. These key PN functions may define the navigator role, and may be an important way to distinguish navigation from other related healthcare roles such as case management and coordination. 23 Furthermore, identifying core functions has important implication for the adaptation and scale up of PN programs. Because functions represent the essential mechanisms of an intervention, they can be preserved, to ensure fidelity to what PN is and what it aims to do, while also allowing flexibility in how they are operationalized across different contexts. 51

Limitations

An important strength of our study is the diversity of programs across Alberta represented by the navigators’ interviews, spanning different contexts, clinical areas, and target populations. There are however limitations that must be acknowledged. First, despite our comprehensive multi-modal recruitment strategy, we were not able to recruit navigators from all possible clinical areas. It is therefore possible that there are unique functions within specific program areas that are not identified or described by this study. A second, related, limitation is the predominance of nursing backgrounds in our sample. It is unclear the extent to which the predominance of nurse navigators is due to our sampling approach or broader workforce patterns. However, the core functions and themes identified did not appear to differ based on navigators’ professional background. Third, we included navigators from health-related programs that were self-reported or self-labelled to be “navigation”, as long as they were patient-facing, without adjudicating whether such programs were truly PN, given the lack of a widely accepted operational definition of PN. While this approach was necessary from a feasibility perspective, it may have led both to the inclusion of potentially very heterogeneous programs with different aims, as well as the inability to capture individuals who deliver navigation services informally (without an explicit “navigator” title). Fourth, only one of the 29 participants was male. This likely reflects some degree of under-recruitment of males, the extent of which is difficult to determine. Nursing and social work, which are common training backgrounds of patient navigators, are over-represented by females. For example, in Canada, over 90% of nurses are female. 52 Our sample may therefore partly reflect workforce composition rather than a recruitment gap alone. However, systematic data on navigator gender are limited, as demographic characteristics of navigators are inconsistently reported in the PN literature. Finally, our interpretation of functions is based on participant perspectives and may not capture or be representative of all mechanisms of action across settings. We have also not identified the forms by which these functions are operationalized. Future work can build on the work by systematically cataloguing the specific activities through which navigators enact these core functions across organizational settings, mapping these onto a function-and-form matrix for PN, similar to what has been done for other complex health interventions. 14 Such work may be best pursued through a scoping review or environmental scan, given the wide variation in activities across PN settings and the need to capture this breadth systematically.

Conclusion

Navigation is a complex, multi-functional intervention that enhances patient care through relational, practical, and systemic support. Creating a safety net for patients by addressing health and psychosocial needs as well as reducing barriers to care, using advocacy and skill-building to enable patients to become more active participants in managing their health, and building relationships to strengthen patient trust and engagement, are core functions that transcend navigation settings and navigator type. Defining and understanding these key functions is critical for effective implementation and integration of PN into health systems and to inform future design, implementation, and evaluation of PN programs. Future studies can build on these findings by examining the link between PN functions, the activities used to enact them, and program outcomes. Standardized descriptions of PN functions would support this work in two ways: they enable measurement and comparison across studies, even where programs have adapted delivery for different populations, and they help researchers identify which programs are meaningfully comparable. This is an important contribution given that programs sharing the “patient navigation” label may differ substantially in what navigators actually do.
