Morphology-based classification program of Achilles tendon ruptures using magnetic resonance imaging: a mapping study
Authors: Ao Sun, Yuan Cao, Zengzhen Cui, Shan Gao, Jixing Fan, Gao Si, Yun Tian, Fang Zhou, Tengjiao Zhu, Yang Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAchilles tendon rupture (ATR) is a common and disabling injury, especially among athletes, and typically requires surgical intervention for optimal recovery. The assessment of tendon stump morphology, traditionally reliant on two-dimensional ultrasound imaging, can be limited in its accuracy and fails to provide a comprehensive understanding of tendon shape and structure, which impedes surgical planning (minimally invasive versus open surgery). While magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers more detailed and three-dimensional visualization, no standardized classification system has been established based on MRI for guiding clinical treatment decisions.MethodsThis retrospective observational study was conducted between 2020 and 2023. We analyzed the records of 1,525 patients diagnosed with ATRs. After excluding patients without MRI or those whose scans did not meet our high-resolution criteria, 262 patients were included. MRI scans were independently reviewed by two musculoskeletal radiologists and two orthopedic surgeons, with discrepancies resolved by consensus, to generate three-dimensional models of the tendon rupture morphology. Boundary lines of the ruptured tendons were extracted and processed in the coronal and sagittal planes. Based on this detailed mapping, a novel classification system was developed and assessed for inter- and intra-rater reliability, and surgical procedures were analyzed in relation to tendon stump morphology.ResultsMRI was successfully utilized to assess the morphology of ATR tendon stumps, revealing detailed three-dimensional mapping that revealed consistent patterns in tendon shape and location. The mapping led to the proposal of a novel classification system, which categorizes tendon stumps into four types: Type A (neat, 9.2%), Type B (partially neat, 68.7%), Type C (irregular, 20.2%), and Type D (insertion, 1.9%). The classification demonstrated substantial inter- and intraobserver reliability (mean Cohen’s kappa = 0.846 and 0.847, respectively). Surgical approaches are tailored based on these classifications, with minimally invasive surgery preferred for neat or partially neat stumps (Type A and Type B), and open repair for irregular stumps (Type C and Type D), with anchor devices used to reattach the tendon to the calcaneus (Type D).ConclusionThis study is the first to systematically map the location and morphology of Achilles tendon stumps via magnetic resonance imaging. Based on these findings, we proposed a reproducible classification system, although prospective validation against surgical and functional outcomes is still required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09795-w.

Background

The Achilles tendon, approximately 12–15 cm in length, is the largest and strongest tendon in the human body, yet it remains the most frequently injured [1]. The incidence of Achilles tendon ruptures (ATRs) has increased significantly in recent decades, with reported rates ranging from nine to 37 cases per 100,000 individuals [2]. When left untreated or inadequately managed, ATRs can lead to a significant decline in heel–rise function, altered gait, and severe impairments in daily living and occupational activities [3]. While multiple treatment options for ATRs exist, the choice of optimal management remains contentious [4, 5]. Numerous randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated that surgical intervention reduces the risk of rerupture, accelerates the return to work, and increases the likelihood of resuming sports activities [6]. Consequently, surgical repair is widely adopted.

However, the choice of surgical technique for ATR repair remains a topic of ongoing debate, with various approaches available depending on the location and morphology of the tendon stump. For example, the Integra™ (Plainsboro, NJ) Achillon® system is recommended for ruptures occurring 2 to 8 cm proximal to the tendon insertion [7]. The channel-assisted minimally invasive repair system is suitable for cases where the distal stump is at least 2 cm from the insertion site [8]. Similarly, the limited small incision with a simple Krackow suture is appropriate for acute, closed ATRs with a rupture located 2 to 6 cm from the insertion site [9]. Imaging methods such as ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging are commonly used to assess rupture sites, but traditional two-dimensional imaging lacks the depth and precision required to guide surgical decisions effectively.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides superior detail and enables three-dimensional visualization of tendon morphology; however, few studies have used this technology to classify ATR tendon stump characteristics systematically. This gap in the literature has created a need for a comprehensive classification system that can inform clinical treatment strategies more accurately and consistently.

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis with three interrelated objectives: (1) to apply MRI to generate detailed mapping of Achilles tendon rupture stump morphology, (2) to establish a systematic classification of stump morphology based on the mapping of Achilles tendon ruptures, and (3) to analyze the correlation between classification type and the surgical procedure performed, thereby exploring the potential utility of the classification system in supporting clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study included patients treated between January 2020 and December 2023. Only acute injuries—defined as MRI examinations performed within 7 days of injury—were considered eligible. A total of 1,525 adult patients (≥ 18 years) with Achilles tendon ruptures (ATRs) without chronic tendinopathy were initially identified. Of these, 169 patients were excluded due to the absence of MRI scans, and an additional 1,094 patients were excluded due to insufficient image resolution (Spacing Between Slices ≥ 1 mm, based on DICOM tag 0018,0088). After applying these criteria, 262 patients with high-resolution MRI scans and complete clinical data were included for analysis (Fig. 1).

MRI protocol

All MRI examinations were performed within 7 days of injury using a 3.0 Tesla scanner (uMR 780, United Imaging, Shanghai, China) and a dedicated ankle coil. Imaging was acquired in sagittal, coronal, and axial planes using a high-resolution 3D proton density–weighted fast spin echo (FSE) sequence with spectral attenuated inversion recovery (SPAIR) fat suppression. The key imaging parameters were: TR = 1000 ms, TE = 55.92 ms, echo train length = 60, flip angle = 74°, field of view = 220 mm, matrix size = 288 × 288, voxel size = 0.492 × 0.492 × 0.66 mm³, and slice thickness = 0.66 mm with no interslice gap. This protocol enabled detailed visualization of tendon end morphology, which was subsequently used to assess rupture patterns and inform surgical planning.

Generation of mapping based on MRI

MRI datasets were reconstructed into three-dimensional models using Mimics Research 21.0 (Materialise NV). Segmentations of the tendon ends and subsequent morphological classifications were independently performed by two experienced musculoskeletal radiologists and two experienced orthopedic surgeons, each with over five years of clinical experience. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion. Intraobserver reliability was evaluated by reclassification of 50 randomly selected cases after a 3-month interval. During image processing, distinct colors were applied to different components of the model to enhance clarity and facilitate detailed analysis (See Additional Fig. 1). The software analysed the MRI data across the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes to reconstruct a 3D model of ATRs (See Additional Fig. 2).

Coronal and sagittal images of the 3D model were extracted and processed using Adobe Photoshop (Photoshop 2024, Adobe Systems Inc) to delineate the boundary lines. Right-sided ruptured Achilles tendons were mirrored to the left side, and each boundary line was imported into Photoshop with a fixed actual size/pixel ratio, allowing for precise overlay and resulting in a composite image of the boundary lines. Mirroring was justified to standardize orientation for overlay analysis, reducing variability. To standardize the insertion reference point across cases, we aligned the midpoint of the intersecting lines between the blue and yellow contours representing anatomical landmarks. This approach enabled consistent assessment of the relative distance between the rupture site and the calcaneal insertion (See Additional Fig. S3). The red region, located anatomically distant from the calcaneus, exhibited marked spatial variability with little overlap across cases, rendering its mapping of limited significance; it was therefore excluded from the final analysis to avoid distortion. Besides, although this post-processing step offered effective visualization, it introduced minor distortions compared to original DICOM data. This methodological limitation is addressed further in the Discussion.

The conduct of surgical procedures

For all patients requiring surgical intervention, three sets of instruments are prepared in advance: one for minimally invasive surgery, one for open repair, and one for open repair with anchor devices for reattaching the tendon to the heel bone when rupture occurs near the insertion site (Fig. 2). During the procedure, a small incision of approximately 3 cm is made to assess the morphology of the tendon stumps, which guides the surgical approach. If the distal stump is neat (protrusion < 5 mm) or partially neat (5 mm ≤ protrusion < 2 cm), minimally invasive surgery is preferred, with trimming based on the condition of the stump. Conversely, for irregular tendon stumps with protrusions exceeding 2 cm, trimming the distal end beyond this threshold may lead to postoperative Achilles tendon shortening, negatively impacting function. In such cases, open repair is preferred. However, if during surgery the distance between the rupture point and the insertion site is found to be less than 2 cm, surgeons will use anchor devices to reattach the tendon to the heel bone.

Variables and data sources

The primary variables for this study included the morphological classification of the Achilles tendon stumps, surgical approach, patient demographics (age, sex, height, weight), and injury cause. Data were collected from medical records, MRI scans, and surgical reports. Surgical findings were independently extracted and coded by two blinded researchers who were not involved in image segmentation, serving as an external reference for validation.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed on patient characteristics, including sex, age, height, weight, length of hospital stay, and cause of injury. These characteristics were summarized using mean values, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS software (IBM SPSS 27.0, IBM Corp.). The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was employed to evaluate data normality. Data following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), whereas data not conforming to a normal distribution are reported as median (Q1, Q3).

Observer agreement for the classification system was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Interobserver reliability was calculated from all pairwise comparisons among four raters, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Intraobserver reliability was evaluated by reclassification of 50 randomly selected cases after a 3-month interval.

Results

Demographics of the study population

A total of 244 male patients and 18 female patients were included in this study (Fig. 3; Table 1). ATRs occurred on the left side in 136 patients (51.9%) and on the right side in 126 patients (48.1%), with no statistically significant difference in distribution. Height, weight, age, and length of hospital stay were tested for normality via the K-S test, with all P values being less than 0.05 (0.000, 0.001, 0.008, and 0.000, respectively), indicating that none of these variables followed a normal distribution. Mapping of Achilles tendon rupture stumps using MRI

We utilized MRI to create a comprehensive, large-scale mapping of the Achilles tendon rupture stumps. This mapping involved overlaying 262 coronal and sagittal plane boundary lines(Fig. 4), revealing that the rupture site was predominantly located in the middle portion of the tendon, approximately 3–6 cm proximal to its insertion on the calcaneus(See Additional Fig. 4 for the distribution of distance). In both the coronal and sagittal planes, the contour lines of the rupture ends were mostly irregular.

Classification of Achilles tendon stump morphology

To classify the morphology of the Achilles tendon stumps, it is crucial to first categorize the boundary lines of the stumps in both the coronal and sagittal planes (Fig. 5). For category (1), Lines A and B are coincident within an allowed error of 5 mm, whereas for categories (2) to (5), Lines A and B are not coincident. In these cases, when the distance between Lines A and B (parameter a) is < 2 cm, minimally invasive repair is generally feasible, whereas a ≥ 2 cm typically requires open surgery. In addition, the distance from Line B to Line C (parameter b) guides the use of anchor devices; when b < 2 cm, anchors are often employed to reattach the tendon to the calcaneus. These thresholds are consistent with our viewpoint during surgery (see ‘The Conduct of Surgical Procedures’ in the Methods).”

On the basis of the alignment of these boundary lines in the coronal and sagittal planes, we propose a novel classification system for ATRs: the PUTH ATRs classification (Figs. 6 and 7). The ruptures were classified into four types: Type A (neat), Type B (partially neat), Type C (irregular), and Type D (insertion). The system effectively distinguished morphological differences that could impact treatment approaches. Specifically, 9.2% of the cases were Type A, 68.7% were Type B, 20.2% were Type C, and 1.9% were Type D. Interobserver reliability analysis showed substantial agreement, with pairwise Cohen’s kappa values of 0.838, 0.851, 0.856, 0.832, 0.846, and 0.850 (mean κ = 0.846). Intraobserver reliability was also high, with kappa values of 0.841, 0.857, 0.817, and 0.871 for the four raters, respectively (mean κ = 0.847).

Surgical decision-making based on stump morphology

In our surgical procedures, all (24/24) Type A cases were treated with minimally invasive surgery, while the majority of Type B cases (173/180, 96.1%) underwent minimally invasive surgery with tendon trimming, and the remaining Type B cases received open repair. All (53/53) Type C and (5/5, 100%) Type D cases were treated with open repair, with Type D cases additionally utilizing anchor devices to reattach the tendon to the calcaneus. This classification system, developed through retrospective analysis of surgical findings, aligns with the clinical decision-making patterns observed in our cohort and provides a reproducible morphological framework that may help guide future surgical interventions, pending prospective validation.

Discussion

MRI mapping of Achilles tendon rupture stumps

A key contribution of this study is the development of a comprehensive 3D mapping of ATRs using MRI, providing a detailed, three-dimensional understanding of tendon morphology. This approach offers enhanced visualization compared to traditional two-dimensional imaging methods.

Tendon end morphology can evolve over time due to biological healing processes. To address this, we limited inclusion to patients imaged within 7 days post-injury, capturing the acute morphology while minimizing confounding due to later-stage remodeling. The proposed classification types were derived from consistent morphological features observed within this time frame.

The length and morphology of the ruptured Achilles tendon are critical factors for surgical treatment planning [10]. Various imaging modalities can be used to assess the distance from the tendon stump to its insertion, with musculoskeletal ultrasound being the most common. Ultrasound is convenient, noninvasive, and cost effective, making it ideal for evaluating sports injuries [11]. However, while ultrasound provides valuable two-dimensional imaging and allows for “point-to-point” distance measurements, such as the distance from the proximal end of the distal tendon stump to the calcaneal insertion (Line A to C, a + b in Fig. 5), it is limited in identifying the precise surgical plane and proximity to the insertion point (Line B to C, b in Fig. 5). Despite its utility, ultrasound lacks the capacity to deliver the detailed three-dimensional information necessary for precise surgical decision-making. Although some 3D ultrasound systems exist, their clinical use in tendon rupture mapping remains limited.

Our mapping consistently revealed that the rupture site was located 3 to 6 cm from the Achilles tendon insertion point (Fig. S4). This region, where the tendon narrows, may represent a biomechanical weak point. Moreover, this area, located 2 to 6 cm from the insertion point, is known to have a relatively poor blood supply, which could suggest a correlation between ATRs and inadequate blood flow [12, 13]. The 3D morphology of the Achilles tendon stumps, as observed by mapping, frequently appears irregular. This irregularity may result from several factors: (1) The Achilles tendon is composed of fused components of the gastrocnemius, soleus, and plantaris muscles, creating an inherently heterogeneous structure [14]. (2) ATRs occur due to a variety of mechanisms, and the direction and magnitude of stress during injury can vary significantly [15].

Critical comparison with existing modalities

Our 3D mapping approach offers distinct advantages. Compared to ultrasound, it provides a more reproducible and detailed assessment of complex tendon end morphology, which is less operator-dependent. In contrast to conventional 2D MRI, our method delivers integrated multiplanar reconstructions essential for surgical planning. Finally, existing classification systems based on gap size or clinical criteria do not describe the specific morphological patterns relevant for matching tendon ends during surgery.

Several studies have explored the use of MRI and three-dimensional morphology to investigate ATRs, providing insights into the mechanisms of rupture, tendon degeneration, and the location and shape of the rupture, which are crucial for preoperative planning [2, 16]. Despite these advances, research on the three-dimensional morphology of ATRs remains limited, and no widely accepted 3D classification system has been established. Our study addresses this gap by utilizing MRI to create a large-scale, 3D mapping of ruptured tendon morphology. This mapping serves as a crucial atlas for ATRs, similar to other mapping studies [17, 18], contributing to a deeper understanding of tendon stump characteristics and supporting the development of a novel classification system.

The classification system for Achilles tendon ruptures

A major contribution of this study is the introduction of the PUTH ATRs Classification system, which addresses a significant gap in the literature. While several classification systems for tendon injuries have been proposed, many lack the anatomical and morphological depth necessary to guide surgical decision-making effectively [19]. Our classification system categorizes ruptures into four distinct types (Type A, B, C, and D), based on both the location of the rupture and the degree of irregularity in the tendon stump morphology. This system not only reflects the anatomical variability observed in ATRs but also has direct clinical relevance by helping to determine the appropriate surgical approach.

This system is based on reproducible criteria derived from 3D MRI morphology and captures the anatomical variability observed in ATRs. Tendon “neatness” can be assessed using specific quantitative measurements (Figs. 6 and 7), providing objective classification standards. Nonetheless, further prospective validation is needed to confirm its clinical applicability.

Surgical decision-making based on stump morphology

Our findings underscore the importance of considering the morphology of the tendon stump when making surgical decisions. The decision-making process between traditional minimally invasive and open repair techniques has been relatively subjective. However, the introduction of the MRI-based morphological classification system has provided a more objective and standardized approach to the surgical decision-making process. In our study, Type A and B cases, which represent neat or partially neat tendon stumps, were treated with minimally invasive techniques, whereas Type C and D cases, with more irregular stump morphologies, were treated with open surgery. Although the classification system is reproducible, it should be prospectively validated in future studies against clinical outcomes, including functional recovery and rerupture rates, to confirm its broader applicability.

Limitations

Although this study provides a novel and large-scale mapping of ATRs based on MRI, there are several limitations to consider. First, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings or populations.Second, this study was retrospective, and the potential for selection bias cannot be fully ruled out, despite the inclusion criteria and exclusions being carefully applied. Notably, the exclusion of 1,094 patients due to insufficient MRI resolution (slice spacing ≥ 1 mm) introduces potential selection bias, as the findings primarily reflect cases with higher-quality imaging. This may limit generalizability to clinical settings with variable imaging protocols. In addition, feasibility was constrained by the labor-intensive nature of manual segmentation, which restricted the final cohort size despite the initially large patient pool. Third, converting 3D model images to 2D formats for processing may introduce slight distortions compared to working directly with DICOM images. Fourth, tendon morphology is not the sole determinant of treatment; the tendon gap distance is also a critical factor that should be integrated into future models. Finally, the classification has not yet been validated against postoperative clinical outcomes such as functional scores or rerupture rates. We are planning a prospective cohort study to track these outcomes and validate the classification’s clinical relevance.

Conclusions

This article is the first to utilize MRI to create a comprehensive mapping of the location and morphology of ATRs, offering a large-scale three-dimensional representation and enhancing our understanding of ATR morphology. Based on this mapping, we propose a new classification system, which demonstrates reproducibility and requires prospective validation against clinical outcomes to establish its clinical utilit.
