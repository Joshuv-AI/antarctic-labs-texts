Artificial intelligence-assisted prediction of bile duct bifurcation site in pure laparoscopic donor right hepatectomy: a retrospective feasibility study
Authors: Jiyoung Baik, Namkee Oh, Gyu-Seong Choi, Jinsoo Rhu, Jongman Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAccurate identification of the bile duct bifurcation site is crucial in pure laparoscopic donor right hepatectomy (PLDRH) for living donor liver transplantation. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a deep learning model to predict the bile duct bifurcation site for surgical precision.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 55 PLDRH procedures conducted between August 2021 and April 2022. A deep learning model combining UNet with a MiT-B3 encoder was trained on 150 manually annotated frames. We then incorporated expert-reviewed pseudo-labels from an additional 901 frames to refine the model. Performance was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation and an independent test set.ResultsClinical evaluation showed a 97% accuracy in 5-fold cross-validation and 93.3% accuracy in the independent test set. From the initial to final model, dice similarity coefficient improved from 0.392 to 0.472, intersection over union from 0.279 to 0.339, and sensitivity from 0.487 to 0.643, while specificity remained consistent at 0.993.ConclusionThe proposed artificial intelligence (AI) model demonstrated strong clinical performance in predicting the bile duct bifurcation site during PLDRH. Despite modest quantitative scores, the high clinical accuracy highlights the potential of integrating AI for precise donor hepatectomy.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Bile ducts, Deep learning, Laparoscopy, Living donors

INTRODUCTION

Living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) is a critical strategy to address organ shortage in end-stage liver disease and hepatic malignancies [1,2]. In regions where LDLT is common, ensuring donor safety is paramount, particularly as donors undergo a major operation for no personal therapeutic benefits [3,4]. Among the most common donor complications is biliary injury, including leaks and strictures.

However, LDLT presents a unique ethical and medical challenge: donors undergo a major surgical procedure without any direct health benefit, exposing themselves to potential risks [5]. Therefore, minimizing and managing these risks during donor surgery is of paramount importance [6]. Among the complications associated with donor hepatectomy, biliary leakage and strictures remain the most common and concerning [7,8]. To mitigate these risks, significant efforts have been directed towards enhancing the understanding of biliary anatomy, such as magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), intraoperative cholangiography, and indocyanine green (ICG) cholangiography [6,9].

A critical step in donor hepatectomy is the accurate prediction of the bile duct bifurcation point at the hepatic hilum. This anatomical landmark is crucial in determining the parenchymal transection plane during right or left donor hepatectomy [10]. Inaccurate prediction of this point can lead to unnecessary manipulation during the bile duct dissection process, potentially increasing risks to the donor (Fig. 1). In recent years, computer vision, a field of artificial intelligence (AI) that enables computational understanding of digital images and videos, has shown promising applications in minimally invasive surgical settings [11,12,13]. Building on these advancements, this study aims to develop and evaluate an AI model that leverages computer vision techniques to predict the location of bile duct bifurcation at the hepatic hilum during pure laparoscopic donor right hepatectomy (PLDRH) procedures. Accurate localization of this critical anatomical landmark could potentially enhance surgical precision, reduce biliary complications, and improve donor safety outcomes.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki and Declaration of Istanbul and was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (No. SMC-2022-07-149-001). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the IRB waived the need to obtain informed consent.

Study design and patients

This single-institution retrospective feasibility study included 55 patients who underwent PLDRH between August 2021 and April 2022.

Data collection and preprocessing

High-quality surgical videos of the PLDRH procedures with a resolution of 1,920 × 1,080 pixels and a frame rate of 30 frames per second (fps) were reviewed. From each video, we extracted the segment showing the identification of the bile duct bifurcation at the hepatic hilum prior to parenchymal transection. Three representative frames were selected from each video, resulting in a total of 150 frames from 50 PLDRH videos. These frames were carefully chosen to ensure clear visibility of the hilum, free from obstruction by surgical instruments or fog. To establish the ground truth for the bile duct bifurcation site, we employed a reverse playback technique. By analyzing the video in reverse from the point of complete identification of the bifurcation site, we could accurately annotate the bifurcation site on the selected frames.

Artificial intelligence model development

This study employed a deep learning segmentation model based on a U-Net architecture with a MiT-B3 encoder, a configuration that has demonstrated promising performance in medical image segmentation [14,15].

Our model development process incorporated a human-in-the-loop approach, which integrates expert surgical knowledge into the AI training process (Fig. 2). This approach involved initial model training on manually annotated frames, followed by pseudo-labeling of additional unlabeled data. These pseudo-labels were then reviewed by surgical experts, who discarded inappropriately labeled images and retained only those deemed accurate. This curated set of pseudo-labeled images, along with the original manually annotated data, was then used to retrain and refine the model, ensuring a high-quality dataset for the final model development.

Human-in-the-loop approach for artificial intelligence (AI) model development in bile duct bifurcation prediction. This figure illustrates the human-in-the-loop methodology used to develop and refine the AI model for predicting bile duct bifurcation sites in pure laparoscopic donor right hepatectomy (PLDRH) procedures. The process involves 4 main steps: 1. Initial model training: An AI model is trained on 150 manually labeled frames extracted from 50 raw video clips. 2. Pseudo-labeling: The initial model generates predictions (pseudo-labels) for additional unlabeled frames extracted at 1 frame per second from the video clips, resulting in 1,992 pseudo-labeled frames. 3. Expert review: Surgical experts review the pseudo-labeled frames, validating or rejecting the AI-generated labels. This step resulted in 901 approved frames and 1,091 excluded frames. 4. Final model training: The final AI model is trained using both the original 150 labeled frames and the 901 expert-approved pseudo-labeled frames, creating a more robust and accurate model. This iterative process combines machine learning capabilities with expert human knowledge, enhancing the model’s performance and reliability in predicting bile duct bifurcation sites during PLDRH procedures.

Model evaluation

To assess the clinical utility of the AI model, we divided the hepatic hilum into 5 segments and evaluated the model’s performance based on its ability to correctly predict the segment containing the bile duct bifurcation (Fig. 3). Additionally, we calculated quantitative metrics for segmentation tasks, including dice similarity coefficient (DSC), intersection over union (IoU), sensitivity, and specificity [16].

RESULTS

Clinical evaluation

The study performed a 5-fold cross-validation using 50 PLDRH surgical videos. From each video, 3 frames were extracted and defined chronologically as 1st, 2nd, and 3rd. The clinical evaluation revealed that the final model accurately predicted the bile duct bifurcation site in 145 out of 150 frames, achieving a 97% accuracy rate. In contrast, the initial model successfully predicted the bifurcation site in 133 out of 150 frames, demonstrating an 89% accuracy rate (Table 1).

Quantitative evaluation

The final model showed a mean DSC of 0.472 (standard deviation [SD], 0.04), compared to 0.392 (SD, 0.04) for the initial model. The IoU for the final model was 0.339 (SD, 0.03), while the initial model had an IoU of 0.279 (SD, 0.03). Sensitivity increased from 0.487 (SD, 0.09) in the initial model to 0.643 (SD, 0.08) in the final model. Specificity remained consistent at 0.993 (SD, 0.001) for both models (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated the potential of AI in accurately predicting the bile duct bifurcation site during PLDRH procedures. The proposed model achieved 97% accuracy in clinical evaluation for 5-fold cross-validation, and this high performance was maintained in the test set with 93.3% accuracy, suggesting robust performance on unseen data. This high accuracy in predicting the bile duct bifurcation site has significant clinical implications for PLDRH. Accurate identification of this anatomical landmark can potentially enhance surgical precision in determining the parenchymal transection plane, reduce the risk of unnecessary manipulation during bile duct dissection, and minimize the potential for biliary complications, a common concern in donor hepatectomy [7,8]. Ultimately, this could lead to improved overall donor safety, which is paramount in LDLT.

Furthermore, the proposed AI model offers several advantages over existing modalities such as MRCP or ICG cholangiography. Unlike MRCP, which provides only preoperative static information, the AI model delivers real-time intraoperative anatomical guidance directly from standard laparoscopic video. In addition, ICG cholangiography requires intravenous dye injection and specialized near-infrared equipment, whereas our method is entirely non-invasive and requires no additional interventions or hardware. This equipment-free, workflow-integrated approach highlights the unique value of AI-based video analysis as a complementary tool for donor hepatectomy.

However, the quantitative evaluation showed relatively lower scores, with a mean DSC of 0.472 and IoU of 0.339, sensitivity at 0.643, and specificity at 0.993. The disparity between the high clinical accuracy and the modest quantitative metrics can be attributed to the unique challenges in identifying and labeling the bile duct bifurcation site. The common hepatic duct’s entry point into the liver, while anatomically present, is often obscured by surrounding connective tissue. Even after dissection, this structure is rarely fully exposed during surgery. Our ground truth labeling method, which used circular annotations to indicate the predicted entry point, was designed to accommodate this clinical reality. However, this approach, while clinically relevant, may have led to lower quantitative scores. The circular annotation, necessary for practical identification, inherently introduces imprecision when evaluated against pixel-level segmentation metrics like DSC and IoU [16].

Our human-in-the-loop approach, integrating expert surgical knowledge into the AI training process, proved highly effective in improving model performance. This method allowed for refinement of the model through expert review of pseudo-labels, elimination of inappropriately labeled images, and ensured a high-quality training dataset. The significant improvement in accuracy from the initial model (89%) to the final model (97%) underscores the value of this approach. Quantitatively, we observed improvements in key metrics, with the DSC increasing from 0.392 (SD, 0.04) to 0.472 (SD, 0.04), and the IoU rising from 0.279 (SD, 0.03) to 0.339 (SD, 0.03). This methodology not only enhanced model performance but also significantly improved the efficiency of the annotation process. While the initial pixel-level segmentation was performed on 150 frames, our approach enabled us to leverage the initial model to incorporate an additional 901 frames into the training process. This substantial increase in training data, achieved without the need for time-consuming manual annotation of each frame, demonstrates the scalability and efficiency of our human-in-the-loop approach [17].

Despite the promising results, several limitations should be addressed in future studies. Firstly, the single-institution study design limits the generalizability of our findings. Secondly, the relatively small sample size, while sufficient for this feasibility study, calls for further validation with larger datasets. Additionally, the challenge of accurately representing the bile duct bifurcation site in ground truth labeling, as evidenced by the discrepancy between clinical and quantitative evaluations, requires further investigation and methodological refinement. Furthermore, the exploration of real-time applications during surgery represents an exciting next step. This would involve integrating the AI model into the surgical workflow and assessing its impact on surgical decision-making and outcomes.

Although all participants in this study were healthy living donors, the model was trained to recognize the anatomical configuration of the bile duct bifurcation itself. Therefore, the underlying concept may also be applicable to patients with hepatocellular carcinoma or other liver diseases. However, cirrhotic patients often exhibit distorted hilar anatomy or fibrotic changes, which may limit generalizability. Future validation in non-donor populations will be essential to confirm broader clinical utility.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the significant potential of AI in enhancing the precision and safety of PLDRH procedures. While challenges remain, particularly in bridging the gap between clinical accuracy and quantitative metrics, the high clinical accuracy of our model suggests a promising step toward integrating AI into liver transplant surgery. Future multi-center studies with larger datasets and refined evaluation methods will be crucial in validating and expanding upon these encouraging results.

In addition, the AI model provides real-time guidance using only standard laparoscopic video, offering a non-invasive and equipment-free complement to existing modalities such as MRCP or ICG cholangiography. Although developed using donor data, the underlying predictive concept may be generalizable to other populations, including patients with HCC; however, further validation is required due to potential anatomical distortion in cirrhotic livers.
