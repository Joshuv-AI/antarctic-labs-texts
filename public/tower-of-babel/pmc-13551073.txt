Mitral Paravalvular Leak Closure Using a Percutaneous and Nonfluoroscopic Approach
Authors: Dong Luo, Haibo Hu
Published: 2026

Mitral paravalvular leak (PVL) is a recognized complication after prosthetic valve implantation and may lead to heart failure or hemolysis.1,2 Transcatheter PVL closure has emerged as an alternative to redo surgery, typically performed under combined fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance.3 However, fluoroscopy exposes both patients and operators to ionizing radiation and provides limited soft tissue resolution. Percutaneous and nonfluoroscopic (PAN) approaches4, 5, 6 have recently been introduced to minimize radiation exposure while maintaining procedural precision. These techniques rely heavily on real-time imaging guidance, particularly transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). Here, we describe a reproducible, step-by-step TEE-guided technique for mitral PVL closure using a PAN framework.

Case Summary

A 66-year-old man was admitted with a >30-year history of recurrent exertional chest tightness and dyspnea. He had been diagnosed with rheumatic heart disease 3 decades earlier. In 2006, he had an ischemic stroke and was found to have atrial fibrillation. In 2007, he underwent mechanical mitral valve replacement at an outside institution and had since been maintained on long-term warfarin therapy, along with diuretics and angiotensin receptor blockers. Despite optimal medical therapy, the patient reported persistent exertional dyspnea and chest tightness, accompanied by bilateral lower-extremity edema. Transthoracic echocardiography performed at another hospital demonstrated moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation, raising suspicion of a mitral PVL. On admission, the blood pressure was 144/70 mm Hg and the heart rate was 66 beats/min with an irregular rhythm. Electrocardiography showed atrial fibrillation with a slow ventricular response and nonspecific ST-T changes. Chest radiography revealed pulmonary vascular congestion and cardiomegaly. The patient was diagnosed with rheumatic heart disease, mitral PVL after mechanical valve replacement, chronic atrial fibrillation, chronic heart failure (NYHA functional class II), pulmonary hypertension, and remote cerebral infarction. Preprocedural TEE (Video 1) and cardiac computed tomography (Figure 1) demonstrated separation between the prosthetic mitral valve annulus and adjacent tissue at the lateral aspect of the mechanical valve, resulting in a paravalvular defect measuring approximately 5 mm in width. Color Doppler imaging revealed a large eccentric systolic regurgitant jet originating from the paravalvular region into the left atrium (Figure 2). Given the patient’s symptomatic status and imaging findings, percutaneous PVL closure was deemed appropriate.

Procedural Steps

After written informed consent was obtained, the patient underwent percutaneous mitral PVL closure under exclusive TEE guidance. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia via right femoral venous access with placement of a 6-F sheath. A 6-F sheath was placed in the right femoral vein, and the working distance was established by measuring the distance from the third intercostal space at the right midclavicular line to the puncture site, which was then marked on the catheter and guidewire to guide safe advancement. Under TEE bicaval-view guidance, transseptal puncture was successfully performed. A 260-cm exchange guidewire was advanced into the left superior pulmonary vein, followed by advancement of a steerable sheath into the left atrium. The insertion depth of the sheath was measured for subsequent reference. Under TEE guidance, the steerable sheath was oriented toward the paravalvular defect, and an multipurpose A2 catheter combined with a hydrophilic guidewire was advanced across the PVL into the left ventricle (Figure 3). After securing the wire position, the catheter and steerable sheath were removed, and an 8-F patent ductus arteriosus delivery sheath was advanced into the left ventricle. A 16-mm Amplatzer vascular plug was selected and carefully deployed across the paravalvular defect (Figure 4). Real-time TEE demonstrated stable device position, adequate device expansion, and near-complete elimination of the regurgitant jet, without interference with prosthetic valve motion or adjacent cardiac structures (Video 2, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7). The delivery system was subsequently withdrawn, and hemostasis was achieved with manual compression. The postprocedural course was uneventful. The patient remained hemodynamically stable, with improvement in functional status to NYHA functional class I. At 1-month follow-up, he reported marked symptomatic improvement, and follow-up echocardiography confirmed stable device position with no significant residual paravalvular regurgitation.

Potential Pitfalls

There are potential pitfalls of this technique that need to be mentioned. First, the PAN approach relies heavily on high-quality, real-time TEE imaging. If the patient has suboptimal acoustic windows, the guidewire may be unable to safely identify and traverse the paravalvular defect. In such cases, the operator must have a low threshold for converting to traditional fluoroscopy or using ultrasound-fluoroscopy fusion imaging to avoid procedural complications. Second, maintaining an adequate working distance is critical when treating mitral PVL in the presence of a mechanical prosthesis. If the delivery sheath is advanced too far into the left ventricle or if the occluder is deployed without sufficient clearance from the valve ring, the device may entrap the mechanical discs or cause acute valve thrombosis. Continuous TEE monitoring must be performed to confirm preserved disc mobility throughout the deployment process. Third, this case involved a relatively small, lateral PVL, which is a favorable characteristic for a purely ultrasound-guided approach. The procedure may be significantly more challenging for a complex PVL. Fourth, even after successful device deployment, a tiny residual shunt may persist. Although such small defects may not cause immediate symptoms, they can occasionally lead to delayed hemolysis. Therefore, close follow-up with serial lactate dehydrogenase and hemoglobin monitoring is essential to ensure clinical success. Finally, although the PAN approach eliminates ionizing radiation for the patient and the heart team, it requires a steep learning curve and close coordination between the interventionalist and the echocardiographer.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of mitral PVL closure guided solely by TEE. This technique was demonstrated to be highly effective for achieving precise defect localization and device deployment while maintaining an adequate working distance from the mechanical prosthesis. By eliminating the need for fluoroscopy, this approach avoids ionizing radiation exposure for both the patient and the heart team without compromising procedural safety. In selected patients with favorable anatomy, this ultrasound-guided strategy should be considered as a safe and viable alternative to redo open-heart surgery or traditional fluoroscopy-guided interventions.
