Identity, Cooperation and Framing Effects within Groups of Real and Simulated Humans
Suhong Moon, Minwoo Kang, Joseph Suh, Mustafa Safdari, John Canny

Abstract
Humans act via a nuanced process that depends both on rational deliberation and also identity and contextual factors. In this work, we study how large language models (LLMs) can simulate human action in the context of social dilemma games. While prior work has focused on “steering” (weak binding) of chat models to simulate personas, we analyze here how deep binding of base models with extended backstories leads to more faithful replication of identity-based behaviors. Our study has these findings: simulation fidelity vs human studies is improved by conditioning base LMs with rich context of narrative identities and checking consistency using instruction-tuned models. We show that LLMs can also model contextual factors such as time (year that a study was performed), question framing, and participant pool effects. LLMs therefore allow us to explore the details that affect human studies but which are often omitted from experiment descriptions, and which hamper accurate replication.

Human decision making is a complex process shaped not only by rational deliberation but also by social identity and other contextual factors.
Decades of research in social psychology and political science have shown that individuals cooperate better with ingroup peers than with outgroups

These dynamics become especially salient in social dilemma games such as the Dictator and Trust Games, where choices directly reveal altruism, trust, and perceptions of reciprocity.
Recent studies demonstrate that partisan identity can exert stronger effects than race on patterns of generosity and cooperation

While recent large language models (LLMs) have emerged as promising tools for simulating human behavior

Predicting behavior in realistic social contexts presents a fundamentally different challenge than replicating survey responses. Behavioral studies require models to generate decisions that simultaneously reflect (1) individual identity characteristics, (2) perceptions of group membership (both their own and others’), and (3) context-dependent strategic reasoning about cooperation and reciprocity.
In particular, partisan animosity has grown steadily from the mid-2000s to the mid-2020s, and various studies have shown stronger effect sizes for
partisan disparities in recent years
(

;
20
;
21
;

. So we explore the date of the study as an additional contextual factor in this work.

In this paper, we explore how well LLMs can model human behavior including identity and temporal context. Specifically, we explore how well LLM virtual personas can reproduce co-partisan favoritism in resource allocation and trust decisions.
We introduce two prompting strategies:
Consistency Filtering
, which reiterates persona identity and group membership throughout long simulations, and
Temporal Grounding
, which situates simulations in the year corresponding to the original human study.
We evaluate these methods using backstory-conditioned pretrained models, which remains the most accurate zero-shot simulation method

;
24
;
55

Our results show that deep backstory binding, temporal grounding and consistency checking improve alignment between LLM-simulated actions and human empirical data.
In the present case, it allows us to
explore the disparities in results
between human replication studies of the same phenomenon but with subtly different experiment design. We can compare effects of obvious differences like the year of study, and more subtle differences like the wording of experiment instructions, and participant pool effects.

3
Why Pretrained LLMs ?

Here we discuss our rationale for simulating users with pretrained language models (aka
base
models) rather than fine-tuned chat models. To be precise, a pretrained model is the first stage of modern LLM training, where the model is trained with next-token or masked token loss on an enormous corpus of training data, typically terabytes, including a significant amount of human dialog data. It is the most expensive step in training current models.
Prior studies
24
;
35
;
50
;
45
have shown that carefully-prompted pretrained models outperform instruction-tuned variants in human simulation tasks.
But it is not just a question of accuracy but of
fair representation
. Instruction-tuning
biases models away from fair representation
of people, as we shall see in a moment.

First of all, it’s important to understand that pretrained models are not “agent models” in the same way that instruction-tuned models are, see
Figure

2
. Pretrained model data consist of billions of text snippets with latent (hidden) context information such as the speaker, listener and other contextual factors. They therefore exactly model the “voices” of billions of users in billions of contexts.

Figure 2:

Conceptual illustration of the differences between pretrained and fine-tuned language models.

To “bind” or “steer” a pretrained model to a particular speaker and context, it suffices to provide sufficient context in a text prompt, and the model will faithfully extend the text from that speaker/context. There are some challenges in doing this reliably, but we address those in a moment. But its important at this stage to reflect on how well pretrained models fit to the task of simulating an enormously diverse population of people. If one were to design a human simulation model from scratch it would arguably be a pretrained model.

But the natural context-dependence of pretrained models, an asset for human simulation, is a liability in agent applications. Unless the speaker, listener and context are fully-constrained, the LLM will sample from a posterior of contexts that fit the text prompt so far, and give different responses based on that sampling. After pretraining, LLM training comprises several QA-prompt optimization stages that remove the pretrained model’s context dependence, freezing the model’s identity to a “helpful agent” persona, the user’s identity to “general user”, and the context to whatever can be inferred
within the current chat session. The model is intentionally steered away from diversity in agent personality, emotion, complex social goals etc, so as to maximize the model’s repeatability on a given prompt.

The effects of this later IT model training can be seen in
Figure

3
. By their nature, post-training datasets are much smaller than pretraining data. It is not possible to preserve the high accuracy of PT models on human dialog during later training, and this can be seen in the difference in perplexity between various base models and their IT variants on human dialog data. Typical increases are 50% to 100% for the IT models. Note also that the best pretrained models have single-digit perplexities, which is extraordinarily low for English text.

Finally the effects of IT model tuning are not uniformly distributed across personal traits, but rather weaken or eliminate negative human traits, and thereby under-represent individuals that have them. In fact, the effect is so strong that it is likely that
most users
are not represented. We call this the
Lake Wobegon Effect
(everyone is above human average in the resulting IT-model cohort).

For example, in
30
the authors found that for IT-model generated virtual personas “Notably
absent are terms reflecting life challenges, social difficulties, or negative experiences, suggesting
LLMs may be systematically avoiding less favorable characterizations.” The authors then go on to describe a variety of biases in results generated by IT models on these personas, concluding that such simulations are almost never in agreement with human studies. Like many recent works, the authors attribute these biases to LLMs in general, rather than the (expected) biases from instruction-tuning. The authors rightfully point out the dangers of using the outcomes from such simulations for decision making, but over-attribute those dangers to all LLMs. Rather, their results are an argument against simulation with IT models. Such biases are not shared by PT models, which instead capture an extraordinarily diverse collection of human traits.

Figure 3:

Per-token perplexity of base and instruction-tuned models on human Reddit data.

Instruction-tuned models (orange on right) exhibit substantially higher perplexity across all model families, compared to same-sized pretrained variants (blue on left).

4
Identity Binding in LLM Personas

We have seen that PT models are naturally contextualizable by prompting, but such prompts must be sufficiently detailed to
capture an individual human “voice”.
We use the “anthology” approach described in
35
and evolved in
24
. In particular
24
showed that longer backstories create deeper persona binding and improved accuracy in a social attitude test.

4.1
LLM Personas via Narrative Backstories

In this work, each persona backstory is generated by interviewing a PT model.
Samples of the backstories used in this experiment are provided in
Appendix

C
.
The interview questions are drawn from the American Voices Project
(
49
)
.
For accurate text generation relative to PT model training data, the models are run at the temperature of
1.0
.
However, not
all
PT model training data is human dialog, and neither is PT-model generated output. So we rejection-sample non-dialog results using an instruction-tuned critic.
These include code blocks, markup, factual inconsistencies, and non-dialog text structure, similar to
24
.
Demographic traits of each virtual persona are then
extracted from their profile using multi-choice questions.

After creating the backstories and demographics, individual human participant demographics (which were kindly provided by the authors of the human studies below), are matched against all virtual participants to find the demographically closest virtual participant.
This pool of matching virtual participants are given the same text prompts as were the human participants.
For more detail of matching process, please refer to
Appendix

E
.

4.2
Temporal Grounding and Consistency Filtering

We add two prompting methods—
Temporal Grounding
and
Consistency Filtering
—to improve the fidelity and stability of simulations.

Temporal Grounding
situates each simulation in the year the corresponding human study was conducted.
The date is bound to the persona with
a forced QA pair “Interviewer: What Year is it?”, “Model: 2007”.
We were motivated to study temporal effects by
58
, which obtained larger partisan effects than
21
, and conjectured that they were due to a increase in partisanship in the US over that time.

Consistency Filtering
is used to mitigate a variety of flaws in pretrained LLM output. This includes semantic drift because of short training segment lengths, but also the out-of-dialog genre cases mentioned earlier. However
Consistency Filtering
prompts must be designed and used with care. Real humans are often inconsistent, e.g. because of mental health issues, dementia, deception etc.

5
Can LLMs Simulate In-/Out-group Biases in Decision Making?

We explored two widely used behavioral economics games: the Dictator Game and the Trust Game.
These games have been used in prior political science research to uncover affective polarization, or the tendency of partisans to exhibit more favorable behavior toward in-group (co-partisan) members and less favorable behavior toward out-group (rival partisan) members.

5.1
Decision Asymmetry in Dictator Games

Dictator Game.

In this game, one participant (the “dictator”) decides how to divide a fixed amount of money between themself and another participant.
Prior studies
15
;
21
;
58
show that partisans are significantly more generous toward co-partisans than rival partisans.
For example,
15
find that participants allocate more to co-partisans than to opposing partisans in dictator games, even at personal cost.
Both altruism and identity-based giving are linked to higher political participation.

Experimental Setup and Metrics.

Our metrics are based on those in
21
;
58
.
We define the in-/out-group trust asymmetry in dictator games as the difference in mean transfer amounts allocated to co-partisans versus rival partisans.

Details of the prompts for
21
and
58
are included in
Appendix

A
. We also prompted for the year as outlined above.

Baselines.

We adopt the
QA
,
Bio
, and
Portray
prompting strategies from
45
as baselines. Each method conditions the model using demographic attributes such as age, gender, race, education, income, political affiliation, and other relevant factors.

•

QA
presents a series of question–answer pairs for each demographic variable (e.g.,
Q: What is your political affiliation? A: Republican
). Both Q and A texts are forced and presented to the model with the answer in the model’s first-person name.

•

Bio
generates rule-based free-text biographies written in the first person (e.g.,
I am a Republican
).

•

Portray
produces similar biographies but written in the second person (e.g.,
You are a Republican
).

5.2
Decision Asymmetry in Trust Games

Trust Game.

This game involves two players: a sender and a receiver. The sender decides how much money to send to the receiver, with the amount tripled in transit; the receiver then decides how much to return. This game measures both trust (by the sender) and trustworthiness (by the receiver). Studies show that senders are more likely to trust co-partisans, and expect more generous returns from them
58
.

Experimental Setup and Metrics.

We use the measures from prior human studies
8
;
58
.
The main difference from the dictator game is that the Trust Game involves both the sender’s disposition toward the receiver, and also their belief about the receiver reciprocating.
This makes the task more complex: models must capture both ingroup trust (greater willingness to send money to co-partisans) and ingroup expectations (belief that co-partisans will return more).

.

Ablation Study: Effects of
Temporal Grounding
and
Consistency Filtering

We adopt the same experimental protocol as in our main experiments, focusing on Mistral-Small (24B) to isolate architectural factors.

Dictator Game.

In both the 2014
(

)
and 2019
(

)

7
Conclusion

We presented a framework for simulating complex human social behavior with language models.
Key to accurate simulation was the use of unbiased pretrained models with consistency filtering, followed by deep binding with richly constructed backstories derived from life-narrative interviews.

We presented simulation studies with good agreement with earlier human studies, along with counter-factual studies that explored the disparities between human studies of the same phenomenon.
While we agree that human studies should remain “gold standard” for behavioral studies, we argue that this work highlights often-neglected challenges of reproducibility in terms of study timing, participant pool and question framing effects, and can serve as a useful tool for informing the design and interpretation of future human studies.

Limitations

Bias and Representational Limitations.

The distribution of generated virtual personas is constrained by the training data of the base LLMs.
To the extent that these models reflect underlying biases—social, political, or cultural—those biases may propagate into the personas we generate.
Even in the absence of explicit under-representation, matching-based approaches tend to yield less reliable estimates for minority groups, whose presence in the feature space is typically more sparse.
This introduces a risk of larger errors for marginalized or ideologically atypical populations.

Data-Dependent Fidelity of Simulation.

The present paper and our prior work has shown promising fit to patterns of human behavior. That fidelity depends on those patterns being adequately represented in the model’s training data. For example, our studies showed fidelity to participants bound to years 2015-2021. Many training documents containing contemporaneous dialog were crawled in those years. We would not expect similar fidelity if we attempted to bind participants to 1950.

Similarly, there is a wealth of training data capturing users’ self-experienced emotions, thoughts, and action descriptions which we expect can be accurately modeled. But a much more limited amount of data on the user’s physiological or latent psychological state. We therefore don’t expect to be able to model physiology, or unconscious psychological state. The issue of long-term behavior change is currently open. Individual dialog examples are short term, but the web does contain a non-trivial amount of “diary” type data from users, which allows us to model long-term effects. This is current work.

Resource Requirements.

Our approach depends on large models with extended context windows, coupled with compute-intensive generation and filtering pipelines.
Generating high-quality backstories at scale—especially with rejection sampling and critic filtering—requires significant computational resources, which may pose barriers for smaller research groups or real-time applications.

Domain Specificity.

Our current experiments focus exclusively on U.S. political partisanship, reflecting the design of the human studies we replicate.
As such, our findings may not generalize to other national, linguistic, or cultural contexts.
Future work should explore broader applications, particularly in multilingual or non-Western settings.

Ethics Statement

Our work aims to simulate aggregate-level human behavior using language models, not to anthropomorphize or personalize LLMs for individual interaction
(
10
)
.
Persona conditioning enables models to better approximate opinion distributions and ingroup biases, and also to better represent the diversity of the human population compared to directly steerable aggregate models.
We caution against interpreting generated personas as real individuals or deploying them in contexts where anthropomorphic framing could lead to user misinterpretation.

A critical component of our methodology is the use of
pretrained
models that have not undergone fine-tuning for helpfulness, instruction-following and positivity.
This is intentional: pretraining preserves a wider range of human-like expression, including disagreement, discomfort, and prejudice.
As a result, some responses may reflect harmful biases, stereotyping, or negative emotion—just as real humans might in similar settings.
By design, we want to be able to reproduce the kinds of dialog that arise in un-moderated social media platforms, as well as more regulated venues.
We therefore urge caution in reading and sharing these responses, especially in public or sensitive domains.

Prior research has documented that LLMs may flatten human diversity, reinforce social stereotypes, or exaggerate partisan divisions—particularly when simulating marginalized groups
(
9
;
11
;
4
;
37
;
56
)
.
We recognize these risks and emphasize that our design choices aim to foreground such biases, not obscure them.

Finally, this work is intended solely for research purposes—especially within the social sciences.
We strongly discourage its use for persuasive messaging, behavioral targeting, disinformation, or the impersonation of real individuals.
Responsible deployment of virtual personas requires careful institutional oversight and transparent safeguards against misuse.

To generate high-quality virtual backstories, we follow the backstory construction pipeline introduced by

We adopt the same set of interview questions used in their work to elicit long-form, coherent, and persona-consistent narratives.

Table

7
