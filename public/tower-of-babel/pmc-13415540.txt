Emergence of NDM-5-producing ST405 and NDM-1-producing ST10 Escherichia coli in bloodstream infections in Apulia, Italy, 2023–2025
Authors: Anna Sallustio, Marisa Accogli, Davide Sacco, Marilina Santantonio, Vito Colella, Domenica Maria Di Palo, Serafina Rutigliano, Grazia Punzi, Francesca Centrone, Maria Chironna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
NDM-producing Escherichia coli (E. coli) are emerging across Europe, especially clones ST405 (NDM-5) and ST10 (NDM-1). Data from Southern Italy remain scarce. We investigated NDM-producing E. coli bloodstream infections (BSIs) detected through regional Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) surveillance in Apulia, 2014–2025. Within the regional CRE surveillance (2014–2025), all carbapenem-resistant (CR) E. coli BSI isolates were collected and screened for carbapenemase genes (GeneXpert: blaKPC, blaVIM, blaNDM, blaIMP, blaOXA-48). NDM-positive isolates (2023–2025) underwent Illumina whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Core-genome SNP analysis assessed clonal relatedness. Resistance, virulence genes, and plasmid replicons were characterized using ResFinder, VirulenceFinder, and PlasmidFinder, respectively. Among 50 CR E. coli BSI isolates, blaKPC and blaNDM were detected in 23/50 (46.0%) and 11/50 (22.0%), respectively; 16/50 (32.0%) had carbapenem resistance mechanisms not identified by the PCR panel. All blaNDM-positive isolates were detected from 2023 onwards. Nine NDM-producing isolates were sequenced: eight blaNDM-5 E. coli ST405 (phylogroup D, O102:H6, fimH29) and one blaNDM-1 E. coli ST10 (phylogroup A, O9:H9, fimH54). All were multidrug-resistant yet colistin-susceptible. ST405 isolates co-harboured blaOXA-1, blaCTX-M-15, sul1, aac(6')-Ib-cr, catB3, dfrA17, and tet(B), and carried multiple virulence genes including fimH, papC, traT, fyuA, iutA/iucC and chuA, consistent with a hypervirulent multidrug resistance (MDR) profile. Core-genome SNP analysis showed a tight cluster of six ST405 isolates from one hospital (< 30 SNPs), and two unrelated ST405 isolates from different hospitals. ST405 strains carried IncFIA, IncFIB(AP001918), IncFII and Col(BS512) plasmids; the ST10 isolate carried IncFIB(pQil). We report the emergence of the high-risk MDR E. coli strains NDM-5 ST405 and NDM-5 ST10 in cases of BSI in Apulia. Integration of WGS into regional CRE surveillance is essential to detect high-risk clones and guide infection control and stewardship interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54754-z.

Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) pose a critical and growing threat to global public health in this century, largely due to their ability to acquire carbapenemase-encoding genes and the limited therapeutic options for infections they cause1. Among CRE, New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM)-producing Escherichia coli (E. coli) are particularly concerning, as these enzymes confer high-level resistance to most β-lactams and are associated with both hospital- and community-acquired infections2–4.

NDM has diversified into multiple variants, with NDM-1 and NDM-5 being among the most clinically relevant. NDM-1-producing E. coli has been detected in multiple lineages, with ST10 being one of the most frequently reported and globally distributed3,5–8. The ST10 clone exhibits remarkable ecological versatility, circulating in human, animal, and environmental reservoirs, and plays a key role in the global dissemination of blaNDM-15–7,9.

Recently, NDM-5 has been increasingly detected in high-risk E. coli lineages, particularly ST405, a globally disseminated high-risk clone characterized by multidrug resistance (MDR), multiple virulence determinants (including increased epithelial cell adherence, invasion capacity, and resistance to human serum bactericidal activity), and a high propensity for acquiring carbapenemase-encoding plasmids2,9–12. As a significant reservoir of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) genes such as CTX-M-15 and carbapenemase genes, including blaNDM-5, ST405 is emerging as a key driver of NDM-5 dissemination9.

The Italian Ministry of Health (MoH) issued the first directive for CRE monitoring and control in 2013 (MoH DGPRE n°4968 26/02/2013), with updates in 201913. According to CRE surveillance, all culture-confirmed bloodstream infections (BSI)—defined as viable Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) or E. coli in blood (≥ 1 positive blood culture) resistant to ≥ 1 carbapenem (ertapenem, imipenem, or meropenem)—must be reported to the national surveillance system. Data from this national surveillance showed that KPC remains the most prevalent carbapenemase, while NDM detection has been rare12,14,15. However, recent reports show an increase in bloodstream infections caused by NDM-producing E. coli isolates16–20, including the first identification of NDM-5-producing ST405 in the mid-2010s21. Subsequently, only a few cases of plasmid-mediated circulation of blaNDM-5 have been documented at the national level, particularly in northern Italy17,22, alongside ST405 carrying NDM-4, highlighting its ability to acquire diverse carbapenemase-encoding elements23. These findings collectively underscore the evolutionary plasticity of ST405 and its central role in the dissemination of NDM variants.

Despite these trends, limited data are available on NDM-producing E. coli in Southern Italy, particularly in the Apulia region, where clonal lineages, plasmid structures, and local transmission dynamics remain poorly characterized. The aim of the study was to describe the emergence of E. coli NDM producing isolated in bloodstream infections in Apulia in post-COVID19 pandemic period.

Methods

Bacterial strains and patient data collection

In the Apulia region, bacterial isolates along with their corresponding antibiograms are forwarded to the Laboratory of Molecular Epidemiology and Public Health, Hygiene Unit, University Hospital Policlinico of Bari, for molecular confirmation of carbapenemase-encoding genes. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was carried out by the network laboratories using either a Phoenix Automated Microbiology System (Becton Dickinson Diagnostic Systems, Sparks, MD, USA) or a Vitek-2 System (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’E´toile, France). The results were interpreted according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines24.

The study included hospitalized patients with hospital-acquired E. coli BSI from January 2014 to August 2025. Participating hospital microbiology laboratories collected consecutive non-duplicate CR E. coli bloodstream isolates (imipenem, ertapenem, or meropenem resistance per EUCAST breakpoints)24. Isolates were stored at − 80 °C in Microbank cryotubes (PROLAB Diagnostics, Richmond Hill, ON, Canada) until further analysis. For each patient, a standardized form captured demographic data (sex, age), hospital (name, province), ward, presumptive BSI source, and phenotypic resistance profiles.

Molecular characterization of E. coli isolates

All CR-E. coli isolates collected during the study period were screened using a commercial multiplex real-time PCR assay (GeneXpert platform, Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) for the main carbapenemase-encoding genes: blaKPC, blaVIM, blaNDM, blaIMP, and blaOXA-48. E. coli isolates harboring the blaNDM gene were subsequently subjected to whole-genome sequencing (WGS).

Whole genome sequencing of NDM-producing E. coli isolates, genome annotation and characterization

Genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Milan, Italy). Whole genome sequencing was performed on the MiniSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) with Nextera XT paired-end library preparation (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and 2 × 100 bp read. Quality control of raw sequence data was performed using FastQC v0.12.1. Reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.4025 and assembled using SPAdes v4.0.026.

To assess the genetic relatedness of ST405 isolates, core-genome SNP analysis was conducted using CSI Phylogeny v1.427. The obtained matrix of the SNP differences was used to construct the phylogenetic tree using the UPGMA algorithm (Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean) [see Additional file 1]. A consensus multi-FASTA alignment of E. coli genomes from NCBI and EnteroBase identified SAMN15719898 as the most suitable reference for SNP calling. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized by uploading the Newick file to the iTOL platform28.

Resistance genes, virulence genes and plasmid incompatibility groups were assessed using BLAST searches against the ResFinder, VirulenceFinder and PlasmidFinder databases, respectively.

Results

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing and characterization of resistance genes

Between January 2014 and August 2025, 50 E. coli isolates resistant to at least one tested carbapenem (meropenem, imipenem or ertapenem) were recovered from inpatients with bloodstream infection through the regional CRE surveillance. All the 50 collected isolates underwent screening for carbapenemase-encoding genes. The following resistance genes were detected: blaKPC, 46% (23/50) and blaNDM, 22% (11/50). The blaVIM, blaIMP and blaOXA-48 genes were not detected. Sixteen isolates (32%) exhibited carbapenem resistance mechanisms, but no carbapenemase genes (blaKPC, blaVIM, blaNDM, blaIMP, and blaOXA-48) were detected using the molecular screening panel used in surveillance. Overall, 11 blaNDM-positive isolates (22.0%) were identified between 2023 and 2025, of which 9 were available for WGS. All isolates were phenotypically and genotypically MDR and colistin-susceptible [see Additional file 2].

Clinical information and overview of the NDM-producing E. coli strains

Among the nine analyzed strains, eight carried the blaNDM-5 gene and one carried blaNDM-1 gene (Table 1). The studied strains belonged to two sequence types (STs): ST405 (eight isolates; phylogenetic group D; serotype O102:H6; all isolates carried fimH29) and ST10 (one isolate; phylogenetic group A; serotype O9:H9; fimH54) (Table 1). The source of bacteriemia was a urinary tract infection in 5/9 (55.6%). One patient died (isolate 39 CAS-LE). The ST10 E. coli isolate harbored multiple resistance genes, including those conferring carbapenem resistance (blaNDM-1, blaOXA-1), plasmid-mediated quinolone-resistance (aac(6’)-Ib-cr and qnrS1), chloramphenicol-resistance (catB3), aminoglycoside-resistance (aph(3)-VI), and ESBL production (blaTEM-220, blaTEM-135, blaTEM-126, blaTEM-106, blaTEM-1B) (Fig. 2). The virulome differed slightly from that of the ST405 isolates, featuring genes encoding for immune evasion (yehABCD), adhesins (nlpl, csgA, fdeC, fimH) and metabolism (gad). The ST10 isolate (240-TA) carried the avian toxin hlyE gene but lacked fyuA and iutC (Fig. 2).

E. coli ST405

The eight ST405 E. coli isolates were MDR and exhibited similar resistance patterns. In particular, resistome profiling revealed the presence of the NDM-5 variant, along with co-harboring of blaOXA-1, blaCTX-M-15 and sul1 genes (Fig. 2). Additionally, the resistome included genes conferring resistance to fluoroquinolones (acc(6')-Ib-cr), chloramphenicol (catB3), trimethoprim (dfrA17), and tetracycline (tet(B)).

Consistent with their MDR profile, all E. coli ST405 isolates were resistant to multiple antibiotic classes, including aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, chloramphenicol, tetracyclines, trimethoprim, carbapenems, and most β-lactams (e.g., amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ampicillin, piperacillin/tazobactam), as well as ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (Additional File 2).

Virulome analysis identified genes encoding immune evasion (yehABCD, traT), adhesins (nlpl, csgA, fdeC, fimH, papC), yersinia-abactin (fyuA and irp2), the avian toxin hlyE gene, heme uptake (chuA), iron acquisition systems including aerobactin (iutA, iucC), and metabolism (gad). Only one isolate (789-BR) carried the colicin-Ia (cia) gene, implicated in host cell invasion and intracellular survival.

Core-genome SNP analysis revealed that 6 of the 8 isolates were closely related (< 30 SNP differences; Fig. 2). These strains were isolated from patients at Casarano Hospital (Lecce province), albeit on different dates. The other two strains (38CAS-LE and 789BR) were unrelated, with 38CAS-LE isolated from a patient at Casarano Hospital and 789BR isolated from a patient at Brindisi Hospital.

Plasmid analysis

PlasmidFinder analysis identified several plasmid replicon types in Escherichia coli ST405 isolates, including Col(BS512), IncFIA, IncFIB(AP001918), Col(MG828), and IncFII. In addition, isolate 789BR also harbored ColpVC and IncB/O/K/Z replicons. Discussion

The spread of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae represents a major public health concern, with nosocomial and community-acquired NDM-producing E. coli emerging as one of the most important metallo-β-lactamase producers in Europe3.

In Italy, the first NDM-producing Enterobacteriaceae were reported as imported cases18,23,29. Subsequently, a concerning increase in NDM-producing K. pneumoniae and NDM-producing E. coli isolates from patients with bloodstream infections has been observed20,30.

This study documents the emergence of NDM-producing E. coli bloodstream isolates in Apulia, Southern Italy, during regional CRE surveillance conducted from 2014 to 2025. Surveillance data from Apulia showed that 1.8% of CRE cases were caused by E. coli, compared with 2.9% at national level. This data may reflect under-reporting or a different prevalence of E. coli as a cause of CRE.

Among 50 carbapenem-resistant E. coli isolates, blaKPC predominated (46%), consistent with national trends where KPC enzymes account for most CRE cases. However, blaNDM was detected in 22% (11/50), with all cases clustered in 2023–2025, signalling a recent upsurge. Notably, 32% carried undetected resistance mechanisms, underscoring limitations of routine PCR panels and the need for WGS in surveillance. Nine blaNDM-positive isolates underwent WGS, revealing two high-risk clones: ST405 (blaNDM-5, n = 8) and ST10 (blaNDM-1, n = 1), predominantly originating from medical wards in Lecce, Brindisi, and Taranto provinces.

The dominance of ST405 aligns with its global status as a high-risk clone for MDR and carbapenemase acquisition9–12. All ST405 isolates exhibited extensive MDR profiles, resistant to β-lactams (including piperacillin/tazobactam), fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and others, but remained colistin-susceptible, a critical last-resort option. Resistome profiling confirmed blaNDM-5 co-carried with blaCTX-M-15, blaOXA-1, aac(6')-Ib-cr, catB3, dfrA17, and tet(B), mirroring resistance patterns previously reported in Europe and Asia (2). Virulome analysis revealed a rich repertoire of genes including adhesins (fimH, papC), iron acquisition (fyuA, iucA), and immune evasion (traT), conferring hypervirulence potential, as reported in recent Chinese reports of ST405 hypervirulent MDR strains12.

All eight ST405 isolates from our study demonstrated high AMR rates and carried IncFII, IncFIA, IncFIB (AP001918), known vectors for blaNDM-5, and IncCol(BS512) plasmid types and fimH29 allele, with isolate 789-BR uniquely harboring IncB/O/K/Z and ColpVC, potentially facilitating horizontal transfer.

Analysis of SNPs in the core genome revealed a tight cluster comprising 6 of the 7 ST405 strains present at Casarano Hospital, even though the timeline analysis and epidemiological data did not reveal any close epidemiological links31. The single ST10 isolate (240TA) carried blaNDM-1 with diverse ESBLs (blaTEM variants), qnrS1, and aph(3')-VI, but lacked macrolide/tetracycline resistance, reflecting its ecological versatility across human-animal-environmental niches3–8. Its reduced virulome (lacking fyuA/iucC) contrasts with ST405, yet hlyE presence supports pathogenicity in urinary tract infection-sourced BSI. Plasmid analysis via PlasmidFinder highlighted conserved IncF replicons (IncFIA, IncFIB, IncFII) in ST405-known vectors for blaNDM-5-with isolate 789-BR uniquely harboring IncB/O/K/Z and ColpVC, potentially facilitating horizontal transfer.

In Italy, NDM-producing E. coli have historically been rare compared to KPC-producing K. pneumoniae14–16. Recent reports, however, document autochthonous blaNDM-5-producing ST405 in northern Italy and early blaNDM-1-producing ST405 cases32. Our findings extend this trend to southern Italy, describing Apulia’s first sequenced blaNDM-5 ST405 cluster (2023–2025), which post-dates national alerts and parallels ECDC-reported increases across the EU/EEA1–3. Bacteremia primarily originated from urinary tract infections (55.6%) and pneumonia, predominantly affecting elderly males in medical wards, a pattern suggestive of post-COVID-19 vulnerabilities, similar to prior KPC outbreaks in the region. The single recorded fatality (isolate 39 CAS-LE) underscores clinical severity, although the limited sample size precludes identification of mortality risk factors.

These findings address a critical gap in NDM epidemiology in Southern Italian, where surveillance lags behind efforts in the north. The IncF plasmid-mediated dissemination of blaNDM-5 in ST405 mirrors EU cross-border alerts, necessitating stricter enforcement of regional Ministry of Health CRE directives13. Integration of WGS enables outbreak detection beyond routine PCR screening. Study limitations include the absence of early blaNDM strains for WGS and unassessed transmission pathways. Future investigations should incorporate long-read sequencing to evaluate plasmid stability and adopt One Health approaches, given the zoonotic potential of ST105–7.

In conclusion, the emergence of NDM-5-producing ST405 in Apulia threatens regional CRE control and underscores the need for enhanced genomic surveillance, antimicrobial stewardship, and inter-hospital coordination to mitigate transmission of high-risk clones.

Conclusions

This study provides a detailed genomic characterization of NDM-5-producing Escherichia coli isolates from Apulia. It also demonstrates the utility of WGS for monitoring and resolving emerging antimicrobial resistance trends. Accordingly, we recommend routine collection, analysis, and integration of detailed epidemiological data within comprehensive genomic surveillance frameworks.
