Complete genome sequences of Sathuperi and Shamonda viruses isolated in Japan
Authors: Yuto SUDA, Katsunori MUROTA, Tohru YANASE
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Sathuperi virus (SATV) and Shamonda virus (SHAV) (family Peribunyaviridae, genus Orthobunyavirus, species Schmallenberg orthobunyavirus) have been suggested to cause congenital abnormalities in ruminants. In this study, we determined the complete genome sequences of SATV KSB-6/C/02 and SHAV KSB-2/C/08 strains, which were obtained from Culicoides biting midges in Japan, by next-generation sequencing and Sanger sequencing. The 3ʹ- and 5ʹ-untranslated region sequences of the M segment of SHAV KSB-2/C/08 strains are distinctly different from those of SATV KSB-6/C/02 and Schmallenberg viruses. This study provides the genome characterization of Japanese strains of SATV and SHAV and presented the genetic variation in the untranslated regions of Schmallenberg orthobunyavirus M segments.

Sathuperi virus (SATV) and Shamonda virus (SHAV) are tripartite, negative-sense RNA viruses belonging to the family Peribunyaviridae, genus Orthobunyavirus, species Schmallenberg orthobunyavirus. These viruses have both been suggested to cause congenital abnormalities in ruminants [4, 10]. SATV and SHAV share some genetic and/or antigenic properties with Schmallenberg virus (SBV), which has been involved in epidemics of abortion and congenital deformities in ruminants in European countries since 2011 [3, 9]. The genome consists of three single-stranded, negative-sense RNA segments. The S segment encodes the nucleocapsid protein and non-structural protein (NSs). The M segment encodes the two surface glycoproteins (Gn and Gc) and the other non-structural protein (NSm). The L segment encodes the RNA dependent RNA polymerase [2]. The M segment of SBV is genetically close with that of SATV while the S and L segments of SBV are genetically close with that of SHAV [3]. Neutralization test revealed by anti-SBV serum that SATV was well neutralized though SHAV was not neutralized [3]. Under experimental conditions in vitro, reassortment was indicated to have occurred between SATV and SHAV [1].

Culicoides midges collected using a light trap at a cowshed located in Kagoshima, Japan (31.58°N, 130.48°E, altitude 150 m) were preserved as pools and subjected to virus isolation [10, 11]. SATV KSB-2/C/08 was isolated on BHK-21 cells and further passaged two-times on BHK-21 cells and four-times on HmLu-1 cells for the virus propagation. SHAV KSB-6/C/02 was isolated on HmLu-1 cells and further passaged five-times on HmLu-1 cells for the virus propagation. The passages were necessary to obtain sufficient titers for analysis though it might have affected the viral genomes. For next-generation sequencing, total RNA was extracted from the cell cultures using ISOGEN II (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) and then used for double-stranded cDNA synthesis with an NEBNext RNA Ultra First Strand Synthesis Module and an NEBNext RNA Ultra Second Strand Synthesis Module (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Libraries were prepared using a TruSeq DNA Nano LT Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols, loaded onto an iSeq 100 i1 Reagent cartridge (Illumina), and sequenced on an iSeq 100 System (Illumina). The raw data obtained by the next-generation sequencing were deposited in the DDBJ DRA under accession numbers DRR438254-DRR438255. The contigs were analyzed by target resequencing (reference sequences: HE795102.1 [SATV L segment], HE795103.1 [SATV M segment], HE795104.1 [SATV S segment], HE795105.1 [SHAV L segment], HE795106.1 [SHAV M segment], HE795107.1 [SHAV S segment]) using CLC genomic workbench 12 (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) with the default parameters. The mapped reads of SATV L, M, and S segments were 6,133 (average coverage: 133.11), 3,829 (average coverage: 131.17), and 668 (average coverage: 116.27), respectively. Those of SHAV L, M, S segments were 18,311 (average coverage: 395.13), 12,849 (average coverage: 439.606), and 5,592 (average coverage: 679.42), respectively. The 3′-terminal sequence of all of the genomic RNA segments was determined with the use of rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) as described [6]. Double-stranded RNAs, which are viral replication intermediates, were extracted from the infected cells using ISOVIRUS II (Nippon Gene), and RACE was performed to determine the 3′ terminal sequence of complementary viral genomes, allowing the identification of the 5′ terminal sequence of the viral genomes, as described [12]. The RACE products were analyzed by Sanger sequencing using a BigDye Terminator v. 3.1 Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on an ABI 3100-Avant Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used for the RACE and sequence reaction were listed in Supplementary Table 1. The contigs of each viral segment were manually assembled with the corresponding terminal sequences by an alignment software (MUSCLE) in MEGA 11 with the default parameters [8]. The viral genome sequences have been deposited in the DDBJ under accession numbers LC741384 (SATV KSB-2/C/08 L segment), LC741385 (SATV KSB-2/C/08 M segment), LC741386 (SATV KSB-2/C/08 S segment), LC741387 (SHAV KSB-6/C/02 L segment), LC741388 (SHAV KSB-6/C/02 M segment), and LC741389 (SHAV KSB-6/C/02 S segment).

The full-length L, M, and S segments of the SATV strain KSB-2/C/08 are 6,882, 4,367, and 840 nucleotides (nt), and those of the SHAV strain KSB-6/C/02 are 6,882, 4,315, and 839 nt, respectively (Table 1). The nucleotide sequence identity between full length of L, M, and S segments of SATV and SHAV were 84.42%, 51.76%, and 93.44% that calculated using Clustal Omega [7] with the default parameters. The sequences of SATV and SHAV were compared with those of SBV strain F6 (accession nos. KC355457 [L segment], KC355458 [M segment], and KC355459 [S segment]) [5]. The nucleotide sequence identity between L, M, and S segments of SBV and SATV were 84.68%, 82.04%, and 93.33%. The nucleotide sequence identity between those of SBV and SHAV were 89.45%, 50.26%, and 97.26%. The result showed the relatively lower identity between M segment of SHAV and other two viruses. Furthermore, we focused on the sequences of the 3′- and 5′- untranslated regions (UTRs). In contrast, the sequences of the 3′- and 5′-UTRs of the M segment differ between SHAV and the other two viruses. The length of the 3′-UTR of the M segment of SHAV is 17 nt whereas those of the M segments of SATV and SBV are 23 nt. The length of the 5′-UTR of the SHAV M segment is 86 nt, and those of the M segments of SATV and SBV are 132 and 135 nt, respectively. In addition, the 5′-UTR of the SHAV M segment has no obvious homology with those of the SATV and SBV M segments. It was revealed that not only the full-length sequence but also the UTR sequence of SHAV M segment was unique in comparison with SATV and SBV. These results revealed the variety of UTR sequences of M segments among the viruses classified in Schmallenberg orthobunyavirus and suggest that the M segment of SHAV has uniquely evolved. We searched for similar nucleotide sequence to the SHAV M segment and the 5′ -UTR using BLAST (National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI)), but no registered viruses other than SHAV were found. There is a possibility that the SHAV M segment was derived from another unknown orthobunyavirus.

It was shown that each segment of Orthobunyavirus genomes has highly conserved and complementary genus-specific terminal sequences that form a panhandle structure and are associated with the genome transcription, replication, and packaging [2]. Fifteen nt of the 3′- and 5′-termini of the M segment of all three viruses are conserved, which suggests that the difference in UTR sequences would not interfere with the reassortment, but the terminal conserved sequences might be essential to generate a reassortant between orthobunyaviruses.

Further studies of the UTRs of the related orthobunyaviruses will help clarify their roles in replication, transcription, and packaging. The molecular mechanisms that underlie the regulation of the reassortment among orthobunyaviruses should be investigated to assess the potential emergence of novel reassortant orthobunyaviruses.
