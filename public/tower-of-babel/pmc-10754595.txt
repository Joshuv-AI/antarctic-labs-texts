Extracorporeal shock wave therapy for low back pain: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhuorao Wu, Tianqi Zhou, Shuangchun Ai
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:To provide high-quality evidence for extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) for low back pain (LBP).Methods:A computerized search screened trials of ESWT for LBP, and the time range was from the establishment of databases to August 2023, including CNKI, CBMdisc, Wanfang Data, VIP, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane databases. The outcomes were extracted, including patients’ pain, physical and psychological conditions, and adverse reactions. Meta-analysis was completed using Revman 5.4 and Stata 15 software, and GRADEpro software quantitated rate the evidence and assigned a recommendation strength.Results:This meta-analysis included 22 studies involving 1749 patients. After treatment, patients in the ESWT group had lower scores in the visual analog scale (VAS) (mean difference [MD] = −1.14, 95% confidence interval [CI] (−1.47, −0.80), P < .00001), Oswestry disability index (ODI) [MD = −6.01, 95%CI (−7.97, −4.05), P < .00001], and Beck depression inventory [MD = −3.89, 95%CI (−6.59, −1.20), P = .005]; finger-floor distance [MD = −3.71, 95%CI (−5.26, −2.17), P < .00001] was shorter; Japanese orthopedic association scores [MD = 3.20, 95%CI (1.56, 4.85), P = .0001] were higher; at the 3-month follow-up, the VAS [MD = −1.29, 95%CI (−2.39, −0.19), P = .02] and ODI [MD = −5.95, 95%CI (−10.06, −1.84), P = .005] remained lower; adverse reactions [odds ratio = 0.19, 95%CI (0.07, 0.52), P = .001] were less, and the valid cases [odds ratio = 3.84, 95%CI (2.47, 5.96), P < .00001] were more. Sensitivity analyses revealed that the results were stable; Egger test found no publication bias in VAS (P = .270) and valid cases (P = .747); the trim and fill analysis tested the robustness of ODI scores due to the publication bias (P = .029), and the effect sizes before and after the trim and fill were consistent, which means the result was robust.Conclusion:ESWT for LBP has efficacy and safety in improving pain, dysfunction, and psychological conditions compared to other therapies. ESWT has shown advantages in terms of long-term efficacy. Randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and more objective outcomes are required.

1. Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is characterized by pain in the lower back, lumbosacral region (usually between the last rib margin and gluteal fold), and buttocks. Low back pain can be classified as mechanical, neuropathic, or nociplastic.[1] These anomalies of the body generate discomfort, and almost everyone experiences acute lower back pain or chronic pain at some point in their lives. A systematic review of low back pain found that the prevalence of LBP, the incidence, and the years lived with disability have increased dramatically over the last 3 decades, that LBP remains the leading cause of years lived with disability globally in 2019, and that the prevalence increases with age, peaking at 45–54 years in both sexes.[2] According to another study, lower back pain ranked 13th in disability-adjusted life years.[3] Because of the heavy economic and psychological burden it imposes on the globe, we should prioritize therapeutic alternatives that can relieve pain quickly, improve patients’ quality of life, shorten treatment periods, and show benefits in long-term follow-up.

The guideline recommends non-pharmacological treatment, such as physical therapy and kinesiotherapy, as the first-line treatment for low back pain. Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT), as a conservative treatment, has been accepted by patients with LBP because of its safe, effective, noninvasive, and non-hospitalization characteristics. The mechanism of ESWT in treating LBP includes anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects, improvement of blood circulation, regulation of central sensitization, and local effects, which are clinically effective in improving pain, dysfunction, and quality of life. An expert consensus from 2023 states that ESWT is effective in lumbar disc herniation, but there is a lack of high-quality evidence to support its use in clinical practice.[4] Lei[5] made the first meta-analysis of ESWT for LBP in 2021, while Liu[6] systematic review in 2023 assessed mental health scores for the first time. However, there are contradictions in these studies; Lei[5] and Li[7] thought that the long-term efficacy of ESWT in patients with LBP was insignificant, but Liu[6] thought it was considerable. In addition, previous systematic reviews had some issues and errors, such as failure to investigate the impact of differences in treatment parameters and duration due to the small number of included studies,[5] failure to discuss the long-term efficacy of ESWT due to a small number of studies,[8] errors in the table of characteristics of the included studies,[7] and lack of methodological rigor in the grouping for subgroup analysis.[6] Due to the continuous updating of studies, controversial results, and the small number of systematic reviews on ESWT for LBP, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the effectiveness and safety of ESWT for LBP again by meta-analysis to judge the quality of evidence objectively and rigorously and to provide high-quality evidence for LBP.[5,6,8]

2. Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Statement (PRISMA), and the protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on the PROSPERO website (registration number: CRD42023453890).

2.1. Search strategy

Computerized searches of 8 databases, including CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, CBMdisc, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane, covered the period from database creation to August 2023. The search strategy used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) combined with free terms. The P’s/participant’s MeSH term was low back pain with free terms, such as mechanical low back pain; the I’s/intervention’s MeSH term was extracorporeal shockwave therapy with free words, such as Shockwave Therapies, Extracorporeal. Using PubMed as an example, Data S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L151 shows the search strategy.

2.2. Inclusion criteria

(1) Participants: Patients with a clinical diagnosis of low back pain (age ≥ 18 years). (2) Intervention: ESWT or ESWT combined with other interventions. (3) Control: Conventional treatment other than ESWT (including physiotherapy, oral medication, acupuncture) or sham ESWT. (4) Outcomes: The primary outcomes included pain (scale and pressure pain threshold), dysfunction (scale and range of motion [ROM]), and psychological health. Examples include the visual analog scale (VAS), pressure pain threshold (PPT), Oswestry disability index (ODI), Japanese orthopaedic association (JOA) scores, finger-floor distance (FFD), ROM, Beck depression inventory (BDI), and Hamilton Depression Scale. Secondary outcomes included adverse reactions and valid cases. (5) Study design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)

2.3. Exclusion criteria

(1) Non-Chinese and non-English literature; (2) duplicate publications: if the baseline and outcomes were the same, keep the first to publish at this time; (3) no full text accessible; and (4) participants with uncertain diagnoses.

2.4. Data extraction

The 2 researchers independently extracted data according to a preestablished data form, which mainly included information about the studies, baseline characteristics, interventions, outcomes, and follow-up. Follow-up was the data source used to evaluate long-term curative effects, and the period was 3 months (or the closest to 3 months). Disagreements were resolved by negotiation or by a third investigator.

2.5. Bias assessment and quality classification

Two researchers assessed the risk of bias in the studies using the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing the risk of bias (Revman 5.4 software), which includes selection, performance, detection, attrition, and reporting bias. GRADEpro objectively grades evidence and provides a reasonable recommendation strength.

2.6. Statistical analyses

Revman 5.4 software for data merging and funnel plotting, while Stata 15 software for sensitivity analysis, Egger test, and meta-regression. The effect sizes for dichotomous variables were odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI); the effect sizes for continuous variables were mean difference (MD) and 95%CI (test level was α = 0.05). The Q-test was evaluated for heterogeneity, with P identifying the existence or absence of heterogeneity and I2 estimating the degree of heterogeneity. When I2 < 50% or P > .10, the studies were homogenous and chose the fixed-effect model; when I2 ≥ 50% or P ≤ .10, the studies were heterogeneous and chose the random-effect model. Sensitivity analyses evaluated the robustness and reliability of the results; meta-regression and subgroup analyses identified probable sources of heterogeneity, and differences were statistically significant at P < .05. The symmetry of the funnel plot determined the publication bias, and Egger test quantified the funnel plot. Publication bias exists when P ≤ .05, in which case the robustness of the results was tested using trim-and-fill analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Selection of studies

A database search identified 689 studies, with 395 remaining after removing duplicates. The meta-analysis comprised 22 studies after reviewing the full text of the studies. The results are shown in Figure 1.

3.3. Risk of bias and quality classification

Fifteen of the included studies used a random number method (random number table or software-generated random numbers), and 7 mentioned only “random” without describing the specific randomization method; 7 studies used an envelope method for allocation concealment, 15 did not; 15 studies had no blinding, 5 had low risks of performance bias, and 2 blinded only the outcome assessment; 22 had no missing data and did not selectively. The risk of bias is shown in Figure 2. GRADE pro software assesses the quality of evidence and assigns a reasonable recommendation strength. The results are presented in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L152.

Risk of bias.

3.4. Meta-analysis

3.4.1. Pain.

3.4.1.1. Visual analog scale (after treatment).

Seventeen studies[9,11–16,19,21–29] with 1389 patients reported VAS scores after treatment with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 90%, P < .00001). A meta-analysis using the random-effects model found that patients in the experimental group had lower VAS scores than those in the control group, indicating that pain relief was better in the ESWT group (MD = −1.14, 95%CI [−1.47, −0.80], P < .00001). Figure 3A shows the results.

Forest plots of the meta-analysis: (A) VAS, after treatment; (B) VAS, 3-month follow-up; (C) ODI, after treatment; (D) ODI, 3-month follow-up; (E) JOA; (F) FFD; (G) BDI; (H) valid cases; (I) adverse reactions. BDI = Beck depression inventory, FFD = finger-floor distance, JOA = Japanese orthopaedic association, ODI = Oswestry disability index, VAS = visual analog scale.

3.4.1.2. Visual analog scale (follow-up of 3 months).

Five studies,[15,19,21–23] including 209 patients, reported a VAS score at the 3-month follow-up with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 64%, P = .03). A meta-analysis using a random-effects model revealed that the long-term efficacy of ESWT in relieving patients’ pain was better than that in the control group (MD = −1.29, 95%CI [−2.39, −0.19], P = .02). Figure 3B shows the results.

3.4.2. Dysfunction.

3.4.2.1. Oswestry disability index (after treatment).

Fourteen studies,[9–13,15,18,19,21–25,29] including 1242 patients, reported posttreatment ODI with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98%, P < .00001). The meta-analysis used a random-effects model, and the results showed that ESWT improved dysfunction better than the others (MD = −6.01, 95%CI [−7.97, −4.05], P < .00001). Figure 3C shows the results.

3.4.2.2. Oswestry disability index (follow-up of 3 months).

Five studies,[15,19,21–23] including 209 patients, reported ODI at the 3-month follow-up, with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 70%, P = .01). The ODI of the experimental group remained lower than that of others according to a meta-analysis using a random-effects model (MD = −5.95, 95%CI [−10.06, −1.84], P = .005). Figure 3D shows the result.

3.4.2.3. Japanese orthopedic association scores.

Five trials,[10,17,20,27,30] including 415 patients, reported posttreatment JOA with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 88%, P < .00001). A meta-analysis using a random-effects model revealed that patients in the experimental group had higher JOA scores than those in the control group (MD = 3.20, 95%CI [1.56, 4.85], P = .0001). Figure 3E shows the results.

3.4.2.4. Finger-floor distance.

Three trials,[15,26,28] including 198 patients, reported posttreatment FFD with good homogeneity (I2 = 49%, P = .14). A fixed-effects model was used to conduct the meta-analysis, and the results indicated that ESWT treatment was more beneficial than the control group in terms of improving lumbar spine mobility (MD = −3.71, 95%CI [−5.26, −2.17], P < .00001). Figure 3F shows the results.

3.4.3. Psychological health.

Two studies[13,15] reported on the BDI, including 100 patients, with homogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = .94). A meta-analysis using a fixed-effects model revealed that ESWT treatment was more beneficial to patient’s mental health than the control group (MD = −3.89, 95%CI [−6.59, −1.20], P = .005). Figure 3G shows the results.

3.4.4. Valid cases.

Ten studies[9–11,14,17,20,24,27,29,30] involving 1221 patients reported valid cases with homogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = .99). A meta-analysis using a fixed-effects model showed that the number of valid cases in the experimental group was higher than that in the control group (OR = 3.84, 95%CI [2.47, 5.96], P < .00001). Figure 3H shows the results.

3.4.5. Adverse reactions.

Two studies[20,25] reported adverse reactions involving 144 patients with homogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = .95). The adverse reactions were mainly pain during treatment and local swelling on the day of treatment. A fixed-effects meta-analysis revealed that the experimental group had fewer adverse reactions than the control group (OR = 0.19, 95%CI [0.07, 0.52], P = .001). Figure 3I shows the results.

3.5. Meta-regression and subgroup analyses

Meta-regression analysis for effect sizes (including more than 10 studies) with significant heterogeneity and covariates included sample size, pretreatment condition, course of LBP, and treatment parameters. The primary sources of heterogeneity in VAS, according to meta-regression, were pretreatment condition (P = .005), course of LBP (P = .021), and treatment parameters (P = .042); in ODI were sample size (P = .010) and pretreatment condition (P = .003). Subgroup analyses of effect sizes with significant heterogeneity were performed using the same 4 variables. This study did not conduct a subgroup analysis of JOA based on the duration of LBP because only 2 studies reported this. Subgroup analysis revealed that treatment parameters may be the source of heterogeneity in VAS and ODI outcomes, whereas sample size may influence heterogeneity in the JOA results. There was no change in the analysis results for each subgroup, which was consistent with the overall results (P < .05). Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L153 presents the results.

3.6. Sensitivity analysis

3.6.1. Stability of result.

The effect size of each outcome was consistent with the original results, indicating that the results were stable. BDI and adverse reactions could not be analyzed because only 2 studies were included. The results are shown in Figure S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L154.

3.6.2. Source of heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by eliminating studies that had high heterogeneity in outcomes. The results showed a significant decrease in heterogeneity after the exclusion of 4 studies for VAS (posttreatment) and one for VAS (follow-up), 6 studies for ODI (posttreatment) and 2 for ODI (follow-up), and 2 studies for JOA. After exclusion, meta-analysis was performed using a fixed-effects model, and the results were consistent with the original results. The results of this study are presented in Figure S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L155.

3.7. Publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots for outcomes (≥10 studies), and Egger test was used to quantify funnel plots. The funnel plot of VAS and valid cases were symmetrical, and Egger test (P = .270, P = .747) revealed no publication bias. Because the funnel plot of ODI was asymmetrical and Egger test (P = .029, P = .05) showed publication bias, the robustness of these results was assessed using trim and fill analysis. The original effect size was MD = −6.01, 95% CI (−7.97, −4.05) (P = .000), and the random-effect model was used for reanalysis after the inclusion of 2 dummy studies, and the result was MD = −6.89, 95% CI (−8.79, −4.99) (P = .000). The results before and after trim-and-fill were the same and not reversed, indicating stability. The results are presented in Figure 4.

Funnel plot: (A) VAS; (B) ODI; (C) valid cases. ODI = Oswestry disability index, VAS = visual analog scale.

4. Discussion

Previous meta-analyses of ESWT for LBP have produced inconsistent results with varying quality. Lei[5] believed that 1 month of ESWT treatment improved pain and dysfunction, but the long-term efficacy was not significant; the meta-regression found that age, sex ratio, and pain at baseline could not explain the heterogeneity of outcomes, and subgroup analysis was not conducted due to the number of participants; there were differences in duration of LBP and parameters of treatment, but they were not investigated. Ma[8] meta-analysis, which was not prospectively registered and included only 5 randomized controlled trials, concluded that more evidence for the safety of ESWT is needed, and the parameters of ESWT require further investigation, short-term efficacy of ESWT was superior to that of the control group, but the long-term efficacy was unclear. Li[7] meta-analysis had some errors in the characteristic table of the included studies: in Eftekharsadat[12] trial, patients in the control group received trigger point injection combined with kinesitherapy, but Li reported it as a sham ESWT; magneto-thermo-vibration therapy in Zheng[28] study was also reported as a sham ESWT. The findings of Liu[6] meta-analysis, which included only English literature, revealed that ESWT had both short-term and long-term efficacy; this study assessed mental health for the first time, but the difference was not significant; the grouping in subgroup analysis was not thorough, in Taheri[22] trial, the observation group received ESWT combined with kinesitherapy, while the control group received sham ESWT combined with kinesitherapy, both groups received same oral drugs, but Liu briefly defined it as ESWT versus oral medication and conducted a subgroup analysis based on it.

Previous meta-analyses reported more subjective outcomes, such as pain and dysfunction scales. Due to the limited number of included studies, they could not find sources of heterogeneity by subgroup analysis or meta-regression. One study pointed out that the difference in courses of LBP and treatment parameters in the included studies may have contributed to heterogeneity.[5,6,8] Therefore, we added objective indicators to the data table; however, only a few studies have reported them. This manuscript examines the variables that may produce heterogeneity using meta-regression and subgroup analyses to explain heterogeneity. Meanwhile, sensitivity analysis provides clues that allow us to identify the potential causes of heterogeneity.

We summarized the shortcomings of previous meta-analyses and aimed to provide high-quality evidence. This study included 22 randomized controlled trials. This study did not merge the JOA scores at the 1-month follow-up, PPT, and ROM for the following reasons: ① 2 studies reported PPT, but one used kg/cm2 as units, and the other did not; ② only one study reported a ROM; and ③ the JOA scores at 1-month follow-up variance from our specified follow-up period (3 months). The meta-analysis found that patients treated with ESWT showed better improvements in pain, dysfunction, ROM, and mental health than those in the control group, and they had fewer adverse effects. The effect size of the patient’s VAS and ODI scores at the 3-month follow-up showed that the long-term curative effect of ESWT was superior to that of other treatments.

The quality of evidence for VAS, ODI, JOA, and BDI posttreatment was LOW, and at the follow-up, VAS, ODI, FFD, valid cases, and adverse events were MODERATE. There was significant heterogeneity in the VAS, ODI, and JOA scores, and meta-regression analyses were performed to identify the cause of heterogeneity. The patient’s condition at baseline, duration of LBP, and treatment parameters were the sources of heterogeneity in the VAS scores; the condition at baseline and sample size were the sources of heterogeneity in the ODI scores. Subgroup analyses differed from meta-regression in that they revealed that the sample size, pretreatment condition, and duration of LBP had no effect on the heterogeneity of VAS and ODI scores, and that only treatment parameters could influence heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis of the JOA found that studies with small sample sizes may be the source of heterogeneity. The results of the subgroups were consistent with the original results, suggesting that the effectiveness of ESWT for LBP was not affected by the severity or duration of the patient’s condition.

Sensitivity analyses excluded studies individually to test the robustness of the meta-analysis results. Only 2 studies reported BDI and adverse reactions that could not assess the robustness of the effect size; the other 5 outcomes were robust and credible. In addition, when running sensitivity analyses, we discovered studies that impact heterogeneity, such as Eftekharsadat, Walewicz, Wu, and Taheri.[12,22,23,25] The full-text reading found clinical heterogeneity: the sample size in these 4 studies was small, with only 32 cases in Taheri[22] study; the course of LBP varied greatly, with the longest duration in Walewicz[23] study (127.4 months) and the shortest in Wu[25] (1.56 months); differences in patient’s condition before treatment between the trials, with the highest VAS score (7.36 points) in Eftekharsadat[12] study and the lowest (4.7 points) in Walewicz study. There was no significant methodological heterogeneity; all 4 trials reported the method of generating the randomized sequence, and all 3 studies, except Wu, used blinding. Funnel plots and Egger test assessed publication bias, and there was no significant publication bias in the VAS or valid cases (P < .05). Although there was some publication bias in the ODI, the effect size was close to that before the trim-and-fill analysis, indicating that the results were robust.

Most RCTs on ESWT for low back pain used subjective outcomes such as scales and fewer objective indicators such as electromyography, pressure pain thresholds, or blood index. In the 22 studies we included, Bao[9] reported that the lumbar ROM after ESWT treatment was better than in the control group; Chang[10] indicated that the levels of C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor-α, and interleukin-8 significantly decreased after treatment with ESWT, which may be related to pain relief; Wang[24] and Zhou[29] reported that β-endorphin levels increased and significant pain relief; the BioRescue system tested the dynamic balance ability in Lee study, and the results showed that the balance function influenced by pain improved after ESWT treatment. Furthermore, some studies were excluded from this meta-analysis because their experimental design did not fulfill our inclusion criteria. However, their methods of detecting dysfunction and pain in individuals with LBP are worthy of reference: Walewicz[35] analyzed the postural stabilization parameter (total sway path, TSP), and Elgendy[31] used electromyography to identify muscle potentials for evaluating lumbar muscle functions, which is objective and dependable. In addition to the mechanisms mentioned in the above studies (anti-inflammatory and analgesic), ESWT for low back pain may also improve blood circulation in the lower back and relieve central sensitization. One trial[34] reported that a single session of ESWT boosted capillary blood flow in the treated areas; Kisch[33] and colleagues found that ESWT resulted in a considerable improvement in capillary blood flow velocity and oxygen saturation; Kenmoku[32] discovered that ESWT can induce the degradation of acetylcholine receptors, and disrupted part of the neurotransmission in abnormally excitable neuromuscular junctions.

5. Limitation

Some of the included trials for this meta-analysis had small sample sizes, and there was no objective indicator to assess the curative effect besides FFD. Influenced by the quality of the studies, some of the evidence quality ratings were LOW, while others were MODERATE. As a result, future randomized controlled trials should try to recruit more participants and use more objective indicators, such as pressure pain thresholds to quantify the patient’s pain, electromyography to evaluate muscle dysfunction, capillary blood flow rate to judge blood circulation, and blood indices to explore the mechanism of analgesia. ESWT treatment parameters should be more standardized, and further comparisons between the curative effect at different treatment locations, such as acupoints and trigger points, should be made. The methodology of clinical trials should also be more rigorous to reduce bias, such as the generation and concealment of randomized sequences and the implementation of blinding.

6. Conclusion

This study performed a meta-analysis to objectively assess the effects of ESWT on pain, dysfunction, and mental health in patients with LBP, and the results showed the treatment is effective and safe. This study identified the sources of heterogeneity, and all results were robust and stable. However, larger sample sizes and high-quality clinical trials are needed to support our findings.
