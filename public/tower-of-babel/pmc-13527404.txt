Temporal Trends in Colorectal Cancer Associated With Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus in U.S. Adults, 1999–2023: A CDC Wonder Database Analysis—A Population‐Based Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Waleed Tariq, Neha Waseem, Mehreen Maka, Bushra Ubaid, Sheetal Verma, Yusra Ejaz, Maryam Owais, Awais Tariq, Soha Waseem, Iftikhar Khan, Ehsanullah Alokozay, Muhammad Hassan Tariq, Shahzaib Ahmed, Shoaib Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsColorectal cancer (CRC) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are significant contributors to global morbidity and mortality, with growing evidence suggesting a synergistic impact on disease outcomes. This research focused on analyzing trends in CRC‐related deaths among individuals with coexisting T2DM in the United States from 1999 to 2023, as well as exploring disparities among different demographic and geographic groups.MethodsData were obtained from the CDC WONDER database for deaths among adults aged ≥ 45 years with CRC as the underlying cause and T2DM as a contributing cause. Age‐adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 population were calculated using the 2000 U.S. standard population. Temporal trends were analyzed using Joinpoint regression to estimate annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two‐sided p < 05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsFrom 1999 to 2023, there were 22,392 recorded deaths. The overall age‐adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) increased from 0.55 to 1.04 per 100,000 people, with an average annual percentage change (AAPC) of 2.55% (95% CI: 2.18–3.06; p < 001). Men experienced higher mortality rates than women, and individuals aged ≥ 65 years had higher rates than those aged 45–64. Significant racial disparities were evident, with the highest AAMRs among Hispanic and non‐Hispanic Black groups. Geographically, the West had the highest AAMRs and the steepest increase over time. Rural areas consistently showed higher AAMRs compared to urban areas. Most deaths (44.06%) occurred at home.ConclusionCRC‐related mortality in individuals with coexisting T2DM has risen sharply in the US over the past twenty years, showing significant demographic and geographic differences. These results highlight the importance of implementing targeted, integrated prevention and management approaches to decrease the combined impact of CRC and T2DM. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are among the leading contributors to the global burden of chronic disease, resulting in considerable morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization worldwide. Globally, CRC accounts for approximately 10% of newly diagnosed cancers and remains the third most frequently diagnosed malignancy. It also continues to be a major contributor to cancer‐related mortality, ranking among the leading causes of cancer deaths. Future estimates suggest that the annual global burden of CRC may increase to approximately 3.2 million incident cases and 1.6 million deaths by 2040. In the United States, the estimated lifetime risk of developing CRC is approximately 1 in 24 for men and 1 in 26 for women [1, 2, 3, 4]. T2DM likewise represents a growing global health challenge, affecting more than 537 million adults worldwide in 2024. It is responsible for an estimated 3.4 million deaths annually, corresponding to approximately one diabetes‐related death every 9 s, and ranks among the leading causes of mortality worldwide [5, 6]. The global prevalence of T2DM is projected to reach approximately 853 million individuals by 2050, with diabetes‐related healthcare expenditures expected to approach US$1 trillion annually [7]. The relationship between T2DM and CRC risk has been well studied and documented in previous studies. Furthermore, the coexistence of these morbidities account for a poor prognosis in patients and higher mortality. The association is further influenced by shared risk factors such as obesity, physical inactivity, poor diet, high intake of processed foods and red meat, and metabolic syndrome [8, 9, 10]. A bidirectional relationship exists: Epidemiological evidence, including a meta‐analysis involving 2,593,935 participants, has found a 1.3‐fold increase in the risk of developing CRC compared with non‐diabetic counterparts. Conversely, studies including that of Amshoff et al. have shown that CRC survivors with coexisting T2DM experience poorer treatment responses, higher recurrence rates, and greater all‐cause mortality [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. The mechanisms behind this link involve chronic high blood sugar in T2DM, promoting an oncogenic environment via advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and reactive oxygen species (ROS). This results in DNA damage, oxidative stress, and the activation of the Warburg effect, where tumor cells mainly rely on glycolysis for energy [9, 10], [16].

The mortality burden from CRC and T2DM differs across demographic and geographic groups. Non‐Hispanic Black individuals consistently have the highest CRC‐related mortality rates. Between 2013 and 2017, CRC death rates in Black populations were nearly 40% higher than in non‐Hispanic whites [17, 18]. Likewise, rural and southern U.S experienced higher mortality due to reduced access to screening and colonoscopies, an advanced stage at diagnosis, and diabetes management services [18, 19, 20].

Addressing these disparities and gaining a deeper understanding of the epidemiology of these diseases will facilitate targeted prevention, intervention, and treatment, especially among high‐risk groups. The CDC's WONDER database offers a comprehensive, nationally representative resource for retrospective and predictive studies. This research focuses on mortality burden rather than incidence or survival, emphasizing the ultimate impact of these conditions on the population. It examines colorectal cancer and type 2 diabetes‐related deaths in the U.S. from 1999 to 2023, analyzing trends by year, age, sex, race/ethnicity, urbanization, and region. The aim is to develop evidence‐based strategies to reduce disease burden and mortality, identify vulnerable populations, and promote health equity, thereby helping to prevent unnecessary deaths in the future. This period also reflects significant changes in screening guidelines for colorectal cancer and advances in diabetes management over the last two decades.

Methods

Study Setting and Population

This retrospective study retrieved and utilized data from the CDC WONDER (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide‐Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research) database, spanning January 1, 1999, to December 31, 2023 [21]. Death certificates were analyzed, and deaths were included only when colorectal cancer (C18‐C20) was listed as the underlying cause of death and type 2 diabetes mellitus (E 11) was listed as a contributing cause. We identified cases of colorectal cancer and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10) codes C18, C19, C20, and E11, respectively [22]. ICD‐10 coding definitions for colorectal cancer (C18–C20) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (E11) remained consistent throughout the study period, minimizing potential misclassification due to coding changes. Cases coded as unspecified diabetes mellitus (E14) were not included to maintain diagnostic specificity. This study included all deaths with type 2 diabetes mellitus as a contributing cause of mortality. The underlying cause of death [i.e., Colorectal Cancer] is defined as the disease that is the primary and direct cause leading to death. In contrast, the contributing causes of death (i.e., Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus) are defined as any other conditions that the patients presented with that are associated with death. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was not required because the study utilized publicly available, de‐identified data from a government source. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [23].

Data Extraction

We extracted data on deaths related to Colorectal Cancer and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, along with demographic information. Demographic data included sex, age, race or ethnicity, urban‐rural classification, population size, state, regional category, and location of death (medical facility, home, hospice, or nursing home/long‐term care facility). The urban‐rural classifications followed the National Center for Health Statistics Urban‐Rural Classification Scheme to categorize population as urban(large metropolitan areas [population 1 million]), medium/small metropolitan areas [population 50,000–999,999), and rural (population < 50,000) based on 2013 U.S. Census classifications [24], and geographic regions were categorized according to the U.S. Census Bureau divisions: Northeast, Midwest, South, and West [25]. Race/ethnicity was classified as Non‐Hispanic White, Non‐Hispanic Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, and other races (Asian, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander) following the standards of the U.S. Office of Management and Budget [21].

Statistical Analysis

To analyze the national mortality trends due to Colorectal Cancer and T2DM, we calculated age‐adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs), with 95% confidence interval, per 100,000 population from 1999 to 2023, stratified by year, sex, race, region, and age group (45–64 years and 65–85+ years). AAMRs were calculated by standardizing deaths to the 2000 U.S. population [26].

We analyzed temporal trends using the Joinpoint Regression Program (Version 5.2.0, National Cancer Institute), which identifies significant changes in trends by fitting log‐linear regression models across time intervals [27]. To calculate the annual trends in CRC and T2DM‐related deaths in older adults, we assessed the annual percent change (APC) as well as the average annual percent change (AAPC) and relative 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). A trend was considered increasing or decreasing if the slope (Δ mortality) differed significantly from zero using a two‐tailed test. A p < 05 was considered statistically significant. Due to inherent limitations of the CDC WONDER database, age‐adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were unavailable for certain stratifications, including state‐level and place‐of‐death analyzes. Therefore, these variables were reported using absolute death counts rather than rates.

Results

Between 1999 and 2023, a total of 22,392 deaths related to colorectal cancer with co‐existing type 2 diabetes among individuals aged 45 and older were reported among U.S. adults.

Demographic Trends in Mortality

The AAMR revealed a significant increase, rising from 0.55 in 1999 to 1.04 in 2023. Joinpoint regression analysis showed that between 1999 and 2006, the AAMR rose significantly (p < 001) from 0.55 to 0.77 (APC: 4.09; 95% CI: 2.14–7.78). However, this initial upward trajectory was interrupted by a significant decline (p = 003) from 2006 to 2014, with the AAMR decreasing from 0.76 to 0.63 (APC: −2.26; 95% CI: −6.80 to −0.80). Subsequently, from 2014 to 2023, the trend reversed, with a significant increase (p < 001), with the AAMR rising sharply from 0.63 to 1.04 (APC: 5.80; 95% CI: 4.71–7.26). Overall, joinpoint regression analysis demonstrated a significant increasing trend across the entire study period (AAPC: 2.55; 95% CI: 2.18–3.06; p < 001). (Figure 1 and Tables 1 and 2).

Sex Stratification

Throughout the study period, the AAMRs of men remained consistently higher than those of women. Among men, joinpoint regression analysis showed that the AAMRs increased significantly from 0.69 in 1999 to 0.95 in 2006 (APC: 3.65; 95% CI: 1.58–9.48; p < 001), followed by a significant decline from 0.95 to 0.83 between 2006 and 2014 (APC: −1.74; 95% CI: ‐7.20 to ‐0.11; p = 04). Thereafter, from 2014 to 2023, the AAMR experienced a pronounced resurgence from 0.83 to 1.28 (APC: 6.08; 95% CI: 4.90–7.70; p = 002). Overall, the AAPC for men was 2.71 (95% CI: 2.30–3.33; p < 001), indicating a statistically significant upward trend across the study period.

Similarly, women showed a significant increase from 0.44 to 0.62 between 1999 and 2006 (APC: 4.32; 95% CI: 2.21 to 7.81; p < 001), followed by a steady and substantial decline to 0.46 between 2006 and 2014 (APC: −2.87; 95% CI: ‐7.74 to ‐1.20; p < 001), and then a significant rise to 0.78 during 2014 to 2023 (APC: 5.25; 95% CI: 3.90–7.04; p < 001). Overall, the AAPC for women was 2.21 (95% CI: 1.81–2.76; p < 001), indicating a substantial upward trend throughout the entire period. (Figure 2, Supporting Infromation Figure S1, and Tables 1 and 2).

Stratification by Age Group

When stratified by age, individuals 65 and older had higher AAMRs than those aged 45–64. Among those aged 45–64, joinpoint regression analysis showed that the AAMRs remained relatively stable over the period, with a non‐significant slight increase from 1999 to 2018, rising from 0.12 to 0.22 (APC: 2.21; 95% CI: ‐1.60 to 3.75; p = 13), followed by a steeper and significant rise from 0.21 to 0.42 in 2023 (APC: 13.43; 95% CI: 5.72 to 32.76; p < 001). Overall, the AAPC for individuals aged 45–64 years was 4.45 (95% CI: 3.01 to 5.75; p < 001), indicating a significant increasing trend across the study period.

In contrast, adults aged 65 years and older saw AAMRs rise sharply from 1.32 in 1999 to 1.80 in 2006 (APC: 3.72; 95% CI: 1.64 to 8.26; p = 002), then decline significantly to 1.41 between 2006 and 2014 (APC: ‐2.40; 95% CI: ‐8.62 to ‐0.73; p = 008), and later increase again to 2.13 by 2023 (APC: 4.91; 95% CI: 3.54–6.85; p = 003). Overall, the AAPC for those aged ≥ 65 years was 2.08 (95% CI: 1.67–2.71; p < 001), showing a consistent upward trend over time. (Figure 3, Supporting Infromation Figure S2, and Tables 1 and 2).

Racial Stratification

When stratified by racial groups, Hispanics had the highest AAMR (1.08), followed by Non‐Hispanic Black or African American (1.03), Non‐Hispanic White (0.80), and the lowest for other races (0.74). Hispanic or Latino individuals experienced a consistent and significant increase (p < 001) in AAMRs, rising from 0.50 in 2001 to 1.34 in 2023 (APC: 4.36; 95% CI: 3.53–5.61). The overall AAPC for Hispanic or Latino individuals was 4.36 (95% CI: 3.53–5.61; p < 001), indicating a strong upward trend. Among Non Hispanic Black or African American individuals, joinpoint regression analysis revealed that the AAMRs increased significantly (p = 009) from 0.78 in 1999 to 1.04 in 2009 (APC: 3.94; 95% CI: 2.11–10.30), followed by a steep downtrend (p = 03) to 0.76 in 2013 (APC: −6.92; 95% CI: −13.37 to −0.37), and then a significant increase (p = 008) was observed, reaching 1.31 in 2023 (APC: 3.80; 95% CI: 1.98–9.40). Overall, the AAPC for Non‐Hispanic Black or African American individuals was 1.99 (95% CI: 1.42–3.10; p < 001), indicating a significant upward trend over time.

Among Non‐Hispanic White individuals, AAMRs increased significantly from 0.52 in 1999 to 0.73 in 2006 (APC: 3.76; 95% CI: 1.49–9.50; p = 004), followed by a significant decline to 0.55 in 2015 (APC: ‐2.21; 95% CI: ‐9.02 to ‐0.53; p = 01), and then showed another significant rise to 0.91 in 2023 (APC: 6.52; 95% CI: 4.69–9.40; p = 004). The overall AAPC for Non‐Hispanic White individuals was 2.37 (95% CI: 1.91–3.10; p < 001), indicating a significant increasing trend. Among other races, AAMRs increased significantly from 0.44 in 2010 to 1.13 in 2020 (APC: 9.53; 95% CI: 6.94–27.20; p < 001), then showed a non‐significant decline in 2023 to 0.88 (APC: ‐7.28; 95% CI: ‐23.37 to 3.32; p =19). The overall AAPC for other races was 5.40 (95% CI: 2.99 to 10.52; p < 001), reflecting a significant increasing trend over the study period. Additionally, American Indian or Alaska Native populations were excluded from age‐adjusted analyzes because of incomplete or suppressed data. (Figure 4, Supporting Infromation Figure S3 and Tables 1 and 2).

Census Region Stratification

During the study period, the highest AAMRs were observed in the Western region, while the lowest were recorded in the Northeast. In the Northeast, joinpoint regression analysis showed an initial non‐significant increase in AAMRs from 0.52 in 1999 to 0.69 in 2005 (APC: 2.86; 95% CI: –0.92 to 16.04; p = 14). AAMRs then significantly decreased to 0.44 in 2015 (APC: –4.45; 95% CI: –13.50 to –2.50; p = 01), before significantly rebounding to 0.59 in 2023 (APC: 4.34; 95% CI: 1.19–10.64; p = 01). Overall, the AAPC for the Northeast region was 0.23 (95% CI: −0.51 to 1.25; p = 50), indicating a non‐significant trend over the study period.

In the Midwest, AAMRs initially increased significantly from 0.80 in 1999 to 1.11 in 2006 (APC: 4.32; 95% CI: 1.23–10.75; p = 003). This was followed by a notable and significant decrease to 0.66 in 2014 (APC: –5.16; 95% CI: –13.90 to –2.64; p = 002). Afterwards, AAMRs again showed a significant upward trend, reaching 0.95 in 2023 (APC: 4.88; 95% CI: 2.62–8.48; p = 002). The overall AAPC for the Midwest was 1.26 (95% CI: 0.61–2.17; p = 002), indicating a significant upward trend.

In the South, AAMRs increased significantly from 0.46 in 1999 to 0.69 in 2007 (APC: 3.51; 95% CI: 1.14–10.82; p = 008). This was followed by a decline to 0.55 in 2014 (APC: –3.07; 95% CI: –12.00 to 0.02; p = 05). Afterwards, AAMRs experienced a sharp and significant rise, reaching 1.07 in 2023 (APC: 7.79; 95% CI: 5.83–10.63; p = 01). The overall AAPC for the South was 3.10 (95% CI: 2.60–4.08; p < 001), showing a strong increasing trend.

In the West, AAMRs consistently and significantly increased throughout the entire study period, rising steadily from 0.44 in 1999 to 1.34 in 2023 (APC: 4.73; 95% CI: 4.12–5.60; p < 001). The overall AAPC for the West was 4.73 (95% CI: 4.12–5.60; p < 001), also indicating a strong upward trend. (Figure 5, Supporting Infromation Figure 4, and Tables 1 and 2).

Urbanization Stratification

Throughout the study period, rural areas exhibited significantly higher AAMRs compared to urban areas. Joinpoint regression analysis revealed that in rural regions, AAMRs initially rose from 0.48 in 1999 to 0.63 in 2006 (APC: 3.02; 95% CI: 0.06–15.81; p = 04), then experienced a significant decline from 2006 to 2014 (APC: –4.23; 95% CI: –15.32 to –1.50; p = 01). From 2014 to 2020, the AAMR increased from 0.41 to 0.75 (APC: 9.21; 95% CI: 4.50–22.45; p = 01). Overall, the AAPC for rural areas was 1.88 (95% CI: 1.04–3.36; p = 01), indicating a significant upward trend over the course of the study.

In urban areas, AAMRs increased significantly from 0.25 in 1999 to 0.35 in 2006 (APC: 4.48; 95% CI: 2.41–9.66; p = 04). This was followed by a modest but non‐significant decline from 0.35 in 2006 to 0.31 in 2015 (APC: –0.94; 95% CI: –6.46 to 0.41; p = 16). Afterward, AAMRs showed a marked and significant rise, reaching 0.47 in 2020 (APC: 6.17; 95% CI: 3.36–12.94; p < 001). The overall AAPC for urban areas was 2.51 (95% CI: 2.01–3.25; p < 001), reflecting a notable increasing trend. Although both urban and rural areas saw increases from 2015 to 2020, the overall colorectal cancer mortality burden with co‐existing type 2 diabetes remained significantly higher in rural populations throughout the study period. (Figure 6, Supporting Infromation Figure 5, and Tables 1 and 2).

State‐Wise Stratification

The states with the highest recorded death counts were Florida (877), New York (804), Pennsylvania (1253), Ohio (1307), Texas (1911), and California (3664). The lowest counts were in Delaware (57), Wyoming (56), Vermont (50), Rhode Island (47), Alaska (17), and the District of Columbia (11). The highest death count was in California (3664), while the lowest was in the District of Columbia (11).

Discussion

Our analysis identified distinct temporal and subgroup‐specific patterns in mortality among U.S. adults with coexisting colorectal cancer and type 2 diabetes mellitus between 1999 and 2023 [28, 29]. The trends highlighted variations over time. After 2014, the continued rise suggests that the burden of both oncologic and metabolic diseases should be addressed through appropriate management and prevention. We observed different parameters. The overall mortality burden increased after 2014, indicating a persistent burden associated with the coexistence of these conditions. Across the examined subgroups, mortality was higher among men and adults aged ≥ 65 years, while Hispanic populations and rural populations demonstrated higher mortality within this study cohort. This finding differs from broader national colorectal cancer mortality trends, in which Non‐Hispanic Black populations typically have the highest mortality rates. The discrepancy may reflect characteristics specific to the colorectal cancer‐type 2 diabetes mellitus comorbidity subgroup and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, there was a marked geographic variation, with rural regions facing the greatest burden. Our study evaluates risk‐based trends and does not include survival outcomes; it is solely a mortality‐rate analysis of the USA population, reflecting risk across different aspects. Although no major ICD‐10 revisions occurred during the study period, subtle variations in reporting practices cannot be entirely ruled out; the consistent rise across demographic groups suggests that coding artifacts alone cannot explain these mortality trends.

From 1999 to 2023, the observed national trends of death rates from coexisting colorectal cancer and type‐2 diabetes mellitus were dynamic. The patients observed in this study were over 45 years of age. The trends showed an increase until 2006, then stabilized, and after 2014 escalated again [28]. The early increase may be coincided with the growing prevalence of metabolic syndromes such as type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity [30]. Poor dietary patterns and physical inactivity may also have contributed to the observed trends [31]. The period of relative stabilization may partly reflect improvements in colorectal cancer screening and the earlier identification of precancerous lesions. Improvement in diabetes management were also noticed during that period. Nevertheless, AAMRs began to increase again in the mid‐2010s. The renewed increase may reflect the continuing metabolic burden despite advances in cancer screening, treatment, and diabetes care [32]. Importantly, these findings represent mortality patterns specifically among individuals with coexisting colorectal cancer and type 2 diabetes mellitus, rather than overall colorectal cancer mortality in the general U.S. population. Although national colorectal cancer mortality rates have declined, the rising metabolic burden and prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus may partially contribute to the divergent trend observed in this comorbidity subgroup. The recent upward trends in mortality reflect that patients with T2DM are exposed to elevated risks when it comes to colorectal cancer [30, 33]. The increase seen in the later years of the study period coincided with the COVID‐19 pandemic, when disruptions in screening, diagnosis, and treatment may have contributed to poorer outcomes. These findings highlight the need for better prevention, timely screening, and improved management, particularly among populations at higher risk.

Sex related disparities were notable. Men had a higher mortality rate than women, which may highlight differences in the pathophysiology, behavioral patterns, and healthcare usage between both sexes [34]. Males are often diagnosed with type‐2 diabetes mellitus earlier in their lives than females, and due to metabolic stress, they are more prone to develop obesity, insulin resistance, and hyperglycemia [35, 36]. Men exhibit certain unfavorable behavioral and healthcare patterns that contribute to a worse prognosis. Men tend to smoke more, have high blood pressure, and elevated cholesterol levels [37]. These are all the contributing factors of tumor growth [38]. Even after their diagnosis, they ignore the preventive measures and avoid treatment facilities. These factors may collectively contribute to the higher mortality rates observed among men. Even so, after 2014, a spike in the mortality rate was reported in both sexes. This highlights that only biological and behavioral factors are not responsible for the worsened outcomes [39]. The systemic detriments, such as poor access to screening, lack of early awareness, and other factors, should also be taken into account.

When we examined the trends, the AAMRs of patients aged over 65 years were higher than those of patients aged 45–64 years. Aging worsens the outcomes of the tumor, and if the patient is diabetic, the results get more aggressive [40, 41]. Metabolic dysregulation is also seen in patients in the advanced age group. With the increase in age, people get exposed to a high amount of carcinogenic substances. All these factors have detrimental effects on precancerous lesions. In recent years, after 2018, there was a rise in mortality of the CRC and T2DM patients in the age group of 45 to 64 years. This represents a shift in the epidemiology of CRC among younger adults.

This study also examines disparities across racial groups. Hispanics had the highest AAMR from CRC and T2DM [42]. Secondly, Non Hispanic‐Black Americans had a higher mortality rate [43]. Non‐Hispanic Whites had the second lowest mortality rate. Biological risks in highly vulnerable races stem from their genetic makeup [44]. The observed differences in mortality may reflect inequities in healthcare access and social determinants of health [44, 45]. Biological risks in these groups have been increased by structural disadvantages such as variation in diabetes control, cancer screening, a lack of quality education, lower income, high labor jobs, insurance gaps, and unfavorable living conditions, leading to a worsened prognosis of the disease [46, 47].

Geographically, the highest mortality rate due to hyperlipidemia and diabetes was observed in the Western states, then in the Midwest and the South [48, 49]. The lowest mortality rate noted was in the Northeast. There was a considerably higher mortality burden among the rural population than among the urban one [50, 51]. Western states and rural counties experience higher rates of diabetes mainly due to socioeconomic conditions and differences in healthcare infrastructure, which could contribute to the observed mortality patterns [52, 53]. States like California and Texas had higher absolute death counts from coexisting CRC and T2DM. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because death counts are influenced by population size and do not necessarily reflect higher age‐adjusted mortality rates [48]. Limited healthcare access and educational resources in some regions may contribute to delayed diagnosis, suboptimal disease management, and poorer outcomes among individuals with coexisting CRC and T2DM. The relationship between CRC and T2DM has become increasingly significant. Hyperinsulinemia and increased IGF are common findings of T2DM [54]. They both signal colorectal epithelial cells to proliferate and enlarge [55]. They also suppress apoptosis in these cells, leading to an increase in all types of colorectal epithelial cells, including tumor cells [55]. The more glucose is present, the more ROS are produced through multiple glucose metabolic pathways. The gut microbiome activity is also disrupted by hyperglycemia. This pathophysiology is also affected by the type of treatment a patient with T2DM receives. Exogenous insulin and drugs containing sulfonylureas are associated with an elevated cancer risk. In contrast, metformin is considered safe because it regulates cell growth by activating AMP‐activated protein kinases, which repress the mTOR pathway, which is essential for cell proliferation [56, 57, 58].

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Although ICD‐10 coding remained consistent during the study period, small variations in coding practices and death certificate reporting over time may have affected the observed trends. However, no major coding updates occurred that would significantly change disease classification. Since this study relies only on death certificates, any deaths from colorectal cancer coexisting with diabetes that were not reported may have been missed. This could have introduced bias by underestimating the true risks. In many cases, when both conditions were present, but T2DM was not considered a potential factor, it may have been overlooked, further adding to this underestimation. No information was available on patients' personal lives, including comorbidities, HbA1c/glycemic control data, work‐life balance, lifestyle choices, and treatment history, all of which could have influenced outcomes. Additionally, there is a lack of data on the distribution of races, ethnicities, and regions, limiting our understanding of which subgroups are most impacted by metabolic syndrome. Furthermore, diagnoses could not be confirmed through imaging or laboratory tests because these data were not available on the CDC Wonder website. Despite these limitations, our study offers strong findings supported by consistent patterns and notable disparities.

Conclusion

This study focuses on patients over 45 years old who are dying from both T2DM and colorectal cancer. The rise after 2014 may suggest that the benefits of improvements in cancer screening and diabetes management haven't fully impacted this group with both conditions. The study highlights that even with better interventions and prevention, metabolic disturbances remain significant modifiable factors. Men, Hispanics, older adults, and residents of non‐metropolitan areas face the highest mortality, with Western states showing the highest AAMRs. Addressing these disparities requires integrated diabetes and cancer care, targeted interventions, and widespread, equitable prevention initiatives to lower mortality and enhance long‐term health. Public health policies should emphasize dual screening efforts, such as integrating colorectal cancer screening into diabetes care and vice versa.
