Unmasking burnout among pediatric residents in eastern China: a qualitative study
Authors: Xinning Wang, Yunxia Hong, Chao Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout among pediatricians has emerged as a significant public health concern in China. This study aims to explore the current state of professional burnout and identify potential solutions by capturing the perspectives of pediatric residents.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted in June 2024 at a children’s hospital in eastern China. Semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were carried out, audio-recorded, and transcribed, followed by thematic analysis.ResultsTwenty pediatric residents participated in the study. Thematic analysis revealed six external factors contributing to burnout: workload, work efficiency, doctor-patient communication, education and research, basic needs, and interpersonal dynamics. Additionally, five internal manifestations of burnout were identified: reduced personal accomplishment, cynicism, emotional exhaustion, health crises, and intent to quit. Potential solutions were also discussed.ConclusionsPediatricians reported substantial experiences of professional burnout. This study highlights the contributing factors and manifestations of burnout among pediatric residents. To address this issue, it is imperative for the government and the public to optimize the standardized residency training system and foster a supportive environment, thereby improving the well-being of pediatricians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09995-x.Keywords: Burnout, Qualitative, Residency, China, Pediatrician

Background

In recent years, China has implemented a standardized training program for residents [1], requiring all prospective physicians, including those specializing in pediatrics, to complete a three-year residency program post-graduation. Throughout this three-year period, residents rotate through various departments within designated training institutions. As part of their responsibilities, pediatric residents are at the forefront of clinical practice, directly interacting with young patients and their families.

Pediatricians in China face severe challenges, characterized by excessive working hours, a high incidence of medical disputes, and a shortage of pediatric specialists, all of which have drawn significant public concern and anxiety [2]. The turnover rate among Chinese pediatricians is as high as 12.6%, with a substantial proportion of those leaving being junior doctors [3].

Professional burnout is a psychological state or reaction to prolonged emotional and interpersonal stressors in the workplace [4]. Burnout among pediatricians poses a significant risk to the well-being of pediatricians and consequently, the safety of their young patients [5]. High levels of burnout within the healthcare sector threaten both the quality and sustainability of healthcare services.

To discern the contributing factors to burnout, cross-sectional analyses of the medical profession have established correlations between burnout and a variety of individual elements, such as gender and age, as well as work-related factors, including long working hours and administrative burdens [6]. In Western countries, a multitude of adverse factors can impact physicians’ career intentions. Among these, heavy workload, low morale, loneliness in practice, limited academic opportunities, and negative media perception are most frequently emphasized [7, 8]. However, the negative influences on burnout among pediatricians in China may differ from those in developed countries, attributable to China’s vast geographic expanse, cultural diversity, and distinct historical context.

Unlike survey-based research, qualitative research that examines burnout from the perspective of frontline clinicians is scarce, particularly in the field of pediatrics. Our research conducted in-depth discussions with pediatric residents to gather their perceptions on the elements leading to burnout and diminished job satisfaction, as well as their suggestions for possible remedies. The objective was to gain a more profound comprehension of burnout from the perspective of frontline pediatric residents, providing evidence for future interventions tailored to both individual and organizational development within China’s standardized residency training framework.

Methods

Study design

Approval was obtained from the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Zhejiang Province, China (No. 2022-IRB-107) to conduct a qualitative research study utilizing semi-structured interviews in June 2024 with pediatric residents at the hospital, a major standardized training base for pediatric residents in eastern China.

This study adheres to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [9].

Participant recruitment

Researchers contacted participants who met inclusion criteria: (1) who belonged to the standardized residency training program (hereinafter referred to as " residency training”), (2) who had participated in the residency training program for at least 10 months.

The interviewer was a female attending pediatrician who had completed her standardized residency training several years prior, establishing rapport with participants through self-introduction and explaining the purpose of the interview beforehand. A total of 20 residents were recruited to participate in the interviews, with approximately 20% of invitees declining to participate. If the participants expressed interest, one-to-one interviews were then scheduled at a date and time that suited the participants. Verbal consent was obtained from the participants before starting the interview.

We attempted to reach maximal variation in sampling by recruiting participants from a wide range of backgrounds, such as gender, years of training and affiliation.

Data collection

Participants were first asked to complete a paper questionnaire as an initial portion collecting demographic information and a second portion exploring overall assessment of burnout by a Maslach Burnout Inventory-General Survey. We adopted the revised Chinese version of Maslach Burnout Inventory-General Survey (MBI-GS) [10]. The MBI-GS consists of three subscales: emotional exhaustion (five items), cynicism (four items) and reduced personal accomplishment (six items). The scale was evaluated using a seven-point Likert scoring method, with scores from 0 to 6 indicating the frequency of their own feelings. For emotional exhaustion and cynicism, higher scores were associated with high levels of burnout. In the case of reduced personal accomplishment, higher scores indicated lower levels of burnout. The MBI-GS was used solely to provide descriptive context for the sample and to help readers understand the burnout severity among participants; no inferential statistical analyses were performed.

The interview guide included open-ended questions about experiences of professional burnout. The study grounded in inductive-deductive thematic analysis, with the core questionnaire of this study was developed based on a review of existing literature. We conducted two pilot interviews and revised the interview guide based on the outcomes of these discussions. A more detailed interview guide is presented in Additional file. Semi-structured interviews allowed participants to freely share their thoughts and perceptions but allowed the researchers to retain some control over the direction of the study. Interviews were conducted where and whenever it suited the participant, lasting a mean of 50 min. Generally, there were only two people (a participant and the interviewer) in the room, to better encourage participants to express themselves truthfully. All interviews were audio recorded, with non-verbal data keenly observed and accurately documented on-site. Transcripts were produced post-interview and returned to participants for confirmation, ensuring data completeness and accuracy.

Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved. After approximately 18 interviews, no substantially new codes or conceptual categories emerged. Two additional interviews were conducted to confirm thematic stability. Consistent with recent methodological literature suggesting that thematic saturation in relatively homogeneous participant groups is often reached within 12–24 interviews, the research team concluded that sufficient depth and breadth of data had been obtained [11].

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel was used for data sorting and analysis. The measurement data are expressed as means ± standard deviations.

In the qualitative analysis, transcripts were archived anonymously. Data analysis was performed following the six-phase thematic analysis framework proposed by Braun and Clarke [12]: familiarization, initial coding, theme searching, theme review, theme definition, and writing. Two researchers independently conducted initial coding on all transcripts, using an inductive approach to generate codes directly from the data. Codes were then organized into candidate themes, which were reviewed against the coded data and the entire dataset. Disagreements in coding or theme development were resolved through discussion or third-party arbitration. A coding framework was developed, with illustrative examples provided in Table 1. Finally, after ensuring the integrity of the data and reaching a consensus on the final themes, the text was translated into English for reporting. These steps were facilitated by QSR NVivo 14 software.

Results

Relationships among the elements related to burnout

External factors

Workload

Residents consistently described excessive workload as a major contributor to burnout. Some residents reported that in some departments during busy times, “one barely has a moment to touch the ground; sometimes there’s not even time to eat or drink (P17).” Relative staff shortages mean that “if one person slacks off, the workload for others can increase dramatically (P1).”

Residents typically work 5–6 days a week, and usually, on rest days, they still need to present for ward rounds and patient management, “By the time the morning work is done, it’s already midday… with only one day off on the weekend, I can only collapse in bed (P6).”

Night shifts, which occur every 5–7 days on average, also place pressure on residents, “At night, all the ward’s pressure falls on you. The hardest thing for me is lying in bed unable to sleep, even when there’s nothing to do. Sometimes, just as you’re starting to feel sleepy, a nurse’s phone call wakes you up (P15).”

Documentation occupies a considerable amount of time, “Amending medical records is very time-consuming and tedious, and there are too many regulations to follow on the records (P4).” Overtime is a common occurrence, “I only start dealing with paperwork after the official end of the shift (P19),” “When patients arrive late, we must finish admitting them (complete the medical records, prescribe orders, and hand over) before we can leave (P3).” Residents have to even take on the role of accompanying patients, “Accompanying patients to examinations takes time… and in emergencies at night, I also need to accompany them (P8).”

Due to individual differences in work capacity, changes in the work environment can be challenging for some residents, “Every time I move to a new department, I feel a tightness in my chest (P17),” “I feel like maybe I’m not doing well enough… a bit helpless (P1).”

Busy work schedules can encroach on personal time, “Your time isn’t really your own; it’s scheduled by work… you lose your freedom (P3),” “I have very little time to spend with my family; we’ve had arguments because I’ve never been home for the holidays (P17).” Although in the minority, residents who are married and have children face even greater family burdens. The demanding clinical work makes it difficult for them to balance their family responsibilities. Resident P20, due to living far from the hospital, had to entrust their young child to the care of elders and rent a separate place near the hospital, “I feel so sorry for my baby. He might be better if we were there to be with him by ourselves… Recently, my baby had a febrile seizure. I took care of him at night and had to work during the day, anxious about his illness… it’s quite distressing.”

Work efficiency

The work environment requires residents to multitask, “A task that supposed to be done quickly… but during the process, you have to see many patients and answer a lot of phone calls (P17),” “There are a lot of trifles at work. Nurses come to you with issues, patients come to you with problems, and superiors entrust you with tasks. This information can be quite overwhelming, and it often tends to erupt at the same time (P14).” Such frequent interruptions make it difficult for residents to concentrate on their tasks at hand, thereby reducing work efficiency.

Frontline residents often need to consult with senior doctors about clinical work, but the workload for superiors is also substantial. Slow decision making by superiors or poor collaboration within the medical team can disrupt the workflow, “If the superior can’t make a decision and we have to wait for the director, it leads to a backlog of our work (P15),” “If a peer isn’t managing a patient well, the superior might hand them over to you, and you feel like, the harder you worked, the more work you got (P12).”

While adhering to treatment guidelines, different departments and physicians may have varying treatment approaches for the same disease, “For the same pneumonia, each department handles it differently… I think it’s a redundancy of resources (P3).” The instability of medical electronic information systems can also affect work efficiency, “I once had to write a medical record three to four times for one patient admission because the system crashed when saving, and then I had to rewrite it all over again (P19).”

Communication with patients and families

Many children are unable to clearly articulate their conditions, necessitating parents to describe their child’s situation, which significantly increases the difficulty of communication for pediatricians, “You have to constantly face parents, explain and communicate with them… Parents like to ask about lab results in the evening… possibly because they only get off work then (P14).” The pressure of pediatric doctor-patient communication not only stems from parents’ concern for their child’s health and desire for medical information but may also from unrealistic expectations about medicine, “For example, in terms of treatment, parents don’t understand that getting sick is a process; they wonder why the child still has fever after being hospitalized and think you might not be caring enough (P10).” In extreme cases, there may even be incidents of parents verbally abusing doctors, as resident P6 recounted an experience after a child’s seizure at night, “The parent burst in and said, ‘Are you fit to be a doctor? You did nothing.’ But I had already done everything I could and called for a superior (P6).”

The medical profession requires pediatricians to provide not only professional medical services but also a significant amount of emotional support, “Parents are quite anxious, and we must comfort them too. This is what makes the job difficult… after providing so much emotional value to others, you can also fall into anxiety (P15).”

Education and research

Residents have dual roles as both doctors and students, necessitating participation in educational activities in addition to clinical work. Most residents stated that although the quality of teaching lectures is acceptable, “I probably won’t have time to listen to the lectures, it’s such a pity, but there’s nothing others I can do when, for example, you need to admit patients or go for examinations, you simply can’t ignore those duties (P13).” Some residents reflected that the persistent work pressure erodes their motivation to learn, “At the beginning, I attended the training courses with an eager attitude to learn, but later on, I would feel increasingly fatigued, subconsciously perceiving lectures as a burden that might take up time and make me leave work late, turning into a wish for it to end quickly (P14).”

Formal staff and professional master students generally bear heavy research responsibilities, often conducting research activities in the mornings, evenings, and on days off, “I’m worried about clinical matters, and under such stress, I fear not doing well in research. It feels almost forbidden to carve out a bit of time for research (P17),” “The best of my energy during the day is all spent at work, leaving me only with the less mentally agile moments for research, which feels like my spirit is willing, but my flesh is weak (P4).” Career advancement requires residents to produce research outcomes, yet they struggle to find a balance between clinical work and research tasks, “I’ve tried hard to strive for both clinical work and research, but after the clinical workload actually increased, I found it beyond my capacity (P18).”

Basic needs

A lack of basic needs is first reflected in the form of financial pressures, with residents noting that the income for pediatricians is low, “Income is incomparable to that of adult hospitals (P13),” “The labor we put in far outweighs what we gain (P4).”

Housing and commuting issues are significant sources of financial pressure, especially for residents from out of town, “There is no hospital accommodation, and the cost of living and housing prices here are quite high (P8).” Moreover, residents may need to rotate through several hospital campus that are far apart, and master students’ university dormitories can also be a considerable distance from the hospital, “Commuting from the dormitory takes two hours each day… Starting this month, I need to rotate in another campus, and my rented apartment is on the opposite side, so it’s quite inconvenient (P7).”

A vacation system is crucial for maintaining residents’ physical and mental health and work enthusiasm, but difficulties in taking leave is a common issue, “You might want to take a break due to academic or health-related issues, but the first thing superiors consider is whether there will be enough staff on duty that day… Sometimes after weighing the options, superiors might not agree to your request (P14),” “Asking for leave is also a burden for me, giving me the feeling that during the residency training I’m not supposed to get sick (P15).”

In terms of hospital support, residents generally view the educational administration as a qualified listener with some improvement measures mentioned, such as adjusting shuttle bus times to accommodate those working overtime. However, some residents feel that hospital administrative departments “are not reliable sources of support (P6).”

Interpersonal dynamics

Within the work environment, residents sometimes lack a sense of professional identity due to insufficient support in communication with colleagues and nurses. Resident P6 recounted an incident where she was mistakenly blamed due to errors made by other departments, leaving her feeling “as if I do not belong to this department.” Resident P18 described being reprimanded by a nurse in front of a patient’s family, “which significantly undermines the personal prestige of the doctor in the eyes of the patient.”

The limited understanding of the medical profession by family and friends also leaves them isolated, “I don’t complain too much about my work to my family because I feel, on one hand, they might not understand, and on the other hand, I don’t want them to be worried (P2).” Resident P1 mentioned that while family members are supportive and even sympathetic, “in their eyes, my work is taken for granted, doctors are supposed to work night shifts.” Resident P17 indicated that her spouse is willing to listen but only offers simple consolation, “Which industry isn’t hard?” Non-medical friends often fail to empathize with the plight of physicians, “Many of them see themselves as patients, unable to empathize with our side, so I don’t talk much with them about it (P12).”

Internal manifestations

Reduced personal accomplishment

Some residents express a feeling of low achievement, “I feel like I’m repeating assembly line work that seems not to require medical expertise, lacking a sense of professional identity (P11),” “Neither superiors nor parents often recognize our value (P6).” Many residents describe themselves using metaphors like “just a cog in the machine (P1, P6).”

Emotional exhaustion

The daily work sense of low achievement also drains the residents’ enthusiasm for their jobs, “In some departments, I feel it’s so meaningless to be a doctor, there’s no feedback from patients, and I’m not using what I’ve learned… I think about changing careers (P2).” This leads to a lack of work motivation, and resident P6 frankly admits, “I’m only motivated on the days I get paid.”

Conflicts with patients also negatively affect the residents’ emotions, to the point of “dreading interaction with the parents (P6).”

Unpredictable changes in patient conditions are a source of emotional swings for the residents, “I’m not as strong as I imagined, I can’t do better, and I start to not want to be a doctor anymore (P1).”

Cynicism

Many residents experience a shift from idealism to realism about the medical profession after residency training, “Before, I thought being a doctor was a glamorous job, but residency training lets you know that practicing medicine is often about doing some repetitive and possibly dull work day after day, even sacrificing your health and rest, and even feeling that the effort and reward are disproportionate (P1).” This shift is accompanied by their withdrawal at work, avoiding taking on too much responsibility, “Now I see being a doctor just as a job… and I’m more cautious, I’ve got to protect myself first (P6),” “Some things should be appropriately delegated to others, to superiors, things that aren’t my responsibility, I shouldn’t take them on as my own (P11).”

Health crises

The prolonged work pressure and irregular schedules can impact everyone’s health. Frequent exposure to pathogens in the environment and shifts in personal immunity cause residents to contract common illnesses such as “respiratory and enteric viruses that are common in children, I had to get all of them once (P3).” Various other physical discomforts have been mentioned: “When I’m tired, I eat, and then I end up with stress-related obesity (P18),” “I don’t eat lunch, maybe because there’s just not enough time… I get thinner each year (P17),” “After starting night shifts, I developed gastroesophageal reflux… and I break out in hives when I’m stressed (P15).” Resident P19 shared that fatigue from night shifts led to an ankle sprain and subsequent fracture, requiring a month of rest and returning to work still on crutches, and she admits “this isn’t even the worst experience.”

In addition to physical ailments, some respondents also face psychological challenges, with anxiety and insomnia being common, “I feel a lot of pressure, and during night shifts, I basically can’t sleep at all (P17),” “Every month I seem to have a breakdown, emotions build up to a point, and then I cry uncontrollably (P19).” Only very few residents have sought help from a psychologist.

When asked about managing their health, most residents state they have neither the time nor energy for exercise and fitness, with a significant part responding that they “just want to sleep” (P3, P10, P12, P15, P17, P18, P20).

Intent to quit

Some residents had passively chosen pediatrics, thus lacking enduring enthusiasm for the specialty, “I was adjusted into pediatrics, only one-sixth of our class had actively chosen it… I tried to transfer to general internal medicine but wasn’t successful (P2),” “There were limited choices at the time of job placement, plus I didn’t understand pediatrics well back then… If I could choose now, I would immediately change jobs… or even leave the medical industry to pursue other medical-related professions (P11).”

There are also those who actively chose pediatrics but want to leave due to disappointment with the current state and uncertainty about the future, “Often I wonder why I didn’t choose an auxiliary department like the ultrasound department (P6),” “If possible, including my parents, they would prefer me to work at a relatively relaxed private hospital, or they would probably support me leaving clinical practice. My greatest wish is to become a civil servant (P15).”

Despite some residents expressing a desire to leave, they are also aware of the difficulties in changing careers, “After all, your medical background is there, and doing something else with limited resources might be even more challenging (P3),” so they do not dare to change their situation lightly.

Participants’ suggestions

Increase income, equal pay for equal work

The most vocal suggestion is to raise the income of residency trainees, a desire stemming not only from the pursuit of a better quality of life but also reflecting their expectation for professional value recognition, “Getting paid increases happiness… it’s like getting a return on your labor (P9),” “Can at least the basic living security in a city be guaranteed, so you don’t have to ask for money from your family (P2)” “Not just for residents, I think all doctors should get a raise (P1).” There is also a hope to improve the treatment of professional master students, “I’m not a master student, but I think they should have equal pay for equal work… I think the government should give them more subsidies, otherwise life is actually very difficult (P15).”

Increase rest and improve basic provisions

The call for more humane rest periods is widespread. In response to the current situation of limited rest and the requirement to make rounds on rest days, residents propose “rotating weekends off for everyone (P15),” “having a colleague from the same group cover rounds on rest days, ensuring proper handover (P8),” and “having a fixed midday break, where no clinical work, except for emergencies, is required (P2).” These suggestions reflect the residents’ need for flexibility in work arrangements and a desire to better balance work and life through teamwork.

Moreover, given economic pressures and the inconvenience of commuting, there are suggestions that “hospitals could provide a 24-hour accommodation environment for residents, or at least have relatively affordable lodgings nearby (P2).”

Increase staffing, attract talent

Short staffing is one of the main reasons for heavy workloads, and as hospitals expand, “there are more hospital divisions, more wards, but not an increase in the number of residency trainees (P10).” There is a general plea for hospitals to enhance staffing to alleviate current work pressures. An increase in personnel could also bring more learning opportunities for overburdened residents, “If everyone has fewer tasks, there might be more time to attend lectures (P4).”

Additionally, resident P1 calls attention to the issue of talent attrition, “Pediatrics needs an infusion of fresh blood. As talent continues to leave, the workload of the remaining pediatricians increases, they become more exhausted, and it’s a vicious cycle.”

Enhance teaching staff and improve education quality

Improving the quality of residency education is also a common demand among residents. To allow more people to participate in education, the work distribution needs to be optimized, “Apart from those on duty, others should have time to attend lectures (P11).” The format of teaching could be more flexible to increase participation across different shifts, “Some online lectures can be organized (P13).” At the same time, there is a need to expand the teaching staff, “Increase the allocation of clinical teachers who can guide us in our work (P5).”

Dynamically adjust rotation plans

The rotation plan, formulated by the Chinese Medical Doctor Association to ensure uniform training quality, could be more flexible and tailored to the individual according to some residents, “Certain departments we want to go to might not be in the entire plan; allowing some personal choice in rotations and time management would be beneficial (P16).” It’s also necessary to consider the balance of staffing across departments, “Arrange personnel more rationally, as indeed some departments are particularly overstaffed while others are understaffed (P19).”

Promote humanistic care and strengthen psychological support

Some residents expressed a desire to forge more harmonious relationships with colleagues, “I hope to be respected and seen as a part of the department, not just as someone rotating through for a few months before being replaced (P10),” “If the department has a good atmosphere, even if it’s busy… I am easily satisfied (P9).” Residents also hope that hospital societies will provide them with more attention and support, “It’s difficult to notice some psychological issues during regular work, so there should be more concern for the residents’ lives, as some are indeed facing significant hardships (P15).”

Discussion

In China, compared to other specialties, pediatrics generally features high intensity, high risk, and low income [13]. Residents represent a unique group within the medical profession, recently graduated and stepping into the workplace, simultaneously students and doctors. This complex social role makes them more susceptible to professional burnout under high-pressure work conditions. Currently, a growing number of studies investigate professional burnout among resident physicians. A French study that included 1,689 internists and residents found that 19.0% of residents exhibited signs of professional burnout [14]. Qualitative research in the UK discovered a prevalent degree of burnout among anesthesiology trainees [15]. The burnout prevalence was 46.3% among resident physicians at a Thai university hospital [16]. A study in China found that 48.0% of 252 obstetrics and pediatrics residents experienced professional burnout [17]. Another quantitative study conducted via an electronic survey indicated a 65.2% (90/138) burnout rate among pediatric residents in eastern China, with mild, moderate, and severe burnout at 32.6%, 21.0%, and 11.6%, respectively [18]. This differs from the 85% burnout rate found in this study, a discrepancy that may be attributed to differences in sample size, survey subjects, and the authenticity between face-to-face and electronic surveys. On the other hand, this may also indicate a skewed sample of more burnout residents in this study. Professional burnout among pediatric residents represents a significant concern requiring urgent attention. This study, for the first time, utilized qualitative methods, including 18 h of one-on-one interviews and an integration of 250,000 Chinese characters of transcribed recordings, to provide an intuitive and comprehensive analysis of the current state of professional burnout among pediatric residents.

The study detailed six external factors contributing to the direct causes of pediatric resident physician burnout. Results showed that on the job level, residents generally face immense workloads, leading to decreased job satisfaction. Inefficiencies negatively impact daily tasks. Difficulties in doctor-patient communication further affect their work and emotional state. The daunting nature of education and research tasks highlights the conflict between pursuing professional growth and meeting work demands. On a personal level, they face issues related to inadequate basic provisions and lack of supportive interpersonal dynamics, all exacerbating pediatricians’ feelings of burnout. These findings are consistent with value consensus and literature reports. Literature suggests that the pediatrician endured large and tedious daily workload and generally tense doctor-patient relationships [19], these may be the reasons conducing high incidence of burnout among pediatricians [20]. Additionally, workload and work environment affect healthcare professionals’ burnout [21, 22], with a significant association found between night shifts and burnout [23, 24]. Medical conflicts intensify burnout [25], and income is an important factor affecting burnout [16, 24]. Of particular note is that in this survey, 55% of residents reported an annual income of less than 100,000 RMB. A global meta-analysis of 170 observational studies, which included a subgroup of residents [26], highlighted workload and low to middle income country context as key drivers of physician burnout. Furthermore, physician burnout has been associated with lower career engagement, reduced job satisfaction, and poorer quality of patient care, emphasizing the importance of addressing burnout as both a workforce and patient-safety priority [26].

Recent international evidence suggests that residents’ burnout is not solely an individual phenomenon but also reflects systemic challenges embedded within residency training programs and healthcare systems. A global meta-regression of 114 studies involving 31,210 postgraduate trainees revealed an overall burnout prevalence of 47.3%, ranging from 51.2% in North America to 30.8% in Europe, and demonstrated that the prevalence of burnout remained unchanged over the past two decades [27]. The persistence of burnout among residents appears to transcend national boundaries and healthcare system configurations. These findings suggest that the experiences reported by pediatric residents in the present study reflect both context-specific challenges within China’s standardized residency training system and broader structural issues affecting physician well-being worldwide. Our study extends these findings to the Chinese pediatric residency context, adding nuanced insights such as the dual role of residents as both learners and service providers, and the specific challenge of balancing research with clinical duties—an underexplored area in Western studies.

In the present study, the above six external factors lead to the internal manifestations of professional burnout among residents, such as emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and a reduced personal accomplishment. Literature reports that workload has a negative impact on healthcare workers’ physical and mental health [28–30]. This study suggests that under the pressure of external factors, residents’ physical and psychological health may be at risk. Previous quantitative research indicates that pediatric residents self-reported rates of stress, anxiety, and depression were 18.8%, 47.8%, and 47.8%, respectively. Negative emotions are significantly negatively correlated with social support and psychological resilience and positively correlated with burnout [18]. When the level of discord exceeds saturation, it may lead to an increase in residents’ intentions to leave their positions. A large cross-sectional study across 105 hospitals in European and the US found that poor well-being was common among hospital physicians, with 29.9% of European physicians and 23.8% of US physicians intending to leave their job within a year [31]. More importantly, the study demonstrated that improvements in nurse staffing adequacy, clinical care environments, and clinician teamwork were significantly associated with favorable physician job outcomes [31] —findings that directly support our participants’ calls for increased staffing and improved workplace dynamics. Standardized residency training requires a balance between education and medical practice. Residents report feeling more like a link in the diagnostic and treatment chain rather than experiencing an educational emphasis, desiring to enhance themselves more in practice. Our findings highlight a previously underexplored tension: the simultaneous pressure to produce research while managing overwhelming clinical loads, a challenge particularly salient in Chinese academic medical centers. The teaching model of residency is significantly different from the college medical education model, necessitating a change in concepts and teaching methods for both instructors and trainees. Some residents still have a strong desire for knowledge and hope to increase their sense of personal achievement by improving the quality of clinical teaching, while others wish to enhance their research capabilities to alleviate the experience of burnout, both of which imply the need for more time and energy investment.

The suggestions proposed by residents—including increasing income, ensuring equal pay for equal work, expanding rest periods, improving basic provisions, enhancing staffing levels, attracting talent, optimizing teaching quality and dynamically adjusting rotation plans—collectively reflect critical frontline insights into systemic gaps within residency training systems. These recommendations, rooted in firsthand experiences, highlight actionable leverage points for policymakers to design comprehensive interventions, such as nationwide workload standards, professional health safeguards, and competency-based training reforms, to mitigate professional burnout. Furthermore, residents emphasize the need for enhanced respect, socio-cultural adjustments to ensure equitable treatment of physicians under sustained pressure. Residents’ perspectives should be incorporated into policy co-creation and translated into scalable solutions, thereby fostering alignment between clinical realities and a culture of respect and collective resilience. Recent evidence suggests that while coaching and resilience-building programs may alleviate emotional exhaustion, organizational strategies that improve learning environments and training systems may be more effective than work-hour restrictions alone in mitigating resident physician burnout [32]. A systematic review on wellbeing interventions for resident doctors identified mentorship and social support as key factors significantly reducing burnout and enhancing professional growth, and reducing work hours alone is insufficient without comprehensive support systems [33]. Those findings resonate with our participants’ emphasis on the need for improved interpersonal dynamics and humanistic care alongside structural reforms. Therefore, effective burnout prevention requires comprehensive interventions at both the individual and organizational levels, addressing the multiple factors embedded within residency training programs and healthcare systems.

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single tertiary pediatric teaching hospital in eastern China. While this institution represents a major residency training center, organizational culture, staffing structures, educational practices, and patient populations may differ across regions and hospitals. Consequently, some results may reflect local contextual factors rather than universally shared experiences among Chinese pediatric residents. Future research could incorporate pediatric residents from different regions and medical institutions to provide a more comprehensive perspective. The findings of this study are situated within the particular context of China, and their transferability may be limited due to differences between China’s residency training system and those in developed countries. Second, the high burnout rate (85%) suggests possible selection bias, as more distressed residents may have been more willing to participate. The integration of MBI-GS data was intended only for descriptive contextualization; no causal or inferential claims should be drawn from these figures. Additionally, we implemented confidentiality measures to protect participant anonymity and ensured no direct power dynamics between interviewers and participants. Nevertheless, some participants may have concealed their true opinions and provided responses they believed were expected by the researchers. To further mitigate acquiescence bias, future studies should engage independent third-party organizations to conduct such investigations. Finally, further research is needed to explore the effectiveness of specific interventions aimed at alleviating professional burnout among pediatric residents, as well as long-term strategies to sustain job satisfaction and well-being among these physicians.

Conclusions

This study identified external factors leading to professional burnout among pediatric residents, including workload, work efficiency, doctor-patient communication, education and research, basic needs, and interpersonal dynamics. Internal manifestations of burnout among pediatric residents include reduced personal accomplishment, cynicism, emotional exhaustion, health crises, and intent to quit. Potential solutions for alleviating burnout may involve increasing income and staffing, improving basic guarantees, optimizing teaching and rotation arrangements, and increasing humanistic care. Addressing the current state of professional burnout among pediatric residents requires sustained governmental efforts to optimize the standardized residency training system and foster a supportive environment. Future studies should prioritize multi-center investigations across diverse Chinese regions to enhance generalizability, incorporate mixed-methods designs with third-party neutral institutions to capture nuanced dynamics of resident physicians’ burnout, and identify effective interventions to support evidence-based policy reforms.
