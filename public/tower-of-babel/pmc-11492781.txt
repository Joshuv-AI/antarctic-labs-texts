Effect of lower-limb isokinetic muscle strengthening on knee function and joint contact force in knee osteoarthritis patients awaiting total knee arthroplasty: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hui Zhang, Junqing Wang, Biao Wang, Kang Li, Yong Nie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnee osteoarthritis (OA) is a common and serious joint disease and patients mainly suffer from knee pain and dysfunction, significantly impacting their quality of life and daily activities. Non-pharmacological treatments and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are the two major treatments for knee OA. TKA is the primary treatment for severe knee OA patients, however, the postoperative rehabilitation results are not entirely satisfactory. To enhance postoperative rehabilitation outcomes, preoperative lower-limb muscle strengthening has aroused attention and demonstrated significant results on functional recovery after TKA. Specifically, isokinetic muscle strengthening (IMS) has been highly anticipated as an efficient training method. Nevertheless, its effects in perioperative and long-term periods of TKA require further investigation. This study aims to evaluate the impact of preoperative lower-limb IMS during the 4 weeks before TKA on the perioperative and postoperative outcomes, with particular attention on knee function, pain, and inflammatory response.MethodsIn this study, 104 patients awaiting TKA will be randomized to 2 groups: the IMS and health education groups. All patients will be evaluated at baseline, post-intervention, discharge day, and postoperative day 14, with follow-up assessments at 6 weeks, 3, 6, and 12 months after discharge. The primary outcome will be the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score 3 months after TKA and the medial-to-lateral knee contact force ratio. The secondary outcomes will be changes in lower-limb muscle strength, knee pain, inflammation response, active range of motion, performance-based function (5-times Sit-to-Stand Test, Time Up-and-Go Test, 3 m walking speed), Berg Balance Scale, and surgical need.DiscussionLower-limb IMS can improve muscle strength, pain, and function for early knee OA patients. However, the effects of different doses of IMS on perioperative and postoperative knee pain, inflammation response, and function after TKA are inconclusive. This study will provide high-quality evidence to assess the efficacy of IMS in end-stage patients who will undergo TKA. Understanding IMS in end-stage knee OA patients will help guide future surgical management for this population.Trial Registration Chinese Clinical Trial Registry ChiCTR2400088136.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis, Total knee arthroplasty, Isokinetic muscle strengthening, Rehabilitation, Exercise therapy

Background

Knee Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease that leads to knee pain and functional limitations, becoming a primary cause of disability among the elderly population and imposing a significant economic burden on individuals and society [1, 2]. Pain and functional limitations are the primary symptoms of knee OA and the major factors leading patients to request total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [3]. Nowadays, TKA-accelerated rehabilitation techniques have not only significantly reduced postoperative complications [4] but also improved surgical safety [5] and patient satisfaction [6]. Nevertheless, patients often have to wait several months before undergoing surgery and suffer from constant severe pain during the waiting period. This limits their daily activities and may lead to prolonged bed rest and lower-limb muscle atrophy [7, 8]. If patients undergo TKA with limited lower-limb muscle strength, it may induce inadequate joint stability, prevent functional rehabilitation, and increase the risk of shortened prosthesis life [9, 10]. Therefore, the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) has emphasized lower limb muscle training as a primary biomechanical treatment for knee OA [11]. Meanwhile, effective clinical techniques for muscle strength management are crucial in the Enhanced Recovery after Surgery (ERAS) of TKA [12, 13]. These techniques aim to prevent and treat the symptoms caused by muscle atrophy, ultimately improving joint function and enhancing patients’ quality of life [14]. However, few studies have explored the long-term benefits of lower-limb muscle strengthening for pain improvement and functional recovery in preoperative patients awaiting TKA.

Previous studies showed that non-pharmacological treatments (including exercise [15, 16], phyical therapy [17], and acupuncture [18]) effectively improved patients’ pain and function both preoperative and postoperative. Similarly, preoperative lower-limb strength training could produce the same effects and immediately increase muscle strength, facilitating postoperative physical and functional recovery [19, 20]. Meanwhile, lower-limb muscle training also reduces surgical trauma and postoperative knee swelling caused by tourniquet-induced ischemia–reperfusion injury [21]. Additionally, studies have shown that muscle strength training and diet control in early-stage knee OA patients significantly reduced inflammatory cytokines (IL-6) [22, 23].

Isokinetic muscle strengthening (IMS), characterized by constant-speed muscle contractions producing high forces, effectively increases muscle strength, size, and athletic performance [24, 25]. Previous investigations have successfully applied IMS in the biomechanical treatment of early-stage knee OA patients [26, 27]. After 6–8 weeks of IMS, early-stage knee OA patients exhibited notable improvements in muscle strength, knee range of motion, pain, walking speed, function, and quality of life [26, 27]. Furthermore, research findings also indicated significant effects of both concentric and eccentric IMS in the recovery of quadriceps strength after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and meniscectomy [28, 29], as well as positively impacting bone mineral density and content in young female individuals [30]. IMS not only facilitates accelerated muscle strength gains and reduces post-training muscle soreness, but it also provides a safer training environment [31]. Additionally, IMS induces a relatively modest increase in heart rate and blood pressure [32] when compared to alternative resistance training modalities such as isotonic and isometric training. These advantages hold particular importance for middle-aged and elderly knee OA patients [31, 32].

Nevertheless, the specific effects of IMS on muscle strength, pain, function, inflammatory response, and knee swelling in the ERAS of TKA remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to explore the effects of preoperative lower-limb IMS on postoperative knee pain, inflammatory response, functional recovery, and swelling in knee OA patients awaiting TKA, as well as the biomechanical correlations between muscle strength and joint dynamic stability.

Objective

The study will assess end-stage knee OA patients awaiting TKA to evaluate the effectiveness of lower-limb isokinetic muscle strengthening. These assessments will investigate the impact of increased muscle strength on patients’ perioperative pain, function, and inflammatory response, aiming to provide novel muscle strength management approaches for perioperative ERAS.

The study will also evaluate the preoperative effects of lower-limb isokinetic muscle strengthening on the biomechanical mechanism of the knee joint based on knee kinematics and kinetics.

Methods

Study design

This study is a single-center, single-blind, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT). Eligible patients with knee OA will be randomly assigned to 2 groups: the IMS group and the health education (HE) group. Patients in the IMS group will receive 4 weeks (three times a week) of lower-limb IMS before TKA, consisting of a 5 min warm-up on a non-weight-bearing cycle, followed by 5 sets of 8 repetitions of knee isokinetic flexion and extension exercises at a speed of 60°s. Rest intervals between sets will be 1 min, and rest intervals between legs will be 5 min. Patients in the HE group will not receive muscle training but receive joint health education, including basic knowledge about knee OA, dietary recommendations, and exercise suggestions. All patients will be assessed at baseline, post-intervention, discharge day, postoperative day 14, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. The study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Participant flow through the randomized controlled trial

All interventions and assessments will take place in the Department of Orthopaedics, West China Hospital of Sichuan University, Chengdu, China, performed by an independent rehabilitation team. Recruitment of participants started in September 2024 and will end in July 2025. The trial will be completed by July 2026.

Sample size

The primary outcome measure of this study is the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). According to previous research reports [33], this outcome measure’s minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is 10, with a standard deviation of 15. The two-tailed significance level is set at 0.025. To achieve 80% power, the calculated sample size is 43. Considering a dropout rate of 20%, the sample size is increased to 52. Therefore, a total of 104 patients are planned to be included, with 52 patients in each group, including the IMS group and HE group.

Randomization and blinding

This study will adopt a two-arm parallel randomized controlled design. Enrolled patients will be randomly allocated to the IMS group and HE group in a 1:1 ratio. Randomization will be conducted by external statisticians using computer software, and the generated randomization list will be sealed in opaque envelopes.

After obtaining consent from all patients, each participant will receive an envelope containing their assigned group. The randomization process will be conducted by an external person not involved in the study. In this research, due to the nature of the intervention, the physiotherapists providing guidance will not be blinded. After completing all data collection, anonymous patient ID numbers will be handed over to the data analysts in the team for further analysis. However, implementing blinding during the data analysis phase presents challenges. The entire study will be a single-blind design, with the surgeons unaware of the patients’ groups.

Participants

Patients will be recruited by an experienced orthopedic surgeon based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. The research team will follow the principle of informed consent and provide detailed information to patients about the trial’s purpose, contents, potential risks, and benefits. Subsequently, all participants will sign a written informed consent form. This study will include end-stage knee OA patients, who will undergo TKA surgery at the end of 4 weeks of strength training. All surgeries will be completed by a single surgeon with over 20 years of experience. During hospitalization, all patients will receive the same treatment protocol and will be discharged 2–3 days after surgery.

Interventions

At West China Hospital, Sichuan University, all participants will undergo baseline testing and undergo their initial TKA surgery after eight weeks. During hospitalization, all patients will receive standardized treatment measures and be supervised by rehabilitation therapists who are not involved in the study, for early postoperative rehabilitation activities. High-dose IMS group

Patients in the IMS group will receive weekly lower-limb isokinetic muscle strengthening 3 times weekly for 4 weeks before hospital admission. Before the start of each session, patients will perform a 5 min weightless cycling warm-up. Next, they will perform 5 sets of isokinetic knee flexion–extension muscle strength training on the IsoMed 2000, with each set consisting of 8 repetitions. The angular velocity during training will be set at 60°s. Between sets, patients will have a 1 min rest period and a 5 min rest period between legs.

Health education (HE) group

Patients in the health education group will not receive muscle training before admission. After baseline assessment, this group of patients will undergo a joint health education session. The main topics covered include basic knowledge about knee OA, dietary recommendations, and exercise guidance.

In addition, to enhance preoperative cardiopulmonary function, patients are advised to cough [34] 100–200 times per day (10–20 times per hour). To supplement protein intake, patients are recommended to consume 3 eggs per day. Regarding functional exercises, patients will perform hip flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and knee flexion, extension exercises. Each exercise will be performed 20 times per hour, with each movement lasting 5–10 s. A total of 300 repetitions will be completed each day.

Rescue medication

If a muscle strain occurs during the IMS in the intervention group, the training will be immediately stopped, and the RICE principle should be applied for prompt treatment [35].

The acute management of muscle strain (RICE principle): ‘R’ stands for rest, which involves ceasing the activity to alleviate hematoma and minimize scar tissue formation; ‘I’ stands for icing, typically performed after a muscle strain to reduce bleeding and relieve tissue damage, facilitating muscle fiber regeneration; ‘C’ stands for compression, which entails applying pressure to reduce blood accumulation within the muscle and promote skeletal muscle recovery; ‘E’ stands for elevation, which involves raising the injured limb above the heart level to decrease hydrostatic pressure and decrease tissue swelling.

Detailed description of outcome measures

Primary outcome

Knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score

KOOS is a knee OA outcome survey consisting of 42 items. It includes 9 items for pain, 7 items for symptoms, 17 items for activities of daily living, 5 items for sports and recreation activities, and 4 items for quality of life. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 to 4. The scores for each subscale will be transformed into a percentage using a conversion formula. A score of 0 on the percentage scale indicates an extremely poor function in that particular aspect, while a score of 100 indicates a completely normal function (the MCID is 10 [33]).

The ratio of medial/lateral knee contact force

The VICON infrared (VICON Motion Systems Ltd., UK) motion capture system will be employed, comprising 12 VICON Vero cameras (mounted on the laboratory walls), VICON Lock Lab, 53 markers, 2 force plates (AMTI), a personal computer (PC), and the VICON NEXUS software. These devices will be utilized to record raw data from all cameras and reconstruct the three-dimensional motion of each optical marker. Additionally, participants will walk on the force plates at a self-selected speed.

Second outcomes

Knee pain

An 11-point Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) will be employed to evaluate patients’ pain levels over the previous 48 h. The NRS is a digital pain assessment tool that typically prompts patients to select a number from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 represents the most severe pain (the MCID is 1.8 [36]).

KL grade

Patients’ knee KL grade will be determined by two orthopedic surgeons with more than 20 years of clinical experience.

Isokinetic muscle strength

The IsoMed 2000 system (IsoMed 2000; D&R Ferstl, Hemnau, Germany) will be utilized to measure and evaluate the IMS of the knee joint. During the assessment, participants will perform maximum voluntary contractions of knee extension and flexion. Verbal encouragement will be consistently provided by the testing personnel during each contraction. Five maximum contractions will be conducted at an angular velocity of 60°s, and the maximum contractions will be used for subsequent data analysis.

Inflammatory response

Patients will be required to undergo an overnight fasting of at least 10 h. Blood sampling should be performed through venipuncture at least 2 h after waking up in the morning, collecting approximately 50 ml of plasma for C-reactive protein (CRP) and IL-6 analysis.

Knee swelling

The circumference of the knee joint during extension will be measured using the same tape measure in a relaxed supine position. Measurements will be taken from 1 and 9 cm above the patella.

Knee active ROM

Knee AROM in a supine position will be measured using a goniometer. The angle will be measured when the knee is fully extended and when the knee is maximally flexed with the ankle brought close to the hip on the same side. Three measurements will be taken for each patient, and the average of the three measurements will be calculated as the final value.

5-times sit-to-stand test (5-STS)

Patients will be instructed to stand up from a straight-backed chair five times without using their arms and maintaining a 90° bend in their knees. They cross their arms over their chest and aim to perform the task as quickly as possible, progressing from a seated to an upright position (the intra- and interclass correlation coefficients are 0.913 and 0.851 [37]). Time measurements are accurate to 0.01 s, with two measurements averaged for accuracy.

Time up-and-go test (TUG)

Patients will be instructed to sit on a chair without armrests. They will quickly stand up, walk forward for 3 m, turn around, and then sit back down. The test will be performed twice, and the average time will be recorded. Time measurements are accurate to 0.01 s.

3-meter walking speed

Patients will be asked to walk on a 3-m flat surface at their daily walking speed, and the assessor will concurrently record the completion time. Time measurements are accurate to 0.01 s.

Proprioceptive test

Patients will be asked to sit on a 90 cm chair above the ground with their eyes closed. First, the evaluator randomly places the patient’s knee joint within its normal ROM and holds it in that position for 3 s, while reminding the patient to remember the position of the joint. Then, the evaluator returns the patient’s knee joint to a normal sitting position. Finally, the patient is asked to place their knee joint back to the initial position based on their perception. During the entire process, the evaluator uses a digital goniometer to take measurements, and the measurement is repeated twice.

Surgical needs

After the 8-week IMS, the patients will be asked by the outcome assessor “Based on your knee symptoms from the previous week, do you believe you need knee surgery?” The responses will be divided into three options: (1) “Yes, I believe I need surgery,” (2) “I’m not sure,” or (3) “No, I don’t believe I need surgery.”

Berg balance scale (BBS)

The BBS is a balanced performance-based scale with 14 items. Each item is scored on a sequential scale (0–4). The test total ranges from 0 (lowest balance) to 56 (highest balance). Total scores < 40 suggest a risk of falling. Scores of 41–56 indicate good balance functioning.

Balance test

Measure balance ability under four conditions: stable surface with eyes open, stable surface with eyes closed, unstable surface with eyes open, and unstable surface with eyes closed.

Charlson comorbidity index (CCI)

CCI is a scoring system used to assess the burden of comorbidities in patients. It assigns different weights to various chronic diseases (such as heart disease, diabetes, cancer, etc.) and calculates a composite score. The higher the final score, the less favorable the situation and the lower the survival rate.

Knee fluid and cartilage sample collection

Synovial fluid from the patient’s knee (approximately 5 ml) and tibial plateau cartilage were taken intraoperatively for inflammatory factor testing. All samples will be stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Intraoperative bleeding

The Total Blood Loss (TBL) formula will be used to estimate the amount of blood loss during the surgery [38].

Hct0 = hematocrit before surgery; Hctf = hematocrit after surgery; HctAV = (hematocrit before surgery + hematocrit after surgery)/2; Female EBV = 0.3561 * height3 (m) + 0.03308 * weight (kg) + 0.1833; Male EBV = 0.3669 * height3 (m) + 0.03219 * weight (kg) + 0.6041.

Statistical analysis

All continuous data will be presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Statistical analysis will be conducted using per-protocol analysis, focusing only on patients who strictly adhered to the study protocol, to more accurately assess the intervention’s effect. Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) will be used to examine data from the same group of participants measured at different time points. Appropriate post-hoc tests will be conducted to determine significant differences.

Before conducting the statistical analysis, assumptions such as normality and homogeneity of variance for the data will be carefully checked. Appropriate data transformations or non-parametric statistical methods will be applied if the assumptions are not met. Categorical data will be presented as frequencies (number of cases) and relative frequencies (percentages). The categorical variables will be compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Statistical significance will be assessed as a two-sided P < 0.05. All data analysis will be performed using SPSS (V26.0).

Data management and quality control

The data from the isokinetic muscle strength assessment will be stored in the dedicated computer of the IsoMed 2000 equipment, and the 3-dimensional gait data will be stored in a dedicated hard drive. All patient self-reported questionnaires will be recorded on standardized case report forms by the assessor through direct inquiry. The data from the functional assessments (including the 5-STS, TUG, and 3 m walking speed) will be recorded by the outcome assessor using a stopwatch, with two measurements taken and the average value calculated.

After all outcome assessments are completed, the assessor will export all data from the dedicated data collection computer to locked Excel sheets. To ensure data integrity, anonymous ID numbers will be used, and data quality, range, and values will be checked to minimize typing errors. All original written documents and case report forms will be stored in a secure cabinet and kept for three years after the completion of the trial at the Department of Orthopaedics, West China Hospital of Sichuan University.

Discussion

A previous study showed a high knee OA incidence rate of 14.6% among 74,908 participants, peaking at 20.1% between 2008 and 2013 [39]. This emphasizes the substantial and continuous growth of knee OA patients in China. With ERAS concepts widely adopted in orthopedics, the significance of TKA accelerated recovery has increased [12, 13].

The weakness of the knee-extensor muscle plays a significant role in the development and progression of knee OA [40]. In patients with end-stage knee OA, the deficit in knee-extensor muscle strength persists and further deteriorates even after 3 months following TKA [41]. This weakness in the knee-extensor muscle may have negative impacts on the patient’s gait, physical abilities, and quality of life.

The study aims to explore the effects of isokinetic muscle strengthening on TKA perioperative and postoperative efficacy. The results will show that higher preoperative muscle strength conditions are more effective in decreasing inflammatory response and knee swelling, improving postoperative function, and reducing total blood loss in total knee arthroplasty. Additionally, the results may offer insights into postoperative lower-limb isokinetic muscle strengthening and its role in treating knee OA.

Strengths

The several strengths of this trial are briefly summarized as follows: firstly, the study employed a well-controlled experimental design with two groups, ensuring reliable and accurate results. The impact of IMS and health education on TKA patients will be evaluated, focusing on increasing muscle strength and improving inflammatory response, knee pain, swelling, and function.

Secondly, comprehensive data collection will be conducted, including assessments such as KOOS, gait, pain, activity abilities (5-STS, TUG, 3 m walking speed, balance test), Berg Balance Scale, and isokinetic muscle data. This thorough data collection provided valuable information and enhanced the scientific value of the study.

Furthermore, the study will explore the biomechanics of isokinetic muscle training on postoperative knee function, considering stability, kinematics, dynamics, and patient self-assessment. The research will offer specific and practical evidence for accelerated TKA recovery.

Overall, the study will exhibit significant advantages, encompassing a controlled design, comprehensive data, insights into biomechanics, and practical clinical implications, thereby providing crucial guidance for the rehabilitation treatments of knee OA patients.

Limitations

Several limitations in this study must be taken into consideration.

Firstly, due to the nature of the exercise intervention study, we excluded patients with severe knee OA who were unable to walk independently or had significant lower limb muscle atrophy, making them unable to complete the isokinetic muscle strengthening. However, this exclusion may introduce a risk of selection bias and does not represent all patients who require TKA surgery.

Secondly, for patients randomized to the health education group, we will not monitor their daily physical activities or family rehabilitation, which could potentially impact the study results. Although we consider this limitation from the study’s outset, we will attempt to minimize its effect by encouraging participants to adhere to the same health education program as much as possible.

Finally, during TKA surgery, the lack of standardized prostheses meant that different patients might receive different models of total knee prostheses, possibly affecting knee kinematics and dynamics.
