Machine Learning-Guided Survival Prediction and Treatment Sequencing in Advanced Soft Tissue Sarcoma Beyond Second-Line Therapy: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Michael Hoberger, Dorit Di Gioia, Romy L Zuber, Michael Völkl, Sinan E Güler, Vindi Jurinovic, Markus Albertsmeier, Alexander Klein, Hans Roland Dürr, Nina-Sophie Schmidt–Hegemann, Thomas Knösel, Wolfgang G Kunz, Michael von Bergwelt–Baildon, Lars H Lindner, Anton Burkhard–Meier, Luc M Berclaz

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvidence guiding the optimal sequencing of later-line systemic therapy in advanced soft tissue sarcoma (STS) remains limited. The aim of this study was to identify prognostic factors for overall survival (OS) beyond second-line treatment and to explore therapy sequencing in routine clinical practice.MethodsA total of 90 patients with advanced STS receiving third-line or later systemic therapy were retrospectively analyzed. Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) was used to identify clinical predictors of 1-year OS. The most influential variables were subsequently evaluated in multivariable Cox models for OS from the start of third- and fourth-line therapy. Sequencing analyses compared OS according to the line of administration of commonly used later-line agents.ResultsIn the third-line cohort (n = 88), inferior OS was independently associated with progression on second-line therapy (hazard ratio [HR] 2.31, p = 0.005). In contrast, lipo-/leiomyosarcoma histology was associated with improved survival (HR 0.37, p = 0.002), as was a time to progression ≥ 12 months on first-line therapy (HR 0.43, p = 0.007). Findings were largely consistent in the fourth-line cohort (n = 57). Sequencing analyses suggested sustained activity of trabectedin in later lines (p = 0.023), greater benefit of earlier pazopanib use (p = 0.022), and no significant impact of treatment line for gemcitabine + docetaxel combination therapy (p = 0.12).ConclusionsMachine learning-guided variable selection identified clinically relevant predictors of survival in later-line STS. Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) comprise a heterogeneous group of rare malignancies for which evidence-based treatment options beyond the second line remain scarce. Although doxorubicin-containing regimens constitute the backbone of first-line systemic therapy, most patients eventually experience disease progression and require subsequent lines of treatment [1]. Beyond the first-line setting, therapeutic decisions are primarily guided by histologic subtype, prior treatment exposure, and the likelihood of achieving disease control rather than cure, underscoring the need for a better understanding of outcomes and prognostic factors across successive treatment lines [2].

Several agents have demonstrated activity in advanced and pretreated STS populations, although benefit is generally modest. Pazopanib was found to significantly prolong progression-free survival (PFS) compared with placebo in patients with metastatic non-adipocytic STS previously treated with anthracycline-based chemotherapy (median 4.6 vs. 1.6 months), without improving overall survival (OS; median 12.5 vs. 10.7 months) [3]. Activity was particularly observed in leiomyosarcoma and synovial sarcoma [4]. Gemcitabine-based regimens are often used in later treatment lines, particularly in leiomyosarcoma. Studies of gemcitabine + docetaxel combination therapy have reported median PFS of approximately 3–6 months and median OS of 10–18 months in predominantly pretreated populations, with higher response rates in leiomyosarcoma compared with other histologies [5–7]. In anthracycline-pretreated non-adipocytic STS, gemcitabine + docetaxel and gemcitabine + pazopanib combination therapies demonstrated similar PFS (4.1 months) and disease control rates [8]. More recently, trabectedin has shown durable disease control in heavily pretreated liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma (L-sarcomas). In a phase III trial, trabectedin significantly improved PFS compared with dacarbazine (median PFS 3.3 vs. 2.3 months), without a corresponding OS benefit (median OS 13.9 vs. 11.8 months) [9]. Subsequent studies confirmed activity across multiple later lines, with a median PFS of 2–4 months and median OS of 9–16 months, particularly in myxoid liposarcoma and translocation-related sarcomas [10–13].

Other later-line options, including dacarbazine and temozolomide, demonstrate limited but measurable activity [14, 15]. Despite these therapies, survival remains poor in heavily pretreated patients, with retrospective cohorts reporting median OS as low as 6.4 months once standard options are exhausted [16]. Eribulin is one of the few agents to demonstrate a significant OS benefit in previously treated liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma (median OS 13.5 vs. 11.5 months with darcabazine) [17]. Taken together, these data underscore that while multiple agents demonstrate activity beyond first- and second-line therapy, OS in patients with advanced STS remains highly variable. Most prospective trials focus on single treatment lines and predefined populations, providing limited insight into how outcomes evolve as patients transition from second- to third- and fourth-line therapy in real-world practice. Comparative data addressing whether the timing and order of commonly used agents, such as trabectedin-, pazopanib-, or gemcitabine-based regimens, influence long-term survival are scarce and often limited due to small subgroups [18]. In this context, data-driven approaches may help to identify clinical and treatment-related factors associated with sustained benefit beyond second-line therapy. In this context, the principal aim of the present study was to characterize survival outcomes across later treatment lines in patients with advanced STS and to apply machine learning–based modeling to identify key predictors of OS. A secondary aim was to explore the potential impact of therapy sequencing in the second-, third-, and fourth-line setting.

Methods

Study Design

This study was designed as an exploratory, retrospective cohort analysis to evaluate clinical outcomes and prognostic factors in patients with advanced STS treated beyond second-line systemic therapy, and to develop machine learning-based models for the prediction of OS. All consecutive patients with histologically confirmed STS who received at least third-line systemic therapy between January 2021 and December 2025 at a high-volume academic sarcoma center were considered eligible for inclusion.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Internal Review Board and the Ethical Review Committee of the LMU Hospital Munich, Munich, Germany (Protocol No. 26-0142).

Patient Selection and Data Collection

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with histologically confirmed STS who had received at least three lines of systemic therapy before inclusion. Histopathological diagnoses were reviewed and confirmed by an experienced sarcoma pathologist (TK). Clinical, pathological, and treatment-related data were extracted from the prospectively maintained institutional sarcoma database. Baseline variables included age, sex, histologic subtype, number of prior systemic therapies, and time from initial diagnosis to first occurrence of recurrent or metastatic disease. Disease-related characteristics comprised metastatic burden at first diagnosis of metastasis, including the presence of oligometastatic disease (defined as ≤ 5 metastatic lesions), and primary tumor location. Treatment-related variables included systemic therapy regimens administered in each line of therapy beyond the second line, use of systemic maintenance therapy with trofosfamide, and the application of local treatment modalities in conjunction with systemic therapy, such as radiotherapy, surgery, or brachytherapy targeting ≥ 1 metastatic lesions. Radiologic response was assessed for each line of therapy according to RECIST version 1.1 (https://recist.eortc.org/recist-1-1/). All imaging studies were reviewed by an experienced sarcoma radiologist (WGK).

Statistical Analysis

Overall survival was defined as the time from the start of the respective line of systemic therapy to death from any cause. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and reported with log–log-transformed confidence intervals. Comparisons between groups were performed using the log-rank test. For machine learning-based variable selection and outcome prediction, we implemented an extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model. XGBoost is a machine learning technique that builds an ensemble of decision trees in a sequential manner, where each tree is trained to correct errors made by previous trees [19]. This approach allows complex, non-linear relationships between clinical variables and outcomes to be captured. The primary machine learning endpoint in the present analysis was binary OS of at least 12 months following the initiation of third-line therapy. Candidate predictors entered into the model comprised age at metastatic disease (> 55 vs. ≤ 55 years), sex, histology (L-sarcoma vs. other), metastatic burden at first diagnosis of metastatic disease (≤ 5 vs. > 5 metastases), primary tumor location (extremity vs. visceral vs. retroperitoneal vs. trunk vs. cutaneous), time to first diagnosis of metastatic disease (< 6 months vs. 6–12 months vs. > 12 months), measures of prior treatment benefit (time to progression after first-line therapy [≥ 12 vs. < 12 months] and radiological response to second-line therapy), and third-line treatment characteristics (third-line regimen: trabectedin vs. other, third-line local intervention [yes/no], and prior maintenance therapy [yes/no], derived from maintenance in line 1 or 2). Best response to first-line therapy was not included because approximately one third of patients received adjuvant treatment after surgery of the primary tumor and therefore had no measurable disease available for radiological response assessment. As progressive disease in the second-line treatment would typically have been detected within 6 months and dichotomization of time to progression would have used the median of 6 months as a cutoff, both variables captured essentially the same clinical information; therefore, only radiological response to second-line therapy was included in the model. Prior to model fitting, near-zero-variance predictors were removed. Model training was performed using stratified k-fold cross-validation (k = 5), with optimization of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a predefined grid exploring combinations of the following parameters: number of boosting rounds (nrounds), maximum tree depth (max_depth), learning rate (eta). The optimal model was selected based on maximal cross-validated AUC. Model performance was assessed using cross-validated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and corresponding AUC values. Variable importance was extracted from the final tuned model using gain-based importance measures. As decision tree-based ensemble methods such as XGBoost suffer from limited interpretability due to their internal decision structure, we selected the highest-ranking variables identified by the model for further regression analyses. To enhance clinical interpretability and account for the limited number of observed events, the number of variables included in the Cox proportional hazards models was chosen proportionally to the number of events to reduce the risk of overfitting. For the analysis of treatment sequencing, a subgroup of patients who had received at least four lines of systemic therapy was analyzed. This restriction was applied to reduce survivor/immortal-time bias by ensuring that patients receiving a given agent in the second, third, or fourth line all belonged to a population able to proceed through at least four treatment lines. OS was measured from the start of second-line therapy. Kaplan–Meier analyses and log-rank tests were used to compare survival according to the line (second, third or fourth) at which therapies of interest (trabectedin, pazopanib, and gemcitabine + docetaxel) were administered. All analyses were performed using R with the packages survival (v3.8.3), survminer (v0.5.0), caret (v7.0.1), and xgboost (v1.7.11.1) (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The study included 90 patients with advanced STS, with a median age at first diagnosis of 50 (interquartile range [IQR] 39–62) years; 54% of patients were female. The most common histologic subtype was leiomyosarcoma (41%), followed by synovial sarcoma (16%), undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS, 10%), and liposarcoma (12%), with other rare subtypes comprising 21%. Tumors were predominantly high grade, with 52% graded G3 and 43% graded G2 according to the Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC). Primary tumors were more frequently visceral (48%) and extremity-based (33%). Of the 90 patients included in the study, 30.6% presented with metastatic disease at initial diagnosis, whereas 69.4% developed metastatic disease metachronously following prior treatment of localized STS. At first diagnosis of metastasis, 59% of patients presented with diffuse metastases, while 41% had oligometastatic disease (≤ 5 lesions). Most patients received ≥ 4 lines of systemic therapy, and 92% were treated with an anthracycline-based first-line regimen. Regarding second-line therapy, most patients either had progressive disease (48%) or stable disease (39%). Partial responses were rare (12%), and there was no complete response. Time to metastatic disease was heterogeneous, occurring within 6 months in 34% of patients, between 6 and 24 months in 36% of patients, and after > 24 months in 29% of patients.

Treatment Patterns

Across treatment lines, therapy selection became increasingly heterogeneous. First-line treatment was dominated by doxorubicin + ifosfamide (62.2%), followed by doxorubicin + dacarbazine (20.0%) and doxorubicin monotherapy (8.9%). In later lines, trabectedin, pazopanib, and gemcitabine + docetaxel were most frequently used, with trabectedin predominating in the second (25.6%) line, while pazopanib (28.9%) and trabectedin (26.7%) were most commonly used in the third line. In the fourth line, trabectedin (27.6%) and pazopanib (24.1%) remained the most frequently administered therapies, while treatment selection became more heterogeneous. This diversification was most pronounced in the fifth line, where all other therapies accounted for 42.3% of treatments. (Fig. 1). Survival Outcomes

From the beginning of third-line therapy, median OS was 20.9 (95% confidence interval [CI] 16.4–25.0) months and median PFS was 3.1 (95% CI 2.8–5.0) months. Following the initiation of fourth-line therapy, median OS decreased to 10.8 (95% CI 9.2–21.2) months, while the median PFS remained similar at 4.0 (95% CI 2.6–5.1) months (Figs. 2, 3).

Machine Learning-Guided Survival Modeling

An XGBoost classification model was implemented to identify the most relevant variables for predicting OS of at least 1 year from the start of third-line therapy, based on clinical, disease-related characteristics, and prior treatment characteristics. The XGBoost model achieved a cross-validated AUC of 0.69, indicating reasonable discriminative performance for predicting OS ≥ 12 months. Variable importance analysis from the XGBoost model identified best overall response to second-line therapy (partial response/stable disease [PR/SD] vs. progressive disease [PD]) as the strongest predictor of OS ≥ 12 months, followed by histologic subtype (L-sarcoma vs. other) and time to progression after start of first-line therapy (≥ 12 vs. < 12 months). Additional influential variables included metastatic burden at diagnosis, age at metastatic disease (> 55 vs. ≤ 55 years), and sex, whereas treatment-related factors such as third-line trabectedin use, time to metastasis, maintenance therapy, and metastasis-directed local interventions contributed comparatively less to model performance (ESM Fig. S1).

The multivariable Cox regression revealed that failure to achieve at least stable disease on second-line therapy was independently associated with worse OS (hazard ratio [HR] 2.31, 95% CI 1.28–4.18; p = 0.005). In contrast, a longer time to progression after first-line treatment (≥ 12 months) was associated with improved OS (HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23–0.79; p = 0.007). Similarly, L-sarcoma histology was associated with improved survival compared to other histological subtypes (HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.20–0.70; p = 0.002) (Fig. 4). In the Cox model evaluating OS from the start of fourth-line therapy, the same set of variables identified for third-line treatment showed largely consistent effects. Failure to achieve at least stable disease during second-line therapy remained independently associated with worse OS (HR 2.06, 95% CI 1.03–4.09; p = 0.040), and L-sarcoma histology was again associated with improved survival (HR 0.28, 95% CI 0.13–0.60; p = 0.001). Time to progression ≥ 12 months after start of first-line therapy continued to show a protective effect, although this did not reach statistical significance in the smaller fourth-line cohort (HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.25–1.06; p = 0.074). Overall, the model remained statistically significant (p = 0.004), supporting the consistency of key prognostic factors across later lines of therapy.

Treatment Sequencing Analysis

When visualized by Kaplan–Meier analysis, distinct but hypothesis-generating patterns in survival according to treatment line were observed for the different agents (Fig. 5). Among patients treated with trabectedin, OS appeared to be the longest when the drug was administered in the fourth line, indicating that trabectedin can be beneficial in later lines of systemic therapy (p = 0.023). In contrast, for pazopanib, survival curves suggested more favorable outcomes when the agent was used earlier in the treatment sequence, particularly in the second line, with a diminishing survival effect when administered in later lines (p = 0.022). For gemcitabine + docetaxel combination therapy, survival curves largely overlapped across second-, third-, and fourth-line use, in line with the absence of a statistically significant association between line of administration and OS (p = 0.12).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of patients with advanced STS treated beyond the second line, we applied a machine learning-assisted approach to identify clinically relevant predictors of OS and explored the impact of treatment sequencing in routine clinical practice. Two principal observations emerge from our data: first, machine learning-based variable selection consistently identified a small set of clinically plausible prognostic factors for OS beyond second-line therapy; second, exploratory sequencing analyses suggest line-dependent differences in the effectiveness of commonly used later-line agents.

Using an XGBoost model to predict OS of at least 12 months from the start of third-line therapy, we identified best overall response to second-line treatment, histologic subtype, and time to progression following first-line therapy as the most influential variables. These factors were subsequently entered into multivariable Cox proportional hazards models for both third- and fourth-line therapy, where their prognostic relevance remained largely consistent. Progressive disease on second-line therapy and non-L-sarcoma histology were independently associated with inferior OS across treatment lines, while prolonged disease control during first-line therapy was associated with improved outcomes. Importantly, these findings reflect well-established clinical concepts: namely, that prior treatment sensitivity and underlying tumor biology strongly shape outcomes in later lines of therapy, and support the clinical face validity of the machine learning-derived variable selection [20, 21].

At the same time, the use of machine learning for variable selection in a retrospective cohort warrants cautious interpretation. The risk of overfitting and “winner’s curse” is inherent to data-driven approaches, particularly in modestly sized datasets, and the identified predictors should therefore be considered exploratory rather than definitive [22]. In this context, the concordance between machine learning-derived variables and clinically intuitive prognostic factors strengthen confidence in the robustness of the findings and supports their further evaluation in independent cohorts.

Beyond prognostic modeling, we explored the timing and sequencing of the three most frequently used later-line regimens, trabectedin, pazopanib, and gemcitabine + docetaxel, across second-, third-, and fourth-line therapy. While this analytical strategy is not suited to a definitive determination of optimal sequencing due to its retrospective and non-randomized nature, it provides insight into real-world treatment patterns and outcomes in heavily pretreated patients. Notably, trabectedin appeared to retain clinically meaningful activity even when administered in the fourth line, consistent with prior reports demonstrating durable disease control with trabectedin in selected patients treated across multiple lines [13]. In contrast, pazopanib showed more favorable survival outcomes when used earlier in the treatment sequence, suggesting that its benefit may diminish with increasing pretreatment burden. The sustained activity of trabectedin in later lines may relate to its distinct working mechanism, relative lack of cross-resistance, capacity for prolonged disease stabilization, and preferential use in sensitive histologic subtypes [23–25]. In contrast, the greater benefit of pazopanib in earlier treatment lines likely reflects lower pretreatment burden, better patient condition, and potentially reduced efficacy of antiangiogenic therapy in heavily pretreated, biologically resistant disease [26]. Consistent with this, subgroup analyses from the PALETTE trial, which led to regulatory approval of pazopanib, demonstrated longer median PFS in patients receiving pazopanib after only one prior line compared with those treated in later lines [27]. In contrast, the comparable outcomes observed with gemcitabine + docetaxel combination therapy across treatment lines may suggest a more line-independent cytotoxic activity, potentially reflecting incomplete cross-resistance with prior therapies and sustained efficacy even in more heavily pretreated disease [6, 28]. This combination may retain activity across lines due to its mechanism, which is less dependent on specific biological pathways and may remain effective in more aggressive tumor states.

Several limitations to this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and non-randomized treatment allocation introduce a risk of selection bias, as treatment choice and patient fitness likely influenced therapy use in later lines. Second, the limited sample size may have precluded detection of modest line-dependent differences. Finally, the predominance of leiomyosarcoma and visceral/retroperitoneal STS in our cohort may limit the generalizability of these findings to cohorts with different histologic and anatomical distributions, particularly extremity-predominant sarcoma populations.

Conclusion

This study identifies clinically meaningful prognostic factors for survival beyond second-line therapy in patients with advanced STS and demonstrates that these factors remain stable across subsequent treatment lines. In parallel, exploratory sequencing analyses suggest sustained activity of trabectedin in later lines, a potential advantage for the earlier use of pazopanib, and line-independent efficacy of gemcitabine + docetaxel. Together, these findings provide a framework for future studies aimed at refining the sequencing of systemic therapies in this challenging clinical setting.
