Use of the sunscreen for the degradation of a toxic methyl blue dye with hydrogen peroxide
Authors: Priyanka Sharma, Mamta Sahu, Mainak Ganguly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To protect against sunlight, the sunscreen (SC) is widely used, and here we demonstrate their novel application in environmental remediation. SC effectively degraded the toxic dye methyl blue (MBD) from aqueous solution under a household bulb within 18 min, while other common dyes (congo red, methylene blue, methyl orange, crystal violet, malachite green) remained unaffected. The process required hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as a co-catalyst. Titanium dioxide in SC generated hydroxyl radicals in the presence of H2O2, enabling MBD degradation. Mechanistic studies with scavengers revealed that both electron and hole pathways were essential, as their inhibition significantly reduced the reaction rate. Fluorescence spectroscopy confirmed the degradation, showing characteristic fluorescent products of MBD breakdown. Comparative studies using SCs from different manufacturers demonstrated that an inexpensive, commercially available, and non-toxic pharmaceutical sunscreen exhibited the highest efficiency. This approach highlights the potential of repurposing consumer products for sustainable wastewater remediation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23637-0.

Introduction

Water contamination is a serious environmental and health hazard. Contaminants found in industrial and municipal wastewaters, such as dyes, medications, pesticides, detergents, and heavy metal ions, are frequently water-soluble and can therefore diffuse not only in surface water bodies but also in groundwater, causing significant water quality problems1,2. Up to 20% of anthropogenic pollution is attributed to organic and inorganic dyes, primarily produced by the textile industry. In addition to their varying toxicity, dyes hinder sunlight’s ability to reach the water and their slow oxidative breakdown lowers the dissolved oxygen content, seriously impairing photosynthesis, aquatic life, and the ability of natural waters to clean themselves3,4.

It has been demonstrated that items including paper, plastics, leather, medications, food, cosmetics, colors, and dyeing effluents pose serious environmental hazards5–7. Several harmful colors can immediately impact the aquatic environment8,9. Organic substances with intricate aromatic molecular structures make up dye molecules. They are responsible for adding vibrant and eye-catching hues to a range of materials. On the other hand, dyes are extremely durable due to their complex aromatic molecular structures, which makes biodegradation more challenging10,11. In a wide range of applications, such as varnish, pigments, rubbers, medicines, textiles, printing, biological stains, and insecticides, methyl blue dye (MBD) is a widely used dye that functions as an antibacterial and coloring agent. Redness, inflammation, and trouble breathing can result from MBD’s irritation of the skin, eyes, and respiratory system when inhaled through dust or fumes. When MBD is consumed, gastrointestinal problems such nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea may occur12–29.

Semiconductor heterogeneous photocatalysts, including TiO2, ZnO, Fe2O3, and CdS, are employed separately or in combination with other semiconductors or with metal or nonmetal doping to enhance photocatalytic activity30–33. It is emphasized that these modifying actions are important instruments for enhancing photocatalytic performance and effectively breaking down organic pollutants using the zero-waste framework. One of the best semiconductors, TiO2, is frequently used to break down organic materials due to its remarkable photocatalytic stability and processability34,35. However, its large bandgap (Eg = 3.2 eV) is not possible in the solar spectrum; hence, it has been necessary to build distinct photocatalysts for optimal functioning in visible light settings while achieving a decreased bandgap36,37. Sultan et al.38 recently reported the synergism for the catalytic degradation of crystal violet dye with silicon/titanium and graphene nanocomposite. The MnFe2O4 nanoparticles39 exhibited strong photocatalytic activity under sunlight: their narrowed band gap and abundant surface oxygen vacancies enhanced visible light absorption and reactive species generation, enabling efficient degradation of dyes like methylene blue and alizarin red. They decolourized these organic pollutants with high efficiency and, because of their magnetic nature, were easily recovered and reused multiple times.

A co-catalyst is defined as a secondary catalyst that functions in conjunction with the primary catalyst in order to improve the overall efficiency, selectivity, or stability of a catalytic reaction. It does not function as the primary active site, but it accelerates charge transfer, produces reactive intermediates, or regenerates the active species of the primary catalyst, which results in an increase in the reaction rate40,41. For instance, co-catalysts can enhance electron-hole separation, decrease recombination rates, and supply extra reactive species for oxidation or reduction pathways in photocatalytic dye degradation. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is frequently utilised as a co-catalyst. This is due to the fact that it may easily breakdown under light or catalytic circumstances in order to create hydroxyl radicals (•OH), which are highly oxidative and provide a major contribution to the mineralisation of dyes. In the same way, sodium borohydride (NaBH4) can serve as a reducing agent or co-catalyst by providing electrons to the system42. This action helps to reduce organic contaminants or increase electron density in order to improve catalytic reactions. Even though they are not the principal catalysts, these co-catalysts have a very important role in creating reactive species that take part in the degrading pathways, hence boosting the overall efficiency of the reaction43. The collaboration between a catalyst and a co-catalyst is especially significant in environmental applications, where it is critical to achieve rapid and full breakdown of harmful dyes. H2O2 and NaBH4 can be thought of as excellent co-catalysts due to the fact that they enhance charge utilisation and speed up the degradation process by way of mechanisms involving electron donation or radicals.

Climate warming and ozone depletion have increased human exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation in recent decades. Extended or excessive exposure to UV radiation poses a serious risk, even if short-term UV light exposure may have some health advantages, such as enhancing vitamin D production44–46. Sunburns, photoaging, and long-term consequences including skin cancer, can all be brought on by solar UV radiation. Sunscreen, sometimes referred to as UV filters, are a range of inorganic and organic chemical compounds that have been used in sunscreens and other personal care products (PCPs) since the late nineteenth century to protect the skin against UV solar radiation-induced damage47–49.

We disclosed the usage of the commercially available, cost-effective, non-toxic sunscreen (SC1) for a toxic dye removal, connected to wastewater remediation. SC1 contained Ti, and Si. TiSi microcomposite in SC1 degraded a toxic dye MBD with the presence of H2O2.

Experimental section

Synthesis of SC1

To isolate the photocatalyst, 0.25 mg of commercial sunscreen was suspended in 20 mL of ethanol and magnetically agitated for 2 h to achieve homogeneous mixing and dissolution of organic components. This technique facilitated the release of inorganic oxide nanoparticles (such as Ti. Si), which are frequently employed as active UV-filtering agents in sunscreens, into the solvent medium. The suspension progressively developed an off-white sol (SC1), signifying the effective dispersion of the photocatalyst particles. Before the dye degradation trials, the SC1 sol underwent ultrasonication for 5 min to disintegrate agglomerates and attain a uniform nanosuspension with enhanced surface exposure. The isolated sol, augmented with the inorganic photocatalyst, was subsequently utilised in the dye removal investigations [Fig. 1].

Degradation of dye and sensing

To conduct the dye degradation experiment, 20 mL (10−3 M) dye, 2 mL SC1, and 0.01 M H2O2 were completely mixed and kept 5 cm below of a 200 W glowing bulb with constant stirring for 18 min.

Results and discussions

Selective degradation of MBD

SC1 was found to be a highly efficient photocatalyst for MBD degradation. The degradation was performed under a glowing bulb (200 W) for 18 min. For this photocatalysis reaction, H2O2 was used as a co-catalyst to generate free radicals, OH., to facilitate degradation. Without H2O2, no remarkable degradation was possible under similar experimental conditions. We also observed insignificant degradation of MBD with SC1 and H2O2 in the dark. UV-vis spectroscopy authenticated the degradation of MBD in the presence of SC1 and H2O2 under a glowing bulb only. To the naked eye, we witnessed a colour change from blue to white. The color change was permanent due to the degradation of MBD. MBD is a colour-sensitive dye. By changing the pH with HCl and NaOH, we could not recover the blue-coloured dye from the colorless solution. So, it was the process of irreversible degradation (Fig. 2).

Under similar experimental conditions, we repeated the experiment with repeated the experiment with other different commonly available toxic dyes (methylene blue, crystal violet, malachite green, methyl orange, methyl red). Only methyl blue could be degraded. Though many dyes are available having similar color (blue), we were able to sense MBD just by the naked eye.

For the first time, we disclosed sunscreen for the degradation of a toxic dye MBD. Different experimental analyses indicated that sunscreen SC1 was a microcomposite of Ti and Si. Though Ti in the form of TiO2 is an efficient photocatalyst for degradation of several dyes (congo red, methylene blue, methyl orange, crystal violet, malachite green etc.), no report is available for the degradation of MBD with TiO2. On the other hand, SC1 could degrade MBD under our experimental conditions selectively. Other tested dyes could not be significantly degraded by SC1. Such observation indicated that the synergism of Ti, and Si in the matrix restricted the degradation of other dyes, unlike MBD. More research is warranted in the future to understand the mechanism of selective MBD degradation with SC1. However, aging for longer times (~ 3 h) under visible light resulted in degradation of other dyes.

According to Table 1, the MBD removal efficiency was enhanced by increasing [SC1] up to 2.00 mL. The unfavourable effect of photodegradation occurred when the amount exceeded 2.00 mL due to the aggregation of SC1, which prevented the surface from absorbing photons. In addition, the solution’s turbidity decreased the penetration of UV light, resulting in a decrease in photodegradation efficiency. The degradation process was determined to be pseudo-zero order and be pseudo-second order.

We also collected SC from different companies (VLCC (S2), Vaseline (S3), Cetaphil (S3)). Substantial degradation was only observed for SC1 (Fig. S3, ESI). Such observation implied that the composition of the sunscreen was different depending on the company. SC1 was found to be an efficient photocatalyst, as it contained a semiconductor metal oxide.

To further understand the degradation process, we studied the fluorescence of (MBD + SC1 + H2O2) under the glowing bulb at various time intervals. It is already reported that the degraded product of MBD was fluorescent12,13. Evolution of fluorescence, along with decolorization of MBD, authenticated the degradation of the dye. Maximum fluorescence was obtained after 18 min, when MBD was completely colorless. In other words, gradual decolorization was associated with enhancement of fluorescence (λex 310 nm, λem 424 nm) [Fig. 3]. It is noteworthy that SC1 had a diminutive emission, and the fluorescence at zero min was due to SC1, which acted as a photocatalyst. As per the existing literature12, the colorless degraded product of MBD had significant fluorescence. We found that after the process of degradation, fluorescence enhancement was observed. Such observation of fluorescence enhancement supported the inference of dye degradation. MBD produced photodegraded products that were fluorescent. LCMS spectra13 already confirmed the fragmented products as shown in Fig. S2, ESI.

Based on the kinetic analysis, the degradation of methyl blue by SC1 showed strong linearity with both the pseudo-zero-order (R2 = 0.983) and pseudo-second-order (R2 = 0.980) models. [Fig. 4] These high correlation coefficients indicated that the degradation process was best described by a pseudo-zero-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic framework. In particular, the pseudo-zero-order behavior suggested that once the catalyst surface became saturated, the degradation rate was no longer influenced by the dye concentration and proceeded at a constant rate over time51. The kinetic modeling revealed that the degradation of methyl blue by SC1 followed both pseudo-zero-order and pseudo-second-order models, indicating surface saturation-controlled reactions along with chemisorption-driven interactions52,53. Such model fitting not only clarified the underlying mechanism but also validated the catalytic efficiency of SC1 in dye degradation processes.

The presence of pseudo-zero-order and pseudo-second-order kinetics in the analysis elucidates the molecular details of the degradation process54. A pseudo-zero-order fit signifies that, under specific conditions, the degradation rate remains unaffected by dye concentration, implying that the catalyst surface is saturated with dye molecules and the reaction is predominantly governed by the availability of active sites or reactive oxygen species rather than substrate concentration. This frequently transpires at elevated initial dye concentrations, where surface coverage is optimised. Conversely, a strong association with pseudo-second-order kinetics indicates that the adsorption of dye molecules onto the catalyst surface is a crucial step, and the reaction rate is contingent upon both the dye concentration and the availability of active sites55. This model indicates that chemisorption or robust interactions, such as electron transfer between dye molecules and catalytic sites, predominate the process. Collectively, these data indicate that degradation occurs through surface-mediated reactions, wherein the production of reactive species and dye adsorption are both essential, and the rate-limiting step may vary based on the reaction conditions.

FESEM and EDS analysis

We performed FESEM and EDS to understand SC1. FESEM indicated that micro particles were flaky. The surface appeared to be smooth as observed from higher resolution FESEM image. Particles were aggregated to form microparticles. EDS as well as elemental mapping indicated the presence of Ti and Si in SC1 [Fig. 5]. Au was found in the EDS spectra due to the gold coating on SC1 before capturing FESEM images. Both were well studied for photocatalytic dye degradation, as obtained in the literature. It was anticipated that such metal in the form of metal oxide would facilitate the dye degradation.

The SC1 sol had steady negative zeta potential (− 55.0 mV), confirming high colloidal stability. The DLS study revealed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 752.9 nm [Fig. 6]. The zeta potential of the SC1 sol was measured to be − 55.0 mV, indicating robust electrostatic repulsion among particles and confirming superior colloidal stability, as values exceeding |30 mV| are typically regarded as stable. This significant negative charge inhibited irreversible aggregation and preserved dispersion stability. DLS indicated a hydrodynamic diameter of 752.9 nm, encompassing the particle core together with its hydration layer or capping molecules. Due to its intensity-weighted nature, DLS can be influenced by a minor proportion of bigger or loosely linked clusters, hence distorting the average size. The combination of a significantly negative zeta potential and a substantial hydrodynamic diameter indicates that the system is electrostatically stabilised, with the observed increase in size resulting from solvation effects or reversible clustering rather than destabilisation.

Whereas photogenerated electrons in the conduction band produced O2·− radicals from dissolved oxygen, photogenerated holes in the valence band produced hydroxyl radicals (•OH) from water or hydroxide ions. Using radical scavengers, the existence of reactive species was demonstrated. The significance of both h⁺ and •OH radicals in the photocatalytic process was highlighted by the considerable reduction in D% (51%) caused by the addition of isopropyl alcohol (•OH scavenger) at a molar ratio of [Scavenger]/[H2O2] = 1. Such an experiment confirmed the role of OH radicals in dye removal. Ascorbic acid (O2·− scavenger) almost stopped the degradation under our experimental conditions. The inclusion of isopropyl alcohol (IPA), a recognised hydroxyl radical (•OH) scavenger62, resulted in a significant reduction in degradation efficiency, thereby affirming the crucial involvement of •OH radicals in the oxidative degradation of dye molecules. Likewise, the addition of ascorbic acid, a superoxide radical (O2·−) scavenger63, resulted in a decrease in activity, indicating the participation of O2·− species in the reaction mechanism. The comparative degree of inhibition by IPA and ascorbic acid underscores the relative significance of these species, with greater suppression signifying the predominant route. Consequently, both •OH and O2·− radicals synergistically contribute to the photocatalytic degradation process; however, the more significant reduction induced by ascorbic acid indicated that superoxide radicals were the predominant reactive species in this system. On the other hand, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (h⁺ scavenger)64 had an insignificant effect. Thus, O2·− was pivotal behind the mechanism of dye degradation, while h+ had no prominent effect65,66 [Fig. 9].

Zinc oxide (ZnO), frequently utilised as an active component in sunscreens, functions as a semiconductor photocatalyst that can produce reactive oxygen species when exposed to UV or visible light irradiation13. In the breakdown of methylene blue (MBD), ZnO promotes the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide radicals (O2·−), which subsequently degrade the dye molecules. The elevated surface area and robust light absorption facilitate the production of electron–hole pairs, hence augmenting the total photocatalytic efficiency. Furthermore, ZnO possesses the ability to adsorb dye molecules onto its surface, facilitating their closeness to reactive species, so expediting the degradation process. The inclusion of organic excipients in the sunscreen may obstruct active sites on ZnO or influence radical production, potentially altering its efficacy. ZnO plays a crucial role in the photocatalytic degradation of MBD by facilitating both adsorption and the formation of reactive species, hence serving as the primary agent responsible for the observed dye elimination. However, we did not find the presence of Zn during our characterization experiments.

Recycling of the catalyst

During the first cycle, an introduction of 2.00 mL of the SC1 sol into a mixture of 20 mL of a 0.01 M MBD solution that contained 0.01 M H2O2 was carried out. The combination was thereafter irradiated for a period of 18 min by means of a visible-light bulb source. Using equation (i), the degradation percentage (D%) was determined on the basis of the difference in absorbance readings with and without the catalyst. It is worth noting that the catalyst (SC1) and co-catalyst (H2O2) were only introduced during the first cycle and that these chemicals were not supplied in the cycles that followed. The second cycle was carried out in the same reaction mixture by adding 0.2 mL of a 1 M MBD solution, then irradiating it for a further 18 min. 0.2 mL 1 M is practically the same as 20 mL 0.01 M. By adding the dye in a concentrated form, it was possible to keep the overall solution volume practically constant. In order to calculate an estimate for D % for the third, fourth, and fifth cycles, the same technique was performed. Within the same irradiation period, the catalyst demonstrated strong activity in the first cycle, with a 96% removal of the dye. Following this, it showed a progressive decline in efficiency in the subsequent cycles, attaining 90%, 78%, 71%, and 63% degradation in the second, third, fourth, and fifth cycles, respectively. A decrease in the degradation efficiency that is observed over time with repeated usage is a consequence of the following factors: the permanent morphological changes that occur on the surface of the nanocatalyst, the partial deactivation of the catalyst, and the continuous blockage of active sites that are caused by the leftover dye molecules and intermediates of the reaction67 [Fig. S4, ESI].

Conclusions

A well-used commercially available material was used for wastewater remediation via degradation of the toxic dye methyl blue. Our experimental strategy reduces the operational cost and time, along with a higher degree of recyclability. The photocatalysis was selective to the dye methyl blue, attributed to the synergism between Ti and Si. Visible light could provide sufficient energy to generate free radicals, responsible for degradation. This piece of work is intensely related to the circular economy, a crying need of the present generation in the context of sustainable water management. Moreover, the tactic may perform as a link between a regular commodity and environmental protection, opening a new ground for pollution management.

The presence of additional active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and excipients in commercial sunscreens can affect the photocatalytic degradation of methyl blue (MBD) in various manners. Excipients include emollients, oils, waxes, and organic UV filters may partially adsorb onto the surface of inorganic photocatalysts (e.g., TiO2, SiO2), potentially obstructing active sites and diminishing the production of reactive oxygen species, hence reducing degradation efficiency. Conversely, certain organic constituents may function as photosensitisers or aid in electron transport, augmenting the generation of radicals such as •OH and O2·−, thereby facilitating dye breakdown. The APIs may compete with MBD for adsorption on the catalyst surface, affecting reaction kinetics, or may engage in photoreactions that produce secondary reactive species that contribute to the degradation process. The intricate composition of commercial sunscreen can either suppress or augment photocatalytic activity based on the characteristics, concentration, and interactions of excipients and active pharmaceutical ingredients with the catalyst and dye.

This study shows that the material’s significant redox activity and electron transfer capabilities make it efficient and stable in dye degradation. Direct methanol fuel cells (DMFCs) need these qualities for methanol oxidation and oxygen reduction. The high surface area, adjustable oxidation states, and reusability imply long-term electrocatalytic applications. Although no direct DMFC data are given here, the observed performance provides a solid framework for future research. Thus, our discovery allows the material to be used in energy conversion devices like DMFCs as well as environmental cleanup.
