Theory for Optimal Estimation and Control under Resource Limitations and Its Applications to Biological Information Processing and Decision-Making
Takehiro Tottori 1,2, Tetsuya J. Kobayashi 2

Abstract
Despite being optimized, the information processing of biological organisms exhibits significant variability in its complexity and capability. One potential source of this diversity is the limitation of resources required for information processing. However, we lack a theoretical framework that comprehends the relationship between biological information processing and resource limitations and integrates it with decision-making conduced downstream of the information processing. In this paper, we propose a novel optimal estimation and control theory that accounts for the resource limitations inherent in biological systems. This theory explicitly formulates the memory that organisms can store and operate and obtains optimal memory dynamics using optimal control theory. This approach takes account of various resource limitations, such as memory capacity, intrinsic noise, and energy cost, and unifies state estimation and control. We apply this theory to minimal models of biological information processing and decision-making under resource limitations and find that such limitations induce discontinuous and non-monotonic phase transitions between memory-less and memory-based strategies. Therefore, this theory establishes a comprehensive framework for addressing biological information processing and decision-making under resource limitations, revealing the rich and complex behaviors that arise from resource limitations.

We apply this theory to a minimal model of biological information processing and investigate optimal estimation strategies under resource limitations.
Although our minimal model considers a simple optimal estimation problem with linear Gaussian dynamics and quadratic costs, it exhibits discontinuous and non-monotonic phase transitions between memory-less and memory-based estimation strategies depending on available resources.
Such phase transitions do not occur in the conventional Bayesian filtering that disregards resource limitations.
Therefore, our analysis suggests that resource limitations may drive a punctuated and complex evolution of biological information processing, leading to qualitatively diverse behaviors across organisms.

This theory also provides a unified framework for both state estimation and control by utilizing memory control instead of Bayesian filtering.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach by applying it to a minimal model of biological information processing and decision-making.
This model also exhibits discontinuous phase transitions between memory-less and memory-based control strategies.
Yet, non-monotonic phase transitions do not appear with respect to sensory noise, while it occurs with respect to environmental uncertainty.
These findings suggest that while non-monotonicity may depend on specific models and parameters, discontinuity is a fundamental characteristic under resource limitations.

II
Optimal Estimation Theory without Resource Limitations

In this section, we briefly review the conventional optimal theory for estimating dynamic environments, Bayesian filtering theory (Fig.
2
(a))
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
This theory considers an environment and an agent.
The state of the environment at time
t\in[0,T]
is denoted by
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, which evolves by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\displaystyle dx_{t}=b(t,x_{t})dt+\sigma(t,x_{t})d\omega_{t},

(1)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows
p_{0}(x_{0})
and
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\omega}}
is a standard Wiener process.
The agent cannot completely observe environmental state
x_{t}
.
Instead, it obtains noisy observation
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, which is generated from probability density function
p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t})
as follows:

\displaystyle y_{t}\sim p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t}).

(2)

The agent estimates environmental state
x_{t}
from noisy observation history
y_{t},y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
.
The state estimator of the agent at time
t
is generally given by

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{t}=\hat{x}(t,y_{t},y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}).

(3)

The objective function the agent minimizes is the following state estimation error:

\displaystyle J[\hat{x}]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t}\right)^{\top}Q\left(x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t}\right)dt\right],

(4)

where
Q\succ O
.
Bayesian filtering theory considers a problem to find optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
that minimizes state estimation error
J[\hat{x}]
:

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{*}:=\arg\min_{\hat{x}}J[\hat{x}].

(5)

By solving Eq. (
5
), optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
is obtained by the conditional expectation of state
x_{t}
given observation history
y_{t},y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
:

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{*}(t,y_{t},y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{t}|y_{t},y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})}\left[x_{t}\right].

(6)

In order to obtain optimal state estimator
\hat{x}_{t}^{*}=\hat{x}^{*}(t,y_{t},y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})
, the agent needs to memorize past observation history
y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
and it becomes harder and harder as time progresses.
Bayesian filtering theory resolves this problem by compressing past observation history
y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
into the posterior probability density function

\displaystyle p(x_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})\in\mathcal{P}[\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}],

(7)

where
\mathcal{P}[\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}]
indicates the space of a probability density function on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
.
The optimal state estimator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{t}|y_{t},p(x_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}))}\left[x_{t}\right],

(8)

which means that the agent estimates environmental state
x_{t}
from observation
y_{t}
and posterior probability density function
p(x_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})
.
This compression does not impair the performance because
p(x_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})
is the sufficient statistic of
y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
for estimating
x_{t}
.

p(x_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})
can be updated sequentially by using Bayes’ theorem:

\displaystyle p(x_{t+dt}|y_{t},y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})=

\displaystyle\int p(x_{t+dt}|x_{t})\frac{p(y_{t}|x_{t})p(x_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})}{p(y_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})}dx_{t}.

(9)

Therefore, in Bayesian filtering theory, the agent obtains optimal state estimator
\hat{x}_{t}^{*}
[Eq. (
8
)] by computing the Bayesian update of the posterior probability [Eq. (
9
)].

However, Bayesian filtering is an ideal theory for optimal estimation and overlooks the resource limitations inherent in organisms.
This oversight prevents organisms from fully implementing Bayesian filtering.
For example, organisms cannot completely store infinite-dimensional posterior probability

,

Additionally, organisms find it challenging to accurately compute the Bayesian update of the posterior probability [Eq. (

,

,

Furthermore, organisms need to minimize not only the state estimation error but also the energy consumption associated with the estimation process, as their available energy are severely limited

Therefore, a new optimal estimation theory that takes into account these limitations is necessary to understand the estimation strategies of organisms.

, but the problem formulation is slightly modified, which allows us to discuss biologically plausible situations (See Appendix

III.1
Problem Formulation

In this subsection, we formulate the optimal estimation problem with resource limitations.
The state
x_{t}
and observation
y_{t}
are identical to those in the Bayesian filtering problem, which are given by Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), respectively.
In contrast, this problem explicitly formulates the finite-dimensional memory that the agent can store and operate,

\displaystyle z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}},

(10)

where the dimension of the memory,
d_{z}
, is determined by the available memory size of the agent.
The agent determines state estimator
\hat{x}_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{t}=\hat{x}(t,y_{t},z_{t}).

(11)

Compared to Eq. (
3
), infinite-dimensional past observation history
y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
is replaced with finite-dimensional memory
z_{t}
, which allows for the implementation in organisms with finite memory capacities.

Memory
z_{t}
is assumed to evolve by the following SDE:

\displaystyle dz_{t}=c(t,z_{t},v_{t})dt+\eta(t,z_{t},v_{t})d\xi_{t},

(12)

where initial memory
z_{0}
follows
p_{0}(z_{0})
,
v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
is the memory control of the agent, and
\xi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
is a standard Wiener process.
The agent determines memory control
v_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
:

\displaystyle v_{t}=v(t,y_{t},z_{t}).

(13)

While the dynamics of the posterior probability [Eq. (
9
)] is deterministic given observation
y_{t}
, that of the memory [Eq. (
12
)] is stochastic due to the intrinsic noise,
\xi_{t}
.

A key challenge in this problem lies in how to optimally compress the information about past observation history
y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
into memory
z_{t}
.
Bayesian filtering theory optimally compresses the past observation information into infinite-dimensional and deterministic posterior probability
p(x_{t}|y_{t-dt},...,y_{0})
using Bayes’ theorem.
However, this optimization method cannot be applied to memory
z_{t}
because it is finite-dimensional and stochastic.
We address this issue by using optimal control theory:
The agent optimally compresses the past observation information into memory
z_{t}
by optimizing memory control
v_{t}
.
This idea leads to the simultaneous optimization of state estimator function
\hat{x}
and memory control function
v
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{*},v^{*}:=\arg\min_{\hat{x},v}J[\hat{x},v].

(14)

Objective function
J[\hat{x},v]
is given by the following expected cumulative cost function:

\displaystyle J[\hat{x},v]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t},z_{t},\hat{x}_{t},v_{t})dt+g(x_{T},z_{T})\right],

(15)

where
f(t,x_{t},z_{t},\hat{x}_{t},v_{t})
and
g(x_{T},z_{T})
are the running and terminal cost functions, respectively.

For comparison with Bayesian filtering theory, we consider the following cost functions as a special case:

\displaystyle f:=\left(x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t}\right)^{\top}Q\left(x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t}\right)+m(t,z_{t},v_{t}),

(16)

\displaystyle g:=0,

(17)

where
m(t,z_{t},v_{t})
is the cost function for memory
z_{t}
and memory control
v_{t}
.
These cost functions indicate that our theory can minimize not only the state estimation error (the first term of Eq. (
16
)) but also the energy cost associated with the estimation process (the second term of Eq. (
16
)).

In summary, this theory achieves optimal estimation under resource limitations, including memory capacity, intrinsic noise, and energy cost, by replacing the Bayesian filtering of the posterior probability with the optimal control of the memory.

III.2
Optimal Solution

The optimal estimation problem with resource limitations can be solved by using Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space, which has been recently employed in mean-field stochastic optimal control problems
[
99
,
100
,
101
,
102
]
and our previous works
[
96
,
97
,
98
]
.
In this subsection, we skip the derivation and only show the main result.
The details of the derivation are shown in Appendix
A
and
B
.

Optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
and optimal memory control function
v^{*}
are obtained by minimizing the conditional expected Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{*}(t,y,z),v^{*}(t,y,z)=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\hat{x},v}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,z,\hat{x},v,w)\right].

(18)

Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t,x,z,\hat{x},v,w):=f(t,x,z,\hat{x},v)+\mathcal{L}_{v}w(t,x,z),

(19)

where
f(t,x,z,\hat{x},v)
is the current cost and
\mathcal{L}_{v}w(t,x,z)
represents the future cost.

\mathcal{L}_{v}
is the time-backward diffusion operator for state
x
and memory
z
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{v}w(t,x,z):=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}b_{i}(t,x)\frac{\partial w(t,x,z)}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{z}}c_{i}(t,z,v)\frac{\partial w(t,x,z)}{\partial z_{i}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{x}}\sigma_{i}(t,x)\sigma_{j}(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}w(t,x,z)}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{z}}\eta_{i}(t,z,v)\eta_{j}(t,z,v)\frac{\partial^{2}w(t,x,z)}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}},

(20)

and
w(t,x,z)
is the value function, which represents the expected cumulative cost starting from time
t
with state
x
and memory
z
to terminal time
T
.
Value function
w(t,x,z)
is obtained by solving the following time-backward partial differential equation, Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial w(t,x,z)}{\partial t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,z,\hat{x}^{*},v^{*},w)\right],

(21)

where the terminal condition is given by
w(T,x,z)=g(x,z)
.
In the optimal solution [Eq. (
18
)], conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[\cdot\right]
appears, which is consistent with the fact that the agent needs to estimate state
x
from observation
y
and memory
z
.
Conditional probability density function
p_{t}(x|y,z)
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle p_{t}(x|y,z)=\frac{p_{t}(y|x)p_{t}(x,z)}{\int p_{t}(y|x)p_{t}(x,z)dx}.

(22)

Probability density function
p_{t}(x,z):=p(t,x,z)
is obtained by solving the following time-forward partial differential equation, Fokker-Planck (FP) equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(t,x,z)}{\partial t}=\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v^{*}}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x,z),

(23)

where the initial condition is given by
p(0,x,z)=p_{0}(x)p_{0}(z)
.

\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v}^{{\dagger}}
is the expected time-forward diffusion operator for state
x
and memory
z
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x,z):=-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\frac{\partial b_{i}(t,x)p(t,x,z)}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{z}}\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[c_{i}(t,z,v)\right]p(t,x,z)}{\partial z_{i}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{x}}\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{i}(t,x)\sigma_{j}(t,x)p(t,x,z)}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{z}}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\eta_{i}(t,z,v)\eta_{j}(t,z,v)\right]p(t,x,z)}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}},

(24)

where
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\cdot\right]
is necessary because memory control
v
depends on observation
y
.
Therefore, optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
and optimal memory control function
v^{*}
are obtained by jointly solving HJB equation [Eq. (
21
)] and FP equation [Eq. (
23
)].

It should be noted that organisms do not need to solve HJB and FP equations [Eqs. (

) and (
23
)] because they may obtain optimal solutions

and
v^{*}
as a result of evolution.
Therefore, it is not necessary to consider the resource limitations of organisms for the computation of optimal solutions
\hat{x}^{*}
and
v^{*}
.
In contrast, it is necessary to consider their resource limitations associated with the dynamics of memory
z_{t}
because memory
z_{t}
depends on real-time observation
y_{t}
such as olfactory, auditory, and visual sensing information.

If cost function
f
is given by Eq. (
16
), optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
[Eq. (
18
)] is given by

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{*}(t,y_{t},z_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{t}|y_{t},z_{t})}\left[x_{t}\right].

(25)

A comparison of Eqs. (
6
) and (
25
) clarifies that our theory replaces infinite-dimensional past observation history
y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
with finite-dimensional memory
z_{t}
.

IV.1
Problem Formulation

In this subsection, we formulate the minimal model of biological information processing (Fig.
3
(a)).
We illustrate this model using cellular information processing as an example following previous studies
[
53
,
55
,
56
]
, but it should be noted that this model is general and can be applied to the information processing of other systems such as neurons and robots.

A cell has to accurately perceive environmental state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
to achieve various physiological functions such as chemotaxis, growth, and differentiation.
Environmental state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is assumed to evolve by the following Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process:

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle=-x_{t}dt+\sqrt{D}d\omega_{t},

(26)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(x_{0}|0,1)
, and
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is a standard Wiener process.

D\geq 0
represents the intensity of the state noise.

The cell observes environmental state
x_{t}
through receptor activities, which correspond to observation
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Observation
y_{t}
is generated from the following Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle y_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{t}|x_{t},E),

(27)

where
E\geq 0
represents the intensity of the observation noise.

The cell memorizes the information about past observation history
y_{t-dt},...,y_{0}
through intracellular chemical molecules, which correspond to memory
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Memory
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
evolves by the following SDE:

\displaystyle dz_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{t}dt+\sqrt{F}d\xi_{t},

(28)

where initial memory
z_{0}
follows standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(z_{0}|0,1)
,
v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the memory control, and
\xi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is a standard Wiener process.

F\geq 0
represents the intensity of the memory noise.

The memory dynamics corresponds to intracellular chemical reactions in cellular information processing.

v_{t}
and
F
represent the intensity and the noise of intracellular chemical reactions, respectively.
If a cell could invest a large number of molecules in information processing, intracellular chemical reactions would become almost deterministic, resulting in
F=0
.
However, in most cases, a cell can only invest a small number of molecules in their intracellular chemical reactions due to their resource limitations, which inevitably leads to significant memory noise
F>0

[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
While the conventional Bayesian filtering theory takes into account extrinsic noise such as state noise
D
and observation noise
E
, it fails to capture intrinsic noise such as memory noise
F
.

A cell determines state estimator
\hat{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
and memory control
v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
based on observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
, such that
\hat{x}_{t}=\hat{x}(t,y_{t},z_{t})
and
v_{t}=v(t,y_{t},z_{t})
.
These functions are simultaneously optimized as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{*},v^{*}:=\arg\min_{\hat{x},v}J[\hat{x},v].

(29)

We use an objective function
J[\hat{x},v]
defined as follows:

\displaystyle J[\hat{x},v]:=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q(x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t})^{2}+Mv_{t}^{2}\right)dt\right],

(30)

where
Q>0
and
M>0
are weighting parameters.
The long-time limit
T\to\infty
means that this problem focuses on the case where the probability density function of state
x
and memory
z
is stationary, but it should be noted that state
x
and memory
z
themselves are non-stationary.

The first term of the cost function in Eq. (
30
) is a quadratic function of the difference between true state
x_{t}
and estimated state
\hat{x}_{t}
, which represents the state estimation error.
The second term of the cost function in Eq. (
30
) is a quadratic function of memory control
v_{t}
, representing the memory control cost.
As the absolute value of memory control
v_{t}
increases, the relative impact of memory noise decreases in the memory dynamics [Eq. (
28
)], which enhances memory effectiveness and improves state estimation.
On the other hand, a larger absolute value of memory control
v_{t}
corresponds to more intense intracellular chemical reactions, leading to greater energy consumption.
Therefore, the objective function [Eq. (
30
)] expresses the trade-off between estimation errors and energy costs.

IV.2
Optimal Solution

The minimal model of biological information processing is composed of the linear Gaussian state, observation, and memory [Eqs. (
26
)–(
28
)] and the quadratic costs [Eq. (
30
)], which corresponds to a Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) problem
[
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
This setup allows for a semi-analytical calculation of optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
and optimal memory control function
v^{*}
[Eq. (
18
)] as follows (See Appendix
C
and
D
):

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{*}(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right],

(31)

\displaystyle v^{*}(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=-M^{-1}\Pi_{zx}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]-M^{-1}\Pi_{zz}z.

(32)

Optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
is the conditional expectation of state
x
given observation
y
and memory
z
, which reflects the fact that a cell needs to estimate state
x
from observation
y
and memory
z
.
Optimal memory control function
v^{*}
is a linear function of conditional expected state
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
and memory
z
, and there are two control gains:
\Pi_{zx}
and
\Pi_{zz}
.
Control gain
\Pi_{zx}
determines how to encode the information from observation
y
to memory
z
because
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
contains information about
y
.
In fact, if
\Pi_{zx}=0
, observation
y
does not appear in the optimal memory control function [Eq. (
32
)] and the information is not encoded from observation
y
to memory
z
.
In this case, since memory
z
has no information about state
x
, the optimal state estimator function reduces to
\hat{x}^{*}(t,y)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]
, resulting in a memory-less estimation.
Conversely, if
\Pi_{zx}\neq 0
, memory
z
accumulates information about state
x
through observation
y
, and optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
depends not only on observation
y
but also on memory
z
as in Eq. (
31
), resulting in a memory-based estimation.
The other control gain,
\Pi_{zz}
, is a negative feedback control gain for memory
z
, which suppresses the divergence and the stochasticity of the memory.
When the weight of the memory control cost
M
is higher, the absolute value of optimal memory control function
v^{*}
is lower.

In this problem, since
p_{t}(x|y,z)
is a Gaussian distribution,
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
becomes a linear function of observation
y
and memory
z
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]=K_{xy}y+K_{xz}z.

(33)

The estimation gains,
K_{xy}
and
K_{xz}
, and the control gains,
\Pi_{zx}
and
\Pi_{zz}
, are nonlinear functions of
D
,
E
,
F
,
Q
, and
M
, which are obtained from HJB and FP equations (See Appendix
C
and
D
).

IV.3
Stochastic Simulation

We conduct a stochastic simulation to validate the behavior of optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
and memory control function
v^{*}
[Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
)] (Fig.
3
(b-f)).
A cell estimates environmental state
x_{t}
(Fig.

(b)) from noisy observation
y_{t}
(Fig.
3
(c)) and limited memory
z_{t}
(Fig.
3
(d)).
The dynamics of memory
z_{t}
is optimized by optimal memory control
v_{t}^{*}
(Fig.
3
(f)).
Despite the high noise in observation
y_{t}
, optimal state estimator
\hat{x}_{t}^{*}
accurately recovers true state
x_{t}
(Fig.

This result demonstrates that accurate state estimation is achieved with limited memory without relying on Bayesian filtering.

The optimal memory dynamics (Fig.
3
(d)) is similar to the state dynamics (Fig.
3
(b)).
This is because memory
z
has a linear relationship with the conditional expected state
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
, as shown in Eq. (
33
).
This property of the optimal memory dynamics is similar to that of Kalman filter
[
103
,
104
,
91
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
In fact, we have proven that the optimal memory dynamics is equivalent to Kalman filter when the LQG problem has no resource limitations
[
96
]
.
However, when the LQG problem has resource limitations, the optimal memory dynamics deviates from Kalman filter.
In particular, as shown in the next subsection, when the observation noise is lower and the memory noise is higher, the optimal memory control becomes zero and the optimal memory dynamics becomes diffusion dynamics, which is entirely different from Kalman filter.

In this subsection, we examine the parameter dependence of the optimal estimation strategy under resource limitations (Fig.
4
).
The optimal estimation strategy is characterized by the memory control gains,
\Pi_{zx}
and
\Pi_{zz}
, in the optimal memory control function,
v^{*}
(Fig.
4
(c)).
If
\Pi_{xz}=\Pi_{zz}=0
, the past observation information is not encoded into memory
z_{t}
, and state
x_{t}
is estimated only from observation
y_{t}
, which corresponds to an estimation strategy without memory (Fig.
4

Conversely, if
\Pi_{xz},\Pi_{zz}\neq 0
, memory
z_{t}
is regulated based on the past observation information, and state
x_{t}
is estimated not only from observation
y_{t}
but also from memory
z_{t}
, which corresponds to an estimation strategy with memory (Fig.
4
(b)).
In the following, we investigate the parameter dependence of the optimal memory control gains.

We first analyze the dependence of the optimal memory control gains on observation noise
E
(Fig.
4
(d–f)).
The memory control gains,
\Pi_{zx}
and
\Pi_{zz}
, are obtained by solving HJB and FP equations (See Appendix
D
for further details), which corresponds to Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space.
Given that Pontryagin’s minimum principle is equivalent to the method of Lagrange multipliers (See Appendix
A.4
), this method identifies not only optimal solutions but also extremal solutions (Fig.
4
(d,e)(blue, brown, and orange dots)).
The optimal memory control gains are identified by the minimizer of the objective function (Fig.
4
(f)).

When observation noise
E
is low, the solution obtained from Pontryagin’s minimum principle is only
\Pi_{zx}=\Pi_{zz}=0
, which is the optimal solution (Fig.
4
(d–f)(blue dots)).
This result indicates that the estimation strategy without memory is optimal when the current observation is sufficiently reliable.
The current observation contains enough information for the accurate state estimation, making it optimal to save on the memory control cost.
As observation noise
E
increases, the solutions where
\Pi_{zx},\Pi_{zz}\neq 0
discontinuously emerge (Fig.
4
(d–f)(brown and orange dots)), while the solution of
\Pi_{zx}=\Pi_{zz}=0
remains optimal (Fig.
4
(d–f)(blue dots)).
As observation noise
E
further increases, the optimal solution switches from
\Pi_{zx}=\Pi_{zz}=0
(Fig.
4
(d–f)(blue dots)) to
\Pi_{zx},\Pi_{zz}\neq 0
(Fig.
4
(d–f)(orange dots)), demonstrating that the estimation strategy with memory is optimal.
This result suggests that the reduction in the state estimation error through the memory control outweighs the memory control cost when the observation is less reliable.

We next analyze the dependence of the optimal memory control gains on memory noise
F
(Fig.
4
(g–i)).
The discontinuous phase transition is also observed for memory noise
F
, but the direction of the transition is different.
While the estimation strategy with memory is optimal when memory noise
F
is low, that without memory becomes optimal as memory noise
F
increases.
This result indicates that the higher intrinsic noise caused by resource limitations renders memory less useful.

The discontinuous phase transition also occurs for the other parameters, i.e., the intensity of the state noise
D
, the weight of the state estimation error
Q
, and the weight of the memory control cost
M
(See Appendix
D
, Fig.
14
).
The phase transition for state noise
D
and state estimation error
Q
is similar to that for observation noise
E
.
This result suggests that the estimation strategy with memory is optimal when the state estimation is either challenging or significant.
In contrast, the phase transition for memory control cost
M
is similar to that for memory noise
F
, indicating that resource limitations, such as the noise and cost associated with intrinsic information processing, render the estimation strategy without memory optimal.

Our minimal model considers linear Gaussian dynamics and quadratic cost functions, avoiding unnecessary nonlinearities that could be potential sources of complex behaviors.
Nevertheless, our model exhibits a discontinuous phase transition in the optimal estimation strategy.
This phenomenon is not observed in the conventional Bayesian filtering theory, which always uses past observation information for accurate state estimation.
It is consistent with the fact that Bayesian filtering does not account for resource limitations, corresponding to
F\to 0
and
M\to 0
.
Therefore, our analysis suggests that resource limitations may drive the punctuated evolution of biological information processing, leading to qualitatively different behaviors across organisms.

We further investigate the relationships among parameters in the phase transition of the optimal estimation strategy.
In order to quantify the performance improvement induced by the memory, we define the memory effect

\displaystyle\chi:=\frac{J_{\rm w/o\ memory}^{*}-J_{\rm w/\ memory}^{*}}{J_{\rm w/o\ memory}^{*}},

(36)

where
J_{\rm w/\ memory}^{*}
and

in the systems with and without memory, respectively (Fig.

and
J_{\rm w/o\ memory}^{*}\geq 0

Moreover,

always holds because the system with memory becomes equivalent to the system without memory by not controlling the memory.
Consequently,

holds.
A higher memory effect
\chi
indicates a greater performance improvement owing to the memory.
Additionally, memory effect
\chi
is useful for analyzing the phase transition.
The estimation strategy without memory is optimal when
\chi=0
, whereas that with memory is optimal when
\chi>0
.
The boundary between
\chi=0
and
\chi>0
corresponds to the phase boundary between the memory-less and memory-based estimation strategies.

Figure

with respect to observation noise

and memory noise

It is consistent with the fact that memory effect

As memory noise

(Fig.

Therefore, despite the simplicity of our model, it exhibits not only discontinuous but also non-monotonic phase transitions due to resource limitations.

We also investigate memory effect
\chi
with respect to the other parameters, i.e., state noise

In contrast, for memory noise

V
Optimal Estimation and Control Theory with Resource Limitations

Our theory achieves optimal state estimation through optimal memory control rather than Bayesian filtering.
This approach allows us to extend our theory straightforwardly to include optimal state control
[
96
,
97
,
98
]
.
In this section, we introduce the unified theory for optimal estimation and control under resource limitations.

In this theory, the agent not only estimates environmental state
x_{t}
but also controls it.
Thus, the dynamics of environmental state
x_{t}
is given by the following SDE:

\displaystyle dx_{t}=b(t,x_{t},u_{t})dt+\sigma(t,x_{t},u_{t})d\omega_{t},

(37)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows
p_{0}(x_{0})
,
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\omega}}
is a standard Wiener process, and
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is the state control of the agent.
Unlike the state dynamics in Sec.
III
[Eq. (
1
)], Eq. (
37
) depends on state control
u_{t}
, which allows the agent to achieve desired state
x_{t}
through state control
u_{t}
.
The agent determines state control
u_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=u(t,y_{t},z_{t}).

(38)

Observation
y_{t}
, memory
z_{t}
, and memory control
v_{t}
are the same as those in Sec.
III
, given by Eqs. (
2
), (
12
), and (
13
), respectively.
The objective function is given by the following expected cumulative cost function:

\displaystyle J[u,v]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t},z_{t},u_{t},v_{t})dt+g(x_{T},z_{T})\right],

(39)

where
f
and
g
are the running and terminal cost functions, respectively.
Compared with the objective function in Sec.
III
[Eq. (
15
)], state estimator function
\hat{x}
is replaced by state control function
u
.
State control function
u
and memory control function
v
are simultaneously optimized as follows:

\displaystyle u^{*},v^{*}:=\arg\min_{u,v}J[u,v].

(40)

This optimal estimation and control problem can be solved in almost the same way as the optimal estimation problem in Sec.
III
, and the optimal control functions are obtained by minimizing the conditional expected Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y,z),v^{*}(t,y,z)=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{u,v}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,z,u,v,w)\right],

(41)

where
w
and
p
are the solutions of HJB and FP equations, respectively.
The details are shown in Appendix
A
and
B
.
This result demonstrates that our theory provides a unified approach to optimal state estimation and optimal state control.

VI.1
Problem Formulation

We first formulate a target tracking problem as a minimal model of biological information processing and decision-making (Fig.
8
(a)).
In the target tracking problem, an agent estimates the position of a target from a noisy observation and controls its own body to track it.
The target tracking problem is prevalent in many biological phenomena, such as cellular chemotaxis
[
105
,
10
,
106
,
60
]
, insect olfactory search
[
107
,
95
,
108
]
, and animal foraging behavior
[
109
,
110
,
111
,
112
,
113
]
.

The positions of the target and the agent are denoted by
x_{t}^{\rm target}\in\mathbb{R}
and
x_{t}^{\rm agent}\in\mathbb{R}
, respectively.
The position of the target evolves by the following diffusion process:

\displaystyle dx_{t}^{\rm target}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{D}d\omega_{t},

(42)

where initial target state
x_{0}^{\rm target}
follows standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(x_{0}^{\rm target}|0,1)
and
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is a standard Wiener process.

D\geq 0
represents the intensity of the state noise.
The agent controls its own position as follows:

\displaystyle dx_{t}^{\rm agent}

\displaystyle=-u_{t}dt,

(43)

where
x_{0}^{\rm agent}=0
and
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the state control of the agent.
The target tracking problem is concerned only with the difference between target state
x_{t}^{\rm target}
and agent state
x_{t}^{\rm agent}
.
Thus, we define the state by
x_{t}:=x_{t}^{\rm target}-x_{t}^{\rm agent}
, which evolves by the following SDE:

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle=u_{t}dt+\sqrt{D}d\omega_{t},

(44)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(x_{0}|0,1)
.

The agent cannot completely observe state
x_{t}
and instead obtains noisy observation
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
that is generated from the following Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle y_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{t}|x_{t},E),

(45)

where
E\geq 0
represents the intensity of the observation noise.

The memory of the agent,
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, evolves by the following SDE:

\displaystyle dz_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{t}dt+\sqrt{F}d\xi_{t},

(46)

where initial memory
z_{0}
follows standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(z_{0}|0,1)
,
v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the memory control of the agent, and
\xi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is a standard Wiener process.

F\geq 0
represents the intensity of the memory noise.

State control
u_{t}
and memory control
v_{t}
are determined based on observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
as
u_{t}=u(t,y_{t},z_{t})
and
v_{t}=v(t,y_{t},z_{t})
, respectively.

The objective function the agent minimizes is given by

\displaystyle J[u,v]:=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(Qx_{t}^{2}+Ru_{t}^{2}+Mv_{t}^{2}\right)dt\right],

(47)

where
Q>0
,
R>0
, and
M>0
.
The first term of the cost function is the distance between the target and the agent, the second term is the state control cost, and the third term is the memory control cost.

Q
,
R
, and
M
are parameters representing the weights for the state tracking error, the state control cost, and the memory control cost, respectively.

The target tracking problem is to find optimal state control function
u^{*}
and optimal memory control function
v^{*}
that minimize objective function
J[u,v]
:

\displaystyle u^{*},v^{*}:=\arg\min_{u,v}J[u,v],

(48)

which means that the agent minimizes the distance to the target with smaller control costs.

VI.2
Optimal Solution

The target tracking problem also corresponds to the LQG problem
[
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
and optimal control functions
u^{*}
and
v^{*}
[Eq. (
41
)] are semi-analitically calculated as follows (See Appendix
E
):

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=-R^{-1}\Pi_{xx}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]-R^{-1}\Pi_{xz}z,

(49)

\displaystyle v^{*}(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=-M^{-1}\Pi_{zx}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]-M^{-1}\Pi_{zz}z.

(50)

While optimal memory control function
v^{*}
is the same as that in the target estimation problem [Eq. (
32
)], optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}
is replaced by optimal state control function
u^{*}
in the target tracking problem.
State control gains
\Pi_{xx}
and
\Pi_{xz}
represent how to control state
x_{t}
based on conditionally expected state
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
and memory
z_{t}
, respectively.
The higher the weight of the state control cost
R
, the smaller the absolute value of optimal state control
u_{t}^{*}
.

By using optimal state estimator function
\hat{x}^{*}(t,y,z)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
in the target estimation problem [Eq. (
31
)], optimal control functions
u^{*}
and
v^{*}
are rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=-R^{-1}\Pi_{xx}\hat{x}^{*}(t,y,z)-R^{-1}\Pi_{xz}z,

(51)

\displaystyle v^{*}(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=-M^{-1}\Pi_{zx}\hat{x}^{*}(t,y,z)-M^{-1}\Pi_{zz}z.

(52)

These equations clarify that the optimal controls are determined based on the optimal state estimator.

In the target tracking problem,
p_{t}(x|y,z)
is a Gaussian distribution, and
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
is a linear function of observation
y
and memory
z
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]=K_{xy}y+K_{xz}z,

(53)

which is the same as Eq. (
33
) in the target estimation problem.
The estimation gains,
K_{xy}
and
K_{xz}
, and the control gains,
\Pi_{xx}
,
\Pi_{xz}
,
\Pi_{zx}
, and
\Pi_{zz}

D
,
E
,
F
,
Q
,
R
, and
M
, which are obtained from HJB and FP equations.
We note that
\Pi_{xz}=\Pi_{zx}
holds, which means that the control from the memory to the state is identical to that from the state to the memory.
The details are shown in Appendix
E
.

To verify the performance of the optimal control functions, we conduct a stochastic simulation (Fig.
8
(b-f)).
Despite the high observation noise, the agent tracks the target accurately (Fig.
8
(b)).

We examine the parameter dependence of the optimal estimation and control strategy under resource limitations (Fig.
9
).
Similarly to the optimal estimation strategy, the optimal estimation and control strategy is also characterized by the memory control gains,
\Pi_{zx}
and
\Pi_{zz}
.
When
\Pi_{zx}=\Pi_{zz}=0
, the past observation information is not encoded to memory
z_{t}
, and the state estimator is reduced from
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y,z)}\left[x\right]
to
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]
.
Furthermore, with
\Pi_{xz}=\Pi_{zx}=0
, the direct control from memory
z_{t}
to state
x_{t}
also disappears.
Thus, optimal state control
u_{t}^{*}
is determined based solely on current observation
y_{t}
, corresponding to the estimation and control strategy without memory (Fig.

In contrast, when
\Pi_{zx},\Pi_{zz}\neq 0
, optimal state control
u_{t}^{*}
is determined based on both observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
, resulting in the estimation and control strategy with memory (Fig.

As observation noise
E
increases, optimal memory control gains
\Pi_{zx}
and
\Pi_{zz}
undergo a discontinuous shift from zero to nonzero values (Fig.
9

Conversely, as memory noise
F

\Pi_{zx}
and
\Pi_{zz}
discontinuously shift from nonzero values to zero (Fig.
9

The discontinuous phase transition also occurs for the other parameters, i.e., state noise

.
These results suggest that the discontinuous phase transition is an inherent feature of biological information processing and decision-making under resource limitations.

We further investigate the relationship between observation noise
E
and memory noise
F
in the optimal estimation and control strategy (Fig.
10

Unlike the target estimation problem, the target tracking problem does not exhibit the non-monotonic phase transition with respect to observation noise
E

Memory effect

monotonically increases with respect to observation noise
E

E
is extremely high and memory noise
F
is at an intermediate level (Fig.

This difference may be attributed to the fact that in the target tracking problem, the objective function diverges to infinity when the agent fails in estimation and control.
In the target estimation problem, even if the agent neglects the sensing information and fails in estimation, the upper bound of the state estimation error is limited to the finite variance of the environmental state (Fig.
6
(e,h,k)), because it considers the environmental state dynamics that are stable near the origin without state control.
As a result, the agent does not need to control the memory when the observation noise is extremely high, leading to the non-monotonic phase transition with respect to the observation noise.
By contrast, in the target tracking problem, if the agent neglects the sensing information in estimation and control, the agent completely loses track of the target, and the state tracking error diverges to infinity (Fig.
10
(f,j,n)).
Therefore, even when the observation noise is extremely high, the agent cannot afford to give up controlling the memory to avoid the diverging tracking error.

We also explore memory effect
\chi
with respect to state noise
D
, state tracking error

, state control cost
R
, and memory control cost

This may be because, even if state noise
D

y_{t}
as long as observation noise

Moreover, while increasing memory noise
F
and memory control cost
M

\chi
(Fig.

Q
and state control cost

(Fig.

The phase boundaries for memory noise
F
and memory control cost

MF

On the other hand, since the phase boundaries for state tracking error
Q
and state control cost

differ from those for memory noise

and memory control cost

Q/MF
or
R/MF

These results indicate that while the discontinuity in the phase transition is a fundamental characteristic under resource limitations, the non-monotonicity and the scaling relationship are model- and parameter-dependent.

VII
Discussion

In this paper, we have proposed a comprehensive theoretical framework for addressing biological information processing and decision-making under resource limitations.
Instead of relying on Bayesian filtering of posterior probabilities, our theory employs the optimal control of memories that organisms can store and operate, which achieves optimal estimation under resource limitations.
Additionally, since this theory realizes state estimation through memory control, it can be straightforwardly extended to include state control, thereby allowing for an integrated approach towards biological information processing and decision-making.
We applied this theory to minimal models of biological information processing and decision-making and investigated optimal estimation and control strategies under resource limitations.
Our analyses reveal that, despite the simplicity of these models, resource limitations induce discontinuous phase transitions between memory-less and memory-based estimations and controls.
Therefore, resource limitations may contribute to the rich diversity observed in biological information processing and decision-making.

For the discontinuous phase transitions we observed, a key question is whether such phase transitions also occur in real biological systems.
One possible example of this phenomenon can be found in the circadian rhythms of the cyanobacteria,
S. elongatus
and
P. marinus

[
114
,
115
,
116
,
117
]
.
In the circadian rhythms of cyanobacteria, it is crucial to estimate the time of day by processing sunlight information through intracellular chemical reactions.
The circadian rhythm of
S. elongatus
exhibits a self-sustained oscillation due to a protein called KaiA, which functions as a negative feedback controller of intracellular chemical reactions
[
118
,
119
,
120
,
121
,
122
]
.
In contrast,
P. marinus
has lost the KaiA protein through evolutionary processes
[
123
]
, and the intracellular chemical reaction exhibits a damped oscillation rather than a self-sustained one
[
124
,
125
,
126
,
127
,
128
]
.
We hypothesize that the KaiA protein regulating the circadian rhythms of cyanobacteria corresponds to the memory control in our theoretical framework.
In this context, the self-sustained oscillation of
S. elongatus
may represent a complex information processing with memory control, while the damped oscillation of
P. marinus
may correspond to a simple information processing without memory control.
In fact,
P. marinus
has a much smaller cell volume than
S. elongatus
, leading to a higher level of intrinsic stochasticity due to the limited number of molecules available for information processing
[
116
]
.
Additionally,
S. elongatus
inhabits a wider range of environments than
P. marinus
, which likely requires more accurate estimations
[
129
]
.
These experimental observations suggest a potential alignment with our theoretical predictions.

Another example can be found in the dual-process theory of human information processing and decision-making
[
130
,
131
,
132
,
133
]
.
This theory posits that human cognition operates through two distinct systems: System 1, which is unconscious, implicit, and intuitive, and System 2, which is conscious, explicit, and logical.
Recent experiments have reported that these systems switch based on available energy resources, such as the level of glucose in the bloodstream
[
134
,
135
,
136
]
.
When the glucose level is low, System 1 dominates, whereas System 2 becomes more active as the glucose level increases.
This transition is similar to the findings in our study.
System 1 represents simple information processing and decision-making, prioritizing energy cost reduction, whereas System 2 involves more complex operations, enhancing performance even at the expense of higher energy costs.
Therefore, the dual-process theory could serve as a good example of how resource limitations can lead to phase transitions in the complexity and capacity of information processing and decision-making.

Investigation of these phenomena requires a more complex and involved setup than those in this study. Our theory is general enough to include such high-dimensional and nonlinear cases, yet we restricted our analysis in this study to the one-dimensional and linear minimal models partly for efficient computation of phase diagrams.
To work on more complex cases, efficient numerical algorithms for solving high-dimensional HJB-FP equations should be developed.
This is a challenging task.
We may employ some techniques
[
137
,
138
,
139
]
and neural network-based algorithms
[
140
,
141
,
142
,
143
]
, which have been developed in the context of mean-field games and control
[
99
,
100
,
101
,
102
]
to resolve this problem.

If being equipped with these algorithms, our theory could address more realistic and complex biological situations where environmental states are higher-dimensional than biological memories
[
144
,
145
,
146
,
147
]
.
The conventional Bayesian filtering theory struggles to address such situations because the dimensions of biological memories are determined based on those of environmental states.
Our theory addresses this issue by allowing the dimensions of biological memories to be determined independently of those of environmental states from which valuable insights would be gained on how organisms compress high-dimensional environmental information into low-dimensional biological memories.
While the dimensionality reduction in machine learning is one approach to this problem
[
148
,
149
,
150
]
, the potential strength of our theory lies in the explicit consideration of the dynamics of biological memories.
This would allow us to potentially reveal phenomena where diverse environmental information is encoded into varied dynamical patterns of biological memories, including sustained, transient, and oscillatory dynamics.
Similar phenomena have recently been observed in cellular information processing, where different ligand information is encoded into the diverse dynamical concentration patterns of the same downstream molecule
[
144
,
145
,
146
,
147
]
.
Therefore, our theory could also serve as a foundation for dynamical information encoding in biological systems.

Apart from future problems in numerical computation, one important theoretical challenge is to extend our theory from a single-agent system to a multi-agent system with communication.
In nature, organisms often form groups, allowing for more complex and sophisticated information processing and decision-making via communication
[
151
,
152
,
153
,
154
,
155
,
156
,
157
,
158
]
.
We have achieved this extension in our previous work where we proposed a very similar yet biologically less useful theoretical framework
[
98
]
.
Therefore, our theory has the potential to provide an effective approach for investigating collective information processing and decision-making and associated phase transitions.

B

It should be noted that OSC and OMSC build upon our previously proposed theoretical framework, memory-limited partially observable stochastic control (ML-POSC)
[
96
,
97
,
98
]
.
ML-POSC is an optimal estimation and control theory with resource limitations.
While OMSC is similar to ML-POSC, the problem formulation is modified, allowing for more biologically plausible setups.
A comparison between OMSC and ML-POSC is provided in Appendix
F
.

In Appendix
A.1
, we formulate OSC.
In Appendix
A.2

In Appendix
A.3
, we derive the optimal solution of OSC using Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space.
In Appendix
A.4
, we show that Pontryagin’s minimum principle corresponds to the method of Lagrange multipliers.

A.1
Problem Formulation

In this subsection, we formulate the optimal estimation and control problem without memory, i.e., observation-based stochastic control (OSC) (Fig.
12
(a)).
OSC considers an environment and an agent.
The state of the environment at time
t\in[0,T]
is denoted by
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, which evolves by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\displaystyle dx_{t}=b(t,x_{t},u_{t})dt+\sigma(t,x_{t},u_{t})d\omega_{t},

(54)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows
p_{0}(x_{0})
,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is the control of the agent, and
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\omega}}
is a standard Wiener process.
The agent cannot completely observe environmental state
x_{t}
and instead obtains noisy observation
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, which is generated from probability density function
p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t})
as follows:

\displaystyle y_{t}\sim p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t}).

(55)

The agent determines control
u_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
:

\displaystyle u_{t}=u(t,y_{t}).

(56)

The objective function is given by the following expected cumulative cost function:

\displaystyle J[u]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t},u_{t})dt+g(x_{T})\right],

(57)

where
f
and
g
are the running and terminal cost functions, respectively.
OSC considers a problem to find optimal control function
u^{*}
that minimizes objective function
J[u]
:

\displaystyle u^{*}:=\arg\min_{u}J[u].

(58)

We refer to this stochastic optimal control problem as observation-based stochastic control (OSC) because the agent determines control
u_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
[Eq. (
56
)].
If observation
y_{t}
is identical to state
x_{t}
, i.e.,
y_{t}=x_{t}
, this problem reduces to the most basic stochastic control, where the agent can completely observe state
x_{t}
and determines control
u_{t}
based on it as
u_{t}=u(t,x_{t})

[
159
,
160
,
161
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
In contrast, in OSC, the agent cannot completely observe state
x_{t}
and needs to estimate it from observation
y_{t}
.
Additionally, the agent is not allowed to use information from past observation history
y_{t-dt},\cdots,y_{0}
to estimate and control state
x_{t}
.
Therefore, OSC is an optimal estimation and control problem without memory.

A.2
Optimal Solution

In the conventional stochastic control, where the agent can completely observe state
x_{t}
, the optimal control function is obtained using Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the state space
[
159
,
160
,
161
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
However, this method is not applicable to OSC because the agent cannot completely observe state
x_{t}
.
To address this issue, we propose Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space, which has recently been used in mean-field stochastic optimal control problems
[
99
,
100
,
101
,
102
]
and in our previous works
[
96
,
97
,
98
]
.
In this subsection, we show the main result.
The details of the derivation are shown in the following subsection, Appendix
A.3
.

The optimal control function of OSC is given by minimizing the conditional expected Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y)=\arg\min_{u}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)\right].

(59)

Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w):=f(t,x,u)+\mathcal{L}_{u}w(t,x),

(60)

where
f(t,x,u)
is the current cost and
\mathcal{L}_{u}w(t,x)
represents the future cost.

\mathcal{L}_{u}
is the time-backward diffusion operator for state
x
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{u}w(t,x):=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}b_{i}(t,x,u)\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{x}}D_{ij}(t,x,u)\frac{\partial^{2}w(t,x)}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}},

(61)

where
D(t,x,u):=\sigma(t,x,u)\sigma^{\top}(t,x,u)
.

w(t,x)
is the value function, which represents the expected cumulative cost from the starting point at time
t
and state
x
to the terminal time
T
.
Value function
w(t,x)
is obtained by solving the following time-backward partial differential equation, Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,u^{*},w)\right],

(62)

where the terminal condition is given by
w(T,x)=g(x)
.
In the optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
59
)], conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\cdot\right]
appears, which is consistent with the fact that the agent needs to estimate state
x
from observation
y
.
Conditional probability density function
p_{t}(x|y)
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle p_{t}(x|y)=\frac{p_{t}(y|x)p_{t}(x)}{\int p_{t}(y|x)p_{t}(x)dx}.

(63)

Probability density function
p_{t}(x):=p(t,x)
is obtained by solving the following time-forward partial differential equation, Fokker-Planck (FP) equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial t}=\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u^{*}}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x),

(64)

where the initial condition is given by
p(0,x)=p_{0}(x)
.

\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}
is the expected time-forward diffusion operator for state
x
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x)

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\frac{\partial\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[b_{i}(t,x,u(t,y))\right]p(t,x)\right)}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{x}}\frac{\partial^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[D_{ij}(t,x,u(t,y))\right]p(t,x)\right)}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}.

(65)

The optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
59
)] is obtained by solving the time-backward HJB equation [Eq. (
62
)] and the time-forward FP equation [Eq. (
64
)].
Since HJB and FP equations are mutually dependent through the optimal control function, they must be solved simultaneously, which corresponds to a two-point boundary value problem of partial differential equations.
This problem can be solved using the forward-backward sweep method, which computes the time-backward HJB equation and the time-forward FP equation alternately
[
97
]
.
While the convergence of the forward-backward sweep method is not guaranteed in deterministic optimal control problems
[
162
,
163
,
164
,
165
]
and mean-field stochastic optimal control problems
[
166
,
167
,
168
,
139
]
, it is guaranteed in OSC because the coupling of HJB and FP equations is limited to the optimal control function.
This proof is almost the same as our previous work
[
97
]
.

We note that Pontryagin’s minimum principle is a necessary condition for the optimal control function, but not a sufficient condition
[
97
]
.
This means that while the optimal control function always satisfies Eq. (
59
), a control function that satisfies Eq. (
59
) is not necessarily the optimal control function.
This is evident from the fact that Pontryagin’s minimum principle corresponds to the method of Lagrange multipliers, as shown in Appendix
A.4
.
In fact, our minimal models yield not only optimal solutions but also non-optimal solutions based on Pontryagin’s minimum principle (See Fig.
4
(d–f)(blue, brown, and orange dots) as an example).

If observation
y_{t}
is identical to state
x_{t}
, i.e.,
y_{t}=x_{t}
, OSC reduces to the conventional stochastic control, and Eq. (
59
) becomes

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,x)=\arg\min_{u}\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w).

(66)

In this case, the optimal control function is obtained by solving only the time-backward HJB equation [Eq. (
62
)], which is consistent with Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the state space
[
159
,
160
,
161
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
Therefore, Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space can be interpreted as a generalization of Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the state space to cases of incomplete observation
[
96
,
97
,
98
]
.

A.3
Derivation

In this subsection, we derive the optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
59
)] using Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space.
In this paper, we derive this result in three steps, following our previous works
[
96
,
98
]
.
First, we convert OSC on the state space into the deterministic control on the probability density function space (Appendix
A.3.1
).
Second, we apply Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space, not on the state space (Appendix
A.3.2
).
Finally, we convert Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space into Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the same space (Appendix
A.3.3
).
It is worth noting that Pontryagin’s minimum principle is directly applicable without going through Bellman’s dynamic programming principle.
We omit this proof because it is nearly identical to our previous work
[
97
]
.

A.3.1
Conversion to deterministic control on probability density function space

We first convert OSC on the state space into the deterministic control on the probability density function space.
The state SDE [Eq. (
54
)] is converted into the following FP equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial t}=\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x),

(67)

where
p(0,x)=p_{0}(x)
and
\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}
is the expected time-forward diffusion operator [Eq. (
65
)].
The objective function of OSC [Eq. (
57
)] is also calculated as follows:

\displaystyle J[u]=\int_{0}^{T}\bar{f}(t,p_{t},u_{t})dt+\bar{g}(p_{T}),

(68)

where
p_{t}(x):=p(t,x)
,
\bar{f}(t,p,u):=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}[f(t,x,u(t,y))]]
, and
\bar{g}(p):=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[g(x)]
.
From Eqs. (
67
) and (
68
), OSC on the state space is converted into the deterministic control on the probability density function space.
While OSC on the state space controls state SDE [Eq. (
54
)], the deterministic control on the probability density function space controls FP equation [Eq. (
67
)].

A.3.2
Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on probability density function space

While OSC cannot completely observe state
x_{t}
, it can obtain complete information about probability density function
p_{t}
because probability density function
p_{t}
is deterministic.
As a result, OSC can be solved by Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space, not on the state space.
In the following, we show Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space.

The minimization of the objective function is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{u}J[u]

\displaystyle=\min_{u_{0:T-dt}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\bar{f}(\tau,p_{\tau},u_{\tau})d\tau+\bar{g}(p_{T})\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{u_{0:t-dt}}\left[\int_{0}^{t-dt}\bar{f}(\tau,p_{\tau},u_{\tau})d\tau+\min_{u_{t:T-dt}}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\bar{f}(t,p_{\tau},u_{\tau})d\tau+\bar{g}(p_{T})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{u_{0:t-dt}}\left[\int_{0}^{t-dt}\bar{f}(\tau,p_{\tau},u_{\tau})d\tau+V(t,p_{t})\right],

(69)

where
V(t,p)
is the value function, which is defined as follows:

\displaystyle V(t,p):=\min_{u_{t:T-dt}}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\bar{f}(t,p_{\tau},u_{\tau})d\tau+\bar{g}(p_{T})\right],

(70)

where
\{p_{\tau}|\tau\in[t,T]\}
is the solution of FP equation [Eq. (
67
)] with
p_{t}=p
.
The optimal control function is obtained by calculating the value function.

The value function is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle V(t,p)

\displaystyle=\min_{u_{t:T}}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\bar{f}(\tau,p_{\tau},u_{\tau})d\tau+\bar{g}(p_{T})\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\left[\bar{f}(t,p,u)dt+\min_{u_{t+dt:T}}\left[\int_{t+dt}^{T}\bar{f}(\tau,p_{\tau},u_{\tau})d\tau+\bar{g}(p_{T})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\left[\bar{f}(t,p,u)dt+V(t+dt,p+\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}pdt)\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\left[\bar{f}(t,p,u)dt+V(t,p)+\frac{\partial V(t,p)}{\partial t}dt+\left(\int\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(x)dx\right)dt\right].

(71)

By rearranging the equation, the following equation is obtained:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial V(t,p)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\left[\bar{f}(t,p,u)+\int\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(x)dx\right],

(72)

where
V(T,p)=\bar{g}(p)
.
We define expected time-backward diffusion operator
\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}
as follows:

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}w(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[b_{i}(t,x,u(t,y))\right]\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{x}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[D_{ij}(t,x,u(t,y))\right]\frac{\partial^{2}w(t,x)}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}.

(73)

Since

\displaystyle\int\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(x)dx=\int p(x)\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)dx,

(74)

the following equation is obtained:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial V(t,p)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\left[\bar{f}(t,p,u)+\int p(x)\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)dx\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)p(x)}\left[f(t,x,u)+\mathcal{L}_{u}\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right],

(75)

where
\mathcal{L}_{u}
is the time-backward diffusion operator [Eq. (
61
)].
From the definition of the Hamiltonian [Eq. (
60
)], the following equation is obtained:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial V(t,p)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)p(x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u,\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right)\right],

(76)

where
V(T,p)=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[g(x)]
.
This time-backward functional differential equation is called Bellman equation.

Optimal control function
u^{*}
is given by the right-hand side of Bellman equation [Eq. (
76
)].
Since control
u
is determined based on observation
y
in OSC, the minimization by control
u
can be exchanged with the expectation by observation
y
as follows:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial V(t,p)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y)}\left[\min_{u}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u,\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right)\right]\right],

(77)

where

\displaystyle p_{t}(y)

\displaystyle:=\int p_{t}(y|x)p(x)dx,

(78)

\displaystyle p_{t}(x|y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{p_{t}(y|x)p(x)}{\int p_{t}(y|x)p(x)dx}.

(79)

As a result, optimal control function
u^{*}
is given by minimizing the conditional expected Hamiltonian as follows:

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y,p)

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{u}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u,\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right)\right],

(80)

where
V(t,p)
is the solution of the following Bellman equation:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial V(t,p)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)p(x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right)\right],

(81)

where
V(T,p)=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[g(x)]
.
Therefore, from Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space, the optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
80
)] is obtained by solving Bellman equation [Eq. (
81
)] backward in time from terminal condition
V(T,p)=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[g(x)]
.

In Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space, the optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
80
)] depends on probability density function
p
as
u^{*}(t,y,p)
, while it is defined by
u^{*}(t,y)
.
In OSC, probability density function
p
is given by the solution of FP equation [Eq. (
67
)], which is denoted by
p_{t}
.
Therefore, in Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space, we first compute Bellman equation [Eq. (
81
)] backward in time from terminal condition
V(T,p)=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[g(x)]
and obtain optimal control function
u^{*}(t,y,p)
.
We then compute FP equation [Eq. (
67
)] forward in time from initial condition
p(0,x)=p_{0}(x)
and obtain optimal control function
u^{*}(t,y):=u^{*}(t,y,p_{t})
.
The agent determines optimal control
u_{t}^{*}
based on real-time observation
y_{t}
as
u_{t}^{*}:=u^{*}(t,y_{t})
.

A.3.3
Conversion to Pontryagin’s minimum principle on probability density function space

Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space is less practical because Bellman equation [Eq. (
81
)] is a functional differential equation, not a partial differential equation.
We circumvent this problem by converting Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space to Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the same space.
This conversion has also been used in mean-field stochastic optimal control problems
[
99
,
100
,
101
,
102
]
and in our previous works
[
96
,
98
]
.

We first define

\displaystyle W(t,p,x):=\frac{\delta V(t,p)}{\delta p}(x),

(82)

which satisfies
W(T,p,x)=g(x)
.
Differentiating Bellman equation [Eq. (
81
)] with respect to probability density function
p
, the following equation is obtained:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial W(t,p,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},W\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x^{\prime})p(x^{\prime})}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}\left(t,x^{\prime},u^{*},W\right)}{\partial u}\frac{\delta u^{*}(t,y,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x^{\prime})p(x^{\prime})}\left[\mathcal{L}_{u^{*}}\frac{\delta W(t,p,x^{\prime})}{\delta p}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},W\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y)p_{t}(x^{\prime}|y)}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}\left(t,x^{\prime},u^{*},W\right)}{\partial u}\frac{\delta u^{*}(t,y,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p(x^{\prime})}\left[\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u^{*}}\frac{\delta W(t,p,x^{\prime})}{\delta p}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},W\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y)}\left[\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x^{\prime}|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x^{\prime},u^{*},W\right)\right]}{\partial u}\frac{\delta u^{*}(t,y,p)}{\delta p}(x)\right]+\int\frac{\delta W(t,p,x)}{\delta p}(x^{\prime})\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u^{*}}^{{\dagger}}p(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

(83)

From the optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
80
)], the following stationary condition is satisfied:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x^{\prime}|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x^{\prime},u^{*},W\right)\right]}{\partial u}=0.

(84)

Therefore, the following equation is obtained:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial W(t,p,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},W\right)\right]+\int\frac{\delta W(t,p,x)}{\delta p}(x^{\prime})\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u^{*}}^{{\dagger}}p(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

(85)

We then define

\displaystyle w(t,x):=W(t,p_{t},x),

(86)

where
p_{t}
is the solution of FP equation [Eq. (
67
)].
The time derivative of
w(t,x)
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial W(t,p_{t},x)}{\partial t}+\int\frac{\delta W(t,p_{t},x)}{\delta p}(x^{\prime})\frac{\partial p_{t}(x^{\prime})}{\partial t}dx^{\prime}.

(87)

By substituting Eq. (
85
) into Eq. (
87
), the following equation is obtained:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},w\right)\right]-\int\frac{\delta W(t,p_{t},x)}{\delta p}(x^{\prime})\underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial p_{t}(x^{\prime})}{\partial t}-\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u^{*}}^{{\dagger}}p_{t}(x^{\prime})\right)}_{(*)}dx^{\prime}.

(88)

From FP equation [Eq. (
67
)],
(*)=0
holds and Bellman equation [Eq. (
81
)] is reduced to the following time-backward partial differential equation, HJB equation:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},w\right)\right],

(89)

where
w(T,x)=g(x)
.
The optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
80
)] is given as follows:

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y)

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{u}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u,w\right)\right],

(90)

where
p_{t}(x|y):=p_{t}(y|x)p_{t}(x)/\int p_{t}(y|x)p_{t}(x)dx
and
p_{t}(x):=p(t,x)
is the solution of FP equation [Eq. (
67
)].
Therefore, the optimal control function of OSC [Eq. (
90
)] is obtained by solving the time-backward HJB equation [Eq. (
89
)] and the time-forward FP equation [Eq. (
67
)].
This optimality condition corresponds to Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space [Eq. (
59
)].

In Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the state space, the time-forward state equation and the time-backward adjoint equation can be expressed by the derivatives of the Hamiltonian
[
169
,
170
,
171
]
.
Similarly, in Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space, the time-forward FP equation [Eq. (
67
)] and the time-backward HJB equation [Eq. (
89
)] can be expressed by the variations of expected Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{H}}(t,p,u,w)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)\right]\right]

(91)

as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(t,p,u^{*},w)}{\delta w}(x),

(92)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\bar{\mathcal{H}}(t,p,u^{*},w)}{\delta p}(x),

(93)

where
p(0,x)=p_{0}(x)
and
w(T,x)=g(x)

[
97
]
.

While Bellman’s dynamic programming principle on the probability density function space is a terminal value problem of a functional differential equation, Bellman equation [Eq. (
81
)], Pontryagin’s minimum principle on that space is a two-point boundary value problem of partial differential equations, HJB and FP equations [Eqs. (
92
) and (
93
)], which simplifies computations.
We note that while Bellman’s dynamic programming principle is a necessary and sufficient condition for the optimal control function, Pontryagin’s minimum principle is a necessary condition, not a sufficient condition
[
97
]
.
Pontryagin’s minimum principle becomes a necessary and sufficient condition when expected Hamiltonian
\bar{\mathcal{H}}(t,p,u,w)
is convex with respect to
p
and
u
, which is shown in our previous work
[
97
]
.

A.4
Relationship with the Method of Lagrange Multipliers

In this subsection, we show that Pontryagin’s minimum principle corresponds to the method of Lagrange multipliers for readers who may be unfamiliar with Pontryagin’s minimum principle.
The method of Lagrange multipliers is a widely used technique for solving constrained optimization problems.
The deterministic control on the probability density function space can be interpreted as the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}\ \ \

\displaystyle J[u,p]=\int_{0}^{T}\bar{f}(t,p_{t},u_{t})dt+\bar{g}(p_{T}),

(94)

\displaystyle{\rm subject\ to}\ \ \

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial t}=\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x),\quad p(0,x)=p_{0}(x).

(95)

As a result, the method of Lagrange multipliers is an effective approach to solving this problem.

In the method of Lagrange multipliers, we consider the following Lagrange function:

\displaystyle\tilde{J}:=\int_{0}^{T}\bar{f}(t,p_{t},u_{t})dt+\bar{g}(p_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}\int w(t,x)\left(\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x)-\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial t}\right)dxdt,

(96)

where
w(t,x)
is a Lagrange multiplier.
Lagrange function
\tilde{J}
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{J}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\bar{f}(t,p_{t},u_{t})dt+\bar{g}(p_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}\int p(t,x)\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}w(t,x)dxdt

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\int\left(w(T,x)p(T,x)-w(0,x)p(0,x)\right)dx+\int_{0}^{T}\int p(t,x)\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int\int p(t,x)p_{t}(y|x)\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)dxdydt+\int p(T,x)g(x)dx

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\int\left(p(T,x)w(T,x)-p(0,x)w(0,x)\right)dx+\int_{0}^{T}\int p(t,x)\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}dxdt.

(97)

We variate Lagrange function
\tilde{J}
with respect to
u
and
p
as follows:

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{J}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int\int p_{t}(y|x)\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)\delta p(t,x)dxdydt+\int_{0}^{T}\int\int p(t,x)p_{t}(y|x)\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)}{\partial u}\delta u(t,y)dxdydt

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\int g(x)\delta p(T,x)dx-\int w(T,x)\delta p(T,x)dx+\int_{0}^{T}\int\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}\delta p(t,x)dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)\right]+\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}\right)\delta p(t,x)dxdt+\int\left(g(x)-w(T,x)\right)\delta p(T,x)dx

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\int_{0}^{T}\int p(t,y)\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)}{\partial u}\right]\delta u(t,y)dydt.

(98)

From stationary condition
\delta\tilde{J}=0
, we obtain the following optimality condition:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)}{\partial u}\right]=0,

(99)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)\right],\quad w(T,x)=g(x),

(100)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial t}=\check{\mathcal{L}}_{u}^{{\dagger}}p(t,x),\quad p(0,x)=p_{0}(x).

(101)

Equations (
59
), (
62
), and (
64
) correspond to Eqs. (
99
), (
100
), and (
101
), respectively.
Therefore, Pontryagin’s minimum principle corresponds to the method of Lagrange multipliers.

B.1
Problem Formulation

In this subsection, we formulate the optimal estimation and control problem with memory, observation-and-memory-based stochastic control (OMSC) (Fig.
12
(b)).
OMSC considers the same state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and observation
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
as OSC, which are given by

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle=b(t,x_{t},u_{t})dt+\sigma(t,x_{t},u_{t})d\omega_{t},

(102)

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle\sim p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t}),

(103)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows
p_{0}(x_{0})
,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is the state control of the agent, and
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\omega}}
is a standard Wiener process.
In contrast, OMSC formulates the memory of the agent,
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
, which evolves by the following SDE:

\displaystyle dz_{t}=c(t,z_{t},v_{t})dt+\eta(t,z_{t},v_{t})d\xi_{t},

(104)

where initial memory
z_{0}
follows
p_{0}(z_{0})
,
v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
is the memory control of the agent, and
\xi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
is a standard Wiener process.
OMSC determines state control
u_{t}
and memory control
v_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle u_{t}=u(t,y_{t},z_{t}),

(105)

\displaystyle v_{t}=v(t,y_{t},z_{t}).

(106)

The objective function is given by the following expected cumulative cost function:

\displaystyle J[u]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t},z_{t},u_{t},v_{t})dt+g(x_{T},z_{T})\right],

(107)

where
f
and
g
are the running and terminal cost functions, respectively.
OMSC considers a problem to find optimal state control function
u^{*}
and optimal memory control function
v^{*}
that minimize objective function
J[u]
:

\displaystyle u^{*}:=\arg\min_{u}J[u].

(108)

OMSC is identical to the optimal estimation and control problem under resource limitations presented in Sec.
V
.
Additionally, the optimal estimation problem under resource limitations presented in Sec.
III
is a special case of OMSC, where
b(t,x_{t},u_{t})=b(t,x_{t})
and
\sigma(t,x_{t},u_{t})=\sigma(t,x_{t})
, and state control
u_{t}=u(t,y_{t},z_{t})
is replaced by state estimator
\hat{x}_{t}=\hat{x}(t,y_{t},z_{t})
.

B.2
Conversion to Observation-based Stochastic Control on Extended Space

Figure 13:

Schematic diagram of the conversion from observation-and-memory-based stochastic control (OMSC) to observation-based stochastic control (OSC) on the extended space.

In this subsection, we show that OMSC can be converted into OSC on an extended space (Fig.
13
) as a preparatory step to obtaining the optimal solution of OMSC.
We define extended state
\tilde{x}_{t}
, extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
, and extended control
\tilde{u}_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{t}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{t}\\
z_{t}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\tilde{y}_{t}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}y_{t}\\
z_{t}^{\prime}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\tilde{u}_{t}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{t}\\
v_{t}\\
\end{array}\right).

We denote the memory in extended state
\tilde{x}_{t}
by
z_{t}
and that in extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
by
z_{t}^{\prime}
to distinguish between them.
In practice,
z_{t}=z_{t}^{\prime}
holds.
Extended state
\tilde{x}_{t}
evolves by the following SDE:

\displaystyle d\tilde{x}_{t}=\tilde{b}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})dt+\tilde{\sigma}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})d\tilde{\omega}_{t},

(115)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{b}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b(t,x_{t},u_{t})\\
c(t,z_{t},v_{t})\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\tilde{\sigma}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma(t,x_{t},u_{t})&O\\
O&\eta(t,z_{t},v_{t})\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\tilde{\omega}_{t}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\omega_{t}\\
\xi_{t}\\
\end{array}\right).

Extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
is generated from the following probability density function:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{y}_{t}|\tilde{x}_{t}):=p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t})\delta(z_{t}^{\prime}-z_{t}).

(122)

Extended control
\tilde{u}_{t}
is determined based on extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}=\tilde{u}(t,\tilde{y}_{t}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u(t,y_{t},z_{t})\\
v(t,y_{t},z_{t})\\
\end{array}\right).

The objective function is given by the following expected cumulative cost function:

\displaystyle J[\tilde{u}]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{f}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})dt+\tilde{g}(\tilde{x}_{T})\right],

(125)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t}):=f(t,x_{t},z_{t},u_{t},v_{t}),\quad\tilde{g}(\tilde{x}_{T}):=g(x_{T},z_{T}).

(126)

Therefore, OMSC can be interpreted as OSC on the extended space.

B.3
Optimal Solution

The optimal solution of OMSC is can be obtained in a similar way to that of OSC because OMSC can be interpreted as OSC on the extended space.
In the following, we present the optimal solution of OMSC, which corresponds to the optimal solutions presented in Sec.
III
[Eq. (
18
)] and
V
[Eq. (
41
)].
We note that the following result is identical to the optimal solution of OSC except for
\tilde{\cdot}
.

The optimal extended control function of OMSC is given by minimizing the conditional expectation of the extended Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{*}(t,\tilde{y})=\arg\min_{\tilde{u}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x}|\tilde{y})}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u},\tilde{w})\right].

(127)

Extended Hamiltonian
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u},\tilde{w}):=\tilde{f}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u})+\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{u}}\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x}),

(128)

where
\tilde{f}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u})
is the current cost and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{u}}\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x})
represents the future cost.

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{u}}
is the time-backward diffusion operator for extended state
\tilde{x}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{u}}\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\tilde{x}}}\tilde{b}_{i}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\frac{\partial\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x})}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\tilde{x}}}\tilde{D}_{ij}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x})}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}\partial\tilde{x}_{j}},

(129)

where
\tilde{D}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u}):=\tilde{\sigma}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\tilde{\sigma}^{\top}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u})
.

\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x})
is the value function, which represents the expected cumulative cost from the starting point at time
t
and extended state
\tilde{x}
to the terminal time
T
.
Value function
\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x})
is obtained by solving the following time-backward partial differential equation, HJB equation:

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\tilde{w}(t,\tilde{x})}{\partial t}=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{y}|\tilde{x})}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u}^{*},\tilde{w})\right],

(130)

where the terminal condition is given by
\tilde{w}(T,\tilde{x})=\tilde{g}(\tilde{x})
.
In the optimal extended control function of OMSC [Eq. (
127
)], conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x}|\tilde{y})}\left[\cdot\right]
appears.
Conditional probability density function
\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x}|\tilde{y})
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x}|\tilde{y})=\frac{\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{y}|\tilde{x})\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})}{\int\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{y}|\tilde{x})\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})d\tilde{x}}.

(131)

Probability density function
\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x}):=\tilde{p}(t,\tilde{x})
is obtained by solving the following time-forward partial differential equation, FP equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{p}(t,\tilde{x})}{\partial t}=\check{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}_{\tilde{u}^{*}}^{{\dagger}}\tilde{p}(t,\tilde{x}),

(132)

where the initial condition is given by
\tilde{p}(0,\tilde{x})=\tilde{p}_{0}(\tilde{x})
.

\check{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}_{\tilde{u}}^{{\dagger}}
is the expected time-forward diffusion operator for extended state
\tilde{x}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\check{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}_{\tilde{u}}^{{\dagger}}\tilde{p}(t,\tilde{x})

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\tilde{x}}}\frac{\partial\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{y}|\tilde{x})}\left[\tilde{b}_{i}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u}(t,\tilde{y}))\right]\tilde{p}(t,\tilde{x})\right)}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\tilde{x}}}\frac{\partial^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{y}|\tilde{x})}\left[\tilde{D}_{ij}(t,\tilde{x},\tilde{u}(t,\tilde{y}))\right]\tilde{p}(t,\tilde{x})\right)}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}\partial\tilde{x}_{j}}.

(133)

Therefore, the optimal extended control function of OMSC [Eq. (
127
)] is obtained by solving HJB equation [Eq. (
130
)] and FP equation [Eq. (
132
)].

Appendix C
Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian Problem

In this section, we apply OSC and OMSC to the Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) problem, where the dynamics are linear Gaussian and the costs are quadratic.
The LQG problem is a well-known problem in stochastic optimal control theory, which allows for semi-analytical calculations
[
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
We demonstrate that this advantageous property also holds in OSC and OMSC.

The minimal models discussed in Sec.
IV
and
VI
correspond to the infinite-horizon LQG problem.
The relationship between these models and the infinite-horizon LQG problem is discussed in Appendix
D
and
E
.

This section is organized as follows:
In Appendix
C.1
, we formulate the LQG problem.
In Appendix
C.2
, we present the optimal solution.
In Appendix
C.3
, we provide the derivation of the optimal solution.
In Appendix
C.4
, we extend the LQG problem from a finite-horizon setting to an infinite-horizon setting.
We mainly discuss the case of OSC for the notational simplicity, but the same discussion is possible for the case of OMSC by adding
\tilde{\cdot}
, which represents extended variables and parameters.

C.1
Problem Formulation

In this subsection, we formulate the LQG problem in OSC.
In the LQG problem, the state of the environment,
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, evolves by the following linear Gaussian dynamics:

\displaystyle dx_{t}=\left(Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}\right)dt+\sigma d\omega_{t},

(134)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(x_{0}|\mu_{0},\Sigma_{0})
,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is the state control of the agent, and
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\omega}}
is a standard Wiener process.
The agent receives observation
y_{t}
generated from the following Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle y_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{t}|Hx_{t},E).

(135)

The agent determines control
u_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
as
u_{t}=u(t,y_{t})
.
The objective function is defined as the following quadratic function:

\displaystyle J[u]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}-2x_{t}^{\top}Su_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}\right)dt+x_{T}^{\top}Px_{T}\right],

where
Q\succeq O
,
R\succ O
, and
P\succeq O
.
The LQG problem is to find optimal control function
u^{*}
that minimizes objective function
J[u]
as follows:

\displaystyle u^{*}:=\arg\min_{u}J[u].

(136)

We note that the LQG problem in OMSC is formulated by adding
\tilde{\cdot}
.

C.2
Optimal Solution

In this subsection, we present the optimal solution in the LQG problem of OSC.
The derivation of the optimal solution is provided in the following subsection, Appendix
C.3
.

The optimal control function in the LQG problem of OSC is given by

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y)

\displaystyle=-R^{-1}\left(\left(\Psi B-S\right)^{\top}\mu+\left(\Pi B-S\right)^{\top}K\left(y-H\mu\right)\right),

(137)

where
K:=\Sigma H^{\top}(E+H\Sigma H^{\top})^{-1}
.

\mu
and
\Sigma
are the mean vector and the covariance matrix of state
x
, respectively, which are the solutions of the following time-forward ordinary differential equations:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left(A-BR^{-1}\left(\Psi B-S\right)^{\top}\right)\mu,

(138)

\displaystyle\frac{d\Sigma}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sigma\sigma^{\top}+\left(A-BR^{-1}\left(\Pi B-S\right)^{\top}KH\right)\Sigma+\Sigma\left(A-BR^{-1}\left(\Pi B-S\right)^{\top}KH\right)^{\top},

(139)

where the initial conditions are
\mu(0)=\mu_{0}
and
\Sigma(0)=\Sigma_{0}
.

\Psi
and
\Pi
are the solutions of the following time-backward ordinary differential equations:

\displaystyle-\frac{d\Psi}{dt}

\displaystyle=Q+A^{\top}\Psi+\Psi A-\left(\Psi B-S\right)R^{-1}\left(\Psi B-S\right)^{\top},

(140)

\displaystyle-\frac{d\Pi}{dt}

\displaystyle=Q+A^{\top}\Pi+\Pi A-\left(\Pi B-S\right)R^{-1}\left(\Pi B-S\right)^{\top}+\left(I-KH\right)^{\top}\left(\Pi B-S\right)R^{-1}\left(\Pi B-S\right)^{\top}\left(I-KH\right),

(141)

where the terminal conditions are
\Psi(T)=\Pi(T)=P
.

Equation (
140
) can be solved backward in time independently.
Equation (
138
) can be solved forward in time based on the solution of Eq. (
140
).
Equations (
139
) and (
141
) are mutually dependent and need to be solved simultaneously, which correspond to a two-point boundary value problem of ordinary differential equations.
The LQG problem reduces the two-point boundary value problem of partial differential equations, HJB and FP equations, to that of ordinary differential equations, Eqs. (
139
) and (
141
), allowing for efficient computation.
This two-point boundary value problem is solved by using the forward-backward sweep method, which computes Eqs. (
139
) and (
141
) alternately
[
97
]
.

The optimal control function in the LQG problem of OSC [Eq. (
137
)] is decomposed into a deterministic first term and a stochastic second term.
The deterministic first term of Eq. (
137
) also appears in conventional optimal control problems, and Eq. (
140
) is known as Riccati equation
[
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
In contrast, the stochastic second term of Eq. (
137
) is unique to OSC, and we refer to Eq. (
141
) as observation-based Riccati equation.
Although Riccati equation and observation-based Riccati equation are similar, the latter includes an additional term that accounts for the observation uncertainty in OSC.
In fact, if observation
y_{t}
is identical to state
x_{t}
, i.e.,
y_{t}=x_{t}
,
H=I
and
E=O
hold, and the last term of observation-based Riccati equation vanishes, reducing it to Riccati equation.

C.3
Derivation

In this subsection, we derive the optimal control function in the LQG problem of OSC.
From Pontryagin’s minimum principle on the probability density function space, the optimal control function of OSC is given by

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y)

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{u}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u,w\right)\right].

(142)

In the LQG problem, Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u,w\right)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}Qx-2x^{\top}Su+u^{\top}Ru+\left(\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial x}\right)^{\top}\left(Ax+Bu\right)+\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial x}\right)^{\top}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\right).

(143)

From

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,w)\right]}{\partial u}=-2S^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]+2Ru+B^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial x}\right],

(144)

the optimal control function of OSC is given by

\displaystyle u^{*}(t,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}\left(B^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial x}\right]-2S^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]\right).

(145)

We first assume that
w(t,x)
is given by the following quadratic function:

\displaystyle w(t,x)=x^{\top}\Pi(t)x+\alpha^{\top}(t)x+\beta(t),

(146)

where
\Pi(t)
is a symmetric matrix.
Under this assumption, HJB equation

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial w(t,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathcal{H}\left(t,x,u^{*},w\right)\right]

(147)

is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle-\left(x^{\top}\frac{d\Pi}{dt}x+\left(\frac{d\alpha}{dt}\right)^{\top}x+\frac{d\beta}{dt}\right)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}\left(Q+A^{\top}\Pi+\Pi A\right)x+\left(\left(A-BR^{-1}\left(\Pi B-S\right)^{\top}\right)^{\top}\alpha\right)^{\top}x+{\rm tr}\left(\Pi\sigma\sigma^{\top}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{\top}BR^{-1}B\alpha

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]^{\top}(\Pi B-S)R(\Pi B-S)^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]\right]-2x^{\top}(\Pi B-S)R(\Pi B-S)^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]\right],

(148)

where
\Pi(T)=P
,
\alpha(T)=0
, and
\beta(T)=0
.
We next assume that
p(t,x)
is given by the following Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle p(t,x)=\mathcal{N}(x|\mu(t),\Sigma(t)),

(149)

where
\mu(t)
and
\Sigma(t)
are the mean vector and the covariance matrix of state
x
, respectively.
Under this assumption,
\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]=\mu+K\left(y-H\mu\right),

(150)

where
K
is defined by

\displaystyle K:=\Sigma H^{\top}\left(E+H\Sigma H^{\top}\right)^{-1}.

(151)

Similarly, the following equations hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(y|x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x|y)}\left[x\right]\right]=\mu+KH\left(x-\mu\right),

Appendix F
Memory-Limited Partially Observable Stochastic Control

In this section, we briefly review our theoretical framework proposed previously, memory-limited partially observable stochastic control (ML-POSC)
[
96
,
97
,
98
]
, and compare it with OSC and OMSC introduced in this work.
OSC and OMSC are introduced in Appendix
A
and
B
, respectively.
This section is organized as follows:
In Appendix
F.1
, we formulate ML-POSC, comparing it with OMSC.
In Appendix
F.2
, we show that ML-POSC can be converted into OSC on the extended space, similar to OMSC.

F.1
Problem Formulation

In this subsection, we formulate ML-POSC
[
96
,
97
,
98
]
, comparing it with OMSC.
The dynamics of state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
in ML-POSC is the same with that in OMSC [Eq. (
102
)], which is given by the following SDE:

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle=b(t,x_{t},u_{t})dt+\sigma(t,x_{t},u_{t})d\omega_{t},

(257)

where initial state
x_{0}
follows
p_{0}(x_{0})
,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is the control for the state,
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\omega}}
is a standard Wiener process.
In contrast, the generation rule of observation
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
in ML-POSC is different from that in OMSC.
While observation
y_{t}
is generated from probability density function
p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t})
as
y_{t}\sim p_{t}(y_{t}|x_{t})
in OMSC [Eq. (
103
)], it is generated from the following SDE in ML-POSC:

\displaystyle dy_{t}

\displaystyle=h(t,x_{t})dt+\gamma(t)d\nu_{t},

(258)

where
\nu_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\nu}}
is a standard Wiener process.
From this difference, the dynamics of memory
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
in ML-POSC is formulated by

\displaystyle dz_{t}=c(t,z_{t},v_{t})dt+\kappa(t,z_{t},v_{t})dy_{t}+\eta(t,z_{t},v_{t})d\xi_{t},

(259)

where initial memory
z_{0}
follows
p_{0}(z_{0})
,
v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
is the control for the memory, and
\xi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
is a standard Wiener process.
Compared with the memory dynamics in OMSC [Eq. (
104
)],
\kappa(t,z_{t},v_{t})dy_{t}
is an additional term in ML-POSC.
While OMSC determines state control
u_{t}
and memory control
v_{t}
based on observation
y_{t}
and memory
z_{t}
[Eqs. (
105
) and (
106
)], ML-POSC determines them based solely on memory
z_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=u(t,z_{t}),

(260)

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=v(t,z_{t}).

(261)

The rest of the formulation in ML-POSC is the same with that in OMSC.
The objective function is given by the following expected cumulative cost function:

\displaystyle J[u,v]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t},z_{t},u_{t},v_{t})dt+g(x_{T},z_{T})\right].

(262)

where
f
and
g
are the running and terminal cost functions, respectively.
ML-POSC is a problem to find optimal state control function
u^{*}
and optimal memory control function
v^{*}
that minimize objective function
J[u,v]
as follows:

\displaystyle u^{*},v^{*}:=\arg\min_{u,v}J[u,v].

(263)

While OMSC encodes observation information into memory through memory control
v_{t}=v(t,y_{t},z_{t})
, ML-POSC encodes it through
\kappa(t,z_{t},v_{t})dy_{t}
.
Since the optimization problem of
\kappa(t,z_{t},v_{t})dy_{t}
lies outside the scope of the LQG problem
[
96
]
, ML-POSC cannot explicitly and efficiently investigate how the agent optimally encodes observation information into memory.
OMSC addresses this issue by encoding observation information into memory through memory control
v_{t}=v(t,y_{t},z_{t})
.

F.2
Conversion to Observation-based Stochastic Control on Extended Space

In this subsection, we show that ML-POSC can be converted into OSC on the extended space, which is similar to OMSC.
We define extended state
\tilde{x}_{t}
, extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
, and extended control
\tilde{u}_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{t}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{t}\\
z_{t}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\tilde{y}_{t}:=z_{t}^{\prime},\quad\tilde{u}_{t}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{t}\\
v_{t}\\
\end{array}\right).

We denote the memory in extended state
\tilde{x}_{t}
by
z_{t}
and that in extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
by
z_{t}^{\prime}
to distinguish between them.
In practice,
z_{t}=z_{t}^{\prime}
holds.
Extended state
\tilde{x}_{t}
evolves by the following SDE:

\displaystyle d\tilde{x}_{t}=\tilde{b}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})dt+\tilde{\sigma}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})d\tilde{\omega}_{t},

(268)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{b}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b(t,x_{t},u_{t})\\
c(t,z_{t},v_{t})+\kappa(t,z_{t},v_{t})h(t,x_{t})\\
\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sigma(t,x_{t},u_{t})&O&O\\
O&\kappa(t,z_{t},v_{t})\gamma(t)&\eta(t,z_{t},v_{t})\\
\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\omega_{t}\\
\nu_{t}\\
\xi_{t}\\
\end{array}\right).

Extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
is generated from the following probability:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{y}_{t}|\tilde{x}_{t}):=\delta(z_{t}^{\prime}-z_{t}).

(276)

Extended control
\tilde{u}_{t}
is determined based on extended observation
\tilde{y}_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}=\tilde{u}(t,\tilde{y}_{t}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u(t,z_{t})\\
v(t,z_{t})\\
\end{array}\right).

The objective function is given by the following expected cumulative cost function:

\displaystyle J[\tilde{u}]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{f}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t})dt+\tilde{g}(\tilde{x}_{T})\right],

(279)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(t,\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{u}_{t}):=f(t,x_{t},z_{t},u_{t},v_{t}),\quad\tilde{g}(\tilde{x}_{T}):=g(x_{T},z_{T}).

(280)

Therefore, ML-POSC can be converted into OSC on the extended space, and its optimal solution can be obtained through OSC, similar to OMSC.
