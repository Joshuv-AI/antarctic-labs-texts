Advanced mirror shapes for mode enhancement in plano-concave cavities
William J. Hughes, Peter Horak

Abstract
Optical cavities are frequently used in quantum technologies to enhance light matter interactions, with applications including single photon generation and entanglement of distant emitters. The Fabry-Pérot resonator is a popular choice for its high optical access and large emitter-mirror separation. A typical configuration, particularly for emitters that should not be placed close to the mirror surface like trapped ions and Rydberg atoms, features two spherical mirrors placed around a central emitter, but this arrangement can put demanding requirements on the mirror alignment. In contrast, plano-concave cavities are tolerant to mirror misalignment and only require the manufacture of one curved mirror, but have limited ability to focus light in the centre of the cavity. Here we show how mirror shaping can overcome this limitation of plano-concave cavities while preserving the key advantages. We demonstrate through numerical simulations that simple mirror shaping can increase coupling between a plano-concave cavity and a central emitter by an order of magnitude, even rivalling misalignment-sensitive concave-concave counterparts for achievable interaction strength. We use these observations to establish the conditions under which plano-concave cavities with shaped mirrors could improve the performance and practicality of emitter-cavity systems.



I
Introduction

Fabry-Pérot cavities are increasingly employed across quantum technology to improve processes such as single photon production
Law and and 1997
;
Keller et al. 2004
and the readout of qubit states
Bochmann et al. 2010
;
Grinkemeyer et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
, finding application in quantum networking
Reiserer and Rempe 2015
;
Ritter et al. 2012
;
Krutyanskiy et al. 2023a
and modular quantum computation
Monroe et al. 2014
;
Li and Thompson 2024
;
Sinclair et al. 2025
. For many applications, high cooperativity, which means a large ratio of coherent emitter-cavity coupling to incoherent decay processes, is crucial. One can modify the Fabry-Pérot cavity to increase cooperativity by either decreasing the cavity round trip loss or tightening the mode waist at the position of the emitter. The former is difficult because the lower bound to round trip loss is set by technical limits to the mirror surface roughness and the absorption of the mirror material
Rempe et al. 1992
;
Shadmany et al. 2025
;
Maier et al. 2025
. The latter requires either a shorter cavity length, or moving towards a ‘concentric’ geometry in which both mirrors focus directly on the emitter
Shadmany et al. 2025
. However, a very short cavity length is not compatible with emitters that are sensitive to electric field noise near the high-reflectivity dielectric mirrors
Teller et al. 2021
, such as Rydberg atoms
Davtyan et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2022
or ions
Ong et al. 2020
, for which the mirrors additionally disrupt the trapping potential
Podoliak et al. 2016
;
Kassa et al. 2025
, and a highly concentric geometry is extremely sensitive to mirror misalignment
Nguyen et al. 2018
. Concave-concave Fabry-Pérot optical cavities with spherical mirrors thus have a limited ability to achieve high cooperativity, reasonable emitter-mirror separation, and good alignment tolerance simultaneously. To circumvent these limits, alternative designs have featured additional mirrors
Cox et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2022
or intra-cavity optics
Shadmany et al. 2025
, but these introduce experimental complexity, include lossy elements in the cavity round trip, or lengthen the round trip time.

In contrast, plano-concave cavities, which have one planar and one curved mirror, do not introduce these disadvantages. They also boast zero sensitivity to the transverse misalignment of the mirrors and require the manufacture of only one curved surface. Plano-concave geometries are therefore often used
Wang et al. 2017
;
Malmir et al. 2022
, particularly for cavities with short lengths, or when the emitter can be placed close to the planar mirror
Farrow et al. 2023
. However, they have limited ability to focus light in the centre of the cavity, which reduces the possible cooperativity in cavities with longer lengths.

Here we show how to overcome this focussing limit with order of magnitude-level improvements. Our approach uses the ability of modern micromirror manufacture techniques, such as focussed ion beam milling
Dolan et al. 2010
or laser ablation
Hunger et al. 2010
, to control the mirror profile shape
Trichet et al. 2015
;
Walker et al. 2021
;
Ott et al. 2016
while achieving low loss
Maier et al. 2025
;
Gao et al. 2025
. While earlier work has considered the benefits of mirror shape control in concave-concave geometries
Ferdous et al. 2014
;
Podoliak et al. 2017
;
Karpov and Horak 2022a
;
Karpov and Horak 2022b
;
Karpov et al. 2023
, we instead investigate plano-concave cavities and show how they can provide optical performance competitive with spherical concave-concave cavities while maintaining their advantages of mode stability and alignment insensitivity.

II
Emitter-independent cavity metric

For many cavity applications, including photon generation
Goto et al. 2019
;
Utsugi et al. 2022
;
Hughes et al. 2024
, adiabatic intracavity gate protocols
Goto and Ichimura 2010
, and intracavity cat-state generation
Kikura et al. 2025
, the internal cooperativity,

C_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{g^{2}}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{int}}\gamma},

(1)

serves as the single figure of merit, where
g
is the rate of coherent interaction between the emitter and the single cavity mode,
\kappa_{\mathrm{int}}
is the partial cavity amplitude decay rate due to internal (i.e. non-transmissive) losses (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}
), and
\gamma
is the amplitude decay rate of the emitter’s excited state, which accounts for spontaneous emission. The internal cooperativity has an alternative form that highlights the focussing of the mode on the emitter,
A^{-1}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{r_{e}})
, which is the ratio of the intensity of the forward-running cavity mode at the emitter (positioned at
\bm{r_{e}}
) to the total forward-running power
Hughes and Horak 2025
,

C_{\mathrm{int}}=\alpha_{B}C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0},\quad C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}=\frac{3\lambda^{2}A^{-1}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{r_{e}})}{\pi\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}},

(2)

where
\alpha_{B}
is the branching ratio of the excited state emission to the cavity mode, which accounts for the spontaneous emission branching ratio and the polarisation power overlap to the desired cavity mode,
\lambda
is the emitter transition wavelength, and it is assumed that the emitter is moved perfectly to an antinode of the intra-cavity standing wave formed by superposition of the forward-running and backward-running modes. Equation (
2
) introduces
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
, which is proportional to
C_{\mathrm{int}}
. Because we can calculate
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
without specifying the emitter, we quote its value throughout the manuscript, but an optimisation of
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
implicitly optimises the performance-relevant
C_{\mathrm{int}}
.

For cavities with spherical mirrors, the eigenmodes are a family of Gaussian beams with central waist
w_{0}
in a transverse plane determined by the cavity geometry
Kogelnik and Li 1966
. Of this family, it is most common to use the ‘fundamental’ mode due to its more compact and simpler spatial structure. An emitter coupling to the transverse centre of the fundamental mode at an axial position where the waist is
w_{e}
has an inverse effective area and, from Eq. (
2
), an internal cooperativity of

A^{-1}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{1}{w_{e}^{2}},\quad C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}=\frac{6\lambda^{2}}{\pi^{2}w_{e}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}}.

(3)

III
Concave-concave and plano-concave cavities with spherical mirrors

To assess whether non-spherical mirrors can improve
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
, we must first establish the limits to
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
in concave-concave and plano-concave cavities with spherical mirrors. For each case we assume that all aspects of the cavity except for the mirror profile have been determined beforehand. These are
L
, the length of the cavity, which may be restricted by the experimental geometry or minimum emitter-mirror distance,
D
, the mirror diameter, which may be restricted by the maximum machinable surface area or optical access constraints,
\lambda
, the resonant wavelength set by the emitter transition, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}
, the lower limit to internal loss containing scattering and absorptive, but not clipping, losses, set by the mirror coating and small scale surface roughness
Bennett 1992
.

The typical configuration of the concave-concave resonator has two mirrors of identical curvature
R

Takahashi et al. 2020
;
Krutyanskiy et al. 2023b
. The smallest waist of the mode (
w_{0}
) is located in the centre of the cavity, and is therefore also the waist at the emitter
w_{e}
, with

w_{e}=w_{0}=w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}\left(\frac{2R}{L}-1\right)^{\frac{1}{4}},\quad w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda L}{2\pi}},

(4)

where
w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}
is the central waist of a ‘confocal’ cavity where
R
is set to the same value as
L
. The waist at the mirror is

w_{m}=w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}\sqrt{\left(\frac{w_{0}}{w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}}{w_{0}}\right)^{2}},

(5)

which has a minimum value
\sqrt{2}w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}
, satisfied in the confocal configuration.

The internal cooperativity depends upon the cavity mode through the ratio
A^{-1}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{r_{e}})/\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}
(see Eq. (
2
)). The numerator of this ratio is the tightness of the mode focus on the emitter, but, to understand the impact of the cavity mode on the denominator, we may decompose the internal loss into

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}},

(6)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}
is loss from the mode escaping beyond the edge of the mirrors. Decreasing the radius of curvature
R
of the cavity mirror from
R=L
(confocal limit) towards
R=L/2
(concentric limit) reduces
w_{0}
and thus increases
A^{-1}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{r_{e}})
. However,
w_{m}
will increase and thus
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}
will increase dramatically. The optimum mirror profile is found when
R
is reduced until
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}
becomes a non-negligible fraction of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}
. We can write this condition as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}=\chi\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}
, where
\chi
is a small fraction of unity, but not orders of magnitude smaller than unity
Gao et al. 2023
. Assuming for algebraic convenience that the mirror diameter
D
is sufficiently large that the central waist is much smaller than
w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}
, we get a maximised
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
of (see App.
A
)

C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{CC}}=\frac{1}{\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left({\frac{\chi}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}}\right)\right\}}\frac{6D^{2}}{L^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}},

(7)

where the precise value of
\chi
has very little impact, so need not be carefully determined. The right-hand side quotient highlights the importance of the cavity numerical aperture
D/L
.

In many actual systems, the limit (Eq. (
7
)) cannot be reached because, if the mirrors are not perfectly transversely aligned, the mode will tilt at an angle, determined through geometric cavity theory
Siegman 1986
;
Hughes et al. 2023
,

\theta=-\frac{M}{L}\left(\frac{w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}}{w_{0}}\right)^{4},

(8)

where
M
is the transverse misalignment between the mirrors. The mode angle diverges very strongly as
w_{0}
decreases to increase
C_{\mathrm{int}}
, dramatically increasing clipping loss
Gao et al. 2023
. This means that often the alignment tolerance, rather than the cavity numerical aperture, limits the achievable
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
in concave-concave systems.

The plano-concave geometry is naturally insensitive to the transverse mirror misalignment, provided that the mode does not clip outside the edge of the planar mirror. However, when the non-planar mirror is spherical, the cavity modes focus on the planar mirror, not the emitter in the centre of the cavity. The smallest possible waist at the emitter is found for mirror radius
R=R^{\mathrm{PC}}_{\mathrm{opt.sph.}}=5L/4
(see App.
B
) taking the value

(w_{e})^{\mathrm{PC}}_{\mathrm{opt.sph.}}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda L}{\pi}}=\sqrt{2}w_{0}^{\mathrm{conf}}.

(9)

Using Eq. (
3
), the
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
of a plano-concave cavity of length
L
is limited to

C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{PC}}=\frac{6\lambda}{\pi L\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}}.

(10)

For future reference, we will extend the notation used in this section such that, for a generic quantity
\zeta
,
\zeta^{\mathrm{PC}}_{\mathrm{opt.sph.}}
refers to the value of
\zeta
in a plano-concave cavity with
R=R^{\mathrm{PC}}_{\mathrm{opt.sph.}}
.

IV
Suggested surface designs

For plano-concave cavities, restricting the non-planar mirror to a spherical shape limits
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
far below the concave-concave limit (Eq. (
7
)) set by the cavity solid angle (see Eq. (
10
)). We will demonstrate that shaping the non-planar mirror can bridge the gap between the two limits, and thus significantly increase
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
for plano-concave cavities. In order to calculate the modes of these plano-concave cavities with shaped mirrors, we use the mode mixing method
Kleckner et al. 2010
, which has been widely used to model cavities with non-spherical mirror profiles
Benedikter et al. 2015
;
Podoliak et al. 2017
;
Walker et al. 2021
;
Karpov and Horak 2022a
;
Karpov and Horak 2022b
;
Karpov et al. 2023
;
Hughes et al. 2023
.

We will use two approaches to design surfaces, which we restrict to having zero azimuthal dependence. The first is the ‘retroreflective optimisation method’
Hughes and Horak 2025
, in which a target cavity mode is optimised for
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
, before the mirror surface profile is constructed to retroreflect this target mode. This method is useful because it approximately finds the limits of
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
achievable with arbitrary mirror shaping.

The second approach is to choose non-spherical mirror shapes with a few variable parameters. We will study three such shapes in this manuscript: The Gaussian-shaped mirror, the ‘dual curvature mirror’, and the ‘spline mirror’.

We consider Gaussian surfaces because laser ablation naturally makes mirrors more Gaussian-shaped than spherical
Muller et al. 2010
, and because they can improve emitter coupling in concave-concave cavities
Podoliak et al. 2017
. The surface profile is

z(r)=D_{G}\left\{1-\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{w_{G}^{2}}\right)\right\},\quad D_{G}=\frac{w_{G}^{2}}{2R},

(11)

where
R
is the central radius of curvature of the mirror,
w_{G}
is the 1/e-waist of the Gaussian profile, the derived quantity
D_{G}
is the depth of the central depression from the asymptotic flats, and
r
is the radial coordinate of the rotationally-symmetric surface profile.

To increase mirror design flexibility, we also study a ‘dual curvature’ profile that transitions smoothly from a central curvature
R_{c}
in the middle of the mirror to an outer curvature
R_{o}
at the edge. The gradient of this profile is

\frac{dz}{dr}=\left(1-\sigma(r)\right)\frac{x}{R_{c}}+\sigma(r)\frac{x}{R_{o}},\quad\sigma(r)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\tilde{r}}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{r}^{2}}}\right),\quad\tilde{r}=\frac{r-\delta_{\sigma}}{w_{\sigma}},

(12)

where
\tilde{r}
is a scaled radial coordinate, and
\sigma(r)
is a ‘sigmoid’ function that transitions between zero and unity. The sigmoid parameter
\delta_{\sigma}
sets the radial centre of the transition between
R_{c}
and
R_{o}
, and
w_{\sigma}
sets the radial width of the gradient transition. This yields an algebraic profile with four surface parameters

\displaystyle z(r)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2R_{c}}+\frac{x^{2}}{2R_{o}}\right)

\displaystyle+

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{R_{o}}-\frac{1}{R_{c}}\right)

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\frac{1}{2}w_{\sigma}^{2}\tilde{r}\sqrt{1+\tilde{r}^{2}}+w_{\sigma}\delta_{\sigma}\sqrt{1+\tilde{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}w_{\sigma}\delta_{\sigma}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\delta_{\sigma}}{w_{\sigma}}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}w_{\sigma}^{2}\left[\mathrm{arsinh}(\tilde{r})-\mathrm{arsinh}\left(\frac{-\delta_{\sigma}}{w_{\sigma}}\right)\right]\Bigg\}.

Finally, to encode a surface in a flexible number of free parameters, we parametrise the mirror surface through a parabolic curvature
R
and an additional cubic spline. The surface amplitude follows

z(r)=\frac{r^{2}}{2R}+s(r),

(14)

where
s(r)
is a cubic spline distortion from parabolic passing through chosen points. The spline’s
N_{p}+1
points are equispaced between radial coordinates of
0
and
D/2
inclusive, where the first point (at the origin) is always set to zero, and the remaining
N_{p}
points (along with
R
) are free parameters. The spline passes smoothly through the points, with zero gradient enforced at the origin.

VI
Parameter sensitivity

We have seen (Sec.
V

C_{\mathrm{int}}
while avoiding the complex surface shape and extreme length sensitivity of a fully optimised design. To make sure the expected improvement is achieved, the mirror design should not be highly sensitive to fabrication errors that cause changes in the mirror parameters. Examples of mirror parameter sensitivity are shown in Fig.

VII
Suggested use cases for plano-concave mirror shaping

Having established the potential for performance improvements (Sec.
V
), we now discuss how to determine whether shaped plano-concave cavities might be useful given a set of geometrical limitations (
L
,
D
,
\lambda
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}
). This discussion assumes there is a target value of
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
(henceforth
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,T}
), above which the cavity performance will be deemed acceptable for the application. Calculating
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{PC}}
(Eq. (
10
)),
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{CC}}
(Eq. (
7
)), and the retroreflective optimised
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}
(henceforth
C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{opt.}}
) establishes four regimes.

•

C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,T}<C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{PC}}
: The target performance is achievable with a plano-concave cavity with spherical mirrors.

•

C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{PC}}<C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,T}<C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{opt.}}
: Mirror shaping can serve to achieve the target performance in a plano-concave design.

•

C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{opt.}}<C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,T}<C_{\mathrm{int}}^{0,\mathrm{CC}}

VIII
Conclusion

We have shown that mirror shaping can overcome the key disadvantage of plano-concave cavities with spherical mirrors: The limited coupling strength to a central emitter. We find even simple, manufacturable mirror profiles achieve the majority of the maximum possible performance increases, yield cavities with modest length sensitivity, and retain the misalignment tolerance and simplicity of fabricating only one non-planar mirror inherent to the plano-concave geometry. The potential order-of-magnitude cooperativity improvements could dramatically improve the rate and fidelity of various quantum technology protocols, such as single photon production or qubit state readout. We further discuss how to determine, during experimental design, whether these shaped plano-concave cavities offer a compelling alternative to traditional concave-concave configurations, and anticipate that this work will encourage their use to realise more stable, scalable, and high-performing emitter-cavity systems across quantum technology applications.
