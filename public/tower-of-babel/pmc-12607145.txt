Role of procalcitonin in identifying systemic inflammatory response syndrome and sepsis in acute-on-chronic liver failure and its impact on survival
Authors: Suriya Nedunchezhian, Athish Shetty, Ganesh Bhat, Shiran Shetty, Balaji Musunuri, C Ganesh Pai, Siddheesh Rajpurohit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and sepsis are commonly observed in patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF). This study aimed to identify the presence of SIRS and sepsis in ACLF patients to assess their impact on patient survival and the role of procalcitonin (PCT) in identifying SIRS and sepsis.MethodologyThis prospective study was performed at a tertiary hospital in India from August 2023 to August 2024. Patients with ACLF according to the APASL classification were included in the study. These patients were assessed for the presence or absence of SIRS or sepsis at baseline and monitored until day 7, and clinical outcome was evaluated on day 28.ResultsA total of 135 patients with a median age of 44 years (IQR: 38–51) and a male predominance (98.5%, 133/135) were included in the study. Alcohol was the primary cause of ACLF in 88.1% of patients. Among these patients, SIRS was present in 59.2% of patients, while sepsis was present in 42.2%. Patients with SIRS and sepsis had significantly higher median MELD-Na, AARC, and CLIF-SOFA scores than those without SIRS and sepsis (p < 0.001). The 28-day mortality rate was greater in the SIRS group (60%) and sepsis group (59.64%) than in the non-SIRS group (p < 0.001). Furthermore, PCT was demonstrated as a significant independent predictor of mortality (OR 1.45, 95% CI: 1.12–1.89, p = 0.004).ConclusionSepsis and SIRS are common in ACLF patients and carry a high mortality risk. PCT measurement may be used to detect SIRS and sepsis in these patients early and can be used to guide prompt intervention to improve the outcome of these patients.Keywords: ACLF, Cirrhosis of the liver, AARC score, CLIFF-SOFA, 28-day mortality

Introduction

Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is a complex syndrome characterized by the sudden deterioration of underlying chronic liver disease (CLD), precipitated by an acute insult, resulting in liver failure with or without the involvement of other organs. ACLF progresses through several phases that significantly influence patient outcomes [1].

Initially, an acute insult, such as hepatic or extrahepatic injury, triggers a cascade of events beginning with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [2]. This phase is marked by a surge in inflammatory mediators and immune activation aimed at combating the insult. However, in patients with underlying cirrhosis, factors such as cytopenia, hypersplenism, and a blunted febrile response can obscure the typical presentation of SIRS [3]. Following SIRS, the body enters a compensatory anti-inflammatory response syndrome phase, characterized by dampening of the inflammatory response to prevent excessive tissue damage. In ACLF, this regulatory mechanism is disrupted, leading to persistent inflammation and immune dysfunction. Immune cells become dysfunctional and energy depleted, contributing to exaggerated SIRS [4].

The interplay between these phases, along with the timing and sequence of events, ultimately determines the trajectory of ACLF. Early identification of systemic inflammatory responses and timely management of underlying precipitants are important, as ACLF is associated with a high risk of rapid progression to multiorgan failure and death [5].

While liver failure, sepsis, and extrahepatic organ involvement are common causes of mortality in patients with ACLF, it is important to note that these are often late-stage phenomena. Early interventions targeting underlying inflammatory and immune dysregulation can improve outcomes and mitigate the progression of ACLF. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiology and timely management of ACLF is paramount in clinical practice [6].

Recognizing this critical time frame, often referred to as the “golden window,” becomes essential for timely intervention. Situations where extrahepatic organ dysfunction precedes liver failure or where sepsis triggers liver failure may necessitate a distinct subgroup classification owing to differences in disease progression [7].

Procalcitonin (PCT), a precursor of calcitonin, originates primarily from the C cells of the thyroid gland. Bacterial toxins (lipopolysaccharides, LPS) and proinflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β) trigger the expression of CALC‑1 and calcitonin (CT) mRNAs. This induction occurs in neuroendocrine cells located in multiple organs including the liver, lungs, intestines, pancreas, brain, kidneys, and adipose tissue as well as in circulating immune cells (monocytes, lymphocytes, and granulocytes). This led to increased serum PCT levels, with notable production from organs such as the liver, kidneys, ovaries, urinary bladder, and adrenal glands [8].

Serum PCT levels serve as an early and sensitive marker for detecting infection and sepsis, aiding in the differentiation between SIRS and sepsis among critically ill patients. In healthy individuals, PCT levels remain low, typically below 0.05–1 ng/mL, and sepsis can almost certainly be excluded when PCT levels are ≤ 0.2–1.2 ng/mL. Following exposure to inflammatory triggers, PCT levels begin to rise within 2 h, peak at approximately hour 14 and are positively correlated with the severity of infection. While bacterial and certain parasitic infections lead to significant increases in PCT levels, high PCT levels do not necessarily rule out viral infections. Compared with bacterial infections, fungal infections are generally associated with lower serum PCT levels [9, 10].

The liver, which is crucial for maintaining homeostasis, plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of sepsis by assisting in blood purification from bacteria and LPS while also serving as a source of proinflammatory mediators. Liver failure, particularly in the context of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) complicating sepsis/septic shock, significantly impacts patient mortality, highlighting the importance of liver function in critical illness [11, 12]. This study aimed to detect the presence or absence of SIRS and the presence of sepsis in ACLF patients, compare the impact of each category on the survival of patients and assess the role of procalcitonin in differentiating SIRS and sepsis.

Methods

This was a prospective, cross-sectional, observational, single-centre study conducted at a tertiary centre in India between August 2023 and August 2024. All patients aged 18 years and above with ACLF according to the APASL classification were enrolled in the study. Patients who survived < 24 h, had a prior decompensation history, were diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and were not willing to participate were excluded from the study. Upon receiving the subject’s consent to participate in the study, patient information, including demographic data, medical history, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension), presenting complaints, and laboratory parameters, was recorded in a predesigned form. Patients were assessed for the presence or absence of SIRS and sepsis at baseline. All patients received standard-of-care treatment according to institutional protocols, which included nutritional optimization, ascites management, anti-encephalopathy measures, and antibiotics, and were reassessed on day 7 from admission for the development of new-onset SIRS or sepsis and were followed up until day 28 (from recruitment) for clinical outcomes.

ACLF was defined according to the APASL criteria as an acute hepatic insult manifesting as jaundice and coagulopathy, which is complicated within 4 weeks by ascites and/or encephalopathy in a patient with previously diagnosed or undiagnosed CLD [1]. The criteria include bilirubin levels ≥ 5 mg/dL, INR ≥ 1.5 or prothrombin time prolonged by over 4 s, and ascites or encephalopathy developing within 4 weeks of jaundice.

SIRS

SIRS is an inflammatory condition affecting the whole body, often in response to infection, trauma, or other stresses. The criteria (presence of two or more) include a body temperature of either < 36 °C or >38 °C, a heart rate >90 beats per minute, a respiratory rate >20 breaths per minute or arterial CO₂ tension < 32 mm Hg, and a white blood cell (WBC) count of < 4,000 cells/mm³ or >12,000 cells/mm³ or more than 10% immature (band) forms [13, 14].

Sepsis

Sepsis was diagnosed based on the Sepsis-3 criteria, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection. Operationally, this was identified by a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score increase of ≥ 2 points from baseline in the presence of a documented or suspected infection [15].

At admission, all patients underwent systematic infection screening, including blood cultures, urine cultures, and ascitic fluid analysis (where ascites was present). Additional diagnostic tests, such as chest radiographs, abdominal ultrasonography, or CT imaging, were performed when clinically indicated. Cultures were repeated if new clinical signs of infection developed during hospitalization. A documented infection was defined as a positive microbiological culture or radiological/clinical evidence of infection requiring antimicrobial therapy. Evidence of systemic inflammation and infection severity was further supported by elevated markers such as procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP). Organ dysfunction was assessed using SOFA components, including hypotension, altered mental status (Glasgow Coma Scale), coagulation profile (INR/platelet count), renal function (creatinine, urine output), hepatic function (bilirubin), and oxygenation (PaO₂/FiO₂). Lactate levels were also recorded as a marker of tissue hypoperfusion [14, 15].

Statistical analysis

We performed data cleaning and analysis using Microsoft Excel and the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 27. The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Data are presented as medians with IQR (interquartile range) for quantitative variables and proportions with percentages for qualitative variables. The Mann‒Whitney U test was used to assess the change in the median across the groups, and the chi-square test was used to assess the associations between the factors associated with sepsis and SIRS. Logistic regression analysis was employed to determine the odds ratio, while the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance and identify the optimal threshold for detecting SIRS and sepsis. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics, with a VIF > 5 considered problematic. Correlation matrices were also reviewed to confirm independence between predictor variables. Statistical significance was determined at the 5% level, with a p-value threshold of < 0.05.

Study approvals

The Institutional Ethical Committee approved this study (IEC-100/2023) before it commenced, and the study was registered with the Clinical Trial Registry - India (CTRI) under the reference number CTRI/2023/09/058160 on 29th September 2023 (Reference number: 069424).

The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki for medical research involving human subjects

Results

Demographic & clinical profile

A total of 135 patients with a median age of 44 years (range 31–50 years), with an interquartile range (IQR) of 44, were enrolled, 133 of whom (98.5%) were male. The major cause of the underlying CLD was alcohol in 119 patients (88.1%). The primary acute precipitant identified in the study population was alcohol (76.3%), followed by drug-induced liver injury (DILI) (18 (13.3%)). Among the 135 patients, hepatic encephalopathy (HE) was most commonly observed at Grade 2 (33.3%), followed by No HE (30.4%) and Grade 1 (23.0%), while severe forms (Grades 3 and 4) were less frequent (13.3% combined). Regarding ascites, moderate severity was most prevalent (42.2%), followed by severe (31.1%) and mild ascites (26.7%). All 135 patients in the cohort received Standard Medical Treatment (SMT), which included anti-encephalopathy (Anti-HE) measures and/or thiamine or de-addiction support. (Table 1).

Prevalence and progression of SIRS and sepsis in hospitalized patients: a baseline and day 7 assessment

At the time of admission, 80 (59.26%) of the 135 patients were diagnosed with SIRS, 57 (42.22%) of whom had sepsis, and 23 (17.04%) had only SIRS at baseline. Of the 55 patients with no SIRS/sepsis at baseline, 17 (30.91%) developed SIRS, and 14 (25.74) developed sepsis on reassessment on day 7. Among the 23 patients with only SIRS at baseline, 5 (21.74%) developed new-onset sepsis by day 7. (Fig. 1)

Comparison of baseline laboratory data and outcomes between SIRS patients and non-SIRS patients

A comparison of baseline laboratory data revealed several significant differences between patients with and without SIRS. Patients with SIRS presented significantly higher values of total leukocyte count (TLC), platelet count (PLT), neutrophil percentage, serum creatinine (sCr), total bilirubin (T. Bil), international normalized ratio (INR), procalcitonin (PCT), and lactate levels (p < 0.005). The median MELD, AARC, and CLIF-SOFA scores were also significantly greater in the SIRS group than in the non-SIRS group (p < 0.001), and the overall mortality rate was 44.4% (60/135 patients). Additionally, there was a marked difference in 28-day mortality between the groups, with a 56% mortality rate in the SIRS group compared with 18.8% in the non-SIRS group (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study underscores the dynamic nature of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and sepsis in the progression of ACLF, emphasizing the need for close monitoring of ACLF patients, particularly those who present without SIRS or sepsis at admission. Among the 135 ACLF patients included, 59.26% (n = 80) had SIRS at admission, 42.22% (n = 57) also had sepsis, and 17.04% (n = 23) had SIRS alone. The remaining 40.74% (n = 55) of patients showed no signs of SIRS or sepsis at baseline. By day 7, 30.91% (n = 17) of these initially unaffected patients had developed new-onset SIRS, and 25.45% (n = 14) had developed new-onset sepsis. Additionally, 21.74% (n = 5) of patients with SIRS alone progressed to sepsis within seven days. Alcohol was the predominant cause of ACLF in our cohort, accounting for 88.1% of the cases (N = 119), a finding that is consistent with global studies that identified alcohol as a leading cause [16, 17]. Furthermore, multicentric study conducted in India reported that alcohol was the most common etiology of ACLF [18]. Among patients with sepsis, 94.7% (N = 54) had alcohol-related liver disease as the primary etiology, whereas 83.3% (N = 65) of nonseptic patients did. These findings are consistent with studies reporting a greater risk of bacterial infections and sepsis in patients with alcohol-induced liver disease [19, 20].

As the majority of our patients had alcohol-related liver disease, we could not assess the effect of alcohol as a risk factor for infections [21–23]. In this study, the median WBC count was significantly greater in the SIRS group than in the non-SIRS group (p-value: 0.007). Additionally, patients with SIRS presented higher sCr levels, with a median of 1.5 mg/dL versus 0.9 mg/dL in those without SIRS. Similarly, ACLF patients with sepsis had significantly elevated WBC counts and sCr levels. A study by Moreau et al., 2013 revealed that ACLF patients were more frequently alcoholic, had more associated bacterial infections, and had greater numbers of leukocytes [16]. Borgonovo et al., 2021 reported that higher INR, T.Bil, sCr, and leukocyte count were associated with SIRS among cirrhotic patients, further supporting the association between these clinical parameters and SIRS in patients with liver disease [24].

The disease severity score, the MELD score (median 30 vs. 27, p < 0.001) and the AARC score (median 9 vs. 7, p < 0.001) were significantly greater in patients with SIRS in our cohort than in patients without SIRS, indicating a greater level of liver dysfunction and a greater propensity toward multiorgan failure [25, 26]. High CLIF-SOFA scores in patients with SIRS reflect a greater likelihood of progressing to severe sepsis and organ failure. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies that reported that increased severity scores, particularly those associated with SIRS, correlate with poor outcomes and even higher mortality rates in the short term [27].

Similarly, patients with sepsis presented significantly higher baseline disease severity scores. The MELD score in the sepsis group was 30 (IQR: 27–32) compared with 26 (IQR: 24–28) in nonseptic patients, and the CLIF-SOFA score was 44 (IQR: 41–49) in septic patients versus 36 (IQR: 32–41) in those without sepsis. These results align with those of studies by Moreau et al. (2013), which reported that higher scores are strong predictors of mortality in septic ACLF patients [16]. Regarding clinical outcomes, both baseline SIRS and sepsis were associated with significantly higher 28-day mortality rates 56% in the SIRS group versus 18.8% in the non-SIRS group (p < 0.001), and 54.3% in the sepsis group versus 30.7% in those without sepsis at baseline. Progression from baseline SIRS to sepsis carried a particularly poor prognosis: all five patients who developed sepsis from an initial SIRS state died. Among the remaining 18 patients with isolated SIRS at baseline, mortality remained high at 50%. Choudhury et al. [28] also emphasized the detrimental impact of persistent SIRS on outcomes, with prolonged inflammatory response correlating with reduced survival. In the current study, new-onset SIRS and sepsis during hospitalization were likewise associated with elevated 28-day mortality rates 35.2% (6/17) and 21.4% (3/14), respectively. These observations underscore the critical importance of early identification and targeted management of systemic inflammation in ACLF to improve short-term survival.

One of the major predictors of poor outcomes in patients with ACLF is SIRS and sepsis since both conditions lead to the failure of an organ and ultimately MODS. Other supporting evidence from studies has revealed the importance of early identification and treatment of ACLF patients with SIRS and sepsis to improve survival rates [29].

Our study revealed that PCT levels are a reliable predictor of both SIRS and sepsis in ACLF patients. A value above 0.55 ng/mL, with an AUROC of 0.886, sensitivity of 0.925, and specificity of 0.72, is associated with a systemic inflammatory response, indicating the presence of SIRS. Moreover, a value greater than 1.087 ng/mL, with an AUROC of 0.892, sensitivity of 0.86, and specificity of 0.67, indicated infection. In our study, we compared the diagnostic performance of the CLIF score, MELD score, and PCT in predicting clinical outcomes. All three markers demonstrated modest discriminatory ability, with AUCs ranging from 62.9% to 64.5%. The CLIF score showed the highest AUC (64.5%) and specificity (80%), indicating better performance in ruling out adverse outcomes, though its sensitivity was relatively low (46.7%). PCT, on the other hand, had the highest sensitivity (80%) but lower specificity (50%), making it more effective for early identification but with a higher chance of false positives. The MELD score offered a more balanced profile, with sensitivity and specificity of 71.7% and 52%, respectively.

A study by Sinha et al. evaluated serum PCT cut-offs of ≥ 0.5 ng/mL and ≥ 2 ng/mL in 40 patients (21 with sepsis, 19 without). Both thresholds showed significant correlation with sepsis (P < 0.0001). At ≥ 0.5 ng/mL, the sensitivity was 90% and specificity 84%, while at ≥ 2 ng/mL, sensitivity was 85.7% with an improved specificity of 94.7% demonstrating that higher cut-offs reduce false positives [30]. Similarly, another study reported an optimal PCT threshold of 0.52 ng/mL for early prediction of septic shock, with an AUC of 0.929, sensitivity of 86.7%, and specificity of 85.3% [31]. These findings closely align with our chosen thresholds and confirm the clinical validity of PCT in identifying systemic inflammatory states and sepsis.

Additionally, Castelli et al. 2004 reported that patients with progressive deterioration and clear signs of sepsis presented elevated PCT levels, with positive correlations between the serum PCT concentration and infection severity, clinical progression, and mortality [32]. Recent research by Wejnaruemarn et al., 2025 from 4,287 cirrhotic patients found PCT has a sensitivity of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.64–0.81), specificity of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.79–0.87), and OR 17.21 (95% CI: 9.57–30.95) for bacterial infection, and proved especially effective as a rule‑in test for SBP (OR 29.50) [33].

This finding aligns with that of Simon et al.2004, who reported that PCT is more accurate than other inflammatory markers, such as CRP, for detecting systemic inflammation [34]. PCT offers several advantages over CRP, including a quicker rise and earlier peak within 24 h after infection, as well as a faster decline once the infection resolves [35]. Studies by Luzzani et al. 1999 and Meisner et al. 2003 have shown that PCT is more sensitive and specific than CRP for diagnosing and prognosticating sepsis [36, 37].

Routine PCT measurement can thus be a valuable tool in differentiating sepsis from non-infectious inflammation [34, 38]. Our research study indicated that increased PCT levels were an early indicator of bacterial infections before any clinical symptoms of sepsis were fully established. Becker et al. noted that PCT elevation occurs before clinical signs of sepsis are detected. Early detection of sepsis in such patients with ACLF is crucial since complicating infections may cause MODS and have a very high mortality rate that can be initiated within hours [39].

Procalcitonin (PCT) is a key biomarker for differentiating sepsis from non-infectious inflammation, aiding early management of ACLF. A recent review revealed that sequential PCT values are useful not only for diagnosing sepsis but also for deciding on the discontinuation of antibiotics in ICU patients [40]. Routine PCT monitoring can improve survival, reduce unnecessary antibiotics, and prevent sepsis-related complications [41]. Furthermore, measuring PCT at admission and 48–72 h to guide antibiotic initiation (PCT ≥ 0.9 ng/mL) and de‑escalation in ACLF, integrating these thresholds into existing sepsis algorithms alongside MELD/CLIF‑SOFA scores. Randomized trials have demonstrated that procalcitonin (PCT)-guided therapy can safely reduce the duration of antibiotic treatment and overall healthcare costs. Additionally, economic evaluations suggest that the savings from reduced antibiotic use often compensate for the cost of PCT testing itself [10, 42, 43]. PCT testing supports timely decision-making when combined with clinical and microbiological assessments. In clinical practice, PCT testing can be integrated with existing diagnostic algorithms such as SOFA score assessments, microbiological cultures, and radiological findings to support early identification of infection and guide antibiotic stewardship decisions in patients with ACLF [43–45].

The diagnostic landscape in ACLF is rapidly evolving, yet our study’s focus on PCT did not fully contextualize its performance alongside other promising biomarkers. In a prospective cohort of 153 ACLF patients, Ign et al. (prospective cohort, n = 153 ACLF patients) demonstrated that both presepsin (cut-off ≥ 2300 pg/mL; AUROC 0.959, sensitivity 81.7%, specificity 92.7%) and PCT (cut-off ≥ 0.9 ng/mL; AUROC 0.909, sensitivity 80.3%, specificity 86.6%) were superior to CRP for early detection of infection in ACLF [46]. Similarly, Chen et al. reported that sTREM-1 and presepsin showed significantly higher diagnostic accuracy than CRP and PCT in patients with sepsis. Combining presepsin or sTREM-1 with the CLIF-SOFA score increased the diagnostic efficiency (AUC = 0.876 or AUC = 0.913) [29]. While our findings support PCT’s utility, future studies should directly compare PCT against established markers CRP, white blood cell count, lactate and emerging candidates such as presepsin, IL-6, IL-1Ra, IL-18, and TREM1 to identify the most reliable early indicators of sepsis in ACLF.

Our study has several limitations. Most patients were on antibiotic coverage, preventing us from analysing the independent effect of antibiotics on survival. The lack of a formal sample size calculation and power analysis limits confidence in the adequacy of our 135-patient cohort to assess the diagnostic accuracy of PCT. The single-centre design, with a predominance of alcohol-related ACLF, further restricts the generalizability of our findings to more diverse populations.

Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to determine whether PCT can effectively guide antibiotic use in patients without SIRS or sepsis, and whether this approach can prevent disease progression and improve clinical outcomes. In addition, previous studies report elevated PCT levels in acute and advanced chronic liver failure even without infection, likely due to bacterial translocation, hepatocyte injury, and resulting systemic inflammation [47]. PCT may also exert direct hepatotoxic effects [48], and further studies are needed to clarify these mechanisms. Future analyses should also incorporate variable selection techniques and assess collinearity to enhance the robustness of predictive models.

Conclusion

In our cohort of ACLF patients, SIRS and sepsis were highly prevalent and were significantly associated with 28-day mortality. Procalcitonin is a useful biomarker for identifying patients with SIRS and sepsis early and may be used as a guide in initiating or discontinuing antibiotics.

A comparison of baseline laboratory data revealed several significant differences between patients with and without sepsis. The median levels of TC, urea, sCr, PCT, and lactate and the international normalized ratio (INR) were significantly elevated in patients with sepsis (P value < 0.005). No significant differences were found in the PLT, neutrophil percentage, sodium level, T. Bil, AST, or ALT. However, the albumin level was significantly lower in sepsis patients than in non-sepsis patients (p = 0.000). Patients with sepsis had significantly higher disease severity scores. The median MELD score, AARC score, and CLIF SOFA score were also notably higher in sepsis patients than in non sepsis patients (p = 0.000). The 28-day mortality rate was significantly greater in sepsis patients, with 31 deaths (54.3%) compared with 24 deaths (30.7%) in the non-sepsis group (p = 0.002) (Table 3).

Comparative analysis of laboratory parameters and clinical scores among the no sepsis/SIRS, SIRS, and sepsis groups

Significant differences in laboratory parameters and clinical scores were observed between the non-sepsis/SIRS, SIRS, and sepsis with SIRS groups. The TLC markedly elevated, with a median of 7,000 cells/µL in the non-sepsis/SIRS group, 14,000 cells/µL in the SIRS group, and 14,600 cells/µL in the sepsis with SIRS group (p < 0.001). The percentage of neutrophils also high notably, with medians of 72.4% in the non-sepsis/SIRS group, 82.9% in the SIRS group, and 81.3% in the sepsis with SIRS group (p = 0.002), reflecting an inflammatory response.

The sCr level was significantly elevated, with a value of 0.84 mg/dL in the no sepsis/SIRS group compared with 1.7 mg/dL in the sepsis with SIRS group (p = 0.001). The T.Bil level was markedly greater in the SIRS group (23.92 mg/dL) than in the non-sepsis/SIRS group (10.35 mg/dL), and the T.Bil level was 13.04 mg/dL in the sepsis with SIRS group (p = 0.001), whereas the albumin level decreased to 2.3 g/dL in the sepsis with SIRS group compared with 2.8 g/dL in the non-sepsis/SIRS group (p = 0.001), indicating worsening liver function.

Procalcitonin (PCT) levels was high in the sepsis with SIRS group, reaching 2.07 ng/mL compared with 0.5 ng/mL in the non–SIRS/sepsis group (p < 0.001), supporting its role as a marker of severe infection. The clinical scores followed a similar trend, with the median MELD score from 27 in the nonseptic/SIRS group to 30 in both the SIRS and sepsis with SIRS groups (p < 0.001). The AARC and CLIF-SOFA scores were significantly elevated across the groups (p < 0.001). The 28-day mortality rates were 18.1% (10/55) in the non-SIRS/Sepsis group, 60.8% (14/23) in the SIRS-only group, and 54.3% (31/57) in the sepsis group, with a statistically significant difference among the groups (p < 0.05). Given the large number of comparisons, we acknowledge the high risk of Type I error. We applied Bonferroni correction (adjusted significance threshold p < 0.003 for 14 parameters) to reduce this risk. After correction, differences in WBC count, PCT, MELD, AARC score, and CLIF-SOFA score remained statistically significant (adjusted p < 0.003) (Table 4).

The course of ACLF in patients with no SIRS/sepsis at baseline

Among the 55 patients with no SIRS or sepsis at baseline, 17 patients (30.91%) developed new-onset SIRS by Day 7. Mortality at 28 days was significantly greater in patients with new-onset SIRS (6/17, 35.29%) than in those without SIRS (4/38, 10.53%) (p = 0.0186). Among the 17 patients with new-onset SIRS, 14 (82.35%) were diagnosed with sepsis by day 7. The mortality rate in the sepsis group was 21.43% (3/14), whereas none of the 3 patients without sepsis died. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.5147) (Table 6).

The course in patients with only SIRS at baseline

At baseline, 23 patients were diagnosed with only SIRS in the absence of sepsis. Upon reassessment on Day 7, 5 of these patients (21.74%) developed sepsis. All 5 of these patients (100%) died within 28 days. Among the remaining 18 patients (78.26%) who did not develop sepsis by Day 7, 9 patients (50%) died by Day 28.

Procalcitonin at baseline as a predictor for SIRS/Sepsis

An area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of 0.886 (95% CI: 0.83–0.94), with a sensitivity of 0.925 and a specificity of 0.72, was found to predict SIRS (Fig. 2A). The optimum critical concentration of baseline PCT in patients who developed SIRS was > 0.55. Upon bivariate logistic regression, PCT was an independent predictor of SIRS, with an odds ratio of 12.88 (95%CI:4.72–35.16; p-value:<0.001). An AUROC of 0.892 (95% CI: 0.83 to 0.94), with a sensitivity of 0.86 and a specificity of 0.67, was found to predict sepsis (Fig. 2B). The optimum critical concentration of PCT in patients who developed sepsis was > 1.087. Upon bivariate logistic regression, PCT was an independent predictor of sepsis, with an odds ratio of 7.57 (95% CI: 3.30–17.34; p-value: <0.001).
