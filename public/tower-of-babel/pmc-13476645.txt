Age-related prognostic disparities in cutaneous melanoma: A population-based study and validated interactive web-based nomogram for individualized prognostic stratification
Authors: Wenhao Xu, Li Zheng, Qinqin Chen, Ling Luo, Cui He, Haiming Chen, Jiaping Zhang, Zhuo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Older adults (≥65y) with cutaneous melanoma show significantly worse survival.•Acral lentiginous and nodular subtypes carry the poorest prognosis in elderly.•Wide excision plus SLNB significantly improves survival in older CM patients.•SEER analysis of 47,972 patients with PSM and IPTW-adjusted survival modeling.•Dynamic online nomogram provides validated personalized risk stratification.

Introduction

Melanoma is an aggressive malignancy of melanocytic origin and a major cause of skin-cancer mortality [1,2]. Cutaneous melanoma (CM) is the predominant subtype of melanoma arising in the skin [3]. Ultraviolet exposure, tumor thickness, ulceration, histological subtype, and stage contribute to its clinical heterogeneity. During the study era, systemic treatment changed substantially with the introduction of immune-checkpoint and BRAF/MEK-targeted therapies. The present analysis evaluates age-related prognosis using clinicopathological and treatment-coded variables available in SEER.

Observational studies show that older adults are more likely to present with thicker or more advanced tumors and poorer melanoma-specific outcomes than younger adults [4]. Chronological age may correlate with stage, comorbidity, frailty, life expectancy, and treatment access, but it is not a substitute for physiologic assessment [4,5]. Early-stage CM can often be treated effectively with surgery, while management of older adults should incorporate function, goals, and shared decision-making.

Previous studies reported age-related differences in melanoma management [[6], [7], [8]]. Surgical approach, nodal staging, and systemic treatment patterns may vary with stage, frailty, and access to care. Characterizing their observed prognostic associations alongside histology and stage can improve risk assessment and support personalized clinical discussions in older adults.

This study quantified age-related survival disparities across histological and registry-coded treatment categories and developed an interactive web-based nomogram for individualized CSS estimation in older adults. The model was evaluated in training, internal-validation, and independent external-validation cohorts.

Methods and materials

Population and data collection

This retrospective cohort study used clinicopathological data from the American Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, complemented by data from the Southwest Hospital in China.

The SEER database encompasses data for roughly 36.7% of cancer patients in the USA and captures follow-up and selected treatment fields (https://seer.cancer.gov).

The database was accessed through the SEER statistical software and website.

The inclusion criteria mandated a primary lesion site characterized as skin and a pathologically verified diagnosis of melanoma. The exclusion criteria were missing histology, lymph-node dissection scope (LNDS), or TNM-related stage fields, age <20 years, unspecified procedures, incomplete survival data or survival <1 month, and multiple primary tumor sites. The SEER analytic dataset was assembled by complete-case filtering for these required fields. No imputation was performed before the age-stratified analyses or model split.

The study encompassed 47,684 SEER patients, including 28,739 patients younger than 65 years and 18,945 patients aged 65 years or older (Fig. 1). The independent Southwest Hospital cohort (n = 288) was retained as a separate validation set and was not included in SEER denominators, PSM, or IPTW analyses.

The enrollment ledger shown in Fig. 1 is: initial SEER records diagnosed during 2000–2020 (N = 376,017), followed by exclusions for age <20 years (n = 558), unknown TNM stage (n = 227,178), unknown histology (n = 65,771), unknown surgery code (n = 387), unknown lymph-node examination (n = 140), more than one primary tumor (n = 32,235), and survival <1 month (n = 2064), leaving 47,684 patients.

The age-group counts were 28,739 younger and 18,945 older patients (Fig. 1). Data collected for each individual comprised age, sex, race/ethnicity, year of diagnosis, laterality, primary tumor site, ulceration, lymph-node dissection scope, TNM-related stage fields, histological type, radiation therapy, surgical procedure, chemotherapy, vital status, and survival duration in months. Staging was interpreted using the diagnosis-year-appropriate SEER/AJCC framework: AJCC 5th-era fields for 2000–2002, AJCC 6th-era/Collaborative-Stage-derived fields for 2003–2009, AJCC 7th-edition-derived or combined-stage recodes for 2010–2017, and EOD 2018-derived fields compatible with the AJCC 8th schema for 2018–2020. We checked edition-specific T1 thickness/ulceration and nodal satellite/in-transit descriptors before retaining only the common broad categories T1–T4, N0–N3, and M0–M1 for modeling. Edition-specific substages and stage-group labels were not used. The diagnosis-year mapping and harmonization rule are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (Part B).

The 65-year threshold defined the epidemiologic age-group comparison and PSM. Within the older-adult nomogram, age was retained as a continuous covariate (β = 0.040,078 per year, HR = 1.0409, 95% CI 1.0318–1.0500) across the deployed 65–90-year input range, preserving age-related variation within older adults. LNDS was categorized as no dissection, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB), or complete lymph node dissection (CLND).

The surgical classifications were based on SEER site-specific surgery codes. These categories represent the recorded procedure and were interpreted in relation to stage and selection factors rather than as guideline-defined margin comparisons.

No individual re-staging was performed. We retained the diagnosis-year-appropriate SEER-derived TNM field and harmonized only the common broad categories used in the analysis. No earlier-edition value was relabeled as an AJCC 8th-edition value.

Comparison of the outcomes between younger and older patients

Based on the threshold of 65 years, patients were stratified into younger and older cohorts.

Clinical characteristics of the younger and older groups were assessed with chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. A 1:1 propensity-score matching (PSM) model was used to reduce baseline differences between groups.

Matching variables included laterality, sex, primary tumor site, ulceration, race, TNM stage, histological type, and therapeutic pattern. The caliper was 0.00,001.

After PSM, we reassessed the clinicopathological characteristics of the patients by age.

To estimate cancer-specific survival (CSS) across diverse groups, Kaplan–Meier curves were applied and log-rank tests were used to compare survival outcomes.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. Hypothesis tests were two-sided with P ≤ 0.05, and exact P values were retained where available. Absolute SMD < 0.10 was interpreted as acceptable covariate balance, whereas SMD ≥ 0.10 indicated residual imbalance rather than statistical significance. Chi-square tests were used when expected cell counts were adequate and Fisher’s exact test when expected counts were small.

Comparison of outcomes according to histological types and treatment approaches in older patients

To evaluate outcomes across histological types and therapeutic approaches, we used inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting (IPTW) before Kaplan–Meier analyses.

Covariates included age, sex, laterality, race, primary site, TNM stage, ulceration, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy.

Unlike PSM, IPTW retains the original sample size. The IPTW analysis was performed with the “ipw” package in R.

The IPTW-weighted estimates were interpreted alongside the unweighted results.

Identification of independent prognostic factors and development of an interactive prognostic nomogram

The primary endpoint was cancer-specific mortality. We defined CSS as the time from diagnosis of CM to cancer-specific mortality. To develop the interactive prognostic nomogram, the older SEER cohort was randomly split into a training set and an internal-validation set using a 7:3 ratio. The independent Southwest Hospital cohort (n = 288) was designated the external-validation cohort. The training set contained 13,261 patients and 1045 cancer-specific events, with 26 fitted model degrees of freedom. This corresponded to 40.2 events per fitted degree of freedom. The final unpenalized Cox model was h(t|X) = h₀(t) exp(ΣβⱼXⱼ), with sex, primary site, T stage, N stage, M stage, continuous age, histology, ulceration, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, and lymph-node dissection as predictors. The coefficient vector, hazard ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and P values are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (Part A). No shrinkage or penalization was applied.

Candidate predictors with P < 0.05 in univariable Cox regression entered the multivariable model, and variables retaining P < 0.05 were included in the nomogram. Model performance was evaluated in the internal- and external-validation cohorts to assess potential optimism from data-driven selection.

The nomogram was deployed as an interactive Shiny tool (https://onepiece.shinyapps.io/MelanomainElderly/). It provides continuous age input over 65–90 years and displays individualized 12-, 36-, and 60-month CSS estimates. The model equation and coefficient set are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (Part A).

Evaluation of the nomogram performance

To evaluate prediction performance, we used receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration plots, and decision-curve analysis (DCA) at 1-, 3-, and 5-year horizons in the training, internal-validation, and external-validation cohorts. Ten-fold cross-validation summarized training-cohort AUCs, and the training model summary reported concordance 0.870 (SE 0.005). Calibration plots assessed agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. DCA was plotted across risk thresholds from 0 to 1 with standard treat-all and treat-none comparators. Time-specific AUCs were reported as point estimates and interpreted together with concordance, calibration, and DCA.

To compare the prognostic model with AJCC staging, we calculated NRI using Kaplan–Meier and inverse-probability-weighted approaches. Table 2 reports the available point estimates and 95% confidence intervals at 12, 36, and 60 months. Because the analysis did not prespecify clinical risk bins, the KM/IPTW estimates were not labeled as categorical NRI and were interpreted together with discrimination, calibration, and DCA.

An NRI estimate was considered positive when its 95% confidence-interval lower bound exceeded zero. NRI was interpreted alongside discrimination, calibration, and DCA.

Primary tumors were more common on the face (17.6%vs. 7.9%), upper limb, and shoulder (28.2%vs. 25.7%).

Older patients more often presented with advanced T stage: T3 occurred in 12.8% versus 8.8%, and T4 in 12.7% versus 7.1%.

Older patients were more frequently diagnosed with lentigo maligna melanoma (19.3%vs. 7%).

Nodular melanoma was also more frequent in older patients (19.1%vs. 13.5%), whereas superficial spreading melanoma was less frequent (53.5%vs. 75.2%).

The survival probability at 1, 3, and 5 years was 97.8%, 89.2%, and 84.3%, respectively, in older patients, and 99.1%, 95.4%, and 93.6%, respectively, in younger patients.

After 1:1 PSM, 12,859 patients in each age group were included in the survival analysis.

No statistically significant differences were identified (P > 0.05 or SMD < 0.10), indicating good covariate balance. After matching, 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 99.2%, 94.7%, and 91.5% in older patients and 99.6%, 97.0%, and 95.2% in younger patients, respectively.

Comparison of survival according to histological type

After 1:1 PSM, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted in 12,859 older and 12,859 younger patients.

Across histological types, older patients generally had poorer survival for superficial spreading melanoma, lentigo maligna melanoma, spindle melanoma, and nodular melanoma.

Among the histological subtypes, lentigo maligna melanoma showed the most favorable survival, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 99.5%, 98.0%, and 96.9%, respectively.

Superficial spreading melanoma followed, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 99.1%, 96.3%, and 94.2%, respectively.

Intermediate survival was observed for desmoplastic and spindle-cell melanomas.

Conversely, nodular melanoma and acral lentiginous melanoma had the poorest survival outcomes. Their 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 95.8%, 80.0%, and 70.6% for nodular melanoma, and 92.9%, 77.1%, and 68.6% for acral lentiginous melanoma, respectively (Fig. 2B). The difference in survival among the histological types may reflect variation in baseline TNM stage.

Acral lentiginous melanoma and nodular melanoma frequently presented with a higher proportion of advanced TNM stages (the histology-stratified analysis). After IPTW weighting, patients with lentigo maligna melanoma and superficial spreading melanoma showed the most favorable survival outcomes.

The 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 99.5%, 98.2%, and 97.1% for lentigo maligna melanoma and 98.9%, 95.9%, and 93.7% for superficial spreading melanoma, respectively.

Desmoplastic melanoma and spindle melanoma showed intermediate survival, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year rates of 97.4%, 89.1%, and 84.6% for desmoplastic melanoma and 95.6%, 87.9%, and 81.2% for spindle melanoma, respectively.

After IPTW weighting, the acral lentiginous melanoma subtype displayed a marginally superior survival trend compared with the pre-IPTW estimate and fell between spindle and nodular melanoma.

Its 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 96.3%, 85.2%, and 78.8%, respectively.

The poorest survival remained associated with nodular melanoma, with rates of 93.0%, 77.8%, and 69.8% at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 2C).

Comparison of survival according to the surgical approach and extent of lymph node dissection

In the registry-coded surgical categories, gross excision and wide excision had different survival distributions between older and younger patients (P < 0.001). Local excision (P = 0.200) and no recorded surgery (P = 0.210) had similar age-stratified trends (Fig. 3A). Before IPTW weighting, gross excision was associated with the longest observed survival, followed by no recorded surgery, wide excision, and local excision (Fig. 3B). Identification of independent prognostic factors and development of a prognostic nomogram

Multivariable analysis identified male sex, older age, tumors of the scalp, neck, lower limb, hip, or trunk, acral lentiginous histology, ulceration, absence of lymph-node resection, advanced TNM stage, local excision, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy as independent prognostic factors for CSS. Relative to T1, T4 was associated with HR 7.17 (95% CI 5.61–9.18), M1 with HR 2.24 (95% CI 1.70–2.94), and ulceration with HR 2.17 (95% CI 1.89–2.49). For the internal-validation cohort, ROC values at 12, 36, and 60 months were 0.870, 0.873, and 0.875, respectively (Fig. 5D). For the external-validation cohort, the corresponding values were 0.871, 0.845, and 0.837, respectively (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

Contemporary treatment and geriatric context. The SEER accrual period overlaps a transition from cytotoxic-era care to anti–PD-1 therapy, combined CTLA-4/PD-1 blockade, BRAF/MEK inhibition for BRAF V600 disease, and later PD-1/LAG-3 blockade. Long-term trials report durable benefit for nivolumab–ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, and nivolumab–relatlimab, although their selected populations underrepresent frail adults aged ≥75 years [[9], [10], [11]]. BRAF/MEK therapy is genotype-dependent and biologically distinct from immune-checkpoint therapy, and randomized sequencing data support an immunotherapy-first strategy for many patients with BRAF V600-mutant advanced melanoma [12]. Geriatric assessment should consider function, comorbidity, cognition, nutrition, polypharmacy, and social support [5,13]. Recent pan-cancer meta-analyses inform monitoring of immune-related adverse events, nutrition/appetite, infusion reactions, and electrolytes [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]].

Treatment-field sparsity is notable: chemotherapy was coded for 173/18,945 older patients (0.9%) and 426/28,739 younger patients (1.5%), while radiotherapy was coded for 305/18,945 older patients (1.6%) and 328/28,739 younger patients (1.1%). These registry fields are not proxies for modern immune-checkpoint or BRAF/MEK exposure. Omission may be less consequential in surgery-dominant early disease but may materially affect advanced-stage calibration, with direction dependent on treatment era, access, and indication.

Future geriatric versions can extend the clinicopathological model with a validated G8 or VES-13 screening score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, ECOG performance status, and measures of function, cognition, and nutrition to refine treatment-tolerance assessment and calibration.

Molecular and liquid-biopsy context. BRAF V600, NRAS, KIT, tumor mutational burden, PD-L1, immune signatures, and serial circulating-tumor-DNA measurements were not available in SEER and were not evaluated in this model. Recent transcriptomic and experimental work indicates that NMNAT3-mediated NAD+ metabolism influences melanoma-cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, complementing existing biomarker and ctDNA evidence [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Future prospective versions can combine baseline clinicopathological risk with validated molecular markers and serial tumor-informed ctDNA for molecular-residual-disease monitoring, followed by independent calibration, discrimination, and decision-curve evaluation.

Clinical use and competing risks. The nomogram provides individualized 12-, 36-, and 60-month CSS estimates and can support surveillance discussions and shared decision-making when interpreted with stage, geriatric assessment, and guidelines. Because cause-specific Kaplan–Meier and Cox analyses censor other-cause deaths, future validation should incorporate competing-risk modeling and interpret reclassification and clinical-utility results using established NRI and DCA guidance [[24], [25], [26], [27]].

Taken together, these considerations position the nomogram as an externally validated clinicopathological tool for individualized CSS estimation in older adults.

Melanoma incidence has increased in recent decades. Older adults, particularly older White men, are a high-risk group, and the growing burden has important implications for healthcare systems serving older populations [1,4].

Traditionally, advanced age has been recognized as an independent prognostic predictor related to unfavorable outcomes in patients with CM. This study revealed that the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of older adults with CM were 97.8%, 89.2%, and 84.3%, respectively, all of which were lower than in younger adults, who showed survival rates of 99.1%, 95.4%, and 93.6%, respectively. This discrepancy may be attributed to several risk factors that are frequently associated with CM in older adults. These include a higher prevalence of thicker melanomas and the presence of ulceration, an increased proportion of male patients, and poorer histological types such as nodular melanoma and acral lentiginous melanoma [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. The findings of this research align with those of prior investigations.

After covariate adjustment and weighting, older age remained associated with poorer observed prognosis. SLNB and gross or wide excision categories were associated with longer observed survival. These registry associations should not be interpreted as causal. Randomized evidence has not shown a melanoma-specific survival benefit from immediate completion lymph-node dissection after a positive sentinel-node biopsy [32].

Histological subtype was associated with survival in older adults in both the unweighted and IPTW-weighted analyses. Nodular and acral lentiginous melanoma had poorer observed survival, whereas the favorable estimate for lentigo maligna melanoma may reflect stage distribution, anatomic site, growth pattern, and earlier detection.

These findings reinforce the importance of early diagnostic evaluation in older adults [[33], [34], [35]].

Prior population-based melanoma nomograms have been reported. Machine-learning survival models can accommodate nonlinear effects and interactions, whereas the Cox nomogram used here retains an explicit coefficient-based equation. In a SEER-based lung papillary adenocarcinoma study, ensemble and Cox models showed good discrimination and calibration, with random survival forest and gradient boosting survival models providing more consistent long-term predictions. In a pulmonary sarcomatoid carcinoma study, a population-based CSS nomogram was evaluated in an independent Southwest Hospital cohort, compared with TNM staging using NRI, and deployed as a web predictor [[36], [37], [38]]. Many molecular signatures require assays unavailable in SEER [[39], [40], [41]]. This study contributes an age-stratified registry analysis that combines PSM/IPTW comparisons with an independently validated interactive nomogram based on routinely available variables. Consistent performance across the training, internal-validation, and Southwest Hospital cohorts supports its use for individualized prognostic communication and surveillance planning. Further head-to-head comparison and expanded calibration and clinical-utility analyses will define its relative performance across settings.

The retrospective design may introduce selection bias and residual confounding despite PSM and IPTW. SEER does not provide Breslow thickness, mitotic rate, lymphovascular invasion, molecular alterations, detailed ICI/BRAF–MEK exposure and response, comorbidity, frailty, or geriatric-assessment domains. External validation used a single-center 288-patient Chinese cohort. Multicenter validation with competing-risk analysis and additional performance-uncertainty measures will further assess generalizability [42].

Conclusion

This large population-based study defines age-related CSS disparities in CM and identifies advanced stage, nodular and acral lentiginous histology, and other clinicopathological factors associated with poorer outcomes in older adults. The independently validated web-based nomogram integrates routinely available variables to provide individualized 12-, 36-, and 60-month CSS estimates. These results support prognostic communication and surveillance planning, while further multicenter and competing-risk validation will extend clinical applicability.
