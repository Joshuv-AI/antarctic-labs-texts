Discrimination of arbitrary unknown quantum channels and measurements
Marcin Markiewicz, Łukasz Pawela, Zbigniew Puchała

Abstract
In this work we provide an efficiency analysis of the problem of discrimination of two randomly chosen unknown quantum operations in the single-shot regime. We provide tight bounds for the success probability of such a protocol for arbitrary quantum channels and generalized measurements.



1
Introduction and motivation

Discrimination of unknown quantum operations is a task in which one is given an
ability to apply two given operations with specified input and output dimensions
on arbitrary quantum state, and decide on whether they are identical or distinct
based on final measurement outcomes. Several classes of channels have been
already analyzed in this context (unitary channels
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, mixed unitary channels
[
4
]
,
von Neumann measurements
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
,
however there are no known results regarding discrimination of

arbitrary
channels and measurements in the most general discrimination scenario. Partial results on this issue are known only for
the scenario of unambiguous discrimination of general measurements
[
8
,
9
]
, in which one assumes that the protocol in each run gives either
perfectly correct answer or gives no answer at all. On the other hand in the scenario discussed in this work one assumes that the algorithm gives definite answer
in each run, however the answer can be erroneous in some percentage of runs.

Although any completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) channel can be
seen from the
dilation
perspective as a unitary channel on an extended
Hilbert space, the specific optimal performance of discrimination task
significantly depends on the dimension of the extension and on the way of
sampling the unknown channel, therefore it cannot be directly reduced to the
unitary case itself.

In this work we provide analysis of optimal performance of discrimination of
unknown quantum channels and a related issue of unknown generalized quantum
measurements in the single-shot scenario. The work is divided into two parts. Firstly we analyze the
performance of discrimination of two random channels as a function of
dimensions, which characterize it. In the second part we analyze discrimination
of two random POVM-type measurements, based on results on discrimination of
arbitrary channels. This can be done since a random POVM measurement, treated as
a channel with classical output, can be represented as a composition of a random
channel with the dephasing channel. Our results provide the most general solution
to the discrimination problem of two quantum operations in the single-shot regime.

2
Discrimination of unknown channels

2.1
Symmetric discrimination

Let us consider a scenario in which we are given two black boxes, each
containing an unknown quantum channel, with specified input and output
dimensions as respectively
d_{i}
and
d_{o}
. Our goal is to determine whether the
boxes contain the same operation or a different ones,
and provide a bound on the probability of
correct guessing in this scenario. For a sketch see Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
A
schematic representation of the single-shot symmetric discrimination scheme.
Note that the scheme allows for usage of an additional quantum register
untouched by both channels.

A bound on the probability of correct distinction between two channels
\Phi

and
\Psi
can be bounded by the diamond norm:

p^{s}_{H}(\Phi,\Psi)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\Phi-\Psi\right\|_{\diamond},

(1)

which can be seen as an old Holevo-Helstrom result
[
10
,
11
]
restated using Kitaev’s diamond norm
[
12
]
.
Let us denote the set of
d\times d
complex matrices by
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.
For a Hermiticity-preserving
\Xi:\mathcal{M}_{d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{M}_{d_{o}}(\mathbb{C})
with a Choi
representation
J(\Xi)\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{o}}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
:

J(\Xi)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{i}}\Xi(\ket{i}\!\bra{j})\otimes\ket{i}\!\bra{j}

we can
provide an upper bound on the diamond norm
[
13
]
:

\left\|\Xi\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\left\|\Tr_{d_{o}}\left|J(\Xi)\right|\right\|.

(2)

Since our task is to find performance of discrimination between
unknown

channels, we have to define a way of sampling such channels. To start, let us define an arbitrary
channel
\Phi_{U}^{(s)}:\mathcal{M}_{d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{M}_{d_{o}}(\mathbb{C})
via the Stinespring dilation with extension denoted as
s
:

\Phi_{U}^{(s)}(\rho)=\Tr_{s}U\rho U^{\dagger}=\Tr_{s}\Phi_{U}(\rho),

(3)

where the input state
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
,
U
is an isometry operation such that:

U:\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{o}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{s},

(4)

and
\Phi_{U}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}
denotes the corresponding isometry channel. Hence, a
random quantum channel
\Phi_{U}^{(s)}
defined in this manner can be seen as a
composition of a random isometry with a partial trace:

\Phi_{U}^{(s)}=\Tr_{s}\circ\,\Phi_{U}.

(5)

Now we can define the measure
\mu_{\Phi_{U}^{(s)}}
on the set of quantum channels to be derived from to the
Haar measure
\mu^{s}_{d_{i}d_{o}}
on the set of random isometries
(
4
), which can be obtained as a measure on random truncated unitaries

\textrm{U}(sd_{o})

[
14
]
. Measure
\mu^{s}_{d_{i}d_{o}}
is a function of three dimensions:
d_{i}
,

d_{o}
and
s
which define the isometry (
4
). For the sake of clarity of further derivations we will denote the volume differential
\differential\mu^{s}_{d_{i}d_{o}}
corresponding to the measure

\mu^{s}_{d_{i}d_{o}}
as simply
\differential U
.

When considering a quantum channel
\Phi
via Stinespring representation
one needs to assume
sd_{o}\geq d_{i}
for the channel to be trace preserving.
At the same time with the same assumption but different
interpretation of
s
one can define two another
measures on the set of quantum channels
[
15
]
,
via random Choi matrices and random Krauss operators. As shown in
[
15
]

all these three ways of sampling random quantum channels are equivalent, in a sense that they
define the same measure on the set of channels. For convenience in this work we solely
utilise the Stinespring version.

In the symmetric discrimination scenario we are discriminating between application of random channels

\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{U}^{(s)}
(identical ones) and
\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{V}^{(s)}
(distinct ones),
drawn according to the measure
\mu^{s}_{d_{i}d_{o}}
.
Such averaged channels are represented by the integrals
\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U
and
\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V

respectively. Hence, in order to find the Holevo-Helstrom
bound (
1
) for the probability
p^{s}_{H}\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U,\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)
, we need to find the value of the following expression:

\left\|\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U-\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right\|_{\diamond}.

(6)

In order to provide the diamond norm bounds we need the Choi
representation of both the channels in (
6
).
For now, we will focus on
the first channel. As the only random part of the channel
\Phi_{U}^{(s)}
(
5
) is the isometry
\Phi_{U}
,
from
[
7
,
16
]
we have:

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}\left(\int\Phi_{U}\otimes\Phi_{U}\differential U\right)&=\frac{1}{(d_{o}s)^{2}-1}\left({\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{(d_{o}s)^{2}}\otimes{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}+S_{(d_{o}s),(d_{o}s)}\otimes S_{d_{i},d_{i}}\right)+\\
&-\frac{1}{(d_{o}s)\left((d_{o}s)^{2}-1\right)}\left({\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{(d_{o}s)^{2}}\otimes S_{d_{i},d_{i}}+S_{(d_{o}s),(d_{o}s)}\otimes{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}\right),\end{split}

(7)

where
S_{a,b}
denotes a swap operation between a
b
-dimensional system and an

a
-dimensional system. Composing this Choi matrix with the Choi representation
of the partial trace via the notion of link
product
[
17
]
we obtain a simple relation:

\mathcal{J}\left(\Tr_{s}\circ\,\Phi_{U}\right)=\Tr_{s}\mathcal{J}(\Phi_{U}),

(8)

which gives, after straightforward yet tedious computations:

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{d_{o}^{2}d_{i}^{2}}(\mathbb{C})\ni\mathcal{J}\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\!\otimes\!\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U\right)&=\frac{1}{(d_{o}s)^{2}-1}\left(s^{2}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}\otimes{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}+sS_{d_{o},d_{o}}\otimes S_{d_{i},d_{i}}\right)+\\
&-\frac{1}{(d_{o}s)\left((d_{o}s)^{2}-1\right)}\left(s^{2}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}\otimes S_{d_{i},d_{i}}+sS_{d_{o},d_{o}}\otimes{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(9)

As for the channel
\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V
we follow the same
reasoning as in
[
7
]
:

\mathcal{J}\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)=\frac{1}{d_{o}^{2}}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}d_{i}^{2}}.

(10)

Finally, in order to apply the bound (
2
), we need to evaluate the absolute value operator of

J\equiv\mathcal{J}\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U\right)-\mathcal{J}\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)

Consider a matrix
W=S_{d_{o},d_{o}}\otimes S_{d_{i},d_{i}}
. As shown in
Appendix
A
we can construct a polar decomposition
of the matrix
J
such that we have
WJ=|J|
.
Hence we have:

\displaystyle\Tr_{d_{o}}WJ

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr_{d_{o}}|J|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s(d_{o}^{2}-1)}{d_{o}^{2}s^{2}-1}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}-\frac{d_{o}^{2}-1}{d_{o}\left(d_{o}^{2}s^{2}-1\right)}S_{d_{i},d_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(d_{o}^{2}-1)}{d_{o}(s^{2}d_{o}^{2}-1)}\left(sd_{o}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}-S_{d_{i},d_{i}}\right).

Using the fact that
\|\alpha{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}-S\|=1+\alpha,\;\alpha>0
, we finally obtain:

\left\|\Tr_{d_{o}}\left|J\right|\right\|=\frac{d_{o}^{2}-1}{d_{o}^{2}s-d_{o}}

(11)

Hence, we get the Holevo-Helstrom bound for symmetric discrimination
between random channels:

\displaystyle p^{s}_{H}\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U,\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{d_{o}^{2}-1}{d_{o}^{2}s-d_{o}}.

(12)

It turns out, that the above bound for probability of correct distinction can be saturated using the following input state:

\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}}\ni\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{01}-\ket{10}\right).

(13)

Appendix
B
shows the detailed proof of the
bound (
12
). What is noteworthy here is that our bound does not
explicitly depend on the input dimension
d_{i}
. However, we made an assumption
that
d_{i}\geq 2
when proving the saturation of the upper bound. Moreover note that
due to the fact that the bound is saturated by a bipartite state (
13
),
there is no need to utilize additional ancillary systems in the optimal discrimination procedure
apart from the ones on which the two channels act (see the lower register in the Fig.
1
). Finally let us mention that
for
d_{o}=2
and
s=1
, namely for the case of discriminating
between two unknown unitary channels, we obtain
p^{s}_{H}=\frac{7}{8}
, which
reproduces the famous result by Hillery et. al.
[
1
]
, see also
[
2
]
.

In the case of trivial input space, we can think about our channels
\Phi_{U}^{(s)}
and

\Phi_{V}^{(s)}
as operations that produce quantum states. In this case the upper bound
is not achieved however, we can calculate the diamond norm directly as

\|J\|_{1}
. This is easily seen as the input space is trivial, hence no entanglement with the ancillary system can help us achieve greater separation. We get the following:

p^{s}_{H}|(d_{i}=1)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|J\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{d_{o}^{2}-1}{d_{o}\left(d_{o}s+1\right)}.

(14)

2.2
Asymmetric discrimination

Here we focus on an approach different from the Holevo-Helstrom one and move
to a framework based on hypothesis testing. We introduce the hypoteses:

\begin{split}H_{0}:&\;\mathrm{operations}\;\mathrm{are}\;\mathrm{identical,}\\
H_{1}:&\;\mathrm{operations}\;\mathrm{are}\;\mathrm{different.}\end{split}

Along with these, we introduce two possible types of errors:

•

The operations in the black boxes are the same, however our discrimination
scheme stated that they were different. This is the so-called type-I error. We
will denote its probability by
p_{\textrm{I}}
.

•

The operations were differenct, however our discrimination scheme stated
that they are identical. This is the so-called type-II error. We will denote its
probability by
p_{\textrm{II}}
.

To estimate the above two errors we assume some initial state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}}
, and that we perform a final binary measurement on
the output state of the black boxes with effects
\{\Omega,{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}-\Omega\}
. The outcome
associated with
\Omega
represents accepting the
null hypothesis
,
which states that measurements are the same,
whereas
{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}-\Omega
indicates acceptance of the
alternative hypothesis
, stating that
they are different. We assume the same channel sampling method as in the
symmetric case. Then the error probabilities in asymmetric discrimination read:

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{I}}=\Tr((\id-\Omega)\rho_{\textrm{id}})=1-\Tr(\Omega\rho_{\textrm{id}}),

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{II}}=\Tr(\Omega\rho_{\textrm{dif}}).

(15)

The output states
\rho_{\textrm{id}}
and
\rho_{\textrm{dif}}
represent
outputs of applying respectively the same and different averaged channels on
the initial state
\ket{\psi}
:

\begin{split}\rho_{\textrm{id}}=&\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U\right)\left(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\right)=\\
=&\frac{s(d_{o}s-\bra{\psi}S\ket{\psi})}{d_{o}(s^{2}d_{o}^{2}-1)}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}+\frac{sd_{o}\bra{\psi}S\ket{\psi}}{d_{o}(s^{2}d_{o}^{2}-1)}S_{d_{o},d_{o}}\\
\rho_{\textrm{dif}}=&\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)\left(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\right)=\\
=&\frac{1}{d_{o}^{2}}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}.\end{split}

Assuming a completely antisymmetric state, such that
\bra{\psi}S\ket{\psi}=-1
, we have:

\rho_{\textrm{id}}=\frac{1}{d_{o}(sd_{o}-1)}\left(s{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}-S_{d_{o},d_{o}}\right).

(16)

Taking
\Omega=\frac{1}{2}\left({\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}-S_{d_{o},d_{o}}\right)
we get error
probabilities of type-I and type-II errors:

\begin{split}p_{\textrm{I}}=&\frac{\left(d_{o}+1\right)\left(s-1\right)}{2\left(sd_{o}-1\right)},\\
p_{\textrm{II}}=&\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2d_{o}}.\end{split}

(17)

There is an important relation between discussed two
error probabilities
p_{\textrm{I}}
and
p_{\textrm{II}}

in the asymmetric scheme and the error probability
in the symmetric scheme specified by the Holevo-Helstrom bound, namely we have:

\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{\textrm{I}}+p_{\textrm{II}}\right)\geq 1-p^{s}_{H},

(18)

in which
p^{s}_{H}
is the optimal success probability of a symmetric
discrimination protocol. In our case we have:

\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{\textrm{I}}+p_{\textrm{II}}\right)=\frac{1}{4}\left(2-\frac{d_{o}^{2}-1}{d_{o}\left(sd_{o}-1\right)}\right),

(19)

which exactly matches the lower bound on
1-p^{s}_{H}
, as directly inferred from the upper bound (
12
). This implies that the proposed
measurement setup for asymmetric discrimination is optimal.

3
Discrimination of unknown POVMs

In this section we shall focus on a subset of all quantum channels and limit the
setting shown in Fig.
1
to the case when we are given the
promise that the unknown operations are some POVMs.

3.1
Symmetric discrimination

As shown in
[
14
]
a Haar random POVM can be defined using random
quantum channels as defined in (
3
).
Let us state here this
correspondence precisely in analogy to the case of
d
-outcome von Neumann
measurements, which can be seen as compositions of a dephasing map and a random
unitary channel
[
7
]
:

\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{vN}}=\Delta\circ\Phi_{\operatorname{U}},

(20)

in which the completely dephasing channel is specified as follows:

\Delta:\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}),\;\rho\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bra{i}\rho\ket{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}.

(21)

Note that we denote unitary operations by normal font
\operatorname{U}
,
whereas isometries are denoted by italic font as
U
, therefore
\Phi_{\operatorname{U}}
represents a unitary channel, whereas

\Phi_{U}
represents the isometry channel.
The composition (
20
) can be used to define a measure on the set of
von Neumann measurements to be just the Haar measure on the unitary group.

Arbitrary POVM measurement with
d_{o}
outcomes can be
defined as the following quantum channel with classical output:

\mathcal{P}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{o}}\Tr(M_i\rho)\outerproduct{i}{i},

(22)

in which operators
M_{i}
are POVM elements (sometimes called
effects
).
Following
[
14
]
we define Haar-random POVM by taking the following effects generated with Haar-random
isometries
U
(
4
):

M_{i}^{U,s}=U^{\dagger}(\outerproduct{i}{i}\otimes{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{s})U.

(23)

Then the following holds:

Proposition 1
.

A POVM channel of the form:

\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{o}}\Tr(M^{U,s}_i\rho)\outerproduct{i}{i},

(24)

with effects of the form (
23
), can be represented as:

\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}=\Delta\circ\Phi^{(s)}_{U}.

(25)

Proof.

Let us rewrite the expression for
\Tr(M^{U,s}_i\rho)
:

\displaystyle\Tr(M^{U,s}_i\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr(U^{\dagger}(\proj{i}\otimes\id_s)U\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr((\proj{i}\otimes\id_s)U\rho U^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr(\sum_{j=1}^s(\proj{i}\otimes\proj{j})U\rho U^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{i}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\bra{j}U\rho U^{\dagger}\ket{j}\ket{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{i}\Tr_{s}U\rho U^{\dagger}\ket{i}=\bra{i}\Phi_{U}^{(s)}(\rho)\ket{i}.

Hence we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d_{o}}\Tr(M^{U,s}_i\rho)\outerproduct{i}{i}

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d_{o}}\bra{i}\Phi_{U}^{(s)}(\rho)\ket{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(\Phi_{U}^{(s)}(\rho))=\Delta\circ\Phi_{U}^{(s)}(\rho).

∎

Due to the relation (
25
), we can sample random POVMs according to the same measure used previously to sample arbitrary random quantum channels,
namely according to the Haar measure on the isometries (
4
).
In full analogy to the case of symmetric discrimination between two unknown random
channels we effectively discriminate between the averaged channels
\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\differential U
and
\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V
.
Based on the above proposition, in order to characterize symmetric discrimination of
unknown POVMs via the Holevo-Helstrom bound for the success probability

p^{s}_{H}\left(\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\differential U,\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)
, we need to calculate the diamond norm:

\displaystyle\left\|\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\differential U-\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right\|_{\diamond}=

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\Delta\otimes\Delta\right)\circ\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U-\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\Phi_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)\right\|_{\diamond}

Following the same reasoning as in Section
2
we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\left(\Delta\otimes\Delta\right)\circ\left(\int\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\Phi_{U}^{(s)}\differential U\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{\left(sd_{o}\right)^{2}-1}\bigg(s{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}\otimes{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}+T_{d_{o},d_{o}}\otimes S_{d_{i},d_{i}}+

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{sd_{o}}\left(s{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}\otimes S_{d_{i},d_{i}}+T_{d_{o},d_{o}}\otimes{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{i}^{2}}\right)\bigg).

where
T_{d,d}=\Delta(S_{d,d})
. On the other hand, due to (
10
), we easily obtain:

\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\otimes\mathcal{P}^{(s)}_{V}\differential U\differential V=\frac{1}{d_{o}^{2}}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}d_{i}^{2}}.

(27)

Again, we introduce the difference of the Choi representations of the two discriminated channels:

J_{\mathcal{P}}\equiv\mathcal{J}\left(\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\differential U\right)-\mathcal{J}\left(\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)

Using a similar approach to the one described
in
[
7
]
we arrive at:

\Tr_{d_{o}}|J_{\mathcal{P}}|=\frac{2(d_{o}-1)}{d_{o}(s^{2}d_{o}^{2}-1)}\left(sd_{o}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}-S\right),

(28)

and a bound for the norm:

\displaystyle\left\|\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\differential U-\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Tr_{d_{o}}|J_{\mathcal{P}}|\right\|=2\frac{d_{o}-1}{d_{o}\left(sd_{o}-1\right)}.

(29)

Hence, we get:

\displaystyle p^{s}_{H}\left(\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\differential U,\int\mathcal{P}_{U}^{(s)}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{P}_{V}^{(s)}\differential U\differential V\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d_{o}-1}{d_{o}\left(sd_{o}-1\right)}.

(30)

Again, the above bound for probability of correct distinction can be saturated using the maximally antisymmetric input state:

\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}}\ni\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{01}-\ket{10}\right).

(31)

The reasoning is similar to that presented in
[
7
]
. Note that for
s=1
, which corresponds
to the case of discriminating von Neumann measurements, we exactly restore the bound
found in
[
7
]
.

In the scenario of discrimination of POVMs the case of trivial input dimension
d_{i}=1
results in a trivial POVM with just one effect.

3.2
Asymmetric discrimination

Finally, we arrive at the asymmetric discrimination of unknown POVMs. Following the same line of thinking as in Section
2

we get:

\displaystyle\rho_{\textrm{id}}=\frac{1}{d_{o}\left(d_{o}^{2}s^{2}-1\right)}\bigg(s\big(sd_{o}-\bra{\psi}S\ket{\psi}\big){\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}+

\displaystyle+\big(sd_{o}\bra{\psi}S\ket{\psi}-1\big)T\bigg)

\displaystyle\rho_{\textrm{dif}}=\frac{1}{d_{o}^{2}}{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}_{d_{o}^{2}}.

(32)

Setting once again
\bra{\psi}S\ket{\psi}=-1
, which corresponds to taking completely antisymmetric state
\ket{\psi}
, we have:

\rho_{\textrm{id}}=\frac{1}{d_{o}\left(d_{o}s-1\right)}\left(s{\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}-T\right).

(33)

In this case, following
[
7
]
as the final bipartite
measurement, we choose the one given by
\Omega={\rm 1\kern-2.20001ptl}-T
. This gives us

\begin{split}p_{\textrm{I}}=&\frac{s-1}{sd_{o}-1}\\
p_{\textrm{II}}=&1-\frac{1}{d_{o}}.\end{split}

(34)

Note that in the case
s=1
we recover the results for discrimination of von Neumann
measurements from
[
7
]
.
In order to compare these results with the symmetric case note that in this case we have:

\frac{1}{2}(p_{\textrm{I}}+p_{\textrm{II}})=\frac{d_{o}^{2}s-2d_{o}+1}{2d_{o}(d_{o}s-1)}.

(35)

This expression exactly equals:

1-p^{s}_{H}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d_{o}-1}{d_{o}\left(sd_{o}-1\right)}=\frac{d_{o}^{2}s-2d_{o}+1}{2d_{o}(d_{o}s-1)},

(36)

in which we take for
p^{s}_{H}
the upper bound from (
30
).
Hence we have saturation of the inequality:

\frac{1}{2}(p_{\textrm{I}}+p_{\textrm{II}})=\frac{d_{o}^{2}s-2d_{o}+1}{2d_{o}(d_{o}s-1)}\geq 1-p^{s}_{H},

(37)

which implies that the proposed asymmetric discrimination scheme is optimal.

4
Discussion

In this work we have analysed the problem of discriminating two
unknown arbitrary quantum channels and arbitrary POVM measurements drawn according
to their corresponding Haar measure using both symmetric and asymmetric
discrimination schemes in the single application regime.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{d_{o}^{2}-1}{d_{o}^{2}s-d_{o}}.

(38)

is depicted in the Figure (

dimension treated for clarity as a continuous parameter, for three different
values of the environement dimension
