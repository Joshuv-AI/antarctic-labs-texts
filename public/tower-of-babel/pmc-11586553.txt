Evaluation of oxidative stress in dogs and cats with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Hilla Chen, Gilad Segev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxidative stress is a potential contributor to chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression but has not been evaluated in dogs and cats with CKD.HypothesisOxidative stress is higher in animals with CKD compared with healthy controls and decreases with the advancing CKD stage. The aim of this study was to determine the presence and intensity of oxidative stress in dogs and cats at different CKD stages.AnimalsSixty dogs and 30 cats with naturally acquired CKD; 10 dogs and 14 cats, healthy controls.MethodsAnalytical cross‐sectional study. Oxidative stress was evaluated by measuring the urinary concentration of F2‐isoprostane (uF2‐IsoPs) normalized to urinary creatinine.ResultsUrinary F2‐isoprostanes normalized to urinary creatinine of healthy dogs and of dogs with CKD Stages 1 to 4 was 3.3 ng/mg, 4.7 ng/mg (range, 1.0‐73.4), 2.4 ng/mg (range, 0.4‐7.8), 0.52 ng/mg (range, 0.01‐2.9), and 0.37 ng/mg (range, 0.01‐0.6), respectively. Urinary F2‐isoprostanes differed among CKD stages (P < .001), but not compared with controls. uF2‐IsoPs of healthy cats and cats with CKD Stages 1 to 4 was 0.68 ng/mg (range, 0.2‐1.4), 0.97 ng/mg (range, 0.4‐1.8), 0.6 ng/mg (range, 0.002‐2.0), 0.94 ng/mg (range, 0.3‐2.3), and 0.2 ng/mg (range, 0.01‐0.4). Urinary F2‐isoprostanes differed among stages (P = .05) but not compared with healthy controls.Conclusion and Clinical ImportanceOxidative stress might be present in dogs and cats with CKD. Its magnitude declines as the disease progresses, therefore, it should be considered a potential therapeutic target mostly at the early stages of the disease.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is common in both dogs and cats, especially in geriatric animals. It is characterized by persistent structural or functional damage to the kidneys. 1 , 2 As veterinary clinical care continues to advance, life expectancy of dogs and cats is anticipated to rise, further contributing to the increasing prevalence of the disease. Early diagnosis and effective management are pivotal in slowing down CKD progression and enhancing the quality of life for animals with CKD. Therefore, the identification of therapeutic interventions aimed at mitigating CKD progression and delaying the complications associated with advanced disease is a major goal.

The pathophysiology of CKD progression is yet to be completely elucidated, but regardless of the inciting cause, cellular injury occurs, setting off a cascade of events in which oxidative stress predominates. 3 Therefore, oxidative stress is a potential key player in the pathogenesis of progression of CKD. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Oxidative stress is characterized by an imbalance between pro‐oxidative processes, particularly the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defense mechanisms. 8 Oxidative stress leads to apoptosis of renal tubular cells and progressive loss of kidney function. 8 The kidneys, especially tubular cells, are highly metabolically active and exposed to high levels of ROS because of their reliance on aerobic metabolism and the high oxygen delivery to the kidneys. This makes pro‐oxidative processes a central feature of CKD pathogenesis. In humans, increased ROS production is associated with proteinuria and CKD progression. 9 , 10 Similarly, oxidative stress is thought to contribute to the development and progression of CKD in dogs and cats. 7 , 11 , 12 , 13

Assessing oxidative stress in vivo is challenging because of the instability of ROS in tissues and their short half‐lives. 14 F2‐isoprostanes (F2‐IsoPs) which are by‐products of arachidonic acid peroxidation, 15 have emerged as a highly favorable indirect markers of oxidative stress in both humans and animals. 16 F2‐IsoPs are considered highly stable compounds, especially in the urine. Additionally, F2‐IsoPs are locally produced in the kidneys, and thus measuring them in the urine increases specificity to the kidneys. 17 , 18 , 19 In humans, the concentration of F2‐IsoPs is inversely correlated with CKD stage. F2‐IsoPs were yet to be thoroughly evaluated in dogs with CKD, however, studies evaluating F2‐IsoPs in cats with CKD demonstrated high urinary and plasma F2‐IsoPs in early CKD stages, which declined with advancing disease stage. 11 , 13 These results suggest that renal oxidative stress in cats is present at early CKD stages, potentially contributing to disease progression.

We hypothesized that oxidative stress is increased in dogs and cats with early CKD, but decreases with advancing disease stage. The primary aim of this study was to determine urinary F2‐IsoPs (uF2‐IsoPs) concentration in dogs and cats at different CKD stages. The secondary aims were to evaluate the correlation between the magnitude of oxidative stress and proteinuria and to assess whether consumption of renal diet attenuates oxidative stress in dogs and cats with CKD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design and animals

Analytical cross‐sectional study including client‐owned dogs and cats presented to the Koret School of Veterinary Medicine Teaching Hospital, The Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and diagnosed with CKD between October 2021 and April 2023. The study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and informed consent was obtained from all owners (Approval number, HU‐NER‐2021‐094). Diagnosis of CKD and stratification to International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) CKD stages were performed based on the IRIS guidelines. CKD Stage 1 was diagnosed in nonazotemic dogs and cats (serum creatinine [sCr] <1.4 and <1.6 mg/dL, respectively), with unconcentrated urine (ie, urine specific gravity <1.030 in cats and <1.020 in dogs) after exclusion of other causes that might hamper urine concentrating ability, and/or sonographic evidence of structural changes consistent with CKD. 20 The control group included clinically healthy dogs and cats presenting for elective procedures, screened by physical examination, urinalysis, complete blood count (CBC), and sCr measurement.

At the time of inclusion (ie, obtaining samples for uF2‐IsoPs measurement), all dogs and cats were evaluated to confirm stable CKD, based on lack of clinical signs indicating acute exacerbation of CKD (ie, acute on CKD), 21 , 22 and <20% change in sCr during the preceding 3 months. Dogs and cats with positive urine culture, any concurrent systemic disease such as neoplasia, endocrine, immune‐mediated disease, systemic infection, or any other disease that might affect the oxidative status, were excluded. Data retrieved from the medical records included age, sex, neuter status, breed, clinicopathologic data, blood pressure measurement, and ultrasonographic examination.

Dogs and cats were classified as proteinuric based on urine protein to creatinine ratio (UPC) >0.5 and >0.4, respectively. If the protein dipstick result was negative, animals were classified as non‐proteinuric. Animals were also classified as primarily consuming renal diet if were reported by their owners to consume ≥75% renal diet.

F2‐isoprostanes measurement

Surplus urine was immediately stored at −80C○ pending uF2‐IsoPs measurement. Gas chromatography and negative ion chemical ionization‐mass spectrometry (GC/NICI‐MS) were utilized for the measurement of uF2‐IsoPs, which is the most studied member of the F2‐IsoPs family. The GC/NICI‐MS assay was performed by the Eicosanoid Core Laboratory in the Division of Clinical Pharmacology at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (Nashville, TN). Their methodology has been published previously and validated in dogs and cats. 16 , 23 The concentration of uF2‐IsoPs was normalized to urinary creatinine concentration and normalized results are presented (ng/mg).

Statistical methods

The distribution pattern of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Since data were distributed non‐normally, nonparametric tests were used. The Kruskal‐Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables among the study groups. The Mann‐Whitney U‐test with Bonferroni's correction was used for post hoc pair‐wise group comparisons. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using the Spearman's rank correlation test. All tests were 2‐tailed, and P < .05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using a statistical software package (SPSS 29.0 for Windows, IBM).

RESULTS

Dogs

Seventy dogs were enrolled, including 10 healthy control dogs and 60 dogs with CKD (Stage 1, 8 dogs; Stage 2, 33 dogs; Stage 3, 12 dogs; and Stage 4, 7 dogs). The most common breeds in the control group included mixed breed dogs (5 dogs, 50%), Pomeranians (2 dogs) American Staffordshire Terrier, King Charles Cavalier, and Shih Tzu (1 dog each). The median age and body weight of the control dogs were 35.5 months (range, 10‐96) and 9.2 kg (range, 3.1‐30.0). The median sCr of the healthy dogs was 0.8 mg/dL (range, 0.6‐1.2). Most dogs in the CKD group were mixed breed (27 dogs, 45%) followed by Yorkshire Terrier (5 dogs, 8%), Golden Retriever and Labrador Retriever (4 dogs each, 7%), and other breeds represented by <2 dogs. The median age and body weight of CKD dogs were 96 months (range, 5‐208), and 20.5 kg (range, 1.8‐86.6), respectively, with no age and weight difference among the CKD stages (Table 1). As expected, urea, sCr, and phosphorus were different among CKD stages (Table 1).

Proteinuria was documented in 24/58 (41%) dogs with CKD. There was neither a correlation between uF2‐IsoPs and UPC (r = −.79, P = .65) nor a difference in uF2‐IsoPs concentration between proteinuric and non‐proteinuric dogs with CKD (median, 1.77 ng/mg range, 0.006‐73.4 vs median, 1.55 ng/mg range, 0.006‐7.9, respectively; P = .88).

Cats

Forty‐four cats were enrolled, including 14 healthy control cats and 30 cats with CKD (Stage 1, 6 cats; Stage 2, 12 cats; Stage 3, 8 cats; Stage 4, 4 cats). The most common breeds in the control group were Domestic Short Hair (12 cats, 86%), Domestic Long Hair and Ragdoll (1 each). The median age and body weight of the healthy cats were 57 months (range, 6‐99) and 4.3 kg (range, 3.7‐5.9), respectively. The median sCr of the healthy cats was 1.0 mg/dL (range, 0.8‐1.2). Most cats in the CKD group (22 cats, 49%) were Domestic Short Hair. The median age and body weight of cats with CKD were 108 months (range, 30‐192), and 5.2 kg (range, 2.7‐8.4), respectively. There was no age difference among the CKD stages, however, body weight decreased with advancing CKD stage (P = .007, Table 2). DISCUSSION

The results of this study suggest that uF2‐IsoPs are different among CKD stages, and significantly decrease with advancing IRIS stage in dogs. Dogs with Stage 1 CKD had the highest uF2‐IsoPs concentrations, yet, the difference did not reach statistical significance when compared with healthy controls. Urinary F2‐isoprostanes were also highest in cats with Stage 1 CKD, however there were no significant differences among stages. These results indicate that oxidative stress might be present in early CKD and decreases with disease progression, therefore intervention to reduce oxidative stress, if found to be effective, is more likely to be beneficial in the early stages of the disease.

The decrease in oxidative stress with advancing CKD stage is reflected by lower urinary uF2‐IsoPs concentration with advancing disease and their negative correlation with kidney function parameters. These findings are consistent with the results of a previous study evaluating oxidative stress in cats with CKD using F2‐IsoPs. 13 A possible explanation for these findings is that higher F2‐IsoPs in the early disease stages arise from intra‐renal inflammation that contributes to oxidative stress. The latter gradually decreases as the disease progresses and metabolically active nephrons are being replaced by fibrotic tissue, therefore it is possible that the overall metabolic activity of the kidney is decreasing and fewer cells can be a source of F2‐IsoPs. Yet, this explanation needs to be further evaluated.

Although the concentration of uF2‐IsoPs was not significantly different between healthy controls and animals with CKD Stage 1, when considering the reduced number of nephrons in CKD, it is plausible that each remaining nephron is exposed to higher levels of oxidative stress and produce more F2‐IsoPs compared with a single nephron of a healthy dog. As a result, the overall production of these compounds in early CKD might not be different compared with healthy animals with normal number of functioning nephrons, however, the single nephron F2‐IsoPs production is increased in CKD. This theory needs to be further explored using models that evaluate single nephron oxidative stress.

The results of this study provide insights regarding potential benefit of early therapeutic interventions, as they suggest that oxidative stress is at its peak in the early stages of CKD in dogs and cats. Unlike humans, in which oxidative stress appears to increase with disease progression, 24 in dogs and cats, it seems to occur early, when a substantial amount of functioning renal parenchyma is still present, and therapeutic interventions are potentially more effective in attenuating the disease progression. Therefore, these results along with the known role of oxidative stress in CKD progression in humans, might advocate a potential merit to antioxidant treatment, especially when considering the low incidence of adverse effects of these supplements.

In this study, there was no correlation between the degree of oxidative stress and the magnitude of proteinuria, or a difference in the concentration of uF2‐IsoPs between proteinuric and non‐proteinuric dogs and cats. These results are consistent with a previous study in cats with CKD. 13 Conversely, an association between proteinuria and oxidative stress, as reflected by uIsoPs, was previously demonstrated in experimental models and in human patients. 25 , 26 The effect of antiproteinuric drugs on uIsoPs is unknown in dogs and cats, and therefore it is possible that some of the proteinuric animals in this cohort received such treatment which could have had an effect on their redox status.

Renal diets might reduce oxidative stress as they are supplemented with antioxidants such as omega‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, which decrease generation of proinflammatory eicosanoids. 27 We therefore compared uF2‐IsoPs in animals that primarily consumed renal diets versus those that were not, yet differences were not found between groups. These results do not prove that renal diets do not decrease oxidative stress, as different diets containing different amounts of antioxidants were fed, and daily amount as well as duration of consumption were not controlled in this study. On the other hand, the amount of antioxidants added to renal diet might need to be revisited, considering the results of this study and a previous study with consistent findings. 13 Potentially, higher antioxidant content needs to be supplemented to mitigate oxidative stress during the course of CKD.

This study has several limitations. One relates to the relatively small number of dogs and cats included in each CKD stage, which might have resulted in underpowered comparisons in some of the analyses, especially when P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons. Notably significant difference in uF2‐IsoPs between healthy animals and animals with CKD Stage 1 was not demonstrated. A larger cohort, mainly of dogs and cats with early stages of CKD is needed. Another limitation relates to the fact that uF2‐IsoPs are not specific markers of renal oxidative stress. In fact, uF2‐IsoPs are also being used to assess systemic oxidative stress, and are increased in animals with experimentally induced oxidative stress or tissue damage. 28 , 29 We have attempted to control for this systemic influence by excluding animals with concurrent systemic illness and with evidence of other urinary tract disease (eg, urinary tract infections). The fact that only animals with stable CKD and no other active inflammatory processes were included, increases the likelihood that uF2‐IsoPs reflect renal oxidative stress. Another limitation is that cats were classified as nonproteinuric based on a negative urine dipstick, which is not 100% sensitive to mild proteinuria in this species. Finally, body condition score might have influenced uF2‐IsoPs. In a previous study in cats, high body condition score was associated with increased uF2‐IsoPs. 30 Possibly, a decrease in body condition score in CKD stages 3 and 4 contributed to the decrease in uF2‐IsoPs concentrations. In this study, body condition score was not included as a cofactor in the analysis since it is a subjective measure and was missing from some of the medical records at the time of urine collection. Yet, we believe that the effect of body condition score on its own is unlikely to account for the difference in uF2‐IsoPs in the late stages of the disease.

To conclude, the results of this study demonstrate a decrease in uF2‐IsoPs with advancing CKD stage in dogs. The increased uF2‐IsoPs in early CKD might indicate that oxidative stress is implicated in CKD progression. Moreover, if oxidative damage is at its peak during Stage 1 CKD, it might be exploited as a therapeutic target, to potentially mitigate CKD progression.
