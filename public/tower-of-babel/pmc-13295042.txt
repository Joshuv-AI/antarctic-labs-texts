Understanding the Flotation Separation of Fluorite from Bayan Obo Deposits Using Phthalic Acid
Authors: Chunlei Guo, Zhao Cao, Weiwei Wang, Hailong Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rising global demand for fluorite has spurred interest in utilizing coassociated fluorite resources. This study explores using phthalic acid (PA) as a collector to enhance flotation performance for coassociated fluorite in the Bayan Obo Nb-REE-Fe ore in China. The real ore was characterized by a low grade, multiple mineral types, fine intergrowths, and complex interlocking and inclusion relationships among the minerals. The flotation of real ore with PA as a collector yielded high-grade fluorite concentrate suitable for use in the production of anhydrous hydrogen fluoride (AHF). Microflotation tests showed PA’s collecting capacity for bastnaesite and fluorite in weakly acidic to weakly alkaline conditions. Zeta potential shifts indicated the adsorption of PA on the surfaces of bastnaesite and fluorite, with electrostatic interactions playing a significant role. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) revealed absorption peaks for carboxyl groups and benzene ring CC skeletons, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis showed shifts in binding energies of C 1s, O 1s, Ca 2p, and Ce 3d, confirming chemical bonding between PA and minerals. These findings expand PA’s application in flotation and offer insights for efficient coassociated fluorite resource utilization in the Bayan Obo deposit.

Introduction

Fluorine (F), with its distinctive chemical properties, plays a critical role in various advanced materials, such as refrigerants, specialty rubbers, electronic gases and fine chemicals. − The supply of fluorine depends on the mining, beneficiation and metallurgical processing of fluorite and fluorapatite. Fluorite (CaF2), as the main source of 95% of industrial fluorine, supports the entire fluorochemical industry and is regarded as a strategic mineral resource in many countries. , Fluorite mines are generally classified into single-type and coassociated types. Single-type mines typically provide medium to high-grade fluorite ore (65%–85% CaF2) directly to the metallurgical industry or, after basic sorting, yield high-purity fluorite concentrates (>97% CaF2) for anhydrous hydrogen fluoride (AHF) production. In contrast, coassociated mines necessitate intricate beneficiation processes to acquire high-grade concentrates. The global fluorochemical market’s continuous growth, driven by strategic sectors such as new energy and semiconductors, is propelling the demand for fluorite. As single-type fluorite deposits diminish, coassociated mines are garnering increasing interest. ,,

The Bayan Obo Mining Area in Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China, hosts a polymetallic coassociated deposit abundant in iron (Fe), rare earth elements (REE), niobium (Nb), and thorium (Th). It also boasts around 150 million tons of fluorite resources, making it the world’s leading deposit in terms of fluorite content. This deposit encompasses nine types and over 170 minerals. The fluorite reserves are characterized by their low grade, fine-grained intergrowth and intricate mineral interlocking relationships. Studies reveal that fine-grained hematite (or limonite) is present as impregnation-type inclusions within the fluorite minerals. Rare earth minerals (REM) and fine hematite particles are intertwined along the peripheries of fluorite. Fine REM form clusters that coexist with fluorite, whereas fine fluorite is interlocked along the edges of REM, with particle sizes below 10 μm. The extremely fine-grained impurity minerals associated with fluorite result in inclusion-type intergrowths during grinding, significant challenges to their effective separation. At present, after coarse and fine classification, iron-rare earth ore dressing tailings are processed via a technological flow sheet involving one roughing, three cleanings, regrinding and reseparation of rough concentrate, as well as magnetic separation. The coarse fraction yields fluorite concentrates with CaF2 content varying from 88% to 93%, while the fine fraction produces concentrate with CaF2 content between 80% and 83%. The overall recovery ranges from 40% to 50%. Leveraging advantages such as reduced processing costs, lower silicon content and advancements in the preparation of AHF from concentrates with over 88% CaF2, Inner Mongolia Jin Ebo Fluorine Chemical Co., Ltd. has constructed the largest AHF production facility globally, boasting an annual output capacity of up to 300,000 tons. Although some high-grade fluorite concentrates (88%–93% CaF2) are employed, low-grade concentrates (80%–83% CaF2) remain unsuitable for application, and the recovery during the beneficiation process is relatively low, leading to considerable resource wastage.

The mineralogical properties of ores and the industrial application of fluorite flotation have demonstrated that the main factors influencing the improvement of the technical and economic parameters of fluorite concentrate from Bayan Obo are the fine-grained intergrowth of fluorite minerals and their intricate association with REM, particularly bastnaesite ((La,Ce)­CO3F). ,, Grinding can alleviate the former issue, while reagent regulation is essential for addressing the latte. The selection of collector is a critical stage in this procedure. Fatty acid collectors are frequently used in fluorite flotation due to their proven effectiveness and cost-efficiency in collection process. However, this characteristic poses challenges in the separation of fluorite from gangue minerals, particularly in the presence of polymetallic ores. The modification of fatty acid collectors by the introduction of new polar groups, such as hydroxyl, amide, amino, and ether groups, has been shown to modify the molecular structure, thereby enhancing flotation separation efficiency. This is a focal point for reagent innovation. The integration of these groups into the nonpolar base of fatty acid collectors has been demonstrated to enhance their solubility in water, reduce sensitivity to calcium and magnesium ions, strengthen mineral binding, improve resistance to low temperatures and broaden the applicability of flotation. In addition, researchers have investigated collectors with alternative functional groups, such as hydroxamic acids, phosphates, and sulfonates. − Despite ongoing development of various collectors in laboratory experiments and their promising prospects, dedicated research and practical applications targeting the fluorite resources in Bayan Obo remain insufficient.

Phthalic acid (C8H6O4, PA), a binary low-molecular-weight carboxylic acid, is widely distributed in the natural environment and acts as an important raw material in the production of resins, fibers, pharmaceuticals, and other materials. − Although its adsorption behavior onto metal (hydr)­oxide surfaces (including goethite, hematite, TiO2, ZrO2, and Al2O3) has been extensively studied, , its application in flotation has mainly been focused on bastnaesite separation. For example, Wang et al. utilized PA as a collector to achieve the separation of bastnaesite and monazite, and to produce high-purity bastnaesite concentrate from the Weishan mine in Shandong, China. − The adsorption mode of PA on oxide surfaces varies with specific systems and conditions, involving both inner-sphere and outer-sphere adsorption. Tejedor–Tejedor et al. proposed that PA adsorbs on goethite surfaces as inner-sphere complexes in single-dentate mononuclear (M-M) and double-dentate binuclear (B-B) configurations, with one carboxylic acid group bound to a single Fe atom. In contrast, Persson et al. noted that PA formed an outer layer adsorption on the surface of boehmite and goethite, including an outer-sphere surface complex (O-S, with one carboxyl group bound to a hydroxylated Fe atom) and a monodentate binuclear complex (M-B, with two carboxyl groups bound to one Fe atom). , Hwang et al. found that PA forms three types of complexes (O-S, M-B, and B–B) on hematite surface. However, Dobson et al. investigated the adsorption structures of PA on four metal oxides (TiO2, ZrO2, Al2O3, and Ta2O5) and concluded that PA only forms B–B complexes. Furthermore, Li et al. suggested that PA binds to Ca2+ ions on the calcite surface at the same sites, forming a cyclic “clamp structure” assigned as the M-B type. It is clear that the adsorption configuration of PA on mineral surfaces is complex and may differ depending on the mineral type or variations in surface properties.

In this study, the characteristics of coassociated fluorite resources in the Nb–REE–Fe ore of the Bayan Obo deposit were investigated through scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and an automatic mineral analysis system (AMICS). Experiments were conducted to investigate the effectiveness of flotation as a separation method for fluorite concentrate, with PA used as the collector in the cleaning stage. The objective of this study was to enhance the technical indicators of the process to optimize the separation efficiency and quality of the concentrate. The adsorption mechanism of PA on the surfaces of bastnaesite and fluorite minerals was comprehensively assessed through zeta potential, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the underlying interactions between PA and fluorite as well as bastnaesite in the flotation process, offering insights into reagent selection for the integrated utilization of complex coassociated fluorite resources.

Materials and Methods

Mineral Samples and Reagents

Single mineral samples of bastnaesite and fluorite were sourced from two locations: the Weishan Lake rare earth mine in Shandong Province and the Bayan Obo mining area in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of China. After undergoing a series of processes including crushing, classification, gravity separation, and magnetic separation, high-purity mineral samples of bastnaesite and fluorite were ultimately obtained. These pure mineral samples were then subjected to wet grinding in an XMCQ 180 × 200 porcelain-lined ball mill (Jilin Province Mechanical Prospecting Plant, China) and screened to isolate a −38 + 74 μm particle size fraction for flotation-related experiments. Concurrently, the −38 μm fraction was further ground to −5 μm using an XQM-100 planetary ball mill (Changsha Tianchuang Powder Co., Ltd., China; both the tank and balls were made of corundum) for subsequent mechanistic analyses, including zeta potential, FTIR, and XPS.

PA and sodium oleate (C17H33CO2Na, NaOl) were procured from Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. in China for utilization as anionic collectors. Tannic acid (C76H52O46, DN), employed as a gangue mineral inhibitor, and methyl isobutyl carbinol (C6H14O, MIBC), utilized as a frother, were sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. in China. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were used as pH regulators, while sodium chloride (NaCl) served as the electrolyte. All of these reagents were obtained from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All of these reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. and were of analytical grade.

In addition, water glass (Na2O·nSiO2, n = 2.4, WG, concentration 40%) and terpineol oil (2# oil) obtained from the Bayan Obo Mineral Processing Branch of China Baotou Iron and Steel (Group) Co., Ltd., were used as inhibitors and frother, respectively. Acidified water glass (SWG) was prepared by mixing WG and HCl at a mass ratio of 1:1. Deionized water (resistivity 18.25 MΩ·cm) was utilized for microflotation, whereas tap water was employed for the real ore flotation.

The ore utilized in bench flotation originated from the Bayan Obo mining area in the Inner Mongolia autonomous region of China and was classified as acmite-type Nb-REE-Fe ore. The ore was crushed and sieved to a particle size of less than −3 mm. The material was then ground at a mass concentration of 66.67% using an RK/ZQM Φ250 × 100 conical ball mill (Wuhan Rock Grinding Equipment Manufacturing Co., Ltd.). The mill used steel balls as the grinding medium, with a volume filling rate of 43% and a rotational speed of 96 rpm. The purpose of this process was to reduce the particle size to 90% by mass passing −74 μm, thereby obtaining a particle size suitable for bench flotation.

Experiment Methods

Mineral Characterization

Pure mineral and real ore samples were analyzed at the physical and chemical testing center of the Baotou Research Institute of Rare Earths in China, which holds the Laboratory Accreditation certificate from the China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment (CNAS). The chemical multielement contents were determined using an ICAP 6300 inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). The purity of the pure minerals was assessed with an X’Pert PRO X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical B.V., Netherlands). The mineral composition, degree of liberation and other pertinent process mineralogical parameters of the samples were analyzed using a Sigma-500 field emission SEM (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) and AMICS (Beijing Oubotong Optical Technology Co., Ltd.).

Bench Flotation Tests

Bench flotation tests were conducted using an XFD-type flotation machine (manufactured by the Prospecting Machinery Factory in Jilin Province, China). The flotation cells had volumes of 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5, and 3 L, and the impeller speed was set at 1900 rpm. The flotation time was 4–7 min, the slurry concentration was 15–30%, and the temperature was maintained at 25 °C. For additional details, please refer to the relevant literature.

Microflotation Tests

Microflotation tests were performed using an XFGCII type flotation machine (manufactured by the Prospecting Machinery Factory in Jilin Province, China). The flotation cell had a volume of 40 mL, and the impeller speed was set at 1900 rpm. For pulp preparation, 2.0 g of mineral sample was mixed with 40 mL of deionized water. A prepared NaOH or HCl solution was added to adjust the pulp pH. After adding the collector and frother, the pulp was stirred for 3 min. Air was then introduced at a flow rate of 330 mL·min–1, and the froth was scraped every 10 s. The total flotation time was 3 min. After filtration, drying, and weighing of the flotation products, the recovery was calculated based on the dry weights. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the average value is reported as the final result.

Zeta Potential Measurements

Zeta potential and isoelectric point (IEP) of pure minerals were determined using a Nano ZS90 potential analyzer (Malvern Instruments Limited, UK) before and after reagent addition. A NaCl solution with a concentration of 1 × 10–3 mol·L–1 was used as the background electrolyte, and pure mineral suspensions were prepared at 0.5 g·L–1 in a total volume of 1 L. These suspensions were ultrasonically dispersed for 90 s and allowed to stand for 24 h before use. For each test, 50 mL of the stock suspension was transferred to a 100 mL beaker, and the target reagent was added. The pH was then adjusted using dilute HCl and NaOH solutions. After stirring for 30 min, the mixture was allowed to settle naturally for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of the upper supernatant was extracted with a syringe for zeta potential measurement. Each sample was measured three times from alkaline to acidic conditions, and the average value was recorded as the final result.

FTIR Measurements

FTIR analysis was performed on pure minerals before and after reagent treatment using a Nicolet iS5 infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). The samples were first treated with reagents at a fixed pH and dosage. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to magnetic stirring for 2 h, followed by filtration and three washes with deionized water at the same pH. The samples were then dried under vacuum at room temperature and analyzed using the KBr pellet technique. Infrared transmittance spectra were scanned over the range of 4000–600 cm–1 at a resolution of 4 cm–1.

XPS Measurements

The samples were scanned before and after PA treatment using an ESCALAB QXi X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) to obtain both wide-scan and high-resolution XPS spectra. The sputtering source was 150 W Al Kα X-rays (1486.60 eV), and the analysis chamber was maintained at a vacuum of 5.5 × 10–6 Pa. Sample preparation procedures were consistent with those used for FTIR analysis. The test results were calibrated for element binding energies by referencing the standard C 1s peak at 284.80 eV, and the data were analyzed using Thermo Advantage software.

Real Ore Flotation

The performance of PA as a collector was assessed through flotation tests using real ore samples. The ore was ground to a fineness similar to that used in the processing plant, with 90% of particles passing a 74 μm sieve. Initially, valuable FeM and REM were separated via magnetic separation and flotation, followed by fluorite flotation. NaOl was used as the collector, while WG was added under alkaline conditions (pH 8.5–9.5) to depress silicate minerals such as acmite and amphibole. A fluorite rough concentrate was obtained after one roughing and three cleaning stages. Subsequently, the concentrate was reground to a fineness of 79.17% passing 38 μm. At this stage, PA was employed as the collector, and WG and DN were used under weakly acidic conditions (pH 6.0–6.6) to depress carbonates and REM. Some fluorite particles were extremely fine and closely associated with FeM and REM, as shown in Figures and . High-gradient magnetic separation was then applied to produce the final fluorite concentrate. The main process flow and results are presented in Figure and Table , respectively.

Flotation Behaviors of Minerals

The quality of fluorite concentrate produced by flotation is primarily influenced by bastnaesite. To investigate the interaction of PA with fluorite and bastnaesite during flotation, microflotation experiments and mechanism studies were conducted. PA, which has weaker collecting ability but better selectivity than fatty acid collectors such as NaOl, was used as the collector. Figure illustrates the effect of pH on the floatability of fluorite and bastnaesite when using PA as the collector. Across the pH range studied, both strongly acidic and alkaline conditions were detrimental to the flotation of fluorite and bastnaesite. Bastnaesite exhibited enhanced floatability in a slightly acidic pH range, consistent with the pH range reported by Wang et al. for the separation of bastnaesite and monazite using PA. In contrast, fluorite showed improved floatability under neutral to slightly alkaline conditions. Furthermore, at the same PA concentration, bastnaesite exhibited higher floatability than fluorite in terms of recovery. Nevertheless, adjusting the pH alone, especially for positively floating fluorite, was insufficient for effectively separating fluorite and bastnaesite, despite a 16.20 percentage point disparity in recovery between the two minerals.

Effect of pH on the floatability of fluorite and bastnaesite (c PA = 6.02 × 10–4 mol·L–1, c MIBC = 0.36 g·L–1).

At pH 6.6, the effect of PA concentration on the floatability of fluorite and bastnaesite was investigated. As shown in Figure , the recoveries of fluorite and bastnaesite increased gradually with increasing PA concentration. At a PA concentration of 1.51 × 10–3 mol·L–1, the recoveries of fluorite and bastnaesite were 83.75% and 73.70%, respectively. Surface Electrical Properties of Fluorite and Bastnaesite

The interaction between PA and minerals was investigated by measuring the surface electrical properties of fluorite and bastnaesite, as illustrated in Figure . As the solution transitioned from acidic to alkaline conditions, the zeta potentials of both fluorite and bastnaesite gradually decreased, with IEPs identified at pH 8.5 and 9.2, respectively. These results align with those reported by other researchers. −

Zeta potentials of fluorite and bastnaesite before and after the addition of PA.

Upon PA addition, the zeta potential of fluorite exhibited a significant negative shift across the pH range of 4.2 to 10.0, with the most pronounced shifts occurring near neutral and weakly alkaline conditions. The magnitude of this shift decreased progressively with increasing pH. For bastnaesite, the introduction of PA resulted in a substantial negative shift in zeta potential within the pH range of 2.8 to 9.0, with the largest shifts occurring in the weakly acidic region. Similarly, the magnitude of the shift diminished as pH increased. Notably, within the pH range of 6.0 to 10.0, the zeta potential of bastnaesite fluctuated near its IEP. These negative shifts in zeta potential indicated that PA was adsorbed onto the surfaces of fluorite and bastnaesite. The larger negative shifts at corresponding pH values suggested enhanced PA adsorption in these ranges, which agreed well with the microflotation results.

Figure illustrates the FTIR spectra of bastnaesite before and after treatment with PA. Characteristic peaks of bastnaesite appeared at 1400 cm–1, 1081 cm–1, 859 cm–1, and 726 cm–1, corresponding to the antisymmetric stretching, symmetric stretching, out-of-plane bending, and in-plane bending vibrations of the carbonate (CO3 2–) anion group. , After PA treatment, new peaks emerged at 1759 cm–1 and 2499 cm–1, attributed to the stretching vibrations of the carboxylic acid CO group and the COO-H group. Interaction with cations caused the conversion of COOH groups to COO– groups, reducing the double-bond character of the CO group and increasing the bond order of the C–O– bond. This resulted in the coupling and splitting of their stretching vibrations. However, these vibrations overlapped with the stretching vibration peaks of the CO3 2– groups in bastnaesite. Consequently, the peak at 1453 cm–1 could correspond to the stretching vibration of either carboxylate COO– or carbonate CO3 2–. This peak was shifted by 53 cm–1 relative to the latter, suggesting that carboxylate ions in PA formed chemical bonds with the bastnaesite surface and promoted chemisorption.

Although F 1s on the fluorite surface exhibited a higher atomic concentration than Ca 2p, fluorine preferentially dissolved into solution. Consequently, the fluorite surface became relatively enriched in calcium, enhancing its floatability and allowing recovery using fatty acid collectors. After PA treatment, the surface atomic concentrations of C 1s and O 1s on fluorite increased by 2.8% and 1.16%, respectively, whereas Ca 2p remained largely unchanged and F 1s decreased by 3.05%. For bastnaesite, the atomic concentrations of surface C 1s and F 1s were also higher than that of Ce 3d. Fluorine was readily hydrated and exhibited high hydrophilicity, necessitating the use of stronger hydroxamic acid collectors for recovery. After PA treatment, the atomic concentration of C 1s increased significantly by 10.28%, while the concentrations of O 1s, Ce 3d, and F 1s decreased by 4.18%, 1.79%, and 4.31%, respectively. The marked increase in C 1s concentration indicated that PA was chemisorbed onto both fluorite and bastnaesite surfaces.

Peak-fitting analysis was conducted on the XPS spectra of fluorite and bastnaesite after PA treatment, with the results summarized in Figure . The C 1s peak of fluorite (Figure (1)) originated from unavoidable carbon contamination and was composed of three component peaks, consistent with literature reports. − Specifically, peaks at 284.80, 286.89, and 288.99 eV corresponded to C–C/C-H, C–O–C/C–OH, and O–CO bonds, respectively. After PA treatment, a new peak appeared at a binding energy of 293.02 eV, attributed to the benzene ring shakeup satellite peak (π→π*) from PA. Furthermore, the binding energies of the C–O–C/C–OH and O–CO bonds decreased by 0.41 and 0.21 eV, respectively. The O 1s peak of fluorite (Figure (2)) consisted of two peaks, with binding energies of 531.49 and 533.76 eV assigned to C = O and C–O bonds, respectively. , After PA treatment, the binding energies of the CO and C–O bonds decreased by 0.18 and 0.24 eV, respectively. The Ca 2p peaks of fluorite (Figure (3)) featured a pair of spin–orbit splitting peaks at 347.96 eV (Ca 2p3/2) and 351.50 eV (Ca 2p1/2). After PA treatment, these binding energies decreased by 0.67 and 0.66 eV, respectively. These results indicated that PA chemically interacted with Ca active sites on the fluorite surface through its carboxyl groups.

Discussion

The flotation of fluorite in the Bayan Obo deposit is challenging due to the complexity of its polymetallic coassociated mineral resources. During the cleaning stage, PA was used as a collector for fluorite flotation, yielding a concentrate with a CaF2 grade of 91.61%. Mineralogical analysis revealed that the CaF2 content in the acmite-type ore was only 13.26%, with fluorite showing a complex intergrown relationship with major gangue minerals including FeM, REM, pyroxene, and dolomite. When the proportion of −74 μm ore particles reached 87.40%, the liberation degree of fluorite was merely 66.27%. To obtain a high-grade concentrate, the concentrate must be reground, as demonstrated by the real ore flotation results (Figure ). To effectively depress silicate and carbonate gangue minerals, especially acmite and REM, different pH conditions and depressants were employed. Under alkaline conditions, WG was used as a depressant. In contrast, SWG and DN were applied as depressants under weakly acidic conditions. A similar approach was adopted by Peng et al. ,

Microflotation tests demonstrated that PA acts as a collector for both fluorite and bastnaesite, exhibiting stronger collecting ability for the latter. However, the optimal pH ranges for the two minerals differed slightly. Compared with NaOl as the collector, real ore flotation produced a higher-grade fluorite concentrate. This suggests that when depressants (specifically SWG and DN) were added to the pulp, they preadsorbed onto the surfaces of gangue minerals. Therefore, using PA as a collector with weaker adsorption intensity significantly reduced the flotation of gangue minerals. Guo et al. also reported that DN has a strong depressant effect on bastnaesite.

Zeta potential measurements revealed that PA adsorbed onto fluorite and bastnaesite surfaces, leading to significant negative shifts in zeta potential within the pH range of 4.0 to 7.0. This differs from typical flotation under alkaline conditions when using NaOl as the collector. This behavior is related to the solution chemistry of PA (Figure ): At pH 0–2.6, PA exists mainly as molecular species with partial first-step ionization; At pH 2.6–4.6, PA undergoes primarily first-step ionization with partial second-step ionization, existing mainly as C8H5O4 –; At pH 4.6–7.0, PA undergoes full second-step ionization and exists predominantly as C8H4O4 2–; At pH above 7.0, PA is completely ionized. Although full ionization occurs, mineral recovery remains low in the alkaline range. This phenomenon is mainly attributed to the increasingly negative surface charge on fluorite and bastnaesite, which strengthens electrostatic repulsion. In addition, hydroxide ions in solution compete with C8H4O4 2– for adsorption sites, thereby weakening the collecting ability of PA. Previously reported batch test results support this observation. , Furthermore, it confirms that electrostatic interactions significantly influence the adsorption of PA, and that chemical adsorption can effectively overcome electrostatic repulsion.

FTIR and XPS analyses demonstrated that the PA collector chemisorbed onto the Ca sites on fluorite surfaces and the Ce active sites on bastnaesite surfaces via its carboxyl groups. Specifically, FTIR revealed absorption peaks corresponding to carboxyl groups (COOH) on both fluorite and bastnaesite surfaces after PA treatment, indicating an O–S adsorption configuration between PA and the mineral surfaces. However, FTIR provided no evidence for other PA adsorption configurations on the mineral surfaces. Owing to the structural similarity between COO– and CO3 2– carbonyl groups, the overlap of the asymmetric stretching vibration (1600–1450 cm–1) and the H–O–H bending vibration (around 1640 cm–1) significantly affected the analytical resolution. Therefore, further investigations, such as first-principles calculations, are required to clarify the exact adsorption configuration.

Furthermore, when PA chelates with mineral surfaces to form surface complexes (M-B), the Ce active sites on bastnaesite are transition metal sites, whereas the Ca active sites on fluorite are alkaline earth metal sites. The chelate complex formed between PA and transition metals is more stable. According to the hard–soft acid–base theory, the interaction between the carboxyl group in PA and the Ca/Ce active sites on fluorite and bastnaesite surfaces can be classified as a hard acid-hard base reaction. Since Ce atoms exhibit greater hardness than Ca atoms, the interaction between the carboxyl group and the bastnaesite surface is stronger. This is congruent with microflotation results, which confirmed that PA exhibits stronger collecting ability for bastnaesite.

In conclusion, as a collector, PA adsorbs on fluorite surfaces through both electrostatic and chemical interactions. When used in combination with appropriate depressants, PA exhibited excellent performance in fluorite flotation from the complex ore of the Bayan Obo deposit. Following further validation in pilot and industrial tests, these findings show potential for practical application in mineral processing plants and may provide valuable insights for the recovery of similar fluorite resources.

Conclusions

This study examined the characteristics of fluorite resources coassociated with Nb–REE–Fe ore in the Bayan Obo deposit. The flotation performance and adsorption mechanism of PA as a collector for fluorite were evaluated during the cleaning stage. The results indicated that the coassociated fluorite ore contained 13.26% CaF2 and comprised multiple minerals. Fine-grained fluorite particles exhibited complex interlocking and encapsulation relationships with gangue minerals including FeM, REM, and acmite. When the proportion of −74 μm particles reached 87.40%, the liberation degree of fluorite was only 66.27%, which was mainly attributed to intergrowths with FeM, REM, and silicate minerals.

Using PA as the collector in the cleaning stage increased the fluorite concentrate grade by 5.39 percentage points compared with NaOl, thus meeting the requirements for AHF production. Microflotation tests showed that, in the weakly acidic to weakly alkaline pH range, the recoveries of fluorite and bastnaesite reached 83.75% and 73.70%, respectively, at a PA concentration of 1.51 × 10–3 mol·L–1. The addition of PA caused significant negative shifts in the zeta potentials of fluorite (pH 4.2–10.0) and bastnaesite (pH 2.8–9.0), indicating that PA adsorbed onto both mineral surfaces, with electrostatic interactions playing a key role. In addition, after PA treatment, the FTIR spectra of fluorite and bastnaesite showed characteristic absorption peaks at 1763 cm–1, 1586 cm–1, 1759 cm–1, and 2499 cm–1 corresponding to carboxyl groups and benzene ring C = C skeleton structures. Meanwhile, XPS analysis revealed benzene ring shakeup satellite peaks (π→π*) at 293.02 and 292.56 eV. The obvious shifts in the binding energies of C 1s, O 1s, Ca 2p, and Ce 3d further confirmed the chemical interactions of PA on these surfaces.

In summary, PA adsorbed onto fluorite and bastnaesite surfaces through both electrostatic and chemical adsorption. As a collector in the cleaning stage, PA exhibited remarkable effectiveness in separating fluorite from complex coassociated ores such as those in the Bayan Obo deposit. These findings deepen the understanding of PA’s mechanism in fluorite flotation and provide guidance for the efficient utilization of the Bayan Obo deposit and other similar coassociated fluorite resources. Future research should focus on carrying out expanded pilot and industrial tests to verify its feasibility, while further exploring the adsorption configurations of PA on mineral surfaces to reveal its interaction mechanism at the molecular level.
