Introduction

Intravesical foreign bodies (IFBs) are uncommon and usually arise from self‐insertion, iatrogenic retention, or migration/erosion of adjacent devices [1, 2]. They may cause urinary tract infection, hematuria, lower urinary tract symptoms, encrustation, bladder stone formation, and rarely perforation [3, 4]. Although enterovesical fistulas from gastrointestinal pathology are well described, bladder‐to‐bowel fistulization caused by an IFB is rare [5]. We describe a case in which a long‐standing IFB became immobilized by a giant vesical calculus, resulting in rectovesical fistula (RVF), and discuss its pathophysiology and staged management.

Case Presentation

A 69‐year‐old man presented to a local clinic with urinary frequency and abdominal distension. A firm lower‐abdominal mass was palpable, and ultrasonography demonstrated an intravesical mass with posterior acoustic shadowing. He was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. Six years earlier, he had inserted a foreign object into the urethra for autoerotic purposes and had been unable to remove it. Despite persistent urinary frequency thereafter, he had not sought medical care. He denied pneumaturia and fecaluria.

On admission, laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (10.2 × 103/μL) and elevated C‐reactive protein (2.35 mg/dL). Hemoglobin was 8.1 g/dL with microcytosis (mean corpuscular volume, 75.1 fL). Renal indices were consistent with postrenal dysfunction: blood urea nitrogen, 25.4 mg/dL; creatinine, 1.63 mg/dL; and estimated glomerular filtration rate, 34 mL/min/1.73 m2. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography revealed a giant vesical calculus measuring 70.8‐mm in maximum diameter, which encased a linear plastic foreign body. The distal tip of the foreign body tracked toward the anterior rectal wall, and bilateral hydroureteronephrosis was present (Figure 1a,b). Flexible cystoscopy was advanced smoothly through the urethra into the bladder and showed a massive vesical calculus, which precluded further intravesical evaluation (Figure 2). No urethral stricture was observed. To assess suspected fistulization, indigo carmine was instilled intravesically; blue dye efflux from the rectum on digital examination suggested bladder–rectal communication. Subsequent colonoscopy identified a green cylindrical object protruding into the rectal lumen through the anterior rectal wall, confirming rectovesical fistula (Figure 3).

Because single‐stage definitive repair was considered high risk in the setting of severe local inflammation and contamination, a staged strategy was planned. At the initial operation, transurethral catheterization was attempted but the catheter could not be appropriately advanced and placed in the bladder because of the giant calculus. Open cystotomy was therefore performed, and the foreign body and fragmented adherent stone were removed. Definitive fistula closure was intentionally deferred because severe inflammation and contamination around the fistula made safe delineation and closure of the fistula tract difficult. A suprapubic cystostomy was placed for urinary diversion, and a diverting transverse colostomy was created. The procedure proceeded uneventfully. The extracted object was a green plastic rod measuring approximately 8‐cm (Figure 4). Stone analysis revealed magnesium ammonium phosphate and ammonium acid urate components. The stone weight was not available because the calculus was fragmented during removal.

Postoperatively, inflammatory markers normalized and renal function improved. Computed tomography on postoperative day 22 demonstrated complete clearance of intravesical calculi and resolution of bilateral hydronephrosis. After the inflammation had subsided, delayed laparoscopic low anterior resection with fistula closure was performed. During this operation, the fistula tract was divided, the rectal segment including the fistula was resected, and the bladder defect was closed with absorbable sutures. Watertight closure was confirmed by saline instillation into the bladder. Omental interposition was not performed, and urinary drainage was maintained with the suprapubic cystostomy.

Two months after fistula closure, follow‐up colonoscopy and contrast study showed no residual fistula or rectal perforation, and noncontrast computed tomography demonstrated no free air or other signs of anastomotic leakage. The suprapubic cystostomy was subsequently closed, and the patient resumed spontaneous voiding without further urinary drainage. Formal urodynamic evaluation was not performed; however, he reported no voiding‐related difficulties in daily life. The diverting transverse colostomy was closed 5 months after fistula closure. The patient remains well without recurrent fistula, urinary infection, or stone formation at 1‐year follow‐up.

Discussion

Intravesical foreign bodies (IFBs) are uncommon and may cause bladder stone formation when retained [1, 3, 6, 7]. Foreign body–associated vesicointestinal fistulas have also been described [7, 8, 9]. However, previous reports mainly addressed direct penetration, migration, or stone/foreign‐body removal. In contrast, the present case demonstrates a distinct process in which prolonged encrustation produced a giant vesical calculus that immobilized a relatively smooth plastic rod. This stone‐mediated fixation likely caused chronic pressure injury extending from the posterior bladder wall toward the anterior rectal wall, resulting in RVF. We speculate that the impacted calculus and foreign body may have partially sealed the fistulous tract, which could explain the absence of pneumaturia and fecaluria despite bladder–rectal communication.

Several conditions have been proposed to precipitate bladder perforation, including rigidity or poor flexibility of the object, sustained bladder distension, and fixation of the object against the bladder wall [2, 7]. Our case supports the importance of fixation as a key factor because the original object was relatively short and smooth but became functionally hazardous after being immobilized by a giant vesical calculus.

Imaging is useful for defining both the mechanism and extent of disease. In the present case, computed tomography delineated the encased foreign body, the 70.8‐mm calculus, and bilateral hydroureteronephrosis. Because cystoscopic evaluation was limited by extensive stone burden, intravesical indigo carmine instillation was used to confirm bladder–rectal communication, and colonoscopy directly visualized the object protruding into the rectal lumen. This stepwise assessment helped establish the diagnosis and plan staged surgical management.

Operative timing is a key management question [10]. A single‐stage procedure involving foreign body/stone removal with immediate fistula repair can shorten the treatment course but may increase the risk of septic complications, suture failure, or anastomotic leakage in contaminated and inflamed fields. In the present case, transurethral catheterization was impossible because the catheter could not be advanced into the bladder beyond the giant calculus, and immediate fistula repair was intentionally avoided because of severe inflammation and contamination around the fistula. We therefore adopted a staged strategy: open cystotomy with extraction of the foreign body and adherent stone, urinary diversion by suprapubic cystostomy, and fecal diversion via transverse colostomy, followed by delayed laparoscopic low anterior resection with fistula closure after inflammation had subsided. This approach resolved hydroureteronephrosis and renal dysfunction, enabled stoma closure, and prevented recurrence.

This case highlights that stone‐mediated immobilization of a retained IFB can drive bladder‐to‐rectum fistulization. When inflammation and contamination are substantial, staged management with urinary/fecal diversion and delayed reconstruction may provide a safe strategy.

Conclusion

A long‐neglected intravesical foreign body became immobilized by a giant vesical calculus and caused rectovesical fistula. Imaging clarified disease extent. Staged management prevented recurrence, underscoring the need for early removal before stone‐mediated complications develop.
