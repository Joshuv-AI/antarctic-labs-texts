Lived experiences of older adults and caregivers on social networks, social support from Chengalpattu, Tamil Nadu: a qualitative study using the Convoy Model of Social Relations
Authors: Sai Prashanthini Sivakumar, Bharathi Palanisamy, Sruthi Sree
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChanging family structures, nuclearization and urbanization are reshaping the socialization and support networks of older adults. Smaller families and migration reduce direct care and engagement opportunities, impacting health and functional ability in the growing aging population. The study explored social networks and perceived social support for older adults in rural and urban Chengalpattu, Tamil Nadu, through 33 in-depth interviews (22 older adults and 11 caregivers). Using the Convoy Model of Social Relations, thematic analysis revealed that immediate close ties, such as spouses and children, provided essential emotional, physical and financial support to maintain functional ability, regardless of the individual's location. The middle circle of the extended family assisted with hospital visits, meals during emergencies and support when the immediate family was absent. The outer circle, comprising non family members and formal caregivers, offered physical, informational, logistical and occasional financial help. Parents of migrated children often receive financial aid and virtual emotional support, whereas extended family or neighbors provide physical assistance when caregivers are absent. In urban areas, work demands and technology use limit in-person family interaction, prompting elders to turn to WhatsApp, gaming apps and online health content to combat loneliness. They also perceived taking care of grandchildren as burdensome. In contrast, rural elders viewed time with children as “medicine” for emotional well-being. Introduction

Population aging is becoming a significant global public health issue, propelled by rising life expectancy and decreasing fertility rates. The World Health Organization projects that the global population aged 60 years and above will increase from 1.1 billion in 2023 to 1.4 billion by 2030 [1, 2]. As populations age, promoting health and well-being later in life requires attention not only to individual health conditions but also to the social and physical environments that shape everyday living, social participation and access to care [3].

India is undergoing a rapid demographic transition, with the population aged 60 years and above projected to more than double from 100 million in 2011 to 230 million by 2036 [4]; by then, nearly one in seven Indians will be older adults, reflecting a substantial restructuring of the country’s age composition [4]. Population aging, declining fertility, accelerated urbanization, labor migration and increasing reliance on digital technologies are all of which are reshaping family structures and systems of care and support in India [5–7]. Together, these processes have substantially altered traditional systems of familial support for older adults. Decreasing coresidential living arrangements and migration-related spatial separation between older adults and their adult children have reduced opportunities for routine, in-person caregiving and everyday social interaction [8]. Consequently, intergenerational reciprocity has weakened, caregiving responsibilities have increasingly shifted beyond the family and digital forms of contact have emerged as partial and indirect substitutes for face-to-face support [7, 9].

Historically, multigenerational joint families in India have played a central role in ensuring intergenerational cohesion, shared caregiving responsibilities and sustained emotional support for older adults [10]. However, urbanization, modernization and migration have sped up the move toward smaller, nuclear families, especially in cities and suburbs. This shift is reflected in the growing proportion of households with one to four members reported in the 2011 Census [11–14]. These structural changes have weakened traditional caregiving roles and reduced everyday social engagement, increasing older adults’ vulnerability to social isolation, loneliness and emotional neglect, especially among those with limited mobility or migrant children [15–18].

A substantial body of evidence indicates that family structure and social relationships are strongly associated with health and well-being later in life [19]. Older adults residing in joint or extended family settings generally report better physical and mental health outcomes than those living in nuclear households, where loneliness, insecurity and reduced caregiving support are more common [20, 21]. Sustained emotional interaction, shared caregiving and a sense of belonging within joint families are known to buffer against psychological distress [22, 23]. Conversely, weakened family ties and limited social support are more prevalent in nuclear or disrupted household contexts and are associated with greater risks of depression, emotional insecurity and poorer mental health outcomes among older adults [24, 25].

Moreover, the increasing use of digital technologies for communication and social interaction has altered family interaction patterns and reshaped social support systems [7, 26]. Care coordination, in particular, has increasingly been mediated through digital systems, with telemedicine, wearable health devices, digital alerts, and virtual consultations enabling continuous monitoring and clinical decision-making within the home, thereby reducing reliance on institutional care settings. While digital platforms enable geographically separated family members to maintain contact, technology-mediated communication may only partially compensate for the loss of face-to-face interaction and everyday caregiving [26]. Several studies indicate that reliance on digital communication can transform standards of emotional availability and caregiving, thereby normalizing remote care over in-person care [27]. Conversely, declining coresidence, migration, and weakened family support may increase older adults’ dependence on digital technologies for social connection, health information, and access to services, particularly in urban and peri-urban contexts [28].

These transformations have important implications given the growing burden of social isolation and loneliness [29]. Globally, one in six individuals experience loneliness, which contributes to more than 871,000 deaths annually [30]. Among older adults, approximately one in three experiences social isolation, which is strongly associated with depression, cognitive decline, functional limitations and premature mortality [31, 32]. These patterns highlight the importance of examining not only the presence of social ties but also the structure, quality and perceived adequacy of social relationships later life [33].

Social networks and perceived social support are well-established protective factors for healthy aging. They encompass both structural dimensions (such as the size and composition of networks and frequency of contact) and functional dimensions (including emotional closeness, availability of help and quality of interactions) [34]. Older adults with stronger social ties through family, friends, or the community tend to report better physical and emotional well-being, greater autonomy and reduced reliance on institutional care [35, 36]. In this research, social support is conceptualized as encompassing emotional, physical, financial and confidant dimensions received through ongoing social relationships [37, 38].

Although a substantial body of research in India has examined aging, family change and social support among older adults, much of this work has emphasized structural family arrangements, kin availability, or broad indicators of support, particularly in rural settings or large metropolitan cities. Comparatively less attention has been given to how social relations and support are subjectively experienced and negotiated by older adults in everyday life, especially in rapidly urbanizing districts shaped by migration, family nuclearization and growing digital engagement. Although few qualitative studies have incorporated the perspectives of both older adults and family caregivers to examine relational support dynamics, few studies have captured the social networks of older adults via the Convoy Model and social support later in life, especially in an urbanizing environment. To address these gaps, the present qualitative study, guided by the Convoy Model, explores the social relationships, social networks and lived experiences of older adults in an urbanizing district of Tamil Nadu alongside caregiver narratives, offering novel insights into the meanings and negotiations underpinning social support in later life.

The Chengalpattu district, a rapidly transitioning urban–rural setting, is a critically underexplored area for addressing these gaps [39]. Undergoing rapid demographic and social transition, the region reflects the evolving realities of aging in India, where older adults navigate changing family structures, shifting caregiving arrangements and emerging forms of social connection across urban and rural contexts. Examining social networks in such transitional settings enables a more complex view of how support systems are maintained, adapted, or reconfigured beyond simplistic urban–rural distinctions.

The study aligns with global initiatives that emphasize social connectedness and age-friendly environments as public health priorities [30, 39]. Guided by the Convoy Model of Social Relations [40], this study explores how social networks are structured, how support is perceived and exchanged and how these relationships shape everyday life and aging experiences amid ongoing social change.

Methodology

Study design

This qualitative study was guided by a social constructivist paradigm within an interpretivist framework, recognizing that meanings related to social relationships, caregiving and aging are coconstructed through lived experiences and social interactions [41, 42]. This approach enables an in-depth exploration of how older adults perceive and experience social networks and social support, as well as how caregivers understand and enact their caregiving roles in relation to factors shaping older adults’ social interactions and network engagement [43].

An in-depth interview technique was adopted to elicit rich, contextualized narratives [43]. The study was conducted between January and June 2025 in selected urban and rural communities of Chengalpattu District, Tamil Nadu, India. The reporting of the study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist to enhance transparency and methodological rigor [44].

The study addressed the following research questions:

How do older adults in rural and urban Chengalpattu describe their social relationships and social networks?

How do older adults in rural and urban Chengalpattu describe their social relationships and social networks?

How do older adults and caregivers perceive and experience social support and in what ways does this support influence older adults’ daily activities?

How do older adults and caregivers perceive and experience social support and in what ways does this support influence older adults’ daily activities?

Sampling strategy and participant recruitment

The participants were recruited via a convenience sampling approach and selected on the basis of practical, contextual and ethical considerations associated with accessing older adults and caregivers within community settings [45]. Older adults and caregivers represent potentially vulnerable populations, and recruitment through local contacts, community networks and trusted communities facilitates respectful engagement, enhances participant comfort and ensures ethical access while minimizing intrusion or distress.

Although purposive sampling is commonly employed in qualitative research, convenience sampling in this study was strategically complemented by deliberate efforts to ensure diversity and variation across key characteristics relevant to the research objectives [45]. Recruitment spanned multiple urban and rural locations and included participants of various ages, genders, living arrangements, health statuses and caregiving roles. This approach enabled the inclusion of a broad range of social and caregiving experiences while remaining responsive to real-world field constraints.

Recruitment and analysis proceeded concurrently. Following each set of interviews, the transcripts were reviewed and coded, with emerging patterns were discussed within the research team. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached independently for older adults and caregivers. Saturation was operationalized as the point at which additional interviews failed to generate new codes, concepts, or analytic dimensions related to social networks, social support and caregiving experiences [43, 46]. Saturation was confirmed through iterative comparisons of successive interviews, reflexive team discussions, and two additional interviews with older adults and two with caregivers, none of which produced new codes or themes. This process ensured that the findings were grounded in analytic depth and conceptual redundancy rather than participant accessibility alone [43, 46].

Eligibility criteria: Older adults were eligible to participate if they:

Were aged 60–80 years.

Had resided in the community for at least one year.

Had resided in the community for at least one year.

Were cognitively able to understand the purpose of the study and provide voluntary informed consent.

Cognitive ability was assessed informally through a brief conversational assessment conducted prior to the interview. This included the evaluation of orientation to person, place and time; comprehension of the study purpose; and the ability to articulate a voluntary decision regarding participation. Only individuals who demonstrated clear understanding and coherent communication were enrolled. This pragmatic approach aligns with ethical qualitative research practices involving potentially vulnerable populations [41, 43, 46].

Older adults with severe cognitive impairment, those who were bedridden or dependent on full-time institutional care, or those who were undergoing prolonged hospitalization were excluded. Participants exhibiting severe depressive symptoms that substantially impaired communication or participation, as observed during initial screening or reported by family members, were also excluded to minimize distress and ensure data quality. Depression status was not clinically diagnosed but was assessed based on the basis of functional communication and emotional well-being.

Caregivers

Caregivers were defined as individuals who had provided regular emotional, physical, or instrumental support to a participating older adult for a minimum duration of six months prior to recruitment. This criterion was applied to ensure sufficient caregiving exposure and experiential depth.

Both informal caregivers (spouses, adult children, children-in-law, or other relatives) and formal caregivers (paid, nonkin individuals assisting with daily activities or care) were included to capture diverse caregiving experiences and perspectives.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face, semistructured interviews [43, 46] guided by the Convoy Model of Social Relations [38, 40, 47]. Separate but conceptually aligned interview guides were developed for older adults and caregivers to reflect their distinct roles and perspectives (provided as a supplementary file). The Interview domains included social network composition, emotional closeness, caregiving roles, perceived support, reciprocity and changes in social relationships over time.

The interview guides were pilot tested for clarity and cultural relevance and refinements were incorporated prior to the main data collection. The interviews were conducted in Tamil by SP, a trained public health researcher experienced in qualitative interviewing, with SS assisting in field-note documentation. The interviews lasted between 20 and 45 min and were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. When audio recording declined, detailed contemporaneous field notes were taken.

All the interviews were transcribed verbatim in Tamil and checked against audio recordings for accuracy and completeness. The transcripts were subsequently translated into English, prioritizing semantic and contextual equivalence rather than literal translation [41, 43, 46]. The translated transcripts were crosschecked by a bilingual researcher to minimize the loss of meaning. Identifying information was removed, and unique participant codes were assigned prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality.

Data cleaning, management, and analysis

Data cleaning involved careful review of all transcripts to ensure completeness, accuracy, and consistency with audio recordings and field notes. The transcripts were anonymized and formatted uniformly prior to analysis. Although verbatim transcripts were not returned to the participants due to literacy considerations and potential participant burden, interpretive member validation was incorporated during the interviews. The interviewers summarized key points and sought participants’ confirmation, clarification, or elaboration, ensuring accurate representation of intended meanings while minimizing burden [43, 44, 46].

The data were analyzed via reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [48]. Analysis commenced with the repeated reading of transcripts to achieve familiarization and immersion. This was followed by line-by-line open coding, during which meaningful units related to social relationships, caregiving practices and perceived support were identified.

A total of 169 initial codes were generated through a combination of deductive coding, informed by the Convoy Model of Social Relations, and inductive coding, grounded in participants’ narratives [46–48]. Codes were reviewed multiple times, compared across transcripts, and combined when they were similar in meaning. However, codes that were analytically distinct were kept to maintain nuance. Codes were subsequently organized into higher-order categories and refined into five analytically distinct themes through recursive engagement with the data, ensuring internal coherence, clear differentiation between themes and alignment with the study objectives [48].

Data management and coding were conducted via Microsoft Excel (2019), which facilitated the transparent organization of codes, iterative comparisons across cases, maintenance of an audit trail and consensus-building within the research team. Two researchers (SP and SS) independently coded all transcripts to enhance analytic rigor. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (BP) [42, 43]. Throughout the analysis, reflexive notes were maintained to document analytic decisions, assumptions and researcher positionality.

Blinding and reflexivity

Participant blinding was not feasible due to the nature of qualitative interview-based research. However, analytic blinding was implemented to reduce interpretive bias [43, 46]. Prior to coding, the transcripts were anonymized and stripped of identifying information related to participant identity, geographic location (urban/rural) and participant role (older adult/caregiver). Initial coding was conducted without reference to these attributes. Contextual identifiers were reintroduced only during later stages of analysis to support comparative interpretation.

Data saturation

Data saturation was defined as the point at which successive interviews yielded no new codes, concepts, or thematic dimensions and incoming data largely reiterated previously identified patterns [43, 46]. Analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection, enabling constant comparison of emerging findings across transcripts. Saturation was observed at the code and category levels for both older adults and caregivers. Confirmation of saturation was achieved through the conduct of additional interviews with each group, none of which generated new analytic insights.

Methodological rigor and trustworthiness

Methodological rigor was ensured through prolonged engagement in the field, independent coding by multiple researchers, reflexive analytic discussions, peer debriefing and adherence to the COREQ guidelines [43, 46]. The inclusion of both older adults and caregivers reflected the relational focus of the study and the Convoy Model of Social Relations. Sample sizes were determined by depth and richness of data rather than numerical equivalence, with saturation assessed independently for each participant group.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the SRM Institute of Science and Technology (Reg. No. ECR/8938/INST/TN/2013/RR19). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

The study was conducted in community settings, enabling participants to engage in a familiar and comfortable environment within their own homes. To minimize potential biases and ensure privacy, all interviews were conducted in a separate space within the participants’ residences.

Given the sensitive nature of discussions related to family relationships, loneliness, caregiving and aging, multiple safeguards were implemented to minimize potential psychological distress. The participants were informed of their right to decline questions, pause interviews, or withdraw at any point without consequence [42, 49]. The interviewers paid close attention to both verbal and nonverbal signs of discomfort and changed the speed or content of the interviews as needed. When emotional distress was expressed, interviews were temporarily paused and supportive, and empathetic listening was employed. No termination or external referral required an interview. These procedures were approved by the ethics committee [46, 49].

Theoretical framework

The study was guided by the Convoy Model of Social Relations [38, 40, 47], which conceptualizes individuals as moving through life surrounded by dynamic social networks that provide varying forms of support. These networks are represented as three concentric circles on the basis of emotional closeness and frequency of interaction, ranging from intimate and reliable ties to less close but still meaningful relationships.

As individuals age, their convoys evolve in response to life events such as retirement, widowhood, illness and migration. The model is particularly relevant in rapidly urbanizing contexts, where shifting family structures and weakening traditional support systems necessitate adaptive social strategies for aging with dignity.

However, themes that emerged inductively revealed clear differences between urban and rural participants in the availability, modality and meaning of support across inner, middle and outer circles. Inner circle – physical, emotional and financial support from close family networks

The inner circle, as conceptualized in the Convoy Model of Social Relations, refers to individuals to whom one feels emotionally close and life without them is difficult to imagine. Across both urban and rural settings, this circle primarily comprised spouses, children, children-in-law, grandchildren, siblings and other close blood relatives. These relationships form the core caregiving and emotional support system, particularly during periods of illness, functional decline, or emotional vulnerability (Fig. 1).

Inner circle networks

Across settings, immediate family members were consistently perceived as the most dependable caregivers, providing assistance with daily living activities such as hospital visits, transportation and emotional reassurance. However, the availability, modality and immediacy of this support differed markedly between urban and rural participants, and was shaped largely by migration patterns and coresidential arrangements.

Urban older adults frequently reported geographical separation from adult children due to labor migration; however, this did not signify an absence of inner-circle support. Inner - circle relationships remain defined by physical proximity and dependable daily care, primarily provided by spouses and coresiding or nearby kin. Migrant children provide emotional, decisional and financial support through digital communication, but physically proximate inner-circle members largely sustain hands-on and intimate care.

“We live alone - just my husband and me but my third daughter, who lives next door, helps us a lot. She cooks at her house and brings us food every day. When we’re sick, she takes complete care of us. There was a time when I was admitted to the hospital for a heart problem, and she did everything—from feeding me to helping me use the washroom. Even before I ask, she gives me what I need.” (P6, 65 years, Female, Urban).

Several urban participants described their dependence on daughters or spouses for intimate caregiving tasks during illness, highlighting both trust and emotional safety within these relationships.

“They (spouse and daughters’) have been helping me every day. I have heart issues and diabetes, and I am also growing older. Sometimes, I’m unable to control my bowel movements and urination. There have been times when I’ve experienced involuntary bowel movements. At times, it feels embarrassing, but my daughters’ and wife never let me feel that way. They are the ones who clean me up as well. Even when I say I’ll do it myself, they don’t allow me to.” (P4, 72years, Male, Urban).

“My sister-in-law and I (daughter) take care of all my mother’s physical needs. Earlier, we used to support her with walking and bathing. But now she is fully bedridden, so we handle everything, including feeding, bathing, dressing and cleaning.” (C5, 49 years, Female, Informal caregiver).

Emotional support from children was particularly salient among urban women who reported strained or abusive spousal relationships, indicating that inner-circle support was not uniformly experienced within marriages and was sometimes selectively accessed.

“For the past 20 years, only my daughter and son have supported me in every way. Even after I was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis, my husband continued to torment and force me to do many household chores. He verbally abused me with harsh words. During those times, I truly felt like dying. My daughter and son have always been there for me, offering comfort and support.” (P7, 68 years, Female, Urban).

Financial dependence was also more frequently discussed by urban participants, particularly among those unable to work due to poor health, whereas financial independence was associated with autonomy and dignity.

“My daughters take care of almost all our expenses. They pay for our food, hospital visits, medicines and even the house rent. I’m unable to work due to my health and my wife has only been doing housekeeping work for the past three months.” (P7, 65 years, Female, Urban).

“I am financially self-sufficient and not dependent on my son, daughter, or anyone else. It is helpful for both our families. Even though they are self-sufficient, my funds also contribute to the family.” (P1, 78 years, Male, Urban).

“We are not dependent on others for money. Financial stability is very important during old age to live a happy and healthy life. Without money, no one respects us.” (P6, 65 years, Female, Urban).

In contrast, rural older adults more commonly reported physical proximity and coresidence with children or extended kin, enabling timely, hands-on caregiving during illness and functional decline. Inner-circle support in rural contexts was characterized by routine physical assistance, a sustained presence during hospitalization and shared caregiving responsibilities within households.

“Last month I was admitted to the hospital for four days. My son stayed beside me the entire time. Though I felt anxious, his presence gave me strength. My daughter-in-law was equally supportive. They consoled me, saying, ‘Don’t worry, appa [dad], you’ll be fine soon. We’ll go home together in good health.’ Their care made me feel I was not alone.” (P10,78 years, Male, Rural).

Rural participants also described greater reliance on family-based caregiving rather than external substitutes, particularly for intimate care needs. Financial constraints, however, shaped caregiving dynamics for some rural older adults, compelling them to seek employment or government support schemes to maintain independence.

“My son doesn’t give me any money. If I ask him, he scolds me. Even my daughter-in-law scolds me at first, but eventually, she gives me a little. I work under the 100-day scheme [MGNREGA – Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act] in the village and with that small amount, I manage to buy my medicines and take care of myself.” (P8, 80 years, Female, Rural).

Social support and social networks of parents of migrated children

Among older adults whose children had migrated predominantly to urban settings, the inner circle was reconfigured to include extended kin and neighbors who were geographically accessible. Children continue to provide emotional and financial support through regular virtual contact, whereas nearby social ties substitute for immediate physical care.

“My son’s friend calls me every day to check on my health and other needs. If she feels I’m not well, she comes home and stays for 4–5 days. She visits at least once a month. I tell her everything; she feels more like a daughter to me. When my leg was hurting, the doctor suggested physiotherapy for 25 days. She accompanied me and stayed throughout the session. I was scared, but she gave me so much support.” (P22,74 years, Female, Urban).

Despite adaptive strategies, participants expressed a persistent longing for coresidential family life, underscoring the emotional limits of mediated support.

“Every morning after we wake up, my husband and I have coffee together, and then we call our sons who live in Dallas and Houston [cities in the United States of America]. Since it’s night time there, we speak to them in the morning. Our calls usually last for an hour or more, we talk about everything and also spend time interacting with our grandchildren.” (P17, 63 years, Female, Urban).

“I live alone, as my children are in Europe. My wife passed away four years ago, and I still miss her deeply. To cope with my loneliness, I go for a walk every morning, talk to my sister over the phone, spend time in the park, or visit the temple nearby. My son and daughter call me every night, and after speaking with them, I usually go to sleep.” (P16, 65 years, Male, Urban).

While the structure of inner-circle relationships remained similar across urban and rural settings, the experience of support differed substantially. Urban inner-circle support was more likely to be emotionally sustained but physically substituted, whereas rural support was characterized by proximity-driven, hands-on caregiving. These differences highlight how geographical context shapes not only who provides support but also how support is accessed, experienced and valued later in life.

Extended ties and infrequent support: the middle circle experience

Middle-circle relationships refer to individuals who are not as emotionally close as inner-circle members but remain important sources of support, particularly when the immediate family is unavailable or during emergencies. Across participants, the middle circle comprised extended kin (such as siblings, maternal aunts, cousins and parents of children’s spouses), friends and neighbors. Despite their generally less frequent contact, these relationships were crucial in supplementing care and support during periods of vulnerability.

Although middle-circle networks are present in both urban and rural settings, their nature, accessibility and role in daily life differ by geographical context, reflecting variations in community cohesion and social norms.

In rural settings, middle-circle relationships, particularly neighbors and extended kin, are described as deeply embedded in everyday life. Support was often reciprocal, informal and sustained over long-standing social ties. Rural participants frequently relied on neighbors and extended families for routine assistance, caregiving tasks and accompaniment during illness, especially when primary caregivers were unavailable.

“I’m now staying with my sister. Her son takes care of all my needs; he paid for my eye surgery and buys my medicines every month. Since I couldn’t conceive, my husband abandoned me long back, and from then my sisters’ son has been taking good care of me like his own mother.” (P9, 62 years, Female, Rural).

Extended kin such as maternal aunts and cousins were perceived as reliable caregivers who could step in to provide physical care, emotional reassurance and logistical support without hesitation.

“Whenever my maternal aunt visits, she talks with my mom and takes care of her. Sometimes, my cousin helps too - feeding her, cleaning, bathing, and even taking her to the hospital when I’m not available.” (C5,49 years Female, Informal caregiver).

These accounts highlight how rural middle-level support functions as a continuation of kinship-based caregiving rather than as a last-resort resource.

In contrast, urban participants described middle-circle relationships as more situational and selectively activated, particularly during emergencies. Neighbors and friends were approached primarily when immediate family members were unavailable, reflecting weaker everyday social cohesion but functional solidarity during crises.

“My neighbors have always been supportive. When I was living alone, they took care of me like their own family. When I fell ill, even though my family members were busy, they looked after the household chores.” (P14, 70 years, Female, Urban).

Urban middle-circle support often involves short-term, task-specific assistance, such as arranging transport, managing household chores, or providing temporary financial help.

“During my heart attack, we had no money for hospital or medicines, and I even missed the golden hour. Our neighbor, understanding our situation, gave ₹5000 [$58], arranged an ambulance, and refused to take the money back. His help felt like God’s blessing without him; I wouldn’t be alive today.” (P2, 68 years, Male, Urban).

Friends were also mobilized as alternative sources of support, particularly when family relationships were strained or unreliable.

“We usually manage my mother-in-law’s medical expenses by ourselves. But during tough times, I ask my friends or my husband’s friends for help, they’ve supported us financially, and I always repay them. I don’t ask relatives because I’ve had bad experiences before and don’t want to go through that again.“(C2, 36 years, Female, Informal caregiver).

Siblings occasionally assumed middle-circle roles by providing emotional reassurance and practical assistance during crises, particularly bereavement or hospitalization.

“My elder sister tried to console me after my son’s death. She was with me to take care and help my wife while I was admitted to the hospital… She supports and has supported me in all situations.” (P13, 67 years, Male, Urban).

While middle-circle relationships serve as critical supplementary support systems across both settings, their function and intensity vary by geographical context. In rural areas, middle-circle ties are a normal part of life and are based on long-term give-and-take. In cities, on the other hand, these ties were more temporary and focused on crises, coming into play mostly during emergencies or when inner-circle support was not available.

These findings underscore how the community context shapes not only the availability of extended support but also the expectations and norms governing its use.

Middle circle networks

Peripheral ties as occasional sources of support: outer circle

The outer circle, as defined by Antonucci, comprises individuals who may not be part of one’s close or extended family but are nonetheless considered important enough to be included in one’s personal social network. In this study, outer-circle members included formal caregivers, neighbors, friends, service providers (such as automobile drivers) and digital community groups. These ties were characterized by infrequent contact and situational support, yet they played a critical role during emergencies and periods when inner- and middle-circle support was unavailable.

Although outer-circle networks were present across both urban and rural settings, the composition, accessibility and perceived trustworthiness of these ties differed by geographical context, reflecting variations in social organization, service availability and community norms.

Urban participants more frequently identified formal caregivers, service providers and digital community groups as part of their outer circle. These relationships are typically task oriented and contractual and are activated in response to specific care needs or emergencies rather than sustained emotional engagement.

Formal caregivers play an important role in supporting older adults with daily routines, particularly in households where family members are unavailable due to work or migration.

Every day, I check her vitals, help with her physical training and also manage her diet. I also accompany her whenever she asks me to. (C11,45 years, Female, Formal Caregiver)

Urban participants also described relying on digital platforms, such as neighborhood WhatsApp groups, to mobilize rapid assistance during emergencies.

“We have a community WhatsApp group. In case of an emergency or if we are in need of anything, we drop a message in the group and immediately someone walks in for help.” (P17,63 Years, female, Urban).

Moreover, some urban participants expressed selective trust in peripheral ties, preferring a small number of known individuals over broader neighborhood reliance.

“We live alone and usually don’t rely on anyone, including our neighbors. My husband is bedridden, and in case of any emergency, especially when I’m not at home, I call my friend who lives on the next street to check on him. I don’t trust anyone else” (C10, 68 years, Female, Informal Caregiver).

Experiences of perceived neglect by immediate or extended family during crises further pushed some urban participants to depend on outer-circle ties.

“When I had a heart attack, my siblings had money but still didn’t help. They (siblings) doubted whether I was really affected, even though they knew I was in the hospital and didn’t even visit to see me.” (P2, 60 years, Male, Urban).

In rural settings, outer-circle support was more commonly drawn from long-standing acquaintances and familiar service providers, particularly auto drivers and community members who were known over extended periods. Trust in these relationships was grounded in personal familiarity and repeated interaction rather than formal arrangements.

“Whenever I need to go somewhere, like to a relative’s house or the temple, I call a familiar auto driver who always picks me up and drops me back safely. Even if he has other work, he still says, ‘Amma [mom], I’m coming.’ Sometimes my son doesn’t allow me to go alone, but when I tell him the auto driver will accompany me, he agrees.” (P20, 68 years, Female, Rural).

Although support from these ties was typically occasional, participants perceived it as reliable and reassuring, especially for mobility, accompaniment and safety during travel.

Outer-circle relationships function as situational safety nets across both urban and rural contexts. However, urban outer-circle support was more formalized, service-oriented and digitally mediated, whereas rural support relied on familiarity-based, relational ties embedded in everyday social life. These differences illustrate how geographical context shapes not only the composition of peripheral networks but also the forms of trust and access through which support is mobilized.

Outer circle networks

Use of media by older adults – a tool for connection, information, and engagement

Digital media and communication technologies play increasingly important roles in older adults’ daily lives by facilitating social connection, access to information and engagement in leisure activities. The participants described technology as a means of maintaining contact with family and friends, accessing health-related information, and alleviating boredom, loneliness and emotional distress. However, the extent and nature of technology use vary substantially by geographical context, reflecting differences in digital literacy, access and everyday routines.

Urban older adults reported frequent and diversified use of smartphones, describing mobile phones as readily accessible tools for communication, information seeking, and personalized entertainment. The participants highlighted the flexibility of digital platforms, which allowed them to engage with content at their pace and according to personal interests.

“Actually, if I don’t have any work, I spend time on my mobile phone. Nowadays, phone usage has increased a lot. Mobile phones have become like kerchief always with us and easy to access. On Television, they schedule shows, but on a mobile phone, we can watch whatever we want, whenever we want. I usually watch content related to politics, general knowledge, sports, history, geography, sometimes economics, and tourism-related information on my phone.”(P1, 78 years, Male, Urban).

Urban participants commonly use platforms such as YouTube to access content related to health, wellness, meditation and lifestyle practices. Digital games and online activities were also used as coping mechanisms to manage free time and emotional distress.

“I used to watch YouTube for videos on reverse aging, dietary habits, meditation, and health tips” (P19, 69 years, Male, Urban).

“Once I wake up, I play the Wordle [web-based word game] game, only after completing it my day begins. Whenever I feel free or upset, I play Sudoku [number-based logic puzzle] or Bubble Shooter [application-based puzzle game where you match and pop-colored bubbles] on my phone to keep myself engaged.” (P17, 63 years, Female, Urban).

Some urban participants further described participation in structured online activities, including spiritual sessions and skill-based classes, indicating more proactive and exploratory engagement with digital media.

In contrast, rural participants reported limited knowledge and use of smartphones, often restricting mobile phone use to basic calling functions. Digital engagement in rural settings was frequently mediated by younger family members, particularly grandchildren, rather than through independent use.

“I use my mobile only to talk to my sisters, other than that, I don’t know much. My granddaughter sometimes shows videos on the phone, and we listen to songs together. But I don’t know how to handle it myself” (P20, 68 years, Female, Rural).

Television emerged as the primary source of entertainment and information for rural older adults and was closely integrated into daily routines.

“Once I complete my daily household chores and cooking, I sit and watch serials in the afternoon until evening. After that, while watching TV, I do all the dinner preparation and cooking. I usually don’t use a smartphone and have only a basic button phone, and use it just to call my relatives and my friend, that’s all.” (P9, 62 years, Female, Rural).

A clear urban - rural divide was evident in technology use among older adults. The urban participants demonstrated greater digital autonomy and diversified engagement, using smartphones for communication, information, entertainment and structured activities. In contrast, rural participants relied predominantly on television and basic mobile phone functions, reflecting constraints related to digital literacy and access. These differences highlight how technological engagement intersects with geographical context to shape opportunities for social connection and everyday engagement later in life.

Theme 5: role of grandchildren in later life

Grandchildren occupy an important place in older adults’ social worlds, serving as sources of emotional connection, daily engagement, and in some cases, caregiving responsibility. Although many participants expressed affection and closeness toward their grandchildren, the experiences of grandparenthood were not uniform and varied significantly by geographical context.

Urban older adults more frequently described involvement in childcare as physically demanding and emotionally taxing, particularly when caregiving extended beyond occasional interaction into routine responsibility. The participants articulated a tension between affection for grandchildren and a desire to preserve autonomy and leisure in later life.

“I spend all my time with my grandsons. It’s not the same feeling all the time—sometimes I feel happy being with them, but most of the time, it feels like a burden and causes me stress.” (P19, 68 years, Male, Urban).

Several urban participants emphasized that, after decades of work, older age was perceived as a phase for rest and self-directed living rather than intensive caregiving.

“We’ve worked for the past 35 or 40 years, now is the time to live leisurely, not to take full responsibility for grandchildren. I love them, but I can’t take care of them all the time. Children should be with their parents. We can enjoy them and cuddle them, but not beyond that. This is our time to enjoy retirement.” (P17, 63 years, Female, Urban).

These accounts suggest that in urban settings, grandparenthood is often negotiated and bounded, shaped by changing expectations of aging, retirement and family roles.

In contrast, rural older adults described grandchildren as a primary source of emotional comfort, companionship and daily engagement. Spending time with grandchildren was framed as enjoyable, restorative and deeply meaningful, often described metaphorically as “medicine” for emotional well-being.

“All the household work is done by my daughter. I just spend time with my grandchildren. I enjoy every little movement and expression of theirs, watching them makes me happy and helps me forget the sad things.” (P10, 63 years, Female, Rural).

Rural participants also highlighted intergenerational closeness and shared communication as central to their daily lives.

My granddaughter is like a friend to me. I spend most of my time with her. She shares everything, even about her college life, and we talk a lot. I can’t imagine my life without her.” (P20, 68 years, Female, Rural).

Caregivers further reported that interaction with grandchildren played a therapeutic role in older adults with mobility limitations, supporting emotional well-being and cognitive engagement.

My mother can’t move without support, and her full-time engagement is with my children. I feel they are like medicine for her mental health. She also shares her childhood memories with them, and that keeps her engaged while I manage some household work.”. (C1, 34 years, Female, Informal caregiver)

The experience of grandparenthood in later life clearly revealed urban - rural differences.

Urban older adults more often perceived routine childcare as burdensome and incompatible with expectations of retirement and autonomy, whereas rural older adults experienced grandchildren as central sources of emotional fulfilment and everyday engagement. These findings underscore how geographical and sociocultural contexts shape intergenerational roles, expectations, and the meaning of care in older individuals.

Discussion

This study advances the application of the Convoy Model of Social Relations by examining how inner, middle and outer circle networks are structured and mobilized by older adults in urban and rural Indian settings. The consistent identification of a tripartite convoy structure across both contexts supports the model’s cross-cultural robustness [50] and aligns with Antonucci’s conceptualization of convoys as dynamic, life-course embedded systems [38]. Moreover, the findings demonstrate that geographical, socioeconomic and technological contexts significantly influence how these convoy structures operate in practice. In this study, the Convoy Model functioned not only as a descriptive framework but also as an analytical lens to interpret how support systems are actively reconfigured in response to migration, urbanization and changing family norms [51].

Across urban and rural settings, spouses and adult children remain central to the inner circle, reaffirming the continued importance of family-based caregiving in collectivist contexts [52–54]. However, the findings highlight a critical distinction between the structural presence of family members and their functional availability for everyday care. In urban contexts, migration and employment-related absences constrained routine caregiving, resulting in greater reliance on middle- and outer-circle actors, including neighbors, friends, digital networks and paid caregivers. In contrast, rural participants benefitted from greater physical proximity and everyday accessibility of kin and neighbors, facilitating more immediate instrumental and emotional support. These findings extend earlier Indian studies that portray family care as uniformly available by demonstrating that family support is increasingly contingent on place, mobility and household structure rather than cultural norms alone [36, 55, 56].

The inclusion of both informal and formal caregivers enabled a more comprehensive application of the Convoy Model [40]. Rather than viewing caregivers solely as components of older adults’ convoys, the findings indicate that caregivers themselves are embedded within their own support networks, drawing emotional, financial and practical assistance from spouses, siblings, friends, neighbors and colleagues. This reveals the presence of intersecting or layered convoys, wherein the sustainability of older adults’ support depends on the resilience of caregivers’ networks. While Antonucci conceptualized convoys as encircling a central individual across the life course [38, 57], the present findings suggest that caregiving contexts require a broader perspective that accounts for multiactor dependency and shared vulnerability, particularly in settings with limited formal care infrastructure [50]. This interpretation is consistent with the literature documenting caregiver strain, emotional fatigue and role overload, especially within urban nuclear households [58–60], and is highly relevant in the Indian context, where population aging is occurring more rapidly than the expansion of formal care services [61].

In addition to the structural characteristics of networks, the findings demonstrate that social support in later life is shaped primarily by how support is perceived, experienced and morally evaluated by older adults. Consistent with the Convoy Model, participants placed greater emphasis on emotional reassurance, respectful caregiving and reliability during illness than on coresidence or the mere presence of family members. This emphasis on the perceived and relational quality of support aligns with evidence from Tamil Nadu and other Indian contexts, where dignity, responsiveness and moral obligation guide older adults’ assessments of support [62]. The participants’ narratives indicated that support was experienced as meaningful when it was emotionally affirming and dependable, particularly during periods of illness and vulnerability. The findings further show that although sustained emotional contact through regular communication helps preserve a sense of support among parents of migrated children, mediated support cannot fully substitute for hands-on care during acute illness [63]. This distinction highlights how rural contexts continue to facilitate proximity-based caregiving, whereas urban settings increasingly rely on negotiated and emotionally mediated forms of support shaped by migration and work-related constraints.

These qualitative insights are consistent with population-based evidence documenting fragmented or inadequate social support among the oldest-old, widowed individuals, those living alone and older adults with functional limitations. Indian studies identify advanced age, solitary living, frailty and restricted mobility as key risk factors for weakened social networks and unmet support needs [64, 65], whereas evidence from Taiwan and other Asian settings similarly indicates heightened vulnerability to social isolation among adults aged over 80 years [66, 67]. By elucidating how older adults evaluate support relationally across inner, middle and outer circles, the present study complements these quantitative patterns and explains why neighbors, friends, or service providers may be perceived as more reliable than emotionally distant kin. Perceived inadequacy or unreliability of support was also linked to increased caregiver strain, which is consistent with prior evidence connecting weak support networks with greater caregiver burden [68].

Financial autonomy emerged as an additional factor shaping convoy dynamics by influencing self-perception and relational positioning [69]. Older adults with independent income reported greater agency, decision-making capacity and social participation, whereas financial dependence particularly among widowed women was associated with constrained autonomy and strained relationships[70]. These findings reflect gendered life-course inequalities rooted in differential access to education, employment and property ownership, which have been widely documented in Indian aging research [33, 71]. From a convoy perspective, economic resources function not only as material support but also as symbolic capital that shapes power relations, reciprocity and emotional security within social networks.

With respect to technology use, the study acknowledges existing evidence of increasing smartphone adoption among urban older adults in India [72]. A more nuanced understanding of how technology is incorporated into everyday life within social convoys is needed. Urban participants’ purposeful use of smartphones for health information, meditation, cognitive engagement, and emotional regulation suggests that digital tools function as adaptive resources, partially compensating for the reduced face-to-face family support associated with migration and dual-income households [28, 73]. In contrast, limited digital engagement among rural participants reflected structural constraints, including infrastructural gaps, lower digital literacy and fewer opportunities for intergenerational digital mediation, rather than a lack of interest or perceived value [74–77]. These findings caution against technodeterministic interpretations and underscore the importance of situating digital engagement within broader socioeconomic and spatial inequalities [63].

Finally, the differentiated role of grandchildren illustrates how intergenerational relationships are reshaped by broader structural changes. In rural contexts, grandchildren are described as important sources of emotional comfort and engagement, which is consistent with evidence linking emotionally embedded and voluntary grandparental involvement to improved psychological well-being, reduced depressive symptoms and a stronger sense of purpose later in life[78, 79]. In contrast, urban participants more frequently experienced routine grandchild caregiving as burdensome, particularly when responsibilities were intensive, obligatory and situated within nuclear households with limited support. Previous research has indicated that high-intensity or custodial grandparenting can lead to role strain, physical fatigue and reduced autonomy, especially in urban settings where older adults face competing expectations regarding retirement, leisure and self-care [80]. These findings challenge idealized representations of grandparenthood in India and highlight the importance of distinguishing between voluntary and obligatory intergenerational care.

Taken together, the findings indicate that urbanization, migration and technological change do not erode older adults’ social convoys but instead reconfigure their composition, functions and meanings. By foregrounding contextual variation, relational interdependence and adaptive strategies, this study strengthens the utility of the Convoy Model as an analytical framework for understanding aging as a dynamic, relational and place-specific process in contemporary India.

Recommendations

On the basis of the study findings, strengthening social convoys through context-specific community-level interventions is essential to support older adults and caregivers in both urban and rural settings. In urban areas where the migration of adult children has diminished everyday family support, neighborhood-based elder groups and resident-led digital forums (e.g., WhatsApp groups) can facilitate peer interaction, informal assistance and emotional support. In rural contexts, existing reciprocal care practices involving neighbors and extended kin should be formally supported through village-level elder forums or self-help groups to reinforce middle- and outer-circle networks. Because older adults in cities use smartphones on purpose, intergenerational digital literacy programs that include grandchildren, youth volunteers or community students can help older adults use smartphones more for communication, health information and fun. However, in rural areas these programs must also address infrastructure problems and low digital literacy to avoid leaving people out of the digital world [83].

Finally, eldercare interventions should explicitly recognize nonkin and caregivers as integral components of older adults’ support convoys by facilitating caregiver support groups and respite mechanisms, thereby reducing emotional burden and sustaining caregiving capacity.

Strengths and limitations

The study offers a unique contribution by capturing the perspectives of both older adults and their caregivers, providing a holistic understanding of social networks and support in the Indian context via the convoy model of social relations. These three concentric circles allow for a deeper understanding of how dynamic social relationships influence daily functioning and well-being later in life.

The study adds context-specific insights relevant to rapidly urbanizing regions in Tamil Nadu. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study’s qualitative design and its focus on a single district may limit the generalizability of the findings [43, 46]. Second, the socioeconomic characteristics of the participants were not captured which may constrain a more profound understanding of the findings as variations in income, education and economic security are likely to shape access to caregiving support, digital resources and community participation.

Third, a more balanced sampling of formal and informal caregivers could have strengthened the analysis and yielded additional insights into caregiving roles and support mechanisms. Additionally, the study relies on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias [81, 82]. Despite these limitations, this study provides an important foundation for future research and informs the development of context-sensitive policy interventions addressing aging and caregiving in India.

Conclusion

The findings demonstrate that social support later in life is shaped by dynamic and interconnected networks influenced by urbanization, migration and technological change. By applying the Convoy Model across contexts and incorporating caregivers’ perspectives, this study contributes theoretically by extending the understanding of social convoys as interdependent systems involving both older adults and caregivers. Practically, the findings demonstrate the importance of context-sensitive aging interventions that recognize caregivers and nonkin networks and support the purposeful use of technology alongside face-to-face care.

Such approaches are essential for strengthening social connectedness and well-being among India’s aging population.
