A new separable property of the joint numerical range of quadratic functions and its applications to the Smallest Enclosing Ball Problem
Van-Bong Nguyen, Huu-Quang Nguyen

Abstract
We explore separable property of the joint numerical range G ⁡ ( ℝ n ) G(\mathbb{R}^{n}) of a special class of quadratic functions and apply it to solving the smallest enclosing ball (SEB) problem which asks to find a ball B ⁡ ( a , r ) B(a,r) in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} with smallest radius r r such that B ⁡ ( a , r ) B(a,r) contains the intersection ∩ i = 1 m B ( a i , r i ) \cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i}) of m m given balls B ⁡ ( a i , r i ) . B(a_{i},r_{i}). We show that G ⁡ ( ℝ n ) G(\mathbb{R}^{n}) is convex if and only if rank ⁡ { a 1 − a , a 2 − a , … , a m − a } ≤ n − 1 . {\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}\leq n-1. Otherwise, rank ⁡ { a 1 − a , a 2 − a , … , a m − a } = n {\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n and G ⁡ ( ℝ n ) G(\mathbb{R}^{n}) is not convex. In this case we propose a new set G ​ ( ℝ n ) ∙ G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet} which allows to show that if m = n m=n then G ​ ( ℝ n ) ∙ G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet} is convex even G ⁡ ( ℝ n ) G(\mathbb{R}^{n}) is not. Importantly, the separable property of G ​ ( ℝ n ) ∙ G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet} then implies the separable property for G ⁡ ( ℝ n ) . G(\mathbb{R}^{n}). As a result, a new progress on solving the SEB problem is obtained.

Introduction

Let
g(x)=x^{T}x-2a^{T}x+\theta,g_{i}(x)=x^{T}x-2a_{i}^{T}x+\theta_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,m,

where
a,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\theta,\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R},
be strictly convex quadratic functions and denote

\Lambda=\{z=(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{m})^{T}:z_{0}<0,z_{i}\leq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,m\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}

the negative orthant in

\mathbb{R}^{m+1}.

In this paper we study the separable property of the
joint numerical range

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{(-g(x),g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{m}(x))^{T},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}

of the quadratic mapping

G=(-g,g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m+1}

in the sense that we find conditions under which
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Lambda
can be separated by a hyperplane whenever

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.
Our study has the main motivation from the smallest enclosing ball (SEB) problem which asks to find
a ball
B(a,r)
centered at
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smallest radius
r
such that
B(a,r)
contains the intersection

\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})

of given balls
B(a_{i},r_{i}),i=1,2,\ldots,m.
To see the relation between the separable property of
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the SEB problem,
we make a short review as follows.
The balls can be expressed as:

B(a_{i},r_{i})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g_{i}(x)\leq 0\},i=1,2,\ldots,m,
and
B(a,r)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(x)\leq 0\}.
Suppose that the intersection
\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})

has a nonempty interior. The SEB problem is then
restated in the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{llll}&\min&r&\\
&{\rm s.t.}&\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})\subset B(a,r),\\
&&r>0,a\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

where
r=\sqrt{\|a\|^{2}-\theta}
is needed to find and
r_{i}=\sqrt{\|a_{i}\|^{2}-\theta_{i}}
are given.
The constraint
\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})\subset B(a,r)
means that

(x\in\mathbb{R}^{n})~g_{1}(x)\leq 0,\ldots,g_{m}(x)\leq 0\Rightarrow g(x)\leq 0.
Or equivalently,
the system of inequalities
g_{1}(x)\leq 0,\ldots,g_{m}(x)\leq 0,-g(x)<0
has no root, and thus
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.

Now, if
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Lambda
are separable by a hyperplane,
problem (
1
) is reformable as an SDP problem and thus solvable in polynomial time
[
13
]
. So the question is that under what conditions,

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Lambda
are separable by a hyperplane?

Separable property of the joint numerical range is an interesting topic in literature with various applications. Normally, if
the joint numerical range is convex, it is separable. Dines
[
6
]
showed
that the joint numerical range of two homogeneous quadratic functions
W(A,B)=\{(x^{T}Ax,x^{T}Bx),x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is always convex, where
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are symmetric matrices.
This result was then applied by
Yakubovich to obtain the well known S-Lemma
[
20
]
.
Brickman
[
5
]
showed that the restriction of
W(A,B)
on the unit sphere:

\{(x^{T}Ax,x^{T}Bx),\|x\|=1\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
n\geq 3
is still convex. This helps to obtain an extension of the S-Lemma, please see
[
13
,
10
]
. However, for nonhomogeneous quadratic functions or more than two homogeneous quadratic functions, the convexity
may not be guaranteed.
Polyak
[
14
]
showed that
the joint numerical range of three quadratic forms
W(A,B,C)=\{(x^{T}Ax,x^{T}Bx,x^{T}Cx),x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

with
n\geq 3
is convex and the quadratic forms
x^{T}Ax,x^{T}Bx,x^{T}Cx
has no common zero except zero if and only if there is a positive definite combination of
A,B,C,
i.e., there are scalars
\lambda,\gamma,\eta
such
that
\lambda A+\gamma B+\eta C\succ 0.

For quadratic functions
q_{i}(x)=x^{T}A_{i}x+a_{i}^{T}x+\alpha_{i},i=1,2,

the joint numerical range
W(q_{1},q_{2})=\{(q_{1}(x),q_{2}(x)),x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
with
n\geq 2
is convex if there are some

\lambda,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda A_{1}+\gamma A_{2}\succ 0

[
14
]
.
For more results on convexity and separable property of the joint numerical range,
the readers are encouraged to see the well known survey
[
13
]
and more recent studies

[
9
,
11
,
12
,
8
]
with
references therein.
For the joint numerical range
G(\mathbb{R}^{n}),
a special type of quadratic mapping, Beck
[
1
]
showed that if
m\leq n-1
then
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex.

The model (
1
) is a special case of the outer approximation problems
which find the minimum volume ellipsoid enclosing a given set
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
see for example

[
4
,
16
]
for the models of the problem and
[
3
,
7
]
for some applications such as the applications in environmental science, in pattern
recognition, in protein analysis, in analysis of party spectra, of

Depending on the set
S,
the outer approximation problem is known tractable or not. For example, if
S
is a finite set of points, a polyhedron, or
a union of ellipsoids, the problem is solvable in polynomial time

[
4
,
15
,
1
]
. Algorithms for those cases can be found, for example in
[
18
]
for the case

is a union of circles in the Euclidian plane; in

[
21
]
when
S
is a union of balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n};
in

[
19
]
when
S
is a finite set of

\mathbb{R}^{n}.

However, when

is the intersection of ellipsoids, the problem is considered difficult, because
only checking whether an ellipsoid contains the intersection of given ellipsoids is already NP-hard

[
4
, Sect. 3.7.2]
. For problem (
1
),

S
is the intersection of balls and the problem is also known as the ball version of the
outer approximation problems. Beck
[
1

2
]
showed that if

then the SEB problem (

) is solvable

showed that the problem is in general NP-hard.
Those results raise a question that how large the number

In this paper
we show that
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex if and only if
{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}\leq n-1.

Otherwise,
{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}=n

is then not convex.
In this case, we propose a new set denoted by

to reveal several interesting properties of

please see Definition
1
below. We show that if

{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}=n

then

is convex even

is not; especially,

implies
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset;
we note that in this case

This new separable property of

allows to obtain a new extension of the S-Lemma, please see Corollary

below, which helps
to reformulate the SEB
problem (

) as an SDP problem. Then, using the same analysis technique in
[

]
we further reformulate the resulting SDP problem as a

2
On the convexity of
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})

In this section we propose a necessary and sufficient condition for
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be convex. Our proof is based on
the following result.

Lemma 1

(
[
1
]
)
.

If
m\leq n-1,
the set

S=\{(x^{T}x-2a^{T}x,a_{1}^{T}x,a_{2}^{T}x,\ldots,a_{m}^{T}x)^{T},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}

is closed and convex.

The main result of this section is now obtained as follows.

Theorem 1
.

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex if and only if
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}\leq n-1.

Proof.

Suppose first that
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}\leq n-1,
we shall show that
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex. Let

p={\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}
and suppose without loss of generality
that the first
p
vectors are linear independent, i.e.,
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{p}-a\}=p\leq n-1.

Then for each
1\leq i\leq m,a_{i}-a
is uniquely written as a linear combination
of
a_{1}-a,\cdots,a_{p}-a
as follows

a_{i}-a=\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\alpha_{ij}}(a_{j}-a),~i=1,2,\ldots,m,

(4)

where
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}.
Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
be a set defined by

C:=\left\{\left(-x^{T}x+2a^{T}x,-2(a_{1}-a)^{T}x,\ldots,-2(a_{p}-a)^{T}x\right)^{T},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p+1}.

Using Lemma
1
, we can easily show that
C

is closed and convex. Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{p+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
be a linear mapping
defined by

T(z_{0},z_{1}\ldots,z_{p})=\left(z_{0},\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{1j}z_{j},\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{2j}z_{j},\ldots,\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{mj}z_{j}\right)^{T}.

We then have

T(C)=\left\{\left(-x^{T}x+2a^{T}x,-2(a_{1}-a)^{T}x,\ldots,-2(a_{p}-a)^{T}x,\ldots,-2(a_{m}-a)^{T}x\right)^{T}\right\}.

Let
L:\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
be an affine transformation defined by

\displaystyle L(z_{0},z_{1}\ldots,z_{m})=(z_{0},z_{1}-z_{0},\ldots,z_{m}-z_{0})^{T}+(-\theta,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m})^{T}.

Then we observe that

\displaystyle L(T(C))=

\displaystyle\left\{\left(-x^{T}x+2a^{T}x-\theta,x^{T}x-2a_{1}^{T}x+\theta_{1},\ldots,x^{T}x-2a_{m}^{T}x+\theta_{m}\right)^{T},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Since
the union
L\circ T
is an affine mapping and

C
is convex, its image

(L\circ T)(C)
is convex,
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is thus convex.

Conversely, we suppose on contrary that
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex but
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n.

Let
D
be defined by

D=\left\{\left(-x^{T}x+2a^{T}x,-2(a_{1}-a)^{T}x,\ldots,-2(a_{m}-a)^{T}x\right)^{T},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}.

Applying Theorem 2.2 in
[
1
]
, we can show that
D
is not convex. But now, by defining the following affine transformation

F:\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m+1},

F(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})=(z_{0},z_{1}+z_{0},\ldots,z_{m}+z_{0})+(\theta,-(\theta_{1}-\theta),\ldots,-(\theta_{m}-\theta))

we will have

F(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=\{(-x^{T}x+2a^{T}x,-2(a_{1}-a)^{T}x,\ldots,-2(a_{m}-a)^{T}x)\}=D.

That is,
D
is the image via an affine transformation of the convex set
G(\mathbb{R}^{n}),
it must be convex and we get a contradiction.
So it must be that

{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}<n.

∎

Example 1
.

Let
g(x)=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-2x_{1}-2x_{2},

g_{1}(x)=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-2x_{2},

g_{2}(x)=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-2x_{1}

be three convex quadratic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Here,
a=(1,1)^{T},a_{1}=(0,1)^{T},a_{2}=(1,0)^{T}
and
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a\}=2=n

and
\theta=\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=0.

We check that

G(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{(-g(x),g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x))^{T}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

is not convex. Indeed, let

{u}=(1,0)^{T},{v}=(0,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we have
G(u)=(1,1,-1)^{T},G(v)=(1,-1,1)^{T}\in G(\mathbb{R}^{2}).
The
midpoint of the line segment
[G(u),G(v)]
is
(1,0,0)^{T}.
However, there is no point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

(-g(x),g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x))^{T}=(1,0,0)^{T}.
That is the line segment
[G(u),G(v)]
is not contained in
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and so
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is not convex.

3
Separable property of
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})

As mentioned, if
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex, the separable property holds for it. By Theorem
1
,

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex if and only if
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}\leq n-1.
In this section,
we will show that when
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n=m,

the separable property still holds for
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
even it is not convex. To do so, we need first to give the following new concept.

Definition 1
.

For a set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
we define a set
D^{\bullet}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows:

D^{\bullet}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v;~u,v\in D,0\leq\lambda\leq 1\}.

By this definition,
D^{\bullet}
is the set of the convex combinations of points of
D
and looks like the convex hull
{\rm conv}(D)
of
D.
But, in fact,

D^{\bullet}
is different from
{\rm conv}(D).

Indeed, we consider the following
example: let
D=\{u=(0,0)^{T},v=(1,0)^{T},\omega=(0,1)^{T}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the set of three points
u,v,\omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Then
D^{\bullet}
is the three edges of the triangle formed by the three vertices
u,v,\omega,

while
{\rm conv}(D)
is the triangle of those three vertices. More concrete,
for any
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
it holds that

D\subset D^{\bullet}\subset{\rm conv}(D).

The following two results are important but their proofs are so simple, we thus omit them.

Lemma 2
.

D=D^{\bullet}={\rm conv}(D)

if and only if
D
is convex.

Lemma 3
.

D^{\bullet}
is invariant under a nonsingular affine transformation. That is, if

L
is an invertible affine transformation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then

L(D^{\bullet})=L(D)^{\bullet}.

Lemma 4
.

(i)

If
f(x)=x^{T}Ax-2a^{T}x+\theta
is a convex quadratic function, i.e.,
A\succeq 0
and
A\neq 0,
then
\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}=\mathrm{epigraph}(f);

(ii)

If
f(x)=x^{T}Ax-2a^{T}x+\theta
is a concave quadratic function, i.e.,
A\preceq 0
and
A\neq 0,
then
\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}=\mathrm{hypograph}(f).

Proof.

We prove only (i). The proof of (ii) is done similarly.

We first show
\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}\subset\mathrm{epigraph}(f).
Let
\omega\in\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet},

by definition, there will be
u=(x_{1},y_{1}),v=(x_{2},y_{2})\in\mathrm{graph}(f),y_{1}=f(x_{1}),y_{2}=f(x_{2}),
such that

\omega=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v=(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2},\lambda y_{1}+(1-\lambda)y_{2})

for some
0\leq\lambda\leq 1.
Since
f
is convex, we have

f(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2})\leq\lambda f(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f(x_{2})=\lambda y_{1}+(1-\lambda)y_{2}.

This shows
\omega\in\mathrm{epigraph}(f)
and implies
\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}\subset\mathrm{epigraph}(f).

For the converse, let
\omega=(\bar{x},t)\in\mathrm{epigraph}(f)
be any point in the epigraph of
f,
i.e.,
f(\bar{x})\leq t,
we need to
show that
\omega\in\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}.
Indeed,

1.

If
f(\bar{x})=t
then
\omega\in\mathrm{graph}(f)
so
\omega\in\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}.

2.

If
f(\bar{x})<t,
we now show that there exist
u,v\in\mathrm{graph}(f)
such that

\omega=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v,

0\leq\lambda\leq 1.
Specifically, the points
u,v
are of the form

u=(\hat{x},t),v=(\tilde{x},t)
such that
f(\hat{x})=f(\tilde{x})=t
and
\bar{x}=\lambda\hat{x}+(1-\lambda)\tilde{x}.

Observe that
\hat{x}
and
\tilde{x}
are solutions of the equation
f(x)=t.

For simplicity when solving this equation we make
A
diagonal as follows.
Since
A\succeq 0
and
A\neq 0,
there is a nonsingular matrix
P
such that
P^{T}AP={\rm diag}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}),

\alpha_{i}\geq 0,~\forall i=1,2,\ldots,n,
and
\alpha_{j}>0
for some

j.
Let
x=Py,
then

x^{T}Ax-2a^{T}x+\theta=y^{T}(P^{T}AP)y-2(P^{T}a)^{T}y+\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}y_{i}^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}y_{i}+\theta,

where
y^{T}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})
and
(P^{T}a)^{T}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}).

The equation
f(x)=t
then becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}y_{i}^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}y_{i}+\theta=t.

(5)

Let
\bar{y}=P^{-1}\bar{x}=(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{j-1},\bar{y}_{j},\bar{y}_{j+1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{n})^{T}.

In (
5
) we fix
y_{i}=\bar{y}_{i}
for all
i\neq j,
then (
5
) becomes the equation of only one unknown
y_{j}.

Let

g(y_{j})=\alpha_{j}y_{j}^{2}-2a_{j}y_{j}+\sum_{i\neq j}\alpha_{i}\bar{y}_{i}^{2}-2\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}\bar{y}_{i}+\theta.

Since
\alpha_{j}>0,

g(y_{j})
is a strictly convex function of
y_{j},
and
g(\bar{y}_{j})<t.
Those imply that the equation

g(y_{j})=t
has two distinct solutions denoted by
\hat{y}_{j}
and
\tilde{y}_{j}
such that

\bar{y}_{j}
is in the line segment connecting two endpoints
\hat{y}_{j}
and
\tilde{y}_{j}.
That is, there is a value

0\leq\lambda\leq 1
such that
\bar{y}_{j}=\lambda\hat{y}_{j}+(1-\lambda)\tilde{y}_{j}.

Let

\hat{y}=(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{j-1},\hat{y}_{j},\bar{y}_{j+1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{n})^{T},\tilde{y}=(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{j-1},\tilde{y}_{j},\bar{y}_{j+1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{n})^{T}.

We have
\bar{y}=\lambda\hat{y}+(1-\lambda)\tilde{y}
and

\bar{x}=P\bar{y}=\lambda P\hat{y}+(1-\lambda)P\tilde{y}=\lambda\hat{x}+(1-\lambda)\tilde{x},

where
\hat{x}=P\hat{y}
and
\tilde{x}=P\tilde{y}.
We now have

f(\hat{x})=f(\tilde{x})=t.
Let
u=(\hat{x},t),v=(\tilde{x},t)
then

u,v\in\mathrm{graph}(f)
such that
\omega=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v
with
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
as desired.
So
\omega\in\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}
and that
\mathrm{epigraph}(f)\subset\mathrm{graph}(f)^{\bullet}.

∎

The main result of this section is now stated as follows.

Theorem 2
.

If either
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}<n
or

{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n=m,
then the following hold.

(i)

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
is convex;

(ii)

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
implies

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.

Proof.

(i) If
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}<n
then, by Theorem
1
,
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex so, by Lemma
2
,
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}=G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and thus

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
is convex.
If
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}=n
and

m=n,

we define a linear transformation
H:\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
by

H(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})=(z_{1}+z_{0},\ldots,z_{m}+z_{0},-z_{0})^{T}.

(6)

Then

\displaystyle H(G(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g_{1}(x)-g(x),\ldots,g_{m}(x)-g(x),g(x))^{T}

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-2(a_{1}-a)^{T}x+\theta_{1}-\theta,\ldots,-2(a_{m}-a)^{T}x+\theta_{m}-\theta,x^{T}x-2a^{T}x+\theta)^{T}.

Let

\displaystyle\begin{split}&y_{i}=-2(a_{i}-a)^{T}x+\theta_{i}-\theta,i=1,\ldots,m,\end{split}

(8)

and
A
be a square matrix defined by

A=\begin{bmatrix}-2(a_{1}-a)^{T}\\
\vdots\\
-2(a_{m}-a)^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

Then we have

y=(-2(a_{1}-a)^{T}x+\theta_{1}-\theta,\ldots,-2(a_{m}-a)^{T}x+\theta_{m}-\theta)^{T}=Ax+\hat{\theta},

where
\hat{\theta}=(\theta_{1}-\theta,\theta_{2}-\theta,\ldots,\theta_{m}-\theta)^{T}
and

H(G(x))=(y^{T},g(x))^{T}.

Since
a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a
are linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m},
the matrix
A
is nonsingular and we have

x=A^{-1}y-A^{-1}\hat{\theta}.
Then

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\left(A^{-1}y-A^{-1}\hat{\theta}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{T}\left(\underbrace{{A^{-1}}^{T}A^{-1}}_{B}\right)y-2\underbrace{\left(\hat{\theta}^{T}{A^{-1}}^{T}A^{-1}+a^{T}{A^{-1}}\right)}_{\bar{a}^{T}}y

(10)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\hat{\theta}^{T}\left({A^{-1}}^{T}A^{-1}\right)\hat{\theta}+2a^{T}A^{-1}\hat{\theta}+\theta}_{\bar{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\bar{g}(y).

Now we have

H(G(x))=(y^{T},\bar{g}(y))^{T},

where
\bar{g}(y)=y^{T}By-2\bar{a}^{T}y+\bar{\theta}
is a strictly convex quadratic function since

B={A^{-1}}^{T}A^{-1}\succ 0.

The image
H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
is then

H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=\{(y^{T},\bar{g}(y))^{T},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

(11)

We apply Lemma
4
to have

H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\bullet}=\{(y^{T},\bar{g}(y))^{T},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}^{\bullet}=\mathrm{graph}(\bar{g})^{\bullet}=\mathrm{epigraph}(\bar{g}).

Since the function
\bar{g}(y)
is strictly convex, its epigraph
\mathrm{epigraph}(\bar{g})

is convex and thus
H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\bullet}
is convex. Moreover,
H
is a nonsingular linear mapping, by Lemma
3
we have

H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\bullet}=H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}).
This shows that
H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet})
is convex and

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
is convex.

(ii) As seen, if
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}<n
then
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})=G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}.

So
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
implies
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.

If
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n
and
m=n,
we will show that

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
implies
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.
Let us assume on contrary that

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset.
Then
there is

\omega=(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n})^{T}\in G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda

(12)

such that
\omega_{0}<0,\omega_{1}\leq 0,\ldots,\omega_{n}\leq 0

and
\omega=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v
for some
u\in G(\mathbb{R}^{n}),v\in G(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

0\leq\lambda\leq 1.

Let

T_{\omega}:=\{\omega+(t,0,\ldots,0)^{T},~t\leq 0\}=\{(\omega_{0}+t,\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n})^{T},~t\leq 0\}\subset\Lambda.

We are going to show that
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap T_{\omega}\neq\emptyset,
which then implies a contradiction that
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset.

Using the same notations as in (i), we first have, by (
11
):

H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=\{(y^{T},\bar{g}(y))^{T}:y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

(13)

Let
z=(y^{T},z_{0})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
f(z)=\bar{g}(y)-z_{0},
then

H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:f(z)=0\}.

(14)

Observe that
f(z)
is a continuously convex function, its lower level set

\Omega:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:f(z)\leq 0\}

(15)

is thus a convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\subset\Omega.

Let
\bar{u}=H(u),\bar{v}=H(v),\bar{\omega}=H(\omega).

Since
u\in G(\mathbb{R}^{n}),v\in G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\omega=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v,
i.e.,
\omega
is in the line segment
[u,v]:

\omega\in[u,v],
we have

\bar{u},\bar{v}\in H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and

\bar{\omega}\in[\bar{u},\bar{v}].
This shows
\bar{\omega}\in\Omega
and thus

\displaystyle f(\bar{\omega})\leq 0.

(16)

Pick a point
\omega_{t}=(\omega_{0}+t,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})^{T}\in T_{\omega},t\leq 0,
and consider

\displaystyle\bar{\omega_{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(\omega_{t})=(\omega_{1}+\omega_{0}+t,\ldots,\omega_{n}+\omega_{0}+t,-\omega_{0}-t)^{T}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\bar{y}_{t}^{T},-\omega_{0}-t)^{T}=(\bar{y}^{T}+t\bar{1}^{T},-\omega_{0}-t)^{T}\in H(T_{\omega}),

where
\bar{y}_{t}^{T}=(\omega_{1}+\omega_{0}+t,\ldots,\omega_{n}+\omega_{0}+t),t\leq 0,\bar{y}^{T}=(\omega_{1}+\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n}+\omega_{0})
and
\bar{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T},
we observe that

\displaystyle g(t):=f(\bar{\omega}_{t})=(\bar{1}^{T}B\bar{1})t^{2}+2\left(\bar{1}^{T}B\bar{y}-\bar{a}^{T}\bar{1}+\frac{1}{2}\right)t+\omega_{0}+\bar{g}(\bar{y})

is a quadratic function of
t
with quadratic coefficient
\bar{1}^{T}B\bar{1}>0.

So
g(t)>0
when
t<0
and
|t|
is large enough. On the other hand, applying (
16
) we
have

g(0)=\omega_{0}+\bar{g}(\bar{y})=f(\bar{\omega})\leq 0.

Those together with the continuity of
g
imply that there exists
t^{*}\leq 0
such that
g(t^{*})=f(\bar{\omega}_{t^{*}})=0.
This indicates that

\bar{\omega}_{t^{*}}\in H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n})).
And as seen,
\bar{\omega}_{t^{*}}\in H(T_{{\omega}})
so

\bar{\omega}_{t^{*}}\in H(T_{{\omega}})\cap H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n})).
Since

H
is a linear isomorphism, it holds that

H(T_{{\omega}})\cap H(G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=H(T_{{\omega}}\cap G(\mathbb{R}^{n})).

That is
\bar{\omega}_{t^{*}}\in H(T_{{\omega}}\cap G(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and it implies that
T_{{\omega}}\cap G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\neq\emptyset.
This further implies
\Lambda\cap G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\neq\emptyset
since
T_{{\omega}}\subset\Lambda.
This contradicts to
our assumption that
\Lambda\cap G(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\emptyset.

∎

As a result we obtain the following extension of the S-Lemma

[
20
,
13
]
. For completeness, we provide its proof below (c.f.
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
).

Corollary 1

(An extension of the S-Lemma)
.

Suppose the interior of the intersection
\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})
is nonempty, i.e., there is
a point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
g_{i}(\bar{x})<0,i=1,2,\ldots,m.
Then if either

{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}<n
or
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}=n
and

m=n,
the following two statements are equivalent.

(i)

The system
\begin{cases}g(x)>0,\\
g_{i}(x)\leq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,m,\end{cases}
is unsolvable,

(ii)

There exist nonnegative scalars
\mu_{i}\geq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,m
such that

\displaystyle-g(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}g_{i}(x)\geq 0~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(18)

Proof.

The implication
(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
is easy to obtain. We therefore prove only the direction

(i)\Rightarrow(ii).

The system (i) is unsolvable meaning that

\displaystyle G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.

(19)

On the other hand, if either

{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}<n
or
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\cdots,a_{m}-a\}=n
and

m=n,
then, by Theorem
2
,
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
is convex and (
19
) implies

\displaystyle G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.

(20)

So,
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
and
\Lambda
are separable by a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}.

That is, there exist scalars
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
not all zero such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}z_{0}+\lambda_{1}z_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{m}z_{m}\leq 0,\forall z=(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})^{T}\in\Lambda,

(21)

\displaystyle-\lambda_{0}g(x)+\lambda_{1}g_{1}(x)+\ldots+\lambda_{m}g_{m}(x)\geq 0,~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(22)

Since
(-1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}\in\Lambda,
(
21
) implies that
\lambda_{0}\geq 0.
Moreover,
substituting

u^{i}=\underbrace{(-\epsilon,0,\ldots,-1,}_{-1\text{ in the }(i+1)th\text{ position }}0,\ldots,0)\in\Lambda,i=1,2,\ldots,m,

with arbitrary small

\epsilon>0
into (
21
) we obtain that
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,2,\ldots,m.

Now, using (
22
) with the assumption that
g_{i}(\bar{x})<0
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,m,
we see that
\lambda_{0}

cannot be zero. That is
\lambda_{0}>0.

Divide both sides of (
22
) by
\lambda_{0}
and let
\mu_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{0}}
we get the desired proof.
∎

4
Applications to the smallest enclosing ball problem

Given a set of
m
balls
B(a_{i},r_{i}),i=1,2,\ldots,m,
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the intersection
\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})
has
a nonempty interior, where
a_{i},r_{i}
are the center and radius
of the ball
B(a_{i},r_{i}).
We now apply the results in the previous sections
to find a ball
B(a,r)
of center
a
and radius
r
such that
B(a,r)
is the ball of smallest radius containing
the intersection
\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i}).

Note that

B(a_{i},r_{i})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x-a_{i}\|\leq r_{i}\},i=1,2,\ldots,m.

The enclosing
\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})\subset B(a,r)
means that

\displaystyle\|x-a_{i}\|\leq r_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,m,\Rightarrow\|x-a\|\leq r.

(23)

Equivalently, the system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\|x-a\|^{2}>r^{2},\\
\|x-a_{i}\|^{2}\leq r_{i}^{2},i=1,2,\ldots,m,\end{cases}

(24)

is unsolvable. By Corollary
1
,

if either

\displaystyle{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}<n\text{~ or }

\displaystyle{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n\text{~ and ~ }m=n,

(25)

then the un-solvability of (
24
) is equivalent to the existence of nonnegative real numbers

\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{m}
such that

\displaystyle-\|x-a\|^{2}+r^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}(\|x-a_{i}\|^{2}-r_{i}^{2})\geq 0,~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(26)

The inequality (
26
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}-1)I&a-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}a_{i}\\
a^{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}a_{i}^{T}&r^{2}-\|a\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}(\|a_{i}\|^{2}-r_{i}^{2})\end{matrix}\right)\succeq 0.

(27)

Thus, under the assumption (
25
), problem (
1
) is now transformed to the following SDP problem
of variables
\eta:=r^{2}>0,a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu_{i}\geq 0:

\displaystyle{\rm(P)}\hskip 34.14322pt\begin{array}[]{llll}&\min&\eta&\\
&{\rm s.t.}&\left(\begin{matrix}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}-1)I&a-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}a_{i}\\
a^{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}a_{i}^{T}&\eta-\|a\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}(\|a_{i}\|^{2}-r_{i}^{2})\end{matrix}\right)\succeq 0,\\
&&\mu_{i}\geq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,m,a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{array}

However,
a
has not been known, we cannot compute the rank of
\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}
to check whether the assumption
(
25
) is satisfied. Fortunately, we always have

{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}\leq{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}.
So we can use

{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}
instead of
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}.
On the other hand,
the proof of Theorem 3.2 in
[
1
]
showed that if
(\eta^{*},a^{*},\mu^{*})
is an optimal solution
of (
4
) then
\mu^{*}=(\mu^{*}_{1},\mu^{*}_{2},\ldots,\mu^{*}_{m})^{T}
must satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}^{*}=1.

The matrix inequality in (
4
) thus becomes

\left(\begin{matrix}0&a^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu^{*}_{i}a_{i}\\
{a^{*}}^{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu^{*}_{i}a_{i}^{T}&\eta^{*}-\|a^{*}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu^{*}_{i}(\|a_{i}\|^{2}-r_{i}^{2})\end{matrix}\right)\succeq 0.

This inequality together with the fact that
\eta^{*}
is the smallest number satisfying the inequality indicate that

a^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu^{*}_{i}a_{i}
and

\eta^{*}=\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}^{*}a_{i}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}^{*}\left(\|a_{i}\|^{2}-r_{i}^{2}\right).

As a result, we have the following formula to find the smallest ball
B(a,r).
The result looks like Theorem 3.2 in
[
1
]
but
under different conditions.

Theorem 3
.

Let
B(a_{i},r_{i}),

i=1,2,\ldots,m,
be the balls such that the intersection

\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})
has a nonempty interior. If either

{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}<n
or
{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}=n

and
n=m,
then the center
a
and radius
r
of the smallest ball
B(a,r)
enclosing the intersection

\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})
are given by

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}a_{i},

(31)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}a_{i}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}\left(\|a_{i}\|^{2}-r_{i}^{2}\right)},

(32)

respectively, where
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{m})
is an optimal solution of the convex quadratic minimization problem

\begin{array}[]{l}\min\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}{a}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}\left(\left\|{a}_{i}\right\|^{2}-r_{i}^{2}\right).\\
\text{ s.t. }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}=1,\mu_{i}\geq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,m.\end{array}

5
Conclusion and remarks

We have shown that the joint numerical range
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of the quadratic mapping
G=(-g,g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
with
g(x)=x^{T}x-2a^{T}x+\theta,g_{i}(x)=x^{T}x-2a_{i}^{T}x+\theta_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,m,

is convex if and only if the rank of
m
vectors
a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is less than
n:

{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}<n.

In case
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is not convex, i.e.,
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n,
we create an extension
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
of
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the sense that

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet},
and
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})=G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
if and only if
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is convex.
We now obtain a new result that
if
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n=m
then
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
is convex, even
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is not.
Interestingly, in this case
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subsetneq G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}
but

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
implies
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\bullet}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset.

Those results allow us to obtain a new separable property of

G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that if
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
then
G(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Lambda
are separable by a hyperplane if
either
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}<n
or
{\rm rank}\{a_{1}-a,a_{2}-a,\ldots,a_{m}-a\}=n=m.

As an interesting application, the smallest
enclosing ball (SEB) problem of finding a smallest ball
B(a,r)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing the
intersection
\cap_{i=1}^{m}B(a_{i},r_{i})
of given balls
B(a_{i},r_{i})

is now solved if
either
{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}<n
or
{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}=n=m.

This new progress on solving SEB problem together with the NP-hard property of SEB problem
[
17
]

raise a conjecture
that whether the SEB problem is NP-hard if and only if
{\rm rank}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}=n
and
m>n?
