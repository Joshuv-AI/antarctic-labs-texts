Projected lives saved and economic value of mRNA cancer immunotherapies in the United States
Authors: Chad R Wells, Abhishek Pandey, Carolyn Bawden, Yang Ye, Bilori Bilori, Lilia Potter-Schwartz, Lamia Ayaz, Seyed M Moghadas, Jeffrey P Townsend, Meagan C Fitzpatrick, Alison P Galvani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMessenger RNA (mRNA) technology helped prevent millions of deaths during the COVID-19 pandemic and is emerging as a promising cancer treatment modality. Early-phase trials suggest mRNA immunotherapies can improve overall and recurrence-free survival. However, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services has terminated investment in this transformative platform. Although the funding cuts target infectious-disease vaccines, they could also slow broader research across the platform, with implications for cancer applications. To assess the potential public health and economic value of mRNA immunotherapies as cancer treatments, we combined early clinical trial evidence with incidence- and demographic-adjusted survival estimates from the National Cancer Institute's SEER program to project outcomes for non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and metastatic melanoma. We then applied the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services' Value of a Statistical Life Year ($604,246; 3% discount rate) to quantify the economic value associated with survival gains. Among U.S. patients diagnosed with these cancers in a single year, mRNA immunotherapies could avert an estimated 49,415 (95% CrI: 28,233–71,085) deaths. The corresponding economic value is estimated at $75.55 (95% CrI: $44.20–$103.43) billion, substantially exceeding the funding cut and highlighting the long-term value of continued innovation in mRNA-based therapeutics.Keywords: mRNA vaccines, Cancer immunotherapy, Lives saved, Public health

Introduction

In August 2025, the United States (US) Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) announced that it would cease federal investment in messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) vaccine development, cancelling over $500 million in ongoing contracts.1 Public funding was instrumental in accelerating mRNA vaccine development for COVID-19, preventing millions of deaths and saving hundreds of billions of dollars in healthcare costs.1,2 The abrupt withdrawal of support marks a sharp inflection point for a technology platform that has reshaped modern vaccinology and risks stalling translational momentum in oncology.

mRNA vaccine technology represents one of the most powerful biomedical innovations to reach clinical use in the past decade.2,3 Its unique advantages over traditional vaccine technologies include rapid design, scalability, and adaptability to both infectious and non-infectious diseases.3 These attributes make the platform exceptionally suited to precision oncology, where tumor heterogeneity and rapid iteration are defining challenges. Unlike conventional vaccines that rely on attenuated or inactivated pathogens, mRNA vaccines deliver genetic instructions that enable the body to produce antigens internally, eliciting strong immune responses with remarkable speed and flexibility.2 The success of COVID-19 vaccines provided population-scale evidence of both biological efficacy and manufacturing feasibility, while demonstrating substantial economic return on public investment in mRNA technology.4,5

Recent advances in our understanding of the role of the immune response in cancer suppression have inspired investigations into mRNA vaccines as cancer treatments, aiming to evoke immune responses against tumor neoantigens.6, 7, 8 By enabling patient-specific antigen design, mRNA cancer immunotherapies offer a fundamentally different therapeutic paradigm compared to conventional immunotherapies. Between January 1, 2020 and March 31, 2022, over $2.2 billion was invested in supporting clinical trials associated with mRNA vaccine development.5 Prior to the pandemic, approximately $337 million was invested in mRNA technology over the course of 35 years, leading to substantial advancements in the field.5 Prior work on developing mRNA technology was primarily driven by efforts to prevent infectious diseases.9 The cuts to federal funding for mRNA vaccine research targeting infectious diseases could impede the advancement of the field, jeopardizing not only the progress made in cancer therapies but also the infrastructure required to sustain rapid vaccine design and manufacturing capacity in response to other emerging health threats.

Landscape of mRNA cancer immunotherapy clinical development

Between September 8, 2025 and October 3, 2025, we reviewed the phase, status, and estimated completion dates of mRNA cancer immunotherapy clinical trials using ClinicalTrials.gov and published literature. Excluding clinical trials that were terminated or suspended, we identified 25 US-based or multinational trials with US sites that were ongoing or completed since 2010 (Table S1). These trials target a wide range of cancers, with malignant melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) as the primary focus. Several candidates have advanced into late-stage development, with five Phase III or Phase II/III trials targeting malignant melanoma, NSCLC, colorectal cancer, head and neck cancer, and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma expected to conclude between 2026 and 2035.

Early results indicate that mRNA immunotherapies can provide substantial improvements in overall, recurrence-free, and progression-free survival across multiple high-burden malignancies compared to standard therapies.10, 11, 12, 13 For instance, Phase II trial (NCT03897881) results from a three-year median follow-up analysis of malignant melanoma showed that the personalized neoantigen immunotherapy mRNA-4157 plus pembrolizumab reduced the risk of recurrence or death by 49% and the risk of distant metastasis or death by 62% compared to pembrolizumab alone,11 providing strong justification for expansion of this platform across multiple malignancies. mRNA-4157 is now being evaluated in combination with pembrolizumab across six ongoing trials in five cancer types: bladder cancer, kidney cancer, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, malignant melanoma, and NSCLC.

Cancer deaths averted through mRNA immunotherapy

We evaluated the potential short-term survival gains of mRNA cancer immunotherapy using preliminary cancer-specific trial results and national age-, sex-, and stage-specific cancer data (Fig. 1; Appendix). Due to the availability of overall survival outcomes for mRNA immunotherapies reported in early clinical trials (Table S2), we focused our quantitative projections on four cancers: NSCLC (i.e., adenocarcinoma, large cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma of the lung and bronchus),12 pancreatic cancer,13 renal cell carcinoma (RCC) (i.e., renal pelvis and kidney),14 and melanoma.11 The extrapolation of these small, early-phase trials to population-level effectiveness measured through deaths averted for select cancers, although hypothetical and subject to substantial uncertainty, is aimed at highlighting the potential of mRNA cancer immunotherapy to mitigate disease burden.

Schematic overview of the framework combining early-phase clinical trial data (blue) and the SEER dataset (purple) to estimate the effectiveness and potential benefits of mRNA immunotherapy on improving overall survival (gray).

Assuming full uptake among patients diagnosed within a single annual cohort, we estimated that mRNA immunotherapy would avert 18,998 (95% Credible Interval [CrI]: 1263–35,393) NSCLC deaths, 20,081 (95% CrI: 9202–33,982) pancreatic cancer deaths, 6634 (95% CrI: 3991–8904) RCC deaths, and 3729 (95% CrI: 3001–4312) melanoma deaths relative to standard care over a three-year period (Fig. 2). Importantly, these estimates reflect only one year of diagnosed patients; cessation of mRNA research could therefore lead to a cumulative and growing burden of preventable mortality and economic loss over successive cohorts.

Over a three-year period following diagnosis, (a) the estimated number of deaths averted for non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and melanoma with 10%, 30%, 80% and 100% uptake of mRNA immunotherapy relative to no mRNA immunotherapy; (b) the associated monetary value of the years of life gained using the HHS central estimate of $604,246 per statistical life year at a 3% discounting rate; and (c) the estimated additional medical costs associated with the increased survival following treatment with mRNA immunotherapy. The horizontal dashed line in (b) reflects the $0.5 billion HHS funding cut for mRNA vaccine development research.

An observational study has linked prior SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination to improved cancer survival, suggesting that mRNA platforms may exert broader immunomodulatory effects.15 Generally, mRNA cancer immunotherapies are evaluated as part of combination treatment strategies rather than as stand-alone therapies. However, current oncology trials have explored only a narrow subset of possible therapeutic combinations. Because mRNA immunotherapy encodes tumor-specific neoantigens derived from genomic and transcriptomic analyses, these therapies are inherently compatible with precision medicine workflows, offering a natural avenue for expansion. More systematic integration of mRNA immunotherapy with molecularly targeted therapies could amplify the survival benefits observed in early clinical trials and represents an important direction for future investigation.

Our estimates indicate that mRNA immunotherapy was associated with meaningful improvements in one-year overall survival across the examined cancer types (Table S2). Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the clinical trials informing our projections had relatively small sample sizes and limited follow-up durations, which likely reduced statistical power to detect differences in overall survival. To address these constraints, our analysis used demographic-specific SEER-reported overall survival rates, weighted by the demographic composition of trial populations, and applied trial-reported survival improvements to these population baselines (Fig. 1). This approach is not intended to replace randomized clinical trials or comparative effectiveness studies, but rather to supplement early-stage decision-making by contextualizing relative effectiveness within a population-level framework. In addition, the mRNA immunotherapies explored in the analysis have only been evaluated in early-phase trials. The extrapolation of their effectiveness in reducing mortality at a national level should be interpreted with caution, as their effectiveness across demographics and comorbidities has not been fully evaluated in the clinical trial process.

Additional limitations arise from the temporal scope and biological assumptions of our model. We restricted our analysis to three-year overall survival due to limited long-term follow-up data from existing trials. Moreover, we assumed that the increase in overall survival associated with mRNA immunotherapy remained constant over this period. This assumption was applied to mitigate potential overfitting of the model due to the limited number of temporal data points available from most clinical trials used in our study. If immunotherapy-induced effects wane over time, our estimates should be interpreted as an average effect over the modeled horizon. Conversely, if mRNA immunotherapy leads to durable immune memory and longer-term survival gains, our analysis may underestimate its true population-level impact. When integrating waning effects of mRNA immunotherapy into the analysis, our estimates of overall deaths averted decreased from 49,415 (95% CrI: 28,233–71,085) to 41,031 (95% CrI: 21,888–62,382). Moreover, the effects of waning had a heterogeneous influence on deaths averted among the individual cancers analyzed in our study (Table S11). Ongoing and future trials with extended follow-up will be critical for refining these estimates and improving biological realism.

Projected societal costs of cancer deaths

Years of life lost are a metric recognized as central to assessing population-level cancer burden and informing research prioritization.16 To quantify the societal costs associated with federal disinvestment in mRNA immunotherapy development, we used the HHS central estimate of $604,246 per statistical life year at a 3% discounting rate to translate projected survival gains into a monetary value.17 Across the four cancers considered, cessation of government investment in the mRNA platform could be associated with a $75.55 (95% CrI: $44.20–$103.43) billion monetary valuation of years of life lost over a three-year period for a single annual cohort of patients (Fig. 2). Cessation of mRNA immunotherapy was estimated to have the largest impact on pancreatic cancer ($34.94 [95% CrI: $17.90–$52.05] billion), followed by NSCLC ($27.92 [95% CrI: $1.96–$49.26] billion) (Fig. 2). Melanoma ($4.18 [95% CrI: $3.39–$4.77] billion) and RCC ($8.51 [95% CrI: $5.21–$11.16] billion) together accounted for 16.8% of the monetary valuation of years of life lost over a three-year period (Fig. 2). Relative to the low and high estimates of the value per statistical life year, the central estimate provides an intermediate monetary valuation of life lost that is recommended by the United States Environmental Protection Agency in all benefit analyses.18 In a scenario analysis applying the HHS low estimate of $281,981 and high estimate of $919,797,17 our monetary valuation of years of life lost over a three-year period for a single annual cohort of patients across these four cancers was estimated at $35.26 (95% CrI: $20.63–$48.27) billion and $115.01 (95% CrI: $67.28–$157.45) billion, respectively (Table S10). The value per statistical life year used in the analysis is based on a reference individual aged 40 years,19 and may not reflect societal valuations for cancer patients who often differ in age and comorbidity profiles. Nonetheless, monetizing years of life lost serves as a transparent and standardized method for comparing health benefits across policy alternatives and underscores the substantial societal returns from continued investment in mRNA cancer immunotherapy development.20

To determine an upper bound of the potential benefits of mRNA immunotherapy cancer treatment, we assumed a baseline scenario of full (100%) uptake among eligible patients. Given that these treatments are in the early stages of development, the indeterminate future access, eligibility, and cost considerations make treatment uptake uncertain among cancer patients and across different cancer types. Using the HHS central estimate for the value per statistical life year, we estimated that the minimum uptake required for the monetary benefit to exceed $0.5 billion was 1.41% (95% CrI: 0.83%–10.59%) for NSCLC, 1.27% (95% CrI: 0.93%–2.79%) for pancreatic cancer, 5.44% (95% CrI: 4.40%–9.63%) for RCC, and 11.61% (95% CrI: 10.43%–14.75%) for melanoma. Even under low levels of uptake among mRNA immunotherapies across these four cancers, the monetary benefits associated with deaths averted through treatment exceed the extent of the federal cuts. The results illustrate the considerable potential for high returns from the development of other effective mRNA immunotherapies targeting cancers outside the four examined in our analysis.

Given the potential for mRNA immunotherapies to extend overall survival time, we evaluated the additional costs of medical services and oral prescriptions attributed to the extended survival period. Among a single cohort of NSCLC, pancreatic cancer, RCC, and melanoma patients, the estimated total additional costs of medical services and oral prescriptions were $36.65 (95% CrI: $34.68–$37.56) billion. Among the cohort with NSCLC, the estimated additional medical costs were $16.93 (95% CrI: $15.90–$17.43) billion, $11.51 (95% CrI: $10.00–$12.40) billion among those with pancreatic cancer, $6.34 (95% CrI: $6.28–$6.40) billion among those with RCC, and $1.86 (95% CrI: $1.83–$1.91) billion among those with melanoma. With the absence of sufficient data to inform the likelihood of disease recurrence, these estimates represent an upper bound, as they were determined under the assumption of disease recurrence over the period of survival. Accounting for disease recurrence would reduce these additional costs, even under the assumption that the recurrence-free survival was equivalent for the two treatment regimens, as the duration of treatment would be shortened. Moreover, with mRNA immunotherapy having the potential to increase overall survival, the likelihood of incurring the burdensome medical costs in the last year of life is reduced relative to the absence of mRNA immunotherapy.

The regulatory timelines and process for mRNA immunotherapy are expected to delay public availability because of the logistics and production processes surrounding the current individualization of the treatment.21 After approvals, additional barriers will be encountered for the widespread utilization of mRNA cancer immunotherapies. One such barrier is the choice between personalized and universalized production of mRNA immunotherapies.22 With personalization, the treatment is specifically developed and optimized for an individual's cancer while universalized production targets dominant antigens found for the specific cancer within the population.22 As a result, the effectiveness of these treatments could be dictated by the choice of personal or universal production.22 The cost and accessibility will also likely differ for personalized versus universalized mRNA immunotherapies. Current mRNA cancer immunotherapies can exceed $100,000 per dose, and treatment often requires multiple doses.23 Consequently, affordability remains an important barrier to large-scale implementation of mRNA immunotherapy. However, the use of artificial intelligence has shown high potential to reduce production costs and improve immunotherapy efficacy.24,25 In addition, improving the efficiency of production, development, and formulation will contribute to lower per-patient costs.25,26 With universal production, the expectation is that the costs will be reduced, making them more widely available to patients. Alternatively, personalized mRNA immunotherapy may face logistical bottlenecks, with increased biopsy and processing for individualized patients, potentially lengthening turnaround times.22 Continued investment in mRNA technology is likely to enhance its economic viability, accessibility, and effectiveness over time.

Conclusion

The implications of disinvestment in mRNA technology may extend beyond infectious disease. Reduced funding could disrupt ongoing and future clinical cancer trials, while delaying innovation and potentially eroding public confidence in vaccines and vaccination, with consequences that extend well beyond oncology.27 Among the mRNA immunotherapy clinical trials conducted by organizations in the United States, 6/11 were terminated due to business reasons, sponsor decisions, or lack of funding between 2022 and 2025 (Table S14). Within 2025 alone, 3/6 clinical trials were terminated for these reasons (Table S14). Investment in mRNA technology is crucial to ensure the health of all, as mRNA platforms are uniquely adaptable and hold promise for a wide range of applications, including infectious, metabolic, autoimmune, genetic, and chronic diseases and disorders.27 They also play a critical role in pandemic preparedness efforts, enabling rapid redesign, scalable manufacturing, and deployment in response to emerging or re-emerging pathogens.27 From this perspective, government investment in mRNA technology represents not only support for cancer therapeutics but also a strategic investment in public health infrastructure and resilience in the face of global health threats.
