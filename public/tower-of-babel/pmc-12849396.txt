Prevalence of serum HBV markers and management of occupational exposure to HBV among healthcare workers in a tertiary hospital
Authors: Yuwen Xu, Ling Li, Jin Wang, Yanzhen Wan, Jingwen Li, Lei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHBV is the primary blood-borne pathogen of occupational exposure among healthcare workers (HCWs) in China. This study aims to analyze the characteristics of occupational exposure to HBV, and the ultimate goal is to optimize post-exposure management strategies for medical personnel.MethodsData of serum HBV markers (HBV-M) from a routine physical examination of HCWs in 2022 were retrieved, and characteristics of HBV-M were analyzed. Background

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is an important public health issue in China, and individuals with chronic HBV infection have a 25% chance of progressing to liver cancer or cirrhosis [1]. HBV is the primary source for occupational exposure to blood-borne pathogens among HCWs in HBV high-prevalence regions, including China [2, 3]. It was reported that HBV infection caused by occupational exposure required only 0.004 ml of HBV-contaminated blood, and the risk of infected with HBV was 10% to 30% if HCWs were exposed to injury by needles stained with HBV [4]. China’s Hepatitis B vaccination program was officially launched in 1992, initially targeting newborns in high-risk areas, and was expanded to all newborns nationwide by 2002. However, HCWs may not have been included in that program. Therefore, effective protection against occupational exposure to HBV is crucial for safeguarding the occupational safety of HCWs. This study aims to assess the risk of occupational exposure to HBV by investigating the serological patterns of HBV markers (HBV-M) among HCWs in Qingdao Municipal Hospital. Additionally, it was designed to explore the optimization of preventive measures for post-exposure of HBV through follow-up observations of HCWs with occupational exposure.

Methods

Study subjects and data collection

A comprehensive analysis was conducted using retrospective observational secondary data analysis of routine clinical and laboratory records. Data of HBV-M in HCWs in a routine physical examination in Qingdao Municipal hospital in 2022 were retrieved via the Hospital Integrated Information Management System. HBV-M included Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), Hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs), Hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), Hepatitis B e antibody (anti-HBe), and Hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc).

From 2018 to 2022, registration information of HBV occupational exposure was collected via the Hospital Infection Real-Time Monitoring System. HCWs who experienced occupational exposure to HBV were injected with hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) if the baseline anti-HBs levels were lower than 100 mIU/mL, and they were followed up to observe the attenuation characteristics of HBIG 1, 3 and 6 months later. This threshold was based on our hospital’s internal protocol, which adopts a more conservative approach than the recommendation of national guidelines, to ensure maximum protection for HCWs [5–7].The management process for occupational exposure to HBV is shown in Fig. 1.

The definition of exposure

We defined “clear source of exposure” as incidents where the source patient’s HBV serological status (HBsAg) was known and documented at the time of exposure or could be confirmed through subsequent testing. “Unclear source of exposure” referred to incidents where the source patient could not be identified, or the source patient’s HBsAg serological status was unknown and could not be ascertained after the exposure.

Laboratory tests

Qualitative determinations of serum HBV-M by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) were applied in the HCWs of routine physical examination. Diagnostic kits were all produced by Shanghai Kehua Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd, China. Experimental procedures and analysis of results were all performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For the HCWs with occupational exposure to HBV, HBV-M were measured by the chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay using an ARCHITECT i-2000 fully automated immunoassay analyzer (Abbott Ireland Diagnostics Division). The positive reference values were set as follows: HBsAg > 0.05 IU / mL, anti-HBs > 10 mIU / mL, HBeAg > 1 COI, anti-HBe < 1 COI, anti-HBc < 1 COI. The anti-HBs titers > 1000, > 100 and ≤ 1000, > 10 and ≤ 100, and ≤ 10 mIU/mL were considered as high-response, medium-response, low-response and non-response, respectively [6, 7].

For the sake of convenience, five items of HBV-M were respectively assigned a specific number as follows: (1) HBsAg, (2) anti-HBs, (3) HBeAg, (4) anti-HBe, (5) anti-HBc. The code for a pattern is determined by the combination of numbers for positive items. For example, if a staff’s HBV-M showed HBsAg positive, anti-HBs negative, HBeAg positive, anti-HBe negative, and anti-HBc positive, then the pattern code is ‘135’. Other combinations followed the same as above. A completely negative pattern is represented by ‘N’.

HBV vaccines

The main vaccines used are Hansenula polymorpha yeast vaccine or brewer’s yeast vaccine.

Statistical analysis

For categorical variables, the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was utilized for analysis. For measurement data, normal distribution was first tested. If the result was of non-normal distribution, data were expressed in terms of median (25% to 75% interquartile range, IQR). SPSS 22.0 software package was used and a p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethics approval

The study was done in accordance with ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and principles of good clinical practices. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Qingdao Municipal Hospital. Informed consent was obtained for experimentation with human subjects. The privacy rights of human subjects have been observed.

Results

Prevalence of serum HBV-M among HCWs in the hospital

In 2022, there were 4,419 HCWs undergoing a routine physical examination in Qingdao Municipal hospital, including 1,044 males and 3,375 females, with median age of 37 years old (IQR: 31 to 46). Among them, 68 cases were HBsAg positive, accounting for 1.54% (68/4419), and 2,899 cases were anti-HBs positive, accounting for 65.6% (2899/4419). There were three patterns of HBV-M found among the 68 HBsAg-positive HCWs, coded as 1–3, with proportions of 4.41%, 85.29%, and 10.29%, respectively.

Among the 4,351 HBsAg-negative HCWs, 2,899 cases were anti-HBs positive, accounting for 66.63%, and three patterns of HBV-M were identified in the HCWs with HBsAg negative and anti-HBs positive, coded as 4–6, with proportions of 3.59%, 5.55%, and 90.86%, respectively. The rest 1,452 cases were anti-HBs negative, accounting for 33.37%, and there were three patterns of HBV-M in the HCWs with HBsAg negative and anti-HBs negtive, coded as 7–9, with proportions of 0.62%, 1.10%, and 98.28%, respectively.

The incidence of occupational exposure to HBV in HCWs

From January 1, 2018 to December 31, 2022, there were 161 incidents of occupational exposure to HBV with median age of 29 years old (IQR: 25 to 37), including 45 males and 116 females, and those with a clear source of exposure accounted for 61.7%. The proportion of cases with a clear source of exposure per year was 75.6% (34/45), 55.4% (36/65), 59.6% (34/57), 63.2% (36/57), and 56.8% (21/37), respectively (χ2 = 5.287, P = 0.259). Discussion

Different patterns of serum HBV-M have distinct clinical implications, which are related to HBV infection and/or the body’s immune response to HBV or HepB Vac. This study was designed to investigate the characteristics of HBV-M among HCWs of a tertiary hospital in 2022, identifying nine HBV-M patterns, all of which were common types (Table 1). In this study, the positivity rate of HBsAg was 1.56%, which suggested that the chronic carrier rate of HBV is 1.56%, lower than 4.4% reported domestically and 3.9% globally [8, 9]. The proportion of HCWs with both HBsAg and HBeAg positivity among HBsAg(+) HCWs was 4.3%, which was also lower than the reported rate of both HBsAg(+) and HBeAg(+) in HBsAg(+) pregnant women (35.1%-36.1%) [6, 10], suggesting a lower proportion of highly infectious individuals compared with other studies.

In this study, among non-HBV-infected HCWs, the positivity rate of anti-HBs was 66.63%, indicating that these individuals had immunity against HBV. Zhang et al. [11] reported that anti-HBs positive rate was 56.3% among pregnant women from 2008 to 2013, while Cheng et al. [12] reported a positivity rate of 46.6% for anti-HBs among the population from 2013 to 2014. The positive rate of anti-HBs in this study was higher than those reported in the aforementioned studies. Among the anti-HBs positive HCWs, 90.49% fell into pattern 2 (with isolated anti-HBs positive), which suggested that these HCWs acquired immunity against HBV through HepB Vac vaccination. Pattern 245 and pattern 25 accounted for 9.51% of the anti-HBs positive HCWs, which indicated that these HCWs were exposed to HBV infection after HepB Vac vaccination, and then their bodies have cleared the virus; or they did not receive HepB Vac vaccination but were previously exposed to HBV, then their bodies have cleared the virus, and they have subsequently acquired immunity against HBV.

Pattern 45 and pattern 5 accounted for 0.57%, which indicated that this population was exposed to and infected with HBV in the absence of immunity to HBV, and achieved immune clearance but did not generate protective antibodies against HBV. Remarkably, it should be noted that 31.39% of HCWs had a completely negative HBV-M pattern, which suggested that they did not acquire immunity to HBV, and were still at high risk of HBV infection after occupational exposure. The proportion (31.39%) is lower than 47.1% in females and 45.4% in males reported by Cheng et al. [12].

From 2018 to 2022, there were 161 HBV occupational exposure incidents, and those with a clear source of exposure accounted for 61.7%, confirming that HBV is the primary source of occupational exposure in this region. As for sociodemographic factors, those with the highest proportion of occupational exposure was gender as female, occupation as a nurse and work experience < 5 years. Ju Hyun Lee et al. [13] and Carlos Pérez-Diaz et al. [14] both reported that nurses had the highest risk of occupational exposure (47.4%-47.5%), which were consistent with our findings in this study. The main instruments involved in occupational exposure to HBV were disposable needles, surgical sutures or surgical blades, which were consistent with the reported results [14, 15]. The primary places of occupational exposure were general wards and operating rooms, consistent with the findings of Romanee Chaiwarith et al. [15]. The incidents mainly occurred during the surgical procedure, sharp instrument handling and withdrawal of needles. Manxia Xie et al. [16] reported that pulling the needle, re-capturing the needle, and processing waste accounted for 46.8%, 17.7%, and 12.7%, respectively. No cases of HBV infection due to occupational exposure were found in this study. Kosgeroglu N et al. [17] reported evidence of hepatitis B and C infections associated with specific types of sharp injuries, and they also examined how different protective measures influenced the incidence of these infections.

Among HCWs receiving HBIG injection after occupational exposure to HBV with baseline anti-HBs ≤ 10 mIU/mL, 70.6% cases received HepB Vac before, of which 50% were vaccinated within ≤ 10 years, suggesting that approximately 50% of anti-HBs negative HCWs might have a weak or no response to HepB Vac, and booster vaccination and enhanced follow-up for anti-HBs levels should be considered for this population. In the group with baseline anti-HBs > 10 and ≤ 100 mIU/mL, all HBV-exposed HCWs had received HepB Vac (vaccination rate of 100%), of which 75% were vaccinated within ≤ 10 years, which was significantly higher than the group with baseline anti-HBs ≤ 10 mIU/mL and the group of unknown anti-HBs status, indicating that HepB Vac administration was an effective measure to ensure positive anti-HBs levels. It was suggested that when anti-HBs was > 10 and ≤ 100 mIU/mL, the immune response against HBV was insufficient [6, 7, 18]. Hence, in this study, HBIG was administered to all exposed to HBV except for those with anti-HBs > 100 mIU/mL. However, other experts believed that when anti-HBs was > 10 mIU/mL, the body was protective against HBV [19], and HBIG injection was unnecessary after occupational exposure to HBV in the group with baseline anti-HBs > 10 and ≤ 100 mIU/mL [17]. It remains debatable whether HBIG should be administered to this group after occupational exposure to HBV. In the group with unknown anti-HBs status, the HepB Vac vaccination rate was 36.4%, which was the lowest among the three groups. Therefore, these HCWs should receive HBIG injection to prevent HBV infection after occupational exposure to HBV.

The HBIG, as a purified solution containing high titers of HBsAb antibodies, is derived from plasma donated by individuals immunized with HepB Vac [20, 21]. In this study, HCWs who received HBIG injection were followed up, and their anti-HBs levels were detected to analyze the decay characteristics of HBIG within the body. In the group with baseline anti-HBs ≤ 10 mIU/mL, seven individuals were followed up one month after HBIG injection. Six of them tested positive for anti-HBs (85.7%) with a median level of 20.98 mIU/mL, and all HCWs tested negative for anti-HBs three and six months later, which indicates that the passively administered 200 IU HBIG have decayed to a lower concentration by one month, when the body retains low titer of antibodies against HBV without strong neutralizing effects on HepB Vac administered at this point. Therefore, it is recommended that HCWs exposed to HBV receive 200 IU HBIG within 24 h of exposure, and then the first dose of HepB Vac should be given one month after HBIG injection, which may lead to better effectiveness of HepB Vac vaccination. Up to now, there are no reports about guidelines on when to start HepB Vac vaccination after occupational exposure to HBV, and our findings may provide some evidence for this issue. In the other baseline anti-HBs groups, some individuals showed positive anti-HBs three and six months later, primarily due to their initial positive anti-HBs levels obtained through active immunization, which would not significantly decline in the short term.

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

The major highlight of this study is the one-month follow-up of individuals occupationally exposed to HBV who received HBIG injections. This research observed that HBIG remains at a low concentration in the body one month later. The elimination kinetics of HBIG in the body offers a one-month protection against HBV while produces a weaker neutralizing effect during hepatitis B vaccination, which helps to preserve the active immunization effect of the vaccine. Conventional follow-up is conducted at 3 and 6 months post-exposure. At these times, the aforementioned phenomenon is no longer observable. But the cases lost to follow-up highlight the practical difficulties in tracking comprehensive post-exposure outcome data, underscoring real-world research constraints, which may also introduce some bias to the results.

Conclusions

The prevalence of HBV infection among HCWs is relatively low, while anti-HBs positive rate is slightly higher than that of the general population. Occupational exposure to HBV with a clear source accounts for more than 60%. It is necessary to strengthen the management and prevention of HBV occupational exposure, including occupational exposure education, testing HBV-M as soon as possible after occupational exposure, and enhanced follow-up for HCWs who received HBIG injection. Most importantly, this research observed that HBIG remained at a low concentration in the body one month after administration. The elimination kinetics of HBIG in the body provide approximately one month of protection against HBV. When the hepatitis B vaccine is administered one month after occupational exposure, it produces only a weak neutralizing effect. Therefore, the findings of this study suggest that, for HCWs receiving HBIG, the optimal timing for hepatitis B vaccination is one month after HBIG administration.
