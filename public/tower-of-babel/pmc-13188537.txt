Response to letter regarding “clinical impact of disease stability on exacerbation and mortality in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease”
Authors: Hyun Woo Lee
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

We appreciate the interest expressed by Dr Maniscalco in our study examining the prognostic implications of disease stability in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [1]. His thoughtful comments provide an opportunity to further clarify several methodological aspects of the proposed stability framework and its potential clinical implications.

First, we agree that the definition of rapid lung function decline remains an area of ongoing discussion in COPD research. Longitudinal studies have consistently reported substantial heterogeneity in the rate of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) decline, and no universally accepted threshold currently defines accelerated deterioration [2]. Our study adopted a decline threshold of 60 mL/year in order to capture clinically meaningful functional deterioration beyond the expected age-related reduction in lung function [1]. This value was selected to reflect decline exceeding physiological aging while maintaining sensitivity to detect clinically relevant progression within the observation period [3]. Importantly, our objective was not to establish a definitive cutoff for accelerated decline but to operationalize a pragmatic component within a multidimensional definition of disease stability. We fully agree that further work will be required to refine and harmonize these thresholds across cohorts to facilitate comparability and improve clinical interpretability.

Second, Dr Maniscalco remarks on the higher proportion of patients achieving disease stability among those receiving single inhaler triple therapy (SITT). Given the retrospective design of the study, the association should be interpreted with caution, as several factors may have influenced both treatment allocation and subsequent outcomes. Differences in baseline disease severity, physician prescribing patterns, and patient characteristics may have contributed to the observed pattern. At the same time, previous studies have reported that SITTs are associated with improved treatment adherence and fewer inhaler technique errors, both of which are known to influence clinical outcomes in COPD [4]. Accordingly, our analysis was intended to describe real world treatment patterns rather than to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of inhaler delivery strategies. Prospective studies specifically designed to address treatment allocation and adherence will be required to determine whether SITT contributes to the likelihood of achieving disease stability.

Finally, we appreciate the recognition that a multidimensional definition of disease stability may offer a useful conceptual framework for evaluating disease control in COPD. The clinical course of COPD reflects complex interactions between symptom burden, exacerbation susceptibility, and physiological decline, and these domains may evolve at different temporal scales [2]. Future investigations may therefore benefit from exploring whether disease stability can be assessed over shorter evaluation intervals, such as three or six months, in order to capture earlier changes in disease trajectory and to facilitate more responsive treatment adjustment. In addition, incorporation of objective biological or imaging markers may further refine the stability construct. Emerging evidence suggests that structural airway abnormalities, including mucus plugging detected on computed tomography, are closely linked to FEV1 decline and exacerbation risk [5]. Integrating such markerswith clinical domains may help identify patients at risk of instability with greater precision and may contribute to a more mechanistically informed definition of disease stability.
