Effects of six weeks of guanidinoacetic acid supplementation with and without creatine monohydrate on cognitive function and markers of health in healthy adults
Authors: Jisun Chun, Khatereh Babakhani, Drew E. Gonzalez, Broderick Dickerson, Ryan J. Sowinski, Chris J. Rasmussen, Richard B. Kreider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGuanidinoacetic acid (GAA) supplementation has been reported to increase brain creatine content more effectively than creatine monohydrate (CrM). However, the effects on cognitive function are unclear.PurposeThe purpose of this proof-of-concept exploratory clinical trial was to determine whether GAA supplementation with and without CrM affects cognitive function and/or markers of health.MethodsIn a double-blind, randomized, and counterbalanced manner, 58 healthy and active adults (33 females, 35.5 ± 14 years, 76.2 ± 14 kg) ingested a PLA (PLA, 2 × 6 g/d maltodextrin), GAA (2 × 1 g/d) + PLA (2 × 5 g/d), or GAA (2 × 1 g/d) + CrM (2 × 5 g/d) for six weeks. The participants donated fasting blood samples and completed a battery of tests and questionnaires assessing various aspects of function, mood, stress, sleep quality, and markers of health at baseline and after six weeks of supplementation. Data were analyzed using General Linear Model (GLM) multivariate and univariate with repeated measures, and mean changes from baseline with 95% confidence intervals, and Chi-squared analysis, and considered significant when the probability of error was 0.05 or less, and approaching significance (p > 0.05–p < 0.10).ResultsGAA supplementation tended to improve overall reaction time (−241.8 ms [−533, 50], p = 0.10) while significant interaction effects were observed in overall reaction time (p = 0.031–330 ms [−629, −31]) and YES reaction time (−290 ms [−484, −96], p = 0.004) while recalled correct reaction time (−178 ms [−374, 21], p = 0.078) and recalled correct YES (7.4 % [−1, 15.8], p = 0.084) approached significance compared to PLA. Limited to no effects were observed on the Delayed Picture Recognition Task Test, Digit Vigilance Task Test, Corsi Block Task Test, or Stroop Color-Word Task Test. The amount of time engaged in moderate physical activity was significantly greater (p < 0.05) in the GAA group compared to PLA. Participants in the GAA group reported a lower frequency of controlling irritations (p = 0.036), and feeling like difficulties are mounting (p = 0.053) on the Perceived Stress Scale. Some positive and potentially undesirable effects were observed in sleep quality assessments. The quality of life assessment revealed that participants in the GAA group reported less frequent feelings of being worn out (p = 0.068) and that their health was excellent (p = 0.092) while those in the GAA + CrM group reported more frequent limitations when bending, kneeling, or stooping (p = 0.053), more often feeling full of life (p = 0.081), and less frequency in feeling downhearted and depressed (p = 0.066). No clinically meaningful changes were observed in blood markers or self-reported side effects among the groups.ConclusionDietary supplementation with GAA (2 g/d) and the combination of CrM (10 g/d + GAA 2 g/d) for six weeks improved delayed verbal episodic recognition memory and retrieval speed and some measures of perceived stress, sleep quality, and quality of life. However, most cognitive tests were not affected, particularly when comparing the GAA to the PLA group; there were some inconsistencies in the findings, and several differences only approached significance. Additional research is needed before conclusions can be drawn. Clinical trial registration: ISRCTN68542582.

Introduction

Creatine is an essential component of phosphocreatine (PCr), which helps maintain adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels, particularly during high-intensity exercise and metabolically stressful conditions [1,2]. Approximately 95% of creatine is stored in skeletal muscle, while the heart, brain, and testes also contain creatine [1–4]. Creatine monohydrate supplementation (e.g. 4 × 5 g for 5–7 days or 3 g/d for 4 weeks) has been consistently reported to increase muscle creatine stores by 20%–40% [2,5,6], thereby improving high-intensity exercise performance and muscle accretion during resistance-training in younger and older populations [7,8]. The blood‒brain barrier contains the creatine transporter 1 (CRT1), which facilitates creatine and GAA transport into the brain [9] in addition to passive diffusion [10]. Creatine monohydrate (CrM) supplementation has been shown to increase the brain creatine content by 5%–15% [11–15]. However, since only approximately 20% of dietary creatine crosses the blood–brain barrier [10], higher daily doses of CrM supplementation (e.g. 10–20 g/d) have been recommended to increase the brain creatine content [10,16,17]. Increasing brain creatine and phosphocreatine has been reported to improve energy availability [18–20] and cognitive function [10,16–18,21–33]. Studies conducted in animals have indicated that creatine supplementation provides neuroprotection [34–37] in response to traumatic brain injury [38], spinal cord injury [39,40], and ischemic stroke [41–44]. Higher dietary intake of creatine has been associated with lower serum neurofilament light chain levels (a marker of neuronal damage) as individuals age [45], less depression [46], and better cognitive performance in older individuals [47]. Consequently, there has been increasing interest in determining whether creatine supplementation may reduce the risk of brain injury, improve cognition, and/or promote brain health [4,7,10,16,48].

Guanidinoacetic acid (GAA) serves as the immediate precursor and regulatory intermediate in creatine synthesis. GAA is synthesized in the kidneys from the amino acids arginine and glycine through the enzyme L-arginine: glycine amidino-transferase (AGAT) and is primarily transported to the liver, where guanidinoacetate N-methyltransferase (GAMT) methylates it to form creatine [1,2,4,7,49–51]. Neurons and astrocytes contain AGAT and can therefore synthesize GAA into creatine in the brain [52,53]. Since GAA has a 10-fold greater affinity to cross the blood‒brain barrier through CRT1 than does creatine [9], there has been interest in determining whether GAA supplementation may be a more effective strategy to increase brain creatine content than creatine supplementation [10,19,54].

Preliminary studies have shown that low-dose GAA supplementation (i.e. 1.2–6 g/d for 4–12 weeks) increases brain creatine concentrations to a similar degree as higher doses of CrM [54–56]. Moreover, when combined with CrM, GAA has been shown to improve the brain creatine content more effectively than CrM alone [57,58]. A recent study indicated that dietary supplementation of 2 g/d of GAA with 2 g/d of CrM for 7 days increased oxygen saturation in the brain before and after a cognitive challenge [20]. Moreover, the combination of GAA and CrM supplementation improved gaming performance (total scores and reaction time) in esports athletes [59]. However, more research is needed to assess the effects of GAA supplementation, with and without CrM, on memory and cognitive function.

The only concern about GAA supplementation has been that it may affect methyl group availability and may increase homocysteine levels (a cardiovascular disease risk factor) compared to creatine supplementation, which has generally been found to reduce homocysteine levels [60–65]. Several studies reported that GAA supplementation had no detrimental effects on homocysteine levels, particularly when GAA was combined with creatine [65,66]. Nevertheless, more research is needed to evaluate the effects of GAA supplementation on homocysteine and markers of health to determine whether GAA is a safe alternative and/or additive nutritional strategy to creatine supplementation to increase brain creatine content.

The purpose of this exploratory proof-of-concept study was to determine whether six weeks of GAA supplementation (2 g/d), with or without CrM supplementation (10 g/d), would improve cognitive function in healthy young adults. Additionally, we aimed to examine the effects of GAA and CrM supplementation on homocysteine levels and markers of health. We hypothesized that GAA and CrM would safely improve markers of cognitive function and that co-ingesting GAA + CrM may provide additive benefits. The primary outcome was cognitive function as determined from a battery of tests assessing recall, memory, attention, vigilance, executive function, and reaction times. The secondary outcomes included assessment of diet, physical activity, body composition, mood states, perceptions of stress, ratings of quality of life and sleep quality, whole blood, and serum health markers (including homocysteine and glycosylated hemoglobin), and self-reported side effects. We conducted this exploratory study in healthy young adults to determine whether the results justified evaluating the effects of GAA and/or CrM supplementation in healthy older populations.

Methods

Research design

This study was conducted at a university research facility in a randomized, counterbalanced, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, repeated-measures manner. This study was approved by the University Human Research Protection Program Internal Review Board (IRB2024-1370, approved January 10, 2025) in agreement with participant research ethics as described by the Declaration of Helsinki. All prospective participants signed written informed consent statements prior to participating in this study. This trial was registered in the International Standard Randomized Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) registry (#68542582, posted November 12, 2025). Dietary supplementation served as the independent variable. The primary outcome was cognitive function as assessed by the Immediate and Delayed Word Recall Task tests, the Delayed Word Recognition Task test, the Delayed Picture Recognition Task test, the Digit Vigilance Task test, the Corsi Block Task test, and the Stroop Color-Word Task test. The secondary outcomes included cognitive impairment screening (Mini-Mental State Examination and Memory Complaint Questionnaire); assessment of total energy and macronutrient intake, physical activity, and body composition; psychological assessment (i.e. Profile of Mood States, Perceived Stress Scale, Quality of Life, and Sleep Quality), whole blood and serum blood panels, and self-reported side effects.

Study participants

Participants were recruited through email, newspaper advertisements, social media platforms, and posted flyers in both online and print formats, and then contacted to assess their eligibility. The inclusion criteria were: (1) apparently healthy or medically controlled males and females between 18 and 65 years of age; (2) willingness to comply with all study procedures; (3) willingness to refrain from alcohol intake and the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aspirin, and other over-the-counter pain medications for 48 h before each of the testing sessions; (4) willingness to complete the study based on the duration of the individual visits and scheduling requirements; (5) willingness to comply with and understand the cognitive function tests; and (6) willingness to not consume dietary supplements (e.g. caffeine, gingko biloba, ashwagandha, paraxanthine, arginine, etc.). The exclusion criteria included: (1) having a known vitamin B12/B6 or folate deficiency; (2) having a known homocystinuria (a rare inherited metabolic disorder that prevents the body from processing the amino acid methionine); (3) having high homocysteine levels (>50 μmol/L); (4) having a history of kidney disease requiring dialysis; (5) having diagnosed with cognitive impairment or neurological disease (e.g. Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, etc.); (6) being pregnant, breastfeeding, or wishing to become pregnant during the study (confirmed via a negative pregnancy test); (7) having uncontrolled heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, thyroid disease, cancer, neurological disease, or medically treated major psychological or depressive disorder that may affect the study results; and (8) having a known allergy to meat or fish (containing GAA and CrM), GAA, CrM, or maltodextrin.

Familiarization

Potential participants responding to study advertisements were initially screened for eligibility using online or phone screening. Those who seemed to meet the entrance criteria were invited to a familiarization session where participants were informed about the study. Those interested in participating signed a written informed consent form, and then had height, weight, body mass index, and resting heart rate and blood pressure determined. Then, the participants were introduced to the study protocol, and they completed three practice trials for each cognitive function test. Prior research in our lab has demonstrated that this approach was sufficient to establish tests to test reliability. Finally, the participants (1) received instructions on how to complete food logs and (2) were asked to document their detailed food and energy-containing fluid intake for 4 days before each testing session. They were instructed to fast for 12 h and avoid intense exercise for 24 h.

Experimental session testing protocol

Figure 1 presents the experimental approach and testing sequence. The participants completed identical baseline and six-week post-treatment testing sessions. Before the first testing session, participants kept a record of their food and beverage intake for 4 days. They were instructed to refrain from atypical caffeine and other stimulant intake for 48 h, fast for 12 h, and avoid intense exercise for 24 h prior to testing. Upon arrival for these experimental sessions (generally 6:00–10:00 am), the participants were asked to return their 4-day dietary food log. Next, they completed a series of questionnaires that assessed their mental state, memory, quality of life, physical activity, mood, sleep, stress, and any side effects they experienced. Following this, the participants were observed for a 6-minute rest period before their resting hemodynamics were assessed, and then their weight was recorded. The participants then completed the Computerized Mental Performance Assessment (COMPASS) Cognitive Function Test Battery described below. The participants subsequently had their bone density and body composition determined and then donated a fasted blood sample. The participants were scheduled for subsequent testing sessions at the same time of day to standardize testing and reduce diurnal and cardician variations.

Supplementation intervention

In a double-blind and randomized manner, the participants were assigned to consume either a PLA (PLA, 2 × 1 g + 5 g), GAA (2 × 1 g GAA + 5 g PLA), or GAA + CrM (2 × 1 g GAA + 5 g CrM) daily for six weeks. Maltodextrin was used as the PLA (Maltodextrin DE 20, batch #07112312, Don Xiao, Dongxiao Co., China). Creatine monohydrate served as the source of creatine (Creavitalis® Special Grade, lot #403841, Alzchem Trostberg GmbH, Trostberg, Germany). We used the special-grade version of Creavitalis (mesh ≈80) rather than the micronized version (mesh ≈250) to match it to the placebo better. Analytical testing described on July 2, 2024 certificate of analysis (CoA) revealed that high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) determined purity of 101.1%, with 30 mg/kg of creatinine, 25 mg/kg dicyandiamide, and <3 mg/kg (limit of detection) dihydrotriazine (Alzchem Trostberg GmbH, Analytical Department, Trostberg, Germany). Creamino® (batch 230114T13, Alzchem Trostberg Group, Trostberg GmbH, Germany) was used as the source of GAA. According to CoA dated January 14, 2023, the Creamino GAA (Batch: 230114T13) contained 98.2% GAA (HPLC), 0.1% water, with the remainder (1.7%) being starch (Alzchem Trostberg GmbH, Analytical Department, Trostberg, Germany). Supplements were distributed to participants in generic stick packets (PLA and CrM) and containers with 1 gram scoops (PLA and GAA), labeled “A,” “B,” or “C,” for double-blind administration. The participants were matched based on sex, age, and weight and randomly assigned in a counterbalanced manner to one of three supplement groups. They were instructed to take the supplements twice daily, either after breakfast or approximately 8:00 am and dinner or approximately 6:00 pm, following the first baseline session (Day 0). The participants continued this routine throughout the approximately 42-day study. Compliance was monitored through online communication, verbal interactions, and supplemental logs with participants.

Procedures

Cognitive screening

Two cognitive tests were carried out to confirm that participants did not have any age-associated memory impairment (AAMI) according to the National Institute of Mental Health criteria. The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used to rule out the possibility of dementia. MMSE scores range from 0 to 30, where 25–30 suggests a questionable level of impairment; 20–25 indicates mild impairment; 10–20 signifies moderate impairment; and 0–10 reflects severe impairment. The MMSE is recognized for its high test‒retest reliability, which ranges from 0.80 to 0.95. Additionally, the Memory Complaint Questionnaire (MAC-Q) was administered to verify that the participants did not have any cognitive concerns related to aging [67]. A score of 25 or higher suggests subjective memory issues.

Diet assessment

Although there was no diet intervention, we assessed diet to assess average energy and macronutrients and to provide insight into whether dietary protein intake changed during the study. To do so, each participant recorded their food and energy-containing beverage intake for 4 days before each testing session, using the MyFitnessPal Calorie Counter phone app (MyFitnessPal, Inc., Baltimore, MD, USA) [68] or using food and beverage logs. Energy and macronutrient intake were analyzed using the MyFitnessPal Calorie Counter application.

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Long Form (IPAQ-LF, 7-day self-administered version) [69]. This tool records the time spent in vigorous, moderate, and walking activities across four areas: work, transportation, domestic/household, and leisure. The participants indicated the frequency (in days/week) and duration (in minutes/day) of activities lasting at least 10 min, as well as the time spent sitting on weekdays and weekends. Data from the IPAQ-LF were used to determine overall physical activity levels and sedentary behavior for analysis.

Hemodynamics and body composition assessment

Height and weight were determined using a Health-O-Meter Professional 500KL scale (Pelstar LLC, Alsip, IL, USA). The resting HR was determined by palpitation of the radial artery according to standard procedures [70]. Both HR and BP were assessed after sitting passively for 5 min using a digital HR monitor and BP cuff (Connex ProBP 3400; Welch Allyn, Tilburg, NL) [70]. Body composition, excluding the cranium (including fat mass, lean tissue mass, and bone mineral content), was measured using a calibrated Hologic Discovery W DXA with APEX Software (APEX Corporation Software, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) [71,72]. Our laboratory's test–retest reliability results indicate that the CV ranges from 0.31% to 0.45% for total mass, fat mass, and lean tissue mass and for bone mineral content, with an average intraclass correlation of 0.98 [73].

Cognitive function assessment

Participants completed computerized cognitive tests using COMPASS software version 6.0 (Northumbria University, Newcastle, UK). The cognitive function assessments included the Word Recall, Word Recognition, Picture Recognition, Digit Vigilance, Corsi Block Task, and Stroop Color-Word task tests using previously described methods [74]. We chose this battery of cognitive function tests in collaboration with applied psychology colleagues to assess a wide array of cognitive domains and measures of function and allow for direct comparison of these findings to previous nootropic intervention studies that we conducted using the same cognitive tests [74–84]. In brief, the Immediate and Delayed Word Recall Task assesses episodic memory by requiring participants to recall and record words within a set time limit [85]. The Delayed Word Recognition and Delayed Picture Recognition Task tests assess episodic verbal and visual memory, respectively, indicating whether the conditions displayed in the assessment are familiar, presenting a percentage of accuracy with the number of words and pictures recognized correctly from an initial list [86]. The Digit Vigilance Task assessed attention and alertness by prompting responses and measuring the amount of false selections and reaction times of correct responses [87]. The Corsi Block Task test assessed attention and vigilance, requiring participants to memorize and accurately reproduce sequences of blue squares presented on a grid [88]. The Stroop Color-Word Task was employed to evaluate cognitive attention and processing skills by asking participants to identify the font color of words that name colors [89]. These tests have been validated and widely used to assess various aspects of memory and cognitive function [90,91].

Psychological assessment

Profile of Mood States (POMS)

The 65-item Profile of Mood States (POMS) questionnaire was used to assess changes in mood state. The POMS questionnaire assesses the domains of confusion, anger, fatigue, depression, tension, and vigor. The total mood disturbance score (TMDS) was calculated by subtracting vigor scores from the combined confusion, anger, fatigue, depression, and tension scores. The POMS is a valid and routinely used assessment of mood states [92,93].

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)

Cohen's Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) assessed how participants perceive stress, focusing on feelings of unpredictability, lack of control, and overwhelming demands [94]. The questions asked about their thoughts and emotions over the past month, with participants indicating how often they experienced specific emotional states. The PSS has a test–retest reliability greater than 0.70 [94].

Sleep quality assessment

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Assessment (SQA) [95] and Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire (LSEQ) assessed the impact of the treatment on sleep quality [96]. The SQA asks quantitative questions related to the amount and duration of sleep and a categorical assessment of various aspects of sleep quality [95]. The participants answered ten questions divided into four subscales: “Ease of getting to sleep,” “Perceived quality of sleep,” “Ease of awakening from sleep,” and “Integrity of behavior following wakefulness.” Each question involves marking a response on an 80-millimeter line. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients range from 0.78 to 0.92 [97]. The LSEQ assesses changes in usual sleep patterns where higher values generally indicate better than usual responses.

Quality of life

The 36-item Short-Form (SF-36) health questionnaire (Rand Health Care, Santa Monica, CA, USA, version 2) was used to assess perceptions of quality of life (QOL) [98]. This questionnaire assesses various aspects of physical and psychological health (test–retest reliability ranging from r = 0.81–0.95) [99,100].

Blood sampling and analysis

Fasting blood samples were collected in 2 × 7.5 mL BD Vacutainer® serum separation tubes (SST) and one 3.5 mL BD Vacutainer ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tube. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 15 min and then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min using a refrigerated Thermo Scientific Heraeus MegaFuge 40R Centrifuge (Thermo Electron North America LLC, West Palm Beach, Florida, USA). Serum from one SST tube was aliquoted for serum storage, while the remaining serum and EDTA tubes were transported to the Clinical Pathology Laboratory (Austin, TX, USA) for analysis of complete blood counts with percent cell differentiation and standard clinical blood panels. The serum was stored at −80 °C in polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes for future testing.

Side effects

Side effects were evaluated using the frequency and severity of subjective side effect questionnaire [77,101,102]. The CV of the items asked ranged from 1% to 3%, with intraclass correlations between 0.6 and 0.88 [77,101,102].

Statistical analysis

We conducted a comprehensive statistical and clinical evaluation [103–109], using procedures previously described in detail [110], with IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Sample size estimates were informed by several considerations. First, our prior studies examining nutritional interventions on cognitive function indicated that approximately 12–20 participants per group were generally sufficient to detect significant treatment effects in the cognitive function tests evaluated [74–84]. Second, we reviewed prior investigations reporting significant cognitive responses to creatine supplementation [32,111] and studies involving guanidinoacetic acid [20,54–59,65,112,113] and found that most of these trials enrolled between 8 and 20 participants per group. We also considered reported means, standard deviations, and statistically significant mean differences from related studies to estimate power, assuming 80% power, variability of approximately 5%– 10% relative to the mean, and expected improvements of 5%–10% in primary outcomes. Collectively, these estimates indicated that a sample size of 15–20 participants per group would likely provide adequate power to detect meaningful between-group differences in cognitive function measures. Accordingly, our recruitment goal was to complete 20 participants per group with 25–30 per sex (although assessing sex × group × time effects was not a primary outcome).

The quantitative data were analyzed using mixed model general linear model (GLM) analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures. Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity, and kurtosis statistics were examined to evaluate normality. For the variables summarized in the tables, omnibus multivariate time and group × time effects were assessed using Wilks’ Lambda to determine whether the intervention affected the overall set of dependent variables included in the model. Univariate repeated-measures GLM analyses were then used to evaluate intervention effects on individual outcome variables. Greenhouse–Geisser adjusted probability values were reported to control for inflation of the F-statistic when the assumption of sphericity was violated [114,115]. Under these conditions, additional alpha-level corrections (e.g. Bonferroni adjustment) were not applied, and Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) procedure was used for pairwise comparisons because more conservative correction strategies may unnecessarily increase the likelihood of type II error [114,115]. We also used these methods to examine whether there were any sex differences to consider and to determine if GAA and/or GAA + CrM resulted in significantly different results when compared to the PLA group independently.

Data were considered statistically significant when the probability of type I error was 0.05 or less. However, because this was an exploratory study and recommendations in sport science have supported interpretation of 90% confidence intervals (CI) equivalent to an alpha level of 0.10 [106,107], we also report outcomes with p-values between 0.05 and 0.10 as trends approaching significance. To assist interpretation of these findings, partial eta squared (ηp2) effect sizes were reported to characterize the magnitude of response and to help inform whether additional investigation in larger samples may be warranted. Effect sizes of 0.01–0.05 were considered small, 0.06–0.13 moderate, and values greater than 0.14 large [116]. Clinical or practical significance was evaluated by examining changes from baseline with 95% confidence intervals (CI) [100]. Mean changes with 95% confidence intervals entirely above or below baseline were interpreted as clinically meaningful changes. Fisher’s LSD post-hoc analyses were then used to determine whether the magnitude of change differed significantly among the groups. We did not incorporate estimates of measurement error or clinician-derived thresholds for minimally meaningful clinical change in this assessment; however, test‒retest reliability coefficients of variation are reported to assist readers in judging the practical relevance of the findings.

Data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SD) or as the mean percent changes from baseline expressed as the mean change [lower limit, upper limit]. Missing data were minimal and limited to isolated observations (i.e. 1 of 58 hip/waist measurements, resting energy expenditure test, blood sample, digit vigilance, and MACQ values), and some measures of one participant’s sleep-quality assessment could not be completed because of an excessively noisy sleeping partner. When necessary, the missing numerical data were replaced using prior observed value or series means [117], while missing ordinal survey data were substituted using the most frequent response/value method [118]. This approach was considered appropriate because the extent of missingness was very small and because the methodologic literature indicates that replacement of isolated missing values in longitudinal or repeated-measures data should be informed by the participant’s observed response pattern over time and should remain plausible for the specific variable being analyzed, with approaches based on within-subject longitudinal information generally performing better than methods that ignore subject-specific response patterns [119–123]. Thus, our intent was to replace isolated missing observations with values that were representative of the individual’s expected response and unlikely to materially influence statistical inference. Overall, this analytic framework was designed to provide a comprehensive and transparent interpretation of the data by integrating omnibus and univariate analyses, effect-size estimates, pairwise comparisons, and clinical change assessments rather than relying solely on p-values [103,104,106].

Results

Participant demographics

Figure 2 shows a Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram. A total of 86 participants responded to the study advertisements. Among these individuals, 59 were familiarized and consented to participate in the study. The remaining participants declined to participate because of time conflicts. A total of 58 individuals met the inclusion criteria and underwent baseline testing. Twenty individuals (12 females, 8 males) were randomized into treatment A (GAA + CrM group), 19 individuals (10 females and 9 males) were randomized into treatment B (GAA group), and 19 participants (11 females and 8 males) were randomized into treatment C (PLA group). As shown in Table S1, the participants were 35.5 ± 13.9 years, 76.2 ± 14.4 kg and had a resting heart rate of 67.5 ± 11.5 bpm, a systolic blood pressure of 114.0 ± 8.3 mmHg, and a diastolic blood pressure of 74.6 ± 5.9 mmHg. Multivariate Wilk's Lambda analysis revealed no statistically significant group (p = 0.674, ηp 2 = 0.073, medium effect), sex (p = 0.324, ηp 2 = 0.111, medium effect), or group × sex (p = 0.594, ηp 2 = 0.080, medium effect) within-subject effects. Additionally, the univariate analysis revealed no statistically significant differences for group, sex, or group × sex in the demographic variables.

Cognitive deficit assessment

Tables S2 and S3 display the MMSE and MAC-Q test results, respectively. The participants had a baseline MMSE score of 28.2 ± 1.0 and a MAC-Q score of 23.1 ± 3.4, indicating that they did not suffer from dementia or cognitive deficits. Multivariate Wilk's Lambda analysis revealed a statistically significant effect of time for MMSE scores (0.38 [0.01, 0.74], p = 0.042, ηp 2 = 0.073, medium effect), but no effect for group × time (p = 0.954, ηp 2 = 0.002, small effect). Multivariate Wilk's Lambda analysis also showed a statistically significant time effect for the MAC-Q score (−1.83 [−2.8, −0.9], p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.222, large effect), but no effect for group × time (p = 0.483, ηp 2 = 0.026, small effect). This analysis revealed that participation in this study (regardless of group assignment) improved scores on these inventories.

Delayed word recognition task test

Table S4 displays the results of the word recognition assessment. Multivariate Wilk's Lambda analysis revealed no time (p = 0.298, ηp 2 = 0.130, large effect) or group × time (p = 0.314, ηp 2 = 0.123, medium effect) within-subject effects. The univariate analysis presented a statistically significant group × time effect for the following word recognition variables: overall reaction time (p = 0.031, ηp 2 = 0.119, medium effect) and “yes” reaction time (p = 0.008, ηp 2 = 0.161, large effect), with no other group, time, or group × time effects found for the remaining variables. Pairwise comparisons revealed that the PLA group observed a significant increase from baseline in overall reaction time (231 ms [51, 410], p = 0.12) and YES reaction time (163 ms [35, 292], p = 0.014) and tended to observe an increase in the NO reaction time (299 ms [−21, 620], p = 0.066). Those in the GAA + CrM group tended to observe an increase in the number of words correctly identified (3.2 [−0.06, 6.4], p = 0.054) and experienced a significant increase in the percentage of correct YES responses (6.4 [0.96, 12.4], p = 0.023) with a faster YES reaction time of −127 ms [−252, −1.8], p = 0.047). The chi-squared analysis revealed that the PLA group reported a higher frequency (p = 0.069) and severity (p = 0.050) of nervousness compared to the other groups. The chi-squared analysis showed no differences between groups in the frequency or severity of dizziness, headache, tachycardia, heart palpitations, dyspnea, blurred vision, or other complaints. Reported side effects were rare, rated as minimal to slight, and similar across groups. No participant withdrew from the study because of issues with the supplement. These findings, along with the assessment of whole and serum clinical blood panels, indicate that GAA (2 g/d) and CrM (10 g/d) supplementation were well tolerated.

Discussion

This exploratory proof-of-concept study comprehensively evaluated the effects of GAA supplementation (2 g/d), with or without CrM (10 g/d), on a battery of cognitive function tests that assessed memory, attention, vigilance, executive function, and reaction time, mood, perceived stress, quality of life, and sleep quality in healthy adults. We also evaluated the effects of GAA supplementation with and without CrM on clinical blood panels (including homocysteine and HbA1c levels) and self-reported side effects. The results support contentions that GAA and the combination of GAA + CrM supplementation improved some measures of cognitive function and perceptions of quality of life in healthy adults. However, several cognitive tests were not affected, and positive benefits were more consistently observed when supplementing the diet with GAA + CrM than with GAA alone. The following section discusses the results of the study and provides recommendations for future research.

Primary outcomes

It is well-known that children with genetic GAMT, AGAT, or CRT1 deficiencies have lower muscle and brain creatine content that impairs cognitive and neuromuscular development [124]. Moreover, that early diagnosis and treatment of children with GAMT and AGAT-related creatine synthesis deficiencies with high-dose CrM supplementation (e.g. 0.35–1.1 g/kg/d) increases the brain and muscle creatine content and thereby helps normalize cognitive and psychomotor development [125–128].

In the late 1990s, Dechent and colleagues [11] reported that a single 20 g dose of CrM increased the brain creatine content by 3.1%–7.7%, while CrM supplementation (4 × 5 g/d for 4 weeks) increased the total brain creatine content by 4.7%–14.6% in various areas of the brain in healthy individuals. Subsequent studies by Lyoo et al. [13] found that creatine supplementation (0.3 g/kg/d for 7 days and 0.03 g/kg/d for 7 days) increased the ratios of brain creatine to N-acetyl aspartate (NAA) by 8.1% and creatine to choline by 9.3%, as well as increased the peak PCr by 3.1%, β-nucleoside triphosphate by 7.8%, and inorganic phosphate by 9.8%. Moreover, Pan and coworkers [14] reported that creatine supplementation (20 g/d for 7 days) increased the ratio of PCr/ATP by 5.2%, particularly in areas of the brain with lower PCr creatine content. Other studies reported that short-term creatine supplementation (i.e. 20 g/d or 0.3 g/kg/d for 7 days) increased brain creatine or PCr content by 3.9% in children [25], 6.4% in adolescent females [129], and 5.9% [130] to 9.2% [18] in adults, while other studies found no effects [131,132]. Lower dose creatine supplementation studies (e.g. 2–5 g/d for 8–24 weeks) have typically reported that the brain creatine content increases in the range of 4%–9% [129,133,134]. Initial studies on GAA supplementation (i.e. 1.2 g/d to 6 g/d for 4–12 weeks) reported that GAA supplementation increases the brain creatine content to a similar degree as higher doses of creatine do [54–56] and that co-ingesting GAA and CrM increases the brain creatine content more effectively than creatine alone does [57,58]. Consequently, supplementation with 10 g/d CrM with or without 2 g/d GAA for 6 weeks is expected to significantly increase the brain creatine content independently and/or in combination.

Creatine supplementation has been reported to improve cognitive function in several studies [18,21–26,28–30,32,135,136], including in response to sleep deprivation [28–30,137,138]. For example, McMorris et al. [139] reported that creatine supplementation (4 × 5 g/d for 7 days) increased forward number recall and picture recall, suggesting improvements in spatial and long-term memory in older men. This group also reported that creatine supplementation (20 g/d for 7 days) attenuated the decline in random movement generation, choice reaction time and static balance, and the profile of mood states [29]. Rae and Broer [140] reported that creatine supplementation (5 g/d for six weeks) increased working memory (backward digit span) and intelligence (Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices), which require processing speed in vegan and vegetarian adults. Conversely, other studies reported no effects of creatine supplementation on measures of cognitive function when supplementing the diet with 4 × 5 g/d for 7 days in children [132], 0.03 g/kg/d for six weeks in adults [141], or 4 × 5 g/d for 7 days and 5 d/d for 24 weeks in older individuals [142].

While less is known about the cognitive effects of GAA supplementation, Bogdan and associates [59] evaluated the effects of ingesting 2 grams of GAA and 2 grams of CrM for 4 weeks on cognitive function in esport athletes. The researchers reported that GAA + CrM supplementation increased gaming performance by 20% on their third game and improved controlled reaction time (22% faster), Stroop Color-Word test overall accuracy (4.4%), and Attention Network Test executive control (16.6% faster). These studies have served as the basis for evaluating the cognitive effects of creatine supplementation. However, authors of recent reviews point out that additional research is needed to validate these findings and determine whether long-term higher doses of CrM and/or GAA supplementation may enhance cognition before conclusions can be drawn [10,17,48,143–145].

In the present study, we evaluated the effects of six weeks of GAA (2 g/d) supplementation with or without CrM (10 g/d) on a wide array of cognitive function tests and measures in healthy adults. The strongest evidence supporting an effect of GAA and GAA + CrM supplementation was observed in the Delayed Word Recognition Task test, which assesses delayed verbal episodic recognition memory and memory retrieval speed by requiring the identification of presented target words among distractors [86]. The results revealed that participants in the GAA group tended to improve overall reaction time while those in the GAA + CrM group experienced a significant increase in the percentage of correct YES responses with faster YES and overall reaction times compared to the PLA group. These findings suggest that GAA + CrM may have an influence on verbal episodic recognition memory and the ability to encode information.

Other cognitive tests revealed no effects from GAA and GAA + CrM supplementation. In this regard, the Immediate and Delayed Word Recall Task test [75] assesses verbal episodic memory, delayed free recall, encoding, retention, and retrieval effort from long-term memory. The participants in the GAA + CrM group showed an increase in correct attempts from baseline and tended to increase the total recall attempts. However, no significant differences were observed compared to changes in the PLA group. The Delayed Picture Recognition Task test assesses visual episodic recognition long-memory and retrieval speed [86]. While the scores associated with GAA supplementation were similar to the PLA values, the percentages of pictures, correct YES, and correct NO pictures tended to decrease, with no changes in reaction time with GAA + CrM supplementation. These findings suggest a potentially negative effect on visual episodic recognition long-term memory. The Digit Vigilance Task test assesses sustained attention (vigilance), visual scanning, and psychomotor processing speed [77]. There was some evidence that those in the GAA + CrM group identified a higher percentage of targets correctly, while those in the PLA group took longer to identify a similar number of targets correctly. However, these changes were not significantly different among the groups. The Corsi Block Task test assesses visuospatial short-term and working memory [78]. There was evidence that those in the GAA group tended to have higher total span scores compared to those in the GAA + CrM group, indicating an improvement in attention, working memory, or cognitive flexibility. However, these changes did not differ from those in the PLA group. Finally, the Stroop Color-Word Test assesses inhibitory/interference control, executive control, and processing speed [89]. This task differs from the previous tests in that it is a conflict-resolution and attention-control task that assesses whether participants can override interference rather than remember words/pictures after a delay. The results revealed that changes in the percentage of congruent words correctly identified increased in the PLA group and tended to be higher than those in the GAA + CrM group. This finding does not indicate that GAA or GAA + CrM improves Stroop-assessed attention control or processing speed. Collectively, these findings suggest that GAA supplementation has limited independent effects on cognitive function measures, while GAA + CrM promoted improvements in some measures of cognitive function, particularly delayed verbal episodic recognition memory and memory retrieval speed. More research is needed to assess the independent and synergistic effects of GAA and CrM, particularly in older populations, who may benefit to a greater degree.

Secondary outcomes

Body composition

Creatine supplementation has been consistently reported to promote a small increase in body mass and greater gains in muscle mass in response to resistance-exercise training [4,8,146,147]. However, the effects of GAA supplementation on body composition are less clear. Baltic and coworkers [148] reported that dietary supplementation with GAA (2 g/d) and CrM (2 g/d) for 7 days increased body mass, and multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance was used to determine total body water, and extracellular mass, while CrM supplementation had no effect on body composition. CrM supplementation increased the amount of intracellular water in males but not in females. In the present study, we evaluated the effects of six weeks of GAA (2 g/d) and GAA + CrM (10 g/d) without exercise intervention on DXA-determined body composition. We found that lean tissue mass significantly increased from baseline in the GAA and GAA + CrM groups, while those in the PLA group remained unchanged. While there was no significant difference from the PLA group and may reflect some fluid retention, these findings are interesting given that there was no exercise intervention, and the GAA group presented increased lean tissue mass to a similar degree as the GAA + CrM. Additional research should evaluate whether GAA and/or GAA + CrM supplementation during a progressive resistance-training program has any effect on body composition.

Perceptions of mood and stress

Since CrM supplementation may affect cognitive function, several studies have evaluated the effects of CrM supplementation on the POMS. For example, McMorris et al. [30] reported that CrM supplementation (4 × 5 g/d for 7 days) during sleep deprivation improved vigor and fatigue scores after 24 h. Conversely, this research group reported no differences in overall mood scores in individuals who were sleep deprived for 36 h after consuming CrM (4 × 5 g/d for 7 days) [29]. These latter findings are consistent with other reports that CrM supplementation (20–24 g/d for 5 days) does not affect mood states [149,150]. We are not aware of any study that has evaluated the effects of GAA supplementation on mood states or perceptions of stress.

In the present study, tension scores significantly increased from baseline in the GAA and GAA + CrM groups, and the change in tension scores in the GAA + CrM group was significantly higher than scores in the PLA group. This finding suggests a negative effect on perceptions of tension. However, the baseline values were lower in the GAA and GAA + CrM groups, and the post-supplementation values were not significantly different among the groups. Additionally, the vigor scores tended to be higher after supplementation in the GAA + CrM group compared to the PLA group, and there was no significant difference in post-supplementation total mood disturbance scores. Consequently, this does not appear to have a negative effect. In the Perceived Stress Inventory, we found that changes in the frequency of controlling irritations and perceptions about feeling on top of things were improved in the GAA group compared to PLA, while those in the GAA and GAA + CrM groups rated things going their way more favorably than PLA. More research is needed to determine whether GAA or GAA + CrM affect perceptions of mood and stress.

Sleep quality

Since studies have reported that a single dose [137,138] or several days of CrM supplementation [29,30] attenuates declines in cognitive function in response to sleep deprivation, we were interested to determine if GAA and/or GAA + CrM may affect measures of sleep quality. In the present study, those in the PLA group tended to take longer to fall asleep than those in the GAA group and the GAA + CrM group, with baseline differences tending to be higher in the PLA group compared to the GAA group. Participants in the GAA + CrM group reported less sleepiness at bedtime (levels) and a trend toward significance in wakeful periods during sleep. While some may consider this finding a negative effect, it supports prior reports that creatine supplementation may attenuate cognitive decline during sleep deprivation [29,30,137,138]. The lower score for sleepiness at bedtime may indicate more vigor (as shown in the POMS analysis) and less fatigue at the end of the day, which could be a positive attribute. However, this finding needs to be interpreted with caution, as differences were primarily due to differences at baseline, as there were no differences at week 6 in these sleep markers. Participants in the GAA + CrM group also reported less trouble falling to sleep because they woke up in the middle of the night compared to the PLA and GAA groups, and reported a significant increase in the number of hours sleeping from baseline. There was also evidence that restlessness/calmness during sleep significantly increased over time in the GAA + CrM group and was significantly higher than that in the GAA group, but not in the PLA group. Improvements were observed from baseline in the GAA group in terms of ease of waking, time to “come around” after waking, functioning state upon wakening, and balance and coordination upon awakening in the GAA group. These findings suggest that GAA and GAA + CrM may have positive effects on measures of sleep quality. However, more research is needed to further explore these findings.

Quality of life

Since creatine supplementation has been reported to improve strength and functional capacity, several studies have evaluated the effects of creatine supplementation on measures of quality of life, particularly in diseased [151–157] and older populations [158,159]. These studies generally indicate that if CrM improves physical functional capacity, participants perceive improved quality of life. For example, Amiri et al. [158]. reported that CrM supplementation (0.1 g/kg/d) during 10 weeks of resistance-training observed double the amount of strength gains and was associated with improved quality of life scores in older individuals. We are aware of only one paper that assessed the effects of GAA supplementation on QOL [54]. In this crossover study, women with chronic fatigue syndrome supplemented with 2.4 g/d of GAA for 12 weeks reported significant improvements in physical and common health-related QOL scores.

In the present study, baseline differences were observed among groups in health-limiting bending, kneeling, and stooping, limitations in the kind of work and other activities, and time feeling full of life in healthy participants. When groups were analyzed independently, those in the GAA group tended to report less frequent feelings of being worn out and a greater frequency of feeling that their health was excellent at Week 6, whereas participants in the GAA + CrM group tended to report more frequent responses to health limitations when bending, kneeling, or stooping; more often, they reported feeling full of life; and less frequency in feeling downhearted and depressed. More research is needed to evaluate the impact of GAA and GAA + CrM supplementation on QOL in healthy younger and older populations with and without exercise intervention before conclusions can be drawn.

Health markers

The primary concern about GAA supplementation has been that it may lead to a clinically significant increase in homocysteine levels, particularly if other methyl donors are not available (e.g. folate, B12, B6, betaine, etc.) [65]. Normal homocysteine levels range between 5 and 15 µmol/L, with mild elevation between 15 and 30 µmol/L, moderate elevation between 30 and 100 µmol/L, and severe elevation greater than 100 µmol/L [160]. In 2012, a New Dietary Ingredient application (NDI 775) was submitted for requesting approval to market GAA (up to 4.8 g/d of GAA with choline and betaine). The FDA approved this NDI at the time of use and dose requested, citing a lack of GAA-specific studies in humans, and the human study cited [161] did not establish safety since it found a 3.5 μmol/L increase in homocysteine with two participants exceeding 15 μmol/L with 4.8 g/d of GAA supplementation for six weeks. The FDA did not prohibit the sale of GAA, it just did not provide FDA approval that safety had been sufficiently established at the intended dosages, which is a higher standard. Consequently, the current regulatory status in the United States is that an FDA New Dietary Ingredient notification is on file (NDI 775), so it may be marketed in dietary supplements if the manufacturer otherwise complies with dietary-supplement law and has a basis to conclude that it is reasonably expected to be safe. Since then, there have been about a dozen or so studies [54–57,59,66,112,113,148,162–165] conducted on GAA in humans and several companies sell it as a dietary supplement (without FDA objection).

In the present study, homocysteine levels averaged 8.9 ± 2.3 (range 8.6–10.1) at baseline and increased by 0.26 µmol/L [−0.41, 0.94] in the PLA group, 0.845 µmol/L [0.17, 1.52] after GAA supplementation, and 0.850 µmol/L [0.19, 1.51] after GAA + CrM supplementation with no significant differences observed among groups at Week 6 (PLA 8.84 µmol/L [7.7, 9.6], GAA 9.79 µmol/L [8.6, 10.9], GAA + CrM 10.05 µmol/L [8.97, 11.1], p = 0.372). While some may be concerned that homocysteine levels increased from baseline, a non-significant 0.59 µmol/L difference from the PLA group values would have no physiological, practical, or clinical relevance considering assay variability and that these values clearly fall within normal ranges (5–15 µmol/L). While we agree that more work is needed to assess the effects of GAA supplementation on homocysteine levels in larger and diverse study populations before conclusions can be drawn, the results do not indicate that six weeks of GAA (2 g/d) and/or GAA (2 g/d) + CrM (10 g/d) increases homocysteine values to clinically concerning concentrations in healthy individuals.

As expected, since creatine is degraded into creatinine, GAA + CrM supplementation led to a small but significant increase in creatinine levels from baseline (0.076 mg/dL [0.038, 0.113]). However, there were no significant differences among the groups in pre- or post-supplementation creatinine levels, and all the groups presented mean creatinine levels <0.94 mg/dL, which is well-below values of clinical concern (i.e. >1.3 mg/dL). Also, as expected, the small increase in creatinine levels led to a significant decrease in the creatinine calculated eGFR from baseline with GAA + CrM (−7 mL/min/1.73 m2 [−10.8, −3.2]). However, the mean change from 99.5 ± 21 to 92.5 ± 21.6 mL/min/1.73 m2 remained above values, suggesting renal impairment (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2). Since dietary creatine, muscle mass, and physical activity increase creatinine levels, the use of creatinine, calculated eGRF as a marker of renal function has been questioned [166–168]. Other studies using more precise methods of assessing renal function have reported no effects on renal function [167,169,170]. In fact, creatine supplementation has been studied as a means of reducing homocysteine levels and managing muscle wasting in renal patients undergoing dialysis [171–174].

Creatine supplementation has been reported to improve insulin sensitivity [175,176] and glucose management in individuals with Type 2 diabetes during exercise training [177–179]. Consequently, we were interested in determining whether GAA and/or GAA + CrM affected fasting blood glucose or HbA1c concentrations. In this study, the HbA1c values tended to decrease from baseline with GAA + CrM (−0.093% [−0.001, 0.19], p = 0.052). However, this change was very small, within normal ranges (<5.7%) and measurement errors, and not significantly different from the PLA or GAA values at Week 6 (PLA 5.67% [5.3, 6.1], GAA 5.37% [5.0, 5.7], GAA + CrM 5.66 [5.3, 6.0], p = 0.180). Additionally, no significant differences were observed among groups in fasting blood glucose responses. Consequently, this small change does not warrant raising concerns.

Finally, no significant interaction effects were observed for the remaining whole blood or serum markers. Additionally, the participants reported no side effects. These findings suggest that 2 g/d of GAA with and without 10 g/d of CrM for six weeks was well tolerated and did not adversely affect markers of health or self-reported side effects. These findings are consistent with other studies reporting no clinically significant effects of creatine supplementation on creatinine, markers of renal function, markers of health, or side effects [4,7,49,180].

Strengths, limitations and future directions

The strengths of this exploratory proof-of-concept study are that we comprehensively evaluated the effects of GAA (2 g/d) with and without higher daily doses of CrM (10 g/d) on several cognitive function tests that measure different types of cognitive function and markers of health and safety in healthy men and women. This study is limited by the amount and length of supplementation, the population studied, and the cognitive measures evaluated. There is also the possibility that, given the number of dependent variables assessed, some findings may have been due to chance findings. Moreover, even though several studies have evaluated the effects of CrM on cognition, it may be helpful to include a CrM group to better identify independent and/or synergistic effects. Additional research should evaluate the effects of GAA and GAA + CrM supplementation during exercise training on cognitive function, training adaptations, and markers of health in larger cohorts of younger and older male and female populations with and without cognitive impairment.

Conclusion

There was some evidence indicating that GAA supplementation (2 g/d for 6 weeks) improved Word Recognition reaction time and self-reports of physical activity, perceived stress, sleep quality, and quality of life. There was also evidence that the GAA + CrM group promoted more consistent results, with improvements in Word Recognition accuracy and reaction times, and some measures of perceived stress and sleep quality. However, most cognitive tests were not affected, there were some conflicting results, and a number of differences only approached statistical significance. Additional research is needed to better understand how GAA, CrM, and the combination of GAA and CrM affect cognitive measures, sleep, and quality of life in healthy younger, older, and clinical populations.
