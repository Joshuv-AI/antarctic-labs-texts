Application of cooked meat codes to improve the validity of nutrient content estimation in a recipe-based food ingredient database
Authors: Seo-Young Yun, Hee Joe Lee, Taenam Lee, Ga-Eun Choi, Minseo Choi, Mi-Kyeong Choi, Nami Joo, Mi-Hyun Kim, Cho-il Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESAccurate estimation of nutrient intake from food consumption requires reliable food composition data. However, since nutrient content changes during cooking, the application of raw ingredient composition may lead to overestimation, particularly for energy and fat in meat dishes. In this study, the suitability of applying cooked food codes for meat dishes to improve the validity of recipe-based nutrient content estimation was evaluated.MATERIALS/METHODSPork (thin-sliced and thick-cut belly and shoulder butt) and beef (tenderloin, loin, rib meat, and brisket point) were examined for cooking yield after grilling and their nutrient contents were estimated. Each cut was portioned to 100 g of raw meat and cooked in triplicate. Nutrient content was calculated using both raw and cooked meat codes from the Korean Standard Food Composition Table, and differences in energy, fat, and protein content were compared.RESULTSWhen grilled under standardized conditions, the average cooking yield was approximately 65% for pork and 80% for beef (P < 0.0001). Thin slices of pork belly had a lower yield (62.6%) than thicker cuts (68.0%), while beef brisket point had the lowest yield among beef cuts (63.6%). Applying cooked meat codes and measured cooking yields resulted in overall decreases in energy (−10% to −50%) and fat (−20% to −60%) content, with slight increase in protein (up to +34%, P < 0.0001). For beef tenderloin, additional comparisons using matched raw and grilled data from the United States Department of Agriculture SR Legacy Database showed smaller percent differences in energy, fat, and protein than those obtained from the Korean database.CONCLUSIONIncorporating measured cooking yields and corresponding cooked meat codes into recipe-based databases can improve the accuracy of nutrient intake estimation. The development of nutrient databases that reflect differences in cooking conditions—including temperature, duration, and doneness—should be prioritized to enhance the validity of national dietary surveys.

INTRODUCTION

The estimation of nutrient intake from food consumption data requires a reliable food composition database. However, because most foods are prepared by combining multiple ingredients, analytical data on the nutrient composition of cooked dishes remain limited. Consequently, many national food composition databases rely on nutrient values for raw ingredients to estimate the nutrient content of mixed foods, although calculation systems vary among countries [12]. In Korea, the dietary intake survey component of the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) constructs its recipe-based nutrient database by assigning food codes to the ingredients used in mixed foods and combining these ingredient quantities with corresponding nutrient values to estimate the total nutrient content of prepared dishes [3].

Nevertheless, nutrient degradation or loss inevitably occurs during cooking because of moisture and fat loss and/or the leaching of water-soluble nutrients [4567]. As a result, the nutrient composition of cooked foods often differs from that of raw ingredients. In particular, grilled meats tend to lose a considerable portion of fat and water, which can lead to overestimation of energy and fat intake when using raw food codes.

To improve the accuracy of nutrient estimations, the European Food Information Resource Network (EuroFIR) introduced the application of yield factors (weight change) and nutrient retention factors in recipe calculations to reflect nutrient losses and gains during food preparation [8]. Foundational work by Bergström and Bognár supported the development of harmonized nutrient retention systems across Europe [910]. Building upon these efforts, several countries have adopted standardized correction systems. The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies employ retention codes and moisture-adjusted yield factors in recipe calculations to account for nutrient changes during cooking [11]. Similarly, Australia’s AUSNUT 2023 database integrates both nutrient retention and weight change factors for cooked foods [12]. Nevertheless, even the EuroFIR report emphasizes that retention rates vary according to the cooking method, temperature, duration, and food part, and few countries currently maintain comprehensive databases of nutrient composition by cooking state [8].

Earlier studies have demonstrated that cooking can substantially alter the retention of nutrients such as vitamins and minerals depending on the method and duration [467]. More recent research has extended these findings to a wider range of foods and nutrients, reporting that cooking conditions markedly affect the retention of antioxidant compounds, trace elements, and macronutrients [13141516]. Collectively, these studies highlight that both the cooking process itself and the conditions materially influence nutrient estimates and underscore the importance of reflecting food cooking states in food composition databases.

In this context, the present study examined whether applying cooked food codes in nutrient calculations can improve the validity of nutrient estimations within the raw-ingredient–based framework used in KNHANES. Specifically, the cooking yield and nutrient composition of pork and beef—commonly consumed meats in Korea—were examined after grilling, and the nutrient content estimated using the Korean Standard Food Composition Table (KSFCT version 10.3, Rural Development Administration) were compared between raw and cooked meat code applications to evaluate their suitability for use in recipe-based nutrient content estimation [17]. This study focused on evaluating the applicability and limitations of existing database-derived estimates, assessing the practical utility of current food composition data and highlighting the need for database expansion to include cooked foods to enhance the accuracy of nutrient content estimation in mixed dishes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample selection and cooking procedure

To evaluate the validity of applying cooked food codes in the nutrient database, pork and beef were selected as representative meats with high consumption frequency among Koreans. Pork belly and shoulder butt, and beef loin, tenderloin, short rib, and brisket point were used. Raw meat samples were portioned into 100 g units and cooked in triplicate (n = 3 for each condition). Grilling was performed on a preheated frying pan maintained at 150–160°C, and the cooking time was standardized for each meat type and cut based on preliminary tests. Specifically, pork belly (10 mm thick) was grilled for 2 min 30 s on one side and 3 min on the other, while thin slices of pork belly (2 mm thick) were grilled for 1 min 30 s on one side only, without flipping. Pork shoulder butt (15 mm thick) was grilled for 2 min per side. Beef loin (10 mm thick) was grilled for 1 min on one side and 1 min 30 s on the other, tenderloin (20 mm thick) for 2 min and 2 min 30 s per side, rib meat (15 mm thick) for 1 min on one side and 1 min 30 s on the other and brisket point (chadolbagi, 2 mm thick) for 1 min 30 s on one side only. Immediately after cooking, all samples were weighed using an electronic balance (WZ-3A; CAS, Yangju, Korea).

Nutrient content calculation using the food composition database

The nutrient content of the sample meat was estimated before and after cooking using the meat codes available in the KSFCT [17]. For raw samples (A), the energy, fat, and protein contents were calculated by applying the raw meat code to 100 g of uncooked meat. For cooked samples (B), the nutrient content was calculated by applying the corresponding cooked meat code currently available in the database to the actual cooked weight obtained from 100 g of raw meat.

In addition, for beef tenderloin, where the raw and cooked codes in the KSFCT originated from different data sources, supplementary calculations were performed using the USDA SR Legacy database to evaluate the effect of data source consistency [18]. In this comparison, raw and grilled beef tenderloin data were matched across all grades combined and within individual USDA grades (Prime, Choice, and Select) to compare percent changes in energy, fat, and protein contents.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation were calculated from 3 cooking replicates. Differences among the 7 kinds of meat samples (3 pork cuts and 4 beef cuts) were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance, and mean separations were tested by Duncan’s multiple range test. To compare species-level differences, the pork and beef groups were further compared using independent t-tests. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and the results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Percent differences in nutrient contents by food code application

The percent differences in nutrient contents calculated using raw food codes and weights versus cooked food codes and actual cooked weights differed across cuts (Table 2). For energy, all cuts except beef loin presented lower values when cooked food codes were applied. Beef brisket point, with greatest weight loss, exhibited an energy value less than half that estimated from raw codes, whereas most other cuts showed only a 10–20% decrease but with significant differences (P < 0.0001). A similar pattern was observed for fat, where all cuts except beef loin showed lower fat contents under the cooked code application. Beef brisket point showed the largest reduction (−62.27%) in fat content while other cuts showed only an approximately 20% reduction (P < 0.0001). For protein, all cuts except pork shoulder butt and beef brisket point showed positive percent differences, although the increases were generally within 10%. Notably, thin-slices of pork belly and beef tenderloin showed larger increases of 16.79% and 34.18%, respectively (P < 0.0001). On average for each species, energy and fat decreased, whereas protein showed a non-significant increase (Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that the use of cooked food codes, together with measured cooking yields, can better reflect nutrient content changes that occur during the cooking of grilled meats. To measure the cooking yield, the cooking conditions were standardized to reflect the typical doneness of grilled meat commonly consumed by Koreans. Pork requires longer cooking times than beef of similar thickness because it is usually consumed well-done or overcooked. Consequently, pork belly—especially when cooked longer and more thoroughly—showed a cooking yield of approximately 62–68%, which was lower than that of beef cuts, most of which retained over 80%. According to Bognár, the yield factors of roasted pork shoulder and butt were 0.84 for rare, 0.79 for medium, and 0.72–0.62 for well done, whereas roasted pork belly with skin had a yield factor of 0.52 in the well-done state [10]. In contrast, Bognár reported yield factors of 0.78 (rare), 0.72 (medium), and 0.62 (well done) for roasted beef [10]. However, despite their classification as “rare” based on Bognár’s yield factors [10], the actual cooking state of the beef samples in this study was more closely aligned with medium or higher doneness, as determined by sensory observation after cooking. This discrepancy may be attributed to various factors such as differences in beef origin (e.g., Korean vs. European beef), fat content and marbling characteristics, cut thickness and size, or variations in cooking methods (pan-grilling vs. oven roasting), which may influence cooking yield and perceived doneness.

When comparing the same pork belly cuts with different thicknesses, the total cooking times at the same temperature differed substantially (5 min 30 s vs. 1 min 30 s), yet the cooking yield of thin-sliced pork belly (62.60%) was approximately 10% lower than that of thick-sliced pork belly (68.03%). Although this reduction likely reflects both fat and water loss, the relative contribution of each cannot be clearly distinguished. Such limitations arise because the use of cooked meat codes and cooking yields alone cannot account for differences in cooking conditions. A similar pattern was observed in beef: brisket point, briefly cooked thin slices of brisket, showed a cooking yield of 63.60%, markedly lower than those of thicker beef cuts (≥ 80%).

When both raw and cooked codes were applied with measured yields, pork showed consistent reductions in energy (approximately 20%), and fat (approximately 20–30%), reflecting expected water and lipid loss during cooking. These values were comparable to Bognár’s reported mean fat retention factor for pan-fried pork (0.80), corresponding to an approximately 20% loss [10]. In contrast to the expected decrease, pork belly showed higher protein content after cooking, likely due to raw and cooked food codes being established in different years (raw pork belly in 2014 and grilled pork belly in 2016), leading to inconsistencies unrelated to actual retention.

In grilled beef loin, energy and fat contents were unexpectedly higher when using cooked food codes, likely reflecting differences in cooking degree between our sample and the KSFCT data [17]. According to Bognár, the mean fat and protein retention factors for pan-fried beef in the well-done state are 0.75 and 0.95, respectively, indicating greater fat loss than in pork [10]. Despite this, the beef loin in this study had a cooking yield of approximately 84.03%, corresponding to the rare state of Bognár [10], yet energy and fat contents increased. This suggests that the cooked sample data in the KSFCT likely represent a more thoroughly cooked state than those in the present study [17]. Because the KSFCT does not specify the degree of doneness, cooking temperature, or cooking yield, a definitive interpretation remains difficult.

For beef tenderloin, the estimated energy (−12.10%) and fat (−26.78%) contents were lower after grilling, whereas the protein content was higher by 34.18%, raising concerns about the validity of cooked code application. Examination of the KSFCT revealed that the raw tenderloin data was derived from domestic Hanwoo beef, whereas the grilled tenderloin data originated from the USDA database, indicating different sample sources [1718]. To verify this, the USDA SR Legacy database—containing matched raw and grilled samples from identical sources—was used for comparison across multiple quality grades [18]. The matched dataset yielded a significantly smaller percent difference for all nutrients, demonstrating that consistency between raw and cooked data sources is crucial for accurate nutrient content estimation (Fig. 3). Although not shown in the Tables, beef rib meat exhibited similar improvements in data consistency when matched USDA data were applied instead of data from a different source [18].

Overall, these results indicate that for grilled meats showing visible fat loss during cooking, applying cooked food codes rather than raw ones can ameliorate the overestimation of energy and fat intake and improve dietary assessment accuracy. However, when cooking-induced weight reduction is minimal or when discrepancies exist between database sources, certain nutrient contents may be overestimated. Such discrepancies appear to stem from differences in cooking degree/duration, cooking conditions, database compilation year, or sample characteristics.

Therefore, to enhance the validity of nutrient intake estimation, it is necessary to expand food composition databases to include a wider range of foods in cooked states and degrees of doneness, reflecting nutrient losses that occur during actual food preparation. Moreover, standardization of sample preparation and source consistency across database entries are essential for improving the reliability of future dietary assessments.

Furthermore, to address discrepancies arising from the compilation of raw and cooked meat data generated at different times and/or by different institutions, it is recommended that national food composition databases, such as the KSFCT, present nutrient content for cooked foods in terms of relative moisture, fat, and protein or retention factors rather than absolute values. This approach would allow more rational and consistent estimation of nutrient changes after cooking, even when different data sources are used.
