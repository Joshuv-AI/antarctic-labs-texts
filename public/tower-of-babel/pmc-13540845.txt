Multidimensional training in dental anatomy teaching of oral anatomy and physiology: practice and effectiveness
Authors: Xianling Gao, Fuping Zhang, Shiyang Yu, Chuanying Hou, Jie Jin, Yuanyuan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate whether introducing 2-D “tooth-drawing” and 3-D “tooth-identification” exercises into conventional wax-carving sessions improves dental anatomy cognition among Chinese dental undergraduates.MethodsThree consecutive cohorts were compared using ANCOVA with pre-course Grade Point Average (GPA) as covariate. The control group (2020 Cohort, n = 126) received traditional wax carving only. The 2021 cohort (n = 102) added supervised tooth-drawing practice and a summative tooth identification test. The 2022 cohort (n = 99) further integrated systematic drawing-to-identification scaffolding into instruction. Outcome measures included carving, identification, drawing and theoretical exam scores, plus a 5-point Likert student satisfaction survey.ResultsAfter adjusting for Pre-course GPA, significant differences were observed across cohorts in both theoretical and practical assessments. Tooth carving scores improved sequentially (2020: 83.23 ± 5.42; 2021: 84.77 ± 5.06; 2022: 85.11 ± 4.90), with the 2022 cohort significantly outperforming the 2020 cohort (p = 0.039). Dental anatomy theory scores showed a highly significant difference across cohorts (p = 0.002), where the 2020 cohort scored significantly lower than both the 2021 and 2022 cohorts (p < 0.001). Furthermore, extracted tooth identification accuracy markedly increased from 2021 to 2022 (62.23 ± 24.33 vs. 80.63 ± 24.23, p < 0.001), and tooth-drawing scores also showed a significant improvement (p = 0.003). Student satisfaction was high (mean 4.27/5), with 84.5% of respondents agreeing that multidimensional training enhanced their understanding of form-function relationships, and 87.6% reporting improved three-dimensional spatial imagination.ConclusionThis quasi-experimental historical-cohort study suggests that embedding structured “draw-identify-carve” training was associated with improved practical performance. The low-technology approach may be adaptable to resource-limited settings with access to paper, pencils, and extracted teeth, providing a scalable complement to high-technology solutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09609-6.

Background

Dental anatomy is universally recognized as the cornerstone of dental education; however, the cognitive leap from two-dimensional (2-D) textbook images to three-dimensional (3-D) mental models remains a global challenge [1, 2]. While Western curricula increasingly rely on high-fidelity virtual reality (VR) or computer-aided design (CAD) packages [3–5], many Chinese schools still deliver anatomy as a stand-alone course with wax carving as the dominant psychomotor task. While wax carving holds educational value, it is notably time-consuming, limited to only a small range of tooth types, and provides students with minimal exposure to natural morphological variation [6]. While drawing-based approaches are established in anatomical education [7, 8], structured drawing-to-identification sequences in preclinical dental curricula remain underexplored. Grounded in dual coding theory and the cognitive theory of drawing construction, learner-generated drawing requires selecting, organizing, and integrating information to externalize mental models—processes that enhance retention, spatial reasoning, and metacognitive monitoring [8, 9]. These benefits naturally extend to the field of dental anatomy, where tooth-drawing functions as an intermediate step bridging 2-D representations and 3-D psychomotor tasks like wax carving. Notably, instructor-guided progressive drawing reduces cognitive load, making the method accessible even to students without artistic training [7]. To address these constraints, we designed a progressive “observe → draw → identify → carve” pipeline that integrates directly into existing syllabi without specialized equipment. We hypothesized that embedding structured tooth-drawing and extracted-tooth identification within conventional wax-carving sessions would be associated with changes in student performance across practical, spatial, and theoretical domains. This quasi-experimental historical-cohort study evaluates whether such a low-cost, scaffolded sequence offers a feasible complement to traditional carving instruction.

Methods

Study design and ethics

A quasi-experimental historical-control study was conducted. With approval from the institutional review board of the Guanghua School of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University (GHKQ-202601-K06-01), with the ethical approval documents provided in Supplementary Material 1. ANCOVA was employed to adjust post-test scores by accounting for pre-course Grade Point Average (GPA), thereby mitigating historical bias. Three consecutive cohorts were enrolled: 2020 Cohort (control), 2021 Cohort (Intervention A) and 2022 Cohort (Intervention B).

Participants and sample size

All students were third-year dental undergraduates who had not previously received formal tooth morphology training. Sample sizes were determined by class size rather than power calculation: 126, 102 and 99 respectively. Notably, the 2021 cohort was admitted under a unique admission policy (the only year implemented at our university), where students entered the university via competitive ranking based on both Gaokao and first-year university performance, with higher-ranked students having priority in major selection. This resulted in a systematically higher mean Pre-course GPA for the 2021 cohort compared to both the 2020 and 2022 cohorts (mean GPA: 2020 = 3.29 ± 0.62; 2021 = 3.36 ± 0.56; 2022 = 3.16 ± 0.73;F = 2.87, P = 0.050).

Educational interventions

Control group (2020 Cohort)

Students performed traditional wax carving of a maxillary central incisor and a maxillary first molar premolar, and mandibular first molar following the standardized protocol from the 8th edition of Oral Anatomy and Physiology. The procedure employed a group-based, stepwise demonstration method with instructor supervision. Using the maxillary central incisor as an example, the carving sequence proceeded as follows: (1) Labial and lingual surfaces: Trace the labial contour on the wax block (Fig. 1A), followed by cutting and shaping the wax to form preliminary labial and lingual surfaces (Fig. 1B); (2) Proximal surfaces: The mesial contour was traced on the established labial surface (Fig. 1C) and carved to approximate the tooth form (Fig. 1D); (3) Final refinement: All surfaces were trimmed to complete the carved tooth (Fig. 1E).

Wax carving sequence for the maxillary central incisor. A Labial contour tracing. B Initial labial and lingual surfaces. C Mesial contour tracing. D Initial tooth form. E Final carved form

Each tooth was carved at 2× natural size. The exercise comprised 8 class hours delivered across two sessions. Final products were evaluated independently by two instructors using a standardized rubric weighted for overall form (40%), axial surfaces (30%) and occlusal/incisal details (30%), with the full scoring criteria provided in Supplementary Materials 2–4.

Intervention A (2021 Cohort)

Total class hours remained unchanged. We added 0.5 h of tooth drawing instruction per tooth to the existing curriculum, using the maxillary first premolar as the demonstration model.

Students drew a 2× scaled drawing of the tooth magnification grid based on standard tooth measurements, marked the long axis, divided the crown into thirds, and identified anchor points (cervico-enamel junction, contact points, cusp apices, cervical margins) to construct the outline. They then rendered anatomical details: cusps, grooves, ridges, and cervical curves.Drawings covered 5 teeth (the maxillary central incisor, canine, first premolar, and first molar, plus the mandibular first molar) and 3 views (labial/buccal, mesial, and occlusal views). Only formative assessment was adopted in this study; detailed assessment rubrics are available in the Supplementary Materials 5.

Besides tooth drawing, we administered a tooth identification test using extracted teeth at the end of the semester, with one month’s prior notification.

Intervention B (2022 cohort)

Total class hours remained unchanged. Building on Intervention A, we delivered a tooth-identification workshop. Teachers explained general identification principles and key anatomical landmarks, then guided students through comparative analysis of commonly confused pairs. Assessment matched Intervention Group A (carving, drawing, identification, theory), enabling direct comparison of teaching effects. Table 1 showed the teaching and assessment in different groups.

Teaching and assessment interventions

Use and safety norms of extracted teeth

The extracted teeth used for teaching were all waste teeth extracted in clinical practice, and their use had obtained informed consent from patients and been approved by the ethics committee. All extracted teeth were processed through a standardized biological disinfection and sterilization procedure (soaking in 3% hydrogen peroxide solution, alcohol cleaning, and autoclaving) to ensure the biological safety of teaching operations. The teeth were further screened to exclude those with severe defects or malformations, thus ensuring only qualified teeth were used for teaching purposes. A standardized answer sheet for tooth identification is provided in Supplementary Material 6. Representative extracted teeth prepared for identification are shown in Fig. 2A.

Likert-scale questionnaire

After class, students of 2022 Cohort completed a 13-item Likert-scale (5-point) questionnaire after the course to rate how helpful it was for theoretical understanding and whether it increased their interest in learning. Items covered basic background (items 1–3), contributions to clinical cognition (items 4–8), and enhancement of learning interest (items 9–12); item 13 was an open-ended question for suggestions. The full questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Material 7.

To address variations in grading standards across cohorts, all practical work was double-blind rescored by two calibrated examiners (κ = 0.84, 95% CI 0.76–0.92). Disagreements exceeding 2 points were resolved by a third senior examiner.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The results indicated that while the theoretical scores of the 2021 cohort followed a normal distribution (p > 0.05), other performance metrics across cohorts exhibited mild deviations from normality (p ≤ 0.05). However, given that the sample size of each cohort approached 100, which satisfies the Central Limit Theorem for large samples, and that no severe skewness or extreme outliers were observed, parametric tests were deemed robust and appropriate for this study.

Bivariate Pearson correlations between pre-course GPA and each outcome measure were calculated to examine baseline associations and verify the linearity assumption for ANCOVA. Correlations were reported for the overall sample and stratified by cohort. For tooth-drawing and tooth-identification scores, only the 2021 and 2022 cohorts were included, as these assessments were not administered to the 2020 control cohort.

To eliminate the confounding effect of baseline pre-course GPA, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was employed to compare the differences in tooth carving, drawing, and recognition abilities among cohorts, with pre-course GPA as the covariate. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction. All tests were two-sided, and a value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Questionnaire data were analyzed descriptively. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α.

Results

Baseline correlations between pre-course GPA and outcome measures

Pearson correlations between pre-course GPA and performance measures are summarized in Table S8 (Supplementary Material 8). GPA showed no significant correlation with tooth-carving scores in the overall sample (r = 0.086, p = 0.125). For tooth-drawing scores, a weak but significant correlation was observed in the overall sample (r = 0.169, p = 0.017). Tooth-identification scores showed no significant correlation with GPA in the overall sample (r = 0.094, p = 0.189). Dental anatomy theory scores showed a weak association with GPA in the overall sample (r = 0.235, p < 0.001), with significant positive correlations in all three cohorts.

Improvement in tooth drawing performance

Representative works from the tooth-drawing assessments of the 2021 and 2022 cohorts of students were selected to visually demonstrate students’ tooth-drawing performance (Fig. 3A–E). To evaluate the difference between the two cohorts, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with Pre-course GPA as the covariate (Table 2). The analysis revealed a statistically significant difference (F = 8.9; p = 0.003), with the 2022 cohort outperforming the 2021 cohort (Fig. 3F). Tooth-drawing scores were compared among undergraduate students of the 2021 and 2022 cohorts, after adjusting for Pre-course GPA. Specifically, the adjusted mean score of the 2022 cohort was 87.76 ± 4.43, which was 1.36 points higher than that of the 2021 cohort (86.40 ± 4.33). This improvement was also reflected in the excellence rate (scores ≥ 90 points), which reached 31.3% in the 2022 cohort, representing a 6.8% point increase over the 2021 cohort (24.5%).

Enhanced ability in extracted tooth identification

The assessment of extracted tooth identification was first implemented for the 2021 Cohort. To evaluate the impact of targeted instructional interventions, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with Pre-course GPA as the covariate (Table 3). The analysis revealed a highly significant improvement in the 2022 cohort compared to the 2021 cohort (F = 8.9, p < 0.001); Fig. 4A). After adjusting for Pre-course GPA, the 2021 Cohort achieved a mean score of 62.23 ± 24.33; in contrast, following the implementation of targeted instructional interventions on extracted tooth identification, the 2022 Cohort demonstrated a significant improvement in mean score, reaching 80.63 ± 24.23 (p) (Fig. 4A). Specifically, the 2022 cohort’s mean score was 18.40 points higher than that of the 2021 cohort, with statistically significant differences observed between the two cohorts (p < 0.001). Additionally, both the pass rate (≥ 60 points) and excellent rate (≥ 90 points) of the 2022 cohort were notably higher than that of the 2021 Cohort (Fig. 4B).

Better outcomes in tooth carving

To evaluate the effect of cohort on tooth carving scores, an ANCOVA was performed with Pre-course GPA as the covariate (Table 4). The analysis indicated that Pre-course GPA did not have a significant main effect on carving scores (F = 2.3, p = 0.133), suggesting that prior academic performance was not a strong predictor for this specific manual skill. However, even after adjusting for GPA, a significant main effect of cohort was observed (F = 3.8, p = 0.023), confirming that carving performance differed across the three years. Adjusted mean scores improved sequentially (2020: 83.23 ± 5.42 ; 2021: 84.77 ± 5.06 ; 2022: 85.11 ± 4.90 ; Fig. 5A). Post-hoc comparisons revealed that the 2022 cohort significantly outperformed the 2020 cohort (p = 0.039), while no significant differences were found between the 2021 and 2020 cohorts (p = 0.184) or between the 2021 and 2022 cohorts (p = 1.00)(Table 5). Furthermore, kernel density estimation (KDE) curves illustrated that the score distribution shifted progressively to the right, reflecting an overall improvement in student performance in more recent cohorts (Fig. 5B).

Teaching satisfaction survey and student feedback

After the experimental course, we administered a teaching satisfaction survey to the 2022 cohort using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Of 99 questionnaires distributed, 97 were valid (response rate 98.0%).

Questionnaire reliability

The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the 8 core items (Q4–Q11) was 0.932, well above the 0.8 threshold for excellent internal consistency (Fig. 7A). Item-deletion sensitivity analysis showed that removing any single core item changed α only to between 0.915 and 0.950, confirming the scale’s stability and item homogeneity.

Baseline competence, assessed before the experiment, Q1–Q3, was generally low: 62.9% (61/97) had no drawing skills, 86.6% (84/97) no carving experience, 63.9% (62/97) no prior exposure to extracted teeth (Fig. 7B).

Teaching effectiveness

The average scores of the 8 core items ranged from 4.11 to 4.40 (out of 5), with an overall mean of 4.27 ± 0.68 (Fig. 7C). Dimension-specific findings included:

Knowledge comprehension and ability improvement

84.5% agreed that multidimensional training “significantly helped understand tooth form–function relationships”; 87.6% felt the “drawing → carving → identification” sequence improved their 3D spatial imagination; 89.7% believed this model enhanced efficiency of tooth structure identification.

Learning interest and engagement

78.4% (76/97) found the teaching model more engaging; 76.3% reported that drawing and identification sessions stimulated interest in dental anatomy.

Total questionnaire scores

Scores ranged from 19 to 40 (mean 33.6 ± 5.0, median 33.0) (Fig. 7D). Nearly 80% scored 31–40, indicating strong overall approval. Additionally, students expressed demands for increased carving practice time and an expanded bank of extracted teeth.

Discussion

Our findings suggested that a structured ‘draw-identify-carve’ approach was associated with students’ performance in practical carving, tooth identification, drawing, and theoretical examinations. This interpretation stems from its alignment with constructivist learning theory, which posits that learning is an active process of knowledge construction rather than passive reception [9]. Analogous to assembling a puzzle, learners integrate drawing, identification, carving and theoretical knowledge into their existing cognitive framework, forming a more complete cognitive structure [10, 11]. This integrated loop also aligns with the information processing model of learning: through elaborative rehearsal, new practical experience connects with original theoretical knowledge in long-term memory, strengthening retention in complex spatial learning [12].

How the four layers work together

Drawing as a generative starting point

Within this loop, drawing serves as both the entry point and the cognitive anchor. As a form of generative learning, it requires students to actively integrate proprioceptive, visual and semantic information, forming robust learning outcomes [7, 8]. In dental anatomy, sketching anatomical landmarks pushes learners to translate abstract theory into concrete visual representations, bringing out subtle structural features easily missed during passive viewing. Training under 2× magnification sharpens perception of tooth proportions and surface curvature—key elements of dental esthetics [13]—and establishes a consistent morphological reference for wax carving. Comparing sketches with reference images or extracted teeth provides immediate visual feedback, supporting self-correction and independent learning [14]. Repeated multi-angle drawing strengthens spatial visualization and creates multi-sensory memory traces that support identification matching and carving translation [15].

Identification as the cognitive bridge

While improved identification scores may partly reflect targeted teaching and test familiarity, the consistent pattern across multiple outcomes suggests broader cognitive benefits [11]. Identification bridges theoretical knowledge to real-world clinical practice. Students actively retrieve theoretical concepts to match two-dimensional mental images formed during drawing with actual three-dimensional extracted teeth, learning to distinguish tooth type, morphological variation, and wear patterns [16]. This process reinforces theoretical memory, deepens three-dimensional spatial understanding, and helps correct perceptual errors, improving accuracy in both drawing and carving [3, 17]. The marked improvement from 62.23 to 80.63 reflects a shift from passive memorization to active analysis. In this sense, identification functions as a bridge between static textbook knowledge and dynamic clinical reality, likely contributing to the parallel improvement in theoretical examination scores.

Carving as the hands-on translation

As the final stage of this teaching model, carving integrates all previous learning phases into practical application. Successful completion of structured carving requires both a solid grasp of theoretical knowledge and precise morphological execution [18, 19]. Teaching practice confirms that the detailed morphological memory developed during drawing training, combined with spatial cognition gained through identification exercises, reduces the mental burden of carving and enables students to accurately replicate complex dental structures [19]. This integrated teaching approach directly connects theoretical understanding with hands-on training.

Additionally, haptic feedback during carving creates a self-reinforcing loop: anatomical theory guides manual operations, while the intuitive tactile experience of shaping materials further deepens students’ theoretical comprehension [15, 20]. ANCOVA results show that GPA is not a significant predictor of carving performance (F = 2.3, p = 0.133). Consistent with this finding, there was no significant bivariate correlation between GPA and carving scores in either the overall sample ( r = 0.086, p = 0.125 ) or individual cohorts (all p > 0.05 ).

In contrast, GPA was significantly positively correlated with drawing scores ( r = 0.169, p = 0.017 ) and theory scores ( r = 0.233, p = 0.001 ), which aligns with the significant covariate effects observed in these ANCOVA models (Tables 2 and 6). These results indicate that the development of hands-on skills has a low correlation with general academic ability, while theoretical performance and drawing ability are more closely associated with students’ prior academic performance.

Theoretical knowledge as the unifying thread

Theoretical knowledge runs through every stage: it provides the anatomical standard for drawing, the evaluative basis for identification, and the operational guideline for carving [21]. Conversely, drawing, identification, and carving turn abstract concepts into tangible experience, forming a continuous loop—theory to practice to perception to refined theory. This tight integration of theory and skill lies at the heart of the paradigm’s educational value [21].

Practical advantages: low-cost, highly feasible

Unlike digital simulations requiring expensive infrastructure [22, 23], our approach uses only paper, pencils, and extracted teeth. Recent comparative evidence indicates that traditional hands-on methods may outperform fully digital approaches in tooth morphology education [24], supporting the value of low-technology, tactile learning. However, the model requires careful implementation. Extracted teeth, biosafety protocols, staff time, and curriculum space remain prerequisites. It can be readily incorporated into existing curricula without major restructuring.

Compared with the stepwise carving method reported by Kilistoff et al. [19], our design uses drawing as a central cognitive activity to strengthen foundational morphological understanding, supported by the four-layer integrated mechanism. This structure lowers learning barriers, enhances learning efficiency, and advances educational equity—particularly in resource-limited settings.

Educational value: early clinical reasoning and broader implications

This paradigm aligns with the international trend toward early clinical integration in dental education [16]. By observing natural morphological variations and wear patterns in extracted teeth during the identification process, students are able to break free from the constraints of idealized “standard tooth” stereotypes. Subsequent drawing and carving exercises further consolidate students’ morphological awareness and hone their technical skills. Together, these integrated activities foster the development of individualized clinical reasoning abilities and better prepare students for clinical practice.

For global dental education, three implications stand out: (1) high-fidelity digital tools should supplement rather than replace foundational cognitive training; (2) clinical observation skills can be introduced much earlier in anatomy courses; (3) curriculum-internal redesign can produce meaningful educational improvement with minimal institutional disruption [16]. These implications are tentative and require validation in diverse educational contexts.

Limitations and future directions

First, the quasi-experimental historical-cohort design limits causal inference. Outcomes were derived from existing records, not prospectively collected [25]. Although assessments were re-scored by two calibrated examiners (κ = 0.84) with double-blind scoring, the lack of randomization and stepwise intervention introduction mean observed associations may reflect temporal effects or unmeasured confounding rather than true causality. Sample sizes were determined by class size without a priori power calculation. Additionally, Pre-course GPA variance stems primarily from the 2021 broad-category admission policy. This one-time policy shift created a distinct academic peak; that year’s mean GPA (3.36 ± 0.56) was significantly higher than that of 2020 (3.29) and 2022 (3.16).

Second, residual confounding persists despite baseline adjustment. Pre-course GPA was used in ANCOVA, but unmeasured factors—such as individual learning motivation and prior drawing or carving experience—may still influence results [26]. ‘Spatial literacy’ and ‘3-D imagination’ were inferred from performance changes, not measured with validated instruments.

Third, the four-layer model is a post-hoc interpretation. Because drawing, identification, and carving were introduced sequentially rather than factorially, the framework fits observed patterns but awaits experimental testing.

Fourth, single-center implementation limits generalizability. Consecutive cohorts from one institution may not represent broader populations. The additional time required for drawing practice should be considered when integrating this approach into other curricula.

Fifth, although the student satisfaction questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.932), it was not subjected to full psychometric validation beyond internal consistency assessment (e.g., test-retest reliability, construct validity, or confirmatory factor analysis) [27]. Future studies should validate this instrument in independent samples.

Conclusion

In conclusion, integrating structured ‘draw-identify-carve’ training into preclinical dental curricula was associated with improved student performance across practical and theoretical domains. The low-technology approach is readily adaptable to resource-limited settings. While causal inference is constrained, these findings warrant further validation through randomized controlled trials (RCTs).
