Roadmapping stroke healthcare in Colombia: a comprehensive telestroke network evaluation
Authors: Antonio Salazar, Carlos Martínez, Laura Espinel, Jaime Rodríguez, Manuel Granja, Hernán Bayona, Brenda Ropero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Colombia, a web-based telestroke network has been developed that provides a framework for the implementation, monitoring, and evaluation of stroke services. This study aimed to present preliminary results of using a telestroke platform to integrate healthcare care delivery while evaluating potential discrepancies between standardized clinical guidelines and actual care provided within our hospitals.MethodsWe included consecutive patients with stroke symptoms who came to the emergency department of three centers of the public health system and a comprehensive private facility. In our telestroke platform, three healthcare settings (HSs) were defined: primary (PHS), intermediate (IHS), and advanced (AHS). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess the correlation between the patient care recommendations generated by the telestroke platform according to the standardized stroke guidelines and the actual care administered, whose information was stored in the electronic health record (EHR).ResultsAmong the 171 patients included in the present study, 89.8% were considered appropriate for transfer (that is, IHS or AHS) within the PHS. A total of 89.3% of the patients exhibited clinical evaluations consistent with acute neurological deficits in the intermediate healthcare setting (IHS), while the documentation indicated similar characteristics in 87.2% of the patients in the advanced healthcare setting (AHS). At the IHS, 29.2% of the patients received intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), 48.9% received intravenous thrombolysis at the AHS, and 17% received mechanical thrombectomy (MT). Excellent agreement was observed between the recommendations generated by the telestroke platform and the actual care administered in the IHS in IVT (ICC = 0.96). At the AHS, good agreement was observed with respect to IVT (ICC = 0.86), and excellent agreement was observed with respect to MT (ICC = 1.0).ConclusionsOur comprehensive telestroke solution facilitates the evaluation of adherence to evidence-based stroke care guidelines, effectively bridging fragmented healthcare systems in rural and urban areas of our country. This approach has the potential to reduce the mortality and disability rates associated with stroke in developing countries.Keywords: Stroke, Telestroke, Reliability, Emergency medicine, Clinical pathways, Intravenous thrombolysis, Mechanical thrombectomy, Software

Background

Among cerebrovascular pathologies, stroke is the main contributor to the significant reduction in years of life adjusted for disability [1]. Limited access to healthcare services in rural areas and limited services in low-complexity medical facilities impact the survival and functionality of stroke patients who are candidates for reperfusion therapies. Hence, there is an imperative need to devise impactful strategies that employ technological tools to mitigate these accessibility disparities.

Emergency telestroke networks play a crucial role in attenuating the short-term mortality risks associated with acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke. They achieve this by expediting on-site treatment decisions and effectively coordinating patient transfers to appropriate healthcare facilities when deemed necessary [2]. Some countries have used smartphone applications that allow real-time information exchange with specialists to improve response times and prompt access to reperfusion therapies [3]. Thus, the telestroke system aims to guide treatment decision making in resource-limited settings by employing decision trees based on the initial characterization of the patient in the emergency department.

Our telestroke system is a web-based platform designed to manage patients with suspected acute stroke. Telestroke-RU connects healthcare facilities at various levels of care for stroke patients. It considers each facility’s staffing levels, equipment, and reperfusion therapy availability. This enables a referral and counter-referral system. The system provides tools that support on-site medical decision-making, from patient admission and triage to evaluation by an emergency physician and a neurologist and diagnostic imaging with interpretation by a neuroradiologist. The system also supports the final decision on reperfusion therapy (thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy) in accordance with standardized international clinical guidelines. If the patient’s point of admission lacks the necessary treatments, the system coordinates transfers to the nearest appropriate healthcare facility. Similarly, if neurologists or radiologists are unavailable at the time of admission to perform the patient’s neurological evaluation or review their diagnostic imaging, the system provides tools for neurological teleconsultation and teleradiology. This enables on-site decision-making even when experts are not physically present with the patient. Staff can use the system to perform triage and neurological status assessments. They can also activate the stroke protocol, assess the severity of neurological damage, apply radiological scales related to stroke to detect large-vessel occlusion, quantify infarct volume in radiological images, and identify risk factors and contraindications for reperfusion therapies.

The goal of this study was to validate our telestroke platform as a support decision-making tool for stroke patients. Our objective is to optimize stroke healthcare delivery while examining possible disparities between care suggested by standardized clinical guidelines (embedded in our telestroke system) and the actual care provided. Validation of this telestroke system is essential for its subsequent deployment in remote healthcare settings that lack established stroke protocols and dedicated stroke healthcare personnel.

Regarding the management of patients with acute stroke symptoms, we have typified and characterized three types of health facilities in our country according to the diagnostic and treatment resources available in them. For each of the three types, we defined protocols and decision trees, which were implemented in web software. The protocols are based on the recommendations of the American Heart Association and the American Stroke Association [4] and were implemented in a web-based software named Appremia® [5], which was optimized for mobile devices, such as smartphone or tablets. The software integrates Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) protocols for image transfer, storage, and processing, enabling teleradiology capabilities and real-time neurology teleconsultation in situations where on-site expertise is unavailable. Furthermore, it includes features to share patient information across facilities, facilitating seamless transfer to higher-level care facilities when necessary.

Our system incorporates 12 clinical and imaging scales that define customized protocol pathways for various healthcare settings. These pathways guide timely decisions regarding on-site treatment or transfers to the most appropriate healthcare facility. A comprehensive account of the protocols, workflow pathways, and clinical and radiological scales utilized in developing our telestroke system has been widely described [6]. The protocols were defined and implemented in Telestroke network software for the following healthcare settings: primary healthcare setting (PHS), intermediate health setting (IHS), and advanced health setting (AHS). Blood glucose levels, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores [7], and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores [8] were measured at all entry points of care, after clinical evaluation using scales such as the BE-FAST score [9], or the Cincinnati scale [10], to achieve early detection of a potential stroke, prompt activation of the stroke code, and transfer of the patient to the IHS or AHS when necessary. The main clinical, diagnostic, and logistic characteristics of the three main health care settings are described below.

PHS: A basic clinical environment without access to neuroimaging or reperfusion therapies. Furthermore, in PHS, the ABCD2 score is calculated [11, 12] to predict the subsequent risk of stroke after a transient ischemic attack (TIA), or the Field Assessment Stroke Triage for Emergency Destination (FAST-ED) score is used to determine the probability of large vessel occlusion [13]. These scores help determine the transfer to an appropriate healthcare setting.

Methods

The Institutional Review Board granted approval for this retrospective study, and the need to obtain informed consent was waived. The study involved three hospitals within the Bogota health system: one PHS, two IHSs, and one private AHS.

We included consecutive patients who presented to the emergency departments of the PHS, IHS or AHS facilities with acute stroke symptoms between August 2021 and January 2022. The patients were treated according to the protocols of each institution. Information on the actual care provided to these patients was documented in the EHR.

The process performed in the present evaluation was as follows: (1) At each healthcare center, patients in the stroke database were consecutively selected in order of arrival. All electronic health records (EHRs) of patients over 18 years of age for whom the stroke code was activated were retained. This occurred several months after the patient received care; (2) From the EHRs, information on procedures, outcomes, and decisions made about the patients regarding stroke management was extracted. Additionally, the records were anonymized; (3) Using this information, a neurology resident simulated filling out the software forms a posteriori. This implies that the resident knows the patient’s outcome. However, his role was only to use the clinical information to determine if he could fill in all the requested fields and execute the algorithm from start to finish without bottlenecks; (4) It was observed whether the software led to the same treatment that was actually applied. If not, the necessary adjustments are made to either the software algorithm or the procedures used in the healthcare center. The true algorithm is taken from the standardized clinical guidelines. If a failure is detected in the software, the code is corrected. In contrast, if the failure is human, the protocols and processes used by the healthcare personnel are reviewed, and training is carried out in accordance with the standardized clinical guidelines. This enabled us to validate that the decision tree and pathways incorporated into the telestroke software facilitated comprehensive patient monitoring from diagnosis to treatment, ensuring a seamless process without bottlenecks and achieving results consistent with the actual care provided by the hospital.

The healthcare providers in the three different clinical settings relayed clinical information and imaging results of patient arrivals to the Telestroke platform. This exchange was aimed at establishing a preliminary working diagnosis, including ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, mimic, TIA, or LVO. Subsequently, the Telestroke platform recommended the next steps in patient management, which could range from basic supportive measures such as dextrose administration or hypertension treatment to acute reperfusion therapies such as IVT, mechanical thrombectomy (MT) or neurosurgery consultation. Additionally, the Telestroke platform assessed the potential benefits of transferring patients to a higher-level healthcare setting. These recommendations were compared with the actual care given to the patients.

To compare the results obtained from the telestroke system with the actual care provided (obtained from the EHR), reliability and equivalence tests were performed. The reliability between the EHR and the telestroke system records was evaluated as their agreement on the following variables: stroke mimics, IVT, MT and LVO. Agreement evaluation was performed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model. The ICC estimates and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated using STATA software (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA). ICC values less than 0.5 are indicative of “poor” reliability, values between 0.5 and 0.75 indicate “moderate” reliability, and values between 0.75 and 0.9 indicate “good” reliability. Values greater than 0.90 indicate “excellent” reliability [17].

A generalized estimation equation (GEE) analysis was performed to compare the recommendations provided by the Telestroke platform with the actual care documented in the EHR, specifically focusing on stroke mimic and LVO diagnoses, as well as recommendations for IVT and MT. This analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Equivalence tests were also performed for those variables using the mean differences of the paired comparisons and their standard errors. The null hypothesis for the equivalence test was |difference (I-J)| - δ = 0, and the alternative hypothesis was |difference (I-J)| - δ < 0 [18–21], where I and J are the systems compared and δ is the maximum difference margin allowed to claim equivalence. The significance level was set to α = 0.05. Furthermore, the (1–2α)% confidence intervals for the paired differences were also calculated. The lower and upper bounds allow us to determine the required equivalence margin to claim equivalence (δequi).

Results

A total of 171 patients were included in the study: 59 from a PHS, 65 from an IHS, and 47 from an AHS. The ages of the patients ranged from 26 to 97 years, with a mean age of 69 years. Among the participants, 105 were women, representing 61.4% of the sample.

At the IHS, neuroimaging availability allowed for a better classification of stroke patients in this setting. A total of 26.2% of patients were diagnosed with ischemic stroke on CT images, while 9.2% of patients were diagnosed with hemorrhagic stroke. Normal CT scans were documented in 18.5% of the patients. One patient (1.5%) presented with a stroke mimic. A total of 44.6% of the patients had wake-up strokes or had a duration of symptom onset between 4.5 and 24 h; consequently, urgent transfers to an AHS were required for these individuals. Regarding the detection of LVO, 9.2% of the patients had confirmed occlusions in this health care setting.

Among the 47 patients in the AHS, 12.8% had hemorrhagic stroke, while 70.2% had ischemic stroke on CT images. According to negative CT scans (17%), 12.7% of the patients had TIA, while 4.3% had persistent neurological symptoms. In this setting, 17% of the patients had LVO involvement. Since this is the top-level setting, no transfers are needed.

Discussion

In our study, 85% of the individuals received a final diagnosis of ischemic stroke, while 9.2% of the patients had hemorrhagic lesions (specifically, IHS data). In the AHS, 74% of the patients were diagnosed with ischemic stroke, and 12.8% had hemorrhagic stroke. These findings are consistent with previously published literature [22]. A difference was observed between IHS (13.8%) and AHS (4.3%) in the percentage of stroke patients with acute neurological deficits but normal CT scans. This may be related to the use of magnetic resonance imaging in IHS to detect smaller lesions that may not be detected on brain CT scans.

High agreement, classified as excellent or good, was evident between the EHR data and Telestroke recommendations on IVT, M) and the identification of stroke mimics and LVO at both the IHS and the AHS. However, the levels of agreement were lower when comparing the different imaging modalities used in each healthcare setting. Specifically, at the IHS, poor agreement was observed with respect to CT indications, with the Telestroke platform suggesting CT scans in 92.3% of cases, while the actual proportion of requests recorded in the EHR was 100%. This discrepancy can be attributed to the widespread use of CT scans due to the lack of accessible magnetic resonance imaging at the IHS during the study period. Furthermore, although Telestroke software recommended transferring certain patients to an AHS for further evaluation or treatment (for example, those with late window presentations, wake-up strokes or LVO), real-world constraints such as health coverage and payment limitations hindered these transfers. With the implementation of the Telestroke network, there is potential for improved identification of stroke mimics and better selection of patients requiring referral to advanced care centers, potentially increasing the number of patients benefiting from reperfusion therapies.

A special agreement was established during the study period to facilitate the transfer of patients from IHS sites to AHS sites in specific scenarios, such as wake-up stroke, extended therapeutic window, and LVO. As a result, the number of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) procedures performed at the AHS increased significantly.

As a result, the number of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) procedures performed at the AHS increased significantly.

One limitation of this pilot study was the small patient sample size. Since the total number of potential pathways implemented was 10 in the PHS, 39 in the IHS, and 1,162 in the AHS, the sample size was insufficient to produce statistically significant results at each pathway node. For example, there were not enough patients with hypoglycemia to produce significant results. However, the primary variables of interest were evaluated according to the protocol. Another limitation was the absence of electronic health record (EHR) data in the PHS, which prevented the same comparisons with the telestroke system from being conducted as were conducted for the IHS and the AHS (see Table 4).

Solutions for stroke have focused on remote consultations with clinical specialists, particularly radiologists and neurologists [23], to promote the early rehabilitation of stroke patients [24]. Other solutions focus on emergency management of acute ischemic stroke [25], triage and transfer for reperfusion therapies [26], clinical and neuroimaging data, and general coordination among stroke teams [27–29].

To our knowledge, there is currently no software tool available in the United States that supports decision-making for stroke patients based on their initial symptoms and time of arrival. Our platform addresses this gap by integrating 12 clinical and radiological scales, creating protocol pathways tailored to each healthcare setting, and facilitating prompt on-site treatment or transfer to the most appropriate facility. Our telestroke system aligns with the European Stroke Organization’s recommendations [30].

The continuous evolution of stroke care guidelines necessitates frequent software updates, posing a significant challenge for stroke applications. Additionally, artificial intelligence (AI) is being integrated into stroke care. AI-powered clinical decision support systems (AI-CDSSs) improve stroke diagnosis and thrombolytic risk assessment [31, 32]. Recent AI recommendations have been made to improve every stage of stroke care, from initial diagnosis to rehabilitation [33]. Artificial intelligence tools that are computer-aided have been developed for processing and analyzing stroke imaging [34–36].

The software’s database includes administrative information on each facility in the telestroke network and possible referral facilities (i.e., facilities contracted to receive patients when a transfer is required). This information enables the rapid selection of the most suitable stroke center according to patients’ needs, after an assessment of clinical requirements and transfer times is made. Since “time is brain,” potential administrative pitfalls between primary and advanced healthcare settings must also be considered to ensure quick and effective transfers. In our country, patients may be transported to various emergency departments until one of them accepts them, resulting in a critical delay in required care, known as “the death ride.”

In addition, the new version of our system, allows for integration with georeferenced ambulance networks and WhatsApp communications for notifications to specialists and patients’ relatives. It also allows for management of specialists’ schedules and provides teleradiology and teleneurology functions, which improve time delays.

Since patients can arrive at any hospital and may be transferred across healthcare settings without receiving adequate stroke care, the starting point of a “case” was defined as the time of the first documented medical contact in the last visited hospital until the final patient outcome was reported prior to discharge. Therefore, when a patient is transferred to a second healthcare setting, all case information is available at the receiving facility, provided that all data are stored in a server database and shared among facilities. This design decision provides universal access to patient health information at any time from any healthcare setting while avoiding redundant data entry. Thus, information on patients’ past medical histories, current clinical conditions, blood test results, imaging evaluations, and procedures is available in real time to all healthcare facilities across the entire spectrum of stroke patient care. Additionally, this allows transfer reporting to referral facilities before patients arrive, thus avoiding prolonged waiting times in emergency departments.

Our AHS is a private primary stroke center certified by the Joint Commission International. It is one of the centers in Colombia with 24/7 endovascular thrombectomy capabilities. Other centers that are not yet accredited can benefit from our system to structure their services. This includes the decision algorithm and the following tools: advance access to patients’ clinical histories from other centers, automated ASPECTS calculation for brain CT image processing, email and WhatsApp communication for notifications and communication with health personnel and patients’ relatives, and scheduling of specialists. These tools provide added value to these centers. Teleradiology and videoconferencing tools enable quick consultations when a second opinion is needed. The entire radiology team is notified as soon as the stroke code is activated. Images are read at any point in the hospital, and depending on the observed severity, physicians move to the patient’s location.

Conclusions

The latest version of our telestroke system includes a global positioning system (GPS) and traffic applications that calculate real-time transfer durations. These applications select the referral facility with the shortest transfer time when necessary. Additionally, it includes notifications to relevant experts (e.g., neurologists or radiologists) via email or WhatsApp.

In summary, our telestroke solution is a valuable resource for evaluating and ensuring compliance with evidence-based stroke care protocols. It has the potential to facilitate connections between healthcare facilities with varying levels of stroke care resources, which is particularly beneficial for institutions lacking neurologists or stroke expertise in rural areas with early stroke systems.

Although telestroke software provides remote access to neurologists and neuroradiologists who specialize in stroke, other resources are needed to serve underserved populations. These resources include thrombolysis and thrombectomy services, CT and MRI equipment, and intensive care units. Additionally, there is a significant lack of knowledge regarding stroke symptoms in our population, even among those at higher risk, such as individuals with hypertension and diabetes [37]; therefore, patient education campaigns are also necessary.
