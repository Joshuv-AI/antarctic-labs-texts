Vietoris–Rips complexes of torus grids
Henry Adams, Adenike Yeside Adetowubo, Hector Barriga-Acosta, Ziqin Feng, John Sterling

Abstract
. We study the topology of Vietoris–Rips complexes of finite grids on the torus. Let T n , n T_{n,n} be the grid of n × n n\times n points on the flat torus S 1 × S 1 S^{1}\times S^{1} , equipped with the l 1 l^{1} metric. Let VR ⁡ ( T n , n , k ) \mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k) be the Vietoris–Rips simplicial complex of this torus grid at scale k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 . For n ≥ 7 n\geq 7 and small scales 2 ≤ k ≤ n − 1 3 2\leq k\leq\frac{n-1}{3} , the complex VR ⁡ ( T n , n , k ) \mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k) is homotopy equivalent to the torus. For large scales k ≥ 2 ​ ⌊ n 2 ⌋ k\geq 2\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor , the complex VR ⁡ ( T n , n , k ) \mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k) is a simplex and hence contractible. Interesting topology arises over intermediate scales n − 1 3 < k < 2 ​ ⌊ n 2 ⌋ \frac{n-1}{3}<k<2\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor . For example, we prove that VR ⁡ ( T 2 ​ n , 2 ​ n , 2 ​ n − 1 ) ≅ S 2 ​ n 2 − 1 \mathrm{VR}(T_{2n,2n};2n-1)\cong S^{2n^{2}-1} for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , that VR ( T 3 ​ n , 3 ​ n ; n ) ≃ ∨ 6 ​ n 2 − 1 S 2 \mathrm{VR}(T_{3n,3n};n)\simeq\vee^{6n^{2}-1}S^{2} for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , and that VR ⁡ ( T 3 ​ n − 1 , 3 ​ n − 1 , n ) ≃ ⋁ 6 ​ n − 3 S 2 ∨ ⋁ 6 ​ n − 2 S 3 \mathrm{VR}(T_{3n-1,3n-1};n)\simeq\bigvee_{6n-3}S^{2}\vee\bigvee_{6n-2}S^{3} for n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . Based on homology computations, we conjecture that VR ⁡ ( T n , n , k ) \mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k) is homotopy equivalent to a 3-sphere for a countable family of ( n , k ) (n,k) pairs, and we prove this for ( n , k ) = ( 7 , 4 ) (n,k)=(7,4) .

Introduction

The Vietoris–Rips complex originates in the work of Leopold Vietoris
[
45
]
, a pioneer in the study of homology.
In 1927, he introduced Vietoris–Rips complexes to construct a canonical simplicial complex on top of any metric space.
Later, Elihayu Rips and Mikhail Gromov
[
31
]
used Vietoris–Rips complexes to study hyperbolic groups in geometric group theory.
More recently, Vietoris–Rips complexes have been used in computational algebraic topology and topological data analysis to process point cloud data
[
13
,
21
,
20
,
19
,
49
]
, based on the existence of fast algorithms to compute persistent homology
[
6
,
50
,
51
]
.
The study of Vietoris–Rips complexes is finding connections to many areas of geometry and topology.

For any metric space
X
and a scale parameter
r\geq 0
, the Vietoris–Rips complex
\mathrm{VR}(X;r)
is a simplicial complex on the vertex set
X
, which has as its simplices the finite subsets of
X
of diameter at most
r
.
The Vietoris–Rips complex is an example of a clique complex because it is the maximal simplicial complex determined by its 1-skeleton.
Part of the applicability of Vietoris–Rips complexes comes from the work of Hausmann
[
33
]
and Latschev
[
36
]
.
Hausmann proved that if
M
is a Riemann manifold and the scale
r
is sufficiently small, then
\mathrm{VR}(M;r)
is homotopy equivalent to
M
.
Latschev also proved that if
X
is Gromov–Hausdorff close to
M
and
r
is in the correct range, then
\mathrm{VR}(X;r)
remains homotopy equivalent to
M
.

Let us now focus attention on the
m
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{m}=(S^{1})^{m}=S^{1}\times\ldots\times S^{1}
.
There are a variety of metrics that one can put on the torus, including the
l^{p}
metric for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
The most natural metric, in our opinion, is the
l^{2}
metric; indeed,
(\mathbb{T}^{m},l^{2})
is the flat torus, a Riemannian manifold with constant curvature zero.
Question 2 in Section 2 of
[
28
]
asks about the homotopy types of Vietoris–Rips complexes of the flat torus
(\mathbb{T}^{m},l^{2})
.
To our knowledge, the only known result is that
\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{m},l^{2});r)
is homotopy equivalent to the torus
\mathbb{T}^{m}
for all scale parameters
r
that are sufficiently small, by Hausmann’s theorem
[
33
]
.

When the
m
-dimensional torus is equipped with the
l^{\infty}
metric, and when each geodesic circle has unit circumference, it is known from
[
2
, Proposition 10.2]
that
\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{m},l^{\infty});r)
is a product of odd-dimensional spheres, namely

\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{m},l^{\infty});r)\simeq\underbrace{\mathrm{VR}(S^{1};r)\times\ldots\times\mathrm{VR}(S^{1};r)}_{m\text{ times}}\simeq\underbrace{S^{2k+1}\times\ldots\times S^{2k+1}}_{m\text{ times}}

for all
\frac{k}{2k+1}<r<\frac{k+1}{2k+3}
.
An analogous formula is true if the different circular factors forming the
m
-dimensional torus have different circumferences; in this case the Vietoris–Rips complex of the
m
-torus with the
l^{\infty}
metric could be homotopy equivalent to a product of odd-dimensional spheres of different dimensions.

In this paper, we instead consider the
l^{1}
metric.
Part of our motivation for considering the
l^{1}
metric is the following.
Though we think of the
l^{1}
and
l^{\infty}
metrics on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
as being less natural than the
l^{2}
metric, if we can understand the inclusion map
\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{1});r)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{\infty});r)
, then since this inclusion factors as

\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{1});r)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{2});r)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{\infty});r),

we can deduce properties of the intermediate space
\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{2});r)
.
For example, if we can show that the inclusion map
\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{1});r)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{\infty});r)
is nonzero on the
k
-th homotopy group
\pi_{k}
, then it follows that
\pi_{k}(\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{\infty});r))\neq 0
, and similarly for homology groups.

See the poster
[
18
]
for some initial computations of the homology of grid graphs in the torus with the
l^{2}
metric.

A second motivation for considering the
l^{1}
metric is that it is the natural (shortest path) metric to put on the grid graphs
T_{n,n}
, which approximate the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, as we describe in the next paragraph.
In this paper, we also focus on the
2
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}=S^{1}\times S^{1}
, as this case is already complicated and points to phenomena that we expect to appear again with the
m
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{m}
.

We study the homotopy types and homology groups of Vietoris–Rips complexes of a finite grid of points on the torus.
Let
T_{n,n}
be the
n\times n
grid graph on a torus
T_{n,n}\subseteq S^{1}\times S^{1}
.
In other words,
T_{n,n}
is the product of the cyclic graph
C_{n}
with itself.
We consider the homotopy types and homology groups of its Vietoris–Rips complexes
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k)
at a growing scale parameter
k\geq 0
.
Here, the input metric space into the Vietoris–Rips construction is the vertex set of the graph
T_{n,n}
, equipped with the
l^{1}
metric.
We think of the
l^{1}
metric as a natural metric on the vertex set of the grid graph
T_{n,n}
: it is the same as the shortest path metric on the natural grid graph with
T_{n,n}
as its vertex set.
We note that as
n\to\infty
, the persistent homology of
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};\frac{\bullet}{n})
converges to the persistent homology of
\mathrm{VR}((\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{1});\bullet)
by the stability of persistent homology
[
16
]
, where
(\mathbb{T}^{2},l^{1})
is the
l^{1}
metric product of two geodesic circles each of circumference one.

Let us describe some initial examples in Table

At scale
0
, we have that
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};0)
is a collection of

\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};0)\cong\vee^{n^{2}-1}S^{0}
.

By an Euler characteristic argument, any connected graph with
v
vertices and
e
edges is homotopy equivalent to
\vee^{e-v+1}S^{1}
.
Since
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};1)
has
n^{2}
vertices and
2n^{2}
edges, we get that

\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};1)\simeq\vee^{e-v+1}S^{1}\simeq\vee^{n^{2}+1}S^{1}\quad\text{for}\quad n\geq 4.

For
k\geq 2
and for
n>3k
, i.e. when the scale parameter
k
is small enough compared to
n
, we prove in Theorem
5.8
that
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k)
is homotopy equivalent to the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

We note that
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k)
is contractible if the scale parameter
k
is large enough:

\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k)\simeq\ast\quad\text{for }k\geq\begin{cases}n&\text{if }n\text{ is even}\\
n-1&\text{if }n\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

Indeed, this follows since the diameter
\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})
is equal to
n
if
n
is even, and equal to
n-1
if
n
is odd.
Once

Interesting topology arises over intermediate scales

2.
Related work

Hausmann’s theorem

Hausmann’s theorem
[
33
]
states that if
M
is a compact Riemannian manifold, then there is a positive scale
r(M)>0
such that if
0<r<r(M)
, then the Vietoris–Rips complex
\mathrm{VR}(M;r)
is homotopy equivalent to
M
.
The value
r(M)
depends on the geometry of the manifold
M
, such as its curvature.
This is extended by Latschev’s theorem
[
36
]
, which further implies that if
X
is sufficiently close to
M
in the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, then the homotopy equivalence
\mathrm{VR}(X;r)\simeq M
is maintained when scale
r
is sufficiently small compared to the curvature of
M
and sufficiently large compared to the density of
X
.

Vietoris–Rips complexes of products

Let
(X_{1},\rho_{1}),(X_{2},\rho_{2}),\ldots,(X_{n},\rho_{n})
be metric spaces.
For each
1\leq p\leq\infty
we define the
l^{p}
metric on the product
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\ldots\times X_{n}
by

d_{l^{p}}((x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}))=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\rho_{i}(x_{i},y_{i}))^{p}\bigg)^{1/p}.

In the case
p=\infty
, we have

d_{l^{\infty}}((x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}))=\max\{\rho_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})~|~1\leq i\leq n\}.

By
[
2
, Proposition 10.2]
, if we equip the product with the
l^{\infty}
metric, then for any
r>0
, we have a homotopy equivalence

\mathrm{VR}(X;r)\simeq\mathrm{VR}(X_{1};r)\times\ldots\times\mathrm{VR}(X_{n};r).

Little is known if we instead equip the product with the
l^{p}
metric for
p<\infty
.
For example, if
I=\{0,1\}
is a two-point metric space with
d_{I}(0,1)=1
, then the Vietoris–Rips complexes are easy to understand:

\mathrm{VR}(I;r)
is two points (the zero-dimensional sphere) for
r<1
,
and
\mathrm{VR}(I;r)
is a (contractible) edge for
r\geq 1
.
So, by
[
2
, Proposition 10.2]
,
\mathrm{VR}((I^{n},l^{\infty});r)
is
2^{n}
points for
r<1
,
and
\mathrm{VR}((I^{n},l^{\infty});r)
is contractible for
r\geq 1
.
But, if instead
p=1<\infty
, then the product
(I^{n},l^{1})
is isometric to
Q_{n}
, the vertex set of the hypercube graph equipped with the shortest path metric.
And, the Vietoris–Rips complexes of these metric spaces
\mathrm{VR}((I^{n},l^{1});r)=\mathrm{VR}(Q_{n};r)
are quite complicated; see
[
3
,
42
,
5
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

There are several papers exploring Künneth formulae for persistent homology
[
40
,
27
,
14
,
11
,
44
]
, though from this perspective the Vietoris–Rips complexes of
l^{\infty}
products are again significantly simpler than Vietoris–Rips complexes of
l^{1}
products.

Vietoris–Rips complexes of platonic solids

In
[
41
]
, the homotopy types of Vietoris–Rips complexes of platonic solids are determined.
In more detail, the vertex set of the platonic solid is equipped with the metric that arises from the shortest path metric in the graph that is the 1-skeleton of that platonic solid.
Many of the higher-dimensional homotopy types that arise are boundaries of cross-polytopes, at a scale parameter one below the diameter of the vertex set.
The most new interesting shape that arises is the Vietoris–Rips complex of the vertex set of the dodecahedron, at scale parameter two below the diameter, when it is homotopy equivalent to a 9-fold wedge of
3
-spheres.

Vietoris-Rips complexes of grid graphs

Let
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be the integer lattice in
n
-dimensional space, equipped with the
l^{1}
metric.
Zaremsky asked the question if for any
n
, there exists a scale parameter
r
sufficiently large such that the Vietoris–Rips complex
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{n};r)
is contractible.
This result was recently proven by Virk in
[
46
]
; see also
[
48
]
.
The case of
n=2
or
n=3
is simpler; see
[
37
,
47
]
.
In order to understand lattice grids in a torus, we need a different proof in the
n=2
case that provides a characterization of the maximal simplices of
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};r)
, which we do in Section
4
.

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the definitions and notation for metric spaces, simplicial complexes, graph theory, and topology that we will need in the remainder of the paper.

3.1.
Metric spaces

We denote the
open ball of radius
r\geq 0
centered at
x\in X
by
B_{X}(x,r)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}
, and the
closed ball
by
B_{X}[x,r]=\{y\in X:d(x,y)\leq r\}
.
When the space
X
is clear, we may write these open and closed balls as
B(x,r)
and
B[x,r]
.
We define the
diameter
of subset
A\subseteq X
as
\mathrm{diam}(A)=\sup_{x,y\in A}d(x,y)
.
If
\mathrm{diam}(A)
is not finite, then we set
\mathrm{diam}(A)=\infty
.

The most frequent metric spaces we will encounter in this paper are the following.
We equip the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with the
l^{1}
metric, defined by
d((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=|x-x^{\prime}|+|y-y^{\prime}|
for all
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
This is also referred to as the word metric on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
We also equip
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
l^{1}
metric, defined in the same way for all
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We let
C_{n}
denote the set
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, equipped with the circular metric
d(i,i^{\prime})=\min\{|i-i^{\prime}|,n-|i-i^{\prime}|\}
.
We let
T_{n,n}
denote the set
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}\times\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, equipped with the
l^{1}
product metric, namely
d((i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}))=\min\{|i-i^{\prime}|,n-|i-i^{\prime}|\}+\min\{|j-j^{\prime}|,n-|j-j^{\prime}|\}
.
The diameter of
C_{n}
is
\frac{n}{2}
if
n
is even and
\frac{n-1}{2}
if
n
is odd.
It follows that the diameter
\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})
is equal to
n
if
n
is even, and equal to
n-1
if
n
is odd.
Note that the metric on
C_{n}
recovers the shortest path metric on the vertex set of a cycle graph, and that the metric on
T_{n,n}
recovers the shortest path metric on the vertex set of a torus grid graph.
For this reason, by an abuse of notation, we will also use the symbols
C_{n}
and
T_{n,n}
to denote graphs in Section
3.5
.

3.2.
Simplicial complexes

A
k
-simplex
is a
k
-dimensional polytope that is the convex hull of its
k+1
vertices.
For example, a 0-dimensional simplex is a point, a 1-dimensional simplex is a line segment, a 2-dimensional simplex is a triangle, a 3-dimensional simplex is a tetrahedron, etc.

The convex hull of any nonempty subset of the
k+1
points that define an
k
-simplex is called a
face
of the simplex.
A
simplicial complex

K
is a set of simplices of an underlying vertex set that satisfies the following conditions:
(1) every face of a simplex from
K
is also in
K
, and
(2) the nonempty intersection of any two simplices in
K
is a face of both of them.
A simplex
\sigma\in K
is a
maximal simplex
or a
facet
in
K
if there is no simplex
\tau\in K
with
\sigma\subsetneq\tau
.
That is, a facet is a simplex that is not the face of any larger simplex.

An abstract simplicial complex
K
consists of a vertex set
V
and a collection of simplices, namely finite subsets of
V
, such that if
\sigma\in K
and
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma
, then
\sigma^{\prime}\in K
.
We identify abstract simplicial complexes with their geometric realizations, described in the paragraph above.

3.3.
Vietoris–Rips complexes

For a metric space
(X,d)
and
r\geq 0
, define the
Vietoris-Rips complex
, denoted as
\mathrm{VR}(X;r)
, as the simplicial complex satisfying:

•

the underlying vertex set of
\mathrm{VR}(X;r)
is
X
, and

•

a finite set
\sigma\subseteq X
is a simplex in
\mathrm{VR}(X;r)
if and only if
\mathrm{diam}(\sigma)\leq r
.

This is the closed Vietoris-Rips complex; the open Vietoris-Rips complex instead uses
\mathrm{diam}(\sigma)<r
.

3.4.
Nerve complexes

We will sometimes understand the homotopy type of a space using nerve complexes and the nerve lemma, which we introduce next.

Given a finite collection of sets
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
with each
U_{\alpha}\subseteq X
,
the
nerve complex
of
\mathcal{U}
is the simplicial complex
N(\mathcal{U})

whose vertex set is the index set
A
, and where a subset
\{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{k}\}\subseteq A
spans a
k
-simplex in
N(\mathcal{U})
if and only if
U_{\alpha_{0}}\cap U_{\alpha_{1}}\cap...\cap U_{\alpha_{k}}\neq\emptyset
.

Theorem 3.1

(Nerve Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a collection of open sets in a metric space
X
with
\cup\mathcal{U}=X
.
If every nonempty intersection
\bigcap^{n}_{i=0}U_{\alpha_{i}}
is contractible, then the nerve simplicial complex
N(\mathcal{U})
is homotopy equivalent to
X
.

This version of the nerve theorem follows from
[
32
, Corollary 4G.3]
since every metric space is paracompact.
We refer the reader to
[
8
,
7
]
for other versions of the nerve theorem.

3.5.
Graphs

A
graph
is a pair
G=(V,E)
consisting of a set of
vertices,
V
, and a set of edges,
E
, where each edge is a pair of vertices.
In some cases, we will use the notation
G=(V(G),E(G))
to emphasize that the vertices and edges correspond to the graph
G
.
The
shortest path metric

d_{G}
on
V(G)
is the metric satisfying that
d_{G}(u,v)
is the minimum length of a path of edges connecting
u
to
v
.
If
u
and
v
are not connected by a path of edges, we say
d_{G}(u,v)=\infty
.
The
diameter
of a finite connected graph
G
is defined as
\mathrm{diam}(G)=\max\{d_{G}(u,v):u,v\in G\}
.

Given a graph
G
and a positive integer
k
, the
graph power

G^{k}
is the graph with
V(G^{k})=V(G)
, and with
(u,v)\in E(G^{k})
if and only if
d_{G}(u,v)\leq k
.

For an integer
n\geq 3
, we define the
cycle graph

C_{n}=(V(C_{n}),E(C_{n}))
, where
V(C_{n})=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
and
E(C_{n})=\{(i,i+1):0\leq i\leq n-1\}\cup\{(n-1,0)\}
.

Given graphs
G=(V(G),E(G))
and
H=(V(H),E(H))
, define the
Cartesian product
(sometimes called
box product
)
G\ \square\ H
as the graph such that: (i)
V(G\ \square\ H)=V(G)\times V(H)
, and (ii)
((u,u^{\prime}),(v,v^{\prime}))\in E(G\ \square\ H)
if and only if either
u=v
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E(H)
, or
u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
and
(u,v)\in E(G)
.
The
torus grid graph
of
n
by
m
vertices is defined as
T_{n,m}=C_{n}\ \square\ C_{m}
.

The
clique complex

\mathrm{Cl}(G)
of a graph
G
is the simplicial complex with

•

vertex set
V(G)
, and

•

a finite
\sigma
as a simplex if and only if
\sigma
induces a complete subgraph of
G
.

We can relate the clique complexes of graph powers to Vietoris-Rips complexes of graphs equipped with the shortest path metric.
Indeed, for every
k\geq 0
, the clique complex
\mathrm{Cl}(G^{k})
is equal to the Vietoris–Rips complex
\mathrm{VR}((V(G),d_{G});k)
.
For ease of notation, we write
\mathrm{VR}((V(G),d_{G});k)
as
\mathrm{VR}(G;k)
.
In particular,
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k)
denotes the same simplicial complex whether
T_{n,n}
is interpreted as the metric space defined in Section
3.1
or as the vertex set of the torus grid graph equipped with the shortest path metric.

3.6.
Homology

Given a simplicial complex
K
, we define
\Delta_{n}(K)
to be the free abelian group consisting of finite integral combinations of the oriented
n
-simplices in
K
.
The
boundary homomorphism

\partial_{n}:\Delta_{n}(K)\rightarrow\Delta_{n-1}(K)
is a function that maps each oriented
n
-simplex to a linear combination of its
(n-1)
-dimensional faces, each with an appropriate sign to respect orientation, such that
\partial_{n-1}\circ\partial_{n}=0
.
The collection of all
\Delta_{i}(K)
connected by boundary homomorphisms,
\partial_{i}
, form a
chain complex

\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{n+2}}\Delta_{n+1}(K)\xrightarrow{\partial_{n+1}}\Delta_{n}(K)\xrightarrow{\partial_{n}}\Delta_{n-1}(K)\xrightarrow{\partial_{n-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{2}}\Delta_{1}(K)\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}\Delta_{0}(K)\rightarrow 0

where
\partial_{i}\circ\partial_{i+1}=0
for each
i
.

The
i
-th homology group
(with
\mathbb{Z}
coefficients),
H_{i}(K)
, is defined to be the quotient group
\mathrm{Ker}\partial_{i}/\mathrm{Im}\partial_{i+1}
.
Moreover, the
i
-th Betti number

\beta_{i}(K)
is the rank of
H_{i}(K)
, i.e.
\beta_{i}(K)=\mathrm{rank}(H_{i}(K))
.

3.7.
Connectivity

We say that a nonempty topological space
Y
is
k
-connected
if the homotopy groups
\pi_{i}(Y)
are trivial for all
i\leq k
.

4.
The facets of
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)

Recall that we equip
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
l^{1}
metric, given by

d((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=|x-x^{\prime}|+|y-y^{\prime}|.

In this section, we classify the facets of
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
for any scale

, where
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is equipped with the
l^{1}
metric.
This classification will be needed for the proofs of Theorems
5.10
and
5.12
in the following section.
As an easy consequence, we also recover the known result that
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
is contractible for scales
k
greater than or equal to
2
.
We begin by introducing the required notation and key lemmas.

Since
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)=\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};\lfloor k\rfloor)
, it suffices to restrict attention to integer values of
k
.
Given a positive integer
k
, we say that a simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is
k
-generated
by a set of vertices
\hat{\sigma}\subseteq\sigma
if
\sigma=\bigcap_{v\in\hat{\sigma}}B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[v,k]
; call the set
\hat{\sigma}
a
generating set
.

In the set
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, for
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
R_{n,m}=\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:0\leq i\leq n-1,0\leq j\leq m-1\}
be the rectangle with
n
vertices on the base and
m
vertices on the height.
Note that
R_{1,1}
is simply a point,
R_{2,1}
is two vertices with distance
1
, and
R_{2,2}
is the set of corners of a square.
We define the following collections of simplices with vertex sets in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
up to isometry (translation and
90^{o}
rotation).

If
k
is even, let

•

\mathbb{Z}(\boldsymbol{\cdot},k)=\{\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\sigma\text{ is a $\frac{k}{2}$-generated simplex and }\hat{\sigma}\cong R_{1,1}\}
.

•

\mathbb{Z}(\square,k)=\{\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\sigma\text{ is a $\frac{k+2}{2}$-generated simplex and }\hat{\sigma}\cong R_{2,2}\}
, and

If
k
is odd, let

•

\mathbb{Z}(\mathrel{\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mkern-4.0mu{-}\mkern-4.0mu\boldsymbol{\cdot}},k)=\{\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\sigma\text{ is a $\frac{k+1}{2}$-generated simplex and }\hat{\sigma}\cong R_{2,1}\}
.

By
\hat{\sigma}\cong R_{n,m}
we mean that
\hat{\sigma}
is isometric to
R_{n,m}
.
Observe that
\mathbb{Z}(\boldsymbol{\cdot},k)
is simply the collection of all closed balls of radius
\frac{k}{2}
centered at a vertex in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Given a non-vertical line
L
in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the form
y=mx+b
, define

T_{L}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:y>mx+b\}\text{ and }B_{L}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:y<mx+b\}.

That is,
T_{L}
and
B_{L}
are the top and bottom parts of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
split by the line
L
.
We say that
L
is
tangent
to a set of vertices
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
if (1)
L
contains at least one point of
\sigma
, and (2) either

(\sigma\subseteq T_{L}\cup L\text{ and }\sigma\cap B_{L}=\emptyset)\text{ or }(\sigma\subseteq B_{L}\cup L\text{ and }\sigma\cap T_{L}=\emptyset).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer and let
\sigma\in\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
be a maximal simplex.
Then there is a
c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with each coordinate an integer or a half-integer so that
\sigma=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a facet in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.
Now, let
L_{1},L_{2}
be lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the form
y=x+k_{1}
,
y=x+k_{2}
so that
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are tangent to
\sigma
and
\sigma\subseteq(B_{L_{1}}\cup L_{1})\cap(T_{L_{2}}\cup L_{2})
.
Note the lines
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are parallel translations of the positive diagonal enclosing
\sigma
in a sandwich fashion.
Also, let
L_{3},L_{4}
be lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the form
y=-x+k_{3}
,
y=-x+k_{4}
so that
L_{3}
and
L_{4}
are tangent to
\sigma
and
\sigma\subseteq(B_{L_{3}}\cup L_{3})\cap(T_{L_{4}}\cup L_{4})
.
Similarly, the lines
L_{3}
and
L_{4}
are parallel translations of the negative diagonal enclosing
\sigma
.
Note
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
P
denote the closed quadrilateral of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
enclosed by the lines
L_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 4
.
This implies
\sigma\subseteq P
.

We claim that
d(L_{1},L_{2})=d(L_{3},L_{4})=k
.
On the one hand, if
d(L_{1},L_{2})>k
, then
\mathrm{diam}(\sigma)>k
: pick points
v_{1}\in L_{1}\cap\sigma
and
v_{2}\in L_{2}\cap\sigma
, which exist since the lines are tangent to
\sigma
.
Because
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are parallel, any pair
u\in L_{1}
and
w\in L_{2}
have distance
>k
by our assumption, so
d(v_{1},v_{2})>k
which is a contradiction since
v_{1},v_{2}\in\sigma
.
Similarly for
L_{3}
and
L_{4}
.
So, we can assume that both
d(L_{1},L_{2}),d(L_{3},L_{4})\leq k
.
On the other hand, if
d(L_{1},L_{2})<k
, then
\sigma
is not maximal: consider the line
L^{\prime}=L_{1}+1
and consider the intersecting vertex
u\in L^{\prime}\cap L_{3}
.
Note that
d(u,v)\leq k
for every
v\in\sigma
, contradicting that
\sigma
is maximal.
We have proven the claim.

From the claim, note that
P
is a diamond: a quadrilateral with sides of length
k
and all sides parallel to the diagonal or the negative diagonal.
Let
c
be the center of mass (intersecting point of the diagonals) of
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Though
c
need not be in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, each coordinate of
c
is either an integer or a half-integer.
We note
P=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]
.
It is clear that all pairs in
B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]
are at distance at most
k
.
Also, since
\sigma\subseteq P=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]
and
\sigma
is maximal,
\sigma
must coincide with
B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer.
The only facets in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
are:

(1)

elements from
\mathbb{Z}(\boldsymbol{\cdot},k)\cup\mathbb{Z}(\square,k)
, if
k
is even,

(2)

elements from
\mathbb{Z}(\mathrel{\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mkern-4.0mu{-}\mkern-4.0mu\boldsymbol{\cdot}},k)
, if
k
is odd.

That is, the facets of
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
are of the form
\sigma=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
where

•

c=(i,j)
or
(i+\frac{1}{2},j+\frac{1}{2})
for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
if
k
is even, and

•

c=(i+\frac{1}{2},j)
or
(i,j+\frac{1}{2})
for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
if
k
is odd.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a facet in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.
By Lemma
4.1
, we can write
\sigma=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with each coordinate of
c
an integer of a half-integer.
Let
L_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq 4
, denote the tangent lines that enclose
\sigma
as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
That is,
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3},L_{4}
are of the form,
y=x+k_{1}
,
y=x+k_{2}
,
y=-x+k_{3}
,
y=-x+k_{4}
, respectively, so that they form a diamond with sides of length
k=d(L_{1},L_{2})=d(L_{3},L_{4})
.

Since the top lines
L_{1}
and
L_{3}
are lattice diagonals (lines of slopes
\pm 1
with integer
y
-intercepts), their intersection point
L_{1}\cap L_{3}
has coordinates
(x,y)
that are either both integers or both half-integers.
Thus
x+y
is an integer.
The center
c
of the ball with
\sigma=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is
\frac{k}{2}
units below the point
L_{1}\cap L_{3}
, namely
c=(x,y-\frac{k}{2})
.
Therefore
c
has integer or half-integer coordinates that add up to an integer if
k
is even, and that add up to a half-integer if
k
is odd.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer.
Then
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a simplex in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
if and only if
\cap_{v\in\sigma}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\frac{k+1}{2})\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

First we claim that for
c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with each coordinate an integer or a half-integer, we have
B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(c,\frac{k+1}{2})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
The
\subseteq
direction is clear.
For the reverse direction, note if
z\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(c,\frac{k+1}{2})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, then
d(z,c)<\frac{k+1}{2}
.
Since
z
and
c
have integer or half-integer coordinates, and since
k
is an integer, this implies
d(z,c)\leq\frac{k}{2}
, and hence
z\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]
.

Hence if
\sigma
is a simplex in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
, then by Proposition
4.2
and the claim above we have
\sigma=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}=B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(c,\frac{k+1}{2})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, where
c
has each coordinate an integer or a half-integer.
Hence
c\in\cap_{v\in\sigma}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\frac{k+1}{2})
, so
\cap_{v\in\sigma}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\frac{k+1}{2})\neq\emptyset
.

Next we claim that if the intersection
\cap_{v\in\sigma}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\frac{k+1}{2})
is nonempty for
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, then that intersection contains some point
c
with each coordinate an integer or a half-integer.
The intersection of any open
l^{1}
balls in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
yields an intersection set equal to
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:k_{2}<y-x<k_{1},\ k_{4}<y+x<k_{3}\}
.
Write

\cap_{v\in\sigma}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\tfrac{k+1}{2})=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:k_{2}<y-x<k_{1},\ k_{4}<y+x<k_{3}\}.

Since the ball centers are in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, it follows that each of
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}
is an integer (if
k
is odd) or an integer plus
\frac{1}{2}
(if
k
is even).
Since this intersection set is nonempty, we have
k_{1}-k_{2}\geq 1
and
k_{3}-k_{4}\geq 1
.
It follows that the point
c=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
y-x=k_{1}-\frac{1}{2}
and
y+x=k_{3}-\frac{1}{2}
, namely the point
c=(\frac{k_{3}-k_{1}}{2},\frac{k_{1}+k_{3}-1}{2})
, is a point in
\cap_{v\in\sigma}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\frac{k+1}{2})
with each coordinate an integer or a half-integer.

Hence if
\cap_{v\in\sigma}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\frac{k+1}{2})\neq\emptyset
, then there is some
c
in this intersection with each coordinate an integer or a half-integer.
Hence
\sigma\subseteq B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(c,\frac{k+1}{2})
.
Since
k
is an integer and all coordinates of
c
are integers or half-integers, this implies
\sigma\subseteq B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}[c,\frac{k}{2}]
.
So
\mathrm{diam}(\sigma)\leq k
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.
∎

This concludes the classification of the facets of
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
, which is the main point of this section.
As a consequence, we can deduce the known result that
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
is contractible for all
k\geq 2

,

.

Theorem 4.4
.

\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
is contractible for every
k\geq 2
, where
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is equipped with the
l^{1}
metric.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.3
, the
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
is isomorphic to the nerve complex
N(\mathcal{U})
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the collection of open balls
\mathcal{U}=\{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v,\frac{k+1}{2})\}_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
.
Since
k\geq 2
, we have that
\mathcal{U}
is a cover of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Since
l^{1}
balls in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are convex, a finite intersection of such balls is convex, and hence either empty or contractible.
Therefore, the nerve theorem (Theorem
3.1
) gives a homotopy equivalence
N(\mathcal{U})\simeq\cup\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
So
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)\cong N(\mathcal{U})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is contractible.
∎

5.
Homotopy types of
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n}{k};
).

In this section, we discuss the homotopy types of some of the complexes
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k)
.
We prove that
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};k)
is homotopy equivalent to the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
for
k\geq 2
and
n>3k
.
We show that for
k\geq 2
,
\mathrm{VR}(T_{3k,3k};k)
is homotopy equivalent to a
(6k^{2}-1)
-fold wedge sum of
2
-spheres.
Finally, for
k\geq 3
, we prove
\mathrm{VR}(T_{3k-1,3k-1};k)
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge sum of
6k-3
many
S^{2}
’s and
6k-2
many
S^{3}
’s.
In Section
5.1
we classify the facets in these simplicial complexes, before determining the homotopy types of the complexes in Section
5.2
.

Though we expect some of the results in this section to have analogs for grid graphs in higher-dimensional tori, we do not pursue that direction here.

5.1.
Maximal simplices

We denote
G_{n}=n\mathbb{Z}\times n\mathbb{Z}
and its group action on the grid
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by
g\cdot(a,b)=(a+i,b+j)
for
g=(i,j)\in G
.
Recall that we equip
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with the
l^{1}
metric.
It is straightforward to verify that
T_{n,n}
is isometric to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/G_{n}
, equipped with the quotient metric, by the natural correspondence.
For convenience, we use
d
to represent the metrics on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/G_{n}
.
Let
\pi_{n}
be the quotient map from
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/G_{n}
.
For each
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let
[x]=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}~|~\pi_{n}(x^{\prime})=\pi_{n}(x)\}
be the corresponding element of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}/G_{n}=\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.
If
x=(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, then we may also write
[x]=([a],[b])
, where
[a]=\{a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}~|~a^{\prime}=a\pmod{n}\}
.
If any distinct pair
x^{\prime}\neq x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfies
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in[x]
, then
d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\geq n
since either the horizontal or the vertical coordinates differ by at least
n
.
Notice that
d([x],[y])=\min\{d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})~|~x^{\prime}\in[x]\text{ and }y^{\prime}\in[y]\}
.
Using these facts, the following result is straightforward to verify.

Lemma 5.1
.

Fix
n>2k
and
[x],[y]\in\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.
Suppose that
d([x],[y])\leq k
.
Then for any
x^{\prime}\in[x]
, there exists a unique
y^{\prime}\in[y]
such that

d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=d([x],[y])\leq k.

Next we discuss the relations between the facets in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
and
\mathrm{VR}(\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2});k)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\sigma
be a facet in the complex
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.
For
n>2k+1
,
\pi_{n}(\sigma)
is a facet in
\mathrm{VR}(\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2});k)
.

Proof.

Clearly
\pi_{n}(\sigma)
is a simplex in
\mathrm{VR}(\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2});k)
.
Suppose that
\pi_{n}(\sigma)
is not maximal.
Then pick
[y]\notin\pi_{n}(\sigma)
such that
d([y],[x])\leq k
for all
x\in\sigma
.
By Lemma
5.1
, for each
x\in\sigma
, there is a unique
y_{x}\in[y]
such that
d(x,y_{x})\leq k
.
If all of the
y_{x}
points are the same, then
\sigma\cup\{y_{x}\}
is a simplex in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
, which contradicts with the maximality of
\sigma
.
Otherwise by the shape of
\sigma
as in Proposition
4.2
, there are
x
and
x^{\prime}
in
\sigma
such that
d(x,x^{\prime})=1
and
y_{x}\neq y_{x^{\prime}}
.
Then
d(y_{x},y_{x^{\prime}})\leq d(y_{x},x)+d(x,x^{\prime})+d(x^{\prime},y_{x^{\prime}})\leq 2k+1
, which contradicts the fact that
d(y_{x},y_{x^{\prime}})\geq n>2k+1
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that
n\geq 3k-1
and
k\geq 1
.
Let
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
x\neq y
, with
3k-n<d(x,y)\leq k
, and with
\pi_{n}(B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[x,k]\cap B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[y,k])=\pi_{n}(B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[x,k])\cap\pi_{n}(B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[y,k])
.
Then any facet
\tau
in
\mathrm{VR}(\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2});k)
containing
[x]
and
[y]
is of the form
\pi_{n}(\sigma)
for some facet
\sigma\in\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.

Proof.

Let
B[x,k]=B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[x,k]
.
Fix a facet
\tau\in\mathrm{VR}(\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2});k)
containing
[x]
and
[y]
.
By Lemma
5.1
,
\pi_{n}(B[x,k])=B_{\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}[[x],k]
for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
since
n>2k
.
Hence,
\tau\subseteq\pi_{n}(B[x,k])\cap\pi_{n}(B[y,k])=\pi_{n}(B[x,k]\cap B[y,k])
.

For each
[z]\in\tau
, Lemma
5.1
guarantees a unique representative
z_{x}
in
[z]
such that
d(x,z_{x})\leq k
.
Note that
y_{x}=y
since
1\leq d(x,y)\leq k
.
Define
\sigma=\{x\}\cup\{z_{x}:[z]\in\tau\text{ and }z\neq x\}
.
It is clear that
\tau=\pi_{n}(\sigma)
and that
\sigma\subseteq B[x,k]
.
We will show that
\sigma
is a facet in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.

First, we show that
\sigma\subseteq B[x,k]
furthermore satisfies
\sigma\subseteq B[x,k]\cap B[y,k]
.
Let
z_{x}
be an arbitrary element of
\sigma
, with
z_{x}\in[z]
for some
[z]\in\tau\subseteq\pi_{n}(B[x,k]\cap B[y,k])
.
So there must be some
z^{\prime}\in[z]
with
z^{\prime}\in B[x,k]\cap B[y,k]
. By Lemma
5.1
,
z_{x}=z^{\prime}
.
Hence
z_{x}\in B[x,k]\cap B[y,k]
, as desired.

We now show that
\sigma
is a simplex in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.
Let
[z]
and
[z^{\prime}]
be arbitrary elements of
\tau
, meaning that
z_{x}
and
z^{\prime}_{x}
are arbitrary elements of
\sigma
.
Since
\tau\in\mathrm{VR}(\pi_{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{2});k)
, we have
d([z],[z^{\prime}])\leq k
.
Clearly
d(z_{x},z_{x}^{\prime})\leq 2k
.
We claim that for any
z^{\prime\prime}\in[z^{\prime}_{x}]\setminus\{z^{\prime}_{x}\}
, we have
d(z_{x},z^{\prime\prime})\geq k+1
.
There are two cases.
Suppose first that
z_{x}
and
z_{x}^{\prime}
have same first or second coordinates.
Since
z_{x},z^{\prime}_{x}\in\sigma\subseteq B[x,k]\cap B[y,k]
have the same first or second coordinates, elementary geometry gives
d(z_{x},z^{\prime}_{x})\leq 2k-d(x,y)
.
For any element
z^{\prime\prime}\in[z^{\prime}_{x}]\setminus\{z^{\prime}_{x}\}
, the inequality
d(z^{\prime}_{x},z^{\prime\prime})\geq n
with the triangle inequality give
d(z_{x},z^{\prime\prime})\geq n-2k+d(x,y)>k
.
For the second case, suppose that
z_{x}
and
z_{x}^{\prime}
are not in the same vertical or horizontal line. Let
z_{x}=(z_{1},z_{2})
and
z_{x}^{\prime}=(z_{1}^{\prime},z_{2}^{\prime})
.
Then
1\leq|z_{1}-z_{1}^{\prime}|\leq 2k-1
and also
1\leq|z_{2}-z_{2}^{\prime}|\leq 2k-1
.
Pick
z^{\prime\prime}\in[z^{\prime}_{x}]\setminus\{z^{\prime}_{x}\}
. Without loss of generality, suppose
z^{\prime\prime}_{2}\neq z^{\prime}_{2}
.
Then by triangle inequality,

|z_{2}-z_{2}^{\prime\prime}|\geq|z_{2}^{\prime}-z_{2}^{\prime\prime}|-|z_{2}-z_{2}^{\prime}|\geq n-(2k-1)\geq k.

This shows that
d(z_{x},z^{\prime\prime})=|z_{1}-z_{1}^{\prime}|+|z_{2}-z_{2}^{\prime\prime}|\geq 1+k
.
Lemma
5.1
therefore implies that
d(z_{x},z^{\prime}_{x})=d([z],[z^{\prime}])\leq k
, and so
\sigma\in\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.

Lastly, we show that
\sigma
is maximal.
Since
n>3k-d(x,y)\geq 2k
, Lemma
5.1
implies that
\sigma=\{x\}\cup\{z_{x}:[z]\in\tau\text{ and }z\neq x\}
is a maximal simplex in
\mathrm{VR}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};k)
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Fix different
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2})
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
If
|x_{1}-y_{1}|<n-2k
and
|x_{2}-y_{2}|<n-2k
, then
\pi_{n}(B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[x,k]\cap B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[y,k])=\pi_{n}(B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[x,k])\cap\pi_{n}(B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[y,k])
.

Proof.

The assumptions imply
n>2k
.
Let
B[x,k]=B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}[x,k]
.
Let
[z]\in\pi_{n}(B[x,k]\cap B[y,k])
.
Then there exists some
z^{\prime}\in[z]
with
z^{\prime}\in B[x,k]\cap B[y,k]
.
Since
z^{\prime}\in B[x,k]
, then
[z]\in\pi_{n}(B[x,k])
.
Similarly,
[z]\in\pi_{n}(B[y,k])
.
So
\pi_{n}(B[x,k]\cap B[y,k])\subseteq\pi_{n}(B[x,k])\cap\pi_{n}(B[y,k])
.

Next, let

And the intersection of each of the
8
remaining
L_{a}^{1}
or
L_{b}^{2}
with
L\cup L_{0}^{1}\cup L_{0}^{2}
is homotopy equivalent to
S^{1}
.
Hence by induction and Lemma
5.7
the complex
\mathrm{VR}(T_{5,5};2)
is homotopy equivalent to
\bigvee_{9}S^{2}
.
∎

6.
Connection between torus grid graphs and cross-polytopes

In this section we explore the connection between the torus grid graphs and cross-polytopes as noted in Proposition
6.2
.
We state first some notation and results.

An
n
-dimensional cross-polytope
is a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the
\ell_{1}
norm, that is, it is a set of the form
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x-c\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\}
, for some
\varepsilon>0
and some
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
All
n
-dimensional cross-polytopes are isomorphic equivalent, and hence we only focus on the case where
\varepsilon=1
and
c=\textbf{0}
(the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
); we refer to this case as “the”
n
-dimensional cross-polytope.
Equivalently, if
\{\hat{e}_{k}
:
1\leq k\leq n\}
is the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the
n
-dimensional cross-polytope is the convex hull of the vertex set
\{\pm\ \hat{e}_{k}:1\leq k\leq n\}
.
The
boundary
of the
n
-dimensional cross-polytope is the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x||_{1}=1\}
, which is homeomorphic to the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Recall that the diameter of the torus grid graph
T_{n,n}
is
n
if
n
is even, and
n-1
if
n
is odd.
For scale
r=\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})-1
, the Vietoris–Rips complex
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};r)
will have all possible edges
except
that each vertex will not be connected to its “antipodes” (the vertices at distance
r
):

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
r=\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})-1
.
Then in the 1-skeleton of
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};r)
, each vertex is connected to all but

•

exactly one vertex, if
n
is even,

•

exactly four vertices, if
n
is odd.

Proof.

First note that in the cycle graph
C_{n}
, given a vertex
u
, there is exactly one antipode of
u
in
C_{n}
, if
n
is even, and exactly two antipodes of
u
in
C_{n}
is
n
is odd.
This implies that given any vertex
(u,u^{\prime})
in
T_{n,n}
there is exactly one (four) other vertex (vertices) at distance
\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})
from
(u,u^{\prime})
if
n
is even (odd).
∎

The previous lemma motivates the following definition.
We say that a graph is
antipode
if every vertex is connected to all but exactly one vertex.
For example, the 1-skeleton of
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})-1)
is an antipode graph when
n
is even.
Note that from the definition, the size of the vertex set of an antipode graph must be even.

Antipode graphs are a special case of the
Turán
graphs
T(m,l)
(see
[
43
]
): a vertex set of size
m
is partitioned into
l
many subsets, with sizes as equal as possible, and then connecting two vertices by an edge if and only if they belong to different subsets.
Hence, an antipode graph is isomorphic equivalent to
T(2m,m)
for some
m
.

We recall that the
clique complex

\mathrm{Cl}(G)
of a graph
G
is the simplicial complex with vertex set
V(G)
, and with a finite subset
\sigma
as a simplex
\sigma
if and only if
\sigma
induces a complete subgraph of
G
.

Proposition 6.2
.

The 1-skeleton of an
n
-dimensional cross-polytope is an antipode graph, in fact of type
T(2n,n)
.
Conversely, the clique complex of an antipode graph of
2n
vertices is toplogically homeomorphic to the boundary of the
n
-dimensional cross-polytope.

Proof.

For the first part, since the
n
-dimensional cross-polytope is the convex hull of the vertex set
\{\pm\ \hat{e}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~1\leq k\leq n\}
, this implies that in its 1-skeleton, each vertex
\hat{e}_{k}
is connected to all vertices except
-\hat{e}_{k}
.

Now we prove the second part.
Note that the boundary of the cross-polytope is a simplicial complex that contains a subset
\sigma
of
\{\pm\ \hat{e}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~1\leq k\leq n\}
as a simplex if and only if
\{\hat{e}_{k},-\hat{e}_{k}\}\nsubseteq\sigma
for all
k
.
The same is true for the clique complex of an antipode graph with
2n
vertices (equivalently, for the Turán graph
T(2n,n)
).
Indeed, if we let
\{\pm v_{1},\ldots,\pm v_{n}\}
be the vertex set of an antipode graph with
2n
vertices, where each vertex
v_{k}
is connected to all other vertices besides
-v_{k}
, then a subset
\sigma
of
\{\pm v_{1},\ldots,\pm v_{n}\}
is a simplex in the clique complex of the antipode graph if and only if
\{v_{k},-v_{k}\}\nsubseteq\sigma
for all
k
.
So, we can obtain a homeomorphism from the boundary of the
n
-dimesnional cross-polytope to the clique complex of an antipode graph with
2n
vertices by mapping each vertex
\hat{e}_{k}
to
v_{k}
, and then extending linearly to simplices.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
n
be even.
Then
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})-1)\simeq S^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}-1}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, the 1-skeleton of
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})-1)
is an antipode graph, indeed of type
T(n^{2},\frac{n^{2}}{2})
.
Also note that
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})-1)
coincides with the clique complex of its 1-skeleton.
Hence by Proposition
6.2
,
\mathrm{VR}(T_{n,n};\mathrm{diam}(T_{n,n})-1)
is homeomorphic to the boundary of the
\frac{n^{2}}{2}
-dimensional cross-polytope, which is homeomorphic to
S^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}-1}
.
∎

It follows from the Hurewicz and Whitehead theorems that we have a homotopy equivalence

This is given by Proposition 4C.1 of

Since we have used Polymake to determine the integral homology of

\mathrm{VR}(T_{7,7};4)

We prove simple connectedness using the appendix by Barmak in Farber’s paper

Recall that a nonempty topological space

Barmak proves that if a simplicial complex is

If any

See

Taking
