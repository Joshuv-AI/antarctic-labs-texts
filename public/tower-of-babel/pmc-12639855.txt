Implementing Clinical Audit in a Aesource-Limited Setting; Pediatricians Perceptions and Perspectives
Authors: Muaath Ahmed Mohammed, Rayan Sharief Shaibo, Salma Shakir Hassan, Elmuntasir Taha Mohamed Salah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical audit (CA) is a quality improvement process aiming to systematically assess and improve patient care. It is among the seven pillars of clinical governance and an important tool for quality improvement in clinical practice. This study aimed to assess the perceptions and perspectives of pediatricians working in resource-limited settings toward clinical audit.MethodsThis cross-sectional facility-based survey included 141 pediatricians. The data were gathered using a questionnaire covering participants knowledge, attitudes, and practices about clinical audits. The questionnaire’s internal reliability was good (0.853). The chi-square test, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regressions were used to evaluate the factors associated with the research domains.ResultsAmong participants, there is an overall knowledge gap (score of 62.2 ± 24.5), positive attitude (score of 75.4 ± 2), and insufficient practice (score of 40.7 ± 41.1) towards different CA aspects evaluated. Additionally, significant statistical differences were observed between the attitudes of the participants and their duration of experience (p = 0.024), seniority (p = 0.034), and knowledge (p = 0.043) levels, and between their practice and knowledge levels (p < 0.001). Furthermore, a strong positive correlation was found between knowledge level and attitude (r = 0.233, p = 0.005), practice (r = 0.328, p < 0.001), and seniority (r = 0.180, P = 0.033) levels. The participants duration of experience and knowledge levels were significant predictors of their attitude and practice levels (p = 0.045, p < 0.001), respectively. About 88 (62.4%) participants have a below than average competency in CA. In addition, 137 (97.2%) participants believed they required professional development activities.ConclusionAlthough pediatricians have a positive attitude, their knowledge and practice levels seem insufficient. Additionally, participants knowledge levels about CA positively impact their attitudes and can predict their practice levels. Thus, future educational programs are required to guarantee that pediatricians are competent enough to participate in clinical audit projects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13692-8.

Introduction

Clinical audit (CA) is a quality improvement tool that systematically examines the structure, process, and outcome of healthcare according to explicit criteria leading to improvement of care and outcome of the patients. Changes can be made at the individual, team, local, and national levels. Careful evaluation and monitoring and of CA implementation and reaudit are essential to ensure the sustainability of improvements in the provision of health services [1].

Unlike in clinical research, in CA we continuously follow the best available practice rather than making it, we based on standards rather than hypotheses and we seek to improve fragile healthcare systems rather than disgraceful or faulty workers. Starting with project planning and determination of a topic of interest, the clinical audit methodology includes continuous and highly coordinated steps (Fig. 1). This methodology applied ethical principles corresponding to all stages of the CA (autonomy, confidentiality, beneficence, data protection, nonmaleficence, and justice). Institutional leadership, support, facilitation, programs, policies, strategies, and the maintenance of a cultural change are also important for ensuring the effective implementation of CA projects [2].

Physicians, particularly trainees in different medical specialties and subspecialties programs, should participate in various clinical audit projects, as they are involved in an enormous amount of work and ensure the greatness of care given in their workplace. Physicians from resource-limited countries are expected to be more enthusiastic due to healthcaresystem vulnerability and clinical ownership [3].

Clinical audit practice in Sudan is limited and personalized, and it is not a requirement in the majority of private or public institutions [4–6]. This puts the national educational and health regulatory agencies in charge of residents and fellows training programs in the spotlight, encouraging them to implement quality improvement measures to develop these programs and, ultimately, enhance the national healthcare system. There are also many obstacles to conducting clinical audits in Sudan and other developing countries, namely, the lack of cycles (support, leadership, reliable data, knowledge, best practices, change culture, and benchmarking) that should be examined and addressed to ensure the success of the implemented projects.

In health care, knowledge, attitudes, and practices surveys are necessary because ignorance is not the only reason for health care deficiencies but also the inability to apply the acquired knowledge [7]. This study aimed to assess the perceptions and perspectives of pediatricians working in a resource-limited setting (Sudan) toward clinical audit.

Materials and methods

Study design and duration

A facility-based cross-sectional survey was carried out between August and September 2023.

Study area

The study was conducted at the Pediatric Council-Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB). The SMSB is the only government postgraduate medical training institution in the Republic of Sudan. The SMSB has many specialties and sub-specialties councils. The Pediatric Council includes approximately 700 registered residents enrolled in a four-year pediatric residency training program (R1-R4). The trainees were awarded a certificate of clinical Medical Doctorate (MD) in Pediatrics and Child Health after fulfilling all the requirements. The MD holder is qualified as a general pediatric specialist.

Study population

All pediatric residents and specialists who were enrolled in a four-year pediatric residency training program or who graduated within the previous four years.

Eligibility criteria

The study included all pediatric residents and specialists who consented and were willing to participate in the study. Pediatricians on annual or sick leave as well as female physicians on maternity leave were excluded.

Sample size determination

According to the data obtained from the secretary’s office of pediatric council at SMSB potential subjects of 1200 pediatricians were determined as the study population. An actual sample size of 291 pediatricians was identified from this finite number of the study population using the Krejcie and Morgan Table [8]. Since the obtained actual sample size was less than 10,000, it was further reduced to 234 participants using a statistical formula; (n=\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{n0}{1+n0/N}$$\end{document}), where n is the reduced sample size, n0 is the actual sample size determined, and N is the number of the finite study population.

Sampling technique and procedure

To choose the participants, simple random sampling was used, and the participants were proportionally allocated based on seniority (junior and senior residents and specialists).

Data collection tool

Study participants completed a structured questionnaire adapted from a similar study [7]; the questionnaire was pretested and validated on thirty professionals with good internal reliability (0.853) measured in Cronbach’s alpha. Before the data collection stage, the questionnaire content was validated by the authors of this study and four consultant pediatricians and professors of medical education. The questionnaire items attempt to cover the clinical audit concept as a quality improvement process, as well as physician’s knowledge and recognition of quality system components (such as clinical governance, quality unit, governor, root cause and risk analysis, team and its importance, ethics committee, and local and national policies). The questionnaire is divided into four sections that assess participant’s knowledge, attitudes, and practice levels toward CA as well as collect their sociodemographic characteristics. We added two direct questions to the original questionnaire to subjectively classify respondent’s knowledge based on their previous participation in CA projects and to determine whether they need professional development activities or not. The questionnaire was sent in English and distributed through various emails and social media platforms.

Study variables

Pediatricians knowledge, attitudes, and practice levels are the dependent variables, while age, sex, seniority level, and duration of experience are independent variables.

Data management and analysis

The data were analyzed using version 25.0 IBM SPSS software package (IBM Corp. Published in 2017. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to verify the normality of the distribution. Continuous variables were reported as the mean and standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentages. The significance of the results obtained was evaluated at a level of 5%. The chi-square test was used for comparisons between different groups of categorical variables. Correlation (Spearman) analysis was used to assess the association between two quantitative variables. Linear regression was used to measure the relationship between the outcome variables and one or more independent variables. The operational methodology was used to classify participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and practice levels into three categories: good, moderate, and poor.

Operational definitions

Junior residents: pediatric trainees in their first and second years of the residency training program.

Senior residents: pediatric trainees in their third and fourth years of the residency training program.

Specialists: pediatricians completed their residency training and were awarded an MD certificate in the past four years.

Good knowledge, attitudes, and practices: Participants scored above or equal to the average score (80%) on a domain-related question.

Moderate knowledge, attitude, and practice: participants scored above or equal to the average score (60–79%) on a domain-related question.

Poor knowledge, attitude, and practice: participants scored below the average score (60%) on a domain-related question.

Above average, average, and below average competency: subjective classification of the study participants according to their previous enrolment in more than one, at least one, or no enrolment in CA projects, respectively.

Results

Participants characteristics

The study enrolled 141 pediatricians, with a 60% response rate. The mean age was 33.65 ± 6.25 years, and the female-to-male ratio was 5:1. Majority of the participants were senior professionals and they had work experience of 6 years or more (Table 1).

Pediatricians knowledge toward clinical audit

Physician’s knowledge about clinical audit has shown that more than two-thirds of doctors know the concept of clinical audit, including their cycle, as an evaluation of the frequency and volume of services, as an evaluation of the risks associated with health care provision, and as an evaluation of the effectiveness of interventions during health care. More than one-third of the participants were familiar with the hospital clinical audit structure and policy and departmental audit teams. The overall knowledge score was 62.15% (Table 2).

Pediatricians attitudes towards clinical audit

The majority of respondents strongly agreed that clinical audits would improve patient care, improve patient satisfaction, encourage efficient use of resources, improve patient care knowledge, increase job satisfaction, and improve teamwork. More than half of the respondents also agreed that clinical audit diminished physician’s responsibility and commitment to their patient’s care (clinical ownership), raised suspicions within the medical practice at all levels including consultants (instigates hierarchical suspicion), strained professional relationships (creates professional discord), and impeded individualized patient care. Similarly, most respondents strongly endorsed a clear CA policy in hospitals, a central CA coordination structure, and a clinical department with a CA team/committee. Participants also believe that doctors should not be alone, that nonmedical personnel may be part of the audit team, and that CA may be part of the annual continuous medical education (CME) credits and normal practice for physicians. The overall percentage of attitude scores is 75.37% (Supplementary file 1: Physicians attitudes towards clinical audit).

Pediatricians practice of clinical audit

In practice, only 47 (33.3%) physicians have received clinical audit training, usually in terms of data collection, identification of audit needs, methodology definition, data analysis, standard establishment, action planning, and change implementation. Mortality audits were the most common form in which CA doctors participated, and they participated when needed. The overall percentage of practice scores was 40.66% (Table 3).

The statistical associations between the study variables

The chi-square test revealed a statistically significant difference between participants attitudes and their duration of experience (p value = 0.024MC) and seniority level (p value = 0.034MC). In addition, there was a significant difference between knowledge level and their attitudes (p value = 0.043MC) and practice (p value = < 0.001) levels. However, no significant difference was found between knowledge and practice levels and other characteristics of the participants (Supplementary file 2: Relationships between participant’s characteristics and study domains).

Correlation (Spearman) Analysis showed a significant positive correlation between participants knowledge level and their attitude (r = 0.233, p = 0.005), practice (r = 0.328, p = 0.001), and seniority levels (r = 0.180, p = 0.033).

Multiple linear regression was used to evaluate the contributions of independent variables in predicting study domains. The duration of a doctors experience is positively associated with their attitudes, meaning that each year of experience increases, the attitude score increases 2.37 times. In addition, knowledge levels are positively associated with practice levels, meaning that every 1 unit of increase in knowledge was associated with a 20.26 increase in practice (Table 4).

Possession of clinical audit competency

Subjectively respondents competency in CA was classified based on their participation in real clinical audit projects. Accordingly, 88 (62.4%) have below average, 46 (32.6%) have an average and 7 (5%) have above average competency. In addition, 137 (97.2%) participants believed that they need professional development activities (courses, workshops, programs) for clinical audit.

Discussion

Our study findings showed that participants had moderate knowledge, positive attitudes, and poor practice about the different aspects of the clinical audit evaluated. A significant difference was observed between the practice and knowledge levels of the participants. The participants duration of experience and knowledge levels were significant predictors of their attitude and practice levels, respectively. Most pediatricians report that their enrolment in CA activities was below the average. These findings require all shareholders efforts to develop, implement, and maintain many professional development and quality improvement projects, such as clinical audit projects, to improve and optimize the delivery of services and strengthen the already fragile national healthcare system [9].

The knowledge of pediatricians about various aspects of CA was evaluated. Most doctors had generally poor or moderate knowledge of different CA areas evaluated. This result supports the findings of Cheater and Keane et al. who report that; CA knowledge is scarce among medical professionals, which limits its use in daily practice [10]. Additionally, this result is different from that reported by Al-Baho et al.; most residents of family medicine have a strong understanding of CA [11]. In addition, our study findings have shown that the concept and process of CA, including its steps, are well-known to more than a third of the participants. This is unlike the findings of Alinnor et al., who documented that; although most participants had general knowledge about CA, a few of them had reported the steps of the clinical audit cycle [7].

The assessment of the attitudes of pediatricians towards CA revealed that; most doctors generally had a positive attitude towards different areas of CA. This result was similar to that reported by Waine et al., who reported that; veterinarians had a positive attitude towards CA and most veterinary surgeons believed that CA helped them meet clinical standards and develop their careers [12]. Additionally, our study reported that; many participants agreed clinical audit reduces doctors responsibility and commitment to their patient’s care (clinical ownership), raises suspicions within medical practice at all levels including consultants (instigates hierarchical suspicion), strained professional relationships (creates professional discord), and impeded individualized patient care. These results were consistent with the findings of Johnston et al. [13], who reviewed 93 articles and reported that reduced clinical ownership, restrictions on clinical freedom, and fear of litigation were perceived as CA disadvantages. Moreover, they found that better communication and job satisfaction are potential benefits of participation in CA, which is similar to that reported in our study.

The assessment of pediatricians practice in CA revealed that; a majority of participants had poor practice toward different areas of CA evaluated. A significant statistical difference was found between doctors knowledge and their attitudes, practice, and seniority levels. These findings suggest that implementing more CA theoretical (courses, workshops, and mentorships) and practical (sessions, programs, and direct supervision) activities will be extremely helpful in strengthening pediatricians knowledge, attitudes, and practice toward CA.

The strengths of our study include that; it explores the knowledge, attitudes, and practice of Sudanese pediatricians toward different aspects of CA. Furthermore, this study is the first study in Sudan that evaluates pediatricians prior need for professional development activities in CA. However, our study has certain limitations. First, the generality of the study results may be limited by the relatively small sample size. Additionally, as this study involved only pediatricians, the findings may not be generalizable to physicians from other medical specialties. Furthermore, recall and response bias limit causal inferences from such cross-sectional surveys. The results of this study can serve as a useful guide for directors of the Pediatric Council at SMSB to develop plans and make effective decisions to improve the quality of pediatric residency training program through the adoption of CA projects as partial fulfillment of the requirements for the MD certificate awarded by the Board.

Conclusions

Overall, Sudanese pediatricians have a positive attitude toward clinical audits, but this hasn’t translated into engagement in actual CA activities. As a result, we can anticipate that pediatricians will welcome more professional development activities related to clinical audits. Furthermore, because most pediatricians were unfamiliar with CA, modifying teaching methods will be a helpful strategy for improving the situation.
