Emicizumab enables rapid tolerance, reducing bleeding during low-dose ITI in pediatric hemophilia A
Authors: Carolina Costa-Lima, Vanessa B Faiotto, Ana P Francisco, Nívia M Foschi, Samuel S Medina, Marina P Colella, Silmara A Lima Montalvão, Gabriela G Yamaguti-Hayakawa, Jéssica O Frade-Guanaes, Margareth C Ozelo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•LD-ITI+EMI accelerates tolerance by reducing bleeding without increasing direct costs.•Bleeding during ITI is associated with delayed achievement of immune tolerance.

Introduction

Neutralizing alloantibodies (inhibitors) against factor VIII (FVIII) remain the most serious complication of replacement therapy in hemophilia A because they compromise treatment efficacy. These inhibitors develop in ∼25% to 30% of patients with severe hemophilia A receiving replacement therapy, most commonly within the first 20 to 50 exposure days.1,2 Before the introduction of emicizumab, bleeding prevention in patients with hemophilia A with inhibitors relied on prophylaxis with bypassing agents (BPAs), which reduced bleeding rates by up to no more than 62%, with a substantial treatment burden.3,4

Immune tolerance induction (ITI) remains the only proven strategy for inhibitor eradication. The International ITI study demonstrated comparable success rates between high-dose and low-dose regimens. However, low-dose ITI (LD-ITI) was associated with a longer time to tolerance and a significantly higher bleeding rate.5 In Brazil, a national ITI protocol has been implemented for over a decade, achieving an overall success rate of 65.5%, although bleeding remains a persistent challenge.6

The advent of emicizumab has transformed the management of hemophilia A with inhibitors by markedly reducing bleeding rates.7,8 In addition, it enables ITI to be conducted under improved hemostatic control. Early reports of concomitant ITI and emicizumab suggest that this approach is feasible and safe, with successful inhibitor eradication and minimal bleeding,9,10 although comparative data remain limited.11 Despite this emerging clinical experience, whether emicizumab meaningfully modifies tolerance acquisition during LD-ITI and whether such modification translates into differences in bleeding burden and treatment exposure remain unknown.

We therefore conducted a single-center cohort study with historical controls to assess whether LD-ITI combined with emicizumab prophylaxis improves tolerance acquisition, bleeding control, and treatment-related resource use in pediatric patients with severe hemophilia A and high-responding inhibitors undergoing their first ITI attempt.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center cohort study with historical controls was conducted at the Hemophilia Comprehensive Care Center of Hemocentro UNICAMP (Campinas, Brazil). The study evaluated clinical outcomes, treatment exposure, and exploratory laboratory analyses in pediatric patients with severe hemophilia A and high-responding inhibitors undergoing LD-ITI, with or without emicizumab prophylaxis. Patients were consecutively enrolled into 2 temporally defined cohorts: a historical cohort treated with LD-ITI alone between January 2010 and December 2020 (LD-ITI) and a prospective cohort treated with LD-ITI combined with emicizumab prophylaxis between January 2021 and December 2023 (LD-ITI+EMI). All consecutive patients who fulfilled the eligibility criteria were included. Both cohorts were managed under standardized institutional protocols with uniform clinical oversight.

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (CAAE 38058320.3.0000.5404) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

The study comprised male pediatric patients aged <14 years with severe hemophilia A (FVIII <1 IU/mL) and high-responding inhibitors (peak historical titer of ≥5 Bethesda units per mL [BU/mL]). Adult patients treated during the same study periods were not included in this analysis. Only patients undergoing their first ITI attempt with a low-dose FVIII regimen were included. Patients were required to have adequate hematological, hepatic, and renal function per institutional protocols, with no clinically significant organ dysfunction that would preclude initiation of ITI.

Treatment protocol

All patients received LD-ITI consisting of 50 IU/kg administered 3 times weekly using recombinant FVIII (rFVIII; Advate; Takeda) or plasma-derived FVIII (pdFVIII) concentrates. Dose escalation to 100 IU/kg 3 times weekly (maximum 100 IU/kg per day) was permitted if the inhibitor titer failed to decrease by ≥20% over any 6-month period after the initial 3 months of ITI, in accordance with the Brazilian ITI protocol.

Patients in the prospective cohort additionally received emicizumab prophylaxis, initiated with a loading dose of 3 mg/kg once weekly for 4 weeks, followed by a maintenance dose of 1.5 mg/kg once weekly throughout the ITI period. Weight-based dosing was administered without rounding. See supplemental Figure 1 for a detailed study design.

Upon achievement of ITI success, patients in the prospective LD-ITI+EMI cohort continued emicizumab prophylaxis for an additional 12 months. During this follow-up phase, FVIII infusions were reduced to 25 IU/kg once weekly. In contrast, patients in the historical LD-ITI cohort who achieved ITI success transitioned to individualized FVIII prophylaxis tailored according to pharmacokinetic parameters after ITI completion.

Bleeding events were managed with a hemostatic agent selected based on the patient’s inhibitor titer and standard clinical practice. In patients receiving emicizumab, breakthrough bleeds were typically treated with activated rFVII.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients and the time to achieve complete ITI success. Secondary outcomes included bleeding episodes during ITI and safety. Exploratory outcomes comprised treatment exposure, inflammatory marker profiling during the ITI period, and cost analysis.

ITI outcome definitions

Complete ITI success was defined by fulfillment of 3 criteria: (1) inhibitor negativity (titer of <0.6 BU/mL) confirmed on 2 consecutive monthly measurements using the Nijmegen-modified Bethesda assay, according to laboratory standardization,12 with residual FVIII consistently quantified using a chromogenic assay; (2) FVIII recovery of ≥66% of the expected level, measured 15 to 30 minutes after infusion; and (3) FVIII half-life of >6 hours after a washout period of ≥72 hours. Partial ITI response was defined as sustained inhibitor negativity without full normalization of FVIII recovery and/or FVIII half-life. ITI failure was defined as the inability to achieve ITI success after 33 months of treatment.

Time to ITI success was defined as the interval from ITI initiation to fulfillment of all success criteria. Time to inhibitor negativity was defined as the time from ITI initiation to the first inhibitor titer of <0.6 BU/mL, once confirmed by a second consecutive inhibitor-negative result.

Bleeding assessment

Bleeding outcomes were assessed using the annualized treated bleeding rate (ABR) and the annualized treated joint bleeding rate (AJBR). Bleeding events occurring at the same anatomical site within 72 hours were considered a single episode. Bleeding rates were calculated for the 12 months preceding ITI initiation and during the ITI period. Data were prospectively recorded in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort and extracted from structured clinical records in the historical cohort (LD-ITI).

Safety assessment

Safety outcomes included adverse events; serious adverse events; and adverse events of special interest, which were defined as any thrombotic complications, thrombotic microangiopathy, catheter-related complications, and the development of anti-emicizumab antibodies, if suggested by loss of clinical efficacy. Detailed adverse event data were available for the prospective cohort. For the historical cohort (LD-ITI), only serious adverse events were systematically captured because of retrospective data collection.

F8 genotype and inhibitor development risk

F8 gene variants were classified as high or low risk for inhibitor development according to PedNet Registry criteria.13 High-risk F8 variants include large deletions, nonsense mutations, and intron inversions. Low-risk F8 variants included missense mutations, small insertions/deletions, and splice-site mutations.

Treatment exposure and costs analysis

Exposure to hemostatic products, such as FVIII, BPAs, and emicizumab during the 12 months before ITI initiation and throughout the ITI period was obtained from clinical records, institutional pharmacy databases, and the national WebCoagulopatias platform. Direct treatment costs were estimated using unit prices from the Brazilian Ministry of Health’s last acquisition in 2025 and were reported in US dollars (USD) as the median per-patient cost over the ITI period.

Exploratory inflammatory and endothelial activation markers

Plasma inflammatory and endothelial activation markers were quantified using a magnetic bead–based multiplex immunoassay kit (ProcartaPlex; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes and centrifuged at 3500g for 15 minutes at 4°C within 2 hours of collection. Plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis. Samples were thawed only once before the analysis. The assay was performed as an exploratory analysis of cytokines involved in systemic inflammatory response, including granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, interferon alfa, interferon gamma, interleukin-1α (IL-1α), IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-17A, tumor necrosis factor α, CXCL10, and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1, as well as markers of endothelial activation, including E-selectin (CD62E), P-selectin (CD62P), and ICAM-1. Analyses included exploratory comparisons between the LD-ITI and LD-ITI+EMI cohorts during ITI. Given the nonstandardized sampling schedule, cytokine results were interpreted descriptively and with caution.

Statistical analysis

Because of the small sample size, inferential analyses for the primary success outcome are presented to characterize the observed associations but should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than confirmatory.

Continuous variables were summarized as medians (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons were performed using the Fisher exact test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Time-to-event analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test and the Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test to account for potential early differences in response patterns.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to explore associations between clinical and treatment-related variables and time to ITI success. Covariates were selected a priori based on known prognostic factors from previous ITI studies and included treatment regimen (LD-ITI vs LD-ITI+EMI), age at ITI initiation, inhibitor titer at ITI initiation, historical peak inhibitor titer, F8 mutation risk category (high-risk vs low-risk), family history of inhibitors, and bleeding episodes during ITI (ABR and AJBR). Given the limited number of events (n = 12), multivariable modeling was not performed to avoid overfitting.

Two-sided P values and false discovery rate–adjusted Q values were reported, with a significance threshold of P value ≤ .05 adopted for all analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0) and RStudio (version 2022.12.0).

Data management

Clinical data were systematically extracted from electronic medical records and entered into electronic case report forms using the REDCap data capture platform.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Between January 2010 and December 2023, 40 patients with hemophilia A initiated ITI at Hemocentro UNICAMP. After applying predefined eligibility criteria, 17 patients were excluded from the analysis: 9 due to age of ≥14 years at ITI initiation, 5 due to a previous ITI attempt, 1 due to receipt of a high-dose ITI regimen, 1 due to a low-responding inhibitor phenotype, and 1 due to early treatment discontinuation. The final cohort for the pediatric analysis comprised 23 patients: 14 treated with ITI without emicizumab prophylaxis (LD-ITI; 2010-2020) and 9 treated with ITI combined with emicizumab (LD-ITI+EMI; 2021-2023; Figure 1).

Historical peak inhibitor titers before ITI initiation were similar between cohorts, with 2 patients (14.3%) in the LD-ITI group and 1 patient (11.1%) in the LD-ITI+EMI group presenting titers of ≥200 BU/mL (P = .99). In contrast, the median inhibitor titers at ITI initiation were lower in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort (2.2 BU/mL [IQR, 0.9-10.2]) than in the historical LD-ITI cohort (11.9 BU/mL [IQR, 4.9-24.3]; P = .01).

When comparing the 12 months preceding ITI initiation, a period during which no patient had yet received emicizumab, the LD-ITI group had a higher median ABR (13.0 [IQR, 6.9-21.0]) than the LD-ITI+EMI group (4.0 [IQR, 2.5-10.5]; P = .02). A similar difference was observed for AJBR (6.0 [IQR, 3.7-10.5] vs 1.0 [IQR, 0.0-4.5]; P = .01), respectively (Table 1). These differences may be partly explained by the lower use of BPA prophylaxis in the LD-ITI cohort compared with the LD-ITI+EMI cohort (35.7% vs 66.7%; P = .21).

ITI outcomes

Treatment characteristics differed between cohorts beyond the use of emicizumab. In the LD-ITI cohort, FVIII product type varied with availability over the study period: 5 patients (35.7%) received pdFVIII, 7 (50.0%) received rFVIII (Advate; Takeda), and 2 (14.3%) received both products during treatment. The LD-ITI+EMI cohort, by contrast, was treated exclusively with rFVIII.

Complete ITI success was achieved in 12 of 23 (52.2%) patients overall, with a higher proportion in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort (7/9 [77.8%]) than in the historical LD-ITI cohort (5/14 [35.7%]). The difference in success proportion did not reach statistical significance (P = .09, Fisher exact test; Table 2). Four patients in the historical LD-ITI cohort and 1 in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort underwent dose escalation during ITI because of a slow decline in inhibitor levels. All 5 ultimately failed to achieve complete tolerance. Among patients who achieved complete ITI success (n = 12), the median time to success was significantly shorter in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort (9 months [IQR, 4-16]) compared with the historical LD-ITI cohort (19 months [IQR, 17-27]; P = .003, Mann-Whitney U test).

Time-to-event analysis further supported this finding, showing a higher cumulative probability of achieving complete ITI success over time among patients from the LD-ITI+EMI cohort than among those from LD-ITI cohort (Figure 2). Separation between the Kaplan-Meier curves was observed early during follow-up and persisted throughout the observation period. In univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis (supplemental Table 1), ITI treatment associated with emicizumab prophylaxis was significantly associated with faster success (hazard ratio [HR], 3.89; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.21-12.4; P = .02). Each 1-year increase in age was associated with slower tolerance (HR, 0.97 per year; 95% CI, 0.94-1.00; P = .05). ABR and AJBR during ITI were associated with delayed success (ABR: HR, 0.77 per unit increase; 95% CI, 0.60-0.98; P = .03; AJBR: HR, 0.43 per unit increase; 95% CI, 0.21-0.89; P = .02). Inhibitor titer at ITI initiation, historical peak inhibitor titer, and peak inhibitor during ITI were not significantly associated with time to ITI success. Given the limited number of events, the association between these titers and the binary success/failure outcome was not formally tested.

Median peak inhibitor titers observed during ITI were numerically lower in patients receiving LD-ITI+EMI than in the historical LD-ITI cohort (3.5 BU/mL [IQR, 1.3-62.2] vs 47.2 BU/mL [IQR, 12.2-172.8]; P = .05). Median time to inhibitor negativity (<0.6 BU/mL) did not differ significantly between cohorts (29 weeks [IQR, 2-60] vs 51 weeks [IQR, 20-97.5]; P = .40; Table 2). Among 11 patients who did not achieve complete ITI success after 33 months, inhibitor titers of <2 BU/mL were observed in 4 of 9 (44%) of the LD-ITI cohort and 2 of 2 (100%) of the LD-TI+EMI cohort, including 1 patient with titers of <1 BU/mL.

All patients with complete ITI success (n = 12) were followed up for ≥12 months after ITI completion (median, 24 months [range, 12-62]). No inhibitor relapses have been documented as of January 2026.

Bleeding outcomes

Bleeding burden during ITI differed substantially between cohorts (Figure 3). Patients in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort experienced markedly fewer bleeding events, with a median ABR of 0.0 (IQR, 0.0-0.4) compared with 5.1 (IQR, 1.9-9.0) in the historical LD-ITI cohort (P < .0001). Of 9 patients in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort, 7 (77.8%) had 0 treated bleeding episodes during ITI, whereas all 14 patients (100%) in the historical LD-ITI cohort experienced at least 1 bleeding episode (P = .0003). All patients in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort (9/9) experienced 0 joint bleeds, compared with a median AJBR of 4.5 (IQR, 2.0-7.2) in the LD-ITI cohort (P < .0001).

ABR and AJBR in the study population. (A) ABR and (B) AJBR were evaluated in the 12 months before ITI and during ITI among pediatric patients in the LD-ITI and LD-ITI+EMI cohorts. Each dot represents an individual patient. Statistical comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Among patients in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort, only 2 bleeding episodes requiring hemostatic therapy occurred in 2 patients (1 posttraumatic head injury treated with a single dose of 3 mg of activated rFVII; 1 posttraumatic head injury treated with an extra dose of rFVIII at 68.2 IU/kg).

In contrast, bleeding in the historical LD-ITI cohort was more frequent and severe despite BPA prophylaxis in 6 of 14 patients (42.9%). One patient experienced a severe bleeding episode requiring 10-day hospitalization due to difficult hemostasis after tongue laceration, necessitating transfusion of 3 units of packed red blood cells. Another patient was hospitalized for 3 days due to right-hand compartment syndrome.

Safety and tolerability

No serious adverse events were observed during ITI in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort. In the LD-ITI+EMI cohort, 28 adverse events were recorded in 8 of 9 patients (88.9%), all classified as mild (grade 1). Of these, 23 events were related to self-limited viral infections (upper respiratory tract infections and gastroenteritis); none required hospitalization or discontinuation of treatment. Notably, nearly half of viral infections (11/23 events [47.8%]) occurred in the 2 patients who did not achieve ITI success. No thrombotic events or thrombotic microangiopathy were observed. No patient showed treatment refractoriness that required assessment of emicizumab plasma concentrations or anti-emicizumab antibodies.

One episode of central venous catheter–related infection occurred in the historical LD-ITI cohort (1/14 patients [7.1%]), representing the only serious adverse event observed during ITI across both cohorts. Procedural interventions in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort included 2 central venous access device removals, all managed without bleeding complications.

Detailed adverse event data for the historical LD-ITI cohort were limited to serious adverse events only, consistent with retrospective data collection practices during the earlier study period (2010-2020). As such, direct comparison of mild adverse event rates between cohorts was not possible. No safety signals leading to modification or discontinuation of ITI were identified in either cohort.

Inflammatory and endothelial activation markers were evaluated using a multiplex Luminex assay to explore their potential influence on ITI response (Figure 4). Among the markers analyzed, only 2 showed significant differences between groups. Plasma levels of CD62E (E-selectin) were significantly higher in patients treated with LD-ITI compared with those receiving LD-ITI+ EMI (P = .001; Figure 4A). Conversely, IL-10 levels were significantly increased in the LD-ITI+EMI group compared with the LD-ITI group (P = .032; Figure 4F). Discussion

In this cohort study with historical controls, the addition of emicizumab to LD-ITI was associated with faster achievement of complete tolerance (median, 9 vs 19 months; HR, 3.89) and complete suppression of bleeding (ABR, 0.0 vs 5.1), without increasing direct treatment costs. Given the small sample size, the observed difference in complete ITI success rates (78% vs 36%) should be interpreted descriptively rather than as a formal inferential finding. However, the magnitude of the observed effect, together with the consistent and statistically significant improvements in bleeding outcomes and time to immune tolerance, suggests that emicizumab may provide a clinically meaningful benefit in supporting ITI success. A critical methodological issue in ITI studies is the definition of treatment success. We applied stringent criteria requiring sustained inhibitor negativity with normalization of FVIII recovery and half-life, consistent with the framework proposed by DiMichele,14 thereby prioritizing biological rigor over apparent response rates. Under this approach, partial responders were classified as nonresponders. When partial response was incorporated, success rates increased to 64% in the historical cohort and 100% in the emicizumab cohort. The 64% rate in the historical group aligns with previously reported outcomes of LD-ITI in a Brazilian cohort,6 supporting the validity of our control cohort. Importantly, the relative advantage associated with emicizumab persisted regardless of end point definition, reinforcing the robustness of the observed effect.

In this study, patients in the LD-ITI+EMI cohort achieved faster and more consistent ITI success with complete suppression of bleeding, whereas those receiving LD-ITI alone experienced frequent hemorrhagic events requiring bypassing therapy. These findings support a biologically plausible mechanism whereby bleeding prevention promotes immune tolerance by modulating inflammation and the context of antigen exposure. Ongoing bleeding during repeated FVIII exposure may amplify inflammatory signaling and promote inhibitor persistence rather than tolerance.15 By providing stable FVIII-independent hemostatic protection, emicizumab may enable antigen exposure under less inflammatory conditions.1,16 Consistent with this model, higher bleeding burden during ITI was independently associated with delayed tolerance in Cox analysis (ABR: HR, 0.77 per unit increase [P = .03]; AJBR: HR, 0.43 per unit increase [P = .02]). Notably, total and rFVIII consumption did not differ significantly between cohorts (P = .11 and P = .28, respectively). Although the historical cohort received additional pdFVIII because of product availability rather than clinical need, immune tolerance was achieved significantly faster in the emicizumab cohort, supporting the hypothesis that reduced inflammatory burden, rather than increased antigen exposure, underlies accelerated immune regulation.

Exploratory findings on inflammatory and endothelial activation markers may provide biologically plausible but not causal insights. Patients receiving LD-ITI exhibited higher E-selectin (CD62E) levels (P = .001), consistent with endothelial activation,17 whereas the LD-ITI+EMI cohort showed higher IL-10 levels (P = .03), suggesting a more regulatory immune profile. Experimental data indicate that endothelial-restricted FVIII expression can elicit anti-FVIII immune responses, because endothelial cells may act as auxiliary antigen-presenting cells and promote CD4+ T-cell activation.18 However, because biomarker measurements were obtained retrospectively and at nonstandardized time points, these findings cannot determine whether the observed profiles preceded ITI response, resulted from ITI initiation, reflected bleeding burden, or were related to treatment context. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a direct relationship with ITI outcome. Prospective studies with predefined longitudinal sampling are needed to determine whether bleeding-associated endothelial activation and regulatory cytokine profiles influence immune tolerance during ITI.

Early clinical experience combining ITI and emicizumab has been reported in small case series focusing on feasibility and safety. The Atlanta Protocol demonstrated inhibitor negativity in 43% of patients after 35 weeks of combined therapy, with durable suppression in 92% of responders.9,10 The Japanese AKATSUKI study confirmed safety over a median follow-up of 1079 days but did not evaluate comparative efficacy.19 The ongoing multinational MOTIVATE study was specifically designed to compare LD-ITI, ITI with emicizumab, and emicizumab prophylaxis alone across patients of any age and disease severity, but comparative results remain unpublished.11 Although these studies established that emicizumab can be safely integrated into ITI protocols, none have yet addressed whether it meaningfully modifies the kinetics of tolerance acquisition. This study provides, to our knowledge, the first quantitative evidence that emicizumab-supported LD-ITI is associated with accelerated tolerance induction in a pediatric cohort.

Beyond clinical efficacy, the absence of cost escalation addresses a critical implementation barrier. Median total direct hemostatic treatment costs during ITI were comparable between cohorts (P = .89), despite the addition of emicizumab. This likely reflects 3 compensating mechanisms: near elimination of BPA use, a shorter time to tolerance, and fewer prolonged treatment failures. FVIII consumption did not differ significantly, confirming that accelerated tolerance in the emicizumab cohort was not achieved through increased antigen exposure. These data should be interpreted as exploratory cost comparisons rather than a formal economic evaluation. Comprehensive cost-effectiveness modeling incorporating indirect costs, quality-adjusted life years, and long-term outcomes would be required to define economic value more precisely within specific health care systems.20

This study has some limitations that merit consideration. The nonrandomized design with historical controls limits causal inference. However, it is important to recognize that this study was conducted in a single center with consistent clinical practice and included patients with broadly similar ethnic and environmental backgrounds. Changes in clinical practice over time and differences in bleeding documentation between cohorts may have influenced the results. Baseline differences in inhibitor titers and pre-ITI bleeding rates likely reflect a temporal shift in clinical practice, consistent with a more cautious “wait-and-see” approach to ITI initiation in the historical cohort, rather than selection bias, because all consecutive eligible patients were included. Although inhibitor titer at ITI start was not associated with time to tolerance in Cox analysis, and data from the PedNet prospective cohort suggest that pre-ITI titer does not independently predict overall ITI success.21 However, the potential contribution of historical peak inhibitor titer to observed success rates cannot be entirely excluded. Importantly, the lack of association with time to tolerance supports a limited confounding effect on the primary outcome. Heterogeneity in FVIII products used in the historical cohort cannot be excluded as a source of residual confounding. However, evidence on the impact of concentrate type on ITI outcomes remains conflicting.22 The limited number of events precluded multivariable adjustments. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. As a single-center study, the study’s generalizability may be limited. However, uniform ITI protocols and consistent laboratory assays strengthen internal validity. Confirmation in larger prospective cohorts will be essential to determine whether the observed acceleration of tolerance translates into improved long-term outcomes and to better define patient selection for ITI in the emicizumab era.

The therapeutic landscape for hemophilia A has evolved, with nonfactor therapies capable of achieving effective bleeding control independent of inhibitor eradication. Although this has prompted reconsideration of routine ITI, inhibitor elimination remains clinically relevant in selected scenarios, including major surgery, anticipated FVIII-based hemostasis, and gene-based therapies. In this context, strategies that preserve the objective of immune tolerance while minimizing bleeding and treatment burden are particularly compelling.

In summary, in this single-center cohort study with historical controls, LD-ITI with emicizumab prophylaxis was associated with accelerated tolerance, elimination of bleeding during treatment, and no increase in overall direct costs, with a consistent benefit observed across all assessed end points. These findings support the association of emicizumab with ITI as a biologically coherent and clinically relevant strategy when inhibitor eradication is pursued in the current hemophilia therapeutic landscape.
