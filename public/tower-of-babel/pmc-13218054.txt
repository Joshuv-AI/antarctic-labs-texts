An investigation into the status of human milk feeding in very low birth weight infants: a multicenter study
Authors: Yan Wang, Lulu Zhao, Yuelan Ma, Juan Xiao, Lili Zhang, Tianchan Lyu, Hui Rong, Dan Liu, Hua Wang, Xiaochun Chen, Weiwei Gu, Qiaoling Fan, Meiyuan Huang, Yunfei Tang, Dan Li, An Qin, Xiaoxia Li, Yingzi Tang, Xiaojing Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman milk provides essential health benefits for very low birth weight infants (VLBWIs). Nevertheless, the utilization patterns of human milk in Chinese neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) remain inadequately characterized. The objective of this multicenter study was to evaluate the current status and variations in human milk feeding practices among diverse types of medical institutions, providing baseline data to support the implementation of full - process human milk feeding in NICUs.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted from July 2021 to July 2022, involving 725 VLBWIs admitted to the NICUs of 16 hospitals in China. Data on infant demographics, clinical outcomes, and detailed feeding records were systematically collected using a standardized questionnaire. A customized human milk feeding module was developed within a multicenter database platform to ensure consistent data capture. All data were entered locally by trained research staff, with subsequent centralized auditing and validation by the research team. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics and chi-square tests to evaluate differences in feeding practices across hospital categories.ResultsThe cohort consisted of 362 males (49.9%) and 363 females (50.1%), with mean gestational age 29.08 ± 1.65 weeks and birth weight 1154.50 ± 208.27 g. The overall human milk feeding rate was 52.3%, with significant disparities among hospital types: general hospitals (4.1%), children’s hospitals (74.5%), and maternal and child health hospitals (89.7%) (χ²=362.141, P < 0.001). Donor human milk was available in 7 hospitals (43.8%), with 31.9% of infants receiving it during hospitalization. Initial feeding was predominantly formula-based (64.4%), while mother’s own milk as initial feeding was uncommon (6.5%). Exclusive human milk feeding duration averaged 53.0 days (IQR 23.0-103.0), significantly longer than exclusive formula feeding (34.0 days, IQR 13.8–46.0).ConclusionsSubstantial variations exist in human milk feeding practices for VLBWIs across Chinese NICUs, with overall utilization rates lower than international benchmarks. These findings highlight the need for standardized protocols and enhanced support systems to promote human milk feeding in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-026-00841-w.Keywords: Very low birth weight infant, Human milk, Feeding, Neonatal intensive care unit, Multi-center

Background

The global incidence of very low birth weight infants (VLBWIs) continues to increase annually. These infants, born with significant prematurity and physiologically underdeveloped organ systems, are highly vulnerable to a range of severe neonatal complications, such as neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS), bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) [1]. The co-occurrence of these conditions is associated with mortality rates ranging from 30% to 70% [2, 3]. Early-stage undernutrition exacerbates risks, exerting detrimental impacts on multiple systems. Specifically, it increases the vulnerability to neurological and cardiovascular disorders, with potential consequences that may endure into adulthood [4].

In recent years, advancements in medical technology, such as sophisticated nutritional support and thermal management, have significantly improved outcomes for VLBWIs and extremely low birth weight infants [5]. Among these interventions, optimized feeding strategies have proven especially effective in supporting growth and development in this high-risk population [6]. In line with the World Health Organization’s infant feeding guidelines, which recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months followed by continued breastfeeding up to two years or beyond [7], human milk is widely recognized as the optimal nutritional source for VLBWIs. It provides a unique blend of nutrients and bioactive components that collectively support infant development and confer natural protective effects [8].

The bioactive constituents in human milk play a critical role in promoting growth and maturation in VLBWIs [9]. For instance, hormones present in human milk enhance gastrointestinal function by facilitating nutrient absorption and bilirubin excretion, thereby supporting systemic growth. Additionally, its immune-modulating factors and highly bioavailable nutrients reduce metabolic stress on immature renal and gastrointestinal systems while ensuring sufficient caloric intake [10, 11]. These components also help fortify intestinal immunity, preserve mucosal barrier integrity, and mitigate risks of bacterial translocation and intestinal inflammation [12]. Beyond its nutritional superiority, human milk offers therapeutic benefits that are particularly vital for preterm infants [13]. A growing body of evidence underscores its irreplaceable role in promoting intestinal maturation, facilitating catch-up growth, and supplying pluripotent stem cells that may aid in organ development and tissue repair [14–17]. For VLBWIs, human milk feeding is associated not only with enhanced growth and physiologic maturation but also with a reduced incidence of multiple complications [18].

When maternal milk is unavailable, pasteurized donor milk is recommended as a feeding alternative for VLBWIs [19]. While its use, either as an exclusive diet or in conjunction with maternal milk, has been shown to reduce the incidence of NEC, it does not appear to provide the broader health benefits associated with a mother’s own milk, such as a decreased risk of late - onset sepsis or improved neurodevelopmental outcomes [20]. Thus, pasteurized donor milk is often regarded as a transitional feeding option until an adequate supply of maternal milk is established.

However, despite these recognized advantages, the rate of human milk feeding remains sub - optimal. A multicenter Danish study conducted from 2016 to 2019 reported that the rates of exclusive human milk feeding at hospital discharge among preterm infants ranged from 58.1% to 66.6% [21]. Similarly, a 2018 U.S. survey indicated that over 90% of infants born before 32 weeks of gestation and weighing under 1,500 g received intensive human milk feeding support during hospitalization [22]. In contrast, human milk feeding rates among VLBWIs in China remain considerably lower, influenced by factors such as restricted family visitation in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), limited parental awareness, and maternal and infant health conditions [23]. Moreover, large-scale multicenter data on human milk feeding practices in Chinese VLBWIs are still scarce. Establishing baseline evidence is thus crucial to inform clinical guidelines and improve in-hospital nutritional outcomes, highlighting the urgent need for robust multicenter studies in this population. Since direct breastfeeding is generally not feasible for VLBWIs admitted to the NICU, this study defines human milk feeding as the provision of mother’s own milk (MOM) or donor human milk (DHM).

Participants and methods

Study design and settings

This prospective, multicenter, longitudinal observational study was conducted across sixteen tertiary hospitals in China. The participating institutions, comprising four general hospitals, seven children’s hospitals, and five maternal and child health hospitals (Supplementary Table 1), were selected based on their status as high-level medical centers. Site selection aimed to ensure geographical diversity to minimize regional bias, and to include hospitals with varying annual admission volumes of VLBW infants, ranging from several to hundreds of cases. An additional inclusion criterion was evidence of active involvement in clinical research.

Sampling and sample size

VLBWIs admitted to these institutions between July 2021 and July 2022 were included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) gestational age ≤ 32 weeks; (2) birth weight < 1500 g; (3) no contraindications to human milk feeding; (4) absence of maternal infectious or major systemic diseases; (5) ethical approval from the hospital ethics committee; and (6) informed consent obtained from patients and their families, with voluntary participation in the study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) congenital malformations; (2) severe birth asphyxia; (3) surgical interventions; (4) absence of enteral feeding during hospitalization; and (5) missing or incomplete feeding records.

Sample size evaluation as follows:

, assuming an expected human milk feeding rate of p = 50%, an allowable error of e = 5%, a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a design effect (Deff) of 1.5, and an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.02, the calculated sample size required was approximately 576. Due to the inability to control the number of VLBWs across centers, we were unable to guarantee a consistent sample size for each center.

Data collection tools and procedure

To align with the study’s objectives, the researchers conducted a literature review to develop a questionnaire specifically focused on human milk feeding practices among VLBWIs. This questionnaire included sections on the characteristics of VLBWIs and their mothers in NICUs, as well as detailed information regarding human milk feeding practices. The questionnaire was subsequently reviewed and revised by five clinical experts in neonatology and nursing.

Using the systematic platform, a database was established based on multi-center developmental supportive care. Within this database, a dedicated human milk feeding module was developed, integrating data collected from the questionnaire. Trained research staff at each participating hospital were responsible for data entry and preliminary review, following standardized training on data entry protocols and definitions. These personnel collected and entered information from their respective units. Subsequently, auditors performed an initial quality check before the data were uploaded to the multi-center research platform for further verification by the project research team.

The classification criteria were as follows:

Exclusive human milk feeding: Infants who were exclusively fed mother’s own milk and/or received donated human milk during hospitalization.

Exclusive human milk feeding: Infants who were exclusively fed mother’s own milk and/or received donated human milk during hospitalization.

Exclusive formula feeding: Infants who were exclusively fed formula milk throughout hospitalization.

Exclusive formula feeding: Infants who were exclusively fed formula milk throughout hospitalization.

Human milk + formula feeding: Infants who received a combination of human milk (from the mother or donor sources) and formula milk during hospitalization.

Human milk + formula feeding: Infants who received a combination of human milk (from the mother or donor sources) and formula milk during hospitalization.

The outcomes were defined as follows:

The human milk feeding (HMF) rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received any amount of human milk during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

The human milk feeding (HMF) rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received any amount of human milk during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

The exclusive human milk (EHM) feeding rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received exclusive human milk during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

The exclusive human milk (EHM) feeding rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received exclusive human milk during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

The exclusive formula feeding (EFF) rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received exclusive formula during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

The exclusive formula feeding (EFF) rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received exclusive formula during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

The human milk + formula feeding (Mix feeding) rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received human milk + formula during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

The human milk + formula feeding (Mix feeding) rate among VLBWIs during their stay in the NICU: (Number of VLBWIs who received human milk + formula during hospitalization / Total number of VLBWIs included) × 100%.

Data analysis

All data were exported in Excel format and analyzed using SPSS 20.0 and Medcalc (version 13.0.0.0) statistical software. For normally distributed continuous variables, results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For variables with skewed distributions, median (M) and interquartile range (P25, P75) were reported. Categorical data were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and received ethical approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (Ethics Number: 2020 − 533). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to data collection. Throughout the processes of data management, storage, analysis, and sharing, the privacy and anonymity of the research subjects were strictly protected.

Characteristics of mothers

The 725 mothers of VLBWIs had a mean age of 31.00 ± 4.98 years. Of these, 691 (95.31%) had undergone regular prenatal examinations, 196 (27.03%) had a history of medical conditions, and 682 (94.07%) had identified pregnancy-related risk factors.

Other feeding status

The total feeding duration across all infants was 38,166 days. The feeding days of 725 hospitalized infants were categorized based on the type of milk received: direct breastfeeding or expressed human milk from their biological mothers (13148 days, 34.45%), donated human milk (1660 days), a combination of maternal and donated human milk (1826 days, 4.78%), maternal human milk combined with formula (2151 days, 5.64%), donated human milk combined with formula (228 days, 5.97%), a combination of maternal human milk, donated human milk, and formula (372 days, 0.97%), as well as exclusive formula feeding (18781 days, 49.21%).

Formula feeding remained the most prevalent feeding method, accounting for the highest proportion among VLBWIs. Specifically, 31.03% of VLBWI received colostrum-based oral care, and 33.38% were fed using specialized bottles or nipples designed for premature infants. Only 74 infants were fed directly by their mothers. Furthermore, 35.17% of VLBWI underwent oral motor intervention, and 49.10% received pacifier-based feeding training. Discussion

According to a 2019 survey, China has the second highest number of preterm births globally [5]. In the present study, a total of 1,968 infants were admitted to NICUs across 16 participating hospitals, among whom 871 were VLBWIs, accounting for 44.26% of total NICU admissions. This proportion appears higher than that reported in a previous study, in which VLBWIs constituted 20% of NICU admissions [24]. The enrolled VLBWIs had a mean birth weight of 1154.50 ± 208.27 g, a mean hospital stay of 53.22 ± 24.96 days, and a mean discharge weight of 2279.43 ± 666.01 g. Notably, 85.24% of these infants survived to discharge. Compared with data from 29 hospitals in Jiangsu Province in 2018 [25], the present cohort exhibited longer hospital stays, higher discharge weights, and improved survival rates.

This study evaluated human milk feeding practices for VLBWIs in the NICUs of 16 tertiary hospitals in China. The results indicate an overall human milk feeding rate of 52.30%, which represents an improvement over earlier national data [26]. It should be noted, however, that only seven of the 16 hospitals provided access to donor human milk (DHM), while two hospitals reported no human milk use at all. These disparities may be attributed to regional differences in medical resources, hospital type, breastfeeding support systems, and clinical management protocols [27]. Clearly, systematic efforts are still needed to further promote human milk feeding among VLBWIs in China. Historical data from a 2009 national survey indicated that formula feeding accounted for 77% of feedings in Chinese NICUs, with mixed feeding comprising only 23% [28]. In contrast, a U.S. center reported a human milk feeding rate of 58.7% in 2011, which increased to 80% by 2015 [29]. By 2016, two U.S. hospitals documented rates exceeding 90% for VLBWIs and extremely low birth weight infants (ELBWIs) [30]. Although the current study reflects notable progress compared to earlier domestic rates, a considerable gap remains when compared with standards in developed regions, underscoring the need for continued quality improvement initiatives [21].

When stratifying the 16 hospitals by type, maternal and child health (MCH) hospitals had the highest human milk feeding rate (89.68%), followed by children’s hospitals (74.50%), while general hospitals had the lowest (4.07%). A chi-square test confirmed that hospitals with stronger maternal–child expertise were significantly associated with higher human milk feeding rates (P < 0.001). It is important to acknowledge that differences in patient demographics, maternal health status, and infant clinical complexity may partly explain these variations. Moreover, the availability of a donor milk bank likely contributed to higher feeding rates in certain hospitals, as DHM serves as a crucial transitional nutrition source when maternal milk is insufficient, particularly in the early postnatal period [21]. These findings suggest that expanding DHM programs could be an effective strategy to improve human milk feeding uptake, especially in settings where maternal milk availability is limited.

Substantial inter - hospital variation was observed. Four hospitals, namely two MCH hospitals and two children’s hospitals, achieved a 100% human milk feeding rate, while two general hospitals recorded 0%. In total, nine hospitals exceeded the average rate of 52.30%. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in hospital type, patient volume, feeding support policies, and clinical management approaches [31]. For instance, Hospitals 2 and 11, both large general hospitals, reported no human milk use, possibly due to high patient loads and operational barriers to implementing standardized human milk feeding protocols.

In terms of feeding patterns, exclusive human milk feeding accounted for 16.41% of the 725 VLBWI cases, exclusive formula feeding for 43.17%, and mixed feeding for 40.41%. Additionally, 31.86% of infants received DHM during their hospitalization. The high prevalence of formula and mixed feeding, coupled with a low rate of exclusive human milk feeding, indicates significant room for improvement in feeding practices across Chinese NICUs. International guidelines emphasize the importance of human milk for preterm infants, recommending the use of DHM when maternal milk is unavailable [32]. While the safety and efficacy of donor milk are well-established, with pasteurization having only minor effects on its bioactive components [33], a global shortage of validated donors persists [34]. Conversely, formula feeding poses digestive challenges because casein-derived clots are energetically costly to digest [35], highlighting the metabolic advantages of human milk. Notably, the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University demonstrated that a structured human milk promotion program could increase the human milk feeding rate from 49.9% to 84.0% over five years [31]. This success highlights the feasibility of improving feeding outcomes through targeted interventions, supporting the need for wider implementation of human milk-focused protocols and donor milk banking in NICUs across China.

Upon hospitalization, formula feeding was the most common initial nutritional source for VLBWIs on the first day, accounting for 64.41% of cases, followed by donor human milk. Only 6.48% of infants received their mother’s own milk at this stage, indicating a delayed initiation of maternal human milk feeding. Research suggests that cytokines in colostrum interact with oropharyngeal lymphoid tissue, enhancing gastrointestinal maturation and improving intestinal absorptive capacity [36]. However, only 31.03% of VLBWIs in this study received colostrum oral care during their hospitalization. Increasing this practice may help reduce feeding intolerance, accelerate the attainment of full enteral nutrition, and promote gastrointestinal function. Thus, obtaining colostrum early and encouraging mothers to initiate milk expression promptly are critical to improving human milk feeding rates. In 2016, China’s “NICU Proposal for Promoting Human Milk Feeding in Preterm Infants” recognized colostrum as a form of immunoreplacement therapy, underscoring its therapeutic value in neonatal care [37]. Clearly, colostrum oral care has evolved from a routine nursing measure to an essential medical intervention, given its unique biological properties.

This study has several limitations. While the statistical analysis of human milk feeding practices across 16 hospitals offers a clear overview, the sample sizes of VLBWIs varied substantially due to regional and institutional differences. Some hospitals contributed relatively small samples, potentially affecting the robustness of inter-hospital comparisons. Furthermore, significant heterogeneity in human milk feeding policies and support strategies across sites limits the generalizability of our findings. Future multicenter studies featuring more balanced recruitment and standardized feeding protocols are needed to validate these results.

Conclusions

This multicenter study demonstrates an improvement in the human milk feeding rate for very low birth weight infants (VLBWIs) across the 16 participating hospitals compared to earlier data from China. However, a considerable gap remains when compared with rates reported in developed countries. Significant inter-hospital variation was also observed, with general hospitals exhibiting particularly low human milk feeding rates due to various operational challenges. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to promote human milk feeding.
