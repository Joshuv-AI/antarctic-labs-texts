Functional dissection of prenyltransferases reveals roles in endocytosis and secretory vacuolar sorting in type 2-ME49 strain of Toxoplasma gondii
Authors: Qiangqiang Wang, Yuanfeng Wang, Jinghui Wang, Wenjie Tian, Naiwen Zhang, Shaojun Long, Shuai Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrenyltransferases act essential roles in the prenylation modification, which is significant for proteins, like small GTPases to execute various important activities in Toxoplasma gondii (T.gondii). The structures and partial functions of prenyltransferases (FTase, GGTase-I, and GGTase-II) in prenylation process have been dissected in T. gondii. However, the cellular effects of prenyltransferases on type 2-ME49 strain of Toxoplasma are largely unknown. To address this gap, CRISPR/Cas9-based gene-editing technology was employed to construct conditional knockdown strains of prenyltransferases in ME49 strain. Subsequent observation of ingestion ability of host cytosolic molecules (e.g, green fluorescent protein [GFP]) and status of secretory vacuolar sorting post-knockdown of prenyltransferases revealed significant findings. Our study demonstrated that degradation of FTase and GGTase-II notably affected the trafficking of endocytic GFP and vacuolar secretory trafficking to rhoptry bulb. Additionally, depletion of GGTase-II led to disordered endoplasmic reticulum and microtubules, as well as impaired gliding motility. The integrity of mitochondrion was damaged after degradation of GGTase-I. These findings underscore the critical functions of prenyltransferases in endocytosis and secretory vacuolar sorting in ME49 strain of T. gondii, thereby enhancing our understanding of prenyltransferases as potential drug targets.

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii), a significant zoonotic pathogen, belongs to the subphylum Apicomplexa and can infect approximately one third of the global population [1]. Being an obligate intracellular parasite, T. gondii actively invades host cells relying on its gliding system, primarily based on actin/myosin proteins [2]. Upon invasion, the parasitophorous vacuole membrane (PVM) is formed and reshaped, providing a secure niche for T. gondii [3]. The propagation and secretion of virulence-associated effectors (ROP and GRA) by the parasite result in varied symptoms in both humans and animals. In hosts with intact immune systems, tachyzoites are inhibited and transformed into bradyzoites, which colonize the central nervous system and persist as tissue cysts, leading to chronic infection without evident clinical manifestations. However, in immunocompromised individuals, the latent bradyzoites may reactivate into tachyzoites, causing acute and potentially fatal infections [4]. Thus, understanding parasite pathogenesis is crucial for developing drugs to combat toxoplasmosis. Among various aspects of parasite biology, post-translational modifications, such as prenylation, play a significant role in ensuring proper protein function within host cells.

Protein prenylation, first identified as an irreversible process in fungi (Rhodosporidium toruloides) in 1978, gained further attention with the discovery of lamin B as the first prenylated protein a decade later, marking the onset of intensive research into this novel lipid modification of proteins [5]. Over the past few decades, significant progress has been made in understanding prenylated proteins in parasites. In Toxoplasma, prenylated protein Rab1b has been implicated in endocytic trafficking and vacuolar sorting to the rhoptry [6,7]. The FYVE-containing coiled-coil protein (FCP), a conserved protein in Plasmodium and Toxoplasma, relies on prenylation for proper localization to the unique compartment food vacuole in Plasmodium [8]. Prenylated Rab5a has been linked to haemoglobin- containing vesicles, with its mislocalization implicated in the delayed death process of Plasmodium falciparum [9,10]. Another prenylated protein, Rab11a, facilitates vesicle recycling and transport of glideosome-associated protein 45 (GAP45, an inner membrane complex protein) [10,11]. Prenylated HSP40 enhances thermotolerance by directing chaperone-client protein interactions [12]. In Leishmania donovani, prenylated Rap1A acts as an effector signal for the antileishmanial activity of monastrol [13]. Besides their significant roles in parasites, prenylated proteins also play essential roles in humans. For instance, prenylated 2′-5′-oligoadenylate synthetase 1 (OAS1), a double-stranded RNA sensor, confers protection against severe COVID-19 [14]. Furthermore, the GTPase activity and prenylation of human GBP1, targeting the parasitophorous vacuole membrane, can induce death in human macrophages by T. gondii [15]. The maturation of prenylated proteins involves three steps: farnesyl (C15) or geranylgeranyl group (C20) transfer to the cysteine residue at the C-terminus by prenyltransferases, followed by the release of AAX amino acids by Ras Converting Enzyme 1 (RCE1), and carboxyl methylation at the C-terminal cysteine by isoprenyl – cysteine carboxyl methyltransferase (ICMT) [16]. This study focuses on the prenyltransferase-mediated first step, crucial for the entire prenylation process.

In T. gondii, three prenyltransferases, namely FTase, GGTase-I, and GGTase-II, are present. During the prenylation process, FTase and GGTase-I, also known as CAAX prenyltransferases, recognize the CAAX motif at the C-terminus, whereas GGTase-II identifies CC or CXC residues at the C-terminus of target proteins [17]. In these motifs, “C” represents a cysteine residue, “A” symbolizes any aliphatic amino acid, and “X” denotes any amino acid. Active FTase, GGTase-I, and GGTase-II exhibit very similar topologies, existing as heterodimers composed of α and β subunits. Moreover, the α subunits of FTase and GGTase-I are typically identical [18]. Based on the structural characteristics of prenyltransferases, inhibitors have been developed to target these enzymes for the prevention of protein prenylation. In Toxoplasma, the synthetic peptide KTSCVIA has been identified as a specific inhibitor of farnesyltransferase [19]. Similarly, in Plasmodium, thiaplidiaquinones A and B have been recognized as novel inhibitors of the parasite’s farnesyltransferase [20]. Despite some progress in understanding prenyltransferases in parasites, the cellular functions of these enzymes remain poorly investigated in T. gondii, particularly in the type-2 ME49 strain. Further research in this area is warranted to elucidate the roles of prenyltransferases in T. gondii pathogenesis.

To address this issue, conditional knockdown strains of prenyltransferases (FTase, GGTase-I, and GGTase-II) were initially generated in the ME49 strain of T. gondii. The effects on growth phenotypes and parasite organelles were analyzed. Additionally, further functional analyses of prenyltransferases were conducted, including assessment of GFP transportation abilities and parasite motility post-depletion of prenyltransferases. Our findings revealed that vacuolar sorting to the rhoptry was severely impaired, and endocytic trafficking of GFP was significantly affected following degradation of FTase and GGTase-II. Moreover, knockdown of GGTase-I and GGTase-II notably hindered parasite motility and proliferation. This study represents the comprehensive investigation into the cellular functions of prenyltransferases in the ME49 strain of T. gondii, contributing to our understanding of their roles in chronic parasite infection, such as nutrient salvage and membrane trafficking in organelle biogenesis.

Material & methods

Parasites, plasmids, cells, and antibodies

The ME49/TIR1 strain and major plasmids pSAG1:CAS9-U6, pLinker-6 hA-DHFR, and pLinker-AID-6Ty-DHFR were obtained from Prof. Shaojun Long. Human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and HFF cells expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) were obtained from Prof. Shaojun Long’s lab. Additionally, Prof. Shaojun Long generously provided several essential antibodies used in this study, including murine ty monoclonal antibody, anti-rabbit GAP45, anti-rabbit Cen1, anti-rabbit ACP, anti-rabbit Tubulin, anti-rabbit Bip, anti-rabbit ROP5, anti-rabbit GAP45, and anti-mouse MLC polyclonal antibodies. Details on all plasmids used in this study can be referred to Supplementary file 1: Table S1.

Construction of ME49/FTase-6 hA, ME49/GGTase-1-6 hA, and ME49/GGTase-2-6 hA strains

The genome-editing technology based on CRISPR/Cas9 was utilized to add HA tags at the C-terminus of FTase, GGTase-1, and GGTase-2 in ME49/TIR1 strains. The construction principles for parasite strains were adapted from our previous work [7]. Briefly, sgRNAs targeting the C-terminus of the three prenyltransferases were designed using the online tool E-CRISP (http://www.e-crisp.org/E$-$CRISP/designcrispr.html). The selected sgRNA sequences (20bp) were integrated into the UPRT site of the original CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid (pSAG1:Cas9-U6:sgUPRT) through homologous recombination. The resulting sgRNA plasmids were transformed into E. coli (DH5α), and monoclonal colonies were selected on ampicillin LB agar plates (100 µg/mL) before undergoing DNA sequencing for validation. Homologous fragments (6 hA-DHFR) were amplified from the plasmid pLinker-6 hA-DHFR using specific primers (refer to Supplementary file 1: Table S2). Subsequently, the prepared materials (sgRNA and homologous fragments) were electroporated into the ME49/TIR1 strain, followed by drug selection using pyrimethamine and confirmation using immunofluorescence assays with anti-HA antibodies.

Generation of conditional knockdown strains of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2

The conditional knockdown strains of prenyltransferases were constructed using the auxin-inducible degron (AID) system [21]. In this approach, the C-terminus of the target proteins was tagged with the AID element, allowing for the activation of ubiquitination upon addition of 3-indoleacetic acid (IAA). This activation triggers the degradation of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2. The process of generating the conditional knockdown strains of prenyltransferases followed a similar protocol to the construction of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2-6 hA strains, with the exception of using homologous fragments containing the AID-6TY-DHFR sequence. Further details of the parasite construction process can be found in Supplementary file 1: Table S3.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay

Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) was extensively employed in this study to examine the localization of the three prenyltransferases and their effects on different cellular organelles following depletion. The assay procedure can be summarized as follows [22]:

Freshly egressed parasites (1 × 105) were seeded onto HFF monolayers on coverslips. After 24 hours of incubation, the culture medium was removed from the plates. The plates were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Solarbio, P1110) for 12 mins, followed by permeabilization with 0.25% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, IT9100) for 15 mins and blocking with 5% BSA for 40 mins. Subsequently, the appropriate primary antibody was incubated with the cell samples for 1 h. The samples were washed five times with PBS before incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody for 30 mins. Finally, the parasite nuclei were stained with the fluorescence dye DAPI (Solarbio, C0065) for 10 mins, and the parasites were observed under a laser-scanning confocal microscope.

Western blotting assay

The degradation of proteins, including ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2 was examined by western blotting assay in this research. The main steps of this assay were described in our previous work [7]. Briefly stated, the prepared protein samples were mixed with protein loading buffer and heated in boiled water for 10 mins, followed by running in SDS-PAGE gels for electrophoresis. The proteins in gels were then transferred into the nitrocellulose filter membrane (NC membrane) (PALL 66,485) by wet transfer style under the set procedures (100 V, 90 mins). Next, the NC membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 40 mins before incubating with the related primary antibodies (murine ty monoclonal antibody and internal control antibody tubulin) for 1 h. After that, the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies (IRDye® 680CW Goat anti-Mouse IgG and IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit IgG) (LI-COR, 926–68050 and 925–32211) were prepared to tagging the target proteins indirectly for 30 mins. Lastly, the NC membranes were checked in one Bio-Rad ChemiDOC MP imaging system.

Plaque formation assay

The plaque formation assay is a crucial method for assessing the growth phenotype of parasites [23]. In brief, when the parasites egress from HFF cells (at a range of 50%-80% egress), the HFF cells are scraped and disrupted using a syringe with 21 G needles for 3–5 times. The parasites are then purified using a 4 μm filter and counted for further dilution. Approximately 600 diluted parasites are added to 16 mL of culture medium and thoroughly mixed before being transferred into the confluent HFF cells in the experimental wells (3 wells of a 6-well plate). Each experimental well receives 4 mL of culture medium containing parasites and is cultured in a CO2 cell culture incubator for 7 days. Subsequently, the culture medium in the plates is discarded, and the cells are fixed using 75% ethanol for 15 mins. Finally, the cells are stained with a 1% crystal violet staining solution (such as Solarbio, G1062) for 40 mins and then scanned using an HP-Scanjet G4050 scanner.

Parasite invasion assay

To assess the invasion ability of parasites, egressed parasites (at 50%-80% egress) were collected and diluted with DMEM to a concentration of 1 × 107/mL [24]. Then, 0.5 mL of the parasite suspension was added onto HFF monolayers with coverslips. The plate was centrifuged at 100 ×g for 2 mins and placed in a water bath at 37°C for 30 mins to facilitate invasion. Subsequently, the invasion process was halted by transferring the plate to an icebox. The culture medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed with cold PBS. The following steps involved fixation with 4% PFA for 15 mins, blocking with 5% BSA for 40 mins, tagging with the first primary antibody (anti-mouse SAG1) for 30 mins, permeabilization with 0.25% TritonX-100 for 15 mins, tagging with the second primary antibody (anti-rabbit GAP45) for 30 mins, and tagging with the secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse and Alexa Fluor 594 anti-rabbit) for 30 mins (ThermoFisher Scientific, A28175 and A-11012). Finally, DAPI staining was performed sequentially, and the parasites were observed under a laser-scanning confocal microscope. The data presented are representative of three independent experiments.

Parasite replication assay

To conduct the parasite replication assay, freshly egressed parasites were collected from HFF cells, purified, and quantified using established methods [25]. The parasite suspension, ranging from 1 × 105 to 2 × 105 parasites, was then added to HFF monolayers with coverslips and cultured in a cell incubator for 24 h. After incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the parasites were fixed with 4% PFA. Subsequently, the fixed parasites underwent the steps of IFA, where the primary antibody used was anti-rabbit GAP45 and the secondary antibody was Alexa Fluor 594 anti-rabbit. Finally, the number of parasite vacuoles containing different quantities of parasites (2, 4, 8, and 16) was determined under a laser-scanning confocal microscope. This experiment was repeated independently three times in triplicate.

Parasite egress assay

The process of parasite egress is obviously affected by the calcium signalling system inside parasites [26]. In this assay, the calcium ion channel carrier molecule A23187 (Sigma-Aldrich, C7522) was utilized to stimulate Toxoplasma to egress in short time. Briefly described, HFF monolayers with coverslips were infected with a large number of parasites (approximately 6 × 105) and placed in a cell culture incubator for 2 h. Following incubation, the culture medium was removed, and warm PBS was added to the plates to eliminate any uninvaded parasites. The plates were then returned to the cell culture incubator and cultured for an additional 34 h. After this incubation period, the culture medium was once again removed, and 0.5 mL of fresh DMEM was added to the plates. The reagent A23187 was then added to the wells and incubated for 5 mins. The reaction was terminated by adding 8% PFA (Solarbio, P1112) to the wells. Subsequently, an indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) was performed. Primary antibodies used were anti-rabbit GRA7 and anti-mouse IMC1, followed by secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 594 anti-rabbit and Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse. The assay was conducted in triplicate, with three independent experiments performed.

Parasite motility assay

The parasite movement in this study was examined by the method of static characterization of parasite movement track through SAG1 staining [27]. In brief, the volume of 500 μL BSA (50 μg/mL) was added into 24-wells plate with coverslips at 4°C for one night. Next, the extracellular buffer (EC buffer: 5 mm KCl, 142 mm NaCl, 1.8 mm CaCl2, 1 mm MgCl2, 5.6 mm D-Glucose, 25 mm HEPES, PH:7.4) was transferred into the wells of plate after removement of BSA coating buffer. The collected parasites were resuspended with EC buffer and adjusted into the concentration of 1 × 107 and about 500 μL parasite resuspension buffer was then added into the cell crawling pieces to incubate for 15 mins in one cell incubator. After that, the parasite resuspension was discarded, followed by fixation with 4% PFA and blocking with 10% FBS. The primary antibody (anti-mouse SAG1) (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA1–83499) and secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488 anti-mouse) were incubated with cell crawling pieces in sequence. Different gliding tracks of parasites were counted under a laser-scanning confocal microscope and analyzed using the ImageJ software. The individual experiment was performed three times.

Deoxycholate extraction assay

Freshly egressed parasites were transferred into the poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips and cultured in one incubator for 30 mins, followed by treating with deoxycholate (DOC, 10 mm) (Solarbio, G0165) for 25 mins at room temperature. Next, the cold methanol was used to fix the parasites for 15 mins and the normal procedure of IFA was executed, where the primary antibody was anti-rabbit tubulin and the secondary antibody was Alexa Fluor 488 anti-rabbit [28]. Data are representative of three independent experiments.

Transmission electron microscope observation

Parasites cultured on HFF cell monolayers were harvested by scraping and subsequently fixed with a solution containing 2% paraformaldehyde and 3% glutaraldehyde at 4°C overnight. The samples were then rinsed, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 50 mm phosphate buffer at 4°C for 1 hour, washed three times, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by acetone and Spurr resin for 1 hour. The specimens were embedded in Spurr resin and sectioned into 100 nm slices using a Leica ultramicrotome (EM UC7). Ultrathin sections were mounted on 100-mesh grids and examined using a JEOL transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400FLASH).

GFP transport assay

The effects on the GFP transport inside the parasite by the degradation of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2 were checked in this study, the involved method was reported in the previous research [29]. Briefly stated, the HFF cells expressed GFP (GFP-HFF) infected with parasites were treated with IAA and morpholinurea-leucine-homophenylalanine-vinyl-sulphone-phenyl (LHVS, 10 μM) (Sigma-Aldrich, SML2857) for 18 h. After that, parasites were collected from GFP-HFF cells and attached on the poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips for 30 mins, followed by fixation with 4% PFA and staining with DAPI. Lastly, parasites were observed under a laser-scanning confocal microscope and counted at least 150 for further analysis. Three independent experiments with triplicates were performed.

In vitro differentiation assay

The RPMI 1640 medium (PH 8.1) was used to induce the ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2 tachyzoites into bradyzoites under the specific culture condition (37°C, absence of CO2) [30]. The influent HFF cells were infected with parasites in DMEM with 5% FBS for 3 h, then the culture medium was removed and parasites were supplemented with fresh RPMI 1640 medium (PH 8.1) for 93 h at 37°C in the absence of CO2. Afterward, the parasites were performed with IFA steps, where the primary antibody was anti-rabbit GAP45 and the secondary antibodies were Alexa Fluor 5688 anti-rabbit and FITC-conjugated DBA (ThermoFisher Scientific, L32474). At last, parasite vacuoles stained with DBA were observed and counted under a laser-scanning confocal microscope. More than 200 vacuoles were analyzed in each replicate and three independent experiments were performed.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using GraphPad v8.0 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison tests were performed for statistical analysis. All data from the experiments described above were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences between groups were indicated as **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Identification of localization and degradation of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2

To explore the localization and effects of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2 on parasites, CRISPR/Cas9-based gene-editing strains were constructed in the ME49/TIR1 strain. Basic gene information about the three prenyltransferases was obtained from the online resource (https://toxodb.org/toxo/). Subsequently, ME49/FTase-6 hA, ME49/GGTase-1-6 hA, and ME49/GGTase-2-6 hA strains were generated to examine intracellular localization, with the principles of parasite construction illustrated in Figure 1(a). Results demonstrated that FTase was uniformly distributed in the cytoplasm of parasites, whereas GGTase-1 and GGTase-2 were localized in both cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure 1(b)). In this study, the plant auxin-inducible degron (AID) system was employed to induce degradation of the three prenyltransferases. The related model figure is displayed in Figure 1(c). Successfully, ME49/FTase-AID, ME49/GGTase-1-AID, and ME49/GGTase-2-AID strains were constructed, and the three prenyltransferases could be effectively degraded upon addition of the inducer drug IAA for 24 h (Figure 1(d–i)). The successful generation of functional parasites of the three prenyltransferases was essential for the further investigation in ME49 strains. Examination of growth phenotypes of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2

The integrated lytic cycle of parasites mainly involves invasion, replication, and egress. To assess whether the degradation of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2 affects parasite growth, a plaque assay was conducted initially. Results revealed that the formation of plaques was greatly affected in the ME49/GGTase-1 and ME49/GGTase-2 groups after addition of IAA, while plaques formed normally in the ME49/FTase group following FTase degradation (Figure 2a). Further statistical analysis demonstrated significant reductions in both plaque size and number in the ME49/GGTase-1+IAA and ME49/GGTase-2+IAA groups compared to control groups (**p < 0.01) (Figure 2b,c). Subsequently, we meticulously evaluated parasite invasion, replication, and egress abilities. Invasion ability was notably weakened in the ME49/ GGTase-1+IAA (**p < 0.01) and particularly in the ME49/GGTase-2+IAA (***p < 0.001) groups (Figure 2d). Replication ability was also dramatically attenuated in the ME49/GGTase-1+IAA and ME49/GGTase-2+IAA groups, characterized by an increase in vacuoles containing 4 parasites and a decrease in those containing 8 parasites (***p < 0.001) (Figure 2e). Furthermore, the egress ability of parasites was notably affected in the ME49/GGTase-2+IAA groups compared to the control group (***p < 0.001), whereas the egress abilities of ME49/FTase-1+IAA and ME49/GGTase-1+IAA groups remained unchanged (Figure 2f). Investigation of gliding motility after degradation of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2

In the current study, the abilities of gliding motility were assessed using SAG1 staining in ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2 groups. As depicted in Figure 3, parasites exhibited robust viability with rotating moving tracks in the ME49/FTase-1+IAA and ME49/GGTase-1+IAA groups. However, short and abnormal sliding moving tracks were observed in the ME49/GGTase-2+IAA group (Figure 3a). Subsequent analysis of the length of moving tracks revealed a significant decrease in track length in the ME49/GGTase-2+IAA group, whereas the track lengths of the ME49/FTase-1+IAA and ME49/GGTase-1+IAA groups remained unaffected compared to the control group (ME49/TIR1+IAA) (***p < 0.001) (Figure 3b). Effects on multiple organelles of parasites after degradation of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2

In this study, the phenotypes of various organelles, including the rhoptry, mitochondrion, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), microtubules, apicoplast, centrosome, inner membrane complex, and micronemes, were examined by IFA following degradation of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2. Degradation of ME49/FTase significantly resulted in rhoptry biogenesis with a non-integrated distribution of ROP5, a marker of the rhoptry bulb (Figure 4a,b). However, the degradation of ME49/FTase did not considerably affect other organelle markers, such as ACP (apicoplast), Bip (ER), Cen1 (centrosome), Hsp60 (mitochondrion), IMC1 (inner membrane complex), MIC2 (micronemes), and tubulin (microtubules) (Figure S1, S2). Similarly, degradation of ME49/GGTase-1 remarkably led to impaired rhoptry biogenesis and incomplete mitochondria, as indicated by the Hsp60 marker (Figure 4c–f). However, the depletion of ME49/GGTase-1 did not cause mislocalization of ACP, Bip, Cen1, IMC1, MIC2, or tubulin (Figure S3, S4). In contrast, degradation of ME49/GGTase-2 notably resulted in broad defects in organelles, including the rhoptry, ER (Bip), and microtubules (tubulin), exhibiting disordered and incomplete structures (Figure 4g–l). The data regarding organelles unaffected by ME49/GGTase-2 can be observed in Figure S5. Green fluorescent protein (GFP) can be endocytosed and metabolized by parasites. In the current study, human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cells expressing GFP were utilized to investigate the effects of the three prenyltransferases on the transport of endocytosed GFP. Results revealed that after degradation of ME49/FTase and ME49/GGTase-2, two types of GFP status were observed in GFP+ parasites (parasites internalizing GFP): accumulated GFP foci and diffused GFP (Figure 7a,c). In parasites with accumulated GFP, a dense green fluorescent focus was observed inside the parasite, while parasites with diffused GFP exhibited uniformly distributed green fluorescence. Conversely, only one GFP status (accumulated GFP foci) was observed in the ME49/GGTase-1+IAA group, which showed similar results to the control group (Figure 7b,d). Discussion

Post-translational modifications play essential roles in the functionality of proteins in T. gondii, and they have been extensively studied in the RH strain (type 1 strain of T. gondii), including protein lactylation [31], S-nitrosylation [32], acetylation [33], phosphorylation [34], and prenylation [35]. However, there has been limited research on post-translational modifications, particularly lipid modifications such as protein prenylation, in the type 2 ME49 strain, which is predominantly associated with chronic infection. In this study, we comprehensively investigated the cellular effects of three prenyltransferases on the ME49 strain from multiple perspectives. Firstly, we examined the localization of the three prenyltransferases in the ME49 strain. Our results showed a general cytoplasmic distribution of these enzymes, with dense distributions of ME49/GGTase-1 and ME49/GGTase-2 in the nucleus of parasite, similar to their localization in the RH strain. This suggests the possibility of multiple roles for these prenyltransferases [17]. Moreover, as for the special localization of ME49/GGTase-1 and ME49/GGTase-2, they also possibly functioned in the prenylation of vesicles which cycled between the cytoplasm and the nucleus.

Next, we investigated the growth phenotype of parasites affected by the three prenyltransferases. The plaque assay revealed that no plaque formation occurred after the degradation of ME49/GGTase-1 and ME49/GGTase-2, indicating the important roles of prenyltransferases in parasite growth. This finding aligns with previous research, such as our work on the plant-like protein phosphatase PPKL, which also caused severe defects in the lytic cycle of parasites [28]. Building upon these results, we sought to investigate which specific step in the lytic cycle of parasites was affected. Initially, we examined the invasion ability of parasites and found that degradation of ME49/GGTase-1 and ME49/GGTase-2 led to pronounced invasion deficiency compared to the control group. Except for some virulence-associated effectors, such as ROP5 and ROP18, the invasion deficiency could stem from various factors involved in the moving junction formed between parasites and host cell membranes, where key proteins like apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) and the ectodomain of rhoptry neck protein 2 (RON2) constitute the core elements [35,36]. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the exact mechanism underlying the invasion defects observed in this study. Furthermore, the replication of parasites, another crucial aspect of their lytic cycle, was impaired after degradation of ME49/GGTase-1 and ME49/GGTase-2, similar to the effects observed with the deletion of other essential proteins like AMT1 and ANT1 [22,37]. Additionally, we investigated the egress and gliding abilities of parasites and found that only degradation of ME49/GGTase-2 among the three prenyltransferases resulted in significant obstacles to egress and gliding motility. These processes are complex and involve various factors, including calcium signalling [38] and microneme proteins (perforin-like protein 1 and toxolysin 4) [39,40]. The dense granule proteins (GRA41 and GRA8) and calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPK1 and CDPK3) were also reported participating and functioning well in the egress process [41–44]. It was well recognized that gliding motility was powered by the glideosome composed of a myosin motor, glideosome-associated proteins, and actin filaments [45]. Further examination is needed to elucidate the mechanism underlying the egress and gliding obstacles induced by the degradation of ME49/GGTase-2. Overall, the growth morphologies were significantly affected by the prenyltransferases in ME49 strain, which shared similar result in RH strain (replication status) [17], suggesting the effects of prenyltransferases on parasite growth might be greatly conserved among different Toxoplasma strains. However, whether the other growth phenotypes, like invasion ability and egress ability would be affected by the RH strain in a similar trend as ME49 strain need to be further investigated.

In the present study, the IFA results showed that degradation of all three prenyltransferases resulted in difficulties in vacuolar sorting to the rhoptry, leading to the diffusion of ROP5 in the cytoplasm of parasites, which were similar with the effects of prenyltransferases on rhoptry in RH strain [17]. The obtained results indicated that the function conservation of prenyltransferases in the vesicle sorting to rhoptry between RH and ME49 strains. Compared to our previous work in RH strain, the defects of rhoptry were also further confirmed through transmission electron microscope in this study and the vesicular immature rhoptry structures were accumulated after degradation of the three prenyltransferases, which led to similar results as the depletion of VHA1 [46]. Except for the rhoptry defects, degradation of ME49/GGTase-1 also led to abnormal mitochondrial morphology, raising concerns about parasite metabolism due to the close relationship between mitochondria and cellular energy [47]. Recent studies have reported that the morphology and function of the ER can be affected by proteins such as TBC9, resulting in the formation of multi-membranous structures and large lipid droplets encircled by ER membranes [48,49]. Our study revealed significant damage to ER morphology after degradation of ME49/GGTase-2, suggesting potential impacts on other parasite structures similar to those observed with TBC9. Interestingly, the microtubules of parasites were also severely disorganized after the depletion of ME49/GGTase-2, similar to the effects observed with PPKL in our previous study [28]. Microtubules play pivotal roles in extruding the conoid and positioning the rhoptries, which are crucial for parasite motility and successful invasion [50], thus contributing to the invasion and motility defects observed upon degradation of ME49/GGTase-2. The molecule GRA7, which was reported to be associated with the maturation of cyst wall of T. gondii and play major roles in the chronic-stage cysts in vivo [51,52]. The localization of GRA7 was not significantly affected by the degradation of the three prenyltransferases, respectively, which coordinated with the cyst differentiation results in this study, where cyst formation was not affected obviously. However, in our previous study, the localization of GRA7 was mislocalized after degradation of GGT-2 in RH strain, one possible reason is that GRA7 implemented different functions in different parasite strains, which should be examined in future experiments. The above results clearly demonstrated the important roles of prenyltransferases in the maintenance of multiple organelles of parasites, which were consistent with the functions of prenylated proteins in the vesicle sorting to organelles within parasites in apicomplexan protozoa [7,10]. However, the exact prenylated proteins and the corresponding controlled prenyltransferases involved in the vesicle sorting to different organelles need to be further investigated.

In Plasmodium falciparum, disruption of the prenylation process during delayed death results in defects in haemoglobin transport, providing valuable insights into endocytic trafficking regulation in apicomplexans [10]. Building on this insight, we explored the relationship between prenylation and endocytic trafficking in the ME49 strain of T. gondii. Our results demonstrated that endocytosed GFP was mislocalized in the cytoplasm of parasites after degradation of ME49/FTase and ME49/GGTase-2, which presented the similar results in RH strain [7], indicating the important roles of these prenyltransferases in endocytic trafficking. It was noteworthy that in our previous work, the protein Rab1b was identified to function in the endocytosed vesicle transport process under the function of GGTase-2 in RH strain [7]. However, the specific molecules governed by the prenyltransferases and involved in this process require further investigation in ME49 strain. The successful establishment of an induced transformation model for cysts is crucial for obtaining bradyzoites in vitro and investigating transformation mechanisms in T. gondii [53]. Different from the RH strain, ME49 tachyzoites own the ability to be transformed into cysts under certain conditions. Hence, in this study, we utilized the alkaline culture method to assess the cyst transformation ratio of ME49/FTase, ME49/GGTase-1, and ME49/GGTase-2. Interestingly, while degradation of the three prenyltransferases did not significantly affect the cyst transformation ratio compared to the control group, the morphology of bradyzoites after depletion of ME49/GGTase-2 was notably abnormal, which was similar to the effects of PPKL on cyst transformation in our previous study [28]. The results suggests that the effects of ME49/GGTase-2 on parasite morphology may persist throughout the entire lifecycle of T. gondii.

In conclusion, our study provides insights into the detailed functions of the three prenyltransferases in the ME49 strain of T. gondii. We found that ME49/FTase and ME49/GGTase-2 play important roles in endocytic trafficking and the three prenyltransferases function in the secretory protein sorting (Figure 9), while ME49/GGTase-2 regulates growth phenotype, organelle morphology, and motility ability. These findings contribute to our understanding of prenyltransferase functions in the ME49 strain and strengthen the theoretical basis for targeting prenyltransferases as drug targets.
