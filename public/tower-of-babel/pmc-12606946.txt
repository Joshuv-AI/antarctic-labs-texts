Transcriptomic profiling reveals tissue-specific and sex-dimorphic lipid storage in Bufo gargarizans
Authors: Yimin Zhang, Xinyue Yang, Tonghang Wang, Ruinan Zhao, Yanjun Zhu, Yinmeng Hou, Zhuo Chen, Xiaohong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe evolutionary conservation of lipid storage mechanisms across metazoans is well illustrated by vertebrate white adipose tissue (WAT) and its functional counterpart, the amphibian fat body. While serving as the principal lipid reservoir, the amphibian fat body is connected to the gonads and demonstrates unique endocrine-reproductive integration, particularly through its bidirectional regulation of gonadal function and energy metabolism. However, our understanding of this organ’s cellular heterogeneity and sex-specific functional adaptations is still limited.ResultsIn this study, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the adipose tissue specialization in Bufo gargarizans, revealing sex- and depot-specific adaptations between visceral fat bodies and subcutaneous adipose tissue. Through comparative transcriptomics, we identified 1,201 visceral and 1,225 subcutaneous sex-biased differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with functional enrichment in calcium signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and vascular smooth muscle contraction. Subcutaneous depots exhibited calcium flux-driven lipogenesis regulation, where CACNA1G and NCX1 orchestrated adipocytes differentiation via PHKG1-mediated phosphorylation and NFATc1 nuclear translocation. In visceral fat bodies, sexually dimorphic metabolic strategies were prominent, females prioritized lipid storage through TCA cycle augmentation, while males upregulated Mucin-type O-glycan biosynthesis, suggesting a protective role against testicular oxidative stress. Regulatory divergence in subcutaneous adipose tissue extended to vascular dynamics, males enhanced KLF2-dependent angiogenesis via the Apelin pathway, whereas females activated MYLK3-mediated vascular permeability through tight junction remodeling.ConclusionsOur study revealed the central role of calcium signaling in adipose tissue diversification in B. gargarizans, linking tissue-biased gene networks to evolutionary adaptations. We found subcutaneous fat depots prioritized calcium-mediated adipogenesis, while visceral fat diverged into female lipid storage versus male gonadal protection via Mucin O-glycans. Our work redefines amphibian adipose systems as vertebrate lipid metabolism models, uncovers evolutionarily conserved modules in vascular regulation and stress-induced glycosylation, and elucidates mechanisms bridging adaptive energy allocation in amphibians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12221-5.Keywords: Bufo gargarizans, Fat body, Subcutaneous adipose tissue, Adaptation, Sex dimorphism, Transcriptome

Background

The evolutionary trajectory of lipid storage mechanisms reveals a conserved yet dynamically adaptive metabolic strategy across phylogenetically diverse taxa [1, 2]. From prokaryote precursors to mammalian systems, this fundamental biological process has undergone progressive specialization while maintaining its energy storage function. Vertebrates display complex anatomical innovations in lipid deposition reflecting ecological pressures and physiological demands [3–5]: teleost fishes evolved hydrodynamically optimized decentralized lipid storage across mesentery, subcutaneous, intermuscular, and neurosensory compartments [6–8]; tetrapod terrestrial transition prompted adipose tissue reorganization with amphibians developing endocrine-integrated abdominal fat bodies for reproduction, metabolic programming, and hibernation [9–11]; reptiles diversified into abdominal fat bodies or specialized depots e.g., Pygopus caudal stores, marine turtle subcarapacial adipose layers [12, 13]; while birds and mammals achieved peak specialization through functionally distinct visceral (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissues (SAT), where VAT regulating insulin sensitivity and lipid homeostasis, SAT providing thermal insulation, mechanical cushioning, and long-term energy storage [5]. This progression from diffuse lipid deposits to specialized adipose organs reveals metabolic systems’ balance of functional conservation with structural innovation.

White adipose tissue (WAT) exhibits depot-specific heterogeneity driving divergent metabolic and pathological effects. Though both mammalian SAT and VAT utilize unilocular adipocytes, advanced lineage tracing reveals specialized angiogenic, immunomodulatory, and extracellular matrix-remodeling adipocytes governing physiological processes via paracrine signaling networks [14, 15]. Microenvironmental programming creates fundamental functional differences, VAT functions as an immunometabolic center with pro-inflammatory adipokine and inflammasome-activating synaptic connections between adipocytes and immune cells (macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and mast cells) [14, 15], whereas SAT employs adipocyte-to-adipocyte gap junction signaling and anti-inflammatory annexin-mediated pathways suppressing macrophage polarization [14, 15]. These differences yield distinct pathologies during expansion. VAT hypertrophy triggers NF-κB activation and ROS mediated spermatogenesis impairment (Sertoli cell tight junctions and sperm DNA fragmentation) and leptin- resistant kisspeptin signaling and GnRH dysregulation [16, 17]. SAT hyperplasia follows an endocrine-disruptive pathway where ectopic lipid induces aromatase-driven testosterone-to-estradiol conversion, suppressing FSH/LH and causing oligospermia and anovulation via hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal axis desensitization [16, 17].

The amphibian fat body acts as a dynamic bioenergetics regulator of reproductive success, with sex-specific functions. In female Rana tigrina, nutritional deprivation or surgical ablation reduces ovarian vitellogenin synthesis, shrinks oocyte diameter, diminishes clutch size [18, 19], and impairs follicular maturation via down-regulation of steroidogenic enzymes [20, 21]. While ovogenesis persists without fat body support, it arrests at the previtellogenic stage, confirming its role in reproductive optimization rather than initiation [21]. Conversely, in male Ranidella species (R. signifera and R. parinsignifera), fat body lipid stores fuel extended lekking behavior, powering vocal sac inflation and territorial defense to enhance mating success [22]. Ablation studies reveal a dual regulatory mechanism where lipid depletion triggers testicular apoptosis via PPARγ suppression, while disrupted adipokine signaling impairs Leydig cell androgenesis, causing spermatogenic arrest at the spermatogonial stage [18]. This evolutionary adaptation positions the amphibian fat body as a key allocator of energy between survival and reproduction, with its cyclical growth and shrinkage synchronizing with seasonal breeding patterns across anurans. However, our understanding of the amphibian fat body’s cellular heterogeneity and sex-specific functional adaptations is still limited.

The Asiatic toad (Bufo gargarizans) exhibits exceptional ecological plasticity, inhabiting a broad latitudinal range (20°N to 50°N) from Pacific islands to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (elevations of 3,000–4,500 m) [23, 24], where it undergoes significant seasonal remodeling of adipose reserves [25, 26]. In this study, we report the initial discovery of subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) in the amphibian B. gargarizans. Notably, the appearance of SAT was restricted to a brief period in July, with no macroscopic evidence found in other months. Even when present, its minimal and localized nature consequently hindered reliable macroscopic visualization. Morphological comparisons and histological anatomy were conducted to determine the seasonal dynamic and sex-specific changes of B. gargarizans, and their related depot-specific adaptive strategies. In addition, comparative transcriptomics were employed to explore (1) the functional differences between visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissues, and (2) the mechanisms underlying sex-specific variations in adipose tissue organization. Our findings will provide novel insights into energy allocation strategies in B. gargarizans, the evolutionary adaptations of amphibian adipose systems, and potential mechanisms driving the adipose tissue specialization in B. gargarizans.

Results

Seasonal dynamics of subcutaneous adipose tissue and visceral fat body in B. gargarizans

Our observations revealed distinct seasonal deposition patterns in the two adipose depots of B. gargarizans (Fig. 1). Subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) showed strictly seasonal deposition limited to the inguinal region, occurring exclusively during the post-breeding period (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the visceral fat body (FB) persisted year-round but displayed significant mass fluctuations (Fig. 1B), reaching minimal levels during breeding, accumulating to peak reserves in the post-breeding phase, and displaying intermediate storage in pre-hibernation (Fig. 1E). Histological analyses identified fundamental structural divergences between SAT and the fat body. SAT adipocytes were smaller and embedded in a denser collagenous extracellular matrix compared to visceral fat body adipocytes (Fig. 1C, D). Sexual dimorphism was evident in fat body dynamics, with females showing higher fat body indices than males throughout all seasons (Fig. 1F, G).

Transcriptomic data processing and filtering

Two male groups (named FM = fat body of male; SM = subcutaneous adipose tissue of male) and two female groups (named FF = fat body of female; SF = subcutaneous adipose tissue of female) for fat body and subcutaneous fat were randomly divided and used for de novo transcriptome sequencing. Each group contained three biological replicates, with individuals randomly assigned to minimize variability and ensure statistical robustness (Supplementary Table 1). High-throughput sequencing of cDNA libraries using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform generated 554,878,532 raw paired-end reads. After rigorous quality control (adapter trimming, removal of low-quality sequences and adapter contaminants, we obtained 529.7 million high-quality clean reads across biological replicates (mean ± SD: 44.1 ± 2.8 million reads per sample; range: 39.3–47.8 million) (Supplementary Table 1). Detailed per-sample metrics including F02, F16-F19, and S02-S18 were shown in Supplementary Table 1. All libraries demonstrated excellent sequencing quality with Q30 scores ranged from 93.49% to 94.75% and GC content between 46.83% and 47.49% (Supplementary Table 1). Among the total number of clean reads from the twelve samples in this study, 93.50% to 93.94% was successfully mapped against the reference genome (Supplementary Table 1). All raw sequence reads data have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under the accession number PRJNA1269961.

Differential gene expression analysis between tissues and sexes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between experimental groups were identified using the transcripts per million fragments (TPM) normalization method to quantify transcript abundance. Volcano plot analysis revealed distinct DEG profiles: 1,201 DEGs (764 upregulated and 437 downregulated) in the FF versus SF comparison (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2), and 1,225 DEGs (759 upregulated and 466 downregulated) in the FM versus SM comparison (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 3). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs identified 22 significantly enriched pathways for FF versus SF (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 4) and 18 for FM versus SM (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 5).

Comparative analysis of subcutaneous adipose tissue (SF versus SM) identified the Apelin signaling pathway as the most significantly enriched pathway, containing the highest number of upregulated DEGs (Supplementary Table 12). Given its key role in lipid metabolism and adipocyte development, the Apelin signaling pathway functionally interacts with the Tight junction pathway, which is crucial for regulating endothelial cell dynamics. Within this interconnected network (Fig. 6), the upregulated DEGs (e.g., LOC122929309) and downregulated DEGs (e.g., MYLK3) downstream of Apelin reflect distinct regulatory patterns in SF and SM groups, respectively. Similarly, Tight junction DEGs (e.g., upregulated CGNL1 and downregulated RAP1A) showed sex-divergent responses to the Apelin signaling pathway. This coordinated pathway activation characterizes sexual dimorphism through metabolic reprogramming and modulation of cellular junction, during sex-specific adipose tissue remodeling.

Although our analysis identified the Apelin signaling pathway as the most significantly enriched pathway in the subcutaneous adipose tissue between sexes (SF versus SM), the total number of enriched pathways in this comparison was lower than in other comparative groups. This relative scarcity limits our ability to draw broader functional inferences. The co-enrichment of the Apelin signaling pathway and Tight junction pathway suggests a potential interaction in regulating sex-dimorphic vascular functions; however, the current transcriptomic data alone are insufficient to establish a direct mechanistic link or causal relationship between them. How exactly the Apelin signaling pathway influences tight junction dynamics, whether through direct transcriptional regulation of junctional components or via indirect mechanisms involving KLF2 or MLCK remains unclear. Future work employing gain-/loss-of-function experiments should be conducted to validate this putative interaction and elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms by which these pathways interconnect during sex-specific adipose tissue remodeling.

Discussion

Heterogeneity between subcutaneous adipose tissue and fat body

We identified the calcium signaling pathway as a key factor distinguishing subcutaneous adipose tissue from the fat body in B. gargarizans, as shown by the significant upregulation of calcium-related genes (CACNA1G, NCX1, ITPR3) in subcutaneous depots. While calcium signaling is known to regulate adipogenesis and lipolysis in mammals and insects [27–30], its role in amphibian adipose tissue remains poorly understood. Notably, studies in mammalian models have demonstrated a Ca2+-dependent inhibition of lipolysis [31, 32]. Our study provides the first evidence, to the best of our knowledge, that calcium signaling drives depot-specific adaptations in an anuran species, suggesting functional conservation of this regulatory mechanism across vertebrates.

In B. gargarizans SAT, we observed coordinated upregulation of key calcium signaling components (CACNA1G, NCX1, BDKRB2, PLCB4, ITPR3), suggesting enhanced Ca2⁺ influx and mobilization. This likely occurs through receptor-mediated pathways (e.g., BDKRB2 triggering PLCB4-mediated PIP₂ hydrolysis to generate IP₃, which binds ITPR3 to release stored Ca2⁺ [33–36]) and store-operated pathways (e.g., CACNA1G facilitating extracellular Ca2⁺ entry [37], and NCX1 potentially increasing cytoplasmic Ca2⁺ via reverse mode [38]). This synergistic Ca2⁺ amplification, maintained through a dynamic balance of influx and removal mechanisms [33–39], distinguishes SAT from FB. This synergistic Ca2+ amplification distinguishes subcutaneous adipose tissue from the fat body, as indicated by our integrated transcriptomic and histological analyses.

The elevated cytosolic Ca2+ acts as a critical secondary messenger in subcutaneous adipose tissue, potentially explaining the observed metabolic differences between depots. Ca2+ binding induces allosteric activation of various effectors including calmodulin and calcineurin [39–41], which subsequently regulate adipocyte differentiation and lipid metabolism. We found it associated with significant upregulation of Phosphorylase Kinase Gamma 1 (PHKG1). Ca2⁺ binding allosterically activates PhK via its calmodulin subunit [39], and PHKG1 upregulation is known to suppress adipogenesis and reduce lipid storage [42–44]-consistent with PHKG1 SNPs affecting fat deposition in pigs [45] and increased lipid deposition upon its knockdown [46]. This aligns with our observed FABP4 downregulation, suggesting PHKG1 upregulation may act as a molecular brake on adipogenesis in B. gargarizans SAT.

Furthermore, elevated cytosolic Ca2⁺ was linked to the upregulation of smooth muscle markers (MYH11 and ACTA2) and the vascular smooth muscle contraction pathway in SAT. In murine models, such perivascular MYH11+ cells constitute a progenitor reservoir for cold-induced beige adipogenesis [47, 48], a process distinct from β3-adrenergic receptor (ADRB3) agonist-induced browning [49]. Human studies show MYH11+ cells can acquire lipogenic features during differentiation [50]. The significant enrichment of these markers in B. gargarizans SAT therefore suggests a progenitor population with potential beige adipogenic capacity, which may represent an important pre-adaptation for thermal regulation in a terrestrially adapting anuran.

We also found that CACNA1G-mediated cytoplasmic Ca2+ elevation significantly upregulates NFATc1 via the MAPK signaling pathway. In resting cells, NFATc1 persists as a phosphorylated cytoplasmic latent form. Elevated Ca2+ activates calcineurin, which dephosphorylates NFATc1, triggering rapid translocation [41–53]. This Ca2+-dependent activation enhances NFATc1 DNA-binding activity, modulating proliferation and differentiation processes [54, 55]. While NFATc1 promotes adipogenesis in normal contexts, its mutated form strongly inhibits 3T3-L1 preadipocyte differentiation [56]. Conversely, Danowska et al. (2024) observed NFATc1 downregulation in human subcutaneous adipocytes under pathological conditions, coinciding with suppressed adipogenic gene expression during adipocyte differentiation [57]. Our findings suggest NFATc1 upregulation may act as a molecular brake limiting subcutaneous fat accumulation in B. gargarizans.

The seasonal expression pattern of inguinal SAT suggests its primary role in lipid storage during food-abundant periods. Contrasting with the fat body, SAT exhibits limited lipogenic and adipogenic capacities, suggesting later evolutionary differentiation. The high enrichment of MYH11 and ACTA2 in SAT indicates potential cold-responsive progenitor cells, though mature beige or brown adipocytes were undetected, requiring further experimental validation. Mechanistically, intracellular Ca2⁺ dynamics regulate key genes (PHKG1, MYH11, ACTA2, and NFATc1), potentially underpinning lipogenic and differentiation heterogeneity between subcutaneous adipose tissue and fat body in B. gargarizans. Our findings establish calcium signaling as a central regulator of adipose tissue heterogeneity in amphibians, providing insights into terrestrial adaptation in anurans. Future studies should delineate calcium signaling mechanisms in vertebrate adipose systems, particularly mammals, to determine the evolutionary conservation of these regulatory pathways and their potential relevance to metabolic diseases.

Metabolic reprogramming promotes lipid accumulation in females

In anurans, the fat body is essential for ovarian cycles, as both nutritional deprivation and fat body deficiency impair oocyte development [19–21]. We revealed sexually dimorphic fat body dynamics in B. gargarizans, with females exhibiting maximal post-reproductive lipid accumulation significantly exceeding male levels. This female-specific pattern supports reproductive strategies requiring enhanced lipid storage and metabolic flexibility for vitellogenesis energetics. KEGG pathway analysis identified two key female adipose signatures, i.e., upregulated macrophage immunomodulation pathways and enhanced tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity. This aligns with evidence that macrophage polarization requires metabolic plasticity through mitochondrial reprogramming and TCA cycle adaptations [58–61]. Specifically, female-biased expression of aspartate-metabolizing enzymes, i.e., ADSS1 (catalyzing aspartate-to-fumarate conversion) and IL4I1 (generating oxaloacetate), suggests substrate channelling to replenish TCA intermediates, potentially sustaining macrophage bioenergetics during tissue remodeling.

The NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2), a crucial effector, mediates pathogen defense via reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [62, 63]. During macrophage phagocytosis, NOX2 has dual effects on ROS production. While essential for pathogen killing, excessive ROS causes host oxidative damage. Previous evidence indicates that TCA cycle remodeling during macrophage activation regulates this balance-succinate accumulation potently increases ROS production [64]. Furthermore, TCA intermediates and derivatives indirectly regulate ROS levels via influencing enzyme activity and signaling pathways [64–67], creating reciprocal interaction that systemically coordinates macrophage immune responses with metabolic homeostasis. This regulatory mechanism likely protects B. gargarizans fat body frominflammatory damage by preventing ROS overproduction during immune activation.

Under lipid overaccumulation pathologies, adipose tissue exhibits elevated lipolysis. NOX2 has been demonstrated to drive ectopic lipid deposition, and our data showed significantly higher NOX2 expression in females than their males. We propose this elevated NOX2 expression promotes lipolysis to mobilize lipids for ectopic transport to ovaries, supporting energy-intensive vitellogenin synthesis. This likely necessitates increased lipid storage in female adipose bodies. However, the specific role of NOX2 in lipolysis and lipid transport requires further validation through functional experiments. Additionally, the elevated NOX2 expression correlates with reduced intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, potentially contributing to the adipose hypocalcemia. This finding is consistent with the tissue-specific differences in calcium signaling pathway gene expression observed in our transcriptomic data, though further validation through direct measurement of intracellular calcium concentrations is warranted.

FB establishes an immune barrier protecting testis from ROS in males

Our findings revealed a distinct sexual dimorphism in the mucin-type O-glycan biosynthesis pathway within the fat body, with males exhibiting significant enrichment. Transcriptomic profiling identified male-specific upregulation of key glycosyltransferases, ST6GALNAC2 and GALNT16, suggesting enhanced mucin glycosylation capacity. In other systems, such O-glycosylation is crucial for maintaining redox homeostasis and protecting against oxidative stress [68]. Thus, this male-biased enhancement in B. gargarizans may serve to reinforce glycocalyx integrity, potentially mitigating oxidative challenges in male-specific physiological contexts.

In contrast to females, male fat bodies exhibited significant downregulation of both NOX2 (the primary ROS generator [69]) and MPO (which produces hypochlorous acid from H2O2 [70]). This coordinated dual suppression likely establishes a potent coupled anti-inflammatory mechanism, limiting both primary ROS generation and subsequent production of damaging hypochlorous acid. This may represent an evolutionary adaptation to mitigate oxidative tissue injury specifically in male adipose tissue, potentially prioritizing gonad protection.

Given the conserved vulnerability of spermatogenesis to oxidative stress [71–76], we propose a synergistic defense system operating in male B. gargarizans. This model involves two components identified in our study: 1) enhancement of mucin-type O-glycosylation (via ST6GALNAC2/GALNT16 upregulation) potentially forming a protective barrier, and 2) coordinated downregulation of the NOX2-MPO axis to restrict endogenous ROS production. This dual strategy of exogenous barrier fortification and endogenous radical suppression likely represents an evolutionary adaptation to prioritize testicular protection and fertility preservation. Future studies should further validate the specific roles of ST6GALNAC2 and GALNT16 in the male B. gargarizans fat body through gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments in both in vivo and in vitro models. In particular, it would be important to investigate how these glycosyl enzymes affect the structural integrity of mucin-type O-glycans and how they interact with the NOX2-MPO axis to synergistically protect the testes from oxidative stress.

Vascular development and homeostasis support subcutaneous adipose tissue development

Our study provides the first integrated anatomical and transcriptomic characterization of subcutaneous adipose tissue in B. gargarizans, laying the groundwork for understanding its energy storage adaptations in anurans. KEGG pathway analysis revealed fundamental sex differences in endothelial cell regulatory networks. Among these, the Apelin signaling pathway emerged as one of the most significantly enriched pathways in the subcutaneous depot, suggesting its potential important role in angiogenic endothelial cell activation. These findings are consistent with the established roles of adipose tissue endothelial cells as adipocyte progenitors and architects of capillary networks that support tissue development and homeostasis maintenance [77, 78].

Our findings indicated that the Apelin signaling pathway was a key regulator of angiogenic endothelial cell activation. Apelin coordinates vascular development, maturation, and functional maintenance by stimulating endothelial proliferation and migration through the APJ receptor [79, 80]. Notably, we found sexually dimorphic regulation of downstream effectors. For instance, KLF2 transcription factor expression is mainly upregulated in male endothelial cells, while MYLK3 activity is significantly elevated in female counterparts. This distinct molecular signature, i.e., KLF2-mediated vascular stabilization in males and MYLK3-driven cytoskeletal remodeling in females, might explain sex-specific angiogenic responses and indicate that the apelin-APJ axis could be a key factor in sex-dependent vascular adaptation. This sex-biased expression of KLF2 (males) and MYLK3 (females) suggests distinct functional outputs promoting SAT angiogenesis: KLF2 is associated with vascular stabilization [81, 82], whereas MYLK3 regulates endothelial permeability and contractility, facilitating neovessel formation [83]. Thus, males and females appear to employ different molecular strategies within the Apelin pathway to support adipose expansion.

Both KLF2 and MYLK3 can influence tight junction (TJ) integrity [84–93]. In B. gargarizans SAT, we propose sex-specific TJ regulation downstream of the dimorphic Apelin response: males may utilize KLF2 to promote stability (e.g., via CLDN1 and CGNL1 [94, 95]), whereas females may employ MYLK3 to dynamically regulate permeability and junctions (e.g., involving ZO-1 and Rap1A [90, 96, 97]). These distinct pathways likely support sex-specific needs for vascularization during adipose expansion.

It is noteworthy that the number of differentially expressed genes annotated to the Apelin signaling pathway and Tight junction pathway in our dataset was limited. This observation is consistent with the overall results of the enrichment analysis for the subcutaneous adipose tissue comparison (SF vs SM), which yielded a smaller set of significantly enriched pathways and genes compared to other tissue comparisons in this study. This, combined with the relatively scarce research on apelin's role in adipose tissue vascularization in non-mammalian models, means our biological interpretation is focused on a core set of genes with high-confidence annotations. Future studies will be essential to fully validate and elaborate on these initial findings.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the molecular mechanisms underlying the adipose tissue heterogeneity and sexual dimorphism in B. gargarizans, revealing the pivotal roles of calcium signaling, metabolic reprogramming, and sex-specific adaptations in lipid storage and energy allocation. Comparative transcriptomics identified distinct gene expression profiles between visceral fat body and subcutaneous adipose tissue. The calcium signaling pathway is suggested as a key regulator of tissue-specific functions like lipogenesis and adipocyte differentiation. Sexual dimorphism was also seen in metabolic pathways, with females exhibiting enhanced lipid storage and TCA cycle activity, while males displayed upregulated mucin-type O-glycan biosynthesis and lower oxidative stress, likely to protect testicular function. Additionally, the apelin signaling pathway and tight junction regulation exhibited sex-biased expression patterns, further underscoring the divergent strategies for vascular development and homeostasis in subcutaneous adipose tissue. Overall, these findings advance our understanding of amphibian adipose systems, highlighting evolutionary adaptations in lipid metabolism and providing potential mechanistic insights into vertebrate adipose evolution.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal sampling and experiments were conducted in strict accordance with China’s national ethical guidelines for laboratory animals and relevant animal welfare legislation, following review and approval by the Institutional Care and Ethics Committee of Henan Normal University.

Animals and sample preparation

From February 2021 to November 2022, we collected a total of 235 adult B. gargarizans specimens (140 males: February–December; 95 females: March-November) from the Taihang Mountain National Nature Reserve in Henan Province, northern China. All specimens were randomly sampled, with body weight (g) and bilateral fat body mass (g) recorded for each individual. Adipose tissue samples were collected immediately after euthanasia. Euthanasia was humanely performed via immersion in a 1% aqueous solution of tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222, Sigma-Aldrich), ensuring loss of consciousness prior to tissue collection. Adipose tissue processing followed standardized protocols: one lateral fat body and corresponding subcutaneous fat sample were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding, sectioned into 4-μm slices, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE) for histological analysis. The contralateral tissues were flash-frozen in RNALater™ stabilization solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and stored at −80 °C for downstream molecular analyses. For RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), tissues were categorized into four experimental groups based on sex and tissue type: fat body of female (FF), fat body of male (FM), subcutaneous fat of female (SF), and subcutaneous fat of male (SM). Three biological replicates were set up for each group. The following comparisons were established: FF versus FM (with FF as the control and FM as the experimental group), SF versus SM (SF as control, SM as experimental), FF versus SF (FF as control, SF as experimental), and FM versus SM (FM as control, SM as experimental) (Supplementary Table 1).

RNA isolation, library preparation, and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from samples (n = 3 per group) using the Trizol® Reagent (Invitrogen, USA), with RNA concentration assessed via NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and integrity verifiedassessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, USA). Staring with 3 μg of high-quality RNA, sequencing libraries were constructed through the following steps: poly-A mRNA enrichment using using poly-T oligo-conjugated magnetic beads (Illumina, USA), RNA fragmentation in Illumina proprietary buffer (94 °C, 8 min) with divalent cations, followed by first-strand cDNA synthesis using random hexamers and SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and second-strand cDNA synthesis with DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. After blunt-end repair and 3′-end adenylation, Illumina TruSeq adapters were ligated, with library fragments (400–500 bp) size-selected using the AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA). Adapter-ligated DNA underwent 15 cycle PCR amplification with Illumina primers, followed by purification and quantification via Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit) and qPCR. Final paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

Quality control and reads mapping

Sequenced samples were processed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate image files, which were converted to raw FASTQ-formatted data (raw reads) using Illumina BaseSpace software (v2.6.2). Initial data quality control revealed adapter contamination and low-quality sequences (Q-score < 20), prompting filtration with fastp (v0.22.0) to yield high quality clean reads. The reference genome and annotation files for B. gargarizans were retrieved from the NCBI Genome Database. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis and KEGG enrichment analysis

Gene expression quantification was performed using HTSeq (v0.9.1) to generate raw read counts, followed by the transcripts per million fragments (TPM) normalization method for cross-sample comparability [98–100]. Differential expression analysis with DESeq (v1.38.3) identified significant genes under thresholds of |log2FoldChange|> 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Bidirectional hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was executed using the Pheatmap R package (v1.0.12), employing Euclidean distance metrics and complete linkage algorithms to visualize expression patterns across samples.

Functional enrichment analysis included KEGG pathway annotation (clusterProfiler v4.6.0, Fisher’s exact test, P-value < 0.05) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (GSEA v4.1.0) using MSigDB C2 curated gene sets, ranked by log2FoldChange. All statistical comparisons utilized two-tailed Student's t-tests with significance at p < 0.05, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Validation of RNA sequencing data reliability by qRT-PCR

To confirm the reliability and accuracy of the sequencing data, we selected six candidate differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and measured their expression levels using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The primers were designed based on the transcriptome sequencing results using Primer 3.0 software Version 0.4.0 (Primer-E Ltd., Plymouth, UK), with specific sequence details provided in Supplementary Table 13. The β-actin gene was used as an internal reference for normalization. The qRT-PCR assays were performed using the Advantage® RT-PCR Kit (Takara, Japan) and the AceQ® qPCR SYBR® Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Beijing, China). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [101], and intergroup differences were compared using an independent samples t-test. Additionally, the consistency between all tested genes and the sequencing results was assessed.
