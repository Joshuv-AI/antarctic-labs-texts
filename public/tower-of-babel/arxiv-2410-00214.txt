A combinatorial approach to phase transitions in random graph isomorphism problems
Dimitris Diamantidis, Takis Konstantopoulos, Linglong Yuan

Abstract
. We consider two independent Erdős-Rényi random graphs, with possibly different parameters, and study two isomorphism problems, a graph embedding problem and a common subgraph problem. Under certain conditions on the graph parameters we show a sharp asymptotic phase transition as the graph sizes tend to infinity. This extends known results for the case of uniform Erdős-Rényi random graphs. Our approach is primarily combinatorial, naturally leading to several related problems for further exploration.

1.
Introduction

Let
G(V,p)
denote (the law of) a random undirected graph on the vertex set

V
in which an edge is present with probability
p
,
independently from edge to edge.
When
V
has finite size
n
the random graph
is the well-known Erdős-Rényi
G(n,p)
graph,
but the term makes sense even when
V
is infinite.
In particular, we let
G(\infty,p)
be the law of this graph on a countably
infinite set of vertices.

In a seminal paper, Erdős and Rényi
[
10
]
showed that

G(\infty,1/2)
is unique up to automorphisms.
More specifically, there is a (deterministic) graph
R
,
unique up to isomorphisms, on a countable set of vertices and a
random bijection from the set of vertices of
G(\infty,1/2)

onto the set of vertices of
R
that preserves edges.

The graph
R
has a number of remarkable properties, as first shown by Rado

[
12
]
. We refer to the papers of Cameron
[
4
,
5
]

for a survey of properties of
R
which
is now known by either of the following names:

Rado Graph
,

Universal Graph
or [
sic
]
The Random Graph
.

R
can be constructed in many ways. Here is one.
Consider first von Neumann’s representation of natural numbers
as finite ordinals. To the dismay of a probabilist, we use the
letter
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
for the set of natural numbers
0,1,2,\ldots

with their standard order type.
This means that
0:=\varnothing
and, recursively,
n:=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
;
e.g.,
1=\{\varnothing\}
,
2=\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\}\}
.
Then each natural number is both an element and a (finite)
subset of natural numbers.
Next, let
V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\{a,b,\ldots\}
be the collection of
all finite subsets of the natural numbers, a countable
set that strictly includes
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
This
V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\{a,b,\ldots\}
is a model of the von Neumann universe,
the class of hereditarily finite sets
[
9
]
.
For
a,b\in V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, write
a\sim b
if

a\in b
or
b\in a
.
By the foundation (or regularity) axiom of ZFC
[
9
]

only one of these memberships can be true.
Considering
\sim
as a set of edges, the graph
(V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\sim)
is
isomorphic to
R
.

A second representation of
R
is obtained by taking
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
to be a set
of vertices instead of
V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
To define edges, we first represent each natural number

n
in binary
by writing
n=\sum_{m\in B(n)}2^{m}
.
Write
m\sim^{\prime}n

if
m\in B(n)
or
n\in B(m)
.
Considering
\sim^{\prime}
as a set of edges, we have that
({\boldsymbol{\omega}},\sim^{\prime})
is
isomorphic to
(V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\sim)
and hence to
R
.

The isomorphism between the two graphs,

(V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\sim)
and
({\boldsymbol{\omega}},\sim^{\prime})
,
is via a function

A:V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\to{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, devised by Ackermann
[
2
]

via the recursive formula

A(a)=\sum_{b\in a}2^{A(b)}
, for
a\in V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
It is easy to see that (i)
A
is a bijection and (ii)
A
preserves edges.
If we view
B
as a function
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\to V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
that assigns the
set
B(n)
to the natural number
n
then we can see that
B
is the inverse
of
A
.

Some properties of
R
are as follows.

First, if we partition the set
V(\text{{{R}}})
of vertices of
R

into finitely many sets then

R
is isomorphic to the induced subgraph on one of these parts.
For example, considering
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
as a subset of
V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
,
we have that the
V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
graph is isomorphic to the induced subgraph
on
V_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\setminus{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
(but not to the induced subgraph
on
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
because this subgraph is complete: for every two distinct natural
numbers
m,n
either
m\in n
or
n\in m
).

Second, every finite or countably infinite graph can be embedded
as an induced subgraph of
R
.
Thus,
R
contains every possible graph!

The key property of
\text{{{R}}}=(V(\text{{{R}}}),E(\text{{{R}}}))
is the following. Given finitely
many distinct vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in V(\text{{{R}}})
,
there is
z\in E(\text{{{R}}})
such that
z
is adjacent to
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}

and nonadjacent to
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
.
In fact, any graph satisfying this key property is isomorphic to
R
;
see
[
4
]
.
It is easy to see that for any countably infinite sets
U_{1},U_{2}

and any
0<p_{1},p_{2}<1
, the graphs
G(U_{i},p_{i})
satisfy this key property and
so, if
X_{1},X_{2}
are two random graphs, independent or not,
with laws
G(U_{1},p_{1})
,
G(U_{2},p_{2})
, respectively,
then
P(X_{1}\cong X_{2}\cong\text{{{R}}})=1
. The symbol
\cong
stands for
“being isomorphic to”; see Def.
2.1
.

As noted by Chatterjee and Diaconis
[
6
]
, this poses a conundrum.
On one hand, if
X,Y
are independent
G(\infty,1/2)
random graphs
then
X\cong Y
a.s.
On the other hand, if
X,Y
are independent
G(n,1/2)
random graphs
then the probability that they
are isomorphic is astronomically small as
n\to\infty
,
namely, at most
n!/2^{\binom{n}{2}}\leq e^{-cn^{2}}
, for some
positive constant
c
.
To shed light to this,
they asked the question whether a
G(m,1/2)

graph can be embedded into an independent
G(n,1/2)
graph when
m
is smaller
than
n
and found that there is a critical value of
m
,
roughly of order
2\log_{2}n
(but the exact value of it
is very important), such that the probability that the
small graph can be embedded into the larger one tends to
1
or
0

depending on whether
m
is below or above the threshold.
For a precise statement of this result see
[
6
, Thm. 1.2]

or the more general Theorem
2.3
below.

In this paper, we consider two problems that we call
“graph embedding” and “common subgraph” problems.
The first one refers to the question whether a
G(m,p)
graph
can appear as an
induced
subgraph of an independent
G(n,q)
graph,
when
m\leq n
.
The second one refers to the question whether two independent

G(n,p)
,
G(n,q)
graphs contain a common
induced
subgraph
of size
m
.
The terminology is not standard. For example, the “graph embedding”
problem is often referred to as “subgraph isomorphism” and is a classical
problem in computer science; see, e.g., Ullmann
[
16
]
.
We note that both problems have been treated by Chatterjee and Diaconis

[
6
]
in the uniform distribution case (random graph of size
n

refers to a uniform probability measure on the set of all graphs on

n
vertices). Deviation from the non-uniform case is a harder problem and
it is what we are interested in in this paper.
The main theorems are Theorem
2.3
(graph embedding)
and Theorem
2.6
(common subgraph).
In both cases we show the existence of a phase transition
occurring at two integers that differ by at most 2.
This implies that we have what is sometimes known as a

two-point concentration
phenomenon.
This means that phase transition occurs at a “boundary”
defined by two integer sequences,
m_{-}(n)
,
m_{+}(n)
, such
that, eventually,
m_{+}(n)-m_{-}(n)
is either
1
or
2
.
We show that there is flexibility and that the set of integers
n

such that
m_{+}(n)-m_{-}(n)>1
can be made as small as possible
by choosing a certain sequence that tends to infinity as slowly as possible
(this is the sequence
C_{n}
appearing in our two main theorems).
However, the set of
n
such that
m_{+}(n)-m_{-}(n)>1
can
never become empty.

Phase transition phenomena in computational problems are closely related to
the complexity of solving these problems and provide significant insights into
the difficulty of certain instances compared to others. In the context of
computational complexity, a phase transition refers to the abrupt change in the
solvability or structure of a problem as some parameter is varied. This concept
is often studied in NP-complete problems like k-SAT
[
7
]
or
graph coloring
[
1
]
, where, as parameters
(e.g., the ratio of constraints to variables) cross a critical
threshold, the likelihood of finding a solution shifts dramatically—from being
almost always solvable to being almost always unsolvable.

We first heard about these problems during a talk given by
Persi Diaconis
[
8
]
. Subsequently, Chatterjee and Diaconis,
provided a phase transition for the case of uniform graphs in
[
6
]
,
a paper that provided further motivation for us.
While pursuing open problems stated in
[
6
]
,
two papers appeared on the ArXiV:
Lenoir
[
14
]
, who studied phase transitions for
uniform hypergraphs, and
Surya, Warnke, and Zhu
[
13
]
, who studied
the phase transition phenomena of interest to us.
The second paper deals with precisely the same problem as ours in a more general
context (that is, without restrictions on the parameters
p
and
q
of the
random graphs.)
In view of this, our paper can only be seen as dealing with the problem under
restrictions on the parameters (see Theorems
2.3
and
2.6
below).
As such, the only new thing in our paper is that it gives a different, essentially
combinatorial proof, for a special case,
exhausting the limits of the second moment method.
On the other hand, Surya, Warnke, and Zhu use a clever probabilistic idea
in order to deal with the general case.

Both problems are also of interest in several applications as well.
In artificial intelligence research,
people are interested in discovering whether a pattern occurs
inside a large target graph. McCreesh
et al.

[
11
]

have studied this numerically and predicted the
existence of a phase transition.
In bioinformatics research, graphs represent biological networks at
the molecular or higher (protein or even species) level.
Again, an important question is that of locating a specific
pattern in a network. An algorithm for the problem, is proposed by
Bonnici
et al.

[
3
]
.
The problems are also of interest in theoretical computer science
since subraph isomorphism problems are related to constraint
satisfaction problems.

Any

Equivalently, we can think of

\mathscr{P}_{2}(V)
,

An element

The two elements of

If

Let

We will omit the tilde over

when no ambiguity arises.
If

Since the notion of isomorphism is central to this paper,
we recall its standard definition below.

Definition 2.1

(isomorphism)
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
,
\Gamma^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be two graphs.
We say that

is an

if

is a bijection
and if

.
In this case,

,

, and
\Gamma^{\prime}=f(\Gamma)
.
The statement that there exists an isomorphism

between
\Gamma
and

\Gamma^{\prime}
is abbreviated as
\Gamma\cong\Gamma^{\prime}
.

An isomorphism between
\Gamma
and itself is called automorphism.
The set of automorphisms forms a group and the larger its size the more
symmetric
\Gamma
is. For example the empty graph (no edges)
and the full graph are fully symmetric. The set of automorphisms of the

R
is quite remarkable and has been studied by Truss
[
15
]
.

In the first part of this paper we deal with an embeddability problem.
We give the definition of the term below.

Definition 2.2

(embeddability)
.

We say that
\Gamma
is

\Gamma^{\prime}
if
\Gamma\cong\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
for some induced subgraph
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
of
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Our first concern is whether an Erdős-Rényi random graph
can be embedded in a bigger independent random graph.
To this end, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3

(phase transition for the graph embedding problem)
.

Let

be two independent random graphs with laws

Let

Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{m(n)}\text{ is embeddable in }Y_{n})=

\displaystyle 1,

m(n)=\left\lfloor 2\log_{2}n+1-\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\right\rfloor=:m_{-}(n)

(I)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{m(n)}\text{ is embeddable in }Y_{n})=

\displaystyle 0,

m(n)=\left\lceil 2\log_{2}n+1+\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\right\rceil=:m_{+}(n)

(II)

where

(i)

(ii)

We should actually read the last sentence as:

(iii)

(iv)

(vi)

.

(vii)

We see that the effect of

.)

. In particular,

3.
The graph embedding problem

Fix two finite sets
U,V
with cardinalities
m,n
respectively, where
m\leq n
.
Elements of
\mathscr{P}_{2}(U)
are called edges of
U
. Similarly, for
\mathscr{P}_{2}(V)
.
Consider two independent random graphs
X\equiv X_{m},Y\equiv Y_{n}
such that

X_{m}
has law
G(U,p)
and
Y_{n}
has law
G(V,1/2)
.
Identifying
X
with a collection of i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)
random variables

\{X(e),e\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(U)\}
, we refer to those

e\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(U)
such that
X(e)=1
as edges of
X
.
Similarly for
Y
.
Let

\mathscr{I}\equiv\mathscr{I}_{U,V}:=\text{ the collection of injective functions
from $U$ to $V$,}

a set of size
(n)_{m}=n(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1)
.
If
f\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}
we let
{\sf R}f\subset V
be
its range. Then

N=\sum_{f\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{f(X)=Y^{{\sf R}f}}

(3.1)

is the number of isomorphisms between the two random graphs, since, by
definition,
Y^{{\sf R}f}
is the restriction of
Y
onto
{\sf R}f
.
The event of interest is

\{X\text{ is embeddable in }Y\}=\{N>0\}.

3.1.
Phase II of the graph embedding problem

Since
m
will be taken to be much smaller than
n
, it is not unreasonable
to postulate, in view of

\mathbb{P}(N>0)\leq\mathbb{E}N,

that the the threshold for
m
will be the “least” function of
n
such that

\mathbb{E}N\to 0
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}}\mathbb{P}(\forall e\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(U)~X(e)=Y(f(e)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}}\prod_{e\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(U)}\mathbb{P}(X(e)=Y(f(e)))

\displaystyle=(n)_{m}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}p+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-p)\right)^{\binom{m}{2}}=(n)_{m}2^{-\binom{m}{2}}.

(3.2)

We can easily see that

\text{$(n)_{m}/n^{m}\to 1$ as $n\to\infty$ when $m=m(n)=O(\log n)$}.

(3.3)

The question then becomes that of finding the least
m=m(n)
such
that

n^{m}2^{-\binom{m}{2}}\to 0.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
m=m(n)=\left\lceil 2\log_{2}n+1+\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\right\rceil

and
C_{n}\to\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}N\to 0
.

Proof.

By (
3.3
), we simply need to show that

.
We have

n^{m}2^{-\binom{m}{2}}=2^{-\frac{1}{2}m(m-2\log_{2}n-1)}.

But

m(m-2\log_{2}n-1)\geq m\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\to\infty,

because
m/\log_{2}n\to 2
and
C_{n}\to\infty
.
∎

This proves the second part (phase II) of Theorem

but also gives us
reasons to suspect that the “cut-off function”

will work for the phase I too.
We remark that, even though this function does not depend on
p
(and this
is because
G(n,1/2)
remains the same in law if we swap edges and non-edges),
this does not make the other part (phase I) of the theorem trivial
when

. We need to work harder to show that the effect of

vanishes
as will be seen below.

3.2.
Phase I of the graph embedding problem

The rest of the proof proceeds on the basis of the inequality

\mathbb{P}(N>0)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}N)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}N^{2}}.

(3.4)

The goal is to show that
\mathbb{P}(N>0)\to 1
under the conditions for phase I
of Theorem
2.3
. Throughout the rest of this section, we let

m\equiv m(n)=\left\lfloor 2\log_{2}n+1-{C_{n}}/{\log n}\right\rfloor,

for some sequence

with
C_{n}/\log n\to 0

Our plan consists in finding a suitable upper bound for the
reciprocal of the right-hand side of (

\mathbb{E}N^{2}/(\mathbb{E}N)^{2}\leq\mathsf{S};

see Proposition
3.11
below;
and then showing that

conclude the proof of phase II of (

).

To achieve this, we need to use an auxiliary edge graph,

This is motivated by the trivial relation (

Such a device will facilitate the computation of correlations.
Subsequently, we make some combinatorial estimates of various sets of

We finally put things together to obtain a good tight bound

in Section

First, some notational convention.

3.2.4.

We return to the ratio

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
X,Y
be independent

,
G(V,1/2)
random graphs
where
U,V
are sets of sizes
m,n
, respectively.
Define

\mathsf{S}:=\frac{1}{(n)_{m}^{2}}\sum_{r=0}^{m}2^{\binom{r}{2}}\sum_{(f,g)\in\mathscr{H}_{r}}({\widehat{p}})^{\binom{m}{2}-|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|},

(3.24)

where
\widehat{p}=\max(p,1-p)
, where
\mathscr{H}_{r}
is given by (
3.14
),
and where
|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|
is the number of connected components of
the auxiliary edge graph
\mathscr{E}(f,g)
.
Then

\frac{\mathbb{E}N^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}N)^{2}}\leq\mathsf{S}.

Proof.

Since

\tau_{j,k}=(p^{j}+(1-p)^{j})2^{-k}\leq{\widehat{p}}^{j-1}2^{-k},

expression (
3.13
) for
\mathbb{E}N^{2}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{f,g\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}}\prod_{j,k}\left({\widehat{p}}^{j-1}2^{-k}\right)^{|\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{f,g\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}}({\widehat{p}})^{\sum_{j,k}(j-1)|\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)|}\times 2^{-\sum_{j,k}k|\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)|}.

The exponent of
{\widehat{p}}
equals

\sum_{j,k}j|\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)|-\sum_{j,k}|\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)|=\binom{m}{2}-|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|,

from (
3.19
) and (
3.18
).
Identity (
3.20
) tells us that the exponent of
2^{-1}
equals

\sum_{j,k}k|\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)|=2\binom{m}{2}-\binom{r(f,g)}{2},

where

r(f,g)=|f(U)\cap g(U)|.

This leads to

\mathbb{E}N^{2}\leq 2^{-2\binom{m}{2}}\sum_{f,g\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}}({\widehat{p}})^{\binom{m}{2}-|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|}\times 2^{\binom{r(f,g)}{2}}.

Dividing this by the square of
\mathbb{E}N=(n)_{m}e^{-\binom{m}{2}}
we have

\frac{\mathbb{E}N^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}N)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{(n)_{m}^{2}}\sum_{f,g\in\mathscr{I}_{U,V}}({\widehat{p}})^{\binom{m}{2}-|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|}2^{\binom{r(f,g)}{2}}.

Since, by definition.
\mathscr{H}_{r}
is the set of pairs
(f,g)

of injections from
U
into
V
such that
r(f,g)=r
,
we immediately have that the right-hand side of the last display is

\mathsf{S}
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2.3
(I) will be complete
if we show that

\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{S}\leq 1.

(3.25)

To achieve this, fix
0<c<1
and write

\mathsf{S}=\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}+\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}},\qquad\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}=:\frac{1}{(n)_{m}^{2}}\sum_{0\leq r\leq cm}2^{\binom{r}{2}}\sum_{(f,g)\in\mathscr{H}_{r}}({\widehat{p}})^{\binom{m}{2}-|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|}.

(3.26)

We now prove that, under the assumptions for phase I,
the upper bound
\mathsf{S}
is asymptotically below 1.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
c
be fixed,
1/2<c<1
.
Define
With
m=m(n)=\left\lfloor 2\log_{2}n+1-\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\right\rfloor
,
where
C_{n}\to\infty
such that
C_{n}=o(\log n)
, we have

(i)

\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}=1.

(ii)

\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}}=0.

Proof of (i).

The number
|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|
of connected components of
\mathscr{T}(f,g)

cannot exceed the cardinality
\binom{m}{2}
of
\mathscr{L}
. Therefore the exponent
of
{\widehat{p}}
in (
3.26
) is nonnegative.
Since
{\widehat{p}}<1
, we immediately obtain

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}\leq\sum_{r\leq cm}\frac{2^{\binom{r}{2}}}{(n)_{m}^{2}}|\mathscr{H}_{r}|.

We computed
\mathscr{H}_{r}
in Lemma
3.7
, so

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}\leq 1+\sum_{1\leq r\leq cm}2^{\binom{r}{2}}~\frac{\binom{m}{r}(n-m)_{m-r}(m)_{r}}{(n)_{m}}=1+\sum_{1\leq r\leq cm}2^{\binom{r}{2}}~\frac{(n-m)_{m-r}(m)_{r}^{2}}{(n)_{m}r!}.

Using
(m)_{r}\leq m^{r}
and
r!\geq 1
we get

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}\leq 1+\sum_{1\leq r\leq\lceil cm\rceil}2^{\binom{r}{2}}m^{2r}\frac{(n-m)_{m-r}}{(n)_{m}}.

Now we calculate

\displaystyle\frac{(n-m)_{m-r}}{(n)_{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-m)\cdots(n-2m+r+1)}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(n-m)^{m-r}}{(n-m)^{m}}=\left(\frac{1}{n-m}\right)^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{m}{n}}\right)^{r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{r}}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{m}{n}}\right)^{m}.

But the term
\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{m}{n}}\right)^{m}
is
1+o(1)
as

n\rightarrow\infty
, because we have
m=O(\log n)
. Hence, we obtain

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}\leq 1+(1+o(1))\sum_{1\leq r\leq\lceil cm\rceil}2^{\binom{r}{2}}\frac{m^{2r}}{n^{r}}.

We use convexity to show that the last sum tends to
0
. Letting

a_{r}:=2^{\binom{r}{2}}\frac{m^{2r}}{n^{r}},

we have that
a_{r+1}/a_{r}=(m^{2}/n)2^{r}
is increasing in
r
and so

a_{r}\leq a_{1}\vee a_{\lceil cm\rceil}
for all
1\leq r\leq\lceil cm\rceil
,
which gives

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm I}}\leq 1+e^{o(1)}\lceil cm\rceil(a_{1}\vee a_{\lceil cm\rceil})
.
It therefore suffices to show that both

ma_{1}\to 0
and
ma_{\lceil cm\rceil}\to 0
.
With our choice for
m=m(n)
, we immediately have

ma_{1}={m^{3}}/{n}\to 0
.
We next have

\displaystyle\log_{2}(ma_{\lceil cm\rceil})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lceil cm\rceil\big(\lceil cm\rceil-1\big)+\big(2\lceil cm\rceil+1\big)\log_{2}m-\lceil cm\rceil\log_{2}n

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lceil cm\rceil\big(\lceil cm\rceil-m\big)+\big(2\lceil cm\rceil+1\big)\log_{2}m,

where we used that, for
m=m(n)
as in the theorem statement,
m<2\log_{2}n+1
.
Since
c<1
, we have
\lceil cm\rceil-m\to\infty

and since the last term in the above display converges to
\infty
much
faster than
m^{2}
we immediately obtain
\log_{2}(ma_{\lceil cm\rceil})\to-\infty
, as needed.
∎

Proof of (ii).

Recall the definition (
3.15
) of
\mathscr{H}_{r,\ell}

and write
\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}}
as

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}}=\frac{1}{(n)_{m}^{2}}\sum_{r>cm}2^{\binom{r}{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\sum_{(f,g)\in\mathscr{H}_{r,\ell}}{\widehat{p}}^{\binom{m}{2}-|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|}.

An upper bound for
|\mathscr{C}(f,g)
, when
(f,g)\in\mathscr{H}_{r,\ell}
, is
obtained by using
(
3.18
), (
3.22
), (
3.23
) and (
3.21
)
of Lemma
3.10
:

\displaystyle|\mathscr{C}(f,g)|

\displaystyle=|\mathscr{C}_{1,1}(f,g)|+\sum_{j\geq 2}|\mathscr{C}_{j,j}(f,g)|+\sum_{j\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{j,j+1}(f,g)|

\displaystyle\leq\left[\binom{\ell}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(r-\ell)\right]+\left[\frac{1}{2}\binom{r}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{\ell}{2}\right]+\left[\binom{m}{2}-\binom{4}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{\ell}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{r}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(r-\ell)\leq\binom{m}{2}-\frac{1}{4}(r-\ell)(r-3).

We thus have

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}}\leq\frac{1}{(n)_{m}^{2}}\sum_{r>cm}2^{\binom{r}{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}({\widehat{p}})^{\frac{1}{4}(r-\ell)(r-3)}|\mathscr{H}_{r,\ell}|.

Writing
|\mathscr{H}_{r,\ell}|\leq\left|\bigcup_{k=\ell}^{r}\mathscr{H}_{r,k}\right|

and upper-bounding this as in (
3.17
), we obtain

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}}\leq\sum_{r>cm}\frac{(n-m)_{m-r}}{(n)_{m}}2^{\binom{r}{2}}\binom{m}{r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\binom{r}{\ell}(m-\ell)_{r-\ell}\,({\widehat{p}})^{\frac{1}{4}(r-\ell)(r-3)}.

Since
r>cm
, the last term is upper-bounded by

({\widehat{p}})^{\frac{1}{4}(r-\ell)(cm-3)}
; using also

(m-\ell)_{r-\ell}\leq m^{r-\ell}
, we upper-bound the last sum
by

\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\binom{r}{\ell}\left(m{\widehat{p}}^{(cm-3)/4}\right)^{r-\ell}=\left(1+m{\widehat{p}}^{(cm-3)/4}\right)^{m}\to 1,\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

We thus obtain

\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}}\leq(1+o(1))\sum_{r>cm}\frac{(n-m)_{m-r}}{(n)_{m}}\binom{m}{r}2^{\binom{r}{2}}\leq(1+o(1))\sum_{r>cm}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\binom{m}{r}2^{\binom{r}{2}},

where we used the facts that
(n-m)_{m-r}\leq n^{m-r}

and that
\lim_{n\to\infty}(n)_{m}/n^{m}=1
.
We use a convexity argument again.
Consider

b_{r}=b_{r}(n)=\frac{1}{n^{r}}\binom{m}{r}2^{\binom{r}{2}},\quad\lfloor cm\rfloor+1\leq r\leq m,

as a function of
r
.
(Note the condition
r>cm\geq\lfloor cm\rfloor
is equivalent to

r\geq\lfloor cm\rfloor+1
because
r
is integer.)
We have

\frac{b_{r+1}}{b_{r}}=\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{m-r}{r+1}\,2^{r}.

Setting

\psi(x):=\frac{x}{x+1}
,
an increasing function on
x>-1
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{b_{r+2}}{b_{r+1}}\bigg/\frac{b_{r+1}}{b_{r}}=2\,\frac{r+1}{r+2}\,\frac{m-r-1}{m-r}

\displaystyle=2\,\psi(r+1)\,\psi(m-r-1)

\displaystyle\geq 2\psi(\lfloor cm\rfloor+2)\psi(2)=\frac{4}{3}\psi(\lfloor cm\rfloor+2)>1,

for
\lfloor cm\rfloor+1\leq r\leq m-3
and
m
large enough,
because
\psi(x)\to 1
as
x\to\infty
. We deduce that
the sequence of ratios
\frac{b_{r+1}}{b_{r}}
is increasing for

\lfloor cm\rfloor+1\leq r\leq m-1
. We notice that

\frac{b_{\lfloor cm\rfloor+2}}{b_{\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}}=\frac{1}{n}\frac{m-\lfloor cm\rfloor-1}{\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}2^{\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}.

The term
\frac{m-\lfloor cm\rfloor-1}{\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}

converges to
\frac{1-c}{c}
.
Moreover,
m=\lfloor 2\log_{2}n+1-\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\rfloor\geq 2\log_{2}n
for
n
large enough, using the
fact that
\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. So, it follows that

\frac{b_{\lfloor cm\rfloor+2}}{b_{\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}}\geq\left(\frac{1-c}{c}+o(1)\right)\frac{1}{n}2^{2c\log_{2}n}=\left(\frac{1-c}{c}+o(1)\right)n^{2c-1}

but the latter term converges to
\infty
because

c>\frac{1}{2}
. So we can pick
some
a>1
such that for
n
large enough

\frac{b_{\lfloor cm\rfloor+2}}{b_{\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}}\geq a>1.

Now using the fact that the sequence of ratios is increasing up to
m-2

we get

\frac{b_{r+1}}{b_{r}}\geq a,

for all
\lfloor cm\rfloor+1\leq r\leq m-2
, which implies that

b_{r}\leq\left(\frac{1}{a}\right)^{m-1-r}b_{m-1}.

Since
m>2\log_{2}n
, we have

\frac{b_{m}}{b_{m-1}}=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{m}2^{m}>\frac{n}{m}.

Therefore

b_{r}\leq\left(\frac{1}{a}\right)^{m-1-r}b_{m},\quad\lfloor cm\rfloor+1\leq r\leq m-1.

This implies that

\sum_{r>cm}b_{r}=b_{m}+\sum_{r=\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}^{m-1}b_{r}\leq b_{m}+b_{m}\sum_{r=\lfloor cm\rfloor+1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{1}{a}\right)^{m-1-r}\leq b_{m}\left(1+\frac{1}{1-(1/a)}\right).

So to show
\mathsf{S}_{\text{\rm II}}\to 0
it suffices to show that
b_{m}\to 0
.
To this end we compute

2\log_{2}b_{m}=m(m-1)-2m\log_{2}n=m(m-2\log_{2}n-1).

However,

m=\lfloor 2\log_{2}n+1-\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}\rfloor\leq 2\log_{2}n+1-\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}

and therefore

2\log_{2}b_{m}\leq-C_{n}\frac{m}{\log n}.

Since
\frac{m}{\log_{2}n}\to 2
and
C_{n}\to\infty
, we conclude that

2\log_{2}b_{m}\to-\infty
and therefore
b_{m}\to 0
which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition
3.12
immediately implies that (

) holds,
and so the proof of Theorem
2.3
is complete.

4.
The common subgraph problem

Assuming now that the two vertex sets
U,V
, have the same size
n
, our goal
is to discover the size of the largest common subgraph as
n\to\infty
.
We shall again prove a phase transition phenomenon occurs, as stated in
Theorem
2.6
.
We let
X=X_{n}
,
Y=Y_{n}
be independent random graphs with
laws
G(U,p)
,
G(V,q)
, respectively.
We now allow
p
and
q
to be different probabilities, both strictly
between
0
and
1
, but restricted in the region defined by (
2.4
)
and depicted in Figure
1
. The method is analogous to that
of the embedding problem, but the details of the analysis are more complicated.

We start with some terminology and notation.
We say that
f
is
partial function
from
U
to
V

if there are sets
U^{\prime}\subset U
and
V^{\prime}\subset V
such that

f
is a function from
U^{\prime}

onto

V^{\prime}
. We denote
U^{\prime}
by
{\sf D}f

and
V^{\prime}
by
{\sf R}f
.
We are interested in partial functions that are also injections.
Recalling that
\mathscr{I}_{U^{\prime},V}
is the collection of all injections
from
U^{\prime}
to
V
, the set

\mathscr{J}_{U,V,m}:=\bigcup_{U^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{m}(U)}\mathscr{I}_{U^{\prime},V}

is the collection of all partial functions
from
U
to
V
that are injections and have domain of size
m
.
Since there are
n\choose m
ways to choose a subset of
U
of size
m

and then there are
(n)_{m}
injections from the chosen set into
V
, it
follows that

|\mathscr{J}_{U,V,m}|=\binom{n}{m}\,(n)_{m}=\frac{(n)_{m}^{2}}{m!}.

(4.1)

Recall the definition of
m
-isomorphism; see Def.
2.5
.
Defining

J_{f}:={\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{f(X_{n}^{{\sf D}f})=Y_{n}^{{\sf R}f}}

(4.2)

(recall that the induced subgraph on a set
A
of a graph
\Gamma
is denoted by
\Gamma^{A}
),
we see that

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{\cong}}Y_{n}\iff\exists f\in\mathscr{J}_{U,V,m}~~J_{f}=1.

Thus

N=\sum_{f\in\mathscr{J}_{U,V,m}}J_{f}

(4.3)

is the number of
m
-isomorphisms between the two random graphs,
and

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{\cong}}Y_{n}\iff N>0.

With
\tau=pq+(1-p)(1-q)
we have, for any
f\in\mathscr{J}_{U,V,m}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}J_{f}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(f(X_{n}^{{\sf D}f})=Y_{n}^{{\sf R}f})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(X_{n}(e)=Y_{n}(f(e))\text{ for all }e\in\mathscr{P}_{2}({\sf D}f)\big)=\tau^{\binom{m}{2}},

so

\mathbb{E}N=|\mathscr{J}_{U,V,m}|\,\mathbb{E}J_{f}=\binom{n}{m}\,(n)_{m}\,\tau^{\binom{m}{2}}.

(4.4)

As usual, we shall sometimes be writing
X
instead of
X_{n}
, etc.

4.1.
Phase II of the common subgraph problem

Phase II, by definition, refers to the asymptotic regime
where the probability of existence of an
m
-isomorphism
tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
for some sequence
m=m(n)
.
A sufficient condition is easily obtained below.

Recall, from (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) that

W(x)=x+2\lambda\log x+\frac{\lambda}{x}\log(2\pi x)
, with
\lambda=1/\log(1/\tau)
, and

W(m_{*}(n))=4\lambda\log n+2\lambda+1
.
Observe that
W
is strictly increasing (and concave),
with
W(1)\geq 1
,
W^{\prime}(x)\geq 1
for all
x
and
W(x)\to\infty

as
x\to\infty
.

Lemma 4.1

(condition for phase II of the graph isomorphism problem)
.

If
m(n)=\lceil m_{*}(n)+(C_{n}/\log n)\rceil
where
C_{n}\to\infty

and
C_{n}/\log n\to 0
,
then
\mathbb{E}N\to 0
.

Proof.

With
m=m(n)
as in the statement, we have

\mathbb{E}N=\frac{(n)_{m}\cdot(n)_{m}}{m!}\tau^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}\sim\frac{n^{2m}}{(m/e)^{m}\sqrt{2\pi m}}\tau^{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}=:b(n),

(4.5)

where we took into account that
m(n)=\lceil m_{*}(n)+(C_{n}/\log n)\rceil=O(\log n)
(because
C_{n}/\log n\to 0
and because of Remark
2.4
) and
used Stirling’s approximation and that
(n)_{m}\sim n^{m}
, as
n\to\infty
.
Recalling the definition of
W
and
m_{*}
from (
2.2
) and (
2.3
)
and doing a little algebra we obtain

-\log b(n)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\,m\,(W(m)-W(m_{*})),

(4.6)

a positive quantity because
\lambda=1/\log(1/\tau)>0
,
m>m_{*}
,

W(m)>W(m_{*})
.
Since, for
x>0
,

W^{\prime}(x)=1+\frac{\lambda}{x}\left(2+\frac{1}{x}-\frac{\log(2\pi x)}{x}\right)\geq 1+\frac{\lambda}{x}(2-2\pi e^{-2})\geq 1

(4.7)

(indeed, the bracketed expression in the second term
achieves minimum at the point
x=e^{2}/2\pi
and
equals
2-2\pi{e^{-2}}>0
at this point),
we have

W(m)-W(m_{*})\geq m-m_{*}

and so

-\log b(n)\geq\frac{1}{2\lambda}\,m_{*}\,(m-m_{*})\geq\frac{1}{2\lambda}\,m_{*}\,\frac{C_{n}}{\log n}.

Since
\lim_{x\to\infty}W(x)/x=1
, we have

\frac{m_{*}}{\log n}=\frac{W(m_{*})}{\log n}\left(\frac{W(m_{*})}{m_{*}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{4\lambda\log n+2\lambda+1}{\log n}\left(\frac{W(m_{*})}{m_{*}}\right)^{-1}\to 4\lambda,

(4.8)

and so
-\log b(n)\to\infty
which implies that
\mathbb{E}N\to 0
.
∎

4.2.
Phase I of the common subgraph problem

We shall use the auxiliary edge graph device
in order to estimate
\mathbb{E}N^{2}
.
Recall that an edge graph is a graph whose Vertices are

U
or
V
, i.e. elements of
\mathscr{P}_{2}(U)\cup\mathscr{P}_{2}(V)

4.2.1.
The auxiliary edge graph

Fix

and define the auxiliary edge graph

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{P}_{2}({\sf D}f)\cup\mathscr{P}_{2}({\sf D}g)\text{ (left Vertices)},

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{P}_{2}({\sf R}f)\cup\mathscr{P}_{2}({\sf R}g)\text{ (right Vertices)}.

\mathscr{E}(f,g)=\big\{\{e,f(e)\}:\,e\in\mathscr{P}_{2}({\sf D}f)\big\}\cup\big\{\{e,g(e)\}:\,e\in\mathscr{P}_{2}({\sf D}g)\big\}.

Clearly,
\mathscr{T}(f,g)

The graph
\mathscr{T}(f,g)

are partial functions
and thus may not be defined on every element of

Note that we write

for the action of

,

for the action of
f
on an edge
e
of

.
Since

.
So if

(equivalently,

)

e^{\prime}=g(e)
(equivalently,
e=g^{-1}(e^{\prime})
).
We let

\operatorname{Deg}_{f,g}(e):=\text{ Degree of Vertex $e$ in $\mathscr{T}(f,g)$},

that is, the number of Vertices
e^{\prime}
such that
\{e,e^{\prime}\}
is an Edge.
Clearly,

\operatorname{Deg}_{f,g}(e)=1\text{ or }2,

according as

or
f(e)\neq g(e)
, respectively.

Let
\mathscr{C}(f,g)
be the collection of connected components of
\mathscr{T}(f,g)

and further let
\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)
be the collection of connected components
with
j
left and
k

Lemma 4.2
.

(i) The set
\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)
is empty unless

or

or
j=k+1
.

(ii) If
C\in\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)

C
is a path.

(iii) If
C\in\mathscr{C}_{j,j}(f,g)

C
is a path or a cycle.

Proof.

Let
C\in\mathscr{C}_{j,k}(f,g)
. For

denote by

, respectively

k_{d}
the number of left, respectively right, Vertices of

of Degree
d
. So

j_{1}+j_{2}=j,\quad k_{1}+k_{2}=k.

(4.9)

To each left Vertex of Degree

of the bipartite graph

there correspond

Edges. Hence

is the number of Edges of

. It also equals

. So

j_{1}+2j_{2}=k_{1}+2k_{2}.

(4.10)

Notice that

is the number of Vertices of
C
of Degree
d
.
Then (
4.10
) immediately gives

j_{1}+k_{1}=2(k_{2}-j_{2}+k_{1}),

(4.11)

that is, the number of Vertices of Degree

is even.

Case 1:

. From (

) we have

and hence,
from (

),

.
Thus,

is a connected component with equal left and right Vertices and all
of Degree

. Hence

is a cycle.

Case 2:

j_{1}+k_{1}>0
. From (

Let then

be distinct Vertices of

Since

\frac{\mathbb{E}N^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}N)^{2}}\geq\chi(n)\frac{m!}{n^{m}\tau^{\frac{1}{2}\binom{m}{2}}}\sim\chi(n)\sqrt{m!}\sqrt{\frac{(m/e)^{m}\sqrt{2\pi m}}{n^{2m}\tau^{\binom{m}{2}}}}=\chi(n)\sqrt{m!}\sqrt{\frac{1}{b(n)}},

(5.2)

where
b(n)
is defined by (
5.2
) itself and is the same quantity
appearing in the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
Hence, as in (
4.6
),

\log b(n)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\,m\,(W(m_{*})-W(m)).

Using the mean value theorem and the fact that
W^{\prime}(x)\leq W^{\prime}(1)
for
all
x\geq 1
, we have

\log b(n)\leq\frac{W^{\prime}(1)}{2\lambda}m(m_{*}-m).

But

m=\lfloor m_{*}-(C_{n}/\log n)\rfloor>m_{*}-(C_{n}/\log n)-1,

hence

\log b(n)\leq\frac{W^{\prime}(1)}{2\lambda}m(1+C_{n}/\log n),

and this implies that
b(n)\leq e^{c_{1}m}
, eventually,
for some positive constant
c_{1}
. On the other hand

m!\geq e^{c_{2}m\log m}
for some positive constant
c_{2}
.
So the right side of (
5.2
) tends to
\infty
as
n\to\infty

and so (
5.1
) holds.
∎

6.
Some open problems

Here is a (partial) list of open problems.

P1.

The graph embedding phase transition problem between

G(m,p)
and an independent
G(n,q)
has been fully solved
when
q=1/2
. The case for general
q
remains open.

P2.

As mentioned in Remark
2.4
(v), there is a delicate question involving
graph embedding of
G(2k+1,p)
and
G(2^{k},1/2)
; this is an open question
that remains delicate even when
p=1/2
.

P3.

Another open question is the study of the distribution of an appropriately
normalized version of
N

(for both the embeddability and the common subgraph problems)
so that it converges to some limit.

P4.

How does a largest common induced subgraph between
G(n,p)
and
G(n,q)

evolve as
n
increases? This is a question also asked in
[
6
]
.

P5.

In the common subgraph problem we considered both graphs to
have the same size
n
. But what happens when one has size
n_{1}
and
the other
n_{2}
, both tending to infinity in a particular way?

P6.

An isomorphism between colored graphs can also be defined. One
can thus ask similar questions in this case. The parameter here
is the whole distribution of the colors in each graph.

P7.

Recently, Lenoir
[
14
]
addressed the common subgraph
problem between two random
d
-hypergraphs, but only
in the uniform case (that is, when we put the uniform
probability measure on the set of all
d
–hypergraphs on
n
vertices).
For
d=2
this corresponds to the case
p=q=1/2
.
One can ask the question of phase transition for more general
than uniform distributions as well.

P8.

As explained, the region
\mathbb{Y}
is only sufficient, but not necessary,
for the existence of phase transition.
See also Proposition
5.1
.
Outside
\mathbb{Y}
the bound (
3.4
) does not give useful information.
So the problem is to investigate what is going on outside
\mathbb{Y}
,
being precisely the region where the second moment method does
not provide any information.

P9.

Finally one can ask same questions when the parameters. e.g.,

p
and
q
depend on
n
. (For example, phase transition
for the chromatic number of sparse graphs
G(n,d/n)
is proved in

[
1
]
.)

The function

.

W(x):=x+2\lambda\log x+\frac{\lambda}{x}\log(2\pi x),\quad x\geq 1.

It is strictly increasing because, for all
x>0
,

W^{\prime}(x)=1+\frac{\lambda}{x}\left(2+\frac{1}{x}-\frac{\log(2\pi x)}{x}\right)\geq 1+\frac{\lambda}{x}(2-2\pi e^{-2})\geq 1

(indeed, the bracketed expression in the second term
achieves minimum at the point
x=e^{2}/2\pi
and
equals
2-2\pi{e^{-2}}>1.1496633
at this point).
It is strictly concave because, for all
x>0

W^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{2\lambda}{x^{3}}\big(\log x-x+\log(\pi^{2})-3/2\big)<0

(indeed
\log x-x\leq-1

\log(\pi^{2})-3/2\approx 0.337877067<1
); and so
W^{\prime}(x)
is a strictly decreasing function

The function
