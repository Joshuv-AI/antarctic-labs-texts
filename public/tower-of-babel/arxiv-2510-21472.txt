Sandwiching between random regular graphs and Erdős-Rényi graphs: configuration model and unions of perfect matchings
Pu Gao, Mikhail Isaev, Xavier Perez-Gimenez

Abstract
We establish new couplings among several random graph and multigraph models related to the random regular graph 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , d ) {\mathcal{G}}(n,d) , including the configuration model and unions of random perfect matchings. As a main result, we verify the Kim–Vu sandwich conjecture for all large degrees d = n − O ⁡ ( log 4 ⁡ n ) d=n-O(\log^{4}n) and prove a weakened version for d = O ⁡ ( log 4 ⁡ n ) d=O(\log^{4}n) , which are the only remaining open cases. Our approach introduces a coupling framework that links 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , d ) {\mathcal{G}}(n,d) and 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , p ) {\mathcal{G}}(n,p) through a chain of intermediate models.

Introduction

The random regular graph model
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
is obtained by sampling a
d
-regular graph on the vertex set
[n]
uniformly at random. (We assume throughout the paper that
dn
is always even.) In contrast to the Erdős-Rényi graph
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
, the analysis of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
is considerably more challenging and relies on combinatorial enumeration techniques developed for graphs with a specified degree sequence. In the study of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
, several random graph models have been proposed to approximate or simulate its properties. Among these, the most prominent is the configuration model introduced by Bollobás
[
1
]
. In this model,
each vertex
i\in[n]
is represented by a bin containing
d
points. The configuration model
\mathcal{P}(n,d)
is obtained by taking a uniform random perfect matching over the total
nd
points, producing what is called a pairing, and two points that are matched to each other are called a pair in the pairing. Each pairing corresponds to a
d
-regular multigraph, obtained by contracting each bin into a single vertex: every matched pair of points
p,q
gives rise to an edge
uv
whenever
p
belongs to vertex
u
and
q
belongs to vertex
v
. Let
G[P]
denote the
d
-regular multigraph produced by pairing
P
. Note that
G[P]
may contain both loops and multiple edges. In general, each
d
-regular multigraph may be obtained from a different number of pairings. However, an easy counting argument shows that every simple
d
-regular graph (i.e. with no loops or multiple edges) corresponds to the same number of pairings. As a result, we obtain the following well-known result (see e.g.
[
18
]
) which we state below as Proposition
1.1
after introducing some notation.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}
be sequences of probability measures on the same sequence of sample spaces
\Omega_{n}
. We say that
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
is contiguous with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{n}
(denoted by
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}\vartriangleleft\mathcal{H}_{n}
) if for every sequence of events
A_{n}
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(A_{n})\to 0
implies that
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}}_{n}}(A_{n})\to 0
. We say that
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}
are mutually contiguous if
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}\vartriangleleft\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}\vartriangleleft{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
. We say
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{H}_{n}
if
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}}_{n}}(A_{n})={\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(A_{n})
for all
A_{n}
, and we say
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\mathcal{H}_{n}
if
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}}_{n}}(A_{n})={\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(A_{n})+o(1)
for all
A_{n}
. All asymptotics here and throughout the paper are taken with respect to
n
.

Proposition 1.1

(
[
18
]
)
.

{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
is equivalent to
G[P]
for
P\sim\mathcal{P}(n,d)
conditional on the event that
G[P]
is a simple graph.

When
d=d(n)\to\infty
, the event that

is a simple graph has probability
o(1)
. However, for constant
d
, it follows from the analysis of the limiting distribution of the number of loops and double edges that
G[P]
is simple with probability
\Omega(1)
. As a result, we obtain the following well-known fact as an immediate corollary of Proposition
1.1
.

Proposition 1.2

(
[
18
]
)
.

For every fixed
d\geqslant 1
,
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
is contiguous with respect to
G[P]
for
P\sim\mathcal{P}(n,d)
.

We can analogously define the loopless configuration model
\mathcal{P}_{*}(n,d)
as
\mathcal{P}(n,d)
conditional on the event that no two points belonging to the same vertex are matched (i.e.
G[P]
has no loops for
P\sim\mathcal{P}(n,d)
). Obviously, Propositions
1.1
and

hold as well if we replace
\mathcal{P}(n,d)
by
\mathcal{P}_{*}(n,p)
.

We can yet define another random multigraph model from the superposition of perfect matchings. Given two (multi)graphs
G
and
H
defined on the same set of vertices, let
G+H
denote the superposition of
G
and
H
, i.e., for every pair of vertices

, the multiplicity of

in

is equal to the sum of its multiplicities in
G
and

(the multiplicity of

is defined to be 0 if

and

are not adjacent). If

and

are simple graphs, let

denote the union of
G
and

(that is, the union of their edge sets), and hence

is again a simple graph.
Suppose that
n
is even. Let
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{+}
denote the random multigraph obtained by taking the superposition of
d
independent uniformly random perfect matchings on
[n]
, and let
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\oplus}
denote
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{+}
conditional on being simple. Let
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\cup}
denote the simple graph obtained by taking the union of

independent uniformly random perfect matchings on
[n]
. In other words,
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\cup}
is obtained from

after replacing all the multiple edges by simple edges. The following proposition shows the relationship between
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\oplus}
when

Proposition 1.3

(
[
18
]
)
.

For every fixed

,
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\oplus}
are mutually contiguous.

Finally, Kim and Vu proposed to approximate
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
by the Erdős-Rényi model
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
for

. Of course
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)

are supported on nearly disjoint set of graphs and thus it is not possible to expect any strong relation like asymptotic equivalence or contiguity. Kim and Vu conjectured that if

whose densities are both asymptotically

Proposition 1.4

(

Suppose that

G_{*}\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n,p_{*}),\quad G\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n,d),\quad G^{*}\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n,p^{*}),\quad\text{and}\ {\mathbb{P}}(G_{*}\subseteq G\subseteq G^{*})=1-o(1).

1.1
Bridging by unions of perfect matchings

The main goal is to embed
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
into
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
for some
p
reasonably greater than
d/n
. Motivated by Proposition
1.3
, we consider
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\cup}
as an intermediate model to bridge
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
and
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
.

Frize and the third author
[
5
]
showed that for every fixed
r\geqslant 3
, the union of random perfect
r
-matchings can be embedded into a random
r
-uniform hypergaph with independent hyperedges.
Extending their proof, we show that such a coupling exists for graphs (
r=2
) as well.

Theorem 1.6
.

There is a continuous and strictly increasing function
f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
g(1)=0
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}g(x)=1/4
such that the following holds.
Let
n
be even.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed, and
p=x\log n/n
for
x=x(n)\geqslant 1+\varepsilon
. Then, for any
d\leqslant(g(x)+o(1))pn
there exists a coupling
(H_{*},G^{*})
such that

H_{*}\sim\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\cup},\quad G^{*}\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n,p),\quad{\mathbb{P}}(H_{*}\subseteq G^{*})=1-o(1).

The next step is to establish the other side of the coupling.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
n
be even.
Suppose that
d=\omega(1)
and
d=o(n^{1/7}/\log n)
. Then, there exist
\tau(d)=(2+o(1))d
and a coupling
(G_{*},H^{*})
such that

G_{*}\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n,d),\quad H^{*}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\tau(d)}^{\cup},\quad{\mathbb{P}}(G_{*}\subseteq H^{*})=1-o(1).

The model
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\cup}
lends itself well to analysis due to the convenient arithmetic identity
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\cup}\cup\mathcal{M}_{b}^{\cup}=\mathcal{M}_{a+b}^{\cup}
for any integers
a,b\geqslant 0
, where the union on the left-hand side is taken over two independent copies, and equality is understood in the sense of distribution. A similar arithmetic property holds for
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
, commonly known as the sprinkling technique: to generate
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
, one may first expose
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p^{\prime})
for some
p^{\prime}<p
, and then sprinkle an independent copy of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,q)
on top, where the parameters satisfy
1-p=(1-p^{\prime})(1-q)
. However,
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
does not have such nice arithmetic identities. The closest known property of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
to the aforementioned arithmetic identities
[
11
]
is that when
d=\omega(1)
and
d=o(n^{1/7})
,

d_{TV}({\mathcal{G}}(n,d),{\mathcal{G}}(n,t)\oplus\mathcal{M}_{d-t}^{\oplus})=o(1),\quad\text{for any $\omega(1)=t\leqslant d-1$}.

(1.1)

where
{\mathcal{G}}(n,t)\oplus\mathcal{M}_{d-t}^{\oplus}
denotes the union of independent
{\mathcal{G}}(n,t)
and
\mathcal{M}_{d-t}^{\oplus}
conditioned on them being disjoint.

There are two major obstacles in proving Theorem
1.7
by the decomposition (
1.1
). First,
\mathcal{M}_{d-t}^{\oplus}
is not the union of independent matchings. It would take a large number, exponential in
d
, of independent matchings to contain a subset of
d
pairwise disjoint matchings. Second, the decomposition (
1.1
) requires

, which prevents a complete decomposition of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
into
d
perfect matchings, as required by Theorem

, even setting aside the independence issue.

To overcome these two obstacles, we turn to the loopless configuration model, and use it to bridge
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\cup}
for some

1.2
Decomposition of the loopless configuration model

Instead of embedding
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
into
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\cup}
for some

We defined the superposition of two (multi)graphs. The superposition of two pairings is defined in a natural way by taking the union of the points and the matchings of the two pairings. Note that a matching can be natually viewed as a pairing and hence
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{+}
can be naturally viewed as the superposition of
d
pairings. In what follows, when we write
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}+{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
where
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
are two random (multi)graphs, it means the superposition of independent
G_{1}\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{1}

Let

Tools

Given two degree sequences
{\boldsymbol{d}},{\bf s}
defined on the same set of vertices, we say
{\boldsymbol{d}}\leqslant{\bf s}
if
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i}\leqslant{\bf s}_{i}
for all
i

denote the degree sequence of

Let
{\mathbb{N}}
denote the set of positive integers.
Let
U
and
V
be two disjoint sets. Let
{\bf s}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{U}
and
{\bf t}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{V}

with degree sequence

\Delta({\bf s})
denote the maximum component in

U

M({\boldsymbol{d}})=\sum_{u\in U}s_{u}=\sum_{v\in V}t_{v}

denote the number of bipartite graphs with degree sequence

The following estimation of

Suppose that

Let

is a directed graph on vertex set
[n]
with each possible directed edge
uv
included with probability
p
independently from all other choices.

Proposition 7.1
.

There is a continuous and strictly increasing function
f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
f(1)=0
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=1/2
such that the following holds for every
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
p=x\log n/n
with
x=x(n)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)
and
d\leqslant f(x)pn
. Then there is a coupling in which
{\mathcal{O}}(n,d)\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
a.a.s.

Proof.

The easiest case is when
x=pn/\log n\geqslant 2+\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon>0
. Assume
x\geqslant 2.1
for simplicity. Let
\vec{G}
be distributed as
{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}(n,\vec{p})
with
\vec{p}=1-(1-p)^{1/2}\sim p/2
. Then, the undirected graph
G
obtained from
\vec{G}
by ignoring the orientation of the edges (and replacing double edges by simple edges) is distributed as
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
.
Since
\vec{p}\sim(x/2)\log n/n
and
x/2\geqslant 1.05
, a standard first moment calculation (see e.g. Lemma 3.2 in
[
17
]
) shows that the minimum out-degree
\delta^{+}=\delta^{+}(\vec{G})
of
\vec{G}
is a.a.s. at least
(1+o(1))f_{1}(x/2)(x/2)\log n
, where

f_{1}(x)=-\frac{(x-1)/x}{W(-(x-1)/(xe))}

and
W
is the lower branch of the Lambert
W
function.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be such event.
Note that
f_{1}(x)
is strictly increasing in
[1,\infty)
,
f_{1}(1)=0
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=1
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}
, by picking
d\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)f_{1}(x/2)(x/2)\log n\leqslant\delta^{+}
random out-neighbours of each vertex
v
in
\vec{G}
independently, we obtain a copy of
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}(n,d)
, so
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}(n,d)\subseteq{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}(n,\vec{p})
and, ignoring orientations,
{\mathcal{O}}(n,d)\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
.

Now, we consider the case
p=(1+\varepsilon)\log n/n
, where
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
is assumed to be sufficiently small for some inequalities to hold. Let
d\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}\log n
. We will and find a coupling in which a.a.s.
{\mathcal{O}}(n,d)\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
.
Let

f_{2}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{1.1^{2}}\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{x}&1\leqslant x\leqslant 2.1\\
f_{1}(x)/2&x>2.1.\end{cases}

Note
f_{2}(x)pn\leqslant(x-1)^{2}\log n=\varepsilon^{2}\log n
for
x=1+\varepsilon\leqslant 1+\varepsilon_{0}
, and
f_{2}(x)pn\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\log n
for
1+\varepsilon_{0}\leqslant x\leqslant 2.1
. Let
f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be any function with
f(1)=0
and
f(x)<f_{2}(x)
in
(1,\infty)
.
Then, by monotonicity and by the easy case above, the coupling holds for all
d\leqslant f(x)pn
and
p\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\log n/n
.
Since
\lim_{x\to\infty}f_{2}(x)=1/2
and
f_{2}
is continuous and strictly increasing in each of the two pieces of the domain, one can trivially build function
f
to be continuous and strictly increasing on
[1,\infty)
and satisfy
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=1/2
. This function satisfies the conditions of the statement.

It only remains to find the promised coupling for
p=(1+\varepsilon)\log n/n
and
d\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}\log n
, where
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
.
We build three random graphs independently:
\vec{E}_{1}
,
E_{2}
and
\vec{E}_{3}
. Note that
\vec{E}_{1}
and
\vec{E}_{3}
are directed, while
E_{2}
is undirected.
\vec{E}_{1}
is distributed as
{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}(n,\vec{p}_{1})
with
\vec{p}_{1}=1-(1-p_{1})^{1/2}\sim p_{1}/2
and
p_{1}=(\varepsilon/2)\log n/n
.

E_{2}
is distributed as
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p_{2})
with
p_{2}=(1+\varepsilon/2)\log n/n
.

\vec{E}_{3}
is distributed as
{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}(n,\vec{p}_{3})
with
\vec{p}_{3}=1-(1-p_{2})^{1/2}\sim p_{2}/2
.
Note that if
E_{i}
(
i\in\{1,3\}
) is obtained from
\vec{E}_{i}
by forgetting the orientation of the edges, then it is distributed as
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p_{i})
with
p_{1}
defined above and
p_{3}=p_{2}
.

Given a vertex
v
in an oriented hypergraph, its out-edges are the edges that have
v
as a tail. Out-degrees, out-neighbourhoods and other related notions are defined in
the obvious way. We say
v\in[n]
is good if its out-degree is at least
d
in
\vec{E}_{1}
.
Otherwise we call vertex
v
bad.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
d\sim\beta\log n
and
p\sim\alpha\log n/n
.
The number of vertices in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
of degree at most
d
is a.a.s. at most
n^{\theta+o(1)}
, where
\theta=1-\alpha+\beta\log(e\alpha/\beta)
. The same holds true for the number of vertices in
{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}(n,p)
of out-degree at most
d
.

Proof.

In either case, the number of such vertices is
\text{Bin}(n-1,p)
, so its expectation is at most

n\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor d\rfloor}\binom{n-1}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-1-k}\leqslant(1+o(1))n\left(\frac{enp}{d}\right)^{d}e^{-pn}\sim n^{\theta+o(1)}.

The result follows immediately from Markov’s inequality.
∎

Note that with
\alpha=\varepsilon/4
and
\beta=\varepsilon^{2}
in Lemma
7.2
, we get that
\theta=1-\varepsilon/4+\varepsilon^{2}\log(e/(4\varepsilon))<1-\varepsilon/8
(for sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{0}
), so a.a.s there are at most
n^{1-\varepsilon/8}
bad vertices. We call this event
\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

Let
V_{G}
be the set of good vertices and
V_{B}=[n]\setminus V_{G}
the set of bad ones. Let
E_{2}^{\prime}=\{e\in E_{2}:|e\cap V_{B}|\leqslant 1\}
, that is, the set of edges in
E_{2}
that are incident with at most one bad vertex. We can orient each edge in
E_{2}^{\prime}
with exactly one bad vertex by making that bad vertex the tail, and edges with no bad vertices are oriented following any arbitrary deterministic rule. The resulting oriented hypergraph is denoted
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{2}
be the event that every bad vertex has degree at least
d+1
in
E_{2}^{\prime}
(or equivalently out-degree at least
d+1
in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
). The reason why we ask degree
d+1
instead of
d
is technical and will become apparent later. Again by Lemma
7.2
with
\alpha=1+\varepsilon/2
and
\beta=\varepsilon^{2}
, we have that
\theta=-\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon^{2}\log(e(1+\varepsilon/2))/\varepsilon^{2})<-\varepsilon/4<0
(for sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{0}
), so
\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds a.a.s.

Then define
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}=\{e\in\vec{E}_{3}:|e\cap V_{B}|>1\}
and let
E_{3}^{\prime}
be the undirected version of
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
resulting from ignoring edge orientations. Edges in
E_{2}^{\prime}
appear with probability
p_{2}
among those potential edges containing at most one bad vertex. Edges in
E_{3}^{\prime}
appear with probability
1-(1-p_{3})^{2}=p_{2}
among the other potential edges that are not elegible for
E_{2}^{\prime}
. In other words, even though each one of
E_{2}^{\prime}
and
E_{3}^{\prime}
depend on the set of good vertices (and thus on
E_{1}
),
E_{2}^{\prime}\cup E_{3}^{\prime}
is distributed as
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p_{2})
and is independent of
E_{1}
. Then
E_{1}\cup E_{2}^{\prime}\cup E_{3}^{\prime}
is distributed as
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{1}p_{2})
which can be trivially included inside of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
since
p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{1}p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}+p_{2}=p
.
Hence, all it remains to show is that in the event that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds, we can find a copy of
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}(n,d)
contained in
\vec{E}_{1}\cup\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}\cup\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
, which after forgetting orientations we just showed is contained in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
. We denote such copy of
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}(n,d)
by
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}
. If
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}
fails, just pick
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}
independently from everything else so the coupling fails, but this occurs with probability
o(1)
.

Expose the out-degrees of
\vec{E}_{1}
and the degrees of
E_{2}^{\prime}
(but nothing else), and suppose that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds. In particular, we know which vertices are good or bad. For every vertex
v\in[n]
, we want to pick a set of
d
out-edges of
v
uniformly at random and independently of all other choices. These will be the out-edges of
v
in
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}
. If
v
is good, this is straightforward, since
v
has out-degree
d_{v}\geqslant d
in
E_{1}
and all sets of
d_{v}
out-neighbours are equally likely and independent of those of other vertices, so we can just pick a random subset of size
d
among those.

If
v
is bad, picking its
d
out-edges in
{\vec{\mathcal{O}}}
is trickier. We will be using edges in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}\cup\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
with tail
v
. In particular, we will only use edges in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
with exactly one bad vertex, and ignore those with no bad vertices. Recall that edges of type 2 were originally unoriented, but we oriented them in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
so that their only bad vertex (if they have one) is the tail. We will also use edges in
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
that have tail
v
. Most bad vertices will not pick vertices from
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
, but some will. Note that the edge densities of
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
and
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
are different since each out-edge of
v
with only one bad vertex appears in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
with probability
p_{2}
(before conditioning on degrees of
E_{2}
) while each out-edge of
v
with more bad vertices appears in
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
with probability
p_{3}\sim p_{2}/2
. This is a delicate issue that complicates things and is possibly the crux of the argument. We cannot make the density of
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
smaller a priori since we want to make sure that bad vertices have enough out-edges and we cannot make the density of
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
larger since otherwise the coupling with
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
would not work.

To make the argument slightly simpler, instead of having each bad vertex
v
pick a set of
d
distinct out-edges, we will have it sample
d+1
out-edges with replacement from all possible edges from
v
to
[n]\setminus\{v\}
. Of course, the same out-edge could be picked multiple times but with probability
1-O(\log^{4}/n)
every bad edge picks at most one repeated pair of out-edges. Call this event
\mathcal{F}_{3}
. In other words, if
\mathcal{F}_{3}
holds, then every bad edge will pick a set of either
d
or
d+1
different out-edges.

Let us proceed to do this carefully. Let
v
be a bad vertex. Let
X=X_{v}
be the set of all possible out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
, i.e. edges with tail at
v
and a good vertex as head. Note
x=|X|=|V_{G}|\sim n
. Let
Y=Y_{v}
be the set of all possible out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
, i.e. edges with tail at
v
and head at another bad vertex. Clearly,
y=|Y|=n-1-x\leqslant n^{-\varepsilon/8}x
by
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. Since we are also assuming that
\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds, the out-degree of
v
in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
is
s_{v}\geqslant d+1
, so the set
S=S_{v}
of out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}
is a random subset of
X
of size
s_{v}\geqslant d+1
. Let
S^{\prime}
be a random subset of
S
of size exactly
d+1
(which is still uniformly chosen among all subsets of
X
of that size). Also, let
T=T_{v}
be the set of out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
. Now
t=|T|
is distributed as
\text{Bin}(y,\vec{p}_{3})
. Our goal is to find a coupling
U\subseteq S^{\prime}\cup T
where
U=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d+1}\}
is obtained by uniformly and independently sampling
d+1
random elements of
X\cup Y
(where recall
X\cup Y
is the set of all possible out-edges of
v
) with replacement (i.e.
|U|\leqslant d+1
and could be strictly smaller if the same edge is sampled twice).

To do this, for each
i=1,\ldots,d+1
, let
a_{i}
be an integer in
[x+y]=\left[n-1\right]
chosen uniformly at random (and independently of other choices). We can think of the edges in
X\cup Y
as having distinct labels in
[x+y]
so we pick
u_{i}
by first picking its label
a_{i}
, and later revealing what edge corresponds to that label. However, all we know so far is that edges in
Y
have labels in
[y]
and edges in
X
have labels in
[x+y]\setminus[y]
. The label of each individual edge is still undecided. The reason to do this is to see: 1) how many different edges are in
U
after possible edge repetitions, and 2) how many of these fall in
X
or
Y
. If
a_{i}\leqslant y
, that means that
u_{i}
will land in
Y
(which has probability
y/(x+y)\leqslant n^{-\varepsilon/8}
). Otherwise, it lands in
X
. Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{d+1}\}
,
A_{X}=A\setminus[y]
and
A_{Y}=A\cap[y]
. Then
u=|U|=|A|
,
u_{X}=|U\cap X|=|A_{X}|
and
u_{Y}=|U\cap Y|=|A_{Y}|
. Let
\ell
be the number of indices
i=1,\ldots,d+1
such that
a_{i}\in[y]
(or equivalently
u_{i}\in Y
). Clearly,
\ell
has distribution
\text{Bin}(d+1,y/(x+y))
and moreover
u_{Y}\leqslant\ell
by construction. If
u_{Y}\leqslant t
, then we can assign the
u_{Y}
labels in
A_{Y}
to a random set of edges in
T
. Similarly, we can always assign the
u_{X}\leqslant d+1=|S^{\prime}|
labels in
A_{X}
to a random set of edges in
S^{\prime}
. Doing so, the
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d+1}
are i.i.d. uniformly chosen from
X\cup Y
as desired. For all of this to work, it is enough to find a coupling in which
\ell\leqslant t
, or in other words
\text{Bin}(d+1,y/(x+y))\leqslant\text{Bin}(y,\vec{p}_{3})
. Ignoring
(1+o(1))
factors in the parameters, all we need is a coupling

\text{Bin}(\varepsilon^{2}\log n,1/n^{\varepsilon/8})\leqslant\text{Bin}\left(n^{1-\varepsilon/8},\frac{1+\varepsilon/2}{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\right).

(7.1)

The coupling in (
7.1
) exists (see e.g. Lemma 6 in
[
6
]
with
N=d+1\sim\varepsilon^{2}\log n
,
P=1/n^{-\varepsilon/8}
,
K=\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{n^{\varepsilon/8}\log n}
and
L=\frac{1+\varepsilon/2}{2\varepsilon^{2}}
).

Summarizing, for each bad vertex
v
we were able to sample
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d+1}
uniformly at random from the set of all
n-1
out-edges from
v
and indepdently from all other vertices in a way that
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d+1}
are also in
\vec{E}_{2}^{\prime}\cup\vec{E}_{3}^{\prime}
(assuming
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
). Also, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{3}
, we have
|\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d+1}\}|\in\{d,d+1\}
and thus we can sample a subset of
d
out-edges from
v
uniformly at random with the same properties. All these edges (after ignoring orientations) are in
E_{2}^{\prime}\cup E_{3}^{\prime}
and thus in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
.
This gives the desired coupling and completes the proof of the proposition.∎

We now proceed to the proof of Theorem
1.6
. Let
p=x\log n/n
and
d\leqslant f(x)\log n
for
x=x(n)\geqslant 1+\varepsilon
and function
f
as in the statement of Proposition
7.1
. Then there is a coupling in which
{\mathcal{O}}(n,d)\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
a.a.s. In turn, we can always couple
\lfloor d/2\rfloor
independent copies
{\mathcal{O}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{O}}_{k}
of
{\mathcal{O}}(n,2)
so that
{\mathcal{O}}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\mathcal{O}}_{k}\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}(n,d)
. It was shown by Frieze
[
4
]
that a.a.s.

contains a perfect matching (for even
n
). As a result, it follows from Markov’s inequality that a.a.s. all but a
o(1)
fraction of the
{\mathcal{O}}_{i}
contain a perfect matching. Pick one random perfect matching
M_{i}
from each
{\mathcal{O}}_{i}
that contains some. These perfect matchings are uniform and i.i.d. by construction. Hence, a.a.s.
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
contains the union of

uniform independent perfect matchings. This finishes the proof of the theorem with

.∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5

By taking the graph complement, part (a) follows immediately from parts (b,c,d).
To prove parts (b,c,d) we consider two cases in terms of the pairity of
n
.

8.1
Even
n

Suppose that

is even. If
d=O(1)
, then let
\omega(1)=\tau=o(\log n)
and by Theorem

(b), there is a coupling such that a.a.s.

, which implies that
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\cup}
. By Theorem
1.6
, there is a coupling such that a.a.s.
\mathcal{M}_{\tau}^{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n,(1+\varepsilon)\log n/n)
.

Suppose
\omega(1)=d=o(\log n)
. By Corollary
1.7
, there exists a coupling such that a.a.s.
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\tau(d)}^{\cup}
for some
\tau(d)=(2+o(1))d
. By Theorem

, there is a coupling such that a.a.s.

. Now part (c) follows by combining the above two cases.

For parts (b), the embedding of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p_{*})
into
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
was already shown in previous works, e.g.
[
13
]
. To embedd
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
into
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p^{*})
, we observe that there is a coupling such that
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\tau(d)}^{\cup}
for some

. Then, there is a coupling such that
\mathcal{M}_{\tau(d)}^{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n,p^{*})

1.6
.

Finally for part (d), the embedding of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
into
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p^{*})
is exactly the same as in part (b).

8.2
Odd
n

Suppose that
n
is odd. In this case,
d
is necessarily even. Couple
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
and
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
as follows.

(i)

Couple
(G_{1},G_{2})
where
G_{1}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n-1,d-1)
, and
G_{2}\in{\mathcal{G}}(n-1,p^{\prime})
where
p^{\prime}=p^{*}
in Theorem
1.6
for even

. In particular, in case (c),
p^{*}
was chosen to satisfy the assertion for
\varepsilon/3
.

(ii)

generate
\hat{G}
by uniformly choosing
d
vertices
x_{1},…x_{d}
in
G_{1}
, join them to the new vertex
n
, and then adding an independent uniform random perfect matching
M
on the remaining
n-1-d
vertices disjoint from
G_{1}
.

(iii)

Let
p=1-(1-p^{\prime})(1-(1-\varepsilon/3)\log n/n)
, and
D\sim\operatorname{\bf Bin}(n-1,p)
. Let
z=\max\{0,D-d\}
.
Construct
G^{*}
by joining vertex
n
with
x_{1},…,x_{d}
, and
z
other vertices uniformly chosen from
[n-1]\setminus\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
, and then taking the union with
G^{\prime}
where
G^{\prime}\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n-1-d,(1+\varepsilon/3)\log n/n)
is independent with

and the choices for the neighbours of vertex
n
.

Now the existence of the coupling such that a.a.s.
{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
is implied by the following lemma, and the observation that
p\sim p^{\prime}+(1+\varepsilon/3)\log n/n
.

Lemma 8.1
.

(a)

There is a coupling
(\hat{G},G_{d})
such that
G_{d}\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n,d)
and
{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{G}=G_{d})=1-o(1)
.

(b)

G^{*}\sim{\mathcal{G}}(n,p^{\prime})
.

(c)

(M,G’)
can be coupled so that a.a.s.

.

Proof.

Part (b) is obvious.
Part (a) follows by a similar proof as Theorem
1.12
, which we briefly sketch as follows. Note that for every realisation
G_{1}
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
, the number of choices of
M
is highly concentrated. On the other hand, for every
d
-regular
G
on

, the number of ways to specify

and

is exactly the number of perfect matchings on the subgraph of

inducced by
[n]\setminus N_{G}[\{n\})]
, which is concentrated following the similar proof as Lemma

.

For part (c) it suffices to show that a.a.s.

has a perfect matching. We already know that there is a coupling such that
G_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(n-1,p^{*})
. Hence, there is a coupling such that
{\mathcal{G}}(n-1-d,\hat{p})\subseteq G^{\prime}\setminus G_{1}
a.a.s. where

, and thus a.a.s.
