Pseudo-Euclidean representations of switching classes of Johnson and Hamming graphs with minimal dimension
Hiroshi Nozaki, Masashi Shinohara, Sho Suda

Abstract
This paper considers minimum-dimensional representations of graphs in pseudo-Euclidean spaces, where adjacency and non-adjacency relations are reflected in fixed scalar square values. A representation of a simple graph ( V , E ) (V,E) is a mapping φ \varphi from the vertices to the pseudo-Euclidean space ℝ p , q \mathbb{R}^{p,q} such that ‖ φ ⁡ ( u ) − φ ⁡ ( v ) ‖ = { a ​ if ( u , v ) ∈ E , b ​ if ( u , v ) ∉ E and u ≠ v , 0 ​ if u = v ||\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)||=\begin{cases}a\text{ if $(u,v)\in E$},\\ b\text{ if $(u,v)\not\in E$ and $u\neq v$},\\ 0\text{ if $u=v$}\\ \end{cases} for some a , b ∈ ℝ a,b\in\mathbb{R} , where ‖ 𝒙 ‖ = ⟨ ⟨ 𝒙 , 𝒙 ⟩ ⟩ = ∑ i = 1 p x i 2 − ∑ j = 1 q x p + j 2 ||\boldsymbol{x}||=\langle\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{q}x_{p+j}^{2} is the scalar square of 𝒙 \boldsymbol{x} in ℝ p , q \mathbb{R}^{p,q} . For a finite set X X in ℝ p , q \mathbb{R}^{p,q} , we define A ( X ) = { | | 𝒙 − 𝒚 | | : 𝒙 , 𝒚 ∈ X , 𝒙 ≠ 𝒚 } . A(X)=\{||\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}||\colon\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X,\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y}\}. A finite set X X in ℝ p , q \mathbb{R}^{p,q} is called an s s -indefinite-distance set if | A ⁡ ( X ) | = s |A(X)|=s holds. An s s -indefinite-distance set in ℝ p , 0 = ℝ p \mathbb{R}^{p,0}=\mathbb{R}^{p} is called an s s -distance set. Graphs obtained from Seidel switching of a Johnson graph sometimes admit Euclidean or pseudo-Euclidean representations in low dimensions relative to the number of vertices. For instance, Lisoněk (1997) obtained a largest 2-distance set in ℝ 8 \mathbb{R}^{8} and largest spherical 2-indefinite-distance sets in ℝ p , 1 \mathbb{R}^{p,1} for each p ≥ 10 p\geq 10 from the switching classes of Johnson graphs. In the present paper, we consider graphs in the switching classes of Johnson and Hamming graphs and classify those that admit representations in ℝ p , q \mathbb{R}^{p,q} with the smallest possible dimensionality p + q p+q among all graphs in the same class. The method not only recovers known results, such as the largest 2-(indefinite)-distance sets constructed by Lisoněk, but also provides a unified framework for determining the minimum dimension of representations for entire switching classes of strongly regular graphs.

A

Let

n

The

\boldsymbol{A}
of

(0,1)
-matrix indexed by

(u,v)

\boldsymbol{A}(u,v)=\begin{cases}1\text{ if $(u,v)\in E$},\\

Let

\overline{G}

A matrix

on
G

.
A dissimilarity matrix
\boldsymbol{D}(a,b)
on
G
is said to be
representable in
\mathbb{R}^{p,q}
if there exists a subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{p,q}

\boldsymbol{D}(X)=\boldsymbol{D}(a,b)
.
Here,
\boldsymbol{D}(X)
denotes the matrix
(||\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}||)_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}
.
This set

\boldsymbol{D}(a,b)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be an

are the coordinates of the
n

X\subset\mathbb{R}^{p,q}

The

of

{\rm rank}(\boldsymbol{X})
among all representations
X
of
\boldsymbol{D}(a,b)
.
Let
\boldsymbol{I}
denote the identity matrix and
\boldsymbol{j}

For a symmetric matrix

Note that the matrix
\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{j}^{\top}
is a projection matrix onto

\boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}
.
By direct calculation, we can show the equality

\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{D}(X)}(\boldsymbol{\ell})=(-2\langle\langle\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{u}\rangle\rangle)_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X},

where
\boldsymbol{u}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the
\boldsymbol{x}
-th entry of
\boldsymbol{\ell}
.

The pair
(r,s)
is called the

of symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
, where

(

Gower

showed that the signature of

Theorem 2.1

(Theorems 8 and 9 in
[
7
]
)

Let

. Moreover,

.

Dimensions of representations of graphs in switching classes

This section considers the dimensionalities of graph representations in a regular graph’s switching class.
For a simple graph
(V,E)
,
Seidel switching
(or simply
switching
) with respect to a subset
U\subset V
is the operation that produces a new graph
(V,E^{\prime})
, where
\{u,v\}\in E^{\prime}
if and only if one of the following holds:

\displaystyle\{u,v\}\in E

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for }u,v\in U,

\displaystyle\{u,v\}\in E

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for }u,v\in V\setminus U,

\displaystyle\{u,v\}\notin E

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for }u\in U,\,v\in V\setminus U.

The
switching class
of
(V,E)
is the set of all graphs resulting from the switching operation of
(V,E)
with respect to any subset
U\subset V
. Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be the adjacency matrix of
(V,E)
, and
\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}
that of its complement graph.
The

of
(V,E)
is
\boldsymbol{A}-\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}
.
For

\boldsymbol{I}_{U}
denote the diagonal matrix indexed by
V
whose

-1

1

The Seidel matrix of the graph
(V,E^{\prime})
obtained by switching with respect to
U

\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}-\overline{\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{I}_{U}(\boldsymbol{A}-\overline{\boldsymbol{A}})\boldsymbol{I}_{U}
,
where
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
is the adjacency matrix of
(V,E^{\prime})
.
Throughout this paper, we use the symbol
′
(apostrophe) to denote the corresponding object associated with the graph obtained by switching.

We investigate the relationship between the dimensionalities of
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{D}(a,b)=a\boldsymbol{A}+b\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}

\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}(a,b)=a\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}+b\overline{\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}}

(i,k)\not\in U
) for any
k\in X
.
If
a_{i}=a_{1}+1
, then
a_{i}+b_{k}=1
(
(i,k)\in U
) for any
k\in X
.
Therefore,
U=U_{I}
such that
I=\{i\colon\,a_{i}=a_{1}+1\}
.

From the assumption
2|U|\leq|V|
, we choose
I
such that
|I|\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor
.
Thus,
\mathcal{U}
consists of
U_{I}=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
and
V_{I}=\bigcup_{i\in I}V_{i}
for any index set
I\subset X
with
|I|\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor
.

The eigenvalues of
2\boldsymbol{D}-3\boldsymbol{J}
(which are the same as those of
2\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}-3\boldsymbol{J}
) are

\mu_{0}=m(m-4),\qquad\mu_{1}=-2m,\qquad\mu_{2}=0

by Lemma
3.3
.
The eigenspace of
2\boldsymbol{D}-3\boldsymbol{J}
associated with the eigenvalue 0 is

E_{2}
for
m\geq 5
and
m=3
, and
E_{0}\oplus E_{2}
for
m=4
.
For
m\geq 5
,
m=3
(
resp
.
m=4
),
if
U\not\in\mathcal{U}
holds, then
the dimensionality of
\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}
is greater than (
resp
. equal to) that of
\boldsymbol{D}
by Theorem
4.1
(1) (
resp
. (i)).

Consider the cases where the number of main eigenvalues is 1 and 2, respectively.

(I) If
m
is even and
|U|=|V|/2
holds for
U\in\mathcal{U}
, then the main eigenvalue of
2\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}-3\boldsymbol{J}
is only
\mu_{1}<0
by Theorem
4.1
(3), (ii).
Since the main eigenvalue is only
\mu_{1}
, we have

\boldsymbol{j}\in\boldsymbol{I}_{U}E_{1}
, and the eigenspace of
2\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}-3\boldsymbol{J}
associated with
0
is contained in
\boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}
.
In this case,
{\rm sign}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\ell}))=(2m-3,0)
for
m=4
and
{\rm sign}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\ell}))=(2m-3,1)
for
m>4
even by Theorem
2.2
(3).
For

and
U\in\mathcal{U}
with
|U|\neq|V|/2
, we have
{\rm sign}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\ell}))=(2m-2,0)
by Theorem

(i).
Therefore the assertion (3) follows.

(II) For
m\geq 5
,
m=3
and
U\in\mathcal{U}
with
|U|\neq|V|/2
, we calculate the signature of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})
with the main angles of
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
.
For
U_{I}\in\mathcal{U}
or
V_{I}\in\mathcal{U}
with
|I|=s
, the main angles satisfy

|V|\beta_{0}^{2}=(m-2s)^{2},\qquad|V|\beta_{1}^{2}=4s(m-s),\qquad\beta_{2}^{2}=0

by Lemma
3.8
.
The signature of the matrix
2\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}-3\boldsymbol{J}
is

\begin{cases}(0,2m-1)\text{ if $m=3$},\\
(1,2m-2)\text{ if $m\geq 5$}.\end{cases}

It follows that

|V|\sum_{i=0}^{1}\frac{\beta_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}=\frac{2s(s-m)(m-2)+m^{2}}{m(m-4)}\begin{cases}<0\text{ if $m=3$, or $t_{m}<s<m/2$ and $m\geq 5$},\\
=0\text{ if $s=t_{m}$},\\
>0\text{ if $0<s<t_{m}$ and $m\geq 5$},\end{cases}

where

t_{m}=\frac{m}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m-4}{m-2}}\right).

We prove that
\sqrt{(m-4)/(m-2)}
is irrational, and that the value
t_{m}
is not an integer.
Assume, for contradiction, that there exist integers
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
(m-4)/(m-2)=(k/l)^{2}
. This assumption is valid since we may take
k,l
to be positive for
m\geq 5
.
The greatest common divisor of
m-2
and
m-4
is 1 if
m
is odd, and it is 2 if
m
is even. If
m
is odd, then
k^{2}=m-4
and
l^{2}=m-2
.
There do not exist positive integers

such that
2=l^{2}-k^{2}=(l+k)(l-k)
holds.
If
m
is even, then
k^{2}=(m-4)/2
and
l^{2}=(m-2)/2
.
There do not exist positive integers
k,l
such that
1=l^{2}-k^{2}=(l+k)(l-k)
holds.

By Theorem
2.2
,
the signature of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\begin{cases}(2m-2,0)\text{ if $m=3$},\\
(2m-3,1)\text{ if $t_{m}<s<m/2$ and $m\geq 5$},\\
(2m-2,0)\text{ if $0<s<t_{m}$ and $m\geq 5$}.\end{cases}

Combining the cases (I) and (II) together with the case

, we obtain

\begin{cases}(2m-3,1)\text{ if $t_{m}<s\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ and $m\geq 5$},\\
(2m-2,0)\text{ if $0\leq s<t_{m}$ and $m\geq 5$}.\end{cases}

This implies the assertion (4).

For
