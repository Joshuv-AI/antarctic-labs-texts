Fedratinib in chronic kidney disease: antifibrotic potential and renal safety signals from integrated network toxicology and pharmacovigilance
Authors: Rubin Zheng, Jiaxi Chen, Qin Wang, Jiayi Lyu, Miao Deng, Jinfen Han, Haiou Wang, Zhixun Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD), a major global health challenge driven by renal fibrosis, lacks effective antifibrotic therapies. Fedratinib, a JAK2 inhibitor for myelofibrosis, shows kinase-modulating potential but uncharacterized utility and safety in CKD. Computational toxicity prediction utilized ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab2.0. Fedratinib-CKD targets were identified via combinatorial screening across databases (ChEMBL, STITCH, SwissTargetPrediction, GeneCards, OMIM, TTD). Core hubs were determined through protein-protein interaction network (STRING) and topological analysis (Cytoscape). Hierarchical docking validated binding (CB-Dock2). Post-marketing safety data (2019–2025) from FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) was analyzed using disproportionality methods, including reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN) and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS). Computational profiling predicted severe respiratory, hepatic and cardiac toxicity but negligible nephrotoxicity. Network analysis identified 281 shared targets, with SRC, AKT1, EGFR and JAK2 as core hubs. Pathway enrichment revealed profound suppression of PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling. Docking confirmed high-affinity binding. FAERS analysis of 1,268 reports identified significant renal and urinary disorders (ROR = 1.71) and extreme vitamin B1 deficiency (ROR = 1262.53), contradicting computational predictions. Fedratinib exhibits therapeutic duality: inhibition of SRC and JAK2 attenuates fibrosis by inhibiting JAK-STAT/TGFβ/Smad3 pathways, while off-target binding to AKT1/EGFR drives renal injury and vitamin B1 depletion-related toxicity with black-box encephalopathy warning. Evidence suggests for CKD patients with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 or age ≥70 years, a 50% dose reduction (200 mg/day) with renal function and thiamin monitoring (<30 nmol/L) may optimize risk-benefit profile.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) poses a continuing global challenge, with its fibrotic progression conferring high mortality and remaining poorly suppressed by standard care [1–3]. Consequently, novel therapeutics targeting fibrogenic signaling constitute an urgent clinical imperative. Currently, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) and renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors constitute cornerstone CKD therapies, yet fail to reverse established renal interstitial fibrosis—a pivotal pathological process involving myofibroblast activation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and inflammatory-metabolic crosstalk [4]. The Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway emerges as a critical convergent node in such fibrogenesis, with persistent activation exacerbating fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition [5]. Traditional single-target cytokine therapies demonstrate limited efficacy against multi-pathway dysregulation, as evidenced by clinical outcomes targeting mediators such as IL-6 or TNF-α [6,7]. This mechanistic insight firmly establishes JAK2 inhibition as a rational antifibrotic strategy. However, the clinical translation of this strategy is critically complicated by a defining of off-target toxicity: thiamin depletion. The pathophysiological basis of its black-box encephalopathy warning, which is rooted in impaired oxidative metabolism, directly justifies urgent renal investigation [8]. The kidney, particularly the proximal tubule, exhibits one of the highest metabolic rates in the body and is fundamentally dependent on thiamin for aerobic energy production [9,10]. Therefore, the proven neurotoxicity of severe thiamin deficiency serves as a powerful predictor of concurrent, yet uncharacterized, nephrotoxicity [11]. This intrinsic link mandates a systematic renal risk-benefit evaluation, positioning encephalopathy not as a distant warning, but as a direct mechanistic rationale for probing its renal sequelae. In contrast, fedratinib—a clinically approved JAK2 inhibitor for myelofibrosis [12,13]—potently suppresses STAT3 and STAT5 phosphorylation, subsequently blocking downstream profibrotic signaling cascades [14]. Its Food and Drug Administration (FDA) black-box warning for encephalopathy, however, mandates rigorous renal safety profiling [15,16].

Significant knowledge gaps persist concerning fedratinib’s renal implications. The potential for off-target toxicities is further complicated by its complex pharmacokinetic profile, which involves interactions with drug transporters [17]. Critically, comprehensive safety profiling—particularly renal-specific adverse signals—remains underquantified in real-world populations, despite documented neurological risks in clinical trials [12]. Conventional unimodal approaches fail to resolve this dual knowledge gap due to inherent limitations: while network pharmacology and in silico toxicology computationally infer system-wide interactions, it cannot validate binding [18]; molecular docking provides validated predictions of binding affinity but ignores physiological context [19]; spontaneous reporting systems statistically detect safety signals with varying specificity yet lack mechanistic insight [20]. To address these constraints, we integrate a synergistic framework that leverages computational network pharmacology and in silico toxicology to decipher global target-pathway interactions, applies hierarchical molecular docking to validate binding conformations at prioritized targets, and implements systematic FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) mining with disproportionality analysis—quantifying multi-organ toxicity signals with particular attention to renal adverse outcomes. This integrated study therefore aims to define the renal risk-benefit profile of fedratinib, with its black-box warning for encephalopathy constituting a critical context for its potential repurposing in CKD. This multidimensional integration uniquely converges mechanistic plausibility, structural feasibility, and clinical relevance.

Beyond these unimodal limitations, our study delineates fedratinib’s renal actions across molecular, structural, and population dimensions. Our integrated network pharmacology and in silico toxicology approach, grounded in JAK-STAT’s established role in renal fibrosis [5], predicts fedratinib’s engagement with non-canonical targets beyond JAK2—such as SRC, AKT1 and EGFR—directly implicating off-target toxicity pathways in CKD progression that warrant experimental validation. Hierarchical molecular docking, a method validated for pose prediction in studies such as [21], confirmed high-affinity binding conformations at the prioritized targets, revealing critical structural determinants of on-target and potential off-target interactions. Complementarily, comprehensive FAERS mining evaluates population-wide adverse event (AE) profiles across all organ systems, with renal-specific disproportionality analysis confirming statistically significant associations for renal and urinary disorders, indicating clinically relevant nephrotoxicity signals.

Collectively, this study establishes a transformative kidney safety framework bridging computational prediction and real-world evidence. The delineated kidney safety profile of fedratinib—characterizing off-target engagements such as SRC and AKT1 alongside CKD-specific toxicity mechanisms—reveals structural and pathological rationales for clinical surveillance, specifically informed by FAERS-driven insights: statistically confirmed renal toxicity of fedratinib signals necessitating vigilance in CKD populations, dose-dependent neurological risks requiring renal function-adjusted dosing, and critical vitamin B1 deficiency serving as an early-warning biomarker for metabolic toxicity exacerbating renal decline. Beyond fedratinib-specific insights, the integrated methodology pioneers a generalizable paradigm for kinase inhibitor (KI) safety assessment, resolving critical gaps in traditional pharmacovigilance through synergistic validation of mechanistic plausibility, structural feasibility, and population-level relevance. By converging multi-scale evidence streams into a unified risk stratification calculus, this work redefines precision nephrology standards for high-risk therapeutics, delivering an evidence-to-practice pipeline translating multi-target drug development.

Methods

Network pharmacology analysis and computational toxicity prediction

In this study, computational toxicity prediction was performed to evaluate the safety profile of fedratinib. The compound’s canonical SMILES structure code (CC1 = CN = C(N = C1NC2 = CC(=CC = C2)S(=O)(=O)NC(C)(C)C)NC3 = CC = C(C = C3)OCCN4CCCC4) was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Subsequently, systematic evaluations were carried out using the ProTox 3.0 (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/) and ADMETlab 2.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com) prediction platforms. These two platforms generate multi - dimensional toxicity parameter data by leveraging the compound’s structural features and established toxicity prediction models. By cross-validating the prediction results of the two platforms, a preliminary cross-validation framework for the toxicological characteristics of fedratinib was constructed.

Collection of fedratinib targets

To comprehensively identify the potential action targets of fedratinib, data from multiple databases were integrated in this study. First, based on the canonical SMILES code obtained, known targets were retrieved from the ChEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) with Homo sapiens specified as the species. Subsequently, the SMILES sequence was simultaneously submitted to the STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de/) and SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) platforms for complementary target prediction, aiming to uncover potentially overlooked interacting targets. After cross-validating the prediction results of the three databases, the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) was utilized to standardize the target names. Then, data from multiple platforms were combined, and nonspecific targets were filtered out. Finally, a structurally complete and data - consistent target dataset was established.

Selection of CKD-related target networks

This study integrated resources from three authoritative databases: GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), and the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD, https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). These databases contain large-scale multi-omics data on the associations between human genes and genetic diseases, enabling systematic screening of potential key targets in CKD. During data processing, differentiated screening strategies were applied based on database-specific characteristics. For GeneCards, a strict screening threshold of “Relevance score > 10” was imposed using its quantitative evaluation metrics. As OMIM and TTD lack quantitative scoring systems, their raw search results were retained to ensure data integrity. A Venn diagram was then employed to analyze and identify overlapping targets between fedratinib’s action targets and CKD-related targets, with intersecting nodes designated as potential drug targets for CKD intervention. The remaining 5451 targets (5732 − 281) represent CKD-specific pathological factors not directly modulated by fedratinib, while the 248 fedratinib-exclusive targets (529 − 281) reflect non-renal pharmacological actions; both were excluded from further mechanistic analysis. This approach prioritizes targets with direct drug-disease interaction for network construction, ensuring biological relevance. Following systematic target analysis, an interaction network between fedratinib and CKD was constructed to characterize its multi-target action profile. Additionally, Cytoscape (version 3.10.0) was utilized to visualize a compound-target-disease network based on the curated target information.

Construction of protein - protein interaction networks and screening of core targets

To characterize the drug-target interaction network, common action genes of fedratinib and CKD were submitted to the STRING online platform (https://cn.string-db.org/), with species restricted to Homo sapiens. A medium confidence threshold (>0.4) was applied to filter protein-protein interaction (PPI) relationships, and disconnected nodes were removed from the network visualization. Following acquisition of preliminary interaction data, Cytoscape was employed to construct a PPI network diagram, with node connectivity parameters calculated using the software’s built-in algorithms. Additionally, core targets of fedratinib in CKD were identified using a threshold of degree value > twice the median.

Enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was carried out using the clusterProfiler package in R. Target gene identifiers were standardized via the org.Hs.eg.db human gene annotation database. The stringr package was employed for differential gene expression data cleaning, aiming to systematically dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying fedratinib’s effects on CKD. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis encompassed three functional domains: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). A dual-significance threshold was applied (p-value < 0.05; false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.05, based on the Benjamini–Hochberg method), with the top 5 significantly enriched terms for each subclass selected based on adjusted p-value ranking and relevance to CKD pathophysiology. The gene ratio, defined as the proportion of genes enriched in each term relative to the total number of input genes, was also reported for each result. For Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, the top 5 enriched pathways were selected. Multidimensional visualizations were generated using the ggplot2, ggpubr, and circlize R packages, intuitively illustrating how the drug regulates disease phenotypes through coordinated BP-signaling pathway interactions.

Molecular docking validation

In this study, the top three key targets ranked by degree value were selected for molecular docking validation with fedratinib. The SDF structure file of fedratinib was first retrieved from PubChem. Core target protein structures in Protein Data Bank (PDB) format were then downloaded from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) using their UniProt IDs. Docking analyses were performed using the CB-Dock2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/), which integrates CurPocket curvature-based cavity detection with AutoDock Vina. The platform automatically performs ligand and target protein hydrogenation, dehydration, energy optimization, and structure minimization, then identifies large surface cavities as potential binding sites to generate multiple docking conformations. CB-Dock2 selects the docking pose with the lowest Vina score, calculates binding energy, and generates 3D visualizations of binding modes to systematically characterize molecular interactions between fedratinib and core target proteins.

FAERS data acquisition and AE screening

This study utilized data sourced from the FAERS database, an internationally recognized open drug safety surveillance platform that aggregates spontaneous AE reports from global consumers, healthcare providers, and pharmaceutical entities. Post-market full-cycle monitoring data (2019–2025) were selected based on fedratinib’s FDA approval timeline, encompassing the complete temporal spectrum of drug safety signals. Raw data were downloaded in American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) report format, comprising AEs reports designating fedratinib as the primary suspect medication. Ultimately, an analytical dataset was constructed containing key variables such as report date, patient age and gender, reporter type, and geographic region.

Data preprocessing and standardization

Drug nomenclature was standardized using Medex UIMA 1.3.8 [22], and adverse reaction terminology was classified and coded according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) 26.1 standard [23], which organizes reports into hierarchical categories: Standardized MedDRA Organ Classes (SOCs) representing organ systems, and Preferred Terms (PTs) describing specific AEs. A strict data-cleaning protocol was applied to maintain data integrity and consistency. Specifically, from the initial 9,854,272 demonstration reports, duplicate records were identified and removed by retaining only the most recent entry for each unique CASEID; this process resulted in the removal of 1,523,414 reports, yielding a deduplicated dataset of 8,330,858 unique cases. Additionally, outliers were corrected, and fields were formatted uniformly [24]. This final dataset encompassed 44,702,290 drug records and 24,754,674 REAC (coded AEs) records (Figure 1). Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) and data flow diagrams were visualized via draw.io.

Disproportionality analysis and signal detection

This study evaluated fedratinib-associated AE signals using four disproportionality analyses: Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN) and Multi-item Gamma Poisson Shrinker (MGPS). All methods were applied to the 2 × 2 contingency table (Table 1). A signal was deemed positive if it met the threshold in any of the four methods, with higher values indicating a stronger drug-event association. This integrated approach ensures the detection of signals with varying strengths and avoids over-reliance on a single metric. The specific formulas and thresholds are summarized in Table 2 [25].

Result

Toxicity analysis of fedratinib

Based on the integrated computational toxicology assessment of ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab2.0, fedratinib demonstrates severe respiratory toxicity and critical drug-induced liver injury (DILI). Significant cardiotoxicity concerns arise from predicted hERG potassium channel inhibition, indicating potential arrhythmogenic effects. The compound exhibits low carcinogenic potential across both evaluation platforms. No substantial toxicological risks were identified for neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, ocular corrosion, or dermal sensitization. Crucially, the dose-dependent toxicity profile necessitates stringent therapeutic dosage control to mitigate systemic exposure hazards, particularly given the compound’s respiratory, hepatic, and cardiac risk associations (Table 3).

Initially, drug target databases such as ChEMBL, STITCH and SwissTargetPrediction were queried to screen 529 molecular targets potentially interacting with fedratinib. Concurrently, 5732 disease genes closely associated with CKD pathological processes were retrieved from disease databases including GeneCards, OMIM and TTD. Through data cleansing and cross-comparison analysis, 281 common targets of significant research value were ultimately identified, constituting a key molecular network through which fedratinib might modulate CKD pathological processes. Visual analysis using a Venn diagram intuitively illustrated the intersection between fedratinib targets and CKD-related genes, offering novel research directions for in-depth exploration of its mechanism of action (Figure 2(A)). Basic characteristics of fedratinib-related AEs

A comprehensive analysis of 1,268 AEs reports associated with fedratinib was conducted using the FAERS database spanning 2019 to 2025. Male patients predominated in the reported cases, accounting for 51.03% of events compared to 45.66% in females, with sex unspecified in 3.31% of reports. The majority of AEs occurred in elderly populations, with patients aged ≥65 years representing 50.24% of cases, while those aged 45–65 years constituted 12.62%. Markedly, 35.96% of reports lacked documented age information. Pharmacists submitted the highest proportion of reports at 42.82%, followed by consumers at 33.44% and physicians at 22.63%. Geographically, the United States originated 80.99% of reports, with other countries collectively contributing 19.01%. Oral administration was the primary exposure route, documented in 89.91% of cases. Regarding clinical outcomes, hospitalization represented 30.27% of serious AEs, followed by death at 21.31% and life-threatening events at 3.03%. Other serious outcomes accounted for 44.43% of documented sequelae. These demographic and clinical patterns are detailed in Table 6.

Fedratinib signal mining

Comprehensive disproportionality analysis of fedratinib AE reports identified 20 SOCs (Table 7). Discussion

Proteins encoded by core targets including SRC, AKT1, EGFR and JAK2 regulate pivotal signaling pathways such as JAK-STAT, MAPK and PI3K-Akt. Our following interpretation of fedratinib’s therapeutic duality must be contextualized by its unequivocal high-risk profile, underscored by the FDA black-box warning for encephalopathy. Dysregulation of these pathways propagates CKD pathogenesis through aberrant control of renal cell proliferation, apoptosis and signal transduction [26,27]. This pathological cascade sustains heightened pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion and extracellular matrix accumulation, cementing the inflammatory-fibrotic microenvironment that drives renal functional decline in CKD [28–30]. Fedratinib modulates CKD pathogenesis through polypharmacological engagement beyond its primary JAK2 inhibition [4]. Network analysis identified 281 shared CKD-related targets (Figure 2A), with topological prioritization confirming SRC, AKT1 and EGFR as core off-target hubs (Figure 4, Table 4). Hierarchical molecular docking, employing a machine learning-optimized protocol [19], validated high-affinity binding at these targets (Vina score: SRC = −8.9 kcal/mol; AKT1 = −7.0 kcal/mol; EGFR = −8.3 kcal/mol; JAK2 = −9.8 kcal/mol; Table 5), structurally rationalizing its perturbation of phosphorylation-driven fibrogenic cascades. Functional enrichment demonstrated suppression of MAPK (adjusted p-value = 4.07 × 10–39; gene ratio = 0.22) and PI3K-Akt pathways (adjusted p-value = 6.71 × 10–26; gene ratio = 0.22) [29,31], with SRC inhibition particularly attenuating TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling—a key antifibrotic mechanism [32]. These coordinated effects reduce tubulointerstitial inflammation and extracellular matrix deposition [3,33]. Significant clinical discordance emerged, driven by predictions of negligible nephrotoxicity made by computational toxicology tools ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab2.0 [34,35], while a comprehensive analysis of the FAERS database revealed statistically robust renal injury signals [20]. This included a significant system-level association for renal and urinary disorders (ROR = 1.71) and, more critically, a strong signal for the objective renal impairment biomarker blood creatinine increased (ROR = 5.18) (Tables 7 and 8). Concurrently, extreme vitamin B1 deficiency risk (ROR = 1262.53) was detected. This divergence underscores a fundamental constraint of structure-based computational profiling. While tools like ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab2.0 are adept at flagging direct structural toxins, they are inherently limited in predicting the complex, polypharmacology-driven pathophysiological cascades initiated by fedratinib’s off-target engagements [34,35]. This blind spot is exemplified by fedratinib’s nephrotoxicity, which is clearly evidenced by the significant FAERS signal for blood creatinine increased (ROR = 5.18), yet likely arises not from a primary renal insult but indirectly from mechanisms such as AKT1/EGFR-mediated metabolic stress and the potent inhibition of key transporters [36]. Such intricate, system-level mechanisms fall entirely outside the training domain of general-purpose in silico predictors. This profound vitamin B1 deficiency provides population-level validation for the mechanism behind fedratinib’s most severe, black-boxed risk, presenting a major barrier to its clinical repurposing [12,15]. FAERS analysis delineated four clinically imperative risk domains: pronounced gastrointestinal and hematological toxicities, severe neurological sequelae linked to vitamin B1 deficiency, laboratory-confirmed metabolic derangements including blood creatinine increased (ROR = 5.18), and nephrotoxicity supported by the significant renal and urinary disorders system-level signal (ROR = 1.71). Collectively, this multi-organ toxicity profile mandates enhanced pharmacovigilance with prioritized renal function monitoring. Taken together, this evidence positions fedratinib as a therapeutic paradox—balancing SRC/JAK2-mediated antifibrotic efficacy against off-target renal and metabolic toxicities driven by AKT1/EGFR disruption, foreshadowing a pathophysiological tug-of-war resolved in subsequent mechanistic analysis (Figure 8).

The therapeutic duality of fedratinib in CKD. This schematic illustrates the balance between its antifibrotic actions, achieved through inhibition of JAK2 and SRC, and its nephrotoxic risks, arising from off-target engagement with AKT1 and EGFR.

Functional enrichment analysis substantiated fedratinib’s polypharmacology: suppression of JAK-STAT signaling inhibited MAPK and PI3K-Akt pathways, attenuating tubulointerstitial inflammation by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production [37]. Concurrent disruption of TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling and direct JAK2 targeting underpinned its dual anti-fibrotic actions—blocking PI3K-Akt-mTOR to limit extracellular matrix synthesis and suppressing EGFR-driven EMT [33,38].

The off-target binding of fedratinib to SRC kinase, initially predicted by molecular docking [19], exerts potent antifibrotic effects by suppressing TGF-β1-induced phosphorylation of Smad3 at Ser423/425 residues, thereby blocking EMT and reducing collagen IV deposition by over 40% in renal tubules [39–41]. Our docking results further revealed fedratinib’s concurrent binding to AKT1, demonstrating its multi-target activity as predicted by molecular docking [19]. This interaction disrupts peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α)-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis [42]. This provokes phosphorylation of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) at serine residue 616, triggering mitochondrial fragmentation that reduces ATP production [43]. Energy-deprived cells shift to anaerobic glycolysis, elevating lactic acid concentrations (>5 mmol/L) and inducing cytoplasmic vacuolization. Complementing this metabolic dysregulation, fedratinib-mediated lipid raft destabilization impaired inflammatory receptor clustering (e.g. EGFR and TLR4), suppressing NF-κB signal transduction and pro-inflammatory cytokine production in renal cells [31]. This membrane-centric synergy with KI reinforces interference with CKD-related inflammatory pathways [15]. The resultant metabolic acidosis activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, paradoxically amplifying collagen secretion—directly counteracting SRC’s initial antifibrotic gains [44–46]. Concurrently, EGFR engagement dysregulates NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) in podocytes, elevating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidizing nephrin clusters [38,47–49]. This structural damage manifests as proteinuria (ROR = 5.18), which delivers albumin-bound fatty acids to proximal tubules [50]. These lipids activate TLR4, stimulating TGF-β1-driven EMT—reigniting the fibrotic cascade that SRC suppression had partially controlled [51]. KEGG analysis further revealed fedratinib’s modulation of calcium-dependent apoptosis and inflammatory responses [52], alongside interference with Ras-mediated fibrotic remodeling [37,53], establishing a comprehensive multi-omics framework for its kidney injury mechanisms. The toxicity apex is thiamin depletion (ROR = 1262.53). Its deficiency reduces pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity about 76–85%, flooding renal tissue with pyruvate that diverts to methylglyoxal pathways [54]. Generated advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) covalently cross-link collagen IV via lysine87-arginine93 bonds, forming protease-resistant fibrils that increase collagen deposition—permanently cementing fibrosis that SRC could not prevent [55,56]. In the central nervous system, thalamic glutamate accumulation (>100 μM) mirrors renal apoptosis through identical mitochondrial failure, explaining Wernicke’s encephalopathy (ROR = 310.65) as cerebral manifestation of systemic metabolic collapse [57–59].

ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab2.0 computational predictions demonstrating underestimated nephrotoxicity—contradicted by empirical renal injury signals (ROR 1.71)—necessitate urgent fedratinib risk mitigation protocols in CKD cohorts. Demographic vulnerability amplifies this imperative, with FAERS analysis indicating that 50.24% of AEs occurred in patients aged ≥65 years—a population exhibiting compromised renal functional reserve and increased susceptibility to off-target mitochondrial toxicity mediated by AKT1/EGFR binding [60,61]. This necessitates dose optimization stratified by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), particularly for individuals with baseline eGFR reduction exceeding 30%—a cohort demonstrating two-year end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) progression risk [62]. Critically, fedratinib’s nephrotoxic mechanism diverges from renoprotective agents: while SGLT-2i attenuate tubular metabolic stress via AMPK activation, and RAAS inhibitors suppress fibrotic pathways, fedratinib’s off-target AKT1 engagement induces metabolic toxicity and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, counteracting its antifibrotic benefits [63–66]. The FAERS-derived signals for both renal and urinary disorders (ROR = 1.71) and blood creatinine increased (ROR = 5.18) mandate vigilant renal monitoring. We therefore recommend routine serum creatinine and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio assessments, especially during the initial 60-day treatment phase, which encompassed 14.86% of the reported renal AEs. Beyond renal-specific toxicity, the profound vitamin B1 deficiency signal (ROR = 1262.53) requires careful contextualization, as the extreme disproportionality may be amplified by recognized pharmacovigilance artifacts [67]. Specifically, stimulated reporting driven by the FDA boxed warning likely contributes to case clustering, while the precise MedDRA coding for “vitamin B1 deficiency” as a known ADR may lead to its preferential reporting over nonspecific symptoms, creating a coding specificity bias against the background noise of general AE reports [68]. Nevertheless, the biological plausibility of this signal remains high, underpinned by fedratinib’s known inhibition of the human thiamin transporter 2 (hTHTR2) [69]. This unique mechanism, when contrasted with the safety profiles of other KIs, brings fedratinib’s risk-benefit duality into sharp focus.

A comparative analysis of fedratinib with other agents in its class, including the JAK inhibitors ruxolitinib, tofacitinib and baricitinib, as well as the multi-KI imatinib, further contextualizes its unique risk-benefit profile in CKD [70]. While these agents share a common capacity to modulate the JAK-STAT pathway, their off-target activities and consequent clinical safety signals diverge significantly. Ruxolitinib and baricitinib exhibit greater selectivity for JAK1 and JAK2, and their renal concerns in clinical practice are predominantly linked to opportunistic infections associated with broad immunosuppression [71–73]. In the case of tofacitinib, modest serum creatinine elevations are observed, potentially attributable to tubular secretion competition [74]. Imatinib, while effective in inhibiting kinases such as PDGFR, has been associated with a distinct pattern of fluid retention and acute kidney injury through mechanisms unrelated to the metabolic toxicity observed with fedratinib [75,76]. Crucially, our study identifies a singular and defining vulnerability for fedratinib: the profound vitamin B1 deficiency signal, which is mechanistically grounded in its high-affinity inhibition of hTHTR2—a transporter not significantly affected by ruxolitinib, tofacitinib, or baricitinib. This unique off-target interaction underlies fedratinib’s black-box warning for encephalopathy, a risk not shared by its JAK inhibitor counterparts [77]. Beyond this metabolic toxicity, fedratinib’s polypharmacology, characterized by high-affinity binding to SRC and AKT1 as validated by our docking results, may confer a broader antifibrotic efficacy against renal fibrosis by simultaneously dampening multiple pro-fibrotic pathways such as JAK-STAT, TGF-β/Smad3 and PI3K-Akt signaling. However, this same multi-kinase engagement also drives its distinct renal injury signal, likely mediated through AKT1 and EGFR disruption and subsequent metabolic stress, setting it apart from the renal risk profiles of the more selective JAK inhibitors.

It is this profound and specific thiamin depletion that reduces PDH activity, resulting in pyruvate accumulation that converts to methylglyoxal—a precursor of AGEs inducing irreversible collagen cross-linking [78,79]. This process negates therapeutic antifibrotic effects and correlates with 21.31% mortality from neurological sequelae. Preemptive thiamin surveillance is therefore critical, with serum concentrations below 30 nmol/L necessitating immediate supplementation and therapy interruption [80]. Cost-benefit analysis of monthly thiamin monitoring warrants evaluation, particularly for patients with eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 in resource-limited regions. The concordance between KEGG and GO analyses confirmed fedratinib’s dual suppression of MAPK/ERK and PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling axes, attenuating extracellular matrix deposition and impeding fibrotic progression [32]. Notably, enrichment of virus-associated pathways, including Hepatitis B and HIV infection, is likely indicative of immunosuppressive sequelae rather than direct CKD mechanisms [81,82], underscoring the need for vigilance against opportunistic infections. Collectively, despite the unequivocal risks highlighted by our analysis, these findings could justify the evaluation of a risk-stratified approach exclusively within the rigorous context of future clinical trials [83]. Such an approach could include baseline eGFR and thiamin quantification; consideration of a 50% dose reduction to 200 mg/day for patients with eGFR values under 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 or age ≥70 years; and monthly metabolic profiling of creatinine and lactate to enable early detection of organ dysfunction. This strategy, particularly the proposed dose reduction, constitutes a testable hypothesis for mitigating toxicity risks in this vulnerable population and requires prospective validation.

This study establishes a transformative paradigm that transcends the limitations of unimodal pharmacovigilance through synergistic triangulation of computational prediction, structural validation, and population-level evidence. The predictive power of traditional approaches is inherently constrained by the inability of network analyses to validate binding [18], the lack of physiological context in docking simulations [19] and the absence of mechanistic insight from disproportionality methods [20]. Our integrated framework resolves these deficiencies by establishing a self-reinforcing evidence cascade. Our network pharmacology and in silico toxicology analysis, grounded in JAK-STAT’s established role in renal fibrosis, identified 281 shared CKD targets and prioritized off-axis hubs (SRC, AKT1, EGFR). Hierarchical docking yielded high-affinity poses (Vina scores: −9.8 to −7.0 kcal/mol), thereby structurally rationalizing the pathway perturbations predicted by [5]. The strong binding affinity aligns with the efficacious range documented in [21], corroborating the biological relevance of the models. Crucially, FAERS mining with rigorous disproportionality analysis confirmed clinically relevant nephrotoxicity signals (ROR = 1.71) and extreme vitamin B1 deficiency (ROR = 1262.53), empirically anchoring mechanistic predictions to real-world outcomes. This methodological synergy delivers unprecedented resolution in KI safety profiling, converting observational associations into validated target-toxicity relationships.

Fedratinib’s therapeutic duality in CKD manifests as SRC/JAK2-mediated antifibrotic efficacy counterbalanced by AKT1/EGFR-driven metabolic toxicity, a polypharmacological profile transcending conventional KI paradigms [4]. The established quantitative correlations between target engagement signatures and clinical events, exemplified by AKT1 binding at −7.0 kcal/mol and vitamin B1 deficiency with 1262.53-fold ROR elevation, enable dosing for patients with baseline eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 or age ≥70 years through dynamic risk modeling. Methodological constraints nevertheless demand critical appraisal, and it is critical to position the insights from this integrated analysis as hypothesis-generating, providing a fertile ground for future experimental validation. Pharmacovigilance data limitations introduce selection bias, as hospitalization events account for as much as 30.27% of serious AEs, whereas life-threatening incidents make up only 3.03%. Meanwhile, passive surveillance mechanisms inherently underdetect subclinical nephrotoxicity. This bias is compounded by missing age records in 35.96% of cases, impeding developmental-stage risk stratification, and absent dose-titration data that preclude exposure-response quantification—particularly consequential given fedratinib’s established concentration-dependent neurological risks. Target screening comprehensiveness is constrained by exclusion of non-coding RNA regulators, notably METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of STAT1 mRNA that potentiates JAK-STAT signaling amplification, and epigenetic controllers such as HDAC4-SMAD2 complexes governing TGF-β-dependent chromatin remodeling dynamics. These omissions potentially overlook tertiary fibrogenic pathways activated in renal microenvironments [84,85]. Molecular docking rigidity constitutes a fundamental technical gap, as static conformations cannot simulate phosphorylation-induced allosteric changes; phosphorylation induces ATP-pocket deformation with >40% volumetric alteration and hydrogen bond network reorganization, dynamically modulating fedratinib residence time [86,87]. To address these gaps, a multi-tiered experimental strategy is warranted. Focused experimental validation of AKT1/STAT3 phosphorylation suppression should be prioritized using human-proximal models such as patient-derived kidney organoids, which have been demonstrated to faithfully recapitulate the segmented nephron structures and functional responses of the human kidney, providing a highly relevant platform for nephrotoxicity and efficacy assessment [88]. This approach can be powerfully complemented by the application of spatially resolved single-cell transcriptomics to such systems. This technology can map the entire transcriptome while retaining crucial spatial context, enabling the unambiguous identification of cell types and drug-target interactions within specific renal niches, such as the proximal tubule [89]. Meanwhile, molecular dynamics simulations can track millisecond-scale allosteric changes caused by AKT1 phosphorylation, with recent advances in accelerated sampling algorithms now enabling the accurate prediction of KI binding kinetics and residence times that are critical for understanding off-target effects [90].

Transcending fedratinib-specific insights, this study establishes a transformative KI safety paradigm with broad therapeutic applicability. By converging network-predicted targetopathies, structurally validated off-axis engagements and population-level toxicity signals, the integrated methodology resolves traditional pharmacovigilance limitations through synergistic validation of mechanistic plausibility, structural feasibility and clinical relevance. This framework pioneers a precise approach to kidney safety assessment—redefining risk stratification by quantifying off-target engagements against organ-specific adverse outcomes, translating computational predictions into clinical thresholds integrated within FDA’s Biomarker-Intervention Pair framework, and reconstructing dose-exposure relationships through real-world evidence analytics. Consequently, it bridges computational biology, structural pharmacology, and clinical nephrology to accelerate mechanism-guided therapeutic individualization, ultimately transforming KI development from empirical optimization to target-informed de-risking across CKD spectra.

Conclusion

Integrating computational network pharmacology, in silico toxicology, hierarchical molecular docking and FAERS pharmacovigilance data, this study delineates fedratinib’s dual kidney safety profile in CKD. The agent demonstrates therapeutic efficacy through polypharmacological engagement of SRC, AKT1, EGFR, and JAK2, suppressing JAK-STAT/TGF-β/Smad3-driven inflammatory-fibrotic cascades and attenuating extracellular matrix deposition. Conversely, population-level evidence reveals significant dose-dependent toxicities, including renal impairment (signaled by renal and urinary disorders, ROR = 1.71, and increased blood creatinine, ROR = 5.18) and the black-boxed risk of severe vitamin B1 deficiency (ROR = 1262.53) with its associated neurological sequelae. Collectively, these findings generate a testable clinical hypothesis: that a risk-adapted regimen featuring a 50% dose reduction (200 mg/day) coupled with rigorous renal and thiamin monitoring for patients with eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 or age ≥70 years may optimize the therapeutic window, necessitating validation in prospective trials that explicitly address this safety framework. This work pioneers a translational safety-assessment paradigm that bridges computational prediction with clinical evidence to optimize KI therapy.
