Temperature-dependent dynamics of DNA methylation and plasmid copy number in Yersinia pestis
Authors: Konanov D.N., Balykova A.N., Vvedensky A.V., Lukina-Gronskaya A.V., Liubimova O.N., Kovrizhnikov A.V., Speranskaya A.S., Krivonos D.V., Eroshenko G.A., Ilina E.N., Govorun V.M., Kutyrev V.V.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A number of mechanisms that control Yersinia pestis behavior during its complex life cycle in both mammalian hosts and arthropod vectors are known to be thermally regulated. In bacteria, the response to temperature shifts often involves epigenetic factors, particularly changes in DNA methylation. In this study, we aimed to fully characterize the methylome of eleven Y. pestis strains from different subspecies. We described its temperature-dependent features by comparing cultures grown at 22 °C (typical flea environment) and 37 °C (mammalian host temperature). Only two methylation motifs were detected: GATC with 6 mA modifications and CCWGG with 4mC modifications. GATC sites were almost fully methylated across the genome, except regions encoding the Type II secretion system and O‑antigen biosynthesis cluster, and four loci in the pMT1 plasmid. CCWGG sites were reproducibly less methylated at 37 °C than at 22 °C. This cytosine under‑methylation was more pronounced in strains from the ancient 0.PE4 lineage than in the highly pathogenic 0.ANT5 and 2.MED1 strains. Six genes showed reproducible differential methylation in CCWGG sites, including the virulence factor gene yopD and the plasmid gene parA, which is involved in plasmid replication and segregation. Subsequently, we observed a significant increase in the copy number of all Y. pestis plasmids in samples grown at 37 °C compared to those at 22 °C.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12915-4.

Introduction

The main functions of DNA methylation in bacteria include control of DNA replication and repair, and defense against bacteriophages, but it also could play other biological roles. For instance, adenine methylation is essential for virulence of Salmonella typhimurium, and Dam-deficient S. typhimurium strains show a reduced ability to invade HeLa cells and proliferate in target organs [1]. Cytosine methylation could also be a factor in bacterial virulence [2], though its biological role is generally less studied [3].

The plague pathogen Yersinia pestis is a highly adaptable organism that can colonize various flea species and cause severe systemic disease in mammals. The expression of a number of Y. pestis proteins is thermally regulated [4–7]. For example, Yop proteins which are one of key virulence factors in pathogenic Yersinia species necessary for infecting mammals, are only produced at temperatures near 37 °C [8]. The pPCP1 plasmid carries the pla gene encoding a bifunctional protein [9] which acts as coagulase at lower temperatures (facilitating flea blockage [10]) and as a fibrinolytic enzyme at higher temperatures (inactivating plasminogen inhibitor PLA-I and α−2-antiplasmin, which leads to fibrin clots degrading [11, 12]).

The link between DNA methylases activity and thermal regulation of the Yersinia virulence factors was partially demonstrated in Yersinia pseudotuberculosis—in wild-type strains, Yop proteins are synthesized only in mammal host conditions, while in Dam-overproducing strains, their expression becomes insensitive to temperature [13]. Similarly, in uropathogenic Escherichia coli, Dam methylase regulates temperature-dependent pili formation by methylating GATC sites in the papBA promoter region [14, 15]. In extremophiles, changes in DNA methylation are thought to be involved in response to cold or heat stress [16]. Thus, epigenetic regulation could play a significant role in thermal regulation of bacterial metabolism.

Recently, the temperature dynamic of the methylome of enteropathogenic Yersinia enterocolitica was characterized using SMRT sequencing [17]. The authors observed temperature-dependent methylation of adenine in several chromosomal and plasmid genes including rRNA clusters and yop genes. The methylome of Y. pestis has not been studied in detail, but there were reports that Dam inhibitors could act as antimicrobial agents against Y. pestis. In part, it could significantly reduce expression of Y. pestis virulence factors [18], while Dam-deficient mutants are significantly attenuated in mice [19], suggesting a link between epigenetic modifications and Y. pestis virulence capabilities.

In this study, we investigated the main patterns of DNA methylation in the plague pathogen cultured at two temperatures, and consider both ancient (line 0.PE4) and highly pathogenic (antiqua and medievalis) Yersinia pestis strains. Temperature conditions close to mammal host and soil environment of insects were used. The optimal temperatures for flea maintenance are between 20 to 30 °C [20], with the highest transmission efficiency around 23 °C [21], that is why temperatures 22 °C and 37 °C were chosen for cultivation.

Results

Some comments about the Y. pestis genomes used in the study

In this study, eleven Y. pestis strains were sequenced and analyzed. The list of considered Y. pestis strains included phylogenetic lines of main (0.ANT5, 2.MED1), and non-main subspecies (0.PE4h, 0.PE4t, and 0.PE4a), where each subspecies was represented by two strains, except for 2.MED1, which was represented by three strains. The phylogenetic tree of the studied strains is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1, the main phenotypic properties of the studied strains are available in Supplementary Table 1.

These eleven strains formed five genetically similar groups. To simplify visualization in the figures, we used one representative strain from each group (115, 177, 1627, 1815, and 2115). Five representative strains were chosen based on higher coverage depth (see Supplementary Table 2) All numeric values of methylation level and differential methylation scores for all eleven strains are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Adenine methylation in Y. pestis

Adenine-N6-methyltransferase presented in all analyzed Y. pestis strains (WP_002208895.1) is a close homolog to the classical Dam-methyltransferase from Escherichia coli, and catalyzes adenine methylation in GATC context producing G6mATC. We found that, regardless of temperature conditions, methylation of GATC was almost total (> 99% of all GATC occurrences had methylation level > 50%, Supplementary Table 3) except for very few loci in the chromosome and the pMT1 plasmid.

In the chromosome, significant under-methylation of GATC sites in all studied strains was detected in O-antigen biosynthesis operon (~ 20.7 Kb, blue in Fig. 1). The same under-methylation effect was observed in the Gsp-like Type II secretion system operon (~ 17.2 Kb, yellow in Fig. 1). The statistical values (Mann–Whitney test p-value and Cohen’s d-effect size) demonstrating the difference of methylation level of these regions compared to the whole chromosome are provided in Supplementary Table 4. Both operons were under-methylated under both temperature conditions tested. We checked if these two regions differed by their k-mer content from the rest chromosome and found that these were the most AT-rich fragments in the Y. pestis chromosome with GC content about 36%, which is significantly less than the global chromosome GC-content (47.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additional analysis of other bacteria harboring Dam-methylase (Klebsiella grimontii 2750 and E. coli K12) suggests that under-methylation of GATC sites in AT-rich regions is likely common in Enterobacterales (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4).Fig. 1Under-methylation of GATC motifs in O-antigen biosynthesis operon (blue) and Type II secretion system operon (yellow). A more pronounced methylation pattern could be observed in the pMT1 plasmid, where all strains had four loci of significant adenine under-methylation (Fig. 2). GATC site, upstream to the gene encoding hypothetical protein (similar to T4 phage ssDNA binding protein, WP_002213132.1, −90 bp position) had a methylation level < 2%, far lower than the average GATC methylation level. The same under-methylation effect was observed for a GATC site within the gene encoding another hypothetical protein (similar to phage portal protein, WP_002211786.1, + 72 bp position). In both loci, total de-methylation was symmetric and presented in both DNA strands.

Additionally, two other reproducible GATC sites were methylated significantly less than the rest of the plasmid. These sites are located in the ymt gene encoding murine toxin (WP_002211758.1), and the caf1 gene encoding F1 antigen precursor protein (WP_002216410.1). In both of these cases, GATC was non-symmetrically methylated, with a methylation rate < 75% on the complement strand, and > 90% on the direct strand, so mainly the complement strand has these sites significantly under-methylated.

We also checked whether the observed under-methylation could be an artifact of Dorado basecalling. To test this, we compared the methylation levels of the identified under-methylated positions with other positions in the genome sharing the same 10-nucleotide sequences, under assumption that specificity of basecalling is determined by approximately 10-nucleotide context surrounding a base. Only positions on the pMT1 plasmids had low methylation level, while rest positions with identical 10-mers had methylation level close to 100%, which indicates that the observed under-methylation is not a technical artifact. It should also be noted, that under-methylation of the four pMT1 sites was not linked to a decrease in local GC content, unlike what was observed on the chromosome. The pCD1 and pPCP1 plasmids did not show recognizable methylation patterns reproduced across the studied strains.

To compare methylation profiles in 22 °C and 37 °C samples, differential methylation analysis was performed using ‘modkit dmr pair’. Since the average fraction of methylated adenine was nearly identical in both temperatures for all strains, no GATC site was reproducibly identified as differentially methylated (DM). The only gene demonstrating significant shift in adenine methylation level was the pla gene on the pPCP1 plasmid, but the effect was only seen in two strains 109 and 1815 (see Supplementary Table 5). However, to avoid false-positive inference, we did not consider effects to be significant if they were not reproduced in at least half studied strains.

Cytosine methylation in Y. pestis

Cytosine methylation in Y. pestis is provided by a cytosine-methyltransferase (WP_002209169.1) catalyzing methylation of 4N-position in the second cytosine in CCWGG, motif producing C4mCWGG. The specificity of this methyltransferase in strain CO92 was previously determined using PacBio sequencing (https://rebase.neb.com/cgi-bin/pacbioget?3092). Although it modifies the same motif as the Dcm methylase from E. coli, it is a totally different enzyme [22]. Like Dcm, it is an orphan methyltransferase, i. e. does not have complementary DNA restriction enzyme.

While the adenine-specific Dam-methylase was identical across all strains studied, the cytosine-methyltransferase appeared in two protein variants. These differ in the number of serine-lysine repeats (SK-repeats) at position 238 in the protein (Fig. 3, A). 0.PE4h, 0.PE4t, and 0.PE4a strains had three SK-repeats, whereas 0.ANT5 and 2.MED1 strains had two.

We observed that cytosine methylation level in CCWGG contexts was slightly different in the analyzed Y. pestis strains, but in all strains, methylation level was lower at 37 °C than at 22 °C (Fig. 3, B; Supplementary Table 3). The lowest methylation level at 37 °C and the clearest difference between methylation profiles at 22 °C and 37 °C was seen in 0.PE4 subspecies strains. The difference in cytosine methylation between 22 °C and 37 °C in 0.ANT5 and 2.MED1 strains was less pronounced, suggesting a possible association between a number of SK-repeats in the methyltransferase and its activity in mammalian host conditions.

In general, cytosine methylation profiles appeared rather chaotic (Fig. 4) but we identified few regions that were reproducibly under-methylated in all considered strains. Firstly, all seven rRNA clusters located in the Y. pestis chromosome were significantly under-methylated in both 22 °C and 37 °C samples, which will be discussed in more detail in the next section. Secondly, the O-antigen biosynthesis cluster, which demonstrated under-methylation in GATC, also had significantly decreased methylation level in CCWGG in 22 °C samples (Supplementary Table 4B).

Despite the fact that in 0.ANT5 and 2.MED1 strains the difference in cytosine methylation between 22 °C and 37 °C was rather weak, differential methylation analysis revealed six genes that were reproducibly under-methylated in all 37 °C samples compared to 22 °C regardless of biovar (Fig. 5). The gene yscD encoding Yop translocation protein D showed a very high difference in cytosine methylation across all strains studied. The traI gene which encodes DNA helicase TraI, also had noticeable change in methylation level in every strain. Furthermore, the genes sbp, parA, rpoH, and istB had a reproducible decrease in methylation at 37 °C, but with lower differential methylation scores (Supplementary Table 6).

Under-methylation of rRNA clusters

In all Y. pestis strains used in this study, we detected significant under-methylation of cytosine in rRNA clusters in both 22 °C and 37 °C samples. Seven chromosomal rRNA clusters including 5S, 16S, and 23S rRNA genes have 85 CCWGG sites which could be methylated. We checked the percentage of methylated cytosines among these 85 sites compared to the total cytosine methylation rate. In 22 °C samples, only 1815 and 1818 strains had a few methylated CCWGG sites (4 out of 85, and 1 out of 85, respectively) while in the rest strains rRNA clusters were totally non-methylated (0 sites out of 85). In 37 °C samples, all strains had rRNA clusters totally non-methylated in cytosine. Numeric values are available in Supplementary Tables 7 and 8.

Adenine methylation of GATC sites inside rRNA genes also showed unexpected patterns. In all 22 °C samples, the methylation level did not differ from the rest chromosome. However, in strain 109, cultivated at 37 °C, we again observed significant under-methylation in all seven rRNA clusters (Supplementary Fig. 5), while its close relative Y. pestis 115 did not have such pattern. This effect was reproduced in two biological repeats of Y. pestis 109, but its nature remains unknown.

Pangenome data analysis revealed restriction-modification systems highly specific to Y. pestis

According to the REBASE records, the repertoire of restriction-modification systems in Y. pestis differs from that in Y. pseudotuberculosis, which indicates that there are R-M enzymes that are present only in Y. pestis but not in its ancestor. To more comprehensively characterize genes specific to Y. pestis and absent in Y. pseudotuberculosis, we performed pangenomic analysis of 164 complete Y. pestis genomes (all Y. pestis complete genomes with assigned phylogenetic lines were collected from [23]) and 337 genomes of Y. pseudotuberculosis (all complete genomes available in GenBank in March, 2025). We considered genes as differentially presented between species if they were present in at least 95% of Y. pestis strains and absent in all considered Y. pseudotuberculosis genomes. Only eight genes satisfied the chosen limitations.

The first was the gene encoding DNA methyltransferase with unknown specificity, (WP_340137219.1, potentially specific to TCCGGA according to REBASE). Two other genes specific to Y. pestis encode the McrBC complex (WP_002209166.1 and WP_002209167.1)—a Type IV restriction-modification system specifically cleaving DNA in 5-methylcytosine preceded by a purine base (guanine or adenine). The rest five genes were either partial or did not have assigned function. The protein sequences of the eight proteins specific to Y. pestis and their BLAST annotations are available in Supplementary Table 9.

The gene encoding CCWGG-specific methyltransferase, whose activity was described in the previous sections, also demonstrated a strong difference in its prevalence between Y. pestis and Y. pseudotuberculosis strains. More than 96% of the analyzed Y. pestis complete genomes had this gene, while in Y. pseudotuberculosis it was identified only in 6% strains. Therefore, it could also be considered highly specific to Y. pestis.

Copy number of Y. pestis plasmids is thermally regulated

In the previous sections, we noted that the gene encoding ParA was significantly under-methylated in CCWGG sites at 37 °C. Expression level of these gene is thermally-regulated [24] and associated with effectiveness of plasmid replication [15, 25], that is why we decided to compare the plasmids copy number (PCN) across temperature conditions. PCN was calculated as described in [26]. It was found, that all three Y. pestis plasmids had increased copy number at 37 °C (Fig. 6), consistent with plasmid gene expression data [27].

The greatest increase in PCN was detected for the smallest Y. pestis plasmid, pPCP1, which rose from 12.1 ± 3.7 × at 22 °C to 32.0 ± 22.9 × at 37 °C (p-value = 0.0006). The PCN of pCD1 increased from 2.9 ± 0.8 × to 7.0 ± 1.8x (p-value = 0.0001), and pMT1 increased from 1.2 ± 0.1 × to 1.8 ± 0.4x (p-value = 0.0003).

Different Y. pestis subspecies showed different PCN fold-changes. Strains from 0.PE4h and 0.PE4t lines, in particular, demonstrated more pronounced temperature-dependent increase in PCN than those from 2.MED1 and 0.ANT5 (Supplementary Table 10).

Discussion

Epigenetic factors play an essential role in the regulation of bacterial metabolism. For example, in Enterobacteriaceae, the Dam-methyltransferase is required not only for DNA repair but also for controlling transcription [28]. The plague pathogen Y. pestis is an organism with a complex life cycle, and a number of its virulence mechanisms are known to depend on environment conditions [29], particularly temperature. This suggests epigenetic factors could also be involved in their regulation. Furthermore, pangenome analysis of Y. pestis and its ancestor Y. pseudotuberculosis conducted during this study reveals the presence of proteins highly specific to Y. pestis involved in the DNA restriction and modification processes.

In this study, we characterized the methylome of eleven Y. pestis strains from different phylogenetic branches. We analyzed cultures grown at 22 °C (flea host) and 37 °C (mammal host) to assess temperature dynamics. Only two active GATC- and CCWGG-specific methyltransferases were identified in all studied strains, with 6NmA and 4mC modification types respectively. GATC-sites were almost completely methylated along the genome in all strains, but we detected two regions that were significantly under-methylated.

The first under-methylated region is the operon encoding Gsp-like Type II secretion system Yts2. In Y. enterocolitica, this secretion system is involved in the process of mice infection and plays a role in survival of bacteria in an environment habitat [30], but the role of this system in Y. pestis virulence has not been described yet. The second chromosomal region demonstrating significant adenine under-methylation was the cluster of O-antigen biosynthesis. Interestingly, unlike related species, Y. pestis strains are not capable of producing O-antigen due to inactivating mutations in four genes in the cluster [31]. Both these regions turned out to be the most AT-rich regions of the Y. pestis chromosome. This effect seems to be common for Dam-methyltransferase, which was shown on other bacteria harboring this methyltransferase. We propose it is likely caused by action of nucleoid-associated proteins H-NS and Lsr2, which provide silencing of AT-rich regions to prevent transcription of foreign genes [32]. These proteins compact DNA by formation of nucleoprotein complexes [33], potentially making the DNA less accessible for methyltransferases. In E. coli, H-NS proteins block methylation of the pap operon [34], whose expression is regulated by specific GATC methylation [14].

The pMT1 plasmid also contained four loci under-methylated in GATC. Two genes encoding hypothetical proteins (similar to phage portal protein and phage T4 ssDNA-binding protein) had one GATC site fully demethylated in both DNA strands. At the same time, the plasmid genes caf1 and ymt had non-symmetrically under-methylated GATC sites, which means that some fraction of bacterial cells in the population had these sites hemi-methylated (i.e., methylated only in one strand). Both caf1 and ymt encode known Y. pestis virulence factors transcribed from the pMT1 plasmid [35].

Cytosine methylation in Y. pestis turned out to be less trivial. CCWGG sites were incompletely methylated in all samples examined. Generally, cytosine methylation profiles looked quite random, but methylation level was significantly lower at 37 °C compared to those at 22 °C across all studied strains, thus demonstrating temperature-dependent dynamics of DNA methylation in Y. pestis. External RNAseq studies show that the metK gene encoding S-adenosylmethionine synthase is significantly under-expressed at 37 °C compared to vector host conditions (LFC = −3.6) [24]. Since SAM molecules are necessary for DNA-methyltransferases activity, reduced level of cytosine methylation might be a consequence of an insufficient amount of SAM in cells cultivated at 37 °C. Indeed, taking into account different methylation levels of adenine and cytosine, cytosine-methyltransferase in Y. pestis seems to be less active compared to Dam, and may therefore be more sensitive to changes in SAM concentration. Additionally, rRNA clusters showed no cytosine methylation at CCWGG sites in any sample.

We observed a notable difference in temperature-dependent dynamics between Y. pestis subspecies. It was found that older Y. pestis strains (0.PE4a, 0.PE4t, and 0.PE4h branches) were significantly less methylated in cytosine at 37 °C than at 22 °C, while in highly pathogenic strains from 0.ANT5 and 2.MED1 branches that difference was less pronounced (Fig. 3). The observed divergence may be partly explained by the divergence in the cytosine-methyltransferase structure, which differs between older and modern Y. pestis strains by one serine-lysine repeat. However, in bacteria, DNA methyltransferases activity is regulated by many factors, including local nucleotide context, co-factors availability, and competition with other DNA- binding proteins [36–38], and specific biological effect of that mutation in methyltransferase gene requires further experimental confirmation.

A number of genes showed reproducible differential methylation in CCWGG sites. The yscD gene, encoding Yop translocation protein D, had the highest differential methylation score, being significantly under-methylated at 37 °C compared to 22 °C in all strains. The expression of yscD is thermally regulated by LcrF regulator protein [39], and it is transcribed actively only at temperatures close to those of mammal hosts [40]. In bacteria, an association between methylation status and expression level has been shown for a number of genes [41, 42], but in this particular case we cannot determine whether gene methylation inhibits transcription at 22 °C, or whether active transcription blocks methyltransferase activity at 37 °C.

Interestingly, the parA gene also was reproducibly under-methylated at 37 °C. parA and parB genes, located in the parAB operon, encode known factors of plasmid copy number control [25] and significantly over-expressed in mammal host conditions (LFC = 7.4 and 5.3 for parA and parB respectively) [24]. In fact, parA is one of genes in Y. pestis the most over-expressed in 37 °C. Our results showed that the plasmid copy number significantly increases with increase in culturing temperature in all strains studied. While temperature-dependent control of plasmid copy number has been shown earlier for both gram negative [43] and gram-positive [44] bacteria, in our knowledge, this is the first study clearly demonstrating thermally regulated changes in plasmid copy number in the plague pathogen.

Materials and methods

Bacterial procedures and DNA extraction

The Y. pestis strains used in this study were obtained from the working collection of the Laboratory of molecular microbiology and are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Strains were grown from single colonies on lysogeny broth (LB) agar (pH 7.2 ± 0.1) for 48 h at 28 °C for biomass build-up [45, 46]. Subsequently, the strains were grown under temperature conditions that mimic the vector (22 °C) and the host (37 °C) on lysogeny broth (LB) agar (pH 7.2 ± 0.1) for 24 h.

For DNA extraction, 5 ml LB (pH 7.2) was seeded with overnight bacterial culture 108 CFU Y. pestis and was cultured at 22 °C and 37 °C in a shaker incubator at 100—110 RPMs to the exponential growth phase (OD565 = 2 McFarland). A McFarland Densitometer DEN-1B (BioSan) was used for OD determination. To inactivate Y. pestis, sodium merthiolate was added to LB with (5 ml) to a final concentration of 1:10,000 and heated at 56 °C. for 30 min. The bacterial cells were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The DNA of the Y. pestis strains was obtained using phenol − chloroform extraction [47].

Library preparation and sequencing

Whole-genome library preparation from the obtained DNA samples was performed using the Ligation sequencing gDNA Native Barcoding Kit (SQK-NBD114) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The libraries were loaded on the PromethION R10 flow cell and sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore PromethION platform.

Genome assembling

Hybrid genome assemblies of 11 Y. pestis strains were obtained during a previous study [48]. DNA was sequenced on two platforms (Illumina Miseq and Oxford Nanopore r9.4.1). The raw reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic and Porechop for Illumina and ONT reads respectively. Unicycler (v0.4.8) was used for genome assembling.

Methylation analysis and used software

For the methylation analysis, the genome assemblies which were obtained earlier (NCBI BioProject PRJNA1292185) were used. The raw POD5-files were re-basecalled with Dorado 0.7.1 using 4mC_5mC, and 6 mA models (v5.0). The resulting bam-files were processed using ModKit (v0.2.4). The –filter-threshold parameters were adjusted using the ‘modkit sample-probs’ command for 4mC and 6 mA independently. Based on inferred modified contexts, the motif sequences were found using the enrichment algorithm implemented in Snapper and subsequently checked manually. In the analysis, only positions with a number of valid ModKit calls > 20 were considered. Differential methylation (DM) analysis was performed using the “modkit dmr pair” command. In DM analysis, we considered only the genes that contained at least two methylation sites so as to obtain more reliable results.

Software and statistical methods used

Mapping of the reads to the genomes was performed using minimap2 (v2.26-r1175). The phylogenetic tree was constructed using Fasttree 2.1.11 [49] based on co-aligned core genomes generated with Roary 3.13.0 [50]. The tree was visualized using iTOL web-service [51]. Pangenome analysis was conducted using Roary. Cohen’s d-effect size was used to estimate effect size. Plasmid copy number (PCN) was calculated based on the coverage depth as described in [26].

Conclusion

All eleven Y. pestis strains studied in this work have two active DNA methyltransferases. The first is GATC-specific Dam-methylase similar to that in E. coli. The adenine methylation provided by Dam is almost total, except in two AT-rich regions in the chromosome and four specific loci in the pMT1 plasmid, including loci within caf1 and ymt genes encoding Y. pestis virulence factors.

The second active DNA-methyltransferase specific to CCWGG is almost unique for Y. pestis species and provides methylation of cytosine in 4th position. The cytosine methylation level demonstrates a significant temperature dependence in all strains. However, in highly pathogenic strains from the antiqua and medievalis biovars, the observed difference in cytosine methylation between cultivation temperatures is notably weaker compared to that in ancient Y. pestis strains from the 0.PE4 line. Significant under-methylation of rRNA clusters in all studied samples was observed.

Regardless of phylogenetic branches, six genes have been identified as reproducibly differentially methylated in cytosine, including the parA gene directly involved in controlling plasmid copy number. Subsequently, it was shown that the copy number of three Y. pestis plasmids is temperature-dependent, with a significant increase of plasmid copy number in 37 °C samples compared to 22 °C samples.
