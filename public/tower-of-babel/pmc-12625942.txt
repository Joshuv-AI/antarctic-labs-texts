Feasibility, efficacy and effectiveness of community health worker-facilitated hearing aid provision: a systematic review
Authors: Caitlin Frisby, Vinaya Manchaiah, De Wet Swanepoel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe WHO recommends task-sharing with community health workers (CHWs) to help overcome the limited access to hearing healthcare and hearing aids in low-income and middle-income settings (LMIs). This systematic review examined the feasibility, efficacy and effectiveness of CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision.DesignSystematic review guided by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines.Data sourcesPubMed, Scopus and Web of Science were searched through 4 June 2025.Eligibility criteriaStudies in which CHWs facilitated hearing aid provision in any context.Data extraction and synthesisData on study characteristics, CHW training and roles, hearing-aid outcomes and implementation factors were extracted. Study quality was determined using the National Institute of Health quality assessment tool, and level of evidence was determined using the Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine. A narrative synthesis was conducted.ResultsSix studies published between 2013 and 2025 were included. Two were conducted in high-income countries but implemented in LMI communities. Four studies were conducted in LMI countries. All provided CHW training, though content and duration varied; only one used WHO-endorsed materials. Sustained device use was high as reported at follow-ups, though only one included 12-month outcomes. All studies included validated outcome measures, including the International Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids, Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly–Screening version, Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit and Self-Efficacy for Situational Communication Management Questionnaire. Implementation facilitators included use of local CHWs, delivery in participants’ home languages, comprehensive CHW training and employing mHealth tools. Barriers included CHW scope constraints and unfamiliarity with outcome subscales in rural contexts. Only two studies used formal feasibility frameworks.ConclusionsCHW-facilitated hearing aid provision is feasible and effective. However, variation in training and implementation highlights the need for standardised training materials, supervision models and culturally adapted outcome measures. Further research should examine long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness and scalability using formal implementation frameworks.

Introduction

Access to hearing healthcare remains a significant challenge in low-income and middle-income settings (LMIs), where the burden of untreated hearing loss is disproportionately high and often inadequately addressed due to a limited number of specialist hearing healthcare services.1 The WHO estimates that over 430 million people globally experience disabling hearing loss, with the majority residing in LMIs.1 In these settings, hearing healthcare services are often scarce, with a limited or total absence of hearing healthcare professionals.1 2 When left untreated, hearing loss has far-reaching negative consequences, including communication difficulties, social isolation, reduced educational and employment opportunities, depression, anxiety and an overall negative impact on quality of life.136

A widely recognised barrier to hearing healthcare is the high cost of hearing aids, which remains unaffordable for many individuals in both high-income and LMI settings.7 8 However, access to trained hearing healthcare professionals poses an even more significant challenge in many settings. While most high-income countries (65%) report having more than 10 audiologists per million persons, 93% of low-income and 76% of lower-middle-income countries have fewer than one audiologist per million persons.1 Even when hearing healthcare services are available, these services are often centralised, leaving rural populations without access or requiring individuals to travel significant distances to urban areas.1 2 9 This challenge is not unique to LMI settings, as even in high-income countries such as the USA, it is estimated that over 5 million individuals travel more than an hour to access an audiologist, with the most remote 10% of the population travelling an average of 34 min.10 A recent scoping review on the barriers to ear and hearing care access in LMI countries found that 13 studies reported geographical location and travel distance as barriers to hearing healthcare.11 As a result, there is a growing need for alternative hearing healthcare service-delivery approaches to improve access and affordability of hearing healthcare.

One such approach recommended by the WHO is task-sharing, which involves delegating specific tasks to less-skilled or lay health workers to improve efficiency and access.1 12 Community health workers (CHWs), a broad category of lay individuals trained to deliver essential healthcare services within their communities, have successfully supported a range of healthcare services, including the management of hypertension,13 diabetes,14 kidney disease,15 HIV/AIDS care,16 mental health services,17 administration of vaccinations and injectable contraceptives, and family planning counselling.18 The WHO has also recommended task-sharing hearing healthcare services among trained CHWs as a priority to improve access to hearing loss screening, diagnosis and management (WHO, 2021, 2024).

CHWs have been able to provide a range of hearing healthcare services, including hearing screenings for adults19 and children,20 21 hearing assessments22 23 and hearing aid fittings.2327 Due to the growing potential of CHW-facilitated hearing services, the WHO Technical Working Group on hearing aids recently developed a comprehensive hearing aid service-delivery approach for implementation in LMI settings.28 29 Through task-sharing among CHWs and highly trained professionals, this community-based hearing aid model aims to improve access to hearing aids by providing a range of hearing healthcare services, from hearing assessments to hearing aid fittings and follow-up support.28

Several reviews have explored related topics concerning community-based hearing care models. Eubank et al conducted a global scoping review of community-based hearing rehabilitation programmes. Their review reported that most studies primarily focused on raising awareness, training and conducting hearing screenings or assessments in underserved communities.30 However, the review did not specifically examine the role of CHWs or consider the income brackets of the countries in which the studies were conducted.30 More recently, Dillard et al synthesised evidence on hearing aid service delivery in LMI and resource-limited settings. Hospital-based, community-based and remote telehealth service delivery were reported as successful approaches to improve access to hearing aid services.8 However, their review did not specifically examine CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision.8 O’Donovan et al conducted a scoping review on the role of CHWs in improving access to ear and hearing services globally. However, their scoping review identified only two studies in which CHWs were directly involved in hearing aid provision, highlighting the limited evidence available at the time.31 Since then, research interest in this prioritised area has expanded, with several recent studies investigating CHW-led hearing aid provision and the feasibility of this approach.23 27 32

Despite this emerging evidence base, there remains a lack of synthesised research evaluating the global extent, feasibility and effectiveness of CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision. The WHO has recently advocated for CHWs to facilitate low-cost hearing aid fittings,28 underscoring the need for a focused, systematic review. This systematic review, therefore, aims to examine the feasibility, efficacy and effectiveness of CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision. Additionally, this review will explore and examine key implementation factors, including the target populations, the settings where these models have been tested, the types of hearing devices used, the nature of CHW training, and the barriers and facilitators influencing the adoption of this approach. The review will assess the impact of this model on outcomes, such as hearing aid uptake, user satisfaction and long-term adherence when reported.

Methods

This systematic review was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines33 and checklist (online supplemental material I). This review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251020853).

Search strategy

Three electronic databases, namely PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science, were searched independently by one of the authors (CF; online supplemental material II). The search strategy included keyword combinations based on two key concepts: community healthcare workers and intervention. The final keyword search was: (“community health worker*” OR “community health volunteer” OR “community health aide*” OR “community health practitioner*” OR “community health supporter” OR “less trained” OR “village health worker*” OR “village health team*” OR “village health guide*” OR “frontline health worker” OR “primary healthcare” OR “primary health care” OR “behvarz” OR “brigadista” OR “manzaneras” OR “rural health assistant*” OR “gramsakhi” OR “lay health worker*” OR “accredited social health activist*” OR “adherence support worker*” OR “community treatment supporter*” OR “community treatment support worker*” OR “community health distributor*” OR “community care worker*” OR “health extension worker*” OR “lay counsellor*” OR “lay health worker” OR “peer educator*” OR “barefoot doctor*” OR “bare-foot doctor*” OR “shasthy shebika*” OR “shasthy kormi*” OR “frontline primary healthcare worker*” OR “frontline primary healthcare worker*” OR “health activist*” OR “community worker*” or “trained community worker*”) AND (“hearing aid*” OR “assistive listening” OR “aural rehabilitation” OR “hearing device” OR “hearing instrument” OR “hearing aid provision” OR “hearing rehabilitation”). The references of the included articles were also screened to identify any missing articles. There were no restrictions based on the date of publication. The final search was conducted on 4 June 2025.

The Population Intervention Comparison Outcome Study Design Timeline framework was used to define the criteria for the inclusion and exclusion of studies (see table 1). Grey literature (including preprints, theses and conference abstracts) was not systematically searched; only peer-reviewed studies published in English were considered for inclusion.

Study selection

One author (CF) imported the studies identified through the database search into Rayyaan (https://www.rayyan.ai/) and used this software to screen for and remove any duplicates. The remaining identified studies were then exported into an Excel spreadsheet to screen and record decisions. One author (CF) screened 100% of the identified studies based on their titles, abstracts and keywords. Two authors (VM and DWS) each cross-checked 25% of the screened articles independently to ensure accuracy and confirm that the exclusion/inclusion criteria were consistently applied. All three authors (CF, VM and DWS) discussed and resolved any disagreements. PDF versions of the remaining studies’ full texts were obtained, and one author (CF) independently screened 100% of the full text. Two authors (VM and DWS) cross-checked 50% (25% each) to ensure agreement.

Data extraction

One author (CF) extracted the data independently. A second author (DWS) cross-checked 25% of the articles to ensure the data were correctly extracted. A data extraction template in Excel designed explicitly for this study was used (online supplemental material III). Specific data extraction items included publication details (eg, authors, country of study, date of publication), study design (eg, type of research approach used, data collection method, data analysis method), population-related (eg, sample size, hearing device used) and outcome-related (eg, study question, key findings).

Data synthesis

A formal narrative synthesis was undertaken.34 35 All authors assisted in identifying, characterising and summarising the available research evidence on the feasibility and effectiveness of CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this systematic review.

Risk of bias (quality) assessment and determination of level of evidence

The quality of the studies included in this review was assessed using the National Institute of Health (NIH) quality assessment tools.36 This tool comprises a series of study design-specific questions, each of which can be answered with ‘yes’, ‘no’ or ‘cannot determine/not reported/not applicable’. This tool was selected due to the study design-specific criteria suitable for the heterogeneous mix of studies included in this review. The study design used for the quality assessment is detailed in online supplemental material II. Each ‘yes’ response was assigned one point, with the total score determining the study’s overall quality rating. The criteria for categorising study quality were adapted from previous systematic reviews, classifying articles as poor (0–4 points), fair (5–10 points) or good (11–14 points).7 37

Furthermore, each study’s level of evidence was determined using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) Levels of Evidence framework.38 This hierarchy, with level 1 representing the highest quality evidence, was selected to help identify the likely best evidence. One of the authors (CF) conducted the quality assessment and independently determined the evidence level. To ensure reliability, two authors (VM and DWS) cross-checked 50% (25% each). All the authors (CF, VM and DWS) discussed and resolved any disagreements.

Results

Study characteristics

The final keyword search identified 240 articles, with 127 on Web of Science, 78 on Scopus and 35 on PubMed. A total of 117 duplicates were removed, and thus, 123 articles were screened based on their titles, keywords and abstracts. The full text of 22 articles was then screened for eligibility. Of these, six articles were included in the current review (figure 1). No additional articles were identified through the manual reference check of the already included articles.

PRISMA flow diagram details the search and selection process applied during the systematic review. CHW, community health worker; PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Publication dates ranged from 2013 to 2025. Two of the studies were conducted in high-income countries, namely the USA, although both studies took place in lower-income communities. Two studies took place in upper-middle-income countries, namely South Africa and China, while the remaining two were conducted in lower-middle-income countries, India and Bangladesh, respectively.

Study designs included two randomised controlled trials (RCTs),26 27 one cluster-randomised trial,24 one open-label RCT39 and two feasibility studies.23 40 Sample sizes ranged from 19 to 151 participants, with a total sample size of 670 across all studies. The included populations varied, with four studies focusing on adults,23 26 27 39 one study focusing on children24 and one study including both adults and children.40

CHW demographics, training, supervision and roles

Limited information was provided on the demographics of the CHWs across the included studies. Only Nieman et al specified that the CHWs were older adults. Four studies reported that CHWs were members of the local communities.23 26 27 39 The number of CHWs involved ranged from 224 40 to 10.39 In two studies, CHWs received remuneration for their participation,27 39 while in two other studies, CHWs were employed by a local Non-Governmental Organization (NGO)23 or a health centre.26

All included studies provided formal training for CHWs. However, the training’s duration, content and structure varied widely (table 2). The length of training ranged from a 10-hour programme39 to more extensive 6-week programmes.40 Only two of the studies reported on CHW certification or formal evaluation post-training. Nieman et al reported that once trained, CHWs obtained a certification that required CHWs to deliver the intervention under the supervision of audiologists. Coco et al evaluated the knowledge and hands-on skills of CHWs through a structured assessment conducted post-training. Most training programmes integrated both theoretical and hands-on practical sessions (table 2). One study included supervised community-based practice.23 Only one study reported that their training programme was based on the WHO’s Primary Ear and Hearing Care training package,24 while the remaining studies did not clearly describe the development or source of their training materials.

The prior experience of CHWs varied considerably across studies (table 2). In two studies, CHWs had background experience in hearing care, such as previous work in childhood screening programmes23 or health promotion initiatives.26 Two studies included CHWs with minimal or no experience but were selected based on community leadership or willingness to learn.24 27 One study included CHWs with previous medical training, although what was covered in this training was not specified.39 Only one study included CHWs with formal educational qualifications.40

The scope of CHW responsibilities was broad, including hearing assessments, hearing aid fitting, counselling and follow-up support (table 2). In five studies, CHWs independently conducted tasks such as video otoscopy, pure tone audiometry and basic hearing aid maintenance.23 24 27 39 40 One study employed a teleaudiology-supported model in which CHWs facilitated synchronous care under remote supervision from audiologists.26 In this study, CHWs assisted with the physical components of hearing aid fitting while receiving real-time guidance during more technical procedures.26

All studies reported some form of ongoing supervision and support for CHWs. The type of supervision varied, including in-person observation23 and monitoring,39 review of results by professionals,24 40 scheduled remote check-ins and continuing education,27 and remote synchronous collaboration.26

Hearing aid adherence and use

All six studies reported hearing aid outcomes using a variety of outcome measures and devices (table 3). CHWs facilitated 607 hearing aid fittings across the included studies. Most devices were behind-the-ear (BTE) hearing aids, including the Unitron Moxi T,26 Siemens Phoenix,24 Poco R160039 and Lexie Lumen.23 Body-worn devices (Siemens Amiga; 24) and personal sound amplification products, such as the Sidekick (BTE style) and SuperEar SE9000 (body-worn with a wired headset), were also used in the study by Nieman et al.

Fitting methods varied across studies. The Lexie Lumen and Sidekick were self-fitting over-the-counter devices programmed in situ via smartphone apps, with CHWs facilitating the process. The Poco R1600 also used in situ testing, with CHWs assisting participants during self-fitting. The Siemens Phoenix and Siemens Amiga devices were preprogrammed and fitted by CHWs. The Unitron Moxi T was remotely programmed by an audiologist, with CHWs providing synchronous assistance during the fitting. The SuperEar SE9000 was fitted directly by CHWs. Duration of device use across studies ranged from 6 weeks to 12 months.

Across the included studies, hearing aid use and follow-up rates varied but generally demonstrated high levels of participant retention and sustained device use. Frisby et al reported that of the 19 participants fitted with hearing aids, 18 returned for the 45-day follow-up, 16 for the 6-month follow-up, and 14 (73.7%) were still making use of their hearing aids at the 6-month follow-up. Similarly, in Coco et al, 27 of the 28 participants completed at least one follow-up, with 9 in the experimental group and 10 from the control group returning for the 17-week follow-up and reporting continued hearing aid use. Emerson et al reported that 105 of 111 (94.5%) participants returned for the 6-month follow-up, and all reported ongoing use of their hearing aids. In the Borg et al study, 75 of 77 participants in the community-based group and all 65 participants in the centre-based group returned for follow-up at 6 weeks, with continued device use reported. Nieman et al also reported strong long-term follow-up, with 73% of the intervention group and 70% of the wait-list control group completing the 12-month follow-up measures. At this follow-up, 72 of 96 participants indicated continued use of their hearing aids for at least 1 hour daily. Lastly, in Jiang et al, follow-up completion was high, with only 5 participants in the intervention group (4.6%) and 10 in the control group (8.9%) lost to follow-up. All participants who completed the 6-month assessment reported ongoing hearing aid use.

Only two studies collected data logging from the hearing aids. Coco et al reported that initially, the CHW-supported group demonstrated significantly longer daily use at the second follow-up (10 weeks) compared with the control group, who were assisted by a trained non-CHW, but this difference was no longer observed at the third follow-up (17 weeks). Jiang et al reported that most participants wore their hearing aids for 4–5 hours per day.

Hearing aid benefit, satisfaction and psychosocial outcomes

All included studies assessed hearing aid benefit or related psychosocial outcomes, each using at least one standardised and validated outcome measure, and four of the six studies specified a primary outcome measure. Two studies used the International Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids (IOI-HA). Frisby et al reported consistently high scores (mean scores ranging from 3.8 to 4.8) across all domains at both 45-day and 6-month follow-ups, with no significant changes over time. The difference in average scores across all domains at the 45-day and 6-month follow-up visits ranged from −0.2 to 0.6.23 Borg et al found comparable outcomes between community-based and centre-based care in terms of IOI-HA scores, with the average scores ranging from 3.45 to 4.08 for the community-based group and 3.52 to 4.25 for the centre-based group. However, small effect sizes favoured the centre-based model for ‘residual participation restrictions’ and ‘impact on others’. Emerson et al used the Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit (APHAB) and observed substantial mean within-group improvements across speech-related subscales (>25-point gains) with minimal aversiveness reported.

Nieman et al, participants in the RCT intervention group, experienced a statistically significant reduction in self-perceived hearing disability, with Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly–Screening version (HHIE-S) scores improving from 21.7 to 7.9 at 3 months (mean difference=–13.2), while the control group showed no meaningful change. Jiang et al used the Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (GDS-15) as the study’s primary outcome to measure change in depression symptoms. The intervention group showed a significant reduction in depression symptoms (mean difference=–1.65; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.58; p<0.001). Secondary outcomes included the HHIE-S, with improvements from 19.51 to 16.87 at 3 months and 16.12 at 6 months. Additional improvements were observed in social isolation (Lubben Social Network Scale 6) and loneliness (University of California, Los Angeles scale), with respective changes of 1.52 and –2.36 at 3 months, and 2.43 and –3.63 at 6 months.

Coco et al reported significant improvements in self-efficacy over time based on both the Hearing-related (Self-Efficacy for Situational Communication Management Questionnaire, SESMQ-H) and Communication-related (SESMQ-C) subscales of the SESMQ). Linear mixed model analyses showed significant main effects of time (p<0.0001), although no between-group differences were observed.

Feasibility of CHW-led hearing aid service delivery models

Only two of the included studies used formal frameworks to evaluate feasibility (table 4). Frisby et al applied Bowen’s framework and identified demand, acceptability and practicality based on expressed community interest, sustained hearing aid use and participant satisfaction. Coco et al applied the RE-AIM framework, reporting high protocol adherence, improved communication self-efficacy and ongoing willingness to pay for devices in the CHW-supported group. Although the remaining studies did not use structured frameworks, several reported barriers and facilitators to implementation, although not consistently measured.

Reported barriers included CHW scope limitations, such as the inability to perform specific tasks, including cerumen removal, due to country-specific regulations and contextual challenges, including hearing aid loss, theft or affordability issues in low-income populations.23 Nieman et al reported that variability in CHW service delivery may influence intervention consistency. Borg et al noted that using custom ear moulds could pose a challenge for children due to frequent changes in ear canal size during childhood. Jiang et al reported that the data loggers only tracked turn-on time and not actual usage; thus, due to recall bias, participants often overestimated usage. Facilitators reported included the use of CHWs from the local community, delivery of services in participants’ home language, community-based delivery, and the portability of care enabled by digital mHealth tools (table 4).

Cost-effectiveness

None of the included studies conducted a formal cost-effectiveness analysis. A few studies briefly mentioned contextual information related to service affordability, such as the use of lower-cost hearing aids23 27 39 or the use of earplugs rather than ear moulds to reduce costs.24 However, no studies provided detailed cost data on device procurement, training, supervision or long-term maintenance.

Level of evidence

The OCEBM was used to determine the evidence level of each study (table 4). Four controlled intervention studies (66.7%) were classified as level two evidence, and two pre–post studies with no control group (33.3%) were classified as level 4 evidence.

Quality assessment

The quality of each study was determined using the NIH quality assessment tool. Quality ratings are reported in table 4. Four studies (66.7%) were rated good quality, and two (33.3%) were rated fair quality. Only four (66.7%) of the included studies gave a justification for sample size. In terms of the RCTs, only two of the four studies had a dropout rate of less than 20%,24 39 and none of the included RCTs blinded participants and providers to treatment group assignment.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesised current evidence on CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision, a model recommended to improve access to hearing care, particularly in LMI settings.28 The six included studies demonstrate that CHWs can successfully deliver a range of hearing aid services, yielding positive outcomes comparable to those of traditional, professionally led service delivery models. While the majority of studies were conducted in LMI settings, two studies were carried out in the USA, a high-income country, but both were implemented in lower-income community settings, demonstrating the global relevance of this service-delivery approach. Despite these promising findings, the evidence base remains limited and preliminary. Only six studies were identified, with small sample sizes, varied study designs and differing outcome measures, which constrain the strength and generalisability of the conclusions. The review also revealed substantial variability in implementation, reporting practices and evaluation methods, indicating a need for standardisation, adequately powered trials and longer-term follow-up to confirm the effectiveness and scalability of CHW-facilitated hearing-aid provision. Training content, duration and structure varied considerably across studies. Only two studies reported formal post-training competency evaluations,26 27 and only one study used WHO-endorsed training materials.24 This variability highlights the need for standardised training materials and post-training assessments tailored to CHW roles. Formal assessment of CHW competency is essential before service implementation to ensure safe and effective care.28 Incorporating CHW monitoring through quality measures and ongoing competency checks post-training would help ensure a minimum standard of care across diverse contexts.28 Resources such as the WHO’s Primary Ear and Hearing Care Training Manual41 and the Training on Assistive Products Hearing Aids module42 are valuable supports for CHW training. Future studies should adopt a consistent approach to reporting training details, including instructional methods, duration, practical components, evaluation procedures, as well as the qualifications and experience levels of the trainers.

CHWs in the included studies successfully performed technical tasks typically reserved for professionals, including pure-tone audiometry, video otoscopy, impression-taking, hearing aid fitting and post-fitting support. These findings are in line with the WHO’s recommendations for task-sharing to improve access to hearing care.1 28 Notably, the inclusion of otoscopy, a procedure traditionally limited to specialists, demonstrates the feasibility of expanding CHW responsibilities with appropriate training and supervision.23 26 A recent study conducted in South Africa demonstrated the effectiveness of a cerumen management protocol within a community-based service setting, where CHWs provided community members with a cerumenolytic agent (sweet oil) for use for a minimum of 5 days before nurse-led removal.43 After the cerumen management protocol, 50.9% of participants presented with normal otoscopy results.43 Although none of the reviewed studies included CHWs performing basic ear canal management, this task has recently been recommended as suitable for CHWs in the WHO’s Hearing Aid Service Delivery Approach for LMICs.28 Future training and research programmes should consider expanding the CHW scope to include low-risk interventions such as dry mopping, administering eardrops or cerumenolytic agents, which may reduce the burden on specialist ear care professionals.31 It is, however, essential to ensure that the CHW’s scope of practice aligns with the relevant regulatory bodies in each country.

All included studies demonstrated the feasibility of CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision. However, none directly compared CHW-led hearing aid provision with professional or self-fitting models. Future studies should consider such comparisons to better understand the relative strengths and limitations of task-sharing in hearing aid provision. These comparisons should also incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses, considering recruitment, training, supervision and reimbursement models, factors not reported in any of the reviewed studies.31 Cost-effectiveness is particularly important in LMI settings, where affordability already impacts access to and uptake of hearing aids.1 A recent study in Malaysia compared access between public hospitals and private hearing aid centres, with those accessing private care typically presenting later and with more severe hearing loss, possibly due to financial barriers to early uptake.44 Similarly, in Singapore, subsidies provided through the Senior Mobility Fund significantly increased hearing aid adoption, although funding alone did not improve sustained use.45 A cost–utility analysis of a mobile, community-based hearing aid fitting programme in Singapore demonstrated hearing aid provision as cost-effective over a period of 5 years, particularly when long-term device use was maintained.46 Future evaluations of CHW-facilitated models should therefore integrate both cost-effectiveness analyses and strategies to support ongoing hearing-aid use.

Supervision practices varied in type, frequency and intensity across studies. Supportive supervision has been shown to enhance CHW performance.30 47 WHO now recommends incorporating innovative digital technologies that offer automated protocols and built-in quality control, such as environmental noise monitoring or flagged response inconsistencies.28 Additionally, cloud-based data management should also be considered, allowing professionals to monitor assessment results and provide remote, asynchronous reviews in more complex cases.48 49

Primary hearing aid outcome measures varied widely across studies, with tools such as the IOI-HA, HHIE-S, APHAB, GDS-15 and SESMQ commonly used. While these tools are all validated and widely used in hearing healthcare research, this variability limits direct comparisons and synthesis. In addition, most tools were developed in high-income settings and may contain culturally specific items that are not relevant in LMI contexts. For example, Emerson et al reported limited participant familiarity with certain subscales in rural areas. Furthermore, only one study27 included longitudinal outcome measures at 12 months postfitting. Future studies should incorporate longer-term follow-up, ideally 6 to 12 months, to better assess sustained hearing aid use and impact.

All studies relied primarily on self-report measures as primary outcomes, making findings potentially susceptible to recall bias, social desirability bias and interviewer bias, particularly when CHWs assist participants with data collection.50 51 While two studies reported hearing aid use through data logging,26 39 no behavioural or performance-based outcome measures, such as speech recognition in quiet or noise, were used. Incorporating behavioural and objective measures would enhance data validity and provide a more holistic understanding of hearing aid benefit. Additionally, future studies should consider using independent assessors to collect follow-up data to reduce potential bias during outcome assessment.

Reported implementation barriers included hearing aid loss, CHW scope constraints and limited rural familiarity with some tools. Conversely, facilitators included employing CHWs from local communities, providing services in participants’ home languages and using mHealth tools for portable delivery. However, only two studies applied formal frameworks (eg, Bowen, RE-AIM) to assess feasibility. Future studies should systematically apply such frameworks to better identify and address implementation barriers and enablers, ultimately guiding more scalable and context-appropriate programme development.

In addition to the specific research priorities mentioned in table 5, this review highlights that effective integration of CHWs into hearing aid service-delivery models will require structured training, ongoing supervision and alignment with existing primary healthcare systems.28 Training should be standardised, competency-based, and adapted to local languages and literacy levels, and include practical skills such as hearing-aid fitting, counselling and basic troubleshooting.28 52 Ongoing supervision by audiologists or other hearing-care professionals, supported by clear referral pathways and mHealth-enabled remote supervision and cloud-based data monitoring, could address existing challenges such as quality assurance and also allow streamlined management of issues such as earwax impaction or device malfunction, which may be beyond CHW scope.9 28

Additionally, this review’s findings support the global priorities set out in the WHO World Report on Hearing (2021) and the WHO Hearing Aid Service-Delivery Approach (2024), both of which recommend task-sharing among CHWs as a strategy to address the limited number of professionals and improve access to hearing aid services in LMI settings.1 28 The evidence that CHWs can successfully facilitate hearing aid service delivery with positive outcomes provides a foundation for national strategies to integrate CHWs into primary healthcare frameworks.1 For successful integration, alignment of CHW roles with national health regulations, standardised training and supervision, and sustainable financing is essential.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Only English-language, peer-reviewed publications were included, which may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages or grey literature. Additionally, the small number of included studies and their variability in outcome measures limited the ability to conduct a meta-analysis. The reliance on diverse self-report tools also made cross-study comparisons challenging. These limitations highlight the need for more robust, standardised and inclusive research to inform policy and scale-up efforts for CHW-facilitated hearing aid services globally.

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision is feasible and effective. Task-sharing hearing aid provision to CHWs, particularly in LMI settings where access to hearing care is limited, is a potential solution to improve access and uptake. Although there was variability in training protocols, outcome measures and evaluation strategies, CHWs were able to deliver a range of hearing healthcare services, including hearing aid fittings and follow-up support, with positive outcomes reported across studies. This review highlights the need for the development and standardisation of training programmes, CHW competency evaluations and trained professional supervision models. Studies should also adopt culturally relevant, validated outcome measures. Longitudinal follow-up and formal implementation evaluations are necessary to inform the scale-up and sustainability of CHW-facilitated hearing aid provision service-delivery models. While these findings indicate promise, the evidence remains limited and preliminary. Further well-designed, adequately powered studies are required to determine scalability.
