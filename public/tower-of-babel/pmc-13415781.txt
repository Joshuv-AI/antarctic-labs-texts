Changing patterns of telework in spain: the growing gap between preference and practice among teleworkers
Authors: Eva Ariño-Mateo, Matías Arriagada-Venegas, Ana M. Ruiz-Ortega, David Pérez-Jorge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drawing on nationally representative data from the Spanish National Institute of Statistics (INE), this study examines changes in teleworking among teleworkers in Spain between 2021 and 2022; two key years marking the transition from pandemic-driven remote work to the consolidation of hybrid models. Using two waves of nationally representative cross-sectional data from 4,606 teleworkers, results reveal a significant decrease in teleworking frequency, accompanied by a simultaneous improvement in employees’ overall evaluations of the experience, indicating growing maturity and acceptance of remote work. However, the widening gap between actual and desired teleworking days exposes a persistent mismatch between teleworkers’ preferences for telework and organizational constraints. This incongruence, interpreted through the lens of the Job Demands-Resources model and Self-Determination Theory, suggests that while teleworking continues to be positively evaluated by many teleworkers, its limited availability may be experienced as misaligned with employees’ preferences, with potential implications for motivation and well-being suggested by prior research. Sociodemographic patterns show that younger, more educated teleworkers express stronger preferences for telework, reflecting broader transformations in values surrounding flexibility and work-life integration. These findings provide comparative evidence of how teleworking in Spain is evolving from emergency adaptation toward a contested terrain of negotiation between employees’ expectations and organizational control. The study offers insights for designing policies and practices that align flexibility demands with sustainable productivity and inclusion.

Introduction

The telework paradox in the post-pandemic era

The rapid normalization of telework during the COVID-19 pandemic marked a significant shift in how organizations conceptualize work arrangements. Prior to 2020, telework remained marginal, with approximately 5.4% of European Union workers reporting full-time telework1. During pandemic lockdowns, teleworking became a mass phenomenon, compelling organizations to implement remote work at scale and to rapidly reorganize managerial, technological, and coordination practices2.

This abrupt transformation exposed a critical paradox. On one hand, employees who telework gained sustained experience with remote work and developed strong preferences for continued flexibility3. These preferences are not merely convenience-driven; they are consistently associated with lower psychological distress and improved work-life balance when accommodated4. On the other hand, despite robust evidence linking telework to reduced strain and higher job satisfaction under supportive conditions5,6, many organizations have progressively restricted telework access in the post-pandemic period. This divergence has generated a growing gap between employee preferences and organizational provisions, constituting a central challenge in contemporary work design.

From an organizational psychology perspective, this incongruence is not merely logistical. Telework is widely conceptualized as a work arrangement capable of enhancing autonomy, flexibility, and work-life balance, but only when embedded within supportive structures, clear expectations, and adequate resources7. When organizations reduce telework opportunities despite employees’ positive experiences, teleworking employees may perceive a loss of valued flexibility, which prior research has associated with negative implications for well-being8.

This raises a critical theoretical question: what are the psychological consequences when what employees want systematically diverges from what organizations allow? The preference-practice incongruence in telework represents not a neutral mismatch, but a specific condition of experienced deprivation. Employees who work fewer teleworking days than they prefer to occupy a fundamentally different psychological position from those whose preferred arrangement has always been constrained. Empirical evidence shows that discrepancies between preferred and actual teleworking frequency are associated with lower job satisfaction, weaker organizational commitment, reduced engagement, and higher work stress, even after accounting for overall job fit9. This misalignment operates as autonomy frustration: employees who experienced high levels of autonomy during the pandemic perceive subsequent restrictions as loss rather than absence, triggering stronger and more persistent negative motivational responses10. Moreover, this form of person–environment incongruence is conceptually distinct from general job satisfaction and explains unique variance in well-being beyond broader indicators of job fit11.

To examine this paradox rigorously, three variables are theoretically central. Teleworking frequency captures the objective restructuring of work arrangements in the post-pandemic context. Employees’ subjective evaluation reflects the psychological acceptance and perceived legitimacy of remote work practices as they mature over time. Most critically, the gap between actual and desired teleworking days directly operationalizes experienced deprivation, a state of autonomy frustration rather than a simple measure of unmet preferences. Together, these variables enable examination of how preference-practice discrepancies function as a mechanism shaping motivation and organizational commitment.

This study analyzes the evolution of this phenomenon in Spain between 2021 and 2022 using nationally representative data. Specifically, it examines changes in teleworking frequency and employee evaluation, the magnitude of the preference-practice gap, and the sociodemographic factors associated with these attitudes. By doing so, the study contributes to understanding how telework is being renegotiated in the post-pandemic context and highlights the motivational and well-being consequences of unmet flexibility demands.

Understanding telework through multiple lenses

Self-determination theory and psychological needs

Understanding teleworking outcomes requires integrating two complementary theoretical frameworks: Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model. SDT posits that well-being and motivation depend on the satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness12. Teleworking can support autonomy and competence by increasing control over task execution and time management13. In post-pandemic contexts, where autonomy was experienced at scale over an extended period, flexible work has come to be perceived not as a discretionary perk but as a legitimate component of employment arrangements. In Spain, this transition occurred under particularly strict and prolonged lockdown conditions, further consolidating employee expectations around flexibility.

At the same time, teleworking may compromise relatedness by reducing informal social contact if not deliberately supported14. SDT further distinguishes between autonomy satisfaction and autonomy frustration, the latter referring to experiences of external control and constraint that undermine motivation more severely than the simple absence of choice15,16. This distinction explains why restrictions on previously available telework generate sharper motivational declines than stable low-autonomy arrangements. The preference–practice gap thus reflects a loss-oriented psychological state with particularly detrimental consequences for engagement and well-being10.

Job demands-resources model

The JD-R model conceptualizes work contexts in terms of job demands and job resources17. Teleworking functions as a resource by providing autonomy, flexibility, and time savings, while simultaneously introducing demands related to boundary management, self‑supervision, and potential work intensification18. A central proposition of the model is that resources become especially critical when job demands are high.

In post-pandemic contexts characterized by sustained workload, organizational reorganization, digital transformation, and ongoing uncertainty, restricting telework reduces access to a key resource precisely when it is most needed. When organizations remove teleworking opportunities that employees have learned to rely on, they create a compound imbalance in which demands remain high while resources diminish. This configuration has been consistently linked to burnout, reduced engagement, and lower job satisfaction19. The degree of alignment between employee preferences and organizational provisions therefore determines whether teleworking functions as a protective resource or becomes a source of strain.

The theoretical significance of the preference-practice gap

The preference-practice gap is theoretically significant for three interrelated reasons. First, it operates as autonomy frustration rather than as a mere absence of opportunity. Within SDT, autonomy frustration reflects the active thwarting of a fundamental psychological need and has been shown to undermine motivation and well-being more severely than low autonomy alone12,16. In post-pandemic contexts, where autonomy was widely experienced, restricting telework creates a particularly salient loss-oriented psychological state with documented effects on motivation and engagement10. Beyond autonomy frustration dynamics, a second mechanism explains the theoretical importance of the preference-practice gap.

Second, the gap reflects resource depletion within demand-rich environments. From a JD-R perspective, restricting telework removes a critical resource while job demands remain elevated. This structural imbalance has clear and measurable consequences for employee well-being that cannot be addressed through general satisfaction interventions alone18,19.

Third, the gap captures a form of person-environment incongruence that is distinct from general job satisfaction. Person-environment fit research differentiates between overall job fit and domain-specific fit, defined as alignment between individual preferences and organizational provisions within specific work domains11,20. Employees may report overall job satisfaction while simultaneously experiencing acute frustration regarding flexibility. As a result, the preference–practice gap represents a theoretically robust predictor of employee outcomes that cannot be fully compensated through other satisfaction-enhancing mechanisms.

Taken together, autonomy frustration, resource depletion, and domain-specific person-environment incongruence position the preference-practice gap as a central construct for understanding post-pandemic work design. Restrictions on teleworking are therefore not neutral organizational decisions but interventions with direct psychological and motivational consequences for the workforce.

The dual nature of telework: how organizations must balance employee autonomy needs against business demands

Benefits of teleworking in organizational settings

Research consistently shows that when autonomy and competence needs are satisfied, teleworking delivers substantial benefits21. Ideally, moderate levels of telework intensity (e.g., 2–3 days per week) are associated with the most favorable outcomes for employee well-being and performance1. Autonomy and flexibility enable employees to organize their working hours and physical environment according to personal and professional needs, reducing commuting time and enabling work during productive hours22.

Work-life balance represents another important advantage. The freedom to coordinate work and private life supports better work-life balance and reduces work-family conflict23. Research indicates that flexible work arrangements and reduced commuting time contribute to employee well-being. Greater control over the work environment is linked to lower absenteeism, reduced turnover, and higher engagement24. Furthermore, organizational support, including technical assistance and trust-based supervision, has been identified as a critical buffer against psychological strain and isolation25.

Job satisfaction associated with telework has been documented repeatedly in the literature, and these benefits emerge when telework enhances mechanisms such as autonomy, flexibility, and control over working time, thereby improving the fit between workers’ needs and job resources26. Taken together, this evidence reveals that the benefits of telework are not inherent to remote work itself but depend on the alignment between employees’ psychological needs and organizational provisions. This theoretical insight underscores the critical importance of the preference practice congruence examined in this study.

Challenges and risks associated with remote work

However, the JD-R framework reveals a critical tension. The same autonomy that benefits employees can create unmanaged demands if organizational support is lacking. Although remote work offers flexibility and autonomy, it can blur the boundaries between work and personal life. When autonomy becomes excessive or unstructured, it may shift from a resource to a demand, leading to overextension and stress27.

Social isolation and relatedness needs pose the main challenge documented in research. The physical separation from colleagues often reduces social support and informal communication, limiting opportunities for collaboration and belonging28. Systematic reviews indicate that high intensity telework can exacerbate these feelings of loneliness, particularly when virtual communication channels lack ‘media richness’ or fail to replace face-to-face interactions29. This lack of social contact may lead to feelings of isolation and reduced motivation. As SDT posits, relatedness is a fundamental psychological need. Its frustration can erode engagement and well-being30.

Research also identifies work intensification and boundary blurring as related challenges. Teleworking has been associated with increased workload31, often due to the absence of clear boundaries between professional and personal domains. Without explicit structural limits, employees may extend their working hours or struggle to disengage at the end of the day32,33.

Overall, research reflects the dual nature of teleworking as both a resource and a demand. While its benefits have become more salient as organizations and employees adapt, persistent issues, including social isolation, intensified workload, and lack of organizational support, suggest that the satisfactory benefits predicted by JD-R and SDT may be compromised when flexibility is constrained or poorly managed.

These risks become particularly salient when teleworking flexibility is restricted rather than jointly regulated, underscoring the importance of examining not only telework availability but its congruence with employee expectations.

Identifying the gap: why employee preferences and organizational provisions matter

The critical incongruence between preference and practice

Although many studies treat teleworking as a dichotomous variable (telework vs. no telework), some research differentiates among different qualities and intensities of telework (e.g., high‑quality telework vs. low‑quality vs. none) and shows that effects on well-being and job outcomes vary accordingly34. The post-pandemic period introduces a critical dynamic: employees have experienced remote work at scale and developed strong preferences based on lived experience. However, despite this preference for continued flexibility, many organizations have begun limiting remote work access. A critical paradox has emerged: while employee satisfaction with telework has increased and the desire for more opportunities has intensified, many organizations have begun limiting remote work access, creating a pronounced gap between employee preferences and organizational policies35,36.

From an organizational psychology perspective, this incongruence is not merely logistical. Rather, it undermines the fulfillment of fundamental psychological needs12, particularly autonomy as conceptualized by SDT, and simultaneously reduces access to key job resources within the JD-R17. As a result, the preference-practice gap constrains the motivational and well-being benefits that teleworking is theoretically expected to provide.

Operationalizing preference-practice incongruence

When employees’ desired work arrangements deviate substantially from organizational provisions, a state of incongruence emerges in which psychological needs remain unmet, and job resources fall below what is required to sustain motivation and engagement. In this study, preference-practice incongruence is operationalized as the distance between employees’ desired teleworking frequency and their actual work arrangements. This gap captures not merely unmet preferences, but a theoretically meaningful condition of autonomy frustration and resource loss.

This dimension of work design has received limited empirical attention, despite its relevance in the post-pandemic consolidation of hybrid work models. Most existing research relies on single-country or sector-specific samples, limiting generalizability. Nationally representative data from Spain during the 2021 to 2022 transition period provides a unique opportunity to examine how diverse teleworker groups experience the institutionalization of hybrid work.

This study examines which worker populations report larger preference-practice gaps and how this incongruence relates to employee satisfaction. It investigates whether preference-practice incongruence, as measured by the gap between desired and actual teleworking frequency, is associated with differences in employee satisfaction and whether these patterns can be interpreted through SDT and the JD-R framework. By integrating descriptive and analytical approaches, the study addresses a critical but understudied aspect of post-pandemic work organization: the organizational and motivational consequences of restricting teleworking despite documented employee preferences and demonstrated well-being benefits.

Method

Participants and procedure

Data were obtained from the Equipment and Use of Information and Communication Technologies in Households survey (TIC_H), conducted annually by the Spanish National Institute of Statistics (INE)37. The survey follows a stratified, two-stage sampling design based on census sections defined in 2015, which are periodically updated to reflect demographic changes. Within each autonomous community, census sections are grouped into strata by municipality size, and 14 to 15 households are selected per section.

The survey covers the entire Spanish territory and targets main family households and residents aged 16 years or older. From each household, one individual is randomly selected to provide detailed information on Internet use and teleworking practices.

For the present study, only respondents who reported teleworking during the reference period and who completed all telework-related items were included in the analyses. The final sample, therefore, consisted of 4,606 teleworkers, representing 45.7% men and 54.3% women, with a mean age of 46.3 years (SD = 10.1). This subsample is nationally representative of employees who engaged in telework across all autonomous communities in Spain, ensuring comparability between the 2021 and 2022 editions.

Data collection combined three complementary methods: computer-assisted web interviewing (CAWI), computer-assisted telephone interviewing (CATI), and computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI). Respondents were first invited to participate via an introductory letter explaining the survey’s objectives, guaranteeing confidentiality, and providing a unique username and password to access the online questionnaire (CAWI). Households that did not complete the questionnaire within the established timeframe were subsequently contacted by telephone or, if necessary, through face-to-face interviews. Data collection took place between 25 May and 23 August 2021 for the 2021 edition, and between 25 April and 20 August 2022 for the 2022 edition.

All responses were collected anonymously and voluntarily, with no incentives provided. The procedure complied with the INE’s confidentiality protocols and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), ensuring ethical research standards and participant privacy.

Both the 2021 and 2022 TIC_H editions collect the same sociodemographic variables, including age, gender, educational level, and cohabitation status. Measures related to teleworking frequency and the overall evaluation of the teleworking experience were also identical in both years. The 2022 edition additionally incorporated dichotomous items capturing perceived advantages and disadvantages of teleworking, which were not included in 2021. Measures and data analysis

Analyses were restricted to respondents who reported teleworking during the reference period and who completed all telework-related items, ensuring that results reflect the experiences and perceptions of active teleworkers. Data were drawn from two official, nationally representative sources provided by the Spanish National Institute of Statistics (INE): the Equipment and Use of Information and Communication Technologies in Households survey (TIC_H)37. and the Labour Force Survey38. Both datasets include specific items addressing teleworking practices and employees’ subjective evaluations.

Teleworking frequency was measured as the number of days per week employees worked remotely, reported as a continuous variable. The overall evaluation of the teleworking experience was assessed using a single-item Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (“Not at all”) to 10 (“Very much”), with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions.

In the 2022 edition, the INE introduced additional dichotomous (Yes/No) items capturing employees’ perceived advantages and disadvantages of teleworking. Reported advantages included: avoiding commuting, self-management of working time, work–family balance, time saving, comfort of working at home, and saving money. Reported disadvantages comprised: lack of social contact with coworkers, difficulty disconnecting from work, excessive workload, lack of technical resources, poor organization or coordination of remote work, and physical discomfort when working from home.

Finally, several sociodemographic variables were included; age, gender, educational level, and cohabitation status, to examine their associations with teleworking frequency and evaluations.

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 29. Descriptive statistics were first obtained for all variables in both survey waves. To examine differences between 2021 and 2022, we applied statistical tests appropriate to the nature of each variable: categorical sociodemographic variables (gender, educational level, and cohabitation status) were compared using chi-square tests, while age, treated as a continuous variable, was analyzed using an independent samples t-test. Teleworking-related variables measured on ordinal or continuous response scales, such as teleworking frequency and the overall evaluation of the teleworking experience, were compared across years using one-way ANOVAs. Finally, analyses involving perceived advantages and disadvantages of teleworking, as well as regression models examining their associations with teleworking outcomes, were conducted exclusively with the 2022 dataset, since these dichotomous items were collected only in that edition.

The average number of teleworking days per week decreased significantly from 3.31 in 2021 to 3.06 in 2022 (F(1,1889) = 25.12, p < 0.001), indicating a partial return to on-site work following the peak of pandemic-related telework. However, teleworkers’ overall evaluation of their teleworking experience improved significantly from 8.12 to 8.33 (F(1,1889) = 6.99, p < 0.001), suggesting that remote work practices have become more normalized and positively perceived. The gap between actual and desired teleworking days widened significantly in 2022 (M = –0.58) compared with 2021 (M = –0.38; F(1,1889) = 11.06, p < 0.001), indicating that employees’ preferences for teleworking still exceed their current opportunities to telework.

Sociodemographic analyses revealed notable differences in teleworking frequency. Education level was significantly associated with the number of teleworking days per week (F(8,3863) = 2.15, p < 0.05), with individuals holding postsecondary or university degrees teleworking more frequently than those with only primary education. Age differences were also significant (F(2,3870) = 17.42, p < 0.001), as younger teleworkers (17–29 years; M = 3.21) reported teleworking more days per week than older employees (50–69 years; M = 2.64). Gender effects were smaller but remained significant (F(1,3871) = 4.06, p < 0.05), with men teleworking slightly more frequently than women. In contrast, cohabitation status did not significantly influence teleworking frequency (p = 0.734).

To assess the robustness of these sociodemographic patterns, we estimated a multivariate linear regression model predicting teleworking frequency while simultaneously controlling for age, gender, education level, and survey year. The results confirmed that the year effect and age remained statistically significant, indicating that the observed differences between 2021 and 2022 are robust to the inclusion of key sociodemographic controls. Discussion

This study provides a nationally representative picture of how telework evolved among teleworkers in Spain between 2021 and 2022, a period in which emergency remote work gave way to more institutionalized hybrid arrangements. The results reveal three clear year-to-year changes. First, teleworking frequency decreased from 2021 to 2022, reflecting a partial return to on-site work as pandemic restrictions faded. Second, employees’ overall evaluation of the teleworking experience increased in 2022 compared with 2021, suggesting a process of adaptation, stabilization, and normalization of remote work practices. Third, the gap between actual and desired teleworking days widened in 2022, indicating that employees’ expectations for greater flexibility grew faster than the organization’s capacity to accommodate them. Together, these findings portray a teleworking workforce that has learned to telework more effectively and values this arrangement, while organizations remain more cautious in extending remote work.

While the INE dataset does not include psychological measures (e.g., need satisfaction, motivation, well-being, burnout), SDT and the JD-R model provide useful frameworks to interpret why a preference–practice gap may matter. Importantly, our theoretical interpretation is not a direct test of these mechanisms but a structured explanation consistent with prior evidence. From a SDT perspective, this pattern may be interpreted as reflecting differences in the extent to which telework arrangements align with autonomy-related preferences and work–life integration. Positive evaluations despite decreased teleworking frequency suggest that telework practices may have become more stabilized and workable for many teleworkers, yet the widening preference-practice gap may indicate that actual arrangements are increasingly misaligned with some teleworkers’ preferred level of autonomy and flexibility. Within the JD-R model, telework can be conceptualized as providing resources such as reduced commuting, self-management of time, and comfort of working from home, all of which were widely endorsed in our data. At the same time, persistent disadvantages such as lack of social contact, difficulty disconnecting, and workload intensification reveal that telework simultaneously introduces new demands. That evaluations improved while these challenges remain frequent may suggest that teleworkers are adapting to telework’s trade-offs as part of the new hybrid landscape. However, because psychological needs are not directly measured in the present dataset, this interpretation should be understood as a theoretical lens rather than a direct empirical test of SDT mechanisms.

Theoretical implications

First, these findings contribute to the literature on the post-pandemic institutionalization of telework. The combination of reduced teleworking frequency and improved overall evaluations suggests a transition from crisis-driven remote work toward more routinized hybrid arrangements. This pattern indicates organizational and individual learning processes, whereby telework becomes embedded in regular work practices and evaluated through accumulated experience rather than emergency conditions.

Second, the preference–practice gap can be conceptualized as a work-design signal rather than as evidence of individual psychological mechanisms. The widening distance between desired and actual teleworking days reflects growing tension between teleworkers’ expectations for flexibility and organizational constraints on implementation. Framed in this way, the gap functions as an indicator of friction in the design and governance of hybrid work, capturing how flexibility is negotiated, restricted, or unevenly applied across contexts.

Third, sociodemographic differences point to potential stratification processes in access to flexibility and perceived job quality. Younger and more educated teleworkers reported stronger preferences for telework and, in our data, tended to telework more frequently. This pattern suggests that telework may become an additional axis of inequality in job quality, where access to flexibility and the capacity to benefit from it are unevenly distributed across groups.

Individual and contextual differences in telework preferences

Sociodemographic differences reinforce the need to interpret telework experiences heterogeneously. Younger and more educated workers teleworked more frequently and reported more favourable evaluations. These groups typically hold more teleworkable jobs and possess stronger digital skills, conditions that facilitate positive telework experiences. Prior research shows that telework preferences also vary according to personality traits, such as self-discipline, need for structure, and sociability. For example, employees high in self-regulation may manage autonomy effectively, whereas employees with high relatedness needs may find remote work less appealing when social cues are limited39. Household and socioeconomic circumstances also play a central role. Teleworkers without a quiet workspace, those living in smaller dwellings, or those with caregiving responsibilities may experience telework as stressful or physically uncomfortable40. These findings align with our data, which show that substantial proportions of teleworkers reported difficulties with work disconnection, social isolation, and discomfort at home.

Job characteristics further constrain preferences. Some roles require physical presence, specialized equipment, or face-to-face service delivery, which restricts the feasibility of teleworking regardless of employee desire. Others involve security-sensitive information or regulated procedures that cannot be performed remotely. The Spanish picture thus reflects not only differences in what workers prefer, but also differences in what jobs permit.

Telework preferences in tension with organizational requirements

The decrease in teleworking frequency between 2021 and 2022, despite increasingly positive evaluations, suggests that organizations are balancing employees’ desire for autonomy with concerns about data protection, coordination, and service delivery. Remote work environments can introduce security vulnerabilities when employees work from shared spaces or use personal equipment. In sectors where confidentiality or regulated infrastructures are central, limiting telework may be operationally necessary. Similarly, tasks requiring physical interaction, on-site materials, or supervisor oversight cannot be fully relocated to remote environments.

Coordination and socialization processes also matter. Our data show that lack of social contact was the most widely reported disadvantage. For organizations, this perceived isolation raises concerns about knowledge sharing, innovation, and team cohesion, particularly for junior employees who rely on informal learning and mentorship. These operational considerations may explain why some organizations offer fewer teleworking days than employees prefer.

Thus, the Spanish case illustrates an inherent tension between teleworkers’ expectations and organizational requirements, reflected in the widening preference-practice gap.

Telework preference–practice mismatch in the broader literature

A growing body of research shows that a mismatch between preferred and actual telework frequency is associated with reduced well-being, higher strain, and lower satisfaction. Employees who telework less than they desire often experience frustration and decreased job satisfaction, whereas those who telework more than they prefer may face isolation and intensified boundary blurring. Recent evidence demonstrates that both forms of mismatch predict lower mental health and higher stress41,42. Studies conducted during and after the pandemic further show that preference mismatch is associated with reduced productivity and higher stress among new teleworkers43 and with poorer well-being when teleworkability and preferences are misaligned4.

Our findings extend this literature in three ways. First, we show that the mismatch grew in Spain despite improving evaluations, suggesting rising expectations around flexibility. Second, we document this mismatch using nationally representative post-pandemic data, adding population-level evidence to a field largely dominated by sector-specific studies. Third, by incorporating sociodemographic patterns and perceived advantages and disadvantages, we show how inequalities in job teleworkability, digital capability, and home environments shape both preferences and evaluations.

From a theoretical standpoint, preference–practice mismatch can be conceptualized as a form of job resource incongruence. Within SDT, unmet autonomy expectations have been associated with lower intrinsic motivation in prior research. Similarly, within the JD-R model, telework arrangements that are misaligned with employee preferences may fail to function as job resources and may instead be experienced as additional demands, such as isolation or work–home interference. However, because the INE data do not include direct measures of job resources, need satisfaction, or well-being outcomes, these links should be understood as theoretical interpretations rather than empirically tested mechanisms in the present study. Future research should distinguish between “too little” and “too much” telework relative to preferences, since these mismatches likely operate through different psychological mechanisms.

Practical implications

This study offers several implications for organizations designing hybrid work. First, telework is increasingly perceived as a marker of job quality, not a discretionary perk. For occupations and roles where telework is feasible, talent retention may require offering credible telework options, particularly for younger and highly educated teleworkers. However, unrestricted telework is not feasible for all roles. Organizations should adopt differentiated telework policies based on job teleworkability, security requirements, and operational needs. Transparent criteria for determining telework eligibility can prevent perceptions of unfairness.

Second, disadvantages identified by employees must be addressed. The prevalence of social isolation and difficulty disconnecting indicates a need for deliberate practices such as structured team interactions, mentoring programs, explicit norms for availability, and right-to-disconnect policies. Providing technical support and ergonomic equipment can reduce discomfort and help equalize conditions among teleworkers with different home environments.

Third, managers should consider individual circumstances and preferences in designing telework arrangements. This includes accounting for care responsibilities, dwelling conditions, digital skills, and personality traits related to autonomy and relatedness needs. Tailoring support to these individual differences can help mitigate mismatch and enhance satisfaction.

Finally, policymakers should ensure that telework does not widen labour market inequalities. Our findings that higher educated and younger teleworkers work more frequently and evaluate telework more positively suggest that flexible work could become a new axis of inequality. Regulatory frameworks must promote safe, equitable access to telework while protecting teleworkers’ rights to disconnect and to healthy home working conditions.

Future research

Future research should employ longitudinal and multilevel designs to examine whether preference-practice mismatches persist or decline as hybrid systems stabilize. Studies should incorporate personality traits, home workspace characteristics, and job teleworkability to explain individual differences better. Distinguishing between employees who telework more than they prefer and those who telework less than they would like will be essential to understanding the psychological consequences of the mismatch.

Limitations

The study relies on cross-sectional self-report data, which limits causal inference and may introduce common method bias. In addition, key outcomes such as the overall evaluation of the teleworking experience are measured using single-item indicators, which may reduce reliability and construct validity. The sample includes only individuals who teleworked, excluding employees who may desire telework but cannot access it; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as representative of teleworkers rather than the full labour force. Finally, the survey lacks detailed indicators of job teleworkability, task characteristics, household working conditions, management practices, and organizational telework policies, limiting our ability to disentangle structural constraints from individual preferences. Moreover, the comparison between 2021 and 2022 captures aggregate changes over time but does not allow for attributing these differences to specific underlying mechanisms, such as organizational policies, sectoral restructuring, or individual adaptation processes. Broader contextual dynamics occurring during the post-pandemic period may also have contributed to the observed patterns. Despite these limitations, the findings offer a robust and timely portrait of telework in Spain during the transition from pandemic-driven remote work to more stable hybrid models, highlighting the central challenge of aligning organizational constraints with evolving employee expectations.
