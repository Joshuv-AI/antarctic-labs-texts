PLA/hydroxyapatite composite scaffolds fabricated by digital light processing for bone regeneration
Authors: V J Garrido Hernández, D Sánchez Campos, C Velasco Santos, L S Villaseñor-Cerón, R Villafuerte-Segura, V Rodríguez Lugo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone tissue regeneration faces a major challenge: traditional therapies such as autografts and allografts are limited by donor site morbidity, restricted availability, and potential immune rejection. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop synthetic scaffolds that mimic the extracellular matrix while providing adequate mechanical, morphological, and biological properties to promote osteogenesis and angiogenesis. In this study, polylactic acid/hydroxyapatite (PLA/HAp) scaffolds were designed using Digital Light Processing (DLP) 3D printing technology, strategically integrating HAp nanoparticles (NPs) at concentrations ranging from 0 to 5 wt%. The scaffolds exhibited a biomimetic cylindrical architecture with interconnected square pores of 350 µm and a theoretical porosity of 78% calculated from the CAD models. Finite element analysis (FEM) revealed high structural stability under physiological loads, with maximum displacements of 3.09 × 10⁻² mm and stress levels well below the elastic limit of polylactic acid (PLA). Morphological characterization by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) showed a progressive increase in surface roughness with higher HAp content, without agglomeration, as confirmed by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. Cell viability studies using 3T3-L1 fibroblasts demonstrated a significant increase in metabolic activity, with the M4 (4%) and M5 (5%) samples reaching viabilities of 79.8% and 86.1%, respectively, classifying them as highly biocompatible according to ISO 10993-5. These results demonstrate that DLP printing enables the synthesis and design of PLA/HAp cellular scaffolds with precise geometry, excellent mechanical performance, and tunable bioactive properties, positioning them as a promising alternative for bone tissue engineering.

Introduction

Bone tissue is one of the most frequently replaced tissues worldwide, surpassed only by skin and blood. The high demand for bone tissue replacement arises from pathologies such as osteoporosis, osteogenesis imperfecta, fractures, and bone tumors [1]. These conditions often lead to critical-size defects, in which bone loses its ability to generate autonomously, limiting its natural healing capacity.

To address these defects conventional regenerative approaches such as autografts, allografts, and xenografts have been widely used [2]. However, these strategies present significant limitations, including donor site morbidity, limited availability, and potential immune rejection [2]. To overcome these limitations, synthetic cellular scaffolds have emerged as an alternative strategy in bone tissue engineering (BTE). In this context, synthetic cellular scaffolds have emerged as a promising solution, as they aim to mimic the extracellular matrix and provide appropriate physical and chemical cues for tissue regeneration [3, 4].

The biological performance of these scaffolds is strongly influenced by both the material and the structural design, particularly pore size, morphology, and interconnectivity [5–7] It has been reported that pore sizes between 200 and 400 µm are optimal for promoting cell adhesion [5, 8], vascularization, and osteogenesis [5, 9], while maintaining efficient nutrient diffusion and oxygen transport [5, 10–12].

Among the available fabrication techniques, 3D printing has gained significant attention due to its ability to produce scaffolds with controlled architectures and high precision [13, 14]. Conventional methods such as fused deposition modeling (FDM) are widely used; however, they are often limited by relatively low resolution (100–300 µm) and challenges in achieving precise control of pore geometry and material distribution, particularly when incorporating ceramic particles [15–17]. In contrast, vat photopolymerization (VP) techniques such as digital light processing (DLP) offer higher resolution (10–100 µm) and improved control over complex architectures by curing photocrosslinkable resins using ultraviolet light [15, 18]. Despite these advantages, the application of DLP in the fabrication of cellular scaffolds for BTE remains relatively underexplored [19–21].

The selection of suitable biomaterials is critical for scaffold performance. Polylactic acid (PLA), derived from renewable resources such as corn starch and sugarcane, is widely used in biomedical applications due to its biodegradability, biocompatibility, and ease of processing [22]. In addition, PLA exhibits adequate mechanical properties and can be processed using various additive manufacturing techniques, making it a suitable candidate for scaffold fabrication. However, its inherent hydrophobicity and limited bioactivity restrict cell adhesion and biological interaction, which may compromise its performance in bone tissue engineering applications [22]. To address these limitations, hydroxyapatite (HAp, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2) has been incorporated into polymer matrices due to its chemical similarity to the mineral phase of bone and its well-documented bioactive and osteoconductive properties [23, 24]. At the nanoscale, HAp can enhance surface roughness and promote protein adsorption, improving cell adhesion and proliferation. Nevertheless, its incorporation into polymer systems presents challenges, including particle agglomeration, potential reduction in mechanical performance, and difficulties in maintaining printing precision and structural fidelity, particularly at higher concentrations [25–27].

Previous studies have explored PLA/HAp composite scaffolds fabricated primarily using FDM technology. For instance, Mondal et al. [28] reported that the incorporation of HAp NPs improved cell adhesion and proliferation, demonstrating the potential of these composites for bone tissue engineering applications. Similarly, Yan et al. [29] and Subramaniyan et al. [30] observed enhanced cell–material interactions with increasing HAp content, supporting the role of HAp as a bioactive reinforcement phase. However, inconsistent results have also been reported. Wang et al. [31] found that the addition of nano-HAp did not significantly influence cell proliferation, highlighting the complexity of the relationship between composition, processing conditions, and biological response. Furthermore, Habibovic et al. [32] demonstrated that increasing HAp content does not necessarily improve biological performance, suggesting that excessive ceramic loading may negatively affect scaffold functionality.

In addition to biological variability, previous studies have shown that high HAp concentrations can compromise printing precision, structural fidelity, and mechanical performance in additively manufactured scaffolds [33, 34]. These findings emphasize the need to optimize both material composition and fabrication technique to achieve a balance between biological performance and structural integrity.

Despite these advances, most PLA/HAp scaffolds have been fabricated using extrusion-based techniques such as FDM, which present limitations in terms of resolution, pore architecture control, and homogeneous particle distribution. In this context, the potential of DLP technology to fabricate PLA/HAp scaffolds with improved structural precision and controlled microarchitecture has not been fully explored. Therefore, there is a need to investigate the combined effect of fabrication method and material composition on scaffold performance. Accordingly, the aim of this study is to design and fabricate PLA/HAp scaffolds using DLP 3D printing, with controlled pore geometry and homogeneous HAp distribution, and to evaluate their cytocompatibility according to ISO 10993-5. The null hypothesis is that variations in HAp content do not significantly affect scaffold cellular viability under the evaluated DLP fabrication conditions.

Materials and methods

HAp synthesis

HAp was synthesized using a hydrothermal method. Briefly, 4.98 g of calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2, ACS Grade, MEYER-México) were dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water under magnetic stirring at 350 rpm. In parallel, 12.33 g of dibasic ammonium phosphate ((NH4)2HPO4, ACS Grade, MEYER-México) were dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water. The calcium hydroxide solution was added dropwise to the phosphate solution under continuous stirring to promote nucleation and growth of the HAp phase. During the reaction, the pH was adjusted to 7.2 by the gradual addition of nitric acid (HNO₃, 10% v/v, ACS Grade, MEYER-México), prepared at a concentration of 2 M.

The resulting suspension was transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 24 h. After cooling, the product was washed five times with deionized water, removing the supernatant at each step. Finally, the precipitate was dried and calcined at 500 °C for 3 h, following the methodology reported by López-Ortiz et al. [35].

Preparation of the PLA/HAp solution

A mixture of 50 mL of liquid PLA-acrylate photopolymer BioMed White Resin (FLBMWH01, Formlabs, Somerville, USA) and 10 mL of acetone (Lab Grade, MEYER-Mexico) was prepared and subjected to magnetic stirring at 350 rpm and 50 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, hydroxyapatite (HAp) was incorporated at different concentrations to obtain the following formulations: M0 (0%), M1 (1%), M2 (2%), M3 (3%), M4 (4%), and M5 (5%). The dispersion of the inorganic phase within the polymer matrix was further optimized by maintaining continuous stirring at 10 °C for 20 min.

Design and 3D printing of PLA/HAp cellular scaffolds using the DLP technique

PLA/HAp cellular scaffolds were designed and fabricated using Digital Light Processing (DLP) 3D printer (Anycubic Photon M3 Premium, Anycubic Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Biomimetic cylindrical structures with a diameter of 4.5 mm and a thickness of 2 mm were designed. The scaffolds featured an interconnected square pore architecture with a pore size of 350 µm and a theoretical porosity of 78%, calculated from the CAD models (Fig. 1).

a 3D models of PLA/HAp cellular scaffolds (M0–M5) designed in SolidWorks, featuring a diameter of 4.5 mm, a thickness of 2 mm, a pore size of 350 µm, a theoretical porosity of 78%, and interconnected pore architecture. b Fabrication of PLA/HAp cellular scaffolds using DLP 3D printing

Printing was carried out at 10 °C with a printing speed of 15 mm/s, a layer height of 50 µm, and a pre-curing time of 5 s per layer. After fabrication, the scaffolds were cleaned through five washing cycles using isopropyl alcohol to remove residual unpolymerized resin. Subsequently, post-curing was performed in a UV curing and washing station (Anycubic Wash & Cure Station UW-01, Anycubic Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) at a wavelength of 350 nm and a power of 48 W for 180 s. Finally, the samples were stored at room temperature (~25 °C) under controlled conditions prior to structural and chemical characterization.

Structural analysis by the finite element method (FEM)

A static structural analysis was performed to evaluate the mechanical behavior of the 3D cellular scaffold designs under compressive and tensile loading conditions using Finite Element Analysis (FEA) within SolidWorks 2023 (Dassault Systèmes, Waltham, USA). The models were assigned the mechanical properties of PLA, considering a yield strength of 60 MPa, a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3, and a density of 1.24 g/cm³. Thermal effects were not considered. For the simulations, a load of 5 N was applied perpendicular to one surface of the scaffold, while the opposite face was constrained with fixed boundary conditions in all directions. Meshing was performed using second-order (quadratic) solid elements with a curvature-based mesh to improve the accuracy of stress and deformation calculations. A uniform element size of 0.788642 mm was used, resulting in a mesh consisting of 109,559 nodes and 60,661 elements.

Characterization

The crystalline phase of the samples was identified using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker AXS, Karlsruhe, Germany), with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å), operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. A thermogravimetric analyzer (STA 449 F3 Jupiter, NETZSCH-Gerätebau GmbH, Selb, Germany) was used with a heating rate of 10 K/min in a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere at a flow rate of 50 mL/min. Functional groups were elucidated by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), using a Spectrum Gx System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA) in a wave number range from 4000 to 500 cm−1. Morphological analysis was conducted with a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-5900LV, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 20 kV, equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS) to determine the elemental composition.

Cytotoxicity assays

The scaffolds were sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for 30 min, followed by drying under a laminar flow hood and exposure to UV-C radiation for 15 min to ensure complete sterilization. In parallel, 3T3-L1 fibroblasts (ATCC CL-173) were cultured as a monolayer at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose, D5640) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich). This cell line was selected as a preliminary in vitro model due to its well-established proliferation capacity, high reproducibility, and suitability for initial cytocompatibility screening of polymer-based biomaterials.

Cytotoxicity assays were performed in triplicate at 24, 48, 72, and 144 h using samples M0–M5 in 96-well plates (1000 cells/well). After incubation, cells were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated for 3 h with 0.5 mg/mL of 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich). The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 96% ethanol, and absorbance was measured at λ = 550 nm using a BIO-RAD microplate reader (model 1681135). Cell viability was calculated according to Eq. (1), where OD₅₅₀e corresponds to the optical density of the experimental samples and OD₅₅₀b to that of the control.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was carried out using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Design and 3D printing of cellular scaffolds

The results demonstrated that the 3D-printed cellular scaffolds fabricated using DLP technology accurately reproduced the designed dimensional and morphological specifications (Fig. 1). Biomimetic cylindrical structures with a diameter of 4.5 mm and a thickness of 2 mm were designed, featuring interconnected square pores of 350 µm and a theoretical porosity of 78%, as calculated from the CAD models. DLP printing enabled high geometric fidelity, with no significant deviations between the designed and fabricated structures. Additionally, this technique allowed precise control over pore distribution and morphology across the different HAp concentrations (M0–M5).

Static structural analysis

A static structural analysis was carried out using FEM to evaluate the mechanical behavior of 3D cellular scaffolds under compressive and tensile loading conditions relevant to bone tissue engineering applications. Under an applied load of 5 N, the models exhibited stable mechanical behavior, with a maximum displacement of 4.41 × 10⁻² mm located at the peripheral zones (Fig. 2a). These regions, represented in green, may correspond to potential zones of stress concentration and possible initiation of structural failure. Therefore, localized reinforcement in these areas could further improve mechanical stability.

The strain analysis (Fig. 2b) showed low or negligible values throughout most of the structure, confirming the adequate stiffness of the scaffold design under the applied loading conditions. Regarding stress distribution, the von Mises analysis (Fig. 2c) indicated that the maximum recorded strain (6.370 × 10⁻² mm/mm) remained well below the yield strength of the material (6 × 10⁷ Pa), suggesting a high safety factor and adequate structural reliability (Table 1).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Figure 4 presents the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curves of PLA and its composites with HAp. The thermal degradation process can be divided into three distinct stages. The first stage (a), occurring between 30 and 100 °C, is characterized by a minor weight loss (1–2%) associated with the evaporation of adsorbed moisture and possible residual solvents from the processing. The second stage (b), beginning around 320 °C and peaking between 370 and 380 °C, corresponds to the primary thermal decomposition of the PLA matrix, where depolymerization and chain scission mechanisms lead to a major mass loss of ~80%. This stage concludes near 420 °C, beyond which the organic content is almost completely degraded. The third stage (c), above 420 °C, reflects the thermal stability of the inorganic phase, with the remaining mass attributable to HAp. The increasing residual mass observed from M0 to M5 indicates a progressive incorporation of HAp into the matrix. The presence of HAp does not significantly affect the onset or peak degradation temperatures of PLA, but it enhances the thermal residue, consistent with the non-volatile nature of the ceramic filler [36].

FTIR spectroscopic analysis

The FTIR spectrum presented in Fig. 5 corresponds to a series of PLA/HAp composites and reveals systematic changes in the relative intensity of characteristic bands as the inorganic phase increases. In the 3600–3000 cm⁻¹ region (highlighted in red), a broad absorption band is observed, which is attributed to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups (O–H) characteristic of HAp. This band is absent in the neat PLA sample (M0) but progressively increases from M1 to M5, indicating a higher contribution of hydroxyl groups associated with HAp incorporation. In the 3000–2800 cm⁻¹ region (highlighted in light green), corresponding to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the methyl (–CH₃) and methylene (–CH2–) groups of PLA, a gradual decrease in relative intensity is observed as the HAp concentration increases. This attenuation can be attributed to the progressive spectroscopic dilution of the polymeric component caused by the increasing presence of the ceramic phase.

FTIR spectra of PLA/HAp composites with increasing hydroxyapatite content (M0–M5) and reference HAp

The band at ~1750 cm⁻¹ (highlighted in light red) corresponds to the stretching vibration of the carbonyl (C=O) groups of the ester moieties in PLA. Its progressive decrease suggests a reduction in the relative contribution of the organic matrix as the ceramic filler content increases. A similar trend is observed in the 1450–1350 cm⁻¹ region, assigned to C–H bending vibrations, and in the 1250–1050 cm⁻¹ region, associated with C–O–C stretching vibrations of the PLA backbone, where band intensities decrease accordingly, reflecting the lower relative contribution of the polymer phase. In contrast, the characteristic phosphate (PO₄³⁻) bands of HAp become more evident with increasing ceramic content. In the 1050–960 cm⁻¹ region (highlighted in light blue), a systematic increase in the intensity of the asymmetric stretching vibrations (ν₃) of PO₄³⁻ is observed, while in the 600–500 cm⁻¹ region (highlighted in dark blue), the bending vibrations (ν₄) of the phosphate group are detected.

Cell viability

Cell viability was calculated using Eq. (1) relative to the negative control, showing a progressive increase over the incubation period (Fig. 8a). Statistical analysis revealed significant differences in cell viability among the groups (M0–M5) over the evaluated time points from 24 to 144 h (p = 0.00915). At 144 h (Fig. 8b), cell viability values of 55.45% and 67.3% were obtained for samples M0 and M1, respectively. Similarly, samples M2 and M3 showed viability values of 72.7% and 73.7%, respectively. The highest cell viability was observed for samples M4 and M5, which reached 79.8% and 86.1%, respectively.

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that DLP-based 3D printing is a promising approach for the fabrication of cellular scaffolds with features relevant to bone tissue engineering applications. This technique enabled the production of scaffolds with well-defined three-dimensional architecture, interconnected pores, and a designed pore size of 350 µm (Fig. 1b), together with a theoretical porosity of 78% calculated from the CAD models. These structural parameters fall within the range commonly associated with favorable conditions for nutrient transport, vascularization, and cell interaction [5, 10–12, 14].

The close agreement observed between the printed scaffolds and the CAD designs supports the ability of DLP to achieve high geometric fidelity in complex architectures. In addition, the incorporation of HAp within the selected range (0–5 wt%) did not appear to compromise printing precision or scaffold morphology. This is consistent with previous studies reporting that moderate ceramic loadings may enhance biological performance while avoiding some of the drawbacks associated with higher HAp concentrations (>10 wt%), such as reduced mechanical performance, particle agglomeration, and loss of structural control [33, 34].

This behavior is particularly relevant when compared with previous reports on FDM-fabricated PLA/HAp scaffolds, where HAp incorporation has been associated with limitations in morphology, pore architecture, and porosity control. In some cases, an adequate distribution of the ceramic phase has only been achieved in scaffolds with pore sizes larger than 500 µm, while additional challenges have been reported during the preparation and extrusion of composite filaments [33]. These limitations highlight the potential advantage of DLP processing for achieving better structural fidelity and more controlled incorporation of HAp at low concentrations.

The FEM-based structural analysis allowed evaluation of the mechanical response of the scaffolds under a 5 N load in both compression and tension. The maximum stress values obtained (2.59 × 10⁷ Pa and 2.51 × 10⁴ Pa, respectively) remained below the material’s yield strength (6.0 × 10⁷ Pa), indicating that the structure operates within the elastic regime and suggesting adequate mechanical reliability under the evaluated conditions. Furthermore, the stress values fall within the range reported for trabecular bone (0.1 × 10⁷ to 3 × 10⁷ Pa) [37, 38], suggesting that the scaffold may withstand physiological loading conditions.

The localization of maximum displacement and strain at the peripheral regions indicates potential stress concentration zones, where structural optimization may be required. Design strategies such as increasing strut thickness, reducing local porosity, or implementing gradient architectures could improve load distribution and reduce the risk of failure. However, since the mechanical behavior was assessed only through finite element simulations, experimental validation will be necessary to confirm the predictive behavior of the proposed structures.

The XRD pattern of the HAp sample (Fig. 3) shows the monoclinic and hexagonal phases of HAp. Overall, these results indicate that the synthesized HAp exhibits well-defined crystallinity and the expected phase composition, supporting its suitability for integration into PLA-based scaffolds, since crystallinity and phase purity can influence both mechanical stability and cell–material interactions. Likewise, FTIR analysis did not reveal new bands or significant peak shifts, indicating that HAp incorporation does not substantially modify the chemical structure of the photocured polymer network under the studied conditions (Fig. 5). These results are complemented by TGA, where the PLA-based system exhibited controlled thermal degradation and remained stable up to ~300 °C. This thermal behavior suggests that the material can maintain its integrity during DLP processing and subsequent handling, supporting its applicability for scaffold fabrication [33, 34].

The morphological analysis by SEM-EDS allows for the evaluation of the impact of the progressive incorporation of HAp NPs. The M0 sample presents a smooth surface (Fig. 6a). Starting from the M1 sample (Fig. 6b), a noticeable change in surface topology is observed, with the appearance of roughness induced by the incorporation of ceramic NPs. This trend gradually increases in the M2, M3, M4, and M5 samples (Fig. 6b–f), directly influenced by the increase in HAp content, consistent with previous studies [34]. The roughness is more pronounced in the M4 and M5 samples, which is a favorable indicator for cell adhesion [39]. In the samples with high HAp concentrations (M4 and M5), the scaffolds maintained their intended morphology and pore geometry. This contrasts with reports using FDM printing, where high HAp content can cause print defects or alter pore structure, highlighting the ability of DLP printing to achieve high-fidelity fabrication even at higher HAp concentrations [25, 33, 34, 40]. SEM-EDS analysis showed a homogeneous distribution of Ca and P on the scaffold surface without evidence of agglomeration. Although internal distribution was not directly evaluated, the uniform biological response and consistent structural integrity across samples suggest an adequate dispersion of HAp within the polymer matrix at the studied concentrations (0–5 wt%). Together, these findings suggest that the selected DLP conditions and HAp concentrations allow preservation of both scaffold morphology and material quality, which are important factors for supporting cell adhesion and proliferation [23, 24].

The MTT assay showed a progressive increase in cell viability from 24 to 144 h for the M0–M5 scaffolds (Fig. 8a). According to ISO 10993-5 [41] samples M0 and M1 (0 and 1 wt% HAp) remained below the 70% viability threshold and, therefore, were classified as cytotoxic, whereas samples M2 and M3 (2 and 3 wt% HAp) exceeded this threshold, with viability values of 72.7% and 73.7%, respectively. The highest viability values were observed for samples M4 and M5 (4 and 5 wt% HAp), which reached 79.8% and 86.1%, respectively, indicating favorable cytocompatibility. Within the evaluated conditions, these results identify M4 and M5 as the most favorable compositions, suggesting that moderate HAp incorporation can enhance biological response without compromising scaffold architecture.

The improved cellular response observed at higher HAp contents, particularly in M5, may be attributed to changes in scaffold surface properties induced by the ceramic phase. Specifically, HAp incorporation may increase surface roughness and promote protein adsorption from the culture medium, thereby enhancing cell adhesion and proliferation. This trend is consistent with previous reports. For example, Mondal et al. [28] developed PLA-based scaffolds fabricated by FDM and modified their surface with HAp Nps, reporting enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation in MG-63 osteoblast-like cells. Although differences in fabrication technique, cell model, and HAp incorporation strategy exist, both studies support the beneficial role of HAp in improving the biological response of PLA-based scaffolds. In contrast, Wang et al. [31] reported that the incorporation of n-HAp into PLA scaffolds fabricated by FDM did not significantly enhance cell proliferation over the evaluated concentration range. Compared with the present study, this discrepancy may be associated with differences in fabrication method, scaffold architecture, HAp dispersion, and cell model. In particular, the DLP-based approach employed here may provide improved control over microstructure and pore interconnectivity, which are factors known to influence cellular response.

Nevertheless, the use of 3T3-L1 fibroblasts provides only a preliminary assessment of cytocompatibility and does not represent osteogenic behavior. In this sense, the present findings should be interpreted as an initial indication of biocompatibility rather than a direct demonstration of bone regeneration potential. Even with this limitation, the present study shows that moderate HAp incorporation, particularly in samples M4 and M5, improves cellular response without compromising scaffold structural fidelity, supporting the relevance of DLP processing as a strategy for the design of PLA/HAp scaffolds with controlled microarchitecture.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that DLP 3D printing enables the fabrication of PLA/HAp scaffolds with controlled architecture, including a designed pore size of 350 µm and a theoretical porosity of 78%, while preserving structural fidelity across the evaluated compositions. HAp incorporation, particularly at 4–5 wt%, was associated with the most favorable cytocompatibility, reaching cell viability values above 79% and up to 86.1% according to ISO 10993-5. In addition, physicochemical characterization confirmed the expected PLA/HAp system, while FEM analysis provided a preliminary indication of adequate mechanical stability under the evaluated loading conditions.

These findings support the potential of the PLA/HAp-DLP approach for the fabrication of scaffolds with controlled microarchitecture and favorable cytocompatibility for bone tissue engineering applications. However, the present study remains a proof-of-concept, and further investigations are required to fully assess scaffold performance, including experimental mechanical validation, degradation studies, and osteogenic evaluation. Future work may also explore alternative TPMS-based architectures to further improve mass transport, mechanical behavior, and cellular response.
