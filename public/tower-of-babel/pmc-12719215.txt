5-HT promotes bronchopulmonary dysplasia via TGM2-mediated serotonylation
Authors: Pengpeng Cai, Ruidong Ding, Chunyu Yin, Xu Chen, Yu Mao, Keyu Lu, Beibei Wang, Yang Yang, Mingshun Zhang, Rui Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a common chronic lung disease in preterm neonates. Untargeted metabolomics of serum samples revealed that tryptophan metabolism may be involved in the pathogenesis of BPD. Specifically, the serum level of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), a metabolite of tryptophan, is increased in preterm infants with BPD. Similarly, the pulmonary level of 5-HT is increased in a mouse model of hyperoxia-induced BPD. Administration of 5-HT directly impaired lung maturation. Tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TPH1) is the rate-limiting enzyme in the biosynthesis of 5-HT. As expected, the TPH1 inhibitor LP533401 mitigated lung injury in pups exposed to 85% oxygen. Mechanistically, 5-HT promotes post-translational serotonylation via transglutaminase 2 (TGM2) in alveolar epithelial cells. TGM2 expression is increased in the pulmonary tissues of BPD-like mice. Blocking TGM2 partially reversed BPD-like damage to the lungs. Collectively, our findings highlight the roles of 5-HT and serotonylation in the pathogenesis of BPD.

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is the most common chronic lung disease in preterm infants and poses a significant threat to their survival and long-term quality of life. BPD extends beyond its classification solely as a neonatal disease; instead, it presents as a systemic disease with a lasting influence on both adult health and overall quality of life.1 Currently, while certain pharmacotherapeutic approaches are employed, there is still no specific treatment for BPD, and prevention or early intervention remains the only way to alter disease severity and prognosis. The pathogenesis of BPD remains largely unclear.

Lung cellular metabolism, including the catabolism and anabolism of glucose, lipids, and proteins, is essential for maintaining cellular functions.2 Emerging evidence indicates that disruptions in these metabolic processes can disturb lung homeostasis, leading to a cascade of physiological and pathological changes. For example, alterations in fatty acid metabolism have been implicated in lung injury and repair, inflammation, and immune modulation.3 Notably, infants with severe BPD often exhibit growth failure and increased metabolic expenditure,4 indicating that abnormalities in metabolic processes may contribute to BPD. Specific amino acid profiles, such as reduced levels of citrulline, arginine, and tyrosine, alongside elevated phenylalanine levels at birth, have been strongly associated with a heightened risk of BPD and its complications.5 These findings prompt further investigation into the metabolic alterations and mechanisms underlying BPD.

Increasing evidence indicates that abnormal tryptophan metabolism is widely involved in diseases of the digestive, nervous, and respiratory systems.6,7,8 Tryptophan metabolism primarily involves three pathways: kynurenine, 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), and indole. The kynurenine pathway primarily occurs in the liver; the gut microbiota metabolizes a small amount of tryptophan into indole, whereas the lung is a crucial site for 5-HT metabolism. Numerous studies have shown that 5-HT is involved in the development of lung diseases.9,10 Targeted therapies for pulmonary hypertension based on 5-HT metabolism have now entered clinical trials.11 These findings suggest that the tryptophan metabolite 5-HT may be related to the pathogenesis of BPD and may have clinical significance. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the role and mechanisms of the tryptophan metabolite 5-HT in BPD.

Results

Metabolic alterations in the sera of preterm infants

To measure circulating metabolites in BPD in a comprehensive manner, we performed untargeted metabolomics on blood serum samples collected from preterm neonates with a postmenstrual age under 32 weeks. These neonates were divided into two groups according to the level of oxygen needed at 36 weeks postmenstrual age (Table S1). We performed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based cation and anion analyses, and 395 serum metabolites across the BPD and NO-BPD groups were identified via LC-MS, each confirmed through MS2 data. The orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) score scatterplot revealed complete separation of sample points among the two groups (Figure 1A), and the precision of the OPLS-DA model was further confirmed by a permutation test (Figure 1B). Through multivariate statistical analysis, we subsequently screened a total of 300 differentially abundant metabolites (Figures 1C and 1D). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis revealed that tryptophan metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, and methane metabolism were highly correlated with the differentially abundant metabolites (Figures 1E and 1F). Increased levels of serum and pulmonary 5-HT in BPD

Tryptophan metabolism primarily involves three metabolic pathways: the kynurenine, 5-HT, and indole pathways. Given the limitations of untargeted metabolomics, we were unable to detect all the metabolites involved in tryptophan metabolism. Nevertheless, an investigation revealed that lung levels of 5-HT, a metabolite of tryptophan, were 34 times greater in infants who developed severe BPD than in control individuals.12 Furthermore, the first neuroendocrine cells identified at 8 weeks of gestation appeared well differentiated compared with adjacent epithelial cells and were immunoreactive for 5-HT.13 These findings highlight the potential association between 5-HT and lung development in BPD patients.

Ideally, the baseline characteristics between non-BPD and BPD preterm infants should be similar. However, in the discovery cohort, the BPD and non-BPD groups were significantly different in the gestational age and birth weight (Table S1). This difference may have influenced the metabolomic analysis. To reduce the potential influence of baseline characteristics, we recruited similar non-BPD and BPD preterm infants in the validation cohort. The peripheral blood serum samples were taken from 11 infants without BPD and 12 infants with BPD (Table S2). To further investigate the effects of 5-HT on BPD, we established a neonatal mouse model of BPD induced by postnatal hyperoxia following established intervention protocols14 (Figure 2B). Compared with those from the control group, histomorphological analysis of lung tissues from the BPD group revealed arrested alveolar development, characterized by a significantly decreased number of alveoli and a pronounced increase in the mean linear intercept (MLI) (Figures 2C–2E). Pulmonary function tests conducted on postnatal day (PN) 14 demonstrated increased resistance of the lung (RL) and decreased respiratory dynamic compliance (Cdyn) (Figures 2F and 2G), confirming the successful modeling of BPD both morphologically and functionally. Subsequently, ELISA revealed significantly higher 5-HT levels in lung tissues from the BPD model mice (Figure 2H), which is in line with the finding that 5-HT was elevated in preterm neonates with BPD. Furthermore, following the isolation and purification of primary alveolar epithelial cells from mouse lung tissues, we again demonstrated that 5-HT concentrations in cell lysates were significantly greater in BPD mice than in control mice (Figure 2I). In summary, these data indicate that 5-HT levels are increased in the development of BPD.

5-HT contributes to BPD development

To substantiate the involvement of 5-HT in the pathogenesis of BPD, the mice in the experimental group received an intraperitoneal injection of 5-HT (1 nmol/g) from PN3 to PN7, whereas those in the control group received injections of normal saline (Figure 3A). Notably, the lung 5-HT levels in the experimental group significantly increased at PN7 (Figures 3B and 3C). Histomorphological analysis of lung tissues revealed that 5-HT impaired alveolar development, leading to a notable reduction in the alveolar count and an increase in the MLI (Figures 3D–3F). Pulmonary function tests revealed that respiratory resistance increased and Cdyn decreased in the experimental group (Figures 3G–3I), suggesting that 5-HT may induce BPD-like alterations in the developing lungs. Furthermore, when BPD group mice were intraperitoneally injected with LP-533401 (25 mg/kg), a TPH1 inhibitor, at PN3 (Figure 4A), lung 5-HT levels were markedly decreased at PN7 (Figure 4B). Notably, the administration of LP-533401 promoted lung development in BPD mice (Figures 4C–4E), as evidenced by reduced respiratory resistance and increased Cdyn (Figures 4F and 4G). 5-HT disrupts alveolar epithelial cell function and contributes to BPD pathogenesis

Alveolar maturation largely depends on the behavior of alveolar epithelial cells, including cell proliferation and migration.15 Notably, 5-HT is recognized for its pivotal role in fetal brain development, exerting influences on cell migration, proliferation, and maturation.16,17,18 We hypothesized that 5-HT may regulate the biological functions of alveolar epithelial cells, thereby contributing to the development of BPD. To validate this hypothesis, flow cytometry was used to assess the apoptosis of MLE-12 cells following 5-HT treatment. Indeed, 5-HT promoted the apoptosis of MLE-12 cells (Figures 5A and 5B). Furthermore, using a CCK8 kit, we observed a notable deceleration in the proliferation of MLE-12 cells following treatment with 5-HT (Figure 5C). Subsequent scratch tests revealed a considerable reduction in the migration area of MLE-12 cells treated with 5-HT (Figures 5D and 5E). To further explore the in vivo relevance of these effects, TUNEL staining was performed on lung tissues from neonatal mice. As shown in Figures 5F and 5G, exogenous 5-HT administration significantly increased the number of TUNEL-positive cells. Consistent with these findings, pups subjected to BPD modeling presented elevated levels of alveolar apoptosis relative to controls, which was notably reduced following treatment with the transglutaminase 2 (TGM2) inhibitor ZED-1227 (vilagletistat) (Figures 5H and 5I). As expected, 5-HT reduced the Ki67+ proliferating cells in the lung tissues (Figures 5J and 5K). Collectively, these data provide further in vivo evidence that 5-HT disrupts alveolar epithelial homeostasis, thereby contributing to impaired alveolar development in BPD.

5-HT increases serotonylation in alveolar epithelial cells

5-HT functions by binding to 5-HT receptors or forming covalent bonds with target proteins via transfer to glutamine residues by TGM2, a modification known as serotonylation.19 Given that alveolar epithelial cells express crucial components of the serotonergic system, including the serotonin transporter (SERT), the 5-HT catabolic enzyme monoamine oxidase A, and the serotonylation enzyme TGM2,20,21,22 we sought to investigate whether serotonylation regulates the behavior of alveolar epithelial cells. Western blot analysis initially revealed a significant increase in TGM2 expression in the lungs of BPD-like mice (Figure 6A). Given this observation, a critical aspect of the pivotal role of TGM2 in alveolar epithelial cell behavior was its transamidation activity, which was assessed via a colorimetric method in the lungs of BPD mice, which revealed a significant increase in TGM2 activity (Figure 6B). Either 85% oxygen or exogeneous 5-HT caused BPD; 85% oxygen increased TGM2 expression in the lung tissues. While the increased expression and activity of TGM2 suggest that serotonylation may be activated in the lungs of BPD mice, the capacity of TGM2 to catalyze serotonylation in alveolar epithelial cells remains uncertain. 5-PT, an alkyne-functionalized 5-HT derivative, can be taken up by living cells and serves as a substrate for serotonylation.23 To identify protein serotonylation in alveolar epithelial cells, we employed copper-click chemistry to immunoprecipitate biotin-conjugated 5-PT, followed by targeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Figure 6D). This approach identified 1,715 proteins in the control group and 1,560 in the 5-PT group, with 121 proteins uniquely detected in the 5-PT group (Figures 6E and 6F). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis indicated that these 5-PT-specific proteins were predominantly associated with cellular processes (n = 105) (Figure 6G). Notably, several proteins linked to cell behavior were identified (Figure 6H), providing preliminary insights into the mechanisms by which serotonylation may contribute to BPD pathogenesis.

To verify the role of serotonylation in BPD, BPD group mice were intraperitoneally injected with ZED-1227 (5 mg/kg) from PN3 to PN7 (Figure 7A). Notably, lung histomorphological analysis revealed that the alveolar count in the ZED-1227 group increased and the MLI decreased significantly (Figures 7B–7D). Discussion

Metabolomics offers a comprehensive view of the metabolic state of organisms, providing a novel approach to studying disease mechanisms. To date, several studies have utilized metabolomics to identify diagnostic biomarkers of BPD. For example, elevated levels of lactate, taurine, myoinositol, and trimethylamine N-oxide in urine have been associated with a greater risk of developing BPD.24 Additionally, metabolomic analysis of amniotic fluid in late gestation indicated that neonates exposed to heightened in utero oxidative stress and adrenocortical insufficiency are more likely to develop BPD.25 Furthermore, disruptions in lipid metabolism, particularly delayed phosphatidylcholine biosynthesis, have been implicated in the development of BPD.26,27 While these studies provide valuable insights, a more comprehensive approach, such as untargeted metabolomics of peripheral blood serum, may offer a deeper understanding of the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying BPD.

As the principal repository of metabolites, serum provides a broader reflection of the metabolic landscape of the lungs, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic alterations in infants with BPD. Through untargeted metabolomics analysis of peripheral blood serum, we identified significant metabolic differences associated with the tryptophan metabolism pathway. Notably, we observed a marked increase in 5-HT levels in the peripheral serum of BPD infants, as well as in the lung tissues and primary lung epithelial cells of hyperoxia-exposed mice. 5-HT is synthesized from L-tryptophan via the rate-limiting enzyme tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), which exists in two isoforms: TPH1 and TPH2.28 Peripheral 5-HT is produced primarily by enterochromaffin cells through TPH1 and is subsequently sequestered by platelets via the SERT.29 Consistent with our findings, SERT expression is significantly elevated in the lungs of mice subjected to hyperoxia-induced acute lung injury.20 The mechanisms of increased tryptophan metabolism and 5-HT in BPD warrant further research.

While the current study focuses on 5-HT as a representative downstream product of tryptophan metabolism, we acknowledge that tryptophan is metabolized through multiple biologically significant pathways, including the kynurenine and indole derivative branches. Ongoing investigations in our laboratory are exploring these additional pathways to determine whether broader perturbations in tryptophan metabolism occur in BPD. These future studies, which will involve targeted metabolite quantification and pathway enrichment analyses, aim to clarify the specificity of 5-HT alterations and delineate the relative contributions of other tryptophan-derived metabolites in the context of BPD.

Furthermore, early postnatal administration of exogenous 5-HT via intraperitoneal injection resulted in histological changes indicative of impaired alveolar development, including a significant reduction in the alveolar count and an increase in the MLI. Functional assessments demonstrated increased respiratory system resistance and decreased Cdyn. Conversely, the TPH1 inhibitor LP-533401, which targets 5-HT synthesis, improved lung development in BPD model mice, reducing respiratory system resistance and increasing Cdyn. Owing to its diverse receptor systems and intricate signaling pathways, 5-HT exerts wide-ranging functional effects. Various immune cells are responsive to 5-HT,30,31,32 and recent research has identified 5-HT as a novel regulator of alveolar macrophage function through the 5-HT2C receptor.33 Moreover, 5-HT has been shown to inhibit the epithelial sodium channel through direct interaction.34 Histone serotonylation has also been found to promote neuronal gene transcription during neuronal cell differentiation.23 Our findings indicate that 5-HT can directly target alveolar epithelial cells, inducing serotonylation of specific proteins, which underlies its role in BPD pathogenesis.

5-HT, a metabolite of tryptophan, is known to regulate diverse cellular functions, including proliferation, migration, and differentiation. BPD is currently considered a disorder of disrupted lung development secondary to repeated prenatal and postnatal injuries and abnormal reparative responses.35 Type II alveolar epithelial cells (AT2) are essential in this process because of their ability to migrate, proliferate, and differentiate into type I cells during epithelial repair.36 In this study, we demonstrated that 5-HT significantly impaired the functional behavior of alveolar epithelial cells: it promoted apoptosis, inhibited proliferation, and attenuated migration in MLE-12 cells. These alterations suggest that excess 5-HT may interfere with the regenerative capacity of the alveolar epithelium, thereby contributing to the impaired alveolarization characteristic of BPD. To gain insight into the underlying molecular mechanisms, we applied a bio-orthogonal labeling strategy using 5-PT and identified serotonylated proteins in alveolar epithelial cells. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that many of these proteins are involved in the regulation of cell behavior. Notably, activating transcription factor 2 (ATF-2) has emerged as the most enriched serotonylated target. ATF-2 is a transcription factor activated by phosphorylation through the p38 and JNK pathways in response to extracellular stressors, including oxidative stress and inflammation.37 Its activation leads to the transcription of genes involved in apoptosis, DNA damage repair, and growth control. Neuron-specific inactivation of ATF-2 results in significant motoneuron loss in regions such as the hypoglossal, abducens, and facial nuclei, with affected motoneurons undergoing caspase-dependent and -independent cell death during later embryonic stages.38 In the cytoplasm, ATF-2 interacts with HK1- and VDAC1-containing complexes at the mitochondrial outer membrane, leading to impaired mitochondrial membrane potential, mitochondrial leakage, and promotion of cell death.39 Serotonylation of ATF-2, as detected in our study, may alter its transcriptional activity, subcellular localization, or interactions with mitochondrial complexes, thereby promoting epithelial apoptosis. This may partially explain the enhanced cell death and impaired migration observed after 5-HT treatment. In addition, we identified sperm-associated antigen 5 (SPAG5), a known regulator of mitotic spindle stability, as a serotonylated protein. Under stress conditions, SPAG5 can sequester raptors to stress granules and inhibit mTORC1 activity, thereby modulating cell growth and survival.40 These findings indicate that 5-HT may interfere with multiple key regulators of cell fate via serotonylation.

While our data provide initial evidence linking 5-HT to alveolar epithelial cell dysfunction via protein serotonylation, the full spectrum of its downstream effects remains to be elucidated. 5-HT can exert effects via both receptor-mediated signaling and covalent serotonylation catalyzed by TGM2. Furthermore, 5-HT has been implicated in the regulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory responses—two major contributors to BPD pathogenesis. Whether the deleterious effects observed in our study are mediated through TGM2-dependent serotonylation or through parallel activation of oxidative or inflammatory signaling cascades remains to be determined. Future investigations combining TGM2 inhibition, antioxidant intervention, and inflammatory profiling will be essential to clarify these mechanisms. In summary, our findings suggest that 5-HT disrupts alveolar epithelial cell function and lung development, at least in part, through the serotonylation of stress-related transcriptional regulators such as ATF-2, providing a potential mechanistic link between serotonin dysregulation and BPD pathogenesis.

In summary, we demonstrated that tryptophan metabolism was the most significant pathway involved in BPD. Specifically, 5-HT was found to mediate cellular behavior through serotonylation, contributing to the development of BPD. The absence of 5-HT directly improved lung development in BPD-like mice and partially restored lung function. 5-HT presents a novel opportunity for the prevention and treatment of BPD.

Limitations of the study

This study is not without limitations. First, untargeted metabolomics covers a limited range of metabolites and makes precise quantitative analysis challenging. The incorporation of targeted metabolomics analysis of the tryptophan metabolism pathway in the future could facilitate a deeper exploration of the biological significance of 5-HT in BPD. Second, the sample sizes for the discovery and validation cohorts were small. Large cohorts from multiple centers would strengthen our observations. Finally, while we identified and screened cell behavior-related proteins undergoing serotonylation, we did not perform further mechanistic studies, which will be the focus of our future work.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals

Experiments were performed on C57BL/6 pups aged 1 to 14 days. Male and female mice were analyzed. Gender as a social construct was not applicable to our study. The C57BL/6 mice used in this investigation were procured from the Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Research and Care Committee of Nanjing Medical University, with the permission number IACUC-2403025.

Infant blood samples

Patients hospitalized in Nanjing Children's Hospital’s neonatal department from January 2021 to July 2021 were selected. With informed consent from their guardians, blood samples were collected by individual venipuncture into tubes without EDTA. Blood samples were then centrifuged at 4°C to isolate the serum, which was subsequently stored at −80°C. None of the blood samples collected from the patients had been subjected to steroid treatment prior to collection. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Children's Hospital (Ethics No: 202110094-1). The sex and age of the human subjects are reported in the current manuscript (Table S1).

Cell culture

The MLE-12 cell line was obtained from ATCC and grown at 37°C with 5% CO2. MLE-12 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), mycoplasma elimination reagent and penicillin/streptomycin. The MLE-12 cell line was used directly from ATCC vials and was not specifically authenticated for this study.

Method details

Animal models

The BPD procedure was established as previously reported.41 Neonatal mice from several litters were immediately mixed after birth and randomly divided into two groups: the normoxia group and the hyperoxia group. In the hyperoxia group, neonatal mice and the nursing dams were kept in a closed container from postnatal day (PN) 1 to PN7, with oxygen continuously supplied to maintain the oxygen concentration at 85%. The temperature inside the high-oxygen container was maintained at 22°C, with humidity controlled within the range of 50%-60%, simulating light‒dark cycles. The control group was maintained in normal air (21% O2), with all other breeding conditions identical to those of the hyperoxia group. To avoid the toxicity of hyperoxia and eliminate maternal differences between groups, the nursing dams in the hyperoxia group were exchanged with those in the control group every 24 hours.

For in vivo administration, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with 5-HT (MCE, USA), LP-533401 (MCE, USA), or ZED-1227 (TargetMol, USA). 5-HT was dissolved in normal saline and administered at a dose of 1 nmol/g via intraperitoneal injection. LP-533401 was dissolved in a mixture of 10% DMSO and 90% 20% SBE-β-CD in saline and administered at a dose of 25 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection. ZED-1227 was dissolved in a solution containing 5% DMSO, 30% PEG300, 5% Tween 80, and 60% ddH2O and administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection. The doses used were selected on the basis of previously published studies.42,43,44 Injections were performed from postnatal day 3 (PN3) to postnatal day 7 (PN7). Vehicle control mice received injections of the respective solvents.

Untargeted metabolomics

Fifty microlitres of serum was transferred to an EP tube. After the addition of 200 μL of extract mixture (acetonitrile:methanol = 1:1, containing an isotopically labeled internal standard mixture), the samples were vortexed for 30 s, sonicated for 10 min in an ice–water bath, and incubated for 1 h at -40°C to precipitate the proteins. The sample was subsequently centrifuged at 12000 rpm (RCF=13800×g, R= 8.6 cm) for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a fresh glass vial for analysis. LC‒MS/MS analyses were performed via a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo, USA) with a UPLC BEH amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) coupled to a Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia hydroxide in water (pH = 9.75) (A) and acetonitrile (B). The autosampler temperature was 4°C, and the injection volume was 3 μL.

A QE HFX mass spectrometer was used for its ability to acquire MS/MS spectra in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode under the control of acquisition software (Xcalibur, Thermo, USA). In this mode, the acquisition software continuously evaluates the full-scan MS spectrum. The ESI source conditions were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 30 Arb; aux gas flow rate, 25 Arb; capillary temperature, 350°C; full MS resolution, 60000; MS/MS resolution, 7500; collision energy, 10/30/60 in NCE mode; and spray voltage, 3.6 kV (positive) or -3.2 kV (negative).45

The raw data were converted to mzXML format via ProteoWizard and processed with an in-house program, which was developed via R and XCMS, for peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration.46 An in-house MS2 database (BiotreeDB, China) was subsequently used for metabolite annotation. The cutoff for annotation was set at 0.3.

Lung histology and morphometry

Pups were euthanized at PN7 by 10 μL/g intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (Merck, Germany). Following euthanasia, the mice were tracheotomized, and the lungs were perfused for more than 15 min at 25 cm H2O hydrostatic pressure with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China). The left lung was subsequently removed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, and then embedded in paraffin. Afterward, the lung tissue was sliced into 5-μm-thick sections. The quantitative assessment of lung histomorphology was conducted via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining following established procedures.47 Five nonoverlapping areas per slide were captured at ×200 magnification under consistent lighting conditions and optical settings. To minimize unintentional observer bias, photomicrographs were captured by an investigator blinded to the assigned groups. Subsequently, ImageJ was used to analyze the images to quantify the number of alveoli and determine the mean linear intercept (MLI).

Immunohistochemistry analysis

For the embedded lung tissues, 5-μm sections were cut and deparaffinized. Antigen retrieval was conducted in 10 mM citrate buffer, pH 6.0, using a pressure cooker for 10 minutes. To inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity, 3% H2O2 solution was applied to the slides for 15 minutes, followed by a 30-minute blocking step with 3% BSA in PBS. The slides were subsequently exposed to rabbit anti-5-HT antibody (Abcam, 1:400), or rabbit anti-ki67 antibody (Servicebio, 1:400) for 1 hour at room temperature. The slides were further incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (EarthOx Life Sciences, 1:500) for 50 minutes at room temperature. Freshly prepared DAB chromogenic reagent was added to mark the tissues. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin staining solution and scanned with a digital tissue section scanner (KF-PRO-020-HI, KFBIO, China). Images of the slides were captured with Kviewer 1.7 viewing software (KFBIO, China). ImageJ was used to analyze the images to quantify average optical density (AOD). Briefly, deconvolving the image and performing color separation to select the DAB-positive brown channel. Convert the grayscale signal values to optical density values. Adjusting the threshold until the red area perfectly covers all brown-positive signal regions. After applying the adjustments to the image, select two key parameters in the results window: Area (area of positive regions) and IntDen (Integrated Density, IOD). Using the formula AOD = IntDen / Area to calculate the final result for analysis.

TUNEL assay

Lung tissues were harvested from P7 mice and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4°C. Fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm. TUNEL staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Servicebio, China). Images were captured via a digital tissue section scanner (KF-PRO-020-HI, KFBIO, China), and quantitative analysis was conducted via ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The cells with brown-stained nuclei were considered apoptotic, and the average optical density (AOD) was calculated to represent the degree of apoptosis.

Measurement of the 5-HT level

For preparation of tissue homogenates, lung tissues were washed with PBS to remove blood components. Following homogenization in standard ELISA buffer, the tubes were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was carefully removed and transferred for further measurement of 5-HT. For measurement of 5-HT levels in mouse primary lung epithelial cells, lung tissues were initially digested to create single-cell suspensions, following the established protocol outlined in the literature.42 The resulting cell suspensions were purified in culture dishes coated with IgG.43 Eventually, 2×106 cells were counted for the measurement procedure. The amount of 5-HT in the mouse lung tissue homogenates, mouse primary lung epithelial cell lysates and serum samples was detected via a commercial ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Elabscience, China). The values from each sample were converted from the normalized optical density (OD) as detected by a microplate reader set to 450 nm.

Transglutaminase 2 enzymatic assay

The activity of TGM2 in lung tissue was assessed via a colorimetric assay kit (Novus Biologicals, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, samples were incubated with 50 μL of reaction buffer for 30 minutes at 37°C, with EDTA serving as the negative control. The plates were subsequently washed with Tris-HCl and then incubated with 100 μL of HRP for 15 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 μL of 0.01% H2O2 and TMB were added. The reaction was terminated with 2NH2SO4, and the OD value was measured via a microplate reader set to 450 nm.

Cell migration ability assay

Upon reaching confluence in 24-well plates, MLE-12 cells were subjected to a linear scratch wound via a sterile yellow pipette tip. The cells were subsequently incubated in the presence or absence of 10 μM 5-HT under normoxic conditions (21% O2) for 24 hours. Wound closure was documented via an Olympus IX73 inverted microscope at ×100 magnification. Representative images were selected for analysis, and further image processing was conducted via ImageJ.

Western blotting

Western blotting was conducted following our established protocol.44 Total protein was extracted from the lung tissue via lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). The samples were homogenized via a high-speed grinding homogenizer (Tissuelyser, China) at 60 Hz for 60 s, which was repeated twice, followed by dissolution on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13,000 × g and 4°C for 15 min, the protein concentration was determined via a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). The supernatant was then mixed with 5× sample loading buffer and heated at 100°C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein were then separated via acrylamide denaturing gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Proteins were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked for 1 h in 7.5% skim milk at room temperature before being incubated overnight at 4°C with a TGM2 primary antibody (CST, 1:2000) and a β-actin primary antibody (Proteintech, 1:1000). The membranes were washed five times with phosphate-buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST) (pH 7.5, 10 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20) and then further incubated for 2 h with HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Multi-Science, 1:5000) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Multi-Science, 1:5000) at room temperature. The immunoreactive bands were visualized via the Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, USA) and the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Densitometric quantification was performed via Image Lab™ software (Bio-Rad, USA). The relative expression of proteins was evaluated by calculating the gray value ratio of the protein to β-actin.

5-PT immunoprecipitation and LC‒MS/MS

MLE-12 cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer following 24 hours of stimulation with or without 100 μmol/L 5-PT (MCE, USA). The total protein concentration was measured, and 5% of the lysates were reserved as input controls. To label the serotonylated proteins, 5-PT was conjugated to a biotin-azide molecule (Thermo, USA) via copper-click chemistry per the manufacturer’s protocol. Magnetic streptavidin beads (MCE, USA) were then added to the reaction mixture to isolate the serotonylated proteins. After incubation, the samples were placed on a magnetic stand to capture the labeled proteins.

The captured proteins were resuspended in SDT lysis buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl), followed by the addition of TCEP/CAA solution and boiling at 100°C for 5 minutes. Subsequently, UA buffer (8 M urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) was added, mixed thoroughly, and transferred into 10 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tubes for centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes. Next, 100 μL of UA buffer and 50 mM NH4HCO3 were used to replace the buffer by centrifugation for 15 min twice separately. An appropriate volume of trypsin buffer (20 ng/μL; 6 μg of trypsin in 40 μL of 50 mM NH4HCO3 buffer) was added to the sample, followed by vortexing at 600 rpm for 1 minute. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 16–18 hours. The solution was transferred to a new collection tube and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes to collect the filtrate. The digested peptides were desalted via C18 StageTips and dried under vacuum. Peptides were separated chromatographically via the Easy nLC 1200 system (Thermo, USA) at a nanoliter flow rate. Buffer A contained 0.1% formic acid in water, whereas Buffer B contained 0.1% formic acid in an 80% acetonitrile solution. The column was equilibrated with 100% Buffer A before sample injection into the trap column (100 μm × 20 mm, 5 μm; C18; Dr. Maisch GmbH). Peptide separation was conducted on an analytical column (75 μm × 150 mm, 3 μm, C18, Dr. Maisch GmbH) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min via the following gradient: 2–5% Buffer B from 0–2 minutes, 5–28% from 2–44 minutes, 28–40% from 44–51 minutes, 40–100% from 51–53 minutes, and 100% from 53–60 minutes. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed via a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo, USA) in Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode over a 60-minute duration. The MS detection mode was positive, and the precursor ion MS scanning range was set to 350–1800 m/z. The full MS scan had a resolution of 60,000 at m/z 200, AGC target of 3e6, and maximum IT of 50 ms. The peptide MSMS data were collected according to the 20 highest-intensity precursor ions after each full scan. The MS2 parameters were set as follows: resolution, 15,000 at m/z 200; AGC target, 1e5; maximum IT, 50 ms; MS2 activation-type HCD; isolation window, 1.6 m/z; and normalized collision energy, 28. The data were analyzed via MaxQuant (version 1.6.1.0) with the UniProt protein database for Mus musculus.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software. All experimental data are expressed as the mean ± SEM (unless otherwise indicated), where n corresponds to independent replicates. Normality was verified via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Significance was determined by one-way ANOVA or unpaired t test. The following results were considered statistically significant: ∗p < 0.05, ∗ ∗p < 0.01, ns: not significant.
