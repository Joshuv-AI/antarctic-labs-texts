Association of metabolic syndrome and hyperuricemia with mortality in patients with chronic kidney disease: a UK biobank study
Authors: Chengkai Wu, Chuqing Pan, Li Liu, Wenyuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) has detrimental effects on health through multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, significantly reducing life expectancy and contributing to a substantial disease burden. Therefore, this study aims to explore the association of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and hyperuricemia (HUA) with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD).MethodsOverall, 28,278 patients with CKD, defined by estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urine albumin creatinine ratio > 3 mg/mmol, MetS (≥ 3 of 5 criteria), and HUA (> 7.0 mg/dL in males, > 6.0 mg/dL in females) were assessed. Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has detrimental effects on health through multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, significantly reducing life expectancy and contributing to a substantial disease burden. According to the latest Global Burden of Disease Study, CKD affected approximately 19.9% of the global population in 2021 [1]. Considering that CKD is an irreversible and progressive condition with limited therapeutic options, it has a high mortality rate. CKD results in 1.2 million deaths and 28.0 million years of life lost annually. The global all-cause mortality rate due to CKD increased by 41.5% between 1990 and 2017 [2]. By 2040, projections indicate that this will become the fifth leading cause of death worldwide, representing one of the largest increments among the major causes of death [3]. Furthermore, CKD often coexists with other diseases, further elevating the risk of mortality. Given these challenges, accurately identifying risk factors associated with CKD is crucial for effective patient management and reducing mortality rates.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a constellation of metabolic disorders characterized by five key components: central obesity, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia, and low levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [4]. Globally, the prevalence of MetS is substantial and continues to rise dramatically, with reported rates of 34.7% among adults (2011–2016) [5] and an increase from 25.8% to 33.9% in the Chinese population (2008–2010) [6]. Extensive evidence indicates that MetS increases the risk of CKD, which is elevated in patients with a high number of MetS components [7, 8]. Previous studies have suggested that each component contributes to alterations in renal microcirculation, promoting inflammation and oxidative stress, ultimately exacerbating kidney injury through synergistic effects [9]. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, comorbid MetS leads to poor health outcomes in patients with CKD.

Hyperuricemia (HUA), the second most prevalent metabolic disorder globally [10], consistently co-occurs with MetS. Substantial research indicates a significant interaction and causal relationship between high uric acid (UA) levels and MetS. HUA has been implicated in the modulation of inflammatory processes [11] and is strongly associated with multiple metabolic disorders, including all the components of MetS [12, 13]. In contrast, HUA is positively correlated with multiple established risk factors for MetS, such as body mass index (BMI) and triglyceride-glucose index [14, 15]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that HUA, regardless of MetS status, is a key contributor to an increased risk of CKD onset and progression and premature mortality in patients with CKD [16]. This association may be mediated by mechanisms such as endothelial dysfunction, heightened oxidative stress, and renin-angiotensin system activation [16, 17]. The pathogenic pathways of HUA and MetS significantly overlapped with those that induce renal injury, suggesting that both conditions exacerbate kidney damage through similar mechanisms. These findings highlight the importance of exploring their combined effects on patients with CKD.

Accumulating evidence has established MetS and HUA as independent risk factors for CKD development, yet their associations with mortality outcomes in patients with CKD have received limited attention. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of UK Biobank (UKBB) data to evaluate the mortality impact of MetS and HUA in the CKD population.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study population was selected from the UKBB database (2006–2010) [18]. The UKBB is one of the largest prospective population-based cohorts across the United Kingdom and has recruited more than 500,000 participants since recruitment began in 2006. Extensive data were collected from the baseline database, including demographic, physical, and biochemical measurements; medical history; genetics; lifestyle; and medical conditions (self-reported and doctor-diagnosed), providing a valuable resource for studying disease risk, progression, and prevention. All participants provided consent for their health to be monitored, and the assessments were conducted at 22 centres across Scotland, England, and Wales. Mortality data in the UKBB were obtained by linking the National Death Registry with its database, with detailed methods available at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/field.cgi?id=40001. The UKBB received ethical approval from the NHS National Research Ethics Service (no:16/NW/0274) and this study was conducted under application number 532,248.

We identified 28,278 participants with evidence of CKD at baseline from the UKBB. The CKD-EPI Creatinine Eq. (2021) [19] was used to calculate the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), without considering race. Participants without data on serum creatinine, urine albumin, or creatinine levels were excluded. According to the “Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2021 Guidelines,” CKD was defined as an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a random urine albumin creatinine ratio (UACR) > 3 mg/mmol [20]. According to the KDIGO risk classification this study further stratified into three categories: “Moderately Increased Risk”, “High Risk”, and “Very High Risk”. After excluding participants with insufficient data on HUA and MetS, we excluded those with missing data on the covariates of interest. The final analysis included 20,625 participants. Assessment of HUA and MetS

Baseline UA levels were centrally measured by the UKBB using uricase-peroxidase-antiperoxidase analysis conducted to determine the presence of HUA, which was defined as UA levels exceeding 7.0 mg/dL in males and 6.0 mg/dL in females [21].

Based on the Joint Scientific Statement from the International Diabetes Federation, National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, American Heart Association, World Heart Federation, International Atherosclerosis Society, and International Association for the Study of Obesity in 2009 [22], the participants were diagnosed with MetS if they met at least three of the following five criteria: (1) elevated waist circumference, a measure of central obesity, was defined as ≥ 102 cm in men and ≥ 88 cm in women, consistent with AHA/NHLBI recommended cut points for waist circumference since most participants were of European descent; (2) high blood pressure (BP), defined as an average systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg, derived from two baseline measurements (manual or machine-automated), or documented history of antihypertensive medication use; (3) Elevated fasting glucose (FG), defined as FG ≥ 100 mg/dL, or treatment to lower elevated FG; Of note, UKBB collected random blood biochemical results in a non-fasting state, and we referred to Eastwood’s method [23], using HbA1c ≥ 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) and random blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL to indicate hyperglycaemia even in the non-fasting state; (4) hypertriglyceridaemia, defined as triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dL; and (5) low-level of HDL-C, defined as HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L in males and < 1.3 mmol/L in females.

Covariates

Data on the following covariates assessed in this study were collected by the UKBB using touchscreen questionnaires during recruitment [18]: age, sex, BMI level, educational level, annual household income, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), educational level, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, baseline cancer status, eGFR and uACR. BMI levels were classified as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²). Education level was categorized as higher (college/university degree), school (A/O levels or equivalent), vocational (NVQ/HND/HNC or equivalent), and other (professional qualifications). Annual household income before tax was classified into three categories: < EUR 31,000, EUR 31,000–100,000, and > EUR 100,000. The TDI (from census/postcode, two decimals) was quartile-categorized. Smoking status was classified as never, previous, or current. Alcohol intake was categorized as never (no consumption), occasional (1–3 times/month), moderate (1–4 times/week), or heavy (daily/near-daily). Physical activity was classified according to the number of days of moderate or vigorous activity performed per week. Regular physical activity was defined as at least 150 min/week of moderate activity and 75 min/week of vigorous activity [24]. The baseline cancer status was defined as the date of cancer diagnosis before cohort entry.

Outcomes

The outcomes of this study included all-cause and cardiovascular mortality data sourced from the UKBB database. All-cause mortality data were obtained using the “date of death” field, with death status assessed as of December 31, 2021. Cardiovascular mortality was determined according to the “primary cause of death” field, with cases identified by matching International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes within the range of I00–I99. In this study, participants with no data on the time of death were assumed to be alive based on the cutoff date.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics were described according to the presence or absence of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Continuous variables were summarised as mean (standard deviation [SD]) when normally distributed and as median (interquartile range [IQR]) otherwise. Categorical variables were reported as counts (percentage, %). Baseline characteristics were compared between the groups using t-tests or Kruskal–Wallis H-tests, depending on their distribution, while categorical variables were analyzed using a chi-square test.

Follow-up duration in person-years was calculated from the date of arrival at the assessment centre until the occurrence of the following: death from any cause, loss to follow-up, or uniform censoring date (December 31, 2021). Potential confounders were selected according to prior evidence indicating associations with either the exposure or outcomes of interest. All candidate variables were subjected to univariate analyses to evaluate their potential relationships with outcomes. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the relationship between the disease state and risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKD. Disease status included HUA, MetS, their interaction groups (neither, no-no; HUA only, yes-no; MetS only, no-yes; both, yes-yes), and different combinations of MetS components. The results of the Cox regression are represented as adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Otherwise, the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and attributable proportion (AP) were utilized to quantify the interaction effects.

Furthermore, we analyzed the data of patients with CKD and coexisting HUA and MetS. To compare the survival probabilities, the participants were stratified by UA level quartiles and number of MetS components. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to visualize survival trends, and Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the relationships between UA levels, MetS components, and mortality outcomes. To explore the potential modification, subgroup analyses were carried out, stratifying participants by sex, age group (< 60 and ≥ 60 years) and CKD risk group.

Cox regression models were constructed in a stepwise manner to assess the associations. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, model 2 was further adjusted for BMI level, annual household income, education level, smoking status, alcohol intake, and physical activity, and model 3 was further adjusted for eGFR and uACR. It should be noted that model 3 reduces the total number of participants since missing eGFR or uACR data. All variables included in the adjustment models were selected according to their significance (P < 0.05) in the preliminary univariate analyses.

To validate the robustness of our findings, we performed the following sensitivity analyses: (1) exclusion of patients categorized as “Very High Risk” at baseline to assess the impact of this high-risk profile, (2) exclusion of patients with baseline cancer to minimize confounding, (3) exclusion of participants with outcome events occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up to address potential reverse causality. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), with a criterion of P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

After applying strict exclusion criteria, 20,625 patients with CKD were included in the final analysis. The baseline population comprised a balanced sex distribution (male-to-female ratio ≈ 1:1), with a mean age of 61.0 years (IQR: 54.0–66.0). HUA was present in 6270 participants (30.4%), with a median UA of 5.7 mg/dL (IQR: 4.6–6.9). In addition, the number of MetS components ranged from 0 to 5, with the presence of two components being the most frequent (n = 5440, 26.4%). MetS was identified in 9197 (44.6%) participants. At the end of the follow-up (December 31, 2021), 3564 deaths (17.28%) were recorded, including 1025 (4.97%) attributed to cardiovascular causes. The participants who died were more likely to be female, older, and to have high serum urate levels and more than three MetS components (P < 0.05) compared to the surviving participants (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). Among the patients with CKD, 5520 had an eGFR > 60 mL/min/1.73 m², while 16,267 had a UACR < 30 mg/g. Based on the KDIGO guidelines, the patients were stratified into three CKD risk groups: 17,822 (86.4%) at moderately increased risk, 2040 (9.9%) at high risk, and 762 (3.7%) at very high risk (Table 2). At baseline, one participant was missing data on eGFR, and 2,592 were missing UACR measurements. Consequently, when performing multivariable-adjusted analysis according to model 3, the total analytical population consisted of 18,032 individuals. Unadjusted hazard ratios from univariable Cox regression for all baseline covariates are presented in Supplementary Table S2 as part of the preliminary variable screening.

Interaction effect between HUA and MetS on all-cause mortality

During a median follow-up of 13.21 years (IQR: 12.37–14.04, total person-years: 258,997.71), all-cause mortality outcomes occurred in 1428 of 6270 with HUA (22.78%) and 1940 of 9197 with MetS (21.09%). Figure 2A shows the effect of disease status on all-cause mortality in patients with CKD. After adjustment for sex, age, and other covariates (model 3), HUA (aHR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.10–1.29) and MetS (aHR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.17–1.371.15–1.36) both showed an association with all-cause mortality. Patients with CKD with coexisting HUA and MetS exhibited a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality than those without HUA or MetS (aHR = 1.45, 95% CI:1.30–1.62). However, measures of additive interaction—RERI = 0.11 (95% CI: − 0.07, 0.29) and AP = 0.08 (95% CI: − 0.04, 0.20)—were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S3).

Additionally, the Kaplan–Meier curves for patients with both HUA and MetS demonstrated that elevated UA levels and an increased number of MetS components strongly reduced the probability of survival (P < 0.001, Fig. 3A and B). The aHRs for different UA levels and MetS components in relation to all-cause mortality risk are shown in Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table S4. Compared to the lowest UA quartile (Q1), each higher quartile was associated with an outstanding elevated risk of all-cause mortality, with the highest quartile (Q4) showing a 1.82-fold (95% CI:1.39–2.38) increase in risk. Similarly, a high number of MetS components was associated with increased mortality risk, with a 1.24-fold (95% CI: 1,00–1.53) higher risk observed in those with five components than those with three components.

Interaction effect between HUA and MetS on cardiovascular mortality

Among the patients with CKD, 478 cardiovascular deaths occurred in those with HUA (7.62%), and 624 occurred in those with MetS (6.78%). After adjusting for multiple variables (model 3), Fig. 2B show that HUA (aHR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.31–1.75) and MetS (aHR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.24–1.71) were significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality, while the presence of both HUA and MetS showed an even stronger association (aHR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.70–2.58). The additive interaction measures, RERI = 0.28 (95% CI: − 0.13, 0.69) and AP = 0.13 (95% CI: − 0.06, 0.32), were not statistically significant. Results of all models in Supplementary Table S5. As shown in Fig. 3C and D, elevated UA levels were significantly associated with reduced survival due to cardiovascular death (P < 0.001), while changes in the number of MetS components did not show a significant trend (P = 0.1802). As shown in Fig. 4B, Cox proportional hazards analysis indicated that elevated UA levels, particularly in the high quartile (Q3: aHR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.01–2.55; Q4: aHR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.27–3.24), were significantly associated with increased cardiovascular mortality risk. Results of all models in Supplementary Table S6.

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analyses were generally consistent with the primary findings, showing a higher risk of mortality in most subgroups when HUA and MetS coexisted. Higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality rates were observed in females than in males. In the high-risk subgroup, the aHR for all-cause mortality was 1.49 (95% CI: 0.94–2.35), which was not statistically significant; however, a significant additive interaction was observed (RERI = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.22–1.35; AP = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.15–0.90). Furthermore, in the very high-risk subgroup, a significantly increased risk was observed with an aHR of 1.47 (95% CI: 1.00–2.16), along with evidence of a significant additive interaction (RERI = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.16–1.06; AP = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.09–0.74). Sensitivity analysis

After excluding baseline very high-risk individuals, those with cancer, or those with outcome events within two years, the results remained consistent for the overall population, further confirming the robustness and reliability of the findings (Supplementary Tables S7–S9).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study, we observed that, compared to that in patients without HUA or MetS, the coexistence of HUA and MetS significantly increased the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKD, although no significant additive interaction was observed. Additionally, further analysis revealed that among patients with CKD, HUA and MetS, the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality was more pronounced in those with high UA levels and an increased number of MetS components. Subgroups and sensitivity analyses were performed to validate the robustness of the above results.

This study provides further insight into the association among HUA, MetS, and CKD. MetS is associated with proteinuria and reduced renal function [25], and numerous studies have established it as an independent risk factor for CKD development. For instance, a German study on CKD reported that MetS was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.26) and cardiovascular mortality (HR = 1.48) [26], consistent with the results of our study. To identify which specific features of MetS drive its association with mortality, we analyzed the differential mortality risk across all possible combinations of its five components using multivariate Cox proportional hazards model 3. Our analyses revealed that combinations involving elevated FG and low HDL-C (Supplementary Figure S1 and S2) were associated with significantly elevated risks of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. These two risk factors are typical clinical manifestations of insulin resistance (IR), which is also a common metabolic alteration associated with CKD progression. Therefore, the presence of comorbid MetS in patients with CKD may heighten the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, potentially mediated through IR-related pathways. A 3-year cohort study from China reported that IR was associated with CKD in patients with MetS but not in those without MetS [27]. Moreover, MetS was closely associated with cardiovascular disease. A case-control study revealed that MetS was associated with arterial stiffness even in the absence of hypertension [28], a finding that was further validated by the Northern Ireland Young Adult Heart Study [29].

Previous studies have established a strong association between elevated UA levels and CKD progression. A prospective study in a chronic renal insufficiency cohort revealed a nonlinear J-shaped relationship between baseline UA level and all-cause mortality, with a high range of markedly increasing risk [30]. Our findings are consistent with these observations, namely that the HUA group had a significantly higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than the non-HUA group. These results suggest that elevated UA levels exacerbate the risks of CKD diagnosis and mortality. HUA directly impairs renal function through UA crystal formation and exacerbates CKD progression in complicated ways. Animal studies have provided valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the promotion of macrophage infiltration in the renal interstitium, thereby exacerbating renal inflammation and injury [31, 32]. However, UA-lowering therapies show inconsistent efficacy in slowing CKD progression, with benefits limited to low-risk patients [33]. Therefore, the effect of HUA on CKD may be modulated by other coexisting risk factors, such as MetS.

Recent studies have identified associations between CKD and conditions such as HUA, hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [34–36]. However, previous research has focused on HUA, MetS, and new-onset CKD [37], few studies have examined their long-term impact on patients with CKD, particularly with regard to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that the coexistence of MetS and HUA significantly increases all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKD, with the association remaining statistically significant after multivariable adjustment including eGFR and UACR. Although no statistically significant additive interaction between HUA and MetS was observed in the overall study population, a significant interaction was identified in the very high-risk CKD subgroup. This indicates that the combined risk of exposure to multiple metabolic conditions is contingent upon the underlying disease severity, with synergistic effects on mortality becoming discernible only after the progression to advanced stages of CKD. Furthermore, when HUA, MetS, and CKD coexisted, the risk of all-cause mortality increased significantly with both elevated SUA levels and a greater number of MetS components. These findings align with the results of Pammer et al., who reported an increasing trend in HRs (HR: 1.5–2.5) with increasing MetS components [26]. Additionally, in terms of cardiovascular mortality, Kangas et al. showed that pulse wave velocity, a predictor of cardiovascular mortality [38], was higher in patients with both MetS and hypertension than in those with either condition alone [28]. Therefore, HUA and MetS may negatively affect CKD mortality through overlapping mechanisms, such as insulin resistance, inflammation, and other pathways. However, the causal pathways underlying the synergistic effects on mortality remain unclear and require further investigation.

This study concurrently examines CKD, HUA, and MetS due to their shared underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. This integrated approach aligns with the American Heart Association’s (AHA) recently proposed Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework [39]. Our study population uniformly met the criteria for at least CKM Stage 2 according to AHA staging criteria. The findings provide empirical evidence supporting the CKM syndrome concept, demonstrating that—consistent with other CKM-related research—these conditions are independently and cumulatively associated with increased risks of both all-cause and CVD mortality [40, 41]. Our findings highlight the critical importance of integrated risk factor management in CKD, with particular emphasis on metabolic health. Clinical practice should implement routine metabolic screening together with individually tailored treatment targets. Integration of social determinants of health (SDOH) screening is also recommended to identify and address socioeconomic barriers. Furthermore, strengthening lifestyle interventions and self-management support is essential to improve long term patient outcomes.

Even after multivariate adjustment, residual confounders such as medication, previous CVD and dietary habits may still influence the results. Additionally, the reliance on baseline data and lack of longitudinal follow-up limited our ability to track dynamic changes in key variables. Despite these limitations, the strengths of this study are noteworthy. Our research is based on a large UKBB cohort and, thus, benefits from rigorous quality control and a well-established follow-up system for data collection, significantly minimizing information and loss-to-follow-up biases. Thus, our finding that the coexistence of MetS and HUA increases the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKD is robust. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings to other regions and populations.

Conclusions

Both MetS and HUA were significant risk factors for fatal outcomes in patients with CKD. Their coexistence exhibited a synergistic effect, increasing the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, particularly in patients with high UA levels and a high number of MetS components. These findings shed light on the intricate connections among HUA, MetS, and CKD while offering insights for designing novel strategies for managing patients with CKD.
