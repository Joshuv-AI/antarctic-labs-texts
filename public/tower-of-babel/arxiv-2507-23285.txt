CLT in high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression with low SNR
Seunghyun Lee, Nabarun Deb, Sumit Mukherjee

Abstract
We study central limit theorems for linear statistics in high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression with product priors. Unlike the existing literature where the focus is on posterior contraction, we work under a non-contracting regime where neither the likelihood nor the prior dominates the other. This is motivated by modern high-dimensional datasets characterized by a bounded signal-to-noise ratio. This work takes a first step towards understanding limit distributions for one-dimensional projections of the posterior, as well as the posterior mean, in such regimes. Analogous to contractive settings, the resulting limiting distributions are Gaussian, but they heavily depend on the chosen prior and center around the Mean-Field approximation of the posterior. We study two concrete models of interest to illustrate this phenomenon — the white noise design, and the (misspecified) Bayesian model. As an application, we construct credible intervals and compute their coverage probability under any misspecified prior. Our proofs rely on a combination of recent developments in Berry-Esseen type bounds for Random Field Ising models and both first and second order Poincaré inequalities. Notably, our results do not require any sparsity assumptions on the prior.

Introduction

We consider the linear regression model:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\varepsilon},

(1.1)

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the response vector,
\mathbf{X}
is an
n\times p
design matrix,
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the vector of regression coefficients, and
\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mcn(\mathbf{0}_{n},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
represents unobserved Gaussian noise with known variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. Within a Bayesian framework, we further assume a prior distribution on the coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}
. A natural starting point is to assume that

\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p}\overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}\mu,

(1.2)

where
\mu
is the prior distribution for each component of
\bm{\beta}
. A popular application of the above model is for estimating effect size distributions or genetic architectures of complex physiological characteristics; see e.g.,
Zhou and Zhao 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2018a
;
O’Hagan and West 2013
;
Morgante et al. 2023
. Under this Bayesian model specification, the posterior distribution
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})=\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}{d\mu^{\otimes p}}(\bm{\beta})\propto

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}+\Big(\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\right).

(1.3)

Here, the
p\times p
matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
is defined as
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}:=\sigma^{-2}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})
and it encodes the dependence structure of the posterior.
Without strong assumptions, such as assuming
\mu
is Gaussian or the design is exactly orthogonal, the above posterior involves an intractable normalizing constant. Consequently, performing statistical inference under
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
is a challenging problem, especially for high-dimensional posteriors with
p\to\infty
. We note that
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}\equiv\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\mu}
also depends on the prior
\mu
, but we suppress this dependence for notational convenience.

Bayesian inference methods for such high-dimensional linear models have been applied across various scientific disciplines, including genomics
(
Guan and Stephens 2011
)
, marketing
(
Lenk and Rao 1990
)
, and finance
(
Rachev et al. 2008
)
, among others
(
O’Hagan and West 2013
;
George and
McCulloch 1997
)
. In a broad sense, existing methods can be divided into two directions: sampling and variational inference.
The first relies on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods
(
Metropolis
et al. 1953
;
Hastings 1970
;
George and
McCulloch 1993
;
Ishwaran and Rao 2005
)
to sample from the posterior. While supporting theory guarantees asymptotically exact samples from the posterior, this becomes computationally intensive as
p
increases. In contrast, variational inference avoids sampling from the posterior by approximating it with a more tractable family of distributions
(
Wainwright
et al. 2008
)
. Despite being computationally attractive, the theoretical understanding of variational inference in high-dimensions has been relatively limited. Furthermore, empirical studies often report problems such as variance underestimation when the variational posterior is used for uncertainty quantification
(
Minka et al. 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2018b
;
Blei, Kucukelbir and
McAuliffe 2017
;
Margossian and
Saul 2023
)
.

In terms of theoretical studies for high-dimensional Bayesian linear models, most existing studies address the classical high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) setting, where the operator norm of the quadratic interaction
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
in (
1.3
) diverges, and consequently the data likelihood dominates the prior asymptotically. In such scenarios, the Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorem typically applies
(
Van der Vaart 2000
;
Ghosal 1999
;
Castillo, Schmidt-Hieber and van der
Vaart 2015
;
Katsevich 2025
)
, and the posterior distribution contracts around the ordinary least squares estimator. In contrast, modern high-dimensional datasets often exhibit bounded signal strength, characterized by a bounded operator norm of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
. This
bounded SNR regime
implies that the prior maintains a non-vanishing influence on the posterior, rendering the BvM contraction around the least squares estimator inapplicable and introducing novel technical challenges. The bounded SNR regime has garnered significant attention in recent literature for Bayesian statistics
(
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
;
Barbier et al. 2020
;
Qiu 2024
;
Mukherjee, Qiu and
Sen 2024
;
Celentano et al. 2023
)
as well as high-dimensional sampling
(
Montanari and
Wu 2024
;
Chewi 2024
)
. It also arises naturally in economics when dealing with partially identified models where point estimation is no longer possible
(
Tamer 2010
;
Moon and Schorfheide 2012
, see)
.

To motivate our scaling choice for the bounded SNR regime (see
2.1
and
Remark

2.1
below), let us focus on the ultra simplistic case
p=1
and compare two simple models:

\textrm{Model 1 : }Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(\theta,1)\quad\mbox{vs}\quad\textrm{Model 2 : }Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(h/\sqrt{n},1),

for parameters
\theta,h\in\mathbb{R}
. Here both models have the same noise/variance level. In Model 1, the frequentist MLE

\bar{Y}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
is consistent for
\theta
, whereas in Model 2 the MLE
\sqrt{n}\bar{Y}
is not consistent for
h
. In fact, there are no consistent estimators for
h
in Model 2. From a Bayesian perspective, suppose we put priors on
\theta\sim\mu_{1}
and
h\sim\mu_{2}
. In Model 1, the posterior is close in total variation to
N(\bar{Y}_{n},1/n)
with high probability, by the classical Bernstein-von Mises theorem. Thus the posterior contracts around the MLE
\bar{Y}
at rate
n^{-1/2}
. Also, the approximate posterior distribution is free of the prior
\mu_{1}
. On the other hand, the posterior in Model 2 is proportional to

\mu_{2}(h)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-n^{-1/2}h)^{2}\right)\propto\mu_{2}(h)\exp(h\sqrt{n}\bar{Y}_{n}-h^{2}/2).

Here the approximate posterior is a linear and quadratic tilt of the prior, which does not contract to the MLE, or any other point. In fact, the posterior is heavily influenced by the prior
\mu_{2}
, even asymptotically. Overall, estimation and inference is harder in the context of Model 2, which makes it a more reasonable model for weak signals. Theoretically, the crucial difference between the above models is that, in view of (
1.1
), Model 1 has a design matrix (
n\times 1
)
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
whereas Model 2 has
\mathbf{X}=n^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}_{n}
. So
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert=\sqrt{n}\to\infty
for Model 1 (hence high SNR) whereas
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert=O(1)
for Model 2 (hence low/bounded SNR). This motivates us to study the posterior (
1.3
) under the bounded SNR condition
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert=O(1)
in the high-dimensional setting.

The existing line of work on statistical guarantees for bounded SNR Bayesian linear regression
(
Fan et al. 2023
;
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
;
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
;
Fan et al. 2025
, see)
focuses on characterizing Bayes optimal risk or average coverage guarantees under the high-dimensional posterior in (
1.3
). By taking a one-dimensional projection of the posterior, our paper takes a step beyond such consistency-type results by understanding
exact limiting distributions
with an explicit characterization of the mean and variance. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first paper to derive limit distribution theory in such non-contracting regimes. Our results allow precise posterior uncertainty quantification such as constructing credible intervals with explicit expressions (as opposed to that based on quantiles of posterior samples), and evaluating its coverage probability. Additionally, by comparing the true posterior variance with the variance under the mean-field variational approximation, we precisely characterize when the variance is underestimated. Surprisingly, in contrast to the folklore, the mean-field posterior can overestimate the variance in certain scenarios (see
Remark

3.2
for details).

1.1
Main Contributions

The primary objective of this paper is to derive limiting distributions of the following:

(i)

the parameter
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
under the posterior distribution (
2.2
) for some
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, given
\mathbf{y}
,

(ii)

the posterior mean
\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}]
under additional data generating assumptions for
\mathbf{y}
.

Here,
\mathbf{q}
is a deterministic vector normalized to have length one, i.e.
\|\mathbf{q}\|=1
. The linear statistic
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
is the mean response of a new observation with feature vector
\mathbf{q}
. Assuming that
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\sim\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
is a posterior sample,
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
can also be understood as the one-dimensional projection of the posterior. The posterior mean
\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}]
is the Bayes optimal point estimator for
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
under a squared error loss (see (
2.9
) below). Therefore, these quantities have been a central focus in linear regression studies

(
Zhu and Bradic 2018
;
Azriel and
Schwartzman 2020
;
Zhao, Zhou and Liu 2023
;
Bellec and Zhang 2023
;
Chang, Kuchibhotla and
Rinaldo 2023
)
. More generally, understanding limiting distributions of the posterior as well as Bayes estimators is a classical topic in Bayesian asymptotics
(
Bickel and Yahav 1969
;
Le Cam 1953
;
Lehmann and Casella 2006
)
.

Our main results in
Section

3
address the above goals (i) and (ii), under popular assumptions on the design matrix and the regression coefficients. To overcome the absence of posterior contraction, we assume that the projection direction
\mathbf{q}
is “delocalized” in the sense that
\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\infty}\to 0
, and prove a
CLT
for
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}=\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\beta_{i}
. Note that in our asymptotic regime, CLT does not hold without this delocalization assumption, similar to CLTs for linear statistics of independent random variables, where this condition is referred to as the uniform asymptotic negligibility (UAN) condition.
To the best of our knowledge, we provide the first Bernstein-von Mises type limiting distributions for
non-contracting posteriors
. Unlike classical Bernstein-von Mises results, our limiting distributions are non-universal in that they heavily depend on the chosen prior
\mu
(see (
1.2
)). In
Section

4
, we use these results to build asymptotic credible posterior intervals as well as quantify their uncertainty, under potential model misspecification. As a by-product of our results, we establish conditions under which the asymptotic behavior of
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
under the Mean-Field variational approximation matches that under the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
. We provide a more detailed description of our contributions below.

•

In
2.4
, we show that the natural point estimator of
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
under the Mean-Field approximation of the posterior is in fact, asymptotically Bayes optimal. We also provide a finite sample regret bound comparing the risk of the Mean-Field estimator to the Bayes optimal risk.

•

In
3.1
, we prove CLTs for
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
and
\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}]
under white noise design when
p\ll n^{2/3}
. Our result accommodates both cases where the true regression coefficients are deterministic or random. In this case, the limit laws under the Mean-Field approximation and the posterior match exactly. As an application, when the coefficients are sampled from the popular spike-and-slab prior, we characterize the precise threshold on sparsity up to which the limit law matches that under a global null (see
3.3
).

•

In
3.4
, we prove CLTs with a general design when the regression coefficient vector
{\bm{\beta}}
are sampled i.i.d from some distribution, say
\mu^{\star}
(which may not be equal to
\mu
). In this case, our results apply to both fixed and random design matrices, including the white noise (random) design, and the analysis of variance (ANOVA) design (see
2.2
). In the ANOVA design, our result highlights that depending on
\mathbf{q}
and the design
\mathbf{X}
, the variance under the Mean-Field approximation can be larger, equal, or smaller than the posterior variance (see
Remark

3.2
for details).

•

In
Section

4
, we propose a
100(1-\alpha)\%
-credible interval for
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
under the posterior, based on
3.4
. In
4.1
, we establish a limiting formula for the coverage probability of the proposed credible interval under any
misspecified prior
. The coverage probability matches the target level
1-\alpha
when the prior is correctly specified. This provides a Bernstein-von Mises type coverage guarantee, under potential model misspecification, for non-contracting posteriors.

Our results apply to any compactly supported prior
\mu
, and complement the limitations of the Laplace approximation
(
Spokoiny 2023
;
Katsevich 2024
;
Giordano et al. 2025
, see)
as a technique for approximating the posterior. This approximation is typically useful when the SNR is high and the effect of the prior
\mu
washes away in the asymptotic limit. Moreover it requires the posterior to have density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and does not allow priors that incorporate exact sparsity (i.e. positive mass at zero). Our results do not impose such restrictions, and are readily able to generalize to fractional posteriors (see
Section

3.3
).

2
Setup and preliminary results

This section formulates the problem setting as well as necessary concepts and notations. Throughout, we will assume that the prior
\mu
in the Bayesian model formulation (
1.2
) is compactly supported and nondegenerate. This is a common assumption in the literature
(
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
;
Celentano et al. 2023
;
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
;
Fan et al. 2023
)
, imposed for various technical reasons such as ensuring that the normalizing constant in the posterior (
1.3
) is well-defined. For the sake of definiteness, in this work we assume that the compact support of
\mu
is the interval
[-1,1]
.

Next, we further simplify the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})
(see (
1.3
)) by separating out the diagonal elements of the
p\times p
quadratic interaction matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}=\text{Diag}(\mathbf{d}^{(p)})-\mathbf{A}_{p}\quad\text{ where }\quad\mathbf{A}_{p}:=-\Off({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}),\quad\mathbf{d}^{(p)}=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{p}).

(2.1)

Absorbing the diagonal elements
d_{i}:=\sigma^{-2}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})(i,i)
into the base measure
\mu
has been useful for understanding similar quadratic interaction models
(
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
;
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
)
. Under these notations, we can re-write the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})
as follows:

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(d\bm{\beta})\propto

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{p}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{c}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mu_{i}(d\beta_{i}),\quad\mbox{where}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\sigma^{2}},\;\;\;\;\frac{d\mu_{i}}{d\mu}(\beta_{i})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{d_{i}\beta_{i}^{2}}{2}\right),

(2.3)

for
i=1,2,\ldots,p
. Here, we caution that the sign of the coupling matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}
has the opposite sign compared to the related works
(
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
;
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
)
. Throughout this section (except for
Section

2.4
),
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
is considered deterministic, and the randomness only arises from the posterior.

2.1
Design matrix assumptions

We make some assumptions on the
n\times p
design matrix
\mathbf{X}
. Using the decomposition (
2.1
), we separate the assumptions for the off-diagonal and diagonal parts. These are satisfied for a various examples of interest, covering both deterministic and random designs as illustrated at the end of the section.

Our first assumption is the so-called “high-temperature” assumption in the literature
(
Gheissari, Lubetzky and
Peres 2018
;
Maillard et al. 2019
;
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
)
. This condition has been often imposed to guarantee uniqueness of the Mean-Field approximation of the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
(see
Lemma

2.1
for a precise statement).

Assumption 2.1

(high-temperature)
.

For some constant
\rho\in(0,1)
, assume that

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{A}_{p}\rVert_{4}\leq\rho.

(2.4)

Noting that
\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{4}\leq\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{\infty}
(e.g. see Lemma 2.1 in
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
), this condition can sometimes be verified by computing the more tractable
(\infty,\infty)
norm, given by
\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)|
. However, it is not always advisable to use the
(\infty,\infty)
norm as a bound, as it can give a sub-optimal result. As an illustration, for the special case when each entry of
\mathbf{X}
is i.i.d. sub-Gaussians (see
2.3
), we can explicitly compute the different norms (see
Lemma

A.7
) as:

\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{2}=O_{P,\mathbf{X}}(\sqrt{p}/\sqrt{n}),\quad\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{4}=O_{P,\mathbf{X}}(p^{3/4}/\sqrt{n}),\quad\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{\infty}=O_{P,\mathbf{X}}(p/\sqrt{n}).

Thus, using the
(4,4)
norm the high-temperature condition holds as soon as
p\ll n^{2/3}
, whereas using the
(\infty,\infty)
norm we require
p\ll\sqrt{n}
. This illustrates the benefits of the
(4,4)
norm as opposed to the more commonly used
(\infty,\infty)
norm in this example, since the resulting high-temperature condition allows a wider range of
p
.

Our second assumption is a “Strong Mean-Field” assumption, which guarantees the approximation accuracy of the Mean-Field approximation (see
Lemma

2.2
for a precise statement).

Assumption 2.2

(Strong Mean-Field regime (SMF))
.

With
\mathbf{A}_{p}
as in (
1.1
), define

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)^{2},

(2.5)

and assume that
\alpha_{p}=o(p^{-1/2}).

We refer to the requirement
\alpha_{p}=o(p^{-1/2})
as a (
2.5
) as a Strong Mean-Field assumption, as it implies the condition

{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{A}_{p}^{2})=o(p)
, which has been used in the literature as a Mean-Field assumption for deriving Law of Large numbers (see Def. 1.3 in
Basak and
Mukherjee 2017
, eq. (1.5) in
Lacker, Mukherjee and
Yeung 2024
, or Thm. 3 in
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
).

Our final assumption involves
\mathbf{d}^{(p)}
, the diagonal elements of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
. This assumption implies that all column vectors have similar length close to
d_{0}
, and can be understood as a homogeneity assumption on
\mathbf{X}
. This assumption will not be imposed on most results in this section, but will be required for the CLTs in
Section

3
.

Assumption 2.3

(homogeneous design)
.

Suppose there exists fixed constants
d_{\text{min}},d_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that
d_{i}\geq d_{\text{min}}
for all
i
and

\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(d_{i}-d_{0})^{2}=O(1).

2.3
essentially assumes that
\{\mu_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
(see (
2.2
)) are all close to some common measure
\mu_{0}
. This will be the case, for example, when the columns of
\mathbf{X}
are all centered to have mean
0
and scaled to have variance
1
. In that case, one can choose
d_{0}=\sigma^{-2}
.

Example design matrices.
Our assumptions can accommodate both deterministic and random design matrices
\mathbf{X}
that commonly appear in statistics.

Example 2.1

(Gaussian sequence model)
.

As a toy example, suppose a statistician fits the Gaussian sequence model
y_{i}=\beta_{i}+\epsilon_{i}
, which corresponds to taking
n=p
and
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
in (
1.2
). In this case the posterior (
2.2
) simplifies to

\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(d\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\langle\mathbf{y},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\beta_{i}^{2}\right)\right)\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mu(d\beta_{i}).

Therefore, conditioned on the data
\mathbf{y}
, the posterior is a product measure which is trivially a product distribution, and also non-contracting for fixed (non-sparse)
\mu
. In this setting,
\mathbf{A}_{p}=0
and Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold.
2.3
holds for any
\sigma^{2}>0
for the choice
d_{0}=\sigma^{-2}
.

Example 2.2

(Deterministic design)
.

Next, we consider the popular two-way ANOVA model with
p/2
levels (assuming
p
is even) and no repeated measurements:

y_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}(\delta_{i}+\gamma_{j})+\epsilon_{i,j},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq p/2

Here,
(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p/2})\in[-1,1]^{p/2}
and
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p/2})\in[-1,1]^{p/2}
denote the levels of the two factors, respectively. The errors
\epsilon_{i,j}
are i.i.d
\mcn(0,\sigma^{2})
. We define
n:=(p/2)^{2}
, and let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the vectorized expression of the matrix
(y_{i,j})
. Similarly, let
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p/2},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p/2})^{\top}\in[-1,1]^{p}
denote the coefficient vector, and let
\mathbf{X}
denote the corresponding
n\times p
design matrix.
Then, it follows that

\mathbf{A}_{p}=-\frac{1}{p\sigma^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{\pt\times\pt}&\mathbf{1}_{\pt\times\pt}\\
\mathbf{1}_{\pt\times\pt}&\mathbf{0}_{\pt\times\pt}\end{pmatrix}

and
d_{i}=1/(2\sigma^{2})
for all
i
. Consequently,
2.1
holds when
\sigma^{2}>\frac{1}{2}
,
2.2
always holds, and
2.3
holds with
d_{0}=1/(2\sigma^{2})
.

Example 2.3

(White noise design)
.

We consider a random design with iid sub-Gaussian rows, i.e. suppose that
X_{k,i}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}F
,
1\leq k\leq n
,
1\leq i\leq p
, where
F
is a sub-Gaussian distribution (see
(
Vershynin 2018
, Section 2.5)
) with mean zero and variance one.
Then in the asymptotic regime
p\ll n^{2/3}
, all three assumptions hold by taking
d_{0}=\sigma^{-2}
. Checking the first two assumptions are somewhat non-trivial, but follows from Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2 in
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
.

Remark 2.1

(Bounded signal-to-noise ratio)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
,
\|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}\|
is bounded by a constant. This puts us in a
bounded SNR
regime where the posterior distribution
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}

does not
contract around the least squares estimator
\hat{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}}^{\text{LSE}}:=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
or any “true parameter” (see e.g.,
(
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
, Corollary 4)
). This is in sharp contrast to the more classical setting considered in
Ghosal 2000
;
Katsevich 2025
, where the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
are assumed to be large, the posterior contracts around
\hat{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}}^{\text{LSE}}
, and the limiting distribution does not depend on the prior
\mu
(i.e. Bernstein-von Mises type theorems hold).

In the absence of posterior contraction, it is unclear how to provide a tractable centering (as opposed to the posterior mean, which is hard to compute) for the statistic of interest
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
, to get analogous results. Below we show that a natural centering based on the
Mean-Field
approximation to the posterior, serves the purpose.

2.2
Mean-Field variational approximation

The Mean-Field approximation of the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
arises by approximating
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
with a product distribution
Q=\prod_{i=1}^{p}Q_{i}
on
[-1,1]^{p}

(
Bishop 2006
;
Blei, Kucukelbir and
McAuliffe 2017
, see)
. In the existing literature, point estimation under the Mean-Field approximation is known to exhibit desirable properties; see
Yang, Pati and Bhattacharya 2020
;
Wang and Blei 2018
;
Zhang and Gao 2020
;
Bhattacharya, Deb and
Mukherjee 2023
;
Bhattacharya, Deb and
Mukherjee 2024
. Commonly, the “best” approximation
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
is defined as the product distribution that minimizes the KL divergence between
Q
and
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
, obtained by solving the optimization:

\displaystyle\argmin_{Q=\prod_{i=1}^{p}Q_{i}}\mathrm{KL}\big(Q|\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}\big)=\argmax_{Q=\prod_{i=1}^{p}Q_{i}}\left(\EE_{Q}\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{p}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}+\mathnormal{\mathbf{c}}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\right]-\mathrm{KL}\big(Q|\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mu_{i}\big)\right).

(2.6)

Here, the expression being maximized on the last term, up to additive constants, is often denoted as the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) (see eq. (13) in
Blei, Kucukelbir and
McAuliffe 2017
or eq. (7) in
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
). The last equality follows by expanding the KL divergence while noting that the posterior normalizing constant (the hidden denominator in (
2.2
))
does not depend on
Q
.
Interestingly, the above maximization over measures reduces to an optimization over real numbers. Under our high-temperature
2.1
, this maximization has a unique maximizer, and can be written as the unique root of a fixed point equation. We summarize these facts in the following Lemma, which requires notion of
exponential tilting
.

Definition 2.1

(Exponential tilting)
.

Recalling the definition of measures
\mu_{i}
from (
2.3
), let
\psi_{i}(\cdot)
denote the log moment generating function of
\mu_{i}
, defined by

\psi_{i}(\theta):=\log\int e^{\theta z}d\mu_{i}(z)

for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. Define the
exponential tilts
of
\mu_{i}
as

\frac{d\mu_{i,\theta}}{d\mu_{i}}(z):=\exp(\theta z-\psi_{i}(\theta)).

By standard exponential family calculations, we have

\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\theta)=\EE_{\mu_{i,\theta}}[Z],\quad\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\theta)={\rm Var}_{\mu_{i,\theta}}[Z].

In particular, note that both
\psi_{i}^{\prime},\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}
are uniformly bounded by
1
.
As
\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)
is strictly positive,
\psi_{i}^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto(-1,1)
is strictly increasing, and is invertible.

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 2.2 in
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
)
.

Under
2.1
, the optimization in (
2.6
) has a unique maximizer
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}.
In particular, by defining
u_{i}\in(-1,1)
as the mean of the
i
th marginal of
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
as

u_{i}:=\EE_{\beta_{i}\sim Q_{i}^{\textnormal{prod}}}[\beta_{i}]
, the vector

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{p})\in(-1,1)^{p}
satisfies the following fixed point equation:

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle=\psi_{i}^{\prime}(s_{i}+c_{i}),\quad s_{i}:=\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)u_{j},\,\,\,\,\forall i=1,\ldots,p.

(2.7)

Also, for each
i
, the marginal
Q_{i}^{\textnormal{prod}}
is uniquely characterized by
u_{i}
via the relationship
Q_{i}^{\textnormal{prod}}=\mu_{i,(\psi_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}(u_{i})}
, where
\mu_{i,{\mathbf{\cdot}}}
is the exponential tilt of
\mu_{i}
defined as in
2.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

We note in passing that both the mean vector
\mathbf{u}
and the product measure
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
depend on
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y},\mu)
, but we suppress this dependence for the sake of simplicity. In the remainder of the paper, we will refer the product measure
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
as the
Mean-Field approximation
of
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
or the
Mean-Field posterior
for short.

Now, given that
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
is well-defined, a fundamental question is whether it is a good approximation of the posterior. One way to address this is to see whether the KL divergence
\rm{KL}(Q^{\mathrm{prod}}\mid\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}})
is small. The following Lemma illustrates that under our assumptions on the coupling matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}
, this is indeed true.

Lemma 2.2

(Corollary 9 in
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
, we have
{\rm KL}(Q^{\mathrm{prod}}\mid\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}})=o(p).

Note that the typical size of the KL divergence between two
p
-dimensional measures is
O(p)
, so
Lemma

2.2
indicates that the true posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
and its Mean-Field approximation
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
is smaller than that. Also, the original claim in
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
is stated under slightly weaker assumptions.

We end this short section on Mean-Field variational approximation by highlighting its computational benefits as opposed to sampling from the posterior.

Remark 2.3

(Computing the Mean-Field optimizer)
.

Note that
\mathbf{u}
(and hence
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
) can be computed from the data
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
and the prior
\mu
, without the need for any posterior sampling. It can be computed efficiently using message passing algorithms (see
(
Jain, Koehler and Mossel 2018
, Theorem 1.10)
). In fact, under the high-temperature condition, the Mean-Field solution can be computed exponentially fast (in the number of iterations) by recursively using the fixed point equation (
2.7
) or using gradient based optimization (see
(
Nesterov 2018
, Chapter 2)
and
(
Nocedal and Wright 1999
, Chapter 5)
).

2.3
Moment bounds with Mean-Field centering

As indicated above, the main goal of this paper is to understand the limiting distribution of
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
under the posterior, and its posterior mean, under the normalization
\|\mathbf{q}\|=1
. This section provides some preliminary moment bounds for
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
centered using the Mean-Field approximation. In all results in this subsection, the implied constants in
\lesssim
depends only on
\rho
(see (
2.4
)).

First, consider the problem of understanding the limiting behavior of

T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}):=\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\,,

where
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\sim\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
is a posterior sample. The following result shows that
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})
concentrates around the Mean-Field posterior mean
\EE_{Q^{\mathrm{prod}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}]=\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
.

Proposition 2.3

(Theorem 2.3 in
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
)
.

Under the high-temperature
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}\left[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}-\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}]\right]^{2}\leq\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}\left[\mathbf{q}^{\top}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}-\mathbf{u})\right]^{2}\lesssim\max\big(1,p\alpha_{p}^{2}\big).

(2.8)

To better understand the bound, consider a toy case with
\mathbf{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\mathbf{1}_{p}
. Then, the problem boils down to understanding the behavior of the coordinate-wise average
\bar{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\beta_{i}
under the posterior.
2.3
gives the bound

\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\bar{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}]^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{p}+\alpha_{p}^{2}.

This implies the law of large numbers
\bar{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}+o_{P}(1)
, as long as
\alpha_{p}=o(1)
. We stress that the requirement
\alpha_{p}=o(1)
is actually needed here, as there are counterexamples to the above law of large numbers when
\alpha_{p}
is bounded away from
0

(
Qiu 2024
;
Celentano et al. 2023
)
. We draw the reader’s attention to another conclusion from (
2.8
). If the SMF
2.2
holds, the RHS above simplifies to
1/p
. Note that this matches the bound one gets under the product approximation
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
:

\EE_{Q^{\mathrm{prod}}}[\bar{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}]^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{p}.

Thus under the SMF assumption, the behavior of
\bar{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}}
is
“very much like”
the average of
p
independent random variables. We will later build on this intuition to derive CLTs under the SMF assumption.

Next, we compare the two centerings in (
2.8
):
\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}]
and
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
.
Here, the first centering is the Bayes estimator for
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
with a squared error loss function:

\displaystyle\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}):=\argmin_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\int\left(a-\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\right)^{2}\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(d\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})=\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}].

(2.9)

The second,
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
is a more tractable alternative to
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
, as illustrated in
Remark

2.3
. Given this, a natural question to ask would be how tight is the first inequality in (
2.8
).
The following Proposition provides a quantification of this bound.

Proposition 2.4

(Regret bound)
.

Under the high-temperature
2.1
, we have:

\displaystyle|\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})-\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}|\lesssim\sqrt{p}\an,

(2.10)

which gives the regret bound

\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}\big(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}-\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\big)^{2}-\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}\big(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}-\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})\big)^{2}\lesssim p\alpha_{p}^{2}.

Remark 2.4

(Bayes optimality of Mean-Field)
.

From
2.4
, it follows that the Mean-Field centering
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
is asymptotically Bayes optimal under the SMF
2.2
, in the sense that the RHS becomes
o(1)
.
In comparison to parallel results on the optimality of the Mean-Field optimizer which are asymptotic in nature (see e.g.
(
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
, Corollary 1)
and
(
Mukherjee and
Sen 2022
, Theorem 1)
), our result provides explicit non-asymptotic rates of convergence.

2.4

This section introduces additional assumptions on how the true data
\mathbf{y}
is generated. Note that the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
is constructed given
any
observation
\mathbf{y}
, and that we have imposed no assumption on
\mathbf{y}
so far. Consequently, all previous results hold for any deterministic vector
\mathbf{y}
. However, this is not enough to derive the precise limiting distributions that will follow, and we impose the following assumption on the data generating process (DGP) for
\mathbf{y}
. Mainly, we assume that
\mathbf{y}
is generated from a regression model with covariates
\mathbf{X}
and “true” parameter
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
. Here,
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
is not required to be supported in
[-1,1]
and may be unbounded. Given
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})
, the statistician generates a sample
{\bm{\beta}}
from the statistician’s posterior
\nu_{{\bf y},{\bf X}}\equiv\nu_{{\bf y},{\bf X},\mu}
(see (
1.3
)), where
\mu
is a prior chosen by the statistician for the Bayesian model. This allows the
“true/data generating” model to be different from the “Bayesian” model (
1.1
), (
1.2
) that was used to build the posterior. In other words, the Bayesian model is allowed to be misspecified. We refer to
Figure

1
for a pictorial representation of the combined model. While our subsequent results can be extended to allow a misspecified regression variance
\sigma^{2}>0
, for the sake of simplicity of notation we will assume that
\sigma^{2}
is correctly specified.

(Data generating process)
.

Assume that the response vector
\mathbf{y}
is generated from the “true/data generating” model:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\star}.

(2.11)

Here,
\mathbf{X}
is the same
n\times p
design matrix that was used to construct the posterior in (
1.1
). The vector
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
collects the true regression coefficients (which may be deterministic or random), and
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\star}\sim\mcn(\mathbf{0}_{n},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
represents unobserved Gaussian noise.

3
Main result: limiting distributions for the linear statistic and posterior mean

This section works under two specific data generating processes that result in asymptotic Gaussian fluctuations for
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})=\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
(when
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\sim\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
) and
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})=\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}]
. We separate our results in this Section into two parts where we consider (i) a white noise (random) design with general coefficients
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
(see
Section

3.1
), (ii) a general design matrix with random potentially misspecified coefficients
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
(see
Section

3.2
).
Throughout the section, we will reserve the notation
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
for the posterior samples.

To present our main results, we require two additional notations. These notations are related to the limiting variance as well as the centering. First, we define
\psi_{0}
as the limiting version of the log mgf
\psi_{i}
s. Here, recall that
d_{0}=d_{0}(\mathbf{X})
is the constant from
2.3
that approximates the diagonal elements of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Define
\mu_{0}
by setting
\frac{d\mu_{0}}{d\mu}(z)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{d_{0}z^{2}}{2}\right)
,
and define
\mu_{0,\theta}
to be the exponential tilt of
\mu_{0}
(similar to
2.1
), given by

\frac{d\mu_{0,\theta}}{d\mu_{0}}(z)=\exp(\theta z-\psi_{0}(\theta)),\text{ where }\psi_{0}(\theta):=\log\int e^{\theta z}d\mu_{0}(z)

for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Next, define
\upsilon_{p}
as the following data-dependent quantity. In both theorems that will follow,
\upsilon_{p}
can be used to consistently estimate the limiting variance.

Definition 3.2
.

Define
\upsilon_{p}=\upsilon_{p}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}):=\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})\in(0,1)
where
\psi_{i}(\cdot)
s are defined in
2.1
and
c_{i}
s are defined in (
2.3
).

3.1
White noise design

In this Section, we assume that the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
is the white noise design from
2.3
. This is a popular choice in high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression
(
Qiu 2024
;
Barbier et al. 2020
;
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
)
. Recall that each entry of
\mathbf{X}
is assumed to be i.i.d sub-Gaussian. In this case we will set
d_{0}:=\sigma^{-2}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
is the white noise design in
2.3
, and suppose
\mathbf{y}
is generated according to
2.4
for some true
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
which satisfies

\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(\beta_{i}^{\star})^{4}=O(p)
. Assume further that
\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
, and the following limit is well-defined for some
\upsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\upsilon

\displaystyle:=\lim_{p\to\infty}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{i}^{\star}/\sigma^{2}+W),

(3.1)

where
W\sim\mcn(0,\sigma^{-2})
.
Then, in the asymptotic regime
p\ll n^{2/3}
, the following statements hold.

1.

We have
\upsilon_{p}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\xp\upsilon
.
Further, with
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\sim\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})-\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\right)\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn(0,\upsilon),\quad\Big(T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})-\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\psi_{i}^{\prime}(c_{i})\Big)\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn(0,\upsilon).

(3.2)

2.

Additionally, suppose that the following limit is well-defined for some
\varsigma^{2}>0

\displaystyle\varsigma^{2}:=\lim_{p\to\infty}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\Var\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\beta_{i}^{\star}/\sigma^{2}+W).

(3.3)

Then

\displaystyle\left(\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})-\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\beta_{i}^{\star}/\sigma^{2}+W)\right)\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn(0,\varsigma^{2}).

(3.4)

We elaborate more on the conclusions of
3.1
. Part (a) shows that the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
projected on
\mathbf{q}
is approximately Gaussian. The first limit in (
3.2
) considers
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})
centered by the Mean-Field centering, as in the moment bound in
2.3
. Recall that computing the Mean-Field centering requires solving the fixed point equation (
2.7
). The second limit instead centers by an explicit quantity of
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\psi_{i}^{\prime}(c_{i})
, which avoids computing the Mean-Field approximation. Note that by using either limits in (
3.2
), we can build credible intervals for
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})
by approximating the limiting variance
\upsilon
with the sample based quantity
\upsilon_{p}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
.

Next, part (b) addresses the fluctuations of the posterior mean
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
. Here, the randomness arises from the DGP for
\mathbf{y}
, conditioned on the covariates
\mathbf{X}
and true parameter
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
. Even for this result, the centering is a (somewhat) explicit function of
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
, in that one doesn’t have to solve a fixed point equation to compute the centering. Interestingly, the centering in part (b) is not
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
, the centering for the Bayes estimator under classical asymptotics (e.g. see Theorem 8.3 in
Lehmann and Casella 2006
). This is another consequence of the bounded SNR setting, where the non-vanishing effect of the prior makes the posterior mean asymptotically biased.

In addition to assuming the white noise design
\mathbf{X}
,
3.1
requires some extra assumptions, and we discuss them below. First, we assumed
\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\infty}\to 0
, which is analogous to the uniform asymptotitc negibility (UAN) condition for the classical Lidbeberg-Feller CLT, and rules out localized vectors
\mathbf{q}
. In other words, we assume that the summand
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\beta_{i}
is not dominated by finitely many terms. This is a necessary condition to get a Normal limit, as each coordinate
\beta_{i}
is generally non-Normal and does not concentrate around
u_{i}
due to our bounded SNR scaling. Thus, we average out the coordinates so that the Central Limit Theorem can kick in to guarantee a Normal limit.

Second, we require the existence of the two limits in (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), which define the limiting variances. This can be reformulated in terms of weighted empirical measures. To wit, define

\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\mathbf{q}}:=\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\delta_{\beta^{*}_{i}}.

If we assume that
\mathcal{L}_{p}^{\mathbf{q}}\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\mathnormal{L}
, then the limits can be written as
\upsilon=\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(B/\sigma^{2}+W)
and
\varsigma^{2}=\EE[\Var(\psi_{0}^{\prime}(B/\sigma^{2}+W)|B)]
, where
B\sim\mathnormal{L}
and
W\sim\mcn(0,\sigma^{-2})
are independent.

Here, the value of the limiting variances
\upsilon
and
\varsigma^{2}
may appear abstract at first glance. Interestingly, these values can be understood by considering the limiting distributions
under the Mean-Field approximation

Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
. Recall that
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
is a product measure where the
i
th marginal
Q_{i}^{\text{prod}}
has mean
u_{i}
. Proving a CLT for independent
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{ind}}\sim Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
is significantly simpler, and it can be checked that
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{ind}}
has mean
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
and approximate variance
\upsilon_{p}
. The following proposition illustrates that the conclusions in
3.1
still hold under the Mean-Field posterior. Thus, under the white noise design in
2.3
, the Mean-Field approximation produces valid limiting distributions. In particular, it does not underestimate the variance, as opposed to what is empirically observed in the literature
(
Blei, Kucukelbir and
McAuliffe 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2018b
)
. Consequently, credible intervals constructed under
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
is still valid under the true posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
.

Proposition 3.2

(Analog of
3.1
under
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
)
.

Suppose
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
satisfy the conditions of
3.1
. Let
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm ind}\sim Q^{\mathrm{prod}}\equiv Q^{\mathrm{prod}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
be a random sample from the Mean-Field posterior (where

is as in

).
Then the following conclusions hold:

1.

The weak limits in (

) hold with

.

2.

Assuming the existence of the limit (

) holds when

Remark 3.1

(Tightness of the asymptotic scaling)
.

While we have omitted displaying the finite-sample Berry-Esseen bounds in the CLTs in
3.1
for brevity, our proof additionally reveals explicit rates of convergence. For example, in part (a), the main error terms in the Berry-Esseen upper bound for the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance (excluding the error resulting from the empirical limit in (
3.1
)) can be written as

\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}+\frac{p^{3/2}}{n}+\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\infty},

and we believe that our asymptotic regime
p\ll n^{2/3}
is a hard threshold. Indeed, this is a bottleneck in terms of verifying both our design matrix assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
.
Compared to existing literature on high-dimensional posterior limiting distributions that consider
p\ll n^{1/3}

(
Spokoiny 2013
;
Ghosal 1999
;
Ghosal 2000
;
Lu 2017
)
or
p\ll\sqrt{n}

(
Katsevich 2025
;
Giordano et al. 2025
)
, our result allows much higher dimensional covariates.

Below, we give applications of
3.1
, by illustrating that the limits in (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) exist under natural assumptions on
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
.

Example 3.1

(Misspecified Bayesian model for
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
)
.

Suppose the true coefficient vector
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
is generated i.i.d from a distribution
\mu^{\star}
on
[-1,1]
. In particular, when
\mu^{\star}
is equal to the prior
\mu
, the model (
1.2
) is correctly specified.
Note that for any
\mathbf{q}
with
\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
, and writing
W\sim\mcn(0,\sigma^{-2})
(independent of
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
), the limits

\displaystyle\upsilon=

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\EE\big[\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{i}^{\star}/\sigma^{2}+W)\mid\beta_{i}^{\star}\big]=\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{1}^{\star}/\sigma^{2}+W),

\displaystyle\varsigma^{2}=

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\Var\big[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\beta_{i}^{\star}/\sigma^{2}+W)\mid\beta_{i}^{\star}\big]=\EE\big[\Var(\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\beta_{1}^{\star}/\sigma^{2}+W)|\beta_{1}^{\star})\big]

exist by a standard second momemt argument.

Example 3.2

(Spike-and-slab
(
Ishwaran and Rao 2005
;
Ročková 2018
;
Castillo and van der
Vaart 2012
)
)
.

Sample
r_{p}
from a
\mbox{Beta}(1,p^{u})
distribution for
u>0
and then generate
(\beta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\star})
as follows:

\beta_{i}^{\star}|r_{p}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\,(1-r_{p})\delta_{0}\,+\,r_{p}\otb,

for some fixed distribution
\otb
supported on
[-1,1]
. Note that
(\beta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\star})
are only conditionally independent given
r_{p}
, and not marginally independent as in the previous example.

In the above example, it is natural to expect that if the
sparsity
is high enough, then the limiting behavior of
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\beta_{i}]
would be the same as in the null case
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
. The following Corollary characterizes this threshold of sparsity in terms of
u
and
\mathbf{q}
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Consider the assumptions of
3.1
. Define
q^{(p)}_{tot}:=\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}
and sample
W\sim\mcn(0,\sigma^{-2})
independent of
\otb
.
In the setting of
3.2
, the following conclusions hold:

1.

Suppose
\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\otb/\sigma^{2}+W)=0
. Then

\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn\big(0,\Var\psi_{0}^{\prime}(W)\big)
.

2.

Suppose
\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\otb/\sigma^{2}+W)\neq 0
and
p^{-u}q^{(p)}_{tot}\to\zeta
for some
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, we then have

\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn\big(\zeta\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\otb/\sigma^{2}+W),\Var\psi_{0}^{\prime}(W)\big)\,.

3.

Finally, if
\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime}(\otb/\sigma^{2}+W)\neq 0
and
p^{-u}q^{(p)}_{tot}\to\pm\infty
, then
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})\,\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\pm\infty.

Here,
3.3
shows that the sparsity threshold depends on the symmetry of
\otb
, and the size of
q_{tot}^{(p)}=\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}
. If
\otb
is a symmetric distribution or
\mathbf{q}
is an approximate contrast satisfying
q_{tot}^{(p)}=o(p^{u})
, then
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
always exhibits null behavior. The latter is always satisfied when
u>1/2
, as the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality gives
|q_{tot}^{(p)}|\leq\sqrt{p}
. Otherwise, the limiting distribution may exhibit a nonzero centering, or even diverge in the limit.

3.2
Misspecified Bayesian model

In this Section, we weaken the random design assumption from
Section

3.1
. We will stick to the data generating
2.4
, but additionally assume that (i) the vector
\mathbf{q}
is an approximate eigenvector of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
, (ii)
\beta^{\star}_{1},\ldots,\beta^{\star}_{p}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\mu^{\star}
for some probability distribution
\mu^{\star}
on
\mathbb{R}
which is symmetric around
0
, and (iii) the Bayesian prior
\mu
used to construct the posterior (
2.2
) is also symmetric. Note that
\mu^{\star}
need not be equal to
\mu
.
While the prior
\mu
used in (
2.2
) is supported on
[-1,1]
,
\mu^{\star}
does not have to satisfy this. Consequently, we allow a wide range of
\mu^{\star}
that ranges from degenerate distributions to measures with unbounded support.

Below, we present the CLTs for
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})
and
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
under this setup.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
satisfies Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
, and that
\mathbf{y}
is generated according to
2.4
.
Also, suppose that
\mathbf{A}_{p}=-\frac{\mathrm{Off}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})}{\sigma^{2}}
has an approximate delocalized eigenpair
(\lambda_{p},\mathbf{q})
in the following sense:

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{A}_{p}\mathbf{q}-\lambda_{p}\mathbf{q}\rVert\to 0,\quad\lambda_{p}\to\lambda\in(-1,1),\quad\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\infty}\to 0.

(3.5)

Suppose that the Bayesian prior
\mu
in (
1.2
) is symmetric.
For a symmetric measure
\mu^{\star}
on
\mathbb{R}
with finite fourth moments, suppose that
\beta_{i}^{\star}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mu^{\star}
.
Let
W_{0}\sim\mcn(0,d_{0})
be a random variable that is independent of
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
.

1.

(i)

Under the above conditions, we have
\upsilon:=\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})>0
(this depends on
\mu,\mu^{\star},d_{0})
, and
\upsilon_{p}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\xp\upsilon
. Further we have

T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})-\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn\left(0,\frac{\upsilon}{1-\lambda\upsilon}\right).

(ii)

Additionally, assume that the eigenpair
(\lambda_{p},\mathbf{q})
satisfies

\displaystyle(1+\sqrt{p\alpha_{p}})\lVert\mathbf{A}_{p}\mathbf{q}-\lambda_{p}\mathbf{q}\rVert=o(1).

(3.6)

Then we can modify the centering as follows:

T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})-\frac{\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\psi_{i}^{\prime}(c_{i})}{1-\lambda_{p}\upsilon_{p}}\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn\left(0,\frac{\upsilon}{1-\lambda\upsilon}\right).

(3.7)

2.

Assume (
3.6
), and slightly strengthen
2.3
by assuming
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(d_{i}-d_{0})^{2}=o(1)
. Then, setting

\varsigma^{2}:=\frac{\EE\left[\Var\Big(\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})\mid\beta_{1}^{\star}\Big)\right]}{(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}}-\frac{\lambda\upsilon^{2}}{(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}}

(which depends on
\mu,\mu^{\star},d_{0},\lambda

. Further, we have

\displaystyle\left(\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})-\frac{1}{1-\lambda\upsilon}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\Big(\EE\big[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star}+W_{0})\mid\beta_{i}^{\star}]-\lambda\upsilon\beta_{i}^{\star}\Big)\right)\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mcn(0,\varsigma^{2}).

(3.8)

We first understand the conclusion of the Theorem. While
3.4
presents CLTs analogous to that as in
3.1
, the centerings and limiting distributions are more complicated. This is because we are allowing general design matrices beyond the white noise design, which modify the limiting variances. To compare the results, note that (
3.5
) holds under the white noise design by taking
\lambda=0
and an arbitrary unit vector
{\bf q}
, as long as
\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\infty}\to 0
. In other words, an arbitrary unit vector
{\bf q}
satisfying the delocalization condition
\|{\bf q}\|_{\infty}\to 0
is an approximate eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}_{p}
with eigenvalue
0
. Thus, plugging in
\lambda_{p},\lambda=0
in (

) and (
3.8
) recovers the claims in
3.1
, as the values
\upsilon,\varsigma^{2}
in both the results agree.

We also discuss the centering in (
3.8
) for
\delta({\bf y},{\bf X})
. By simple algebra, we can re-write the centering as

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}+\text{bias}_{p}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}),\quad\text{bias}_{p}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}):=\frac{1}{1-\lambda\upsilon}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\Big(\EE\big[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star}+W_{0})\mid\beta_{i}^{\star}]-\beta_{i}^{\star}\Big).

(3.9)

This can be viewed as the true parameter of interest plus some bias due to the non-vanishing effect of the prior. Under the true model assumption
\beta_{i}^{\star}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mu^{\star}
for some symmetric
\mu^{\star}
,
\text{bias}_{p}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})
is a random variable with mean
0
and non-vanishing variance. This implies that the Bayes estimator will be asymptotically biased (with high probability) for each realization of
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
, but the bias will have expectation
0
when
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
is marginalized out.

Next, we discuss the approximate eigenpair assumptions on the off-diagonal matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}=-\Off({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}})
(see (
3.5
) and (
3.6
)). This is a somewhat technical requirement that results from
(
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
)
, which is a large building block for our proofs. Under
2.3
, the diagonals of the gram matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}=\sigma^{-2}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})
are essentially constant, and so we can equivalently understand the vector
\mathbf{q}
as an approximate eigenvector of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}=\sigma^{-2}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})
, due to the decomposition in (
2.1
). Also, under the high-temperature assumption

, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{p}
must satisfy

|\lambda_{p}|\leq\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|\leq\|\mathbf{A}_{p}\|_{4}\leq\rho<1,\quad\forall p.

The following Proposition re-addresses comparing the posterior limiting distribution to that under the Mean-Field approximation, but for general design matrices
\mathbf{X}
in the setting of Theorem
3.4
.

Proposition 3.5

(Analog of
3.4
under

)
.

Consider the setting of

, except for assuming that

is a random sample from the Mean-Field approximation

.

1.

Recall the definition of
\upsilon
from part (a) of
3.4
. Then we have

T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})-\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\upsilon).

2.

Under the setting of part (b) in

) holds when

Remark 3.2

(Variance under/overestimation)
.

First, note that the limiting distributions of the posterior mean
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
in
3.4
, part (b), and
3.5
, part (b), are the same. This is not surprising, since we have already established that they are close under milder assumptions in (
2.10
). However, the limiting distributions of
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})-\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
in
3.4
, part (a), and
3.5
, part (a), are different. The limiting variance under the true posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
is
(1-\upsilon\lambda)^{-1}\upsilon
whereas the variance under
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
is
\upsilon
. This is not equal unless
\lambda=0
, which was the case under the white noise design in the previous section. In general, depending on whether
\lambda<0
,
=0
, or
>0
, the limiting variance under
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
is smaller, equal, or larger, than that of the corresponding Mean-Field approximation.

For a concrete example of this phenomenon, recall the high-dimensional ANOVA design in
2.2
. There, we have two exact eigenpairs with nonzero eigenvalues: (i)
\lambda_{p}^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}<0
,
\mathbf{q}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\mathbf{1}_{p}
and (ii)
\lambda_{p}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}
,
\mathbf{q}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}(\mathbf{1}_{p/2}^{\top},-\mathbf{1}_{p/2}^{\top})^{\top}
. Therefore, in case (i), the Mean-Field variance is higher than the posterior variance, whereas it is lower in case (ii). All other eigenvectors (any delocalized vector
\mathbf{q}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{q}^{(1)},\mathbf{q}^{(2)}
) correspond to
\lambda=0
, so the Mean-Field approximation captures the correct posterior variance. We refer the reader to
Figure

2
for a visual illustration.

3.3
Detour: Extension to fractional posteriors

An alternative to the posterior in (
2.2
) is the popular notion of
\gamma
-fractional posteriors (see
Grünwald 2012
;
Bhattacharya, Pati and Yang 2019
;
Miller and Dunson 2019
;
Avella Medina et al. 2022
;
Ray, Medina and
Rush 2023
; also known as tempered, power, or
\alpha
-posteriors in the literature). Here,
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
is the exponent for the likelihood when defining the posterior.
In the context of the linear model (
1.1
), the
\gamma
-fractional posterior is given by

\displaystyle\nu^{(\gamma)}_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}(d\bm{\beta})\propto\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{p}^{(\gamma)}\bm{\beta}+(\mathbf{c}^{(\gamma)})^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mu^{(\gamma)}_{i}(d\beta_{i}),\hskip 18.49988pt\mbox{where}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{(\gamma)}:=\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\sigma^{2}/\gamma},

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{p}^{(\gamma)}(i,j)=-\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})(i,j)\mathbf{1}_{i\neq j}}{\sigma^{2}/\gamma},\;\;\frac{d\mu_{i}^{(\gamma)}}{d\mu}(\beta_{i})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{d^{(\gamma)}_{i}\beta_{i}^{2}}{2}\right),

and
d^{(\gamma)}_{i}:=\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})(i,i)}{\sigma^{2}/\gamma}
, for
i,j=1,2,\ldots,p
. Note that the above display can be viewed as the usual posterior (
2.2
), but with a re-scaled standard error
\sigma^{(\gamma)}:=\sigma/\sqrt{\gamma}
.
Consequently, both Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
directly apply to
\gamma
-fractional posteriors, after this simple change of variance.

In particular, the
\gamma<1
case has received a lot of attention, as the resulting posterior exhibits robustness against model misspecification
(
Avella Medina et al. 2022
)
and contracts with respect to the associated Rényi divergence under mild requirements
(
Bhattacharya, Pati and Yang 2019
, Theorem 3.2)
. Our results do not require any hard constraints on
\gamma
, and the dependence of
\gamma
is more delicately reflected on the high-temperature
2.1
. Note that
2.1
becomes weaker as
\gamma
decreases, allowing a wider range of design matrices.

4
Application: Bernstein-von Mises analogs

An important result in the literature is the Bernstein-von Mises theorem
(
Van der Vaart 2000
;
Kleijn and van der Vaart 2012
)
. This theorem implies that the Bayesian level
100(1-\alpha)\%
credible intervals are also asymptotically
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence intervals, under a contracting posterior that satisfies some regularity conditions. An immediate question arises: do similar conclusions apply to bounded SNR settings with a non-contracting posterior? We answer this question in an
average sense
, under the setting of
Section

3.2
.

Given
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
c_{\alpha/2}
be a constant such that
\PP(|\mcn(0,1)|\geq c_{\alpha/2})=\alpha
. Note that
3.4
, part (a), yields the following level
(1-\alpha)
posterior credible set for
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})=\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
:

\mathcal{I}\equiv\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\mu):=\Big[\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\pm c_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\upsilon_{p}}{1-\lambda_{p}\upsilon_{p}}}\Big].

Here, we have replaced the limiting variance
\upsilon/(1-\lambda\upsilon)
by its sample-based approximation
\upsilon_{p}/(1-\lambda_{p}\upsilon_{p})
, so all terms in
\mathcal{I}
depend only on the data
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}
and the prior
\mu
. As in
Section

3.2
, we assume
\beta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\star}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mu^{\star}
, which may be different from
\mu
. Given a credible interval
\mathcal{I}
and distribution
\mu^{\star}
, define its average coverage probability as:

\text{AC}(\mathcal{I};\mu^{\star}):=\PP\Big(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\in\mathcal{I}\mid\mathbf{X}\Big).

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the setting and notation from part (b) of
3.4
. Recall that we denote the Bayesian prior as
\mu
and data generating prior as
\mu^{\star}
. For random variables
\beta_{1}^{\star}\sim\mu^{\star}\perp W_{0}\sim\mcn(0,d_{0})
, set

\tau^{2}:=\frac{\Var\big(\beta_{1}^{\star}-\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})\big)-\lambda\upsilon^{2}}{(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}},

where
\upsilon=\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})
is the same constant from
3.4
, and
\psi_{0}
is defined in
3.1
. Note that the constants
\upsilon,\tau^{2}
depend both on
\mu
(implicitly via the function
\psi_{0}
) and
\mu^{\star}
.

1.

Let
u_{i}
be the Mean-Field optimizer in (
2.7
). Then, the following unconditional limit (on
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
) holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}(u_{i}-\beta_{i}^{\star})\mid\mathbf{X}\xd\mcn\big(0,\tau^{2}\big).

(4.1)

2.

The average
100(1-\alpha)\%
coverage probability of the credible interval
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\mu)
satisfies

\displaystyle\textnormal{AC}(\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\mu);\mu^{\star})\to 1-2\Phi\left(c_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\upsilon}{(1-\lambda\upsilon)\tau^{2}}}\right).

(4.2)

In particular, when the nondegenerate prior is well-specified in the sense that
\mu=\mu^{\star}
, the RHS of (
4.2
) is equal to
1-\alpha
.

We elaborate on the results.
Part (a) provides an unconditional limit (i.e., averaged over the distribution of
\beta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\star}
) for
\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}u_{i}
. Recalling from
2.4
that
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}+o(1),
part (a) can be viewed as the unconditional analog of the limiting distribution of the posterior mean
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
(see part (b) in
3.4
) around
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
, the “correct” centering. The proof additionally reveals that the unconditional variance
\tau^{2}
is strictly larger than the conditional variance
\varsigma^{2}
in
3.4
, which is a natural consequence of marginalizing out the randomness of the centering bias (recall the decomposition (
3.9
)).

Part (b) computes the asymptotic coverage probability of the Bayesian credible interval
\mathcal{I}
under potentially misspecified priors. In particular, when the prior is well-specified with
\mu=\mu^{\star}
, the theorem shows that the credible interval
\mathcal{I}
also functions as an asymptotic confidence set with an
average true coverage
of level
(1-\alpha)
. This is analogous to the usual consequence of the Bernstein-von Mises theorem in the high SNR regime
(
Van der Vaart 2000
)
. To elaborate, when
\mu=\mu^{\star}
, the proof of part (b) shows that
\tau^{2}=(1-\upsilon\lambda)^{-1}\upsilon
. This value is identical to the limiting
posterior variance
of
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
in part (a) of
3.4
. The fact that the two variances (under randomness of the true data and posterior, respectively) are the same implies the same level of uncertainty, which is again analogous to standard results for contracting posteriors with high SNR. Additionally, the averaging out the randomness of
\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
in (
4.2
) is necessary, as the conditional coverage probability would have
O(1)
fluctuations otherwise (due to the random bias in (
3.9
)).

2

\alpha=0.05
and visualize the limiting true coverage probability (in the RHS of (
4.2
)) with respect to

.

\mu^{\star}=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{-1}+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{1}
. “Exact” denotes the coverage of the
95\%
credible intervals in
4.1
, and “NMF” denotes the coverage of the
95\%
credible intervals based on the mean-field posterior approximation
Q^{\mathrm{prod}}
.

5
Proof overview and technical challenges

The main results of this paper are Theorems
3.1
,
3.4
, and
4.1
. Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
both derive a CLT for linear statistics of the form
\mathbf{q}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}
, and of the Bayes estimator
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})=\EE[{\bf q}^{\top}{\beta}]
, under two different sets of assumptions. The main idea behind these results is that the log-posterior of
{\bm{\beta}}
in a linear regression model has a quadratic form, which essentially reduces it to a
“Random Field Ising model”

(
Chatterjee 2024
;
de Matos and Perez 1991
;
Kabluchko and
Löwe 2024
, see)
. In a recent paper
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
, we studied Berry-Esseen type CLTs in the setting of Random Field Ising models, where the
“external magnetic field”
consists of i.i.d random variables.
The main subtlety arises from the entrywise dependence of the
“external magnetic field”
vector
\mathbf{c}=\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
as

\mathbf{c}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\sim\mcn({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}})

under the data generating assumption (see
2.4
), where
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}
is defined after (
1.3
). Moreover, the high-dimensional nature of the problem where both
p,n\to\infty
also makes it challenging to deal with the entrywise dependence. Consequently, bounding the individual error terms in the CLT from
Lee, Deb and Mukherjee 2025
requires a much more delicate analysis.

To further highlight the technical challenges, let us consider the random design setting of
3.1
, and also assume
\sigma^{2}=1
. Here it might be tempting to use the approximation
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}\approx\mathbf{I}_{p}
to directly approximate the interaction term in the posterior (see (
1.3
)) with a coordinatewise separable term. To wit, note that

\sup_{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\in[-1,1]^{p}}\big|\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}-\lVert\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\rVert^{2}\big|=\sup_{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\in[-1,1]^{p}}\big|\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}-\mathbf{I}_{p})\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}\big|=O_{P}\left(p\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right)=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p^{3}}{n}}\right).

Here we have used the approximation
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathnormal{p}}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\rVert=O_{p}(\sqrt{p/n})
(see
(
Vershynin 2018
, Theorem 5.6.1)
). The above display shows that a direct product measure approximation of the entire posterior in total variation distance would require the stringent condition
p=o(n^{1/3})
. However, leveraging significant cancellations in the analysis of
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
(see Lemmas
A.9
and
A.10
), we show that for linear projections our CLT holds under the much weaker condition
p=o(n^{2/3})
. We note that even in the high SNR setting in the random design case, to the best of our knowledge, Bernstein-von Mises type results are known only up to
p=o(\sqrt{n})

(
Spokoiny 2013
;
Katsevich 2025
, see)
.
Possibly more challenging is the proof of
3.4
, where the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
is assumed to be non random. In this case we assume that in the data generating model
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{\star}
the true coefficient vector
{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
is generated as an i.i.d vector from some prior
\mu^{\star}
(potentially different from
\mu
). In this case, Lemmas
A.11
and
A.12
play the analogues of Lemmas
A.9
and
A.10
respectively.

The above discussion applies to the CLT of
{\bf q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
under the posterior. To analyze the Bayes estimator
\delta({\bf y},{\bf X})=\EE_{\nu_{{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{X}}}}{\bf q}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}
, we use Proposition 2.4 to first get the approximation
\delta({\bf y},{\bf X})\approx{\bf q}^{\top}{\bf u}
in terms of
{\bf u}
, the solution to the mean field variational problem (see (
2.7
)). Noting that
{\bf u}
is a function of
{\bf y}
which is multivariate Gaussian (given
\mathbf{X},{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})
, we then derive a CLT for
{\bf q}^{\top}{\bf u}
. For this goal, we first use the fixed point equation (
2.7
) to approximate
{\bf q}^{\top}{\bf u}
by a more tractable object, and then use a CLT for non-linear functions of dependent Gaussians (the so-called “second-order” Poincaré inequality, see
(
Chatterjee 2009
, Theorem 2.2)
). Along the way, we also use the usual Gaussian Poincaré inequality (see
(
Van Handel 2014
, Corollary 2.27)
) to control various error terms.

We move on to
4.1
, for which we need to study the limit distribution of
{\bf q}^{\top}({\bf u}-{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})
, under a potentially misspecifed prior. By again leveraging the approximation
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})\approx\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
, we express

{\bf q}^{\top}({\bf u}-{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})
as a sum of two terms, a correctly centralized version of the Bayes estimator
\delta({\bf y},{\bf X})
, and a bias term which is (approximately) a separable function of
{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
. The first term has a Gaussian limit, using the CLT for Bayes estimators in
3.4
, part (b), and the second term has a CLT as
{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}
has marginally i.i.d coordinates (coupled with the Lindeberg-Feller CLT). Combining the asymptotics for the two terms, the CLT for
{\bf q}^{\top}({\bf u}-{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})
follows, from which we can readily compute the limiting coverage of the proposed credible interval explicitly.

6
Discussion

This work provides limiting distributions of (i) one-dimensional projections of the posterior
T(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}})=\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}
for some
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert=1
, and (ii) the posterior mean
\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
. Our results are novel as we establish limiting distributions under a challenging high-dimensional asymptotic regime without posterior contraction. We also de-mystify uncertainty quantification of Mean-Field variational inference, by providing explicit conditions for its validity as well as illustrating the possibility of both over/under estimating the variance. Finally, we construct credible intervals for linear projections and characterize their asymptotic coverage under model misspecifications. Our result demonstrates that Bayesian procedures can enjoy true coverage under the low SNR scaling in an average sense, under the correct prior specification.

We believe this work creates many interesting questions for future research. First, it would be important to improve on our technical conditions — (a) extending beyond the high-temperature regime (see
2.1
), (b) accommodating non-compactly supported priors
\mu
, (c) studying CLTs for linear projections in the white noise design setting in the
n^{2/3}\ll p\ll n
regime, etc. We believe that it would be meaningful to go beyond these assumptions and derive limiting distributions under a more flexible setup.

Another intriguing direction is to derive limiting distributions and credible intervals in generalized linear models in a low SNR regime. Currently, our proof technique relies on the quadratic nature of the posterior
\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}
. While there have been some recent developments in establishing consistency-type results in such settings
(
Mukherjee, Qiu and
Sen 2024
, see)
, studying asymptotic distributions requires considerably finer analysis.

Finally, it would be interesting to think beyond the subjective Bayesian framework considered in this paper (see (
1.2
)), and consider an empirical Bayes setting where the prior
\mu
is estimated from the data
({\bf y},{\bf X})
. In particular, it would be interesting to extend the coverage guarantees in
4.1
to analogous statements for credible intervals built using an estimated prior
\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
. While consistent estimators for the data generating prior
\mu
in regression problems has been recently established
(
Mukherjee, Sen and
Sen 2023
;
Fan et al. 2023
)
, our current proof does not immediately generalize due to double-dipping of
\mathbf{y}
(once for sampling from the posterior and again for constructing
\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
), and we leave this question for future research.

Preliminaries: Central Limit Theorems

Asymptotic distribution theory for linear projections under quadratic interaction models with bounded SNR is relatively unexplored in the literature; with a few exceptions such as
Deb and Mukherjee 2023
;
Chatterjee and
Shao 2011
;
Deb et al. 2024

(see (

Our proof arguments build upon recently developed finite-sample CLTs for (i) Gibbs measures with quadratic interactions
(

(
Chatterjee 2009
)
. Mainly, we use two results from

Definition A.1

(KS and BL distances)
.

For random variables
X
and

supported on
\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle d_{KS}(X,Y)=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|P(X\leq t)-P(Y\leq t)\big|.

(A.1)

On the other hand, the bounded-Lipschitz (BL) distance (see
(

\displaystyle d_{BL}(X,Y)=\sup\left\{\EE f(X)-\EE f(Y):\,\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1,\sup_{z_{1}\neq z_{2}}\frac{|f(z_{1})-f(z_{2})|}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|}\leq 1\right\}.

(A.2)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}}\bar{\Gamma}_{j}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)^{2}q_{i}^{2}\leq\big(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)^{2}\big)\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\leq\an.

\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}}\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{j}^{2}\bar{\Gamma}_{j}^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}}\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\mathfrak{F}_{j}^{2}\leq\an\bigg(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}^{2}\bigg)p\an=p\alpha_{p}^{2}=o(1).

Finally, another second moment computation using the independence of
\beta_{i}^{\star}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}}\Big[\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)q_{i}q_{j}(\phi_{2}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})-\upsilon)(\phi_{2}(d_{0}\beta_{j}^{\star})-\upsilon)\Big]^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i\neq j}q_{i}^{2}q_{j}^{2}\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{p}(i,j)^{2}\leq\an=o(1).

Positivity of
\varsigma^{2}
. Let
\upsilon=\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})
as in
3.4

in (

(see

(the error term is bounded via Cauchy-Schwartz alongside

as an upper bound for

Here, recall that

By using Stein’s identity (while conditioning on

By taking another expectation and squaring the above display, using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality twice (for the outer expectation and the inner covariance, respectively), we have

\displaystyle\big(\EE\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\EE\left[\frac{\Cov(W_{0},\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})\mid\beta_{1}^{\star})}{d_{0}}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\EE\left[\Cov(W_{0},\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})\mid\beta_{1}^{\star})^{2}\right]}{d_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\EE\Big[\Var\big(\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})\mid\beta_{1}^{\star}\big)\Big]}{d_{0}}.

The last inequality uses the fact that

. This shows (

sign. For equality to hold in the above Cauchy-Schwartz,

must be linear which implies

must be Gaussian, which is ruled out by our compact support assumption on

Application to credible intervals

To prove

Lemma A.13
.

Let

Proof of
4.1
.

(a) Using (
2.10
) and the decomposition (
3.9
), we can write the LHS of (

\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}(u_{i}-\beta_{i}^{\star})=\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})+o(1)-\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\beta_{i}^{\star}=\text{LHS of \eqref{eq:regression CLT annealed}}+\text{bias}_{p}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})+o(1).

where the notation
\text{bias}_{p}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})
is defined in (
3.9
).
We separately control the two terms in the above expression. For notational convenience, define

\displaystyle G_{p}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})

\displaystyle:=\text{LHS of \eqref{eq:regression CLT annealed}}=\delta(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})-\frac{1}{1-\lambda\upsilon}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\Big(\phi_{1}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})-\lambda\upsilon\beta_{i}^{\star}\Big)

and

where

is defined as in

First, use part (b) of
3.4
to conclude

G_{p}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})\mid\mathbf{X},\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\xd\mcn(0,\varsigma^{2}).

Proceeding to analyze

) and the definition of

from

\text{bias}_{p}(\mathnormal{\bm{\beta}}^{\star})=\frac{1}{1-\lambda\upsilon}\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}(\phi_{1}(d_{0}\beta_{i}^{\star})-\beta_{i}^{\star}).

We then use the symmetry of
\mu
and
\mu^{\star}
to conclude that

Consequently, by the Lindberg-Feller CLT for independent random variables, we have

Now, by applying

A.13
to combine the two limiting distributions, we have

\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}\big(u_{i}-\beta_{i}^{\star}\big)=(G_{p}+\text{bias}_{p})\mid\mathbf{X}\xd\mcn\big(0,\vartheta^{2}+\varsigma^{2}\big).

Finally, it remains to prove that the above limiting variance matches the value
\tau^{2}
in (
4.1
), i.e.
\vartheta^{2}+\varsigma^{2}=\tau^{2}
.
For notational convenience, define random variables
B,C
(given
\mu^{\star},d_{0}
) by setting

\displaystyle B\sim\mu^{\star},\quad C\mid B\sim\mcn(d_{0}B,d_{0}).

Then, we can write

(see

) so we have
\EE\phi_{1}(d_{0}B)=\EE[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(C)]=0
. Using this notation, we can write

\displaystyle(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}\vartheta^{2}

\displaystyle=\Var\Big[\phi_{1}(d_{0}B)-B\Big]=\Var\Big[\EE[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(C)-B\mid B]\Big],

\displaystyle(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}\varsigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\EE\Big[\Var[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(C)\mid B]\Big]-\lambda\upsilon^{2}=\EE\Big[\Var[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(C)-B\mid B]\Big]-\lambda\upsilon^{2}.

\displaystyle\vartheta^{2}+\varsigma^{2}=\frac{\Var[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(C)-B]-\lambda\upsilon^{2}}{(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}}=\tau^{2}.

(A.50)

(b) Note from

In the remainder of the proof, we assume

and write

as the quadratic tilt of

(see

Using the notations

from (

\tau^{2}=\frac{\Var\Big(\beta_{1}^{\star}-\psi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{0}\beta_{1}^{\star}+W_{0})\Big)-\lambda\upsilon^{2}}{(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}}=\frac{\Var[\psi_{0}^{\prime}(C)-B]-\lambda\upsilon^{2}}{(1-\lambda\upsilon)^{2}}

Finally, using the law of total variance to expand

1\leq k_{1},s_{1},k_{2},s_{2}\leq n
. Using
Z_{k,i}

Other choices of

For part (d) we use the generalized Efron-Stein inequality from

\displaystyle\leq\big|(\tilde{\beta}_{i}^{\star}-\beta_{i}^{\star})\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\gamma_{j}\mathbf{A}_{p}(j,i)\Phi(\beta_{j}^{\star})\big|+2\big|\gamma_{i}\mathfrak{F}_{i}\big|.

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(S_{p}-S_{p}^{(i)})^{2}\Big)^{2}\lesssim\left(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}(\tilde{\beta}_{i}^{\star}-\beta_{i}^{\star})^{2}\left(\sum_{\mathnormal{j}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\gamma_{j}\mathbf{A}_{p}(j,i)\Phi(\beta_{j}^{\star})\right)^{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}\gamma_{i}^{2}\mathfrak{F}_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}.

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{\mathnormal{i}=1}^{\mathnormal{p}}q_{i}(\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}[\beta_{i}]-u_{i})\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim\EE_{\nu_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}}}\lVert\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{s}\rVert_{4}^{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\ell}\lesssim\sqrt{p}\an.

Here, we have used the fact that

Lemma

alongside

(C.2)
