The impact of socioeconomic determinants of health and resulting health inequalities on children and young people with long-term health conditions in the UK: a scoping review protocol
Authors: G Sekhon, J Wray, A Ifederu, J Gungor, S Durham, P Sipanoun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocioeconomic determinants of health (SDoH) have a significant impact on healthcare, health outcomes, and resulting health inequalities. A wealth of research relating to the adult population exists, including those with long-term health conditions. However, research is lacking on the impact of these SDoH on children and young people with long-term health conditions. Children and young people are also often forgotten when it comes to policies, legislation, and funding to reduce health inequalities. This scoping review aims to explore the extent and quality of research, legislation, and policies in the United Kingdom, in relation to SDoH and the resulting health inequalities in children and young people with long-term health conditions.Methods and designA flexible, iterative approach to this scoping review will enhance our ability to explore the impact of SDoH on children and young people with long-term health conditions. This will involve six steps: (1) defining the research question; (2) identifying relevant articles; (3) study selection; (4) charting the data; (5) collating, summarising, and reporting the results; and (6) consultation exercise with key stakeholders. A search of EMBASE, EMCARE, Medline, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, Sociological Abstracts, and APA PsycINFO will be conducted from 2010 to present to evaluate the published literature following the Equality Act (2010), which set out the legal framework, and the Marmot Review (2010), a strategic review of health inequalities. Manual searching of national and international conferences, government white papers, published reports, and research summaries searched via the Health Research Authority website, and grey literature will be included.DiscussionThis review will inform concurrent work being undertaken at Great Ormond Street Hospital, which aims to develop and implement targeted, evidence-based interventions to reduce the impact of these SDoH and the resulting health inequalities. As such, this review is focused on UK-specific evidence relevant to our healthcare systems and patient population. The inclusion of UK policy and legislative literature will also ensure a real-world perspective and identify current gaps in policy that need to be addressed.Systematic review registrationThis review protocol has been registered within the Open Science Framework database (osf.io/5ut9x).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-03008-6.Keywords: Health inequalities, Socioeconomic determinants of health, Long-term conditions, Chronic illness, Children and young people, Legislation, Health policy, Guidance

Background

Socioeconomic determinants of health (SDoH) have a significant impact on the health inequalities that are experienced worldwide. Health inequalities are unfair and avoidable differences in health across the population and between different groups within society [1]. Yet, in the United Kingdom (UK), children and young people (CYP) are often not considered or included in health policy, legislation, or guidance. With funding being disproportionately allocated to adult healthcare, one could argue that being a child is a health inequality in itself due to inequitable access to appropriate paediatric-focused healthcare. In particular, CYP with chronic or long-term health conditions are likely to be more affected due to the increased need for engagement with health services over a prolonged period. Interactions of socioeconomic determinants of health and the health and wellbeing of children and young people

Although SDoH can influence health equity in positive and negative ways, as described by the World Health Organisation [24], we will focus on aspects that have a negative effect on CYP with long-term health conditions. A long-term, or chronic, condition is an illness, disease, or condition that lasts longer than 6 months in duration, as defined by the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health [25]. Long-term conditions are linked with increased incidence of mental health conditions and increased morbidity and mortality [13, 25, 26]. There are 1.7 million CYP in England alone with long-term conditions such as asthma, diabetes, and epilepsy [26].

Social determinants of health have been widely studied in children [27–32] but very few reviews have focussed on children with long-term health conditions. A scoping review by Blume et al. [29] investigated the mediating and moderating effects of family characteristics on health inequalities in children. However, their scope was limited to families’ financial position and interpersonal dynamics, rather than other SDoH such as ethnicity, health literacy, digital literacy, and geographical location, all of which warrant exploration. Another scoping review by Flasch et al. [30] examined the difficulties which all children with medical complexity or special health care needs face, identifying issues relating to access to healthcare, transportation, and health insurance. However, our interest is to understand the ways SDoH lead to such health inequalities within this group of CYP. Moreover, the evidence used in this review was US-focussed and all but one of the included articles were expert opinion pieces or unstructured reviews.

At Great Ormond Street Hospital (GOSH), we have formed a Health Inequalities Steering Committee which has commenced a trust-wide Quality Improvement (QI) project, as advocated by the RCPCH for tackling health inequalities [33], to ensure we are collecting better quality and more complete data, demonstrating increased transparency, and shaping and monitoring improvement activity to further reduce health inequalities. This scoping review is therefore key to ensuring evidence-based interventions and recommendations are made to positively impact health inequalities for CYP with long-term health conditions.

This scoping review will explore health policy, legislation, published guidance and reports, and research findings to understand the impact of health inequalities on CYP with long-term health conditions. The review will include studies and reports from the United Kingdom (UK) only. We have specifically targeted literature, legislation, policy and guidance from the UK to understand our country-specific issues relating to health inequalities. Although there is literature available from other developed countries, the UK is unique in its free-to-access healthcare system, and there are specific challenges faced within the NHS, including both financial and resource-based issues. Focussing on UK-specific policy, legislation and guidance is also necessary to gain an understanding of the expected standards and legal framework within which future initiatives to improve health inequalities for this cohort must be implemented.

This review will include publications focused on or related to, but not limited to, children and young people with long-term conditions which affect them physically, cognitively, and/or emotionally, including chronic mental health conditions, cancer, and congenital conditions which lead to long-term health needs. For the purposes of this review, we have defined CYP as infants, children, and adolescents from birth up until their 18th birthday, as set out by the General Medical Council [34].

Objective of this scoping review

The objective of this scoping review is to understand the extent and quality of research, evidence, legislation, and policies in the United Kingdom, in relation to SDoH and the resulting health inequalities in CYP with long-term health conditions.

Methods and design

This review protocol has been registered within the Open Science Framework database (osf.io/5ut9x) and is being reported in accordance with the reporting guidance provided in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [35].

The theoretical framework for scoping reviews proposed by Arksey and O’Malley will be used to guide our review [36]. We will aim to identify all relevant literature—regardless of study design or origin—to enable us to examine the extent, range, and nature of the evidence to provide an understanding of the impact of SDoH and the resulting health inequalities on children and young people with long-term health conditions.

This scoping review will take an iterative approach, enabling a reflexive review of the search strategy, further refining of the research question as part of the process, as required [37]. This flexible, iterative approach will enhance our ability to explore this complex topic. The six steps to be employed in this scoping review include (1) defining the research question; (2) identifying relevant articles; (3) study selection; (4) charting the data; and (5) collating, summarising, and reporting the results [36]. We have included an additional step, (6) consultation exercise with key stakeholders.

Step 3: Study selection

The key area of focus is the impact of SDoH on CYP under 18 years of age with long-term health conditions which require management via regular involvement of health services.

As health services for this cohort are influenced by parents, carers, and healthcare professionals, these groups will also be included provided they care for children or young people with long-term health conditions. Health can also be affected by environmental and social factors, such as mould in housing, affecting CYP with asthma [42]. Therefore, we will also extend the scope of causes and SDoH to other relevant services, including housing, financial aid, social care, and specialist equipment provision.

The potential impact of SDoH and resulting health inequalities on CYP with long-term conditions is broad. As such, we have categorised the impact into the various stages of involvement with health services that CYP with long-term conditions will likely face—the initial interface with health services, the diagnostic process, the ongoing management of the long-term condition(s), and, where relevant, the process of young people’s transition into adult services.

The initial interface with health services refers to the access for CYP and parents to health services during the onset of the long-term illness. This encompasses areas such as health-seeking behaviours of CYP and their parents or carers, the availability of health services, and the initial appointments with clinical teams.

The diagnostic process refers to the time from the initial consultation to the communication of a diagnosed long-term illness. This process can be extremely variable, in terms of timeframe, the need for referral to specialist services, and the number and type of investigations. This process is sometimes described as a ‘diagnostic odyssey’, referring to the potential for a long, drawn-out, and emotionally taxing process for all those involved [43].

The ongoing treatment and management of the long-term condition covers a variety of aspects. This can relate to medication issues (e.g. availability, understanding of use, and adherence), as well as access to therapies and equipment. It also covers the ongoing access to the managing clinical team and their relationship with CYP and their parents or carers.

Transitioning into adult services includes the preparation of CYP and their families to move away from paediatric services, the timing of the transfer into adult services, and the access to appropriate adult services which can continue management of the long-term illness. Related papers will be included if they highlight inequalities, leading to inequities in access to services.

We will include evidence from legislative papers, reports, and policy documents which describe the expected standards in relation to the impact of SDoH and resulting health inequalities on CYP with long-term health conditions.

Once the literature searches have been completed by one team member (GS), this will be charted and managed using the Covidence review system [41]. Title and abstract screening will be performed by two members of a study team of six (GS, JW, AI, JG, SD, PS) to determine inclusion or exclusion for full text review. Subsequent full text review will be conducted by any two members of the study team. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion with the lead investigator (PS) at bi-monthly study team meetings.

Type of studies to be included:

Study types will include experimental and quasi-experimental study designs, including randomised controlled trials and non-randomised control studies, meta-analyses, population studies, observational studies, cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, retrospective and prospective cohort studies. Studies with completed or interim data will be included. Qualitative studies will be considered if clear methodology is present. Case series and individual case studies will be excluded. Guidelines, legislation, policies, and position statements will be included. Book chapters and conference proceedings will only be included if they include full papers or abstracts containing data. Scoping reviews and systematic reviews will not be included themselves, but their references will be reviewed to identify any literature missing from our initial searches.

Step 4: Charting the data

A bespoke data extraction sheet will be developed to facilitate consistent collection of data. Key data points for studies will include (where available): study type, methodology, scope, type of long-term illness, participant group, age range of CYP, specific area of the UK, type of inequality/which SDoH/aspect of care studied, interventions, and outcome measures. Qualitative data will be charted, including individual quotations, thematic analysis, verbatim text, and field notes, along with demographic information. Any data describing infrastructure, cost analysis, and sustainability will be collected. We will also collect information describing standards of care or recommendations within legislative documents, policies, and position statements. For each paper, the study team will identify further factors to be charted. Studies which have key data missing will be discussed with the lead investigator (PS) and may be excluded from some subsections of analysis and included in other subsections as deemed appropriate.

Step 5: Collating, summarising, and reporting the results

Following data extraction, the team will perform both numeric, descriptive counts, and thematic analyses. The numerical data will be analysed using descriptive statistics to report years of publication, the number of publications per country, participant populations, and age groups of young people included in the studies. The types of settings, methodologies and interventions studied, and outcomes/effectiveness of the interventions will also be collated, summarised and reported. How the literature relates to our research questions will be explored in relation to, but not limited to, the different themes of access to healthcare, diagnostics, treatment and transition from paediatric into adult services. Thematic analysis will involve examining excerpts relevant to our research questions and coding them accordingly [44, 45]. This will involve discussion amongst the whole team to clarify, refine and ensure consistency in the coding of data throughout the process. Importantly, the analysis will be reflexive in nature, which is described as being ‘a reflection of the researcher’s interpretive analysis of the data’ [46], ‘produced at the intersection of the researcher’s theoretical assumptions, their analytic resources and skill, and the data themselves’ [45] which allows for patterns of meaning across the dataset to be identified that address the research aims, within a constructionist framework [44]. This iterative process allows for recoding as our understanding of the literature evolves during the analysis [47]. The data collation will map the relationships between SDoH, the resulting health inequalities, and the different aspects of caring for CYP with chronic illnesses across research, policy and legislative papers. A narrative approach will be employed to report these data, using excerpts and commonality of data to illustrate our findings, which will be presented in diagrammatic, tabular and descriptive summaries.

Step 6: Consultation exercise

The consultation exercise with key stakeholders is core to translating the findings of this work into practical application within the hospital for patient benefit. This will involve dissemination and discussion of the findings of this scoping review, and making recommendations for practical implementation of interventions required to reduce the impact of SDoH and resulting health inequalities on CYP across the hospital. In doing so, we continuously strive for an evidence-based approach to providing more equitable, exceptional quality healthcare by improving access to care, patient experience, and outcomes for those most at risk of experiencing health inequalities.

Discussion

A comprehensive, multi-disciplinary, and multi-pronged approach is required to address the SDoH of health and the resulting health inequalities affecting CYP with long-term conditions, involving healthcare providers, legislators, policy makers, community organisations, and charity partners to ensure that this cohort receives equitable care and support.

At Great Ormond Street Hospital for Children NHS Foundation Trust (GOSH), we are committed to reducing health inequalities affecting our patients and families, with the aim of bridging the gap for patients with long-term health conditions and complex health needs who are impacted by health inequalities. Through a strategic, collaborative approach, steps are being taken to address inequalities and resulting inequities to improve access to care, patient experience, and outcomes for those most at risk of experiencing health inequalities [49]. Tackling digital exclusion is also a high priority following the findings from the Going Digital study, a three-phase concurrent mixed-methods study exploring the experiences and perceptions of young people, parents, and staff members before and after electronic patient record and patient portal implementation [10, 11]. The Going Digital study reported barriers experienced by young people and parents/carers in accessing the patient portal, which were exacerbated during the pandemic due to the rapid shift to digital health modes of care, especially if the child or parent had a physical, intellectual, or sensory disability, did not have English as a first language, experienced digital poverty, or did not want to access digital health for personal, cultural, or religious reasons [10, 11].

The GOSH health inequalities steering committee is leading organisational change through the formation of five working groups to examine and implement initiatives related to improving access to care, patient experience, patient outcomes, reducing digital exclusion, and raising awareness of health inequalities [49]. Our work aligns with NHS England’s (NHSE) Healthcare Inequalities Improvement Programme’s vision for the NHS to deliver ‘exceptional quality healthcare for all; ensuring equitable access, excellent experience and optimal outcomes’ [50], NHSE’s Digital Inclusion Framework [51], and NHSE’s Core20Plus5 for children and young people [52].

As already discussed above, a trust-wide QI project is underway with the aim of further reducing health inequalities. As part of this QI project, listening events have been held with young people, parents, and staff members within the hospital to explore health-inequality issues they experience. This has informed subsequent stages of the QI project (priority setting and the development of self-assessment standards) and further emphasised the need for this scoping review. These key stakeholder groups, and professionals on the GOSH Health Inequalities Steering Committee, will continue to be consulted on matters relating to the Health Inequalities initiatives ongoing at GOSH.

The results of this scoping review will be synthesised with the findings of the QI project, with the aim of providing an evidence-based approach to sustainable, lasting change across the organisation. The overall aim is to be an exemplar for tackling health inequalities in a children’s hospital setting.
