Clinical Benefits of Medical Therapy in Adults With Congenital Heart Disease and Systemic Left Ventricular Presenting With Heart Failure With Reduced Ejection Fraction
Authors: Mohamed Ellabbad, Zeyad Kholeif, Shannon M Dunlay, Maan Jokhadar, David Majdalany, Alexander C Egbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to assess the clinical benefits of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT) for heart failure (HF) in adults with congenital heart disease and HF with reduced ejection fraction.MethodsHF with reduced ejection fraction was defined as stage B/C HF and systemic left ventricular ejection fraction <50%. GDMT score was assessed at baseline encounter (baseline GDMT score) and 1‐year follow‐up. GDMT uptitration was calculated as defined as ∆GDMT score from baseline. Cox regression was used to assess the relationship between HF therapy (baseline and ∆GDMT score) and outcomes (HF hospitalization and mortality).ResultsOf 778 patients (age 44±16, 480 [62%] males), baseline GDMT score was 2 (1, 3) and ∆GDMT score was 0.62 (95% CI, 0.55–0.69). Of 778, only 258 (33%) had GDMT uptitration (∆GDMT score >0). Higher use of GDMT at baseline (adjusted HR per 1 point increase in GDMT score 0.81, 95% CI, 0.74–0.88, P<0.001), and increased use of GDMT over time (adjusted HR for 1 point increase in ∆GDMT score HR 0.70, 95% CI, 0.61–0.79, P<0.001) were associated with lower risk of HF hospitalization. Higher baseline GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.85, 95% CI, 0.76–0.94, P<0.001), and ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.82, 95% CI, 0.71–0.94, P<0.000) were also associated with lower mortality.ConclusionsGDMT use at baseline and GDMT uptitration were associated with improved outcomes and suggest a dose‐dependent relationship between use of GDMT and risk of adverse outcomes. Furthermore, 67% of patients did not receive GDMT uptitration, suggesting suboptimal therapy, and opportunities for improvement.Keywords: clinical outcomes, heart failure therapy, prognosticationSubject Categories: Congenital Heart Disease

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Guideline‐directed medical therapy use at baseline and guideline‐directed medical therapy uptitration during follow‐up were associated with lower risk of heart failure hospitalization and mortality in adults with congenital heart disease presenting with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and the results suggest a dose‐dependent relationship between guideline‐directed medical therapy and outcomes.

Guideline‐directed medical therapy use at baseline and guideline‐directed medical therapy uptitration during follow‐up were associated with lower risk of heart failure hospitalization and mortality in adults with congenital heart disease presenting with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and the results suggest a dose‐dependent relationship between guideline‐directed medical therapy and outcomes.

What Are the Clinical Implications

Guideline‐directed medical therapy may improve clinical outcomes in patients with congenital heart disease with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

Guideline‐directed medical therapy may improve clinical outcomes in patients with congenital heart disease with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

There has been significant improvements in the medical and surgical management of adults with congenital heart disease (CHD), leading to improvements in long‐term outcomes of this population. 1 , 2 However, in spite of these improvements, the life expectancy of adults with CHD remain significantly lower than the general population, and heart failure (HF) is the leading cause of mortality in this population. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The cause of HF in CHD is multifactorial, and includes structural heart disease (valvular heart disease and baffle/conduit dysfunction), rhythm abnormalities (atrial or ventricular arrhythmias and conduction system disease), and arteriosclerotic cardiovascular disease. 6 , 7 The management of HF in CHD centers on early detection and treatment of these underlying causes to prevent (and possibly reverse) ventricular dysfunction. 8 , 9 Unfortunately, some patients develop progressive ventricular systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction despite these interventions, and this in turn, is associated with HF hospitalization and mortality. 6 , 10 , 11 , 12

In the acquired HF population, HF is broadly classified based on left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF) as HF with reduced EF (HFrEF), and HF with preserved EF. 13 , 14 This classification has important clinical implications since the clinical benefits of HF therapy are greater in patients with HFrEF compared with those with preserved EF. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Based on robust empirical data from multiple clinical trials, the guidelines for the management of HF recommend the use of these therapies in patients with HFrEF, and hence the term “guideline‐directed medical therapy” (GDMT). 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 The definition of GDMT has evolved over time as new therapies become available, and this makes it difficult to compare outcomes across studies. 19 , 20 , 21 The GDMT score, also referred to as the HF Collaboratory score, was developed to address this problem, by providing a standardized framework to assess intensity of HF therapy and enable comparison of outcomes across different studies. 19 , 20 , 21

Adults with CHD were excluded from the landmark clinical trials that form the basis for GDMT recommendation, and hence there are no large‐scale clinical trials assessing the efficacy of GDMT in this population. 8 , 9 , 13 , 14 While several observational studies have assessed the efficacy of GDMT in adults with CHD, the clinical benefits of GDMT have not been consistent across studies. 10 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 This is because these studies were based on a heterogeneous population (differences in ventricular morphology, differences in ventricular systolic function, and differences in the definition of HF), and the lack of standardized criteria for assessing intensity of HF therapy in the different studies. 10 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 The current study aims to address these limitations by studying the effect of GDMT (using the GDMT score) on well‐defined cohort of patients with CHD with HFrEF. We hypothesized that higher GDMT score was associated with lower risk of HF hospitalization and mortality in adults with CHD and HFrEF.

METHODS

Study Population

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The Mayo Clinic institutional review board approved this study. Informed consent was not required because the study was based on retrospective chart review. This is a retrospective cohort study of adults (age ≥18 years) with CHD, biventricular physiology with systemic LV, and LVEF <50% who had >1 year of follow‐up at Mayo Clinic from January 1, 2003 to December 31, 2023. The patients were identified through the MACHD (Mayo Adult Congenital Heart Disease) registry. We excluded patients with Fontan physiology, congenitally corrected transposition of great arteries, d‐transposition of great arteries status post atrial switch operation, and patients with unrepaired/palliated cyanotic CHD.

HF Classification

Based on the echocardiogram performed during the first outpatient evaluation in the adult CHD clinic after January 1, 2003 (baseline encounter), patients were classified as HFrEF if they had stage B or C HF and LVEF <40%, or as HF with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF) if they had stage B or C HF and LVEF 40% to 49%. 13 Stage B HF was defined as presence of structural heart disease such as reduced LVEF without current or prior history of HF symptoms while stage C HF was defined as the presence of structural heart disease such as reduced LVEF with current or prior history of HF symptoms. 13 , 29

While the patients with LVEF ≥50%, were excluded from the study, they were used as a reference group in estimating the incidence of HF hospitalization.

GDMT Score

We created a modified GDMT score, and the HF medications and point system used for calculating GDMT score are shown in Table 1. 19 , 20 , 21 While prior GDMT scores included medications such as hydralazine, isosorbide dinitrate, and ivabradine, these medications were excluded from the GDMT score in the current study because these medications were not routinely used for the treatment of HF in our clinical practice. 19 , 20 , 21

We calculated the GDMT score at baseline encounter (baseline GDMT score) and at 1‐year follow‐up using each patient's medication list at these clinical encounters. We assessed GDMT uptitration (∆ GDMT score) as the difference between GDMT score at baseline and 1‐year encounters. This was calculated as follows: ∆ GDMT score=GDMT score at 1 year follow‐up minus GDMT score at baseline encounter, whereby a ∆ GDMT score >0 signified GDMT uptitration. GDMT use in HF has evolved over time, and to account for era effect, we divided the study period into early era (January 1, 2003 to December 31, 2013) versus late era (January 1, 2014 to December 31, 2023) based on the year baseline encounter.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was HF hospitalization, defined as hospital admission for volume overload requiring intravenous diuretics, and the secondary outcome was all‐cause mortality ascertained by review of medical records and the Accurint mortality database. HF hospitalization and all‐cause mortality were assessed as time‐to‐event outcomes and ascertain from 1‐year follow‐up (time zero) until outcome of interest, last clinical encounter, or December 31, 2023.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean±SD, median (Q, Q3), and count (%). Between‐group comparisons were based on Fisher exact test, analysis of variance test, or Wilcoxon rank sum test, as appropriate. Cumulative incidence of HF hospitalization was assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis, and between‐group comparisons were based on log‐rank test. Cox regression analysis was used to assess the relationship between GDMT use and titration with risks of HF hospitalization and all‐cause mortality. We used Martingale residual plots to evaluate the functional form of continuous covariates in the Cox proportional hazards models. We plotted residuals against each continuous predictor, and nonlinearity was assessed visually by inspection of smoothed curves. We examined influential observations using Martingale residuals in conjunction with dfbeta diagnostics. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. The models were created using the following prespecified variables: GDMT score, study era (early versus late), cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED) at baseline encounter, LV systolic dysfunction (HFrEF versus HFmrEF), echocardiographic indices, demographic indices, CHD severity, and comorbidities. Covariates without statistically significant association with outcome were eliminated from the model, and P<0.01 was used as the criteria for a covariate to remain in the model. Separate models were created in the subgroup of patients with biomarker data (both NT‐proBNP [N terminal pro‐b‐type brain natriuretic peptide] and model for end‐stage liver disease excluding international normalized ratio), and patients with cardiac catheterization data. Subgroup analysis was performed in patients with versus without CIED.

In the primary analysis, outcomes were ascertained from the 1‐year encounter (time zero), and this creates a selection bias excluding patients who died or were lost to follow‐up prior to the 1‐year encounter, as well as underestimating the incidence of HF hospitalization for the patients hospitalized for HF within 1 year from the baseline encounter. To mitigate the study limitation, we performed an exploratory analysis based on the entire cohort of patients HFrEF and HFmrEF who had baseline assessment (including those without 1 year encounter) and ascertained the occurrence of outcomes using the baseline encounter as time zero (Figure S1).

We handled missing data using conditional imputation. All statistical analyses were performed with BlueSky Statistics software (version. 7.10; BlueSky Statistics LLC, Chicago, IL, USA), and JMP statistical software (version 17.1.0, JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, NC). P value <0.05 was statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Of 8369 patients with biventricular physiology and systemic LV, we identified 7981 (95%) patients who had both baseline and 1‐year follow‐up encounters (Figure S1). Of the 7981 patients, 778 (10%) had LVEF less than 50% at baseline encounter. Among the 778 patients, 327 had HFrEF, while 451 had HFmrEF. The underlying CHD diagnoses are shown in Table S1.

Table 2 shows a comparison of baseline clinical and hemodynamic characteristics of the patients with HFrEF using the patients with preserved LVEF (n=7203) as the reference group. The patients with HFrEF were older, more likely to have chronic kidney disease, and had higher MELD‐XI score and NT‐proBNP levels compared with the patients with HFmrEF (Table 2). Similarly, the HFrEF group also had worse left atrial function, larger LV volumes, worse right atrial function, worse right ventricular (RV) systolic function, and higher pulmonary artery mean pressure and wedge pressure (Table 2).

Of the 778 patients, 281 (36%) and 497 (64%) had baseline encounters in the early and late era, respectively. The late era cohort had higher baseline GDMT score (mean 2.6±1.4 versus 2.1±1.2, P<0.001), and higher ∆ GDMT score (0.84 [95% CI, 0.69–0.89] versus 0.43 [95% CI, 0.31–0.56], P<0.001) compared with the late era cohort.

GDMT and HF Hospitalization

Among the 778 patients with LVEF <50%, 183 (24%) were hospitalized for HF, yielding event rate of 4.9 per 100 patient years. The 15‐year cumulative incidence of HF hospitalization was significantly higher in the HFrEF group versus the HFmrEF group versus patients with preserved LVEF (66% versus 37% versus 18%, P<0.001) (Figure 3).

Kaplan–Meier curves comparing the cumulative incidence of HF hospitalization based on LVEF.

Compared with a control group of adults with congenital heart disease with preserved EF, the HFmrEF and HFrEF groups had higher cumulative incidence of HF hospitalization. EF indicates ejection fraction; HFmrEF, heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction; HFrEF, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; and LVEF, left ventricle ejection fraction.

Using the median baseline GDMT score to dichotomize the cohort, we observed that patients with lower GDMT score (GDMT score ≤2) had higher incidence of HF hospitalization compared with patients with baseline GDMT score >2 (54% versus 42% at 15 years, P=0.006) (Figure 4A). Similarly, patients without GDMT uptitration (∆ GDMT score ≤0) had higher incidence of HF hospitalization compared with those with GDMT uptitration (57% versus 34% at 15 years, P<0.001) (Figure 4B).

On subgroup analysis in patients with HFrEF, we observed a similar pattern of a higher incidence of HF hospitalization in patients with GDMT score ≤2 compared with others (73% versus 54% at 15 years, P=0.001), as well as in patients without GDMT uptitration compared with others (81% versus 43% at 15 years, P<0.001) (Figure 5A and 5B). Among patients in the HFmrEF group, patients without GDMT uptitration had a higher incidence of HF hospitalization (39% versus 22% at 15 years for ∆ GDMT score <1 versus ≥1, P=0.01) compared with patients with GDMT uptitration (Figure 4C and 4D).

Table 4 shows univariable models of correlates of HF hospitalization. On multivariable analysis, we observed an 19% reduction in the risk of HF hospitalization for every unit increase in baseline GDMT score (hazard ratio [HR] 0.81, 95% CI, 0.74–0.88, P<0.001), as well as a 30% reduction in the risk of HF hospitalization for every unit increase in ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.70, 95% CI, 0.61–0.79, P<0.001) (Table 5). We observed similar association between GDMT and HF hospitalization on subgroup analysis of patients with biomarkers data, as well as patients with cardiac catheterization data (Table 5).

GDMT, HF Hospitalization, and All‐Cause Mortality

Of the 778 patients, 218 (28%) died during follow‐up of 5.3 (1.6–9.2) years, yielding event rate of 5.2 per 100 patient years. HF hospitalization was associated with a 4‐fold increase in risk of mortality (adjusted HR 4.29, 95% CI, 1.68–9.77, P<0.001) on multivariable analysis. There was a 15% lower risk of mortality for every unit increase in baseline GDMT (adjusted HR 0.85, 95% CI, 0.76–0.94, P<0.001), as well as a 18% lower risk of mortality for every unit increase in ∆ GDMT (adjusted HR 0.82, 95% CI, 0.71–0.94, P<0.001), after adjustment for HF hospitalization, biventricular systolic function, demographic indices, and comorbidities (Table S2 and Table 6). Similar associations between HF hospitalization, GDMT score, and all‐cause mortality were observed in subgroup analysis restricted to patients with biomarker data, and patients with cardiac catheterization data (Table 6).

Subgroup Analysis Based on CIED Implantation

Of 778 patients, 107 (14%) had CIED implantation at baseline encounter, another 94 (12%) underwent CIED implantation during follow‐up. Overall, 201 (26%) had CIEDs.

Among the patients without CIED (n=577), baseline GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.83, 95% CI, 0.70–0.93, P<0.001) and ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.72, 95% CI, 0.61–0.84, P<0.001) were associated with HF hospitalization. Similarly, both higher baseline GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.84, 95% CI, 0.71–0.98, P=0.02) and higher ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.85, 95% CI, 0.73–0.97, P=0.006) were associated with lower all‐cause mortality.

Among the patients with CIED (n=201), higher baseline GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.86, 95% CI, 0.70–0.98, P=0.02) and higher ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.71, 95% CI, 0.52–0.94, P=0.002) were associated with lower risk HF hospitalization. However, only higher ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.85, 95% CI, 0.74–0.98, P=0.01) was associated with lower all‐cause mortality.

Subgroup Analysis in Patients With HFrEF

Among the patients with HFrEF (n=327), baseline GDMT score was 2.5±1.3 versus 2.2±1.1, ∆GDMT score was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.65–0.88), and 41% (135/327) had GDMT uptitration. Higher baseline GDMT (adjusted HR 0.78, 95% CI, 0.64–0.93, P<0.001) and ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.69, 95% CI, 0.57–0.91, P<0.001) were associated with HF hospitalization. Similarly, both higher baseline GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.76, 95% CI, 0.65–0.90, P<0.001) and higher ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.82, 95% CI, 0.68–0.95, P<0.001) were associated with lower all‐cause mortality.

Subgroup Analysis in Patients With HFmrEF

Among the patients with HFmrEF (n=451), baseline GDMT score was 2.1±1.3 versus 2.1±1.1, ∆GDMT score was 0.43 (95% CI, 0.36–0.51), and 27% (123/451) had GDMT uptitration. Higher baseline GDMT (adjusted HR 0.84, 95% CI, 0.69–0.99, P=0.03) and ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.78, 95% CI, 0.59–0.98, P=0.008) were associated with HF hospitalization. With regards to all‐cause mortality, baseline GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.83, 95% CI, 0.66–0.98, P=0.02) but not ∆ GDMT score (adjusted HR 0.86, 95% CI, 0.69–1.02, P=0.12) was associated with all‐cause mortality.

Exploratory Analysis Using Baseline Encounter as Time Zero

Overall, there were 840 patients with LVEF <50% who had baseline encounter (Figure S1). Among these 840 patients, 191 (23%) were hospitalized for HF, yielding event rate of 4.8 per 100 patient years from the baseline encounter. Higher baseline GDMT was assessed with lower risk of HF hospitalization (unadjusted HR 0.76, 95% CI, 0.66–0.87, P<0.001), and this association remained significant after adjustment for study era, CIED, echocardiographic indices, demographic indices, CHD severity, and comorbidities (adjusted HR 0.80, 95% CI, 0.73–0.87, P<0.001). In this cohort of 840 patients, 235 (28%) died, yielding event rate of 5.3 per 100 patient years from the baseline encounter. Similarly, higher baseline GDMT was assessed with lower risk of mortality (unadjusted HR 0.79, 95% CI, 0.68–0.89, P<0.001), and this association remained significant after adjustment for the same covariates (adjusted HR 0.85, 95% CI, 0.76–0.94, P<0.001).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we assessed the relationship between GDMT use and HF hospitalization and all‐cause mortality in adults with CHD. The main findings are as follows: (i) GDMT use at baseline encounter and GDMT uptitration during follow‐up were associated with lower risk of HF hospitalization. (ii) While HF hospitalizations were associated with all‐cause mortality, GDMT use at baseline and GDMT titration during follow‐up were associated with lower mortality. (iii) About 67% of the patients did not receive GDMT uptitration during follow‐up suggesting suboptimal therapy and a critical gap in care and provides an opportunity for improvement.

The association between HF and mortality in adults with CHD has been consistently demonstrated across different studies. 3 , 6 , 30 , 31 However, the clinical benefits (or lack thereof) of GDMT in the management of HF in adults with CHD has been inconsistent across studies, 10 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 and this is related to 2 main study limitations. First, there were significant differences in the definition of HF and inclusion criteria used in the different studies. 10 , 22 , 23 , 24 Some studies were based on patients with systemic LV, some were based on patients with systemic RV, while others included all patients with CHD regardless of ventricular anatomy or systolic function. 10 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Second, there was no standardized metrics for assessing intensity of HF therapy across studies and this confounds comparisons across studies because of the dose‐dependent relationship between the intensity of HF therapy and outcomes. 10 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 The current study overcame these limitations by restricting the inclusion criteria to patients with morphologic LV and HFrEF, and by assessing intensity of medical therapy using the GDMT score, albeit a modified version of the score.

In a recent study, Neijenhuis et al demonstrated that patients who were already on GDMT had a 3‐fold reduction in HF hospitalization following the initiation of sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i). 22 In that study, more than 60% of the patients were 2 HF medications, and more than 40% were on 3 HF medications prior to the initiation of SGLT2i, and this suggests a greater intensity of HF therapy in that study compared with our study. 22 Regardless, the reduction in the rate of HF hospitalization following initiation of SGLT2i suggests incremental improvement in outcomes following intensification of HF therapy. 22 This is consistent with previous studies showing clinical benefits following initiation of SGLT2i in patients with systemic RV, and systemic LV. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

The GDMT score has been applied in several studies of HF outcomes in patients with acquired heart disease. 21 , 32 , 33 In a study of more than 26 000 patients with HFrEF from acquired heart disease, Johansen et al demonstrated an association between higher GDMT score and lower risk of HF hospitalization and all‐cause mortality, similar to the findings in the current study. 32 In a different study, Polsinelli et al observed a significant variability in GDMT score across different hospitals, and that hospitals with higher GDMT scores had better clinical outcomes (lower risk of readmission for heart failure and mortality) and lower cost. 33 While we used a modified version of the GDMT score, the current study builds on these prior studies, and provides novel insights. It demonstrates, for the first time, that higher intensity of HF therapy at baseline encounter (higher baseline GDMT score) was associated with lower risk of HF hospitalization and mortality. Furthermore, uptitration of HF therapy during follow‐up (higher ∆ GDMT score) was associated with lower risk of HF hospitalization and mortality independent of baseline ∆ GDMT score, underscoring the dose dependent relationship between HF therapy and outcomes. Finally, the mean GDMT score was 2 (and this is lower than the median GDMT score of 4 reported in the acquired heart disease literature), and 67% of the patients did not have uptitration of GDMT during follow‐up, suggesting suboptimal HF therapy. These findings have important clinical implications.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The association between GDMT use and clinical outcomes observed in the current study, the lower baseline GDMT, and GDMT uptitration in only 33% of the cohort, suggests suboptimal therapy and needs improvement. One of the concerns regarding intensification of HF therapy includes risk of side effects and cost. While the current study did not assess side effects and tolerability of GDMT, we assume that therapy was well tolerated since none of the patients had a lower GDMT score at 1 year follow‐up. This is also consistent with previous studies showing that GDMT was well tolerated in adults with CHD. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 With regards to issue of cost, GDMT has been shown to reduce health care cost and hence beneficial to the patient and the health care system. 33

The prognostic role of the GDMT score in CHD provides a framework for future studies to address the following question. (i) What is the optimal intensity of HF treatment (GDMT score), and the optimal rate of uptitration (time to optimal GDMT score)? (ii) What is the impact of GDMT on cardiac remodeling, and the relationship between cardiac remodeling and outcomes? (iii) What is the role of GDMT in other CHD subgroups (patients with systemic RV, and patients with Fontan physiology)? There are limited data about outcomes of GDMT in patients with systemic right ventricle, and these studies have reported divergent results with some studies demonstrating improvement in functional status and right ventricular systolic function, 34 , 35 and others reporting no clinical benefit (no change in right ventricular systolic function, aerobic capacity, or biomarkers of heart failure). 36 These differences in outcomes highlight the complexity of the pathophysiology of heart failure in patients with systemic right ventricle. There is also a need for further studies to assess the role of GDMT in patients with Fontan palliation.

Limitations

The current study had some limitations. It is a retrospective single center study, and it is therefore prone to selection and ascertainment bias. We included patients with different CHD diagnoses with potential differences in the pathophysiology of heart failure, which in turn increases population heterogeneity. We were unable to ascertain adherence with prescribed medications due to the retrospective study design. While we used robust statistical methodology to adjust for confounders, there is a risk of model over‐fitting due to the number of covariates included in the model. We did not adjust for temporal changes in GDMT after the first year of follow‐up and the potential effects of co‐interventions such as pacemaker implantation (RV pacing versus cardiac resynchronization therapy) and valve interventions. However, we observed clinical benefits of GDMT in patients with versus without CIED, suggesting that the positive prognostic benefit associated with GDMT in the current study was due to GDMT and not confounders.

CONCLUSIONS

GDMT use at baseline and GDMT uptitration during follow‐up was associated with lower risk of HF hospitalization mortality and suggests a dose‐dependent relationship between the GDMT and outcomes in adults with CHD presenting with HFrEF. The median GDMT score was low, and most of the patients did not receive GDMT uptitration during follow‐up, suggesting suboptimal treatment, and opportunities for improvement. Collectively, these data support the use of GDMT in adults with CHD and HFrEF and provide empirical framework for further studies to address the clinical questions outlined in the study.
