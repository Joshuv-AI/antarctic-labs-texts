Cytonuclear evolution in fully heterotrophic plants: lifestyles and gene function determine scenarios
Authors: Xuelian Guo, Hanchen Wang, Dongliang Lin, Yajun Wang, Xiaohua Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence shows that full mycoheterotrophs and holoparasites often have reduced plastid genomes with rampant gene loss, elevated substitution rates, and deeply altered to conventional evolution in mitochondrial genomes, but mechanisms of cytonuclear evolution is unknown. Endoparasitic Sapria himalayana and mycoheterotrophic Gastrodia and Platanthera guangdongensis represent different heterotrophic types, providing a basis to illustrate cytonuclear evolution. Here, we focused on nuclear-encoded plastid / mitochondrial (N-pt / mt) -targeting protein complexes, including caseinolytic protease (ClpP), ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCo), oxidative phosphorylation system (OXPHOS), DNA recombination, replication, and repair (DNA-RRR) system, and pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins, to identify evolutionary drivers for cytonuclear interaction.ResultsThe severity of gene loss of N-pt PPR and pt-RRR genes was positively associated with increased degree of heterotrophy in full mycoheterotrophs and S. himalayana, while N-mt PPR and mt-RRR genes were retained. Substitution rates of organellar and nuclear genes encoding N-pt/mt subunits in protein complexes were evaluated, cytonuclear coevolution was identified in S. himalayana, whereas disproportionate rates of evolution were observed in the OXPHOS complex in full mycoheterotrophs, only slight accelerations in substitution rates were identified in N-mt genes of full mycoheterotrophs.ConclusionsNuclear compensatory evolution was identified in protein complexes encoded by plastid and N-pt genes. Selection shaping codon preferences, functional constraint, mt-RRR gene regulation, and post-transcriptional regulation of PPR genes all facilitate mito-nuclear evolution. Our study enriches our understanding of genomic coevolution scenarios in fully heterotrophic plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05702-4.

Background

The fitness of eukaryotic organisms depends on a close collaboration between organellar and nuclear genomes. Many multisubunit enzymatic complexes encoded by both nuclear and organellar genes play essential roles in a wide range of physiological and developmental functions, including plastid-localized heteromeric acetyl-coA carboxylase (ACCase), caseinolytic protease (ClpP), ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCo), and mitochondria-localized oxidative phosphorylation system (OXPHOS) [1–3]. Enzymes involved in the DNA recombination, replication, and repair (DNA-RRR) system also interact with the plastome and mitogenome and affect molecular mutation rates [4, 5]. Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins are localized in plastids or mitochondria, where they play crucial roles in the post-transcriptional regulation of organellar gene expression [6, 7]. The compensatory cytonuclear coevolution hypothesis has been proposed to reconcile the variation in nuclear genes that interact with mitochondrial and plastid genes, alleviating the dysfunction caused by changes in organellar genomes [8–14].

Heterotrophic plants are a remarkable guild of plants that survive by obtaining nutrients from hosts, relaxing the need for carbon fixation from photosynthesis. They are divided into two broad categories, mycoheterotrophs and parasites, and rely on the fungi or plants [15, 16]. The transition from an autotrophic to a heterotrophic lifestyle has had a dramatic impact on the plastid genome of heterotrophic plants [17, 18]. Evolutionary models of plastid genome degradation have been proposed, conforming to a largely linear progression of gene loss by functional classes that took place in parallel within and between different parasitic and mycoheterotrophic lineages [13, 19, 20]. However, most heterotrophic taxa still possess an almost complete core gene set in mitochondrial genome, and a number of horizontally transferred genes were identified in holoparasitic plants [21–25]. Moreover, mitogenomes of parasitic plants display extraordinary size divergence, high frequency of HGT, ordinary or increased substitution rates compared with that of autotrophic plants [5, 25–30]. It was proposed that the relaxation of selection in heterotrophic plants can lead to more rapid evolution in both organellar and nuclear genes [2, 13]. The dysfunction of DNA-RRR genes is a potential mechanism for rate accelerations across angiosperm organelle genomes [4, 31, 32]. In the holoparasitic plant Lophophytum (Balanophoraceae), rampant horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and subtle elevated rates of evolution were identified in the mitochondrial genome, however, no obvious acceleration in substitution rates was identified in nuclear-encoded mitochondria-targeting (N-mt) genes [5, 21]. The interpretation of these results is mixed, and mechanisms that maintain cytonuclear coadaptation across evolutionary time scales remain poorly understood in heterotrophic plants.

Sapria himalayana (Rafflesiaceae, Malpighiales) is a typical endoparasite with a nearly complete core gene set and a number of horizontally transferred genes in the mitogenome but with possible complete loss of the plastome [24, 33, 34]. Gastrodia (Orchidaceae, Asparagales) are fully mycoheterotrophic orchids with high substitution rates and divergence in genome size in both the plastome and mitogenome [18, 25, 28]. Platanthera guangdongensis and Pl. zijinensis (Orchidaceae) are sister species with full and partial mycoheterotrophic lifestyle, respectively [35]. These species are from different angiosperm lineages and represent different heterotrophic types, however, less is known about the evolution of nuclear genes that encode plastid-targeting (N-pt) or mitochondria-targeting (N-mt) proteins in the context of cytonuclear interactions in endoparasitic plants and full mycoheterotrophs. In this study, we focused on protein-complexes encoded by both nuclear and organellar genes to identify variation in nuclear genes of different lineages in light of divergent plastid and mitochondrial genome evolution and to estimate cytonuclear evolution. Furthermore, we investigated evolutionary factors driving cytonuclear evolution, and eventually compared the similarities and differences of cytonuclear evolution in different heterotrophic lineages, combined with results reported in Lophophytum [5].

Results

Degeneration of photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic functions in plastid associated with different heterotrophic lineages

Photosynthetic functions were lost in different heterotrophic lineages, and some plastid genes became pseudogenes and were not lost completely in partial and fully mycoheterotrophic Pl. zijinensis and Pl. guangdongensis (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1). Endoparasitic S. himalayana lost the plastome entirely. Genes involved in photosynthesis were lost and the photosynthetic machinery was inactivated in fully mycoheterotrophic and holoparasitic plants, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table 1). Nuclear genes encoding plastidic enzymes involved in starch synthesis were entirely lost in S. himalayana, Balanophora subcupularis, and B. fungosa var. globosa but were present in fully mycoheterotrophic orchids (Supplementary Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table 2). Genes encoding ferredoxin-dependent glutamate synthase (Fd-GOGAT) and regulating the Ca2+ concentration in the cytoplasm, affecting stomatal closure signaling in arid conditions, were present in fully mycoheterotrophic Pl. guangdongensis and partially Pl. zijinensis but were lost in other fully mycoheterotrophic and holoparasitic plants (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Plastid rbcL and nuclear rbcS genes encoding the RuBiSco complex were all lost in fully mycoheterotrophic orchids and endoparasitic S. himalayana; however, they were present in partial mycoheterotrophic Pl. zijinensis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Two TOC/TIC genes were absent in fully mycoheterotrophic Gastrodia and Pl. guangdongensis (Supplementary Fig. 3). Except in Pl. guangdongensis, N-pt genes responsible for essential plastid nonphotosynthetic functions [36–38] were largely present in initial, partial, and full mycoheterotrophs and were expressed in different tissues of G. menghaiensis (Supplementary Table 4), indicating that orchid plastids retained important functions for development. In contrast, plastid and N-pt genes involved in photosynthetic functions were entirely lost in S. himalayana (Supplementary Fig. 1, 2). N-pt genes encoding enzymes related to aromatic amino acids and lysine biosynthesis were expressed in floral tissues of S. himalayana (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 5).

Conservation of the mitogenome and OXPHOS complex among heterotrophic lineages

A nearly complete core gene set was retained in the mitogenomes of S. himalayana, Gastrodia, and Platanthera in a comparison with mitogenomes of autotrophic plants (Supplementary Fig. 5). Fourteen horizontally transferred genes were identified in S. himalayana, and 12 foreign gene replaced native mitochondrial genes (Supplementary Fig. 5). Some horizontally transferred genes were expressed in different floral tissues of S. himalayana (Supplementary Table 6). Importantly, N-mt genes involved in OXPHOS systems were mostly present in S. himalayana and Gastrodia and they were expressed in different tissues of these species (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 7, 8). Except for abundant horizontally transferred genes in S. himalayana, nuclear and mitochondrial genes belonging to OXPHOS systems were similar to those in autotrophic plants.

Genome-wide analysis of PPR genes and their essential roles in organellar RNA metabolism

Compared with those in autotrophic plants, there were fewer PPR genes in holoheterotrophic plants, especially in B. subcupularis (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Plastid-localized PPR (pt-PPR) genes involved in plastid biogenesis and RNA metabolism were largely lost, including genes involved in photosynthesis, plastid-encoded RNA polymerase (PEP) complex, plastidial ribosome biogenesis, or plastidial RNA editing, splicing, stabilization, and retrograde signaling from the chloroplast to nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 7B; Supplementary Table 9). The number of PPR genes responsible for mitochondrial ribosome biogenesis and RNA metabolism was decreased but these genes were not completely lost in fully heterotrophic plants (Supplementary Fig. 7B; Supplementary Table 9). The numbers of predicted editing sites were reduced in both foreign and native genes in S. himalayana and L. mirabile (Balanophoraceae) compared with estimates in closely related species, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8). The severity of pt-PPR loss was consistent with the extent of reduction of plastomes in fully mycoheterotrophic and holoparasitic plants, and decreasing trend in number of predicted editing sites of mitochondrial genes was associated with the decrease of mitochondria-localized PPR (mt-PPR) genes (Supplementary Fig. 7B, 8).

Evolutionary rate covariation of plastid protein complexes in heterotrophic lineages

Rates of evolution of plastid and N-pt genes involved in the ClpP complex showed a positive correlation (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. 9), and slight evolutionary rate covariation was also found in the ACCase complex, matK partition, and ycf partition, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 10). Highly elevated substitution rates in all single-gene and concatenated analyses were identified in N-pt genes involved in the TOC/TIC pathway in S. himalayana (Fig. 1B). However, obvious accelerations in substitution rates of N-pt genes were not identified in the TOC/TIC pathway among initial, partial, and full mycoheterotrophs (Fig. 1C). Substitution rates of DNA-RRR genes across heterotrophic lineages

In total, 33 DNA-RRR genes reported to function in plastid, mitochondria, or both organelles were evaluated in different heterotrophic lineages. A prominent bias towards losses of plastid-localized DNA-RRR (pt-RRR) genes was detected in fully mycoheterotrophic and holoparasitic plants, especially in S. himalayana (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table 10). Mitochondria or dual-targeted DNA-RRR genes were not lost in fully mycoheterotrophic or holoparasitic plants, consistent with the nearly complete core gene set in mitogenomes of different heterotrophic lineages (Supplementary Fig. 5). DNA-RRR genes were expressed in different tissues of S. himalayana and G. menghaiensis (Supplementary Tables 11, 12). Only one pt-RRR gene, ARP, was identified in S. himalayana, and its dN was higher than that of ARP in closely related species (Fig. 4B). Marked accelerations in substitution rates of mitochondria and dual-targeted DNA-RRR genes were found in S. himalayana compared with those in closely related species (Fig. 4B). Cytonuclear evolution

The dN and dS values of mitochondrial genes were two times higher in S. himalayana than in closely related autotrophic plants (Fig. 5A). Notably, dN and dS values of 45 nuclear encoded non-organellar-targeting genes (NO genes) in S. himalayana were also two times higher than those of closely related autotrophic plants. There were some differences in dN and dS values between fully mycoheterotrophic and autotrophic orchids; however, the difference was not greater than two-fold (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. 16). dN and dS values of N-pt and N-mt genes involved in RuBisCO, OXPHOS, and DNA-RRR systems in S. himalayana were about two times higher than that of closely related autotrophic plants (Supplementary Fig. 15). More HGTs were identified in L. mirabile than in S. himalayana (Fig. 5C, D). However, substitution rates were substantially higher in S. himalayana than in L. mirabile (Fig. 5E). Discussion

Lineage-specific cytonuclear interactions in heterotrophic plants

Cytonuclear interactions were identified in organellar complexes encoded by organellar and N-mt or N-pt genes (Fig. 5E). Nuclear compensatory evolution, relaxed selection, organellar DNA-RRR systems, and accessory proteins related to complex integrity caused by mutational erosion of the organellar genomes all contribute to cytonuclear interactions in plants [11, 39]. The unique endoparasitic lifestyle enables access to nutrients from the host in S. himalayana, permitting substantial changes in rates of evolution of organellar and nuclear genes (genomes); however, the indispensable OXPHOS function in mitochondria of holoparasitic plants probably promoted cooperation between mitochondrial and N-mt genes. Consistent with this, cytonuclear coevolution in the OXPHOS complex was identified in S. himalayana. Combined with protein sequence similarity, ENC-plot and RSCU analyses indicated that selection acted on protein sequences of horizontally transferred mitochondrial genes shaped codon preferences of these genes in S. himalayana and L. mirabile (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Fig. 13).

More than two-thirds of mitochondrial genes in mitochondrion of L. mirabile were HGTs. In addition, native mitochondrial genes in L. mirabile were more similar to genes in its host than to genes in closely related species (Fig. 3B). We speculated that native genes of L. mirabile took place codon usage bias in order to cooperate well with HGT-acquired genes. Dysfunction of DNA-RRR genes has been suggested to cause highly elevated nucleotide substitution rates and structural variation in genomes [4, 32, 40, 41]. The substantial bias towards losses of pt-RRR genes and good ERC between some mitochondrial genes and mt-RRR genes in S. himalayana and Gastrodia (Supplementary Tables 7, 8) support the hypothesis that the selection pressure on pt-RRR genes was relaxed during plastid genome reduction and cascading the relaxation of selection pressure on pt-RRR genes.

Mycoheterotrophs did not display a sharp (i.e., two-fold) acceleration in substitution rates of plastid and mitochondrial genes involved in OXPHOS and DNA-RRR systems, and this may be explained by two scenarios. First, mycoheterotrophs could not directly and quickly obtain adequate nutrients from the host, and their growth still need these protein complexes encoded by organellar and nuclear genes to fulfill, and the vital functions of above protein complexes prevented the divergence of N-pt genes (Supplementary Fig. 16). Compared with Gastrodia, Pl. guangdongensis is a recently evolved fully mycoheterotrophic orchid [35] and has more gene loss in OXPHOS complex (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, N-mt genes of Pl. guangdongensis have similar evolutionary rates compared to close autotrophic species. It seems that N-mt genes were still under functional constraints in recently evolved full mycoheterotrophs and the timescale from autotrophy to heterotrophy also affects the evolutionary rates of N-mt genes. Besides, our predictions for functional constraint were not totally at odds with the nuclear compensatory evolution in mycoheterotrophs. Here, positive correlations between mitochondrial and N-mt genes in complexes IV and V were identified (Figs. 2B and 5E), suggesting nuclear compensation might make certain contributions to mito-nuclear coevolution of subunits IV and V in mycoheterotrophs. Similar result has been reported in Silene [42]. Second, the nearly complete gene set of DNA-RRR would alleviate variation in organellar genes and allow them to cooperate with N-pt genes.

Signatures and cascading effects of the loss of photosynthesis in heterotrophic taxa

Rampant gene loss and elevated substitution rates were often reported in reduced plastid genomes of full mycoheterotrophs and holoparasites, and deeply altered to conventional evolution in mitochondrial genomes were also reported [8, 10, 13, 19, 21, 26, 39]. Evolutionary pressure were more extreme in S. himalayana than in holoparasitic Lophophytum [5] and full mycoheterotrophs (Fig. 5E). It is likely that the effect of endoparasitism on evolutionary rates was particularly strong. In Bilateria, mitogenomic evolutionary rates were highest in endoparasitic lineages, and the endoparasitic lifestyle had a greater effect on rates of evolution than those of other factors [43]. In contrast, mycoheterotrophs absorb limited fungus‐derived carbon and other nutrients; consequently, the plastids of these taxa retain essential nonphotosynthetic functions. In the nuclear genome, the extent of gene loss in full mycoheterotrophs was not substantially greater than that of holoparasites [24, 34, 44–47].

Starch is a major form of stored carbohydrates and is synthesized in the plastid of plants. Genes involved in starch biosynthesis were absent in S. himalayana, B. subcupularis, and B. fungosa var. globosa (Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with previous results based on histological analyses in Rafflesia patma [48]. Holoparasitic plants may utilize sucrose directly. Genes involved in starch biosynthesis were present in mycoheterotrophic orchids (Supplementary Fig. 2). Fully mycoheterotrophic orchids could not directly absorb abundant sucrose for reproductive growth; thus, the storage of nutrients in advance was necessary for reproductive success (the period from the visible inflorescence to flowering and fruiting is roughly one month).

The role of stomata is closely related to photosynthesis, nutrient supply, and plant resistance. Genes that regulate stomatal closure in response to drought via Ca2+ dynamics were lost in fully holoheterotrophic plants (Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with the loss of stomata. Plants in the family Rafflesiaceae, root parasites, gain nutrients directly through their haustorium, which connects directly to the host phloem. Complete dependence on host makes endoparasitic plants under a host-parasite arms-race, which might prompt the evolutionary rate of genes responsible for energy metabolism. This is why the substitution rates of genes in S. himalayana increased faster than that of Gastrodia when they were compared with their closely related species, respectively.

Conclusion

The switch in trophic mode renders heterotrophic plants an ideal system for understanding the processes underlying genome evolution and cytonuclear coevolution. Several evolutionary factors including nuclear compensatory evolution, functional constraint, and selection shaping codon preference were estimated in protein complexes encoded by plastid or mitochondrion and nuclear genomes. The extent of host dependence, parasitic lifestyles, and the timescale from autotrophy to heterotrophy drove changes, to various extents, in genome composition and evolutionary rates. Findings in the study have implications for theories detailing vital roles for cytonuclear coevolution in broad evolutionary processes such as key transition in lifestyles and adaptation to local environments or metabolic needs.

Methods

Heterotrophic plants in different angiosperm lineages

This study focused on cytonuclear interactions in holoheterotrophic plants. Sapria himalayana, G. elata, G. menghaiensis, Pl. guangdongensis, Pl. zijinensis, and L. mirabile represent endoparasitic, mycoheterotrophic, and root holoparasitic plants that evolved independently from different angiosperm lineages (Table 1). The estimated divergence time between the subfamily Orchidoideae (Platanthera) and subfamily Epidendroideae (Gastrodia) is approximately 60.38 Ma, while the divergence time between Pl. zijinensis and Pl. guangdongensis was estimated at approximately 11.63 Ma [35]. Pl. zijinensis, Pl. guangdongensis, and Gastrodia represent different stages of plastid degradation from the initial stage to extreme plastome reduction [19]. Due to the lack of nuclear genome data of L. mirabile, we evaluated the nuclear genomes of the closely related B. subcupularis and B. fungosa var. globosa. The partial heterotrophic S. parasitica var. graciliflora was evaluated for comparison. Additionally, various closely related autotrophic plants were chosen for comparisons among heterotrophic lineages (Supplementary Table 15).

Identification and alignments of homologous genes

Homologs of organellar and nuclear genes involved in plastid and mitochondrial protein complexes (ACCase, ClpP, RuBisCo, and OXPHOS) and DNA-RRR systems were identified in each species. Additionally, plant PPR and photosynthesis-related gene sets were identified. Homologs of these genes in Arabidopsis thaliana were used as queries, BLASTP with e-value ≤ 1e − 5, Rcciprocal Blast (e-value ≤ 1e − 5) and Find Best Homology tools in TBtools [49] were conducted to identify homologs in each species examined in the study. As a control, 45 single- or low-copy NO genes reported in a previous study were selected [50].

For each candidate gene, homologs in each species were extracted. Protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.471) [51], with default parameters. PAL2NAL [52] was then used to generate the corresponding CDS alignment, and trimAL [53] was used to remove ambiguous sites in the alignment with the parameter -gt 0.8. Concatenated alignments of mitochondrial OXPHOS subunit genes in various species and mt-RRR genes were generated.

Gene family clustering, sequence alignment, and phylogenetic inference

OrthoFinder (v2.5.2) [54] was used to group the orthogroups of S. himalayana and 12 representative eudicot species (Supplementary Table S10). Likewise, the orthogroups of G. elata, G. menghaiensis, Pl. guangdongensis, Pl. zijinensis, and seven representative monocot species were identified (Supplementary Table 16). Because S. himalayana belongs to the core eudicots and Gastrodia and Platanthera are monocots, different data sets were used for phylogenetic analyses. Prior to identifying orthogroups, proteins corresponding to primary transcripts in each of the selected plants were obtained. Amino acid sequences of each single-copy orthologue were aligned using MUSCLE (v3.8) [55], with default parameters. A CDS alignment was generated and ambiguous sites in the alignment were pruned as described in the previous section. Concatenated alignments of all single-copy genes were generated using PhyloSuite (v1.1.15) [56]. Then, the best model parameters were predicted using IQ-TREE2 [57]. Phylogenetic analyses (Supplementary Fig. 17) with 1000 bootstrap replicates were conducted using RAxML v.8.2.11 [58].

Estimation of evolutionary rates

Substitution rates of each organellar, N-pt, and N-mt gene in plastid and mitochondrial protein complexes and DNA-RRR systems were estimated based on individual and concatenated alignments. Branch-model tests implemented in PAML [59] were employed to evaluate synonymous (dS) and nonsynonymous (dN) substitution rates. Horizontally acquired mitochondrial genes in S. himalayana were placed in the trees considering their origin. Next, root-to-tip substitution rates were calculated using the R package ape considering the last common ancestor in species tree constructed in the previous section as the root. Substitution rates were evaluated in comparison with those in autotrophic plants; if substitution rates of genes in heterotrophic plants exceed that of their autotrophic relatives but not twice the latter, we consider it a slight increase.

Evolutionary rate covariation (ERC) analysis

An ERC analysis provides the best way to verify that proteins in a complex jointly encoded by nuclear and organellar genes are coevolving [60]. Organellar and nuclear genes involved in the ClpP and OXPHOS complexes were evaluated by an ERC analysis, and evolutionary correlations between mt-RRR genes and mitochondrial genes were also calculated. The ERC analysis was performed following the pipeline described by Forsythe et al. [60]. Thus, gene trees based on organelle, N-pt, and N-mt genes involved in ClpP and OXPHOS complexes and DNA-RRR systems were inferred using IQ-TREE2 [57] with ultrafast bootstrap approximation to evaluate node support (1,000 pseudo-replicates). Gene trees based on concatenated alignments of each mitochondrial OXPHOS subunit were also constructed. The cumulative lengths of branches from the species tips to the node representing the most recent common ancestor of all selected species were calculated using the root-to-tip method, allowing for a phylogeny-based measurement of nucleotide substitutions in each lineage.

A correlation analysis of the plastid ClpP1 gene against each nuclear gene involved in the ClpP complex was conducted. Additionally, pairwise analyses of concatenated mitochondrial genes against each nuclear gene involved in each OXPHOS complex subunit as well as mt-RRR genes against each mitochondrial gene were also performed. For each pairwise comparison, Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated.

Functional annotation

The identified plant PPR and photosynthesis-related gene sets were further annotated using the Mercator web tool [61]. Based on MapMan categories, we compared retained and lost functions among different holoheterotrophic lineages.

Identification of selection pressure shaping codon preferences in HGT genes in holoparasites

The protein sequence similarities between mitochondrial protein-coding genes in S. himalayana and closely related species (Manihot esculenta and Ricinus communis) and its host (Tetrastigma hemsleyanum) were calculated using TBtools with the protein pairwise similarity matrix plugin [49]. Protein sequence similarities of mitochondrial protein-coding genes in L. mirabile and closely related species (Erythropalum scandens) and its host (Acacia ligulata) were also calculated. Paired rank-sum tests were used to examine differences between two groups using R.

CodonW (v1.4.2) [62] was used to calculate the effective number of codons (ENC) and GC3 with default settings. ENC values indicate the capacity for codons to encode amino acids. GC3 represents GC content at the third codon positions. Then, based on ENC values (y-axis) and GC3 (x-axis), a two-dimensional scatter plot was created using the ggplot2 R package, and it compares observed ENC against expected ENC (expENC) for different GC3. Points falling above or on the curve indicate that codon usage is random. Conversely, points below the curve indicate mutation pressure is the sole force acting on third-position bases in a given sequence. The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of mitochondrial protein-coding genes was also calculated using CodonW.

Prediction of RNA editing

RNA editing sites of mitochondrial protein-coding genes were predicted using the online tool PREPACT3 (http://www.prepact.de/prepact-main.php) with a cutoff value of 0.05, and the codons containing editing sites were manually removed from the alignments.
