Multivariate phenotyping reveals thermoregulatory patterns in crossbred cattle
Authors: Robson Mateus Freitas Silveira, Isadora Emanoela Pereira Costa Andrade, Luciano Barreto-Mendes, Larissa de Melo Coelho, Concepta McManus, Luiz Paulo Fávero, Débora Andrea Evangelista Façanha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving the adaptation of beef cattle production to climate change requires crossbreeding strategies that enhance the ability of animals to maintain thermal stability under hot conditions. The objective of this exploratory study was to assess how coat characteristics are associated with rectal-temperature variation and to identify thermoregulatory patterns in Red Angus × Nelore crossbred cattle raised under tropical conditions. Thirty clinically healthy crossbred bulls from two genetic compositions (½ Red Angus × ½ Nelore and ¾ Red Angus × ¼ Nelore) were evaluated at 210 and 360 days of age. Morphological, physiological, and environmental variables were recorded, including coat thickness, hair length, hair quantity, hair density, and rectal temperatures measured in the morning and afternoon. Univariate comparisons did not detect differences (p > 0.05) between the genetic groups in the evaluated coat traits or rectal temperatures, indicating substantial overlap between genetic compositions. In an exploratory logistic model developed to discriminate the two genetic groups, rectal temperature was positively associated with group classification [odds ratio (OR) = 1.41, p = 0.023], whereas hair length showed a negative association (OR = 0.53, p < 0.001), indicating that morphological and physiological traits jointly contributed to the observed differentiation. Correlation analysis showed that thicker coats tended to contain fewer and less densely distributed hairs, whereas hair quantity and density were strongly and positively associated, suggesting compensatory coat architectures. The exploratory clustering solutions suggested four candidate coat-related configurations: (i) short, dense coats and (ii) thick but less dense coats, both associated with lower daily rectal-temperature variation; and (iii) intermediate coats and (iv) long, dense coats, associated with greater thermal fluctuation. Although some overlap among clusters was observed, these configurations provide an exploratory basis for understanding how combinations of coat characteristics may influence thermoregulatory responses. The findings indicate that thermal regulation in crossbred cattle is better understood through combinations of morphological traits than through isolated characteristics. Future studies with larger, independent populations and additional physiological and productive measurements are needed to confirm the consistency and broader applicability of these patterns.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Exploratory phenotyping, Coat traits, Heat dissipation, Tropical climate, Resilience

Introduction

The growing global demand for beef imposes pressure on this type of system, particularly in tropical regions where excessive heat threatens productivity (Silveira et al. 2026). This challenge is exacerbated by the climate change crisis, which increases the frequency and intensity of heat stress events. Brazil, the world’s largest beef exporter and second-largest producer (FAO 2023), addresses this challenge by, among other strategies, combining high-yield breeds with lineages adapted to tropical climate.

Crossbreeding between taurine and zebuine cattle is widely used to capture heterosis for growth and carcass quality without compromising their capacity of quickly dissipating heat in case of a heat wave, i.e. increasing their heat resilience, hereby defined as the ability to maintain thermal homeostasis and physiological stability under acute heat stress in tropical environments (Sejian et al. 2018). Nevertheless, the performance of crossbreeds can be limited by the lower heat tolerance of European breeds under hot climates (Michael et al. 2022). The heritability of thermoregulatory traits, such as rectal temperature, is moderate; however, their phenotypic expression is strongly modulated by environment and by animal morphology (Hernandez et al. 2024).

Heat dissipation is driven primarily by evaporation (sweating and panting) and, to a lesser extent, by conduction and radiation. Zebu (Bos indicus) breeds typical of the tropics, such as Nelore (Bos taurus indicus), display body attributes that favor heat transfer shorter and less dense coats, higher relative surface area, and larger sweat glands (Silveira et al. 2021). In contrast, taurine breeds from temperate climates generally have thicker, more compact coats, higher metabolic rates, and reduced heat dissipation capacity, making them more susceptible to heat stress in hot environments (Brown-Brandl, 2018; Camargo et al. 2023). For instance, Nelore cattle exhibit significantly finer coats and higher sweat-gland density than 5/8 Bos taurus × 3/8 Bos indicus crosses (Barreto et al. 2024).

Extensive evidence in ruminant thermal biology indicates that coat characteristics—such as length, thickness, density, pigmentation, and skin features—play a central role in heat exchange and thermoregulatory efficiency in production animals raised in hot environments. However, despite this consolidated body of knowledge, relatively little is known about how genetic recombination in crossbred animals reshapes coat phenotypes and modifies their functional relevance for heat dissipation under tropical and semi-arid conditions. Crossbreeding programs can generate wide phenotypic variability in coat traits, potentially producing animals with contrasting thermoregulatory capacities even within the same production system (Brown-Brandl 2018; Silveira et al. 2021; Hernandez et al. 2024). Based on this framework, we hypothesized that crossbred animals exhibit distinct coat morphologies arising from genetic recombination and that this phenotypic variability differentially modulates sensible and latent heat dissipation, resulting in measurable differences in core temperature stability and overall thermoregulatory efficiency under tropical conditions.

Against this backdrop, multivariate approaches may enable the concomitant assessment of physiological, environmental, and genetic indicators in order to comprehensively assess heat tolerance. Multivariate models have been used to predict thermal states in cattle by integrating environmental variables, such as air temperature, relative humidity, the temperature–humidity index, and the black globe humidity index, with physiological responses (Silveira et al. 2023; da Silva et al. 2025). Previous studies have shown that multivariate approaches can integrate physiological, morphological, environmental, and genetic information to characterize thermal responses in livestock (Silveira et al. 2024). By examining multiple traits simultaneously, these approaches complement traditional univariate analyses and may reveal thermoregulatory patterns that would otherwise remain obscured. Integrating physiological responses, genetic composition, and coat characteristics can provide a more comprehensive understanding of heat adaptation and may inform future phenotypic screening, breeding, and management strategies in tropical production systems.

In this context, we used an exploratory multivariate framework to assess how coat characteristics are associated with heat dissipation and rectal-temperature stability in Red Angus × Nelore crossbred cattle raised under tropical conditions. By integrating low-cost morphophysiological measurements with dimensionality-reduction and clustering techniques, this approach was designed to identify coat-related thermoregulatory patterns and to investigate compensatory relationships among morphological and physiological traits. The resulting patterns provide a biologically grounded basis for understanding thermal responses in crossbred cattle and may support future research on phenotypic screening and heat-stress management after validation in larger, independent populations. The Red Angus × Nelore cross was selected because it combines a taurine breed with high productive potential but comparatively lower heat tolerance with Nelore, a zebuine breed widely recognized for tropical adaptation. The two genetic compositions evaluated herein therefore provide a biologically relevant gradient of taurine and zebuine inheritance for exploring coat-related thermoregulatory variation under tropical conditions.

Materials and methods

This study combined experimental and computational approaches to investigate heat-adaptation traits in crossbred cattle. Data were collected from thirty Red Angus × Nelore bulls reared under tropical feedlot conditions in northeastern Brazil, including morphological (coat traits), physiological (rectal temperature), and environmental variables.

Further detail on the description of the experimental conditions, data collection and choices of analyses is provided below.

Study site, animals, and management conditions

The trial was carried out on a commercial farm in Ceará-Mirim, Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil (05°38′02″ S; 35°25′33″ W). The regional climate is hot semi-arid, classified as BSh according to the Köppen climate classification, with a rainy season typically occurring between March and July (Alvares et al. 2013).

A total of 30 clinically healthy bulls from two genetic groups (n = 15 each) [(½ Red Angus × ½ Nelore (NEL) and ¾ Red Angus (RED) × ¼ Nelore] were used. Dams were of similar age, hormonally synchronized, and artificially inseminated with conventional semen; all calves were born within a two-day interval. During the cow–calf phase, calves stayed with their dams under natural suckling and rotational grazing on Brachiaria grass (Brachiaria brizantha) and Mombasa grass (Panicum maximum) until approximately seven months of age, when they were weaned and moved directly to the feedlot, remaining there until slaughter at 15 months.

The finishing diet included elephant grass, Mombasa silage, barley, cottonseed, and a protein–mineral supplement. Throughout finishing, animals received a ration formulated to 12% crude protein, offered ad libitum in two daily meals, with free access to water. The diet met nutritional requirements for growing/finishing beef cattle targeting 1.3–1.6 kg/day average daily gain, according to NRC (1996). In both phases (cow–calf and finishing), the genetic groups were kept under identical housing and feeding management conditions.

Environmental, physiological, and hair-coat measurements

The present study considered two evaluations conducted when the animals were 210 and 360 days old. The first evaluation was performed in January, at approximately 210 days of age, corresponding to the weaning period, whereas the second was performed in June, at 360 days of age, during the feedlot-finishing phase. This design allowed changes in hair-coat characteristics and physiological responses between the two evaluation periods to be assessed.

On each evaluation date, meteorological conditions inside the housing facility were recorded at 10:00 and 15:00 h. The measured variables included air temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), and black-globe temperature (°C). The Radiant Heat Load (RHL; W m⁻²) was calculated from black-globe temperature according to Silva (2008), using the Stefan–Boltzmann equation: RHL = σ × (Tbg + 273.15)⁴, where σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant (5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴) and Tbg is black-globe temperature (°C). Rectal temperature was measured at the same times using a digital clinical thermometer inserted directly into the rectum of each animal.

Hair-coat morphological characteristics were assessed on the same evaluation dates at a standardized anatomical site on the flank, approximately 20 cm ventral to the vertebral column. Hair-coat thickness was measured using a caliper inserted perpendicularly to the epidermal surface. At the same anatomical site, a hair sample was removed using duck-bill pliers, placed in an individually labeled plastic bag, and transported to the laboratory.

In the laboratory, hair quantity was determined as the raw number of hairs counted in each collected sample. Numerical hair density was then calculated by standardizing the raw hair count according to the sampled skin area and was expressed as hairs cm⁻². In addition, the ten longest hairs from each sample were visually selected and individually measured using a caliper. The arithmetic mean of these measurements was used to determine mean hair length, following the procedure described by Udo (1978).

Statistical analyses

To ensure a clear, reproducible, and interpretable analytical workflow, the statistical strategy followed a structured pipeline integrating univariate inference, dimensionality reduction, exploratory genetic-group discrimination, and unsupervised clustering to investigate coat-related thermoregulatory patterns (Fig. 1). First, inclusion and exclusion criteria and the study time window were defined. Treatment effects were quantified as within-animal changes over time (Δ). For each variable, deltas were calculated as the difference between the last (360 days) and first (210 days) measurements, following recommendations for longitudinal and repeated-measures designs (Festing 2006). For body temperature, daily variation (ΔT) was defined as the difference between mean afternoon and morning rectal temperatures.

Exploratory analytical workflow used to investigate coat-related thermoregulatory patterns in crossbred cattle

Prior to analysis, inferential assumptions were systematically evaluated. Univariate outliers were screened using boxplots, z-scores (|z| > 3), and the interquartile-range criterion. Observations below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR were considered potential outliers. Multivariate outliers were assessed using Mahalanobis distance with multiple-testing correction. Normality was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots, while homogeneity of variances between groups was assessed using Levene’s test.

Exploratory visualization and hypothesis testing were then performed. Differences between genetic groups in morphophysiological traits and temperature variables were evaluated using independent-samples Student’s t-tests. Tested variables included Δ coat thickness, Δ hair length, Δ hair quantity, Δ hair density, and morning and afternoon rectal temperatures. Variable distributions were visualized using violin plots generated with Seaborn and Matplotlib, with complementary interactive graphics produced using Plotly.

Linear associations among morphophysiological traits were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients, considering coat traits measured at 360 days of age and daily temperature variation (ΔT). This step provided an initial assessment of pairwise relationships and potential compensatory mechanisms among traits.

To uncover latent structures associated with thermoregulatory adaptation and to reduce data dimensionality, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted. Sampling adequacy was explored using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) statistic (> 0.55) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001). Given the limited sample size, the EFA was interpreted as an exploratory dimensionality-reduction procedure rather than as confirmatory evidence of a stable latent structure. Variables exhibited communalities > 0.60, indicating that, within this exploratory solution, a substantial proportion of their variance was represented by the retained factors. Factor extraction was performed using exploratory factor analysis, and factors were subsequently rotated to improve interpretability. Biological interpretation of the latent constructs was based on the rotated component (loading) matrix, allowing correlated morphophysiological variables to be exploratively summarized into components with potential biological relevance to thermoregulatory mechanisms.

In parallel, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to standardized adult (data collected at 360 days) coat traits and daily temperature variation to support multivariate visualization and interpretation. PCA scores were subsequently used as the input space for unsupervised clustering.

To explore discrimination between the two genetic groups, binary logistic regression models were fitted within a generalized linear modeling framework using a binomial distribution and a logit link function. Let Yi denote the binary response for animal i, with Yi = 1 for ¾ Red Angus × ¼ Nelore and Yi = 0 for ½ Red Angus × ½ Nelore, and let πi = P (Yi = 1 | Xi) represent the probability of classification into the target group given the predictor vector Xi. Three alternative predictor sets were examined: (i) longitudinal changes in coat and physiological variables, including Δ thickness, Δ length, Δ quantity, Δ density, and Δ rectal temperature; (ii) latent factor scores derived from exploratory factor analysis; and (iii) the original environmental, physiological, and coat-related variables. Within each predictor set, stepwise variable selection using a significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied exclusively as an exploratory screening procedure to identify candidate multivariable patterns. Given the small sample size and the consequent risk of model overfitting, these models were not intended for prediction, clinical classification, or confirmatory inference. Model discrimination was summarized using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). AUC values close to 0.5 indicate discrimination similar to chance, whereas values approaching 1 indicate stronger apparent discrimination. The reported AUC values represent apparent in-sample discrimination rather than validated predictive performance, and regression coefficients and odds ratios should therefore be interpreted only as hypothesis-generating associations. Model coefficients were expressed as odds ratios (OR), calculated as OR = exp(β).

To identify exploratory coat-related thermoregulatory patterns beyond genetic-group averages, unsupervised clustering analyses were conducted in PCA space. Two complementary methods were applied: agglomerative hierarchical clustering using Ward’s linkage with Euclidean distance, and K-means clustering.

The two methods were applied to explore the sensitivity of the clustering structure to different partitioning procedures. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering captures nested data structure, whereas K-means seeks to minimize within-cluster dispersion. Agreement between the resulting solutions was evaluated descriptively, without assuming equivalence between their cluster labels.

Cluster quality was assessed using the Silhouette Score, which ranges from − 1 to + 1, with higher values indicating better cluster separation. For K-means, WCSS was obtained from the inertia attribute, while for hierarchical clustering it was computed using Euclidean distances. Prior to clustering, all variables were standardized using z-score transformation. After clustering, cluster-wise means were calculated on the original data scale. Finally, the relative contribution of each variable within each cluster was classified as Very Low, Low, Moderate, or High based on variable-specific quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3) estimated from the global empirical distribution. Cluster means were categorized as Very Low (≤ Q1), Low (Q1–Q2], Moderate (Q2–Q3], or High (> Q3).

Results and discussion

No statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) were detected between genetic groups for any of the evaluated variables (Δ coat thickness, Δ hair length, Δ hair amount, Δ hair density, or mean rectal temperature) (Table 1; Fig. 2). Accordingly, under the univariate analytical framework, no evidence of between-group differences was detected for the evaluated morphophysiological traits.

Principal Component Analysis provided a coherent multivariate picture. In both crossbreds, the first two axes explained ~ 63–64% of the variance (Axis 1 ≈ 33–34%; Axis 2 ≈ 29–30%; Fig. 4). Some differentiation between crossbreds was observed along PC2. In ½ Nelore × ½ Angus (Fig. 4, left panel), hair length loaded strongly and positively, whereas rectal temperature (RT) loaded negatively, suggesting a decoupling between coat length and body heat load. This interpretation is consistent with the literature. For example, Davila et al. (2019) demonstrated that, for every 25% increase in Brahman composition, undercoat length, topcoat length, core body temperature under low temperature–humidity index (THI), and core body temperature under high THI decreased by 1.32 mm, 2.94 mm, 0.11 °C, and 0.14 °C, respectively. These findings confirm that crossbreds with greater zebuine influence develop shorter coats and maintain lower body temperatures (Façanha et al. 2019; Silveira et al. 2021).

In ¾ Angus × ¼ Nelore (Fig. 4, right panel), RT appeared close to hair length in the upper quadrant, indicating a direct association—longer coats were positioned in the same multivariate direction as higher RT values, suggesting an association between coat length and rectal temperature within this exploratory ordination. These patterns align with reports showing that animals with higher proportions of Angus (and reduced zebuine fraction) exhibit the longest coat lengths and the highest body temperatures under heat stress. The literature consistently documents that European/taurine breeds, when exposed to thermal challenge, exhibit higher rectal temperatures than zebuine cattle and their crossbreds, reinforcing that long and dense coats elevate thermal load (Brown-Brandl 2018).

Hair thickness remained positive on both axes, indicating an ambiguous role: on one hand, greater thickness can shield against solar radiation without increasing RT when combined with low hair density—a strategy commonly reported in zebuine breeds (Gebremedhin et al. 2023). On the other hand, when associated with high density, thickness aligns with the radiant heat load gradient, hindering heat dissipation. Recent reviews emphasize that haircoats act as insulators by trapping air, but at the same time may impede sweating efficiency, and that long, dense coats retain sweat and reduce convective heat loss (Brown-Brandl 2018). Although sweat-gland morphology was not measured directly in the present study, the combined effects of coat length, density, and thickness may be related to variation in sweating capacity and sweat-gland characteristics reported in the literature. For example, Brahman cattle have been reported to possess larger and more numerous sweat glands than taurine breeds, resulting in greater sweating capacity (Mateescu et al. 2023). Thus, this mechanism should be regarded as a biologically plausible interpretation rather than as a directly measured explanation for the patterns observed here.

In summary, the multivariate interpretation of the exploratory factor analysis aligns closely with the literature: crossbreds with greater zebuine influence (shorter coats) show enhanced ability to maintain lower body temperatures even when coat thickness increases, whereas those with greater taurine influence (long and dense coats) exhibit proportionally higher thermal load. This body of evidence reinforces that thermal biology emerges from the integrated interaction among coat length, density, thickness, and sweating capacity, explaining the separation patterns observed between groups in the multivariate analysis. Consequently, individual traits cannot be interpreted in isolation; thermophysiological responses operate as systemic, interconnected processes.

In the exploratory binary logistic regression based on morphophysiological variables (Table 4), rectal temperature (β = 0.3434; OR = 1.41; p = 0.023) and hair length (β = −0.6414; OR = 0.53; p < 0.001) were associated with the probability of classification into the target genetic group. Conditional on the model and the adopted group coding, a one-unit increase in rectal temperature was associated with a 41% increase in the odds of classification into the target group, whereas a one-unit increase in hair length was associated with a 47% reduction in those odds. These associations should be interpreted as exploratory, in-sample group-discrimination signals rather than as validated predictors of heat tolerance. Although rectal temperature and coat morphology are biologically relevant indicators of thermoregulatory responses in cattle (Sejian et al. 2018; Amorim et al. 2019; Mascarenhas et al. 2023), the present analysis does not demonstrate that either genetic group is intrinsically more heat tolerant or more susceptible to heat stress. Rather, the findings suggest that physiological and morphological characteristics may jointly contribute to the observed differentiation between the two genetic compositions.

This contrast supports a compensatory hypothesis: different coat architectures can contribute to thermal stability. The blue cluster’s success aligns with Farias et al. (2024), who reported greater heat tolerance in Angus with short, fine hair under tropical conditions. Both profiles maintain homeothermy—vital to prevent heat stress—consistent with literature describing insulation- versus dissipation-driven routes to thermal stability.

Two other clusters exhibited greater daily thermal variation (lower acclimation efficiency), but for opposite reasons:

Green: sparse, short hair of moderate density—possibly insufficient insulation combined with only partial dissipation;

Green: sparse, short hair of moderate density—possibly insufficient insulation combined with only partial dissipation;

Yellow: long, abundant, very dense hair—strong insulation useful at night/cold periods but hindering heat loss during hot hours.

Yellow: long, abundant, very dense hair—strong insulation useful at night/cold periods but hindering heat loss during hot hours.

Compensatory strategies (e.g., increased respiratory rate) may be engaged in the latter. The yellow cluster was dominated by ¾ Angus × ¼ Nelore, consistent with a heavier coat and lower heat-loss capacity typical of taurine breeds (McManus et al. 2009). This duality is in concordance with Barreto et al. (2024): thick, long, larger-diameter, dense coats protect against solar radiation but hinder heat dissipation, whereas fine, short, less-dense coats facilitate heat loss but offer less solar protection. The hierarchical and K-means analyses both suggested four exploratory clusters, but the solutions differed in the allocation of some individuals and in the configurations associated with their method-specific labels. Therefore, the clusters should not be interpreted as directly equivalent across methods. The variables characterizing each solution are summarized separately for the hierarchical clustering in Table 7 and for K-means in Table 8. Given the observed overlap between clusters, these configurations should be regarded as exploratory candidate patterns rather than as stable or validated phenotypic groups.

Cluster-validity metrics indicated weak separation and partial overlap between the exploratory clustering solutions. Silhouette scores were low for both methods, although the hierarchical solution showed slightly better separation than K-means (0.271 vs. 0.243). Because the WCSS values were obtained from different clustering procedures, they were not used to establish the superiority or greater compactness of either method. Overall, these findings indicate that the identified configurations should be interpreted as exploratory candidate patterns rather than as robust, stable, or validated phenotypic groups.

Recent research has demonstrated the potential of artificial intelligence for large-scale heat-tolerance phenotyping. Chlingaryan et al. (2025), for example, combined approximately three million dairy production records with 20 years of climatic data and integrated artificial intelligence with a statistical genetic model. Heat tolerance was represented by the individual slope of milk-yield decline when the temperature–humidity index exceeded 60. In contrast, the present study uses direct, low-cost measurements of coat morphology and rectal temperature in a small group of beef crossbreds. Therefore, our contribution is exploratory and mechanistic rather than predictive: we examine candidate combinations of coat traits associated with rectal-temperature variation, without estimating breeding values or validated heat-tolerance phenotypes. These approaches are complementary in scale and purpose, but the patterns identified here require validation in larger independent populations.

Implications

The substantial overlap observed between the genetic groups, together with the limited apparent in-sample discrimination of the logistic models, reinforces an important implication for the thermal physiology of production animals: thermoregulatory responses are multivariate and context-dependent. Univariate analyses remain useful for identifying general tendencies and pairwise associations; however, they should be considered complementary to multivariate analyses rather than as independent alternatives. By integrating coat morphology, physiological responses, and changes over time, the exploratory multivariate framework used in this study provided a more comprehensive representation of compensatory relationships that may remain obscured when traits are evaluated separately. In particular, the combined use of longitudinal changes, correlation analysis, dimensionality reduction, exploratory genetic-group discrimination, and unsupervised clustering helped identify candidate coat-related thermoregulatory configurations. These patterns may contribute to a better biological understanding of thermal responses in crossbred cattle, but their practical relevance for phenotypic screening, breeding, or management decisions requires confirmation in larger and independent populations.

Limitations

This study provides exploratory insights into the relationships between coat characteristics and thermoregulatory responses in Red Angus × Nelore crossbred cattle; however, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the analyses were based on a relatively small sample of 30 clinically healthy bulls, which limits statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Although model assumptions were evaluated, the limited sample size increases uncertainty around the logistic-regression estimates and the stability of the clustering solutions.

Second, the study focused primarily on coat morphology and rectal-temperature variation and did not include other physiological indicators, such as respiratory rate, sweating rate, or skin temperature. Productive outcomes, including weight gain, feed efficiency, carcass characteristics, and meat quality, were also not evaluated. Consequently, the study does not establish whether the identified morphophysiological patterns are associated with productive resilience or validated heat-tolerance phenotypes. In addition, the absence of purebred Nelore and Red Angus reference groups prevented direct quantification of how the observed crossbred phenotypes compare with the thermal and coat-related characteristics of their parental breeds.

Finally, the logistic-regression and clustering analyses were exploratory and were not externally validated. The reported AUC values represent apparent in-sample discrimination, while the low Silhouette Scores indicate weak separation and partial overlap among the clustering configurations. Therefore, the identified patterns should be interpreted as candidate thermoregulatory configurations rather than as stable or validated phenotypic groups. Future studies should include larger samples, independent herds, purebred parental reference groups, contrasting environmental conditions, repeated physiological measurements, and productive and economic indicators to assess the consistency and broader applicability of these exploratory findings.

Conclusion

In summary, the two Red Angus × Nelore genetic groups showed substantial similarity in individual coat traits and rectal temperature under the evaluated tropical semi-arid conditions. However, the integrated multivariate approach revealed biologically meaningful combinations of coat characteristics associated with daily rectal-temperature variation. Coat thickness, hair length, hair quantity, and hair density acted as an interconnected system, indicating that different integumentary configurations may contribute to thermal regulation through alternative balances between insulation and heat dissipation.

The exploratory analyses suggested four candidate coat-related thermoregulatory configurations. Short, fine, and dense coats and thicker but less dense coats were associated with lower daily rectal-temperature variation, whereas intermediate and long, dense coats were associated with greater thermal fluctuation. These findings highlight the value of multivariate phenotyping for detecting compensatory relationships that may remain obscured when traits are evaluated individually. The identified patterns provide a biologically grounded framework for understanding coat-related thermal responses in tropical crossbred cattle and warrant validation in larger, independent populations and under different environmental conditions.
