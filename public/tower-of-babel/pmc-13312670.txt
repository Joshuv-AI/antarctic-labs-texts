Relationships between vigorous physical activity and psychological complaints in adolescents: findings from 240,951 participants
Authors: Mingjun Xia, Ping Wang, Bin Feng, Qingru Zhang, Jin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeThis cross-sectional study explores the association between vigorous physical activity (VPA) and psychological complaints in adolescents. While psychological complaints are a crucial aspect of adolescent development, the role of VPA in relation to specific complaints remains unclear, as existing evidence is mixed and varies by symptom type.Materials and methodsData were drawn from the 2017/2018 wave of the Health Behaviors in School-aged Children (HBSC) survey, comprising 240,951 students aged 11, 13, and 15 years randomly selected from participating countries. Psychological complaints were assessed using four self-reported items from the HBSC Symptom Checklist in the six months prior to data collection: feeling low, irritability, nervousness, and sleep difficulties. VPA frequency was measured using a single-item question regarding out-of-breath or sweaty activity during free time. Statistical analyses were performed using binary logistic regression, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsCompared to those reporting never engaging in VPA, daily VPA was associated with a 22% higher likelihood of feeling low (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.17–1.27, p < 0.001), with similar increases for 4–6 times/week (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.11–1.19, p < 0.001) and 2–3 times/week (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.14, p < 0.001). Daily VPA was also associated with slightly higher odds of irritability (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02–1.10, p = 0.01). In contrast, daily VPA was linked to a 9% reduction in nervousness (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.88–0.95, p < 0.001), with consistent results at lower frequencies. No significant association was observed between daily VPA and sleep difficulties (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.98–1.06, p = 0.37).ConclusionThis study reveals a heterogeneous pattern of associations between VPA and adolescent psychological complaints. While higher VPA frequency appears to reduce nervousness, it may be linked to increased risks of low mood and irritability. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causality and underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Adolescence is a distinctive development phase marked by notable shifts in physical functions, cognitive skills, and social surroundings [1–3]. Adolescents experience rapid brain development during this period, leading to increasingly complex cognitive abilities. These transformations are crucial in shaping their perceptions, interactions, and overall growth [4]. A large-scale longitudinal study using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has demonstrated that the human brain undergoes substantial structural and functional changes throughout adolescence. Notably, the prefrontal cortex, which is responsible for executive functions such as decision-making, impulse control, and emotion regulation, continues to develop well into early adulthood. Additionally, the limbic system—particularly the amygdala and hippocampus, which are involved in emotional processing and memory—also undergoes significant changes during this period [5]. Importantly, these regions mature at different rates, with the limbic system developing earlier than the prefrontal cortex, potentially leading to heightened emotional reactivity and increased vulnerability to mental health challenges such as anxiety and depression during adolescence [6]. However, this period also marks heightened vulnerability to mental health challenges, as an estimated one in seven adolescents aged 10–19 years globally (about 14.3%) experience a mental health condition such as depression, anxiety, or behavioral disorders, with initial episodes often occurring before the age of 14 [3, 7, 8].In addition, many mental health conditions that emerge during adolescence persist into adulthood, making adolescent mental health a strong predictor of long-term mental health outcomes later in life [9, 10].

Adolescence is a critical developmental period marked by heightened emotional sensitivity and increased vulnerability to mental health problems. Psychological health plays a foundational role in overall adolescent well-being, as this life stage is associated with a significantly elevated risk for emotional disorders such as depression and anxiety [11]. Compared to adults, adolescents tend to experience more intense and volatile emotions [4], which may contribute to psychological distress and, in more severe cases, self-injurious behaviors [4]. While some emotional fluctuations reflect normative developmental processes, others may serve as early warning signs of underlying mental health conditions. Therefore, identifying factors that support psychological health during adolescence is essential for early intervention [12] and long-term psychological resilience [13].

Physical activity (PA) is widely recognized as a protective factor for both physical and mental health [3, 14, 15]. For adults, regular PA reduces the risk of chronic diseases and all-cause mortality [16–18], while for adolescents, PA promotes healthy development, including healthier bones, weight regulation, and reduced chronic disease risk [19–22]. Beyond physical health, PA positively influences adolescents’ mental well-being, reducing anxiety, depression, and psychological distress while improving self-esteem, academic performance, and life satisfaction [23–26]. Conversely, sedentary behavior is linked to increased risks of physical and psychological problems [27–29].

However, the relationship between PA and mental health in adolescents is complex and multidimensional. PA can be classified into three categories based on intensity: light physical activity (LPA), moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), and vigorous physical activity (VPA) [30]. Among these, VPA is particularly associated with physical health benefits, including improved cardiorespiratory fitness and reduced risks of heart disease and chronic illnesses [31]. Research has also linked VPA to lower anxiety, depression, and stress levels, alongside increased positive affect (pleasurable emotional experiences) during and after exercise [32]. For instance, Huang and Li (2025) [33] found that adherence to strenuous PA guidelines correlated with better mental health outcomes. However, other studies suggest that higher exercise intensity may decrease such positive affective responses [34–36].In summary, while VPA has been associated with certain mental health benefits, evidence specific to adolescent populations remains limited and findings are often inconsistent across symptom types [37, 38]. Moreover, few large-scale studies have examined the relationship between VPA frequency and specific psychological complaints separately. The present study therefore addresses this gap by using a large, multi-national sample to evaluate the associations between VPA frequency and four distinct psychological complaints in adolescents.

In this study, we examine four psychological complaints (feeling low, irritability, nervousness, sleep difficulties) from the HBSC-SCL [39], which capture negative symptom dimensions distinct from positive emotional well-being [40]. This subscale has demonstrated good internal consistency and cross-national validity in adolescent populations [39, 41].Extant evidence on vigorous physical activity (VPA) in relation to these complaints is heterogeneous: VPA is inversely associated with nervousness, whereas findings for low mood are inconsistent. Associations with sleep difficulties appear contingent on factors such as exercise timing and recovery [42], and evidence concerning irritability is limited, though excessive training with inadequate recovery has been implicated in heightened irritability among young athletes [43]. Furthermore, observed associations frequently depend on adjustment for key covariates—namely age, sex, BMI, and socioeconomic status [44, 45]. Accordingly, this study evaluates each complaint independently while accounting for these confounders in a large, multinational sample.

This cross-sectional study examines the association between VPA frequency and four psychological complaints in adolescents using data from the Health Behavior in School-aged Children (HBSC) project, selected for its large, multinational sample and standardized assessment of both VPA and psychological complaints. Findings may inform future longitudinal research and, ultimately, evidence-based guidance for adolescent physical activity promotion.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

Data were drawn from the latest HBSC project, an international research network launched in 1982 that monitors school-aged children’s health behaviors across countries [46]. It was later recognised as a collaborative study by the World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Office for Europe. The HBSC project, initiated in 1982 by researchers from Finland, Norway, and the United Kingdom, is conducted every four years to investigate the health, well-being, and health behaviors of adolescents aged 11, 13, and 15 across a growing number of countries [47]. The present study utilized data from the 2017/2018 wave of the HBSC survey, which included participants from over 40 countries and regions across Europe, Central Asia, and North America [48].

In this study, a total of 240,951 students were randomly selected, typically by selecting one class per target age group in each participating school (accessed on 15 December 2024). Given the secondary nature of this study utilizing HBSC data, the sample size was determined by the HBSC international protocol, which ensures nationally representative samples through stratified random sampling of schools and classes within age groups.The HBSC study aims to monitor and understand adolescent health behaviors in their social contexts across countries and over time. For more detailed information on the history and development of the HBSC study, you can refer to a study published by Currie and colleagues [49]. All survey procedures for each data collection cycle have been documented and are available for download at this link: https://hbsc.org/. More information about the HBSC can be found at www.hbsc.org. For the 2017/2018 survey, the study protocol can be accessed at https://hbsc.org/publications/survey-protocols/. Ethical approvals for the study were secured at the national or regional level, with each country obtaining approval from an ethics review board or an equivalent regulatory body specific to the government, and also approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (SUDA20240626H06). Consent was obtained from participating schools, students, and their guardians. The participant’s legal guardian provided written informed consent to participate in this study. All procedures were performed in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Table 2 presented the demographic characteristics of participants in this study. The sample included 240,951 individuals, with a nearly even gender distribution: 49.3% female and 50.7% male. The participants were grouped into three age categories—11, 13, and 15 years—with approximately one-third of participants in each group. BMI classifications showed a prevalence of normal weight (69.2%), followed by underweight (14.2%), overweight (13.0%), and obesity (3.6%).The HBSC has been widely utilized in prior peer-reviewed studies on adolescent health [50–52].

Measures

Psychological complaints

Psychological complaints were assessed using the four items psychological subscale of the Health Behavior in School-aged Children Symptom Checklist (HBSC-SCL).Adolescents were asked to indicate how often they had experienced the following complaints during the past six months: (1) feeling low, (2) irritability or bad temper, (3) feeling nervous, and (4) difficulties in getting to sleep [39]. Response options included “about every day,” “more than once a week,” “about every week,” “about every month,” and “rarely or never.” These four items are commonly used as indicators of psychological complaints and psychological distress in international adolescent health research. The psychological symptom subscale comprising these items has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.76) in adolescent populations, indicating acceptable reliability, and has shown construct validity through consistent associations with depressive and anxiety symptoms in large-scale epidemiological studies [53–55].

Vigorous physical activity

VPA was assessed using a single-item question from the Health Behavior in School-aged Children (HBSC) survey: “How often do you usually exercise so much that you get out of breath or sweat?” Response categories included “every day,” “4–6 times a week,” “2–3 times a week,” “once a week,” “once a month,” “less than once a month,” and “never.” This single-item VPA measure has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in large-scale youth surveys. In adolescent populations, test–retest reliability coefficients typically range from 0.70 to 0.82, indicating good temporal stability [56, 57].

Covariates

Participants in the study were asked to report on their demographic characteristics, such as class, age, gender, and self-rated health status (excellent, good, fair, poor). Participants’ body mass index (BMI) was categorised into four levels according to the International Obesity Task Force (IOTF) [58, 59]: underweight (thinness), normal weight, overweight, and obese. Socioeconomic status was measured using the Family Affluence Scale (FAS), which includes items on material wealth (e.g., car ownership, bedroom occupancy, holidays, computers). Scores range from 0 to 13, with higher scores indicating greater family affluence [41]. The FAS has been jointly validated through the HBSC Development Project [60]. All covariates listed above were included as confounding variables in the regression models (see Table 1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA BE 18.0 (College Station, Texas, USA). Descriptive statistics were first computed to summarize the participants’ demographic characteristics. Missing data varied across variables. Based on the HBSC 2017/18 dataset documentation, missingness was approximately 0.6% for age category, 0% for gender, 1.1% for self-rated health, 2.0% for VPA, 2.7%–4.5% for the psychological complaint variables, 5.2% for family socioeconomic status, and 22.5% for BMI category. Missing values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) under the assumption of missing at random, with 10 imputed datasets generated and results pooled according to Rubin’s rules. The imputation model included all variables used in the analyses, and variable-appropriate imputation models were specified according to the distribution and measurement level of each variable. For binary logistic regression analyses, each psychological complaint was dichotomized. Responses of “about every day” or “more than once a week” were coded as 1 (presence of complaint), while responses of “about every week,” “about every month,” or “rarely or never” were coded as 0 (absence of complaint). This cut-off is consistent with prior HBSC-based studies examining frequent subjective health complaints [9, 41].Binary logistic regression models were employed to examine the associations between the frequency of VPA and indicators of psychological complaints.Four separate models were constructed to predict each outcome: (1) feeling low, (2) irritability, (3) feeling nervous, (4) difficulty sleeping. In each model, the independent variable was the frequency of VPA, categorized into seven levels (every day, 4–6 times/week, 2–3 times/week, once/week, once/month, less than once/month, and never), with “never” as the reference group. All models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, gender, BMI category, self-rated health status, and family socioeconomic status (as measured by the Family Affluence Scale), as well as country/region. The results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was determined at the p < 0.05 level.

Nearly half of participants reported rarely or never feeling low (47.7%), and approximately one-third reported rarely or never feeling nervous (34.9%). In terms of VPA, a minority of the sample engaged in VPA daily (17.4%), while the most common frequency was 2–3 times per week (30.0%). Participants who engaged in VPA less than once a month accounted for 6.8% of the sample.

Regarding nervousness, daily VPA was associated with a 9% reduction in the likelihood of feeling nervous (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.88–0.95, p < 0.001). Engaging in VPA 4–6 times per week was linked to a 10% decrease (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.87–0.94, p < 0.001), while participating 2–3 times per week corresponded to an 8% reduction (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.89–0.95, p < 0.001). Similarly, VPA once a week was associated with an 8% decrease in odds (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.88–0.95, p < 0.001). Participation once a month was linked to an 11% reduction in the likelihood of feeling nervous (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.85–0.94, p < 0.001), and less than once a month showed a 10% decrease (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94, p < 0.001).

For sleep difficulties, engaging in VPA daily had a negligible effect (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.98–1.06, p = 0.37), indicating no significant difference compared to the reference group.In contrast, engaging in VPA 4–6 times per week (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.89–0.96, p < 0.001), 2–3 times per week (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.89–0.96, p < 0.001), and once per week (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.93, p < 0.001) was significantly associated with reduced odds of experiencing sleep difficulties. Participation once a month showed no change in likelihood (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.95–1.05, p = 0.91), while engaging in VPA less than once a month was associated with a slight, non-significant reduction (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.92–1.02, p = 0.22).

Participation less than once a month was associated with a slight, non-significant decrease in irritability (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.92–1.01, p = 0.17).

Discussion

This study extends prior work by providing a large-scale, multinational examination of the associations between VPA frequency and four distinct psychological complaints in adolescents. By analyzing each complaint separately rather than relying on composite mental health scores, we reveal heterogeneous patterns that may otherwise be obscured. Specifically, VPA frequency was differentially associated with feeling low, irritability, nervousness, and sleep difficulties, with both protective and adverse associations observed depending on the outcome. These findings underscore the complexity of the VPA–psychological health relationship and suggest that blanket recommendations regarding vigorous exercise may be insufficient for adolescent mental health promotion.The modest effect sizes observed (ORs ranging from 0.89 to 1.22) are consistent with some prior evidence, yet the divergent directions of association across complaints are noteworthy. Most studies have shown that PA significantly contributes to the emotional well-being of individuals, both adults and adolescents, by reducing the occurrence of negative emotions [23, 61]. Giurgiu et al. (2022) [62] showed that episodes of PA were positively associated with three basic mood dimensions—valence (feeling well vs. unwell), energetic arousal (awake/energized vs. tired), and calmness (relaxed vs. tense). A study by Brockmann et al. (2020) [63] showed that people with regular PA habits were less likely to suffer from depression and anxiety than those who did not engage in regular PA and that the association was bidirectional. However, it is worth noting that most of these studies only looked broadly at the relationship between PA and mental health but did not take into account the intensity of PA. It has been shown that the improvement in mood observed after moderate exercise does not occur after a single session of high-intensity physical exercise [64]. A study by Golshani et al. (2021) [65] confirmed that PA might have negative mental health consequences and that excessive exercise and overtraining syndrome can be detrimental to mental health. Several factors may help explain this result. First, engaging in very frequent high-intensity physical activity may trigger physiological stress responses, such as elevated cortisol levels or sympathetic nervous system overactivation, which may, in turn, contribute to negative emotional states [66]. Second, excessive or unstructured vigorous activity may lead to overtraining or fatigue, particularly among adolescents who lack proper recovery strategies, potentially resulting in irritability, low mood, or even burnout [43].

VPA frequency was weakly associated with feeling low from 2 to 3 times per week upward, although the effect sizes were small (ORs around 1.10–1.22) and should be interpreted cautiously. This finding is in line with other studies. Franklin et al. (2021) [66] reported that higher VPA frequency was associated with reduced symptoms, including feeling low. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the observed associations between VPA and mental health. First, engagement in vigorous physical activity has been directly linked to improvements in body image and reductions in depressive symptoms among adolescents, with a clear dose-response relationship favoring higher intensities [67].Second, VPA—particularly in social or team-based contexts—may foster psychological resilience through enhanced social support. Recent evidence indicates that social support serves as a key mediator linking psychological resources to sports participation, operating in tandem with exercise motivation to promote sustained engagement [68, 69]. Conversely, mental health may also influence VPA behaviors. Causal inference research suggests that team sports participation is associated with reduced dimensional psychopathology in youth [70], though the directionality of this relationship warrants further investigation. Additionally, lower socioeconomic position has been consistently associated with lower levels of VPA among adolescents in both European and Asian contexts [71, 72]. Concurrently, low SEP has been robustly linked to poorer mental health outcomes in youth [44].Notably, large-scale school-based trials of HIIT-style VPA interventions have yielded null findings for mental health outcomes, likely due to implementation challenges and low compliance [73], underscoring the complexity of translating VPA recommendations into real-world benefits. Thus, the relationship between VPA and mental health is likely bidirectional and complex, warranting further investigation.

The present findings indicate that higher VPA frequency was associated with lower odds of nervousness, whereas adolescents who never engaged in VPA reported higher levels of nervousness. Although beneficial associations between VPA and adolescent mental health have been frequently reported, evidence specific to anxiety remains inconclusive [45, 74].For example, Biddle et al. (2019) [45] showed that PA was causally related to cognitive functioning in children and adolescents, partially related to diepression, and not related to self-esteem.

This association could be linked to the nature of the physical activities undertaken. Previous studies indicated that individuals engaged in team sports exhibit the lowest anxiety and tension levels alongside the hghest well-being levels [75]. Neurobiologically, vigorous exercise—more so than moderate-intensity activity—elicits pronounced release of endorphins and monoamines (e.g., serotonin, dopamine), which modulate mood and stress responses via enhanced prefrontal cortex activity and attenuated amygdala reactivity [76]. These neurochemical and functional changes are thought to facilitate emotion regulation and reduce anxiety symptoms, which may explain why adolescents engaging in regular VPA report lower nervousness.

However, excessive or unbalanced VPA—particularly without adequate recovery—may elevate cortisol levels and increase the risk of emotional dysregulation. Overtraining and performance pressure have been associated with psychological fatigue and depressive symptoms, particularly in youth populations [77, 78]. Adolescents may be especially susceptible to such effects because prefrontal cortical maturation and stress-regulatory systems remain under development during this period, limiting their capacity to buffer physiological and psychological loads [5, 6]. Consequently, when physical overload occurs without sufficient recovery, adolescents may be more vulnerable to irritability, mood disturbances, and other negative affective states [43].

The association between VPA and sleep difficulties in this study exhibited a non-linear pattern. Adolescents who engaged in VPA one to six times per week showed lower odds of reporting sleep difficulties compared with those who never engaged in VPA (ORs ranging from 0.90 to 0.93), whereas daily VPA was not significantly associated with sleep difficulties (OR = 1.02). This pattern aligns with evidence suggesting that moderate, but not excessive, physical activity is associated with better sleep outcomes [42, 79]. Physiologically, PA is known to promote sleep through increased homeostatic sleep pressure and improved circadian regulation. However, very vigorous or frequent exercise may also induce acute sympathetic activation, elevate core body temperature, and increase cortisol levels, which can delay sleep onset or impair sleep initiation when recovery is insufficient [42, 80].

Because the HBSC questionnaire does not capture the timing of physical activity, it is possible that some adolescents reporting daily VPA engaged in exercise later in the day—a pattern associated with prolonged sleep onset and reduced sleep quality in prior work [81, 82].In addition, adolescents may be particularly sensitive to training load and recovery balance due to ongoing maturation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and circadian regulatory systems, which heightens physiological reactivity to evening exercise and insufficient recovery [83, 84]. Insufficient recovery from frequent VPA may contribute to fatigue, restlessness, and sleep complaints, even when such activity is otherwise health-promoting [43]. Taken together, these findings indicate that the relationship between VPA and sleep is dose-dependent: moderate frequencies (1–6 times/week) may support sleep through enhanced homeostatic sleep pressure and circadian entrainment, whereas daily VPA may confer diminishing returns or even adverse effects when recovery is inadequate [42, 80]. This pattern is consistent with evidence suggesting that the exercise–sleep relationship is not strictly linear, with potential thresholds beyond which additional activity yields no further benefit [79, 85].

Daily VPA was associated with a small increase in irritability, whereas lower frequencies showed weaker or no such association, suggesting a dose- and recovery-dependent emotional effect rather than a uniformly beneficial or harmful influence. Vigorous exercise activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, increasing cortisol and physiological arousal [86]; when performed frequently without adequate recovery, this state may persist, contributing to fatigue, emotional dysregulation, and irritability, consistent with research on exercise-induced stress and overreaching [87]. In addition, adolescents engaged in frequent VPA are often exposed to competitive and performance-related pressures, and ongoing maturation of emotional regulation systems may increase sensitivity to both physical and psychological stressors [15, 88, 89]. Together, these mechanisms suggest that moderate or well-recovered VPA may support mental health, whereas very frequent or insufficiently recovered VPA may increase irritability in adolescents.

Study limitations

While this study provides meaningful insights, several significant limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and the reliance on self-reported measures introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, despite the use of validated instruments. Additionally, unmeasured confounding factors—such as academic stress, screen time, diet, sleep environment, and family mental health history—may have influenced the observed associations. The study also examined only the frequency and intensity of VPA, without accounting for type, timing, or social context, all of which may moderate emotional outcomes. Furthermore, the use of a single-item measure for VPA may limit construct precision and comparability with other studies.These limitations underscore the need for future longitudinal research that utilises objective measures and multidimensional assessments. Given the heterogeneous pattern of associations observed, future studies should examine whether tailored activity recommendations—such as moderate VPA frequencies for adolescents with elevated nervousness, or socially engaging forms of vigorous activity for those reporting low mood—yield differential benefits. Such work is necessary before specific practice guidelines can be formulated.Finally, it is important to acknowledge that lower scores on these four items reflect reduced psychological complaints rather than the presence of positive emotional well-being [40], which encompasses broader dimensions such as life satisfaction and sense of meaning .

Practical implications

Future research should further examine how different frequencies and intensities of VPA relate to specific psychological complaints in adolescents, particularly given the small effect sizes (ORs near 1.0 with wide confidence intervals for several comparisons) and heterogeneous patterns observed in this cross-sectional study. Longitudinal and experimental designs are needed to clarify temporal sequencing and to identify whether optimal VPA ranges differ across symptom domains. Until such evidence becomes available, practitioners should be cautious in promoting VPA as a universal strategy for adolescent mental health. The present findings underscore the potential value of assessing multiple psychological outcomes separately, as the direction and strength of associations varied considerably across complaints. Further work is required to determine whether tailored activity prescriptions yield differential benefits for specific psychological profiles.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study provides evidence of differential associations between VPA frequency and specific psychological complaints in adolescents. Higher VPA frequency was associated with modestly elevated odds of feeling low and irritability, and reduced odds of nervousness, whereas VPA frequency was not significantly associated with sleep difficulties. These heterogeneous patterns suggest that the relationship between VPA and adolescent psychological complaints varies by symptom type. Given the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported measures, longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify temporal sequencing and underlying mechanisms. Until such evidence is available, VPA should not be promoted as a universal strategy for adolescent mental health.
