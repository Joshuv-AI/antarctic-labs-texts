Response to the Letter to the Editor: “Hypertensive Urgency: Which Patient will be Fine in a Quiet Room?”
Authors: Grethzel Prado, Carlos Bea, Fernando Martínez
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

We thank the authors for their thoughtful comments and for highlighting the important issue of clinical decision‐making in the management of hypertensive urgencies in the emergency department (ED) [1].

As correctly pointed out, blood pressure (BP) in this setting may follow different trajectories, including spontaneous reduction, persistence, or further elevation due to stress and underlying mechanisms. In our study, reflecting its retrospective and real‐world nature, treatment decisions were not guided by a predefined protocol but by the attending physician's clinical judgment, integrating BP level, symptom burden, cardiovascular risk, and suspicion of acute hypertension‐mediated organ damage.

To further address this point, we performed an additional analysis comparing patients who received antihypertensive treatment with those managed conservatively. We did not observe significant differences between groups in terms of age, sex, or clinical presentation. However, initial BP values were significantly lower in untreated patients (systolic BP: 183.6 ± 26.9 mmHg vs. 195.8 ± 23.4 mmHg; diastolic BP: 94.8 ± 19.9 mmHg vs. 100.9 ± 19.5 mmHg; both p < 0.001), suggesting that treatment decisions were primarily driven by the absolute BP level at presentation.

We agree that inappropriate selection may entail risks, including overtreatment with subsequent hypotension or under treatment with delayed BP control. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that clinicians tend to individualize treatment decisions based on initial severity, which may help balance these competing risks in routine practice.

Regarding the white‐coat effect, we concur that it may contribute to BP variability and spontaneous reductions, particularly in high‐stress environments such as the ED. Although guidelines recommend rest in a quiet environment, implementation is often challenging, underscoring the need for pragmatic approaches and repeated BP assessment.

Regarding clinical worsening during ED stay, including the development of acute hypertension‐mediated organ damage, our study was not designed to capture these dynamic changes, and therefore we cannot provide a precise estimate. However, hospital admission rates were similar between treated and untreated patients (12.2% vs. 13.2%, p = 0.503). Longitudinal follow‐up data are available, and analyses addressing short‐term outcomes are on‐going.

Regarding the route of antihypertensive drug administration, the oral/sublingual route was most frequently used in the prehospital setting, whereas intravenous administration was more common after hospital arrival. Sublingual administration was documented mainly for nitrates, while nifedipine was given orally. However, oral and sublingual routes were grouped in the dataset, precluding precise differentiation. Overall, the oral/sublingual route was used in 58.4% of cases. For captopril, although it was the most frequently used drug, the route was not consistently recorded. Therefore, while oral or sublingual use can be confirmed, adherence to guideline recommendations cannot be fully assessed.

We also thank the authors for their comment regarding the lower range of BP values and potential sex‐related differences, particularly among younger patients.

In our study, hypertensive urgency was defined as presentation to the ED with markedly elevated BP in the absence of acute target‐organ damage, without predefined thresholds. To characterize the lower boundary, we examined minimum BP values after exclusion of outliers using the interquartile range method. The lowest systolic BP values were 120 mmHg in men and 133 mmHg in women, and the lowest diastolic values were 73 and 75 mmHg, respectively, with similar findings in patients aged <55 years. No significant sex differences were observed for systolic BP (p = 0.66), and although diastolic BP differed slightly (p = 0.02), the absolute difference was minimal.

We acknowledge that relative increases in BP may influence symptom perception, particularly in individuals with lower baseline values. However, baseline BP prior to ED presentation was not available, precluding this assessment. Importantly, absolute BP values at presentation were consistently high across sexes, and we did not observe lower BP thresholds in women, including younger patients. In addition, the 25th percentile of systolic BP was 161.5 mmHg in men and 166.0 mmHg in women, while diastolic BP was 102.0 mmHg and 99.0 mmHg, respectively, confirming that even the lower end of the distribution corresponded to markedly elevated BP levels.

Overall, our findings do not support systematically lower absolute BP values in women, including younger patients, in this setting.

Sincerely,
