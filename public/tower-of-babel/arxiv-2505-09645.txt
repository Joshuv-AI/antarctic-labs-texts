A Tauberian approach to the orthorecursive expansion of unity
Benoit Cloitre

Abstract
We establish a Tauberian theorem connecting the unknown asymptotic behavior of the partial sums ∑ n ≤ x a n \sum_{n\leq x}a_{n} to the known asymptotics of weighted sums ∑ n ≤ x a n ​ g ​ ( n / x ) \sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}g(n/x) , as x → ∞ x\rightarrow\infty , where g : ( 0 , 1 ] → ℝ g:(0,1]\to\mathbb{R} is a given function. Our approach relies on an identity relating a modified Mellin transform of g g to the Dirichlet series ∑ n ≥ 1 a n ​ n − s \sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}n^{-s} . As an application, we solve an open problem posed by Kalmynin and Kosenko regarding the orthorecursive expansion of unity associated with a sequence ( c n ) n ≥ 0 (c_{n})_{n\geq 0} . Specifically, we improve their partial-sum bound C N = ∑ 0 ≤ n ≤ N c n = 𝒪 ( N − 1 / 2 ) C_{N}=\sum_{0\leq n\leq N}c_{n}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2}) , by obtaining the optimal estimate C N = 𝒪 ⁡ ( N − α 1 + ϵ ) C_{N}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-\alpha_{1}+\epsilon}) , where α 1 ≈ 1.3465165 \alpha_{1}\approx 1.3465165 is the smallest real part among the zeros of a transcendental function related to the digamma function.



Keywords:

Tauberian theorem; Mellin transform; asymptotic analysis; orthorecursive
expansion; digamma function.

Primary: 40E05; Secondary: 41A60, 33B15, 30E15, 42C05.

Introduction

In their 2020 paper
[
7
]
, Kalmynin and Kosenko introduced
and studied the sequence
(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
defined by
c_{0}=1

and, for
N\geq 1
, satisfying the recurrence relation

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{c_{n}}{N+n+1}=0.

This sequence arises naturally when quantifying how far the non-orthogonal
system
\{x,x^{2},x^{3},\dots\}
in
L^{2}[0,1]
is from spanning
the constant function
1
. Indeed, the
c_{n}
’s are precisely the
coefficients of the orthorecursive (norm-minimising) expansion of
unity with respect to this system. A central open question arising
from their work, and a particularly challenging one, concerns the
precise asymptotic behavior of the terms
(c_{n})
and especially
their partial sums
C_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}
. While their intricate
arguments yielded the bounds

c_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-3/2})\quad\text{and}\quad C_{N}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2}),

the optimal rate for
C_{N}
remained to be determined.

The challenge of determining the precise asymptotic behavior of coefficients
arising from such
L^{2}
approximation schemes holds significant
interest. Conceptually, the problem posed by Kalmynin and Kosenko—quantifying
the approximation of the constant function ’1’ by polynomials generated
from a non-orthogonal system—shares structural similarities with
other deep questions in analytic number theory that also hinge on

L^{2}
approximation.

For instance, the celebrated Nyman–Beurling criterion for the Riemann
Hypothesis
[
11
,
3
]
(see also
[
2
]

for a version without the linear constraint, and
[
1
]

for a recent perspective and further generalizations) reformulates
the Riemann Hypothesis in terms of approximating the indicator function

\chi_{(0,1)}
in
L^{2}(0,\infty)
by linear combinations of dilations
of the fractional part function
t\mapsto\{\theta_{k}/t\}
. The crucial
connection to the Riemann
\zeta
-function is then typically established
via the classical Mellin transform of the fractional part function.

In a similar vein, the Tauberian approach developed in this paper
relies on a modified Mellin transform,

g^{\star}(z)=-z\int_{0}^{1}g(t)t^{-z-1}\,dt.

The analytic properties of this transform—particularly the location
of its zeros—decisively govern the asymptotic behavior of the sums

\sum a_{n}
that arise from the foundational relation
\sum a_{k}g(k/n)\sim x^{-\beta}
.

When applied here to the Kalmynin–Kosenko problem with the weight
function
g(t)=2/(1+t)
, this method reveals that the asymptotics
of the partial sums
C_{N}
are dictated by the zeros of a specific
transcendental function involving the digamma function
\psi
. It
is noteworthy that this methodological framework, centered on an adapted
Mellin transform and the analysis of its zeros, has also
provided
a direct Tauberian characterization of the Riemann Hypothesis itself,
in that case involving the Ingham function and its connection to
\zeta(1-z)

[
5
]
.
While the orthorecursive expansion problem is distinct and does not
purport an equivalence with the Riemann Hypothesis, the efficacy of
such Tauberian tools across these varied problems underscores the
value of exploring these approximation structures and the analytic
methods for their resolution.

In this article, we resolve this question by obtaining the optimal
asymptotic estimate for the partial sums
C_{N}
. Namely, we show
that

C_{N}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-\alpha_{1}+\epsilon})\quad\text{for any }\epsilon>0,

where
\alpha_{1}\approx 1.34651649147585
is the smallest
real part among the zeros of the function

z\mapsto z(\psi(-z/2)-\psi((1-z)/2)).

Here
\psi(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\log\Gamma(z)
denotes the digamma function.
Our approach leverages a new Tauberian theorem, presented in Section
1
.
Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real numbers, let
g:(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a given weight function, and let

\alpha_{g}:=\inf\{\operatorname{Re}(z)\mid g^{\star}(z)=0\}

denote the infimum of the real parts of the zeros of
g^{\star}(z)
.
If the weighted sum
A_{g}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}g(n/x)
has known
asymptotics of type

A_{g}(x)=x^{-\beta}+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{-c}\right),\quad\text{as }x\to\infty,

(where the first term
x^{-\beta}
is omitted if
\beta=\infty
),
the Tauberian theorem relates the asymptotics of the unweighted sum

A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}
to the parameters
\alpha_{g}
,
\beta
,
and
c
.

This article is organized as follows: Section
1

presents and proves our Tauberian Theorem. Section
2

applies this theorem to solve the orthorecursive expansion problem
mentioned above.

1
The Tauberian Theorem

1.1
The Mellin-Flajolet trick

Our method is inspired by the powerful techniques involving Mellin
transforms, notably highlighted in the work of Flajolet et al.
[
6
]

for solving asymptotic problems in combinatorics and analysis.
1
1

1

We refer to the core identity used here as the “Mellin-Flajolet trick”
in tribute to Philippe Flajolet’s significant contributions and his
generosity in sharing knowledge (see
https://algo.inria.fr/flajolet/Publications/books.html
).

The connection between the Dirichlet series
L(a,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}n^{-s}

associated with the sequence
(a_{n})
and the weighted sum
A_{g}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}g(n/x)

is established via the Mellin transform. Let

f^{\ast}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{s-1}dx

be the standard Mellin transform and

g^{\star}(z)=-z\int_{0}^{1}g(t)t^{-z-1}\,dt

be the modified transform. They are related by
f^{\ast}(s)=\frac{g^{\star}(-s)}{s}

if we set
f(t)=g(t)
for
t\in(0,1]
and
f(t)=0
for
t>1
.
Under suitable convergence conditions allowing the interchange of
summation and integration, the Mellin transform of the weighted sum

A_{g}(x)
satisfies
A_{g}^{\ast}(-s)=L(a,s)f^{\ast}(s)
.
This leads to the fundamental identity:

L(a,s)=s\frac{A_{g}^{\ast}(-s)}{g^{\star}(-s)}.

(1)

This identity relates the analytic properties of the Dirichlet series

L(a,s)
to those of the Mellin transform
A_{g}^{\ast}(-s)
and
the zeros of the modified transform
g^{\star}(-s)
.

Theorem 1.1

(Tauberian Theorem)
.

Let

g:(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that its modified Mellin
transform

g^{\star}(z)=-z\int_{0}^{1}g(t)t^{-z-1}\,dt

exists for
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
and can be analytically continued. Assume
further that:

1.

\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}g^{\star}(z)=g^{\star}(0)\neq 0
exists.

2.

\alpha_{g}:=\inf\{\operatorname{Re}(\rho)\mid g^{\star}(\rho)=0\}>0
.

3.

1/g^{\star}(z)
is analytic in the open half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(z)<\alpha_{g}
.

4.

The growth condition
\lvert 1/g^{\star}(\sigma+it)\rvert=\mathcal{O}((1+\lvert t\rvert)^{\epsilon})

holds as
\lvert t\rvert\to\infty
for every
\epsilon>0
, uniformly
for
\sigma
in compact subsets of
(-\infty,\alpha_{g})
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real numbers.
Define the partial sum
A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}
and the weighted
sum
A_{g}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}g(n/x)
. Suppose that for some fixed
real values
\beta
and
c
:

A_{g}(x)=x^{-\beta}+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{-c}\right)\quad\text{as }x\to\infty.

(If
\beta=\infty
, this means
A_{g}(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-c})
for
some
c
.)

Then, as
x\to\infty
we have:

•

If
\beta<\min(c,\alpha_{g})
, then

A(x)\sim\frac{1}{g^{\star}(\beta)}x^{-\beta}.

•

If
\beta,c\geq\alpha_{g}
, then

A(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-\alpha_{g}+\epsilon})\quad\text{for any }\epsilon>0.

Proof.

Let
A_{g}(x)=x^{-\beta}+E(x)
, where
E(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-c})

as
x\to\infty
. The Mellin transform of
A_{g}
is given by

A_{g}^{\ast}(-s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}A_{g}(x)x^{-s-1}\,dx=\frac{1}{s+\beta}+h(s),

where

h(s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}E(x)x^{-s-1}\,dx.

Since
E(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-c})
, the integral defining
h(s)
converges
absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>-c
, and
h(s)
is analytic in this half-plane.
Moreover, for any fixed
\sigma>-c
, we have

\lvert h(\sigma+it)\rvert\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}\lvert E(x)\rvert x^{-\sigma-1}\,dx=\mathcal{O}(1),

uniformly in
t
. (The term
1/(s+\beta)
is omitted if
\beta=\infty
).

From the Mellin–Flajolet identity (
1
), we have

L(a,s)=s\cdot\frac{A_{g}^{\ast}(-s)}{g^{\star}(-s)}=\frac{s}{g^{\star}(-s)}\left(\frac{1}{s+\beta}+h(s)\right).

This representation expresses the Dirichlet series
L(a,s)
in terms
of the Mellin transform of
A_{g}
and the modified Mellin transform

g^{\star}
of
g
.

To recover
A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}
, we apply Perron’s formula
(see
[
13
, Theorem II.2.3]
):

A(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma_{0}-iT}^{\sigma_{0}+iT}\frac{L(a,s)x^{s}}{s}\,ds+R(x,T),

where
\sigma_{0}>0
,
T\geq 1
, and the error term
R(x,T)
satisfies

R(x,T)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{x^{\sigma_{0}}}{T}\sup_{\lvert t\rvert\leq T}\lvert L(a,\sigma_{0}+it)\rvert\right).

We shift the line of integration to the left, moving from
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\sigma_{0}

to
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\lambda+\eta
, where
\lambda=\min(c,\alpha_{g},\beta)

and
0<\eta<\lambda
. (If
\beta=\infty
, then
\lambda=\min(c,\alpha_{g})
.)
The integrand has a simple pole at
s=-\beta
(if
\beta<\infty

and distinct from other singularities) and possible singularities
arising from zeros of
g^{\star}(-s)
, which lie to the left of or on

\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\alpha_{g}
by assumption. Singularities of
h(s)
are
to the left of
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-c
.

Case 1:
\boldsymbol{\beta<\min(c,\alpha_{g})}
.
The pole
at
s=-\beta
is the rightmost singularity. By Cauchy’s residue theorem,
we have

A(x)=\Res_{s=-\beta}\left(\frac{L(a,s)x^{s}}{s}\right)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\sigma_{1}-iT}^{-\sigma_{1}+iT}\frac{L(a,s)x^{s}}{s}\,ds+R^{\prime}(x,T),

where
\beta<\sigma_{1}<\min(c,\alpha_{g})
and
R^{\prime}(x,T)
includes
errors from horizontal segments and truncation. Computing the residue
at
s=-\beta
(assuming
\beta\neq 0
; if
\beta=0
,
g^{\star}(0)\neq 0

is used), we find

\Res_{s=-\beta}\left(\frac{L(a,s)x^{s}}{s}\right)=\lim_{s\to-\beta}(s+\beta)\frac{s}{g^{\star}(-s)}\left(\frac{1}{s+\beta}+h(s)\right)\frac{x^{s}}{s}=\frac{x^{-\beta}}{g^{\star}(\beta)}.

To estimate the integral along the new vertical line
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\sigma_{1}
,
note that for
s=-\sigma_{1}+it
:

\lvert L(a,s)\rvert\leq\lvert s\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\frac{1}{g^{\star}(-s)}\right\rvert\cdot\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{s+\beta}\right\rvert+\lvert h(s)\rvert\right).

Since
1/g^{\star}(-s)
is analytic for
\operatorname{Re}(-s)<\alpha_{g}
(i.e.,

\operatorname{Re}(s)>-\alpha_{g}
) and satisfies the growth condition
\lvert 1/g^{\star}(\sigma^{\prime}+it^{\prime})\rvert=\mathcal{O}((1+\lvert t^{\prime}\rvert)^{\varepsilon})

for any
\varepsilon>0
, and
h(s)
is bounded on vertical lines

\operatorname{Re}(s)>-c
, it follows that on
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\sigma_{1}
(where
-\sigma_{1}>-\alpha_{g}

and
-\sigma_{1}>-c
),
\lvert L(a,s)\rvert=\mathcal{O}((1+\lvert t\rvert)^{1+\varepsilon})
.
Furthermore,
\lvert x^{s}\rvert=x^{-\sigma_{1}}
and
\lvert s\rvert^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(\lvert t\rvert^{-1})

as
\lvert t\rvert\to\infty
. The integral along the vertical line
is
\mathcal{O}(x^{-\sigma_{1}}T^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})
for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}
.
Choosing
T
appropriately (e.g.,
T=x
) and managing error terms
carefully leads to:

A(x)=\frac{x^{-\beta}}{g^{\star}(\beta)}+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{-\sigma_{1}}\right).

Since this holds for any
\sigma_{1}<\min(c,\alpha_{g})
, we conclude

A(x)=\frac{x^{-\beta}}{g^{\star}(\beta)}+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{-\min(c,\alpha_{g})+\delta}\right)\quad\text{for any }\delta>0.

Case 2:
\boldsymbol{\beta\geq\alpha_{g}}
and
\boldsymbol{c\geq\alpha_{g}}
.

(This includes
\beta=\infty
). In this scenario, the rightmost singularities
determining the main order are no further right than
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\alpha_{g}
.
Any pole at
s=-\beta
(if
\beta<\infty
) is at or to the left
of
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\alpha_{g}
. The singularities from
h(s)
are at or
to the left of
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-c\leq-\alpha_{g}
. The ”dominant” singularities
are thus from the zeros of
g^{\star}(-s)
(at
s=-\rho_{k}
with
\operatorname{Re}(\rho_{k})\geq\alpha_{g}
,
so
\operatorname{Re}(s)\leq-\alpha_{g}
). Shifting the contour to
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\alpha_{g}+\eta
.
Here
\eta
is chosen small and positive, such that
0<\eta<\alpha_{g}
(which ensures
-\alpha_{g}+\eta<0
as
\alpha_{g}>0
by condition (2) of the theorem).
The integral along this line, using the growth condition on
1/g^{\star}

and bounds on
h(s)
, gives the estimate. The standard Perron machinery
and contour shift lead to

A(x)=\mathcal{O}\left(x^{-\alpha_{g}+\varepsilon}\right)\quad\text{for any }\varepsilon>0.

This completes the proof.
∎

2
Application to the Orthorecursive Expansion

Preliminaries

We now apply the Tauberian theorem to analyze the sequence
(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}

studied by Kalmynin and Kosenko in
[
7
]
. This sequence
is defined by
c_{0}=1
and the recurrence relation

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{c_{k}}{n+k+1}=0,\quad\text{for }n\geq 1.

(2)

The authors investigated the asymptotic behavior of
c_{n}
and its
partial sums
C_{N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{k}
. Relevant sequences are
cataloged in the OEIS
[
12
]
: A323490 (numerators of
c_{n}
),
A323491 (denominators), and A323492 (indices where
c_{n}
changes
sign). Kalmynin and Kosenko established the bounds:

\lvert c_{n}\rvert\leq\sqrt{30782}\,n^{-3/2}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{N}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2}).

Establishing these bounds was a non-trivial task achieved by Kalmynin
and Kosenko through ingenious analytic arguments involving
L^{2}
-norm
estimates of associated polynomials (see
[
7
, Section 3]
).
These bounds are crucial prerequisites for Lemma
2.2

below, which provides the necessary input estimate
A_{g}(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-3/2})

— acting as the effective Tauberian condition — for our Theorem
1.1
.
Our main result concerns the partial sums:

Theorem 2.1

(Orthorecursive theorem)
.

Let
(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be defined by
c_{0}=1
and

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{c_{k}}{n+k+1}=0\ \text{for}\ n\geq 1.

Then the partial sums satisfy

\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\alpha_{1}+\epsilon})\quad\text{for any }\epsilon>0,

as
n\to\infty
, where

\alpha_{1}\approx 1.346516491475859...

is the smallest positive real part of the zeros of
z\mapsto z(\psi(-z/2)-\psi((1-z)/2))
.

To apply Theorem
1.1
to prove this theorem,
we first transform the problem. The key step is to relate the sequence

(c_{n})
to a sequence
(a_{n})
and choose a suitable weight function

g(t)
so that the known recurrence translates (via Lemma
2.2
)
into the required asymptotic estimate for the weighted sum
A_{g}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}g(n/x)
.

2.1
Transformation of the Problem

Let
a_{n}=c_{n-1}
for
n\geq 1

(so
a_{1}=c_{0}=1
). The recurrence relation (
2
)
implies

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{a_{k}}{N+k-1}=0\quad\text{for }N\geq 2.

We define the auxiliary function
G(n,k)=\frac{2n-1}{n+k-1}
. This
choice ensures that for
n\geq 2
:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}G(n,k)=(2n-1)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{n+k-1}=0.

Now, let
g(t)=\frac{2}{1+t}
be our target weight function for the
Tauberian theorem. We need to relate the known condition
\sum a_{k}G(n,k)=0

to the asymptotics of the weighted sum
A_{g}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}g(n/x)
.
The following lemma establishes that
A_{g}(x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-3/2})
,
based on the results from
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
g(t)=\frac{2}{1+t}
.
Under the known estimations

•

a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-3/2})
(see
[
7
, Theorem 1, p.5]
),

•

A(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
(see
[
7
, Theorem 2, p.13]
).

We have
A_{g}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}g(n/x)=\mathcal{O}(x^{-3/2})

as
x\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
x=N+\delta
where
N=\lfloor x\rfloor
and
0\leq\delta<1
.
First, we estimate the difference
A_{g}(x)-A_{g}(N)
.

A_{g}(x)-A_{g}(N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\Bigl(g\bigl(\frac{n}{x}\bigr)-g\bigl(\frac{n}{N}\bigr)\Bigr)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\,\frac{2n\delta}{(x+n)(N+n)}.

Using
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-3/2})
, and
N\sim x
,

\lvert A_{g}(x)-A_{g}(N)\rvert\ll\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-3/2}\frac{n}{N^{2}}=N^{-2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-1/2}\ll N^{-2}N^{1/2}=N^{-3/2}.

So,
\lvert A_{g}(x)-A_{g}(N)\rvert=\mathcal{O}(x^{-3/2})
.

Next, we estimate
A_{g}(N)
. Let:

•

G(N,k)=\frac{2N-1}{N+k-1}
(so
\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}G(N,k)=0
for

N\geq 2
)

•

f_{N}(k)=G(N,k)-g(k/N)=\frac{N-k}{(N+k-1)(N+k)}
.

For
N\geq 2
,
A_{g}(N)=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}f_{N}(k)
. Using Abel
summation:

A_{g}(N)=-\left(A(N)f_{N}(N)-\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}A(k)\bigl(f_{N}(k+1)-f_{N}(k)\bigr)\right).

Since
f_{N}(N)=0
,
\lvert f_{N}(k+1)-f_{N}(k)\rvert=\mathcal{O}(N^{-2})

and using
A(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
, we get

\lvert A_{g}(N)\rvert=\left\lvert\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}A(k)\bigl(f_{N}(k+1)-f_{N}(k)\bigr)\right\rvert\ll N^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}k^{-1/2}\ll N^{-3/2}.

Combining the estimates,

A_{g}(x)=A_{g}(N)+(A_{g}(x)-A_{g}(N))=\mathcal{O}(x^{-3/2})+\mathcal{O}(x^{-3/2})=\mathcal{O}(x^{-3/2}).

∎

2.2
The Mellin transform of
g

Lemma 2.3
.

The modified
Mellin transform of
g(t)=2/(1+t)
is

g^{\star}(z)=z\left(\psi\left(-\frac{z}{2}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{1-z}{2}\right)\right).

It has
g^{\star}(0)=2
, and simple poles at
z=1,2,3,\dots
.

Proof.

For
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
, the definition is

g^{\star}(z)=-z\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2}{1+t}\,t^{-z-1}\,dt.

Because
\lvert t\rvert<1
on
(0,1)
we may expand
\frac{1}{1+t}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}t^{k}
.
Interchanging summation and integration yields:

g^{\star}(z)=-2z\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\int_{0}^{1}t^{k-z-1}\,dt=2z\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{z-k}.

This can be rearranged and related to the digamma function
\psi

via its series representation, yielding

g^{\star}(z)=z\left(\psi\left(-\frac{z}{2}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{1-z}{2}\right)\right).

Using
\psi(x)\sim-1/x
near
x=0
, we find
\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}g^{\star}(z)=2
.
The poles at
z=1,2,3,\dots
arise from the poles of the
\psi

function.
∎

2.3
Zero-free region for
g^{\star}

Lemma 2.4
.

The
function
g^{\star}(z)
has no zeros in the closed half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0
.

Proof.

We want to show that
g^{\star}(z)=z(\psi(-z/2)-\psi((1-z)/2))
has
no zeros for
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0
. Let

\phi(a)=\psi(a)-\psi(a+1/2).

Recall
g^{\star}(z)=z\phi(-z/2)
for
z\neq 0
.

Case 1:
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
.
Let
a=-z/2
, so
\operatorname{Re}(a)>0
. We need
to show
\phi(a)\neq 0
. We use the representation
\phi(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{n}(a)
,
where

L_{n}(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{a+k+1/2}-\frac{1}{a+k}\right).

The zeros of
L_{n}(a)
correspond to the finite critical points
of

H_{n}(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\log(a+k+1/2)-\log(a+k))=\log(Q_{n}(a)/P_{n}(a)),

where
P_{n}(a)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(a+k)
and
Q_{n}(a)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(a+k+1/2)
.
The zeros of
L_{n}(a)
are thus the finite zeros of
H_{n}^{\prime}(a)
.
We consider the rational function
h_{n}(a)=P_{n}(a)/Q_{n}(a)
(so

H_{n}(a)=-\log h_{n}(a)
). The zeros of
H_{n}^{\prime}(a)
(excluding
poles) are the same as the zeros of
h_{n}^{\prime}(a)
.

•

P_{n}(a)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(a+k)
.

•

Q_{n}(a)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(a+k+1/2)
.

We apply Marden’s theorem:

Theorem (Marden, Th. (21.1)
[
9
]
).

For
j=0,1,\dots,p
let
f_{j}(z)
denote a polynomial of degree

n_{j}
having all of its zeros in the circular region
\alpha_{j}C_{j}(z)\leq 0

where
\alpha_{j}=\pm 1
and
C_{j}(z)=\lvert z-c_{j}\rvert^{2}-r_{j}^{2}
.
Then every finite zero
Z
of the derivative of the rational function

f(z)=\frac{f_{0}(z)\dots f_{q}(z)}{f_{q+1}(z)\dots f_{p}(z)}\quad(0\leq q<p)

satisfies at least one of:
\alpha_{j}C_{j}(Z)\leq 0
(
j=0,\dots,p
),
or
E(Z)\leq 0
(where
E(Z)
involves
C_{j}(Z)
and geometric data).

To apply this to the critical points of
h_{n}(a)=P_{n}(a)/Q_{n}(a)

(and thus to the zeros of
L_{n}(a)
), we identify the locations
of the zeros of
P_{n}(a)
(which are the poles of
Q_{n}(a)/P_{n}(a)
)
and
Q_{n}(a)
(which are the zeros of
Q_{n}(a)/P_{n}(a)
):

•

Zeros of
P_{n}(a)
are
\{0,-1,\dots,-n\}
. These lie in the closed
disk
\bar{D}_{1}
centered at
c_{1}=-n/2
with radius
r_{1}=n/2
.

•

Zeros of
Q_{n}(a)
are
\{-1/2,-3/2,\dots,-(n+1/2)\}
. These lie
in the closed disk
\bar{D}_{0}
centered at
c_{0}=-(n+1)/2
with
radius
r_{0}=n/2
.

Marden’s theorem then implies that any finite zero
Z
of
L_{n}(a)

must lie in
\bar{D}_{0}\cup\bar{D}_{1}
. The rightmost point of
this union is
a=0
. Since
a=0
is a pole of
L_{n}(a)
(specifically,
of the term
1/(a+1/2)-1/a
for
k=0
), any zero
Z
of
L_{n}(a)

must satisfy
\operatorname{Re}(Z)<0
. By Hurwitz’s Theorem
[
9
, Th. (1.5), p. 4]
,
since
L_{n}(a)\to\phi(a)
uniformly on compact sets avoiding poles,
any zero
a_{0}
of the limit function
\phi(a)
must also satisfy

\operatorname{Re}(a_{0})\leq 0
. Given
a=0
is a pole for
\phi(a)
as well,
its zeros must satisfy
\operatorname{Re}(a_{0})<0
. Therefore,
\phi(a)\neq 0

for
\operatorname{Re}(a)>0
. This implies
g^{\star}(z)=z\phi(-z/2)\neq 0
for
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
.

Case 2:
\operatorname{Re}(z)=0
.
If
z=0
, then
g^{\star}(0)=2\neq 0

(Lemma
2.3
). Let
z=iy
for some

y\in\mathbb{R},y\neq 0
. Let
a=-iy/2
. Since
z\neq 0
,
g^{\star}(z)\neq 0

is equivalent to
\phi(a)\neq 0
. We have

\operatorname{Im}(\phi(a))=\operatorname{Im}(\psi(a)-\psi(a+1/2)).

Substituting
a=-iy/2
, this becomes

\operatorname{Im}(\phi(-iy/2))=(-y/2)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}+y^{2}/4}-\frac{1}{(k+1/2)^{2}+y^{2}/4}\right).

Since for any
k\geq 0
and
y\neq 0
, the term
\frac{1}{k^{2}+y^{2}/4}-\frac{1}{(k+1/2)^{2}+y^{2}/4}

is strictly positive, it follows that the sum
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\dots)

is a strictly positive quantity. Therefore, as
y\neq 0
,
\operatorname{Im}(\phi(-iy/2))\neq 0
.
This implies
\phi(-iy/2)\neq 0
, and thus
g^{\star}(iy)\neq 0
for

y\neq 0
.

Combining Case 1 and Case 2,
g^{\star}(z)
has no zeros in the closed
half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0
.
∎

2.4
Location of the principal zero

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\alpha_{g}=\inf\{\operatorname{Re}(\rho)\mid g^{\star}(\rho)=0\}
.
Then

\alpha_{g}=\alpha_{1}\approx 1.34651649147585.

Proof.

Since from Lemma
2.4
,
no zeros exist for
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0
, we seek zeros of
g^{\star}(z)

in
\operatorname{Re}(z)>0
. Zeros must occur in conjugate pairs. We analyze the
strip
S=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid-0.25\leq\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 2.5,\operatorname{Im}(z)\geq 0\}
.

Step 1: No zeros for
g^{\star}(z)
when
\operatorname{Im}(z)
is large
(
y_{z}\geq 10
)

The objective of this subsection is to demonstrate that
g^{\star}(z)\neq 0

for
z=x_{z}+iy_{z}
in the domain
D_{10}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid x_{z}\in[-0.25,2.5],y_{z}\geq 10\}
.
We will analyze two consolidated cases based on the value of
x_{z}=\operatorname{Re}(z)
.

Case 1:
\operatorname{Re}(z)\in[-0.25,0]

For
z
such that
x_{z}=\operatorname{Re}(z)\in[-0.25,0]
, the condition
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0

is satisfied. As established in Lemma
2.4

(which covers
\operatorname{Re}(z)<0
and
\operatorname{Re}(z)=0,z\neq 0
), the function
g^{\star}(z)

has no zeros for
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0
. Therefore,
g^{\star}(z)\neq 0
in
the region
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid x_{z}\in[-0.25,0],y_{z}\geq 10\}
.

Case 2:
\operatorname{Re}(z)\in(0,2.5]

For
z
such that
x_{z}=\operatorname{Re}(z)\in(0,2.5]
and
y_{z}=\operatorname{Im}(z)\geq 10
,
we will demonstrate that
|g^{\star}(z)-1|<1
, which implies that

g^{\star}(z)\neq 0
. The function
g^{\star}(z)
is defined by
g^{\star}(z)=z(\psi(-z/2)-\psi((1-z)/2))
.
Let
w_{1}=-z/2
and
w_{2}=(1-z)/2
. The digamma function
\psi(w)

has the asymptotic expansion

\psi(w)=\log w-\frac{1}{2w}+R_{\psi}(w),

where
R_{\psi}(w)
is the remainder term. Substituting this expansion
into the expression for
g^{\star}(z)-1
, we obtain:

g^{\star}(z)-1=z\left(\log w_{1}-\frac{1}{2w_{1}}+R_{\psi}(w_{1})-\left(\log w_{2}-\frac{1}{2w_{2}}+R_{\psi}(w_{2})\right)\right)-1.

This expression can be separated into a main part
T_{\text{main}}(z)

and a remainder part
T_{\text{rem}}(z)
:

g^{\star}(z)-1=\underbrace{z\left(\log\frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{w_{1}}-\frac{1}{w_{2}}\right)\right)-1}_{T_{\text{main}}(z)}+\underbrace{z(R_{\psi}(w_{1})-R_{\psi}(w_{2}))}_{T_{\text{rem}}(z)}.

Analysis and Bound for the Main Part
T_{\text{main}}(z)

The main part is
T_{\text{main}}(z)=z\left(\log\left(\frac{z}{z-1}\right)+\frac{1}{z(1-z)}\right)-1
.
For
y_{z}\geq 10
, it can be shown that:

|T_{\text{main}}(z)|\leq\frac{0.5}{y_{z}}+\frac{19}{108y_{z}^{2}}.

Thus, for
y_{z}\geq 10
:

|T_{\text{main}}(z)|\leq\frac{0.5}{10}+\frac{19}{108\cdot 100}\approx 0.05+0.00175926\approx 0.05176.

Bound for the Remainder Part
T_{\text{rem}}(z)

The remainder part is
T_{\text{rem}}(z)=z(R_{\psi}(w_{1})-R_{\psi}(w_{2}))
.
The term
R_{\psi}(w)
is the remainder of the digamma function expansion,

R_{\psi}(w)=\psi(w)-\log w+\frac{1}{2w}
. We use the bound derived
from G. Nemes
[
10
]
:

|R_{\psi}(w)|\leq\frac{1}{12|w|^{2}}\left|\sec\left(\frac{\arg w}{2}\right)\right|^{3}.

This bound is valid for
|\arg w|<\pi
. For
z=x_{z}+iy_{z}
with

x_{z}\in(0,2.5]
and
y_{z}\geq 10
:

•

For
w_{1}=-x_{z}/2-iy_{z}/2
:
\operatorname{Re}(w_{1})\in[-1.25,0)
and
\operatorname{Im}(w_{1})\leq-5
.
Thus,
\arg(w_{1})\in(-\pi,-\pi/2)
, and
|\arg(w_{1})|<\pi
.

•

For
w_{2}=(1-x_{z})/2-iy_{z}/2
:
\operatorname{Re}(w_{2})\in[-0.75,0.5)
and

\operatorname{Im}(w_{2})\leq-5
. Thus
|\arg(w_{2})|<\pi
.

The applicability condition is satisfied. The term
\left|\sec\left(\frac{\arg w}{2}\right)\right|^{3}=\left(\frac{2}{1+\operatorname{Re}(w)/|w|}\right)^{3/2}
.
Given
y_{z}\geq 10
, we have
|w_{k}|\geq y_{z}/2\geq 5
.

For
w_{1}=X_{1}+iY_{1}
, where
X_{1}=-x_{z}/2\in[-1.25,0)
. The
ratio
X_{1}/|w_{1}|
satisfies
X_{1}/|w_{1}|\geq-0.2425
. Thus,

1+X_{1}/|w_{1}|\geq 0.7575
. It follows that
\left|\sec\left(\frac{\arg w_{1}}{2}\right)\right|^{3}\leq\left(\frac{2}{0.7575}\right)^{3/2}\approx 4.295
.
So,

|R_{\psi}(w_{1})|\leq\frac{4.295}{12|w_{1}|^{2}}\leq\frac{4.295}{12(y_{z}/2)^{2}}=\frac{4.295}{3y_{z}^{2}}.

For
w_{2}=X_{2}+iY_{2}
, where
X_{2}=(1-x_{z})/2\in[-0.75,0.5)
.
If
X_{2}\geq 0
, then
1+X_{2}/|w_{2}|\geq 1
, leading to
\left|\sec\left(\frac{\arg w_{2}}{2}\right)\right|^{3}\leq(2)^{3/2}\approx 2.828
.
If
X_{2}<0
,
X_{2}/|w_{2}|\geq-0.1483
. Then
1+X_{2}/|w_{2}|\geq 0.8517
,
leading to
\left|\sec\left(\frac{\arg w_{2}}{2}\right)\right|^{3}\leq\left(\frac{2}{0.8517}\right)^{3/2}\approx 3.602
.
Thus, we take the overall bound
\left|\sec\left(\frac{\arg w_{2}}{2}\right)\right|^{3}\leq 3.602
.
So, we get

|R_{\psi}(w_{2})|\leq\frac{3.602}{12|w_{2}|^{2}}\leq\frac{3.602}{12(y_{z}/2)^{2}}=\frac{3.602}{3y_{z}^{2}}.

The factor
|z|
is bounded by
|z|\leq y_{z}\sqrt{1+(2.5/y_{z})^{2}}\leq y_{z}\sqrt{1+(0.25)^{2}}\approx 1.03078y_{z}
.
Let
C_{z}\approx 1.03078
. Then, we have

|T_{\text{rem}}(z)|\leq|z|(|R_{\psi}(w_{1})|+|R_{\psi}(w_{2})|)\leq C_{z}y_{z}\left(\frac{4.295}{3y_{z}^{2}}+\frac{3.602}{3y_{z}^{2}}\right)=\frac{C_{z}(7.897)}{3y_{z}}.

For
y_{z}\geq 10
this gives:

|T_{\text{rem}}(z)|\leq\frac{7.897\times 1.03078}{3\times 10}\approx\frac{8.1397}{30}\approx 0.27132.

Conclusion for
|g^{\star}(z)-1|
with
\operatorname{Re}(z)\in(0,2.5]
and
y_{z}\geq 10

Combining the bounds for
y_{z}\geq 10
:

|g^{\star}(z)-1|\leq|T_{\text{main}}(z)|+|T_{\text{rem}}(z)|\leq 0.05176+0.27132=0.32308.

Since
0.32308<1
, we conclude that
|g^{\star}(z)-1|<1
. This implies
that
g^{\star}(z)\neq 0
for
z
in the region
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid x_{z}\in(0,2.5],y_{z}\geq 10\}
.

Overall Conclusion for
D_{10}

Considering the two consolidated cases for
D_{10}
:

1.

For
\operatorname{Re}(z)\in[-0.25,0]
and
\operatorname{Im}(z)\geq 10
,
g^{\star}(z)\neq 0

(by Lemma
2.4
).

2.

For
\operatorname{Re}(z)\in(0,2.5]
and
\operatorname{Im}(z)\geq 10
,
g^{\star}(z)\neq 0
(as
demonstrated above).

We conclude that
g^{\star}(z)\neq 0
for all
z
in the domain
D_{10}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid x_{z}\in[-0.25,2.5],y_{z}\geq 10\}
.

Step 2. Argument Principle Application.
The function
g^{\star}(z)=z(\psi(-z/2)-\psi((1-z)/2))

has simple poles at
z=1,2,3,\dots
. The poles at
z=1
and
z=2

are inside the chosen rectangle
R
(vertices
-0.25,2.5,2.5+10i,-0.25+10i
).
To use the Argument Principle for counting zeros,
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{C}\frac{f^{\prime}(w)}{f(w)}dw=N_{0}-N_{P}
,
the function
f(w)
must be analytic within
C
and non-zero on

C
. Since
g^{\star}(z)
has poles inside
R
, we consider the auxiliary
function

H(z)=(z-1)(z-2)g^{\star}(z).

The factors
(z-1)
and
(z-2)
precisely cancel the poles of
g^{\star}(z)

at
z=1
and
z=2
. The other poles of
g^{\star}(z)
(at
z=3,4,\dots
)
are outside the rectangle
R
. The point
z=0
is not a pole of

g^{\star}(z)
(since
g^{\star}(0)=2
). Thus,
H(z)
is analytic
inside and on the contour
R
(Step 1 ensures no zeros on the upper
boundary for
y_{z}\geq 10
; other boundaries can be checked for zeros
of
H(z)
). The zeros of
H(z)
inside
R
are identical to the
zeros of
g^{\star}(z)
inside
R
, as the multiplicative factor

(z-1)(z-2)
does not introduce new zeros within
R
.

Counting the zeros of
H(z)
using
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{R}\frac{H^{\prime}(z)}{H(z)}dz

is therefore equivalent to counting the zeros of
g^{\star}(z)
in

R
. Moreover, from a computational standpoint, integrating
H^{\prime}(z)/H(z)

is numerically more stable than integrating
{g^{\star}}^{\prime}(z)/g^{\star}(z)

directly, as it avoids evaluating functions very close to the poles

z=1,2
. As detailed in Appendix A (which provides the PARI/GP script
used to compute the number of zeros of
H(z)
via the argument principle,
ensuring high precision for the contour integration) numerical integration
yields

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{R}\frac{H^{\prime}(z)}{H(z)}dz\approx 1.

Since the number of zeros
N
must be an integer,
N=1
. There is
exactly one zero of
g^{\star}(z)
(and
H(z)
) inside
R
.

Step 3. Computation of the zero.
Numerical root-finding methods
locate this unique zero
\rho_{1}
of
g^{\star}(z)
within
R
.
See Appendix B for a high-precision value:

\rho_{1}\approx 1.34651649147585+i\,1.0551601360....

Conclusion.
Since zeros of
g^{\star}(z)
for
\operatorname{Re}(z)>0
occur
in conjugate pairs (as
g^{\star}(\bar{z})=\overline{g^{\star}(z)}
),
and
\rho_{1}
is the only zero found in the upper-half part of the
search strip
S
,
\rho_{1}
must be the zero with the smallest
positive real part in the upper half-plane. Thus,

\alpha_{g}=\operatorname{Re}(\rho_{1})=\alpha_{1}\approx 1.34651649147585>0.

∎

2.5
Proof of Theorem
2.1

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
2.1
using the preceding
lemmas.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
relies
on the application of our Tauberian Theorem (Theorem
1.1
).
We consider the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
defined by
a_{n}=c_{n-1}

for
n\geq 1
(so
a_{1}=c_{0}=1
), and the specific weight function

g(t)=2/(1+t)
. Our objective is to determine the asymptotic behavior
of
A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}
, which directly relates to
C_{N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{k}

via
C_{N}=A(N+1)
.

We systematically verify that all hypotheses of Theorem
1.1

are satisfied:

(1)

Properties of the modified Mellin transform
g^{\star}(z)

for
g(t)=2/(1+t)
:

•

Lemma
2.3
establishes that
g^{\star}(z)=z(\psi(-z/2)-\psi((1-z)/2))
.
This function is meromorphic over
\mathbb{C}
.

•

The same lemma shows that
\lim_{z\to 0}g^{\star}(z)=g^{\star}(0)=2\neq 0
.

•

Lemma
2.4
demonstrates
that
g^{\star}(z)
has no zeros in
\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq 0
.

•

Lemma
2.5
shows that
\alpha_{g}
(defined as
\inf\{\operatorname{Re}(\rho)\mid g^{\star}(\rho)=0\}
and required to be
>0
by Thm.
1.1
, condition 2) is
\alpha_{1}\approx 1.34651649147585
. This value is indeed positive, satisfying the condition as a consequence of Lemma
2.4
.

•

Consequently,
1/g^{\star}(z)
is analytic in the open half-plane

\operatorname{Re}(z)<\alpha_{1}
.

•

The growth condition
\lvert 1/g^{\star}(\sigma+it)\rvert=\mathcal{O}((1+\lvert t\rvert)^{\epsilon})

holds as
\lvert t\rvert\to\infty
for every
\epsilon>0
, uniformly
for

in compact subsets of

(where

(2)

Properties of

and the weighted sum

:

•

The sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with

is well-defined from

(c_{n})
.

•

Lemma
2.2
shows that

,

. Thus Case 2 of Theorem

yields:

Since

.
∎

Concluding remarks

We have established the optimal decay rate
\mathcal{O}(N^{-\alpha_{1}+\epsilon})

for the partial sums
C_{N}
of the orthorecursive expansion of unity,
thereby improving the result given in
[
7
]
. Our method
relies on the preliminary bounds from that work, which effectively
serve as the necessary Tauberian conditions for our main theorem to
apply.

The asymptotic behavior of the individual coefficients
c_{n}
, however,
remains an open question. Kalmynin and Kosenko, citing a conjecture
by A.V. Ustinov, suggested that
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\lvert c_{n}\rvert}{\log n}=-7/3

[
7
]
. While their numerical evidence supports this, the
observed slow convergence, coupled with the role of
\alpha_{1}

in determining the behavior of
C_{N}
, might suggest an alternative
scenario. We conjecture that this limit superior is dictated by
\alpha_{1}
,
specifically:

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\lvert c_{n}\rvert}{\log n}=-\alpha_{1}-1\approx-2.3465164914758\ldots.

This value would naturally arise if the partial sums were of the form

C_{N}\sim N^{-\alpha_{1}}\omega(N)
for some bounded, slowly oscillating
function
\omega(N)
, as then
c_{N}=C_{N}-C_{N-1}
would typically
behave as
\mathcal{O}(N^{-\alpha_{1}-1})
. The complex behavior
of
(c_{n})
, including sign changes at exponentially increasing
intervals (cf. OEIS sequence A323492
[
12
]
), is consistent
with such an oscillating component. Further investigation into this
specific conjecture for
c_{n}
is warranted. Moreover, we have a potential approach
related to our general theory of regular arithmetic functions introduced in
[
5
]
,
by considering the function
G(n,k)=\frac{2n-1}{n+k-1}
(defined in Section
2.1
).

The Tauberian theorem presented and applied in this paper (Theorem
1.1
)
is a versatile tool. Its potential applications extend beyond the
current problem, for instance, it also underpins the Tauberian characterization
of the Riemann Hypothesis presented for the first time in
[
4
]

and proved using another method in
[
5
]
.
