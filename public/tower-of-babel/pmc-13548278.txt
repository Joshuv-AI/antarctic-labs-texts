‘Digital impressions’ for use in pediatric patients with cleft lip and palate
Authors: Salvador Reyes-Fernández, Daniel Sandoval-Guevara, Alicia García-Verónica, Natalia Hernández-Treviño, Aldahir Gaona-Bracamontes, Rafael Luna-Aguilar, Norma Samanta Romero-Castro
Published: 2026

Within the interdisciplinary management of pediatric patients with cleft lip and palate, the procedures provided by dentistry are particularly important. This treatment includes presurgical orthopedics and requires the fabrication and management of orthopedic appliances. These are made from various materials, such as quick-setting acrylic. Traditionally, impressions have been taken using custom-made impression trays with different impression materials.1

In a retrospective cohort study, Benitez et al. concluded that intraoral scanning offers clear advantages over traditional techniques; there were no complications during the digital scanning process.2

In a systematic review, Unnikrishnan et al. concluded that digital impressions are as accurate as conventional impressions in patients with clefts. However, digital impression systems are more readily accepted by both parents and treating clinicians.3

Intraoral digital impressions are safe, accurate, and time efficient, making them a viable alternative to conventional impressions. This helps overcome the challenges and complications frequently associated with conventional techniques, thus reducing the workload for the patient’s family and for healthcare professionals.4

While conventional techniques are considered by many as the gold standard,5 they carry significant risks, particularly aspiration of impression materials and subsequent airway obstruction; this is especially the case in newborns.6 7 Consequently, conventional techniques have been gradually replaced by digital impressions.2

Other drawbacks of using conventional impression materials and techniques include incomplete anatomical replication, omitting key surgical structures such as the vomer and nasal aperture.6 Moreover, there is the potential for residual impression material to cause inflammation of soft tissues lining the cleft.7

Digital impression techniques eliminate the risks associated with impression materials and could therefore be a safer alternative.7 Digital impression technology also provides high-quality, colorful 3D models that improve both safety and logistic efficiency, are non-invasive and reduce the complications associated with traditional methods. With conventional impression techniques, the impression material must harden and reach sufficient firmness before being removed from the patient’s mouth. In contrast, intraoral scanning allows image acquisition to be paused and resumed at any time.6 This eliminates the need for sedation or general anesthesia and therefore reduces the need for emergency airway management equipment. It also minimizes anxiety and discomfort for infants and their caregivers.6

Some authors believe that using this technology reduces the risk of vomiting, aspiration, and asphyxiation, thus lessening the burden on both children and parents.8

In addition, digital impressions are known to accurately and reliably reproduce cleft morphology9 (figure 1).

Comparative studies have been conducted between intraoral scanning and scanning of models of patients with cleft lip and palate; there were no differences between the two techniques.7 There are several treatment steps for patients with orofacial malformations along with their families. Any advance that reduces both the burden and risk and improves treatment outcomes is of high value, especially at young ages. Alternatives to conventional impressions with reduced complications require less technical expertise (impression material, custom trays, and dental training) and resources for airway management. Collectively, conventional impressions for cleft lip and palate are no longer used in several centers.6

As professionals in the care and treatment of patients with clefts, we must consider that intraoral scanning technology offers clear advantages over conventional impression taking.2

High cost of equipment, learning curve for staff training, and the appointment of trained personnel are some of the difficulties in the application of these techniques. This is especially the case in cleft lip and palate care centers in developing countries.

It is important that healthcare institutions providing these cleft lip and palate care services manage the purchase of scanners and printers, which would allow successful implementation of this technology.2 For those centers that do not have this equipment, traditional printing will continue to be the best option.

One of the limitations for scanning pediatric patients is the size of the scanner tips. To address this problem, we acquired a set of Medit brand pediatric tips. However, these were not small enough to fit comfortably in the patients’ mouths.

Within the activities of our cleft lip and palate program at the Faculty of Dentistry of the Autonomous University of Guerrero, we have chosen to implement these technological advances, replacing traditional impressions with digital alternatives. Our experiences are both positive and safe for patients, and it is recommended to take impressions in pediatric patients with cleft lip and palate.
