Learning-Based Progressive Barrier Control for Robot Manipulators with Initial Errors Outside Prescribed Tracking Bounds
Hamed Rahimi Nohooji, Danial Zafaranchizadeh Moghaddam, Abolfazl Zaraki, Holger Voos

Abstract
Robot manipulators may start a new task with a tracking error larger than the prescribed tolerance. Conventional barrier controllers generally require the initial error to lie within this tolerance, which prevents their direct use under such conditions. This paper develops a progressive barrier controller that gradually contracts an initially relaxed error bound to the required value within a prescribed time. The robot can therefore start outside the final bound, while the direct joint-position error is constrained after the transition. The closed-form control law combines progressive barrier feedback with online actor–critic compensation for uncertain dynamics and disturbances. A Lyapunov analysis establishes bounded closed-loop signals and gives sufficient conditions for satisfaction of the final tracking bound. Two-link simulations consider large initial errors, torque saturation, dynamic variations, disturbances, and measurement errors. The adaptive term reduces the median root-mean-square tracking error by 45.1% compared with the same progressive barrier controller without learning. The simulations also show how the selected transition time and available torque determine the range of initial errors that can be handled. Finally, an experiment on a Niryo Ned3 Pro illustrates tracking performance using measured position and motor-current data.



I
Introduction

Robot manipulators often move between tasks, reference trajectories, and
operating modes. At the start of a new motion, the joint tracking error can be
larger than the tolerance required during task execution, even though the
robot remains within its joint limits and the planned motion is collision
free. This situation is common after a reference switch, replanning event, or
recovery action. The tracking tolerance therefore has a different timing
requirement from a physical safety constraint: joint and collision limits must
hold throughout the motion, whereas a tight tracking bound may only be needed
after a short transition. The control problem is to bring an initially
out-of-bound tracking error into the required tolerance within a specified
time, despite uncertain dynamics and finite actuator torque, and to maintain
that tolerance thereafter.

There are several constrained-control frameworks used in robotics. Control barrier functions (CBFs)
are well suited to joint limits, collision avoidance, and other safe sets that
must remain invariant from the initial time

[
1
,
2
]
. Their implementation commonly uses a
quadratic program and must account for feasibility, sampling, and model
uncertainty
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Model predictive control (MPC) can impose state and input constraints over a
prediction horizon
[
6
]
, but it requires a prediction model and
the repeated solution of a constrained optimization problem. Prescribed-performance control (PPC) shapes transient and steady-state accuracy through a
time-varying error envelope
[
7
]
; in its standard
form, the error transformation must be defined inside that envelope at the
initial time. Global- and deferred-performance designs relax this condition

[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
,
including for manipulators with input nonlinearities

[
12
]
. These are useful alternatives. Here we seek a
closed-form low-level controller for a task-level tracking tolerance that is
allowed to become active after a prescribed transition, without solving an
optimization problem online.

Barrier Lyapunov functions (BLFs) offer a closed-form alternative for
constrained nonlinear control. A BLF grows without bound as its argument
approaches the boundary, and boundedness of the closed-loop Lyapunov function
then prevents the boundary from being reached
[
13
]
. This
principle has been used for uncertain robot manipulators with joint-space and
time-varying constraints
[
14
,
15
]
. However, the
standard BLF is finite only when its initial argument already lies
inside the prescribed set. Consequently, a controller designed directly for a
tight final tracking tolerance cannot be initialized when a new task starts
with a larger error. Enlarging the bound for the entire motion avoids this
problem, but also weakens the accuracy requirement after the transient.

Uncertain inertial parameters, friction, payload changes, and external loads
introduce an additional tracking error. Learning-based compensation has been
used to improve the tracking of uncertain Lagrangian systems

[
16
]
, and its use in robotic control has been studied more
broadly in
[
17
]
. Actor–critic methods provide one mechanism
for adapting a control contribution from tracking and control-effort data

[
18
,
19
]
. In this paper, the actor–critic
structure is used as an online torque compensator. The stability result is stated in terms of the
combined uncertainty that remains after compensation, including disturbances,
sampling effects, and the difference between commanded and applied torque.

To address these points, we introduce a progressive barrier controller for
robot joint tracking. A raised-cosine factor smoothly scales the direct
joint-position error from zero to its full value over a prescribed transition
time. The barrier is therefore well defined at initialization for
any finite tracking error. After the transition, the scaling is exactly one,
so the variable constrained by the barrier is the direct joint-position error.
The resulting control law is closed form and does not require an online
optimizer. Actor–critic torque compensation is included for uncertain
dynamics and disturbances, and the analysis is written for the torque applied
to the robot rather than only for the unconstrained controller command.

The main contributions are as follows:

•

A progressive barrier is formulated directly from the
joint-position error. It removes the initial-domain restriction associated
with the final tracking tolerance and recovers that tolerance at a prescribed
time.

•

The barrier feedback is combined with online actor–critic compensation,
and a residual-based Lyapunov condition is derived for bounded closed-loop
signals and satisfaction of the post-transition tracking bound. The condition
does not require convergence of the learned weights or an optimal-policy
claim, and it explicitly uses the applied torque.

•

The controller is evaluated on a manipulator subject to joint
torque limits, initial errors outside the final tolerance, dynamic variations,
disturbances, and position-measurement errors. A numerical map relates the
initial error and transition time to feasibility under the available torque.
Measured position and motor-current data from a Niryo Ned3 Pro provide an
additional single-joint hardware evaluation.

II
Problem Formulation

Consider an
n
-joint rigid manipulator
[
20
]

M(q)\ddot{q}+C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}+g(q)=u_{\rm ap}+d,

(1)

where
q,\dot{q},\ddot{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the joint positions, velocities, and
accelerations, respectively. The matrix

M(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the inertia matrix,

C(q,\dot{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
contains the Coriolis and centrifugal
terms, and
g(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the gravity vector. The vector
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

collects additive joint-space disturbances, including external torques and
unmodeled effects. The signal
u_{\rm ap}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the torque actually
applied to the robot; it may differ from the controller command

u_{\rm cmd}
because of actuator saturation and interface effects. The
inertia matrix satisfies
M=M^{\mathsf{T}}\succ 0
and

\underline{m}I\preceq M(q)\preceq\bar{m}I
on the operating region.
Moreover,
\dot{M}-2C
is skew-symmetric.

Let the desired trajectory
q_{d}(t)
be twice continuously differentiable and define the direct
tracking error

e=q-q_{d}.

(2)

The required tracking tolerance is specified by the smooth scalar envelope

k(t)\geq k_{\min}>0
. The control objective is

\left\lVert e(t)\right\rVert<k(t),\qquad t\geq T_{c}.

(3)

No initial condition of the form
\left\lVert e(0)\right\rVert<k(0)
is required. The bound
in (
3
) is imposed only for
t\geq T_{c}
; before
T_{c}
, the
direct tracking error may lie outside
k(t)
. Because the same scalar envelope
is applied to all joints, this condition is conservative. If every desired
joint position remains at least
k(t)
away from its physical limits,
satisfaction of (
3
) also guarantees that the joint limits are
respected for
t\geq T_{c}
.

To introduce the final tracking bound smoothly, we define the raised-cosine
transition function

\gamma(t)=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}[1-\cos(\pi t/T_{c})],&0\leq t<T_{c},\\
1,&t\geq T_{c},\end{cases}

(4)

and the transformed direct error

z=\gamma e.

(5)

Thus
z(0)=0
for every finite
e(0)
, while
z=e
after
T_{c}
. For

\gamma>0
,
\left\lVert z\right\rVert<k
is exactly the temporary condition

\left\lVert e\right\rVert<k/\gamma
; this is a time-varying relaxation, not a new barrier
geometry.

III
Progressive Barrier Control with Adaptive Compensation

Using the scaled error
z
, we define the progressive barrier Lyapunov
function

B(z,t)=\frac{1}{2\beta}\log\left(\frac{k^{2}}{D}\right),\qquad\beta>0,

(6)

where
D:=k^{2}-z^{\mathsf{T}}z
. The condition
D>0
is
equivalent to
\left\lVert z\right\rVert<k
. The constant
\beta
scales the barrier function
and its corresponding feedback term but does not change this admissible
domain.
Define the virtual velocity and
velocity error as

\displaystyle\alpha={}

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{d}+\frac{\dot{k}}{k}e-K_{1}e-\frac{a\dot{\gamma}^{\,2}e\left\lVert e\right\rVert^{2}}{\beta D},

(7)

\displaystyle s={}

\displaystyle\dot{q}-\alpha,

(8)

where
K_{1}\succ 0
and
a>0
. The commanded input is

u_{\rm cmd}=T_{a}-K_{2}s-\frac{\gamma z}{\beta D},\qquad K_{2}\succ 0.

(9)

Since
\gamma=1
for
t\geq T_{c}
, the barrier acts directly on the
joint-position error after the prescribed transition time.

III-A

Actor–Critic Compensation

Let
\phi_{a}(x_{a})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{a}}
and
\phi_{c}(x_{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{c}}
be radial basis function (RBF) feature maps with fixed centers and widths. With actor matrix
\hat{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{a}\times n}
and critic
weights
\hat{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{c}}
,

T_{a}=\hat{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=\hat{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi_{c}\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

The implementation uses

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{A}}={}

\displaystyle-\sigma_{a}\phi_{a}\left(T_{a}+\frac{s}{\sigma_{a}}+\kappa\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{1}_{n}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}-\sigma_{a}\chi_{a}\hat{A},

(11)

\displaystyle\xi={}

\displaystyle\ell+\hat{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\pi_{c},\qquad\dot{\hat{c}}=-\sigma_{c}\xi\pi_{c}-\sigma_{c}\chi_{c}\hat{c},

(12)

where
\ell=[e^{\mathsf{T}}\;s^{\mathsf{T}}]Q[e^{\mathsf{T}}\;s^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}+u_{\rm ap}^{\mathsf{T}}Ru_{\rm ap}
,
with
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
,
Q\succeq 0
, and

R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
R\succ 0
. The critic approximates the discounted cost

\mathcal{C}(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-(\tau-t)/\nu}\ell(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad\nu>0.

(13)

The gains
\sigma_{a},\sigma_{c},\chi_{a},\chi_{c}
, and
\kappa
are positive.
Here
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of ones. To avoid numerical
differentiation,
\pi_{c}
uses a stable feature filter:

\tau_{f}\dot{\bar{\phi}}_{c}+\bar{\phi}_{c}=\phi_{c},\qquad\pi_{c}=(\phi_{c}-\bar{\phi}_{c})/\tau_{f}-\phi_{c}/\nu.

(14)

Here
\tau_{f}>0
is the feature-filter time constant. The filter acts only on
the critic feature vector and supplies a filtered approximation of

\dot{\phi}_{c}
; it does not alter the tracking error used in the barrier.
The update laws satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sigma_{c}}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\hat{c}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ell^{2}}{4}-\chi_{c}\left\lVert\hat{c}\right\rVert^{2},

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sigma_{a}}\frac{d}{dt}\left\lVert\hat{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\lVert\frac{s}{\sigma_{a}}+\kappa\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{1}_{n}\right\rVert^{2}-\chi_{a}\left\lVert\hat{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

(16)

Therefore, bounded stage cost and tracking signals imply bounded actor and
critic weights on the considered operating region. These estimates establish
boundedness but do not imply convergence of the weights, value function, or
control policy. The constraint analysis below does not require such
convergence.

III-B

Applied Torque and Constraint Analysis

The applied torque is written as

u_{\rm ap}=u_{\rm cmd}+\Delta u,

(17)

where
\Delta u
includes saturation, rate limits, sample-and-hold error,
and interface effects. Define

f=C\alpha+g+M\dot{\alpha}-d,\qquad r_{c}=T_{a}-f+\Delta u.

(18)

Theorem 1

Consider the closed-loop system under
(
7
)–(
9
), with
T_{c}>0
and
\gamma
defined by
(
4
).
Suppose
q_{d}
and
k
are twice continuously
differentiable and are bounded together with their derivatives up to second
order, with
k\geq k_{\min}>0
. Assume that

\left\lVert r_{c}\right\rVert\leq\bar{r}
and

\underline{m}I\preceq M\preceq\bar{m}I
hold throughout the considered
closed-loop operation. Then the time-varying set defined by

\left\lVert z(t)\right\rVert<k(t)
is forward invariant. Consequently,

\left\lVert e(t)\right\rVert<k(t),\qquad t\geq T_{c},

(19)

without requiring
\left\lVert e(0)\right\rVert<k(0)
.

Proof:

Consider the Lyapunov function candidate

V=B+\frac{1}{2}s^{\mathsf{T}}Ms.

Differentiating
B
in (
6
) and substituting
(
7
) gives

\dot{B}=-\frac{z^{\mathsf{T}}K_{1}z}{\beta D}+\frac{\gamma z^{\mathsf{T}}s}{\beta D}+X-aX^{2},\qquad X=\frac{\gamma\dot{\gamma}\left\lVert e\right\rVert^{2}}{\beta D}.

(20)

Since
M\dot{s}+Cs=u_{\rm ap}-f
, the skew-symmetry property and
(
9
), (
17
), and (
18
) yield

\dot{V}=-\frac{z^{\mathsf{T}}K_{1}z}{\beta D}-s^{\mathsf{T}}K_{2}s+s^{\mathsf{T}}r_{c}+X-aX^{2}.

(21)

Define

\lambda=\min\left\{2\lambda_{\min}(K_{1}),\frac{\lambda_{\min}(K_{2})}{\bar{m}}\right\},\qquad\eta=\frac{\bar{r}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\min}(K_{2})}.

(22)

Using
X-aX^{2}\leq 1/(4a)
,

s^{\mathsf{T}}r_{c}\leq\lambda_{\min}(K_{2})\left\lVert s\right\rVert^{2}/2+\eta
, and

\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}/(\beta D)\geq 2B
, we obtain

\dot{V}\leq-\lambda V+\eta+\mathbf{1}_{{t<T_{c}}}/(4a)
.
The comparison lemma then gives
V(t)\leq H(t)
, where

\displaystyle H(t):={}

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda t}V(0)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-\tau)}\left[\eta+\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<T_{c}\}}}{4a}\right]\,d\tau.

(23)

These estimates hold on the maximal interval over which
D>0
. Since

D(0)=k^{2}(0)>0
and
H(t)
is finite on every finite interval,

\frac{D}{k^{2}}=e^{-2\beta B}\geq e^{-2\beta V}\geq e^{-2\beta H}.

Consequently,

\frac{\left\lVert z(t)\right\rVert}{k(t)}\leq\sqrt{1-e^{-2\beta H(t)}}<1.

(24)

The trajectory therefore cannot reach the boundary
\left\lVert z\right\rVert=k
in finite

\left\lVert z(t)\right\rVert<k(t)
is forward invariant. Finally,

\gamma=1
and
z=e
for
t\geq T_{c}
, which proves
(
19
).
∎

The residual bound in Theorem
1
includes the effect of
actuator saturation and the control interface. A bound on
u_{\rm ap}
alone
does not guarantee a bound on

\Delta u=\operatorname{sat}(u_{\rm cmd})-u_{\rm cmd}
because the raw barrier command can
grow as
D
approaches zero. The theorem must therefore be applied on an
operating region where the assumed residual bound is valid. The numerical map in Section
IV
empirically examines which initial-error and transition-time combinations satisfy the final bound under the specified torque limits; it is not a numerical verification of the
residual assumption.

During the transition, the bound on
z
should not be divided by
\gamma

because
\gamma(0)=0
. Boundedness of the direct error can instead be examined
by defining
x=e/k
. Its upper-right Dini derivative satisfies

\mathrm{D}^{+}\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq-\lambda_{\min}(K_{1})\left\lVert x\right\rVert+\frac{\left\lVert s\right\rVert}{k}.

(25)

The term containing
\dot{\gamma}^{2}
is radial and nonpositive in this
derivative. Together with the comparison bound on
s
,
(
25
) keeps the direct error finite while
\gamma
increases
from zero.

Behavior after the transition

For
t\geq T_{c}
, the transition-dependent term vanishes, and
(
23
) gives

\displaystyle V(t)\leq{}

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda(t-T_{c})}H(T_{c})

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\left[1-e^{-\lambda(t-T_{c})}\right].

(26)

A nonzero residual therefore produces a bounded tracking neighborhood. If the
residual is zero, the bound decays. The corresponding asymptotic error ratio
satisfies

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert e(t)\right\rVert}{k(t)}\leq\sqrt{1-\exp(-2\beta\eta/\lambda)}.

(27)

Because the controller parameters also affect the realized residual, this
bound should not be used independently for gain selection.

Selecting the transition time

The transition function in (
4

\left\lVert\dot{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\pi/(2T_{c})
. A shorter
T_{c}
requires the robot to
reduce a given initial error more quickly and increases the
transition-dependent term in (
7

therefore be selected together with the expected initial-error range and the
available torque and rate limits. The numerical map in

2
shows this relation for the tested settings and retains the

At each sample, the controller first computes

,

,

,
\alpha
, and

, followed by the RBF features and
T_{a}
. The command in
(

weights. The value of

is checked before evaluating terms containing

III-C

Parameter Selection for Robot Tasks

The envelope should be chosen from the task before the feedback gains are
tuned. If it represents tracking accuracy,
k
can follow the tolerance of a
machining, inspection, or handover phase. If it is also meant to imply joint
travel limits, the desired trajectory must retain componentwise clearance of
at least
k
. The scalar Euclidean envelope is conservative for that purpose,
but it avoids switching between jointwise minima and keeps
\dot{k}
well
defined. A smooth conservative approximation is preferable when several
task margins have to be combined.

The transition time is then selected subject to the available actuator
limits. For a known range of task-switch errors, one can sweep
T_{c}
using the
same torque and rate limits that will be applied online. The resulting map
does not prove feasibility between sampled points, but it identifies transition
times that are clearly too short. During commissioning, the barrier margin

D/k^{2}
and both the commanded and applied torques should be logged. A
decreasing domain margin together with sustained saturation indicates that

T_{c}
should be increased rather than modifying
D
numerically.

K_{1}
sets the nominal contraction of the normalized position error, while

K_{2}
damps the velocity mismatch. Raising either gain can shorten the
transient but also increases the raw command. The coefficient
a
appears in
the activation correction and in the proof term
1/(4a)
; changing it therefore
affects both transient input and the conservatism of the comparison bound.
The parameter
\beta
scales the logarithmic storage and barrier feedback but
does not widen the admissible set. These quantities should first be set with
the adaptive weights fixed at zero. Actor and critic gains can then be
increased only while the base controller retains an adequate domain margin.
This ordering is useful because the theorem needs a bound on the realized
residual, not successful convergence of a learning algorithm.

The proof is continuous-time. In a digital implementation, the feature
filter, learner, barrier calculation, and actuator interface are all sampled,
so intersample constraint preservation does not follow from
Theorem
1
. The step-size check below addresses numerical convergence of the
reported simulation, while a hardware certificate would additionally require
a sampled-data analysis or a measured intersample margin.

VI
Discussion and Conclusion

The progressive scaling resolves the initialization problem of a logarithmic
barrier when the initial joint error lies outside the required tracking bound.
At the initial time, the scaled error is zero and the barrier is well defined.
The scaling then increases smoothly until it becomes one, after which the
barrier acts directly on the joint-position error. Under the stated residual
condition, the final tracking bound is satisfied after the prescribed
transition time.

The two-link simulations show both the capability and the practical limit of
this approach. All three controllers remain within the final bound at the
logged post-transition samples in the selected perturbation cases. Across
these cases, the proposed controller gives the lowest median RMS error, the
smallest worst-case normalized error, and a lower median last-entry time than
computed torque. Compared with the same progressive barrier controller
without learning, the actor–critic term reduces the median RMS error by
45.1%. The two progressive controllers have the same
median entry time, indicating that learning improves tracking accuracy and
the constraint margin rather than determining the transition speed.

The feasibility map shows why the transition time cannot be selected
independently of the initial error and actuator torque. A large initial error
combined with a short transition requires more torque than the actuator can
provide. Increasing the transition time allows a wider range of initial errors
to be handled. This relation is important when the controller is used together
with a motion planner: the planner specifies a collision-free reference, the
required joint-tracking tolerance, and the time by which that tolerance should
hold. Joint limits and collision avoidance before this time must still be
handled by the planner or an independent safety layer.

The physical record shows single-joint tracking using measured joint-position
and motor-current signals. Its full-record RMS error is
1.154
∘
, decreasing to 1.125
∘
after the initial
portion of the motion. This experiment supports implementation on physical
hardware, while the initially out-of-bound condition and coupled-joint
operation are evaluated in simulation. Future work will extend the experiment
to repeated multi-joint trials with calibrated torque, learner states, and
barrier variables recorded directly.

The results show that progressive barrier control provides a practical
closed-form method for robot tracking when the required error bound cannot be
satisfied initially. The method preserves a direct interpretation of the
joint-position bound, includes adaptive compensation for uncertain dynamics,
and makes the tradeoff between transition time, initial error, and available
torque explicit.
