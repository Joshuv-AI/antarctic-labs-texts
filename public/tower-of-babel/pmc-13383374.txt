BiatriaL cardiac extension of Burkitt lymphoma: a case report of successful surgical correction

Background

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, and cardiac involvement by Burkitt lymphoma is exceptionally uncommon, particularly when presenting with extensive intracavitary growth [1, 2]. When present, cardiac lymphomas may rapidly compromise hemodynamics through obstruction of venous inflow, valvular dysfunction, or impaired cardiac filling, frequently resulting in acute, life-threatening conditions requiring urgent surgical intervention [3, 4]. Establishing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) in such scenarios can be technically challenging, especially when conventional venous cannulation sites are partially or completely obstructed by tumor mass [5].

We report a unique case of biatrial cardiac Burkitt lymphoma causing near-complete obstruction of venous return, in which standard inferior vena cava (IVC) cannulation was not feasible during emergency surgery. Femoral vein cannulation was impossible due to very prominent lower extremity edema. An unconventional endotracheal tube–assisted IVC cannulation technique was successfully employed to rapidly achieve adequate venous drainage. To our knowledge, no previous reports have specifically described the application of this method in cardiac lymphoma. This case highlights a potential bailout strategy in extreme intraoperative circumstances. This case report was prepared in accordance with CARE guidelines.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male was admitted on an emergency basis to a tertiary cardiac surgery center with signs of severe hemodynamic compromise. He presented in critical condition with progressive generalized weakness, dyspnea at rest, bilateral lower-extremity edema, and episodes of cough with blood-tinged sputum. The patient had no history of chronic kidney disease, malignancy, or prior cardiac surgery. There was no family history of hematologic or oncologic disorders, and no relevant genetic conditions were reported. Social history was unremarkable, with no substance abuse or occupational exposures (Fig. 1).

CARE-based flow diagram illustrating the clinical course, diagnostic evaluation, surgical management, and follow-up

On physical examination, the patient was tachycardic (113 bpm) and hypotensive (85/50 mmHg), with jugular venous distension, bilateral lower-extremity edema, muffled heart sounds, and reduced breath sounds at both lung bases.

Laboratory investigations revealed: red blood cells 4.51 × 10¹²/L, hemoglobin 122 g/L, white blood cells 11.16 × 10⁹/L, platelets 250 × 10⁹/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 31 mm/h, total protein 58.6 g/L, creatinine 96 µmol/L, glucose 5.2 mmol/L, urea 4.8 mmol/L, alanine aminotransferase 70 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 43 U/L, total bilirubin 26.6 µmol/L, cholesterol 3.24 mmol/L, and procalcitonin 0.128 ng/mL.

Chest radiography demonstrated left-sided hydrothorax with decreased lung aeration due to pleural effusion. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 115 bpm.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular systolic function (EDV 69 mL, ESV 23 mL, EF 67%). Mild mitral, aortic, and tricuspid regurgitation (grade I) was present. Mean pulmonary artery pressure was 10 mmHg. Large intracavitary masses were visualized in both atria, causing obstruction of blood flow at the level of the atrioventricular valves. In the left atrium, a mass measuring 3.6 × 2.5 cm was identified along the posterolateral wall, likely originating from the left upper pulmonary vein and nearly filling the atrial cavity with intermittent mitral valve obstruction (Fig. 2A). A larger heterogeneous mass measuring 6.0 × 4.5 cm occupied the right atrium, completely obstructing tricuspid inflow with possible extension toward the ventricular cavity.

(A) Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography (apical four-chamber view) demonstrating large tumor masses occupying both atria (arrows), resulting in severe obstruction of atrioventricular inflow (B) Gross specimen of friable tumor fragments removed from the right and left atrial cavities during emergency surgical debulking

Multidetector computed tomography confirmed marked biatrial enlargement with large soft-tissue–density masses. The right atrial mass measured 70 × 60 mm, extending into the distal inferior vena cava and right atrial appendage, while the left atrial mass measured up to 53 × 35 mm, extending into the proximal pulmonary veins. A small pericardial effusion (4–6 mm) was also noted.

Preoperative PET/CT imaging was not feasible due to the emergency nature of the case, which limits direct comparison with follow-up imaging and represents a study limitation. Diagnostic challenges included the critical hemodynamic condition of the patient, which limited the ability to perform comprehensive preoperative oncological staging.

Differential diagnosis included intracardiac thrombus, myxoma, and metastatic tumor. However, imaging characteristics and rapid clinical deterioration were more consistent with a malignant intracardiac neoplasm.

Given the profound hemodynamic compromise and imminent risk of sudden circulatory collapse, emergency surgical intervention was indicated. Preoperative imaging suggested a high likelihood of difficulty with standard IVC cannulation. A stepwise cannulation strategy was planned, including SVC cannulation and attempted IVC access, with consideration of alternative techniques.

The patient underwent emergency median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). The heart was carefully dissected free from adhesions. Due to partial tumor extension into the inferior vena cava (IVC), conventional IVC cannulation and snaring were not feasible. To initiate CPB, the ascending aorta was cannulated using a 22 Fr DLP™ straight-tip arterial cannula (Medtronic Inc., MN, USA), and the superior vena cava was cannulated with a 28 Fr DLP™ single-stage venous cannula with right-angle metal tip (Medtronic Inc., MN, USA). CPB was initially established at a flow rate of approximately 1.6–1.8 L/min/m² during the initiation phase. Prior to effective venous drainage, central venous pressure (CVP) was elevated (10–16 mmHg), reflecting impaired venous return due to tumor-related obstruction. Following right atriotomy, large friable tumor masses were identified occupying the right atrial cavity, infiltrating the atrial wall and partially extending into the IVC. Tumor debulking of the right atrium was performed (Fig. 2B).

Subsequently, a cuffed 8.0-mm endotracheal tube (ETT) was inserted using a conventional IVC cannulation approach (Fig. 3). The uninflated tube was advanced until the cuff reached the intrahepatic segment of the IVC, after which the cuff was inflated to secure positioning. The tube was then connected to the venous line using a 3/8-inch connector, enabling effective venous drainage. Following endotracheal tube–assisted inferior vena cava cannulation, adequate venous return was achieved, allowing escalation to full CPB flows of 2.4–2.6 L/min/m² without the need for vacuum-assisted drainage. Subsequently, CVP decreased to 0–2 mmHg, with no evidence of venous congestion or impaired preload, confirming effective decompression of the venous system and adequate drainage. No evidence of vascular injury, air entrainment, or tumor embolization was observed intraoperatively.

Intraoperative view demonstrating endotracheal tube–assisted inferior vena cava cannulation used to establish venous return during cardiopulmonary bypass in the presence of tumor-related venous obstruction

Left atriotomy revealed similarly large friable masses, with invasion into the atrial wall and partial extension into the pulmonary veins. Debulking of the left atrial tumor was performed. After completion of tumor debulking, no residual obstructive masses were identified. Hemostasis was achieved, and the atrial incisions were closed. The patient was successfully weaned from CPB, and the ETT was removed from the IVC without evidence of vascular injury or stenosis. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed restored unobstructed atrioventricular flow and preserved ventricular function.

Specimens were sent for histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis, which confirmed the diagnosis of Burkitt lymphoma, characterized by a high proliferation index (Ki-67 > 95%) and c-MYC translocation (Fig. 4).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis of tumor tissue demonstrating c-MYC gene translocation in 100% of analyzed cells, consistent with Burkitt lymphoma

Early postoperative echocardiography confirmed complete resolution of the atrioventricular obstruction with preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 65%). The immediate postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was successfully extubated 4 h after surgery. The patient was discharged postoperative day 12 in stable condition, with referral for urgent systemic chemotherapy and scheduled cardiology follow-up. Following surgical stabilization, the patient received systemic chemotherapy according to institutional protocols for Burkitt lymphoma, including an intensive rituximab-based CODOX-M/IVAC regimen with central nervous system prophylaxis using intrathecal chemotherapy. Preventive measures for tumor lysis syndrome, including aggressive hydration, uric acid–lowering therapy, and close metabolic monitoring, were also implemented. The patient tolerated chemotherapy well, with no major treatment interruptions or dose reductions required.

At one-year follow-up after surgery and completion of six cycles of chemotherapy, ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated mild FDG uptake in the anterior mediastinum, most consistent with thymic hyperplasia and post-therapy changes (Fig. 5). No other areas of pathological FDG uptake were detected, with no evidence of active lymphoma (Deauville score 1).

Follow-up ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT one year after surgery and chemotherapy showing no evidence of FDG-avid lymphoma

Discussion and conclusions

Primary cardiac lymphoma (PCL) represents an exceptionally rare clinical entity, accounting for less than 2% of all primary cardiac tumors and approximately 0.5% of extranodal lymphomas [3, 6]. A focused literature review was performed using PubMed and Scopus databases with the search terms “cardiac lymphoma”, “Burkitt lymphoma heart”, and “cardiac tumor surgery”. Articles published up to 2025 were screened, with inclusion of reports describing cardiac involvement of lymphoma, particularly those requiring surgical intervention. The majority of reported cases involve diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, whereas cardiac involvement by Burkitt lymphoma is extraordinarily uncommon, with only isolated cases described in the literature [4] (Table 1). To the best of our knowledge, only 22 such cases have been published in the current medical literature.

Cardiac lymphomas most frequently affect the right-sided chambers, particularly the right atrium, owing to lymphatic drainage patterns and lower intracavitary pressure [7]. Biatrial involvement, as observed in the present case, is extremely rare and is typically associated with rapid tumor progression and profound hemodynamic deterioration [8]. Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and include dyspnea, peripheral edema, arrhythmias, or signs of right-sided heart failure, frequently leading to delayed diagnosis [4].

The distinction between primary and secondary cardiac lymphoma remains diagnostically challenging, particularly in the setting of acute hemodynamic compromise. In the present case, the predominant intracardiac tumor burden and absence of clinically evident extracardiac disease at presentation supported the initial consideration of a primary cardiac lymphoma. However, the pattern of tumor extension, involving both the inferior vena cava and pulmonary venous structures, raises the possibility of secondary cardiac involvement. Importantly, comprehensive preoperative oncological staging, including positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) and bone marrow biopsy, could not be performed due to the urgent need for surgical intervention. Therefore, the diagnosis should be interpreted with caution, and this case is more appropriately described as a cardiac lymphoma with predominant intracardiac manifestation. This limitation highlights the challenges of diagnostic classification in emergent settings and underscores the importance of integrating clinical, imaging, and pathological findings in such complex presentations.

Systemic chemotherapy is considered the cornerstone of treatment for cardiac lymphoma, as surgical resection alone does not provide oncologic control [9]. However, emergency surgical intervention may be life-saving in selected patients with critical obstruction of cardiac inflow or outflow, cardiac tamponade, or refractory heart failure [10, 11]. In such cases, surgery serves primarily as a bridging strategy, enabling hemodynamic stabilization and allowing subsequent initiation of definitive chemotherapy.

Although the surgical procedure itself was not technically complex, this case highlights the importance of intraoperative decision-making in the setting of emergent conditions. Establishing cardiopulmonary bypass in patients with intracardiac lymphoma may present substantial technical challenges. Tumor invasion of the inferior vena cava or atrial walls can render standard venous cannulation impossible or unsafe [12]. Several alternative strategies have been reported, including femoral cannulation, dual-stage venous cannulas, or partial bypass techniques; however, each carries limitations in the setting of extensive intracardiac obstruction [13].

Femoral venous cannulation was considered but deemed suboptimal due to severe lower-extremity edema indicative of significant venous congestion, which could compromise effective venous drainage. In addition, tumor extension into the inferior vena cava raised concerns regarding partial luminal obstruction, difficulty in cannula advancement, and the risk of inadequate flow, while the friable nature of the tumor increased the potential for fragmentation and embolization during manipulation. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) was also evaluated; however, it was considered unsuitable as it does not provide direct intracardiac decompression or address mechanical obstruction caused by large intracavitary masses. Moreover, compromised venous return could have limited ECMO flow and made its initiation technically challenging. Vacuum-assisted venous drainage (VAVD) was similarly deemed insufficient, as its effectiveness relies on unobstructed venous inflow, which was likely impaired by tumor involvement of the inferior vena cava, and the application of negative pressure raised additional concerns regarding tumor fragmentation and embolization. Given the patient’s hemodynamic instability and the need for rapid, reliable venous return with direct relief of intracardiac obstruction, immediate establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass via alternative access was considered the most appropriate strategy. Finally, circulatory arrest was considered; however, the use of hypothermic circulatory arrest would have introduced additional risks, including neurological injury and coagulopathy, without resolving the primary challenge of achieving adequate venous drainage prior to bypass initiation.

Given the anticipated challenges related to inferior vena cava involvement, the use of an endotracheal tube was predefined as a contingency strategy within the preoperative plan, to be employed in case conventional cannulation proved ineffective. The use of a cuffed endotracheal tube as an alternative venous cannulation strategy has been previously described by Stoklosa et al. [5], who reported its successful application as a temporary venous return cannula during cardiopulmonary bypass in complex surgical circumstances. In their technical report, the authors demonstrated that inflation of the endotracheal tube cuff within a large venous structure can provide an effective seal and allow adequate venous drainage when conventional cannulation is not feasible. However, the application described by Stoklosa et al. was limited to non-oncologic cardiac surgery and did not involve intravascular tumor obstruction. In contrast, the present case represents the first reported use of endotracheal tube–assisted inferior vena cava cannulation in the setting of extensive intracardiac lymphoma, where venous inflow was nearly completely obstructed by friable tumor tissue. This distinction is clinically relevant, as tumor-related venous obstruction poses unique challenges, including distorted anatomy, high risk of embolization, and inability to safely place standard cannulas or snare the vena cava. In such scenarios, rapid establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass is critical to prevent circulatory collapse. The technique adapted from Stoklosa et al. provided a simple, immediately available, and effective bailout option, enabling controlled tumor debulking and hemodynamic stabilization.

In our case, the adequacy of venous return was ensured by careful positioning of the ETT within the inferior vena cava and controlled inflation of the cuff to optimize the seal and minimize blood leakage, thereby improving drainage efficiency. To reduce the risk of vascular injury, the ETT was inserted under direct surgical control with gentle manipulation, avoiding excessive advancement or force. The use of a soft, compliant tube rather than a rigid cannula may have further reduced the risk of endothelial trauma. Given the friable nature of Burkitt lymphoma, particular attention was paid to minimizing tumor manipulation. The risk of tumor fragmentation and embolization was mitigated by limiting instrumentation within the tumor-involved segment of the IVC and avoiding excessive negative pressure. Notably, no intraoperative or postoperative embolic events were observed.

This technique has several important limitations and should be applied with caution. Its use may be restricted in cases with a small inferior vena cava diameter, extensive intraluminal thrombus, or when prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass is anticipated. In addition, this approach should not be considered a standard cannulation strategy but rather a bailout or adjunctive option in highly selected cases with challenging venous access. Careful patient selection and awareness of these limitations are essential to ensure both safety and procedural success.

Our patient represents an extremely rare case of cardiac biatrial involvement by Burkitt lymphoma. Emergent surgical intervention offers effective hemodynamic stabilization, which is essential for subsequent chemotherapy. Technically, the procedure is challenging due to the extensive ingrowth of tumor tissue into the cardiac chambers and structures, providing a valid, effective, and simple bailout in cases when direct IVC or femoral cannulation for venous drainage is impossible.
