Post activation potentiation effect of different preloading protocols on sprint performance
Authors: Seyfullah Celik, Gamze Erikoglu Orer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the postactivation potentiation effects of different preloading protocols involving blood flow-restricted plyometric jumps on sprint performance. In this randomized crossover experimental study, 15 athletes with at least seven years of sporting experience participated voluntarily. The participants underwent several anthropometric measurements (height, body weight, body mass index, and body fat percentage) as well as performance measurements (10-meter and 20-meter sprints). The data were analyzed using the SPSS statistical software. The effect size and percentage changes are reported to illustrate the practical significance of the differences. Cohen’s d test was used to determine the effect size. Differences between repeated measures data were analyzed using repeated-measures one-way ANOVA. Tukey’s post hoc test was used to determine the protocols responsible for the differences. The eta-squared (η²) value was used to express the effect size. The significance level was set at p = 0.05, with 95% confidence interval. In conclusion, all preloading protocols demonstrated a positive post-activation potentiation effect on sprint performance. However, the greatest potentiating effect was observed with the preloading protocol involving blood flow-restricted plyometric jumps with a weighted vest.

Introduction

The blood flow restriction (BFR) method involves exercising with external equipment applied to the proximal muscles of the limb to physically restrict arterial blood flow1–4. When low-intensity exercises (20–40% of 1RM) are performed in combination with blood flow restrictions created by equipment that prevents venous return to the area, adaptations similar to those resulting from high-intensity exercises (60–70% of 1RM and above) occur, including increased muscle hypertrophy and strength. Studies have reported that low-intensity exercise with blood flow restriction (20–50% of 1RM) leads to faster muscle hypertrophy than does conventional high-intensity exercise5–10. One of the main advantages of using blood flow restriction is that it allows for gains similar to those obtained with high resistance training, but with reduced intensity and lower joint stress, which has been a key factor in promoting its use as an alternative6,11–17.

Post-activation potentiation (PAP) is defined as an increase in force production following a near-maximal muscle contraction performed during a preloading protocol1. It occurs after activities involving high-intensity voluntary muscle contractions, known as preloading. The aim is to enhance the performance during subsequent explosive activities by performing these preloading activities beforehand. Muscles contracted during preloading activities have been reported to positively influence performance in subsequent contractions18,19. Recent studies have indicated that blood flow-restricted (BFR) exercise can increase muscle activation, a significant physiological mechanism behind post-activation potentiation20–24.

A review of the literature revealed that few studies have incorporated the blood flow restriction (BFR) method into preloading protocols. However, the primary aim of these studies was not to investigate the effects of post-activation potentiation on sprint performance24–28. The importance of athletic performance has rapidly increased in recent years. Therefore, studies exploring how to achieve maximum efficiency in post-activation potentiation by preloading protocols without associated risks. On the basis of these considerations, the primary aim of our study was to investigate the effects of different preloading protocols involving blood flow-restricted plyometric jumps on sprint performance and post-activation potentiation.

Materials and methods

Experimental approach to the problem

In this study, a randomized crossover design was employed in which participants underwent all protocols in a random and balanced order29.

Subjects

Fifteen athletes with a minimum of 7 years of sports experience participated voluntarily in the study (Table 3). The group included 4 football players, 4 futsal players, 2 handball players, 2 track and field athletes (short-distance runners), 1 basketball player, 1 rugby player, and 1 field hockey player. The participants were required to declare any known musculoskeletal injuries or cardiovascular conditions on the “Athlete Information and Measurement Form” to be eligible for the study. All participants signed an “Informed Consent Form.” The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the ethics committee of the Ankara Yildirim Beyazit University (No: 2022 − 1157; Date: 10.11.2022). All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Procedures

The “Athlete Information and Measurement Form” developed by the researcher, was used as a data collection tool to identify participants’ descriptive characteristics and record measurement results.

Anthropometric measurements

Heights were measured using a Holtain brand stadiometer (Holtain Ltd., Crymych, Dyfed, UK) with a precision of ± 1 mm. Each participant’s height was recorded in centimeters (cm). The athletes’ body weight, body mass index, and body fat percentage were assessed using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (BC-310, Tanita Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and recorded.

Performance measurements

Warm-Up Protocol: After emptying the bladder and bowels, the participants underwent a modified standard warm-up protocol based on the methods described by Ebben and Petushek. This warm-up method was selected because it aligns with the results of previous studies30.

Sprint Performance Measurement: Sprint performance was measured using photocells (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy) positioned at the starting point, 10 m and 20 m as shown in Fig. 131,32.

The study was conducted over 2 weeks, with a total of 6 sessions. The first week served as the familiarization period (Table 1). On Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, the participants engaged in familiarization sessions with the experimental methods and participated in the reference sprint test measurements. All sessions were consistently scheduled at 10:00 AM to control for daily variations up to the final test day. A minimum of 48 h was allocated between all activities. Additionally, participants were instructed to avoid consuming caffeine and alcohol-containing food and beverages and to refrain from engaging in strenuous activities within 48 h before the sessions. These procedures were completed during the week of familiarization. The following week was designated as the application week. During this period, the athletes were randomly and equally assigned to three groups (A, B, and C). Each group underwent preloading protocols on different days. The participants were prepared by emptying their bladders and bowels for all sessions, as they did before the reference sprint test. The group subjected to blood flow restrictions completed the warm-up protocol first, followed by the blood flow restriction adaptation protocol. The group that did not use blood flow restriction completed the warm-up protocol only. During the application week, all groups participated in sprint tests 5 min after the different preloading protocols were applied on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays.

Blood flow restriction protocol

The Occlusion Caff Pro® device was used to restrict blood flow. The participants were instructed to lie quietly on their backs for 15 min prior to measuring limb occlusion pressure. The cuffs of the device were placed on the proximal parts of both the legs. In accordance with the literature, cuffs with widths ranging from 5 cm to 23 cm were used for the lower extremities. While wider cuffs restrict blood flow with less pressure, they are associated with greater perceived effort. Therefore, the width of the cuffs was set to 7 cm33,34.

A handheld vascular Doppler probe (The Occlusion Cuff Vascular Doppler) was placed on the posterior tibial artery at the ankle to determine limb occlusion pressure. Since the femoral artery was not deemed suitable owing to its location, it was decided to monitor the pulse from the posterior tibial artery. The cuff was inflated to the minimum pressure at which the arterial pulse was not detected, and this pressure level was considered as the limb occlusion pressure35,36.

In this study, the limb occlusion pressure was calculated as 80% of the most commonly used value and was used as the personalized limb occlusion pressure in the application of blood flow-restricted preloading protocols (Table 2)37,38.

Blood flow restriction adaptation protocol

After the warm-up protocol on each test day, an adaptation protocol was applied to stimulate the peripheral and central circulation before restricting blood flow with personalized limb occlusion pressure. Following the warm-up protocol, the participants stood in an anatomically upright position. The cuffs of the Occlusion Cuff Pro® device were then applied to the proximal parts of both legs (Fig. 2). The device was initially set to a pressure of 50 mmHg, and blood flow was restricted at this pressure for 30 s. Subsequently, a 10-second rest period was provided. The pressure was then increased in 10–20 mmHg increments until personalized limb occlusion pressure was reached. At each increment, a 30-second application was followed by a 10-second rest. Once the personalized limb occlusion pressure was reached, the cuffs were removed after 30 s, and participants transitioned to the preloading protocol after a 1-minute rest39–41.

Blood flow restriction.

Preloading protocol

The double leg bound was performed in preloading protocols with 15 repetitions and three sets, totaling 45 repetitions per preloading protocol (Fig. 3). The rest period between the sets was 30 s. When using the blood flow restriction method with low or no resistance was used, the ideal structure consisted of 4 sets of 75 repetitions. A study related to blood flow restriction recommended a training structure of 3–4 sets, 15–25 repetitions, and 30–45 s of rest. Therefore, in this study, preloading protocols were designed with multiple sets and increased repetitions to achieve both post-activation potentiation effects and the recommended ideal structure for blood flow restriction applications42,43.

Double leg bound.

Usage of a weighted vest

Studies have shown that performing plyometric jumps with a vest equivalent to 10% of body weight is an effective method for creating post-activation potentiation, thus enhancing sprint and acceleration performance. As shown in Fig. 4, in the preloading protocol consisting of plyometric exercises with additional weight, the weight vest was adjusted to be equivalent to 10% of the body weight31,32.

Weighted vest.

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed using the SPSS 22 software. Descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum values, were provided. Normality tests were conducted using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Additionally, the data were examined using histograms, boxplots, and Q-Q plots. Differences between repeated measurements were analyzed using one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Bonferroni post hoc correction was used to identify the protocols responsible for the differences. The effect size was expressed using eta squared (η²) and classified as follows: no effect ≤ 0.039, minimum = 0.04–0.24; moderate = 0.25–0.63 and strong ≥ 0.6444. To determine the practical significance of the differences between sprint tests before and after the preloading protocols, effect sizes and percentage changes were determined. Cohen’s d test was used to determine the effect size, which was classified according to Hopkins’ 2002 Tables (45,46). The percentage changes between measurements (%Δ) were calculated using the formula shown in Fig. 547,48. The reliability level in the study was interpreted on the basis of a confidence level of 95% and significance level of p = 0.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the effects of post-activation potentiation of different preloading protocols combined with blood flow-restricted plyometric jumps on sprint performance. Athletes completed adaptation sessions (three sessions over one week) and preloading protocols (three sessions over one week) for a total of six sessions using a randomized, balanced crossover research design. Fifteen male athletes participated in the study. During the implementation week (three sessions over one week), they performed different preloading protocols on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, followed by sprint tests 5 min later. The findings are interpreted and discussed in this section, on the basis of the literature.

A review of the literature, revealed that few studies investigated the post-activation potentiation effects of preloading on sprint performance, although this number is gradually increasing. Chatzopoulos et al.49 subjected participants to preloading by performing a single set of squats at 90% of their one-repetition maximum (1RM) for 10 repetitions. Sprint performance was measured and evaluated at the 3rd and 5th minutes after preloading. The study reported no change in sprint performance at the 3rd minute after preloading, but a 3% improvement in 10-meter sprint time and a 2% improvement in 30-meter sprint time at the 5th minute after preloading. Bevan et al.50 conducted a study with 16 professional rugby players, utilizing a preloading protocol consisting of three back squats at 91% of 1RM. This study reported a 5% improvement in 5-meter sprint performance and an 8% improvement in 10-meter sprint performance as a result of the preloading protocol. Bevan et al.51 also administered a preloading protocol consisting of a single set of squats with three repetitions at 91% of 1RM to the participants. The results indicated positive effects of post-activation potentiation on 10-meter sprint times. Comyns et al.52 administered a preloading protocol consisting of maximal squats with three repetitions to professional rugby players. An improvement in 30-meter sprint performance was reported 4 min after the application of the preloading protocol. DeRenne53 reported that 10-meter sprint performance increased with a positive post-activation potentiation effect 5 min after the application of a preloading protocol consisting of back squats with 10 repetitions at 90% of one repetition maximum (1RM). Crewther et al.54 administered a preloading protocol of back squats at three times the individual’s body mass (3 TM) in their study on rugby players. The results revealed a 2.6% improvement in 5-meter sprint performance and a 1.8% improvement in 10-meter sprint performance. Okuno et al.55 reported an increase in sprint performance following preloading protocols in their study with handball players. Seitz, Trajano, and Haff56 applied a preloading protocol of a single set squat exercise consisting of 3 repetitions at 90% of 1RM to rugby players. An improvement in 20-meter sprint performance was reported 7 min after the preloading protocol, and athletes showed increased average speed and average acceleration values compared with the reference values. Byrne, Kenny, and O’Rourke57 compared the post-activation potentiation effects of control, dynamic warm-up, and dynamic warm-up plus 3 depth jumps preloading protocols on 20-meter sprint performance. The recovery period was 1 min. The 20-meter sprint performance measured 1 min after preloading consisting of dynamic warm-up and three depth jumps showed a 2.93% improvement. Evetovich, Conley, and McCawley58 reported improvement in sprint performance measured after the application of the preloading protocol in their study with soccer players. Sanchez-Sanchez et al.59 investigated the post-activation potentiation effects of different preloading protocols on repeated sprint performance in their study of eight national and eight regional league soccer players. The participants performed squats at intensities of 60% and 90% of 1RM as the preloading protocol. The study revealed that the highest post-activation potentiation effect on sprint performance improvement occurred with squats at 90% of 1RM. Additionally, greater sprint improvement was observed in national league soccer players. Byrne et al.60 administered a preloading protocol consisting of a single set of drop jumps with three repetitions. The results revealed an improvement in 20-meter sprint performance measured at the 15th second. Overall, the results of these studies have demonstrated that the implementation of preloading protocols leads to post-activation potentiation and improves sprint performance. However, some studies do not support these findings42,54,61,62.

Research has shown post-activation potentiation can be influenced by factors such as an individual’s training level, sports age, chronological age, sex, and muscle fiber type. Additionally, factors such as the type of loading used in the preloading protocol, recovery time provided, and level of fatigue induced by the protocol are important for achieving positive effects from post-activation potentiation43,63–66.

Despite conflicting results regarding the positive effects of post-activation potentiation (PAP) on sprint performance following preloading protocols, researchers have shown that positive PAP effects can be achieved when preloading protocols are designed while considering the factors influencing PAP. Studies conducted to date have generally favored the use of high-intensity traditional exercises as preloading protocols. Scientific evidence indicates that high-intensity exercise is necessary to achieve superior PAP effects67,68. This is because high-intensity exercises are considered effective in generating muscle activation, which is one of the physiological mechanisms underlying PAP. Therefore, preloading protocols often involve loads ranging from 80 to 100% of the one-repetition maximum (1RM)24,69. However, MacIntosh et al. argued that intense fatigue resulting from high-intensity traditional exercise may limit the effect of PAP on athletic performance and may even decrease performance70. Furthermore, high-intensity traditional exercises increase the risk of injury in athletes during preloading18. To induce PAP with preloading protocols, coaches often subject athletes to high loads before competitions, risking injuries and requiring cumbersome weights. The practicality of transporting heavy loads to the competition area for use in preloading protocols has been questioned and is seen as a limitation of PAP studies. Therefore, alternative methods exist32,71. The importance of sports and athletic performance is increasing rapidly every day. Therefore, studies on how to achieve the highest efficiency from post-activation potentiation (PAP) through preloading protocols without incurring risks are needed. In this study, to mitigate the aforementioned risks and simultaneously obtain the highest possible efficiency from PAP, methods other than high-intensity traditional loading were included in the preloading protocols24. The incorporation of plyometric exercises into athletes’ warm-up is a practical approach for inducing a PAP effect72. Similarly, weighted jumps have been reported to enhance subsequent performance32. A meta-analysis conducted by Wilson et al. argued that PAP can be elicited with preloading protocols that do not involve excessively heavy loads66. Consequently, some studies have employed weighted vests in preloading protocols to elicit the effects of PAP on various performance parameters and sprinting31,32,73. Turner et al. investigated the effects of preloading protocols consisting of plyometric jumps with body weight and body weight adjusted by 10% using a weighted vest on sprint and acceleration performance in 23 males with at least 2 years of plyometric jumping experience. The study found that after plyometric jumps with a weighted vest, the 20-meter sprint performance measured at 10-meter intervals increased by 2.2% at the 10-meter mark and 2.3% at the 20-meter mark at the 4th minute, and by 2.9% at the 10-meter mark and 2.6% at the 20-meter mark at the 8th minute. When comparing the effects on the 10-meter sprint performance between the preloading protocol consisting of plyometric jumps with a weighted vest and the preloading protocol consisting solely of plyometric jumps, it was reported that the protocol with plyometric jumps with a weighted vest resulted in the greatest improvement. Thus, in addition to generating more PAP effects than the control group does, the protocol consisting solely of plyometric jumps demonstrated the highest efficacy when a weighted vest was included32. Piper et al.73 examined the effects of different preloading protocols on jump and sprint performance in college students with resistance training experience. After a standard warm-up, the participants’ 20-meter sprint performance was measured. They subsequently performed one of four different protocols and exhibited their 20-meter sprint performance at 10-meter intervals for 20 s at the 4th, 8th, 12th, 16th, and 20th minutes. The study revealed that after performing plyometric jumps with a weighted vest adjusted to 10% of the participant’s body weight, there was no improvement at any time point for the 10-meter mark, whereas the 20-meter sprint time measured at the 8th minute significantly improved. These results support the findings of our study regarding the positive effects of plyometric jumps using a weighted vest adjusted to 10% of body weight as a preloading protocol on sprint performance in terms of post-activation potentiation. Recent research has also indicated that blood flow restriction exercises may increase muscle activation, a significant physiological mechanism underlying post-activation potentiation20–24. Furthermore, because it allows for increased muscle activation without the need for heavy loads, it also provides an opportunity to reduce the risk of injury associated with heavy loads24,74.

A review of the literature revealed that few studies have incorporated blood flow restriction (BFR) into preloading protocols. Some studies have applied the BFR method to preloading protocols and reported that the resulting post-activation potentiation (PAP) enhances athletic performance. However, the primary aim of these studies was not to specifically examine the effects of post-activation potentiation on sprint performance25–28. Yasuda et al. reported that incorporating blood flow restriction during exercise may activate physiological mechanisms that elicit a post-activation potentiation effect28. Doma et al. examined the effects of blood flow-restricted (BFR) lunge exercises as part of preloading on vertical jump performance in 18 trained men. The study revealed that following the BFR lunge exercise preloading protocol, vertical jump height increased by 4.5 ± 0.8% within 6–15 min. There was a significant post-activation potentiation effect on vertical jump performance at 9 min, with a moderate effect at 15 min after the preloading protocol25. Wilk et al. investigated the effects of blood flow restricted (BFR) resistance exercises (bench press) on the post-activation potentiation (PAP) of power output in 10 healthy resistance-trained men. The study concluded that blood flow restriction induced PAP and was beneficial for enhancing power output27. Lauber et al. suggested that blood flow restriction facilitates muscle adaptation75. In addition to these findings, Miller et al. reported that although there were positive effects on jump height with the addition of blood flow restriction to the protocols in their study involving 20 recreationally active male individuals, statistically significant differences were not observed26.

In contrast to these findings, Cleary and Cook reported that, in their study with university students who had been engaging in resistance exercises for at least 2 years, blood flow restricted high resistance, low volume, and low resistance, and high volume squat exercise protocols resulted in a negative effect on vertical jump performance. This study attributed the inability to elicit post-activation potentiation and the negative effect on vertical jump performance to fatigue overriding the post-activation potentiation effect. The authors emphasized the importance of establishing appropriate protocols to ensure that fatigue does not negate post-activation potentiation76.

According to the results of these studies, blood flow-restricted (BFR) preloading protocols play a role in the development of post-activation potentiation effects and have the potential to enhance performance. Evaluating the study findings in light of these results suggests that the observed increases in 10- and 20-meter sprint performance can be attributed to the post-activation potentiation effect induced by the BFR preloading protocol. In this study, a preloading protocol consisting of plyometric exercises performed with blood flow restriction and weighted vests was implemented to activate the physiological mechanisms necessary for post-activation potentiation through more intense contractions. Compared with other protocols, the preloading protocol consisting of BFR and weighted vest plyometric jumps (BFR-WV) resulted in greater improvements in both 10-meter and 20-meter sprint performance. No study has been reported in the literature that applies a preloading protocol consisting of plyometric exercises with blood flow restriction and weighted vests and investigates its effects on sprint performance. Considering the positive post-activation potentiation effects of plyometric jumps performed with a weighted vest equivalent to 10% of body weight, along with the role of blood flow restriction in enhancing post-activation potentiation, it can be argued that a combined preloading protocol could activate physiological mechanisms more effectively, leading to greater post-activation potentiation effects25–28,31,32,73.

Conclusions

The conclusion of this study, which aimed to investigate the post-activation potentiation effects of different preloading protocols involving blood flow restricted (BFR) plyometric jumps, revealed that all applied preloading protocols had a positive effect on athletes’ 10- and 20-meter sprint performance. In other words, an increase in sprint performance was observed following the implementation of preloading protocols. However, the post-activation potentiation effect of the preloading protocol consisting of BFR plyometric jumps with weighted vests (BFR-WV) was greater than that of the other protocols. This indicates that the largest improvements in sprint performance were attributed to the BFR-WV preloading protocol. To enhance sprint performance without using high resistance, reducing intensity, and subjecting joints to lower stresses, experts may incorporate preloading protocols with blood flow restrictions. Moreover, this approach is considered a robust alternative, as it eliminates the need to transport heavy equipment to athletes’ competitive areas.

Considering the limitations of this study, the following suggestions are proposed for future research.
