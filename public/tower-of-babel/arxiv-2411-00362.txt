Heterogeneous Multiscale Method for elliptic problem without scale separation
Tao Yu, Xingye Yue, Changjuan Zhang

Abstract
This paper shows that the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method can be applied to elliptic problem without scale separation. The Localized Orthogonal Method is a special case of the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method. Keywords : Heterogeneous Multiscale Method; Localized Orthogonal Method; Elliptic Problems; Scale Separation.



1
Introduction

Consider the classical multiscale elliptic problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a^{\varepsilon}(x)\nabla u(x))=f(x),&x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\displaystyle u=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\varepsilon\ll 1
is a small parameter that represents the
multiscale nature in the problem. Several multiscale methods have been developed for the numerical solution of this multiscale elliptic problem. The most well known method is the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method (HMM), developed in
[
3
]
. The method is applied into elliptic homogenization problem in
[
4
]
and parabolic homogenization problem in
[
9
]
. HMM is a general framework for designing multiscale algorithms. It consists of two components: selection of a macroscopic solver and estimating the missing macroscale data by solving locally the fine scale problem. The convergence analysis usually assumes certain periodicity and scale separation. In this paper, we deduce that HMM can be applied to multiscale elliptic problem without scale separation, and the optimal error estimate can also be obtained for this case.

Recently, a Localized Orthogonal Decomposition (LOD) method was introduced for elliptic multiscale problems in
[
7
]
, fitting into the general framework of the Variational Multiscale Method (VMS)
[
5
]
. The method constructs local generalized finite element basis which are exponential decayed. The analysis does not rely on high regularity of the solution or scale separation in the coefficient. The method is also applied to parabolic problems
[
8
]
. Peterseim review the VMS methods for Linear multiscale PDEs in
[
6
]
and give a new simply proof for the exponential decay property. In this paper, we show that the LOD method is a special case of HMM. Thus, the error estimate for HMM can be obtained from the LOD method directly.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, we review the original definition for the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method. The localization technology is developed in Section 3.

2
Heterogeneous Multiscale Method

In this section, we review the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method, first introduced in
[
3
]
. Consider a microscopic variational problem:

\min_{v\in V}J(v),\hskip 11.74988ptJ(v)=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)-(f,v),

(2.1)

where
V=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and

a(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}a^{\varepsilon}\nabla u\cdot\nabla vdx,\hskip 11.74988pt(f,v)=\int_{\Omega}fvdx.

(2.2)

The exact solution of the microscopic variational problem (
2.1
) is

u=\argmin_{v\in V}J(v).

(2.3)

Let
Q:V\rightarrow V_{h}
be an appropriately chosen compression operator. Here
V_{h}
can be the standard
P^{1}
finite element space. For each macro function
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, define the macroscopic energy

J_{h}(v_{h})=\min_{v\in V,\;Q(v)=v_{h}}J(v),

(2.4)

then the macroscopic variational problem reads

\min_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}J_{h}(v_{h})\triangleq\min_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\left(\min_{v\in V,\;Q(v)=v_{h}}J(v)\right).

(2.5)

Define the reconstruction operator
R:V_{h}\rightarrow V
,
according to the compression operator
Q
by

R(w_{h})=\argmin_{v\in V,\;Q(v)=w_{h}}J(v).

(2.6)

The macroscopic variational problem (
2.5
) can be rewritten as

\min_{v_{h}\in V}J(R(v_{h})).

(2.7)

The corresponding solution can be written as

u_{h}=\argmin_{v_{h}\in V}J(R(v_{h})).

(2.8)

Obviously
R(u_{h})=u
.
Different choice of the compression operator
Q
deduced different multiscale method. In this paper, we choose
Q=P_{0}
be the
L_{2}-
projection, defined by

(P_{0}v,w)=(v,w),\hskip 11.74988pt\forall w\in V_{h}.

(2.9)

For this choice of
Q
, the microscopic space
V
can be decomposed into two parts

V=V_{h}\oplus V_{f},

(2.10)

where
V_{f}={V_{h}}^{\perp}
in the sense of
L_{2}-
projection
P_{0}
.

The following Lemma helps to understanding the reconstruction operator
R
.

Lemma 2.1

Let
u\in V
be the solution of (
2.6
), i.e.,

u=\underset{v\in V,\;P_{0}v=v_{h}}{\argmin}J(v),

(2.11)

then
P_{0}u=v_{h}
and

a(u,w)=(f,w),\ \forall w\in{V_{f}}.

(2.12)

Proof

Rewrite (
2.12
) as an minimal value problem without constrained conditions

(u,P_{0}\mu)=\argmin_{v\in{V},\;\mu(x)\in L_{2}(\Omega)}\left(J(v)+<P_{0}\mu,P_{0}v-v_{h}>\right),

(2.13)

where
P_{0}\mu
is the Lagrange multiplier.
Define two-variables function

G(\lambda,\epsilon)=J(u+\lambda w)+<P_{0}\mu+\epsilon\gamma,P_{0}(u+\lambda w)-v_{h}>,

(2.14)

where
\lambda,\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
,
w\in V
and
\gamma\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. When
\lambda=0
and
\epsilon=0
, function
G(\lambda,\epsilon)
has a minimizer. Hence

\left.\frac{\partial G}{\partial\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0,\epsilon=0}=0,\left.\frac{\partial G}{\partial\epsilon}\right|_{\lambda=0,\epsilon=0}=0.

(2.15)

That is to say

\left.\frac{\partial G}{\partial\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0,\epsilon=0}=a(u,w)-(f,w)+(P_{0}\mu,P_{0}w)=0,\hskip 11.74988pt\forall w\in V,

(2.16)

\left.\frac{\partial G}{\partial\epsilon}\right|_{\lambda=0,\epsilon=0}=(\gamma,P_{0}u-v_{h})=0,\hskip 11.74988pt\forall\gamma\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(2.17)

From (
2.17
), we get

P_{0}u=v_{h}.

(2.18)

Let
w=w_{f}\in{V_{f}}\subset V
, then
P_{0}w=0
by the definition of
V_{f}
. Thus
(P_{0}u,P_{0}w)=0
and

a(u,w)=(f,w),\ \forall w\in{V_{f}},

(2.19)

from (
2.16
).
Denote
u=P_{0}u+\Phi=v_{h}+\Phi
, where
\Phi\in{V_{f}}
. Brought it into (
2.19
),

a(\Phi,w)=(f,w)-a(v_{h},w),\ \forall w\in{V_{f}}.

(2.20)

Through this, we can detest
\Phi
and then determine the solution
u
. After the solution
u
has determined and from (
2.16
),

(P_{0}\mu,P_{0}w)=<<F,w>>\triangleq-a(u,w)+(f,w),

(2.21)

which can determine the Lagrange multiplier
P_{0}\mu
.

Define an approximation of the reconstruction operator
R
by

R_{h}(v_{h})=\argmin_{v\in{V},P_{0}(v)=v_{h}}J_{0}(v),

(2.22)

where
J_{0}(v)=\frac{1}{2}a(v,v)
. Then the corresponding approximation solution
u_{h}^{ms}
is defined by

u_{h}^{ms}=R_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h})=\argmin_{v_{h}\in R_{h}({V_{h}})}J(v_{h}),

(2.23)

where

\tilde{u}_{h}=\argmin_{v_{h}\in{V_{h}}}J(R_{h}(v_{h})).

(2.24)

The following Lemma shows the error between
R(\tilde{u}_{h})
and
R_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h})
.

Lemma 2.2

R
and
R_{h}
are defined by (
2.6
) and (
2.22
), respectively. Then

R(\tilde{u}_{h})=R_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h})+R_{f}(f),

where
R_{f}(f)=\underset{v\in{V_{f}}}{\argmin}J(v)
.

Proof

From Lemma
2.1
,
R(\tilde{u}_{h})
satisfies

a(R(\tilde{u}_{h}),v)=(f,v),\ \forall v\in{V_{f}}.

(2.25)

Similarly,
R_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h})
satisfies

a(R_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h}),v)=0,\ \forall v\in{V_{f}}.

(2.26)

Denote
R_{f}(f)=R(\tilde{u}_{h})-R_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h})
, then

a(R_{f}(f),v)=a(R(\tilde{u}_{h})-R_{h}(\tilde{u}_{h}),v)=(f,v),\ \forall v\in\ {V_{f}},

(2.27)

by subtracting (
2.25
) and (
2.26
).
That is to say
R_{f}(f)=\underset{v\in{V_{f}}}{\argmin}J(v)
.

Lemma 2.3

u_{h}
and
\tilde{u}_{h}
are defined by (
2.8
) and (
2.24
), respectively. Then they are the same solution, i.e.

\tilde{u}_{h}=u_{h}.

(2.28)

Proof

Denote
R_{f}(v_{h})\triangleq R({v_{h}})-R_{h}({v_{h}}),\;\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}
, then

P_{0}(R_{f}(v_{h}))=P_{0}(R({v_{h}}))-P_{0}(R_{h}({v_{h}}))=v_{h}-v_{h}=0

by the definition of
R
and
R_{h}
, i.e.
R_{f}(v_{h})\in{V_{f}}
.
From Lemma
2.2
, the remainder term
R_{f}(v_{h})=\underset{v\in{V_{f}}}{\argmin}J(v)
does not relied on
v_{h}
. Abbreviate
R_{f}(v_{h})
by
R_{f}
and from the definition of energy
J
, then

\displaystyle J(R(v_{h}))\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{2}a(R(v_{h}),R(v_{h}))-(f,R(v_{h}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{2}a(R_{h}(v_{h})+R_{f},R_{h}(v_{h})+R_{f})-(f,R_{h}(v_{h})+R_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{2}a(R_{h}(v_{h}),R_{h}(v_{h}))-(f,R_{h}(v_{h}))+\frac{1}{2}a(R_{f},R_{f})-(f,R_{f})+a(R_{h}(v_{h}),R_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!J(R_{h}(v_{h}))+J(R_{f}),

by
a(R_{h}(v_{h}),w)=0,\;\forall w\in V_{f}
and
R_{f}\in V_{f}
. As
R_{f}
does not relied on
v_{h}
,
J(R_{f})
also does not relied on
v_{h}
. Thus

\argmin_{v_{h}\in{V_{h}}}J(R(v_{h}))=\argmin_{v_{h}\in{V_{h}}}J(R_{h}(v_{h})),

that is to say
u_{h}=\tilde{u}_{h}
.

Define the energy norm
|||\cdot|||:=|||\cdot|||_{\Omega}:=||a^{\varepsilon}\nabla\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
on space
V
. The error estimate between the exact solution
u
and the approximation solution
u_{h}^{ms}
is deduced by the following Theorem.

Theorem 2.4

Let
u\in V
be the exact solution of (
2.1
) and
u_{h}^{ms}
be the approximation solution defined in (
2.23
). Then it holds that

|||u-u_{h}^{ms}|||\leq Ch||f||_{0}

with positive constant
C
that does not depend on
h
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof

Due to Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
, it holds that

u-u_{h}^{ms}=R(u_{h})-R_{h}(\tilde{u_{h}})=R(\tilde{u_{h}})-R_{h}(\tilde{u_{h}})=R_{f}(f)

by the definition (
2.1
) and (
2.23
). The remainder term
R_{f}(f)
satisfies
a(R_{f}(f),v)=(f,v),\ \forall v\in{V_{f}}
from Lemma

. The application of

-projection error estimate and Hölder inequality yield

\begin{split}|||u-u_{h}^{ms}|||^{2}&=|||R_{f}(f)|||^{2}=a(R_{f}(f),R_{f}(f))\\
&=(f,R_{f}(f))\leq||f||_{0}||R_{f}(f)||_{0}\\
&=||f||_{0}||R_{f}(f)-P_{0}(R_{f}(f))||_{0}\\
&\leq Ch||f||_{0}|||R_{f}(f)|||,\end{split}

(2.29)

since
P_{0}(R_{f}(f))=0
. This concludes the proof.

For every

where

3
Localization

From Theorem
2.4
, we know that
u_{h}^{ms}
is a good approximation of the exact solution
u
. But
u_{h}^{ms}
is solved in the whole domain
\Omega
, which is too expensive to compute. In this section, we deduce that we can compute it locally, and then the same error estimate can be obtained.

For every vertex
z\in\mathcal{N}
, the vertex set of
V_{h}
, denote the corresponding nodal basis function
\lambda_{z}
, satisfying

\lambda_{z}(z)=1\ and\ \lambda_{z}(x)=1\ for\ all\ x\neq z\in\mathcal{N}.

(3.1)

Define corresponding basis function
R_{h}(\lambda_{z})=\underset{v\in{V},P_{0}(v)=\lambda_{z}}{\argmin}J_{0}(v)
, which satisfies

a(R_{h}(\lambda_{z}),w)=0,\ \forall w\in V_{f},

(3.2)

as Lemma
2.1
similarly.
From the space decomposition (
2.10
),
R_{h}(\lambda_{z})
can be decomposed into

R_{h}(\lambda_{z})=P_{0}(R_{h}(\lambda_{z}))+\phi_{z}=\lambda_{z}+\phi_{z},

(3.3)

where the corrector
\phi_{z}\in V_{f}
satisfies

a(\phi_{z},w)=-a(\lambda_{z},w),\ \forall w\in V_{f},

(3.4)

from (
3.2
).
These corresponding basis functions from basises of
R_{h}(V_{h})
, that is to say,

R_{h}({V_{h}})=span\{R_{h}(\lambda_{z})|z\in\mathcal{N}\}=span\{\lambda_{z}+\phi_{z}|z\in\mathcal{N}\}.

(3.5)

Thus, the multiscale method (
2.23
) can be rewritten as: find
u_{h}^{ms}\in R_{h}({V_{h}})
, such that

a(u_{h}^{ms},v)=(f,v),\ \forall v\in R_{h}({V_{h}}).

(3.6)

The following Lemma shows that the corrector
\phi_{z}
is exponential decayed, which is proved in
[
6
]
.

Lemma 3.1

There exists a constant
c>0
independent of
h
and
R
such that

|||\phi_{z}|||_{\Omega\setminus B_{R}(z)}\leq\exp\left(-c\frac{R}{h}\right)|||\phi_{z}|||_{\Omega},

where
B_{R}(z)
denotes the ball of radius
R>0
centered at
z
.

Denote nodal patch
\omega_{z,k},\ z\in\mathcal{N}
by

\begin{split}&\omega_{z,1}=supp(\lambda_{z})=int\left(\cup\{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}|z\in K\}\right),\\
&\omega_{z,k}=int\left(\cup\{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}|K\cap\bar{\omega}_{z,k-1}\neq\varnothing\}\right),\ k=2,3,4,\cdots.\end{split}

(3.7)

Define localized basis function

\tilde{R}_{h}(\lambda_{z})=\argmin_{v\in H_{0}^{1}(\omega_{z,k}),P_{0}(v)=\lambda_{z}}J_{0}(v),

(3.8)

then the corresponding localized space
\tilde{R}_{h}({V_{h}})
is obtained by

\tilde{R}_{h}({V_{h}})=span\{\tilde{R}_{h}(\lambda_{z})|z\in\mathcal{N}\}.

(3.9)

Then the localized approximation solution is defined by

u_{h,k}^{ms}=\argmin_{v_{h}\in\tilde{R}_{h}({V_{h}})}J(v_{h}).

(3.10)

That is to say, the localized multiscale method reads: find
u_{h,k}^{ms}\in\tilde{R}_{h}({V_{h}})
, such that

a(u_{h,k}^{ms},v)=(f,v),\ \forall v\in\tilde{R}_{h}({V_{h}}).

(3.11)

Next, we shall show the expression of
\tilde{R}_{h}(\lambda_{z})
. Similarly as (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), it’s easily shown that

\tilde{R}_{h}(\lambda_{z})=\lambda_{z}+\phi_{z,k},

(3.12)

where the localized corrector
\phi_{z,k}\in V_{f}\cap H_{0}^{1}(\omega_{z,k})
satisfies

a(\phi_{z,k},w)=-a(\lambda_{z},w),\ \forall w\in V_{f}\cap H_{0}^{1}(\omega_{z,k}).

(3.13)

The error estimate between
u
and
u_{h,k}^{ms}
is shown in the following Theorem, which can be found in
[
6
]
.

Theorem 3.2

Let
u\in V
be the exact solution of (
2.1
) and
u_{h,k}^{ms}
be the localized approximation solution defined in (
3.10

|||u-u_{h,k}^{ms}|||\leq Ch||f||_{0}

with moderate choice
k
and positive constant
C
that does not depend on
h
and
\varepsilon
