Eight-Year Clinical, Radiological, and Survival Outcomes of Knee Chondral Lesions Treated With High-Density Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation
Authors: Isabel Guillen-Vicente, Juan Manuel Lopez-Alcorocho, Marta Guillen-Vicente, Elena Rodríguez-Iñigo, Patricia Recio-López, Borja Ruiz, Adrián Barrera, Juan Carlos López Ortiz-Tallo, Carolina Stambolsky, Tomás F Fernández-Jaén, Steve Abelow, Pedro Guillen-García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArticular cartilage lesions of the knee are difficult to manage due to their limited healing capacity. High-Density Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation (HD-ACI) has emerged as a promising treatment for symptom relief and functional recovery.MethodsThis retrospective study included 124 patients (88 men, 36 women; median age 35.5 years) with symptomatic chondral lesions of the knee treated with HD-ACI between 2010 and 2016. Patients were followed for up to 8 years. Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain, International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) scores for knee function, and MOCART scores based on MRI evaluations of cartilage repair tissue were used for clinical assessment.ResultsPain significantly decreased from a mean VAS of 6.7 to 1.1 at 8 years (p<0.001). IKDC scores improved from 39.4 to 81.6 (p<0.001), being higher than the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). MOCART scores progressively increased over time. Knee mobility in both extension and flexion showed statistically significant improvement. Adverse events were mild and infrequent. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed an implant survival of 97.5% and an overall treatment survival of 92.6% at 8 years.ConclusionsHD-ACI is a safe and effective treatment for knee cartilage lesions, with long-term improvements in pain, joint function, confirmed clinically and radiologically.Keywords: cartilage and bone tissue engineering, cell based therapies, knee, articular cartilage, autologous chondrocyte

Introduction

Articular cartilage injuries of the knee represent a significant clinical challenge since this tissue has a limited intrinsic repair potential. Unaddressed, focal chondral defects can worsen into osteoarthritic changes, leading to chronic pain, mechanical issues, and progressive joint degeneration that may eventually culminate in osteoarthritis.1,2

Autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI) which was first introduced by Brittberg et al in 1994, 3 represents a milestone in cartilage repair. This technique is based on the implantation of in vitro-expanded autologous chondrocytes into the chondral defect. Subsequently, this technique has had several improvements, including second-generation approach using alternative delivery systems as a carrier, such as the Matrix-Assisted Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation (MACI) technique.4-6 Clinical studies have reported mid-to long-term good-to-excellent results for ACI and MACI.7-9

More recently, advances in cell therapy introduced the idea of high-density autologous chondrocyte implantation (HD-ACI).10,11 This approach involves implanting a much larger number of cartilage cells per unit area compared to traditional ACI. The rationale behind this, is that implanting more cells could boost the quality of cartilage regeneration, which encourages the growth of more natural, hyaline-like tissue, and ultimately improving joint mechanical function.11,12 Early results even suggested that having a higher cell density might lead to a better production of the extracellular matrix and enhanced structural integration within the tissue. 13 However, long-term evidence regarding HD-ACI durability, structural evolution, and survival outcomes remains limited.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the long-term clinical, radiological, and survival outcomes of HD-ACI in patients with focal chondral lesions of the knee after an eight-year follow-up. It was hypothesized that HD-ACI would provide durable clinical improvement together with favorable structural repair over time.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

Retrospective observational study that includes patients who underwent high-density autologous chondrocyte implantation (HD-ACI) for symptomatic focal cartilage lesions in the knee treated between 2010 and 2016 at a single specialized orthopedic center. Follow-up assessments were extended up to 8 years post-implantation. During the study period, 141 patients underwent HD-ACI. Of these, 124 patients completed the scheduled long-term clinical and radiological follow-up and were included in the final analysis. Seventeen patients were excluded due to incomplete follow-up data related to the retrospective nature of the study and the inability to complete all scheduled evaluations over the 8-year follow-up period. Therefore, a complete-case analysis was performed. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. The indication for HD-ACI was established based on the presence of symptomatic focal full-thickness chondral lesions of the knee in patients with persistent pain and functional limitation despite previous conservative treatment, including physiotherapy, activity modification, and pharmacological management. Surgical indication was determined by the treating orthopedic team according to clinical symptoms, MRI findings, lesion characteristics, and patient functional demands.

To be eligible for inclusion in this study, patients had to meet the following criteria: (i) provide written informed consent, (ii) be male or female and aged between 18 and 55 years, (iii) present a grade III or IV Outerbridge chondral lesion of the knee confirmed by imaging, and (iv) have one to four lesions ranging from 1 to 5 cm2 in size. Exclusion criteria were: (i) diagnosis of osteoarthritis, (ii) mirror lesions on the ipsilateral femoral condyle and tibial plateau, (iii) limb malalignment exceeding 10° of varus or valgus, (iv) known allergies to penicillin and/or streptomycin, (v) hypersensitivity to bovine-derived products, and (vi) active infections, tumoral conditions, or systemic diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis or other autoimmune joint disorders.

Surgical Technique

HD-ACI was performed following a standardized, two-step surgical protocol, previously published.10,11 Briefly, in the first step an arthroscopic procedure was performed in order to obtain a biopsy of healthy cartilage from a non-weight-bearing area of the knee, typically from the intercondylar notch or the superomedial margin of the trochlea. Chondrocytes were isolated with collagenase and cultured for the obtention of high cell density (5 million viable chondrocytes per cm2 of lesion). The interval between cartilage harvesting and HD-ACI implantation was approximately 4-6 weeks, depending on cell expansion and quality control assessment.

The second-stage implantation was performed through a surgery in which autologous chondrocytes were seeded in a porcine type I/III collagen membrane, that is previously cut according to the lesion size and shape. The membrane is then sutured to the surrounding cartilage and secured to the lesion bed with fibrin glue.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation followed a standardized protocol. The rehabilitation program was tailored according to the size and location of the lesion, the surgical technique used, and whether any concomitant procedures (e.g., osteotomies or meniscal repair) have been performed. In the immediate postoperative period (first 48–72 hours), the limb was immobilized in extension using a knee brace to protect the graft and minimize intra-articular bleeding. From the third day post-op, continuous passive motion (CPM) was carried-out in all patients. CPM is typically maintained for 4–6 hours per day during the first 4 weeks, increasing gradually the range of motion within pain-free limits.

Partial weight-bearing (≤20% body weight) was allowed from the second week after surgery, with progression to full weight-bearing typically between weeks 6 and 8. In patellofemoral lesions, unloading was maintained for longer (up to 8–10 weeks) due to higher motion forces. Muscle strengthening, especially of the quadriceps and hamstring groups, was progressively introduced starting in the fourth week. Cycling without resistance, and aquatic therapy were incorporated from the 8th week. Running and low-impact sports were generally allowed after the 6th postoperative month, depending on functional recovery. Return to pivoting or high-impact sports was delayed until at least 12 months post-surgery.

Clinical Evaluation

Clinical outcomes were assessed using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), Pain intensity was measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable). 14 Knee function was assessed with the subjective International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) score, ranging from 0 to 100, in which higher scores indicate better function. Validated Spanish versions of the PROMs were used. 15 Clinical evaluations were performed preoperatively and during postoperative follow-up visits at 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, 5 years, and 8 years after implantation. Joint mobility was recorded as two separate categorical variables for extension and flexion. Extension was categorized as ≤10°, complete, or hyperextension; flexion was classified as <90°, ≥90°, or complete, that were also assessed at five time points: preoperatively (baseline), and postoperatively at 6 months, 1 year, 4, and 8 years.

Imaging Evaluation

Cartilage repair was assessed using Magnetic Resonance Observation of Cartilage Repair Tissue (MOCART) scoring at 1, 4, and 8 years post-surgery. MRI was performed using a standardized protocol, and MOCART 16 was scored by two independent musculoskeletal radiologists blinded to clinical outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated good reliability and reproducibility of the MOCART system, with almost perfect interobserver agreement (ICC > 0.81) reported for most variables. 16

Safety Assessment

Adverse events were recorded at each time point, paying special attention to joint effusion, bone marrow edema, and other postoperative issues.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 22.0.0.0. (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (normal-distributed variables) or median and minimum and maximum (variables without normal distribution). Categorical variables were reported as absolute and relative frequencies. Normality of continuous variables was checked using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For longitudinal comparisons of non-normally distributed variables across multiple time points, Friedman’s test for repeated measures was used. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between time points were performed to determine the timing of statistically significant changes. The clinical relevance of changes in the IKDC score was assessed by comparing the mean change at 6 months, 1 year, 4 years, and 8 years against the published Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) of 16.7 points, 17 using one-sample Student’s t-tests. Knee mobility was analyzed as a categorical variable.

Changes in mobility distribution over time baseline to 8 years were evaluated using the Cochran’s Q test for related samples (repeated binary data) and Pearson’s Chi-square test for overall comparisons across all categories. For categorical safety outcomes (e.g., joint effusion, bone marrow edema, adverse events), repeated measurements were also analyzed using Cochran’s Q test to detect statistically significant changes over time, with post-hoc comparisons between baseline and each follow-up point.

Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method in the subgroup of 124 patients with complete long-term clinical and radiological follow-up data included in the final analysis. Time-to-event was calculated from the date of HD-ACI implantation to the occurrence of the corresponding event or to the last available clinical follow-up evaluation. Patients who did not experience the corresponding event during follow-up were treated as censored observations at their last available clinical evaluation. Censored observations were represented in the Kaplan–Meier curves and the number of patients at risk at each yearly time point was also reported.

Two different survival endpoints were analyzed separately in order to distinguish graft-related structural failure from any subsequent surgical intervention performed in the treated knee. The first endpoint corresponded to graft-related failure-free survival (implant survival), in which an event was defined as any structural failure directly attributable to the implanted cartilage graft, including lack of graft integration or graft delamination confirmed by clinical assessment and MRI evaluation, revision cartilage surgery at the index lesion, or conversion to knee arthroplasty.

The second endpoint corresponded to overall treatment failure and included both graft-related failures and any additional surgical procedure performed in the treated knee during follow-up, regardless of etiology. These procedures included arthroscopic revision procedures, meniscal surgery, soft tissue procedures, or other reoperations documented during postoperative follow-up. This broader endpoint was included to provide a more clinically comprehensive assessment of long-term treatment durability.

Survival probabilities, mean survival times, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated up to 8 years after implantation.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Population

A total of 141 patients underwent HD-ACI during the study period. Of these, 124 (87.9%) patients completed the required clinical and radiological follow-up and were included in the final analysis. Seventeen patients (12.1%) were excluded due to incomplete long-term follow-up data and inability to complete all scheduled evaluations. The median age at surgery was 34.5 years (range: 18–54), 88 (71.0%) were male and 36 (29.0%) females (Table 1). As shown in Table 1, most patients (82.3%: 102/124) were physically active, participating in sports either at a professional or recreational level.

Pain Evolution (VAS)

Mean pain scores on the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) decreased significantly over time, from 6.7 ± 2.4 at baseline to 1.1 ± 1.7 at 8 years (p < 0.001) (Figure 1). Pairwise comparisons showed a statistically significant reduction at each time point compared to the previous one up to year 1. Beyond this point, no further significant changes were observed.

Box and whisker plot showing the evolution of pain measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) up to 8 years of follow-up

Functional Outcomes (IKDC)

The mean IKDC score improved from 39.4 ± 13.4 at baseline to 81.6 ± 17.1 at 8 years (p < 0.001) (Figure 2). The improvement exceeded the MCID of 16.7 points at all follow-up points, with statistical confirmation via one-sample t-tests (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). This result suggests that patients not only improved, but perceived the change as clinically meaningful as well.

Joint Mobility

At baseline, 90.3% of patients had complete extension, 8.1% had limitation (≤10°), and 1.6% presented hyperextension. Eight years after implantation, 99.2% of patients had regained full extension (p = 0.004). The improvement was progressive and statistically significant across time points, particularly between baseline and years 1 to 4 (Figure 3A).

Bar graphs showing the evolution of joint mobility up to 8 years of follow-up. (A) Extension. (B) Flexion

Flexion improved significantly over time (p < 0.001). At the basal visit, only 64.5% of patients had full flexion, increasing to 96.7% at 8 years. Flexion <90° decreased from 5.6% to 0%. The distribution of flexion categories showed a consistent shift toward recovery of full range of motion (Figure 3B).

Radiological Outcomes (MOCART)

Mean MOCART scores improved from 71.4 the first year to 75.9 at year 8 (p < 0.001), being the differences among years 1, 4, and 8 statistically significant, which seemed to indicate progressive maturation of the neo formed cartilage. MRI evaluations confirmed the stability and integration of the neocartilage with the native tissue over time (Figure 4).

Safety and Adverse Events

Proportion of patients who showed joint effusion decreased from 24.2% at baseline to 1.6% at 8-year follow-up (p < 0.001). Bone edema followed a similar pattern, with 0.8% of patients affected at the last follow-up visit.

Treatment Survival Analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis considering graft-related failure as the endpoint identified three failures among the 124 treated patients (2.4%), corresponding to lack of graft integration (n = 1), graft delamination (n = 1), and conversion to total knee arthroplasty (n = 1). The estimated implant survival rate at 8 years was 97.5%, with a mean survival time of 7.89 years (95% CI: 7.76–8.02 years). Failures occurred at 1.99, 2.92, and 6.28 years after implantation (Figure 5A).

When considering overall treatment failure as the endpoint, nine events were observed (7.3%), including the three implant-related failures and six additional surgical procedures performed in the treated knee during follow-up, consisting of arthroscopic procedures (n = 3), soft tissue procedures (n = 2), and meniscal surgery (n = 1). The estimated overall treatment survival at 8 years was 92.6%, with a mean survival time of 7.68 years (95% CI: 7.46–7.91 years). Treatment failure events occurred between 1.29 and 6.98 years after implantation, with no further events thereafter (Figure 5B).

Discussion

The present study provides long-term confirmation for the safety and effectiveness of HD-ACI for repair of focal chondral lesions in the knee. After eight-year follow-up, significant improvement in pain, functional capacity, and joint mobility were confirmed. These clinical benefits were complemented by maturation at the repaired tissue, which was distinguishable on MRI using the MOCART scoring system. Most notably, the intervention had an extremely robust safety profile confirmed by low complication incidence and no severe adverse events. These findings further confirm that HD-ACI is a valid long-term treatment option for repair of articular cartilage.

Pain improvement is one of the outcomes in patients undergoing cartilage repair. Many patients indicated that their pain decreased significantly relatively early, and this benefit persisted at the eight-year follow-up. That trend is in agreement with what is seen in prior literature on ACI and MACI, where scores for pain are improved during the first year and plateau thereafter.18,19 Knutsen et al 20 reported that patients treated with ACI had significantly less pain and a more satisfactory function for up to 15 years in comparison with those treated with microfracture. These results show evidence that cell-based treatments can yield durable benefits. Together with the improvement in pain, functional gains were also observed, noted in PROMs (patient-reported outcome measures) and confirmed by the fact that IKDC difference is significantly above the MCID, which shows that improvement has a high impact in everyday life’s activity. Overall, these results align with long-term outcomes in other ACI/MACI studies. 7

Joint mobility restoration is an essential clinical endpoint of cartilage repair, as the quality of life is severely impaired by stiffness and limited range of motion, even if pain is resolved. In line with other reports from studies using MACI and ACI, that document restoration of useful range of motion in most patients, progressive and sustained gains in knee mobility was also found in the patients included,.21,22

MRI evaluation with the MOCART scoring system showed a progressive improvement in the repair tissue, suggesting maturation at long-term follow-up. The increase in MOCART scores was consistent with the clinical improvements observed in pain and function, a correlation that has also been described in previous reports.23,24 A systematic review published in 2021 by Iordache E. et al 25 showed that the maturation of the ACI graft often has a peak around two years after implantation. At this moment, characteristic patterns are often seen in parameters such as defect filling, graft signal intensity and structure, and border integration. These observations support the value of MRI assessment with the MOCART system in evaluating HD-ACI. Imaging findings provide a complement to clinical outcomes, offering deeper insight into repair tissue quality and supporting the interpretation of long-term efficacy.

With respect to adverse events appearance and safety of the treatment, in this study, HD-ACI was associated with a low rate of complications, and no severe adverse events over the eight-year follow-up. This favorable safety profile is consistent with prior reports of chondrocyte implantation therapies, including ACI and MACI, where most complications are minor and include graft hypertrophy, effusion, or transient stiffness.4,26,27 These findings support the notion that HD-ACI represents a safe long-term intervention for cartilage repair. This is particularly relevant in patients who have undergone multiple prior surgical interventions, where intra-articular adhesions and altered biomechanics often represent additional challenges. 28 The observed improvements in this study emphasize the capacity of HD-ACI to both restore pain-free function and promote a biomechanically favorable joint environment, with a low adverse event profile, even in previously operated knees, suggesting that higher cell density may mitigate some of the limitations observed in patients with prior cartilage surgical treatments.

An additional relevant aspect of the present series is the survival of the repair over time. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed an implant survival of 97.5% at eight years, with only three graft-related failures observed during follow-up. When a broader definition of treatment failure was considered, including any subsequent surgical procedure in the treated knee, overall treatment survival remained high at 92.6%. These findings are in line with long-term reports of ACI and MACI, where survival rates above 80% have been described beyond 10–15 years of follow-up. 29 The relatively low number of failures observed in this cohort suggests that HD-ACI provides durable structural repair in the majority of patients, even when a more stringent definition of treatment success is applied. Nevertheless, these survival estimates should be interpreted considering the complete-case analysis design of the study.

Concomitant pathologies such as meniscal deficiency, malalignment, or ligamentous instability can negatively influence outcomes if not addressed simultaneously.30-32 Comprehensive preoperative planning and correction of coexisting abnormalities remain critical to optimizing the long-term results of HD-ACI.

Comparing HD-ACI with the published literature about other established cartilage repair methods, microfracture has been one the first-line surgical option for focal cartilage lesions due to its simplicity and apparent cost-effectiveness. However, several studies have demonstrated a significant decrease in clinical outcomes 2–5 years after surgery, especially in high-demand or younger patients, with evident structural deterioration on imaging. 33 Other method, such as osteochondral autograft transfer (OAT) represents a widely utilized technique, particularly in small defects (<2 cm2). Although OAT provides hyaline cartilage restoration, some limitations including donor-site morbidity, restricted graft availability, and technical complexity for larger or irregular lesions are presented. 34 In contrast, HD-ACI provides durable functional and pain relief over an extended follow-up, as observed in the patients included and can address medium-to-large defects without compromising healthy cartilage areas, and thus it is not limited by donor tissue availability.

With respect to cell-based techniques, approaches such as bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) and mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based have emerged in recent years. Although early outcomes are promising, there are some concerns regarding heterogeneity in cell populations, variability in regenerative potential, and lack of long-term data. 35 Other chondrocyte-based techniques, such as MACI, have demonstrated good long-term outcomes in cartilage repair, with survival rates exceeding 80% at 10–15 years. 29 HD-ACI is based on the same principles but may offer additional advantages due to the use of a higher chondrocyte density. Increasing the number of implanted cells could support earlier matrix deposition, improve the mechanical stability of the repaired tissue, and reduce the likelihood of incomplete defect filling. In other published works using HD-ACI, a lower incidence of subchondral bone edema compared with rates reported in studies using MACI was observed. These findings could support the fact that higher chondrocyte density may promote the development of neo-formed hyaline or hyaline-like cartilage tissue.

One of the most critical issues in cartilage repair is the duration of the clinical benefit and the potential to prevent or delay the onset of osteoarthritis (OA). Long-term studies of traditional ACI have reported graft survival and sustained functional improvements for up to 20 years in a high proportion of patients. 29 In this cohort, HD-ACI demonstrated stable outcomes over eight years, suggesting that this technique may contribute to long-term joint preservation. Recent imaging studies support the hypothesis that successful cell-based cartilage repair reduces the risk of secondary degenerative disease. 36 MRI biomarkers such as cartilage thickness, subchondral bone remodeling, and absence of bone marrow lesions correlate with lower rates of OA progression. 37 In this regard, the robust MRI findings in this series reinforce the idea that HD-ACI may exert a protective effect against long-term joint degeneration. However, definitive confirmation deserves further randomized comparative studies with other surgical or non-surgical approaches.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and absence of a control group do not allow direct comparisons with other cartilage repair techniques, which could limit the conclusions on the relative superiority of HD-ACI. Moreover, it is possible that a selection bias occurred because of the nature of the study and its methodology. Only those patients who had successfully undergone a complete follow-up were considered for inclusion in the analysis. There were 17 participants in the study that did not undergo a full examination during the 8 years of the observation period. Even though this number constituted a minor percentage of all the patients involved in the study, it remains possible that patients lost to follow-up had different outcomes. Thus, the long-term clinical results achieved might be overestimated. Nevertheless, complete-case analysis was considered the most appropriate methodological approach in this retrospective study to ensure consistency across all longitudinal clinical and imaging evaluations. Second, the heterogeneity of lesions (location, size, and previous surgical history) could have influenced the outcomes. .Finally, although eight years is a considerable follow-up period, longer-term prospective studies are needed to confirm the durability of the clinical and structural benefits.

Despite these limitations, the findings of the present study strengthen the role of HD-ACI as a promising technique for the treatment of focal chondral defects of the knee. Future research focused on prospective comparative trials against other regenerative procedures, on identifying prognostic factors for success, and on cost-effectiveness analyses to better define the role of HD-ACI in clinical practice will be performed.
