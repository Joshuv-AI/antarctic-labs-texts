Combined retinal vein occlusion and retinal artery occlusion in a patient undergoing therapy for metastatic breast cancer: a case report
Authors: Mariaelena Malvasi, Giovanni Bellisario, Giacomo Panozzo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis case involves a patient on aromatase inhibitor therapy for breast carcinoma who experienced combined retinal artery and vein occlusion.Case presentationA 56-year-old Caucasian woman of northern Italian origin, who was initially diagnosed with central vein occlusion in the left eye treated with ranibizumab and laser photocoagulation, later experienced a new episode of visual decrease in the same eye secondary to retinal artery occlusion. These vascular occlusions followed a diagnosis of stage G2 infiltrating ductal breast cancer, for which she was receiving systemic therapy with tamoxifen and letrozole.ConclusionThis case report highlights the factors associated with retinal occlusion during aromatase inhibitor treatment for breast cancer, aiming to prevent ocular adverse events and visual deterioration, and underscoring the importance of ophthalmic screening for early diagnosis and appropriate treatment, as visual disturbances may be underestimated owing to the systemic pathology.

Background

Breast cancer is a significant public health concern for women. In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported 2.3 million new breast cancer diagnosis and 685,000 deaths worldwide [1]. Estrogen receptors play a major role in tumor pathogenesis [2], with most breast cancers relying on the presence of the estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PgR), or both. Identifying these receptors is essential for determining eligibility for adjuvant hormonal therapy [3].

In premenopausal women, the ovaries are the main source of estrogen, whereas in postmenopausal women, estrogens are produced peripherally through the aromatization of androstenedione and testosterone. In extragonadal tissues such as muscles, adipose tissue, and the breast, estrogen concentration can be 4–6 times higher than serum levels, with even greater concentrations in breast tumors, due to active uptake from the circulation and local aromatization within tumor cells [4].

Aromatase inhibitor (AI) therapy is the preferred choice for postmenopausal women with estrogen receptor (ER)-positive breast cancer. Among the third-generation AIs approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the adjuvant treatment of postmenopausal breast cancer is letrozole (2.5 mg/day), which can be used from the initial stages for a continuous 5-year period [5] as confirmed by clinical trials.

Tamoxifen blocks estrogen receptors in the breast, but it acts as a partial agonist in other tissues such as vascular endothelium, increasing the risk of endometrial cancer and thromboembolic events [6]. Third-generation aromatase inhibitors, such as letrozole, are more potent and specific, reducing estrogens to undetectable levels without affecting other hormones [7].

Clinical studies have shown that AIs are generally more effective and better tolerated than tamoxifen in postmenopausal women with early-stage or advanced ER-positive breast cancer; moreover, they have fewer side effects compared with tamoxifen, reducing the risk of thrombotic events and cerebrovascular diseases, which have been attributed to the partial estrogen-agonist action of tamoxifen [8].

Case presentation

A 56-year-old Caucasian woman of northern Italian origin presented to our emergency room complaining of recent worsening of her visual acuity in the left eye.

The general past history of the patient included, remarkably, a diagnosis of breast cancer: in 2016 a “moderately differentiated” (G2) infiltrating ductal left breast carcinoma was found; it was characterized as low proliferative index (KI67 10%), 16 mm in diameter, ER 70%, progesterone receptor (PR) 100%, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) negative (score 1+), with no metastases at the beginning. She underwent left breast surgery followed by adjuvant radiotherapy (50 Gy) and adjuvant hormonal therapy with tamoxifen 20 mg (one tablet daily). In 2019, owing to menopausal status, the hormonal therapy was switched from tamoxifen to aromatase inhibitor (exemestane) and then switched back to tamoxifen for a perimenopausal syndrome with spotting. In 2020, a scheduled positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) highlighted hypermetabolic foci compatible with stage IV metastasis (bones): the cranial vault, right first rib, sternum, D5, D6, D8, D9, D10, pelvis were affected. Consequently, the patient stopped tamoxifen therapy and started a first-line therapy with LHRa and aromatase inhibitor letrozole combined with abemaciclib (a CD4/CD6 cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor, CDKi) administered orally every 28 days, along with monthly intravenous zoledronic acid. Additional radiotherapy for hypermetabolic bone foci was performed “as needed” in the following 2 years, while the oral systemic therapy was unchanged.

The patient reported the onset of initial ophthalmic symptoms in October 2023 (Table 1), describing a sudden decrease in visual acuity, which prompted further investigation. The ophthalmic history included a diagnosis of central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) in the left eye, made 5 months earlier at another center. The examination also revealed the presence of a sea-fan-like configuration with significant peripheral ischemia in the contralateral, the unaffected eye, a characteristic finding often observed in patients with an underlying systemic hypercoagulable state [9].

She was treated with 3 monthly ranibizumab intravitreal injections and three sessions of scatter laser photocoagulation. At this point, the patient decided to discontinue further treatment for personal reasons.

In March 2024, we performed our first comprehensive ophthalmological examination: the best correct visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.9 in the right eye and counting fingers at 1 m in the left eye; an unremarkable anterior segment and a normal intraocular pressure were found in both eyes. The fundus examination of the right eye revealed a regular appearance of the macula and of the blood vessels and absence of any peripheral retinal lesions (Fig. 3A). The superotemporal and inferotemporal arteries appeared nonperfused, without clear evidence of emboli, and were associated with a relatively pale and opaque macula; these signs were suggestive of a possible central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) (Fig. 3B).

An optical coherence tomography (OCT) was performed: the right eye appeared normal (Fig. 4A), whereas the left eye showed a complete disruption of the retinal architecture, with severe cystoid macular edema secondary to chronic central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO). Notably, despite the evident inner retinal hyperreflectivity and thickening, the classic cherry-red spot was not visible on fundus examination. This finding aligns with previous reports showing that CRAO coexisting with retinal vein occlusion may lack the typical cherry-red appearance, especially when extensive flaming retinal hemorrhages, such as those observed in this case, partially or completely obscure the fovea. Furthermore, various factors such as incomplete inner retinal coagulative necrosis, attenuation of the retinal transparency, and masking hemorrhages can all contribute to the absence of cherry-red spots in CRAO, particularly when combined with CRVO.

An optical coherence tomography (OCT) was performed: the right eye appearance was normal (Fig. 4A), while the left eye showed a total disruption of the anatomical structures with severe cystoid macular edema secondary to the old CRVO but also a marked hyperreflectivity of the inner retinal layers as seen in CRAO and also possible in ischemic CRVO (Fig. 4B).

Considering the severe damage to the retinal structures and the poor visual prognosis, we decided not to proceed with intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injections; a subconjunctival injection of triamcinolone acetonide was administered to reduce intraretinal edema and we referred the patient to the emergency department, for the stroke-unit protocol treatments. The cardiological examination reported a normal electrocardiogram (ECG) with sinus tachycardia of 103 bpm; no abnormalities were found on the echocardiogram and on the color Doppler ultrasound of supra-aortic vessels.

A month later (April 2024), we performed a follow-up examination (Table 1). The patient reported a slight visual acuity (VA) improvement to 0.05 in visual acuity, and the OCT examination showed a reduction in macular edema (Fig. 5A and B). Discussion and conclusion

Among the several risk factors for CRVO, metabolic syndrome (hyperglycemia, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia) and previous vascular events are considered the most important [10]. In our patient, CRVO occurred without any of these risk factors.

However, there is strong evidence about the association of CRVO with breast cancer due to a hypercoagulability state induced by the tumor [11]. Furthermore, CRVO has been associated with other systemic malignant tumors [11, 12]. In neoplasms, CRVO is considered a paraneoplastic process: interactions among tumor cells, the blood coagulation system, vascular endothelium, leukocytes, and platelets contribute to its pathogenesis. Hematological changes in cancer promote thrombosis, as revealed by the increase of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 and the reduction of tissue factor pathway inhibitor activity in patients with breast cancer [13]. Therapeutic agents used in cancer treatment may also contribute to CRVO [14]. Approximately 60% of patients with simultaneous retinal occlusion have vascular risk factors. Several systemic comorbidities associated with retinal vein occlusions, such as coagulopathies or hyperviscosity blood disorders, can damage the adjacent artery and lead to combined vascular occlusion. In this context, oxidative stress and the activation of proinflammatory factors may play a role in the pathogenesis [15–17].

For patients without any other recognizable risk factors for CRVO, hyperviscosity syndromes should be ruled out. The serum levels of homocysteine, activated protein C resistance, protein C activity, protein S activity, antithrombin III activity, antiphospholipid antibodies, and anticardiolipin antibodies should be evaluated [18].

Although the systemic neoplasia likely caused a hyperviscosity state in our patient, thus leading to the development of the vein occlusion, the role of antitumor drugs should be also evaluated. Her pharmacological therapy was composed of AI (letrozole) combined with CDK inhibitor (Abemaciclib). While the latter, approved by the FDA to treat metastatic breast cancer, has not been associated with any ocular side effects [19], the former, letrozole in particular, has been strongly associated with retinal artery occlusion [20] as shown by an analysis of the FDA’s Adverse Events Reporting System from 2004 to 2022 conducted by Feng et al. A literature review by Serban et al. found nine articles from 2008 to 2021 reporting various neuroretinal disorders in patients with breast cancer treated with AIs, ranging from mild to severe vision loss. These disorders included hemicentral retinal artery occlusion, bilateral optic neuritis, and bilateral uveitis with macular edema [21].

Furthermore, the use of tamoxifen as breast cancer treatment has been correlated to several ocular side effects, including retinopathy [22]; the American Academy of Ophthalmology recommends regular ophthalmic examinations for patients on tamoxifen to detect signs of retinal toxicity [22].

Estrogens have protective roles in the retina, regulating ocular blood flow, mitigating oxidative stress, and reducing inflammation, and also the trophic role through VEGF expression and its receptors, and anti-inflammatory effects that attenuate interleukin (IL)−6 response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or modulate ocular blood flow [23]. Experimental studies have shown that estrogen deficiency can increase proinflammatory markers such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and endothelin-1 [24].

Although retinal artery occlusion is less common with hormone therapy, some cases have been reported [25]. Tamoxifen and AIs have been associated with retinal artery occlusion and hemorrhages, since estrogen depletion with AIs might impact the retinal vascular system, as studies indicate that estrogen affects retinal blood flow [23]. Although retinal veins have been reported to be involved more frequently than retinal arteries in tamoxifen treatment, anecdotal cases of retinal artery occlusion associated with tamoxifen use have been described in literature [26].

Estrogen receptors are widely distributed in the body, including vascular smooth muscle, endothelial cells, and the retina [24]. Depletion of estrogen levels caused by AIs may impact the retinal vascular system, as studies have shown that estrogen administration increases peak systolic and diastolic velocities of the central retinal artery [25]. Thus, maximal estrogen depletion with AIs might affect retinal vascular health.

Despite the absence of hyperlipidemia in our patient, and without the typical risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension, it is likely that AIs and tamoxifen treatment contributed to hypercoagulability status that occurs with the combined retinal occlusion in our patient.

As shown in Fig. 1, the patient already exhibited a sea-fan-like retinal neovascular configuration in the contralateral, unaffected eye during the initial diagnostic examinations, a manifestation well documented in chronic retinal ischemic conditions such as proliferative sickle cell retinopathy, although it may also occur in other ischemic retinal diseases. This type of neovascularization typically develops in the mid-peripheral retina [9].

However, to date, no evidence in the current literature directly links tamoxifen or letrozole to the development of peripheral sea-fan-like retinal neovascularization. The presence of this vascular pattern in patients undergoing treatment with these agents may suggest significant peripheral retinal ischemia, but further studies are needed to establish a direct causal relationship.

The tumor-induced hyperviscosity state, along with the effects of antitumor drugs, represents a significant risk factor for major retinal vasculopathies in oncology patients. Given the fairly significant incidence of retinal vein and artery occlusions in the general population, greater awareness is needed for patients with additional tumor-related risk factors. The aim of our study is to highlight the importance of careful monitoring by oncologists and ophthalmologists for patients undergoing aromatase inhibitor and tamoxifen treatment. Prompt recognition and appropriate management of ocular issues, including the evaluation of AI interruption and replacement in collaboration with the oncology team, are crucial to preserving visual function. Although letrozole may cause fewer thromboembolic complications than tamoxifen, further longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the incidence of ocular side effects. Careful monitoring and targeted ophthalmological screening are essential to improve patient quality of life, especially for those with comorbid conditions. Additional evidence from longitudinal studies on a larger sample of patients is required to document the incidence of ocular side effects of AIs in breast cancer therapy. Many visual disturbances may be overlooked or attributed to aging by patients, underestimating the potential side effects of hormonal therapy.

In this context, targeted ophthalmological screening becomes essential to preserve visual function and improve patients’ quality of life. A comprehensive evaluation should include advanced ophthalmic imaging, such as spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT), fluorescein angiography (FA), and detailed fundus examination, in combination with a systemic workup, including a thorough assessment of the patient’s metabolic and cardiovascular status. In the present case, no evidence of diabetes, hypertension, or hyperlipidemia was found, emphasizing the importance of identifying nontraditional risk factors, such as pharmacologically induced vascular vulnerability.

Therefore, careful monitoring of hormonal therapy in patients with breast cancer, particularly those with comorbid conditions, is essential. Although current evidence on the retinal side effects of aromatase inhibitor (AI) treatment remains limited, further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether these effects stem primarily from estrogen deprivation or are direct consequences of the drugs themselves.

One of the main diagnostic challenges in these cases lies in attributing retinal vascular events to underlying systemic or pharmacologic factors in the absence of classic risk factors, such as diabetes, hypertension, or hyperlipidemia. This scenario often leads to underdiagnosis or misinterpretation of ocular symptoms, as documented in recent literature [20–22]. In our case, the reasoning process was supported by the lack of metabolic syndrome and the presence of an underlying neoplastic condition treated with hormonal therapy, a context in which a paraneoplastic hypercoagulability state is well established [10–12].

The hypothesis that aromatase inhibitors and estrogen deprivation contribute to vascular vulnerability is corroborated by several studies [23, 24]. These agents may influence the retinal vasculature through mechanisms such as endothelial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and altered VEGF signaling [24–26], even in the absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. This reinforces the need for systematic ocular screening protocols in oncology patients receiving hormonal therapy, aimed at early detection and management of vision-threatening complications.
