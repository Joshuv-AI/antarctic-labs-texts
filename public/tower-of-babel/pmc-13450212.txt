MBNL1 hijacks a structured single-stranded distal DNA element to sustain FLT3 expression in KMT2A-rearranged leukemias
Authors: Meixia Che, Shaela Fields, Siqi Yi, Judith Hyle, Mengli Zhang, Beisi Xu, Yong Cheng, Peng Xu, Yajun Jiang, Chunliang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The molecular mechanisms by which KMT2A-rearranged (KMT2A-r) leukemias maintain the oncogenic FLT3 expression remain largely unclear, limiting therapeutic opportunities. Here, we identify the RNA binding protein MBNL1 as an unexpected positive regulator of FLT3 by DepMap dataset exploration and combinatorial CRISPR screens. MBNL1 promotes leukemia cell survival in cell lines and primary tumors by sustaining FLT3 expression in a KMT2A-r context-dependent manner. Mechanistically, we discover that MBNL1 recognizes a structured single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) element containing five consecutive guanines within the FLT3 enhancer, through MBNL1’s zinc finger domains and the carboxyl-terminal unstructured region. Such MBNL1 protein/ssDNA interaction was evident in KMT2A-r leukemia using ChIP-seq and KAS-seq. Mutations of key amino acids of MBNL1’s ssDNA binding surface or the critical guanines in ssDNA markedly abrogate the protein-ssDNA interactions. These findings implicate MBNL1 as a distinct FLT3 activator by recognizing a structured enhancer ssDNA element, highlighting an unexpected role for RNA binding proteins in transcriptional regulation through direct ssDNA recognition.

INTRODUCTION

KMT2A-rearranged (KMT2A-r) events were identified in more than 90% of infant leukemia, ∼5% of B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), and ∼10% of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cases (1–3). Although most of the low-risk ALL and AML are curable with standard chemotherapy combinations, the KMT2A-r subtype, conferred a particularly poor diagnosis and treatment outcome (4, 5). It is well recognized that the KMT2A-r fusion protein is much more challenging to target therapeutically. By treatment with conventional multiagent chemotherapy, KMT2A-r patients initially reach remission but frequently end up with relapse due to complex mechanisms (6, 7). Novel therapies targeting epigenetic modifiers and the immune response offer new promise, such as menin inhibitors and blinatumomab (8–14). Moreover, the lineage-switching capacity of KMT2A-r leukemia enables tumors to evade immunotherapy (15, 16). Based on these observations, there is an urgent need to identify novel regulatory mechanisms underlying KMT2A-r leukemia and to develop an alternative therapy.

FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) is one of the most essential oncogenes in high-risk leukemia. Overexpression and gain-of-function mutations were frequently detected in KMT2A-r and in most patients with AML (17, 18). FLT3 inhibitors, such as quizartinib and gilteritinib, demonstrated favorable effects in patients with relapsed or refractory AML in recent clinical trials (19, 20). However, complete remission with these drug treatments remains challenging. In the past decade, several elegant individual studies have revealed molecular mechanisms underlying, in part, FLT3 activation and transcriptional maintenance (21). However, the systematic screening of RNA binding proteins (RBPs) and their regulation of FLT3 have not yet been comprehensively illustrated, despite the promising potential of targeting RBPs (22).

Muscleblind-like (MBNL) proteins are a highly conserved, developmentally regulated family of RNA binding factors that includes three members: MBNL1, MBNL2, and MBNL3 (23). The MBNL1 protein relies on the two pairs of highly conserved zinc fingers (ZnF1-4) to bind to pre-mRNA and regulate alternative splicing (24–27). Mbnl1 is widely expressed during mouse development and is predominantly expressed in muscle and the heart. Compound knockout of Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 leads to severe syndromes, including cardiac arrhythmias and conduction block, severe myotonia, skeletal muscle weakness, and reduced lifespan (28). The functional role of MBNL1 in cancer biology remains poorly explored. For instance, Itskovich et al. (27) reported that MBNL1 regulates essential alternative RNA splicing in KMT2A-r leukemia genome-wide and sustains cancer cell proliferation. However, MBNL1 was also considered a suppressor of breast cancer metastasis, and its expression is associated with reduced clinical metastatic relapse outcomes (29).

Recently, we systematically conducted dropout CRISPR screens targeting HOXA9-bound peaks in KMT2A-r leukemia cell lines and identified an HOXA9/FLT3 regulatory axis via HOXA9 genomic occupancy at the distal enhancer of FLT3 (30). To continue efforts to identify previously unknown and functional regulators of FLT3 expression, we used two independent and complementary approaches: a CRISPR-based functional screen to identify upstream regulators of FLT3 and an algorithm, called “context-dependent association analysis” (CDAA), to explore potential KMT2A-dependent FLT3 regulators using DepMap (31) datasets. An RBP and splicing factor, MBNL1 (32–34), was identified as the top hit regulating FLT3 expression in KMT2A-r leukemia cells. Unexpectedly we found that, in addition to its canonical splicing factor role, the MBNL1 protein uniquely and directly binds to the single-stranded distal enhancer DNA of FLT3 through its zinc finger domains and the C-terminal region. By integrating multidisciplinary cell biology, biochemistry, and structural modeling, we proposed a noncanonical transcriptional regulatory pattern for the MBNL1/FLT3 axis, in addition to MBNL1’s canonical RNA binding function.

RESULTS

Identification of RBP MBNL1 as a regulator of FLT3 in human KMT2A-r leukemias

To identify yet-unknown regulators controlling FLT3 expression in KMT2A-r leukemia, we reasoned that the candidate gene should be an essential survival gene and that its expression would positively correlate with FLT3 mRNA levels. Therefore, we leveraged data mining and functional screens to identify such candidates. First, we developed an algorithm called context-dependent association analysis (CDAA) to unbiasedly explore DepMap datasets to identify genes whose expression positively correlates with FLT3 mRNA (Fig. 1A). The computational permutation (100,000 times) among random cancer lines revealed specific genes whose expression positively correlated with FLT3 mRNA in KMT2A-r leukemia cells [Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) > 0.4, P < 0.05], and the RBP MBNL1 was identified as the top candidate. MBNL1 has been recognized as an RBP in the CCCH-type zinc finger family (35–38), which plays a critical role in RNA splicing regulation, RNA export, and stability maintenance (39, 40). To evaluate whether the newly identified FLT3 regulator MBNL1 functions as an essential gene in KMT2A-r leukemia cells, we conducted an unbiased CRISPR dropout screen in KMT2A-r AML cell line MOLM13 using a homemade nuclear factor library against RBPs, transcription factors (TFs), and epigenetic modifiers (Fig. 1B). The Cas9-expressing MOLM13 cells infected with low multiplicity of infection (MOI; <0.3) lentiviral single guide RNA (sgRNA) library were cultured for about 2 weeks, followed by sgRNA sequencing to interrogate genes essential for leukemia cell survival. As a result, MBNL1 was among the top hits. The raw counts of four of the five individual sgRNAs against the MBNL1 coding sequence significantly decreased in cells on day 12 compared with those on day 0 (Fig. 1C). In addition, we independently conducted FLT3 protein staining-based reporter CRISPR screens (Fig. 1D and fig. S1, A to C). Cas9-expressing KMT2A-r SEM cells were infected with pooled sgRNAs and sorted by FLT3 antibody–based staining. The corresponding FLT3-high and FLT3-low populations were used for DNA extraction, sgRNA amplification, and sequencing. Again, multiple sgRNAs against coding exons of MBNL1 were enriched in the FLT3-low population, and negative control sgRNAs showed no difference (Fig. 1E), highlighting MBNL1’s role in controlling FLT3 expression. In summary, the systematic data-mining and functional screening approaches identified MBNL1 as a plausible positive regulator of FLT3 in KMT2A-r leukemia cells.

MBNL1 contributes to KMT2A-r leukemia cell fitness maintenance

Given that FLT3 was known as a survival essential gene in the leukemia context, we tried to determine the effects of MBNL1 on KMT2A-r leukemia proliferation. Three indel-validated sgRNAs against coding exons of MBNL1 were delivered to Cas9-expressing KMT2A-r AML MOLM13, OCI-AML2, and KMT2A-r B-ALL SEM cells. Reduced MBNL1 protein levels were observed in all three cell lines (Fig. 2A and fig. S2A). We then conducted a time-course competitive proliferation assay (CPA) to characterize cell proliferation in the context of impaired MBNL1 expression. The percentage of cells with a cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) signal in the sgRNA-expressing lentiviral vectors was quantified by flow cytometry to monitor blunted cell survival targeted by sgRNAs. We observed a decrease in the normalized percentage of CFP+ cells over time in all sgMBNL1-targeted KMT2A-r leukemia cells (Fig. 2, B to D), indicative of a compromised cell-survival phenotype due to MBNL1 protein loss. We next sought to determine whether the compromised cell fitness phenotype is restricted to FLT3 loss in MBNL1-knockout cells. To this end, we used a lentiviral expression system to ectopically express FLT3 cDNA in MBNL1 knockout cells (Fig. 2E) and counted the number of cells targeted by sgMBNL1 and sgNT, with or without FLT3 addback. The results demonstrate a substantial survival benefit upon FLT3 addback in these KMT2A-r leukemia cells with impaired MBNL1 expression (Fig. 2F). These results indicate that FLT3 expression is at least partially dependent on MBNL1 protein and that the MBNL1/FLT3 regulation axis is essential for KMT2A-r leukemia cells.

To further investigate the regulation of FLT3 by MBNL1, we infected validated MBNL1 sgRNAs targeting the coding exon and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in Cas9-expressing MOLM13 cells. MBNL1 knockout cells showed a notable decrease in FLT3 at the mRNA level (fig. S2B). Reanalysis of the RNA-seq data collected from MBNL1 short hairpin RNA (shRNA)–targeted MOLM13 and MV4,11 AML cells (27) also showed a significant decrease in FLT3 mRNA expression in the shMBNL1-targeted group compared with that from shNT (fig. S2C). Unbiased comparison of both RNA-seq datasets revealed that FLT3 was among the overlapped genes with significantly decreased expression (fig. S2, D and E). Further, pathway analysis by gene set enrichment analysis revealed that the top gene signatures in shMBNL1 knockout are those related to FLT3 inhibitor treatment, including TP0903 and gilteritinib in AML cells (fig. S2F). These results highlighted FLT3 as a major downstream effector of MBNL1 in multiple KMT2A-r models. To evaluate whether such positive regulation of FLT3 by MBNL1 is due to the canonical splicing activity of MBNL1, we revisited our RNA-seq data to investigate the splicing changes between sgMBNL1 and sgNT (fig. S3A). Consistent with a previous study using shRNA to target MBNL1 in AML cells, we found a notable number of aberrant splicing events upon MBNL1 loss by CRISPR editing (fig. S3, B to D). FLT3 mRNA splicing remained unchallenged (fig. S3E). Collectively, these data demonstrate that MBNL1 governs cell fitness by positively modulating FLT3 expression in KMT2A-r leukemia cells, a phenomenon that may not be due to its canonical RNA splicing activity.

The MBNL1 protein exhibits highly selective and noncanonical chromatin occupancy

Recently, RBPs were reported to be pervasively engaged with chromatin to regulate transcription (41, 42). Therefore, we proposed conducting chromatin immunoprecipitation and sequencing (ChIP-seq) of MBNL1 (Fig. 3A) to investigate its potential to regulate FLT3. Seven independent ChIP-seq assays were conducted using either endogenous MBNL1 or ectopically expressed HA-MBNL1. Notably, all ChIP-seq results revealed minimal binding peaks of MBNL1 at the genome-wide scale (Fig. 3B). When comparing peak number and location among KMT2A-r leukemia cell lines SEM and MOLM13, about 20 peaks overlapped, and two were in the gene regulatory regions, including the EEF1A1 promoter and the distal enhancer of FLT3 (30). Several lineage-specific TFs (e.g., HOXA9, MEIS1, ERG, FLI1, and MAZ) and major epigenetic regulators (BRD4, EP300, MED1, MED12, and MED26) also demonstrated chromatin occupancy adjacent to the MBNL1-binding peak, suggesting the collaborative role of these proteins in downstream gene regulation is possible (Fig. 3C and figs. S4 and S5). In summary, the MBNL1 protein exhibits limited, highly selective chromatin occupancy, as determined by conventional ChIP-seq analysis, suggesting a unique regulatory role in gene expression control.

MBNL1 binds and regulates the FLT3 enhancer to control KMT2A-r cell survival

To explore whether MBNL1 governs KMT2A-r cell fitness by occupying the FLT3 enhancer, we used genome-editing approaches to disrupt the MBNL1-binding site (BS) in the FLT3 distal enhancer. First, we used the CRISPR interference assay to block chromatin accessibility of the MBNL1-BS by introducing an sgRNA (sgMBNL1-BS) located in the middle of MBNL1-binding peak in SEM and MOLM13 cells stably expressing dCas9-KRAB (Fig. 3D). Consistent with the MBNL1 coding exon targeting result, retarded cell growth was observed in sgMBNL1-BS targeted cells compared with sgNT in MOLM13 and SEM cell lines, as indicated by the time-dependent decrease of the percentage of CFP+ signal (Fig. 3, E and F). Because the occupancy of dCas9-KRAB on the MBNL1-BS would heterochromatinize a large DNA region and may cause a broad effect, we then adopted a more specific CRISPR-Cas9 tool to introduce minimal perturbation of the peak center of the MBNL1-BS at the FLT3 enhancer with the same sgMBNL1-BS in KMT2A-r and non–KMT2A-r cells stably expressing Cas9 (Fig. 3G). Cell growth was notably retarded, as indicated by the time-dependent decrease of the portion of the CFP+ cells in KMT2A-r SEM, MOLM13, and MV411 cells, but not in non–KMT2A-r K562 and Nalm6 cell lines (Fig. 3, H to L). Note that the MBNL1-binding peak resided in a super-enhancer region, which was genomically amplified in SEM cells, but not in MOLM13 and OCI-AML2 cells (Fig. 3M). We next designed an additional ten sgRNAs distal to the sgMBNL1-BS (Fig. 3N). When the individual CPA assay was performed across all 10 sgRNA-targeted cells, we found that most sgRNAs targeting the amplified super-enhancer of FLT3 led to compromised cell survival (Fig. 3O). One of the 10 sgRNAs showed a moderate impact on cell survival in MOLM13 cells (Fig. 3P), and none affected OCI-AML2 cells (Fig. 3Q) as expected. Together, these data demonstrate that the MBNL1 protein regulates the proliferation of KMT2A-r cells by occupying a specific noncoding region within the FLT3 enhancer.

MBNL1 plays a critical role in primary KMT2A-r AML but not in HSPCs

Although fully characterized human cell lines served as robust resources for functional screening and phenotypic characterization, the potential culture adaptation of cell lines warned us that the observed MBNL1 role should be tested in a more physiology-relevant context. To mitigate this concern, we used a lentiviral shRNA targeting MBNL1 mRNA to conduct the loss-of-function assay in three human KMT2A-r patient-derived primary AML cells (Fig. 4A). Immunoblotting revealed a notable reduction of MBNL1 protein in shMBNL1-2–targeted cells compared with that in the shNT control, leading to a decrease of FLT3 expression (Fig. 4B). A colony-formation unit (CFU) assay was conducted and monitored for 14 days in culture from three primary AML samples targeted with shMBNL1-2 and shNT. Consistent with our observations in human cell lines, loss of MBNL1 significantly impaired colony formation in primary AML cells (Fig. 4C). We also examined the impact of MBNL1 loss in normal hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells collected from healthy donors. MBNL1 targeting did not change FLT3 expression levels (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, there are no notable changes in colony formation or cell expansion upon induced hematopoietic differentiation after 5 days of in vitro MBNL1 suppression (Fig. 4, E and F). In summary, these efforts supported the selective role of MBNL1 in the leukemia setting and might be an ideal therapeutic target for high-risk leukemia. Of course, long-term characterization of MBNL1’s function in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in a transplantation model or a genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) is needed to consolidate this observation.

MBNL1 binds FLT3 enhancer DNA in its single-strand configuration with secondary structure

To gain further insights into the noncanonical engagement of MBNL1 with chromatin, we systematically conducted biochemical assays to test the direct interaction between MBNL1 and the FLT3 enhancer DNA. The MBNL1 protein consists of four zinc fingers (namely, ZnF1234) at the N terminus responsible for RNA recognition, followed by a largely unstructured C terminus (named as CT) (Fig. 5A). We purified recombinant full-length and various truncated MBNL1 proteins. First, we confirmed that ZnF1234 is as efficient as the full-length MBNL1 in binding to a previously reported RNA S10 probe by both electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and fluorescence polarization (FP) (fig. S6, A and B). The apparent dissociation constants (Kd) for both proteins binding to the synthetic S10 RNA are around 90 nM. The EMSA assays further revealed that the full-length MBNL1 protein retards the RNA probe in wells, whereas the ZnF1234 truncation protein does not. It is possibly because the CT of MBNL1 forms oligomers, as evidenced by the size exclusion chromatography profile in vitro (fig. S6, C and D). Thus, we used the well-structured four–zinc finger domains for most of the following study. In parallel, two independent domain forms, ZnF12 and ZnF34, were included to specify the most essential DNA binding elements (Fig. 5A). We also arbitrarily divided the 207–base pair (bp) double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) fragment located in the distal enhancer of FLT3, as identified by MBNL1 ChIP-seq (Fig. 3C), into C1 through C4 (dsC1 to dsC4) (Fig. 5B) from 5′ to 3′ to explore the minimally required interaction sequence. Pure recombinant ZnF1234 protein at varying concentrations was incubated with the aforementioned oligo fragments for EMSA to assess MBNL1’s dsDNA binding activity in vitro. Unexpectedly, MBNL1 proteins bind the FLT3 enhancer dsDNA in a nonspecific manner with low affinity (fig. S7A), which is inconsistent with our previous ChIP-seq results.

Given that enhancer regions subjected to active transcription often exist in a single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) form (43, 44), we hypothesized that MBNL1 might recognize the FLT3 enhancer DNA in ssDNA conformation. Thus, we evaluated whether MBNL1 interacts with the FLT3 enhancer ssDNA in vitro. We synthesized the ssDNA version of C1 through C4 (ssC1 to ssC4), with 6-carboxyfluorescein (6-FAM) labeled on the 5′ end of the DNA. We evaluated whether MBNL1 binds to these ssDNA probes by EMSA assays. The result showed the presence of an unexpected lagging band of ssC4 during polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Fig. 5C) and that ZnF1234 preferentially binds to this lagging band, albeit weakly binds linear ssDNA at high concentration (fig. S7B). To identify the specific DNA sequence that forms higher-order conformation, three smaller fragments arbitrarily derived from C4 ssDNA (Fig. 5B) were synthesized and subjected to electrophoresis. The result revealed a 22-nucleotide (nt) ssDNA (named ssC4-3) that also exhibits a lagging band during electrophoresis (Fig. 5C), suggesting a noncanonical secondary structure. Next, we examined the primary sequence of ssC4-3 and found that it contains a five-guanine box (polyG5) (Fig. 5D), which often forms secondary DNA structures (45). Thus, we used site-directed mutagenesis to assess the contribution of each guanine to secondary structure formation. Electrophoresis revealed that the guanines in the middle of the polyG5 stretch are critical, as a single substitution of each guanine (Fig. 5D and fig. S8) disrupted the higher-order conformation.

Next, we sought to characterize the direct interaction between MBNL1 and the ssDNA by analyzing the binding activities of ZnF1234 with representative DNA probes such as wild-type (WT) or G3A-mutated ssC4-3, complementary reversed ssDNA chain of ssC4-3 (antisense ssC4-3), dsC4-3, or the 22-nt ssDNA preceding ssC4-3 (ssC4-2) (Fig. 5B). The EMSA results demonstrate that ZnF1234 preferentially recognizes ssC4-3 with secondary structure. At the same time, it barely binds with the ssC4-3 DNA in linear conformation, or other linear ones, either in ssDNA or dsDNA versions (Fig. 5E and fig. S9). Smeared bands in EMSA assays were observed for ZnF1234 binding to ssC4-3 DNA, indicating a transient association with varying numbers of ZnF1234 protein molecules, partially due to reduced binding affinity between the two. The FP assays show that the ZnF1234 protein binds to ssC4-3 with a Kd of around 620 nM (Fig. 5, E, G, and H), which is sevenfold higher than that for RNA binding (fig. S6B). The shorter ZnF12 and ZnF34 truncated proteins bound to the enhancer ssDNA with the Kd values of 1.2 and 5.18 μM, respectively (Fig. 5, F to H), in alignment with a previous report that ZF1234 performs better than an individual tandem zinc finger as per binding to RNA (46). Full-length MBNL1 exhibits the highest affinity for C4-3 ssDNA, as evident by both EMSA and FP assays (Fig. 5, F to H), with a Kd value of 78 nM. To explain the increased binding affinity in full-length MBNL1, we considered the possibility that the CT of MBNL1 might contribute to the ssDNA binding. We used recombinant MBNL1-CT protein and compared its binding activity to ssC4-3 and RNA S10 probes by FP assays. The results demonstrate that MBNL1-CT exhibits limited binding to S10 RNA (Kd of ∼150 μM) but recognizes ssC4-3 with a Kd of 4.7 μM (fig. S10). Together, these data demonstrate that the zinc fingers and CT domain of MBNL1 contribute to direct and specific recognition with FLT3 enhancer ssDNA, among which ZnF12 exhibits the highest binding affinity.

To gain further insights into the secondary structure of ssC4-3, which is preferentially recognized by the MBNL1 protein, we first used heat denaturation, which typically shifts the equilibrium between ssDNA’s secondary and linear conformations. The lagging band of C4-3 ssDNA resolved upon heating but reformed upon recovery (fig. S11A), confirming the higher-order conformation of ssC4-3 DNA. Next, the 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum evaluation for ssC4-3 WT or ssC4-3 with G3A substitution showed that only the WT ssC4-3 displays a classic signal for imino 1H in G-tetrads from G-quadruplex–like DNA (G4-like) between 10 to 12 parts per million (Fig. 6A) (47). Given that the primary sequence of ssC4-3 contains only one polyG5 stretch (Fig. 5D) and that the broadened spectral linewidth suggests an oligomer state, we speculated that ssC4-3 forms a tetrameric G4-like structure. To test this hypothesis, we mixed Texas Red–labeled ssC4 and FAM-labeled ssC4-3, heated, and then refolded (Fig. 6B) before analyzing it by electrophoresis. The result revealed that the refolded mixture consists of either hetero- or homotetramer, migrating to the expected location (Fig. 6C). The formation of such tetrameric G4-like structure in the cells can only be achieved either by DNA copy number gain for FLT3 enhancer (ssDNA only) or incorporation of the enhancer RNA (eRNA) transcribed from the FLT3 enhancer (mimicking ssDNA/eRNA hybrid) that recruits MBNL1 protein. To test this hypothesis, we first synthesized the RNA version of C4-3 labeled with FAM and found that it forms a similar secondary structure, as revealed by electrophoresis (fig. S11B). MBNL1 protein exhibits similar binding patterns for ssC4-3 and C4-3 RNA, as assessed by EMSA titration (Fig. 6D). Next, we mixed and heat-quenched the Texas Red–labeled ssC4 and FAM-labeled C4-3 RNA, allowing it to recover (as in Fig. 6B) to obtain the RNA/ssDNA heterotetramer. Electrophoresis results showed that, after recovery, RNA and ssDNA form a series of ssDNA/RNA hybrids in a tetrameric structure, which can be recognized by MBNL1 proteins as assayed by EMSA (Fig. 6, E and F). Collectively, these data show that polyG5 induces a distinct tetrameric G4-like structure within the FLT3 enhancer region that recruits MBNl1 protein in vitro.

Characterization of the FLT3 enhancer–binding interface of the MBNL1 protein

To identify potential enhancer-binding surfaces for MBNL1, we used NMR spectroscopy to map the interaction between MBNL1 and FLT3 enhancer DNA, with a focus on the G4-like structure formed by ssDNA because the binding pattern for MBNL1 with ssDNA and ssDNA/eRNA hybrids are similar (Fig. 6, D, F, and G). The 1H-15N heteronuclear single-quantum coherence (HSQC) spectra provide signals for the amide group of each residue except proline. The HSQC spectra of individual ZnF12 and ZnF34 superimpose well with the full-length ZnF1234 (fig. S12, A to D), excluding the possibility of long-distance interactions between these domains as reported previously (48). The cross peaks from the linker region between ZnF12 and ZnF34 are all located in the center region, indicative of a flexible, unstructured linker. Next, we titrated representative DNA probes into 15N-labeled ZnF1234 and monitored chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) or changes in peak intensity, which provide highly sensitive reports of local and global structural changes between different protein states (49). The overall difference was negligible when the ZnF1234 protein was titrated with dsC4-3 or ssC4-2 (fig. S12E and Fig. 7A) as neither notable CSP nor changes in peak intensity were observed. By contrast, many assigned cross peaks (48) [Biological Magnetic Resonance Data Bank (BMRB) (50), entry IDs 30207 for ZnF12 and 30208 for ZnF34] of 15N labeled MBNL1 ZnF1234 exhibit considerable changes in peak intensity after the addition of ssC4-3 (Fig. 7B), reflecting protein-DNA interaction.

Due to massive signal loss observed upon ssC4-3 addition in ZnF1234, we used individual tandem zinc finger constructs (ZnF12 and ZnF34) to better characterize the potential secondary-structured DNA BSs. As expected, less notable peak intensity reduction was detected in MBNL1-ZnF12 [exemplified by G48 and T32 in Fig. 5 (A to C)] and ZnF34 when titrated by ssC4-3 (Fig. 7, B to D). Analysis of most disturbed cross peaks from both ZnF constructs (arbitrary peak height ratio less than 0.1 for ZnF12 and 0.2 for ZnF34) leads to five regions responsible for interaction, ZnF1 through 4, and the long helix I located at the end of ZnF12 (Fig. 7, C and D). To gain further insights, we aligned the primary sequences of four CCCH zinc finger motifs, with the amino acid residues contributing to interaction highlighted in purple (Fig. 7, E and F). Similar interactions are observed for individual zinc fingers, for example, the conserved positively charged lysine and arginine (indicated by a star), in alignment with reports that each pair of zinc fingers binds to a single CGCU recognition site in single-stranded RNA (48). Aromatic amino acid residues from ZnF2 contributing to RNA binding (such as F54 and Y68) (48) also showed perturbation upon ssC4-3 addition, albeit to a lesser degree. Similarly, R84 from helix I (48), as well as E187 and D223 (51) from ZnF34 responsible for RNA binding, also participates in binding to ssC4-3 DNA in this study. These data suggest that ZnF1234 has a similar binding surface for both RNA and ssDNA. To test this hypothesis, we examined whether cold ssC4-3 can compete with MBNL1 proteins for prebound labeled S10 RNA probe. The EMSA assays showed that the prebound S10 probe by both MBNL1-FL and ZnF1234 protein can be stripped off by excess unlabeled ssC4-3 DNA (fig. S13), suggesting overlapped binding surfaces on zinc fingers.

To validate the identified ssDNA binding surfaces on MBNL1, we introduced several mutations in MBNL1 ZnF1234, with a focus on the most important ZnF12 (K67, I51, F54, and Y68 in ZnF2 CCCH motif and R84 in helix I) and the most conserved positive lysine contributing to binding in ZnF4 (K235) (Fig. 7F). The recombinant ZnF1234 proteins harboring one or two combined substitution(s) to alanine(s) (e.g., Y68A, I51/F54A, K67/R84A, and K67/235A) were prepared. The thermal shift assay confirmed that all of these proteins exhibited melting curves similar to those of the WT control ZnF1234, suggesting that the global structure of the mutant proteins remains intact as in WT (fig. S14). Then, EMSA assays was conducted by incubating ZNF1234 WT, Y68A, I51/F54A, K67/R84A, and K67/235A proteins with either ssC4-3 DNA or S10 RNA probes (Fig. 7G and fig. S15). As a result, the Y68A substitution slightly decreased ssDNA binding activity but retained RNA binding activity. The I51/F54A mutation drastically impaired ssDNA binding, with RNA binding moderately compromised. The introduction of K67/235A mutagenesis completely abolished the ssDNA binding affinity but retained moderate RNA binding activity. The S10 RNA complexed with the ZnF1234 K67/235A variant migrates faster during electrophoresis, possibly due to an increase in net negative charge resulting from the K-to-A substitutions. Similarly, K67/R84A abrogated MBNL1’s ability to bind ssDNA and RNA (fig. S15). Collectively, these data suggest that the secondary structured ssDNA binding activity of MBNL1 depends on critical positively charged amino acids and that the aromatic ones reside in the zinc fingers and helix I of ZnF12, which are at least partially shared for RNA interaction.

To determine the biological impact of the ssDNA binding–deficient form of MBNL1, we leveraged the power of the FKBP-dTAGv1 degron system (52) by fusing an FKBP tag to the CT of the WT and ssDNA binding–deficient form of MBNL1. In Cas9-expressing SEM cells, the endogenous MBNL1 gene was knocked out using sgMBNL1 targeting the coding sequence, followed by overexpression of the MBNL1-FKBP fusion protein in the knockout cells. The nature of MBNL1’s survival dependency shifted from endogenous MBNL1 to exogenous MBNL1-FKBP fusion protein (Fig. 7H). Administration of dTAGv1 conjugated the FKBP tag to the CRBN-mediated ubiquitination complex, inducing proteasomal degradation within a couple of hours. In the WT-MBNL1-FKBP setting, acute MBNL1 degradation decreased FLT3 expression at the protein level (Fig. 7I). When comparing the cell proliferation between WT and mutant form (K67A/K235A, ssDNA binding deficient as shown in vitro) of MBNL1 in CPA assay, compromised proliferation of SEM cells with mutant form expression was observed (Fig. 7J). Again, these functional data suggest that the ssDNA binding affinity of MBNL1 is required for FLT3 regulation and cell survival.

FLT3 enhancer adopts a ssDNA conformation in KMT2A-r cells

To test whether the FLT3 enhancer bound by MBNL1 exists as ssDNA in vivo, we established and performed Kethoxal-assisted single-strand DNA sequencing (KAS-seq) (53) (Fig. 8A and fig. S16, A and B) to obtain a genome-wide ssDNA profile in KMT2A-r and non–KMT2A-r cells. In total, we identified 72,369 peaks representing ssDNA in KMT2A-r SEM cells by KAS-seq. Integrating KAS-seq and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) data in SEM cells revealed that 129,638 sites were unique to ATAC-seq, 1347 were unique to KAS-seq, and 71,022 were shared between the assays (Fig. 8B). Motif enrichment analysis suggested the shared peaks enriched for the CTCF motif, highlighting the importance of the TF function and chromatin looping interaction (fig. S16, C and D). We further integrated publicly available ChIP-seq data for key cofactors in leukemogenesis, including KMT2A, MED26, MEIS1, SP1, and USF2. The heatmap revealed that only the shared sites were enriched for these TFs, as evidenced by the higher overall peak intensity in the shared group compared with ATAC-seq–specific sites (Fig. 8B). These data indicate that the KAS-ATAC shared sites represent open chromatin undergoing active transcription. We next annotated KAS-ATAC shared sites and ATAC-seq unique sites in the genome and compared their distributions. The result showed that KAS-ATAC shared sites retrieved more promoter regions than ATAC-specific sites, whereas fewer intergenic or intronic regions were retrieved by KAS-ATAC shared sites (Fig. 8C), suggesting that KAS-ATAC shared sites prefer the ssDNA conformation to accommodate active transcription. The FLT3 enhancer bound by MBNL1 displays a strong and unique signal in the KAS-ATAC shared dataset in KMT2A-r SEM cells, but not in non–KMT2A-r K562 cells (Fig. 8, D and E), which overlaps with the specific expression correlation and functional regulation of FLT3 by MBNL1 in the KMT2A-r setting. Overall, these data suggest that MBNL1 regulates FLT3 expression by specifically recognizing the FLT3 enhancer region in a single-stranded configuration during extensive transcription (Fig. 8F).

DISCUSSION

Multiple lines of evidence indicate that several RBPs are essential for leukemia cell survival and differentiation by regulating key oncogenes (54–57). In this study, we identified the RBP MBNL1 as a unexpected positive regulator of the oncogenic FLT3 in KMT2A-r leukemia cells through systematic genetic screens. Many RNA splicing regulation proteins, including RBM5, were identified as top positive regulators of HOXA9 in our recent whole-genome CRISPR screens. Intriguingly, the regulation of HOXA9 by RBM5 is not dependent on its splicing activity but requires the DNA binding C2H2 zinc finger domain of RBM5 (56). Our findings that MBNL1 loss, targeted by either sgRNA or shRNA, particularly impairs FLT3 expression, but not splicing events, and that KMT2A-r cells enrich for gene signatures related to FLT3 inhibition upon MBNL1 loss highlight the regulatory axis between MBNL1 and FLT3 in KMT2A-r cells. Furthermore, ectopic expression of FLT3 cDNA could restore impaired cell proliferation caused by MBNL1 loss. Collectively, in addition to the conventional splicing activity, the transcriptional regulatory mechanism is plausibly involved in MBNL1-mediated control of FLT3 expression and the subsequent fitness of KMT2A-r leukemia cells. In addition to our current study, multiple RBPs have been implicated in transcriptional control. For instance, Xiao et al. (41) systematically conducted a scale of RBP ChIP-seq analysis, revealing RBP binding on selective chromatin regions of the human genome. However, no detailed regulation mechanism has been provided yet. Our findings that MBNL1 displays noncanonical chromatin binding and gene regulation provide clues to the molecular mechanisms underlying transcriptional regulation by RBPs.

Our biochemical assay identifies that MBNL1 recognizes a specific secondary conformation of ssDNA element within the FLT3 enhancer, primarily through its ZnF12 domains, with stabilization by ZnF34 and the newly identified CT of MBNL1. This noncanonical structured ssDNA binding activity uses surfaces on the zinc finger domains, previously assumed to be dedicated to RNA recognition, and exhibits nuanced specificity as mutagenesis of key residues selectively disrupted ssDNA binding. Furthermore, the unstructured C-terminal region of MBNL1, which contributes minimally to RNA binding, especially enhances ssDNA affinity, highlighting a specialized, cooperative domain architecture that enables engagement with a secondary structure-prone, polyG-rich ssDNA element. Note that, in our NMR assay, ZnF1234 contains an unstructured region of amino acids 93 to 172 between ZnF12 and ZnF34. This flexible linker region allows free motion between ZnF12 and ZnF34. The structural arrangement may help MBNL1 accommodate DNA of different lengths.

Our KAS-seq data from both SEM and K562 cells indicate that the CTCF motif was most enriched in shared peaks between ATAC-seq and KAS-seq. CTCF was considered a critical TF and chromatin-looping mediator to maintain cross-talk between promoters and distal enhancers, further supporting the importance of the distal ssDNA state for corresponding gene regulation. Our results also demonstrate that the MBNL1-BS at the FLT3 enhancer exists in a single-stranded conformation specifically in KMT2A-r leukemia cells, but not in non–KMT2A-r cells, providing compelling physiological evidence for this protein-structured ssDNA interaction occurring in a functional chromatin context. This context-dependent ssDNA signature, in combination with selective MBNL1 binding in this enhancer region, not only provides a mechanistic basis for the notable lineage-specificity of MBNL1’s transcriptional function but also sheds light on why MBNL1 regulates FLT3 expression in a singular pair. A similar regulatory pair was previously observed between ZNF410 and CHD4 in erythroid cells (58, 59). Our finding that MBNL1 selectively recognizes the structured ssDNA element may contribute to its unique singular regulation. However, future studies are warranted to test whether ssDNA within the FLT3 enhancer region adopts a higher-order conformation and enables the singular regulation of FLT3 in KMT2A-r cells.

In conclusion, this work redefines MBNL1 from a splicing regulator to a context-dependent transcriptional activator that drives leukemia maintenance through ssDNA enhancer binding. It unveils a new layer of gene regulation in which the DNA structural state serves as a critical determinant of RBP recruitment and function. This paradigm shift not only deepens our understanding of the KMT2A-r leukemia regulatory network but also opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention by targeting the MBNL1-ssDNA interface. Several RBPs were found to be druggable. For instance, the carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, indisulam, induces a ternary protein complex between RBM39 and the E3 ubiquitin complex, resulting in rapid proteasomal degradation of RBM39, aberrant RNA splicing, and cell death in AML (60). Targeting RBM39 with indisulam demonstrated promising therapeutic outcomes in treating neuroblastoma (61) and AML (62). To this end, we anticipate that our studies will pave the way to targeting MBNL1 in the future.

Limitations

Although the elegant degron system and biochemistry approaches defined the immediate role of MBNL1 in FLT3 gene regulation, the long-term effect of MBNL1 is a combination of splicing and transcriptional regulation. It is very challenging to separate the two mechanisms at work in leukemia biology. Also, the limited number of MBNL1-binding peaks suggested the unique role of downstream target selection. However, it is also possible that the current ChIP-seq platform is not optimal for characterizing the RBPs. Epigenomic profiling suggested that KMT2A-fusion proteins, epigenetic factors, and multiple TFs bind adjacent to the MBNL1-BS, a region that might play a similar role in FLT regulation and warrants further investigation. Last, the forward movement of MBNL1 into drug discovery and the long-term effects on normal development require stringent investigation using GEMM models.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Vector construction

The internal ribosomal entry site (IRES)–CFP expression cassette was cloned into Lenti-Guide-Puro (Addgene, no. 52963) to generate a new sgRNA-expressing plasmid, Lenti-Guide-Puro-IRES-CFP. The MBNL1 coding exon sgRNA and sgMBNL1-BS were synthesized (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cloned into the Lenti-sgRNA-Puro-IRES-CFP construct. The Lenti-Cas9-Blast (Addgene, no. 83480) and Lenti-dCas9-KRAB-blast (Addgene, no. 89567) were purchased from Addgene. The constitutive overexpression lentiviral backbone pCDH-MND-FLT3-EF1-RFP was created by inserting FLT3 cDNA into pCDH-MND-MCS-EF1-RFP (30). Doxycycline-inducible Tet-On lentiviral vector was created by subcloning the MND promoter-driven blasticidin expression cassette into the TRE3G vector (Takara, no. 631363). The guide RNA sequences were provided in table S1.

Cell culture

The SEM, MOLM13, MV411, OCI-AML2, K562, and Nalm6 leukemia cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone), 2 mM glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in an incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity. High-titer viruses of Lenti-sgRNA-Puro-IRES-CFP, Lenti-Cas9-Blast, and Lenti-dCas9-KRAB-Blast were generated by cotransfection of helper plasmids (VSVG, RTR2, and GAGPOL) in 293T cells, which were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (HyClone) and 2 mM l-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C in 5% CO2. Leukemia cells were transduced with either Lenti-Cas9-Blast or Lenti-dCas9-KRAB-Blast and selected for stable transduction by blasticidin (InvivoGen). All cell lines were validated by Short Tandom Repeat (STR) (institutional core facility) and verified to be free of mycoplasma by the Lookout Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, no. MP0035).

Patients with primary AML and normal bone marrow samples

All primary cells from patients with AML and normal donor bone marrow were obtained from the Hematological Biobank, Jiangsu Biobank of Clinical Resources. The informed consent forms were approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Following Ficoll-Paque separation, mononuclear cells were stored in the vapor phase of liquid nitrogen in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide, 30% FBS, and 60% DMEM. Primary samples were thawed in warmed 50% FBS, 50% X-VIVO 15 with deoxyribonuclease (100 mg/ml; AML), or 100% FBS before use for in vitro assays. Primary AML samples were grown in X-VIVO 15 with 20% BIT Serum Substitute (STEMCELL Technologies, 09500) with human Stem Cell Factor (SCF) (100 ng/ml; PeproTech, 300-07), human FLT3 (100 ng/ml; PeproTech, 300-19); human thrombopoietin (TPO; 20 ng/ml; PeproTech, 300-18), human interleukin-6 (IL-6; 20 ng/ml; STEMCELL Technologies, 78050), and human IL-3 (10 ng/ml; STEMCELL Technologies, 78042). The human CD34+ HSPCs were mobilized from normal subjects by granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, collected by apheresis, and enriched by immunomagnetic bead selection using the EasySep Human CD34-Positive Selection Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 18056), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The CD34+ HSPCs were cultured in Stem-Span SFEM II (STEMCELL Technologies, 09650) with human TPO (20 ng/ml), human SCF (100 ng/ml), human FLT3 (100 ng/ml), human IL-6 (20 ng/ml), and penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Primary human AML cells and CD34+ HSPC transduction

For primary AML samples, 0.5 million cells were infected with lentivirus at an MOI of 50 in 24-well ultralow attachment plates containing 500 μl of total growth medium; an additional 500 μl of growth medium was added 16 hours after infection, followed by puromycin selection for 24 hours. For CD34+ HSPC transduction, lentivirus was added at an MOI of 50, and cells were selected with puromycin for 24 hours. Cells were grown for another 2 days before further study.

Colony-forming unit assay

The clonogenic potential was assessed by seeding the indicated number of cells in methylcellulose medium. Sixteen hours (for primary AML) or 24 or 72 hours (for CD34+ HSPCs) post–lentiviral infection, freshly selected using puromycin for 24 hours (healthy shRNA-expressing), cells were plated in complete methylcellulose medium (STEMCELL Technologies, H4230) (primary AML) or Human Methylcellulose Complete Media (STEMCELL Technologies, H4034) (CD34+ HSPCs) and grown in 37°C and 5% CO2 incubator for 10 to 14 days before imaging and counting. For primary AML, 7000 to 30,000 cells/ml were plated. For CD34+ HSPCs, 750 to 1000 cells/ml were plated.

Cell proliferation experiments

About 1 × 105 cells per well (in a total volume of 2 ml) were seeded in each well of a six-well plate and cultured in SFEM II supplemented with StemSpan Myeloid Expansion Supplement. Every 3 days, 1 ml of fresh medium was added. Cell numbers were counted on days 3 and 5, respectively, and the proliferation curve was plotted using Prism software.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer, and lysates were run on an SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Bio-Rad) at 100 V for 1 hour. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBS-T [10 mM tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5% Tween 20] for 1 hour at room temperature before incubating with target antibodies overnight at 4°C with gentle rocking [HSP70 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, no. sc7298), MBNL1, (Merck Millipore, no. MABE70), HA (Cell Signaling Technology, no. 3724S), and ACTIN (Sigma-Aldrich, no. A2228)]. Following three 10-min washes in TBS-T, membranes were incubated with sheep anti-mouse immunoglobulin G horseradish peroxidase (GE Healthcare, NA931) in 5% nonfat milk/TBS-T for 1 hour at room temperature. Blots were developed with Enhanced Chemiluminescence detection reagent (ECL; PerkinElmer) and visualized with the Li-Cor System.

Quantitative real-time PCR

The High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcriptase kit (Applied Biosystems, 4374966) was used to make cDNA from RNA, and FAST SYBR Green Master Mix was used for real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR; Applied Biosystems, 4385612) with primers to amplify FLT3 and GAPDH transcripts. FLT3 forward (F): CCGCTGCTCGTTGTTTTT; FLT3 reverse (R): GGCAGATCTTGATTTGTAATAGTCC; GAPDH (F): 5′AGGGCTGCTTTTAACTCTGGT3′, GAPDH (R): 5′CCCCACTTGATTTTGGAGGGA3′. The ΔΔCT method was used to determine relative expression levels (63).

RNA-seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from replicate samples using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 15596026). About 200 ng of total RNA was treated using Kapa ribosomal RNA depletion reagents to remove ribosomal RNA, followed by conversion into cDNA libraries using Kapa RNA HyperPrep Kit with RiboErase (Roche HMR). After the end repair procedure, dA-tailing, and adapter ligation, each cDNA library was purified and enriched by 11 cycles of PCR amplification. We performed the paired-end 101-cycle sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) and analyzed the data using a standard in-house pipeline. Briefly, raw reads were trimmed using TrimGalore (version 0.6.3, “--paired --retain_unpaired”) and aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome GRCh37.p13(hg19) using STAR (version 2.7.9a) (64). Gene-level read quantification was performed using RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization (RSEM; version 1.3.1) based on the Gencode annotation version 19 (65). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the trimmed mean of M values (TMM) normalization method (genes with counts per million of <1 in all samples were removed), followed by Limma-voom analysis using the “voom,” “lmFit,” and “eBayes” functions from the limma R package (66).

ChIP-seq and data analysis

ChIP assays were performed using antibodies against MBNL1 (Merck Millipore, no. MABE70) and HA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, no. sc-7392) in SEM and MOLM13 cells. Biological replicates were prepared for ChIP-seq according to the previously described protocol with minor modifications (67). About 10 million cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/ml. Fixed samples were then aliquoted into 10 million cells per tube and snap-frozen in dry ice. We performed single-end 51-cycle sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina). Raw reads were trimmed using TrimGalore (version 0.6.3) and aligned to the H. sapiens reference genome GRCh37.p13(hg19) genome using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA; version 0.7.17-r1198). Duplicated and low-mapping-quality reads were removed using the “bamsormadup” function from the biobambam2 tool (version 2.0.87) and SAMtools (version 1.9, parameter “-q 1 -F 1024”) (68). The fragment size in each sample was estimated from the cross-correlation profile calculated with SPP (version 1.11). Fragments were extended to a fragment size and normalized to 15 million reads to generate bigwig files. Macs2 was used to call peaks using parameters “-g hs --nomodel –extsize <SPP_fragmentSize>.” For each sample, both “high confidence peaks” (parameter “-q 0.05”) and “low confidence peaks” (parameter “-q 0.5”) were called. Reproducible peaks (called a high-confidence peak in at least one replicate that overlapped with a low-confidence peak in the other replicates) were generated for each cell type. Differential peaks were identified using the empirical Bayes method from the limma R package. For downstream analyses, heatmaps and Spearman’s correlation coefficients were generated using deepTools (69).

CRISPR library construction and screening

A set of ∼10,000 sgRNA oligonucleotides that targeted ∼1800 human RBPs was designed for array-based oligonucleotide synthesis (CustomArray). The unique binding pattern of each sgRNA was verified by sequence blast against the entire human genome (hg38). The synthesized oligonucleotide pool was amplified by PCR and cloned into the Lenti-sgRNA-Puro-IRES-CFP backbone using an In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Clontech, no. 638909). The Cas9-expressing KMT2A-r SEM cell line was infected with the pooled sgRNA library at a low MOI (∼0.3). Transduced cells were selected by puromycin to enrich for sgRNA integration. After the cells recovered from the antibiotic selection process, the pooled population was selected with puromycin for 3 days and sorted by FLT3 staining into high- and low-expression populations. Staining of FLT3 was conducted using CD135 (FLT3) monoclonal antibody (BV10A4H2) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 17-1357-42) at a 1:400 dilution with 5% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The sgRNA sequences were recovered by genomic PCR and deep sequencing on a Novas-Seq system (Illumina) using single-end 150-bp reads. The raw FASTQ data were demultiplexed and counted as 20- or 19-mers using bbmap (version 37.28, “kmercountexact.sh fastadump = f mincount = 1 k = 20 rcomp = f”), and the counts were assigned to each sgRNA. MAGeCK (version 0.5.9.4, default parameters) algorithm was used for statistical analysis of CRISPR screen results.

Expression and preparation of MBNL1 recombinant proteins

The coding sequence of MBNL1 (UniProtKB, Q9NR56) was cloned into the pCoofy expression vector (Novagen) containing a His10-tag and a maltose-binding protein (MBP) tag, followed by an HRV 3C protease cleavage site at the N terminus. The following MBNL1 constructs were prepared using sequence and ligation-independent cloning approach (SLIC, Vazyme): the zinc fingers 1 and 2, ZnF12 (residues 1 to 92); the zinc fingers 3 and 4, ZnF34 (residues 173 to 255), the zinc fingers 1, 2, 3, and 4, ZnF1234 (residues 1 to 255); and the CT (residues 256 to 388). The MBNL1 mutants were constructed by site-directed mutagenesis using Phanta High Fidelity DNA polymerase (Vazyme). Unlabeled protein samples were produced in BL21 (DE3) cells grown in Luria-Bertani medium at 37°C in the presence of kanamycin (50 μg/ml) to an absorbance at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.8. Isotopically labeled samples for NMR studies were prepared by growing the cells in minimal medium (M9) supplemented with 15NH4Cl (1 g/liter) to an OD600 of ≈0.6. Protein induction was induced by adding 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside and 0.1 mM ZnCl2, and cells were cultured for an additional 16 hours at 18°C. Cells were harvested and resuspended in lysis buffer [50 mM tris-HCl and 500 mM NaCl, (pH 8.0)], sonicated, and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min. Polyethylenimine was added drop by drop into the supernatant to precipitate the nucleic acids to a final concentration of 0.22%. The cleared supernatant was reprecipitated with sulfate (80% saturation) and then resuspended in lysis buffer. For ZnF12, ZnF34, and MBNL1-CT, proteins were purified using Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow resin (Smart-Lifesciences) followed by gel filtration using Superdex 75 16/60 column (GE Healthcare). Tag removal by HRV 3C protease treatment at 4°C (incubation for 16 hours) was applied to ZnF12 and ZnF34 before the gel filtration. For ZnF1234 and the mutant forms, the resuspended protein from ammonium sulfate precipitation was buffer exchanged into buffer A [20 mM tris and 5 μM ZnCl2 (pH 8.0)] and loaded onto a HiPrep Q HP column (GE Healthcare). The protein of interest was eluted by increasing the percentage of buffer B [20 mM tris, 1 M NaCl, and 5 μM ZnCl2 (pH 8.0)]. The MBP tag was then cleaved by HRV 3C protease treatment at 4°C (16 hours of incubation) and removed by a second HiPrep Q HP column elution. The ZnF1234 protein variants were then further purified by a Superdex 75 16/60 column. Protein concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 280 nm using the corresponding extinction coefficient. All primer sequences were provided in table S1.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The full-length double- or single-stranded C1, C2, C3, C4, and C4-3 probe fragments from the FLT-3 enhancer (207 bp) DNA used in the EMSA assays were synthesized, with FAM labeled on the 5′. The RNA (S10) oligonucleotides 5′UCGCUUUUCCCCUCCGCU3′ used in the EMSA assay were synthesized with a 5′ FAM label (General Biol). For the preparation of ssDNAs or ssDNA/RNA heterotetramer probes, 5′ Texas Red–labeled (General Biol) ssC4 probe was mixed with 5′ FAM–labeled ssC4-3 or C4-3 RNA with a molar ratio 1:3, subjected to heat quench at 95°C for 5 min, and recovered at 4°C for 24 hours. MBNL1 proteins and individual probes were mixed in binding buffer [20 mM tris, 50 mM NaCl, 5 μM ZnCl2 (pH 7.4), and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)] to a final sample volume of 6 μl. The concentration for DNA and RNA probes was 1 μM. The amount of MBNL1 proteins ranged from 0 to 28 μM. After a 10-min incubation at 25°C, samples were separated by 6% nondenaturing PAGE in running buffer containing 45 mM tris-borate and 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.2). Gels were visualized by the module green fluorescence lamp and the module red fluorescence lamp of a Tanon 5100 instrument (Bio-Tanon) for 5′ FAM–labeled probes and 5′ Texas Red–labeled probes, respectively, with the help of Tanon Image software. All probe sequences were provided in table S1.

FP assays

FAM-labeled DNA or RNA was used as fluorescent tracers (General Biology Inc.). Increasing amounts of MBNL1 proteins (0.002 to 8 μM) were mixed with tracer (30 nM final concentration) in a 384-well microplate (Labselect, 781900) and incubated in Hepes buffer [25 mM Hepes/NaOH (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 2 mM DTT] for 10 min at room temperature. Changes in FP were monitored by a Spark microplate reader (Tecan) equipped with a FP filter unit. The mP change in fraction bound was plotted against MBNL1 protein concentration and fit using the formula [y = x/(Kd + x)] in OriginLab. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

NMR spectroscopy

NMR samples were prepared in solution with 20 mM tris (pH 7.0), 75 mM KCl, 5 μM ZnCl2, 2 mM DTT, and 7% D2O. NMR spectra were recorded at 25°C on a Bruker 600-MHz spectrometer with a 5-mm Triple Resonance Inverse CryoProbe. The protein concentration is 0.1 mM. For the NMR titration, 0.05 mM MBNL1 ZnF1234, ZnF12, or ZnF34 proteins were mixed with an equal amount of DNA probes, and the NMR spectra were collected. All NMR spectra were processed using NMRPipe (70) and analyzed using Sparky. The 1H-15N assignments of the MBNL1 protein were obtained from the BMRB (50) database (entry IDs: 30207 and 30208).

Thermal shift assay

Purified MBNL1 ZnF1234 and other mutant proteins were diluted to 1 mg/ml in DNA binding buffer. Nineteen microliters of each protein were transferred to one well in a 96-well plate, and 1 μl of 1× SYPRO Orange (Thermo Fisher Scientific, S6650) was added to each well. The plate was sealed and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 2 min. The fluorescent signal at 570 nm was recorded using a QuantStudio 3 RT-PCR machine, with a temperature ramp from 25° to 95°C. The data were analyzed and visualized using DSFworld (71, 72).

Competitive proliferation assay

To evaluate candidate sgRNAs’ impact on leukemia cell expansion, Cas9-expressing or dCas9-KRAB-expressing leukemia cells were transduced with individual sgRNAs in CFP expressing lentiviral vectors, followed by measurement of the percentage of CFP-positive cells at various days post-infection. The percentage of CFP-positive cells was normalized to the starting point (day 3) for sgNT cells and monitored over 14 days. The decline trend was used to infer a defect in cell proliferation conferred by a given sgRNA, as reflected in survival relative to uninfected cells in the same culture. For CPA with rescued FLT3 expression, Cas9-expressing or dCas9-KRAB-expressing leukemia cells were first infected with lentiviral-expressing FLT3 cDNA, followed by reinfection with individual sgRNAs in CFP-expressing lentiviral vectors.

KAS-ATAC-seq

The ATAC-RBS was made with 10 mM tris-HCl, 10 mM NaCl, and 3 mM MgCl2. Approximately 150,000 viable cells were collected by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min. Add 150 μl of cold ATAC-RSB+++ [0.1% NP-40, 0.1% Tween 20, and 0.01% digitonin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BN20060)]. Pipette samples and then incubate them on ice for 3 min. Add 1 ml of ATAC-RBS+ (0.1% Tween 20) and invert to mix. Split samples into two tubes for labeling. Centrifuge the mixture again at 500g for 5 min at 4°C and then discard the supernatant. Resuspend the pellets with 100 μl of 10 mM N3-Kethoxial (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A8793) in PBS. Incubate at 37°C with shaking at 500 rpm for 10 min. Following incubation, wash the nuclei by adding 1 ml of PBS and centrifuging at 500g for 5 min, 4°C to remove the supernatant. Conduct the transposition reaction by resuspending the nuclei pellet in 1× transposition mix and incubating the mixture at 37°C for 30 min in a thermomixer at 1000 rpm. The reaction is stopped by adding 250 μl of DNA binding buffer from the DNA Clean and Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research, D4013). Purify the DNA following the kit’s instructions. Elute the DNA by 43 μl of 25 mM boric acid (K3B03, pH 7.0) (Sigma-Aldrich, 15663). Next, perform a biotinylation reaction by adding 5 μl of 10× PBS and then 2.5 μl of 20 mM DBCO-PEG4-biotin (Sigma-Aldrich, 760749). Incubate the mixture at 37°C for 1.5 hours at 500 rpm. Following incubation, add 2.5 μl of ribonuclease A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12091039) and incubate for an additional 5 min at 37°C. Purify the DNA using the DNA Clean and Concentrator Kit. Elute by 55 μl of K3B03 (pH 7.0). Save 5 μl as an input and keep the remaining DNA for the enrichment procedure. Resuspend the prewashed Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 in 50 μl of 2× binding and wash buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 2 M NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20]. Mix the beads with 50 μl of fragmented DNA. Incubate at room temperature for 15 min with gentle rotation. After incubation, place on magnetic rack, remove the supernatant, and wash the beads with 100 μl of 1× binding and wash buffer [5 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 M NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20]. Repeat the wash step five times and elute with 15 μl of dH2O. The enriched DNA should be heated to 72°C for 5 min and then to 95°C for 10 min and then used for 12 cycles of amplification (98°C, 30 s; 63°C, 30 s; and 72°C, 1 min). Purify the amplified DNA using AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, A63880), index, and conduct deep-sequencing.

CDAA algorithm

Expression data and CRISPR-dependent score were downloaded from the DepMap database (“Expression_22Q2_Public_subsetted.csv”) for blood lineage and “CRISPR_gene_dependency.csv” (https://ndownloader.figshare.com/files/34990033). We first get a subset of genes with a CRISPR dependency score of >0.9 in any of the 13 KMT2A-r cell lines. Then, for each dependent gene in KMT2A-r, we calculated its expression in Pearson’s correlation to each of the other genes in the KMT2A-r cell lines. Then, we randomly select 13 cell lines from the blood lineage and calculate the correlation again as one permutation. We repeat this permutation 100,000 times and count how many times the correlation in KMT2A-r cells is higher than in permutated cells. The P value is the count divided by 100,000, indicating that the confidence level of a gene is correlated with the gene, specifically in KMT2A-r leukemia cell lines.
