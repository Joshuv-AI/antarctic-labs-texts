Capillary minimal slicing and Scalar curvature rigidity
Dongyeong Ko and Xuan Yao

Abstract
. We develop minimal slicing via capillary hypersurfaces to understand positive scalar curvature metric on manifolds with boundary. The method provides rigidity statements once the regularity of minimizers of capillary area functional holds. In particular, in dimension 4 4 , we prove following comparison and rigidity statement: given a compact Riemannian 4 4 -manifold ( M 4 , g ) (M^{4},g) with a mean convex boundary whose boundary is diffeomorphic to boundary of a connected convex domain in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} , if the scalar curvature is non-negative and the scaled mean curvature comparison holds along the boundary, then M M is isometric to the Euclidean domain.



1.
Introduction

Studying geometry and topology of manifolds with positive scalar curvature has been a fundamental topic in differential geometry, which appears both in geometry and general relativity naturally. Among the questions involving with scalar curvature including comparison theorems, positive mass theorem, topological classification, interactions between interior scalar curvature and boundary mean curvature have given clues on the local and global geometry of positive scalar curvature metric.

There have been several major approaches to understand manifolds with positive scalar curvature such as Dirac operator method, the variational method of area functional and harmonic functions. In particular, the variational approach with minimal hypersurfaces has played an important role in developing the theory, while many fundamental questions on manifolds with scalar curvature lower bound remains unknown. More specifically, many known methods to deduce the comparison and rigidity statement via variational methods rely on the stability property of a minimizer of area functional of hypersurfaces. The stability inequality gives comparison and rigidity of geometric and topological quantities. In particular, obtaining rigidity statements through variational methods have been successful in three dimensional geometry, where we have more direct ways to extract geometric and topological quantities using classical theorems such as Gauss-Bonnet. However, despite recent progress on the control of quantities in higher dimensions, for instance,

1.1.
Main results

We now describe our capillary minimal slicing which provides applications in comparison and rigidity statements, in particular, in non-spin settings. We adopt Schoen-Yau’s weighted minimal slicing technique to manifold with boundary. In the slicing
X\subset X\times S^{1}\subset...\subset X\times T^{n-k}

1.2.
Outline of the proof

We outline the proof of main results. The result mainly divides into the proof of the slicing theorem (Theorem

1.3

The first key ingredient is the local mean curvature estimate (Proposition

\partial M

\partial\bar{M}
of an Euclidean domain. In particular, for any orthonormal frame

and orthonormal frame
\{\bar{\tau}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of

(3)

\displaystyle H_{\partial M}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\textup{II}(\tau_{i},\bar{\tau}_{i}),

This is a generalization of the local curvature estimate in dimension
3
in Ko-Yao
[
28
]
(similar trace type quantity and estimate appear in Bär-Brendle-Chow-Hanke
[
2
]
and Wang-Wang-Zhou
[
45
]
). The estimate follows from the boundary mean curvature comparison
H_{g}^{2}g|_{\partial M}\geq H_{0}^{2}g_{E}|_{\partial\bar{M}}
for principal curvature directions and is extended to general directions by rotations. In particular, the equality condition aligns
\tau_{i}
to
\bar{\tau}_{i}
for all

The main part of the proof of the main slicing theorem (Theorem
1.2
) is devoted to obtaining a winding number in the bottom slice and then obtaining the Euclidean rigidity. More precisely, we use the generalization of the local curvature estimates and an inductive procedure to prove that the boundary integral term which comes from the stability inequality is bounded below by a winding number.
In dimension
4
, we prove the existence of the minimal slicing. In each slicing, the construction of a graphical foliation which serves as a barrier which has nonnegative mean curvature and contact angle which is larger than the prescribed angle functions
\{\bar{\theta}_{i}\}
. In particular, the construction of the foliation in the first slicing is the direct generalization of the construction in dimension
3
in Ko-Yao
[
28
]
, where the nonnegative mean curvature of leaves follow from the mean curvature comparison between the boundaries in Riemannian and Euclidean metric. However, we do not have comparison (weighted) mean curvature at points of the slice where the direction of normal vector of Riemannian and Euclidean slicing do not agree. In other words, the normal vector of
\partial\Sigma_{1}
in
\partial M
does not agree with the normal vector of
\partial\bar{\Sigma}_{1}
in
\partial\bar{M}
in general. To overcome lack of comparison, we pick a point
p_{2}
on
\partial\Sigma_{1}
such that the
x_{4}
-coordinate of
p_{1}
is maximum over
\partial\Sigma_{1}
and take the axis
x_{3}
to achieve the maximum coordinate at
p_{1}
. With this procedure, we can identify
T_{p_{1}}\partial\Sigma_{1}
and
T_{p_{1}}\partial\bar{\Sigma}_{1}
and apply the strong version of the local curvature estimate, and obtain the desired foliation on each slicing with a specific coordinate.

The proof of the rigidity statement (Theorem
1.3
) separates into two parts: extracting the infinitesimal rigidity condition on slices from the stability inequality, and proving the global rigidity from the infinitesimal rigidity. We obtain the infinitesimal rigidity from the stability analysis of the (weighted) capillary area functional. Assuming the existence of a codimension
n
stable minimal slicing, we can modify the second variation formula by slicing identities and obtain the estimate on the bottom slice
\Sigma_{n}^{2}
(Proposition
2.3
). Another important ingredients are angle identities (Proposition
2.4
). The identities transform the first derivative of the angle function on the boundary by the second fundamental form on the boundary of the Euclidean domain. Combining the angle identity and the local estimate (
3
) with Proposition
2.3
, we obtain the global comparison and rigidity by the winding number argument as in dimension
3
.

We prove the global rigidity using the infinitesimal rigidity by proving the local splitting theorem. Assuming the existence of a flat nontrivial minimizer, we adopt the construction of dense set of flat slices on small neighborhood of the minimizer developed in Carlotto-Chodosh-Eichmair
[
6
]
, relies on the conformal deformation of the metric near the minimizer. Since the argument does not depend on the dimension, we can apply the construction as far as the regularity of the minimizer is guaranteed. In dimension 4, minimizers of the capillary area functional are smooth and we can prove the curvature estimate for area-minimizing capillary minimal hypersurfaces by Chodosh-Edelen-Li
[
11
]
, which gives the compactness of minimizing hypersurfaces.

1.3.
Discussion in higher dimensions

The obstructions to prove the rigidity theorem (Theorem
1.4
) in dimensions larger than or equal to
5
are twofold. One is the lack of regularity of minimizer of the capillary area functional and another is that it is hard to find an extrinsic third angle functional that would guaranty the existence of a nontrivial minimizer.

The best full regularity of the capillary minimizer known to authors is codimension
4
singularity in
[
11
]
, and this may preclude us from the stability analysis of minimizers (See
[
16
]
and
[
37
]
for partial regularities in higher dimensions). Since the possible singularities of capillary minimizers are isolated singularities in dimension
5
, the regularity issue may be diverted by analyzing stability inequality outside those singular sets (See
[
25
]
and
[
7
]
for such analysis on point singularities).

Another significant issue on iterative capillary minimal slicings is the choice of angle functionals that ensure the existence of a nontrivial minimizer. We obtain the existence of a nontrivial minimizer by constructing a barrier which achieves a strictly negative capillary area functional. An eligible foliation can be achieved by a large contact angle with nonnegative mean curvature on leaves. As we discussed earlier, this foliation can be obtained in a small neighborhood at all points for the first slicing, and can be obtained at a point where tangent planes of codimension
2
slices in
g
metric and Euclidean metric agree for the second slicing by maximum principle type arguments. However, we cannot guaranty the existence of such a point for the third slicing. Without the coincidence of two tangent planes
T_{p_{3}}\partial\Sigma_{3}
and
T_{p_{3}}\partial\bar{\Sigma}_{3}
, We have neither a mean curvature comparison derived from the local estimate (
3
) nor a contact angle comparison.

In terms of a contact angle comparison, a local foliation in a Euclidean metric is necessary to compare contact angles with one in a Riemannian metric. However, we may not have a local foliation without a fixation of two tangent planes since we do not have a control of contact angle function
\bar{\theta}_{3}
on different slices.

1.4.
Organization

The organization of this paper is as follows. In Section

Proposition 3.1

\displaystyle H_{\partial M}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textup{II}(\tau_{i},\bar{\tau}_{i}),

where
H_{\partial M}^{2}g|_{F^{\#}\textup{II}_{+}}=\bar{H}_{\partial M}^{2}g_{E}|_{\textup{II}_{+}}
when the equality holds.

Proof.

Since the inequality is symmetric, we can rotate
\{\bar{\tau}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
appropriately and take the matrix
(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}=(\textup{II}(\bar{\tau}_{i},\bar{\tau}_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
simultaneously with
\{\bar{\tau}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. More specifically there exists an orthogonal matrix
(O_{ij})_{n\times n}\in SO(n)
such that
\tau_{i}^{\prime}=O_{ij}\tau_{j}
and
\bar{\tau}_{i}^{\prime}=O_{ij}\bar{\tau}_{j}
and we replace
\tau_{i}
and
\bar{\tau}_{i}
by
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
and
\bar{\tau}_{i}^{\prime}
, respectively. Note that
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is preserved to be an orthonormal set in metric

since a rotation is represented by an orthogonal matrix.

Let

W_{i}=\frac{a_{ii}}{\bar{H}_{\partial M}}

for
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,3\}
and note that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}=1
. By the boundary comparison condition, we have

(24)

\displaystyle H_{\partial M}\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\bar{H}_{\partial M}g_{E}(\tau_{i},\bar{\tau}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}g_{E}(\tau_{i},\bar{\tau}_{i})

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textup{II}(\tau_{i},\bar{\tau}_{i}),

where (

) gives

(
25
) follows from the coordinate decomposition and the fact that
(a_{ij})
is a diagonal matrix.
Also note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textup{II}(O_{ij}\tau_{j},O_{ik}\bar{\tau}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}O_{ij}O_{ik}\textup{II}(\tau_{j},\bar{\tau}_{k})

\displaystyle=\delta_{jk}\textup{II}(\tau_{j},\bar{\tau}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textup{II}(\tau_{i},\bar{\tau}_{i}).

Hence the right hand side sum is an invariant over rotations. The equality condition also follows from the rotation.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain the key boundary estimate.

Corollary 3.1

(Key boundary estimate)

For any

\displaystyle H_{\partial M}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{j\leq i}\sin\bar{\theta}_{j}\partial_{\nu_{i}}\bar{\theta}_{i}\geq\textup{II}(\tau_{1},\bar{\tau}_{1}).

Since

.

n

(28)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma_{n}}\frac{1}{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\sin\bar{\theta_{i}}}\left(H_{\partial M}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{j\leq i}\sin\bar{\theta}_{j}\partial_{\nu_{i}}\bar{\theta}_{i}\right)\geq 2k\pi,

Proof.

By Proposition

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma_{n}}\frac{1}{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\sin\bar{\theta_{i}}}\left(H_{\partial M}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{j\leq i}\sin\bar{\theta}_{j}\partial_{\nu_{i}}\bar{\theta}_{i}\right)dl_{g}\geq\int_{\partial\Sigma_{n}}\frac{\textup{II}(\tau_{1},\bar{\tau}_{1})}{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}}dl_{g}

Since
\bar{M}
is a convex domain in

(29)

\displaystyle\Psi(\vec{u})=\Psi(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n+1}):=(x(\vec{u}),y(\vec{u}),u_{2},u_{3},\cdots,u_{n+1}),

Lemma 3.1
.

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{n}=\mathcal{X}_{n-1}+c_{n+2}\hat{i}_{n+2}.

Proof.

For any
\hat{i}_{m}
component of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
when

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{matrix}M_{n-1,m}&\vec{0}\\

Our next step is to compute

Lemma 3.2
.

(31)

\displaystyle c_{n+2}=x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{n+1}}-x_{u_{n+1}}y_{u_{1}}.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle c_{n+2}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-1}\left((-1)^{n-2}x_{u_{n+1}}\det\left(\begin{matrix}y_{u_{1}}&0&0&\cdots&0\\

y_{u_{n}}&0&0&\cdots&1\end{matrix}\right)+(-1)^{n-1}y_{u_{n+1}}\det\left(\begin{matrix}x_{u_{1}}&0&0&\cdots&0\\

x_{u_{n}}&0&0&\cdots&1\end{matrix}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{n+1}}-x_{u_{n+1}}y_{u_{1}}.

∎

We then have that

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{n}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{1}+\sum_{m=3}^{n+2}\left(x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{m-1}}-x_{u_{m-1}}y_{u_{1}}\right)\hat{i}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{u_{1}}\hat{i}_{1}-x_{u_{1}}\hat{i}_{2}+\sum_{m=3}^{n+2}\left(x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{m-1}}-x_{u_{m-1}}y_{u_{1}}\right)\hat{i}_{m}.

Since

\displaystyle g_{E}(\nabla_{\gamma^{\prime}(t)}\bar{X},\bar{\tau}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}g_{E}(\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{X}_{n},\bar{\tau}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{1}u_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime}-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}y_{u_{1}}x_{u_{1}u_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime}}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{1}u_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime}-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}y_{u_{1}}x_{u_{1}u_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{y_{u_{1}}^{2}+x_{u_{1}}^{2}+\sum_{m=3}^{n+2}(x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{m-1}}-x_{u_{m-1}}y_{u_{1}})^{2}}\sqrt{x^{2}_{u_{1}}+y^{2}_{u_{1}}}}.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
m\in\{1,2\cdots,n\}
, we have that

(33)

\displaystyle\Pi_{i=1}^{m}\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}=\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n-m}|}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}.

Proof.

We prove by induction. For
m=1
, we have that

\displaystyle\cos\bar{\theta}_{1}=

\displaystyle g_{E}(\bar{X},e_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}g_{E}(\mathcal{X}_{n},e_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{n}}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}.

Now we have that

\displaystyle\sin\bar{\theta}_{1}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\bar{\theta}_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|^{2}-|\mathcal{X}_{n-1}|^{2}}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n-1}|}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|},

where we used (
30
).

We verified (
33
) for
m=1
. Assume that
(
33
)
is true for

, we consider the case when
i=m+1
.

Since for any
m\in\{1,2,\cdots,n-1\}
, we have that

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}_{m}=\frac{1}{\sin\bar{\theta}_{m}}\left(\bar{\eta}_{m-1}-\cos\bar{\theta}_{m}e_{n+2-m}\right).

By definition

\displaystyle\cos\bar{\theta}_{m+1}=

\displaystyle g_{E}(\bar{\eta}_{m},e_{n+1-m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sin\bar{\theta}_{m}}g_{E}(\bar{\eta}_{m-1},e_{n+1-m})

Observe that

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}_{m-1}=\frac{1}{\sin\bar{\theta}_{m-1}}\left(\bar{\eta}_{m-2}-\cos\bar{\theta}_{m-1}e_{n+3-m}\right),

where the
e_{n+2-m}
component is perpendicular to
e_{n+1-m}
, and thus we have that

\displaystyle\cos\bar{\theta}_{m+1}=\frac{1}{\sin\bar{\theta}_{m}\sin\bar{\theta}_{m-1}}g_{E}(\bar{\eta}_{m-2},e_{n+1-m}).

The above procedure proceeds, and we finally have that

\displaystyle\cos\bar{\theta}_{m+1}=

\displaystyle\frac{g_{E}(\bar{X},e_{n+1-m})}{\Pi_{i=1}^{m}\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{n+2-m}}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\Pi_{i=1}^{m}\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{n+2-m}}{|\mathcal{X}_{n-m}|},

where we used the induction hypothesis in the last step.
We then have that

\displaystyle\sin\bar{\theta}_{m+1}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{c_{n+2-m}^{2}}{|\mathcal{X}_{n-m}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n-m-1}|}{|\mathcal{X}_{n-m}|},

where we used (
30
).
Now we have

\Pi_{i=1}^{m+1}\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}=\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n-m-1}|}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|},

the induction completes.
∎

Now, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma_{n}}\frac{\textup{II}(\tau_{1},\bar{\tau}_{1})}{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma_{n}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{1}u_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime}(t)-y_{u_{1}}x_{u_{1}u_{i}}u_{i}^{\prime}(t)\right)}{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\sqrt{x_{u_{1}}^{2}+y_{u_{1}}^{2}}}\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\sin\bar{\theta}_{1}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma_{n}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(x_{u_{1}}y_{u_{1}u_{i}}-y_{u_{1}}x_{u_{1}u_{i}}\right)u_{i}^{\prime}(t)}{x_{u_{1}}^{2}+y_{u_{1}}^{2}}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma_{n}}\frac{d}{dt}\arctan(\frac{y_{u_{1}}}{x_{u_{1}}})dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2k\pi,

for some
k\geq 1
.
∎

4.
Non-trivial minimizer

In this section, we prove that there exists a non-trivial minimizer for
\{\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{2}
in

. To prove the existence of a non-trivial minimizer, we construct a foliation near the point

for

then prove that the foliations we construct act as good barriers.

4.1.
First Slicing

Let
\{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{3}
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{p_{1}}\partial M
with respect to the Euclidean metric.

By the implicit function theorem, we can write
\partial M
in a neighborhood of

as

(34)

\displaystyle\partial M\cap N_{p_{1}}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},-c_{ij}(x)x_{i}x_{j}+O(|x|^{3}))\},

where

is a small neighborhood of
p_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, and
C(x)=(c_{ij}(x))
can be written as

c_{ij}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\textup{II}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}})+O(|x|^{4}),

where
c_{ij}
is a positive definite matrix whose entries are all non-zero when
|x|\neq 0
.

Remark 4.1
.

The reason to define our matrix
C
depending on the point
x
is to guarantee that all the entries of
C
are non-zero and we can apply the following linear algebra techniques.

Note that
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
is also a local coordinate of
M^{4}
in a small neighborhood of
p_{1}
, by rotating the coordinate accordingly, we can write the metric
g
at the point
p_{1}
as
g(p_{1})=(a_{ij})_{4\times 4}
and the
3\times 3
principal matrix of
g(p)
to be
g(p_{1})|_{\partial M}
and a diagonal matrix i.e.
a_{ij}=0
for
i\neq j
and
1\leq i,j\leq 3
.

We can also assume that
g=g_{0}+th+O(t^{2})
where
h
is a bounded symmetric
2
-tensor. We construct the following graphical foliation near
p_{1}
.

(35)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{s,t}:=\{(tx_{1},tx_{2},tx_{3},t^{2}\{c_{ij}\left(b_{ij}(1+s)-1\right)x_{i}x_{j}+O(t^{4})\}):\vec{x}\in E_{s}\},

where

E_{s}:=\{\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:c_{ij}b_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}<(1+s)^{-1}\},

and
B=(b_{ij})
is a symmetric
3\times 3
matrix defined as follows. We abbreviate the notation when
s=0
:
\Sigma_{t}:=\Sigma_{s,t}
.

Let
D=\frac{1}{a^{44}}g(p_{1})|_{\partial M}^{-1}
and this follows
D_{ii}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_{ii}a^{44}}}
where
D
is a diagonal matrix. Since
C
is a positive definite matrix, we define

S=\left(D^{\frac{1}{2}}C^{T}CD^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and

V:=D^{-1/2}SD^{-1/2}.

It is straightforward to notice that both
S
and
V
are symmetric.

Finally, we define
B=(b_{ij})
as

b_{ij}=\frac{V_{ij}}{c_{ij}}.

By definition,
B
is a symmetric
3\times 3
matrix.

Remark 4.2
.

In fact, all the matrices defined above depend on the coordinate to a higher order. Since all our estimates allow higher order errors, we do not emphasize the dependence.

Proposition 4.1

(Contact Angle Comparison)
.

Suppose
\theta^{g}_{s,t}(x)
is the contact angle of
\Sigma_{s,t}
and
\partial M
at
x\in\partial\Sigma_{s,t}
with respect to the metric
g
; and
\bar{\theta}
is a prescribed angle function with respect to the Euclidean metric.
We have

(36)

\displaystyle\cos\theta^{g}_{s,t}(x)=\cos\bar{\theta}(x)-4st^{2}\sum_{i}\left(\frac{(\sum_{j}c_{ij}b_{ij}x_{j})^{2}}{a_{ii}a^{44}}\right)+O(s^{2})+O(t^{3}).

Proof.

By straightforward computations as in dimension
3

[
28
]
, we have

\displaystyle\cos\theta^{g}_{s,t}=1-2t^{2}\sum_{i}\frac{(c_{ij}b_{ij}(1+s)x_{j})^{2}}{a_{ii}a^{44}}+O(t^{3}),

and

\displaystyle\cos\bar{\theta}(x)=1-2t^{2}\sum_{i}(c_{ij}x_{j})^{2}+O(t^{3}).

By the definition of
B
, we have the following identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{c_{ik}c_{il}b_{ik}b_{il}}{a_{ii}a^{44}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{V_{ik}V_{il}}{a_{ii}a^{44}}

\displaystyle=(VDV)_{kl}

\displaystyle=(D^{-1/2}S^{2}D^{-1/2})_{kl}

\displaystyle=(C^{T}C)_{kl}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}c_{ik}c_{il},

for any
k,l\in\{1,2,3\}
.
The desired estimates then follow.

We compute the mean curvature formula of parametrized hypersurfaces.

Proof.

The formula follows directly from the proof of Lemma 5.4 in
[
28
]
.
∎

By applying Lemma
4.1
, we obtain the following mean curvature difference directly. We denote the graph representation of

(tx_{1},tx_{2},tx_{3},G(tx_{i}))

Proposition 4.2
.

On an intersection point

5.1.
Curvature estimate of area-minimizing capillary minimal hypersurfaces

We prove curvature estimates of area-minimizing capillary minimal hypersurfaces when the dimension of an ambient manifold is bounded by
4
. The argument is based on the standard rescaling argument. Recall that the rescaling limit of the area-minimizer of capillary minimal hypersurface is capillary minimal hypersurface with a constant contact angle in a half-space. We first prove the following Bernstein-type lemma when the capillary angle is constant based on the classification of minimizing cone of a capillary area functional by Chodosh-Edelen-Li (Theorem 1.2 of
[
11
]
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
n+1\leq 4
and
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.
\Sigma^{n}
is a properly embedded capillary minimal hypersurface with a contact angle
\theta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
which minimizes
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
-functional. Then
\Sigma^{n}
is a flat half-hyperplane.

Proof.

Suppose
\Sigma^{n}
is not a half-hyperplane and we prove by contradiction. Take

and consider a blow-down limit
\Sigma_{x,\infty}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{r,x}(\Sigma)
, where
\mu_{r,x}(y)=(y-x)/r
is a scaling map by scale
1/r
for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. Then by the classification of area-minimizing cone of capillary area functional by Chodosh-Edelen-Li (Theorem 1.2 of
[
11
]
), we have
\Sigma_{x,\infty}
is a half-hyperplane. Now let us consider a density functional

\Theta_{\theta}(x,r)(\Sigma)=\frac{|\Sigma\cap B_{r}(x)|-\cos\theta\,|\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}\cap\partial\Omega_{x,\infty}\cap B_{r}(x)|}{\omega_{n-1}r^{n}/n},

where
\Omega_{x,\infty}
is a fixed domain separated by
\Sigma_{x,\infty}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. Then by the monotonicity formula of the capillary functional by Kagaya-Tonegawa
[
27
]
,
\Theta_{\theta}(x,r)
is a monotonically increasing functional by
r
for a fixed
x
. Since
\Theta_{\theta}(x,r)(\Sigma)=\Theta_{\theta}(x,1)(\mu_{r,x}(\Sigma))
, the density function
d_{x}(r):=\Theta_{\theta}(x,1)(\mu_{r,x}(\Sigma))
is a monotonically increasing function by
r
. Also, since the area-minimizing capillary minimal hypersurfaces have a Euclidean volume growth, there exists a density at infinity
d_{x,\infty}:=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}d_{x}(r)<\infty
. Since
\Sigma_{x,\infty}
is a half-hyperplane,
d_{x,\infty}=(1-\cos\theta)/2
. Also, since
d_{x,0}:=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}d_{x}(r)=(1-\cos\theta)/2
, we have
d_{x}(r)\equiv(1-\cos\theta)/2
and this gives that
\Sigma
is a flat half-hyperplane and this contradicts our assumption.
∎

We now prove the curvature estimate based on Lemma

Suppose

and
\Sigma

Proof.

Let us prove by contradiction and assume that there is a sequence of

such that

goes to infinity. Then there exists a sequence of points

such that

. Consider the scaled sequence of capillary minimal hypersurfaces

R_{\Sigma}=0

R_{\Sigma}=R_{M}-2\text{Ric}_{M}(N_{1},N_{1})+\|A_{\Sigma}\|^{2}-\|H_{\Sigma}\|^{2}

and we obtain
\text{Ric}_{M}(N_{1},N_{1})=0
from the minimality of
\Sigma
in
M
. Since we have an isometry between
\Sigma
and a Euclidean model,
H_{\partial\Sigma}=\frac{H_{\partial M}}{\bar{H}_{\partial M}}H_{\partial\bar{\Sigma}}
on
\partial\Sigma
follows.
∎

5.3.
Global rigidity

We now prove the main rigidity theorem. The idea is to construct an infinitesimally rigid capillary hypersurface in a conformally deformed metric of
g
near the infinitesimal rigid hypersurface
\Sigma
. This gives a dense set of Euclidean capillary slices on both sides of
\Sigma
. We adapt the technique of
[
6
]
and
[
35
]
for this construction up to dimension
4
, where they developed the technique in dimension
3
which can be extended directly (See
[
33
]
for high dimensional free boundary setting). We take the construction of conformally deformed metric from Appendix
A
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We prove the statement by induction, namely we prove the rigidity of
\Gamma
in
\Sigma
and those of
\Sigma
in
M
with the same idea, while the latter statement will be proven under the hypothesis of the former one. Since
\Gamma
is a capillary minimal surface with a contact angle
\bar{\theta}_{2}
in
\Sigma
with the metric
g^{\prime}=f_{1}g
, we prove at once by unifying the notation by
\Sigma^{n-1}\subseteq M^{n}
, the contact angle by
\bar{\theta}
, and the extreme point by
p_{+}
without loss of generality, where
n=3,4
. Denote the region of
M
separated by
\Sigma
which contains
p_{+}
by
M_{+}
. Denote the unit normal vector field on
\Sigma
by
N
.

Now let us take
p\in M_{+}
and take the metric
g(t)
by applying Proposition
A.1
. Then
(M_{+},g(t))
is a mean convex solid whose wedge part is on
\partial\Sigma
. Let us take
\mathcal{E}
as a class of open sets with finite perimeter containing
p_{+}
. We consider a minimization problem on
(M_{+},g(t))
as follows:

(63)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{t}:=\inf\{\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\theta}}(U):U\in\mathcal{E}\}.

We denote a minimizer of (
63
) by
U_{t}
. Then
\Sigma_{t}:=\partial_{rel}U_{t}
is achieved with a capillary minimal hypersurface which is disjoint with
\Sigma
by maximum principle and the convexity condition (5) of Proposition
A.1
. Also by the construction,
\Sigma_{t}
must intersect
B_{3r_{0}}(p)
by the metric comparison, otherwise it contradicts to the fact that
\Sigma
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\theta}}
in
(M,g)
. If
\Sigma_{t}
does not intersect
B_{3r_{0}}(p)
, then we have

\mathcal{A}^{g}_{\bar{\theta}}(\Sigma)\leq\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\theta}}^{g}(\Sigma_{t})=\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\theta}}^{g(t)}(\Sigma_{t})<\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\theta}}^{g(t)}(\Sigma)\leq\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\theta}}^{g}(\Sigma),

where we used the fact that
\Sigma
is strictly mean convex at one interior point with respect to the metric
g(t)
. Suppose
\Sigma_{t}
does not intersect
B_{r_{0}}(p)
. Then
R_{g(t)}\geq 0
along
\Sigma_{t}
and there exists a point
q\in\Sigma_{t}\cap(B_{3r_{0}}(p)\setminus B_{r_{0}}(p))
with
R_{g(t)}(q)>0
by the condition (4) in Proposition
A.1
. This contradicts the rigidity in Theorem
5.2
and Proposition
5.1
, which gives
R_{g(t)}\equiv 0
on
\Sigma_{t}
. Hence,
\Sigma_{t}
intersects
B_{r_{0}}(p)
.

For small
\epsilon>0
, there exists a family of capillary hypersurfaces
\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\in(0,\epsilon)}
. By Theorem
5.1
,
\Sigma_{t}
smoothly converges to area-minimizing capillary minimal hypersurface
\Sigma^{\prime}
in
(M_{+},g)
which is disjoint to
\Sigma
since
\Sigma^{\prime}
intersects
B_{r_{0}}(p)
. Note that
\Sigma^{\prime}
is an infinitesimally rigid hypersurfaces in
(M,g)
by the rigidity theorems.

With different choices of small
r_{0}
and
p
, we can repeat the previous procedure to obtain
\Sigma^{\prime}
and let
\Sigma^{\rho}
be a corresponding infinitesimally rigid hypersurface with
\rho=r_{0}
in
(M,g)
. Let us take
\mathcal{C}
by a dense subset of
(0,\epsilon)
and consider
\{\Sigma^{\rho}\}_{\rho\in\mathcal{C}}
. Then
\cup_{\rho\in\mathcal{C}}\Sigma^{\rho}
is dense near
\Sigma
and each surface
\Sigma^{\rho}
is an infinitesimally rigid area minimizing capillary hypersurface. Hence, we can assume that there exists
\Sigma^{\rho}
such that
\Sigma^{\rho}
intersects
B_{\epsilon}(q)
for any
q\in\partial\Sigma
and
\epsilon>0
. By the curvature estimate (Theorem
5.1
), there exists uniform constant
C
such that
\partial\Sigma^{\rho}
intersects
B_{C\epsilon}(q)
which only depends on
M
and
g
. This gives a boundary denseness of
\{\Sigma^{\rho}\}_{\rho\in\mathcal{C}}
on a small neighborhood of
\Sigma
on
M_{+}
.

Let us take a vector field
X
on
\Sigma
such that
\langle X,N\rangle=1
on
\Sigma
and tangential to
\partial M
on
\partial\Sigma
and extend to
M
and denote this vector field by
Y
. We take a one-parameter family of diffeomorphism
\phi_{t}
on
M
which is generated by
Y
. For
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
, each
\Sigma^{\rho}
can be represented as a graph by

\{\phi_{u^{\rho}(x)}(x)\,:\,x\in\Sigma\}.

We fix a point
x_{0}\in\Sigma
and define
d_{\rho}=u^{\rho}(x)/u^{\rho}(x_{0})
. By arguing as in
[
35
]
, which computes the first variation of the second fundamental form of
\Sigma_{\rho}
, and by taking the limit
f
of
d_{\rho}
as
\rho\rightarrow 0
, we obtain

(64)

\nabla_{\Sigma}^{2}f(Z,W)+\text{Rm}_{M}(N,Z,W,N)=0

for all tangential vectors
Z,W
on
\Sigma
. By tracing (
64
), we have
\Delta_{\Sigma}f=0
since
\text{Ric}_{M}(N,N)=0
by infinitesimal rigidity. Moreover, since
f
is induced by Jacobi field on
\Sigma
, we have a boundary condition as follows by applying
|A_{\Sigma}|\equiv 0
from infinitesimal rigidity:

(65)

\partial_{\eta}f=\left(\frac{1}{\sin\overline{\theta}_{1}}A_{\partial M}(\nu,\nu)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\overline{\theta}_{1}}\partial_{\nu}\cos\overline{\theta}_{1}\right)f.

Moreover,
\partial_{\eta}f=0
follows from the rigidity conditions of the stability inequalities in Proposition
2.1
,
2.2
, and Lemma
2.4
. By Green’s formula, we obtain that
u
is a constant and
\text{Rm}_{M}(N,Z,W,N)=0
. By Codazzi equation, we obtain
\text{Rm}_{M}\equiv 0
on
\Sigma
. Since
\{\Sigma^{\rho}\}_{\rho\in\mathcal{C}}
is a dense subset in a small neighborhood of
\Sigma
together with a boundary curvature condition of Theorem
5.2
and Proposition
5.1
, there exists a small neighborhood of
\Sigma
that is isometric to Euclidean domain (up to rescaling). We can iterate this procedure along all
M_{+}
and obtain global isometry by applying these arguments to
M\setminus M_{+}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By assuming the regularity of capillary area functional in higher dimension, the curvature estimate in Section 5.1 follows. The infinitesimal rigidity follows from our estimates in Section 2 and 3. The global rigidity with the construction of a dense set of Euclidean capillary slices do not depend on the dimension as far as the curvature estimate holds (See
[
33
]
also), Theorem
1.3
holds with the assumptions in the statement.
∎

Appendix A
Conformally deformed metric near infinitesimally rigid hypersurfaces

The following proposition follows from an extension of Carlotto-Chodosh-Eichmair
[
6
]
, see also Li
[
33
]
for adaption in the high dimensional free boundary setting. We include the construction for the sake of completeness here.

Proposition A.1

(Liu
[
35
]
, Carlotto-Chodosh-Eichmair
[
6
]
, Li
[
33
]
)
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{n-1}
is an infinitesimally rigid hypersurfaces in
M^{n}
. Suppose
r_{0}>0
is a fixed small enough positive parameter and
p\in\Omega
is a fixed interior point such that
\text{dist}_{g}(p,\Sigma)\in(1.5r_{0},2.5r_{0})
,
\text{dist}_{g}(p,\partial M)>4r_{0}
and
\frac{\partial r}{\partial N_{1}}>0
in
\Sigma\cap B_{3r_{0}}(p)
. Then there exists an
\epsilon>0
and a family of Riemannian metrics
\{g(t)\}_{t\in(0,\epsilon)}
in the conformal class of
g
with the following properties:

(1)

g(t)\to g
smoothly as
t
goes to
0
;

(2)

g(t)=g
on
M\setminus B_{3r_{0}}(p)
;

(3)

g(t)\leq g
as metrics on
M
, and the inequality is strict on the annuli
B_{3r_{0}}(p)\setminus B_{r_{0}}(p)
;

(4)

R_{g(t)}>0
on
B_{3r_{0}}(p)\setminus B_{r_{0}}(p)
;

(5)

\Sigma
is weakly mean convex and strictly mean convex at one interior point with respects to the metric
g(t)
and the unit normal vector field pointing outward from
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
r_{0}
be small enough such that on any geodesic ball
B_{3r_{0}}(q)
, the inequality
\Delta_{g}(\text{dist}_{g}^{2}(\cdot,q))<4n
holds. The existence of
r_{0}
follows from the compactness of
M
. Let us take a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose support is in
[0,3]
and satisfies

f(s)=-\text{exp}\left(\frac{16n+8}{s}\right)

for
s\in(1,3)
. Note that
f
satisfies
f^{\prime}(s)>0
and
(4n-1)f^{\prime}(s)+sf^{\prime\prime}(s)<0
in
(1,3)
. We consider a function
v:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

v(x)=r_{0}^{2}f\left(\frac{\text{dist}_{g}(x,p)}{r_{0}}\right).

In
B_{3r_{0}}(p)\setminus B_{r_{0}}(p)
, we have

\Delta_{g}v=r_{0}f^{\prime}\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)\Delta_{g}r+f^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)<(4n-1)\frac{r_{0}}{r}f^{\prime}\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)+f^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)<0.

Now we take
g(t)=(1+tv)^{\frac{4}{n-2}}g
. The conditions (1)-(3) are satisfied directly and we now check the conditions non-negativeness of scalar curvature (4) and the preservation of weakly convex boundary (5). We have

R(g(t))=(1+tv)^{\frac{n+2}{n-2}}\left(\frac{-4(n-1)t}{n-2}\Delta_{g}v+R(g)(1+tv)\right)>0

for positive
t
and we verify (4) in
B_{3r_{0}}(p)\setminus B_{r_{0}}(p)
.
Similarly, by the mean curvature formula by conformal change, along
\Sigma
we have

\displaystyle H_{\Sigma,g(t)}=(1+tv)^{-\frac{n}{n-2}}\left(H_{\Sigma,g}+\frac{2(n-1)t}{n-2}\frac{\partial v}{\partial N_{1}}\right)>0,

the condition (5) is verified.
∎
