Eigenvalue distribution of some random matrices
Mohamed Jebalia, Ahmed Souabni

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the eigenvalue distribution of a class of kernel random matrices whose ( i , j ) (i,j) -th entry is f ⁡ ( X i , X j ) f(X_{i},X_{j}) where f f is a symmetric function belonging to the Paley-Wiener space ℬ c \mathcal{B}_{c} and ( X i ) 1 ≤ i ≤ N (X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N} are i.i.d. random variables. We rigorously prove that, with high probability, the eigenvalues of these random matrices are well approximated by those of an underlying estimator. A particularly notable case is when f = s ​ i ​ n ​ c f=sinc , which has been widely studied due to its relevance in various scientific fields, including machine learning and telecommunications. In this case, we push forward the general approach by computing the eigenvalues of the estimator. More precisely, we have proved that the eigenvalues are concentrated around zero and one. In particular, we address the case of large values of c c with respect to the matrix size N N , which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been studied in the literature. Furthermore, we establish that the frequency of eigenvalues close to one is proportional to c c . Numerical results are provided in order to illustrate the theoretical findings.

Introduction

In this work, we are interested in the study of the distribution as well as the computation of the eigenvalues of random matrices
A_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
that is written as

A_{N}(i,j)=f(X_{i},X_{j})

where
f
is a real valued function and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are i.i.d random variables. The function
f
is assumed to be symmetric implying that
A_{N}

is also symmetric. This model covers various types of random matrices, for example Euclidean matrices

A_{N}(i,j)=g(\left\|X_{i}-X_{j}\right\|)
and
A_{N}(i,j)=g(<X_{i},X_{j}>)
for a given function
g
. The study of these matrices is motivated by the fact that they appear in many scientific areas such as big data, statistics, machine learning, geophysics, telecommunication, optics, see for example
[
4
,
8
,
10
,
15
,
11
,
12
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

In statistics for example, a common problem is the non-parametric regression problem having the form

Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,N

where the
X_{i}
are i.i.d random entries and
(Y_{i})
are the response variables.
Among resolution methods, Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) computes the estimator of the regression function
f
as a linear combination using a positive definite kernel function
{\mathbb{K}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, that is

\widehat{f}_{N}(.)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{K}}(X_{i},.)\widehat{c}_{i}

where the expansion coefficients vector
\widehat{c}
is the solution of the equation

G\widehat{c}=Y,\quad\quad Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})^{T}.

(1.1)

Here, the kernel (or Gram) matrix
G
is a random matrix positive-definite, given by

G(i,j)=({\mathbb{K}}(X_{i},X_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
. It has been shown that the performance of KRR methods is closely related to the behaviour of the eigenvalues of the matrix
G
, see for example
[
2
,
3
,
16
]
.

Random matrices appear also in Physics. For example, wave propagation in random media in a three dimensional space is modeled by the Helmholtz equation

(\nabla^{2}+c^{2}+i\eta)A_{N}(X_{i},X_{j})=-\frac{4\pi}{c}\delta(X_{i}-X_{j}).

where the solution is the Green function given by the random matrix

A_{N}(i,j)=\frac{\exp(ic||X_{i}-X_{j}||)}{c||X_{i}-X_{j}||},

where the
X_{i}
are three dimensional random vectors and
||\cdot||
is the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. The study of this wave equation is achieved through the study of the eigenvalue distribution of the real and imaginary parts of
A_{N}
(see
[
11
]
).

Another application of random matrices in telecommunications is given in
[
8
]
, where the authors studied the distribution of the spectrum of a random matrix in order to estimate the capacity of a Multi Input Multi Output (MIMO) wireless communication model.

In many applications, the order
N
of the random matrix has to be large for improving the efficiency of the underlying methods. For example, regression problems need a large data size
N
.
For this reason, many studies have investigated the eigenvalues distribution of large random matrices. Mainly, three approaches have been developed to achieve this goal.

The first approach is to prove that the spectrum of the kernel random matrix
A_{N}
remains close to the one of the integral operator associated with the same kernel
[
1
,
8
,
18
,
22
]
. Therefore, we rely on the extensive literature on the eigenvalues of kernel integral operators. In
[
22
]
, the authors have estimated the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix of the kernel-PCA method through the eigenspectrum of the corresponding continuous operator.
The studies
[
1
,
8
]
have investigated kernel random matrices. They have shown that their eigenvalues are close to those of the associated integral operator, both in terms of quadratic mean and with high probability. Then, they computed the so-called degree of freedom of the associated integral operator. At a fixed level
\epsilon>0
, the degree of freedom of a semi-definite positive and self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt operator
T
is the number of eigenvalues of
T
that exceed
\epsilon
. In particular,
[
8
]
focused on the sinc kernel

A_{N}(i,j)=\frac{\sin(c(X_{i}-X_{j}))}{\pi(X_{i}-X_{j})}

where the
X_{i}
are
N
i.i.d. random variables. In the specific case where
c\rightarrow+\infty
and
\frac{N}{c}\rightarrow+\infty
(implying that
c<<N
), it has been shown that the sinc kernel matrix has approximately
c
significant eigenvalues.

The second approach to investigate the eigenvalues distribution of the random matrix
A_{N}
is to study its spectral distribution for large values of
N
. The spectral measure associated with a random Hermitian matrix
A_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
is given by

\mu_{A_{N}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(A_{N})}

where
\lambda_{i}(A_{N}),i=1,\ldots,N
are the eigenvalues of
A_{N}
.
The idea is to prove that the distribution of
\mu_{A_{N}}
converges to a deterministic one denoted by
\mu
(see for example
[
17
]
). This can be done by computing the limit of the Stieljies (or Cauchy) transform of the measure
\mu_{A_{N}}
. The Stieljes transform of a measure
\lambda
is given by

z\mapsto G_{\lambda}(z)=\int\frac{d\lambda(t)}{t-z}.

It is well known that

G_{\mu_{A_{N}}}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\mbox{Trace}\left((A_{N}-zI_{N})^{-1}\right).

If the sequence of functions
(G_{\mu_{A_{N}}})_{N\geq 1}
converges pointwise then
\mu_{A_{N}}
converges weakly to some measure
\mu
. In the framework of kernel random matrices, N. El Karoui
[
17
]
has investigated this problem where the
(i,j)^{th}
entry has the form
f\left(X_{i}^{T}X_{j}/p\right)
or
f\left(\frac{||X_{i}-X_{j}||_{2}^{2}}{p}\right)
where
X_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
are independent data vectors. The function
f
has to be locally smooth.

A third way to study the eigenvalue distribution of a random matrix has been proposed in
[
11
]
. It has been applied in the specific case of the three dimensional sinc-kernel matrix. This method consists on the approximation of
A_{N}
by the product
HTH^{T}
where
H
is a random matrix and
T
is a deterministic one. Then we conclude by Free probability arguments.

In this work, we investigate the eigenvalue distribution of the random matrix
A_{N}
using the approach introduced in
[
11
]
. A key advantage of this method lies in its generality, as it does not rely on the theory of integral operators and can be applied to any kernel
K
within the Paley–Wiener space. In the case of the sinc kernel, we show that our findings are consistent with those previously reported in
[
1
,
8
]
. It is important to note that the method given in
[
1
,
8
]
is restricted for small values of
c
with respect to the matrix size
N
, whereas our approach remains valid for arbitrary values of
c
.

More precisely, we rigorously derive and generalize the heuristic result proposed in
[
11
]
for a one-dimensional search space. Our analysis is based on approximating the matrix
A_{N}
by the product
\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}=HT_{M}H^{T}
where
H\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times M}
is a random matrix and
T_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}
is a deterministic one. We rigorously prove the result of
[
11
]
, that is
\displaystyle A_{N}=\lim_{M\rightarrow+\infty}\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}
for a broad class of random matrices (see Proposition
2.2
). Furthermore, using McDiarmid’s inequality and Weyl’s perturbation theorem, we establish that the eigenvalues of
A_{N}

are closely approximated by those of
\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}
with high probability (see Theorem
2.7
).
In the particular case of the sinc kernel, we also present several structural and approximation results concerning the matrix
T_{M}

(Section
3
). Moreover, we prove that the eigenvalues are concentrated around zero and one depending on the ratio
c/N
(Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.6
). The frequency of eigenvalues close to one is proportional to
c
. Numerical experiments are provided following each key theoretical result in order to illustrate and validate the analysis, particularly in the sinc kernel setting.

General Approach

A useful approach to deal with the statistical properties of
A_{N}
was developed in
[
11
]
for some kernel random matrices. It consists on writing the matrix
A_{N}
as the limit of an estimator. We generalize the result in
[
11
]
for any random matrix
A_{N}
with entry elements
A_{N}(i,j)=f(X_{i},X_{j})
where
f
is a symmetric (i.e.,
f(x,y)=f(y,x)
) real valued function of
L^{2}([-1,1]\times[-1,1])
.

2.1
Approximation model of random matrices and convergence in average

To determine the eigenvalues distribution of the matrix
A_{N}
, we will prove in the following paragraph that it can be approximated by a simpler one. Firstly, we will define matrices that will be used throughout this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\phi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([-1,1])
and let

f\in L^{2}([-1,1]\times[-1,1])
be a symmetric real valued function and let
(X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
be
N
i.i.d. random variables with values on
[-1,1]
. We define the following matrices.

•

H
is an
N\times M
random matrix with elements

H(i,m)=\phi_{m-1}(X_{i}),\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\},\forall m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}.

•

T_{M}
is a symmetric deterministic
M\times M
matrix with elements

\displaystyle T_{M}(m,n)=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}f(x,y)\phi_{m-1}(x)\phi_{n-1}(y)dxdy.

•

\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}=H.T_{M}.H^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
is a random matrix.

The approximation, in an
L^{2}
sense, is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Under the notations of Definition
2.1
, we have

A_{N}=\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}+R_{N}^{(M)},

(2.1)

where

R_{N}^{(M)}
is a random matrix defined as

R_{N}^{(M)}(i,j)=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}<r_{M}(.,X_{j});\phi_{k}(.)>_{L^{2}([-1,1])}\phi_{k}(X_{i})+r_{M}(X_{i},X_{j})

with

r_{M}(x,y)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=M}^{+\infty}<f(.,y),\phi_{k}(.)>_{L^{2}([-1,1])}\phi_{k}(x).

Therefore

A=\lim_{M\to\infty}\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}.

(2.2)

Remark 2.3
.

The previous result can be easily generalized to any range
[a,b]
instead of
[-1,1]
. Moreover,
it can be generalized to the case where the
X_{i}
are random vectors. This recovers Euclidean random matrices which can be written as

A_{N}(i,j)=h(||X_{i}-X_{j}||)\quad\mbox{or}\quad A_{N}(i,j)=h(<X_{i},X_{j}>)

for any real valued function
h
.

Proof.

By applying two successive decomposition of the function
(x,y)\mapsto f(x,y)
on the basis
(\phi_{n})
first according to the variable
x
then according to the variable
y
, we get:

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{l=1}^{M}\left[\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t,u)\phi_{l-1}(u)\phi_{k-1}(t)du\ dt\right]\phi_{k-1}(x)\phi_{l-1}(y)

(2.3)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\phi_{k-1}(x)\phi_{k-1}(t)r_{M}(t,y)dt+r_{M}(x,y)

with
\displaystyle r_{M}(x,y)=\sum_{k=M}^{+\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t,y)\phi_{k}(t)dt\phi_{k}(x)
.
The quantity
R_{N}^{(M)}
converges to 0 since
(\phi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a basis.
∎

The expected error
{\mathbb{E}}[||R_{N}^{(M)}||]
resulting from the approximation of
A
by
\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}
is estimated in the following statement.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f\in L^{2}([-1,1]^{2})
and let
||.||_{HS}
be the Hilbert-Schmidt matrix norm.Then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[||R_{N}^{(M)}||_{HS}\right]\leq N(M+1)||r_{M}||_{L^{2}([-1,1]^{2})}.

Proof.

From the decomposition established in Proposition
2.2
, we have

||R_{N}^{(M)}||_{HS}=\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}(R_{N}^{(M)})^{2}(i,j)}=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}<r_{M}(\cdot,X_{j});\phi_{k}(\cdot)>\phi_{k}(X_{i})+r_{M}(X_{i},X_{j})\right]^{2}}.

Using Minkowski inequality, one gets

||R_{N}^{(M)}||_{HS}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}<r_{M}(\cdot,X_{j});\phi_{k}(\cdot)>^{2}\phi_{k}^{2}(X_{i})}+\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}r_{M}(X_{i},X_{j})^{2}}.

By taking the expectation of the previous inequality, one obtains

{\mathbb{E}}\left[||R_{N}^{(M)}||_{HS}\right]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}{\mathbb{E}}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}<r_{M}(\cdot,X_{j});\phi_{k}(\cdot)>^{2}\phi_{k}^{2}(X_{i})}+{\mathbb{E}}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}r_{M}(X_{i},X_{j})^{2}}.

The inequality
{\mathbb{E}}[\sqrt{t}]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[t]}
implies that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[||R_{N}^{(M)}||_{HS}\right]_{H-S}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[<r_{M}(\cdot,X_{j});\phi_{k}(\cdot)>^{2}\phi_{k}^{2}(X_{i})\right]}+\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[r_{M}(X_{i},X_{j})^{2}\right]}.

We notice now that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{i},X_{j}}\left[<r_{M}(\cdot,X_{j});\phi_{k}(\cdot)>^{2}\phi_{k}^{2}(X_{i})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{X_{j}}\left[<r_{M}(\cdot,X_{j});\phi_{k}(\cdot)>^{2}\right]\underbrace{{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{i}}\left[\phi_{k}^{2}(X_{i})\right]}_{=1}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{j}}\left[||r_{M}(\cdot,X_{j})||_{L^{2}([-1,1])}^{2}\underbrace{||\phi_{k}||_{L^{2}([-1,1])}^{2}}_{=1}\right]

\displaystyle=||r_{M}||_{L^{2}([-1,1]^{2})}^{2}.

Moreover
{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{i},X_{j}}\left[r_{M}^{2}(X_{i},X_{j})\right]=||r_{M}||_{L^{2}([-1,1]^{2})}^{2}.

Consequently
{\mathbb{E}}\left[||R_{N}^{(M)}||_{HS}\right]\leq(M+1)N||r_{M}||_{L^{2}([-1,1]^{2})}
.

∎

In the following section, we will show that the eigenvalues of
A_{N}

2.2
Case of random matrices related to band-limited kernels and convergence of eigenvalues in high probability

In the sequel, let
c
be a positive real number. Let
{\mathcal{F}}(f)

be the Fourier transform of
f
given by

{\mathcal{F}}(f)(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-itx}f(t)dt.

We define the Paley-Wiener space
{\mathcal{B}}_{c}=\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):{\mathcal{F}}f\in[-c,c]\}
and the space
{\mathcal{D}}
of
[-1,1]
-time limited functions. Let
B_{c}
and
D
be the projection from
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
onto
{\mathcal{B}}_{c}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
respectively :

B_{c}f:={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{\mathds{1}}_{[-c,c]}{\mathcal{F}}f\qquad Df=f(x){\mathds{1}}_{[-1,1]}(x).

Throughout this section, we assume that
f\in B_{c}
.

Next, we recall that Legendre polynomials can be defined, for example, through the Rodriguez formula,

P_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(1-x^{2})^{n},\ x\in I=[-1,1].

The normalized Legendre polynomials is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{n}(x)=\sqrt{n+\frac{1}{2}}P_{n}(x).

The family
\{\widetilde{P}_{n}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(I)
. In the sequel, we expand
f
in the basis of the normalized Legendre polynomials. We denote
\pi_{n}
its associated projection kernel given by

\pi_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(y).

We also denote the associated integral operator by
\Pi_{n}
and
r_{M}(f)=f-\Pi_{M}(f)
.

Lemma
2.4
induces that if
{\left\|{r_{M}(f)}\right\|}

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\left\|{R_{N}^{(M)}}\right\|}\right]

{\left\|{r_{M}(f)}\right\|}

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose that, for all

Under the condition

(2.5)

x

(2.7)

(2.8)

\int_{x\in[-1,1]}|(f-\Pi_{M}(f))(x,y)|^{2}dx=\sum_{k\geq M}<f(.,y),\widetilde{P}_{k}(.)>^{2}.

(2.9)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})-{\mathbb{E}}(f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))>\varepsilon|\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{c_{1}^{2}+\ldots+c_{n}^{2}}\right).

3.1
General properties of the matrix
T_{M}

In order to derive the general properties of the matrix
T_{M}
, we will first recall some facts about the time-frequency limiting operator.

Let
c>0
. Since,
\displaystyle K_{c}(x,y)=\frac{\sin(c(x-y))}{\pi(x-y)}
is a Mercer’s kernel, then it can be written as

K_{c}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}(c)\psi_{n,c}(x)\psi_{n,c}(y).

(3.1)

Here
(\lambda_{n}(c))_{n\geq 0}
are the eigenvalues of the Sinc integral operator related to the kernel
K_{c}
that we denote
{\mathcal{Q}}_{c}
; and
(\psi_{n,c})_{n\geq 0}
are the associated eigenfunctions known in the literature as Slepian’s functions or prolate spheroidal wave functions (PSWFs) For more details on these functions we refer reader to
[
23
,
25
]
.
Let us consider the Fredholm integral equation :

\mbox{Find $\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and $\psi\in L^{2}([-1,1])$ :}\ {\mathcal{Q}}_{c}.\psi(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\sin c(x-t)}{\pi(x-t)}\psi(t)dt=\lambda\psi(x),\quad x\in(-1,1).

The operator
{\mathcal{Q}}_{c}
is of type Hilbert-Schmidt. Then, it has a countable set of eigenvalues and corresponding eigenfunctions satisfying the following properties:

1.

The eigenvalues
\left(\lambda_{n}(c)\right)_{n\geq 0}
are simple, real and positive. They can be ordered as

1>\lambda_{0}(c)>\lambda_{1}(c)>\cdots>\lambda_{n}(c)>\cdots>0.

2.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}(c)=0.

3.

The corresponding eigenfunctions
\left(\psi_{n,c}\right)_{n\geq 0}
are orthogonal and form a complete system in
L^{2}(-1,1)
.

We also note that
{\mathcal{Q}}_{c}
writes as
\frac{2c}{\pi}\mathcal{F}^{*}_{c}\mathcal{F}_{c}

where

{\mathcal{F}}_{c}:L^{2}([-1,1])\to L^{2}([-1,1])
is given by

{\mathcal{F}}_{c}(f)(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)e^{-icxt}dt={\sqrt{2\pi}}{\mathcal{F}}({\mathds{1}}_{[-1,1]}f)(cx),\ f\in L^{2}([-1,1]).

We can then enounce the following properties on the coefficients of the infinite deterministic matrix
T_{M}
that hold regardless of the basis in which
T_{M}
is expressed.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any orthonormal basis
(\phi_{n})_{n\geq 0}

Conclusion

In this paper, we have investigated the distribution of the eigenvalues of the some class of random matrices
A_{N}
and especially the sinc kernel case. First, we have shown that the random matrix can be approximated by the product
\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}=HT_{M}H^{T}
. We have shown that the eigenvalues of
A_{N}
are well approximated by those of
\widehat{A}_{N}^{(M)}
.
In the case where

\delta_{0}

c
