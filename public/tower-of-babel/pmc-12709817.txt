Perinatal depression among women in pregnancy and postpartum periods in Gaborone, Botswana
Authors: Rebecca Ryan, Aamirah Mussa, Chibuzor M Babalola, Emily Hansman, Selebaleng Simon, Bame Bame, Neo Ndlovu, Lefhela Tamuthiba, Jeffrey D Klausner, Chelsea Morroni, Adriane Wynn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPerinatal depression is an important cause of maternal and infant morbidity. Rates of perinatal depression amongst pregnant and postpartum women in Botswana are not well-documented. We examined the prevalence of symptoms of depression and potential associated factors among pregnant and postpartum women participating in a sexually transmitted infection screening study.MethodsWe conducted a repeated time-point cross-sectional analysis of the Maduo study, a non-randomized clinic-controlled trial evaluating the benefit of antenatal Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae screening in Gaborone, Botswana. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) was used to screen for symptoms of depression at the first antenatal care and 6–8 week postnatal care visit. Prevalence of depression was assessed using EPDS cut-off scores of ≥11 and ≥13. Participants with an EPDS score of ≥13 were referred to local mental health services. We used multivariate logistic regression to identify potential associations with symptoms of perinatal depression (EPDS score ≥13) at first antenatal care visit, 6–8 week postnatal visit and overall, including socioeconomic, pregnancy-related and relationship factors. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis with EPDS score specified as a continuous variable.ResultsAll 500 enrolled women completed at least one EPDS questionnaire during the study: 495 (99%) at first antenatal care and 428 (86%) at the postnatal visit. Median EPDS score was 3 (IQR 1–6) at the first antenatal visit and 2 (IQR 0–5) at the postnatal visit. Similar proportions of women had EPDS scores suggestive of depression at first antenatal (EPDS ≥13, 5% (95% CI 3–7%); EPDS ≥11, 7% (95% CI 5–10%)) and postnatal visits (EPDS ≥13, 3% (95% CI 1–5%); EPDS ≥11, 7% (95% CI 5–10%)), however there was minimal overlap in women with antenatal and postnatal symptoms of depression. Symptoms of depression were strongly associated with experience of intimate partner violence both antenatally (aOR 5.41, 95% CI 1.94–15.10, p = 0.001) and postnatally (aOR 5.18, 95% CI 3.35–8.02, p = < 0.001); whilst symptoms of depression at the first antenatal care visit were also associated with unintended pregnancy (aOR 29.44, 95% CI 2.75–314.6, p = 0.005).ConclusionRates of perinatal depression in this study were lower than estimated rates in Southern Africa. Nonetheless, screening pregnant women for depression as part of routine antenatal care in Botswana provides an opportunity to identify those most in need of support and specialist services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08473-4.Keywords: Depression, Pregnancy, Perinatal, Antenatal, Postnatal, Botswana

Background

Perinatal depression, the onset of depressive symptoms [1] during pregnancy and up to one year postpartum [2], is among the most serious complications of pregnancy. Perinatal depression is associated with adverse outcomes for both the mother and her family. Women with perinatal depression may experience substantial morbidity, including impaired ability to care for themselves and their family, as well as an increased risk of self-harm and suicide. At the family level, perinatal depression can disrupt maternal-infant bonding, and negatively influence children’s emotional, cognitive and behavioural development [3–6]. The aetiology of perinatal depression is commonly multifactorial, and may include biological, social and cultural factors, including history of depression [7], lack of social support [8, 9], stressful life events [10], unintended pregnancy [11–13], relationship difficulties or experience of intimate partner violence [13–15], and low socio-economic status [7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately one in five women experience perinatal depression in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), compared to one in ten women in high-income countries [16]. In the African continent, a recent systematic review found a depression prevalence of 22.8% in pregnant and 21.2% in postpartum women, with a higher prevalence in Central and Southern Africa, and lower in Eastern Africa [17]. Few studies to date have investigated depression in pregnant and postpartum women in Botswana, although one recent nested observational study identified a lower prevalence of postpartum depression among women in Botswana compared to regional estimates for Southern Africa (6–8% versus 21%), with no significant association between HIV infection and screening positive for postpartum depression [18].

Screening and identification of women with symptoms of depression during pregnancy and the postnatal period is important to provide timely support and reduce adverse outcomes for the woman and family. In settings such as Botswana, where access to specialist mental health services and resources may be limited, identifying those most in need of support and referral is key.

We examined the prevalence and correlates of symptoms of perinatal depression at the first antenatal care visit and a postnatal visit among women enrolled in a sexually transmitted infection (STI) screening study in Botswana.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted a repeated time-point cross-sectional analysis of the Maduo study. The Maduo study was a non-randomised clinic-controlled trial conducted in Gaborone, Botswana, to provide preliminary data on the impact of antenatal Chlamydia (C.) trachomatis and Neisseria (N.) gonorrhoeae screening and treatment on post-delivery prevalence and vertical transmission to infants. Detailed study methods have been described elsewhere [19].

We recruited participants from midwife-led antenatal clinics in four government-run primary healthcare clinics with dispensing facilities in Gaborone. Clinics were selected in consultation with the District Health Management Team administrators and clinic staff, and all clinics had experience of involvement in research studies. Based on clinic registry data from the preceding 6 months, all study clinics had an average of 40–74 new antenatal bookings per month, and three of the four clinics had on-site delivery facilities. In Botswana, 95% of women are estimated to deliver in healthcare facilities [20]. National guidelines recommend that pregnant women attend a minimum of 6 antenatal appointments including a first-trimester booking visit.

Study population and procedures

Five hundred pregnant women attending their first antenatal appointment were recruited between February 2021 and April 2022 by consecutive sampling of eligible participants. Women were eligible to participate if they were aged ≥ 15 years, 27 weeks’ gestation or less, planned to reside in Gaborone up to time of delivery and willing to be tested for C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae. Women were excluded from the study if they had been treated for an STI in the past 30 days.

Pregnant women who met eligibility criteria were provided with study information by a research assistant at recruiting clinics. After completion of the antenatal booking appointment with the clinic midwife, interested women provided informed consent to participate and completed enrolment procedures. Participants attended three study visits: the first antenatal care (booking) visit, third trimester (30–34 weeks’ gestation) visit and postnatal care (6–8 weeks post-delivery) visit. At each study visit, participants completed a questionnaire in English or Setswana, according to participant preference, on socioeconomic and demographic characteristics, obstetric and medical history. The questionnaire included questions on perinatal depression at the first antenatal and postnatal visits only. Participants who experienced a miscarriage prior to 20 weeks’ gestation were not expected to attend the postnatal study visit.

Inclusion in the intervention versus standard-of-care arm was determined by enrolment clinic. Women in the intervention arm provided a self-collected vaginal swab which was tested for C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae using Xpert® CT/NG assays at all three study visits, whilst women in the standard-of-care arm were tested postnatally only. Women testing positive for C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae were treated in accordance with US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines [21].

Assessment of depression during pregnancy and postnatally

Perinatal depression was assessed using the 10-item Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), which uses a Likert scale to assess emotional experiences (items 1 to 9), including feelings of sadness, worry, difficulty sleeping, and thoughts of self-harm (item 10), over the past seven days [15]. The EPDS was conducted to screen for symptoms of perinatal depression at two time points: the first antenatal care and the 6–8 week postnatal care study visits. The EPDS was administered by a research assistant in either English or Setswana according to participant preference. Prior to study commencement, the EPDS was translated from English to Setswana independently by two bilingual research assistants with experience of translating research study materials, who mutually agreed the final wording. The translated EPDS questionnaire was tested by the administering research assistants during study training and piloted as part of the full study questionnaire with at least 5 participants, with adjustments to aid comprehension made as required.

We chose the EPDS because it is a robust screening tool for perinatal depression and has been validated in African settings [22, 23]. An EPDS cut-off value of ≥11 out of 30 has been found to maximise the combined sensitivity and specificity of the screen, whilst a higher cut-off value of ≥13 out of 30 has demonstrated the greatest specificity in identifying women with high perinatal depression symptom levels [24]. We therefore examined the proportion of women experiencing perinatal depression symptoms using an EPDS cut-off of both ≥11 and ≥13 at the first antenatal care visit and the postnatal care visit. We additionally examined ‘overall perinatal depression’, which we defined as an EPDS score ≥11 or ≥13 at either or both visits. To identify factors associated with perinatal depression, we used an EPDS threshold of ≥13 to focus on participants most likely to benefit from additional mental health support, in the context of a resource-constrained setting such as Botswana. Participants with an EPDS score ≥13 or an affirmative response to the self-harm item were reviewed by a study clinician trained in providing initial mental health assessment and support in non-specialist settings [25], and were referred to local government and non-governmental organisation (NGO) mental health services. Where there was immediate risk of harm to self or others, participants were urgently referred by the study physician to the Psychiatry clinic at the tertiary referral hospital.

Potential correlates of perinatal depression

Potential correlates of symptoms of perinatal depression were selected based on previous literature [7, 11–15, 26–29], which has identified factors such as low education or socioeconomic status [7, 30], primigravidity or multigravidity [7, 28, 29], unintended pregnancy [7, 11, 12], experience of intimate partner violence [7, 13–15], alcohol use [7], lack of social support [30], and history of prior mental illness [7] to be associated with increased odds of perinatal depression.

In our study, covariates that were examined included: maternal age (≤25 versus > 25 years), number of previous pregnancies (none vs. ≥1); education level (junior secondary or less vs. senior secondary or higher), household income (< 2,000 Botswana Pula (BWP) vs. ≥2,000 BWP per month), relationship status (married and/or cohabiting vs. not), whether the partner has other partners (no or don’t know vs. yes), financial independence (earns independent income vs. does not), IPV (experience of IPV in 12 months preceding antenatal booking appointment vs. none), unintended pregnancy (intended vs. unintended), timing of antenatal booking appointment (attendance in first trimester prior to 14 weeks’ gestation vs. second trimester or later), alcohol use during pregnancy (any vs. none) and HIV status (positive vs. negative or unknown). We also examined for an association by clinic and intervention group. Postnatally, we examined for an association between antenatal depression (antenatal EPDS ≥13 vs. < 13) and postnatal depression.

Experience of IPV was assessed using an adapted Conflict Tactics Scale [31], a validated screening tool for verbal abuse, physical abuse (e.g. being pushed, hit, kicked, strangled, burned or attacked with a weapon) and sexual abuse (e.g. coerced or forced intercourse or sexual acts) from an intimate partner in the 12 months prior to the first antenatal appointment. For the purposes of this study, experience of IPV was defined as any affirmative response to this screen. In line with WHO guidance [32], IPV screening questions were only asked when privacy could be assured; where it was not safe to proceed with IPV questions or participants declined, this was documented and the questions were omitted. Unintended pregnancy was defined as women reporting that they had not intended to become pregnant, or that their intentions kept changing, when questioned at their first antenatal care appointment. Alcohol use in pregnancy was defined as any alcohol use during pregnancy reported at the first antenatal care visit, using the first question from the adapted AUDIT-C alcohol screening questionnaire [33]: ‘Since you became pregnant, how often did you have a drink containing alcohol?’, with any response other than ‘Never’ being recorded as alcohol use in pregnancy.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, percentages for categorical variables) were used for study population characteristics. Next, we estimated the prevalence of participants with symptoms of perinatal depression (EPDS scores ≥11 and ≥13) among participants attending their first antenatal care (ANC) and a postnatal care (PNC) visit, with 95% confidence intervals. After ensuring normal distribution, a paired t-test was used to assess mean difference, with standard error and 95% confidence intervals, between EPDS scores at the postnatal visit compared to the first antenatal care visit.

The primary outcome for regression analysis was the presence of symptoms of depression, as defined by an EPDS score of ≥13. An exploratory analysis was performed using unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression analysis to look for potential associations between correlates of interest and symptoms of depression at [1] the first antenatal visit [2], the 6–8 week postnatal visit and [3] the first antenatal visit and/or postnatal visit, i.e. factors associated with ‘overall perinatal depression’. In the multivariable model, participant age and study arm (intervention versus standard of care) were included a priori. We used linear regression with standard errors clustered at the clinic level to account for non-independence of observations within clinics. Next, we included covariates significantly (p < 0.05) associated with an EPDS score ≥13 in the unadjusted regression analysis. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) are reported. We additionally conducted a sensitivity analysis using a multivariable linear regression model, treating the total EPDS score at the first antenatal care and 6–8 week postnatal visits as a continuous outcome. Covariates that were significant in the unadjusted analyses were included in the model, and standard errors were clustered at the clinic level. In all statistical tests, significance was based on an alpha level of 0.05.

Reasons for non-completion of EPDS at the first antenatal and postnatal visits are summarised in Fig. 1. Baseline characteristics were compared between those missing and not-missing postnatal EPDS data and no significant differences were identified. We therefore conducted complete case analysis. Where there were missing data in baseline characteristics, n numbers are provided. We conducted a retrospective power calculation to evaluate whether our sample size of 500 participants provided adequate precision for sampling the prevalence of perinatal depression. Assuming a perinatal depression prevalence of 6% in Botswana [18], a sample size of 500 would yield a 95% confidence interval with an approximate half-width (precision) of ±2.1%.

Results

Participant characteristics and demographics

Five hundred pregnant women were enrolled in the Maduo study. All participants completed the EPDS at least once during the study – 495/500 (99%) at the first antenatal visit and 428 (86%) at the postnatal visit (Fig. 1). Participant characteristics are reported in Table 1. The median age was 27 years. Two-thirds (66%) were educated to senior secondary level or higher, half (50%) had a household income of ≤2000BWP/month (equivalent to approximately 140 USD/month), and half (50%) earned an independent income. A third (36%) of women were primigravida, with the majority (79%) attending their first antenatal appointment in the second trimester (gestational age of 14 to 27 weeks). The pregnancy was intended for 44% of participants. Approximately a fifth (17%) of women were living with HIV. Half of participants (52%) were married or cohabiting with the father of their pregnancy. Thirty-seven women (7%) had experienced IPV in the 12 months preceding their first antenatal care visit. It was not safe to ask IPV questions for 81 participants (16%), meaning 419/500 participants had complete data on IPV.

Prevalence of symptoms of perinatal depression

The median EPDS score was 3 (IQR 1–6) at the first antenatal visit and 2 (IQR 0–5) at the postnatal visit (Table 2), with a mean reduction in EPDS score of 0.54 points (standard error 0.22, 95% CI 0.11–0.97) at the postnatal visit compared to the antenatal visit. Similar proportions of women had an EPDS score suggestive of depression at the first antenatal and postnatal visits. At the first antenatal care visit, the prevalence of depression symptoms was 7% (95% CI 5–10%) using an EPDS score ≥11 and 5% (95% CI 3–7%) using an EPDS score ≥13. At the postnatal visit, the prevalence of depression symptoms was 7% (95% CI 5–10%) using an EPDS score ≥11 and 3% (95% CI 1–5%) using an EPDS score ≥13 (Table 2; Fig. 2). There was little overlap in women who experienced depression symptoms antenatally compared to postnatally (Supplementary Table 1): only 1 out of 36 women with perinatal depression symptoms scored ≥13 at both antenatal and postnatal visits; whilst 6 out of 60 women scored ≥11 at both visits. ‘Overall perinatal depression’ prevalence, i.e. depression symptoms at the antenatal and/or postnatal care visit was 12% (95% CI 9–15%) using an EPDS of ≥11 and 7% (95% CI 5–10%) using an EPDS of ≥13. Thirty-eight women (8%) reported thoughts of self-harm at either visit (Table 2), with more women reporting thoughts of self-harm at the first antenatal visit (29/495, 6%) compared to the postnatal visit (10/428, 2%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.41, Chi-squared test).

Factors associated with perinatal depression

Results from multivariable logistic regression analysis identifying associations with symptoms of depression (EPDS score ≥13) overall, at first antenatal care visit and 6–8 week postnatal care visit are summarised in Tables 3, 4, and 5. Unintended pregnancy (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 7.76, 95% CI 1.65–36.48, p = 0.01) and experience of intimate partner violence (aOR 5.58, 95% CI 2.75–11.34, p < 0.001) were strongly associated with higher odds of symptoms of overall perinatal depression (Table 3). Maternal HIV status, education level, household income, earning independent income, and alcohol use in pregnancy were not associated with increased odds of overall perinatal depression symptoms.

We also examined associations with symptoms of antenatal and postnatal depression separately. Unintended pregnancy (aOR 29.44, 95% CI 2.75–314.6, p = 0.005) and experience of intimate partner violence (aOR 5.41, 95% CI 1.94–15.10, p = 0.001) were associated with increased odds of antenatal symptoms of depression (Table 4). Postnatally, experience of intimate partner violence (aOR 5.18, 95% CI 3.35–8.02, p = < 0.001) and being a first-time mother (aOR 3.83, 95% CI 2.35–6.27, p = < 0.001) were strongly associated with increased odds of symptoms of depression (Table 5). No association was identified between having symptoms of depression (EPDS score ≥13) antenatally and experiencing symptoms of depression postnatally. No association was identified between study arm (intervention vs. standard-of-care) and antenatal, postnatal or overall perinatal depression.

In the sensitivity analysis using EPDS score as a continuous variable (Supplementary Tables 2a and 2b), intimate partner violence was significantly associated with higher EPDS scores at both first antenatal (adjusted β 4.77, 95% CI 3.30–6.23, p = 0.002) and postnatal (adjusted β 1.86, 95% CI 0.54–3.19, p = 0.02) visits. Unintended pregnancy was significantly associated with higher EPDS scores both antenatally (adjusted β 2.15, 95% CI 0.07–4.24, p = 0.046) and postnatally (adjusted β 1.14, 95% CI 0.81–1.47, p = 0.002), in contrast with the primary logistic regression analyses, where unintended pregnancy was significantly associated with an EPDS score ≥13 antenatally only. In addition, first pregnancy was associated with higher odds of postnatal depression in the logistic regression model, but this association was not significant when EPDS scores were analysed as a continuous outcome.

Discussion

In this study, we found up to 12% of pregnant women enrolling in a Botswana STI-screening study had Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale scores indicative of perinatal depression either antenatally, postnatally or both. The prevalence of depression symptoms ranged from 5 to 7% at the first antenatal care visit and 3–7% at the postnatal visit, depending on the EPDS score cut-off used. Most women with antenatal depression symptoms did not have symptoms suggestive of depression postnatally, and vice versa. Unintended pregnancy and intimate partner violence were the factors most strongly associated with increased odds of perinatal depression.

The prevalence of perinatal depression symptoms in our Botswana study is lower than previously reported rates in Southern Africa, where a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis reported regional prevalence rates of 27.9% in pregnant and 23.1% in postpartum women [17]. This discrepancy may be partly due to variability in EPDS cut-off scores across studies, with some using lower EPDS scores (e.g. 8–10) [17], which increases case detection. Similarly, in our study, using an EPDS cut-off of ≥11 identified more women with symptoms of depression compared to a cut-off of ≥13. Moreover, Botswana’s status as an upper-middle-income country may partly explain why our observed perinatal depression prevalence aligns more closely with estimates for high-income countries of approximately 10%, compared to LMICs where perinatal depression prevalence is approximately 20% [16]. No studies from Botswana were included in the 2021 systematic review, highlighting a lack of national data. Given substantial inter-country variation in social, cultural and economic factors which may influence rates of perinatal depression, even within a WHO region, country-specific studies like ours are essential.

Although data on perinatal depression in Botswana are limited, one recently published nested observational study comparing symptoms of postnatal depression at 2, 6 and 12 months postpartum similarly reported a lower prevalence of postnatal depression compared to regional estimates for Southern Africa. Using an EPDS cut-off score of 10, the study identified a postnatal depression prevalence of 8% for women living with HIV and 6% for HIV seronegative women [18]. These findings are broadly comparable to those of the present study, in which 7% women had an EPDS score of ≥11 at their postnatal assessment. Consistent with our study and the literature from the African region [34, 35], no association was observed between maternal HIV status and postpartum depression. However, while that study reported higher odds of perinatal depression among multigravid women, our study found increased odds of postnatal depression symptoms among primiparous women who became first-time mothers. Both primi- and multigravidity have been linked to elevated risk of perinatal depression [26, 28, 29, 36–38], although this has not been well-studied in the African region. For primigravid women, limited experience and lower maternal confidence may contribute to symptoms of perinatal depression, whilst for multigravid women, depression symptoms may be associated with the social and economic pressures of caring for multiple children; or previous pregnancy loss.

Although our study identified similar proportions of women with symptoms of depression antenatally and postnatally, there was minimal overlap, and antenatal symptoms were not associated with increased odds of postnatal symptoms. This suggests potentially distinct contributing factors at each stage. For example, unintended pregnancy was associated with increased odds of antenatal, but not postnatal, depression (using an EPDS cut-off of ≥13), which is consistent with prior studies showing that initial depressive symptoms in response to an unplanned pregnancy may lessen over time as the pregnancy becomes accepted [39, 40]. A majority of women with antenatal depression symptoms did not exhibit symptoms postnatally. Almost all women with symptoms suggestive of depression at their first antenatal care visit were assessed by a trained study clinician and were referred to perinatal mental health services (Supplementary Table 3). Although data on service uptake and perceived impact were not collected, the observed reduction in postnatal symptoms suggests a potential benefit. In a resource-limited context like Botswana, with few trained mental health providers and a single tertiary psychiatry clinic in Gaborone, the EPDS cut-off of 13 effectively identified women most in need of support, with referral appearing to positively influence EPDS scores.

Experience of intimate partner violence in the 12 months prior to the first antenatal care visit emerged as a significant association with increased odds of antenatal, postnatal and overall depression, in keeping with previous research [13–15]. Women experiencing IPV may be at higher risk of social isolation, in addition to negative impacts on physical and mental health, which may contribute to vulnerability to depression. Although recall bias is possible, the 12-month timeframe was chosen to identify women who may be most at risk of ongoing or escalating IPV during pregnancy. IPV typically involves repeated incidents of abusive behaviour, and while limited data exist on IPV recall, repeated-event memory research suggests adults can reliably report overall experiences, even if specific details are forgotten [41]. To minimise risk of recall bias, future studies could screen for IPV at defined timepoints across the perinatal period. In our study, IPV screening was omitted when privacy could not be ensured, for example when the male partner was present throughout the study visit. While this approach prioritised participant safety, it may have excluded those at highest risk of IPV, potentially leading to an underestimation of both IPV prevalence and its association with perinatal depression.

Our study had some limitations. Firstly, this was a secondary analysis of data collected from the Maduo study, and we therefore used the sample size from the parent study. Consequently, our analysis may be underpowered to identify smaller associations with the risk of perinatal depression. The wide confidence intervals for some associations with perinatal depression may reflect limited precision in subgroup analyses, and underscore the need for larger studies to improve precision. Our prevalence estimates for antenatal and postnatal depression symptoms fall close to the expected range of precision for a sample size of 500. Although a retrospective power calculation was performed, post hoc power analyses can be theoretically flawed and should be interpreted with caution [42]. Our 95% confidence intervals provide a more robust measure of the precision of our prevalence estimates. These have a relatively narrow range (±3%) and therefore offer sufficient precision to help guide clinical and public health decisions in a setting where even modest rates of perinatal depression warrant targeted mental health support for pregnant and postpartum women. An additional limitation of this secondary analysis is that we were restricted to the variables included in the primary study questionnaire. While we assessed most key correlates of perinatal depression identified in the literature, such as education level, socioeconomic status, experience of IPV and unintended pregnancy [7, 11–15, 26–29], there were some important factors that were not captured, including history of maternal mental health problems [7], physical health problems during pregnancy [13, 43, 44], overall life stress [10, 30] and level of social support [8, 30].

Secondly, while the EPDS is widely recognized and validated for detecting depression during pregnancy and the postpartum period [45], it has not been formally validated in the specific context of Botswana. Although the Setswana-translated EPDS was not formally validated with participants, it was thoroughly tested by our bilingual research team to ensure clarity and accuracy prior to study initiation, and the full study questionnaire, including the EPDS, was piloted with at least five participants, with adjustments made to improve comprehension where required. It is worth noting that concerns have previously been raised regarding the discriminant validity of local-language translations of the EPDS [46]. However, translated versions of the EPDS have been successfully employed and validated to screen individuals at risk of perinatal depression in various global settings, including neighbouring South Africa [47]. Thirdly, our utilization of EPDS scores of ≥11 and ≥13, selected based on systematic review data [24] to either maximise combined sensitivity and specificity of the EPDS (score ≥11) or to have the greatest specificity in identifying individuals with the most significant depressive symptoms (score ≥13), should be interpreted cautiously as these cut-offs have not been locally validated. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis treating the EPDS score as a continuous variable to assess the robustness of our findings. Although most results were consistent with the logistic regression analyses, some differences were observed, likely reflecting the use of a continuous rather than a dichotomous outcome and the associated variation in statistical power. The finding that unintended pregnancy was associated with depressive symptoms in both models, but reached statistical significance only at the antenatal visit in the logistic analysis, likely reflects limited power due to the smaller number of participants with elevated EPDS postnatally. Fourthly, participants were recruited exclusively from clinics in Gaborone, the capital city, which may limit the generalisability of findings, although sociodemographic features of our participants including income, marital status and education level were consistent with national figures [48, 49]. Finally, given our study design, depression screening was conducted at two time points only: the initial antenatal and 6–8 week postnatal visit, with data on potential correlates collected at baseline only. This limited our ability to evaluate time-varying exposures or changes in depression symptoms over time. Future longitudinal research that includes repeated assessments of depression symptoms and associated factors throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period could provide a more comprehensive understanding of how symptoms evolve over time.

Our study found lower rates of perinatal depression within a cohort of pregnant women participating in an STI screening study in Botswana, in contrast to previous prevalence estimates in Southern Africa and in low resource settings. Nonetheless, the incorporation of routine depression symptom screening during antenatal care in Botswana would offer a valuable avenue for identifying individuals requiring support and specialized services. This approach has the potential to mitigate the risk of maternal and infant morbidity and mortality associated with untreated perinatal depression.
