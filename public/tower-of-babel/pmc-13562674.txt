Engineering tolerogenic mRNA-LNPs for allergen-specific immune reprogramming in pollen allergy
Authors: Zhiying Yao, Dingyou Wang, Yuhan Zhang, Peilin Xin, Yutong Li, Elif Naz Cerav, Di Tang, Zhirui Zhao, Zhili Wang, Huiyang Wang, Huizi Liu, Zhihui Liang, Changying Xue, Han Xiao, Gang Cheng, Xiubo Fan, Shan Deng, Tian Xia, Bingbing Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPollen allergy is a prevalent respiratory allergic disease, yet current allergen immunotherapy (AIT) remains limited by safety concerns and insufficient induction of durable immune tolerance. Here, we develop an engineered mRNA-LNP platform for allergen-specific tolerance induction by integrating allergen and delivery system optimization. A non-secretory mRNA encoding the major mugwort pollen allergen Art v 1 fused with invariant chain (mArt v 1) was designed to reduce IgE-mediated activation while promoting tolerogenic allergen processing. This antigen was delivered using a mannose-functionalized LNP formulation (mAr-C18mann) optimized for low inflammatory activation and enhanced immune regulation. The engineered platform promotes a tolerance-associated hepatic immune environment, regulates antigen-presenting cell responses, and enhances allergen-specific regulatory T-cell induction. In a murine model of pollen allergy, both mAr-C18mann and a simplified epitope-based variant (mdT-C18mann) attenuate allergic inflammation and allergen-specific IgE levels. Consistently, mAr-C18mann reprograms immune responses toward Treg- and Th1-dominated profiles, providing effective protection in a prophylactic allergy model. These findings support the mRNA-LNP platform as a modular next-generation AIT strategy for inducing durable immune tolerance to pollen allergy.Keywords: mRNA vaccine, Lipid nanoparticles, Pollen allergy, Immune tolerance, Regulatory T cell

Introduction

Pollen allergy is a major cause of respiratory allergic diseases and has gradually become a public health issue, affecting one-third of the global population [1]. It commonly causes persistent ocular, cutaneous, and respiratory symptoms and is closely associated with asthma and cross-reactivity to plant-derived food allergies [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Allergen extracts remain the most widely used immunologically active component in allergen immunotherapy (AIT) [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. However, conventional extract-based AIT is limited by heterogeneous clinical efficacy and limited disease-modifying potential in a substantial proportion of patients [13,14]. To address these challenges, diverse vaccine platforms have been explored, including recombinant protein vaccines, adjuvant-subunit vaccines, and delivery systems designed to induce immunological tolerance [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Despite these advances, the clinical translation of pollen vaccines remains constrained by inefficient allergen delivery, the risk of inducing IgE-mediated allergic responses, and the insufficient induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which are essential for establishing durable immune tolerance [21,22]. Therefore, developing a next-generation vaccine platform that simultaneously ensures absolute safety and precisely redirects the immune response toward a protective Treg- and Th1-dominated profile remains a challenge.

Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines offer unique advantages for AIT, including programmable antigen expression and reduced risk of IgE cross-linking mediated by allergens [23,24]. However, their clinical translation critically depends on lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery systems, which possess intrinsic adjuvant properties [25]. While such immunostimulatory effects are beneficial in infectious disease settings, their non-specific nature can trigger untargeted inflammation, disrupting the precise immune redirection required for effective AIT [26,27]. To overcome this barrier, pioneering strategies have focused on mRNA sequence optimization and rational formulation design [28,29]. For instance, low-inflammogenic mRNA-LNPs have been utilized in autoimmune disease models to promote Treg expansion and durable immunosuppression [28,30]. In allergy research, delivering mRNA encoding peanut epitopes fused to the invariant (Ii) chain has proven effective in targeting Major histocompatibility complex Class II (MHCII) processing and boosting Treg responses [31]. Additionally, rational LNP formulation screening, optimizing the molar ratio of ionizable lipids to mRNA, has successfully minimized the intrinsic immunostimulatory properties of LNPs, as evidenced by reduced expression of co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80, CD86, and CD40) on dendritic cells [32]. Although these major advances are promising, reversing the firmly established Th2-skewed environment typical of pollen allergies will require a vaccine delivery system that does more than just reduce inflammation. At the structural level, it must direct the immune response to prevent pathogenic Th2 activation. Additionally, it must deliver active, receptor-mediated signals to promote the formation of a protective network of Tregs and Th1 cells. How to achieve this precise immune modulation within a single integrated LNP platform remains largely unexplored.

To address these challenges, we hypothesized that the immunological fate of mRNA vaccines could be redirected toward immune tolerance through rational engineering of both mRNA sequences and LNP components (Scheme 1). In this study, we developed an engineered mRNA-LNP platform for allergen-specific immunotherapy by integrating a non-secretory Art v 1-based allergen design with an optimized LNP delivery system. The allergen construct was designed to minimize IgE-mediated activation while facilitating tolerogenic antigen processing through invariant chain fusion. In parallel, the LNP formulation was systematically optimized by modulating PEG-lipid architecture and incorporating mannose-mediated APC targeting. This study establishes a rational framework for designing mRNA vaccines as immunomodulatory platforms to induce antigen-specific immune tolerance in allergic diseases.

Engineering a tolerogenic mRNA-lipid nanoparticle (LNP) platform for pollen allergy through epitope and formulation design. Two mRNA sequences were engineered, one encoding the major mugwort pollen allergen Art v 1 fused to the invariant chain (Ii) to direct endogenous MHC II presentation (mArt v 1), and a simplified dual-epitope construct (mdT). The chemical structure of the PEG library and the relationship between LNP formulations and their immunogenicity trends were examined. Upon subcutaneous administration, mannose-decorated lipid nanoparticles (mAr-C18mann) preferentially accumulate in the liver, where the encapsulated mRNA is translated. The Ii-fused allergen is processed via the endogenous MHC II pathway. Tolerogenic signals from the liver drive the systemic expansion of allergen-specific regulatory T cell (Treg; CD25+FoxP3+ Treg) in the spleen, shifting the immune balance away from a pathogenic Th2 response. In both therapeutic and prophylactic murine models of Artemisia pollen allergy, vaccination with mAr-C18mann significantly alleviated allergic symptoms, reduced lung inflammation, and suppressed allergen-specific IgE.

The goat anti-mouse polyclonal secondary antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Antibodies used in flow cytometry were listed in Table S1.

mRNA construct design for Art v 1 protein

Two mRNA constructs were designed based on the sequence of recombinant UniProt Art v 1 (UniProt accession Q84ZX5). The mArt v 1 construct encoded the mature-region fragment of rArt v 1 (residues 25–132). The Ii (1−80) sequence was linked to the Art v 1 fragment via a glycine–proline-rich flexible linker (GPGPG). Another mRNA construct (mdT) was generated using in silico MHC II binding predictions performed with the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) analysis platform, incorporating ANN- and SMM-based algorithms (e.g., NetMHCIIpan). The top-ranked epitopes were selected, reverse-translated into cDNA, concatenated using a flexible linker, and fused downstream of Ii (1−80) to form a minimal epitope-based antigenic unit. For both constructs, the coding sequence was codon-optimized using the GenSmart™ codon-optimization tool. The mRNA was synthesized in vitro by GeneScript (Nanjing, China). This synthesis also included the complete substitution of uridine with N1-methyl-pseudouridine, along with 5′ and 3′ UTRs and a Poly(A)100 tail. The resulting mRNA was purified according to standard procedures and used for subsequent LNP encapsulation and biological studies.

Formulation and characterization of mRNA-laden LNPs

PEG-based LNP formulations were composed of DLin-MC3-DMA (MC3), DSPC, cholesterol, and PEG-lipids, including DMPE-PEG2000 (C14), DPPE-PEG2000 (C16), DSPE-PEG2000 (C18), or DSPE-PEG2000-mannose, at a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5 dissolved in ethanol. mRNA was dissolved in 50 mM sodium citrate (pH 4.0) to create the aqueous phase and added at an N/P molar ratio of 4:1. All devices were pretreated with DEPC water overnight. The ethanol and aqueous phases were mixed using two syringe pumps, with rapid flow through a T-shaped microfluidic device set to predefined pump speeds of 3 mL/min and 9 mL/min, respectively [33]. The resulting LNPs were dialyzed against PBS using 3.5 kDa MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer™ G3 Dialysis Cassettes at room temperature for over 2 h to remove ethanol. If needed, formulations were concentrated with the ultra-centrifugal filters (EMD Millipore) and stored at 4 °C. Following encapsulation, mRNA-LNP formulations were named according to the mRNA type and PEG-lipid composition, using the format mRNA type-LNP, e.g., mAr-C14, mAr-C16, mAr-C18, and mAr-C18mann. For the morphologies, mAr-C18mann were vitrified on glow-discharged Quantifoil grids using a Vitrobot Mark IV and imaged on a 200 kV Glacios2 cryo-TEM (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a Falcon III detector at 72,000× magnification. The ζ potentials, hydrodynamic sizes, and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured with ZetaPALS (90Plus Zeta, Brookhaven, USA). The mRNA encapsulation efficiencies were measured by Quant-iT™ RiboGreen. Fi represents the fluorescence value of unencapsulated mRNA content in the LNP solutions. Ft denotes the fluorescence value of the total mRNA obtained after incubation with 2% (v/v) Triton X-100. The formula for % Encapsulation Efficiency (% EE) is (%EE) = (Ft -Fi)/Ft × 100.

Identification of mArt v 1-laden LNP formulation-stimulated APC immunophenotype

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) derived from ICR mice were purchased from BlueFBio (BFB Qingqi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). LSECs were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. BMDCs were generated from the bone marrow of female BALB/c mice following a standard GM-CSF/IL-4 differentiation protocol. Briefly, bone marrow cells were isolated from femora and tibiae and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 20 mM HEPES, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. Recombinant murine GM-CSF (25 ng/mL) was added on day 0. On days 2, 4, and 7, half of the medium was refreshed with GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) and IL-4 (10 ng/mL). Immature BMDCs were harvested on day 8, washed with PBS, and used for subsequent experiments. For in vitro stimulation, LSECs or BMDCs were incubated with mArt v 1-laden LNP formulations at 2 μg/mL mRNA for 48 h. After incubation, cells were collected, washed with PBS, and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD80, CD86, and MHC II, respectively. Cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (CS6, BD) and FlowJo software. To assess cytokine secretion, BMDC culture supernatants were collected after 48 h of stimulation with mRNA-laden LNP formulations (2 μg/mL mRNA). Concentration of IL-10 was quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Evaluation of the biodistribution of mfL-laden LNP formulations in mice

To evaluate the in vivo biodistribution of LNP-C18mann, Firefly Luciferase mRNA was encapsulated in LNP formulations and subcutaneously injected into 8-week-old female BALB/c mice at a dose of 10 μg of mRNA per mouse. For early biodistribution analysis, four formulations (mfL-C14, mfL-C16, mfL-C18, and mfL-C18mann) were imaged at 6 h post-injection. At the 24 h time point, a subset of formulations (mfL-C14, mfL-C18, and mfL-C18mann) was evaluated. At the indicated time points (6 h or 24 h), mice were intraperitoneally injected with 15 mg/kg D-luciferin and anesthetized with tribromoethanol prior to bioluminescence imaging. After 10 min, the entire mouse was imaged for luminescence intensity using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer). Following in vivo imaging, the major organs of sacrificed mice were collected for ex vivo imaging. Bioluminescence signals were quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer) by defining regions of interest (ROIs) at a 25% threshold of peak signal intensity.

Animal vaccinations

For analysis of mRNA vaccine-generated regulatory T and regulatory B cells, female BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animal protocol was approved by the Animal Research Committee at Dalian University of Technology (DUTSCE220301_08). BALB/c (female, 8 weeks old, n = 6) mice were subcutaneously immunized with mArt v 1-laden LNP formulations at doses of 5, 10, 20, and 30 μg on days 0, 2, 4, and 6. The allergen protein control group received recombinant Art v 1 protein (rArt v 1) at doses of 2, 4, 8, and 16 μg on the same immunization schedule. The doses were selected based on the literature-supported optimal therapeutic window [28,34,35]. PBS and Poly(A)-laden LNP-C18mann (P(A)-C18mann) were included as control groups. On day 13, mice were euthanized, and the liver, dLNs, and spleen were harvested. Liver tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction. For gene expression analysis, total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA and analyzed by qRT-PCR. Relative mRNA expression levels of FoxP3, TGF-β1, and IL-10 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. dLNs were processed into single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry of dendritic cell activation markers. Splenocytes were isolated to assess intracellular cytokine production and restimulated with 2 μg/mL rArt v 1 for 84 h to analyze allergen-specific T cell responses.

The animal protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee (LAWEC) of Dalian Medical University, Dalian, China (AEE24080). In the pollen allergy therapeutic model, BALB/c mice (female, 8 weeks old, n = 6) were sensitized via subcutaneous injection in the lower neck region using a 200 μL solution containing 25 μg CAPE and 100 μL Alhydrogel on Days 0, 7, and 14. Mice injected with PBS served as the non-sensitized group. Following sensitization, mice were treated with epitope-based mRNA vaccines formulated in mannose-decorated C18 LNPs. mRNAs encoding either the modified Art v 1 fragment (residues 25–132) or the dual T-cell epitope construct (mdT) were encapsulated in LNP-C18mann and designated mAr-C18mann and mdT-C18mann, respectively. Mice received s. c. immunizations with 5, 10, 20, and 30 μg mRNA on Days 19, 21, 23, and 25. For comparison, a protein-based therapeutic control group was immunized with 300 μg CAPE formulated with AlOOH nanorods (100 μg Al3+). CAPE-sensitized mice that received no treatment served as sensitized controls. To evaluate therapeutic efficacy, mice were challenged by inhalation with 1% CAPE using a nebulizer at an atomization rate of 0.2 mL/min and a gas flow rate of 7.0 L/min (403M, Yuwell), for 30 min daily on Days 30 to 34. The non-sensitized group underwent inhalation without CAPE added to PBS. Pollen allergy symptoms were recorded during the 30-min post-challenge period. Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) was assessed in the therapeutic pollen allergy model using whole-body barometric plethysmography (TOW-INT TECH, China) following methacholine challenge. On Day 35, mice were placed individually into plethysmography chambers and exposed to increasing concentrations of aerosolized methacholine (0, 3.1, 6.2, 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/mL in PBS). The enhanced pause (Penh), a dimensionless parameter derived from respiratory waveform variables, was continuously recorded. For each methacholine dose, the average Penh value during the 3-min period following aerosol exposure was calculated and used for analysis. On Day 36, blood, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), spleen, and lung tissues were collected for downstream analyses. BALF was obtained by instilling and withdrawing 1 mL PBS per mouse. Lung tissues were processed for histological evaluation, and splenocytes were isolated for flow-cytometric analysis following antigen re-stimulation.

For the pollen allergy preventative model, mice (n = 6) were subcutaneously immunized with 5, 10, 20, and 30 μg of mArt-C18mann on Days 0, 2, 4, and 6. AlOOH NRs (100 μg Al3+) were formulated with rArt v 1 in doses of 2, 4, 8, and 16 μg at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio, respectively, and administered during the pretreatment process. Control groups received PBS. Mice were sensitized through subcutaneous injection of a 200 μL solution containing 25 μg CAPE and 100 μL of Alhydrogel on Days 14, 21, and 28. CAPE-sensitized mice without any pre-treatment served as the sensitized group. 200 μL PBS-injected mice served as the non-sensitized group. The subsequent inhalation (inh) challenge was administered with 1% CAPE using a nebulizer at an atomization rate of 0.2 mL/min and a gas flow rate of 7.0 L/min, lasting 30 min daily from days 35 to 39. Symptom scores were recorded during the 30-min post-challenge period. At the endpoint, serum was collected to determine the CAPE-specific antibody titers. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected into PBS (1 mL/mouse). The spleens were processed into single cells using a mild tissue dissociator (GentleMACS Octo Dissociator, Miltenyi). The collected dLNs were disrupted to obtain a single-cell suspension. The lung was divided into two parts to determine T cell-mediated immunity and airway inflammation by staining with H&E and AB-PAS. The organs were collected and stained with H&E. The cultured cell supernatant (12 h) was assessed by ELISA, and the cells were collected to evaluate T and B cell differentiation by flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry analysis of T and B cell differentiation

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from draining lymph nodes (dLNs) and spleens as described above. Before antibody staining, cells were incubated with an anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (1000 times dilution) to block Fc-mediated reactions. For analysis of dendritic cell activation in dLNs, cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC class II following standard surface-staining procedures. Single-cell suspensions from spleen and dLNs were first stained to exclude dead cells. After centrifugation and resuspension, 0.1 μg of antibodies against CD3, CD4, CD25, and CTLA-4 (including CD45 for pneumonocytes) was mixed and incubated with the cell suspension in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. After fixation/permeabilization, 0.3 μg of anti-FoxP3 and anti-Helios antibodies were added to the cell suspension in the dark for 45 min. After centrifugation and resuspension, the 96-well round-bottom plate containing the cells was kept on ice before flow cytometry (FACSymphony A1, BD) analysis.

For intracellular cytokine staining, to block cytokine secretion, eBioscience™ Cell Stimulation Cocktail (500 times dilution) was added to the cell suspension for 4 h. The stimulated cells were collected and treated with CD16/32 antibody to block Fc-mediated reactions. After fixation and permeabilization, IFN-γ, IL-10, and IL-4 monoclonal antibodies stained the cell suspension for 45 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation and resuspension, the 96-well round-bottom plate containing the cells was kept on ice prior to analysis using flow cytometry (FACSymphony A1, BD).

All samples were analyzed with FlowJo software. Gating strategies were established based on unstained controls, and fluorescence compensation was performed using single-stained controls.

qRT-PCR analysis

The isolated liver was immediately ground in liquid nitrogen at the endpoint of the animal experiment for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. cDNA synthesis was performed using the RevertAid Master Mix with DNase I. qRT-PCR was conducted using a mixture containing the cDNA template, forward and reverse primers, RNase-free water, and Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, for a final volume of 20 μL. Primers’ sequences were presented in Table S2. After gently mixing the qRT-PCR mixture and briefly centrifuging, it was loaded into the CFX Connect fluorescent quantitative PCR detection system (CFX Connect, Bio-Rad). Thermal cycling conditions were referenced from a published article [36]. The 2–ΔΔCt method was used to determine relative gene expression levels [37].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum samples were collected as described above. CAPE-specific IgE and CAPE-specific IgG subclasses (IgG1 and IgG2a) were quantified by ELISA. 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with CAPE (4 μg/mL) in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer. Plates were blocked with 10% FBS in DPBS and incubated with serially diluted serum samples. After washing, HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgE, IgG1, or IgG2a secondary antibodies were added and incubated as directed by the manufacturer. The reaction was developed using TMB substrate and stopped with 2 M H2SO4. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Endpoint titers were defined as the highest serum dilution yielding an optical density (OD) value exceeding the background-derived cutoff threshold. Raw reciprocal antibody titers were log10-transformed before statistical analysis and presented as geometric mean titers (GMTs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Raw reciprocal antibody titers were log10-transformed and presented as GMTs with 95% CIs. Clinical allergy symptom scores (ordinal data) are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Other data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

For group comparisons, discrete ordinal data were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test. Continuous data exhibiting unequal variances across groups, as determined by the Brown-Forsythe test, were evaluated using Welch's analysis of variance (ANOVA). Other comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using standard one-way ANOVA.

Results

Design of the pollen allergen coding sequences with the MHC II leading function

Art v 1, the dominant allergen in mugwort pollen, is recognized by specific IgE antibodies in more than 95% of patients allergic to this pollen (Fig. 1A) [38]. To develop a safe therapeutic vaccine, we designed an mRNA vaccine using the cDNA sequence of recombinant Art v 1 (rArt v 1). The design was based on the rArt v 1 sequence (UniProt Q84ZX5), from which the N-terminal signal peptide (residues 1–24) was removed. By preventing entry into the classical secretory pathway and its associated pro-allergenic glycosylation, this structural modification physically eliminates the risk of extracellular allergen secretion and systemic IgE cross-linking (Fig. 1B and C) [[38], [39], [40]]. To decouple this safety feature from the need for antigen presentation, an alternative intracellular strategy was required. Here, the Ii chain (1−80) sequence was fused upstream of the Art v 1-coding sequence to facilitate targeting of the translated protein to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and subsequent localization to the MHC II compartment, thereby enhancing intracellular antigen presentation [31,41]. To provide broad antigenic coverage, the first construct encodes a modified fragment of rArt v 1 (25–132) (Fig. 1D). The DNA sequence encoding the human MHC II-associated Ii chain (1−80) was fused via a glycine-proline-rich flexible linker (encoded amino acid sequence: GPGPG) to a codon-optimized cDNA sequence of rArt v 1 corresponding to the mature protein.

To focus on the precise T-cell immune response to a specific epitope, we designed a second construct based on in silico predictions. We predicted MHC II binding affinity across the Art v 1 sequence using the Immune Epitope Database and Analysis Resource (IEDB) platform, along with integrated algorithms including Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Specific Matrix Modeling (SMM) (e.g., NetMHCIIpan) (Fig. 1E and F). The top two predicted epitopes were selected, reverse-translated into cDNA, concatenated with a flexible linker, and fused to the Ii chain sequence to form a dual-epitope-based antigenic unit. The optimized coding sequences were subsequently cloned into a plasmid vector containing flanking 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) and a poly(A)100 tail. Following sequence verification, the plasmid DNA was linearized to serve as a template for in vitro transcription (IVT) (Fig. 1H). Together, this rational sequence engineering effectively decoupled antigen presentation from IgE reactivity, endowing the mRNA vaccine with an absolute safety profile while preserving its capacity to drive targeted T cell responses.

Mannose-decorated LNPs enable mArt v 1 delivery and tolerogenic presentation to antigen-presenting cells (APCs)

A critical challenge for mRNA vaccines in allergy immunotherapy is overcoming the inherent adjuvanticity of conventional delivery systems, which can trigger unwanted nonspecific immune activation [42,43]. Thus, we engineered a panel of LNP formulations with varying PEG-lipid hydrophobic chain lengths to establish a structurally silenced baseline, and we used a mannose-decoration strategy to enhance APC targeting and tolerogenic antigen presentation (Fig. 2A). During LNP formulation screening, mArt v 1 was selected. The lipid formulation consisted of (6Z,9Z,28Z, 31Z)-heptatriaconta-6,9,28,31-tetraen-19-yl 4-(dimethylamino)butanoate (DLin-MC3-DMA, MC3), distearoylphosphatidylcholine (DSPC), cholesterol, and PEG-lipids, including 1,2-Dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy (polyethylene glycol)-2000 (C14-PEG2000), 1,2-Dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy (polyethylene glycol)-2000 (C16-PEG2000), 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-PE-Polyethylene Glycol-2000 (C18-PEG2000), and C18-PEG2000-mannose, in a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5. The mRNA-LNPs were prepared by mixing lipid and aqueous streams in a T-type microfluidic device, followed by dialysis. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed that mArt v 1-laden LNP formulations exhibited hydrodynamic diameters ranging from 100 nm to 150 nm, with polydispersity indices (PDI) of ∼0.2 (Fig. 2B). The near-neutral zeta potential (Fig. 2C) and the high mRNA encapsulation efficiency (>90%, Fig. 2D) collectively indicate successful and effective mRNA loading. Cryo-transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) revealed that mAr-C18mann was spherical, with a bilayer lipid membrane and a solid core (Fig. S1). After three consecutive freeze-thaw cycles and during storage at 4 °C for up to 6 weeks, mAr-C18mann generally maintained a near-neutral charge and high mRNA encapsulation efficiency (>95%), although particle size increased after freeze-thaw cycles or prolonged refrigerated storage (Tables S3 and S4). Transfection of murine liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) demonstrated substantial nanoparticle uptake (Fig. S3), and flow cytometry quantified an uptake efficiency greater than 99.9%. Western blot analysis of LSECs following mAr-C18mann treatment revealed a specific band at approximately 33 kDa, matching the predicted molecular weight of the engineered Ii-Art v1 fusion protein (p_mAr) (Fig. S3). In vivo mRNA delivery efficiency and tissue distribution of these LNP formulations were assessed by encapsulating firefly luciferase mRNA and subcutaneously injecting the formulations into BALB/c mice. In vivo imaging system (IVIS) analysis at 6 h post-injection showed that the luciferase signal was localized to the upper abdomen. Further ex vivo imaging confirmed liver-specific mRNA expression (Fig. S2). Notably, the mannose-modified formulation (LNP-C18mann) significantly increased mRNA expression compared to LNP-C18 and yielded the highest chemiluminescence signal.

After a comprehensive characterization of the delivery system, we next assessed the immunogenic profile of these rationally designed LNPs when delivering the therapeutic allergen mArt v 1. Prior to immunological evaluation, endotoxin levels in all formulations were quantified using a recombinant factor C (rFC)-based endotoxin detection assay and confirmed to be below 0.05 EU/mL, minimizing the risk of endotoxin-driven immune activation (Fig. S4A). We used flow cytometry to determine the immune profile of LSECs following mArt v 1 delivery. After 48 h of transfection with mArt v 1-laden LNPs, the MTS assay confirmed that none of the formulations induced significant cytotoxicity in LSECs (Fig. S4B). Among the examined formulations, both mAr-C18 and mAr-C18mann increased MHC II expression in LSECs (Fig. 2E and Fig. S5) without increasing CD80 (Fig. 2F) or CD86 (Fig. 2G). However, mAr-C14 and mAr-C16 significantly upregulated CD80, CD86, and MHC II, with mAr-C14 inducing the strongest activation. This MHC II high/CD80low/CD86 low phenotype is characteristic of tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells, suggesting that C18-based LNPs, with or without mannose modification, may promote Art v 1 presentation in a tolerogenic manner. Consistent with the responses observed in mArt v 1-laden formulations, non-therapeutic GFP mRNA-laden LNP-C14 induced significant upregulation of CD80 and MHC II expression (Fig. S4), indicating an intrinsic immunostimulatory property of the short-chain PEG-lipid formulation.

Based on this established delivery profile, we used an APC model with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) to determine whether the tolerogenic effect was independent of cell type. Maturation analysis revealed that mAr-C18 and C18mann elevated MHC II (Fig. 2H and Fig. S6) without concomitantly increasing CD80 (Fig. 2I) or CD86 (Fig. 2J). While mAr-C14 significantly upregulated CD80, CD86, and MHC II. To assess the functional implications of low co-stimulatory molecule expression, IL-10 levels were measured in the supernatants of stimulated BMDCs. The results showed that IL-10 secretion increased in all stimulated groups, with mAr-C16, mAr-C18, and mAr-C18mann reaching 60 pg/mL, significantly higher than in the control group. This enhanced IL-10 functionally complements the silenced maturation profile of BMDCs. Together, these phenotypic and cytokine readouts demonstrate how PEG-lipid carbon chain length critically regulates APC maturation. By synergizing this non-inflammatory foundation with mannose-mediated targeting, the system successfully induces a tolerogenic APC profile.

mAr-C18mann induces local and systemic immune tolerance

Based on their matched physicochemical characteristics and distinct immunological profiles in vitro, we advanced mAr-C14, mAr-C18, and mAr-C18mann for subsequent in vivo evaluation of the local and systemic immunomodulatory effects. Mice were subcutaneously injected with either PBS, rArt v 1, or mArt v 1 mRNA encapsulated in various LNP formulations at gradually increasing doses on Days 0, 2, 4, and 6. This established immune tolerance to Art v 1 through stable allergen exposure, similar to the clinical treatment process for pollen allergy (Fig. 3A) [44]. Poly(A)-laden LNP-C18mann (P(A)-C18mann) served as a carrier control. On Day 13, livers, draining lymph nodes (dLNs), and spleens were harvested. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis showed that, compared with rArt v 1, only mAr-C18mann upregulated FoxP3 (Fig. 3B), TGF-β1 (Fig. 3C), and IL-10 (Fig. 3D) gene expression in the liver, indicating that mAr-C18mann effectively established a local tolerogenic milieu in the liver [45,46]. To directly confirm hepatic Treg markers in situ (Fig. 3E and F), multi-color immunofluorescence staining of liver cryosections demonstrated a striking accumulation in the mAr-C18mann group compared with other groups. Quantitative spatial analysis further confirmed that hepatic CD4+FoxP3+ density was significantly upregulated from 285.7 cells/mm2 in Control to 755.1 cells/mm2 in mAr-C18mann (p < 0.001). dLNs were analyzed by flow cytometry for DC activation status in vivo. DCs from the mAr-C18mann group exhibited a significantly modulated phenotype, characterized by lower expression of the co-stimulatory molecules CD40, CD80 and CD86 on CD11c+ cells (Fig. 3G–H, Fig. S7) compared with the rArt v 1 and other LNP formulation groups. In contrast, mAr-C14 triggered a significant upregulation of the co-stimulatory molecules CD40, CD80, and CD86 on CD11c+ DCs. This suggests that the structurally silenced mAr-C18mann system promoted allergen presentation without inducing the pro-inflammatory DC activation program observed with mAr-C14. The differentiation of Th0 cells into distinct subsets is crucial for shaping immune responses. Intracellular staining was used to identify Th2, Th1, and Treg cells based on the endogenous percentages of IL-4, IFN-γ, and IL-10 in CD4+ T cells. mAr-C18mann upregulated the highest frequencies of IL-10+ (Fig. 3I and Fig. S8) and IFN-γ+ (Fig. 3J) in CD4+ T cells, but not IL-4+ (Fig. 3K), compared with other groups, suggesting a balanced Treg, Th1, and Th2 immune response mediated by mAr-C18mann.

Furthermore, splenocytes were restimulated ex vivo with rArt v 1 to assess antigen-specific responses. Following gating CD3+CD4+ cells from live lymphocytes, mAr-C18mann treatment significantly increased the percentage of CD25+ cells, which are highly expressed on activated lymphocytes or Tregs, by 7.71% compared to the control and 12.25% compared to the rArt v 1 group (Fig. 3L and Fig. S9) [47]. Further analysis of the main regulatory factor FoxP3+ demonstrated that mAr-C18mann induced Treg generation, with increased percentages of 1.55% and 1.83%, respectively (Fig. 3M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that subcutaneous administration of mAr-C18mann targets the liver, establishing a local IL-10/TGF-β1-rich tolerogenic milieu that educates DCs toward a non-inflammatory phenotype in the dLNs and ultimately drives the systemic expansion of antigen-specific Tregs in the spleen.

Therapeutic efficacy of the designed epitope-encoding mRNA-Laden LNP-C18mann against Artemisia pollen allergy

The mAr-C18mann vaccine was further studied for the treatment of pollen allergies because of its ability to promote both local and systemic antigen-specific immune tolerance (Fig. 3). BALB/c mice were sensitized via subcutaneous injection with crude Artemisia pollen extract (CAPE) and Alhydrogel, an adjuvant used in the clinically evaluated pollen vaccine, BM32 [16,38], to enhance sensitization (Fig. 4A). During the treatment phase, allergic mice were subcutaneously injected with mRNA vaccines, including LNP-C18mann-encapsulated mRNA encoding either the modified fragment Art v 1 (mAr-C18mann) or simplified dual-epitope variant (mdT-C18mann). CAPE formulated with aluminum oxyhydroxide nanorods (AlOOH NRs) was used as a treatment control (cAe-AlOOH). Compared with commercial Alhydrogel, our AlOOH NRs possess a highly crystalline boehmite structure and a well-defined nanorod morphology [48]; these properties result in more consistent adjuvant activity and batch-to-batch reproducibility, making AlOOH NRs a more standardized comparator for therapeutic efficacy assessment. CAPE-sensitized mice that received no treatment served as the sensitized control. Upon challenge with 1% CAPE, the sensitized and cAe-AlOOH groups exhibited immediate and progressively worsening allergic symptoms to CAPE (Fig. 4B). According to the allergic symptom scoring criteria in Table S5, the symptoms gradually worsened. In contrast, mice treated with mAr-C18mann exhibited reduced allergic symptoms during the challenge. On Day 35, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), as assessed by whole-body plethysmography, was ameliorated. Both mAr-C18mann and mdT-C18mann treatments suppressed the enhanced pause (Penh) parameter, measured during forced or active expiration to overcome resistance, during the methacholine challenge from 0 to 50 mg/mL (Fig. S10) [49]. Significant suppression was observed at 50 mg/mL, with mAr-C18mann showing a slightly stronger effect (Fig. 4C). To further substantiate these functional improvements at the tissue level, we performed a systematic histopathological evaluation of lung inflammation. Histopathological analysis showed marked immune cell infiltration and mucus hypersecretion in the sensitized and cAe-AlOOH groups, as evidenced by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Fig. 4D and E, and Table S6) and Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) staining (Fig. 4F and G, and Table S7). In contrast, the mRNA vaccine groups exhibited significantly lower pulmonary inflammation and mucus production, indicating effective attenuation of allergic lung pathology, with mdT-C18mann showing slightly stronger alleviation of mucus hypersecretion. Following pulmonary function and histopathological assessments, inflammatory cell infiltration was quantified by performing differential cell counts in BALF. Compared with the sensitized group, mAr-C18mann treatment reduced total BALF cell counts, whereas cAe-AlOOH increased them (Fig. S11). The cAe-AlOOH-treated groups exhibited a marked granulocyte-predominant infiltration, with neutrophils and eosinophils together accounting for 48–91% of total cells (Fig. 4H). In contrast, mAr-C18mann treatment prevented the significant spike in granulocyte proportions, which were lower than in sensitized mice. Although mdT-C18mann also showed an overall reduction, some individuals retained elevated neutrophil levels.

To evaluate the adaptive immune response, CAPE-specific IgE and IgG subclass levels were analyzed. The non-sensitized group showed low baseline IgE levels, whereas sensitization significantly increased CAPE-specific IgE levels (Fig. 4I). Compared with the sensitized group, both mRNA vaccines significantly reduced CAPE-specific IgE levels. In contrast, the cAe-AlOOH treatment further elevated IgE levels across all groups. This shift away from a hypersensitive phenotype was also reflected in the IgG subclass balance. The sensitized group showed the highest IgG1/IgG2a ratio (Fig. 4J), ranging from 4 to 32, compared with the other groups, indicating a Th2-skewed immune response characteristic of pollen allergy. In contrast, the groups that received mRNA-LNP vaccine treatment reduced the IgG1/IgG2a ratio to about 3 by increasing IgG2a levels while maintaining low IgG1 levels (Fig. S12), suggesting a shift toward a less Th2-skewed immune profile. Together with the reduced allergic symptoms and airway inflammation, these findings suggest that mAr-C18mann effectively remodels allergen-associated immune responses toward a less pathogenic immune state.

To evaluate the development of allergen-specific immune tolerance, the Treg-related markers in splenocytes were analyzed following ex vivo re-stimulation with rArt v 1 [50]. After gating CD3+CD4+ cells from live lymphocytes by flow cytometry, a clear difference in Tregs induction between the two mRNA formulations was observed (Fig. S13). Only the mAr-C18mann significantly increased the frequencies of CD25+ (by 3.62% compared with mdT-C18mann) (Fig. 4K) and CD25+FoxP3+ (by 3.09% compared with mdT-C18mann) (Fig. 4L), indicating the expansion of activated Treg. This was further supported by a significant increase in CD25+CTLA-4+ expression among CD4+ T cells in the mAr-C18mann group (Fig. 4M), underscoring the induction of suppressive Tregs. In contrast, mdT-C18mann did not significantly elevate these markers above control levels. Additionally, analysis of Helios+FoxP3+ expression within CD25+CD4+ T cells revealed that all treatment groups increased the levels compared to the non-sensitized control (Fig. 4N). Importantly, the mAr-C18mann group demonstrated the most pronounced increase, which was also significantly higher than that induced by the mdT-C18mann. Collectively, these data demonstrate that the mAr-C18mann mRNA vaccine organized a comprehensive redirection of the allergic immune response, evidenced by: (1) a significant reduction in CAPE-specific IgE alongside a restored IgG1/IgG2a balance and (2) the coordinated upregulation of functional Treg markers (CD25, FoxP3, CTLA-4, and Helios), which was uniquely prominent in the mAr-C18mann group. These effects were further supported by the enhanced IL-10 and IFN-γ cytokine profile in the mAr-C18mann group (Fig. S14).

To assess the durability of the induced immune reprogramming and distinguish antigen-specific effects, we established an animal model comprising sensitization (sensitization), allergen exposure (challenge), vaccine intervention (treatment), and allergen re-exposure (re-challenge) (Fig. S15). To benchmark allergen- and traditional adjuvant-driven effects, we also included rArt v 1 and rArt v 1 formulated with AlOOH (rAr-AlOOH) controls. Serum CAPE-specific IgE levels in the sensitized group continued to rise significantly from the challenge through the re-challenge process, highlighting the persistence of allergic inflammation and the harm of repeated exposure. In comparison, the mAr-C18mann group showed a decrease in CAPE-specific IgE after treatment on Day 32, and a significant decrease on Day 42, suggesting the induction of a durable functional tolerance that significantly restrained IgE recurrence (Fig. S15B–D). This stable protective effect was consistently confirmed in total cell counts (Fig. S15E) and lung histology (Fig. S15F–G). Serum biochemical analysis revealed no significant elevation in serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity (Fig. S15H) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity (Fig. S15I) after repeated administration of mAr-C18mann, indicating no apparent systemic toxicity under the current treatment. Furthermore, endpoint analysis of lymphoid organs showed that mAr-C18mann uniquely shifted the immune balance toward a Th1-dominated profile (via intracellular IL-4+/IFN-γ+ staining in CD4+ T cells) (Fig. S15J). While the rAr-AlOOH also induced a measurable increase in Treg, it failed to decrease CAPE-specific IgE levels (Fig. S15K).

Prophylactic efficacy of the mAr-C18mann against Artemisia pollen allergy

Building on the therapeutic efficacy shown in Fig. 4, in which the mAr-C18mann construct demonstrated a superior capacity to induce allergen-specific T cell responses and modulate the immune response compared to its simplified dual-epitope counterpart, we further explored its preventive potential. In the prophylaxis model, BALB/c mice were pretreated with PBS, rAr-AlOOH, or mAr-C18mann before establishing a model of pollen allergy (Fig. 5A). In the prophylactic setting, rAr-AlOOH was used as the comparator rather than cAe-AlOOH to avoid the potential sensitizing effects associated with CAPE. Subsequently, the pretreated mice were sensitized by subcutaneous injection with CAPE formulated with Alhydrogel. During inhalation challenges, typical allergic symptoms were recorded. When exposed to 1% CAPE, sensitized mice exhibited severe allergic symptoms following five repeated exposures, confirming CAPE sensitization. In contrast, mAr-C18mann prophylaxis significantly reduced allergic symptoms on Day 39 compared with both the sensitized and rAr-AlOOH groups (Fig. 5B). It also resulted in a marked reduction in immune cell infiltration in BALF (Fig. 5C), particularly in neutrophil, eosinophil, and basophil counts (Fig. S16). This decrease in cellular inflammation was further supported by histopathological analysis, which showed markedly decreased inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5D and E) and mucus hypersecretion (Fig. 5F and G) in the lungs, indicating effective prevention of allergic lung pathology. rAr-AlOOH exhibited a mild inhibitory effect without statistical significance. Additionally, H&E analysis of the treated mice revealed no significant toxicity in the major organs (Fig. S17).

Quantitative ELISA analysis was used to assess the adaptive immune profiles elicited by the two prophylactic strategies. Compared with rAr-AlOOH, pretreatment with mAr-C18mann significantly reduced both CAPE-specific IgE (Fig. 5H) and IgG1 (Fig. 5I–S18), indicating coordinated suppression of Th2-associated antibodies. Additionally, compared with the sensitized group, mAr-C18mann significantly increased the CAPE-specific IgG2a titer (Fig. 5J). The collective effect of these shifts was reflected in the IgG1/IgG2a ratio (Fig. 5K). The sensitized group exhibited an extremely skewed IgG1/IgG2a ratio, ranging from 89 to 1400-fold. rAr-AlOOH and mAr-C18mann prophylaxis significantly decreased the IgG1/IgG2a ratio. Notably, a substantial proportion (50%) of mAr-C18mann-pre-treated mice exhibited an IgG1/IgG2a ratio of 0.5–1, indicating a shift toward a balanced or Th1-biased immune response. In comparison with rAr-AlOOH, mAr-C18mann prophylaxis avoided boosting the IgE/IgG1 above the sensitized baseline, while simultaneously eliciting a higher IgG2a response. These findings suggest that the mAr-C18mann platform may more effectively redirect allergen-specific responses away from Th2-dominant sensitization associated with allergic inflammation.

To comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of mAr-C18mann in pollen prophylaxis, the differentiation of Tregs and the secretion of cytokines were analyzed in both systemic immune organs (spleen and dLNs), and the local lung tissues. mAr-C18mann prophylaxis significantly increased the percentage of CD25+FoxP3+ Treg among CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5L and Fig. S19) compared with the non-sensitized, sensitized, and rAr-AlOOH groups. This finding is consistent with the robust expansion of Treg observed in the spleen in the mechanistic (Fig. 3M) and therapeutic models (Fig. 4L). Cytokine analysis of splenocytes revealed a significant increase in IL-4 in the sensitized group, produced by Th2 cells, which promote IgE production and eosinophil activation, indicating a severe allergic response. Furthermore, compared with the sensitized group, mAr-C18mann prophylaxis reduced IL-4 and increased IL-10 and IFN-γ levels (Fig. 5M). mAr-C18mann prophylactic intervention also upregulated CD25+FoxP3+/CD4+ Treg in dLNs, decreasing IL-4 levels while increasing IL-10 and IFN-γ levels (Fig. S19). mAr-C18mann also increased the percentage of CD25+FoxP3+/CD4+ Tregs in the lung (Fig. S20), although this increase was not statistically significant. Collectively, compared with the sensitized group, mAr-C18mann prophylaxis enhanced expansion of functional Tregs (Fig. 5L), resulting in reduced allergic symptoms (Fig. 5B), alleviated lung inflammation (Fig. 5C–G), decreased CAPE-specific IgE (Fig. 5H), and corrected the IgG1/IgG2a ratio (Fig. 5I–K). These findings demonstrate that the mAr-C18mann platform treatment effectively prevents pollen allergy.

Discussion and conclusion

This study establishes a rational design strategy for mRNA-based allergen immunotherapy by integrating allergen engineering with immune-regulating delivery optimization. Through the coordinated regulation of antigen presentation and delivery-mediated immune modulation, mAr-C18mann established a tolerance-associated immune environment characterized by APC modulation and allergen-specific Treg induction, ultimately suppressing allergic responses in both therapeutic and prophylactic settings. These findings establish a structurally tuned mRNA-LNP platform as a modular strategy for directing immune responses toward systemic Treg-mediated tolerance in allergen immunotherapy.

Recent studies on mRNA-based allergen vaccines have highlighted the importance of sequence-level engineering to minimize intrinsic allergenicity while preserving antigen-specific immune regulation. In particular, removal of the native signal peptide has been explored as an effective approach to limit extracellular allergen release and reduce the potential risk of IgE-mediated cross-linking [39,40,51], consistent with recent house-dust-mite mRNA allergy vaccine designs that emphasize intracellular antigen retention to improve safety [52]. However, limiting extracellular secretion may also reduce access to conventional antigen presentation pathways. To overcome this limitation, we deleted the signal peptide and incorporated the Ii chain, previously explored in DNA vaccine systems for pollen allergens [53], into an mRNA-LNP platform to achieve controlled allergen presentation within an immunoregulatory context. With this antigen-engineering rationale, mAr-C18mann treatment also significantly reduced IgE levels, ameliorated allergic symptoms, and attenuated lung inflammation upon allergen rechallenge in pollen-allergic mice, and enhanced allergen-specific Treg activity in the spleen following allergen rechallenge (Fig. S15).

Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that epitope-based allergen vaccines provide an alternative to allergen immunotherapy by minimizing direct allergen exposure while modulating allergen-specific T-cell responses [[54], [55], [56], [57]]. Therefore, we generated the epitope-based mdT-C18mann construct to explore whether antigen minimization could be accommodated within the tolerogenic mRNA-LNP platform. Although mdT-C18mann retained partial anti-allergic activity, its weaker Treg-inducing capacity relative to mAr-C18mann suggests that antigen configuration is a key determinant of the magnitude of tolerance. Further understanding of how epitope composition and antigen configuration influence APC-mediated processing, MHC II presentation, and allergen-specific T-cell recognition will be essential for rationally designing minimized mRNA antigens that maintain robust tolerance induction.

The immunological outcome of mRNA vaccines is strongly influenced by the properties of the delivery formulation, which affect cellular uptake, antigen presentation, and downstream immune responses. In contrast to conventional mRNA-LNP systems developed for infectious diseases, which often rely on intrinsic immunostimulatory effects, tolerogenic applications require reduced baseline immune activation [58]. One parameter influencing this balance is the hydrophobic chain length of PEG-lipids [[59], [60], [61]]. Previous studies have shown that the rate of PEG-lipid desorption directly influences the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of lipid nanoparticles [59,60]. Moreover, recent progress highlights that the precise structure of PEGylation is crucial for understanding and improving mRNA delivery to the liver [61]. In this context, we evaluated LNP formulations with different PEG-lipid chain lengths and found that longer chains (e.g., C18) were associated with reduced nonspecific immune activation (Fig. 2). However, this property was accompanied by lower in vivo transfection efficiency, consistent with slower PEG-lipid dissociation [59,60]. To improve delivery while maintaining a low-inflammatory profile, mannose was incorporated into the C18-based formulation to enhance interactions with antigen-presenting cells via mannose-recognizing receptors (e.g., CD206). This modification improved in vivo mRNA expression while preserving a tolerogenic immune profile, including reduced activation of antigen-presenting cells and a cytokine environment associated with regulatory responses. However, because mannose decoration was introduced only after the C18 backbone was identified, the current screening strategy was not designed to fully separate the independent contributions of PEG-lipid architecture and mannose-mediated targeting. In vivo imaging at 24 h post-injection revealed exclusive hepatic accumulation of mRNA, with no signal detected in the spleen (Fig. S22). This precise hepatic accumulation established a local tolerogenic milieu characterized by the gene upregulation of FoxP3, IL-10, and TGF-β1. Consistent with previous studies demonstrating the role of hepatic accumulation in promoting systemic immune tolerance [28,62], this platform effectively reversed established allergic phenotypes in vivo, driving systemic Treg expansion and attenuating pulmonary inflammation (Fig. 4). This aligns with the recognized capacity of tolerogenic mRNA-LNPs to drive naive CD4+ T cell differentiation into Treg, thereby shaping robust systemic immune tolerance [30]. A hallmark of this precise immune redirection was partial rebalancing of Th2-associated humoral responses. Unlike traditional adjuvanted vaccines (such as rAr-AlOOH), which boost IgE and IgG1 above sensitized baselines, the mAr-C18mann platform uniquely avoided this hypersensitivity spike.

Admittedly, several aspects of this platform warrant further investigation. Although the stepwise optimization strategy successfully identified mAr-C18mann as a lead tolerogenic formulation and previous studies support the role of mannose-mediated targeting in peanut allergen immunotherapy, the precise relationship between the low-inflammatory C18-based carrier architecture and mannose-mediated enhancement of antigen delivery in establishing robust tolerance remains to be further elucidated in the current Artemisia pollen allergy model. Furthermore, while our findings establish an association between hepatic mRNA expression, tolerogenic APC modulation, and systemic Treg expansion, the causal relationship among hepatic immune regulation and peripheral tolerance induction, as well as the functional suppressive capacity of expanded Tregs, requires further validation through approaches such as lineage tracing and functional suppression assays. Furthermore, advancing translational efforts will involve assessing the long-term stability of the induced tolerance and the safety of repeated doses. It will also include optimizing storage stability through improvements in formulation, buffer components, or cryoprotectant use.

In conclusion, this study presents a strategy for next-generation allergen immunotherapy by integrating antigen design and delivery engineering. By combining an Ii chain-fused, non-secretory allergen mRNA with a mannose-modified LNP formulation, the system enables intracellular antigen presentation while reducing nonspecific immune activation. This approach promotes a tolerogenic immune environment associated with modulation of antigen-presenting cells and expansion of allergen-specific Treg responses, accompanied by a shift toward Th1-associated immunity. Compared with conventional adjuvanted formulations, the platform supports a more regulated immune response and alleviates allergic symptoms in vivo. The same delivery system retains functionality when applied to a simplified epitope-based construct, indicating its potential flexibility for antigen design. Collectively, this work provides an mRNA-LNP platform and a design framework for inducing antigen-specific immune tolerance, supporting the development of mRNA-based strategies for the prevention and treatment of allergic diseases.
