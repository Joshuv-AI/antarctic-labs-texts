ACE inhibitor–induced isolated intestinal angioedema mimicking functional bowel disorder: a case report
Authors: Sai Prasad, Jay Nagda, Aarushi Ahuja, Shaily Agrawal, Vidhi Parmar
Published: 2026

Background

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors remain among the most frequently prescribed antihypertensive agents worldwide and are first-line agents in current international hypertension guidelines [1, 2]. Angioedema is a recognised class effect of ACE inhibitors, occurring in approximately 0.1–2.2% of patients [3–6], with a relative risk substantially higher than that observed with angiotensin receptor blockers or other classes of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system blockade [7]. The literature is, however, dominated by cases involving the lips, tongue, face, or upper airway. Isolated intestinal (visceral) angioedema, in which the bowel wall is the sole site of involvement and the cutaneous and oropharyngeal structures are spared, is far less well recognised and is frequently misdiagnosed as acute gastroenteritis, irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, or mesenteric ischaemia [8, 9].

The pathophysiology is mediated by impaired bradykinin degradation, leading to local accumulation of bradykinin and other vasoactive peptides, increased vascular permeability, and submucosal oedema of the bowel wall [10–12]. Because the mechanism is bradykinin-mediated rather than histamine-mediated, the condition does not respond to antihistamines, corticosteroids, or adrenaline; the cornerstone of management is permanent withdrawal of the offending agent [13]. Latency from drug initiation to symptom onset varies widely, ranging from hours to several years, which substantially complicates recognition [14].

Most published cases of ACE inhibitor–induced visceral angioedema describe abrupt, severe abdominal pain over hours to a few days, often prompting evaluation for an acute surgical abdomen [9, 15]. In contrast, presentations that evolve more insidiously over several weeks—and that are clinically indistinguishable from a functional bowel disorder—are uncommon in the literature and represent an important diagnostic blind spot. We add to this small but clinically relevant subset by describing a case of subacute, isolated intestinal angioedema in which the diagnosis was supported by formal causality assessment using the Naranjo and WHO–UMC criteria [16, 17] and was confirmed by an unequivocal dechallenge response. This report has been prepared in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines [18], and a completed CARE checklist is provided as a supplementary file.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old Indian woman of South Asian ethnicity, a homemaker with no occupational exposures, was referred to our department for evaluation of recurrent abdominal symptoms. She had been diagnosed with essential hypertension five years earlier and had been managed on amlodipine 5 mg once daily as monotherapy throughout this period, with adequate blood pressure control. She had never previously been exposed to an ACE inhibitor or an angiotensin receptor blocker, and her antihypertensive regimen had not otherwise been altered during the five-year interval. Approximately three months prior to presentation, enalapril 10 mg once daily was added to her regimen for tighter blood pressure control. She had no personal or family history of angioedema, urticaria, atopy, or hereditary angioedema, and no history of food or drug allergy. She denied non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, oral contraceptive use, hormone replacement therapy, or use of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, neprilysin inhibitors, fibrinolytic agents, immunosuppressants, or herbal preparations. There was no history of inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, recent travel, sick contacts, dietary change, or alcohol or tobacco use.

Approximately six weeks after the introduction of enalapril, she developed recurrent episodes of diffuse colicky abdominal pain accompanied by intermittent loose stools, nausea, and occasional non-bilious vomiting. She also reported progressive abdominal bloating and a sensation of fullness. There was no fever, haematochezia, melena, weight loss, rash, joint pain, lip or tongue swelling, voice change, or breathing difficulty.

She was first managed at a primary care centre for presumed acute infective gastroenteritis and received a course of oral antibiotics together with probiotics, with no clinical response. Owing to the persistence of symptoms over several weeks, a working diagnosis of irritable bowel syndrome was made at a secondary-care facility. Dicyclomine 20 mg three times daily and dietary modification were prescribed, but the frequency and intensity of episodes continued to increase, prompting referral to our centre.

On examination at our facility (Day 1 of admission), she was conscious and oriented, in mild discomfort but not in acute distress. Vital signs were: blood pressure 132/84 mmHg, heart rate 86 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, temperature 36.9 °C, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Body mass index was 24.6 kg/m². There was no facial, peri-orbital, lip, or tongue swelling, no voice change, no stridor, and no urticarial rash, ecchymosis, or pruritus on full-body cutaneous examination. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. The abdomen was soft and mildly distended, with mild diffuse tenderness on deep palpation predominantly in the peri-umbilical region; there was no rebound, no guarding, no rigidity, and no palpable mass. Bowel sounds were normal, and there was no organomegaly. Digital rectal examination was unremarkable. Importantly, the absence of urticaria and pruritus, in the presence of a clinical syndrome consistent with mucosal/submucosal oedema, was an early clue toward a bradykinin-mediated rather than a histamine-mediated process [13, 19].

A focused review of her medication history revealed that her gastrointestinal symptoms had begun approximately six weeks after enalapril was first introduced and had then continued and progressively worsened over the subsequent six weeks before referral to our centre, prompting an ACE inhibitor–related aetiology to be considered.

Differential diagnosis

The principal differential diagnoses considered were irritable bowel syndrome, infectious gastroenteritis, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, hereditary or acquired angioedema, and chronic mesenteric ischaemia. Each was systematically excluded on the basis of clinical features, laboratory results, and the temporal relationship to enalapril (Table 1). The clinical diagnosis of ACE inhibitor–induced isolated intestinal angioedema rested on four converging strands of evidence: (i) an angioedema-type clinical syndrome in the absence of urticaria or pruritus, characteristic of bradykinin-mediated rather than histamine-mediated angioedema [13, 19]; (ii) a clear temporal association between enalapril initiation and symptom onset; (iii) systematic exclusion of alternative aetiologies; and (iv) complete and durable resolution of symptoms after drug withdrawal. To formalise the assessment of drug causality, both the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale [16] and the WHO–UMC causality criteria [17] were applied (Table 2). The Naranjo score was 7, corresponding to a “probable” association, and the WHO–UMC assessment was likewise “probable”.

Treatment

Enalapril was discontinued immediately upon clinical suspicion of a drug-induced aetiology. Supportive care during the acute symptomatic phase comprised intravenous crystalloid fluid administration (used to correct mild dehydration following several days of poor oral intake rather than for haemodynamic instability) and a brief 24-hour period of bowel rest, followed by re-introduction of oral feeds as tolerated. Dicyclomine, which had been prescribed earlier for presumed irritable bowel syndrome, was discontinued. A short course of ondansetron 4 mg orally twice daily was administered for symptomatic relief of nausea for a total of three days. No antihistamines, corticosteroids, adrenaline, fresh frozen plasma, C1-inhibitor concentrate, or icatibant were administered, in keeping with the bradykinin-mediated pathophysiology and the rapid clinical improvement observed after dechallenge; although icatibant has been investigated as a targeted bradykinin B2-receptor antagonist for ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema, randomised trial evidence has been inconsistent, the drug is not validated for the visceral phenotype, and it was not indicated in our patient given prompt symptomatic recovery after drug withdrawal [20, 21].

The selection of an alternative antihypertensive agent was made jointly with the patient after a detailed risk discussion. Because amlodipine alone had not been sufficient for blood pressure control, an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) was considered. We acknowledge that some experts advocate a wash-out interval of at least four weeks before introducing an ARB after an episode of ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema, given reports of cross-reactivity in approximately 2–10% of patients [22, 23]. In our patient, the wash-out interval was five days, dictated by sustained elevations in office blood pressure on amlodipine alone and by the patient’s preference to avoid prolonged uncontrolled hypertension. During this wash-out period, daily office blood pressure measurements ranged from 152/96 mmHg to 168/102 mmHg, with a mean of 159/98 mmHg, despite continued amlodipine 5 mg once daily. Alternative bridging strategies were considered and ruled out as discussed in the Discussion below. Losartan 50 mg once daily was initiated under close clinical observation, with explicit counselling about angioedema warning symptoms and a low threshold for discontinuation.

Outcome and follow-up

At two-week, one-month, and three-month follow-up visits, the patient remained completely asymptomatic. Blood pressure was well controlled on amlodipine 5 mg plus losartan 50 mg once daily. Repeat laboratory investigations at one and three months were unremarkable (Table 1). No recurrence of gastrointestinal symptoms, cutaneous, or oropharyngeal angioedema was observed throughout follow-up. The patient was counselled that ACE inhibitors are now contraindicated for her, and this has been added prominently to her medical record.

Patient perspective

The patient reflected that she had spent several months consulting multiple clinicians and receiving repeated courses of antibiotics, probiotics, and antispasmodic medication without any benefit, and that the persistence and progressive worsening of her symptoms had been deeply distressing both physically and emotionally. She expressed considerable relief that an underlying cause had ultimately been identified and that her symptoms had resolved promptly after enalapril was withdrawn, and she has subsequently emphasised the importance of a complete and up-to-date medication history with each clinician she has consulted. She encouraged that other patients with unexplained, treatment-refractory abdominal symptoms have their full medication list re-examined before further empirical treatment is prescribed.

Discussion

This case illustrates two clinically important points: ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema can present with isolated intestinal involvement, sparing the cutaneous and oropharyngeal sites that ordinarily prompt the diagnosis; and such presentations can evolve subacutely over weeks, mimicking a functional bowel disorder rather than the dramatic surgical-abdomen picture more familiar to clinicians. The combination of these two features creates a substantial diagnostic blind spot.

The pathophysiology is well established. ACE inhibitors block the principal pathway for bradykinin degradation, allowing local accumulation of bradykinin and other vasoactive peptides at vascular endothelium and leading to increased vascular permeability and submucosal oedema [10–12, 24]. Genetic polymorphisms in genes encoding aminopeptidase P (XPNPEP2) and bradykinin receptors have been proposed as susceptibility factors and may explain why only a small minority of exposed patients develop angioedema [25, 26]. Concurrent use of agents that further impair bradykinin metabolism—dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, neprilysin inhibitors, immunosuppressants such as mTOR inhibitors, and tissue plasminogen activator—has been reported to increase risk; our patient was not on any such agents [11].

There are recognised demographic predilections for ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema. Patients of African or African-Caribbean ancestry have a two- to fourfold higher reported incidence than patients of European ancestry, attributable at least in part to ancestry-specific variation in genes encoding aminopeptidase P and other components of bradykinin metabolism [7, 25, 26]. Female sex and older age have also been associated with a modestly increased risk in several large pharmacovigilance cohorts, although the magnitude of this effect is less consistent across studies [3, 7]. South Asian ethnicity, as in our patient, has been comparatively under-represented in the published cohorts, and the absolute risk in this group is not well characterised; the present case adds to the limited descriptive literature available for South Asian patients.

Reported latency from ACE inhibitor initiation to symptom onset varies enormously, from hours to several years [14]. Our patient developed symptoms approximately six weeks after starting enalapril, consistent with a subacute pattern that has been described but is less commonly emphasised in the published literature [9]. The slowly progressive nature of the symptoms in this case, together with the absence of any external angioedema, almost certainly contributed to the prolonged trajectory of misdiagnosis through gastroenteritis and irritable bowel syndrome.

A central diagnostic feature, and one we wish to underline, is that the diagnosis of ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema is fundamentally clinical: it rests on the presence of an angioedema-type syndrome in the absence of urticaria and pruritus, a temporal association with the drug, exclusion of alternative aetiologies, and resolution following dechallenge [13, 19]. The absence of urticaria distinguishes this syndrome from histamine-mediated mechanisms and is a discriminator that can be elicited at the bedside without laboratory support. We applied formal causality instruments—the Naranjo scale [16] and the WHO–UMC criteria [17]—to make this judgement transparent and reproducible (Table 2).

Cross-sectional imaging is helpful when available. Reported CT and ultrasound findings include segmental or diffuse small-bowel wall thickening, mucosal stratification with the so-called “stacked-coin” or “doughnut” appearance, mesenteric oedema, peri-hepatic free fluid, and ascites [27]. The absence of dedicated CT in our case is a methodological limitation that warrants explicit justification. In our patient, haemodynamics were stable, peritoneal signs were absent, inflammatory markers were normal, bedside ultrasound was unremarkable, and symptoms began to subside within 48 h of empirical enalapril withdrawal; contrast-enhanced CT was therefore not deemed likely to alter management. We accept that this is a pragmatic rather than an ideal approach. Where the clinical picture is less clear, where peritoneal signs are present, or where the differential includes surgical pathology, cross-sectional imaging should be obtained whenever feasible. We have made this point explicit to inform future clinical practice and reporting.

Selection of an alternative antihypertensive agent after ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema is a recognised clinical challenge. Although ARBs act downstream of ACE and do not directly affect bradykinin metabolism, cross-reactivity with respect to angioedema has been reported in approximately 2–10% of patients previously affected by ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema [22, 23]. Several authors recommend a conservative wash-out of at least four weeks before introducing an ARB, particularly in patients whose index event involved airway compromise. In our patient, the wash-out interval was five days, shorter than the conservative recommendation, and was driven by sustained office blood pressure elevations on amlodipine alone (range 152/96–168/102 mmHg, mean 159/98 mmHg) over the wash-out window.

We considered several alternative bridging strategies during this short wash-out interval. Up-titration of amlodipine to 10 mg once daily was discussed but offered limited additional blood pressure reduction in the short term and carried a dose-dependent risk of peripheral oedema; addition of a thiazide diuretic was considered but was judged to provide only modest reductions in office blood pressure over the timescale required and to introduce a risk of electrolyte disturbance; and a short-term beta-blocker bridge was felt to be sub-optimal in the absence of a compelling cardiac indication. After explicit risk discussion, the patient declined a prolonged wash-out and elected to proceed with losartan, having been fully counselled about the small but recognised risk of ARB cross-reactivity. The patient was instructed to discontinue losartan and seek emergency care for any new oral, peri-orbital, lingual, or respiratory symptoms. She remained asymptomatic on losartan over three months. We acknowledge that this trajectory cannot be generalised and that more conservative wash-out should be considered when clinical circumstances permit, particularly after an episode of airway involvement.

Several investigations would have strengthened this report. C1-inhibitor antigenic and functional assays, and serum tryptase, would have helped formally exclude C1-inhibitor deficiency and mast-cell–mediated processes; these assays were not available at our institution at the time of evaluation. Laboratory measurement of plasma bradykinin levels is technically demanding, is not routinely available in our setting, and remains a research tool rather than a routine clinical investigation [24]. A referral has been made to the regional clinical immunology service for outpatient C1-inhibitor antigenic and functional testing, and the patient has been advised to attend; the unequivocal dechallenge response and the normal serum C4 nevertheless make hereditary angioedema very unlikely as a competing diagnosis.

The principal teaching points are that a meticulous medication history is essential in any patient with unexplained, treatment-refractory gastrointestinal symptoms; that ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema can manifest with isolated intestinal involvement, even after weeks of previously well-tolerated therapy; that the clinical syndrome of angioedema without urticaria should prompt active consideration of a bradykinin-mediated mechanism; and that empirical drug withdrawal can serve as both a diagnostic and a therapeutic intervention.

Conclusions

ACE inhibitor–induced isolated intestinal angioedema is an under-recognised cause of unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms and may evolve subacutely over weeks rather than abruptly over hours, closely mimicking a functional bowel disorder. The diagnosis is fundamentally clinical and can be supported by the absence of urticaria or pruritus, by a temporal association with the offending agent, by systematic exclusion of alternative aetiologies, by formal causality instruments such as the Naranjo and WHO–UMC scales, and by an unequivocal dechallenge response. Cross-sectional imaging, although not always essential, should be obtained where feasible to support the diagnosis and to exclude surgical pathology. Once the diagnosis is established, ACE inhibitors are permanently contraindicated; ARBs can be a reasonable alternative in carefully selected and counselled patients, with a conservative wash-out interval whenever clinical circumstances permit.
