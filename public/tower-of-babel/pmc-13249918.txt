Projecting environmental improvements in mineral processing pathways: the case of cathode active material production
Authors: Sophia Roy, Ketan Vaidya, Jean-Philippe Harvey, Louis Fradette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRapidly eliminating carbon emissions to the atmosphere to stabilize the Earth’s temperature challenges the retrieval of critical minerals in a responsible way. This work introduces a methodology to consider technological switches in mineral processing pathways as they will come online in the future and supports the prospective analysis of their impacts. MethodsExamined technological switches span seven categories: 1) next-generation mineral processing pathways; 2) novel chemical production processes; 3) feedstock substitution for fuels and reductants; 4) reagent substitution; 5) circularity of industrial by-products; 6) valorization of tailings and other waste; and 7) improved water and emissions management practices. Process models of technological switches — sourced from process simulations and company data — are linked to our reconstructed granular inventories of mineral processing pathways. We illustrate our method with a case study on climate impacts, human carcinogenic toxicity and water consumption of three cathode active material (CAM) chemistries; nickel-manganese-cobalt in a 90%, 5% and 5% stoichiometric ratio (NMC955), lithium iron phosphate (LFP) and a nickel-rich (30%) sodium-ion variant (NMMT). Results and discussionBy projecting improvements in nickel sulfate and iron phosphate processing pathways, we demonstrate absolute reductions of 16-86% in climate impacts (GWP100), 43-99.8% in human carcinogenic toxicity (HTP-c) and 19-63% in water consumption (WCP) by 2060; where long adoption periods for emerging technologies shrink cumulative reductions to 6% / 4% / 9% in GWP100, 57% / 55% / 24% in HTP-c and 10% / 0% / 29% in WCP between 2025-2060 for NMC955 hydroxide, NMMT and LFP CAM respectively. Cumulative reductions stand much lower than predictions from the prevailing premise tool (REMIND SSP2-PkBudg1150) for climate impacts of NMC955 hydroxide (-39%) and NMMT (-27%). In fact, projected impacts in premise are systematically lower for both climate and human toxicity (carcinogenic) across all CAM chemistries between 2025-2040. ConclusionsOur proposed conceptual framework prevents over-optimistic modeling while retaining the required level of granularity in order to provide action-specific guidance to reduce environmental impacts of mineral processing pathways. We recommend its implementation within existing and emerging prospective tools and its application to a wider set of mineral-intensive ecosystems, such as the solar and wind industries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11367-026-02658-5.Keywords: Prospective life cycle assessment, Critical minerals, Batteries, Nickel sulfate, Iron phosphate, Climate change, Toxicity, Water consumption

Introduction

Critical minerals are at the heart of the projected development of an electrified society. Their extraction largely contributes (when renewable energy is sourced) to the environmental footprint of energy transition technologies such as batteries and solar panels. Despite their recognized contribution to the global impacts of energy transition technologies, the environmental impacts of extraction and processing pathways for critical metals remain understudied, where most prospective life cycle assessments (PLCAs) focus on projecting changes in the background electricity mix (Harpprecht et al. 2024).

Over 30 PLCA studies have utilized projections from integrated assessment models (IAMs) to reconciliate background databases, i.e. generate future life cycle inventories (LCIs) for electricity, fuels and raw materials (Nurdiawati et al. 2025). Notably, the premise tool facilitates the modification of the ecoinvent (v.3.5 to v.3.12) database according to quantitative outputs from IAMs, calculated for various plausible socio-economic scenarios with a 5-year resolution between 2020 and 2100 (Sacchi et al. 2022). Dependent on the described political ambition to tackle climate change, over 10 sectors of the ecoinvent database (Wernet et al. 2016) are modified, noticeably electricity and heat production, by presenting slow to accelerated decarbonization. As a result, the premise tool enables a streamlined comparison of the environmental assessment of an emerging technology under various levels of climate action, referred to as shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs). While multiple LCIs are added by the premise tool to model emerging technologies enabling decarbonization, such as the production of lithium and sodium-ion batteries, scenarios for the evolution of critical mineral processing pathways are lacking (Pehl et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2022). Life cycle modelling of critical inputs to batteries therefore relies on current ecoinvent datasets. A major hurdle arises from the data aggregation in ecoinvent market datasets for metals and chemicals, which were found to underestimate environmental impacts of current mineral processing pathways and chemical manufacturing processes, potentially misleading prospective assessments (Manjong et al. 2024; Oberschelp C, 2023; Peiseler et al. 2024; Roy et al. 2025; Schenker and Pfister 2025).

Prospective scenarios published so far focus on metal demand change, the share of secondary production, ore grade developments, and efficiency improvements in the foreground and background systems (Harpprecht et al. 2024). Emerging processing pathways for metals bear large uncertainties associated with their future scale up (Istrate et al. 2024) and market share (Harpprecht et al. 2021; Van der Voet et al. 2019), which supported the rationale to exclude them from prospective scenarios. Few frameworks (Lamers et al. 2023; van der Hulst et al. 2020) guide the assessment of emerging technologies in future systems, where fewer describe projections in mineral processing pathways (Gailani et al. 2024; Istrate et al. 2024). When examining existing PLCA frameworks applicable to mineral processing pathways (refer to Table 1), we find five studied emerging technology switches: next-generation mineral processing pathways, novel chemical production processes, feedstock substitution for fuels and reductants, reagent substitution and improved water and emission management.

We are missing a conceptual framework that explores, in addition to the five aforementioned technology switches, the valorization of tailings and other waste streams and the circularity of industrial by-products, bringing the count to seven categories. Since tailings and other waste streams rarely contribute to more than 1–9% of life-cycle GWP for battery metals (Istrate et al. 2024), they are often neglected from LCIs, potentially hiding important sources of heavy metal emissions to the environment (Doka 2018). While no framework currently guides the environmental assessment of industrial by-product recirculation within mineral processing pathways, punctual LCAs are starting to explore such alternatives (Lappalainen et al. 2025). Furthermore, broadening the scope of study to air and water emissions (not only CO2) and improved water management ensures to synchronously assess toxicity impacts and water consumption. Such additions are aligned with principles of circular hydrometallurgy (Binnemans and Jones 2023), which notably instruct to close water loops, safely dispose of potentially harmful elements, regenerate reagents, and combine circular hydrometallurgy with zero-waste mining.

How to create process models for emerging technology switches remains to be developed. While a well-established framework details how to conduct the PLCA of an energy technology up to a mature industrial phase — via process changes, pinch analysis, material recovery, size scaling, industrial learning and external developments (van der Hulst et al. 2020) — its ability to describe changes within mineral-intensive background systems is limited. The external development step of the framework suggests to link the foreground model with background projections from IAMs. As a result, say a new battery technology is assessed, projections of discrete improvements in the battery material processing pathways are excluded.

If we were to apply the aforementioned framework to a novel mineral processing pathway, other limitations occur. At a technology readiness level (TRL) higher than three, size scaling provides reductions in energy consumption and material intensity. While exponent scaling works for equipment or mechanical product manufacturing (van der Hulst et al. 2020), it cannot be applied to all chemical process-based flows, such as reaction energy demand and reagent consumption, which need to respect thermodynamic limits, reaction extent and kinetic limitations (Piccinno et al. 2016). For instance, while the stirring energy can be scaled proportionally to the reactor’s volume, the reactor’s overall heat and energy balance will also depend on the specific enthalpy of the reaction, the heat capacity of the solvent and the operating temperature (Piccinno et al. 2016). Moreover, when reaching TRL 9, applying industrial learning curves on the environmental impacts of all inventory flows (% reduction per year based on cumulative production doubling) represents an over-simplistic and non-physical based approach of environmental impact reduction. Whether or not learning rates can be applied to all environmental impacts remains to be debated (Lamers et al. 2023; van der Hulst et al. 2020). Recent research attempted to correlate economic learning curves (as an objective function) with environmental impact curves derived from process simulations of improved flowsheets for e-ammonia production (Fozer et al. 2025). Whether the correlation can be expanded to externalities (environmental impacts for which the cost is not completely captured, e.g. toxicity impacts of tailings) and to mineral processing pathways requires future work (van der Hulst et al. 2025).

Notwithstanding such future work, environmental improvements for the metallurgy sector need to be evaluated based on discrete changes in mineral processing and chemical manufacturing flowsheets. The use of process simulation tools allows to synchronously assess process feasibility and product quality while closing the mass and energy balances required for a granular LCA (Harvey et al. 2025). Lack of granularity was observed in the proposed framework by Gailani et al. (2024), where maximal emission savings of emerging technologies were only presented as a percentage reduction in comparison to the most CO2-intensive incumbent technology on the market. With process models not made available, this approach leads to a highly aggregated dataset lacking transparency, which prevents reproducibility and detailed contribution analyses.

Therefore, we propose a 7-category conceptual framework for the inclusion of emerging technology switches within process-based LCIs of mineral refining. These seven categories are judged sufficient for now, where unforeseen technologies may require to update our framework with additional categories in the future. While our framework strictly focuses on the analysis of potential improvements within mineral processing pathways, it can integrate seamlessly with other granular modelling approaches reliant on process-based LCIs, which could oversee other system-wide phenomena such as ore grade decline and increases in recycling rates. Our methodology describes how to construct process models that preserve granularity and enables analysis across all impact categories, but most notably across climate change (GWP100), human carcinogenic toxicity (HTP-c) and water consumption potential (WCP), essential impacts to track for metal production (Schenker et al. 2022). We also propose a timeframe for introducing emerging technologies according to their TRL, since guidelines are currently missing (Marson et al. 2025; Nurdiawati et al. 2025). We illustrate our framework with a case study on nickel sulfate and iron phosphate processing pathways for two lithium-ion and one sodium-ion chemistries.

Methods

The proposed method consists of four steps before conducting the PLCA of emerging technology switches within the material ecosystem: (1) reconstruction of process-based inventories, (2) identification of emerging technology switches, (3) creation of process models for emerging technology switches and (4) determination of an adoption period. Emphasis will be set on the third step, which is at the heart of the prospective assessment.

To illustrate our method, we conduct a case study on the prospective environmental impacts of three battery cell chemistries — nickel-manganese-cobalt in a 90%-5%-5% ratio (NMC955), lithium iron phosphate (LFP) and commercialized nickel-rich sodium-ion chemistry (NaNi0.3Mn0.5Mg0.05Ti0.05O2) hereby referred to as NMMT — and their corresponding cathode active material (CAM) production. Using the ScenarioLink plugin (v.0.1.6) in Activity Browser v.2.11.2 (Steubing et al. 2020), we source associated LCIs provided in premise (Sacchi et al. 2022) with ecoinvent v.3.9.1 as the background database, which is modified according to the REMIND IAM under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 2 (SSP2) PkBudg1150 scenario (premise generator v.2.2.3). The PkBug1150 scenario represents political will to cap remaining cumulative worldwide emissions to 1150 gigatonnes, for a 50% chance to limit the Earth’s temperature increase to 2 °C (Paris Agreement Objective) (Lamboll et al. 2023). The REMIND IAM was selected for results comparability with a previous case study on prospective climate impacts of lithium-ion batteries (Xu et al. 2022). For NMC955, we select the precursor (p-CAM) production step (NMC955 hydroxide) for our analysis to enhance granularity when assessing prospective improvements, and since it contributes to 77% of GWP100, 83% of HTP-c and 98% of WCP prospective impacts for NMC955 CAM (oxide) production in 2050 (GLO dataset, under the REMIND SSP2-PkBudg1150 scenario, refer to Supplementary Figure S3 and Table S6). Our modifications to the premise battery product systems are described in the following sections.

Reconstruction of process-based inventories

General framework description

The prospective evaluation procedure starts with a first-tier contribution analysis (which measures the direct contribution of the functional unit process and indirect contributions of intermediate flows consumed (van der Meide et al. 2025)) of the targeted energy technology process system to guide the selection of materials for which LCIs need to be reconstructed. We recommend reconstructing LCIs for materials accounting for at least 50% of non-energy first-tier contributors, under a cut-off of 5% (Zampori et al. 2016), to GWP100, HTP-c or WCP impacts as a priority. We have previously demonstrated a methodology for reconstruction via disaggregation of main processing routes —from extraction to refining at the desired product grade (Roy et al. 2025). This modelling approach reveals insights regarding key contributing processing steps and flows which cannot be retrieved via conventional aggregated datasets currently used in premise (Istrate et al. 2024).

Case study illustrations

We find that nickel is an overwhelming contributor (67/52% of GWP100, 79/66% of HTP-c and 98/92% of WCP) for both NMC955 hydroxide and NMMT CAM (refer to the first-tier contribution analysis with cut-off percentage set to 5% presented in Supplementary Note 1, Figure S1/Table S1). Therefore, we select nickel as the key material LCI to reconstruct and source process-differentiated datasets (Roy et al. 2025) for the two expected dominant production routes: (1) the high-pressure-acid-leaching (HPAL) of nickel laterite ores; (2) the rotary-kiln and electric furnace route for nickel pig iron (or ferronickel) production (RKEF-NPI) — both pathways concluding with refining to nickel sulfate hexahydrate (battery-grade). For LFP CAM, the phosphorous source (i.e. iron phosphate in the hydrothermal process and diammonium sulfate for the solid-state process) is a main contributor across GWP-100 (up to 50%), HTP-c (up to 56%) and WCP categories (up to 69%) (refer to first-tier contribution analyses in Supplementary Figures S1-S2, and Tables S1-S2). To assess alternative iron phosphate sourcing, we first reconstruct a pathway for lithium iron phosphate production from iron phosphate in China (Quan et al. 2022), a pathway unavailable in premise. We then construct a process model for iron phosphate production in China as a baseline, again sourcing data from Quan et al. (2022). Reconstructed processing pathways are summarized in Fig. 1.

Identifying emerging technology switches in the mineral supply chain

General framework description

For each mineral processing pathway considered in the reconstruction, key processing steps or flows are identified via a prospective first-tier contribution analysis (set to the year 2050, under the selected IAM scenario) under a cut-off percentage of 3%. Selected emerging technology switches target higher-ranked contributors to GWP100, HTP-c or WCP impacts until a preliminary cumulative potential of at least 50% is reached for each category (Zampori et al. 2016). Informed matches of emerging technology switches to contributors are established from a literature review and their formal reduction potential is quantified after process model development.

Case study illustrations

Following the prospective first-tier contribution analysis of the HPAL process under the REMIND SSP2-PkBudg1150 in 2050 (refer to Supplementary Table S3), we explore limestone substitution with quicklime (Nickel-Industries, 2023) to mitigate associated process emissions during iron and aluminium impurity removal (43% of GWP100). To avoid pushing CO2 emissions upstream, we study a low-carbon production method for quicklime (Laveglia et al. 2024). We also investigate a valorization pathway for HPAL tailings (Qiao et al. 2025), which are responsible for 99% of HTP-c impacts (refer to Supplementary Table S3). The valorization pathway produces iron phosphate which has the potential to mitigate climate, toxicity and water impacts for LFP CAM (refer to Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). We also explore plume capture at cooling towers for phosphoric acid production, since such evaporation at cooling towers is responsible for half of LFP CAM’s WCP. For RKEF-NPI (refer to Supplementary Table S4 for the first-tier contribution analysis) we explore a switch to hydrogen-plasma-based reduction (HPSR) (Manzoor et al. 2025) instead of reduction with hard coal, the latter being responsible for 76% of climate impacts and 46% of HTP-c. We also select tailings bioleaching (Di Maria et al. 2024) as a next-generation pathway to reduce toxicity impacts of primary extraction (the first-tier contribution analysis of tailings bioleaching can be found in Supplementary Table S5). Lastly, as the main source of waste from NMC955 hydroxide production, we explore the recirculation of sodium sulfate by-product as sodium hydroxide to be utilized in the same step (Burns and Brereton 2018). Development of process models for emerging technology switches

Process model creation is unequivocally the most challenging step in terms of data availability, due to the early stages of the assessed technology (Arvidsson et al. 2018). We describe our methodology for process model creation for each of the seven categories of emerging technology switches with case study illustrations.

Constructing process models for next-generation mineral processing pathways

General framework description

As next-generation mineral processing pathways are currently excluded from premise, we retrieve LCI data from a process simulation based on a pilot study which estimates mass and energy balances of a future industrial operation at thousands of tons of processed ore per annum. To complete the processing pathway up to (battery)-grade material, we source inventory data from a process simulation of the most likely refining route for the intermediate and allocate impacts per kg of refined metal.

For next-generation processing pathways that are reprocessing tailings, allocation of impacts is particularly critical. Vast amounts of reagents and cement may be required to both extract metals and stabilize the inert fraction into sand and gravel (Di Maria et al. 2024). To allocate impacts, we adopt a mass-based allocation exclusively on metals, since the process is operated for their retrieval only; and sand and gravel are by-products that are not driving the economic incentive to operate the tailings reprocessing process. Presenting results per kg of metal also avoids changing allocation with fluctuating commodity price (Kozakiewicz et al. 2019).

Case study illustration

We model the recovery of nickel from the bioleaching of sulfidic tailings based on existing company-sourced process simulation data and LCA study (Di Maria et al. 2024), where the resulting LCI is accessible in our previous work (Roy et al. 2025). The bioleaching process co-produces nickel, zinc, copper and cobalt; therefore, impacts are allocated on a mass basis to all metals. To note that when comparing mass and economic allocation methods for nickel produced in this tailings bioleaching process, we obtain climate impacts converging to 38 kgCO2−eq/kg Ni (non-prospective climate impacts). The nickel-cobalt sulfide intermediate from bioleaching need to undergo further purification to reach battery-grade, which process model is based on an environmental permit for hydrometallurgical refining of similar commercial operations (Terrafame, 2021), with impacts allocated per kg of refined metal. This additional reference allows to estimate wastewater characterization and emissions to air, which are neglected by the bioleaching pilot study focusing on costing and CO2−eq emission estimates. We could not model directly the release of ammonium sulfate to air when refining nickel sulfate, since ammonium sulfate or ammonium to air (as proxy) are not present in the imported biosphere flows (biosphere3) within the Activity Browser, only sulfate to air is available. Therefore, we model the release of ammonium sulfate as an emission of particulate matter (PM) < 2.5 μm. Similarly, we model nickel refining dust as PM > 2.5 μm and < 10 μm.

Constructing process models for novel chemical production processes

General framework description

Since novel chemical production processes are only available for ammonia, bio- and synthetic fuels in premise (Paul-Scherrer-Institut, 2025), LCI data for emerging production routes of chemical reagents is sourced from industrial scale-up process simulations. While process simulations provide enhanced granularity in reporting energy consumption and CO2−eq impacts, they lack wastewater composition as well as a detailed accounting of all other emissions and waste streams. To identify potential gaps in the process simulation LCI, we measure the deviation in results for each impact category in comparison to the ecoinvent database’s incumbent LCI for this chemical. A positive deviation reveals that the emerging route is already more impact-intensive than the ecoinvent baseline, while a negative deviation may indicate that important contributing flows are missing. The identification of data gaps therefore provides a first data quality assessment of the emerging process model, outlining directions for future research.

Case study illustration

To simulate low-carbon quicklime production, the emerging direct separation reactor (DSR) route is modelled according to a published process-based inventory (Laveglia et al. 2024). Compared to the conventional route, DSR allows complete electrification of the kiln and purifies the process CO2 streams, facilitating direct integration with post-combustion carbon capture technologies (Laveglia et al. 2024). The inventory was designed to assess potential CO2 reductions in comparison to a process-based baseline, the parallel flow regenerative kiln (PFRK) pathway (Laveglia et al. 2024). Since this baseline is said to exhibit higher representativeness in terms of CO2 emissions in comparison to the European dataset in ecoinvent (Laveglia et al. 2024), we also updated our quicklime production baseline with the PFRK process model. While achieving improved representativeness in terms of CO2 emissions, the LCI lacks other emissions to water and air, which are included in the incumbent quicklime dataset in ecoinvent. For each impact category of the ReCiPe 2016 midpoint method, we measured the percentage of deviation of LCIA results using the PFRK or DSR inventory in comparison to the ecoinvent v.3.9.1 baseline to identify any critical gaps in the process simulation inventory (refer to Supplementary Note 2, Figures S6-S7).

Constructing process models for feedstock substitution for fuels and reductants

General framework description

We examine new process configurations that explore the use of new reductants, instead of simply replacing the reductant inventory flow in the incumbent process LCI. This strategy is in line with current trends in the metallurgy industry, especially for a switch to hydrogen reduction, where energy efficient pathways will often involve a redesign of the process flowsheet or a change in equipment (Harvey et al. 2022). During process LCI creation, energy efficiency measures introduced during redesign are therefore factored in, where the incumbent design’s energy consumption is used as a reference. For downstream refining of the metal intermediate, we assume incumbent processing pathways and allocate impacts per kg of metal.

Case study illustrations

We model the solution developed by Manzoor et al.(2025) to directly reduce laterite ores in an electric arc furnace with a hydrogen plasma environment (hereby referred to as hydrogen plasma smelting reduction, HPSR). The HPSR process substitutes the conventional 3-step RKEF route to produce ferronickel (drying, partial reduction in a rotary kiln (RK) and final reduction in the electric furnace (EF). We select this case study to highlight that switching from coal-based RKEF to hydrogen-based reduction may not be a simple drop-in solution and could require major flowsheet or reactor design changes. Specifically for hydrogen, its direct replacement in the conventional process is inhibited by its high cost and difficult transport and storage (Pintowantoro et al. 2025). To scale up the energy consumption of the process, we follow the technology developers estimates of 18% energy savings from HPSR (Manzoor et al. 2025) in comparison to the demand for RKEF of 467 GJ/t Ni, the latter estimated from an energy flow analysis (Liu et al. 2016).

Further purification of ferronickel (30% Ni) to nickel sulfate is required for use in batteries, which will be examined via two processing routes. First, we consider a pyrometallurgical route consisting of sulfidation to matte in a Peirce-Smith converter, followed by oxidative roasting, reductive roasting and ammoniacal precipitation with hydrogen (Abdelbaky et al. 2023). Second, we compare with a hydrometallurgical route, using a process simulation of nickel pig iron (2% Ni) conversion to nickel sulfate as a proxy (Dry et al. 2019), due to lack of available data for ferronickel. The proxy is most likely a very conservative estimate due to the much lower content of nickel in the ferronickel intermediate. We compare the environmental impacts of the HPSR process to the dominant RKEF-NPI technology, using our previously built process-based LCI for the incumbent technology (Roy et al. 2025).

Introducing reagent substitution in processing pathways

General framework description

Stoichiometry and chemical properties are utilized to substitute reagents. We focus on neutralization reactions since they induce consequential CO2 emissions in mineral processing pathways. The basicity factor (BF) is used as an indicator of the equivalent reagent demand (i.e. mass of new reagent mnew−reagent) to achieve the same neutralization power as the current reagent used (with mass mold−reagent, refer to Eq. 1) (Lewis, 1976). In the situation where no studies have been performed on the implications of the substitution within the existing processing pathway, we assume direct drop-in of the new reagent, which is a consequential assumption since introducing new reagents may impact metal product recovery (Dry et al. 2019) and cause problems in downstream refining (Tijsseling and Whattoff 2023). We accept this shortcut for reagent substitution while we refuse it for reductants, since limitations in both technical and economic performance have yet to be extensively documented for direct substitution of reagents, compared to reductants (Harvey et al. 2022; Istrate et al. 2024, 2026). To compensate for such assumption, we apply a longer technology adoption period, as described in the following section. Nevertheless, evaluating reagent substitution based on the approximation (best-case scenario) still allows to decide if such switch is worth pursuing in technology roadmaps.

Case study illustration

Limestone (CaCO3) is used to precipitate iron and aluminium impurities during high acid pressure leaching (HPAL) of laterite ores for nickel-manganese-cobalt recovery under hydroxide form (MHP) (Dry et al. 2019). Similarly, limestone is used for iron impurity precipitation following the bioleaching of sulfide ores for nickel-cobalt, copper and zinc recovery (Di Maria et al. 2024). Inherent to the neutralization reaction, every utilized tonne of limestone results in 0.44 tonne of direct CO2 emissions from the industrial plant. To mitigate such emissions, quicklime (CaO) has been proposed as a prospective replacement of limestone (Nickel-Industries, 2023). We evaluate the environmental implications of this replacement as CO2 will be pushed upstream of the value chain, during limestone calcination into quicklime. Therefore, we combine limestone replacement with low-carbon quicklime production in our best-case prospective scenario. To model the switch from limestone to quicklime, we scale the quantity of lime (mlime) according to the neutralization power of both reagents (refer to Eq. 2).

Introducing circularity of industrial by-products in processing pathways

General framework description

Since circularity of industrial by-products in mineral processing pathways is absent from premise scenarios, precise mass and energy balances of emerging circularity solutions are retrieved from company data at advanced technology development level, which ensures industrial process feasibility and product quality. At lower TRL, data could be sourced from research-based process simulations. The complexity of modelling circular processing pathways lies in crediting avoided impacts. Following the cut-off approach, end-of-life impacts of by-products are not associated with the energy technology’s product system (considered out of boundaries) (ecoinvent, 2024). Treating such by-products for re-use within the product system will inevitably add energy and material flows and may not necessarily improve all midpoint indicators such as the product’s carbon footprint. However, from a responsible corporate citizen perspective, companies are held accountable for the fate of their by-products and should aim at minimizing their impact on the environment (Noti et al. 2020). To evaluate the impact of recirculating by-products within the product system, we (1) credit the avoidance of the most probable end-of-life alternative for the by-product and (2) replace the relevant reference material flow(s) in the product system with the resultant of the by-product treatment process.

Case study illustration

Sodium sulfate is the main by-product of NMC precursor (pCAM) production — that is sulfuric acid dissolution of metal sulfates (NiSO4, MnSO4, CoSO4) and preparation of metal hydroxides (NiMnCo(OH)2) (Chordia et al. 2021) — and of hydrometallurgical recycling of black mass (during LiOH precipitation). Both chemical processes rely on sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to achieve hydroxide formation, a reagent which contributes to 13% of the embodied carbon footprint of pCAM production (Chordia et al. 2021). Electrochemical salt-splitting was designed as a circular solution to treat the sodium sulfate effluent on-site, avoiding both transport and disposal emissions, and allowing re-circulation of NaOH into battery manufacturing and recycling operations (Liu et al. 2023). Oxygen, hydrogen and weak sulfuric acid by-products are also generated during the electrolysis step. We allocate all environmental burden to sodium hydroxide since it is the only product reaching battery-grade. Nevertheless, sulfuric acid could theoretically be used for mineral leaching, at a concentration of 5 wt%.

A preliminary mass and energy balance was provided by NESI, a Canadian company developing commercial electrolysers for the battery industry (NESI, 2025). The required fraction (i.e. item) of fuel stack (ffuel−stack) per tonne of NaOH produced was estimated based on the energy requirement of the process, using the proxy fuel cell stack dataset available in the REMIND premise database (Sacchi et al. 2022), which has a power output of 1 kW and a lifetime of 20,000 h (refer to Eq. 3).

Constructing process models for valorization of tailings and other waste

General framework description

We first create an LCI model for the valorization process based on available industrial-scale simulations informed by pilot studies, to ensure industrial operability. Next, two tailings (or waste) impoundment models are created: one before treatment and a second for the remaining tailings after valorization, to assess changes in toxicity impacts and remaining effects on groundwater contamination. Furthermore, a replacement of the relevant reference material flow(s) produced from the tailings valorization process is assessed by considering the deviation in environmental impacts observed in comparison to the incumbent production method. The same methodology can be expanded to other waste streams, considering different models for end-of-life (landfill configuration, incineration, etc.) (Doka 2018).

Case study illustration

Due to their high iron content and production intensiveness (~ 100 ttailings/tNi), HPAL tailings constitute an interesting feedstock for battery-grade iron phosphate production — a precursor of LFP CAM production. We build our process model from an industrial-scale simulation of the pilot process proposed by Qiao et al. which notably showcases a reduction in chromium content — the main driver of human carcinogenic toxicity of HPAL tailings, under hexavalent form (Roy et al. 2025) —from 0.47 wt% Cr to 0.09 wt% (Qiao et al. 2025). We use the HPAL tailings impoundment model developed in our previous work (Roy et al. 2025) to ensure that most toxic chemical elements (notably copper, lead, cadmium, chromium, arsenic) are included (Doka 2018) and modify composition of elements measured by Qiao et al. (Fe, Al, S, Si, Ti, Zn, Ni, Co, Mn, Ca, Cr, Mg and Na). The mass of tailings waste is reduced by half after valorization (Qiao et al. 2025).

In Qiao et al.’s proposed flowsheet, tailings are first leached with phosphoric acid, and iron powder is added to the mix to generate iron phosphate (FePO4) (2025). With no publicly available LCI for iron powder, we use iron sinter as a proxy (Rivera and Sutherland 2015), considering that iron powder impacts are likely to be underestimated (Chen et al. 2022). With the proposed flowsheet, nickel sulfate solution is also retrieved at a rate of 0.06 t/t of tailing treated. Due to the nickel sulfate solution likely undergoing further purification and crystallization before being introduced in NMC CAM, we attribute all process burdens to iron phosphate.

Introducing improved water and emissions management practices

General framework description

Since process emissions of mineral processing pathways are simply represented via elementary flows, we apply a reduction rate directly on the biosphere flow, from efficiencies achieved by emerging control technologies indexed in publications. When relevant, additional consumables and energy consumption from the operation of the capture equipment must be added to the LCI. We estimate the timeframe before reduction achievement from the TRL of the published emission control technology, as outlined in the next section.

Case study illustration

Multiple innovations exist to recuperate water vapour from cooling towers (Li and Flynn 2021), where we explore hydrophobic membrane condenser modules (Nurrohman et al., 2025). We apply the hydrophobic membrane condenser unit’s efficiency of 75% reduction of vapour flows to the atmosphere for both phosphoric acid production via the dihydrate process to fertilizer grade (70% H3PO4) and subsequent purification of phosphoric acid to industrial grade (85% H3PO4). We selected both processes since they are together responsible for half of the water consumption impacts of LFP CAM (refer to Supplementary Table S1). Furthermore, we consider that the reduction of emissions to groundwater is also covered by the updated HPAL tailings impoundment model after valorization.

Determination of a technology adoption period

General framework description

We derive technology adoption timeframes from a logarithmic-linear regression of TRL versus time periods (refer to Supplementary Figure S12 and Table S7), since technological maturation is often found to behave logarithmically under learning curves (Louwen and Lacerda 2020). Time periods were set conservatively according to key milestones in technological development (i.e. PhD thesis completed after 5 years, 15 years to reach proven full scale in industrial environment (TRL 7), and 10 other years for full commercial adoption), before being tuned to maximize the regression coefficient. Resulting time periods are listed in Table 2. A time period of 30 years to reach TRL 9 and substantial market deployment was selected to mimic the commercialization process of technologies within the metallurgical industry which often span decades (Van Rooyen et al. 2020). Under a 30-year horizon, time is also found to perform similarly to cumulative production as an independent variable to describe technological learning (Nagy et al. 2013).

We are aware that this representation of technology adoption is an oversimplification. Indeed, TRL-specific timeframes are not applicable as a one-size-fit-all proposition in all fields of engineering, as portrayed above. For instance, if estimations could be retrieved from the OECD intellectual property database for time between patent licensing (proxy for TRL 9) and filing for each sub-domain (e.g. for the metallurgical industry, chemical industry, etc.) (Dussaux et al. 2023), the slope of the logarithmic regression (a) could be adapted in function of the updated period to reach TRL 9 (refer to Eq. 4). Due to data unavailability, our particular framework is narrowed to one regression to ensure consistency when comparing multiple technology switches for the same product system.

Prospective assessment of the mineral processing pathways

Process models are implemented into the Activity Browser v.2.11.2 (Steubing et al. 2020) to make use of the superstructure approach for IAM-generated prospective scenarios (Steubing and de Koning 2021). Process models are constructed using a modified version of ecoinvent v.3.9.1, under REMIND’s SSP2-PkBudg1150 scenario, obtained via the ScenarioLink plug-in with premise generator v.2.2.3. The ReCiPe 2016 midpoint impact assessment method (hierarchist) is selected for its consideration of long-term emissions to groundwater in its toxicity assessment, which is crucial for tailings impoundments (Doka 2018). We do not use the more recent IMPACT WORLD+ v2 method because it sets all fate characterization factors for metals in the groundwater compartment to zero (IMPACTWORLD+, 2024). We analyze results for three impact categories: GWP100 (global warming potential over 100 years), human carcinogenic toxicity (HTP-c) and water consumption potential (WCP), which are deemed the most essential impacts to track for metal production (Schenker et al. 2022). We select a 35-year window (until 2060) after which the projection uncertainty is deemed too high (Sacchi et al. 2022), considering our maximal technology adoption period of 30 years.

Prospective life cycle impact assessment (PLCIA) results are plotted as discrete steps between every five or ten year timestep prescribed by the IAM from 2025 to 2060. As a new methodological approach, the cumulative deviation in environmental impacts from introducing one or multiple emerging technology switches is calculated, in opposition to only assessing the absolute impact value at a fixed year. The cumulative approach allows to factor in the effect of technology adoption periods on the resulting mitigation potential.

We calculate cumulative deviations using a left-hand rule Riemann Sum to compute the area (A) under the step function (refer to Eq. 5). Therefore, cumulative deviation values will vary according to their bounds, y0 and yf, which are set to 2025 and 2060 in the present study unless specified otherwise. The subinterval size (∆x) is set to 5 years, except between 2050 and 2060 where it is set to 10 years.

A positive deviation in comparison to the baseline area (Aref) corresponds to an increase in cumulative impacts, while a negative deviation corresponds to a reduction in impacts (refer to Eq. 6). Both improved (with emerging technology switches) and baseline scenarios are assessed under the same IAM outcome to provide a fair comparison under a decarbonizing electricity grid.

We modelled PLCIA results as a step function since disruptive technologies are introduced at timesteps spanning multiple years (following the technology adoption period presented in Table 2), whereas linear interpolation between timesteps would not capture this dynamic. Furthermore, IAMs determine the projected background electricity mix as an output of a multi-optimization with a 5 year resolution (Baumstark et al. 2021). All in all, selecting the step function is at worst underestimating the cumulative reduction potential, for a conservative estimate.

Considerations for the replacement of coal-based reduction with hydrogen

As a replacement to the incumbent coal-based (RKEF) reduction of nickel-rich laterite ores, our last emerging technology switch explores complete electrification via the hydrogen-plasma smelting route (HPSR). Considering both pyro- and hydro-metallurgical routes for the refinement of the ferronickel intermediate to nickel sulfate under the renewable grid mix of the SSP2-PkBudg1150 scenario, we obtain impressive GWP100 reductions of 84 and 86% respectively over the 2050–2060 period (refer to Fig. 5). When projecting both processing pathways in a coal-powered grid, which reflects the current situation in Indonesia with industrial parks organized around coal power plants; climate impacts raise to 129 and 159 kgCO2−eq/kg Ni in nickel sulfate hexahydrate respectively, in comparison to 142 kgCO2−eq/kg Ni for the conventional RKEF-NPI process (refer to orange, blue and grey dots on Fig. 5). In the present situation, we stress how IAM scenarios can present over-optimistic pictures of industrial decarbonization overlooking industrial park dynamics.

Sensitivity analysis on the technology adoption period

We accelerate technology adoption periods by 5 years to assess the effect on cumulative reduction potential computed for the 2025–2060 period (refer to Fig. 6). For GWP100, we find additional reductions of up to 3% for the accelerated scenario compared to the baseline, while additional reductions reach 14% for HTP-c. This difference is explained by the HPAL tailings valorization process having a shorter adoption period (15 years) in comparison to the switch from limestone to quicklime (25 years). Therefore, the higher the current TRL of an emerging technology, the more sensitive its cumulative reduction potential is to our 30-year span regression model. Since the switch to tailings bioleaching necessitates more water than the HPAL process, accelerating its adoption period is all the more detrimental to WCP, as illustrated by peach-coloured bars directed toward the negative side of the x-axis on Fig. 6.

The discrepancy is probably due to our sourcing of the global electricity mix from the IAM for battery manufacturing in comparison to the Chinese grid. Our results notably differ for LFP, at around 7 gCO2−eq/kWh delivered over the battery’s lifetime versus 4.5 for Zhang et al. (2024). Learnings from the methods and limitations

While our proposed framework provides enhanced granularity in potential improvements of mineral processing pathways, constructed process models for emerging technology switches exhibit many limitations and uncertainties (related to their future scale-up) which may alter our cumulative deviation potential results (also addressed in Supplementary Note 3). Moreover, we demonstrate how PLCA results for emerging technologies are only valid under specific (often optimistic) circumstances.

For instance, only if renewable electricity infrastructure increases as planned can the HPSR process meet its full reduction potential. To convert a projected 1.3 million-tonne production of RKEF-NPI nickel in 2025 in Indonesia, we estimate that 171 TWh are required to complete the HPSR processing pathway to nickel sulfate. Assuming an operating factor of 90%, the power demand equals to 22 GW or 31% of the power production in Indonesia in 2020. Forecasting in 2060, the same power demand only occupies 4% of the targeted Indonesian renewable power production, totaling 587 GW (Kawai, 2024).

The introduction of emerging technology switches in mineral processing pathways also occurs under highly optimistic circumstances. For instance, the direct drop-in solution is only valid if operating parameters for reagent substitution are optimized to ensure economically-viable metal recovery, which process adaptations would have to be reflected in the LCI. Therefore, reagent substitution solutions proposed within our framework should only be integrated in PLCA tools after comprehensive studies have confirmed satisfying metal recovery (Istrate et al. 2024). While economics were not a central point of study in the present manuscript, they play a central role in the adoption of emerging technologies in comparison to the incumbent. More specifically, the bioleaching of sulfidic tailings is only economically favorable under high cobalt recovery (Di Maria et al. 2024), since externalities related to toxicity impacts of tailings are not captured in the present economic system. Economic-based technology adoption periods would be an interesting direction for future research.

We also did not model expected ore grade decline, or the introduction of recycled feed in the CAM production systems, which would provide a more holistic modelling of mineral supply chains. Our proposed framework could integrate seamlessly with such granular modelling approaches reliant on process-based LCIs. For instance, electrochemical splitting of sodium sulfate could be applied to the waste stream from hydrometallurgical recycling of NMC batteries, where sodium hydroxide accounts for 98% (Ali et al. 2024) of the GWP during LiOH precipitation.

While improved processing pathways were always compared to a baseline under the same IAM scenario, the choice of IAM could influence resulting climate impacts (Šimaitis et al. 2025); where future research should compare cumulative reduction potential under multiple IAM scenarios. Another important note on cumulative reduction potential is that its value changes drastically depending on the integral bounds (i.e. start and end year of study), which will always need to be communicated for replicability and comparability. Lastly, while high uncertainty is associated with our regression-based technology adoption periods, we adopted a conservative approach for TRL progression (30 year adoption period from TRL1-9), which could be refined with meta-studies of patent licensing for a plethora of technology domains, hopefully increasing cumulative reduction results.

Since we aimed at quantifying differences from prospective results obtained with premise databases, we used available inventories for CAM production directly linked to markets for battery capacity. For sodium-ion battery modules, cells are expected to be sourced from the NMMT chemistry. However, nickel could be completely phased out of NMMT chemistries by 2040, analogous to nickel reduction in NMC chemistries (Yao et al. 2025). Since nickel-free NMMT chemistries or Prussian White analogs still exhibit lower performances than the prevailing NMMT chemistry, some scenarios maintain a static 33% nickel stoichiometry for 2040 onwards (Yao et al. 2025), as it is the case in this study.

Conclusion

We propose a methodology to consider seven categories of emerging technological switches in mineral processing pathways which are currently excluded from prospective databases. Our framework supports their evaluation by building LCIs from process-based datasets and determining an adoption period set from their current TRL; preventing over-optimistic modeling while retaining the required level of granularity in order to support the prospective evaluation of environmental impacts. We also develop a new indicator, cumulative deviation, to account for the adoption period of emerging technologies in the overall assessment of their impact reduction potential.

Overall, we found that the application of emerging technologies switches to process-based LCIs results in two to seven times lower cumulative reduction potential than what is predicted solely using premise inventories for battery materials. This result implies current underestimation of climate and toxicity impacts when using market inventories, due to misrepresentation of process emissions and aggregation of energy flows into electricity inputs which are swiftly decarbonized. For water consumption, however, future impacts of nickel sulfate hexahydrate production could be much lower than results expected with premise, due to potential overestimation of evaporation at hydro-reservoirs.

While the current version of this framework targets climate, toxicity and water consumption impacts, which are recognized as critical for the assessment of mineral processing operations (Schenker et al. 2022), we recommend its expansion to other impact categories. Particularly, emerging technology switches reducing land use impacts would be of high interest, where reprocessing of tailings can already contribute.

Evaluating the mitigation potential of emerging technologies early in the development process can inform industry actors on where to orient their investments next, in order to close the gap between currently identified actions and a more ambitious sustainability target (absolute or not). Ultimately, if the addition of many more emerging technology switches still results in plateauing cumulative reductions, far from targets, the social acceptability of such mineral processing pathways should be reevaluated.
