Going with the flow to solve for symmetry-driven PDE dynamics with physics-informed neural networks
Authors: Michail E. Kavousanakis, Gianluca Fabiani, Anastasia S. Georgiou, Constantinos Siettos, Panayotis G. Kevrekidis, Ioannis G. Kevrekidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We address a computational framework that integrates symmetry reduction into Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) for analysing symmetry-driven dynamics in nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs). Using an auxiliary network to learn time-dependent transformations, we render the symmetry-invariant solutions stationary or slowly varying in rescaled coordinates while simultaneously inferring the symmetry parameters (e.g., wave speed, scaling rates). This yields a modified evolution equation coupled with algebraic constraints on symmetry parameters, producing index-2 differential-algebraic equation (DAE) systems. Since conventional standard PDE/ODE solvers struggle or even fail with such high-index DAEs, we employ PINNs as an alternative approach that naturally unifies PDE residuals and algebraic constraints in a single loss function. This allows simultaneous inference of the invariant solutions and the transformation properties without large domains, mesh adaptivity, or front tracking. Beyond forward simulation, our framework further enables robust parameter inference from sparse, spatially offset data where vanilla PINNs fail. Our numerical demonstrations include, among others, the 2D porous medium, the generalised Korteweg-de Vries and Burgers PDE, showcasing our proposed approach as a powerful tool for the solution of both the forward and inverse problems for index-2 DAEs arising in nonlinear wave and scaling dynamics.

Introduction

Many nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) modeling complex spatio-temporal dynamics across physics, biology, and engineering exhibit solutions characterized by symmetry-driven behavior, such as traveling waves, self-similar collapse, finite-time blow-up, rotating patterns, or radial expansions1–13. These behaviors often reflect underlying continuous symmetries in the governing equations, such as translational, scaling, or rotational invariance. Exploiting these symmetries can simplify the analysis and reduce the complexity of the observed dynamics, but it typically requires reformulating the PDE by introducing additional terms—such as convective or scaling components—that account for the effects of translation, dilation, or rotation. These modifications effectively anchor the solution in a co-moving or rescaled frame, removing symmetry-induced drift or growth, but result in a transformed system that includes additional unknowns and algebraic constraints, often increasing the mathematical and numerical complexity of the problem6. For example, traveling wave problems can be reformulated in a moving frame with unknown speed, whereas self-similar problems may involve dynamically rescaled coordinates, often governed by parameters, such as the rate of change of the width or of the amplitude, that must be inferred as part of the solution.

To uniquely determine the transformed solution, one often supplements the reformulation with template-based conditions or additional ad hoc constraints. These may include pointwise anchoring (e.g., fixing a value or derivative at a reference location, such as an inflection point), orthogonality conditions, or minimization of integral functionals measuring the distance between the evolving solution and a prescribed template function. Such constraints facilitate isolating the intrinsic dynamics from symmetry-related modes and guide the solution toward a unique identification in the transformed frame. When applied systematically, these approaches typically yield systems of differential-algebraic equations (DAEs), where the algebraic part enforces normalization, symmetry breaking, or projection conditions tailored to the specific traveling or scaling behavior of interest6.

High-index (≥2) DAEs pose severe challenges for traditional numerical solvers14–17. Such systems are frequently stiff, sensitive to initial condition consistency, and require specialized integration techniques. These difficulties are compounded in the presence of multiple time scales, conservation laws, or quasi-steady-state approximations, where standard time-stepping methods may fail to capture the correct dynamics or preserve invariants. Additionally, when dealing with singularities or long-range traveling structures, classical PDE solvers require either large computational domains or adaptive mesh refinement—both of which increase computational cost and can lead to numerical instabilities.

Recent advances in scientific machine learning have opened new pathways for solving forward18–22 and inverse problems23–31 of nonlinear PDEs and index-1 DAEs32. Early works in the 1990s already explored the use of neural networks for approximating solutions33–36 and identifying system dynamics37–42, setting the foundations for modern physics-informed approaches. Building on these ideas, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)43–45 have emerged as a promising framework, where the governing equations, along with initial, boundary, and possibly algebraic constraints, are embedded directly into the training of neural networks via a loss function that can incorporate sparse measurement data. This approach allows for the solution of both classical initial/boundary value problems and more complex scenarios involving data-driven calibration and/or discovery of unknown parameters and hidden dynamics, with applications across fields, such as fluid mechanics and materials science46–51. The framework has further been extended to more general nonlinear functional equations arising in dynamical systems, including feedback linearization52, observer design53, identification of slow-invariant manifolds in multiscale systems54,55 and learning normal form coordinate transformations56. Such efforts have extended past continuum problems to nonlinear dynamical lattice ones57,58.

PINNs can be readily applied to DAEs by incorporating both differential operators and algebraic constraints directly into the loss function. This approach avoids the need for index reduction procedures, specialized DAE solvers, or dedicated time-stepping schemes59,60. Constraints arising from template conditions, normalization requirements, or even integral identities can be enforced seamlessly as additional (soft) penalty terms in the training objective. This flexibility can be particularly useful in capturing self-similar and solitonic behaviors, where traditional numerical solvers often face difficulties due to mesh adaptivity or domain deformation requirements. Recent applications include PINN-based modeling of spontaneous imbibition and self-similar flows61, the identification of asymptotically self-similar solutions of axisymmetric Euler flows62, the study of optical solitons with conservation constraints63, and nonlinear wave equations with adaptive training strategies64.

In this work, we propose a computational framework that integrates symmetry reduction into the PINN formulation for the solution of both the forward and inverse problems for index-2 DAEs. Such approach is specifically tailored to solving PDEs that exhibit traveling waves, self-similarity, and collapse/blow-up behavior through transformation into an index-2 DAEs system. The methodology builds on the template-based representations7,8,65,66 to reduce the system while retaining the essential dynamics. Although the individual components (PINNs and symmetry-reduction frameworks) exist, their integration into a unified framework for directly solving index-2 DAE systems without index reduction or specialized solvers constitutes the main contribution of the present work. The PINN architecture jointly learns the rescaled solution and the evolution of group parameters (front velocities, scaling rates). Concretely, the architecture trains two interconnected networks simultaneously: (i) a network admitting space and time as inputs to represent the rescaled solution, and (ii) an auxiliary network that takes time alone to learn time-dependent transformations, that renders the symmetry-invariant solutions stationary or slowly varying in rescaled coordinates. We demonstrate that this approach avoids the need for large computational domains or adaptive meshing by working directly in the transformed coordinates, solving the full coupled high-index DAE system using the PINN loss formulation.

Beyond the solution of the forward problem, the proposed framework naturally extends to the solution of inverse problems. By embedding sparse observational data into the loss function, our proposed PINN framework can simultaneously learn the reduced solution, the symmetry parameters, and unknown physical coefficients. In this way, the dynamically reconstructed co-moving frame aligns the front with the sensor, enabling accurate calibration even with delayed, spatially offset measurements. Moreover, the framework is further extended to a steady-state symmetry-reduced formulation that directly computes self-similar blow-up solutions by treating the blow-up rate as a trainable parameter. This enables, for example, the study of singularity-forming PDE dynamics through their associated self-similar stationary structures.

We validate our framework across several representative examples: (i) the Nagumo PDE, which exhibits traveling fronts; (ii) the linear diffusion PDE in two dimensions with self-similar decay; (iii) the 2D porous medium equation exhibiting self-similarity and nonlinear spreading; (iv) the Burgers PDE, combining both traveling and scaling features; and (v) the generalized Korteweg-de Vries (gKdV) PDE with self-similar blow-up solutions. Additionally, the 1D diffusion and the axisymmetric 2D porous medium PDE benchmarks are presented in the Supplementary Material (Sections 1 and 2). These cases illustrate the generality of the approach and highlight the potential of PINNs to serve as a robust tool for analysing symmetry-driven PDE dynamics that naturally lead to high-index DAE formulations. Moreover, by transforming to a frame where the solution is nearly stationary, our framework reduces stiffness and simplifies the learning task, thereby improving PINN performance on problems that would otherwise require adaptive meshes or front tracking.

Results

In this section, we showcase the application of the PINN-assisted MN framework across five distinct case studies: one involving a traveling wave solution (Nagumo), three exhibiting self-similar behavior (the 2D diffusion, the 2D porous medium, and the generalized Korteweg-de Vries PDE), and one combining both scaling and translational invariance (Burgers PDE). For each case, we formulate an index-2 DAE system and demonstrate how the PINN-assisted methodology can be used to learn both the invariant (translational or self-similar) profile and the corresponding wave speed and/or self-similar exponents within a dynamically rescaled coordinate system. All simulations were performed in MATLAB on a laptop with an 11th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-111800H @2.30CPU GHz processor, 32 GB RAM, and Windows 11 Home operating system. The neural networks were implemented using dlnetwork and dlarray objects to enable automatic differentiation. The architecture of the neural networks follows a simple feedforward structure with hyperbolic tangent activations. Each model is defined using the functions featureInputLayer, fullyConnectedLayer, and tanhLayer. Training was performed using a quasi-Newton method (L-BFGS) via the lbfgsupdate routine, suitable for low-batch, PINN settings.

Remark

As a practical strategy to improve convergence, we introduced a warm-up phase prior to full training. Specifically, the network is first trained to approximate the initial condition alone, extended trivially over all times (i.e., enforcing that the initial condition remains constant across time). This preconditioning step facilitates the network in establishing a suitable parameter initialization before minimizing the full physics-informed loss, leading to more stable convergence during the subsequent optimization. No further training complexities (such as adaptive loss weighting, greedy training or domain decomposition) were needed, as the symmetry reduction already regularizes and reduces the stiffness of the PDE problem.

Discussion

In this work, we developed a PINN-based framework for the solution of both the forward and inverse problems for nonlinear PDEs with continuous symmetries—specifically translations and scalings—by reformulating them as high-index DAEs in dynamically rescaled coordinates. These index-2 DAEs simultaneously encode the evolution of the invariant profile and the symmetry parameters (such as wave speed or similarity exponents). In particular, our MN-PINN framework enforces both the PDE residuals and the symmetry-reduction-induced algebraic constraints through the loss function.

The method was tested on canonical problems, involving traveling waves and first- and second-kind self-similar behavior (the latter presented in Supplementary Material, Section 2). In all cases, the proposed framework was able to solve the resulting constrained systems, making this a promising approach for studying scaling and front-propagating phenomena. One of the key advantages of this symmetry-reduced approach is its ability to handle problems that are otherwise difficult to solve using traditional numerical schemes. Many self-similar and moving-front problems would require adaptive meshes or moving domains to maintain resolution near sharp features. In contrast, the dynamic rescaling used here maintains the structure of steep or concentrating solutions (e.g., solitons or traveling waves) at a constant scale in the transformed coordinates, reducing the need for mesh refinement; notice that this is a potential advantage in comparison to PINN methods that operate in the original frame within such problems58. This also tends to reduce stiffness and removes fast transients (we found that the reduced dynamics often converge to stationary or slowly varying states, facilitating training), potentially improving numerical stability. Additionally, our implementation bypasses the need to derive scaling conditions a priori, as well as the technical difficulty of large PINN residuals that can arise at under-constrained points when applying the method without pinning or template conditions62. In previous approaches, addressing this issue required introducing extra—often solution-specific—loss terms to enable identification of scaling exponents. In the present formulation, such considerations are systematically accounted for by the template-based MN formalism and are therefore absent.

Traditional solvers for PDEs (e.g., FD or FEM with method-of-lines) are typically computationally more efficient, but they struggle with high-index DAE formulations, requiring index reduction, projection methods or specialized time integrators that are not standard in environments like MATLAB and often demand advanced/specialized software that ensures consistent initialization and preserves algebraic and hidden constraints. On the other hand, PINNs offer a more flexible alternative: they unify differential and algebraic residual treatment within a single loss function, simplifying implementation when dealing with algebraic constraints and dynamic symmetries—not necessarily due to superior performance, but due to ease of use and adaptability. Moreover, the meshless and optimization-based nature of PINNs may provide advantages in higher-dimensional problems, where classical grid-based methods are strongly affected by the curse of dimensionality67. Under suitable assumptions, neural-network approximations can exhibit approximation rates that are substantially less sensitive to dimension, such as Barron-type O(1/N) rates, compared to standard polynomial approximation methods72.

The approach is not without drawbacks. PINNs are known to be sensitive to hyperparameters and network architecture, and the choice of optimizer plays a crucial role. To this extent, we made an effort to systematically assess the robustness of our PINN framework to different random initializations and pretraining strategies in Supplementary Material Section 4, confirming that for the presented case studies, the method reliably converges to the correct self-similar profiles and scaling exponents across varying conditions. We used a simple feedforward network with tanh activations; other architectures may perform better or worse depending on the specific problem, but choosing optimal configurations is not trivial. We also observed that standard gradient-based optimizers, such as ADAM, often fail to achieve sufficient accuracy, particularly when the loss includes algebraic constraints that must be satisfied to high precision. The L-BFGS optimizer, although more effective in this context, is expensive. Its memory and computational cost scales poorly with network size, limiting the method to small- and medium-sized networks in practice. To mitigate convergence issues, we employed warm-up strategies, first fitting initial and boundary conditions before enforcing the full physics and algebraic constraints. We also tested greedy, continuation-inspired training schemes52,53, where the solution is first learned on a small subdomain (e.g., close to initial conditions or pinning conditions) and then gradually extended. In the transformed coordinates, however, the dynamics were already sufficiently regular that such strategies offered no clear benefit - indicating that the symmetry reduction itself effectively regularizes the problem, removing the need for these additional training complexities. Additionally, in the present work, we have primarily focused on allowing the integrator to converge to a stable self-similar or traveling (or both) solution. However, as a proof of concept, we demonstrate the extension to stationary formulations, for the computation of self-similar blow-up solutions of the generalized KdV problem; a more comprehensive analysis of fixed-point approaches remains a topic of future work66,73. Looking ahead, more efficient alternatives, such as parsimonious Random Projection Neural Networks (RPNN), as in ref. 32—which were shown in ref. 32 to reach accuracies comparable to classical index-1 DAE solvers like ode15s and ode23t in MATLAB —might offer significant computational advantages, by employing randomized features and linear-algebraic solvers (e.g., SVD, COD) rather than gradient-based optimization. We plan to investigate RPNN-based PINNs with method-of-lines discretization to enable a more direct comparison with classical solvers while retaining the flexibility of the PINN framework. Moreover, it would be of particular interest to explore models with more complex dynamics and potential bifurcations of self-similar solutions, such as the nonlinear Schrödinger13,74. In summary, this method provides a flexible approach to high-index DAEs arising from symmetry-driven PDEs—a class of problems where standard techniques struggle—opening a path toward structure-aware neural PDE solvers. However, systematic exploration of network architectures, hyperparameters, and optimization strategies remains necessary to identify best practices for the models of interest.

Related works: the method of slices, the freezing method, and MN-dynamics. Several techniques have been introduced to eliminate continuous symmetries, such as translations and scalings, in the dynamics of PDEs. One classical approach is the “method of slices,” introduced in ref. 7. This method defines a slice hyperplane that intersects the group orbit transversely, allowing the full system trajectory to be projected onto a reduced state space where the symmetry is factored out. The evolution in this reduced space is then coupled to reconstruction equations for the symmetry parameters. A related construction is the “MN-dynamics framework” in ref. 6, where self-similar solutions are studied by introducing moving and rescaled coordinates. The authors derive equations for both the evolving profile and the parameters (e.g., speed, scale) by imposing additional constraints-often involving moment conditions or orthogonality—that regularize the dynamics. Although similar in objective to the template-based method, MN-dynamics differs in the form of these constraints and in its treatment of the modulated profile. Such renormalization methods, already well-established in the dispersive wave literature, e.g., through the dynamic rescaling method and variants thereof13,74, have recently been used to extract normal forms for the bifurcation of collapsing solutions65,71, as well as to characterize the stability of the collapsing solutions (as stationary states) in the self-similar (so-called “co-exploding”) frame. The “freezing method,” proposed in ref. 75, adopts a similar idea by transforming the PDE into a comoving and rescaled frame, yielding a differential-algebraic system. Phase conditions are imposed to “freeze” the solution in the transformed coordinates, typically by requiring orthogonality to the group tangent directions or by minimizing a suitable functional.

Index-reduction. One can arbitrarily select or combine any of the strategies described at points (i), (ii), or (iii) to impose a number of algebraic constraints matching the unknown transformation parameters (e.g., A(τ), B(τ), c(τ)). All the algebraic constraints presented can close the transformed PDE system, in Eq. 47, but result in an index-2 DAE, since they involve only linear functionals of w (either at specific spatial points or over domains) and do not explicitly depend on the transformation parameters. Consequently, two derivatives with respect to τ are needed to obtain an explicit ODE system. A common index-reduction technique involves differentiating the constraint once with respect to τ, reducing the system to index-1. Let the constraint take the generic form Lcons(w) + Gcons = 0, where Lcons is a linear operator, and Gcons is independent of w and τ. The original index-2 formulation can be enforced directly through the loss function, which simultaneously penalizes both the PDE residual and the algebraic constraints. This flexibility allows the method to bypass the derivation of auxiliary evolution equations, which are typically required by conventional solvers.

Ultimately, in the context of symmetry-reduced formulations—such as co-moving frames for traveling waves or self-similar scaling frames-additional algebraic constraints may arise from the coordinate transformation. These include template conditions, normalization constraints, or integral identities used to fix the degrees of freedom introduced by symmetry. PINNs can naturally incorporate these terms as soft penalties into the loss function, enabling the consistent solution of mixed PDE-DAE systems arising in the evolution of rescaled or traveling profiles.
