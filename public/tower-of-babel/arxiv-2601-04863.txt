Convergence to stable laws for products of random matrices
Axel Péneau

Abstract
. Under reasonable algebraic assumptions and under an infinite second order moment assumption, we show that the logarithm of the norm (log-norm) of a product of random i.i.d. matrices with entries in ℝ \mathbb{R} or in any other local field satisfies a generalized Central Limit Theorem (GCLT) in the sense of Paul Lévi. The proof is based on a weak law of large number for the difference Δ n \Delta_{n} between the log-norm of the product of the first n n matrices and the sum of their log-norms. This weak law of large numbers morally says that Δ n \Delta_{n} behaves like a sum of i.i.d. random variables that have a finite moment of order 2 ​ q 2q as long as the log-norm of each matrices has a finite moment of order q q for a given q > 0 q>0 . This gain of moment is the central result of the present paper and is based on the construction of pivotal times. Moreover, these results admit a nice higher rank extension when one looks at the full Cartan projection instead of the log-norm.

Let us detail the framework behind the proof of Theorem

Let us first remind the statement of the classical GCLT for non-negative random variables.

We say that a sequence of

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}3C^{\prime}e^{-\beta^{\prime}k}\mathbb{P}\left(N(g_{0})>\frac{t-4C}{3k}\right)

For
t>8C
, we have
\frac{t-4C}{3k}\geq\frac{t}{6k}
and:

\forall t>8C,\;\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}3C^{\prime}e^{-\beta^{\prime}k}\mathbb{P}\left(N(g_{0})>\frac{t}{6k}\right)=\sum_{k\in 6\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}}3C^{\prime}e^{-\beta^{\prime}k/6}\mathbb{P}\left(N(g_{0})>\frac{t}{k}\right).

Hence, we have:

(44)

\mathbb{P}\left(|\Delta\kappa(\gamma_{i,j},\gamma_{j,k})|>t\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}3C^{\prime}e^{-\beta^{\prime}k/6}\mathbb{P}\left(N(g_{0})>\frac{t}{k}\right)

For

\sum_{k=\lceil 8CM/B\rceil}^{+\infty}3C^{\prime}e^{-\beta^{\prime}k/6}\mathbb{P}\left(N(g_{0})>\frac{t}{k}\right)\geq\frac{3C^{\prime}}{1-e^{-\beta^{\prime}/6}}\frac{e^{-\beta\lceil 8CM/B\rceil/6}}{M}

We can always assume that
C^{\prime}\geq M(1-e^{-\beta^{\prime}/6})e^{\beta\lceil 8CM/B\rceil/6}/3
, in that case, we have:

\forall t\leq 8C,\;\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}3C^{\prime}e^{-\beta^{\prime}k/6}\mathbb{P}\left(N(g_{0})>\frac{t}{k}\right)\geq 1\geq\mathbb{P}\left(|\Delta\kappa(\gamma_{i,j},\gamma_{j,k})|>t\right)

In conclusion, we have (
37

and
\beta^{\prime\prime}=\beta^{\prime}/6
.
∎

3.
Study of almost additive processes

A consequence of Theorem
1.3
is that the random process
(\kappa(\gamma_{m,n}))_{0\leq m\leq n}
is almost additive in a way that we define in the first paragraph of the present section.
To be able to use some of the result in further works, we study almost additive processes in an axiomatic way.
For the moment, it is not clear whether or not the formulation in terms of almost additive processes yields result that could not already be proven using classical ergodic theoretic tools.
We state results in full generality to avoid having to give different statements for
\kappa
and
\dot{\kappa}
and to be able to apply them in a broader context.

3.1.
Definition and motivation

In the present section, we call non-additive process in an Abelian group
V
a family
(S_{m,n})_{0\leq m<n}
of elements of
V
.
We assume
V
to be endowed with a second countable
\sigma
-algebra.
In the present section, we study non-additive processes in a second countable Hilbert space
V
, over
\mathbb{R}
endowed with a scalar product denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the associated norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Definition 3.1

(Mixing process)
.

Let
S=(S_{m,n})_{0\leq m<n}
be a random process.
We say that
S
is time-invariant if it has the same law as the time-shift of
S
, defined as
TS:=(S_{m+1,n+1})_{0\leq m<n}
.
We say that
S
has no memory if for all
0\leq m
, the process
T^{k}S:=(S_{m+j,m+k})_{0\leq j<k}
is independent of the joint data of
(S_{j,k})_{0\leq j<k\leq m}
.
We say that
S
is mixing if it has no memory and is invariant.

For now, we have not used the group structure on
V
to define the probabilistic notions of having no memory and being time invariant.
To be able to say anything meaningful on mixing processes, we assume the following moment condition.

Definition 3.2

(Almost-additivity)
.

Let
S=(S_{m,n})_{0\leq m<n}
be a random process in a Hilbert space
V
.
Given
q>0
and
C\geq 0
, we say that
S
is
C
-almost additive in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
if for all
l<m<n
, we have:

(45)

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|S_{l,n}-S_{l,m}-S_{m,n}\right\|^{q}\right)\leq C.

From now, let us denote by
\Delta S(l,m,n)
the quantity
S_{l,n}-S_{l,m}-S_{m,n}
.
We remind that given a (random or not) sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in V^{\mathbb{N}}
, and
0\leq m\leq n
, we write
x_{m,n}=\sum_{k=m}^{n-1}x_{k}
for the partial sum of
x
, that way
(x_{m,n})
is an additive process
i.e.
,
0
-almost additive.
To avoid confusion without overloading the notations, we use upper case letters for general processes and lower case letters for those that come from sequences of random variables.
Contrary to additive processes, the distribution law of an almost additive process
(S_{m,n})_{0\leq m\leq n}
can not be deduced from the distribution law of
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
or
(\overline{S}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.

Instead of making the strong lack of memory assumption of Definition
3.1
we could have made the weaker assumption that the law of
S
is ergodic for
T
(meaning that all
T
-invariant events have probability
0
or
1
).
In
[
Kin68
]
, Kingman proves a law of large numbers for ergodic processes on
\mathbb{R}
that are sub-additive
i.e.
, if
\mathbb{E}(S_{0,1})<+\infty)
and if
\Delta S(l,m,n)\leq 0
almost surely and for all
l<m<n
, then
S_{0,n}/n
converges almost surely to a non-random limit in
[-\infty,+\infty)
.

In the present work we make the mixing assumption, that is stronger than a simple ergodicity assumption but allows us to give more natural links between the probabilistic behaviour of
S_{0,n}
and the constants
C
and
q
of (
45
).

3.2.
Almost additivity of the Cartan projection

Let us now explain how Theorem
1.3
implies that the random family
(\Delta\kappa(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m,n}))_{0\leq m\leq n}
, when
\nu
is strongly irreducible and proximal or
(\Delta\dot{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m,n}))_{0\leq m\leq n}
, when
\nu
is totally irreducible is almost additive in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
as soon as soon as
N(\gamma_{0})^{q/2}
is integrable.

This follows from a simple argument of integration by parts.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
x
be a real non-negative random variable.
For all
q>0
, we have:

(46)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}qt^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(x>t)^{2}dt\leq 2\mathbb{E}(x^{q/2})^{2}.

Proof.

Note that for all
t
, we have
qt^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(x>t)^{2}=2(t^{q/2}\mathbb{P}(x>t))(\frac{q}{2}t^{q/2-1}\mathbb{P}(x>t))
.
Therefore, by Hölder’s inequality, we have:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}qt^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(x>t)^{2}dt\leq 2\sup_{t\geq 0}t^{q/2}\mathbb{P}(x>t)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q}{2}t^{q/2-1}\mathbb{P}(x>t)dt.

By Markov’s inequality applied to
x^{q/2}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(x>t)\leq\mathbb{E}(x^{q/2})/t^{q/2}
for all
t
, therefore,
\sup_{t\geq 0}t^{q/2}\mathbb{P}(x>t)\leq\mathbb{E}(x^{q/2})
.
Moreover, by integration by parts, we have
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q}{2}t^{q/2-1}\mathbb{P}(x>t)dt=\mathbb{E}(x^{q/2})
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\nu
be a strongly irreducible and proximal probability distribution over
\mathrm{GL}(E)
and let
(\gamma_{n})\sim\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
q>0
and assume that
\mathbb{E}(N(\gamma_{0})^{q/2})<+\infty
.
Let
C,\beta>0
be as in Theorem
1.3
and let
C^{\prime}:=2\mathbb{E}(N(\gamma_{0})^{q/2})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}Ce^{-\beta k}k^{q}
.
Then the processes
(\kappa(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m,n}))_{0\leq m\leq n}
and
(\Delta\kappa(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m,n}))_{0\leq m\leq n}
are
C^{\prime}
-almost additive in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
.

Proof.

We want to show that for all
0\leq l<m<n
, we have
\mathbb{E}(|\Delta\kappa(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|^{q})\leq C^{\prime}
.
Let
0\leq l<m<n
be fixed.
By Theorem
1.3
, for all
t\geq 0
, we have

\mathbb{P}(|\Delta\kappa(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|>t)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}Ce^{-\beta k}\mathbb{P}(N(\gamma_{0})>t/k)^{2}.

By integration by parts and permutation of the sum and integral:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\Delta\kappa(\gamma_{i,j},\gamma_{j,k})|^{q})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}qt^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(|\Delta\kappa(\gamma_{i,j},\gamma_{j,k})|>t)dt

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}Ce^{-\beta k}\int_{0}^{+\infty}qt^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(N(\gamma_{0})>t/k)^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}Ce^{-\beta k}k^{q}\int_{0}^{+\infty}qt^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(N(\gamma_{0})>t)^{2}dt.

Hence, by Lemma
3.3
, we have:

\mathbb{E}(|\Delta\kappa(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|^{q})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}Ce^{-\beta k}k^{q}2\mathbb{E}(N(\gamma_{0})^{q/2})^{2}.\qed

To study the higher rank case we simply look at the exterior products representations.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\nu
be a totally irreducible probability distribution over
\mathrm{GL}(E)
and let
(\gamma_{n})\sim\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
q>0
and assume that
\mathbb{E}(N(\gamma_{0})^{q/2})<+\infty
.
Then the processes
(\dot{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m,n}))_{0\leq m\leq n}
and
(\Delta\dot{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m,n}))_{0\leq m\leq n}
are
\mathrm{L}^{q}
almost additive.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{\nu}
be the smallest closed semi-group of full
\nu
-measure.
For all
1\leq k\leq d
and for all
g\in\mathrm{GL}(E)
, we write
\overline{\kappa}_{k}(g)=\kappa(\bigwedge^{k}g)
.
Then for all
k
, we have formally
\overline{\kappa}_{k}=\kappa_{1}+\cdots+\kappa_{k}
.
Let
\Theta(\nu)
be the set of indices
1\leq k<d
such that
\bigwedge^{k}\nu
is proximal.
Using the fact that
N\circ\bigwedge^{k}\leq kN
and by Lemma
3.4
applied to
\bigwedge^{k}\nu
, for all
k\in\Theta(\nu)
, there exists a constant
C_{k}
such that for all
0\leq l\leq m\leq n
, we have:

(47)

\mathbb{E}\left(|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{k}(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|^{q}\right)\leq C_{k}.

Moreover, by sub-additivity of the determinant, we have
\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{d}=0
on
\mathrm{GL}(E)
.

By
[
Pé25
, Lemma 3.11]
(credited to Guivarc’h and Raugi
[
GR89
]
in the specific setting of products of invertible matrices), for all
k\notin\Theta(\nu)
, there exists a constant
B_{k}
such that

0\leq\kappa_{k}(g)-\kappa_{k+1}(g)\leq B_{k}
for all
g\in\Gamma_{\nu}
.
Therefore, for all
g,h\in\Gamma_{\nu}
, we have:

(48)

|\Delta\kappa_{k}(g,h)-\Delta\kappa_{k+1}(g,h)|\leq 2B_{k}.

For all
1\leq k\leq d
, we write
\lceil k\rceil_{\Theta(\nu)}
for the smallest element of
\theta(\nu)\cap\{d\}
that is larger than
k
.
By (
48
), for
B=\sum_{k\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}\setminus\Theta(\nu)}B_{k}
and for all
0\leq j,k\leq d
such that
\lceil k\rceil_{\Theta(\nu)}=\lceil j\rceil_{\Theta(\nu)}
, we have formally on
\Gamma_{\nu}
:

(49)

|\Delta\kappa_{k}-\Delta\kappa_{j}|\leq 2B.

We use the convention
\overline{\kappa}_{0}=\kappa_{0}(g)=0
for all
g
.
Now let
j>\lceil 1\rceil_{\Theta(\nu)}
, let
k=\lceil j\rceil_{\Theta(\nu)}
and let
i=\lfloor j\rfloor_{\Theta(\nu)}
be the maximal element of
\{0\}\cup\Theta(\nu)
such that
i\leq j
.
We have
\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{k}-\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{i}=\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}\Delta\kappa_{j}

so by a barycentric inequality, for all
i\leq j\leq k
, we have:

\left|\Delta{\kappa}_{j}-\frac{1}{k-i}(\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{k}-\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{i})\right|\leq 2B

and taking the partial sum:

\left|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{j}-\frac{j-i}{k}(\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{k}-\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{i})\right|\leq 2B(k-i).

and by triangular inequality:

|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{j}|\leq|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{k}|+|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{i}|+2Bd.

Therefore, for all
0\leq l\leq m\leq n
, and for
0<q\leq 1
, by triangular inequality for
|\cdot|^{q}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left(|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{j}(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|^{q}\right)\leq C_{k}+C_{i}+2Bd,

and for
q>1
, by Minkowski’s inequality, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left(|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{j}(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq C_{k}^{1/q}+C_{i}^{1/q}+2Bd

with the convention
C_{0}=C_{d}=0
.
Therefore, there exist a constant
\overline{C}
such that for all
0\leq j\leq d
, we have:

(50)

\mathbb{E}\left(|\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{j}(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|^{q}\right)\leq C.

Moreover, for all

, we have
\Delta{\kappa}_{j}=\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{j}-\Delta\overline{\kappa}_{j-1}
so by Minkowski’s inequality, we have:

(51)

\mathbb{E}\left(|\Delta{\kappa}_{j}(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})|^{q}\right)\leq\max\{2,2^{q}\}C.

Taking the sum coordinates by coordinates, and by Minkowski’s inequality again, we have:

(52)

\mathbb{E}\left(\|\Delta\dot{\kappa}(\gamma_{l,m},\gamma_{m,n})\|^{q}\right)\leq\max\{d,d^{q/2}\}\max\{2,2^{q}\}C.\qed

3.3.
A dichotomy formula

In the present paragraph, we prove a non-probabilistic formula that allows us to view non-additive additive processes as an infinite sum of additive processes.

Definition 3.6

(
\Delta
-process)
.

Let
(S_{m,n})_{m<n}
be a process.
By convention, we set
S_{n,n}=0
for all
n
.
To help formulas fit in their own line, we write
\overline{S}_{n}
as short for
S_{0,n}
and
S_{n}
as short for
S_{n,n+1}
for all
n\geq 0
.
To avoid having to distinguish cases, we use the convention
S_{n,m}:=-S_{m,n}
for all
m<n
and
S_{n,n}:=0
for all
n
.
We use the following notations:

•

For all
j\geq 1
and for all family
(n_{i})_{0\leq i\leq j}\in\mathbb{N}^{j+1}
of integers, we write
\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})=S_{n_{0},n_{j}}-\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}S_{n_{i},n_{i+1}}
.

•

We denote by
\Delta S
the error process associated to
S
, defined as
\Delta S_{m,n}=S_{m,n}-\sum_{j=m}^{n-1}S_{j}
for all
0\leq m<n
.

•

For all
k\geq 1
, we write
S^{k}
for the process defined as
S^{k}_{m,n}=S_{km,kn}
for all
0\leq m<n
.

We say that
S
is additive if
\Delta S=0
.

Given a sequence of matrices
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, and
S
defined as
S_{m,n}=\kappa(\gamma_{m,n})
for all
0\leq m<n
, we have
\Delta S_{m,n}=\Delta\kappa(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m,n})
.

For all real number
x
, we write
\lfloor x\rfloor
for the integer part of
x

i.e.
, the largest integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n\leq x
.
Given
r>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
\lfloor x\rfloor_{r}=r\lfloor x/r\rfloor
.

Before giving the proof of (
56
), let us break down the notations.
In practice, we are only interested in the quantity
\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})
when
j\geq 2
and
n_{0}<n_{1}<\dots<n_{j}
.
Note that by a telescopic sum argument, for all process
S
and all family
(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})
, we have
\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})=\Delta(\Delta S)(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})
.
Hence, for all
0\leq m<n
, we have
\Delta S_{m,n}=\Delta S(m,m+1,m+2,\dots,n)=\Delta(\Delta S)_{m,n}
.
However, we do not have
\Delta(S^{k})=(\Delta S)^{k}
for
k\geq 2
, since we are not interested in the process
(\Delta S)^{k}
, we write
\Delta S^{k}
for
\Delta(S^{k})
, that way for all
m<n
, we have
\Delta S^{k}_{m,n}=S^{k}_{m,n}-\sum_{j=m}^{n-1}S^{k}_{j}=S_{km,k_{n}}-\sum_{j=m}^{n-1}S_{kj,kj+k}=\Delta S(km,km+k,\dots,kn)
.
More generally, for all
(n_{i})_{1\leq i\leq j}
and for all
k
, we have:

(53)

\Delta S^{k}(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})=\Delta S(kn_{0},kn_{1},\dots,kn_{j}).

In fact it is enough to know the data of
(\Delta S(l,m,n))_{0\leq l<m<n}
to compute the values of
\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})
for all
(n_{i})_{1\leq i\leq j}
, using the formula:

(54)

\forall j\geq 2,\forall(n_{i})\in\mathbb{N}^{j+1},\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})=\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{j-1})+\Delta S(n_{0},n_{j-1},n_{j}).

This is a particular case of the fact that for all
1\leq k<j
and all
(n_{i})\in\mathbb{N}^{j+1}
, we have:

(55)

\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{j})=\Delta S(n_{0},\dots,n_{k})+\Delta S(n_{0},n_{k},n_{j})+\Delta S(n_{k},\dots,n_{j}).

Let us now prove the following formula.
By convention, we set
\sum_{k=0}^{-1}x(k)=0
for all expression
x(k)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(S_{m,n})_{0\leq m\leq n}
be a family of elements of an Abelian group
V
.
For all
i,n\geq 0
, we have:

(56)

\overline{S}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}})+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1)).

Moreover, for all
j>\log_{2}(n)
, we have
\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor=\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}}=0
so the sum may be taken over
j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor\}
.

Proof.

First note that for all
n
, we have
\overline{S}_{n}=S_{0,n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{n}+\Delta S_{0,n}
.
By (
55
) applied to
j=k+2
and
(n_{i})_{i}=(i)_{i}
, for all
k\geq 0
, we have
\Delta S_{0,2k+2}-\Delta S_{0,2k}=\Delta S(0,2k,2k+2)+\Delta S(2k,2k+1,2k+2)
. By induction, we have for all
n\geq 0
:

\Delta{S}_{0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}(\Delta S(0,2k,2k+2)+\Delta S(2k,2k+1,2k+2)).

Moreover, by (
54
) and by induction, we have:

\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\Delta S(0,2k,2k+2)=\Delta S(0,2,4,\dots,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2})=\Delta S^{2}_{0,\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Therefore, for all
n\geq 0
, we have:

(57)

\Delta{S}_{0,n}=\Delta S_{0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2},n}+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2k,2k+1,2k+2)+\Delta S^{2}_{0,\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Now we have show that (
57
) holds for all process
S
.
Therefore, it holds for
S^{2}
, and in fact for
S^{2^{j}}
for all
j
.
So for all
n,j\geq 0
, we have:

\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor}=\Delta S^{2^{j}}(0,\lfloor\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor\rfloor_{2},\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor)\\
+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j+1}\rfloor-1}\Delta S^{2^{j}}(2k,2k+1,2k+2)+\Delta S^{2^{j+1}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j+1}\rfloor}.

Now we multiply everything by
2^{k}
, following (
53
) and we get:

(58)

\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor}=\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j+1}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}})\\
+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j+1}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}2k,2^{j}(2k+1),2^{j}(2k+2))+\Delta S^{2^{j+1}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j+1}\rfloor}.

Note that for all
0\leq n<2^{j}
, we have
\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor}=\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,0}=0
and for
2^{j}\leq n<2^{j+1}
, we have
\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor}=\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,1}=0
.
By a telescopic argument, for all
n\geq 0
, we have:

(59)

\Delta S_{0,n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor-1}(\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor}-\Delta S^{2^{j+1}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j+1}\rfloor})=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}(\Delta S^{2^{j-1}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j-1}\rfloor}-\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor}).

By replacing
j
by
j-1
in (
58
), we have:

\Delta S^{2^{j-1}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j-1}\rfloor}-\Delta S^{2^{j}}_{0,\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor}=\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}})+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1)).

Therefore, for all
n
, we have:

(60)

\Delta S_{0,n}=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}})+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1)).

We conclude using the fact that
\overline{S}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{n}+\Delta S_{0,n}
and that the terms of index
j>\log_{2}(n)
are all zero.
∎

3.4.
Weak law of large numbers

In the present paragraph, we prove the weak law of large numbers for almost additive processes in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
for
0<q<2
. Let us fix
V
to be a real second countable Hilbert space, endowed with a scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the associated norm
\|\cdot\|
.
In the present article, we only use the results for
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}
but the proofs works the same in infinite dimension.
We remind that second countable Hilbert spaces are all isometric to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})=\{(x_{i})_{i\geq 0},\sum x_{i}^{2}<+\infty\}
.
We do not need to consider larger Hilbert spaces because all measurable events can be expressed in term of a second countable quotient.

Definition 3.8

(Fractional variance)
.

Let
1\leq q\leq 2
and let
x\in V
be a random variable.
We write:

(61)

\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x):=\mathbb{E}(\|x-\mathbb{E}_{0}(x)\|^{q}).

That way
\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x)=+\infty
when
x
has infinite moment of order
q
.

With our notations,
\mathrm{Var}_{2}
is the classical variance.
We remind that the variance of a sum of independent random variables is equal to the sum of their variances.
For
1\leq q<2
, this equality becomes an inequality.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
x,y\in V
be two random variables and let
1\leq q\leq 2
.
By Minkowski’s inequality, we have
\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x+y)^{1/q}\leq\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x)^{1/q}+\mathrm{Var}_{q}(y)^{1/q}
.
If moreover
x
and
y
are independent, then
\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x+y)\leq\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x)+\mathrm{Var}_{q}(y)
.

We have two extremal case: for
q=1
, the equality stays true without the independence assumption and for
q=2
the inequality is in fact an equality.
The proof is based on the following functional inequality:

(62)

\forall 1<q\leq 2,\forall x,y\in V,\;\|x+y\|^{q}\leq\|x\|^{q}+\|y\|^{q}+q\|y\|^{q-2}\langle y,x\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the scalar product associated to the norm
\|\cdot\|
on
V
.
To prove (
62
), we consider a decomposition
x=x_{1}y+x_{2}
, with
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\langle y,x_{2}\rangle=0
.
Note that (
62
) is trivial when
y=0
and homogeneous, so we may assume that
\|y\|=1
.
That way, we have
\|x+y\|^{2}=(x_{1}+1)^{2}+\|x_{2}\|^{2}
and (
62
) reduces to:

(\|x\|^{2}+1+2x_{1})^{q/2}\leq(\|x\|^{2})^{q/2}+qx_{1}+1,

which follows from the concavity of the map
t\mapsto t^{q/2}
on
[0,+\infty)
.

From (
62
), we deduce that
\mathbb{E}(\|x+y\|^{q})\leq\mathbb{E}(\|x\|^{q})+\mathbb{E}(\|y\|^{q})
when
x
is centred and independent of
y
, which proves Lemma
3.9
.
As a consequence, we know that given an i.i.d. sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in a Hilbert space
V
, for all
1\leq q\leq 2
, we have:

(63)

\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\overline{x}_{n})\leq n\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x_{0}).

When
q=2
this is an equality and when
q<2
, we moreover have
\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\overline{x}_{n})/n\to 0
as we will see in the next Lemma.

We remind that for all
x,y\in V
, by concavity of the map
t\mapsto t^{q}
on
[0,+\infty)
, we have:

(64)

\forall 0<q\leq 1,\,\|x+y\|^{q}\leq\|x\|^{q}+\|y\|^{q}.

From this, we deduce that
\mathbb{E}(\|x+y\|^{q})\leq\mathbb{E}(\|x\|^{q})+\mathbb{E}(\|y\|^{q})
for any pair of random variables
x
and
y
.

Given a random variable
x
that is not centred, we do not necessarily have
\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x)\leq\mathbb{E}(\|x\|^{q})
but by a naive application of Minkowski’s inequality, we always have
\mathrm{Var}_{q}(x)\leq\mathbb{E}(\|x\|^{q})+\|\mathbb{E}(x)\|^{q}\leq 2\mathbb{E}(\|x\|^{q})
.
In fact the infimum of
\mathbb{E}(\|x-b\|^{q})
, which is sometimes used as
q
-variance proxy, is reached for a value of
b
called
q
-barycentre of the law of
x
.
The
2
-barycentre is the usual mean, the
1
-barycentre is the median, which is not necessarily

Lemma 3.10

(Weak law of large numbers)
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be i.i.d. random variables in a Hilbert space
V
.
Let
q\in(0,2)
and assume that
\mathbb{E}(\|x_{0}\|^{q})<+\infty
.
Let
b=\mathbb{E}(x_{0})
if
q\geq 1
and
0
otherwise.
Then
\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{x}_{n}-nb\|^{q})/n\to 0
or in other words,
(\overline{x}_{n}-nb)/n^{1/q}
converges to
0
in probability and in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
.

Proof.

Up to a translation, we may always assume that
b=0
.
We want to show, using the dominated convergence Theorem, that:

(65)

\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{x}_{n}\|^{q})/n=\int_{t=0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|\overline{x}_{n}\|>(nt)^{1/q})dt\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

For that, we use the following decomposition.
For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
s\geq 0
, we write
y^{s}_{k}=x_{k}\mathds{1}_{\|x_{k}\|^{q}\leq s}
.
Note that for all
n
, and for all
s
, if
\|x_{k}\|\leq s^{1/q}
for all
k\leq n
, then
\overline{x}_{n}=\overline{y}^{s}_{n}
.
Therefore, for all
n\geq 1
and all
t>0
, we have:

(66)

\mathbb{P}(\|\overline{x}_{n}\|>(nt)^{1/q})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq k<n}\|x_{k}\|>(nt)^{1/q}\right)+\mathbb{P}(\|\overline{y}^{nt}_{n}\|>(nt)^{1/q}).

To control the first term, note that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq k<n}\|x_{k}\|>(nt)^{1/q}\right)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\|x_{k}\|>(nt)^{1/q}\right)\leq n\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|^{q}>nt)

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq k<n}\|x_{k}\|>(nt)^{1/q}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{1,n\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|^{q}>nt)\}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{1/n,\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|^{q}>t)\}dt

Moreover
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|^{q}>t)dt
is finite and
1/n\to 0
so by dominated convergence, we have:

(67)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k<n,\;\|x_{k}\|>(nt)^{1/q}\right)dt\underset{n\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Let us now bound the second term.
The random variables
(y^{nt}_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
are i.i.d. and
\overline{y}_{n}^{nt}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}y^{nt}_{k}
so we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\overline{y}_{n}^{nt}\right)=n\mathbb{E}\left(y_{0}^{nt}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Var}\left(\overline{y}^{nt}_{n}\right)=n\mathrm{Var}\left(y^{nt}_{0}\right)

For all
s>0
, write
b_{s}:=\|\mathbb{E}({y}^{s}_{0})\|
and
v_{s}:=\mathrm{Var}(\overline{y}^{s}_{0})
.
Then, by triangular inequality for
\|\cdot\|
and by Chebyshev’s inequality, for all
n,t
, we have:

(68)

\mathbb{P}(\|\overline{y}^{nt}_{n}\|>(nt)^{1/q})\leq((nt)^{1/q}-nb_{nt})_{+}^{-2}nv_{nt}.

Here we use the notation
(s)_{+}:=\max\{s,0\}
and
0^{-1}=+\infty
.
For
q<1
, we use the estimate:

b_{nt}\leq\mathbb{E}(\|y^{nt}_{0}\|)\leq\int_{0}^{nt^{1/q}}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds

For
q\geq 1
, we rely on the fact that
\mathbb{E}(x_{0})=0
to use the estimate:

b_{nt}\leq\mathbb{E}(\|x_{0}-y^{nt}_{0}\|)=\int_{nt^{1/q}}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds

By integration by parts, for
q>1
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{u^{1/q}}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds\right)u^{-1/q}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{s^{q}}u^{-1/q}du\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{s^{q-1}}{1-1/q}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q-1}\mathbb{E}(\|x_{0}\|^{q}).

For
q<1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{u^{1/q}}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds\right)u^{-1/q}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{s^{q}}^{+\infty}u^{-1/q}du\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{s^{q-1}}{1/q-1}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-q}\mathbb{E}(\|x_{0}\|^{q}).

In both cases, we have:

(69)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}b_{u}u^{-1/q}du\leq\frac{1}{|q-1|}\mathbb{E}(\|x_{0}\|^{q})

and therefore we have
\lim_{u\to+\infty}b_{u}u^{1-1/q}=0
.
Finally, for
q=1
, we have
\lim_{s\to+\infty}b_{s}=\|\mathbb{E}(x_{0})\|=0
.
Therefore, there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that for all
u\geq C
, we have
ub_{u}\leq u^{1/q}/2
.
So for all
t\geq C
and for all
n\geq 1
, we have
nb_{nt}\leq(nt)^{1/q}/2
.
For all values of
q<2
, we use the estimate:

v_{u}\leq\mathbb{E}(\|y_{0}^{u}\|^{2})=\int_{0}^{u}2s\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds.

Therefore, for all
t\geq C
and for all
n\geq 1
, we have:

(70)

((nt)^{1/q}-nb_{nt})^{-2}nv_{nt}\leq 8(nt)^{-2/q}\int_{0}^{(nt)^{1/q}}s\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)ds.

We integrate in
t
and for all
n\geq 1
we have:

\displaystyle\int_{C}^{+\infty}((nt)^{1/q}-nb_{nt})^{-2}nv_{nt}dt

\displaystyle\leq 8\int_{0}^{+\infty}(nt)^{-2/q}\int_{0}^{(nt)^{1/q}}s\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)dsdt

\displaystyle\leq 8\int_{0}^{\infty}s\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)\int_{s^{q}/n}^{\infty}(nt)^{-2/q}dtds

\displaystyle\leq 8\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{s^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(\|x_{0}\|>s)}{(1-2/q)n}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{(q-2)n}\mathbb{E}(\|x_{0}\|^{q}).

Moreover for all
t>0
, we eventually have
nt\geq C
and therefore,
((nt)^{1/q}-nb_{nt})_{+}^{-2}nv_{nt}\to 0
for all
t
so we have:

\int_{0}^{C}\min\{1,((nt)^{1/q}-nb_{nt})^{-2}nv_{nt}\}dt\to 0,

by dominated convergence.
Then by (
68
), we have:

(71)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|\overline{y}^{n,t}_{n}\|>(nt)^{1/q})dt\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\min\{1,((nt)^{1/q}-nb_{nt})_{+}^{-2}nv_{nt}\}dt\to 0.

And by (
66
), we have

\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{x}_{n}\|^{q})/n\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|\overline{y}^{n,t}_{n}\|>(nt)^{1/q})dt+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\max_{0\leq k<n}\|x_{k}\|>(nt)^{1/q})dt

And by (
67
) an (
71
), both terms have limit
0
.
∎

Theorem 3.11

(Weak law of large numbers without drift for almost additive processes)
.

Let
V
be a real Hilbert space.
Let
0<q<1
and
C_{q}\geq 0
.
Let
(\mathrm{S}_{m,n})_{0\leq m\leq n}
be a
V
-valued mixing process that is
C_{q}
-almost-additive in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
.
Assume also that
\mathbb{E}(\|S_{0}\|^{q})<+\infty
.
Then we have:

\lim_{n}\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{S}_{n}\|^{q})/n=0.

Moreover, for all
n\geq 1
, we have:

(72)

\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{S}_{n}\|^{q})\leq n\left(\mathbb{E}(\|S_{0}\|^{q})+C_{q}\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{-j}\right)+\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor C_{q}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
, we have for all
n\geq 1
:

\overline{S}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}})+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1)).

By (
64
), we have:

\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{S}_{n}\|^{q})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}(\|S_{k}\|^{q})+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\mathbb{E}(\|\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}})\|)^{q}\\
+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\mathbb{E}(\|\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1))\|^{q}).

Moreover, we have
\mathbb{E}(\|\Delta(l,m,n)\|^{q})\leq C_{q}
for all
l\leq m\leq n
so we have:

\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{S}_{n}\|^{q})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}(\|S_{k}\|^{q})+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}C_{q}+\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor C_{q},

which proves (
72
).
By Lemma
3.7
and (
64
) again, we have:

\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{S}_{n}\|^{q})\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}\right\|^{q}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1))\right\|^{q}\right)\\
+\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor C_{q}.

For all
j\geq 0
, we have:

K^{q}_{j}:=\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1))\right\|^{q}\right)\leq\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor C_{q}\leq n2^{-j}C_{q}

and
K^{q}_{j}/n\to 0
by Lemma
3.10
.
Therefore, by dominated convergence, we have:

\forall n\geq 0,\;\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}K^{q}_{j}/n\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}C_{q}=C_{q}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}K^{q}_{j}/n=0.

By Lemma
3.10
again, we have
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}\right\|^{q}\right)/n\to 0
. Therefore:

\mathbb{E}(\|\overline{S}_{n}\|^{q})/n\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}\right\|^{q}\right)/n+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}K^{q}_{j}/n+\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor C_{q}/n\to 0.\qed

Theorem 3.12

(Weak law of large numbers with drift for almost additive processes)
.

Let
V
be a real Hilbert space.
Let
1\leq q<2
and
C_{q}\geq 0
.
Let
(\mathrm{S}_{m,n})_{0\leq m\leq n}
be a
V
-valued mixing process that is
C_{q}
-almost-additive in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
.
Assume also that
\mathbb{E}(\|S_{0}\|^{q})<+\infty
.
Let

(73)

b:=\lim_{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}(S_{0,n})}{n}.

Then,
\|b\|\leq\mathbb{E}(S_{0})+C_{q}^{1/q}
and we have:

\lim_{n}\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\overline{S}_{n})/n=0.

Moreover, for
q\geq 1
, and for all
n\geq 1
, we have:

(74)

\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\overline{S}_{n})^{1/q}\leq n^{1/q}\left(\mathrm{Var}_{q}(S_{0})^{1/q}+V_{q}^{1/q}\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{-j/q}\right)+\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor V_{q}^{1/q}.

with
V_{q}=\max_{k\leq l\leq m}\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\Delta S(k,l,m))\leq 2C_{q}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
, we have for all
n\geq 1
:

\overline{S}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}})+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1)).

If we take the expectation, using the fact that
S
is time-invariant, we get:

\mathbb{E}(\overline{S}_{n})=n\mathbb{E}(S_{0})+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\mathbb{E}(\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}}))+\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor\mathbb{E}(\Delta S(0,2^{j-1},2^{j})).

Let
b:=\mathbb{E}(S_{0})+\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{-j}\mathbb{E}(\Delta S(0,2^{j-1},2^{j}))
.
We remind that we have
\|\mathbb{E}(\Delta S(l,m,n))\|\leq C_{q}^{1/q}
for all
l\leq m\leq n
and that
n/2^{j}-\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor\leq 1
for all
j\leq\log_{2}(n)
and
\sum_{j>\log_{2}(n)}2^{-j}n\leq 2
for all
n
.
Then, by triangular inequality, we have
\|b\|\leq\mathbb{E}(S_{0})+C_{q}^{1/q}
and for all
n\geq 1
, we have:

\|\mathbb{E}(\overline{S}_{n})-nb\|\leq 2C_{q}^{1/q}\lfloor\log_{2}(n)+1\rfloor,

which proves (
73
).

By the mixing assumption, the sequence
(S_{k})_{k}
is i.i.d. and therefore, we have:

\forall n\geq 0,\;\mathrm{Var}_{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}\right)\leq n\mathrm{Var}_{q}(S_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n}\mathrm{Var}_{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}\right)/n=0.

Moreover, for all
j
, and for all
k
the quantity
\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1))
is given by a function of
(S_{l,m})_{2^{j}k\leq l\leq m\leq 2^{j}(k+1)}
so the sequence
(\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1)))_{k\geq 0}
is i.i.d. for all
j\geq 0
.
Therefore, we have:

\mathrm{Var}_{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1))\right)\leq\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\Delta S(0,2^{j-1},2^{j})),

for all
n\geq 0
and:

\lim_{n}\mathrm{Var}_{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2^{j}\rfloor-1}\Delta S(2^{j}k,2^{j}(k+1/2),2^{j}(k+1))\right)/n=0.

Moreover, by Minkowski’s inequality, we have:

(75)

\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\overline{S}_{n})^{1/q}\leq\mathrm{Var}_{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}\right)^{1/q}+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\mathrm{Var}_{q}(\Delta S(0,\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j}},\lfloor n\rfloor_{2^{j-1}}))^{1/q}\\

In this section,

In practice, we want to see

Let

We write

Given an orthonormal basis

We denote by
\mathrm{Sym}^{2}_{+}(V)
the space of symmetric positive matrices.
We remind that any element

can be written as a diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}(a_{i})_{i}
with non-negative coefficients in an orthonormal basis
(e_{i})_{i}

One cane easily check that in any basis, we have

.

In the present section, we endow the space of matrices in

with the norm

.
This norm is sub-multiplicative and does not depend on the choice of pair of orthonormal bases, however it is not equal to the operator norm.

It is clear that given two independent random variables

\mathrm{Cov}(x+y)=\mathrm{Cov}(x)+\mathrm{Cov}(y)
.
Without assuming the independence of

and

\left\|\sqrt{\mathrm{Cov}(x+y)}-\sqrt{\mathrm{Cov}(x)}\right\|^{2}\leq\mathrm{Var}(y).

In other words, the map

is contracting in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
.

We remind that every
\mathrm{L}^{2}

.
Moreover, we have

Given two probability distributions

and

for the minimum of

\frac{\check{\kappa}(\overline{\gamma}_{n})-b_{n}+nb}{a_{n}}=\frac{\overline{x}_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}-y_{n}+\frac{\Delta\check{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,n})+nb}{a_{n}}

Hence, for
q\leq 1
, by triangular inequality and monotonicity of the expectation we have:

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\check{\kappa}(\overline{\gamma}_{n})-b_{n}+nb}{a_{n}}\right\|^{q}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\overline{x}_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}-y_{n}\right\|^{q}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\Delta\check{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,n})+nb}{a_{n}}\right\|^{q}\right)

And for
q>1
, by Minkowski’s inequality, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\check{\kappa}(\overline{\gamma}_{n})-b_{n}+nb}{a_{n}}\right\|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\overline{x}_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}-y_{n}\right\|^{q}\right)^{1/q}+\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\Delta\check{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,n})+nb}{a_{n}}\right\|^{q}\right)^{1/q}

For
q<2
, by Theorem
1.6
or Theorem
1.9
applied to
(\gamma_{n})
, we have:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\Delta\check{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,n})+nb}{n^{1/q}}\right\|^{q}\right)=0.

For
q=2
, by Theorem
1.7
or Theorem
1.10
applied to
(\gamma_{n})
, up to a coupling, there exist a sequence
y^{\prime}_{n}
of identically distributed Gaussian variables such that we have:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\Delta\check{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,n})+nb}{\sqrt{n}}-y_{n}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}\right)=0.

Therefore, by triangular inequality for the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
norm, we have:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\Delta\check{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,n})+nb}{\sqrt{n}}\right\|^{2}\right)=\mathbb{E}(\|y^{\prime}_{0}\|^{2})<+\infty.

By (
3
) in Lemma
4.1
, we have
n^{1/q}/a_{n}\to 0
.
So, in both cases, we have:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\Delta\check{\kappa}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,n})+nb}{a_{n}}\right\|^{q}\right)=0.

Moreover, by (
2
) in Lemma
4.1
, we have:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\overline{x}_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}-y_{n}\right\|^{q}\right)=0.

Therefore:

\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\check{\kappa}(\overline{\gamma}_{n})-b_{n}+nb}{a_{n}}\right\|^{q}\right)^{\min\{1/q,1\}}=0.

We use the fact that
\min\{1/q,1\}>0
to conclude.
∎

Here, we have in fact proven the following intermediate Lemma and applied it to
\Delta\check{\kappa}\widetilde{\gamma}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S
be a mixing process that takes values in a finite dimensional real vector space

.
Let

and assume that there exists
C\geq 0
and

S
is
C
-almost additive in
\mathrm{L}^{q}

Assume that the law of
S_{0}
is in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law
\mathcal{L}
and that
S_{0}

2
, let
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})

(y_{n})
be a random sequence such that for all

y_{n}\sim\mathcal{L}

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{k}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}-y_{n}\right\|^{p}\right)=0.

Then there exists a constant

\lim_{n}-\mathbb{E}(\Delta S_{0,n})/n
otherwise and such that:

(83)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\frac{S_{0,n}-b_{n}+nb}{a_{n}}-y_{n}\right\|^{p}\right)=0.

.

to

.

q

\alpha<q<2

We also know that
