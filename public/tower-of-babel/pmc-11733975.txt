Atypical Location of Papillary Fibroelastoma in a Patient With Significant Oncologic History
Authors: Adham Makarem, Patrick Udeh, Carly Liquori, Lucy Nam, Amy Sarma, Jonathan Salik, Edwin Choy, David D'Alessandro, Suzanne J Baron, Asishana Osho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report discusses the case of a 57-year-old woman with a history of breast cancer and gastric cancer, germline CDH1 gene mutation, who presented to the hospital with syncope. Diagnostic workup revealed a mass in the right ventricular outflow tract. Transcatheter biopsy suggested papillary fibroelastoma, which was confirmed with surgical pathology. This case highlights an atypical presentation of papillary fibroelastoma, which was successfully managed through a multidisciplinary approach.Key Words: breast cancer, cardiac mass, gastrectomy, papillary fibroelastoma, resection, right ventricular outflow tract, syncope

History of Presentation

A 57-year-old woman with a history of breast cancer (status post bilateral mastectomy) and prophylactic gastrectomy due to a gastric cancer gene mutation presented to medical attention following 2 recent syncopal episodes, the latter of which resulted in a head injury after collapsing at home.

A diagnostic workup, including transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), revealed an 18 × 17-mm mass in the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), initially suspected to be a thrombus, prompting immediate evaluation in the emergency department. On arrival, she was hemodynamically stable with adequate oxygen saturation on room air. Laboratory investigations were unremarkable except for normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 11.6 g/dL). A head computed tomography (CT) scan showed no acute abnormalities. Cardiology consultation was obtained, and intravenous heparin infusion was initiated.

During her admission to the hospital floor, the patient confirmed the details of her recent medical events and remained asymptomatic. A review of systems corroborated pertinent positives and negatives consistent with the history of present illness, with additional systemic reviews yielding no further findings. There was some debate among providers about the mechanism by which a fibroelastoma of this size in the RVOT could cause syncope. The leading hypothesis was intermittent obstruction of the RVOT with resultant decrease in blood flow from the right to the left cardiac chambers and eventually decreased systemic and cerebral perfusion. In addition, arrhythmias or conduction abnormalities caused by the tumor’s presence in the RVOT could also have contributed to syncopal episodes. These episodes were thought to be potentially more impactful in the setting of dehydration, where the low-volume state could further compromise RVOT emptying.

Past Medical History

The patient has a complex medical history, including breast cancer, asthma, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and hypertension. Her surgical history is significant for a prophylactic gastrectomy with Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, bilateral mastectomy, and salpingo-oophorectomy. She also underwent hernia repair with mesh placement, which was later removed. Family history includes a paternal cousin with breast cancer at age 32 and a CDH1 genetic mutation. The patient’s genetic testing confirmed the familial CDH1 mutation: p.E763∗ (c.2287G>T). Otherwise, there was no history of clotting disorders.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included thromboembolic disease, intracardiac tumors, and RVOT muscle. The initial consideration was a thrombus, given the patient’s age and risk profile. A cardiac sarcoma was considered because of the mass’s location, the possibility of transmural involvement, and the patient’s increased malignancy risk based on the CDH1 germline mutation and prior history of multiple cancers. In addition, a cardiac myxoma was also considered, despite these tumors typically arising in the left atrium.

Investigations

Diagnostic workup

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE): A TTE identified a round, well-circumscribed echodensity measuring 18 × 17 mm in the RVOT. The mass was attached to the anterior RVOT and exhibited some mobility. There was no evidence of RVOT obstruction. Normal biventricular size and systolic function were observed, with an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 59%. There was no hemodynamically significant valvular dysfunction. Right ventricular systolic pressure could not be estimated because of an insufficient tricuspid regurgitation Doppler envelope (Figure 1).

Real-Time 3-Dimensional Transthoracic Electrocardiogram

Imaging shows mass attached to the right ventricular outflow tract (arrows).

CT: A CT scan showed a 15 × 12 × 9-mm hypodense mass adherent to the anterior wall of the RVOT, without significant obstruction (Figure 2).

Images of (A) arterial phase and (B) 1-minute delayed phase after contrast injection highlight the hypodense mass adherent to the anterior wall of the right ventricular outflow tract (arrows).

Magnetic resonance imaging: A magnetic resonance image showed a well-circumscribed mass measuring 1.7 × 1.2 cm in the RVOT, appearing isointense on T1-weighted and hyperintense on T2-weighted images. Fat-saturated images showed no significant intralesional fat. First-pass perfusion and delayed gadolinium enhancement images revealed enhancement within the mass, confirmed by late gadolinium enhancement images, including long T1 time sequences (Figure 3).

Magnetic Resonance Image Showing a Well-Circumscribed Mass in the Right Ventricular Outflow Tract

(A) Axial T2-weighted fat-saturated image, (B) first-pass perfusion, and (C) axial phase sensitive inversion recovery late gadolinium enhancement, demonstrate a T2 hyperintense mass in the right ventricular outflow tract with enhancement on first-pass perfusion and delayed gadolinium enhancement (arrows).

Biopsy: Percutaneous biopsy was performed in the catheterization laboratory under general anesthesia. Access was obtained in the right internal jugular vein and an 8-French 45-cm sheath was advanced to the right ventricle. Under transesophageal and fluoroscopic guidance, a 7-French Bioptome was advanced to the RVOT and 6 tissue samples were obtained. Pathology suggested the presence of a papillary fibroelastoma (PFE) (Figure 4).

Histopathology Sections

Both the biopsy (A) and the surgical resection (B) show the tumor to be composed of branching cores of connective tissue covered by a layer of endothelial cells, indicative of a papillary fibroelastoma. Magnification: ×40 for both panels.

Management

Surgical intervention for excision was pursued given the location of the lesion, possibility of associated syncopal symptoms, and residual diagnostic uncertainty. During the operation, the main pulmonary artery was opened transversely to access the valve. The mass in the RVOT was identified and visualized via diagnostic needle ventriculoscopy using the Arthrex NanoScope Arthroscopy System.1 The lesion was then completely resected along with a small cuff of RVOT muscle (Video 1).

Histopathology

Histopathological analysis confirmed that the resected mass was a PFE (Figure 4). An endomyocardial biopsy showed no evidence of myxoma, carcinoma, or sarcoma. Immunohistochemistry for calretinin and pan-keratin was also evaluated.

Discussion

PFE is a benign primary endocardial tumor increasingly diagnosed due to advancements in cardiac imaging modalities.2 Despite its benign nature, PFEs can lead to severe clinical outcomes such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and embolism. Uncertainty persists regarding their etiology, prevalence, and optimal management,3 often leading to misdiagnosis and incorrect treatment.4 Recent studies report a prevalence of 1 PFE per 1,100 echocardiograms,5 highlighting the need for accurate diagnosis given their potentially significant complications and mortality risk due to tumor mobility.6

PFEs primarily affect the valvular endocardium, notably the aortic valve (52%) and less frequently the left ventricular outflow tract (18%).4,7 Conversely, right ventricular involvement, especially in the RVOT, is exceedingly rare (<3%).8 Our case highlights an unusual instance of RVOT PFE in a middle-aged woman with a complex medical history, including breast cancer and a familial genetic cancer syndrome. The PFE was incidentally discovered during syncope evaluation, initially posing diagnostic challenges given concerns for a possible malignant tumor based on her oncologic history.

Surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for symptomatic or large PFEs, as demonstrated here. Biopsy may enhance diagnostic certainty in such cases, guiding appropriate treatment decisions.

A similar case by Chang et al9 in 2005 described an 81-year-old man with an RVOT PFE discovered during an evaluation for vertigo, highlighting the unusual location and a rare pathological feature. Despite successful excision in our patient, recurrence rates post-surgery are approximately 12.2%, necessitating long-term surveillance.10 Incorporating imaging modalities such as transesophageal echocardiography is critical for early detection of recurrent masses, minimizing risks and optimizing outcomes.

Follow-Up

Following the procedure, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit, weaned from sedation, and extubated that evening. Overnight, she experienced several episodes of 2:1 atrioventricular block as well as an asystolic run for 26 seconds not requiring any compressions or shocks. A temporary wire was placed, and she recovered without inotropic or vasopressor support. The wire was removed on postoperative day 6, a mobile telemetry monitor was placed, and the patient was subsequently discharged home. Follow-up in clinic at 2 weeks and 2 months revealed no complications or ongoing syncopal symptoms since her initial discharge. The plan includes a follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram at 6 months, followed by annual evaluations thereafter.

Conclusions

This case underscores the significance of recognizing rare cardiac tumors such as PFE in patients presenting with syncope and unexplained cardiac masses. Early diagnosis and timely surgical intervention are crucial in preventing potentially life-threatening complications. Continued research and reporting of similar cases are vital to enhance diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for patients with PFEs in atypical cardiac locations.
