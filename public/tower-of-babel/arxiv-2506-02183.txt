Natural, Artificial, and Human Intelligences
Emmanuel M. Pothos Dominic Widdows City, University of London Independent Research emmanuel.pothos.1@city.ac.uk dwiddows@gmail.com

Abstract
Human achievement, whether in culture, science, or technology, is unparalleled in the known existence. This achievement is tied to the enormous communities of knowledge, made possible by (especially written) language: leaving theological content aside, it is very much true that `` \accpsili \accpsili \accperispomeni \accpsiliperispomeni \accdasia \acctonos ´´ (in the beginning was the word). There lies the challenge regarding modern age chatbots: they can ‘do’ language apparently as well as ourselves and there is a natural question of whether they can be considered intelligent, in the same way as we are or otherwise. Are humans uniquely intelligent? We consider this question in terms of the psychological literature on intelligence, evidence for intelligence in non-human animals, the role of written language in science and technology, progress with artificial intelligence, the history of intelligence testing (for both humans and machines), and the role of embodiment in intelligence. For the most unique accomplishments of human intelligence (such as music symphonies or complex scientific theories), we think that, together with language, there are four essential ingredients, which can be summarised as invention, capacity for complex inference, embodiment, and self-awareness. This conclusion makes untenable the position that human intelligence differs qualitatively from that of many non-human animals, since, with the exception of complex language, all the other requirements are fulfilled. Regarding chatbots, the current limitations are localised to the lack of embodiment and (apparent) lack of awareness.



Keywords

artificial intelligence, human intelligence, animal intelligence, chatbots, large language models, intelligence testing, Imitation Game, embodiment

1
Statement of the Problem: are Chatbots Intelligent?

We are witnessing one of the most important and transformative technological revolutions in our history, the emergence of human-like intelligence. A discussion with a modern chatbot, such as chat GPT (we refer to large language models as chatbots in a generic way), is undeniably human-like and several studies have been establishing that chatbots display human cognitive traits, including similar biases
(
Binz and Schulz, 2023
;
Suri et al., 2024
)
, the recognition of emotional states

(
Schaaff et al., 2023
)
and empathy
(
Roshanaei et al., 2024
)
, and other mental processing similarities (
Mei et al., 2024
;
Strachan et al., 2024
).
One effect of this revolution is existential: we, humans, often assume our place in the world is unique, by virtue of the uniqueness of our intelligence. Is human intelligence unique?

A more practical question is whether chatbots will take our jobs. Together with being human-like, chatbots also encapsulate, in some sense, all human knowledge. Asking GPT-4 whether it will take my lecturer job, it is humble and reassuring: “As a university lecturer, you’re definitely in a role where expertise, creativity, and human connection are key. While AI, like ChatGPT, can help with things like answering questions, explaining concepts, or generating teaching materials, it’s not likely to replace the heart of your job.”
However, increasingly in higher education, new policies are introduced to regulate the use of chatbots, given their dual ability to generate plausible coursework and to mark it with some competence (e.g.,
Gultom et al., 2024
), questioning the role of humans in the process. Moreover, there are already indications that chatbots can carry out research roles
(
Lehr et al., 2024
)
.

The observation that technological advancements can lead to job losses is hardly new — there is a name for this, “technological unemployment”
(
Peters, 2020
)
— and there are some compelling examples, e.g., the reduction of the U.S. workforce employed in agriculture between 1900 (41%) and 2000 (2%,
Autor, 2014
).
Unsurprisingly, such concerns have been raised for chatbots too (Callum, 2016). The balancing perspective is that technological innovation also leads to new jobs (
Autor, 2015
;
Mincer and Danninger, 2000
) and over-productivity could be compensated in other ways (such as universal income).
Additionally, some jobs require a human touch, do not depend on high levels of intelligence, or are even unnecessary (perhaps the result of vested interests).
However, there is a unique role of chatbots in this debate in that they are not ‘just’ another technological innovation with potential to eliminate some jobs: in all cases of technological automation, the replacement concerned tasks that were either too laborious (e.g., agricultural tasks) or seen as beyond the competencies which we have considered central to our notion of human intelligence (e.g., multitudes of rote arithmetical operations in accounting). Instead, with chatbots, we are faced with an artificial agent which seems just like us, concerning some competencies which we have considered distinctly human.

Overall, the consideration of whether chatbots can replace humans in various jobs is important and complements the existential question of whether chatbots are human-like. The present focus is intelligence. Can chatbots be considered intelligent? A difficulty in addressing this question is that our understanding of intelligence is itself incomplete. We consider some relevant ideas below. Briefly, on the one hand, it appears that traditional notions of intelligence can be covered by chatbots – and, moreover, a careful discussion reveals challenges to a uniqueness premise for human intelligence. On the other hand, the fundamental question is whether there is a special ingredient in human intelligence, which allows us to rise to apparent amazing intellectual accomplishments. What is it about our minds which, for example, has enabled us to invent quantum mechanics?

2
What is Intelligence: Psychological Approaches

Much of the psychological tradition regarding intelligence concerns psychometric theories, which aim is to explain individual differences in test scores, on the basis of factors (e.g., see
(
Sternberg, 2012
)
).
The earliest proposal of this kind is due to
Spearman, 1904
, who argued for two factors, a general one (later associated with ‘mental energy’) and a specific one, comprised of competencies for particular tasks.

Thurstone, 1938
, instead, identified seven ”primary mental abilities” as the determinants of intelligence. These mental abilities include verbal fluency, spatial visualization, and perceptual speed. While originally
Spearman, 1904
and
Thurstone, 1938
were interpreted as antithetical, this is not necessarily the case. One way to reconcile the two views is if the general vs. more specific intelligence factors are hierarchical, so that the latter informs specific aspects of the former. This hierarchical approach has been taken up by other intelligence psychologists, such as
Carroll, 1993
, who expanded both the specific and general abilities.

This literature has recognised that some abilities reflect knowledge, while others processing power (in some general sense).
Cattell, 1943
introduced the distinction between fluid and crystallized abilities, with the former concerning performance that depends on prior knowledge minimally (e.g., problem-solving on a novel task) and conversely for the latter (e.g., vocabulary tasks). There is a converse relation between these two kinds of abilities and age, with fluid decreasing, while crystallized increasing, with age (e.g.,
Ghisletta et al., 2012
).

A more processing account of intelligence has been that it is characterized by the speed of information processing
(
Deary et al., 2001
)
,
sometimes in simple perceptual tasks, such as whether two lines are the same length
(
MacKintosh, 1981
)
(but see
(
Kiesling and Hentschel, 1988
)
).
There have been related proposals; for example,
Hunt et al., 1973
considered the speed with which information is ’absorbed’ into memory and subsequently retrieved.
Such approaches do not depend on extent of knowledge or other culture-specific skills, such as social skills.

There have been other attempts to understand intelligence in more cognitive terms, e.g. in terms of working memory capacity
(
Conway and Engle, 1996
)
, underpinned by high correlations between measures of working memory and performance with reasoning ability (e.g.,
(
Kyllonen and Christal, 1990
;
Ackerman et al., 2005
)
). Interestingly, in cognitive science, working memory capacity
limitations
have been argued to enable learning in complex domains
(
Newport, 1988
)
.

Another processing idea is that intelligence relates to the ability to solve problems and achieve goals.
Sternberg, 1985
proposed that intelligence is characterized by three abilities: practical intelligence (environment interaction), creative intelligence (new problems and situations), and analytical intelligence (primarily problem-solving). Problem-solving ability has had a prominent role in other proposals (e.g.,
(
Danner et al., 2011
;
Hambrick et al., 2019
)
), as well as often being a component in most others (starting from
(
Spearman, 1904
)
).
Sternberg, 1985
’s theory is an example of approaches positing multiple types of intelligence.
Gardner, 2006
proposed a longer list, including musical, interpersonal, spatial, and linguistic intelligence, and
Salovey and Mayer, 1990
introduced the idea of emotional intelligence as the ability to perceive and utilize emotions in cognition.

Psychometric approaches to intelligence have demonstrated practical value. For example, they predict longevity
(
Deary, 2001
;
Deary et al., 2007
)
, health outcomes
(
Hart et al., 2003
)
, job performance
(
Schmidt and Hunter, 2004
;
Ericsson and Pool, 2016
)
, and academic success
(
Deary et al., 2007
)
.

Particular aspects of intelligence have been prioritised in intelligence testing:
the approaches of
(
Spearman, 1904
)
,
Binet and Simon, 1905
were translated into educational and cultural settings that emphasized the importance
of written answers to prearranged test questions
(
Rogele, 2020
)
. A preference for written demonstrations of intelligence, particularly in
English-speaking educational systems, has profoundly affected the way artificial intelligence has been viewed and pursued, a relationship that we explore throughout
the rest of this paper.

Summing up some relevant points, first, regardless of whether a single general intelligence is postulated or not, it is assumed that there are cognitive abilities that correlate with intelligence/the intelligences
(
Corr and Krupic, 2024
)
. Second, some of these abilities concern processing characteristics, for example, processing speed or memory efficiency.
Third, intelligence involves several aspects, e.g., problem-solving, linguistic/verbal, emotional, social, etc. Regarding the present focus, can the psychometric literature help us understand what, if anything, sets apart human intelligence compared to biological species and artificial agents? We can eliminate some possibilities. For example, regarding non-human animals, they often demonstrate the competencies (at least to a degree) cited for humans too, such as spatial, emotional, musical abilities etc. (cf.
Gardner, 2006
).
Moreover, artificial agents, including chatbots, exceed human capacity in essentially all processing characteristics (speed of processing, memory, information absorption and retrieval, problem solving ability, at least in specific categories). The linguistic competence of most chatbots is mostly equivalent to that of humans, even if the extent of understanding is unclear
(
Mahowald et al., 2024
)
. Despite the many profound insights in the psychometric literature on intelligence, there is not very much concerning the characteristics (if any) that make it special.

3
Animal Intelligence

Humans are not the fastest, toughest, most enduring etc. creatures, but we are distinguished for our cultural, technological, and scientific accomplishments – our achievement in these respects surpasses that of all other creatures. This has allowed us to consider ourselves the most intelligent species. This is probably true, but there is a question of whether other species are intelligent like us, but to a lesser extent, or less intelligent in some fundamental way.

There are signs of intelligence throughout the animal kingdom (Bukart et al., 2017). For example, dogs have highly developed social awareness, including sensitivity to human body language, human gestures, and parts of language

(
Hare et al., 2002
;
Kaminski et al., 2004
)
.
Dogs are able to solve a variety of logical/ reasoning problems, such as a means end task
(
Range et al., 2011
)
or spatial puzzles
(
Barrera et al., 2015
)
.
Theory of mind is the ability to attribute mental states (such as beliefs and desires) to others. It is typically considered an integral part of intelligence, especially social intelligence
(
Navarro, 2022
)
.
There have been arguments that dogs and other primates have a theory of mind
(
Catala et al., 2017
;
Premack and Woodruff, 1978
)
, though there is controversy
(
Heyes, 1998
)
.
Chimpanzees have extensive tool-using abilities, including for food gathering/ hunting, grooming, and communication

(
Koops et al., 2015
)
. Chimpanzees can recognize abstract symbols
(
Rumbaugh and Washburn, 2003
)
and can innovate solutions to unfamiliar problems
(
Bandini and Harrison, 2020
)
. Similar evidence has accumulated for other species, e.g., corvids, who appear to have problem-solving abilities comparable to those of chimpanzees
(
Güntürkün et al., 2024
)
. Perhaps unsurprisingly, there is much evidence regarding musical intelligence in birds, e.g., relating to rhythm perception and processing
(
Fitch, 2015
;
Patel, 2006
)
or the structural properties of birdsong
(
Kawaji et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2023
)
. Further, there is some evidence of musical synchronisation amongst birds, though for duetting birds at most
(
Hall, 2009
;
Mann et al., 2005
)
. Overall, bird musical ability does not appear to match the complexity of human music
(
Lerdahl and Jackendoff, 1983
;
Levitin, 2006
)
.

What about, for example, the homing instinct of a pigeon or the way a leopard calculates the exact time and distance before it pounces onto prey? For example, pigeons have been known to successfully return home across distances exceeding 100km
(
Schmidt-Koenig and Schlichte, 1972
)
.
We would not call an individual pigeon intelligent for demonstrating its homing instinct, in the same way we would not call an individual human intelligent for learning to walk or ride a bicycle — such skills are considered default competencies that most members of a species are endowed with, as a result of evolutionary adaptation. There is intelligence at the species level, allowing for different competencies, developed through evolution (e.g.,
(
Jerison and Jerison, 1988
)
). A similar case can be made regarding the remarkable ingenuity in, for example, the way ants or bees build their nests
(
Winston, 1987
)
.

Beyond evolutionary intelligence, for some species, there is collective intelligence, the ability to achieve more than what an individual could accomplish, through cooperation.
In the animal kingdom, for example, ants use pheromones to communicate about food sources or dangers and often work together in foraging tasks
(
Hölldobler and Wilson, 1990
)
; the way African wild dogs coordinate in hunting has sophisticated characteristics, including role differentiation and vocal communication (
Creel and Creel, 2002
; see for an example regarding rats
Schuster, 2002
).
However, no other species has been more successful in transcending what is possible for an individual than humans.
Fernbach and Sloman, 2018
coined the term ‘community of knowledge’, for the idea that even seemingly simple aspects of our lives are underwritten by massive knowledge contributions of which we are mostly ignorant. Our ability to form sophisticated communities of knowledge is fundamentally linked to the unique expressivity of human language (e.g.,
Lakoff and Johnson, 2003
;
Pinker, 1995
)
and the fact that our language has a written form
(
Latour and Woolgar, 1987
)
.
Written language has enabled global communities of knowledge; arguably, it is the cornerstone of our civilization (
Goody, 1977
; concerning the interaction between climate, the durability of early writing mediums, such as papyrus, and the emergence of early civilizations see
Gaudet, 2014
).

Is there any evidence for art, science, technology amongst non-humans? Regarding art, some animals can learn to discriminate based on the aesthetics of pictures and show preference towards higher aesthetics (e.g., songbirds, for musical stimuli;
Watanabe, 2012
). Some birds create and decorate elaborate structures to attract mates
(
Borgia, 1985
)
and some animals can create pictorial patterns in the sand or using rudimentary tools, which resembles artistic expression
(
Pelé et al., 2021
)
. However, such evidence is a far cry from demonstrations that non-human animals show human-like appreciation of these behaviours and their outputs, in the way we do for art
(
English et al., 2014
)
. Regarding science, the scientific endeavour implies experimentation and exploration of testable hypotheses, to identify general principles and so generalize from known situations to novel ones. There is no evidence that animals can do science. However, there is some evidence that animals can experiment and adjust strategies, create artifacts, and develop rudimentary tools. For example, chimpanzees make tools to help them forage for ants or termites, through a process of successive improvement before the desired form is achieved, sometimes before actual tool use (indicating that they have an idea of the required form for a tool prior to use;
Boesch and Boesch, 1990
); and New Caledonian crows have been reported to construct hooked tools, to aid them in insect capture
(
Hunt et al., 1973
)
. Moreover, there is evidence of animals adapting behaviour from trial and error and changing circumstances (e.g.,
Fiorito and Scotto, 1992
). Such evidence indicates that animals are capable of technology and innovation, though it appears that their only path is through gradual trial and error, as opposed to the sudden realization which often accompanies human technological innovation (famous examples are penicillin and the discovery of the Benzene chemical structure,
Fleming, 1929
;
Kekulé, 1865
, as cited in
(
Rocke, 2015
)
).

4
The Word and Western Culture

A consideration of animal intelligence reveals differences with humans, but more so in terms of outcomes (such as science) rather than some essential ingredient of human intelligence, which is lacking in animals – one obvious exception is complex, written language.
Debate on our origins is common throughout cultures. In some mythical traditions, humans are a kindred species with much to learn from wise animals; in others, humans are set apart, placed in the world by gods to preserve cosmic harmony or to provide service.
The psychometric science has grown within a cultural tradition in which humans have
always
been set-apart by the faculties of word and reason: the defining characteristic of humanity has been assumed to be “the word made flesh”, a picture of humans which has persisted. Reviewing some of the roots of western science can help understand why chatbots present such an uncomfortable challenge, even if written language is not the whole story.

In standard historic accounts, western civilization is the product of Hebrew religion and Greek
science, becoming the Judeo-Christian tradition. Both of these roots embody the distinctiveness of
human origins and intelligence, with an emphasis on knowledge expressed in words. In Genesis (Ch
3), Adam and Eve eat the fruit of “the tree of the knowledge of good and evil” and become self-aware. In Aristotle’s
Politics
(Bk VII Ch 3), “Virtue arises through nature (
physis
), habit (
ethos
), and
reason (
logos
)”, and while some animals demonstrate ethos as well as physis, only humans have
logos.
This combination became central to Christianity, whose most mystical Gospel starts with “In the beginning was the Word” (
\accpsili

\accpsili
\accperispomeni

\accpsiliperispomeni

\accdasia

\acctonos
, John Ch 1).
As Christianity became established, so did the belief that humans are verbal, rational animals,
and that this is the divine core of our identity.
Beyond religious education, this was taught in introductory logic and classification (we use classification in the logical sense) from the third century onwards,
using the tree of Porphyry (Figure
1
). This is perhaps history’s most famous decision-tree, until the mid-1800s, when Boolean propositional calculus and truth-tables supplanted
Aristotelean classification and syllogism in the teaching of logic
(
Boole, 1854
)
.
The claim that humans are uniquely rational was not just part of biology and theology, it was taught as a foundational principle in western logic!

Figure 1:
Tree of Porphyry, used to teach introductory logic
from 3rd to 19th centuries in European schools.

Writing was elevated as a sacred medium in many civilizations. The Law of Moses (in the Jewish Torah, the first five books or Pentateuch of Christian Bibles) was meant to record the word of God. The Qur’an emphasizes the sacred status of its own words and respect for Jewish and Christian scriptures, whose followers are described as “people of the Book” (Surah 5:65–69). In China, the tradition of Confucian literary scholarship became central to the famous civil service examinations, in theory a great source of social mobility; these exams were entirely written
(
Elman, 2013
)
. Overall, there is evidence for an appreciation of the primacy of written language throughout human civilization, but there are exceptions. For example, in Sanskrit scholarship, Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī (c. 5th to 4th century BCE), one of the earliest revered works on grammar and language, emphasized the sacred power of the Vedas (ancient Hindu scriptures), particularly through sound, rather than as static written texts.

During the scientific revolution, authors from Copernicus to Newton made efforts to
separate their scientific, philosophical, and theological writings, but maintained the emphasis on human
intelligence being uniquely rational and second only to that of God. Physical capabilities were considered less important, sometimes dismissed, most famously
with Descartes’ “Je pense, donc je suis” (from
Discours de la Méthode
in 1637, the more famous Latin
“Cogito ergo sum” appearing in
Meditationes de Prima Philosophia
in 1641).
The term ‘scientific works’ became identified with what scientists had published, not
physical objects. For example, in ancient times, Archimedes’ screw or Heron’s steam engine would be counted as scientific works. However, by the 1600s, Hooke’s work on mechanics and Leibniz’ on calculus would be taught as part of their scientific contributions, but not Hooke’s gear-cutting machines and Leibniz’ mine-pumps.
This suggests an emerging distinction between science (expressed in writing) and technology,
with the former considered a more intellectual activity.

Written science was enabled by key technology advances and ultimately led to more advanced scholarship.
The printing press encouraged the spread of books and a preference for
alphabetic and arithmetic symbols, instead of diagrams: for example, the
Treviso
Arithmetic
, the first movable-type printed textbook of arithmetic,
contains none of the diagrams used to visualize and compare ratios
in a traditional copy of Euclid’s
Elements of Geometry
.
Printing made it much easier to share accurate ‘ephemerides’ (tables of astronomical sightings), which, along with
the mariner’s compass and the sextant, allowed more accurate long-distance navigation.
The refracting telescope was carefully adapted for celestial accuracy by Galileo and Kepler, leading to the discoveries that planets had their own moons and elliptical orbits
(
American Institute of Physics, 2025
)
.
Later, the desire to explain these patterns led to Newton’s axiomatic development of mechanics from three axioms, or laws, of motion.

Technological inventions gradually became more directly driven by scientific predictions. Literally, the pendulum swung — Galileo’s observation in the 1580s that the swing of a lantern kept the same period for large and small angles led to the pendulum clock built by Huygens in 1656 and a new era of temporal measurement accuracy, at least on land. Onboard ship, however, the pendulum’s period varied, and the naval chronometer, a clock accurate enough to keep time over several weeks, even as the time of noon onboard ship changed,
was developed in the 1700s by John Harrison, in this case using careful craftsmanship, rather than scientific advances at the time.

Steam engines became pivotal in the industrial revolution and illustrate the emergence of a new, causal relationship
between technology and science. Ancient inventors, including Heron of Alexandria, had built demonstration steam engines, but they were
never used to replace human labour.
From the early 1700s, Newcomen’s steam engines were in active use pumping water out of mines,
but the steam was heated and then condensed in the same chamber, an expensive and dangerous
combination. Watt’s brilliant innovation during the 1760s was to separate these duties between two different chambers, leading to reliable rotational power that could drain mines, then power a whole weaving factory, and eventually leading to steamships and locomotives.
In trying to understand the relationship between heat inside boilers and mechanical work in piston chambers, scientists including Carnot, Kelvin, and Clausius developed modern thermodynamics between 1820s and 1860s. Thermodynamics was arguably the first branch of physics
that was deliberately motivated by the goal of optimizing an existing technology.

Maxwell’s electromagnetic theory in the 1860s went leaps further, explaining the
propagation of light waves in traditional optics and predicting the behaviour of an entire spectrum
of oscillations, invisible to human senses. During the 1880s, Hertz used these theoretical predictions
to transmit and receive real radio waves.
This formed a new relationship: science had brought about a technology that was
beyond our natural imagination
(
Oerter, 2006
, Ch 1)
.
In this new relationship, written works of science led directly to new kinds of machines,
and scientific progress enabled a landslide of innovations throughout the late 1800s and early 1900s.
Since that time, it has become assumed that written science is the main source of technological inspiration and reliability.
Nonetheless, a small sample of inventions
(fermentation, wheels, metalworking, textiles, clocks, gunpowder, printing, telescopes, steam engines, dried noodles, vacuum cleaners, velcro) demonstrates immediately that
written science is just one path to invention
(
Mokyr, 1992
)
.

Beyond science, the identification of human intelligence with written language skills has sometimes been even more emphatic.
While the French
Baccalaureate
and German

Abitur
exams, formalized since the early-1800s,
included practical and oral components, British qualifications, like the Chinese civil exams long before, were standardized entirely by writing.
Given the emphasis on STEM education today, it is easy to assume that science education was developed along with science itself, but this would
be an anachronism: Newton, Priestley, Cavendish, and Darwin did not go to secondary schools with classes in physics, chemistry, and biology,
but instead, aside from geometry and algebra, a principal focus would have been classical languages and literature.
British “grammar schools” had existed since the late middle-ages and the redirection of endowment funds for “other useful Branches of Literature and Science in addition to or (subject to the Provisions hereinafter contained)
in lieu of the Greek and Latin Languages” was controversial enough to motivate the Grammar Schools Act of 1840
(
Parliament of the United Kingdom, 1840
)
.
The use of written examinations as the measure of intelligence
was explicitly promoted in the Northcote and Trevelyan report
that led to the British Civil Service exams. Their main
stated goals included promoting merit over patronage, and also
“establishing a proper distinction between mechanical and intellectual labour”
(
Northcote et al., 1854
)
.
While moral and physical fitness was to be
vouched for in-person, for the purposes of choosing civil servants,
intellectual labour was identified with writing. Overall, such tests prioritized written over manual skills: to fix a broken boiler, the person who could
write down the problem, order the replacement parts etc., was considered intelligent, whereas the person who actually mended the boiler less so.

Well before the time AI was invented, assumptions about the relationship
between science, schooling, written words, and intelligence were part of the fabric of western European culture.
For centuries, there were serious philosophical and scientific claims that abstract reasoning is impossible without language, and only
recently, brain imaging studies have shown that these activities are
dissociated from one another
(
Fedorenko et al., 2024
)
.
Language abilities have clearly helped to distinguish us from other species, but idolizing these abilities made us
particularly vulnerable to the shock of fluent chatbots.

5
Artificial Intelligence Joins the Stage

The theory of computing was developed by Church and Turing in the 1930s, and Turing’s calculating machines were built in the hidden limelight of wartime codebreaking in the 1940s.
By the end of the decade, Turing, von Neumann, and many collaborators had developed
reprogrammable machines, whose instructions and calculations were stored in electronic
memory and were capable of performing thousands arithmetic calculations within seconds,
using cascading patterns of Boolean logic to process numbers represented in binary digits.

Shannon’s landmark
Mathematical Theory of Communication

(
Shannon, 1948
)
developed the quantification of information,
using the statistics of character and word sequences in English as grounding examples.
These are still foundational examples in statistical natural language processing:
Shannon’s zeroth, first, and second-order approximations to English would nowadays be called

n
-gram models
(
Manning and Schütze, 1999
, Ch 6)
. Rather than trying to program and run these
models on an early electronic computer, Shannon’s samples were taken from random pages in books –
but he still accurately described the computational possibilities, and scaling limitations,
of
n
-gram models.

Turing’s paper on
Computing Machinery and Intelligence
in 1950
(
Turing, 1950
)
is a foundation
of the field that was eventually called AI. Turing’s proposal was particularly important for text processing, introducing a challenge called
the
Imitation Game
. Two humans, or a human and a machine, try to imitate one another, communicating directly
with a judge, with interactions restricted to the inputs and outputs of a teleprinter.
Whether the judge
can distinguish a machine from a human through this interface alone has come to be called the

Turing Test
.

Using the same distinction between physical and intellectual capacities as
(
Northcote et al., 1854
)
,
Turing reckoned that
“The new problem has the advantage of drawing a fairly sharp line between the physical
and the intellectual capacities of a man.”
Turing carefully avoided the question of whether such a machine is
‘thinking’, saying instead that such behaviour would typically be
regarded
as demonstrating intelligence. His paper examined common objections to the idea that such a machine
could be called intelligent
(e.g., not having a soul or just learning ‘parrot-fashion’), arguing that they are rooted in prejudice and other mistakes,
and speculated that the most effective way to
build
such a machine would be to
create a ‘child machine’ with the programming and resources to be able to learn, and provide this machine with training experiences.
He estimated that such a machine could be built by the year 2000 and, while that timeline was not achieved, Turing’s paper broadly anticipates the current approach to language processing,
and the tendencies to resist these achievements,
noting that this urge is “likely to be quite strong
in intellectual people, since they value the power of thinking more highly than others, and
are more inclined to base their belief in the superiority of Man on this power.”
(
Turing, 1950
, §6)

Some of the core components of today’s chatbots were pioneered in the 1950s and 1960s. Most famously,
Rosenblatt’s invention of the perceptron
(
Rosenblatt, 1958
)
and Widrow and Hoff’s use
of continuous, gradient-based iterations to train perceptron networks
(
Widrow and Hoff, 1960
)
are
generally considered the precursors of today’s neural networks
(
Raschka et al., 2022
, Ch 2)
.
Representing words as vectors learned from data, usually called
‘word embeddings’ since the success of the
word3vec
package in 2013
Mikolov et al., 2013
,
was pioneered in the SMART (System for the Mechanical Analysis and Retrieval of Text) project,
led by Gerard Salton during the 1960s
(
Salton, 1971
)
. The success of the SMART
system was demonstrated using early statistical evaluations of precision and recall, developed
during the Cranfield experiments, whose purpose was indexing and retrieval techniques, particularly for scientific reference and military intelligence gathering
(
Cleverdon et al., 1966
)
. The Cranfield project was foundational in information retrieval, not only
for its results, but for establishing the practice of evaluating search engines by measuring the precision and recall of sets of search results, when compared with held-out test data. This practice was followed in the 1990s
in the Text Retrieval Evaluation Conference series
(
Harman, 1993
)
, and submitting results to a “TREC Style Evaluation” is a key forerunner of many machine learning metrics and leaderboards, such as Kaggle and the MLCommons, used to compare models today.
One of the key surprises from the Cranfield experiments was that automated indexing strategies, based purely on the words occurring in each document, outperformed indexing by human experts. This surprised, and initially
dismayed researchers:
“the results which seem to offend against every
canon on which we were trained as librarians”

(
Cleverdon et al., 1966
)
. ‘Computer’ had been a job title
for skilled arithmeticians during World War II, but since the rules of arithmetic had been formalized,
it stood to reason that a faster electronic machine that performed arithmetic could produce results that were
just as good. ‘Indexer’, however, was a different matter: the job had been
performed by expert librarians since the 1800s, and the notion that skilled work with words could be done by a machine
just as well, if not better, was a shock. Statistical term-weighting (i.e., how much weight should a term be given in a retrieval engine) is an example:
instead of weighting terms using controlled vocabularies of keywords and subject-headings,
it worked better to assign weights, or importance scores, to each word in a document, based
on how many times that word occurs in that document and how few other documents it occurs in.
This family of weighting schemes is called
tf-idf
(term frequency, inverse document frequency).
The insight that statistical term-weighting (or term specificity) is modelled so effectively by tf-idf is due
particularly to
(
Sparck Jones, 1972
)
, and though it might be considered a blunt challenge to more
formal logical semantics, it is today common to trace the linguistic and philosophical heritage of such statistical
observations to the works of
Firth, 1957
and
Wittgenstein, 1953
.

Given such strong foundations, it may be surprising that statistical language processing using neural networks
took so long to fully flourish. One reason is that it required decades of hardware advancements, including the
development of graphics processing units and their adaptation to neural networks, to realize the potential of neural
network methods. Another factor is that most research in AI and computational linguistics during the later decades of the
1900s was focused elsewhere, particularly on symbolic, logical, and syntactic methods. In linguistics,
Chomsky wielded great influence and his early work on syntactic structures claimed both that
“The most
that can be reasonably be expected of linguistic theory is that it shall provide an evaluation procedure for grammars”

and
“probabilistic models give no particular insight into some of the basic problems of syntactic structure”

(
Chomsky, 1957
)
.
In AI, the book
Perceptrons: An Introduction to Computational Geometry

(
Minsky and Papert, 1969
)
,
demonstrated key problems encountered by neural networks with the learning of non-additive functions, including
cases as simple as the logical XOR. As key inventors of early neural networks, Minsky and Papert emphasized
in their book that their objections were not meant to divide the field, but rather that research on neural networks would fade
as other ideas took over; the 1988 edition included an epilogue saying
“
In any case, the 1970s became the golden age of a new field of research into the representation of knowledge.
”
The new techniques listed as part of this ’golden age’ included word-expert parsers, nonmonotonic logic, and planning procedures,
which led many researchers to “
set aside their interest in the study of learning in favor of examining the representation of knowledge
.” With hindsight, this is seen more as a dead-end than a golden age and the beginning of
a period that is sometimes called the “AI winter”. (Ironically, the term AI winter was also coined by Minsky,
during the 1980s, as a warning against what might happen if expert systems did not bear fruit.)
But for some decades, symbolic logic was considered to be the core of artificial intelligence and linguistic theory
(
Jones, 1994
)
and the most established textbooks on mathematical and computational linguistics from
this era focus on discrete symbolisms, barely mentioning statistics, probability, or continuous mathematical
foundations such as vectors and gradients
(
Partee et al., 1993
;
Allen, 1995
)
.

Today’s chatbots come from the very techniques that were supposed to have been left behind:
machine learning in general and neural networks in particular are the cornerstones of AI today.
During the 1980s, researchers including Hinton and LeCun developed the backpropagation method,
which enables deep neural networks to be trained efficiently, by passing feedback on classification results back
through layers in the network, using the chain rule from multivariable calculus
(
Rumelhart et al., 1986
)
.
Neural nets built in this way started to outperform expert systems at several tasks, for example image recognition for handwritten digits
(
LeCun et al., 1995
)
. In 1997, Deep Blue, a chess computer program invented by Hsu, a doctoral student at Carnegie Mellon University, defeated the world chess champion Gary Kasparov, relying on a massively parallel expert fuzzy-rule-based system and
effective use of a dataset of Grandmaster games
(
Campbell et al., 2002
)
. In 2000s, the first neural net model for next-word prediction
was trained successfully to produce relatively consistent natural language phrases, over longer context distances
than those achieved with
n
-gram models. AI was beginning to produce systems that outperformed humans on complex symbolic
tasks and, rather than doing this using rules created by human domain experts, this was achieved by leveraging fast massive
computation.
The game of Go, more combinatorically demanding than chess by a wide margin, was mastered by the Alpha Go system,
using databases of real games for training, and then even more strikingly, by Alpha Go Zero, which learned
solely by playing against itself, with no human supervision except for being given the rules and the resources to simulate play

(
Silver et al., 2017
)
.

Early on, attempts at AI development focused on creating systems which would excel at specific tasks, assuming that such systems put together would create a human-like, general intelligence: for example, Shannon
famously proposed that the game of chess would be an ideal challenge for developing intelligent systems, since it is

generally considered to require “thinking” for skilful play

(
Shannon, 1950
)
.
Based on the combinatoric explosion of possible chess positions, Shannon argued that a brute-force approach, leveraging large computational power, would at best enable the creation of a weak chess player.
But, fast forward to a few decades later and it turns out that one can design an extremely effective chess system, mainly by applying brute-force computation over large datasets. This is both an insight and a challenge to humans;
some of the most poignant reflections on this theme have been written by Kasparov himself
(
Kasparov, 2017
)
.

AI agents have become better by carefully leveraging larger example datasets and computational resources, a process which contrasts with the
way humans accomplish similar expertise, typically requiring years of devotion
Gladwell, 2008
.
Understandably, this is often unwelcome news to human experts, and the theme

that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin
has even been dubbed “The Bitter Lesson”
(
Sutton, 2019
)
.
But, the two questions, are we trying to construct systems which behave intelligently and are we trying to re-create human intelligence, are not the same, though the recognition of this difference has taken some time

(
Russell and Norvig, 2022
, Ch 1)
.

The large language models (LLMs) that drive modern chatbots are a striking example of this engineering approach. They are
built using neural networks to predict sequences of word-embedding vectors, the most recent key ingredient being
the use of attention mechanisms, which enable sequential behaviour to be modelled using parallel computation
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
. Subsequent models, including GPT-3
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
, Llama
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
, and DeepSeek
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
, are trained and optimized to whole new levels of performance, though the core components
of word embeddings, feed-forward neural networks, and attention-heads using projection (with the whole system trained to
optimize a loss-function throughout the network using backpropagation) are fundamentally those established in the
transformer model of
Vaswani et al., 2017
.

These models use none of the syntactic parsers, logical forms, or knowledge representation techniques that dominated
much of late-1900s AI. Instead, they work much as
(
Turing, 1950
)
suggested: a ‘child machine’ is
initialized with enormous computational resources and relatively few built-in procedures, just enough to learn from masses of training examples, after which it performs well —
so well that its output, as exemplified by modern chatbots, produces language successfully enough that it would be considered
a clear sign of intelligence in many established tests.

6
History of (Artificial) Intelligence Tests and Moving the Goal Posts

Some of the early approaches attempted to test intelligence in terms of sensory ability
(
Galton, 1883
;
Jensen, 2011
)
,
an idea which was criticized by Wilhelm Wundt, who favoured complex cognitive thought, such as problem solving
(
Blumenthal, 1975
;
Wundt, 1896
)
.
Wundt’s ideas influenced the first standardised intelligence test, the Binet-Simon scale, and the idea of mental age, as a way to characterise developmental delays in intelligence (
Binet and Simon, 1907
).

The Binet-Simon scale inspired later intelligence tests, such as the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS;
(
Wechsler, 1939
)
) and, in its US reincarnation
(
Terman, 1916
)
, the Army Alpha (verbal-based) and Beta (non-verbal, for e.g. non-English speakers) tests, originally used for screening US Army recruits during the first world war
(
Yerkes, 1921
)
.
This work also led to the term intelligence quotient (IQ), the ratio of mental to chronological age
(
Stern, 1912
)
.
At around that time, researchers started recognising how some of these tests are culturally biased, favouring those from Western, educated, industrialised backgrounds
(
Bond, 1934
)
.
Such concerns led to the development of ostensibly culturally fair and non-verbal, such as Raven’s Progressive Matrices
(
Raven, 1938
)
and the culture-fair intelligence test
(
Cattell, 1943
)
,
an effort which continues to this date
(
Nisbett et al., 2012
)
.
An interesting assumption in these proposals is that verbal ability is largely due to education and so should not be part of intelligence testing.
Today, successors to the Stanford-Binet test
(
Terman, 1916
)
and the WAIS test are still widely used, in e.g. clinical diagnosis, education, and cognitive functioning evaluations.
Pertinently, there is evidence that the more recent chatbots (such as GPT-4) can solve a variety of problems used in IQ tests, including in mathematics, verbal reasoning, comprehension of complex ideas etc.
(
Bubeck et al., 2023
)
,
as well as perform highly in standard college applicant aptitude tests, such as the Stanford Achievement Test (SAT) and the Graduate Record Examination (GRE;
OpenAI, 2023
).
Strong performance has already been observed with the type of problems typical in Raven’s Progressive Matrices test
(
Webb et al., 2023
)
and the recent versions of the WAIS. In February 2025, Grok AI announced expert-level achievements
on advanced math (AIME), science (GPQA), and programming (LiveCodeBench) tests
(
xAI Team, 2025
)
. Where recent chatbots (such as GPT-4) do not perform as well, this can often be traced to technical limitations of such models (e.g., visual processing;
Hernández-Orallo, 2017
).

Using tests designed for human examinees to benchmark AI systems has attracted recent attention, enabled by the sophistication of today’s AI models. In previous decades, AI
testing methodologies have typically focused on basic competencies, such as proportion of working transistors. Software tests have traditionally been carried out at different levels: unit testing
checks that individual functions perform their calculations correctly; integration testing checks that the parts,
when assembled, demonstrate the expected overall system behaviours; and regression testing checks that an updated
version or specific instance of a system does not unexpectedly fall behind established performance expectations.
None of these processes has been called ‘intelligence testing’, though
there are analogies with the approaches of
(
Binet and Simon, 1907
)
and educational testing since:
for example, the 2 times table is such a simple test, it should be passed by all students and assumed to be
known thereafter.

Evaluation in machine learning has gradually adopted a more statistical approach, as outlined in Section
5
. Instead of asking human experts to engineer rules for how to classify objects as a basis for (initially) relevance judgments
(
Cleverdon et al., 1966
)
and (later on) AI, human experts were asked to judge what the results should be. These judgements or annotations are then used as training examples for assessing AI accuracy.
Examples include relevance judgments for document retrieval, what characters should be recognized in
a handwriting sample, and whether an email
message is spam or junk mail
(
Cormack et al., 2008
)
.
The use of standardized measures on particular datasets raises important questions, including sampling bias and whether a good benchmark result
guarantees appropriate behaviour in real applications. Another challenge is that human judges do not always agree:
measuring and studying inter-annotator agreement, or inter-rater reliability, is an important part of the
methodology
(
Hallgren, 2012
)
, though once an annotated dataset is available, it is common for
machine learning evaluations to ignore this issue, even referring to labels from human annotators as ‘ground truth’.
Notwithstanding these issues, the gradual adoption
of established datasets and quantitative evaluations is part of what has enabled AI to make consistent progress since the
1990s, including the use of BLEU scores to evaluate machine translation
(
Bahdanau et al., 2014
;
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
,
and the development of comprehensive sets of language processing benchmarks designed to cover a variety of competencies

(
Wang, 2018
)
.

Evaluation of systems by competing directly with human performance has been less of a motivating force in AI until recently. Initially, it was obvious that computers were faster and more reliable at arithmetic calculations
(this is what they were built for), so there was little point in comparing human with mechanical performance,
just as we would not race a powered vehicle against a human runner. By contrast, most other human behaviours
regarded as intelligent were clearly beyond early computers. Games like chess and Go
became exceptions, but organized competitions for Turing-like tests (particularly the prize offering by
AI inventor Hugh Loebner) have declined since the late 2010s
(
Singh and Beniwal, 2022
)
(though just
as this paper was being written, the experiments of
(
Jones and Bergen, 2025
)
found that GPT-4.5 fooled human judges
into choosing it as the ‘real’ human in an astonishing 73% of cases).
The recent strong performance of AI systems on traditional intelligence tests for humans is not the culmination
of decades in pursuit of this goal: it is more the result of decades of progress in lower-level benchmark
evaluations, with the surprising outcome that systems trained to predict next words in a document or appropriate
answers to relatively simple questions ended up performing well on tests designed for humans.

By the early 2010s, it was already clear that natural language processing systems could reach human-level performance on some
language tasks, including syntactic tagging, information extraction, and textual inference.
This motivated the development of new tests designed to highlight areas that are easy for humans,
and where AI was demonstrably still weak. For example, the Winograd schema challenge (named after a pioneering advocate for the study of ‘common sense’ in AI) asked simple
questions like:

The trophy doesn’t fit in the brown suitcase because it’s too big. What is too big?

Answer 0: the trophy. Answer 1: the suitcase.

(
Levesque et al., 2012
)

As emphasized by
(
Chollet, 2019
)
, a pioneer of more general tests in AI, challenges
like this are designed to stimulate, not to disparage, progress in AI, and sure enough, several
systems had successfully tackled the Winograd schema challenge by 2020
(
Kocijan et al., 2023
)
.

Chollet, 2019
proposed a set of challenges called the Abstract Reasoning Corpus (ARC),
which focus on shapes in grid patterns and how they might interact physically: for example,
it is easy for humans to see that a red shape is moved until it collides with a blue shape and to apply the
same ‘rule’ immediately to the next scene. As of early 2025, performance of AI systems on ARC tests
has leaped dramatically and, while there is discussion on whether the test has been conclusively passed (due particularly to energy considerations – the official ARC test included energy-consumption limits that were breached by GPT-03),

Chollet, 2024
praises the groundbreaking progress in AI.

It is easy to find evidence that connecting words with images in an exact fashion is still challenging for
AI systems. State-of-the-art AI systems can generate stylish images
from text prompts, but without preserving key facts: for example, a C major scale should have
the notes CDEFGABC, and a chessboard at the beginning of the game should have exactly 64 squares and 32 pieces;
AI-generated images still fail to meet these simple requirements (Figure

). Such gaps are perhaps even more surprising given the achievements of these models at tasks considered hard for humans (including the skilful drawing of shape,
texture, and shading).

Summarizing the apparent trends, in many IQ tests, chatbots already outperform most humans and it should not be long before this is the case for all IQ tests. GPT-4’s own estimate of when this will happen is 2030. Such demonstrations invite a criticism that AI systems can accomplish this with vastly more resources, compared to human brains. For example, GPT-4 has approximately one trillion parameters and an estimated cost of about $100M to train, while Deep Blue had about 14,000 specialized chess chips.
However, comparing complexity between human and artificial agents is not straightforward. First, the human brain is thought to have more than 80 billion neurons and 100 trillion neuronal connections
(
Azevedo et al., 2009
;
Herculano-Houzel, 2012
)
,
leading some scientists to claim that it is “…the most complicated object in the known universe”.
Second, the brain has optimised many hardwired structures, making it efficient in cognitive tasks related to our physical constraints, environment, and needs.
This optimisation has taken place across hundreds of millions of years for some generic abilities (e.g., visual perception; the time references the emergence of the first mammals,
Kemp, 2005
) and across hundreds of thousands of years for cognitive abilities more specific to humans
(the time references the appearance of Homo Sapiens,
Hublin et al., 2017
).
So, while it might appear that some of these AI systems have frightening complexity, our brains are not exactly simple either.

Another possible objection is that some of the AI solutions to particular problems may appear uncompelling, because they involve brute force approaches. For example, Deep Blue can evaluate approximately 200 million positions per second and, even though it uses various pruning algorithms to reduce calculation, it still searches through millions of moves
Campbell et al., 2002
. Cholet’s
Chollet, 2019
ARC test, intended to be the new gold standard regarding testing for AI, has recently been partially overcome, by GPT-o3
Chollet, 2024
. Recent developments leading to this achievement include
Chain-of-Thought prompting, which automates the process of breaking a problem into basic steps
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
, and test-time scaling, which devotes extra computational resources at inference time to check and revise answers
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
. The techniques used by such systems could be described as successful implementations of the proverbial advice “think before you speak”. Arguably, the success of inference-time improvements is just the application of brute force computation outside the training process.
However, the human brain is also an example of a brute force approach to intelligence, if not in how we solve problems, certainly in how evolution has optimised cognitive processes for the range of problems relevant to us.
We suggest that the reluctance to call e.g. Deep Blue intelligent is less so about its brute force approach and more about domain specificity: we are unlikely to call intelligent an agent who excels (however exceptionally) in a single domain (cf. the distinction between skill and intelligence,
(
Chollet, 2019
)
).

Finally, it may look that, as soon as an artificial system passes the current gold standard test for intelligence, we decide that the test was inadequate and invent a more elaborate one. That is, every time an AI test is passed, we move the goal posts and change our definition of intelligence, (apparently) deliberately so as to keep out the most sophisticated AI system. A critical perspective would be that we just rig the game to retain a perception of superiority. Some such examples are noted above (e.g., chess), but there are others:having a flawless conversation with another human or translating a document into other languages would have been unquestioned signs of intelligence, until computers could do such tasks. An alternative perspective is that revisions in AI tests simply reflect an increasing understanding of what intelligence is.

7
The Relation between Embodiment and Intelligence

Human intelligence is tied to the way we perceive and manipulate objects in the world
(
Wilson et al., 2021
)
), as well as our cultural and social context
(
Cobb, 2001
)
. One can ask what is the value of intelligence for solving embodiment problems — we have developed a wide range of tools, which allow successful embodied problem-solving.
Some researchers go as far as to suggest that all knowledge we have is either embodied or has its origins in embodied knowledge
(
Johnson and Lakoff, 2008
;
Lakoff and Johnson, 1999
)
.
For example, even in the most abstract scientific theories we have constructed, such as quantum mechanics, some of the basic ideas are extensions of embodied concepts (e.g., waves and particles); the most profound conceptual challenges in such theories come from ideas with no embodied analogues (e.g., superposition, non-locality;
Einstein et al., 1935
).

Could one rephrase the main question in this work from “is chat GPT intelligent”, to “can chat GPT think”?
However tempting the equivalence between the two questions, it is misleading, since the latter version is too anthropomorphic to be useful.
This helps bring into focus the anthropocentric bias throughout the literature regarding human intelligence and AI tests. As we saw, this has been recognised, in that there has been effort in inventing ‘disembodied’ tests of intelligence, not tied to our specific embodiment constraints.
However, this approach has led to an over-emphasis on intelligence tests based on language and manipulation of information through linguistic means. Chatbots expose a fatal weakness to such tests, because, even though they are supremely capable wordsmiths, we are left uncertain as to whether they are intelligent or not. Arguably, as shown in Figure
2
, key problems with state-of-the-art chatbots are less so about the question “Can chatbots think?” but rather about the question “Can chatbots see what they are doing?” — that is, the problem is not about the thought process, but about basic awareness.
Indeed, Figure
2
highlights major problems with state-of-the-art chatbots,
which translated into human terms would not be “Can chatbots think?” but “Can multimodal AI systems
see what they’re doing?”: the problem is not the quality of the thought process, but lack of perceptual ability. Note, this point is separate from recognising that
some
means of abstractly expressing knowledge (not necessarily human-like language) is essential for intelligence.

There is no doubt that to understand human intelligence, we have to consider embodiment. Likewise, some artificial intelligence researchers have argued that artificial agents need some form of embodiment (e.g., through robotics;

Anderson, 2003
), before they can develop intelligence. Regarding chatbots, given that they lack any aspect of embodiment and their only basis for learning is input from language, it is unsurprising that any intelligence they develop would be different from ours. However, ideally our notion of intelligence would be versatile enough to recognise different kinds of intelligence (e.g., based on agents embodied in different ways or no embodiment at all).
That is, to understand what it means to be human and human intelligence, we need embodiment, but our notion of intelligence in general should not be tied to a particular embodiment. Of course, historically we (humans) have been appropriating the notion of general intelligence to mean human intelligence, though we (the authors) think current evidence suggests otherwise (that is, that there can be general intelligence unlike ours).

The discussion of embodiment can perhaps help disentangle skill from intelligence, a challenge which arises both for human and artificial intelligence (c.f., Aristotle’s
Metaphysics
, Bk 1, Ch1, and
(
Chollet, 2019
)
).
We are part of a culture that has assumed, for some centuries, that penmanship is intelligence, whereas manual dexterity is “mere” skill, and the use of physical force is sometimes disparaged as brutish.
A cardinal example is the phrase “the pen is mightier than the sword” — an adage celebrated even on the walls of the Library of Congress (the quote is from an 1839 play by Edward Bulwer-Lytton, in the mouth of Cardinal Richelieu, First Minister of France from 1622 to 1642). If we remove the warlike associations and propose more blandly that “the pen is more useful than the knife”, then the claim is
obviously not true in general and depends on the work to be done.
For example, knives did not depend on pens for their invention, but the other way round. So, is the use of a pen or a knife more indicative of human intelligence? One way to address these questions is by arguing that intelligent behaviour does not depend on whether the task involves language or mathematics or manual labour etc. Rather, the key factor is the complexity of the task.

8
Our Proposal

Chatbots have fundamentally challenged our notion of intelligence. One reason is the role of (especially written) language in human intelligence and civilization.
Language has been a key facilitator of progress in our species, by allowing inventions which persist beyond the individual, collaboration/ role differentiation, and communities of knowledge
(
Fernbach and Sloman, 2018
)
. Historically, human linguistic ability has been pillar in our self-concept of intelligence and assumption of superiority — so, with the emergence of chatbots, an observer might say that we have to either declare chatbots intelligent or argue that being a wordsmith is no longer enough and change the rules for determining intelligence that we have insisted on for centuries.

We think there are some aspects of the intelligence debate which can be resolved fairly simply. To summarise, first, intelligence is more than just skill, however exceptional, whether this skill concerns arithmetic ability (we do not call a calculator intelligent), agility in some sport, or language
(
Chollet, 2019
)
. A conundrum is that, for example, a human who is exceptionally good at arithmetic might be called intelligent, but not a machine designed for this purpose. The resolution concerns generality of scope: intelligence implies a degree of generality, adaptability, and transferability. Regarding chatbots, if they were exceptionally good just in language, we would be (more) reluctant to decide that they are intelligent; the evidence suggests that their capability exceeds language (e.g.,
(
xAI Team, 2025
)
). Second, there is a criticism that we should not talk about intelligence before understanding what intelligence is in the first place. It would help to have such an understanding. However, there is a long list of concepts we have studied successfully prior to understanding what they are, such as life, love, space, gravitation etc. If we agree that certain examples of behaviour reflect intelligence, we can use these examples as a guide of when we have intelligence. Third, speaking of such outcomes, the ultimate outcomes of human intelligence are our scientific, cultural, and artistic achievements; so we can use these as a guide regarding whether chatbots are intelligent.

As
Turing, 1950
noted,
there may be a problem that, in assessing AI against such outcomes, we are comparing average AI against the very best of humans.
We think the issue is what human intelligence is capable of, rather than the intelligence of the average human. Indeed, in studies of animal intelligence a similar approach has been adopted (e.g.,
(
Range et al., 2011
)
,
(
Güntürkün et al., 2024
)
). It is also true that a huge enabling factor for even the most celebrated scientists, artists etc. is the vast community of knowledge we have, largely thanks to written language — as Newton famously said, ”if I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants.” However, we can recognise intelligence (for both humans and otherwise) both at the individual level and species level. Most organisms benefit from a degree of evolutionary intelligence, which often leads to amazing competencies – humans benefit from evolutionary intelligence too, however, with the advent of written language, the contribution from communities of knowledge has been far important.

There are a few more issues which we think are straightforward. Should it matter how an intelligent behaviour is produced, before we characterise an animal/ system etc. as intelligent? For example, does it matter whether similar priors were employed in solving a problem
(
Chollet, 2019
)
? We think not, because the means of achieving an intelligent behaviour would depend on many factors, including hardwired vs. learnt structures, embodiment constraints, collaboration opportunity with other agents etc. Also, relatedly, does it matter whether some behaviour is accomplished more efficiently by one agent vs. less so by another? For similar reasons, we think not.

With these thoughts in mind, our proposal is as follows. We suggest that intelligence entails invention, which presupposes the ability to learn new skills, imagination/ creativity, hypothesis testing including trial and error (the ability to turn error and novelty into opportunity), and generalisation. Consider penicillin: the basic discovery was a matter of trial and error, but then Fleming’s genius as an inventor came from imagining how useful that error could be: this is an example of invention combining trial and error, hypothesis making, and generalisation
(
Fleming, 1929
)
. Intelligence also requires capacity for complex inference, knowledge, and the ability to create general thoughts. That is, intelligent agents must have some mechanism for organising elementary thoughts and pieces of information into a complex narrative. The catalytic role of language is yet again apparent, since it provides a fairly precise way to organise and work on large amounts of knowledge.
Next, if we consider some of the most impressive accomplishments of human intelligence, such as the creation of scientific theories, would the above suffice? To develop such theories, we think that an agent needs to have awareness of its own understanding and so how this understanding is limited; for this to be possible, a prerequisite is a sense that the agent is distinguished from its environment. That is, together with the above, we think that the process of doing (notably) science is self-referential: it relates to what we do not understand and what we would like to understand/ how we can go beyond our current knowledge. Therefore, we suggest that an intelligent agent has to be self-aware and understand the distinction between itself and its environment. Finally, we think that intelligence requires embodiment and knowledge of its embodiment. For most agents, embodiment is what drives behaviour, because embodiment creates needs for resources. Also, embodiment constrains the means of acquiring knowledge about the environment and provides ground truths.

To summarise, we propose there are four elements to intelligence, which can be labelled as invention, inference, self-awareness, and embodiment. To the perennial question of whether necessity or curiosity is the mother of invention, we think both. Our proposal implies a graded approach to intelligence which encompasses most other living organisms. In particular, we have to accept that many mammals are intelligent to some degree, in a way very similar to humans; additionally, for many other organisms, there is a species-wide intelligence, which approximates our powerful communities of knowledge. Theological implications aside, this phrase captures well the catalysing importance of language in human achievement.

What about chatbots? They share this powerful tool with us, but they seem to lack some of the other characteristics of intelligence. They lack knowledge of their embodiment and so they have no needs and no curiosity — this limits what they could be doing. They are not self-aware (that we know of) and so this limits their capacity to reflect on what they know and what they could be trying to discover.
However, they seem to be capable of inference, as is evidenced by their ability to follow through complex arguments. Regarding whether they are capable of invention, when chatbots produce something new, we tend to say they are hallucinating. Is this unfair dismissal of what might be capacity for creativity? The boundaries between imagination, creativity, and just getting things wrong are difficult to pin down. Nevertheless, for facts which can be easily verified and without insight of diverging from some established belief, we think it would be hard to justify a statement as creative vs. just wrong and chatbot hallucinations seem to be instances of the latter.
We apply a similar criterion to human thought, for example, false memories in legal proceedings are not considered instances of creativity (e.g.,
Howe, 2013
); however, using the term ’hallucinations’ for chatbots is alarmist and obscures the fact that there similar human behaviours.

This does not mean that our jobs are safe from chatbots: chatbots could replace humans in any task which is reduced to wordsmithing and the simple but somewhat embarrassing truth is that unique human competencies invariably correspond to the animal stuff — tasks in which we excel because of our particular embodiment and for which a human empathic connection is useful. This is not necessarily a bad thing: perhaps if we, as a species, revised our perception of how unique our intelligence is, we might approach each other, our environment, and other species with a bit more humility. Equally, the idea that just training bigger and better chatbots will eventually lead us to true Artificial General Intelligence is probably wrong: insofar as rapid symbol-manipulation and enormous memory are part of intelligence, chatbots already surpass human capabilities,
but also demonstrate that there is more to intelligence than the heavily-emphasized ability to write intelligent words.
