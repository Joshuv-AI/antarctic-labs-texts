Psychotic-like experiences and non-suicidal self-injury in the community population: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Rite Si, Chenxi Wang, Yushen Ding, Mathangie Gananathan, Hang Liu, Hexuan Liu, Qijing Bo, Tingting Zhao, Fuchun Zhou, Chuanyue Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of the study was to systematically examine the association between psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in community populations through meta-analysis, with a focus on prospective evidence regarding this association and potential psychosocial correlates.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, PsycINFO, and Embase from inception to August 2025. Studies reporting quantitative data on the association between PLEs and NSSI in community populations were included. A random-effects meta-analysis was performed to estimate the pooled odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). This association was further examined through a preliminary prospective meta-analysis of two longitudinal studies reporting adjusted odds ratios for the prediction of incident NSSI by baseline PLEs. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored by exploratory subgroup analyses across study design, geographical region, gender composition, NSSI assessment methods, and assessment time window match between PLEs and NSSI.ResultsEight studies involving 19,719 participants were included in the meta-analysis, and an additional eleven studies were included in the narrative synthesis. Individuals with PLEs had a significantly increased odds of NSSI compared to those without PLEs (OR = 3.09, 95% CI [2.05–4.66], p < 0.001). A prospective analysis of two longitudinal studies indicated that baseline PLEs significantly predicted incident NSSI at follow-up (OR = 2.17, 95% CI [1.56–3.02]). The estimated prevalence of PLEs and NSSI in community populations was 11.0% (95% CI [7.9%-15.2%]) and 6.9% (95% CI [4.3%-11.1%]), respectively. Narrative synthesis suggested that depressive symptoms, childhood adversity, bullying victimization, and pandemic-related stress were associated with higher odds of NSSI, whereas social support was associated with lower odds.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrate a robust association between PLEs and NSSI in community populations. Preliminary prospective evidence from a limited number of longitudinal studies further suggests that baseline PLEs may predict incident NSSI at follow-up, although this finding warrants confirmation in future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13034-026-01103-7.Keywords: Psychotic-like experiences, Non-suicidal self-injury, Prospective association, Community population, Depression

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) refers to the deliberate and repetitive infliction of harm to one’s own body tissue without suicidal intent, such as cutting, burning, biting, or scratching the skin [1]. Increasingly recognized as a significant public health concern, NSSI has been linked to a range of adverse mental health outcomes and heightened risk for later psychopathology. Under the DSM-5 framework, NSSI is further characterized by the presence of persistent urges or thoughts of self-injury and associated distress or functional impairment, underscoring its clinical significance [2]. Conceptually, it is regarded as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy that distinguishes it from both socially sanctioned behaviors and suicidal acts [3].

Given its clinical significance and public health implications, understanding factors associated with NSSI has become increasingly important. Recent research has drawn attention to the potential link between NSSI and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs)—subclinical phenomena mirroring psychotic symptoms such as hallucinations, delusional ideation, and disorganized thinking, which occur below the diagnostic threshold for psychotic disorders [4]. PLEs are relatively prevalent in the general population, particularly among adolescents and young adults, with estimates ranging from 5 to 15% [5]. Although these experiences may reflect transient, normative aspects of development, their persistence or severity has been linked to elevated risk for psychopathology, especially among individuals with familial liability for schizophrenia or bipolar disorder [6]. Emerging evidence further suggests that PLEs may be associated with NSSI, highlighting their potential role as early indicators of psychological vulnerability [7–9].

Despite growing evidence linking psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), critical gaps remain in understanding their relationship. A previous meta-analysis confirmed this association but included few studies focused specifically on NSSI [7]. Furthermore, NSSI—distinct from suicidality—is more common and has unique implications for daily functioning in community populations. This analysis also did not explore the influence of potential psychosocial correlates. The majority of studies included were cross-sectional, limiting the ability to examine the prospective association between PLEs-NSSI relationship. In the decade since, several longitudinal studies with improved methodologies and updated epidemiological data have emerged; however, no comprehensive synthesis has yet integrated these developments.

This meta-analysis addresses these gaps by: (1) focusing specifically on NSSI to clarify its distinct association with PLEs; (2) incorporating longitudinal studies to prospectively examine whether baseline PLEs are associated with incident NSSI, thereby providing preliminary prospective evidence regarding this relationship; (3) updating the evidence base with studies published after 2016 to reflect contemporary findings. By addressing these questions, this study aims to inform early identification strategies and targeted interventions for at-risk populations.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024551713) and conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [10].

Two independent investigators (CXW and RTS) performed a comprehensive systematic literature search across three major electronic databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, and Embase) from database inception through August 26, 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms with free-text keywords across title and abstract fields, adapted for each database, encompassing two concept domains: (1) PLEs (including terms such as psychotic exp*, subclinical psych*, subthreshold psych*, attenuated psych*, psychotic-like, psychotic sympt*, delusion*, hallucinat*, schizotyp*, prodrom*, ultra-high risk, clinical high risk, APS, at-risk mental state, CHR, UHR, and ARMS) and (2) self-injurious behaviors (including suic*, parasuic*, self-harm, selfharm, self-injur*, and self injur*). These concept domains were combined using the Boolean operator "AND."

To ensure comprehensive literature coverage, we additionally conducted manual searches of reference lists from all included studies, relevant review articles, and existing meta-analyses to identify potentially eligible studies that might have been missed in the electronic database search. The full search strategies for all three databases are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for this review were defined as follows:

Inclusion criteria:

Population: Studies involving adolescents or young adults presenting with subclinical psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), defined as psychosis-like symptoms assessed using validated self-report instruments with established cut-off scores or structured clinical interviews for psychosis risk, in individuals who did not meet diagnostic criteria for any psychotic disorder according to the DSM or ICD.

Comparison: Studies including a control group of individuals who screened negative for PLEs using the same or equivalent validated instruments.

Outcomes: Studies reporting effect size estimates for the association between PLEs and NSSI, including odds ratios (OR), risk ratios (RR), or incidence rate ratios (IRR) with corresponding measures of variance (e.g., 95% confidence intervals or standard errors), or providing sufficient raw data (i.e., event counts and sample sizes) to calculate these estimates. For longitudinal studies, adjusted odds ratios for the prospective association between baseline PLEs and incident NSSI at follow-up were additionally extracted. Studies relevant to the research question but lacking sufficient quantitative data for meta-analysis were retained for narrative synthesis.

Study design: Observational studies with cross-sectional, case–control, or cohort designs.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers (CXW and RTS) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review, to identify eligible studies. The same reviewers subsequently extracted data using a standardized extraction form. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion with the third reviewer (YSD) and a senior researcher (FCZ). When an article that met the inclusion criteria did not provide sufficient data for meta-analysis, the corresponding or first author was contacted via email with up to two reminder emails sent at 2-week intervals for additional information. In cases where multiple publications from the same cohort contained potentially overlapping data, the authors were contacted to clarify the extent of overlap, and only the publication with the most comprehensive dataset was included to avoid double-counting participants. For longitudinal studies, adjusted odds ratios for the prospective association between baseline PLEs and incident NSSI at follow-up were directly extracted from each study.

For studies that met the inclusion criteria but lacked sufficient quantitative data for meta-analysis, two reviewers (CXW and RTS) independently extracted key findings related to the PLEs-NSSI relationship for narrative synthesis, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

Quality assessment

The same two reviewers (CXW and RTS) assessed the methodological quality of included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data [11]—selected because all included studies reported cross-sectional prevalence data of NSSI regardless of overall study design. Each quality assessment item was rated using a standardized classification system: "Yes" (criterion fully met), "No" (criterion not met), "Unclear" (insufficient information provided), or "Not Applicable" (criterion irrelevant to the study design). Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through consensus discussion or, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (YSD). Quality assessment was conducted for the eight studies included in the meta-analysis. Studies retained for narrative synthesis only were not formally appraised.

Statistical methods

Meta-analysis of effect sizes

Data analysis was conducted using R version 4.3.2, utilizing the "meta" and "metafor" packages. For the primary outcome, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the association between PLEs and NSSI. Inter-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q-test and the I2 statistic. The I2 statistic quantified the proportion of total variation attributable to heterogeneity, with I2 ≥ 50% indicating high heterogeneity [12]. If the Q-test yielded a P-value < 0.05, indicating significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation was applied for data synthesis, which accounts for both within-study and between-study variability. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used for data pooling.

Prospective analysis

To provide preliminary prospective evidence on the PLEs-NSSI association, we extracted adjusted odds ratios (ORs) directly reported in longitudinal studies for the prospective association between baseline PLEs and incident NSSI at follow-up. One longitudinal study (Stanyon et al.) was excluded from this analysis, as it reported effects specific to auditory hallucinations rather than broader PLEs, representing a more circumscribed exposure definition than that used in the other two longitudinal studies. A random-effects model using the REML estimator was applied to pool the extracted ORs from the remaining two longitudinal studies, with heterogeneity assessed using the I2 statistic.

Meta-analysis of prevalence

For the secondary outcomes, pooled prevalence estimates of PLEs and NSSI in community populations were calculated using a random-effects model using the REML estimator, as implemented in the R meta package. The I2 statistic was used to assess heterogeneity across studies. Studies using clinical high-risk samples or non-community-based sampling frames were excluded from prevalence analyses, as their estimates would not reflect population-level prevalence. Accordingly, one study utilizing a clinical high-risk sample matched with healthy controls was excluded from the prevalence meta-analysis but retained in the primary outcome analysis.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted on an exploratory basis to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity in the primary outcome. Given the limited number of included studies, these analyses were not powered to formally test between-subgroup differences and were intended to identify patterns that might inform future research rather than to support confirmatory conclusions. Considering potential cultural and methodological differences, the following categorical variables were examined: (1) geographic region (Asia vs. other countries); (2) study design (longitudinal vs. cross-sectional); (3) gender composition (male/female ratio ≥ 0.6 vs. < 0.6); (4) NSSI assessment methods (self-developed questionnaire vs. standardized scale); (5) assessment time window match between PLEs and NSSI (matched vs. mismatched). Studies were classified as 'matched' if both PLEs and NSSI were assessed over the same or comparable time frames (e.g., both lifetime, or both within the past year), and 'mismatched' otherwise. For the secondary outcomes of prevalence, only geographic region and gender composition were examined in subgroup analyses based on data availability. Between-subgroup differences were evaluated using the χ2 test. Additionally, a scatter plot of sample size versus effect size was created to visually assess the presence of small-study effects.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Publication bias was assessed using Egger's regression test and visual inspection of funnel plots [13]. A P-value < 0.05 in Egger's test or apparent asymmetry in the funnel plot was considered indicative of potential publication bias. To evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect size, sensitivity analyses were performed using a leave-one-out approach, sequentially removing each study to determine whether any single study disproportionately influenced the overall results. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

Study selection and characteristics of included studies

A total of 19 articles were included in the systematic review. Of these, eight were incorporated into a meta-analysis, and the remaining eleven were included in the narrative synthesis. The publication years of the included studies ranged from 2010 to 2023 (Fig. 1). The 8 studies (4 longitudinal, 4 cross-sectional) encompassed 19,719 participants from Japan (n = 3), the UK (n = 2), Australia (n = 2), and Poland (n = 1). The mean ages of participants ranged from 12.0 to 25.3 years, and the proportions of female participants ranged from 47.0% to 71.6% (Table 1). Characteristics of the eleven studies included in the narrative synthesis are also presented in Table 1. Reasons why the eleven studies were retained for narrative synthesis only are provided in Supplementary Table S1. It should be noted that assessment time frames varied considerably across included studies. For PLEs, measurement windows ranged from lifetime to the past month; for NSSI, windows similarly ranged from lifetime to the past month. This heterogeneity in assessment time frames was noted and considered in the interpretation of results, particularly for the prospective analysis.

According to the JBI quality assessment tool, the overall quality of included studies was generally adequate (Supplementary Table S2). All studies used appropriate sampling frames, adequate sample sizes, clearly described settings, employed valid methods to assess PLEs and NSSI, and applied appropriate statistical analyses. However, standardized measurement conditions were unclear in 7 studies (87.5%), and 3 studies (37.5%) had limitations in recruitment methods.

Association between PLEs and NSSI

Meta-analysis of 8 studies revealed a significant positive association between PLEs and NSSI, with a pooled OR of 3.09 (95% CI: 2.05–4.66), indicating a substantially increased odds of NSSI among individuals with PLEs (Fig. 2). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 83.5%, P < 0.001).

Meta-analysis of studies examining the association between psychotic experiences and non-suicidal self-injury

Publication bias assessment showed no evidence of significant asymmetry. The funnel plot appeared symmetrical (Supplementary Figure S1), and Egger's test yielded a non-significant result (P = 0.615). A scatter plot of sample size versus effect size showed no significant correlation (Spearman's ρ = − 0.381, P = 0.36), indicating no apparent small-study effect (Supplementary Figure S2). However, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of included studies.

Prevalence of PLEs and NSSI in community populations

Seven of the eight meta-analytic studies were included in the prevalence analyses; one study using a clinical high-risk sample was excluded as previously described in the Methods section. Pooled prevalence analyses indicated that PLEs occurred in 11.0% of individuals in community populations (95% CI: 7.9%–15.2%), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 98.1%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S3). The prevalence of NSSI was 6.9% (95% CI: 4.3%–11.1%), also exhibiting considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 99.2%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S4).

Prospective association between baseline PLEs and incident NSSI

To provide preliminary prospective evidence on the PLEs-NSSI association, a prospective analysis was conducted based on two longitudinal studies that reported adjusted odds ratios for the prediction of incident NSSI by baseline PLEs. The third longitudinal study (Stanyon et al.) was not included in this analysis, as it reported effects specific to auditory hallucinations rather than broader PLEs, representing a more circumscribed exposure definition. The pooled result indicated that individuals with PLEs at baseline had higher odds of developing incident NSSI at follow-up (OR = 2.17 [95% CI: 1.56, 3.02]), with no evidence of heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 0%, P = 0.444) (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analysis

Exploratory subgroup analyses for the primary outcome examined potential sources of heterogeneity in the association between PLEs and NSSI across five factors: geographic region, study design, gender composition, NSSI assessment methods, and assessment time window match between PLEs and NSSI (Table 2). No statistically significant between-subgroup differences were detected for any of these factors (all P > 0.05). Given the small number of studies included, these p-values should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence across subgroups, and any numerical variation in point estimates between subgroups should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of meaningful subgroup differences. Descriptively, point estimates differed numerically across subgroups: (1) the OR for studies using standardized scales (OR = 4.59) was numerically higher than for those using self-developed questionnaires (OR = 2.84); (2) the OR for longitudinal studies (OR = 3.51) was numerically higher than for cross-sectional studies (OR = 2.78); (3) an additional subgroup analysis examining the match between PLEs and NSSI assessment time windows showed numerically different point estimates (matched: OR = 2.61 [1.27–5.37]; mismatched: OR = 3.50 [2.39–5.14]). However, given the small number of studies in each subgroup and the wide and overlapping confidence intervals, these numerical differences should not be overinterpreted as evidence of meaningful between-group differences. Considerable heterogeneity persisted within most subgroups, indicating that the identified factors did not fully account for the observed heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis of primary outcomes in the meta-analysis

Subgroup analyses for the secondary outcomes examined the prevalence of PLEs and NSSI across geographic regions and gender compositions (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). For PLEs prevalence, studies with higher female proportions reported higher prevalence (17% vs. 9%, P < 0.01). For NSSI prevalence, no significant differences were observed across subgroups, although point estimates suggested a similar trend by gender composition. Considerable heterogeneity persisted across all subgroups for both outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach demonstrated the robustness of the primary outcome (Supplementary Figure S5), indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall effect estimate.

Narrative synthesis

Narrative synthesis of 11 studies that could not be included in the meta-analysis provided complementary insights into the relationship between PLEs and NSSI. Regarding hallucination characteristics, negative or commenting auditory hallucinations were associated with persistent elevated odds of NSSI, whereas positive auditory content was associated with lower odds [21]. Persistent and incident, but not remittent, patterns of PLEs were associated with elevated NSSI severity [22].

Several studies suggested that depressive symptoms may play an important mediating role. Network analysis suggested that depression may represent a key pathway linking PLEs to NSSI, a pattern observed across both Chinese college student and Korean adolescent samples [8, 9]. Auditory hallucinations were indirectly associated with NSSI via traumatic life events, psychological distress, and diminished self-esteem [25]. Childhood adversity may also be indirectly associated with self-injury through its relationship with PLEs [23].

Several contextual factors associated with NSSI were also identified. Within vulnerable populations, basic self-disturbance in clinical high-risk adolescents was associated with elevated odds of NSSI [28]. Similarly, early childhood psychotic experiences in children at familial high risk of psychotic disorders were associated with elevated odds of NSSI [6]. Experiences of bullying were consistently associated with higher odds of NSSI, and pandemic-related stress was similarly linked to elevated NSSI among adolescents [8, 24]. Academic achievement and social support were associated with lower odds of NSSI [8, 9]. Additionally, approximately 40% of adolescents who self-harmed were found not to seek help, suggesting that poor help-seeking behavior may represent an additional contextual barrier [29].

Finally, longitudinal evidence suggested complex bidirectional associations among PLEs, NSSI, and environmental stressors, with PLEs longitudinally associated with subsequent bullying victimization through NSSI as a potential mediating pathway [20]. Emerging evidence also indicated that self-harm ideation may be prospectively associated with PLEs, suggesting possible bidirectional effects over time [22].

Discussion

This meta-analysis demonstrated a robust and significant association between PLEs and NSSI in community populations (OR = 3.09, 95% CI: 2.05–4.66, N = 19,719). Complementing this cross-sectional evidence, a prospective analysis of two longitudinal studies further indicated that individuals with PLEs at baseline had significantly higher odds of developing incident NSSI at follow-up (OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.56–3.02), providing preliminary prospective evidence consistent with a temporal pattern in this association. Furthermore, a narrative synthesis of the broader literature uncovered several factors associated with the PLEs-NSSI pathway. Together, these converging lines of evidence deepen our understanding of potential pathways linking PLEs to NSSI.

The findings of this study regarding the association between PLEs and NSSI are highly consistent with previous research while providing updated evidence and extending prior findings to include preliminary prospective evidence and psychosocial correlates. The observed effect size in this study (OR = 3.09) closely aligns with prior meta-analytic findings (OR = 3.20), suggesting the robustness of this association across different time periods and populations [7]. Convergent evidence from an independent, nationally representative sample of adults in England further corroborates this estimate, with any PLEs associated with an OR of 2.78 (95% CI: 1.88–4.11) for NSSI [30]. The consistency of these findings across a meta-analytic synthesis and an independent population-based study strengthens confidence in the magnitude of the PLEs-NSSI association.

The prospective analysis further suggested that PLEs at baseline were associated with elevated odds of incident NSSI at follow-up (OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.56–3.02), with no evidence of heterogeneity across the two longitudinal studies (I2 = 0%). This finding extends the cross-sectional evidence by providing preliminary prospective support for a temporal pattern in the PLEs-NSSI association, consistent with previous longitudinal research documenting prospective links between psychotic experiences and self-injurious behaviors [31]. The absence of heterogeneity across studies, despite differences in geographic context and assessment methods, suggests a degree of robustness in this prospective association. Although the limited number of longitudinal studies precludes definitive causal conclusions, these findings suggest that PLEs may represent an early marker that prospectively indexes vulnerability to NSSI, highlighting the potential value of early identification and intervention among individuals with PLEs. Importantly, given that this prospective evidence is based on only two longitudinal studies with differing geographic contexts, sample characteristics, and exposure definitions, these findings should be considered preliminary, and further longitudinal research with larger and more diverse samples is warranted to confirm the temporal pattern observed here.

Regarding prevalence estimates, the NSSI prevalence observed in this study (6.9%) was substantially lower than the lifetime prevalence reported by Xiao et al. among adolescents (22.0%). This discrepancy primarily stems from differences in assessment timeframes: Xiao et al. assessed lifetime prevalence specifically in adolescent samples, whereas this study included studies with varying assessment windows (ranging from past-month to lifetime prevalence) and incorporated adult samples. The PLEs prevalence found in this study (11.0%) was slightly lower than the pooled prevalence reported in a recent meta-analysis of child and adolescent samples (17.3%), a difference that may partially reflect the inclusion of adult samples in the present study, as well as variability in assessment time windows across included studies (ranging from past-month to lifetime) [32]. Systematic review evidence indicates that NSSI incidence peaks during adolescence and gradually declines in early adulthood, suggesting that the inclusion of adult samples in this study may partially explain the relatively lower prevalence estimate [33]. Of particular note, subgroup analyses in this study revealed, for the first time at the meta-analytic level, the moderating role of gender composition on PLEs prevalence estimates: studies with lower male-to-female ratios reported higher PLEs prevalence (17% vs. 9%, P < 0.01). This finding is partially consistent with gender-related patterns observed in multinational research, potentially reflecting gender differences in reporting tendencies toward internal experiences, hormonal influences, or specific psychosocial stressors faced by females [34]. These findings underscore the importance of considering population characteristics and methodological factors when interpreting prevalence estimates, and highlight the value of integrating diverse samples from multiple cultural contexts when synthesizing prevalence estimates to establish a generalizable understanding of the PLEs-NSSI relationship.

The robust association between PLEs and NSSI observed in this study suggests the existence of multiple potential mechanisms connecting these phenomena. Based on the narrative synthesis from this study and previous empirical evidence, the formation pathways of this association can be explored from psychological, social, and developmental perspectives. Depressive symptoms may represent a core psychological mechanism linking PLEs and NSSI. Network analysis suggested that depressive symptoms may be a key pathway linking PLEs to NSSI, with PLEs potentially exerting their influence primarily through this depressive pathway [9]. This pattern was observed across both Chinese college student and Korean adolescent samples: depression was found to be associated with both PLEs and NSSI, while auditory hallucinations were indirectly associated with NSSI and suicide attempts through emotion-related factors including recent traumatic life events, elevated psychological distress, and diminished self-esteem [8, 25].

It is important to note, however, that the strong association of depression with both PLEs and NSSI also admits an alternative interpretation: rather than acting as a mediator, depression and related psychopathology may serve as a common cause that partly accounts for the observed PLEs–NSSI association. This concern is reinforced by the limited control for co-occurring psychopathology across the included studies, with only two of the eight meta-analytic studies explicitly adjusting for depressive symptoms; the pooled estimate is therefore likely to overestimate the independent contribution of PLEs to NSSI. With this caveat in mind, the available evidence remains consistent with the possibility that PLEs may increase the likelihood of individuals adopting NSSI as an emotion regulation strategy by exacerbating depressive affect and emotion dysregulation, although disentangling this mechanism from shared psychopathological processes will require future studies designed for that purpose. From a developmental perspective, stressful events may trigger PLEs and ultimately lead to NSSI through a chain reaction. A chain mediation analysis in a college student sample suggested a potential developmental trajectory from negative life events triggering sleep disturbances, subsequently leading to PLEs, and ultimately being associated with NSSI, pointing to a possible stress-sleep-cognition-behavior cascade effect [35].

Beyond immediate stressors, childhood adversity as an earlier and more distal source of stress may similarly shape this developmental pathway. Narrative findings further suggested that early adverse experiences such as childhood maltreatment may not only be associated with increased likelihood of PLEs but also that PLEs may serve as a partial mediator in the relationship between childhood adversity and NSSI, suggesting that early traumatic experiences may be indirectly associated with subsequent self-injury through their impact on cognitive development and emotion regulation capacity [23]. Notably, the relationship between PLEs and NSSI is not unidirectional causality but involves complex bidirectional interactions and dynamic interplay with environmental factors. Yang et al. further conducted a three-wave longitudinal investigation among rural Chinese adolescents and found complex bidirectional associations among PLEs, NSSI, and bullying victimization, with PLEs indirectly increasing the likelihood of subsequent bullying victimization through NSSI as a mediating pathway, suggesting dynamic transactional relationships between environmental factors and internal symptoms [20]. This pattern of bidirectional associations may contribute to a self-perpetuating cycle: PLEs are associated with higher odds of self-injury, while NSSI may in turn exacerbate social isolation and interpersonal difficulties, thereby maintaining or reinforcing PLEs. Notably, these mechanisms are not mutually independent but more likely form a complex interactive network.

Exploratory subgroup analyses revealed no statistically significant differences across subgroups defined by study design, geographical region, gender composition, NSSI assessment methods, or the match between PLEs and NSSI assessment time windows. Given the limited number of included studies and the consequently low statistical power, these analyses should be regarded as hypothesis-generating; non-significant p-values should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence across subgroups, and the numerical variation observed in point estimates should not be overinterpreted as evidence of meaningful between-group differences. Persistent within-subgroup heterogeneity further suggests that additional unmeasured factors may moderate this association, and these candidate moderators warrant systematic investigation in adequately powered future studies.

The substantial heterogeneity observed in the primary analysis (I2 = 83.5%) and prevalence estimates (I2 = 98.1% for PLEs and 99.2% for NSSI) warrants careful consideration when interpreting the pooled findings. This heterogeneity likely reflects both clinical and methodological sources. Clinical heterogeneity stems from variation in sample characteristics, including age ranges spanning early adolescence to young adulthood, differing gender compositions, diverse cultural contexts, and the use of different PLEs measurement instruments (e.g., DISC-C, CAPE-8, CAARMS, PQ-16) capturing somewhat different facets of subclinical psychotic phenomena. Methodological heterogeneity arises from differences in study design, variability in assessment time frames, and differing breadth of covariate adjustment across studies. Although subgroup analyses examined several of these factors, considerable within-subgroup heterogeneity persisted, indicating that no single factor fully accounts for the observed variability. The pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted as a summary of the average association across heterogeneous studies rather than as a precise population-level effect size, and the true magnitude of the association may vary meaningfully across subpopulations and assessment contexts.

Among these methodological sources, the variability in assessment time frames for PLEs and NSSI deserves particular emphasis as a key methodological consideration influencing both prevalence and association estimates. Across the included studies, assessment windows for both PLEs and NSSI ranged from the past month to lifetime, with frequent mismatches between the two within the same study. Lifetime windows tend to capture cumulative occurrences and yield higher prevalence estimates than shorter recent windows, contributing directly to the wide range of pooled prevalence estimates for both PLEs (11.0%) and NSSI (6.9%). Time-window variation also influences the magnitude of the PLEs–NSSI association, as comparable windows are more likely to capture genuinely co-occurring experiences while mismatched windows may attenuate or distort the observed association; the exploratory subgroup analysis comparing matched and mismatched studies (matched: OR = 2.61; mismatched: OR = 3.50) provides preliminary illustration of this concern. Future research adopting harmonized assessment time frames would substantially improve cross-study comparability.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the majority of studies included in the meta-analysis employed cross-sectional designs, limiting definitive inferences regarding the direction of causality. Second, both primary and secondary outcomes exhibited considerable heterogeneity, which persisted across the exploratory subgroup analyses and could not be fully explained by study design, geographical region, gender composition, or assessment methods. Given the limited number of included studies, the subgroup analyses were underpowered to formally test between-subgroup differences, and any observed numerical variation in point estimates should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Third, the included studies varied considerably in the assessment time frames for both PLEs and NSSI, ranging from the past month to lifetime, with frequent mismatches between the two within the same study. This variability constitutes a key methodological consideration affecting both the pooled prevalence estimates and the magnitude of the observed PLEs–NSSI association, and the absence of harmonized assessment windows across studies limits direct comparability and may have contributed to the heterogeneity observed in the pooled estimates. Fourth, the prospective analysis was based on only two longitudinal studies, limiting the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the two included studies differed in their geographic contexts, sample characteristics, and exposure definitions, which should be considered when interpreting the pooled prospective estimate. The exclusion of Stanyon et al. due to its focus on auditory hallucinations rather than broader PLEs may limit the representativeness of the prospective analysis to studies using broader PLEs definitions. Fifth, as only 8 studies were included in the primary meta-analysis, the non-significant result of Egger's test should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, the included studies varied considerably in the degree to which they adjusted for co-occurring psychopathology, with only two of the eight meta-analytic studies explicitly controlling for depressive symptoms. Given that depression is strongly associated with both PLEs and NSSI, residual confounding by shared psychopathology cannot be excluded; the observed association may therefore partly reflect shared psychopathological processes rather than the independent effect of PLEs, and the pooled estimate likely overestimates the independent contribution of PLEs to NSSI. Seventh, this study primarily included English-language publications with unbalanced geographical distribution of included studies, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to other linguistic and cultural populations.

Conclusion

This study confirms a significant association between PLEs and NSSI in community populations (OR = 3.09). Preliminary prospective evidence from a limited number of longitudinal studies further suggests that baseline PLEs may be associated with elevated odds of incident NSSI at follow-up (OR = 2.17), although this temporal pattern requires confirmation in future research with larger longitudinal samples. Depressive symptoms, childhood adversity, and other factors may be involved through complex interactive pathways. These findings suggest that PLEs may warrant consideration in self-injury risk assessment, with potential implications for early screening during adolescence. Future research should further identify associated factors, potential moderators, and mediating mechanisms influencing the PLEs-NSSI association to develop more precise and effective intervention strategies.
