Chronic hepatitis B virus infection and the risk of extrahepatic malignancies: an updated review of epidemiology, mechanisms, and clinical implications
Authors: Zhongda Chen, Haiwen Zhuang, Jin Zhang, Yong Zhang, Sanrong Xu, Qing Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection affects nearly 300 million people worldwide and remains a leading cause of the global cancer burden. While its causal relationship with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is well established, accumulating epidemiological and mechanistic evidence suggests that HBV may also contribute to the development of extrahepatic malignancies, including gastric, colorectal, pancreatic, lymphoid, and respiratory cancers. Cohort studies and meta-analyses consistently report an increased risk in individuals infected with HBV, but causality remains controversial due to confounding factors such as coinfection, lifestyle factors, and genetic susceptibility. Possible mechanisms include viral DNA integration into the host genome, HBV X protein (HBx)-mediated activation of oncogenic signaling pathways (PI3K-Akt, Wnt), epigenetic modifications, and alterations in the immune microenvironment that promote tumor immune evasion. Advances in biomarker discovery, imaging technologies, and antiviral and targeted therapies offer opportunities for early detection and improved management of HBV-related cancers. This review summarizes the current knowledge on the epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnostic strategies, and treatment approaches of HBV-related extrahepatic malignancies, as well as the current controversies, with the aim of guiding future research and clinical practice.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a serious global health problem. Despite the availability of effective vaccines and antiviral therapies, HBV continues to cause significant morbidity and mortality, primarily through progressive liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The causal relationship between HBV and HCC has been well established for decades, and HBV-related liver disease accounts for a significant proportion of liver cancer deaths worldwide [1].

Beyond the liver, accumulating evidence suggests that HBV may also contribute to the development of extrahepatic malignancies. Epidemiological studies in Asia and Europe have reported an increased risk of gastrointestinal, hematologic, and respiratory cancers in individuals infected with HBV [2, 3]. Molecular and immunological studies further suggest that HBV integration, HBx protein activity, epigenetic modifications, and altered immune microenvironments may promote carcinogenesis in extrahepatic tissues [4]. These findings raise the important question of whether HBV is a direct carcinogen outside the liver or acts indirectly through modulation of host immunity and co-exposures [4, 5].

However, current studies are often retrospective, geographically limited, or influenced by coinfections such as Helicobacter pylori, hepatitis C virus (HCV), or human papilloma virus (HPV) [2–8]. Furthermore, the role of HBV treatment in modifying the risk of extrahepatic cancers remains incompletely understood. Therefore, a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence is needed to elucidate the epidemiological burden, mechanistic pathways, diagnostic implications, and treatment strategies of HBV-related extrahepatic malignancies. This review aims to address this gap by synthesizing recent research findings and highlighting future research directions.

Epidemiology of chronic hepatitis B

Global Burden

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) virus infection is a significant global public health threat, causing substantial liver-related morbidity and mortality. It is estimated that approximately 257–291 million people worldwide are chronically infected with HBV [9]. The prevalence of chronic HBV infection varies geographically. In regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, the prevalence can be as high as 8% or more, while in North America and Western Europe, it is generally lower, around 0.5% [10]. In the United States, although the incidence of acute hepatitis B has declined by about 80% between 1987 and 2004 due to vaccination programs, the prevalence of chronic HBV infection remains a concern [11]. Estimates suggest that there are approximately 1.59 million persons (range 1.25–2.49 million) with chronic HBV infection in the US [9]. In China, which has the world’s largest burden of HBV infection, there are 86 million HBV carriers, including 32 million CHB patients [12].

Transmission and disease progression

HBV is mainly acquired at birth or through person-to-person transmission, such as via sexual contact, blood-to-blood exposure, or from mother-to-child during childbirth. In chronically infected patients, an elevated serum hepatitis B virus DNA concentration is a major risk factor for disease progression. However, other clinical and viral parameters also influence disease outcomes. For example, the presence of hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) is associated with higher viral replication and a more aggressive disease course in some cases [13].

The natural history of chronic HBV infection typically progresses through different phases, including the immune-tolerant phase, immune-clearance phase, HBeAg-negative hepatitis phase, and HBeAg-negative quiescent phase. During the immune-tolerant phase, although there is high-level viral replication, liver inflammation may be minimal [13]. However, recent studies have shown that even in this phase, there can be significant HBV-DNA integration and clonal hepatocyte expansion, suggesting that hepatocarcinogenesis may be underway [14].

Chronic hepatitis B in extrahepatic cancer risk

A growing body of evidence from epidemiological studies suggests an association between CHB and an increased risk of extrahepatic malignancies [Figure 1] [2, 3]. A nationwide cohort study in South Korea found that patients with CHB had an elevated risk of developing primary extrahepatic malignancy. The CHB+/NA- group (patients with CHB not receiving nucleos(t)ide analogue treatment) had a higher overall risk of extrahepatic malignancy compared to the CHB+/NA+ group (patients with CHB receiving nucleos(t)ide analogue treatment) (adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio [aSHR] = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.12–1.45, P < 0.001) or controls (patients without CHB) (aSHR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.18–1.26, P < 0.001) [2]. Another study in Sweden reported that among 10,197 patients diagnosed with chronic HBV infection, there was an increased risk of several extrahepatic cancers. The standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) for cancers of the upper aerodigestive tract, lung, kidney, skin (squamous cell carcinoma), and thyroid gland, as well as lymphoma and leukemia, were elevated. The SIR for hepatocellular carcinoma in patients with HBV infection was 40.58 (95% CI: 30.50-50.07), and for non-Hodgkin lymphoma and acute myeloid leukemia, the risks were increased in both Swedish- and foreign-born patients with HBV infection [3].

Esophageal cancer

Esophageal cancer is a significant global health concern, with an estimated 576,529 new cases and 538,602 deaths in 2021, according to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study [15]. Epidemiological evidence linking HBV and esophageal cancer remains limited and inconclusive [6]. Most existing studies suggest only weak or indirect associations. For example, data from East Asian populations suggest that viral infections, including HBV and HCV, may increase the risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), although these findings are often confounded by other risk factors, such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and coinfection with HCV [6, 16]. To date, evidence from large prospective cohorts remains limited, and a definitive causal relationship has not been established.

Mechanistic evidence linking HBV and esophageal cancer is also scarce. A study from northern China, a region with a high incidence of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, has identified genetic susceptibility loci within the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) region (e.g., HLA-DQA1, TRIM27) that may interact with chronic HBV infection to promote carcinogenesis [16]. However, these data remain preliminary and largely indirect, as direct molecular evidence, such as HBV DNA integration or HBx expression in esophageal tissue, remains scarce.

Overall, current evidence is insufficient to definitively establish HBV as an independent risk factor for esophageal cancer. Rather, in high-risk populations with overlapping viral infections and environmental exposures, HBV may act as a contributing factor. Future large-scale studies are needed to clarify whether HBV, in addition to known risk factors, contributes to esophageal cancer.

Gastric cancer

Several epidemiological studies suggest that chronic HBV infection may increase the risk of gastric cancer, although the strength of association varies across regions and study designs. In a large retrospective case-control study from China, HBsAg positivity was significantly associated with gastric cancer, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 1.49 (95% CI: 1.06–2.10, P = 0.02) [17]. This association remained significant even among patients without a family history of gastric cancer. Furthermore, among HBsAg-negative individuals, those who were anti-HBc positive/anti-HBs negative-suggestive of occult HBV infection-also showed increased susceptibility [17]. A meta-analysis further supported the link between HBV infection and gastric adenocarcinoma, though some hospital-based studies within the analysis failed to detect a significant relationship, likely due to selection bias and smaller sample sizes [18].

Despite these direct observations, the evidence remains heterogeneous. Differences in study design, population genetics, environmental exposures, and coinfections (e.g., Helicobacter pylori, Epstein-Barr virus) complicate the relationship [7, 19–21]. For example, a Chinese study found a synergistic effect between HBV infection and blood type A on gastric cancer [17]. A case-control study in Fujian province, China, showed that HBV infection increased the risk of gastric cancer, and there was a super-additive interaction between HBV infection and tobacco smoking [22]. Other factors such as genetic polymorphisms also play a role. For example, polymorphisms in genes like prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) have been associated with the susceptibility to gastric cancer [23]. Moreover, Helicobacter pylori infection, which is a well-established risk factor for gastric cancer [7], may also interact with HBV. Although the exact nature of this interaction is not fully understood, it is likely that the two pathogens may act synergistically or independently to increase the risk of gastric cancer development.

Generally, existing research primarily relies on retrospective case-control and cohort studies to demonstrate a potential association between HBV infection and an increased risk of gastric cancer, but causality remains uncertain [17, 18]. Future studies should carefully control major confounding factors and conduct molecular biological studies to explore the causal relationship.

Colorectal cancer

The global prevalence of HBV in colorectal cancer patients has been a subject of several studies, aiming to clarify the association between the two conditions [24–26]. A cross-sectional study on 155,674 asymptomatic examinees who underwent a colonoscopy and serologic testing for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) found that 3.5% (5476 out of 155,674) were positive for HBsAg. The prevalence of colorectal neoplasia (CRN) was higher in the HBsAg-positive group (16.9%) compared to the HBsAg-negative group (15.6%, P = 0.009), and after adjusting for confounders, HBsAg positivity was still correlated with an increased risk of CRN (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19, P = 0.025) [24]. In another cohort study of CRN patients, the incidence of liver metastasis in the HBV-infected group was significantly lower than that in the non-infected group, but the incidence of extrahepatic metastasis in the HBV-infected group was significantly higher (31.9% vs. 17.0%, P < 0.01) [25]. These findings suggest a complex relationship between HBV infection and colorectal cancer, with potential implications for disease progression and metastasis. However, a retrospective study using propensity score matching to adjust for confounding variables found no significant association between HBV infection and metachronous colorectal liver metastases, both before and after propensity score matching [26]. These conflicting results highlight the need for further large-scale, well-controlled studies to clarify the causal relationship.

In addition, multiple risk factors have been identified in relation to HBV and colorectal cancer. A study on 133 patients with chronic HBV infection and a matched healthy HBV-uninfected group found that HBV infection (OR = 23.961, 95% CI: 9.400-61.076), diabetes mellitus (OR = 2.633, 95% CI: 1.071–6.473), and age (OR = 1.057, 95% CI: 1.020–1.095) were significantly associated with advanced adenoma in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Within the HBV group, HBV DNA (OR = 1.236, 95% CI: 1.029–1.485) was associated with advanced adenoma [27]. This suggests that chronic inflammation or immune regulation caused by persistent infection may theoretically promote adenoma-carcinoma progression.

While direct data on demographic variations specifically for HBV-related colorectal cancer is scarce, it can be hypothesized that similar racial and geographic factors may play a role. Different ethnic groups may have varying genetic susceptibilities to HBV infection and colorectal cancer, and regional differences in HBV prevalence, access to healthcare, and lifestyle factors could contribute to variations in the incidence and outcomes of HBV-related colorectal cancer. Further research is needed to comprehensively understand these demographic variations.

Overall, available data indicate a possible association between HBV infection and colorectal neoplasia, although findings regarding tumor progression and metastasis remain inconsistent.

Pancreatic cancer

Epidemiological evidence suggests a possible association between chronic HBV infection and pancreatic cancer, but findings vary across populations. One meta-analysis including 9 studies (6 case-control and 3 cohort studies) showed that the risk of pancreatic cancer was positively correlated with HBV infection. The pooled relative risk (RR) was 1.39 (95% CI: 1.22–1.59, P < 0.00001) in chronic HBV carriers, 1.41 (95% CI: 1.06–1.87, P = 0.02) in past exposure to HBV, and 3.83 (95% CI: 1.76–8.36, P = 0.0007) in active HBV infection [28]. And another meta-analysis including 17 studies (7 case-control and 10 cohort studies) found that individuals with HBsAg or HBV DNA seropositivity had a significantly increased risk of pancreatic cancer, with a RR of 1.39 (95% CI: 1.19–1.63). However, when subgrouped by patient’s region, the RR and 95% CI in Europe and Oceania were 1.44 (0.88–2.34) and 1.47 (0.38–5.71) respectively, suggesting some regional variability in the association [29].

Moreover, multiple risk factors may link HBV to pancreatic cancer development. From the REVEAL-HBV cohort study, chronic carriers of HBsAg had a significantly increased risk of pancreatic cancer, with a multivariate-adjusted hazards ratio (HR(adj)) of 1.95 (95% CI: 1.01–3.78). This association was more prominent in females, individuals ≤ 50 years, non-smokers, and non-drinkers. HBeAg-seropositive carriers had an even higher HR(adj) of 5.73 (95% CI: 1.73–19.05) compared with HBsAg-seronegative non-carriers [30]. Another study suggested a potential interaction between ABO blood type and HBV infection in pancreatic cancer risk. In a retrospective analysis of 645 pancreatic adenocarcinoma patients and 711 controls in China, A blood type, HBsAg-positive/anti-HBc-positive, and anti-HBs-positive/anti-HBc-positive were significantly associated with pancreatic cancer. The estimated adjusted odds ratios (AORs) were 1.425 (95% CI: 1.071–1.894) for A blood type, 1.610 (95% CI: 1.125–2.304) for HBsAg-positive/anti-HBc-positive, and 1.526 (95% CI: 1.159–2.011) for anti-HBs-positive/anti-HBc-positive. There was also a synergism between A blood type and HBV infection in pancreatic cancer development [31].

In summary, current data suggest that HBV may slightly increase the risk of pancreatic cancer, but causal inferences are limited by heterogeneity across studies and the lack of strong prospective evidence. More large-scale, well-designed studies are still needed to clarify this causal relationship.

Duodenal cancer

Evidence linking HBV infection with duodenal cancer is extremely limited. Specific prevalence data in duodenal cancer patients are scarce, but indirect studies provide some context. For example, in a general population undergoing serologic testing, the rate of HBsAg positivity was 3.5% [24], while in inflammatory bowel disease cohorts, 0.3% were HBsAg-positive and 7.3% anti-HBc-positive [32]. Although not specific to duodenal cancer, these findings suggest background HBV exposure levels in different patient groups. In cancer patients receiving everolimus, HBV reactivation occurred in 11.36% of untreated HBsAg-positive cases compared with 5.56% of those given nucleos(t)ide analogue (NUC) prophylaxis, underscoring the importance of HBV screening and management in oncology, which may also be relevant for duodenal cancer [33].

Additional risk factors may interact with HBV to influence gastrointestinal carcinogenesis. In a large cohort of Taiwanese men with HBV infection, those with three or more metabolic risk factors (obesity, diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia, and hypertension) had a 10-year cumulative HCC incidence of 13.60%, compared with 4.83% in patients with low metabolic risk (adjusted HR 2.32, 95% CI: 1.18–4.54) [34]. Although this study focused on hepatocellular carcinoma, it highlights how metabolic comorbidities and smoking might modify HBV-associated cancer risk, potentially extending to duodenal malignancies. Similarly, in patients with primary biliary cirrhosis, prior HBV infection was independently associated with HCC (OR 6.6, 95% CI: 3.7–11.9, P = 0.001), suggesting broader carcinogenic implications of HBV [35]. Furthermore, in familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)-related duodenal cancer, factors such as large polyp size (77% vs. 47%, P = 0.015), high-grade dysplasia (29% vs. 6%, P = 0.003), and villous histology (80% vs. 22%, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with malignant transformation [36]. Whether HBV infection interacts with these hereditary risk factors remains unexplored.

In summary, current evidence for HBV as a risk factor in duodenal cancer is largely indirect, derived from prevalence surveys, metabolic comorbidity studies, and extrapolations from HCC research. Dedicated epidemiological studies and mechanistic investigations are urgently needed to clarify whether HBV plays a direct or modifying role in duodenal carcinogenesis.

Lung cancer

The association between chronic HBV infection and lung cancer remains unclear, with most studies focusing on prognosis and treatment-related complications rather than direct carcinogenicity. A cohort study of 188 patients with chronic HBV infection and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) showed that pre-treatment serum HBV DNA copy number was an independent prognostic indicator, with higher viral loads predicting shorter overall survival (median overall survival: 34 months) [37]. These findings suggest that active viral replication may negatively impact the prognosis of patients with lung cancer.

Despite these clinical observations, the epidemiological evidence supporting a causal role for HBV in lung cancer incidence remains limited to date. The reported associations may primarily reflect comorbidities, viral reactivation, or immune modulation in the context of cancer treatment rather than a causal relationship in lung cancer development. Regional differences and potential confounding factors such as smoking, air pollution, and genetic susceptibility further complicate the interpretation of the results.

In summary, current evidence primarily supports an effect of HBV on prognosis rather than providing clear evidence for an association with lung cancer incidence. Larger prospective studies are needed to clarify whether HBV directly causes lung cancer, and mechanistic studies are needed to explore the role of viral replication and immunosuppression in regulating the prognosis of HBV-infected lung cancer patients.

Bronchial cancer

The burden of tracheal, bronchial, and lung (TBL) cancer was assessed based on the GBD database. From 1990 to 2017, global TBL cancer cases, deaths, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) increased by 100.44%, 82.30%, and 61.27%, respectively. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) remained relatively stable, while the DALY rate generally decreased [38, 39]. Smoking is a well-known major risk factor for lung cancer, and combined with other cancers, it is inferred that it may interact with HBV in the carcinogenic process. To date, epidemiological evidence specifically examining the association between chronic HBV infection and bronchial cancer remains extremely limited. Any potential contribution of HBV would need to be evaluated in the context of established environmental and behavioral risk factors. Further well-designed studies are required to clarify whether HBV plays any role in bronchial carcinogenesis.

Laryngeal cancer

The potential association between HBV infection and laryngeal cancer has attracted increasing attention. A large retrospective case-control study including 774 head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) patients and 1518 controls found that chronic HBV infection was significantly more common among cancer patients, with an OR of 2.76 (95% CI: 1.64–4.64) [40]. Since laryngeal cancer represents a major subtype of HNSCC, this suggests a possible role for HBV in its pathogenesis. However, the underlying biological mechanisms remain unclear.

The overall incidence of laryngeal cancer varies significantly across regions. A global analysis of tracheal cancer, which can provide some context due to its related anatomical location, showed that the global age-standardized rate of incidence of tracheal cancer in 2022 was 2.9 per 10 million, with the highest regional incidence in Central and Eastern Europe (ASR = 9.0) and the highest national incidence in Hungary (12.5) [41]. Regarding laryngeal cancer specifically, a study analyzing data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database in the US found that the age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs) for laryngeal cancer were 5.98 and 1.25 per 100,000 population for men and women, respectively, from 2000 to 2019, with a significant reduction in ASIRs during this period [42]. In China, a retrospective analysis of 1,234 cases of laryngeal cancer in Shanxi Province from 2008 to 2012 reported an average annual incidence rate of 0.70/105, with the Chinese population-standardized incidence rate of 0.57/105 and the world population-standardized incidence rate of 0.60/105 [43]. These regional differences may be attributed to a combination of factors, including variations in HBV prevalence, environmental factors, genetic susceptibility, and differences in healthcare access and screening practices.

In addition, multiple risk factors contribute to the co-occurrence of HBV and laryngeal cancer. A case-control study on laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma found that Helicobacter pylori infection in the larynx was significantly higher in patients with laryngeal cancer than in control subjects (71.6% vs. 25.3%, P < 0.001), and regression analysis indicated that it was an independent risk factor for laryngeal cancer (OR = 7.15, 95% CI: 3.29–15.53, P < 0.001) [44].Genetic factors also play a role. A study on the Chinese Han population investigated polymorphisms in the cytochrome P450 1B1 (CYP1B1) gene and found that carriers of the T allele of rs1056827 had a significantly higher risk of laryngeal cancer than G-allele carriers (OR = 1.4339, 95% CI: 1.1268–1.8247, P = 0.0034), and this difference remained significant after adjusting for factors such as age, sex, smoking, and drinking (adjusted OR = 1.743, 95% CI: 1.124–3.743, P < 0.001). Moreover, there was a mutually synergistic effect between smoking, alcohol consumption, and CYP1B1 gene polymorphisms regarding laryngeal cancer [45].

The causal relationship between HBV and laryngeal cancer remains a subject of debate. While some studies have shown an association between HBV infection and laryngeal cancer, establishing a direct causal link is challenging. A study on the association between CYP1B1 gene polymorphisms and laryngeal cancer susceptibility among the Chinese Han population focused on genetic and environmental risk factors [45]. Although it demonstrated the role of genetic polymorphisms in laryngeal cancer, it did not directly address the causal relationship with HBV. The complexity of the carcinogenic process, which involves multiple genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors, makes it difficult to isolate the role of HBV. Additionally, the lack of large-scale, well-controlled studies specifically designed to establish causality further complicates the issue. More research, including longitudinal studies and in-depth molecular investigations, is needed to clarify this relationship.

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma

The global prevalence of HBV in Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (NPC) patients varies across different regions. In a study of 1301 non-metastatic, histologically confirmed NPC patients treated with radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, 142 (10.9%) were HBsAg-positive, indicating chronic HBV infection [46]. Similarly, a large big-data analysis from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, encompassing 46,919 NPC patients diagnosed between 2008 and 2016, reported an HBV screening rate of 24.8% (11,616/46,919) [47]. These findings suggest that HBV infection is relatively common in NPC patients in endemic areas, highlighting the need for careful evaluation of the interplay between these conditions.

The coexistence of HBV and NPC is influenced by a constellation of risk factors. Chronic HBV infection has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in NPC patients. In a study analyzing 876 NPC patients, 106 (12.1%) were HBV-infected, and the HBsAg-positive group had a lower CD4 + T cell count than the HBsAg-negative group [48]. Multivariate analysis indicated that HBsAg status was an independent risk factor affecting OS, disease-free survival (DFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) of NPC patients.

The causal relationship between HBV and NPC remains a topic of debate. Some studies suggest an association, such as a retrospective study of 722 NPC patients that found HBV infection was an independent negative prognostic factor for long-term survival, and distant-failure especially liver metastasis was more common in HBsAg-positive patients [49]. However, other studies question the direct causal link. A study on the association between HBV infection status and liver metastasis of NPC found that an inactive HBV carrier status and resolved HBV infection were related to a decreased risk of liver metastasis in NPC patients compared with those without HBV infections [50].

In summary, although HBV infection is prevalent in patients with NPC in endemic areas and appears to negatively impact prognosis in certain populations, evidence for a direct causal relationship is inconclusive. Future studies should focus on large-scale, prospective, mechanistic studies stratified by HBV activity (chronic active, inactive, remitted) to elucidate whether HBV is a driver of NPC progression or modifies the disease course through immune and metastatic pathways.

Uterine cancer & cervical cancer

So far, some studies have shown that chronic HBV infection is associated with cervical cancer. In a case-control analysis of 838 cervical cancer patients and 838 controls with benign disease, chronic HBV infection (HBsAg+/anti-HBc+) and prior HBV infection (HBsAg–/anti-HBc+) were more frequent in cases than controls (6.3% vs. 4.4%; 11.6% vs. 7.3%, respectively). Both chronic infection (OR 1.6, 95% CI: 1.0-2.4) and prior infection (OR 1.7, 95% CI: 1.2–2.4) were associated with increased cervical cancer risk. Subgroup analyses provided further nuance: among HPV-positive women, the HBV-cervical cancer association was not significant (OR 1.2, 95% CI: 0.4–3.4), while in patients younger than 50 years, the relationship remained significant even after adjustment for HPV infection and parity (adjusted OR 2.1, 95% CI: 1.0-4.4). Importantly, women with both HBV and HPV positivity showed a dramatically increased risk (adjusted OR 67.1, 95% CI: 23.4-192.7), suggesting a synergistic effect between the two viruses in cervical carcinogenesis [8].

Although HBV infection appears to increase susceptibility to cervical cancer, particularly in young women, and may synergize with HPV, further investigation into the causal relationship is needed. Strong evidence for the impact of HBV infection on uterine cancer is lacking. Further prospective and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify whether HBV is an independent cause of gynecological cancers or primarily acts as a cofactor, modifying HPV-related risk.

Breast cancer

The global prevalence of HBV in breast cancer patients varies across different regions. In a study from China, a cross-sectional study of 6072 female breast cancer patients first diagnosed from September 2012 to July 2020 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University found that the prevalence of HBV infection in the breast cancer group was 7.9% [51]. Another study in an HBV-endemic area in China involving 1904 patients with early breast cancer reported that 11.1% of the patients had chronic HBV infection as indicated by serum HBsAg positivity [52]. In a Korean study, among 1912 breast cancer patients who had received anthracycline-based adjuvant chemotherapy, 131 (6.85%) were HBV surface antigen positive [53]. These findings highlight that HBV is relatively common among breast cancer patients in regions of high endemicity. In addition, the presence of HBV in breast cancer patients can have implications for their prognosis. For instance, in the study with 1904 Chinese patients, HBsAg-positive patients had inferior overall survival (84.9% vs. 90.4%, P = 0.005) and hepatic metastasis-free survival (92.5% vs. 97.1%, P = 0.016) at 5 years compared to HBsAg-negative patients [52].These results suggest that chronic HBV infection may adversely affect long-term survival and increase the risk of hepatic metastasis in breast cancer patients.

The causal relationship between HBV and breast cancer remains controversial. Some studies suggest a potential association, while others are inconclusive. A study exploring the link between HBV and breast cancer risk found that the relationship between Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and breast cancer is controversial, and similar debates may exist for HBV [54]. In the case of HBV, although some epidemiological studies show an increased prevalence of HBV in breast cancer patients, it is difficult to establish a direct causal link. One of the challenges is that many factors can confound the relationship, such as lifestyle factors, genetic background, and other co-existing infections.

In summary, HBV infection is relatively common among breast cancer patients in endemic regions and may be associated with poorer survival outcomes. However, a direct causal relationship between the two remains to be established. Large-scale, prospective, and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify whether HBV directly causes breast cancer or primarily modifies disease progression and prognosis.

Vaginal cancer

The global prevalence of HBV in vaginal cancer patients is not well-established due to the rarity of vaginal cancer and the lack of large-scale, dedicated studies. Although studies specifically addressing vaginal cancer are lacking, HBV infection has been reported in other gynecological malignancies in endemic areas [55]. Given the rarity of vaginal cancer, current understanding of any potential relationship with HBV remains limited.

Leukemia

The relationship between HBV infection and leukemia development is an area of growing interest. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), NOTCH mutations are frequent and associated with disease progression and drug resistance. A study of 98 previously untreated HBV-positive CLL patients and 244 HBV-negative CLL patients found that NOTCH mutations were more frequent in the HBV-positive CLL subgroup (P = 0.033). By survival analysis, HBV infection was associated with disease progression and poor survival (P = 0.0099 for overall survival (OS) and P = 0.0446 for time-to-treatment (TTT)) [56]. This indicates that HBV infection may contribute to the development and progression of CLL through its association with NOTCH pathway mutations. And the prevalence of HBV in leukemia patients varies across different studies. In a cross-sectional study, one of the molecular and immunological markers of HBV was found in 24% of leukemia patients, compared to 12% in the control group. A significant difference was detected in the detection of HBsAg (P = 0.02) between leukemia patients and the control group [57]. In China, the prevalence of occult HBV infection was higher in leukemia patients (10.5%) than in the control group (2.9%) (OR = 3.92; P < 0.001) [58]. These findings suggest a relatively high prevalence of HBV infection in leukemia patients, which may have implications for their treatment and prognosis.

Despite these observations, the causal relationship and mechanism between HBV infection and leukemia are not fully understood and require further exploration.

Lymphoma

The association between HBV infection and lymphoma has been a subject of extensive research. The global prevalence of HBV in lymphoma patients varies across different regions. In high-endemic areas such as China, HBV prevalence among lymphoma patients is markedly elevated. A retrospective study of 420 patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) reported an HBsAg positivity rate of 30.2% [59]. By contrast, studies in low-endemic regions such as Italy also found an increased risk: patients with DLBCL had higher rates of both past HBV infection (OR 2.4, 95% CI: 1.5-4.0, P = 0.0006) and active infection (OR 2.8, 95% CI: 1.2–6.3, P = 0.014) compared with indolent NHL [60].

Large-scale case-control and pooled analyses further support these findings. In China, a 1:2 case-control study including 3502 NHL cases and 7004 controls found that individuals with serological markers of past infection and natural immunity (HBsAg–/anti-HBc+/anti-HBs+) had an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 2.25 (95% CI: 1.96–2.57) for B-NHL. Patients with chronic active infection (HBsAg+/HBeAg+) showed an even higher risk (AOR 6.23, 95% CI: 3.95–9.82) [61]. A meta-analysis encompassing more than 40,000 NHL cases reported that HBV-infected individuals had more than a twofold increased risk of NHL (OR 2.24, 95% CI: 1.80–2.78; P ≤ 0.001) [62].

Subtype-specific analyses suggest that HBV preferentially associates with aggressive B-cell lymphomas. A Chinese cohort demonstrated HBV positivity in 10.46% of aggressive B-NHL, 5.09% of indolent B-NHL, 3.67% of multiple myeloma (MM), and 6.67% of solitary plasmacytoma (SP), with HBV significantly associated with aggressive B-NHL (P < 0.01) [63]. These patterns, consistently observed across studies, suggest a potential link between HBV infection and lymphomagenesis.

Despite robust prevalence data, the impact of HBV infection on survival outcomes in patients with lymphoma remains unclear. Overall, HBV infection has been significantly associated with an increased risk of lymphoma, particularly aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, in both endemic and non-endemic areas. Future research should prioritize prospective studies and mechanistic investigations to elucidate causal pathways and determine the impact of HBV infection status and antiviral treatment on survival.

Multiple myeloma

The global prevalence of HBV in Multiple Myeloma (MM) patients varies across different studies. In an endemic Asian setting, a study from 2000 to 2008 screened 273 newly diagnosed MM patients and reported a HBV infection prevalence of 5.5% [64]. Another multicenter retrospective study in China from 2006 to 2011, which included 739 newly diagnosed MM patients, found that the prevalence of MM patients infected with HBV was 3.4% [65]. A retrospective case-control trial including 299 MM patients showed a relatively higher HBsAg positivity rate of 19.4% in the MM group compared to 12.0% in the acute leukemia group, indicating a potentially higher prevalence of HBV infection in MM patients compared to some other hematological malignancies [66]. These variations might be due to differences in study populations, geographical locations, and screening methods. Furthermore, the rates of acute or chronic HBV infection and resolved HBV infection in 165 MM patients in Nanjing, China were 12.12% and 26.06% respectively [67]. The differences in prevalence across regions and studies highlight the need for more comprehensive and standardized global epidemiological studies. Understanding the global prevalence is essential for developing appropriate prevention, screening, and treatment strategies for MM patients with HBV infection.

Multiple factors contribute to the risk of HBV infection in MM patients. Advanced stage (ISS stage III), more severe anemia (Hb < 90 g/L), and elevated CRP (> 10 mg/L) were identified as independent risk factors for infection in newly diagnosed MM patients, and HBV was one of the common viruses detected in these patients [68]. In a study of 109 patients with undetectable HBsAg undergoing chemotherapy for malignant lymphoma or multiple myeloma, lower peripheral lymphocyte counts before chemotherapy were associated with HBV reactivation in anti-HBc positive patients [69]. Autologous stem-cell transplantation (HDT/ASCT) is also a significant risk factor for HBV reactivation in MM patients. In a Japanese nationwide retrospective study, around 20% of all patients with HBV reactivation developed HBV reactivation after 2 years from the initiation of therapy, and autologous stem cell transplantation was identified as a risk factor [70]. Additionally, the use of novel agents such as bortezomib, thalidomide, and immunomodulatory drugs may also play a role in HBV reactivation, although the exact mechanisms need further elucidation [64].

In summary, HBV infection is relatively common among patients with multiple myeloma in endemic regions and poses a significant risk to disease management due to viral relapse during treatment. Identifying high-risk patients based on clinical and treatment-related factors is crucial for developing early prevention strategies. Furthermore, the specific mechanisms underlying the relationship between HBV and multiple myeloma require further investigation.

Thyroid cancer

The prevalence of HBV infection among thyroid cancer patients remains poorly characterized, with only limited studies addressing this relationship directly [3]. Dedicated epidemiological studies specifically evaluating HBV prevalence or incidence in thyroid cancer cohorts remain scarce. Interestingly, other hepatotropic viruses have been implicated in thyroid disease. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that HCV infection was significantly associated with an increased risk of thyroid disease (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.54–2.10) and thyroid cancer (OR = 16.36, 95% CI: 4.65–57.62) [71]. These findings suggest that chronic viral infection and immune-mediated mechanisms may contribute to thyroid pathophysiology. Whether similar mechanisms apply to HBV infection remains unclear. Although HBV and HCV differ in viral biology and tissue tropism, chronic immune activation, systemic inflammation, and viral persistence may represent shared pathways warranting investigation.

Overall, data regarding HBV and thyroid cancer are sparse, and no definitive conclusions can be drawn. Future well-designed epidemiological and mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether HBV plays a direct or modifying role in thyroid carcinogenesis.

Kidney cancer

Currently, research on the relationship between HBV infection and renal cancer is still limited. A population-based case-control study from Taiwan found that renal cancer risk was elevated in HBV carriers, with an OR of 1.38 (95% CI: 1.24–1.54) compared with non-carriers. Stratified analyses revealed stronger associations in younger men: for those < 55 years, the OR was 1.94 (95% CI: 1.57–2.39), and for those aged 55–64 years, the OR was 1.40 (95% CI: 1.05–1.86) [72]. But more evidence is needed to prove their relationship.

The prevalence of HBV in kidney cancer patients is an important aspect in understanding the relationship between the two. In a study on 1294 kidney transplant patients, 76 patients showed preoperative HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-positive, and HBV-DNA-negative results [73]. Among them, 48 patients received a rituximab dose of 200 mg/body, and HBV reactivation was found in 1 patient in the rituximab-treated group (2.2%) and 1 patient in the non-rituximab-treated group (3.6%). Although the study was not directly on kidney cancer patients, it provides some insights into the behavior of HBV in a kidney-related patient population.

In summary, available evidence suggests a modest association between HBV infection and kidney cancer risk; however, most available data are limited to specific populations, such as transplant recipients or East Asians. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multiregional studies and mechanistic investigations to elucidate causal relationships and assess the potential impact of HBV infection status on kidney cancer outcomes.

Pathophysiological mechanisms linking hbv to extrahepatic cancer

Molecular pathways of HBV in oncogenesis beyond the liver

HBV-associated oncogenesis involves multiple molecular alterations, many of which have been extensively studied in HCC, but may also be relevant to extrahepatic malignancies. A key event is HBV DNA integration into the host genome. Genome-wide analyses have shown integration events in 86.4% of HCC tumors compared with 30.7% of adjacent non-tumorous liver tissues [74]. These integrations promote chromosomal instability, with recurrent breakpoints occurring at cancer-related genes such as TERT, MLL4, and CCNE1, leading to upregulated oncogene expression. While HBV DNA integration is well documented in HCC, its occurrence in extrahepatic tissues remains unclear, and current evidence beyond the liver is limited [Figure 2].

HBV-encoded proteins also play a pivotal role. The HBx protein can modulate transcription, protein degradation, proliferation, and apoptosis. In particular, HBx activates the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, thereby promoting cell survival and proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis [4]. While most data are derived from hepatic models, the same oncogenic pathways are dysregulated in many extrahepatic cancers, suggesting that HBx-driven signaling may contribute more broadly to tumorigenesis.

Immune response alterations due to HBV infection and cancer risk

HBV infection can lead to significant alterations in the immune response, which in turn may increase the risk of cancer. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) play a crucial role in immune tolerance. In the context of HBV infection, Tregs are increased, and they can suppress CD4/CD8 T cells, leading to a state of immune hyporesponsiveness [75]. The increase of Tregs is inversely proportional to HBV DNA titres, and they exert their suppressive effect via cell-to-cell contact or through the release of cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-2, IL-10, TGF-β, and IL-35 [76].

Single-cell RNA sequencing studies have characterized the human liver immune microenvironment during the progression from HBV infection to cirrhosis and HBV-associated HCC. There is an accumulation of scar-associated macrophages during disease progression, and specific immune subsets such as Macrophage-CD9/IL18 and macrophage-CD9/IFI6 expand at different stages. The proportion of effector CD8 T cells and Tregs also changes, with Tregs increasing and effector CD8 T cells decreasing from cirrhosis to HCC, creating an immunosuppressive environment conducive to cancer development [Figure 2] [77]. Similar immune dysregulation may also occur in extrahepatic tissues, leading to gastric cancer, lymphoma, and other HBV-associated cancers, although direct evidence is still scarce.

Diagnostic advances in HBV-associated extrahepatic cancer

Biomarkers for early detection of HBV-related extrahepatic malignancies

The discovery of effective biomarkers for the early detection of HBV-related extrahepatic malignancies is crucial. In the context of HCC, which is closely related to HBV, several biomarkers have shown promise. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is a well - known biomarker, but its sensitivity in the early stages is limited. Lectin-reactive AFP (AFP-L3) and des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP) have been investigated as complementary biomarkers. A study showed that combining AFP and AFP-L3 had a higher area under the receiver operator characteristic curve (AUROC) (0.83, 95% CI : 0.74–0.92) compared to using them alone, and adding DCP did not further improve the AUROC significantly (0.86, 95% CI : 0.76–0.95) [78].

Some studies have explored the role of microRNAs (miRNAs) as biomarkers. For example, a 2-miRNA panel (miR-27b-3p and miR-192-5p) showed a high diagnostic accuracy for HBV-positive HCC (AUC = 0.842 P < 0.0001 for the training set; AUC = 0.836 P < 0.0001 for the validation set), and it also differentiated HCC from healthy individuals and cirrhosis patients [79]. However, biomarker development specifically targeting HBV-associated extrahepatic malignancies remains limited, and most available data are extrapolated from HCC research. Dedicated studies are required to identify tumor-specific molecular markers in extrahepatic sites.

Imaging techniques for diagnosing HBV-associated extrahepatic cancers

Imaging techniques play a vital role in the diagnosis of HBV-associated cancers. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are commonly used. In the diagnosis of HCC, dynamic MRI and CT are regarded as the best imaging techniques for non-invasive diagnosis. CT and MRI can detect characteristic features such as arterial enhancement followed by washout during the portal venous and/or delayed phases, which are typical of HCC [80].

For extrahepatic metastases of HCC, positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scan can be valuable. A study evaluating the value of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT scan in HCC found that it was invaluable for detecting lung metastases larger than 1 cm and bone metastases. The accuracy of chest CT was superior for detecting lung metastases < 1 cm, while the accuracy of PET imaging was superior for detecting bone metastases compared to bone scan [81].

In contrast, imaging strategies for HBV-associated extrahepatic primary malignancies generally follow organ-specific diagnostic guidelines rather than HBV-specific protocols. Currently, no dedicated imaging modality exists for the early detection of extrahepatic tumors specifically attributable to HBV infection.

Therapeutic strategies for HBV-related extrahepatic cancer

Antiviral therapies and their impact on extrahepatic cancer outcomes

Antiviral therapies are an important part of the treatment strategy for HBV-related diseases, and they may also have an impact on extrahepatic cancer outcomes. Nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) are commonly used antiviral drugs for HBV infection. A few studies have shown that NAs can induce a viral response and may improve some extrahepatic diseases associated with HBV. For example, in some cases of HBV-related nephropathies, the eradication of HBV following NA therapy appears to improve renal function [82].

However, the impact of antiviral therapy on the risk of extrahepatic cancer development is still under debate. Some studies suggest that long-term NA treatment may be associated with a lower risk of extrahepatic malignancy development among patients with CHB. In a South Korean cohort study, the CHB+/NA+ group had a lower risk of extrahepatic malignancy compared to the CHB+/NA- group [2]. But other studies, such as a nationwide cohort study in Taiwan, found that adjuvant antiviral therapy did not reduce the risk of HCC progression or mortality in HBV-related HCC patients after adjusting for cancer status [83]. This highlights the complexity of distinguishing antiviral effects from tumor biological mechanisms.

Chemotherapy and targeted therapies for HBV-associated cancers

Chemotherapy and targeted therapies are important treatment modalities for HBV-associated cancers. In the case of hepatocellular carcinoma, sorafenib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), has shown clinical efficacy in patients with advanced HCC. However, the genetic heterogeneity of HCC cells affects its response to therapeutics. There are currently many clinical trials evaluating TKIs for HCC, including those tested in combination with other drugs or compared with sorafenib as a first-line therapy [84].

For extrahepatic cancers associated with HBV, such as biliary tract cancers, chemotherapy with regimens like gemcitabine and cisplatin is commonly used. Targeted therapies are also being investigated. For example, in extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and gallbladder cancer, given the significant human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) alterations observed, various anti-HER2 therapies, including trastuzumab, pertuzumab, trastuzumab-deruxtecan, zanidatamab, and neratinib are being explored [85].

Controversies and challenges in HBV and extrahepatic cancer research

Debates on the causal relationship between HBV and extrahepatic cancers

Although many observational studies suggest an association between HBV and extrahepatic cancers, the causal relationship remains a subject of debate. Some studies have used Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate this relationship. A two-sample MR study of Biobank Japan and Korean GWAS databases found that genetic liability to chronic HBV infection was causally associated with extrahepatic cancers including cervical cancer (OR 1.57, 95% CI: 1.29–1.91, P = 0.0001) and gastric cancer (OR 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.19, P = 0.0001), but no causal association has been found for other extrahepatic cancers [5]. However, some researchers argue that more evidence is needed, especially considering the potential confounding factors in observational studies.

The complex nature of the relationship also contributes to the debate. HBV infection may interact with other factors such as lifestyle, genetic background, and coinfections, making it difficult to isolate the direct causal effect of HBV on extrahepatic cancers. For example, the presence of other liver-damaging factors or certain genetic polymorphisms may modify the impact of HBV on cancer development [4].

Challenges in differentiating HBV-related cancer risks from other factors

Differentiating HBV-related cancer risks from other factors is a significant challenge. There are multiple factors that can increase the risk of cancer, such as genetic mutations in genes like BRCA1 and BRCA2, which are associated with an increased risk of male breast, pancreatic, stomach, and prostate cancers [86]. Lifestyle factors including smoking, alcohol consumption, and diet al.so play important roles in cancer development.

In patients with HBV infection, it can be difficult to determine whether the increased cancer risk is solely due to HBV or is a result of a combination of HBV and other factors. For example, patients with HBV-related liver disease may also have a higher prevalence of alcohol-related liver damage, which can independently increase the risk of liver and other cancers [87]. Additionally, the presence of coinfections, such as with HCV or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), can further complicate the assessment of HBV-related cancer risks.

Future directions in HBV and extrahepatic cancer research

Emerging technologies for studying HBV and extrahepatic cancer link

Emerging technologies offer new opportunities for studying the link between HBV and extrahepatic cancer. MR analysis, as mentioned earlier, can be used to investigate causal relationships. By using genetic variants as instrumental variables, it can help overcome some of the limitations of observational studies in determining causality [5].

Advances in single-cell sequencing technologies also provide a powerful tool. Single-cell RNA sequencing can be used to characterize the immune microenvironment and cellular heterogeneity in HBV-related diseases and associated cancers. This can help identify specific cell populations and molecular pathways involved in the development of extrahepatic cancers, providing insights into potential therapeutic targets [77].

Potential for vaccine development in preventing HBV-related cancers

Vaccine development holds great potential in preventing HBV-related cancers. The existing HBV vaccines are highly effective in preventing HBV infection, which in turn can reduce the risk of HBV-related HCC. However, there is a need to further explore the potential of vaccines in preventing extrahepatic cancers associated with HBV.

Some research is focusing on developing therapeutic vaccines that can not only prevent HBV infection but also target HBV-related cancer cells. For example, vaccines targeting HBV-specific antigens could potentially stimulate the immune system to recognize and eliminate HBV-infected cells that may be at risk of developing into cancer. Additionally, combination vaccines that target both HBV and other factors associated with extrahepatic cancer development may be a future direction [88].

Strategic priorities for global research on HBV and extrahepatic cancer risk

Global research on HBV and extrahepatic cancer risk should prioritize several aspects. Firstly, there is a need for more large-scale, long-term epidemiological studies to clarify the causal relationship between HBV and extrahepatic cancers, especially in different populations and geographical regions. These studies can help in better understanding the true burden of HBV-related extrahepatic cancers and the factors that modify this risk. Secondly, research on the molecular mechanisms underlying the association between HBV and extrahepatic cancers should be intensified. This can lead to the identification of novel therapeutic targets and the development of more effective treatment strategies. Finally, efforts should be made to improve the implementation of existing prevention and treatment measures, such as increasing the coverage of HBV vaccination, optimizing antiviral therapy, and improving early detection methods for HBV-related extrahepatic cancers, especially in resource-limited settings [89].

Conclusion

Chronic hepatitis B virus infection is a well-established etiological factor for HCC and accumulating evidence suggests a potential association with several extrahepatic malignancies. Epidemiological studies, including large cohort analyses and meta-analyses, consistently support this association; however, causality remains controversial due to residual confounding factors such as coinfections (HCV, HPV, Helicobacter pylori), genetic susceptibility, and lifestyle factors. Mechanistic insights, such as viral DNA integration, HBx-driven oncogenic signaling, epigenetic modifications, and immune dysregulation provide plausible biological explanations but require further validation in extrahepatic tissues. Clinically, HBV-infected individuals may benefit from broader cancer surveillance beyond the liver, and antiviral therapy as well as vaccination may hold preventive value. Future research should focus on large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies, incorporating advanced molecular techniques such as single-cell sequencing and MR, and strengthening international collaboration. This will be crucial in clarifying causal relationships, identifying high-risk populations, and developing targeted prevention and treatment strategies, thereby reducing the overall burden of HBV-related cancers.
