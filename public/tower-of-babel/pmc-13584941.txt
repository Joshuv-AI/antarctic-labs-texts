Problematic Social Media Use and Self-Perception of Dental Aesthetics in Adolescents: Associations with Body Esteem, Social Anxiety and Gender
Authors: María Victoria Pérez Suárez, Ana Ruíz Guillén, María José González-Olmo, Marta Olmos Valverde, Martín Romero Maroto, María Carrillo Díaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBody esteem and self-perceived dental aesthetics play an important role in adolescents’ psychosocial well-being. Problematic social media use may be associated with greater comparison with idealised aesthetic standards. However, evidence regarding the association between problematic social media use, body esteem, and self-perceived dental aesthetics remains limited.ObjectiveThe aims of this study were to examine the associations among problematic social media use, body esteem, social anxiety and self-perceived dental aesthetics in adolescents and to evaluate the roles that Fear of Negative Evaluation (FNE), Body Mass Index (BMI), and gender play in these relationships.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 393 adolescents aged 13–18 years. They were asked to complete the Social Media Disorder Scale (SMD), body esteem (BESAA), self-perceived dental aesthetics (PIDAQ), and fear of negative evaluation (FNE) questionnaires, and BMI was calculated. Multiple linear regression and gender moderation analyses were performed.ResultsProblematic social media use was significantly associated with lower body esteem (r = −0.165, p =0.013) and poorer dental self-image (r =0.288, p <0.001). In the final regression model, FNE (β = −0.429, p <0.001) and BMI (β = −0.332, p <0.001) were significant predictors of lower body esteem, whereas problematic social media use was no longer significant (β = −0.066, p =0.267). The final model explained 29.6% of the variance in body esteem. Gender significantly moderated the relationship between problematic social media use and dental self-image (p =0.05). Among females, problematic social media use predicted higher PIDAQ scores (b = 2.99, p <0.001), whereas this relationship was not significant among males (b = 0.53, p =0.62).ConclusionProblematic social media use was associated with poorer dental self-image, particularly among females, whereas body esteem was mainly associated with FNE and BMI. These findings suggest that psychosocial factors and the digital environment may play a relevant role in adolescents’ psychosocial well-being and oral health.

Introduction

Adolescence is a developmental stage during which individuals are particularly sensitive to the formation of identity and self-image and physical appearance and social acceptance take on considerable importance. In this context, social media has become one of the main spaces for interaction and self-presentation among adolescents and young adults, fostering constant exposure to idealised aesthetic standards and ongoing processes of social comparison.1,2 The widespread dissemination of carefully selected and digitally edited images on social media may negatively influence body image and increase appearance-related concerns, particularly among adolescents who are more vulnerable to social comparison and interpersonal evaluation.3,4

In recent years, various studies have linked problematic social media use to anxiety, depressive symptoms, sleep disturbances, lower self-esteem, and greater psychological distress.5,6 Similarly, frequent exposure to appearance-focused content has been associated with higher levels of body dissatisfaction and social comparison; these effects are particularly evident among adolescent girls.7 Furthermore, factors such as the “fear of missing out” (FoMO) or the need for online social validation could intensify self-monitoring and concern about one’s personal image.8

In this context, social anxiety is a variable of particular research interest. Fear of negative evaluation (FNE), one of the core components of social anxiety, has been closely linked to problematic social media use and to greater sensitivity to interpersonal judgement.9 A recent meta-analysis confirmed a significant association between social anxiety and problematic social media use, suggesting that adolescents with greater interpersonal difficulties may be particularly vulnerable to the psychological effects of digital environments.10 Furthermore, gender differences appear to play a significant role, with females showing greater susceptibility to aesthetic pressure and social comparison on social media than males.11 These differences suggest that gender may moderate the relationship between problematic social media use and psychosocial outcomes related to dental aesthetics. The smile is one of the most visible facial features during social interaction and can significantly influence self-confidence, perceived attractiveness, and interpersonal acceptance.12 Although the influence of social media on body image has been extensively investigated,13 considerably less is known about its potential influence on dental aesthetics and the psychosocial perception of dental appearance. Emerging evidence suggests that social media can influence perceptions of the smile and interest in cosmetic dental treatments, increasing concerns about dental appearance and dissatisfaction with one’s own smile.14,15 Furthermore, physical variables such as body mass index (BMI) may contribute to a negative perception of body image and psychological distress associated with appearance, particularly among adolescent girls.16

Despite the growing body of research on social media and body image, there is limited evidence regarding the specific relationship between problematic social media use and the psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics during adolescence. Few studies have simultaneously integrated psychological variables such as social anxiety and fear of negative evaluation with perceptions of dental aesthetics and physical variables such as BMI within a single explanatory model. Understanding these relationships could prove particularly relevant at a stage characterised by heightened sensitivity to social evaluation and aesthetic self-perception.

The aim of this study was therefore to analyse the relationship between problematic social media use and various psychosocial variables related to dental aesthetics and body self-esteem in adolescents, including dental self-image, social impact, psychological impact, and social anxiety. It also aimed to assess the possible moderating role of gender in these relationships and the value of problematic social media use, FNE, and BMI in predicting body self-esteem.

This study explores the hypothesis that greater problematic use of social media will be associated with poorer psychosocial adjustment relating to dental aesthetics, and posits its possible link to poorer dental self-image, greater social and psychological impact, and higher levels of social anxiety. Furthermore, it is hypothesised that FNE and BMI will act as significant predictors of body self-esteem and that these associations will be more pronounced among females.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

An observational, cross-sectional, and analytical study was conducted with a sample of 393 adolescents, aged between 13 and 18, from schools in the eastern part of the city of Seville (Spain). The mean age of the participants was 15.5 years (SD = 1.44). Data were collected between February and June 2024 in the classrooms of participating schools using non-probabilistic convenience sampling. Schools were contacted through their school directors and invited to participate in the study. Participating school´s students accessed the anonymous questionnaire through a QR code under the supervision of the researchers and teaching staff. Responses were collected in a single anonymous database, and no school-level identifier was retained.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted using G*Power to evaluate the adequacy of the overall sample size. With a total sample of 393 participants, α =0.05, and three predictors, the study was sufficiently powered (80%) to detect small effect sizes (f2 = 0.028). Furthermore, according to Green’s recommendation for multiple regression analyses (N > 50 + 8m, where m represents the number of predictors), a minimum sample of 74 participants would be required for a model including three predictors.17 The final analytic sample used in the regression models (n = 227) substantially exceeded this recommendation. The inclusion criteria were adolescents aged between 13 and 18 years, proficiency in the Spanish language, and voluntary agreement to participate in the study. Questionnaires that were incomplete or contained inconsistent responses were excluded from the analysis. Likewise, subjects with previous diagnoses of psychiatric or neurodevelopmental disorders that might interfere with the assessment of social anxiety or social media use were excluded, as were those with systemic diseases or relevant medical conditions that might influence their perception of oral health-related quality of life.

Ethical Approval and Consent

Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants before inclusion in the study. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Rey Juan Carlos University (URJC) under reference number 090120240272024 and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Collection of Sociodemographic and Anthropometric Data

Basic sociodemographic data, including age and gender, as well as anthropometric data (weight and height), were collected through self-reported responses from the participants. BMI was calculated using self-reported weight and height data provided by the participants, a procedure previously used in adolescent populations.18–20 It was calculated by dividing body weight, expressed in kilograms, by height in metres squared (kg/m2). In the present study, BMI was used exclusively as a continuous variable in the statistical analyses and was not used to classify participants into age- and sex-specific weight-status categories.

Measurement of Psychosocial Parameters

Problematic social media use was assessed using the Social Media Disorder Scale (SMD).21,22 The scale was cross-nationally validated in adolescents from 44 countries, including Spain, by Boer et al23 This instrument comprises nine items with dichotomous responses (yes/no) that assess symptoms related to problematic social media use, such as loss of control, interpersonal conflict, and interference with daily activities. The SMD assesses problematic social media use independently of specific platforms or duration of use, focusing on the presence of addiction-like symptoms with its use.23 According to the criteria proposed by the authors, six or more affirmative responses indicate the presence of problematic social media use.24 For the purposes of the present study, the total SMD score (range 0–9) was analysed as a continuous measure of problematic social media use, with higher scores indicating a greater number of problematic social media use symptoms. Although a score of six or more affirmative responses has been proposed as a threshold for identifying probable problematic social media users, this cut-off was not used in the correlation, regression, or moderation analyses conducted in the present study. In the study sample, the scale demonstrated adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.749).

Social anxiety was assessed using the Social Anxiety Scale for Adolescents (SAS-A).25,26 The scale was validated in a Spanish-speaking adolescent population by Olivares et al26 The instrument comprises 22 items scored on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never true; 5 = always true). The scale assesses three main dimensions: FNE, social avoidance and distress in new situations (SAD-N), and social avoidance and distress-general (SAD-G). The total score is obtained by summing the items, with higher scores indicating greater levels of social anxiety. In this study we analysed both the total social anxiety score and the fear of negative evaluation subscale. In the study sample, internal consistency was adequate for the total scale (α = 0.88) and the FNE subscale (α = 0.869).

The psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics was assessed using the Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire (PIDAQ).12 The questionnaire was validated in Spanish adolescents by Montiel-Company et al27 This instrument comprises 23 items rated on a five-point Likert scale and measures how the perception of dental appearance influences an individual’s psychological and social well-being. The questionnaire comprises four dimensions: social impact, psychological impact, aesthetic concern, and dental self-confidence. Higher scores reflect a greater negative psychosocial impact associated with the perception of dental aesthetics. This study analysed both the total PIDAQ score and the subscales for social impact, psychological impact, and aesthetic concern. In the study sample, internal consistency was adequate for the total scale (α = 0.954).

Body self-esteem was assessed using the Body Esteem Scale for Adolescents (BESAA),28 which comprises 23 items across various dimensions relating to appearance, weight, and attribution. The instrument assesses adolescents’ evaluation of their own bodies and physical appearance, with items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”). The total score is obtained by summing the responses, with higher scores indicating higher levels of body self-esteem. In the study sample, the internal consistency of the scale was adequate (α = 0.907).

The questionnaires were administered in a self-report format under the supervision of the researchers, who provided standardised instructions and addressed any queries that arose during the completion process. The anonymity and confidentiality of the responses were always guaranteed.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0.0.0. Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) were calculated for all study variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between problematic social media use, psychosocial variables, body self-esteem, and BMI.

To identify predictors of body self-esteem (BESAA), a hierarchical linear regression analysis was performed. Problematic social media use was entered in Step 1, fear of negative evaluation (FNE) in Step 2, and BMI in Step 3. This order was conceptually determined, with problematic social media use entered first as the primary variable of interest, followed by FNE as a psychosocial factor and BMI as an anthropometric factor potentially associated with body self-esteem. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion. Therefore, the number of participants varied across analyses according to the availability of complete information for the variables included in each model. Most missing data were related to self-reported height and weight used to calculate BMI. The hierarchical regression analysis was conducted using data from 227 participants with complete information on problematic social media use, fear of negative evaluation, BMI, and body esteem. Regression coefficients (B and β) explained variance (R2), and changes in explained variance (ΔR2) were examined. This order was conceptually determined, with problematic social media use entered first as the primary variable of interest, followed by FNE as a psychosocial factor and BMI as an anthropometric factor potentially associated with body self-esteem.

Gender differences in the associations between problematic social media use and the study variables were tested using moderation analyses with Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS (Model 1). Problematic social media use was specified as the predictor, gender as the moderator, and each psychosocial variable was tested separately as the outcome. Significant interactions were probed using simple slope analyses. Statistical significance was set at p <0.05.

Results

Sample Description and Main Study Variables

The sample comprised 393 participants, of whom 67.2% were female and 32.8% were male. The participants’ ages ranged from 13 to 18 years, with a mean of 15.5 years (SD = 1.44), indicating a predominantly adolescent sample.

Regarding the main study variables, participants reported moderate levels of social media use or impact (M = 2.48; SD = 2.26). In psychological terms, FNE had a mean score of 19.26 (SD = 6.62), whilst social anxiety had a mean score of 43.35 (SD = 12.57).

Regarding dental self-image, the total PIDAQ score averaged 23.44 (SD = 18.90). The subdimensions of the instrument also reflected variability in the scores: social impact (M = 5.26; SD = 6.89), psychological impact (M = 3.89; SD = 4.82), and aesthetic concern (M = 3.57; SD = 3.95).

Regarding general body self-perception, body self-esteem (BESAA) had a mean of 53.96 (SD = 15.45). Finally, the BMI had a mean of 2179 (SD = 4.16), falling within a range consistent with normal weight for most participants.

Similarly, problematic social media use was associated with the various sub-dimensions of the PIDAQ, including social impact (r =0.301, p <0.001), psychological impact (r =0.238, p <0.001), and aesthetic concern (r =0.156, p =0.018). In the first model, problematic social media use was introduced as the sole predictor. The model was statistically significant—F (1,225) = 6.29, p =0.013—explaining 2.7% of the variance in body self-esteem (R2 =0.027).

Problematic social media use showed a significant negative effect on body self-esteem (B = −1.128; β = −0.165; p =0.013), indicating that greater exposure to social media is associated with lower body self-esteem.

Model 2

In the second model, FNE was included. This inclusion resulted in a significant increase in the explained variance, reaching 18.7% (ΔR2 =0.159, p <0.001).

In this model, FNE emerged as a robust predictor of body self-esteem (B = −0.978; β = −0.419; p < 0.001). Conversely, the association between problematic social media use and body self-esteem was no longer significant after FNE was included in the model. This finding indicates that FNE explained additional variance in body self-esteem beyond that accounted for by problematic social media use.

Finally, the third model incorporated BMI, which increased the explained variance to 29.6% (ΔR2 =0.109, p <0.001).

Gender Moderation in the Relationship Between Problematic Social Media Use and Psychosocial Variables

Models Without Significant Moderation

The following models did not show a significant interaction effect, indicating that the impact of problematic social media use was similar across both genders: FNE: p =0.15 (not significant); social anxiety: p =0.31 (not significant); dental aesthetic concern: p =0.28 (not significant); body self-esteem: p =0.10 (not significant); BMI: p =0.35 (not significant).

These results suggest that, for these variables—which are primarily associated with evaluative sensitivity, general social anxiety, dental self-confidence, and overall body perception— problematic social media use exerts a relatively uniform effect across genders.

Overall PIDAQ

The model showed a significant interaction effect between problematic social media use × gender (p =0.05), indicating that the relationship between problematic social media use and dental self-image differs by gender. Among females, problematic social media use predicted a significant increase in negative dental self-image, whilst among males, the slope was not significant.

The analysis of simple slopes revealed clear differences by gender. Among females, problematic social media use predicted significant increases in the PIDAQ score (b = 2.99, p <0.001), indicating that greater exposure to social media is associated with a more negative dental self-image.

Conversely, among males, the slope was small and not significant (b = 0.53, p =0.62), suggesting the absence of a significant relationship between problematic social media use and dental self-image in this group.

Social Impact of Dental Aesthetics

The interaction was significant (p <0.001). Among females, greater problematic social media use was associated with a greater negative social impact related to dental aesthetics, whilst among males, there was virtually no effect.

The simple slope analysis revealed clear differences according to gender. Among girls, problematic social media use predicted significant increases in the social impact related to dental aesthetics (b = 1.23, p <0.001), indicating that higher levels of problematic social media use are associated with greater levels of perceived social interference. In contrast, among boys, the slope was virtually zero and not significant (b = –0.06, p =0.89), suggesting the absence of a relationship between problematic social media use and social impact in this group.

Psychological Impact of Dental Aesthetics

A significant interaction was also observed (p =0.02). Among females, problematic social media use predicted a greater psychological impact related to their dental appearance; among males, the relationship was not significant.

The analysis of simple slopes also revealed differences between genders. Among females, problematic social media use was associated with significant increases in the psychological impact of dental aesthetics (b = 0.69, p <0.001), showing that increased exposure to social media is linked to greater emotional distress related to dental self-image. Conversely, among males, the slope was not significant (b = –0.06, p =0.82), indicating that problematic social media use does not predict variations in this dimension within this group.

Taken together, these results indicate that gender moderates the relationship between problematic social media use and dental self-image, showing that girls are more vulnerable to the impact of social media on their perception of dental aesthetics than boys are.

Discussion

The results showed positive and significant associations between problematic social media use, fear of negative evaluation and social anxiety. Specifically, adolescents with higher levels of problematic social media use scored higher on both fear of negative evaluation and social anxiety. Furthermore, these two psychological variables were closely correlated, suggesting that they share relevant cognitive and emotional mechanisms during adolescence.

These findings are consistent with the meta-analysis conducted by Shannon et al29 and the theoretical model proposed by La Greca and López,25 in which they observed that problematic social media use is associated with various indicators of social distress, including social anxiety, with fear of negative evaluation being one of the central components of social anxiety in adolescents. Similarly, Wu et al10 found a moderate positive correlation between social anxiety and problematic social media use, confirming that this association holds consistently across different cultural contexts and age groups. In this context, Piko et al30 highlighted that adolescence is a particularly sensitive period for the development of concerns relating to social acceptance and self-image; continuous exposure to contexts of social evaluation, such as social media, may therefore be associated with greater anxiety among adolescents with greater psychological vulnerability.

Furthermore, our results showed that both fear of negative evaluation (FNE) and body mass index were significantly associated with body self-esteem. In this regard, Yang et al31 (2020) proposed an explanatory model according to which exposure to appearance-focused content encourages physical comparison with others and increases anxiety about potential negative evaluations related to physical appearance, thereby affecting body self-esteem.

Furthermore, BMI also showed a negative association with body self-esteem, such that higher values were associated with a poorer assessment of one’s own body. This finding is consistent with the studies by Griffiths et al,32 Quick et al33 and Voelker et al,34 who identified that BMI and perceptions of one’s own weight status are factors closely linked to body satisfaction during adolescence.

In the sample analysed, greater problematic use of social media was associated with a poorer dental self-image, reflected both in higher scores on the overall PIDAQ and higher levels on the subscales relating to dental appearance. These findings suggest that problematic social media use may be associated with how adolescents perceive and evaluate their own dental aesthetics. Baik et al15 and Freire et al35 observed that digital platforms can alter patients’ expectations regarding the ideal smile and increase interest in cosmetic dental treatments driven by an altered self-perception, rather than by an actual clinical need.14,36 This may be due to continuous exposure to carefully selected images of ideal smiles, which encourages the internalisation of unattainable aesthetic standards in the pursuit of social validation and online approval.31,37

Particularly relevant is the observed association between problematic social media use and the “social impact” and “psychological impact” dimensions of the PIDAQ. These findings suggest that problematic social media use may also associate with how adolescents perceive their social relationships and emotional well-being. In this regard, Ali et al38 demonstrated that individuals with greater sensitivity to social evaluation are more vulnerable to the effects of digital interactions.

Another finding of this study was the moderating effect of gender on the relationship between problematic social media use and various dimensions of dental self-image. Specifically, female participants showed a greater impact on the total PIDAQ score, as well as on the social impact and psychological impact dimensions, suggesting a strong association between problematic social media use and perceptions of dental aesthetics among females. However, this moderating effect was not observed in variables such as FNE, social anxiety, and body self-esteem, indicating that gender differences appear to be concentrated specifically in the aesthetic and dentofacial sphere, in line with the findings of Rai et al.39 Although the additional variance explained by the interaction terms was modest, the fact that significant moderation effects were consistently observed across several dental self-image outcomes, but not in the other psychosocial variables examined, suggests a specific role of gender in the relationship between problematic social media use and dental self-perception. In studies specifically focusing on dental aesthetics, it was observed that adolescent girls had significantly higher scores on the total PIDAQ, as well as across all its dimensions, suggesting a greater emotional and social impact of dental appearance compared with boys.40,41 The findings suggest that problematic social media use may be linked to a more negative perception of dental aesthetics and a greater psychosocial impact associated with dental appearance, particularly among adolescent girls. These results reinforce the need to promote digital education and prevention strategies focused on developing a healthy body and dental image, as well as on the early identification of adolescents who are particularly vulnerable to aesthetic pressure and social evaluation. Collaboration among paediatric dentists, orthodontists, psychologists, and education professionals could contribute to a more comprehensive approach to these issues.

Among the limitations of this study is the cross-sectional design, which prevents establishing causal relationships or determining the directionality of the associations between the variables analysed. Furthermore, the data were collected via self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, on the other hand, as no school identifiers were retained in the anonymised database, it was not possible to assess whether participants had been grouped by school. Additionally, potentially relevant variables, such as the time spent using different platforms, the type of visual content consumed, previous or current orthodontic treatment, and other dental conditions that could affect self-perceived dental aesthetics, were not collected and could influence the observed associations, potentially resulting in residual confounding. In addition, weight and height were self-reported, which may introduce measurement bias in the derived BMI values. As several outcomes were examined, the potential for Type I error associated with multiple testing should be considered when interpreting the moderation findings. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the directionality of the relationships between problematic social media use and the psychosocial variables related to dental aesthetics, as well as design and evaluate the effectiveness of multidisciplinary preventive interventions.

Conclusion

Problematic social media use was associated with poorer dental self-image, greater social impact, and greater psychological distress among female adolescents, whereas higher levels of social anxiety were observed in both genders. In contrast, fear of negative evaluation and body mass index were independently associated with body esteem, while problematic social media use showed no additional independent association after accounting for these variables. Overall, these findings suggest that the associations between problematic social media use and psychosocial outcomes are particularly evident in the dental and psychological domains among female adolescents.
