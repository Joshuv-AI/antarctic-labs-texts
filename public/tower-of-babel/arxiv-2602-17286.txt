Comparing numbers of diagonal subsemigroups and congruences for semigroups
Callum Barber, Nik Ruškuc

Abstract
. Given a semigroup S S , a diagonal subsemigroup ρ \rho is defined to be a reflexive and compatible relation on S S , i.e. a subsemigroup of the direct square S × S S\times S containing the diagonal { ( s , s ) : s ∈ S } \{(s,s)\colon s\in S\} . When S S is finite, we define the DSC coefficient χ ⁡ ( S ) \chi(S) to be the ratio of the number of congruences to the number of diagonal subsemigroups. In a previous work we observed that χ ⁡ ( S ) = 1 \chi(S)=1 if and only if S S is a group. Here we show that for any rational α \alpha with 0 < α ≤ 1 0<\alpha\leq 1 , there exists a semigroup with χ ⁡ ( S ) = α \chi(S)=\alpha . We do this by utilizing the Rees matrix construction and adapting the congruence classification of such semigroups to describe their diagonal subsemigroups.

1.
Introduction

One of the most fundamental concepts in algebra is that of a congruence.
Given a semigroup
S
a
congruence
on
S
is an equivalence relation
\rho
that is compatible with the multiplication, i.e.
(x,y),(z,t)\in\rho\implies(xz,yt)\in\rho
.
It is easy to see that every congruence is a subsemigroup of the direct square
S\times S
.
A more general type of subsemigroup of
S\times S
is a
diagonal subsemigroup
, which is a reflexive relation that is compatible with the multiplication of the semigroup.
Our previous paper on this topic
[
1
]
explored semigroups with the property that every diagonal subsemigroup is a congruence.
We call such a semigroup
DSC
.
The starting point in that paper is the following foundational observation:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S
be a finite semigroup. Then
S
is DSC if and only if
S
is a group.

For infinite semigroups things are more interesting in several ways.
Periodic groups are still DSC, but the additive group of integers is not DSC.
We actually currently do not know whether there exists a DSC group that contains an element of infinite order.
Furthermore, in
[
1
]
we showed that there exists DSC semigroups that are not groups.

We can re-phrase Theorem
1.1
as follows.
Let
\text{Cong}(S)
be the set of congruences on
S
and
\text{Diag}(S)
the set of diagonal subsemigroups on
S
.
When
S
is finite both of these sets are finite. We define the
DSC coefficient
of such
S
to be:

\chi(S)=\frac{|\text{Cong}(S)|}{|\text{Diag}(S)|}.

Clearly
\chi(S)\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1]
.
Then Theorem
1.1
asserts that:

\chi(S)=1\iff S\text{ is a group}.

One can think of the DSC coefficient as a measure of how close to being DSC a semigroup is and wonder what values
\chi(S)
takes as
S
ranges over all finite semigroups.
The main aim of this paper is to prove the following result:

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1]
there is a finite semigroup
S
with
\chi(S)=\alpha
.

To prove this result we will use the Rees matrix construction.
This construction was originally introduced by Suschkewitsch who employed it to give a full structural description of completely simple semigroups
[
9
]
.
Here we take a group,
G
, two index sets
I
and
\Lambda
and a
\Lambda\times I
matrix
P
with entries from
G
.
The Rees matrix semigroup
\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P]
is the set
I\times G\times\Lambda
with multiplication:

(i,g,\lambda)(j,h,\mu)=(i,gp_{\lambda j}h,\mu).

The main tool we will use to prove Theorem
1.2
is characterizing the diagonal subsemigroups on a Rees matrix semigroup.
There is a description of the congruences on a Rees matrix semigroup in terms of normal subgroups of the group and equivalence relations on the index sets, originally due to Preston
[
7
]
.
It turns out that there is an analogous description for the diagonal subsemigroups on a Rees matrix semigroup, namely we can describe them using normal subgroups of the group and reflexive relations on the index sets, with the caveat that the group must be DSC.

The paper is structured as follows. In section 2 we give the characterization of the diagonal subsemigroups on a Rees matrix semigroup.
In Section 3 we use this characterization to evaluate the DSC coefficient for a Rees matrix semigroup, and show that we can obtain any rational number between zero and one as the DSC coefficient of a Rees matrix semigroup.
In the final section we make some observations about the DSC coefficient of Clifford semigroups and contrast this to the result for Rees matrix semigroups.

We will only require some basic concepts from semigroup theory which will be introduced as they are needed.
For this paper we will use
\mathbb{N}
to denote the set
\{1,2,3\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
Given a set
X
we will let
\Delta_{X}=\{(x,x):x\in X\}
be the diagonal relation on
X
.
For a subset
\rho
of
X\times Y
, we will write
\rho^{-1}
for the set
\{(y,x):(x,y)\in\rho\}
.

2.
Diagonal Subsemigroups on Rees matrix semigroups

Let
S
be a Rees matrix semigroup
\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P]
over the group
G
.
We start this section by reviewing the congruence description for Rees matrix semigroups; for a more detailed exploration see
[
4
, Section 3.5]
.
For any
i,j\in I
and
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
we define an
extract
of the matrix
P
to be
q_{\lambda\mu ij}=p_{\lambda i}p^{-1}_{\mu i}p_{\mu j}p_{\lambda j}^{-1}
.
We will be interested in triples of the form
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
, where
N
is a normal subgroup of
G
,
\mathcal{S}
is a relation on
I
and
\mathcal{T}
is a relation on
\Lambda
.
A triple is
linked
if for all
i,j\in I
and
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
:

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\implies q_{\lambda\mu ij}\in N,

\displaystyle(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\implies q_{\lambda\mu ij}\in N.

And a triple is an
equivalence triple
if the relations
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{T}
are equivalence relations.
Normally in the literature linked equivalence triples are simply called
linked triples
.
However, as we will need another type of triple, where the relations are just reflexive, we will use the full term linked equivalence triple.

If we have a relation
\rho
on
S
, then we can get a triple
(N_{\rho},\rho_{I},\rho_{\Lambda})
where:

(1)

\displaystyle N_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\{g\in G:(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,1,\lambda),\forall i\in I,\lambda\in\Lambda\},

(2)

\displaystyle\rho_{I}

\displaystyle=\{(i,j):(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)\,\rho\,(j,p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\lambda),\forall\lambda\in\Lambda\},

(3)

\displaystyle\rho_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\{(\lambda,\mu):(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,p_{\mu i}^{-1},\mu),\forall i\in I\}.

If
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
is a triple, we define a relation
\rho_{N,\mathcal{S,T}}
by:

(4)

\begin{split}(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho_{N,\mathcal{S,T}}\,(j,h,\mu)\iff&(i,j)\in\mathcal{S},(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathcal{T},\\
&p_{\nu i}gp_{\lambda k}p_{\mu k}^{-1}h^{-1}p_{\nu j}^{-1}\in N,\forall k\in I,\nu\in\Lambda.\end{split}

Then we have the following result due to Preston
[
7
]
that gives a direct correspondence between the congruences on a Rees matrix semigroup and the collection of linked equivalence triples:

Theorem 2.1
.

If
\rho
is a congruence then
(N_{\rho},\rho_{I},\rho_{\Lambda})
is a linked equivalence triple and if
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
is a linked equivalence triple then
\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
is a congruence.
Furthermore the maps
\rho\mapsto(N_{\rho},\rho_{I},\rho_{\Lambda})
and
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})\mapsto\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
are mutual inverses. ∎

This bijection also preserves the structure of the congruence lattice, as shown by Kapp and Schneider
[
5
]
, but it will not be required for this paper.

In the rest of this section we show that a very similar result holds for the diagonal subsemigroups on a Rees matrix semigroup under the additional assumption that the group
G
is DSC.
The classification will feature a weaker version of linked equivalence triples

(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
where
the relations
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}
are only required to be reflexive; we call them
linked reflexive triples
.

Before we set up a bijection between linked reflexive triples and diagonal subsemigroups we will require the following fact about diagonal subsemigroups on Rees matrix semigroups where the group is DSC.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\rho
be a diagonal subsemigroup of
S
.
If
(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,h,\lambda)
then
(i,g^{-1},\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,h^{-1},\lambda)
.

Proof.

The
\mathcal{H}
-class
H=H_{i\lambda}=\{(i,g,\lambda):g\in G\}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P]
that is isomorphic to
G
.
When we restrict
\rho
to
H
we get a diagonal subsemigroup on
H
, which must be a congruence as
H
is DSC.
So by symmetry,
(i,h,\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,g,\lambda)
.
Therefore:

\displaystyle(i,g^{-1},\lambda)

\displaystyle=(i,g^{-1}p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)(i,h,\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1}h^{-1},\lambda)

\displaystyle\hskip 4.5pt\rho\hskip 4.5pt(i,g^{-1}p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)(i,g,\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1}h^{-1},\lambda)=(i,h^{-1},\lambda).\qed

The proofs of the remainder of the results in this section are adapted from
[
4
, Section 3.5]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\rho
be a diagonal subsemigroup, then
(N_{\rho},\rho_{I},\rho_{\Lambda})
is a linked reflexive triple.

Proof.

As
\rho
is reflexive, it is easy to see that
\rho_{I}
and
\rho_{\Lambda}
are reflexive, and
1\in N_{\rho}
.
Take
g,h\in N_{\rho}
, then for all
i\in I
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
we have
(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,1,\lambda)
and
(i,h,\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,1,\lambda)
.
Hence we have:

\displaystyle(i,gh,\lambda)=(i,g,\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-2},\lambda)(i,h,\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,1,\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-2},\lambda)(i,1,\lambda)=(i,1,\lambda).

It follows that
gh\in N_{\rho}
, and so
N_{\rho}
is a subsemigroup of
G
.
As
(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,1,\lambda)
, by Lemma
2.2
we have that
(i,g^{-1},\lambda)\,\rho\,(i,1,\lambda)
for all
i\in I
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
.
Thus
g^{-1}\in N_{\rho}
and
N_{\rho}
is a subgroup of
G
.
Finally if we have
n\in G
then:

\displaystyle(i,n^{-1}gn,\lambda)

\displaystyle=(i,n^{-1}p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)(i,g,\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1}n,\lambda)

\displaystyle\hskip 4.5pt\rho\hskip 4.5pt(i,n^{-1}p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)(i,1,\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1}n,\lambda)=(i,1,\lambda).

Therefore
n^{-1}gn\in N_{\rho}
and
N_{\rho}
is a normal subgroup of
G
.

Now let
i,j\in I
and
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
.
Suppose that
(i,j)\in\rho_{I}
.
So
(i,p_{\mu i}^{-1},\mu)\,\rho\,(j,p_{\mu j}^{-1},\mu)
and for arbitrary
k\in I
and
\nu\in\Lambda
we have:

\displaystyle(k,p_{\lambda i}p_{\mu i}^{-1}p_{\mu j}p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\nu)

\displaystyle=(k,1,\lambda)(i,p_{\mu i}^{-1},\mu)(j,p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\nu)

\displaystyle\hskip 4.5pt\rho\hskip 4.5pt(k,1,\lambda)(j,p_{\mu j}^{-1},\mu)(j,p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\nu)

\displaystyle=(k,1,\nu).

Hence
q_{\lambda\mu ij}=p_{\lambda i}p_{\mu i}^{-1}p_{\mu j}p_{\lambda j}^{-1}\in N_{\rho}
.
The case when
(\lambda,\mu)\in\rho_{\Lambda}
is analogous, and thus

(N_{\rho},\rho_{I},\rho_{\Lambda})
is a linked reflexive triple.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

The relation
\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
is a diagonal subsemigroup on
S
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
are reflexive it follows that
\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
is reflexive.
If we have
(i_{1},g_{1},\lambda_{1})\,\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}\,(j_{1},h_{1},\mu_{1})
and
(i_{2},g_{2},\lambda_{2})\,\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}\,(j_{2},h_{2},\mu_{2})
then we want to show that:

(i_{1},g_{1}p_{\lambda_{1}i_{2}}g_{2},\lambda_{2})\,\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}\,(j_{1},h_{1}p_{\mu_{1}j_{2}}h_{2},\mu_{2}).

This is equivalent to showing:

(1)

(i_{1},j_{1})\in\mathcal{S}
,

(2)

(\lambda_{2},\mu_{2})\in\mathcal{T}
,

(3)

Np_{\nu i_{1}}g_{1}p_{\lambda_{1}i_{2}}g_{2}p_{\lambda_{2}x}=Np_{\nu j_{1}}h_{1}p_{\mu_{1}j_{2}}h_{2}p_{\mu_{2}x},\forall k\in I,\nu\in\Lambda
.

Statements (1) and (2) follow immediately from the definition of
\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
.
For (3) fix
k\in I
and
\nu\in\Lambda
.
From the definition of
\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
we get that
Np_{\nu i_{1}}g_{1}p_{\lambda_{1}i_{2}}=Np_{\nu j_{1}}h_{1}p_{\mu_{1}i_{2}},Np_{\mu_{1}i_{2}}g_{2}p_{\lambda_{2}k}=Np_{\mu_{1}j_{2}}h_{2}p_{\mu_{2}k}
.
Now we have:

\displaystyle Np_{\nu i_{1}}g_{1}p_{\lambda_{1}i_{2}}g_{2}p_{\lambda_{2}k}

\displaystyle=Np_{\nu i_{1}}g_{1}p_{\lambda_{1}i_{2}}Np_{\mu_{1}i_{2}}^{-1}Np_{\mu_{1}i_{2}}g_{2}p_{\lambda_{2}k}

\displaystyle=Np_{\nu j_{1}}h_{1}p_{\mu_{1}i_{2}}Np_{\mu_{1}i_{2}}^{-1}Np_{\mu_{1}j_{2}}h_{2}p_{\mu_{2}k}

\displaystyle=Np_{\nu j_{1}}h_{1}p_{\mu_{1}j_{2}}h_{2}p_{\mu_{2}k}.\qed

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
be a linked reflexive triple with
\rho=\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
.
Then
N_{\rho}=N,\rho_{I}=\mathcal{S}
and
\rho_{\Lambda}=\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

To see that
N_{\rho}=N
notice that:

\displaystyle g\in N_{\rho}

\displaystyle\iff(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}\,(i,1,\lambda),\forall i\in I,\lambda\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\iff(i,i)\in\mathcal{S},(\lambda,\lambda)\in\mathcal{T},Np_{\nu i}gp_{\lambda k}=Np_{\nu i}p_{\lambda k},\forall i,k\in I,\lambda,\nu\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\iff g\in N.

Noting that, as the triple is linked, if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}
then
Np_{\nu i}p_{\lambda i}^{-1}=Np_{\nu j}p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\forall\lambda,\nu\in\Lambda
.
So we have:

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\rho_{I}

\displaystyle\iff(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)\,\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}\,(j,p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\lambda),\forall\lambda\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\iff(i,j)\in\mathcal{S},(\lambda,\lambda)\in\mathcal{T},Np_{\nu i}p_{\lambda i}^{-1}p_{\lambda k}=Np_{\nu j}p_{\lambda j}^{-1}p_{\lambda k},\forall k\in I,\lambda,\nu\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\iff(i,j)\in\mathcal{S},Np_{\nu i}p_{\lambda i}^{-1}=Np_{\nu j}p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\forall\lambda,\nu\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\iff(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}.

Hence
\rho_{I}=\mathcal{S}
.
The proof of
\mathcal{T}=\rho_{\Lambda}
is dual to this.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\rho
be a diagonal subsemigroup on
S
with
N=N_{\rho},\mathcal{S}=\rho_{I}
and
\mathcal{T}=\rho_{\Lambda}
.
Then
\rho=\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho^{\prime}=\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
.
Suppose
(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho\,(j,h,\mu)
,
to show
(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho^{\prime}\,(j,h,\mu)
we have to prove that:

(1)

(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}
,

(2)

(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathcal{T}
,

(3)

Np_{\nu i}gp_{\lambda k}=Np_{\nu j}hp_{\mu k},\forall k\in I,\nu\in\Lambda
.

Which is in turn equivalent to:

(1)

(i,p_{\nu i}^{-1},\nu)\,\rho\,(j,p_{\nu j}^{-1},\nu),\forall\nu\in\Lambda
,

(2)

(k,p_{\lambda k}^{-1},\lambda)\,\rho\,(k,p_{\mu k}^{-1},\mu),\forall k\in I
,

(3)

(l,p_{\nu i}gp_{\lambda k}p_{\mu k}^{-1}h^{-1}p_{\nu j}^{-1},\omega)\,\rho\,(l,1,\omega),\forall k,l\in I,\nu,\omega\in\Lambda
.

For (1) let
\nu
be any element of
\Lambda
and note that:

\displaystyle(i,p_{\nu i}gp_{\lambda i}^{2},\lambda)

\displaystyle=(i,1,\nu)(i,g,\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i},\lambda)

\displaystyle\hskip 4.5pt\rho\hskip 4.5pt(i,1,\nu)(j,h,\mu)(i,p_{\lambda i},\lambda)=(i,p_{\nu j}hp_{\mu i}p_{\lambda i},\lambda).

Let
g^{\prime}=p_{\nu i}gp_{\lambda i}^{2}
and
h^{\prime}=p_{\nu j}hp_{\mu i}p_{\lambda i}
.
It follows from Lemma
2.2
that
(i,(g^{\prime})^{-1},\lambda)\rho\\
(i,(h^{\prime})^{-1},\lambda)
.
Therefore:

(i,g,\lambda)(i,1,\lambda)(i,(g^{\prime})^{-1},\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\nu)\,\rho\,(j,h,\mu)(i,1,\lambda)(i,(h^{\prime})^{-1},\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\nu).

Evaluating both sides gives
(i,p_{\nu i}^{-1},\nu)\,\rho\,(j,p_{\nu j}^{-1},\nu)
.
The proof of (2) is analogous to this.
For (3) let
k,l\in I
and
\nu,\omega\in\Lambda
. Then:

\displaystyle(l,p_{\nu i}gp_{\lambda k}p_{\mu k}^{-1}h^{-1}p_{\nu j}^{-1},\omega)

\displaystyle=(l,1,\nu)(i,g,\lambda)(k,p_{\mu k}^{-1}h^{-1}p_{\nu j}^{-1},\omega)

\displaystyle\hskip 4.5pt\rho\hskip 4.5pt(l,1,\nu)(j,h,\mu)(k,p_{\mu k}^{-1}h^{-1}p_{\nu j}^{-1},\omega)

\displaystyle=(l,1,\omega).

Hence
(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho^{\prime}\,(j,h,\mu)
.
For the reverse containment suppose
(i,g,\lambda)\,\rho^{\prime}\,(j,h,\mu)
.
It follows from the definition that of
\rho^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle(i,p_{\lambda i}gp_{\lambda i}p_{\mu i}^{-1}h^{-1}p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\lambda)

\displaystyle\,\rho\,(i,1,\lambda),

\displaystyle(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)

\displaystyle\,\rho\,(i,p_{\mu i}^{-1},\mu),

\displaystyle(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)

\displaystyle\,\rho\,(j,p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\lambda).

Therefore, the following two elements are
\rho
-related:

\displaystyle(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-2},\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}gp_{\lambda i}p_{\mu i}^{-1}h^{-1}p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\lambda)(j,h,\mu)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-1},\lambda)=(i,g,\lambda),

and

\displaystyle(j,p_{\lambda j}^{-1},\lambda)(i,p_{\lambda i}^{-2},\lambda)(i,1,\lambda)(j,h,\mu)(i,p_{\mu i}^{-1},\mu)=(j,h,\mu).

Hence
\rho=\rho^{\prime}
and this completes the proof.
∎

By combining the above lemmas we obtain the following analogue of Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
S=\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P]
be a Rees matrix semigroup over a DSC group
G
.
The maps
\rho\mapsto(N_{\rho},\rho_{I},\rho_{\Lambda})
and
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})\mapsto\rho_{N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
are mutual inverses and so give a one to one correspondence between diagonal subsemigroups on a Rees matrix semigroup and the set of linked reflexive triples. ∎

Any finite group is DSC, so the above result certainly applies to finite Rees matrix semigroups, which is the context within which we will work in the next section.

3.
DSC Coefficient for Rees Matrix Semigroups

For this section let
S=\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P]
be a finite Rees matrix semigroup over a group
G
.
Recall the DSC coefficient of
S
is defined as:

\chi(S)=\frac{|\text{Cong}(S)|}{|\text{Diag}(S)|}.

As already observed, both Theorems
2.1
and
2.7
apply in this case, yielding:

(5)

\chi(\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P])=\frac{\text{number of linked equivalence triples}}{\text{number of linked reflexive triples}}.

In what follows we will need some easy facts about extracts.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
N
be a normal subgroup of
G
,
i,j,i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\in I
and
\lambda,\mu,\lambda_{0},\dots,\\
\lambda_{n}\in\Lambda
be arbitrary. Then the following hold:

(1)

If
i=j
or
\lambda=\mu
then
q_{\lambda\mu ij}=1\in N
.

(2)

If
q_{\lambda\mu ij}\in N
, then
q_{\mu\lambda ij},q_{\mu\lambda ji},q_{\lambda\mu ji}\in N
.

(3)

If
q_{\lambda\mu i_{0}i_{1}},q_{\lambda\mu i_{1}i_{2}},\dots,q_{\lambda\mu i_{n-1}i_{n}}\in N
, then
q_{\lambda\mu i_{0}i_{n}}\in N
.

(4)

If
q_{\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}ij},q_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}ij},\dots,q_{\lambda_{n-1}\lambda_{n}ij}\in N
then
q_{\lambda_{0}\lambda_{n}ij}\in N
.

Proof.

(1)

Obvious.

(2)

Follows from
q_{\lambda\mu ij}^{-1}=q_{\lambda\mu ji}
and
p_{\mu i}p_{\lambda i}^{-1}q_{\lambda\mu ij}p_{\lambda i}p_{\mu i}^{-1}=q_{\mu\lambda ji}
.

(3)

Follows from
q_{\lambda\mu i_{0}i_{1}}q_{\lambda\mu i_{1}i_{2}}=q_{\lambda\mu i_{0}i_{2}}
.

(4)

Analogous to (3).

∎

From this we immediately obtain the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

The triple
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
is linked if and only if the triples
(N,\mathcal{S},\Delta_{\Lambda})
and
(N,\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})
are linked. ∎

If we have a relation
\rho
on a set
X
, we define
\langle\rho\rangle
to be the intersection of all equivalence relations on
X
that contain
\rho
.
So
\langle\rho\rangle
is the smallest (by inclusion) equivalence relation on
X
that contains
\rho
.
It is well known that
(x,y)\in\langle\rho\rangle
if and only if there is a sequence
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}=y
, where for each
i=0,1,\dots,n-1
, either
(x_{i},x_{i+1})\in\rho
or
(x_{i+1},x_{i})\in\rho
.
If
\mathcal{R}
is a set of relations on
X
then define
\langle\mathcal{R}\rangle=\langle\bigcup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\rho\rangle
.
It turns out that generating an equivalence relation in this way respects the notion of being linked.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
N
be a normal subgroup of
G
.
Suppose we have a set of relations
\mathcal{R}_{I}
on
I
such that for each
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{R}_{I}
the triple
(N,\mathcal{S},\Delta_{\Lambda})
is linked.
Then
(N,\langle\mathcal{R}_{I}\rangle,\Delta_{\Lambda})
is a linked triple.
Dually if
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}
is a set of relations on
\Lambda
such that for each
\mathcal{T}\in\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}
the triple
(N,\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})
is linked, then
(N,\Delta_{I},\langle\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\rangle)
is also linked.

Proof.

Let
i,j\in I
and
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
.
If
(\lambda,\mu)\in\Delta_{\Lambda}
then
\lambda=\mu
and so
q_{\lambda\mu ij}=1\in N
.
Now suppose that
(i,j)\in\langle\mathcal{R}_{I}\rangle
.
There is a sequence
i=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=j
such that for each
k=0,1,\dots,n-1
,
(x_{k},x_{k+1})
or
(x_{k+1},x_{k})\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
for some
\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}_{I}
.
As the triples
(N,\mathcal{S}_{k},\Delta_{\Lambda})
are linked we have that

or
q_{\lambda\mu x_{k+1}x_{k}}\in N
.
It follows from Lemma

that
q_{\lambda\mu x_{0}x_{n}}=q_{\lambda\mu ij}\in N
.
Hence the triple
(N,\langle\mathcal{R}_{I}\rangle,\Delta_{\Lambda})
is linked.
It follows similarly that
(N,\Delta_{I},\langle\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\rangle)
is also linked.
∎

As
G
is finite we can list the normal subgroups of

as
G=N_{1},\dots,N_{n}=1
.
Consider the following sets:

\displaystyle E_{I,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{S}:(N_{k},\mathcal{S},\Delta_{\Lambda})\text{ is a linked equivalence triple}\},

\displaystyle E_{\Lambda,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{T}:(N_{k},\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})\text{ is a linked equivalence triple}\},

\displaystyle R_{I,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{S}:(N_{k},\mathcal{S},\Delta_{\Lambda})\text{ is a linked reflexive triple}\},

\displaystyle R_{\Lambda,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{T}:(N_{k},\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})\text{ is a linked reflexive triple}\}.

It follows from (

that:

(6)

\chi(\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P])=\frac{|E_{I,1}||E_{\Lambda,1}|+\dots+|E_{I,n}||E_{\Lambda,n}|}{|R_{I,1}||R_{\Lambda,1}|+\dots+|R_{I,n}||R_{\Lambda,n}|}.

Thus we will be interested in trying to evaluate the sizes of these sets.
As
N_{1}=G
we have that the triples
(N_{1},\mathcal{S},\Delta_{\Lambda}),(N_{1},\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})
are always linked for any
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
.
So we have that:

(7)

|E_{I,1}|=B(|I|),\quad|E_{\Lambda,1}|=B(|\Lambda|),\quad|R_{I,1}|=2^{|I|^{2}-|I|},\quad|R_{\Lambda,1}|=2^{|\Lambda|^{2}-|\Lambda|}

and hence:

\frac{|E_{I,1}||E_{\Lambda,1}|}{|R_{I,1}||R_{\Lambda,1}|}=\frac{B(|I|)B(|\Lambda|)}{2^{|I|^{2}-|I|}2^{|\Lambda|^{2}-|\Lambda|}}.

Here

is the

is the number of reflexive relations on a set of size

The number on the right of the equation is the DSC coefficient of the rectangular band

.
It turns out that for fixed

and

the DSC coefficient of

cannot be lower than this.
The main aim of this section is to prove the following result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
I
and
\Lambda
be sets of size
a,b>1
respectively.
Then for any group
G
and
\Lambda\times I
matrix

with entries from

we have that:

\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}}\leq\chi(\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P])<1.

Furthermore for any rational
\alpha\in\left[\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}},1\right)
, there is a group
G
and a matrix
P
with
\chi(\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P])=\alpha
.

Remark 3.5
.

Let us pause to observe what happens when

or
\Lambda
has size

.
First assume that
|I|=1
. For any relation
\mathcal{T}
on
\Lambda
and normal subgroup
N
of
G
we have that
(N,\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})
is a linked triple.
This is because every extract is equal to

.
So as

is the number of normal subgroups of

\chi(\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P])=\frac{nB(|\Lambda|)}{n2^{|\Lambda|^{2}-|\Lambda|}}=\frac{B(|\Lambda|)}{2^{|\Lambda|^{2}-|\Lambda|}}

An analogous result holds when

.

To prove Theorem

we will require the following two auxiliary results. The first of which is obvious:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let

, with
\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}\leq\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
for all

.
Then

∎

\displaystyle\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}\leq\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{n}}{b_{1}+b_{2}+\dots+b_{n}}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let

be such that

.
Then:

\frac{B(n)}{2^{n^{2}-n}}\leq\frac{B(k_{1})}{2^{k_{1}^{2}-k_{1}}}\frac{B(k_{2})}{2^{k_{2}^{2}-k_{2}}}\cdots\frac{B(k_{r})}{2^{k_{r}^{2}-k_{r}}}.

Proof.

First we will show that:

B(s+t)\leq B(s)B(t)2^{st}.

Let

be a set of size

and let
S,T
be a partition of

into sets of size

and

respectively.
Given an equivalence relation
\rho
on

we obtain equivalence relations
\rho_{S}=\rho\cap S\times S,\rho_{T}=\rho\cap T\times T
on
S
and
T
respectively.
We can also obtain a subset of
S\times T
from
\rho
:

Y_{\rho}=\{(s,t):s\in S,t\in T,(s,t)\in\rho\}.

Clearly
\rho=\rho_{S}\cup\rho_{T}\cup Y_{\rho}\cup Y_{\rho}^{-1}
, so any equivalence relation on
X
can be constructed from an equivalence on
S
, an equivalence on
T
and a subset of

.
There is at most

such triples, so we have

.
We will actually only require the weaker result:

B(s+t)\leq B(s)B(t)4^{st}.

To prove the main result we will use induction on

.
The base case (

) is clear.
Suppose that:

\frac{B(k_{1}+\dots+k_{r-1})}{2^{(k_{1}+\dots+k_{r-1})^{2}-(k_{1}+\dots+k_{r-1})}}\leq\frac{B(k_{1})}{2^{k_{1}^{2}-k_{1}}}\cdots\frac{B(k_{r-1})}{2^{k_{r-1}^{2}-k_{r-1}}}.

From above we have that
B(n-k_{r})B(k_{r})\geq B(n)4^{-k_{r}(n-k_{r})}
, and so:

\displaystyle\frac{B(k_{1})}{2^{k_{1}^{2}-k_{1}}}\dots\frac{B(k_{r})}{2^{k_{r}^{2}-k_{r}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{B(k_{1}+\dots+k_{r-1})}{2^{(k_{1}+\dots+k_{r-1})^{2}-(k_{1}+\dots+k_{r-1})}}\frac{B(k_{r})}{2^{k_{r}^{2}-k_{r}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{B(n-k_{r})}{2^{(n-k_{r})^{2}-(n-k_{r})}}\frac{B(k_{r})}{2^{k_{r}^{2}-k_{r}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{B(n)4^{-k_{r}(n-k_{r})}}{2^{(n-k_{r})^{2}-(n-k_{r})}2^{k_{r}^{2}-k_{r}}}=\frac{B(n)}{2^{n^{2}-n}}

as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Recall the sets:

\displaystyle E_{I,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{S}:(N_{k},\mathcal{S},\Delta_{\Lambda})\text{ is a linked equivalence triple}\},

\displaystyle E_{\Lambda,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{T}:(N_{k},\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})\text{ is a linked equivalence triple}\},

\displaystyle R_{I,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{S}:(N_{k},\mathcal{S},\Delta_{\Lambda})\text{ is a linked reflexive triple}\},

\displaystyle R_{\Lambda,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{T}:(N_{k},\Delta_{I},\mathcal{T})\text{ is a linked reflexive triple}\}.

Let
\sigma_{k}=\langle E_{I,k}\rangle
. By Lemma
3.3
we have that
\sigma_{k}\in E_{I,k}
.
The equivalence
\sigma_{k}
contains every element or
E_{I,k}
and it is easy to see that for any equivalence
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma_{k}
the triple
(N_{k},\sigma^{\prime},\Delta_{\Lambda})
is linked.
And so we have the following:

E_{I,k}=\{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\sigma_{k}:\mathcal{S}\text{ is an equivalence relation}\}.

Now consider
\langle R_{I,k}\rangle
. Clearly
\sigma_{k}\in R_{I,k}
and so
\sigma_{k}\subseteq\langle R_{I,k}\rangle
.
Furthermore, for any

we have
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\langle\mathcal{S}\rangle\subseteq\langle E_{I,k}\rangle=\sigma_{k}
.
Hence
\sigma_{k}=\langle R_{I,k}\rangle
and:

R_{I,k}=\{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\sigma_{k}:\mathcal{S}\text{ is a reflexive relation}\}.

Let
I_{1},\dots,I_{l}
be the equivalence classes of
\sigma_{k}
, with sizes

respectively.
Any equivalence relation on

.
As such there are

such relations.
And similarly any reflexive relation on

contained in

is a union of reflexive relations on
I_{1},\dots,I_{l}
.
And so there is
2^{a_{1}^{2}-a_{1}}\dots 2^{a_{l}^{2}-a_{l}}

Therefore:

(8)

\frac{|E_{I,k}|}{|R_{I,k}|}=\frac{B(a_{1})\dots B(a_{l})}{2^{a_{1}^{2}-a_{1}}\dots 2^{a_{l}^{2}-a_{l}}}

where
a_{1}+\dots+a_{l}=a
.
Dually we have relations
\tau_{k}
on
\Lambda
such that:

\displaystyle E_{\Lambda,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{T}\subseteq\tau_{k}:\mathcal{T}\text{ is an equivalence relation}\},

\displaystyle R_{\Lambda,k}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{T}\subseteq\tau_{k}:\mathcal{T}\text{ is a reflexive relation}\}.

Let
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m}
be the equivalence classes of
\tau_{k}
, with sizes
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
.
Then:

(9)

\frac{|E_{\Lambda,k}|}{|R_{\Lambda,k}|}=\frac{B(b_{1})\dots B(b_{m})}{2^{b_{1}^{2}-b_{1}}\dots 2^{b_{m}^{2}-b_{m}}}

where
b_{1}+\dots+b_{m}=b
.
By (
7
), (
8
), (
9
) and Lemma
3.7
we have:

\frac{|E_{I,1}||E_{\Lambda,1}|}{|R_{I,1}||R_{\Lambda,1}|}=\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}}\leq\frac{B(a_{1})\dots B(a_{l})}{2^{a_{1}^{2}-a_{1}}\dots 2^{a_{l}^{2}-a_{l}}}\frac{B(b_{1})\dots B(b_{m})}{2^{b_{1}^{2}-b_{1}}\dots 2^{b_{m}^{2}-b_{m}}}=\frac{|E_{I,k}||E_{\Lambda,k}|}{|R_{I,k}||R_{\Lambda,k}|}

for each

.
Finally by (

) and Lemma

\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}}=\frac{|E_{I,1}||E_{\Lambda,1}|}{|R_{I,1}||R_{\Lambda,1}|}\leq\frac{|E_{I,1}||E_{\Lambda,1}|+\dots+|E_{I,n}||E_{\Lambda,n}|}{|R_{I,1}||R_{\Lambda,1}|+\dots+|R_{I,n}||R_{\Lambda,n}|}=\chi(S).

As

is not a group, so by Theorem

.
This completes the proof of the the first statement in the theorem.

For the second part we want to show that for any rational
\alpha\in\left[\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}},1\right)
, there is a group
G
and a matrix
P
with
\chi(\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P])=\alpha
.
We saw in the first part that:

\frac{|E_{I,k}|}{|R_{I,k}|}=\frac{B(a_{1})\dots B(a_{l})}{2^{a_{1}^{2}-a_{1}}\dots 2^{a_{l}^{2}-a_{l}}}

where

are the sizes of the equivalence classes of

.
Suppose that for some

.
In this situation we have:

\frac{|E_{I,k}|}{|R_{I,k}|}=\frac{B(a)}{2^{a^{2}-a}}.

At the other extreme we might have
\sigma_{k}=\Delta_{I}
for some

If it does, then:

\frac{|E_{I,k}|}{|R_{I,k}|}=\frac{B(1)\dots B(1)}{2^{1^{2}-1}\dots 2^{1^{2}-1}}=1.

Analogous results hold for
\frac{|E_{\Lambda,k}|}{|R_{\Lambda,k}|}
.

We shall show that there exists a group
G
and
\Lambda\times I

with entries from

•

or

,

•

\frac{|E_{\Lambda,k}|}{|R_{\Lambda,k}|}
can only take values
\frac{B(b)}{2^{b^{2}-b}}
or

,

•

then
\frac{|E_{\Lambda,k}|}{|R_{\Lambda,k}|}=\frac{B(b)}{2^{b^{2}-b}}
for the same

values,

•

if there are

\frac{|E_{I,k}|}{|R_{I,k}|}=1
then
\frac{|E_{\Lambda,k}|}{|R_{\Lambda,k}|}=1
for the same

It will then follow from (

\chi(\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P])=\frac{cB(a)B(b)+d}{c2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}+d}.

Furthermore we will show that we can obtain any

and

.

To this end let

be a prime greater than

and let

, the cyclic group of order

, for some arbitrary

.
The normal subgroups of

form the chain:

\textbf{1}=p^{k}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}\leq p^{k-1}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}\leq\dots\leq p\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}\leq\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}=G.

Let

be arbitrary and consider the

p^{r},2p^{r},2^{2}p^{r},\dots,2^{ab-2}p^{r},2^{ab-1}p^{r}.

Let

be any
\Lambda\times I
matrix with all of these numbers as its entries.
We will show that any extract

with

and

is a generator for
p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}
.
If we consider the entries of the matrix as elements of
\mathbb{Z}
this is equivalent to showing that
p^{r}
divides
q_{\lambda\mu ij}
and that
p^{r+1}
does not divide
q_{\lambda\mu ij}
. Let:

p_{\lambda i}=p^{r}2^{s},\quad p_{\mu i}=p^{r}2^{t},\quad p_{\mu j}=p^{r}2^{u},\quad p_{\lambda j}=p^{r}2^{v}.

Then by definition
q_{\lambda\mu ij}=p^{r}(2^{s}-2^{t}+2^{u}-2^{v})
, and so
p^{r}
divides
q_{\lambda\mu ij}
.
Note that:

\displaystyle|q_{\lambda\mu ij}|

\displaystyle=p^{r}|2^{s}-2^{t}+2^{u}-2^{v}|

\displaystyle\leq p^{r}(2^{s}+2^{t}+2^{u}+2^{v})

\displaystyle\leq p^{r}(2^{ab-1}+2^{ab-1}+2^{ab-1}+2^{ab-1})=p^{r}2^{ab+1}<p^{r+1}.

As each element of

is distinct we have that

and

are distinct, without loss of generality let

be the largest of these.
Then:

\displaystyle|q_{\lambda\mu ij}|

\displaystyle=p^{r}|2^{s}-2^{t}+2^{u}-2^{v}|

\displaystyle\geq p^{r}(2^{s}-(2^{t}+2^{u}+2^{v}))

\displaystyle\geq p^{r}(2^{s}-(2^{s-1}+2^{s-2}+2^{s-3}))

\displaystyle>p^{r}(2^{s}-(2^{s-1}+2^{s-2}+2^{s-2}))=0.

If follows from

, that
p^{r+1}
does not divide

.
Hence

is a generator for

for any distinct

and

.

If
N
is a normal subgroup containing

for any
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
and

.
So for any relations
\mathcal{S}
on

and
\mathcal{T}

There are
r+1
such subgroups.
Any other normal subgroup

is properly contained in
p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}
and so does not contain any generator for
p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}}
.
The only extracts

\lambda=\mu
or

.
It follows that
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})

and
\mathcal{T}=\Delta_{\Lambda}
.
There is
k-r
such subgroups.
We finally have that:

(10)

\chi(S)=\frac{(r+1)B(a)B(b)+k-r}{(r+1)2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}+k-r}.

For any
c\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we can let

and

,
\chi(S)
now becomes:

\frac{cB(a)B(b)+d}{c2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}+d}

as claimed.
To complete the proof let
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
with
\alpha=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\in\left[\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}},1\right)
.
Letting

and

gives

.
∎

We can now return to a theorem we stated in the introduction about the spectrum of values we can get for the DSC coefficient.

Theorem
1.2
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1]
there is a finite semigroup
S
with
\chi(S)=\alpha
.

Proof.

If
\alpha=1
, then take
S
to be any finite group, otherwise
\alpha<1
.
The number of reflexive and symmetric relations on a set of size
n
is
2^{\frac{n^{2}-n}{2}}
.
As every equivalence relation is reflexive and symmetric, we have that
B(n)\leq 2^{\frac{n^{2}-n}{2}}
.
Dividing by
2^{n^{2}-n}
and letting
n\rightarrow\infty
gives:

\frac{B(n)}{2^{n^{2}-n}}\leq 2^{-\frac{n^{2}-n}{2}}\rightarrow 0.

So for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
there is
a,b\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
such that
\alpha\in\left[\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}},1\right)
.
By Theorem
3.4
there is a finite semigroup
S
with
\chi(S)=\alpha
.
∎

4.
Conclusion and Further Questions

The DSC coefficient associates a number to a semigroup, so it natural to ask whether every number can be obtained in this way.
Theorem
1.2
gives a complete characterization of the numbers we can obtain.
We have done this by analyzing the DSC coefficient for completely simple semigroups, It is natural to try and do the same for some other classes of semigroups.
Clifford semigroups have a natural description of their congruences so we will conclude with some observations about the DSC coefficient for them.

Clifford semigroups were originally introduced by Clifford
[
2
]
and were used to give a structural description of regular semigroups where the idempotents are central.
Here we follow the account from
[
4
, Section 4.2]
.
Suppose we have a semilattice
Y
, a collection of pairwise disjoint groups
\{G_{\alpha}:\alpha\in Y\}
indexed by
Y
and for each
\alpha\geq\beta
a homomorphism
\phi_{\alpha,\beta}:G_{\alpha}\to G_{\beta}
such that:

•

\phi_{\alpha,\alpha}
is the identity map on
G_{\alpha}
,

•

\phi_{\alpha,\beta}\phi_{\beta,\gamma}=\phi_{\alpha,\gamma},\forall\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in Y
with
\alpha\geq\beta\geq\gamma
.

We define the Clifford semigroup
S=\mathcal{S}[Y;G_{\alpha};\phi_{\alpha,\beta}]
to be the set
\bigcup_{\alpha\in Y}G_{\alpha}
, with multiplication:

x\cdot y=(x\phi_{\alpha,\alpha\beta})(y\phi_{\beta,\alpha\beta})

where
x\in G_{\alpha},y\in G_{\beta}
and
x\phi_{\alpha,\alpha\beta}y\phi_{\beta,\alpha\beta}
is calculated in
G_{\alpha\beta}
.
Let
1_{\alpha}
be the identity element of
G_{\alpha}
and
E=\{1_{\alpha}:\alpha\in Y\}
be the collection of idempotents in
S
.
Then
E
is a subsemigroup of
S
that is isomorphic to the semilattice
Y
via the map
1_{\alpha}\mapsto\alpha
.

Clifford semigroups are a special class of inverse semigroups.
The congruences on an inverse semigroup were originally described by Scheilblich
[
8
]
and later developed by Green
[
3
]
and Petrich
[
6
]
.
We will use the same language and notation as
[
4
, Section 5.3]
, only adapted to Clifford semigroups.
The congruences on a Clifford semigroup are described by what is called a congruence pair.
For each
\alpha\in Y
we take a normal subgroup
N_{\alpha}
of
G_{\alpha}
such that
N_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha,\beta}\subseteq N_{\beta}
for all
\alpha\geq\beta
and let
N=\bigcup_{\alpha\in Y}N_{\alpha}
, we refer to
N
as a
kernel
.
Let
\tau
be a congruence on
E
, which we will call a
trace
.
We say
(N,\tau)
is
congruence pair
if for
\alpha\geq\beta
and
x\in G_{\alpha}
we have:

(11)

x\phi_{\alpha,\beta}\in N\text{ and }(1_{\alpha},1_{\beta})\in\tau\implies x\in N.

Given a congruence pair we can construct the following congruence:

\rho_{N,\tau}=\{(x,y):(xx^{-1},yy^{-1})\in\tau,xy^{-1}\in N\}.

Every congruence on a Clifford semigroup can be constructed in this way and each congruence pair gives rise to a unique congruence.
If
S
is finite, then the number of kernels,
K
, and the number of traces,
|\text{Cong}(Y)|
, are both finite.
Thus
K|\text{Cong}(Y)|
is an upper bound for
|\text{Cong}(S)|
.

We can deploy a similar construction to obtain a nice family of diagonal subsemigroups on a Clifford semigroup.
We define a diagonal subsemigroup pair in similar way to a congruence pair.
The kernel is defined in the exact same way and the trace
\tau
is now a diagonal subsemigroup on
E
.
We say the pair
(N,\tau)
is a
diagonal subsemigroup pair
, note we do not require
N
and
\tau
to satisfy (
11
).
We construct
\rho_{N,\tau}
in the same way as above.

Lemma 4.1
.

The relation
\rho_{N,\tau}
is a diagonal subsemigroup.
Furthermore the map
(N,\tau)\mapsto\rho_{N,\tau}
from the set of congruence pairs to the set of diagonal subsemigroups is an injection.

Proof.

Clearly
(xx^{-1},xx^{-1})\in\tau
as
\tau
is reflexive and
xx^{-1}\in N
as
N
contains the identity of each
G_{\alpha}
.
Let
(x,y),(z,t)\in\rho_{N,\tau}
, with
x\in G_{\alpha},y\in G_{\beta},z\in G_{\gamma}
and
t\in G_{\delta}
.
Then:

\displaystyle(xzz^{-1}x^{-1},ytt^{-1}y^{-1})

\displaystyle=(x1_{\gamma}x^{-1},y1_{\delta}y^{-1})

\displaystyle=(xx^{-1}\phi_{\alpha,\alpha\gamma},yy^{-1}\phi_{\beta,\beta\delta})

\displaystyle=(1_{\alpha\gamma},1_{\beta\delta})

\displaystyle=(1_{\alpha},1_{\beta})(1_{\gamma},1_{\delta})

\displaystyle=(xx^{-1},yy^{-1})(zz^{-1},tt^{-1})\in\tau.

We have
xy^{-1},zt^{-1}\in N
, so
xy^{-1}\in N_{\alpha\beta}
and
zt^{-1}\in N_{\gamma\delta}
.
It follows that
y^{-1}x=(y\phi_{\beta,\alpha\beta})^{-1}xy^{-1}y\phi_{\beta,\alpha\beta}\in N_{\alpha\beta}
.
As
\alpha\beta,\gamma\delta\geq\alpha\beta\gamma\delta
, we see that
y^{-1}x\phi_{\alpha\beta,\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
and
zt^{-1}\phi_{\gamma\delta,\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
are both contained in
N_{\alpha\beta\delta\gamma}
.
Hence
y^{-1}xzt^{-1}\in N_{\alpha\beta\delta\gamma}
.
Finally conjugating by
y^{-1}\phi_{\beta,\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
gives
xzt^{-1}y^{-1}\in N_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\subseteq N
.
Hence
(xz,yt)\in\rho_{N,\tau}
and
\rho_{N,\tau}
is a diagonal subsemigroup.

Now suppose that
(N,\tau)
and
(N^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
are diagonal subsemigroup pairs with
\rho_{(N,\tau)}=\rho_{(N^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})}
.
It is easy to see that
1_{\alpha}1_{\beta}^{-1}=1_{\alpha\beta}\in N,N^{\prime}
, so:

(1_{\alpha},1_{\beta})\in\tau\iff\rho_{(N,\tau)}=\rho_{(N^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})}\iff(1_{\alpha},1_{\beta})\in\tau^{\prime}

and therefore
\tau=\tau^{\prime}
.
To show that
N=N^{\prime}
take
x\in G_{\alpha}
, then
(xx^{-1},1_{\alpha}1_{\alpha}^{-1})=(1_{\alpha},1_{\alpha})\in\tau
and:

x\in N\iff(x,1_{\alpha})\in\rho_{(N,\tau)}=\rho_{(N^{\prime},\tau)}\iff x\in N^{\prime}.\qed

It is worth noting that this family of diagonal subsemigroups is far from describing all of the diagonal subsemigroups on a Clifford semigroup.
For example let
G
be a group with identity
e
and adjoin a new identity
1
to give
G^{1}
.
If
H
is any proper subgroup of
G
then it is easily seen that
\{(1,1)\}\cup G\times G\cup\{1\}\times H
is a diagonal subsemigroup of
G^{1}
that is not of the form
\rho_{N,\tau}

It follows from Lemma
4.1
that there is at least
K|\text{Diag}(Y)|
diagonal subsemigroups on
S
.
And hence we have:

\chi(S)=\frac{|\text{Cong}(S)|}{|\text{Diag}(S)|}\leq\frac{K|\text{Cong}(Y)|}{K|\text{Diag}(Y)|}=\chi(Y).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
S=\mathcal{S}[Y;G_{\alpha};\phi_{\alpha,\beta}]
be a finite Clifford semigroup.
Then
{0<\chi(S)\leq\chi(Y)}
. ∎

The example of a group with an identity adjoined discussed above shows that the second equality can be strict.

It is perhaps interesting to compare Theorem
4.2
with Theorem
3.4
for Rees matrix semigroups.
Both theorems exhibit a subinterval of
(0,1]
which necessarily contains
\chi(S)
.
However, in Theorem
3.4
, as the index sets
I,\Lambda
become larger this interval rapidly converges towards
(0,1]
.
We do not know what the possible values of
\chi(Y)
are for a semilattice
Y
, but examples seem to suggest that it converges to
0
as
|Y|
increases, yielding very narrow intervals for
\chi(S)
.

We also note that distinguished homomorphic images of
S
play a prominent role in both inequalities.
Indeed the semilattice
Y
is a natural homomorphic image of
S
in the Clifford case, and
\chi(Y)
is an upper bound for
\chi(S)
.
By way of contrast, when
S=\mathcal{M}[G;I,\Lambda;P]
is a Rees matrix semigroup with
|I|=a,|\Lambda|=b
, the
I\times\Lambda
rectangular band is a homomorphic image of
S
.
As we observed earlier its DSC coefficient is
\frac{B(a)B(b)}{2^{a^{2}-a}2^{b^{2}-b}}
which is precisely the lower bound for
\chi(S)
as obtained in Theorem
3.4
.
