A 16-year evaluation of opportunistic lung cancer screening with low-dose CT in China: comparative findings between non-smokers and smokers
Authors: Yanyan Tang, Shijun Zhao, Lina Zhou, Yao Huang, Jianwei Wang, Min Liang, Fei Wang, Haohua Zhu, Linlin Qi, Li Zhang, Li Liu, Donghui Hou, Zhijian Xu, Kai Zhang, Wei Tang, Ning Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening effectively reduces LC mortality in high-risk individuals with a history of smoking in China, the feasibility and efficacy of lung cancer screening (LCS) in individuals who never smoked versus individuals who smoked remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of prospective cohort studies at the National Cancer Center (NCC) in China from January 2006 to December 2022. A comprehensive LCS initiative was undertaken, involving 30,468 participants (54.5% male). Participants underwent LCS using LDCT. Potential malignancies were managed through joint consensus between patients and their physicians. Epidemiology, screening eligibility criteria, and LC detection rates and survival outcomes were compared between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked.ResultsAmong 30,468 participants, 339 LCs were pathologically confirmed in 289 patients. The LC detection rate was 0.9% (289/30,468) overall, 0.8% in individuals who smoked (71/9,042), and 1.0% in individuals who never smoked (218/21,426). In individuals who smoked, LC detection rates were 0.5% (21/4516) and 1.1% (50/4526) in the < 20 and ≥ 20 pack-year subgroups, respectively (P = 0.001). Early-stage LC (stage 0 or I) was detected in 73.8% of the individuals who smoked and in 78.8% of individuals who never smoked, while advanced LC (stage III–IV) was found 8.8% of individuals who smoked and 4.2% of individuals who never smoked, respectively. Significant differences in histologic types were found between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked (P = 0.01), although adenocarcinoma was the most prevalent in both groups, at 83.0% and 78.8%, respectively. The median nodule size was 9.9 mm (IQR, 8.0–13.8) in individuals who smoked and 9.2 mm (IQR, 6.8–13.6) in individuals who never smoked (P = 0.228). Individuals who never smoked tended to favour surgical treatment alone (88.0%) more than individuals who smoked (81.3%). The 10-year survival rate was higher in individuals who never smoked (92.6%) than in individuals who smoked (88.8%). Only 15.6% (45/289) of patients with LC met the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) criteria for LDCT eligibility, while 29.0% (84/289) met the China guideline for the screening and early detection of lung cancer (CGSL) criteria. Median follow-up for those followed was 25.4 (IQR, 13.7–43.3) months.ConclusionsLDCT screening improves early LC detection and treatment outcomes for both individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked. Significant differences exist in epidemiology, histologic type, and survival between these groups. The USPSTF and CGSL criteria miss a significant number of LC cases, particularly among individuals who never smoked. Integrating individuals who never smoked into LCS programs is essential, yet it comes with its own challenges, such as managing radiation risks, allocating resources effectively, and considering financial aspects. Consequently, there is an urgent need for LCS programs in China to better identify the “high-risk” non-smoker population susceptible to LC and to ensure that potential risks associated with screening are reduced.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13056-1.Keywords: Lung cancer screening, LDCT, Detection rates, Smoked, Eligibility criteria

Background

Lung cancer (LC) is the second most diagnosed cancer and the primary cause of cancer-related deaths globally [1]. LC was the predominant malignancy in China in 2022, with a total of 1 060 600 incident cases diagnosed, and also the primary cause of cancer-related mortality, causing 733 000 deaths [2]. This high incidence underscores the significant impact of LC on the Chinese population. Large-scale randomised clinical trials have demonstrated that low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening can reduce LC mortality [3, 4], particularly among individuals who smoked. The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) conducted in the United States indicated a survival benefit from LDCT screening but also reported that it could miss over 50% of LCs [3]. LC in individuals who never smoked should also be considered [5]. Non-smoking-related LC is more prevalent in East Asia than in Europe or the United States [6]. In China, tobacco smoking causes 75% of LCs in men and 18% in women [7], suggesting that LC incidence and death rates in eastern women are less dependent on smoking. LC in individuals who never smoked exhibits clinical and molecular features distinct from that in individuals who smoked, suggesting that it is a different disease [8].

In a large cohort study, applying the current United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) screening guidelines to a Chinese asymptomatic population resulted in a 90.8% missed diagnosis rate [9]. Therefore, the target population for LDCT screening in China should include individuals who never smoked and those not meeting the USPSTF2021 smoking criteria. An observational study in Anhui Province in China reported an LC incidence of 0.488% among 4102 individuals who never smoked [10]. The Cancer Screening in Non-smoker Trial (TALENT) involved 12 011 individuals who never smoked selected based on specific risk factors. The first LDCT scan detected LC in 2.6% of participants, higher than the detection rates for individuals who smoked in the NLST (1.1%) and NELSON (0.9%) studies, and all but one were adenocarcinomas, and 96.5% were stage 0 or I [11]. However, these studies lacked detailed information on smoking quantity and cessation duration, preventing further subgroup analyses. Comparative studies on quantitative indicators of smoking and eligibility for LDCT screening between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked in mainland China are lacking. Observational studies in other Asian countries, such as Japan and South Korea, indicate differences in LC between individuals who never smoked and ever smoked groups [12, 13], highlighting the need for a similar large LDCT study in China.

Thus, this study aimed to evaluate LC detection rates in individuals who never smoked versus those in individuals who smoked in a real-world LDCT screening setting in China and to compare epidemiology, LC outcomes (imaging, pathologic features, treatment modalities, and survival rate), and screening eligibility criteria between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked.

Methods

Study design and participants

This hospital-based single-centre prospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center of China, Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Science, Beijing, China (No.14–115/905) and was conducted according to the tenets of the Helsinki Declaration. Participants provided informed consent, allowing their institutions to financially support the LCS.

Adults aged ≥ 18 years who underwent LDCT screening for LC at the Department of Cancer Prevention of the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and the National Cancer Center of China between January 2006 and December 2022 were included regardless of smoking history. The exclusion criteria were inability to understand the study’s purpose, risks, or benefits or to provide informed consent; medical conditions preventing curative LC surgery (e.g. severe heart disease, advanced respiratory disease, or other comorbidities); previous diagnosis of LC; symptoms potentially indicating LC, such as unexplained weight loss or unexplained haemoptysis; and unknown smoking history. The participants completed a pre-LDCT cancer-related risk assessment questionnaire developed by our institution. All participants underwent baseline LDCT screening; if screening was positive, we made follow-up recommendations based on nodule assessment, and the detailed screening protocol was shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 of our published article [14].Participants with negative screening results were recommended for annual screening, but some still did not return to our hospital for repeat screening.

Flowchart of participant selection and baseline LDCT screening results

The questionnaire collected data on demographics, smoking status, exposure to second-hand smoke (SHS), occupational exposure to hazardous substances, family history of LC, and lung- and non-lung-related comorbidities. Demographic information included age, sex, and educational level (low: primary education or less; medium: junior/senior high school; high: college/university or higher). Lung-related comorbidities included chronic bronchitis or bronchiectasis, emphysema, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, asbestosis, and tuberculosis. The non-lung-related comorbidities included coronary artery calcification, angina pectoris, diabetes, and hypertension. SHS exposure was defined as exposure to passive smoking for > 20 years by living or working with individuals who smoked. Based on smoking status, the participants were classified as individuals who smoked (those with ≥ 20 pack-years [smoked one pack/day for 20 years or equivalent, including current individuals who smoked and those who quit within the last 15 year and those with < 20 pack-years) and individuals who never smoked (individuals who smoked < 100 cigarettes in their lifetime). Our study did not include people who had quit smoking for more than 15 years.

Imaging analysis

Detailed information on image analysis and LDCT screening can be found in the Supplementary Material section in Additional File 1 and in our recently published article [14]. Detection rate = number of pathologically confirmed lung cancers / total number of people screened.

Screening criteria and management of lung nodules

Table S1 in Additional file 1 outlines the specific screening criteria used in this study. Non-calcified nodules with the longest diameter ≥ 4 mm detected by LDCT were considered positive. Management of participants with lung nodules followed referral to thoracic surgeons for further evaluation or diagnostic procedures per Lung-RADS (edition 1.1) [15] and the International Early Lung Cancer Action Program (I-ELCAP) schedule (I-ELCAP Screening Protocol 2006) [16]. As Lung-RADS was published in 2014, images predating 2014 were retrospectively assessed. Regarding multiple nodules, the one with the highest Lung-RADS score was analysed. Histopathological assessment and tumour staging followed the World Health Organization 2015 classification [17] and the eighth edition of the TNM classification for LC [18].

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as the median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data normality. For normally distributed data, a two-sample independent t-test was used to analyse quantitative variables; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical variables are expressed as percentages. Frequencies of categorical variables were compared between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Life expectancies for detected cancers were calculated from LC detection to death or the latest follow up. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to determine the survival rates of the participants with LC and adjusted for accuracy, and a log-rank test was used to determine between-group differences. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0; SPSS Inc.) and R (version 3.6.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/).

Results

Participant characteristics

Among the 40 288 individuals who participated, 9820 participants were excluded, leaving 30 468 participants eligible for analysis. Among them, 21 426 participants were individuals who never smoked and 9042 were individuals who smoked. Among individuals who smoked, 4526 participants had a pack-year history of ≥ 20, and 4516 participants had a pack-year history of < 20. Figure 1 illustrates the study flowchart.

The baseline participant characteristics are described in Table 1. Individuals who smoked were predominantly male, younger, and had lower educational attainment. They also reported higher exposure to occupational hazardous substances and SHS (P < 0.001). Individuals who smoked had higher prevalences of COPD, emphysema, asbestosis, coronary artery calcification, angina pectoris, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. In contrast, individuals who never smoked had higher incidences of tuberculosis and a history of other cancers (all P < 0.001) For individuals who smoked, 34.5% had Lung-RADS category 1 nodules, higher than the 31.5% for individuals who never smoked. Regarding individuals who never smoked, 65.7% had Lung-RADS category 2 nodules, higher than the 61.9% for individuals who smoked. These differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001; Table 1). However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding positive nodules, chronic bronchitis or bronchiectasis, or family history of LC (all P > 0.05).

Characteristics of lesions in LC patients stratified by smoking status

Table 2 summarises the characteristics of cancer lesions and treatment modalities according to smoking status. A total of 339 LC lesions were detected in 289 participants. The median nodule size was 9.5 mm (IQR,7.1–14.0) and 10.0 mm (9.0–14.0) for individuals who smoked with pack-years < 20 and ≥ 20, respectively, whereas they were 9.9 mm (IQR,8.0–13.8) and 9.2 mm (IQR,6.8–13.6) for individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked, respectively (P = 0.228). Overall, 73.8% of individuals who smoked and 78.8% of individuals who never smoked were found to have stage 0 or I lung cancer (P = 0.347). In addition, 8.8% of individuals who smoked and 4.2% of individuals who never smoked had stage III-IV lung cancer (P = 0.116). In the individuals who smoked, 71.4% of those with < 20 pack-years and 74.6% of those with ≥ 20 pack-years had stage 0 or I LC, and 9.6% and 8.5% had stage III–IV disease, respectively (both P > 0.05). The histologic type was significantly different between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked (P = 0.01); however, adenocarcinoma was more common than other types in both individuals who never smoked (83.0%) and individuals who smoked (78.8%). The two groups had similar lesion consistency, number, Lung-RADS category, stage, and initial treatment. However, part solid and solid nodules were most common in individuals who smoked, with 30 (37.5%) and 20 (25.0%) lesions, respectively. In contrast, nonsolid nodules were most common in individuals who never smoked (37.8%). Surgery alone was the treatment modality for 293 of the 339 LCs and was more common in the never-smoking group (228 [88.0%]) than in the smoking subgroups (65 [81.3%]).

Participant characteristics by smoking status

Table 3 shows the participant characteristics by smoking status. More men in the smoking group had LC, whereas more women in the never-smoking group had LC (P < 0.001). The mean age of the participants with LC was 55.9 ± 9.5 years for individuals who never smoked and 56.4 ± 10.0 years for individuals who smoked (P = 0.679). The mean age of LC participants with ≥ 20 and < 20 pack-years was 59.5 ± 9.1 years and 49.1 ± 8.2 years, respectively (P < 0.001). SHS exposure in the smoking and never-smoking groups was 95.6% and 84.4%, respectively (P = 0.013). Family history of LC, occupational exposure to hazardous substances, history of other cancers, COPD, and educational level were not significantly different between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked. For those followed up, the median number of LDCT per person was 2 (IQR, 1–3), within a median follow-up duration of 25.4 (IQR, 13.7–43.3) months.

The overall LC detection rate was 0.9%, with rates of 0.8% for individuals who smoked and 1.0% for individuals who never smoked.Among individuals who smoked, the detection rates were 0.5% and 1.1% for those with pack-years < 20 and pack-years ≥ 20, respectively (P = 0.001). Among participants aged < 40 years, the LC detection rate was slightly higher individuals who never smoked than for individuals who smoked (0.4% vs. 0.1%, P = 0.027). However, there were no significant between-group difference in detection rates in the other age groups. LC detection rates for all participants, individuals who smoked, individuals who never smoked, and individuals who smoked with ≥ 20 pack-years increased progressively with age. Female participants had a higher overall LC detection rate at 1.1% than male participants at 0.8% (P = 0.002). In the never-smoking group, women had a significantly higher detection rate at 1.1% than men at 0.8% (P = 0.033). Similarly, in the < 20 pack-year smoking group, women had a significantly higher detection rate at 1.3% than men at 0.4% (P = 0.03). LC-specific survival

Overall, 16 (5.5%) of the 289 participants with LC died. In individuals who never smoked, 9 (4.1%) of the 218 participants with LC died; in individuals who smoked, 7 (9.9%) of the 71 participants died. The 5-year and 10-year overall survival rates were both 88.8% in individuals who smoked, whereas they were 98.1% and 92.6% in individuals who never smoked, respectively. The median follow-up time was 6.4 (0–15.7) years for individuals who smoked and 6.4 (0–15.8) years for individuals who never smoked. The Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that participants with LC in individuals who never smoked had a higher survival rate than those individuals who smoked (P < 0.05, Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this 16-year, large-sample study, we compared the epidemiology, LC outcomes (imaging, pathologic features, treatment modalities, and survival), and screening eligibility criteria between individuals who smoked and nonindividuals who smoked and evaluated LC detection rates by smoking status. Compared with individuals who never smoked, individuals who smoked were predominantly male, younger, less educated, had more comorbidities, and were more susceptible to occupational hazards and SHS exposure. Differences were noted in pathology type and survival risk, with individuals who never smoked having a higher incidence of adenocarcinoma. Individuals who never smoked exhibited a higher survival rate than individuals who smoked. Our study shows that screening with LDCT has significantly improved the early detection of LC, thus providing therapeutic options for both individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked. However, current guidelines from the USPSTF and CGSL may overlook a considerable number of LC cases, particularly among individuals who never smoked.

The incidence of positive LCS results in this study was 38.6%, which exceeds the rates reported in previous studies: 20.8% for NELSON [19], 27.3% for NLST [20], and 23% for ELCAP [21]. A community-based randomised controlled trial in China reported a 22.9% incidence of positive LCS results [22]. The higher incidence in our study may be attributed to differences in slice thicknesses, criteria for positive screening results, study enrolment selection criteria, or study populations.

A South Korean study reported cumulative LC detection rates of 0.45% among individuals who never smoked and 0.86% among ever-smokers, with a significant difference (P < 0.001) [13]. Conversely, our current LDCT screening programme showed no significant difference between the groups, with cumulative LC detection rates of 1.0% in individuals who never smoked and 0.8% in individuals who smoked. These findings are consistent with those of an observational study in Japan that reported a cumulative LC detection rate of 1.1% in both groups [12]. The significantly higher LC detection rate in South Korean ever-smokers than in individuals who never smoked may be because the results from the study populations differ. Future randomised controlled trials are needed to further evaluate differences in LC detection rate between individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked.

In the present study, 77.6% of all detected LCs were stage 0 or I, and 82.0% were adenocarcinomas (TNM staging system version 8). In a community-based LCS study in China, the detection rates for stage IA and adenocarcinoma were 80.4% and 92.2% (TNM staging system version 8), respectively [22]. Similarly, an LDCT screening study in Tokyo, Japan reported rates of 85.5% for stage IA LC and 88.8% for adenocarcinoma (TNM staging system, version 7) [12]. A Japanese observational study in Nagano Prefecture found that 88% of detected LCs were clinical stage IA, and 85% were adenocarcinomas [23]. The results of these studies conducted in Asian countries were similar. Conversely, the percentages of stage IA and adenocarcinoma in the Chinese and Japanese studies were higher than those reported in Western countries [3, 24]. For instance, the NLST found that 40% of detected LCs were stage IA, and 46.8% were adenocarcinomas (TNM staging system, 6th edition) [3]. Early-stage LC detection rates are higher in East Asian nations than in their Western counterparts, potentially because of the prevalence of ground-glass density nodules (GGNs) in non-smoking Asian patients and the higher thresholds for surgical intervention in Western medical practices. Many Western guidelines, including the Lung-RADS (version 1.1), typically advise monitoring pure ground-glass nodules and only taking action if they develop a solid component [25]. However, the TALENT study employs a more proactive approach to nodule management. Numerous patients in this study have opted for surgical removal of nodules that exhibit pure ground-glass opacities, even in the absence of growth or the emergence of a solid component [25]. This practice may contribute to higher overdiagnosis and overtreatment rates in Asian countries. Furthermore, research from Taiwan [26], Korea [27], and Shanghai [28] has indicated a rise in detected early-stage cases, without a corresponding decrease in the incidence or mortality rates of more advanced lung diseases, which further supports the notion of overtreatment.

Our LC detection rates did not differ significantly by Lung-RADS category. Meanwhile, a South Korean study found a significantly higher detection rate in ever-individuals who smoked with Lung-RADS category 1 or S nodules, reporting that more LCs were detected in the ever-smoking group during follow-up [13]. This discrepancy may be due to differences between the two study imaging reviewers or the inconsistent timing of the procedure. Our results differed from those of an observational study of individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked in Nagano, Japan, in the following ways. In their study, individuals who never smoked had smaller LC lesions than individuals who smoked (mean 12 mm vs. 18 mm), there were more pure GGNs or mixed GGN lesions (86%; 38/44), and the difference was statistically significant [29]. In contrast, our results did not show a significant difference. This difference is because the screening involved different populations; our screening population was mainly from urban areas, whereas the study in Nagano conducted screening in rural areas. Further, other factors such as lifestyle, living environment, and genetic profiles may differ.

Our study found steady 5- and 10-year survival rates at 88.8% for individuals who smoked with LC, while the rates were 98.1% and 92.6% for individuals who never smoked, respectively. A study from Japan showed higher 5- and 10-year survival rates for individuals who never smoked with LC (96.8% and 96.8%) than for individuals who smoked (90.4% and 77.4%) [12]. An observational study in South Korea showed a significant difference in cancer-specific survival for individuals who never smoked and individuals who smoked (5-year rates of 96.0% vs. 67.4% and 10-year rates of 96.0% vs. 62.7%) [13]. According to a study from Singapore, current and former individuals who smoked with non-small-cell LC have a higher mortality rate than individuals who never smoked [30]. Meanwhile, the extended I-ELCAP cohort reported a lack of difference in the 20-year LC-specific survival rates across different smoking types (< 10, 10–29, and ≥ 30 pack-years) [31]. Individuals who smoked often have higher rates of smoking-related comorbidities, including COPD, cardiovascular diseases, and other respiratory issues, which may complicate LC treatment and prognosis. More randomised controlled trials will be needed in the future to assess the survival risk of LC in individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked.

A concerning finding of our study is the low percentage of patients meeting the USPSTF2021 criteria (15.6%,45/289) and the CGSL2021 criteria (29%,84/289) for LDCT screening eligibility. This discrepancy indicates a significant gap between current screening guidelines and the actual population at risk, suggesting that existing criteria may not sufficiently capture all individuals who could benefit from early detection efforts, particularly in Asian populations in whom individuals who never smoked predominate. In light of these findings, there is a pressing need for LCS programs in China to more precisely identify the “high-risk” non-smoking population that is susceptible to LC. To accomplish this, it is necessary to adopt a comprehensive strategy that encompasses advanced risk assessment tools, public health initiatives, the detection of biomarkers, and well-considered allocation of resources.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first real-world study of individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked in mainland China to investigate whether there are differences in smoking in terms of epidemiology, LC outcomes (imaging, pathologic features, treatment modalities, and survival), and screening eligibility criteria. In addition, our study is a real-world study with a large sample size of up to 16 years, and the results provide a comprehensive picture of the real clinical situation in mainland China. In addition, our study provides detailed information on the amount of cigarettes smoked and the duration of cessation, allowing for subgroup analyses of the smoking group and analyses of guideline eligibility criteria.

Our study had some limitations. First, our single-centre study may have selection bias, warranting multicentre validation. However, its location at China’s National Cancer Center allowed for a diverse participant pool, partially offsetting single-centre constraints. Second, the screening was paid for by the participants or their institutions, which likely attracted individuals with at least some socioeconomic status or health awareness, introducing potential selection bias. Additionally, most participants who dropped out of the individual follow-ups and did not comply with follow-up recommendations possibly perceived themselves to be at a lower risk of LC. Our future efforts should focus on educating participants on the importance of LDCT for early LC detection. Finally, owing to the relatively few LCs and deaths in this study, the small sample may not accurately estimate key parameters such as survival functions and risk ratios; of the 289 LC patients, more than 30 were lost to follow-up, 203 were followed for more than 5 years, and only 55 were followed for more than 10 years, which may have affected the accuracy of the survival analysis. We will expand the sample and conduct multifactorial analyses in the future.

Conclusions

In this study based on a large sample over a sustained 16-year period, LDCT screening significantly enhanced early LC detection and provided curative treatment options for both individuals who smoked and individuals who never smoked. Non-smoking patients with LC had a higher survival rate than individuals who smoked. Demographic characteristics and pathology type differed, with a higher incidence of adenocarcinoma in individuals who never smoked. Meanwhile, the LC detection rates, imaging characteristics, initial treatment, or tumour staging did not significantly differ between the two patient groups. Following the USPSTF2021 and CGSL2021 criteria could result in a significant underdiagnosis of LC, especially among individuals who never smoked. Implementing LDCT-based LCS programs for individuals who smoked entails its own set of challenges, including concerns about radiation exposure, the allocation of resources, and financial implications. Consequently, there is an urgent need for LCS programs in China to more accurately delineate the “high-risk” non-smoking population susceptible to LC to ensure that the goal of expanding the LCS population is achieved while balancing the need to reduce the range of risks associated with screening.
