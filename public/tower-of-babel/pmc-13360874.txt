Gestational psittacosis: a systematic review of clinical manifestations and outcomes
Authors: Jing Feng, Yongsheng Wang, Juan Han, Jiqiong Li, Wenhao Xu, Xiaobo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational psittacosis is a rare but severe zoonotic infection caused by Chlamydia psittaci. Background

Psittacosis is a zoonotic infectious disease caused by Gram-negative, obligate intracellular bacterium Chlamydia psittaci. The pathogen primarily circulates among wild birds, psittacine birds, and poultry [1], although livestock such as sheep, goats, cattle, and horses also serve as significant reservoirs [2]. Human infection typically occurs via the inhalation of contaminated aerosols or direct contact with infectious animal excreta [3], while human-to-human transmission is rare [4]. Psittacosis is an uncommon cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). A meta-analysis found that C. psittaci is responsible for 1% of CAP cases annually [5]. However, the pathogen exhibits potent pathogenicity and rapid intracellular proliferation, which can provoke intense inflammatory responses and severe pulmonary involvement [6], and has been identified in approximately 8% of severe CAP cases in China [7]. Gestational psittacosis, defined as the occurrence of psittacosis during pregnancy, is rare but can result in increased maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality. One study reported fetal and maternal mortality of 94% and 6%, respectively [8]. Similarly, the analysis by Daisuke Katsura et al. [9] documented corresponding rates of 83% (19/23) and 9% (2/23). However, due to its non-specific clinical presentation and the paucity of pregnancy-specific data, gestational psittacosis remains frequently under-recognized, and consensus regarding its diagnosis and management remains limited. In this context, we conducted a retrospective analysis of published cases of gestational psittacosis to characterize its clinical features, diagnostic approaches, management, and maternal–fetal outcomes, with the goal of improving clinical recognition and pregnancy outcomes.

Methods

Registration and eligibility criteria

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [10]. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251275911) on 30 December 2025. Registration occurred after the literature search but before completion of the final analysis.

Gestational psittacosis was defined as Chlamydia psittaci infection during pregnancy. We adopted the clinical definitions from the original studies, contingent upon meeting our laboratory criteria. In this review, a laboratory-supported diagnosis was established if at least one of the following criteria was met: (1) Serological evidence, requiring a four-fold or greater rise in specific antibody titers between paired acute and convalescent sera, or a single highly elevated titer deemed diagnostic; (2) Microbiological or histopathological evidence, including pathogen isolation from clinical specimens or characteristic histopathological identification, such as typical basophilic inclusions in the placental syncytiotrophoblast; or (3) Molecular evidence, requiring positive detection via specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS). For mNGS, given the obligate intracellular nature of Chlamydia psittaci and its absence in the normal human microbiome, the detection of at least one strictly mapped specific read (reads ≥ 1), combined with a compatible clinical syndrome, was considered diagnostic. Consequently, suspected cases relying solely on clinical symptoms and exposure history without any of the aforementioned laboratory or pathological confirmations were strictly excluded.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) review articles, non-original publications (abstracts or correspondence), or animal and in vitro studies; (2) reports with insufficient clinical details or lacking laboratory confirmation; and (3) duplicate publications.

Search strategy and study selection

A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang Data databases from their respective inception dates up to October 31, 2025. The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms, detailed in Table 1. The search was restricted to English and Chinese publications. Although grey literature was not systematically searched, preprints and indexed conference abstracts were eligible for inclusion to ensure a comprehensive evidence synthesis for this rare condition, provided they contained sufficient clinical and laboratory data.

The study selection process followed systematic stages of identification, screening, and eligibility assessment. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened independently and in duplicate by two investigators (JF and YW) to determine final inclusion. Any discrepancies were resolved through detailed discussion to reach a consensus, with arbitration by a third senior investigator (XH) when necessary. Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was independently performed by two investigators using a standardized form. The primary outcomes extracted were maternal and fetal/neonatal survival, the incidence of severe complications (e.g., DIC and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis [HLH]), and ICU admission. Secondary outcomes encompassed patient demographics, exposure history, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, diagnostic modalities, and antimicrobial regimens.

Due to the inclusion of only case reports and small case series, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Data were synthesized narratively using descriptive statistics: continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Formal statistical assessment of publication bias (e.g., funnel plots) was not applicable; however, the potential reporting bias toward severe or fatal cases was qualitatively assessed and considered in the outcome interpretation.

To assess the methodological quality of the included case reports, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports was used. Two investigators (JF and YW) independently evaluated each study across eight domains. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, with arbitration by a third senior investigator (XH) when needed. Detailed quality assessment results for each study are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The limitations and potential biases of case-based evidence are further discussed in the limitations section.

Results

Patient characteristics and exposure history

A total of 30 studies involving 32 cases were ultimately included after a systematic screening of the initial records [8, 9, 11–38] (Fig. 1), including two reports [12, 13] that documented two cases each. The median maternal age was 29 years (IQR: 26–32; n = 31). Infection spanned all stages of pregnancy (range: 6–36 weeks), with a median gestational age of 26.5 weeks (IQR: 21–30) at diagnosis. Geographically, cases were predominantly reported from the United Kingdom (13/32, 41%) and China (8/32, 25%). Among the 31 cases with available exposure data, the most frequent suspected sources of infection were contact with sheep in 42% (13/31 with available data) [11–18, 23, 24, 37] and avian species (9/31, 29%) [9, 21, 22, 26, 29–31, 34, 38], followed by goats (3/31, 10%) [8, 19, 25], and occupational exposure to multiple animal species in a slaughterhouse (1/31, 3%) [20]. Five patients (5/31, 16%) [27, 28, 32, 35, 36] denied any known animal exposure. Comprehensive demographics and detailed exposure histories for all 32 cases are summarized in Table 2.

Clinical manifestations

The incubation period following human exposure to C. psittaci is typically 5–14 days [4]. Among the nine cases with a documented incubation period [8, 13, 15, 18–20, 24, 34, 38], the interval from exposure to symptom onset ranged from 5 days to 1 month, most commonly 1–2 weeks.

Although C. psittaci infection may involve multiple organ systems, pulmonary involvement predominated in the present cohort. Among the 32 patients included, the majority initially presented with influenza-like systemic symptoms accompanied by respiratory manifestations. Fever was the most prominent clinical manifestation, present in all cases (100%). Among patients with documented temperature measurements, peak body temperatures frequently exceeded 39 °C, reaching up to 40.5 °C. Other common symptoms included headache (17/32, 53%), cough (15/32, 47%), dyspnea (15/32, 47%), myalgia (10/32, 31%), fatigue (9/32, 28%), chest pain (5/32, 16%), and pharyngalgia (4/32, 13%).

Gastrointestinal symptoms primarily presented as nausea and vomiting (10/32, 31%). Neurological involvement (7/32, 22%) included impaired consciousness (3/32, 9%), dizziness (2/32, 6%), and photophobia (2/32, 6%). Less frequent features (e.g., abdominal pain, rash, and hemoptysis), alongside a comprehensive summary of all clinical manifestations, are detailed in Table 3.

Fatal cases typically exhibited fulminant progression. One patient rapidly developed severe DIC and circulatory failure after a 48-hour prodrome, resulting in fetal demise within 12 h of admission and maternal death 14 h later [12]. Two additional cases reported rapid progression to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), severe DIC, and shock, ultimately causing irreversible multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) and death within hours to days [9, 35].

A subset of cases developed severe complications. Sun et al. [30] reported a patient with secondary HLH after pneumonia control, presenting with high-grade fever, pancytopenia, hypertriglyceridemia, hyperferritinemia, and bone marrow hemophagocytosis, who achieved remission with etoposide and dexamethasone. Yoshimura et al. [32] described fulminant HLH without respiratory symptoms, rapidly progressing to mortality within 48 h. Ye et al. [36] documented a TTP-like syndrome with fever, headache, acute kidney injury, thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and reduced ADAMTS-13 activity, successfully treated with plasma exchange, immunosuppressive therapy, and antimicrobial treatment.

Radiological characteristics

Among 21 patients with available chest imaging, predominant findings included consolidation and infiltrative opacities, mainly involving the lower lobes and basal segments [8, 11, 14, 16, 18, 20, 21, 24, 28–30, 33, 34], consistent with typical imaging features of C. psittaci pneumonia [39]. Rapid radiological progression was noted in some cases, with development of multifocal confluent consolidation and ground-glass opacities (GGO) [9, 20, 30, 37]. Pleural effusion was present in 43% (9/21 with available data), typically small-to-moderate and often bilateral [14, 16, 22, 28–30, 33, 34, 36]. Hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy occurred in a minority [11, 18], including one sarcoidosis-like presentation [18].

Laboratory findings

In this cohort, thrombocytopenia occurred in 81% (26/32), with 38% (12/32) < 50 × 10⁹/L, 22% (7/32) < 20 × 10⁹/L, and a nadir of 9 × 10⁹/L [17]. White blood cell (WBC) counts were normal in 59% (19/32). Leukocytosis occurred in 22% (7/32) with a peak of 46 × 10⁹/L [20], while leukopenia occurred in 19% (6/32) with a nadir of 2.0 × 10⁹/L [8]. Anemia affected 63% (20/32), with hemoglobin as low as 60 g/L [19]. Coagulation abnormalities occurred in 47% (15/32), often with DIC or severe coagulopathy and peak D-dimer > 20 mg/L [36].

Elevated transaminases (84%, 27/32) and hyperbilirubinemia (41%, 13/32) were prevalent, with hepatosplenomegaly noted in some cases. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was elevated in all 15 patients with available data. Renal impairment occurred in 56% (18/32) of the cohort. Procalcitonin (PCT; 100%, 7/7 with available data) and C-reactive protein (CRP; 100%, 13/13 with available data) were elevated, including in cases without evidence of co-infection. Hyponatremia occurred in 63% (20/32) of the cohort. The primary laboratory findings are summarized in Table 3.

Fetal and neonatal outcome

In the present study, one case [36] did not specify the neonatal outcome. Fetal and neonatal mortality was 68% (21/31 with available data), likely overestimated due to publication bias. Fatal outcomes included spontaneous abortion (n = 7) [11, 17, 18, 20, 25, 27, 34], therapeutic induction (n = 3) [23, 24, 30], intrauterine death or stillbirth (n = 10) [9, 12–16, 19, 28, 32, 35], and early neonatal death (n = 1) [13].

Of the 10 surviving neonates, 5 cases [12, 21, 26, 31, 38] had stable outcomes and required no specialized medical intervention. The remaining five survivors [8, 22, 29, 33, 37] required respiratory support or intensive care due to complications. For instance, the neonate reported by Walder et al. [8] (31 weeks, 970 g) required mechanical ventilation for 9 weeks but demonstrated normal development at 6 months.

Histopathological examinations of placental tissue across multiple studies [14, 16, 23, 25, 32] have consistently revealed characteristic acute placentitis, featuring diffuse intervillositis with dense neutrophilic infiltration, perivillous fibrinoid necrosis and deposition, extensive trophoblast necrosis, and the presence of distinctive basophilic inclusions within the cytoplasm of syncytiotrophoblasts. Diagnostic methods

Diagnostic confirmation across the 32 cases relied on serology (14/32, 44%), mNGS (9/32, 28%), pathogen isolation or histopathology (5/32, 16%), and targeted polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (4/32, 13%) (Table 5). Exact daily intervals from symptom onset to diagnosis could not be consistently determined due to variable reporting details.

Serological testing, primarily complement fixation tests (CFT) and micro-immunofluorescence (MIF), was historically the mainstay of diagnosis. Confirmation based on a fourfold or greater increase in C. psittaci–specific antibody titers resulted in retrospective diagnosis after clinical recovery or pregnancy termination. In the cases confirmed via histopathology or isolation, specimens primarily included placental tissue, fetal heart blood, and fetal lung. These cases were identified through pathogen isolation from clinical samples or the histopathological observation of characteristic basophilic inclusions within the syncytiotrophoblast cytoplasm.

Molecular diagnostics, including PCR and mNGS, were utilized in the remaining cases. PCR-based detection was first recorded by Pospischil et al. [25] using placental tissue. mNGS was subsequently applied to identify C. psittaci nucleic acids in clinical specimens including blood, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), sputum, and archived lung tissue obtained at autopsy [27, 28, 34].

Treatment

Initial empirical antimicrobial therapy varied, with β-lactams most commonly used before definitive diagnosis [9, 12–16, 24, 35]. Upon suspicion or confirmation of C. psittaci infection, therapy was guided by gestational status: macrolides predominated antenatally [21, 22, 26], whereas tetracyclines, mainly doxycycline, were preferred postpartum [8, 13, 21]. Severe cases often received multidrug regimens [28, 29, 34].

Among maternal survivors, most received targeted intracellular agents, though some received only β-lactams, aminoglycosides, or no specific antibiotics, often in association with pregnancy termination [12, 14, 16, 23, 24, 27]. In four maternal deaths, treatments included meropenem [9, 35], cephalothin/gentamicin/erythromycin [12], or no antibiotics due to rapid deterioration [32]. One report described maternal recovery and a healthy neonate after a 24-day azithromycin-tigecycline course [38]. Treatment duration and neonatal follow-up were frequently underreported.

Discussion

Pregnant women are more susceptible to intracellular pathogens, likely due to altered cell-mediated immunity [35]. Our analysis of 32 cases shows gestational psittacosis as a severe disease with rapid deterioration and multisystem involvement, with 66% requiring ICU care, maternal mortality of 13%, and fetal/neonatal mortality of 68%. For comparison, Katsura et al. [9] reported maternal and fetal mortality of 9% and 83%, respectively. These figures may overestimate population-level risk due to reporting bias.

High fetal loss is driven by multiple factors. Maternal complications such as septic shock, DIC, and ARDS exacerbate fetal hypoxia and hypotension. Histopathology in several cases [14, 16, 23, 25, 32] shows active pathogen replication in the placenta causing acute placentitis and trophoblast necrosis, resulting in utero-placental malperfusion and fetal death without direct fetal infection. Iatrogenic termination or early delivery due to maternal deterioration also contributes to fetal and neonatal loss.

Gestational psittacosis is a rare zoonotic infection, with 81% (26/32) of patients in our cohort reporting animal exposure. Clinical presentations vary, reflecting differences in exposure intensity and host immunity. Common laboratory abnormalities include hepatic (84%) and renal impairment (56%), alongside thrombocytopenia (81%). Platelets play a key role in vascular immunity [40], and their progressive decline signals increasing disease severity. In our cohort, severe thrombocytopenia was linked to fulminant complications such as DIC and secondary hemophagocytic syndrome [30, 34]. Due to its nonspecific clinical and laboratory features, gestational psittacosis can be easily confused with pregnancy-related conditions such as HELLP syndrome or acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP). Consequently, when encountering pregnant women presenting with unexplained persistent fever (> 48 h) and thrombocytopenia, clinicians should be alert to the possibility of psittacosis, especially if an epidemiological link to avian or ruminant sources exists. To facilitate early recognition and clinical management of suspected cases, we propose a diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm for gestational psittacosis based on the findings of this review (Fig. 2).

Traditional serology is limited by its retrospective nature, cross-reactivity with other Chlamydia species, and blunted antibody responses from early antibiotic therapy [21, 41], reducing specificity and delaying acute diagnosis. mNGS shows promise for diagnosing severe or atypical psittacosis [39, 42–44], though its clinical utility requires further validation. Regarding management, macrolides are the mainstay of treatment during pregnancy, with tetracyclines typically reserved postpartum [45]. Early delivery or termination was often necessary to prevent maternal deterioration [23, 24, 30]. Although a recent case reported successful use of azithromycin-tigecycline [38], its safety and efficacy in pregnancy remain unestablished.

Given the zoonotic nature of gestational psittacosis, public health strategies should emphasize exposure counseling for pregnant women regarding the risks associated with livestock, particularly birds and ruminants. Furthermore, high-risk occupational groups warrant heightened vigilance, and adherence to standard biosafety measures is recommended.

This review synthesizes evidence exclusively from retrospective case reports and small case series, which constitutes its primary limitation. Despite the rigorous literature search and structured quality assessment, the overall quality of evidence remains of low certainty due to the absence of controlled observational studies or randomized trials for this rare condition. These study designs are inherently susceptible to significant publication and selection bias. The overrepresentation of severe, fatal, or diagnostically challenging cases likely leads to an overestimation of morbidity and mortality rates. Consequently, these estimates reflect outcomes among reported cases rather than true population-level risks, as milder infections remain underrepresented. Furthermore, the heterogeneity in diagnostic methods, clinical practices, and reporting completeness across several decades affects data consistency. Together, these constraints necessitate a cautious interpretation of the synthesized clinical patterns and highlight the critical need for systematic, prospective data collection.

Conclusions

Gestational psittacosis is a rare zoonotic infection predominantly characterized by severe atypical pneumonia and multi-organ dysfunction. Our synthesis of 32 cases demonstrates substantial risks of maternal mortality (13%) and adverse fetal outcomes (68%). These estimates should be interpreted cautiously, as publication bias may overestimate true population-level risks. The adoption of molecular diagnostics, particularly mNGS, has facilitated earlier diagnosis in recent reports and can aid in initiating targeted antimicrobial therapy, pending further comparative data. Heightened clinical awareness is necessary to differentiate this condition from pregnancy-specific syndromes and optimize maternal–fetal outcomes.
