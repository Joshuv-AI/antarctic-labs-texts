Association Between the WHO Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease Interventions and Clinical Outcomes Among Patients With Hypertension and Diabetes in Palestine: A Comparative Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Zeenat Mesk, Mohammad Qtait, Ethar Harahsha, Amani Abu Haltam, Shaima Ashour, Yahya Khallaf, Hadeel TiTI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsNon‐communicable diseases (NCDs), particularly hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus, represent a major public health burden in Palestine. The World Health Organization's Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease Interventions (PEN) was introduced to strengthen primary healthcare services in low‐resource settings. However, evidence on its association with patient‐level clinical outcomes in Palestine remains limited. This study aimed to assess the association between PEN implementation and clinical outcomes, including blood pressure, glycemic control, and disease‐related complications.MethodsA comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted across 18 primary healthcare clinics in Hebron, Palestine, between February and May 2025. A total of 440 patients (220 receiving PEN‐based care and 220 receiving conventional care) were recruited using convenience sampling. Data were collected through structured questionnaires and clinical records. Bivariate analyses were performed using Mann–Whitney U and χ 2 tests. Multivariable logistic and linear regression models were used to adjust for potential confounders. Effect estimates were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and beta coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsPatients receiving PEN‐based care had lower systolic blood pressure (mean difference −2.93 mmHg; β = −2.93, 95% CI [ − 5.61, −0.25], p = 0.032) and lower HbA1C levels (β = −0.42, 95% CI [ − 0.78, −0.06], p = 0.021) compared with conventional care, although the absolute difference in HbA1C was small. Fasting blood glucose was substantially lower in the PEN group (mean difference −30.15 mg/dL, p < 0.001). The PEN approach was associated with higher odds of controlled blood pressure (AOR = 1.78, 95% CI [1.15, 2.74]) and HbA1C (AOR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.05, 2.56]), and lower odds of complications (AOR = 0.52, 95% CI [0.34, 0.79]). Patients in the PEN group also reported significantly higher healthcare utilization and adherence indicators.ConclusionThe PEN approach was associated with modest improvements in clinical outcomes, reduced complications, and enhanced healthcare utilization among patients with hypertension and diabetes in primary care settings. However, given the cross‐sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Further longitudinal and experimental studies are required to confirm these findings and evaluate long‐term impact.Keywords: blood pressure, complications, diabetes, HbA1C, hypertension, non‐communicable diseases, Palestine, PEN approach, primary care

Introduction

Non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) represent a leading global health challenge, accounting for approximately 71% of all deaths worldwide, with a disproportionate burden in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [1]. Among these conditions, hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus are highly prevalent and contribute substantially to morbidity, mortality, and long‐term disability. Effective management of these chronic diseases requires sustained monitoring, integrated care delivery, and patient‐centered interventions within primary healthcare systems, particularly in resource‐constrained settings [2].

In response to this growing burden, the WHO introduced the Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease Interventions (PEN), a strategic framework designed to support the delivery of standardized, cost‐effective NCD care at the primary healthcare level. The PEN approach emphasizes integrated management through risk stratification, standardized clinical protocols, essential medication availability, and continuity of care. This model is intended to strengthen primary healthcare capacity and support non‐specialist providers in delivering evidence‐based services in LMICs (WHO, 2020 [3, 4]). Evidence from several countries suggests that PEN implementation is associated with improvements in service delivery processes, screening coverage, and selected clinical indicators; however, findings remain context‐dependent and vary across settings [5, 6].

In Palestine, NCDs constitute a major public health concern, with increasing prevalence of hypertension and diabetes and a growing burden of related complications. The Palestinian Ministry of Health adopted the PEN approach in 2015 to strengthen NCD management within primary healthcare clinics. Despite this national implementation, existing evidence on the performance of PEN in the Palestinian context remains limited. Previous local studies have primarily focused on process indicators such as documentation and risk assessment, with less emphasis on patient‐level clinical outcomes, including blood pressure and glycemic control [7, 8]. Furthermore, structural challenges such as resource constraints, variability in service delivery, and ongoing socio‐political instability may influence the implementation and outcomes of such interventions [9].

The co‐occurrence of hypertension and diabetes further complicates disease management, as these conditions share common risk factors and synergistically increase the risk of cardiovascular, renal, and microvascular complications [10]. Integrated care models, such as PEN, have been proposed as a suitable approach to address multimorbidity within primary healthcare systems. However, the extent to which such models are associated with improved clinical outcomes in real‐world settings, particularly in Palestine, remains insufficiently explored.

Given these gaps, there is a need for empirical evaluation of PEN implementation using robust analytical approaches that account for potential confounding factors and avoid overinterpretation of associations. Importantly, observational designs, such as cross‐sectional studies, are limited to identifying associations rather than causal relationships, and findings should be interpreted within this context.

Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the association between the implementation of the WHO PEN approach and selected clinical outcomes, including blood pressure control, glycemic levels (HbA1C), and the occurrence of disease‐related complications among patients with hypertension and diabetes in primary healthcare settings in Hebron, Palestine. By comparing patients managed under the PEN approach with those receiving conventional care, this study seeks to provide context‐specific evidence to inform policy and practice in NCD management within resource‐limited settings.

Literature Review

Non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) remain the leading cause of global mortality, accounting for approximately 74% of deaths worldwide, with a disproportionate burden in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [1]. Hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus are among the most prevalent NCDs and frequently coexist, sharing common behavioral and metabolic risk factors such as obesity, physical inactivity, and unhealthy dietary patterns [2]. The co‐occurrence of these conditions is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, renal impairment, and other long‐term complications, thereby necessitating integrated and sustained management approaches [10].

In response to the growing burden of NCDs, the WHO developed the Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease Interventions (PEN), a structured framework designed to strengthen primary healthcare services in resource‐limited settings. The PEN approach focuses on standardized clinical protocols, risk‐based assessment, essential medication availability, and continuity of care, enabling non‐specialist healthcare providers to deliver consistent and evidence‐based management for patients with chronic diseases (WHO, 2020). Unlike disease‐specific vertical programs, PEN adopts an integrated care model aimed at addressing multiple conditions simultaneously within primary healthcare systems.

Evidence from LMICs suggests that the implementation of the PEN model is associated with improvements in healthcare delivery processes, including increased screening rates, enhanced follow‐up, and better adherence to clinical guidelines. For example, a mixed‐methods study conducted in Myanmar reported improvements in service coverage and provider capacity following PEN implementation [6]. Similarly, a systematic review by Aminpour et al. [5] found that integrated NCD care models, including PEN, were associated with improvements in selected clinical indicators such as blood pressure and glycemic control. However, the authors emphasized that the magnitude and consistency of these outcomes varied across settings, highlighting the influence of contextual factors such as health system capacity, workforce training, and resource availability.

Further evidence from Iran indicated that scaling up PEN interventions in rural clinics was associated with reductions in cardiovascular risk factors, including blood pressure and fasting blood glucose levels [11]. Nonetheless, these findings were derived from observational designs, and therefore should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Similarly, global evidence synthesized by Jameel et al. [12] suggests that integrated primary care models may contribute to improved treatment adherence and monitoring; however, persistent challenges such as medication shortages, limited infrastructure, and variability in implementation fidelity may affect outcomes.

In the Palestinian context, the Ministry of Health adopted the PEN approach in 2015 as part of national efforts to strengthen NCD management within primary healthcare services. Despite its widespread implementation, empirical evidence evaluating its impact on patient‐level clinical outcomes remains limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on process indicators rather than clinical endpoints. For instance, a retrospective study conducted in Gaza reported improvements in documentation and risk assessment practices but did not evaluate clinical outcomes such as blood pressure or HbA1C levels [7]. Another study suggested improvements in glycemic control among diabetic patients; however, methodological limitations, including study design and lack of comparative analysis, restrict the generalizability of these findings [8].

Moreover, the implementation of NCD interventions in Palestine occurs within a complex socio‐political and economic environment characterized by resource constraints, fragmented healthcare delivery, and intermittent disruptions to service provision [9]. These contextual challenges may influence both the delivery and outcomes of interventions such as PEN, underscoring the importance of context‐specific evaluation. In addition, variability in provider training, adherence to clinical protocols, and availability of essential medications may contribute to differences in outcomes across healthcare settings.

While the global literature supports the potential role of integrated care models such as PEN in improving NCD management, important gaps remain. Specifically, there is limited evidence examining the association between PEN implementation and clinical outcomes in real‐world primary healthcare settings in Palestine. Furthermore, few studies have employed comparative approaches that allow for the examination of differences between PEN‐based care and conventional care within the same health system.

Therefore, a critical need exists for contextually grounded research that evaluates the association between PEN implementation and key clinical indicators, while accounting for methodological limitations inherent in observational designs. Such evidence is essential to inform policy decisions, optimize implementation strategies, and strengthen primary healthcare responses to the growing burden of NCDs in Palestine and similar LMIC settings.

Aim of the Study

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of the World Health Organization's Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease (PEN) interventions compared to conventional care in managing hypertension and diabetes among patients in primary healthcare settings in Palestine. Specifically, the study seeks to assess the impact of the PEN approach on controlling blood pressure and HbA1C levels, and on reducing the incidence of disease‐related complications.

Main Research Question

Does the implementation of the World Health Organization's Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease (PEN) interventions significantly improve the control of blood pressure and HbA1C levels, and reduce the incidence of complications among patients with hypertension and diabetes compared to conventional care in primary healthcare settings in Palestine?

Methods

Study Design

A comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted to examine the association between the implementation of the WHO Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease Interventions (PEN) and selected clinical outcomes among patients with hypertension and/or type 2 diabetes mellitus. This design was selected to enable comparison between two patient groups those receiving PEN‐based care and those receiving conventional care at a single point in time. Given the observational nature of the design, the findings are limited to identifying associations rather than causal relationships.

Study Setting and Timeline

The study was conducted in 18 primary healthcare (PHC) clinics in the Hebron district, Palestine, including both urban and rural facilities. These clinics operate under the Palestinian Ministry of Health and have implemented the PEN approach since 2015. Data collection was carried out between February 10 and May 1, 2025.

Study Population and Sampling

The study population comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with hypertension and/or type 2 diabetes mellitus for at least 6 months and attending selected PHC clinics during the study period. A non‐probability convenience sampling technique was used due to operational constraints within clinical settings. A total of 440 participants were included, with 220 patients receiving care under the PEN approach and 220 receiving conventional care. Although random sampling would enhance representativeness, it was not feasible in this context; therefore, this limitation was considered in the interpretation of findings.

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed based on existing literature and clinical guidelines. The instrument consisted of three sections:

1.Sociodemographic characteristics: age, sex, marital status, education level, and income

Sociodemographic characteristics: age, sex, marital status, education level, and income

2.Clinical and management indicators for hypertension: blood pressure monitoring frequency, adherence to treatment, and hypertension‐related complications

3.Clinical and management indicators for diabetes: HbA1C levels, fasting blood glucose, treatment adherence, and diabetes‐related complications

Clinical outcome data, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure, HbA1C levels, and fasting blood glucose, were extracted from patients' medical records to enhance measurement accuracy.

Validity and Reliability

Content validity of the instrument was assessed through expert review by academic and clinical specialists in nursing and public health. A pilot study was conducted with 25 patients to evaluate clarity and feasibility. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach's α, yielding a coefficient of 0.88, indicating acceptable reliability. Necessary modifications were made based on pilot findings.

Data Collection Procedures

Data were collected through direct patient interviews and review of clinical records. Trained researchers visited participating clinics daily during the data collection period. Participants were informed about the study objectives and procedures prior to enrollment, and written informed consent was obtained. All collected data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 28. All statistical analyses were conducted using two‐sided tests, with a predefined level of significance set at α = 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD), whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Based on the distribution of the data, non‐parametric tests were applied where appropriate. Differences between patients receiving the PEN approach and those receiving conventional care were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, such as blood pressure and HbA1C levels, and the χ 2 test for categorical variables, including the presence of disease‐related complications. For all comparisons, the statistical test used and corresponding p‐values were reported, and mean differences were calculated to provide an estimate of effect size for continuous outcomes.

To account for potential confounding variables, multivariable regression analyses were performed. Logistic regression models were used to examine the association between type of care (PEN vs. conventional) and binary outcomes, such as controlled blood pressure and the presence of complications. Linear regression models were applied for continuous outcomes, including systolic blood pressure and HbA1C levels. Adjusted effect estimates were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) for logistic regression and beta coefficients (β) for linear regression, each presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI) to indicate the precision of the estimates.

Variables included in the multivariable models were selected based on theoretical relevance and results from bivariate analyses. Prior to model interpretation, assumptions of regression analyses were assessed, including independence of observations and absence of multicollinearity. Model adequacy was evaluated to ensure the validity and robustness of the findings.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Palestine Polytechnic University (Approval No. EA.013‐2025) as well as from the Palestinian Ministry of Health. All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national ethical standards.

Prior to participation, all eligible participants were informed about the purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. Participants were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences to their care.

Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the research process. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all data were securely stored and used solely for research purposes

Discussion

The present study examined the association between the implementation of the WHO Package of Essential Non‐Communicable Disease (PEN) interventions and key clinical outcomes among patients with hypertension and type 2 diabetes in primary healthcare settings in Hebron, Palestine. Overall, the findings indicate that the PEN approach was associated with improved clinical indicators, enhanced healthcare utilization, and reduced prevalence of disease‐related complications compared with conventional care. However, these findings should be interpreted within the limitations of the cross‐sectional design and the potential influence of residual confounding.

The results demonstrated that patients receiving PEN‐based care had lower systolic blood pressure compared to those receiving conventional care, with an adjusted mean difference of approximately −2.93 mmHg. Although statistically significant, the magnitude of this difference is modest. Nevertheless, even small reductions in systolic blood pressure at the population level are clinically meaningful, as they are associated with reduced cardiovascular risk, particularly in high‐risk populations [10]. The absence of a statistically significant difference in diastolic blood pressure suggests that the observed benefit may be more pronounced in systolic control, which is consistent with evidence highlighting systolic pressure as a stronger predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in older adults.

Regarding glycemic control, HbA1C levels were statistically lower in the PEN group; however, the absolute difference (− 0.02%) was minimal. This finding underscores an important distinction between statistical significance and clinical relevance. While the adjusted linear regression analysis showed a modest but significant association (β = −0.42), the clinical impact of this reduction should be interpreted cautiously. In contrast, the substantial reduction in fasting blood glucose levels (mean difference of −30.15 mg/dL) suggests that PEN implementation may have a more immediate effect on short‐term glycemic control than on long‐term markers such as HbA1C.

These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies conducted in LMICs, where integrated care models such as PEN have been associated with improvements in selected clinical indicators, although with varying effect sizes across settings [5, 11]. Importantly, the current study extends this evidence to the Palestinian context, where empirical data on patient‐level outcomes remain limited.

A key finding of this study is the significantly lower prevalence of both diabetes‐ and hypertension‐related complications among patients receiving PEN‐based care. The multivariable analysis further supports this association, demonstrating that the PEN approach was associated with a 48% reduction in the odds of complications (AOR = 0.52, 95% CI [0.34, 0.79]). This suggests that integrated, standardized care may contribute to improved long‐term disease management and complication prevention.

Notably, large differences were observed in specific complications, such as diabetic retinopathy and cardiovascular events. These findings align with global evidence indicating that integrated chronic disease management models can reduce complication burden through improved monitoring, early detection, and adherence to treatment protocols [13]. However, it is important to consider that these associations may also be influenced by differences in diagnostic practices, as more structured care in the PEN group could lead to earlier identification and management of risk factors.

An unexpected finding was the slightly higher proportion of kidney‐related complications in the PEN group. Although not statistically significant, this may reflect detection bias or differences in screening practices rather than a true increase in disease burden. This highlights the need for standardized diagnostic protocols across care models to ensure comparability [14, 15].

The study also demonstrated substantial differences in healthcare utilization and management practices between groups. Patients in the PEN group reported significantly higher rates of lifestyle counseling, medication management, regular monitoring, and follow‐up. These findings suggest that the observed improvements in clinical outcomes may be mediated by enhanced service delivery and patient engagement.

The PEN model emphasizes patient‐centered care, risk stratification, and continuity of care, which are critical components of effective chronic disease management. Improved adherence observed in the PEN group (80.5% vs. 69.0%) further supports this mechanism, as adherence is a well‐established determinant of treatment success in both hypertension and diabetes [12]. Therefore, the benefits associated with PEN implementation are likely multifactorial, involving both clinical and behavioral pathways.

A major strength of this study is the use of multivariable regression analysis to adjust for potential confounders, addressing a key limitation highlighted by reviewers. The findings from both logistic and linear regression models consistently demonstrated that the PEN approach was independently associated with better outcomes, even after controlling for age, gender, education, and duration of illness [16, 17].

In addition, increasing age and longer disease duration were consistently associated with poorer outcomes, including higher blood pressure, higher HbA1C levels, and increased likelihood of complications. These findings are consistent with existing literature and reinforce the importance of early intervention and sustained disease management.

The implementation of the PEN approach in Palestine occurs within a complex healthcare environment characterized by resource constraints, fragmented service delivery, and socio‐political instability [9]. Despite these challenges, the findings suggest that the PEN model is adaptable and can be effectively integrated into primary healthcare systems.

The observed improvements in service utilization and adherence are particularly notable in this context, as they indicate that structured care models can enhance healthcare delivery even in constrained settings. However, variability in resource availability, provider training, and infrastructure across clinics may influence the consistency of implementation and outcomes. Therefore, scaling up the PEN approach should be accompanied by system‐level strengthening, including workforce development and supply chain management.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including a relatively large sample size, inclusion of multiple primary healthcare clinics, and the use of multivariable analytical approaches. The combination of clinical record data and patient‐reported information enhances the validity of the findings.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of effectiveness. Second, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Third, residual confounding cannot be excluded, despite statistical adjustment. Additionally, some outcomes, such as adherence and lifestyle behaviors, were self‐reported and may be subject to reporting bias.

Implications for Practice and Research

The findings of this study have important implications for policy and practice. The association between PEN implementation and improved clinical outcomes supports the continued expansion of integrated NCD care models within primary healthcare settings. However, attention should be given to ensuring consistent implementation, monitoring quality of care, and addressing gaps in specific areas such as renal screening.

Future research should focus on longitudinal and experimental designs to establish causal relationships and assess the sustainability of observed improvements. Additionally, cost‐effectiveness analyses are needed to inform resource allocation decisions in low‐resource settings such as Palestine.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that the PEN approach is associated with improved clinical outcomes, reduced complications, and enhanced healthcare utilization among patients with hypertension and diabetes in primary healthcare settings. While the magnitude of some effects was modest, the overall pattern of findings supports the potential value of integrated care models in strengthening NCD management in resource‐limited contexts. Nevertheless, further rigorous research is required to confirm these associations and guide optimal implementation strategies.
