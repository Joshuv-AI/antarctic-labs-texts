Game-based blended learning versus traditional lecture for evidence-based medicine (EBM) competencies in undergraduate medical students: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Vahid Zand, Ramin Bazi, Fatemeh Keshmiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEvidence-based medicine (EBM) competencies are crucial for medical students, yet they are often not fully developed. The best teaching methods for cultivating these skills remain uncertain. This study aimed to compare the effects of a game-based blended learning approach versus traditional lecture-based instruction on the development of EBM competencies among undergraduate medical students.MethodThis quasi-experimental study was conducted at the Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences (SSU). Fifty-eight clerkship students were cluster-assigned to intervention (puzzle-based blended, n = 27) or control (interactive lecture, n = 31). The intervention group participated in the blended game-based learning, combining asynchronous pre-class learning with an in-person, puzzle-based game. The control group received interactive lecture-based sessions delivered by an EBM expert. EBM competency was measured pre/post with the Assessing Competency in Evidence-Based Medicine (ACE) tool. ACE consists of a short clinical scenario followed by 15 binary questions. Learners in the intervention and control groups participated in the ACE examination, which was administered one week before the training and one week after the training. Data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Independent t-tests and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) were used to assess the intervention effect. A statistically significant result was considered 0.05.ResultsIn the intervention group, the mean score for EBM competency increased significantly from 3.55 (SD = 1.71) before the intervention to 13.11 (SD = 2.24) after the intervention (p < 0.001). In the control group, the mean EBM competency score rose from 4.12 (SD = 2.20) pre-intervention to 10.12 (SD = 2.23) post-intervention (p < 0.001). After adjusting for covariates, the impact of the intervention on total EBM competency scores remained statistically significant (F (1, 53) = 21.46, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (partial η² = 0.292).ConclusionThe game-based blended learning is effective for teaching Evidence-Based Medicine because it organizes the sequential and interconnected aspects of the EBM decision-making pathway. Notably, the findings indicate that the method is effective in developing higher-order EBM skills that require critical thinking, integration, and clinical judgment. The game-based blended learning—a specific, low-technology game genre—is highly effective for EBM education, making it accessible to institutions with limited technological resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09628-3.

Introduction

Evidence-based medicine (EBM) is defined as the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of the best available evidence to make decisions about patient care [1]. This approach integrates the best research evidence with clinical expertise while considering patient values and preferences, thereby fundamentally transforming healthcare delivery [2]. EBM comprises five core steps: (a) formulating answerable clinical questions, (b) efficiently searching for the best available evidence, (c) critically appraising the evidence for validity and applicability, (d) applying the evidence in clinical practice, and (e) evaluating outcomes and performance [3, 4].

The increasing complexity of healthcare systems, characterized by rapidly expanding medical knowledge, accelerated research production, and continuous technological innovation, has made EBM competencies indispensable for future physicians [3, 5]. Despite the recognized importance of these competencies, substantial deficiencies in EBM knowledge and skills persist among undergraduate medical students and residents worldwide [6–8]. Consequently, identifying effective instructional approaches for EBM education has become a priority in medical education research.

Blended learning, which combines face-to-face and online instructional modalities, has emerged as a promising pedagogical approach for EBM education [9]. In particular, flipped classroom models promote active engagement by allowing students to acquire foundational knowledge before class and apply higher-order cognitive skills during in-class activities [10]. Previous study have reported that blended learning can improve EBM knowledge acquisition, learner satisfaction, and clinical reasoning skills among medical students [11].

Beyond blended learning, specific active-learning strategies such as puzzles may further enhance EBM education. Puzzle-based learning encourages learners to organize fragmented information into meaningful patterns, thereby promoting critical thinking, collaborative learning, and problem-solving abilities [12, 13].

From a theoretical perspective, puzzle-based learning may reduce extraneous cognitive load by organizing complex information into structured and manageable learning tasks while simultaneously facilitating collaborative knowledge construction through peer interaction and shared reasoning processes [14, 15]. Studies in pharmacology, anatomy, and clinical decision-making have indicated positive effects of puzzle-based educational games on learner engagement and academic performance [13, 16–18].

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have further supported the effectiveness of blended and gamified learning approaches in health professions education, particularly for improving learner motivation, engagement, and higher-order cognitive skills [19–22]. However, no study has directly compared a blended, puzzle-based approach with traditional lecture-based instruction for teaching all five EBM steps to undergraduate medical students. Furthermore, evidence regarding the integration of game-based learning strategies within blended EBM instruction remains limited.

In the educational context examined, formal EBM instruction is not systematically integrated into the undergraduate medical curriculum. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effects of a game-based blended learning approach with traditional lecture-based instruction on the development of EBM competencies among undergraduate medical students. We hypothesized that students in the game-based blended learning group would demonstrate significantly higher post-intervention EBM competency scores.

Methods

Study design and setting

This quasi-experimental study was conducted at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences (SSU), Iran, during the 2025–2026 academic year. The study was designed and reported in accordance with recommendations for quasi-experimental educational research in medical education [23, 24].

Participants

Participants were undergraduate medical students enrolled in clerkship rotations at SSU. Eligibility criteria included: (a) completion of at least three months of clerkship training, and (b) provision of written informed consent. Students who withdrew consent or did not complete either the pre-test or post-test assessments were excluded from the final analysis.

Sample size and sampling procedure

Cluster sampling was used to recruit all eligible clerkship students during two consecutive monthly rotations. Students enrolled in January 2026 constituted the control group, whereas students enrolled in February 2026 constituted the intervention group. Temporal separation between groups was intentionally implemented to minimize contamination and information exchange between participants exposed to different instructional methods.

The required sample size was estimated using G*Power software based on pilot data, assuming a significance level of 0.05, statistical power of 80%, and an anticipated effect size of 0.80. The analysis indicated a minimum sample of 27 participants per group (Fig. 1).

Although temporal separation reduced the risk of contamination, it may also have introduced potential confounding related to differences in clinical exposure, rotation schedules, or examination timing between the two cohorts. To minimize this risk, baseline demographic characteristics and pre-intervention ACE scores were statistically controlled during data analysis using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).

Educational content

The educational content for both groups was identical and focused on the five core steps of evidence-based medicine: (a) formulating answerable clinical questions, (b) searching for the best available evidence, (c) critically appraising the validity and applicability of evidence, (d) applying evidence in clinical decision-making, and (e) evaluating clinical performance and outcomes [3, 4].

Both groups received a total of 10 instructional hours delivered across five sessions of two hours each. However, the instructional strategies differed substantially between groups.

Educational interventions

Intervention group: game-based blended learning

Design of the puzzle-based EBM game

The intervention integrated asynchronous learning with a synchronous puzzle-based educational game. The puzzle activity was designed to reinforce the sequential and integrative nature of EBM decision-making through collaborative problem-solving and active learning principles.

A total of 12 puzzle sets were developed. Each set contained four clinical scenarios and seven EBM-related component categories, resulting in 28 puzzle elements per session. The components included patient history elements, PICO question formulation, research question development, evidence-searching strategies, critical appraisal indicators, evaluation of selected studies, and final evidence-informed clinical decision-making.

The puzzle materials were designed as card-sorting and sequencing activities rather than interlocking physical puzzle pieces. Students were required to match and organize shuffled cards into the correct logical sequence corresponding to each clinical scenario.

Implementation of the game-based blended learning approach

The instructional design of the intervention was informed by principles of active learning and cognitive load theory [14, 25]. Specifically, the puzzle-based format was intended to reduce extraneous cognitive load by organizing complex EBM processes into structured, manageable learning tasks while simultaneously promoting collaborative knowledge construction.

Asynchronous phase

Before each in-person session, students received written educational materials covering foundational EBM concepts and principles. This pre-class preparation allowed learners to acquire essential theoretical knowledge independently and promoted active participation during face-to-face sessions.

Synchronous phase

During in-person sessions, students worked collaboratively in small groups to complete puzzle-based EBM activities. Each group received four simulated clinical cases alongside shuffled cards representing different EBM components. Students were required to identify the correct components for each case and arrange them in the appropriate sequence according to the EBM process.

In each session, participants completed EBM documentation for four simulated patients and assembled a total of 28 puzzle cards. Across the intervention period, students completed 12 distinct EBM puzzle activities.

Following group discussions, a trained facilitator reviewed each group’s completed puzzle and provided structured formative feedback. The facilitator was an EBM expert with more than five years of experience in undergraduate medical education. To ensure consistency across sessions, a standardized facilitation and feedback protocol was used throughout the intervention. At the end of each session, the facilitator summarized key EBM concepts and clarified common misconceptions identified during the activity.

Control group: traditional lecture-based instruction

The control group participated in five instructor-led lecture sessions, each lasting two hours and delivered by an expert in evidence-based medicine. Each session included approximately 30 min of didactic teaching, 30 min of case-based question-and-answer discussion, and 60 min of guided worked examples demonstrating the practical application of EBM principles in clinical settings.

The lectures addressed all five EBM steps and emphasized evidence appraisal and clinical application through instructor-led demonstrations and structured discussion. No game-based or blended learning components were included in the control condition.

Outcome measure: the ACE tool

EBM competency was assessed using the Persian version of the Assessing Competency in Evidence-Based Medicine (ACE) tool [4], which has previously demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability in the investigated context (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79) [26]. The original ACE tool, developed by Ilic et al. [4], consists of a clinical scenario followed by 15 dichotomous questions assessing four domains of EBM competency: clinical question formulation, evidence searching, critical appraisal, and application of evidence to patient care [4] (Appendix 1).

Each item was scored as correct (1) or incorrect (0), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 15. Participants in both groups completed the ACE assessment one week before and one week after the intervention period.

To minimize assessment bias, ACE examinations were independently scored by two trained researchers who were blinded to group allocation. Inter-rater agreement between assessors was excellent (Cohen’s kappa > 0.90).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as means with standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare baseline characteristics between groups.

The effect of the intervention on post-test EBM competency scores was evaluated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), controlling for pre-test scores, age, and sex. Before conducting ANCOVA, assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variances, and homogeneity of regression slopes were assessed and confirmed (all p > 0.05).

Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (ηp²), with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively [27]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee before study initiation. (ID: IR.SSU.MEDICINE.REC.1404.244). Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The ACE assessment was conducted exclusively for research purposes and did not affect students’ official academic scores. Following completion of data collection, the control group was offered access to the puzzle-based learning materials to ensure educational equity between cohorts.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 58 medical students participated in the study. This included 27 students (46.6%) in the intervention group, which used game-based blended learning, and 31 students (53.4%) in the control group, which followed traditional lecture-based instruction. All eligible students completed both assessments, and there were no dropouts. The overall sample included 36 males (62.1%) and 22 females (37.9%). The mean age of the participants was 22.56 years (SD = 1.25). No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups concerning age (p = 0.13) or sex distribution (p = 0.13), indicating that the groups were comparable at baseline (see Table 1).

Discussion

The game-based blended intervention led to significantly greater improvements in EBM competency, resulting in a large overall effect size. Notably, the advantage of the intervention increased with task complexity. There was a non-significant difference for question formulation (small effect), a moderate effect for literature searching, and a large effect for evidence appraisal and application of evidence. These findings indicate that game-based blended learning may be particularly effective for developing higher-order EBM skills that require critical thinking, integration of knowledge, and clinical judgment.

Both the intervention and control groups showed significant improvements in EBM knowledge and skills from pre- to post-intervention, indicating that both educational approaches were beneficial. However, the magnitude of improvement was substantially greater in the intervention group, which may be attributed to the active and constructive nature of the puzzle-based blended learning process. The flipped classroom structure enabled students to acquire foundational EBM knowledge asynchronously before participating in collaborative, in-person puzzle activities that required active application of concepts. Each puzzle piece represented a component of the EBM decision-making pathway, including patient history, PICO question formulation, search strategies, critical appraisal, and evidence application. The process of organizing and integrating these components may have facilitated schema construction and deeper cognitive processing of the EBM workflow [28].

From the perspective of cognitive load theory, the intervention may have supported learning by reducing extraneous cognitive load and facilitating germane cognitive processing through structured, sequential activities and immediate formative feedback [25, 29, 30]. Rather than passively receiving information, students actively constructed knowledge through discussion, reasoning, and collaborative problem-solving, which may have contributed to stronger encoding and retention of EBM concepts.

The incorporation of gameplay elements, such as team-based competition and recognition for successful puzzle completion, likely enhanced learner engagement and motivation [31, 32]. According to self-determination theory [33], the puzzle-based intervention addressed learners’ psychological needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness. Students experienced competence through successful puzzle completion, autonomy through collaborative decision-making, and relatedness through peer interaction and teamwork. These motivational mechanisms may partially explain the superior performance observed in the intervention group. Furthermore, the collaborative small-group format promoted peer-assisted learning and social knowledge construction, consistent with sociocultural learning theory [31, 34–36]. Students discussed alternative interpretations, justified their reasoning, and negotiated decisions collaboratively during the puzzle activities.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of blended and game-based learning approaches in medical education. Ilic et al. [11] reported that blended learning significantly improved medical students’ EBM competencies compared with traditional instruction, particularly in clinically oriented EBM tasks [11]. Similarly, Perron et al. [37] found that learners exposed to serious game–based instruction in pediatric emergency medicine achieved superior performance in simulated clinical scenarios compared with traditional teaching methods [37]. Bass et al. [38] further emphasized that gamification strategies enhance learner engagement, motivation, and clinical decision-making through interactive and low-stress learning environments [38].

Although gamification and active learning strategies are generally associated with improved learner engagement and higher-order cognitive outcomes, several studies and systematic reviews have reported inconsistent or limited effects on lower-order knowledge acquisition and factual recall [39, 40]. This pattern is consistent with our findings in the domain of question formulation, where only a small and non-significant between-group effect size was observed. Structured and algorithmic tasks such as PICO-based question formulation may be sufficiently supported through conventional lecture-based instruction and worked examples without requiring resource-intensive active-learning interventions.

A notable finding of this study is the progressive effect size gradient across different EBM domains, which aligns with predictions derived from cognitive load theory. In question formulation, no statistically significant difference was observed between groups. However, moderate effects emerged for literature searching, whereas large effects were observed for evidence appraisal and application of evidence. This pattern suggests that the educational advantages of the game-based blended intervention became more pronounced as the cognitive complexity of the targeted skills increased.

Question formulation using the PICO framework is generally considered a structured and relatively algorithmic task that involves limited intrinsic cognitive load. Such tasks can often be learned effectively through explicit instruction and guided demonstrations. In contrast, evidence appraisal and evidence application are complex, and ill-structured cognitive processes requiring learners to integrate multiple sources of information, evaluate methodological quality, interpret statistical findings, and apply evidence to uncertain clinical contexts [5, 41, 42]. These higher-order tasks impose substantially greater intrinsic cognitive load and require advanced reasoning and schema integration.

The puzzle-based instructional design may have addressed these challenges by organizing complex information into manageable components (“chunking”), sequencing the EBM process logically, providing immediate feedback, and enabling repeated practice across multiple clinical scenarios. In addition, puzzle-solving activities required learners to recognize conceptual relationships between EBM components rather than relying solely on memorization. This process likely strengthened cognitive schemas and facilitated long-term knowledge organization.

In contrast, lecture-based instruction typically presents information in a more linear and passive format, which may be less effective for promoting active schema construction and clinical reasoning. Our findings are consistent with the scoping review by Koelewijn et al. [43], which concluded that serious games are particularly effective for teaching higher-order clinical reasoning and decision-making skills rather than simple factual recall [43]. Similarly, Lee et al. [44] reported that gamification approaches in EBM education produced the greatest effects in higher-order cognitive domains such as analysis, synthesis, and evaluation [43]. López-Navarrete et al. [45] also emphasized that active and simulation-based instructional approaches are particularly valuable for developing reasoning and decision-making skills in complex clinical situations [44].

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the quasi-experimental design with non-equivalent groups may have introduced selection bias despite baseline similarities between groups. Although temporal separation reduced contamination, differences in clinical exposure, workload, or examination timing between cohorts may have influenced outcomes. Second, the modest sample size (N = 58) and single-institution setting may limit the generalizability of the findings and prevent subgroup analyses to identify which learners benefit most from game-based blended learning approaches. Multi-center randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm the generalizability and sustainability of these findings.

Third, EBM competency was assessed using simulated clinical scenarios through the ACE tool rather than direct observation in authentic clinical practice. Consequently, the extent to which these findings translate into real-world clinical performance remains uncertain. Moreover, the post-test assessment was conducted one week after the intervention, limiting conclusions regarding long-term retention of EBM competencies. Finally, learner engagement and motivation were not directly measured.

Future studies should incorporate validated engagement questionnaires, motivational scales, or learning analytics/log data to better understand the mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of game-based blended learning interventions.

Implications for educational practice

The findings of this study suggest that puzzle-based blended learning may represent an effective and practical instructional strategy for teaching complex EBM competencies in undergraduate medical education. In particular, this approach appears beneficial for higher-order cognitive skills such as evidence appraisal, clinical reasoning, and evidence application.

The puzzle format requires only printed materials, making it low-cost and technology-independent. This characteristic may facilitate implementation in educational settings with limited technological infrastructure or financial resources. Furthermore, the asynchronous pre-class component supports self-paced learning and may accommodate diverse learner needs and learning preferences.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a game-based blended learning approach significantly improved EBM competencies among undergraduate medical students compared with traditional lecture-based instruction. The educational benefits of the intervention became increasingly pronounced as the cognitive complexity of EBM tasks increased, with the largest effects observed for evidence appraisal and evidence application.

Educators should consider incorporating puzzle-based blended learning activities into undergraduate EBM curricula, particularly for teaching complex clinical reasoning and evidence appraisal skills. Future research should investigate long-term retention, transfer of learning into clinical practice, cost-effectiveness, and optimal implementation strategies across diverse educational contexts. Multi-center randomized controlled studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to establish the broader generalizability of these findings.
