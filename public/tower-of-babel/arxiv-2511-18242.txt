EgoVITA: Learning to Plan and Verify for Egocentric Video Reasoning
Yogesh Kulkarni, Pooyan Fazli

Abstract
Reasoning about intentions and actions from a first-person (egocentric) perspective remains a fundamental challenge for multimodal large language models (MLLMs). Unlike third-person (exocentric) videos that capture scenes from an outside observer, egocentric videos reflect the actor’s continuously changing viewpoint, introducing partial observability, limited field of view, and self-referenced motion. We introduce EgoVITA , a reinforcement learning framework that enables MLLMs to reason through structured planning and verification. Built on Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), EgoVITA alternates between two stages: (1) an egocentric planning phase , where the model reasons from a first-person viewpoint to predict a step-by-step plan of future actions, and (2) an exocentric verification phase , where it switches to a third-person perspective to check the visual and logical consistency of that plan. Through GRPO, the model learns to make plans that are causally predictive of upcoming visual observations, leading to more coherent and visually grounded reasoning. EgoVITA achieves significant gains on egocentric reasoning tasks, outperforming the baseline Qwen2.5-VL-7B by + 7.7 \mathbf{+7.7} on EgoBlind and + 4.4 \mathbf{+4.4} on EgoOrient, while maintaining strong generalization on exocentric video tasks.

Introduction

Egocentric, or first-person, videos play a central role in applications such as life-logging, personal memory assistants
40
, assistive technology
14
;
37
, robot learning
47
, sports analysis
3
, and immersive AR/VR experiences
44
. Interpreting these videos is challenging due to constant camera motion, limited field of view, and frequent occlusions.
Effective understanding requires procedural reasoning, the ability to predict action outcomes, infer goals, and reason about object interactions over time. However, current multimodal large language models (MLLMs) show significant weaknesses in first-person video understanding. They frequently generate plausible but incorrect descriptions
30
, show weak spatiotemporal reasoning
28
;
21
;
20
, poor memory of object states
42
, and limited understanding of user goals and intentions
19
;
26
;
32
. Their accuracy also remains low on scene-text reading
46
, object orientation
7
;
12
, and cross-view alignment
9
, lagging far behind human performance even on large-scale, challenging benchmarks such as HD-EPIC
27
. These shortcomings reveal a key gap: current MLLMs lack mechanisms to model how actions, objects, and observations evolve from a first-person perspective. While supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on egocentric datasets can improve performance, it often reduces accuracy on standard exocentric benchmarks. This trade-off highlights the broader challenge of viewpoint generalization and the need for models that reason effectively from an egocentric perspective without losing exocentric competence.

We argue that robust egocentric reasoning requires moving beyond SFT, which merely imitates fixed action sequences. Effective reasoning is outcome-driven: the model must generate action sequences whose combined effect achieves the desired goal, making success dependent on the coherence of the entire sequence.
Methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
29
;
15
;
17
improve on SFT by ranking candidate sequences.
However, DPO is offline and limited to pre-collected data with feedback reflecting relative preferences rather than the actual trajectory success. This restricts the model’s ability to explore novel sequences or adapt to unseen scenarios.
In contrast, reinforcement learning (RL) provides true outcome-driven optimization, where feedback is based on the success of actions generated by the agent. In particular, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
8
enhances standard RL by evaluating trajectories within a group using relative rewards, providing denser, more stable learning signals. This enables the model to explore new reasoning paths, maintain temporal and causal consistency, and generalize to unseen situations
16
.

Related Work

Egocentric Video Understanding.

Egocentric video reasoning is uniquely challenging due to rapid camera motion, frequent object occlusions
20
;
27
, and the need to infer the intentions of an unobserved, active person
25
;
32
.
As a result, recent MLLM research has focused mainly on creating
benchmarks
that highlight specific cognitive failures rather than developing new
methods
. These failures include deficits in long-term memory and procedural reasoning
41
;
40
;
33
, fundamental temporal understanding
28
, spatial orientation
12
;
7
, and intention or Theory of Mind grounding
32
;
26
;
19
. Models also struggle with fine-grained perception of dynamic object states
42
or scene-text
46
, and often show hallucinations
30
or provide unsafe answers in assistive contexts
37
;
14
.
The few methods proposed, such as EgoThinker
25
and EgoVLM
34
, employ reinforcement learning. However, their reliance on simple, sparse rewards for output format and correctness limits long-term procedural reasoning and often causes catastrophic forgetting of general exocentric knowledge. To address this, we introduce a novel framework that leverages dense, predictive rewards to teach complex egocentric procedures while preserving exocentric generalization, tackling a key challenge in ego-exo knowledge transfer
43
;
23
;
10
.

Ego-Exo Video Understanding.

Applying MLLMs trained on large corpora of exocentric images and videos to egocentric video faces a fundamental
ego-exo gap

9
. This domain shift introduces several challenges, including viewpoint disparity that disrupts feature alignment
23
, difficulty in matching objects across views
24
, and complications in relative spatial reasoning
7
. Prior work attempts to bridge this gap by learning view-invariant representations
23
, using audio as a “sound bridge”
10
, or employing SFT pipelines for direct knowledge transfer
43
;
38
. While these approaches improve cross-view alignment, they struggle to teach dynamic procedural reasoning and often suffer from catastrophic forgetting of exocentric knowledge during fine-tuning. EgoVITA overcomes these limitations by explicitly separating reasoning into an egocentric planning phase and an exocentric verification phase. This design enables the model to learn robust first-person understanding while using verification to preserve third-person generalization. Unlike prior work
13
that relies on synchronized ego-exo video pairs, EgoVITA achieves scalable cross-view consistency without requiring paired data.

3
EgoVITA

We introduce EgoVITA, a framework that enables MLLMs to reason effectively about egocentric video. EgoVITA is trained in two stages. In
Stage I
, SFT establishes a stable base policy capable of generating structured outputs. In
Stage II
, this policy is refined using GRPO by exploring diverse reasoning trajectories and their relative performance. The framework separates reasoning into two complementary components. First,
egocentric planning
operates from a first-person viewpoint to generate an explicit plan of anticipated actions. Second,
exocentric verification
uses a third-person perspective to evaluate the plan for visual and logical consistency. Trained with novel dense rewards that capture future visual states and task outcomes, EgoVITA achieves stable learning and robust egocentric understanding. The overall framework is illustrated in Figure
2
.

format. In Stage II GRPO, it generates reasoning trajectories for a given video and query, scores them with a composite reward, and refines the model accordingly.

3.1
Problem Formulation

We formulate egocentric video reasoning as a sequential decision-making problem.
The MLLM acts as a policy
\pi_{\theta}
, parameterized by weights
\theta
.
Given an input video
V
and a text prompt
X
, the policy autoregressively generates a reasoning trajectory
Y=\langle y_{1},\dots,y_{T}\rangle
, where each token
y_{t}

represents a reasoning step conditioned on the video, prompt, and prior steps
y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid V,X,y_{<t})
. Each trajectory
Y
is a sequence consisting of an egocentric plan (
<ego_plan>
), an exocentric verification (
<exo_verify>
), and an answer (
<answer>
) block. The quality of a generated sequence
Y
is evaluated by a composite reward function
R
that combines syntactic, semantic, and visual signals to assess overall reasoning quality.
The objective is to learn a policy that maximizes the expected reward:

\theta^{*}=\arg\max_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi_{\theta}(Y\mid V,X)}[R(Y)].

(1)

3.2
Stage I: SFT

SFT establishes a stable initial policy
\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}
by training the MLLM to perform two complementary reasoning skills:
egocentric planning
and
exocentric verification
. This stage helps the model learn how to plan and verify effectively while becoming familiar with the structured output format (i.e., generating text within
<ego_plan>
,
<exo_verify>
, and
<answer>
tags). By grounding the model in these reasoning patterns, SFT initializes the policy in a well-conditioned region of the parameter space, which improves the stability and effectiveness of GRPO optimization for Stage II.

Egocentric Planning.

To train the model to generate feasible plans and temporally grounded action sequences from a first-person viewpoint, we use the EgoProceL dataset
2
, which provides time-aligned action annotations for egocentric videos. Each sample includes a query (e.g., “How do I prepare the vegetables for cooking?”) paired with a ground-truth plan formatted as an instruction-following sequence (e.g.,
<ego_plan>
1. Pick up the knife. 2. Place it on the board.
</ego_plan>
). We use Qwen2.5-VL-72B model
1
to generate the egocentric plans leveraging the ground truth data (Prompt provided in Supp.).

Exocentric Verification.

To train the model to verify plans against visual evidence, we construct a dataset using HD-EPIC
27
and the Qwen2.5-VL-72B model
1
. For each egocentric video clip in HD-EPIC, Qwen2.5-VL-72B generates a chain-of-thought rationale from a third-person perspective, leveraging human-annotated ground truth to ensure factual grounding (Prompt provided in Supp.). For example:

<exo_verify>
The plan is valid because the knife is visible on the counter.
</exo_verify>
.
This process teaches the model to assess the logical and visual consistency of its plans, complementing planning with external verification.

Joint SFT Objective.

We merge both datasets into a unified corpus,
D
, and train the policy using standard cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(V,X,Y^{*})\sim D}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(Y^{*}\mid V,X)\right],

(2)

where
(V,X,Y^{*})
denotes a video, prompt, and ground-truth reasoning sequence. Training on the combined corpus lets the model learn planning and verification simultaneously.

3.3
Stage II: GRPO

After SFT, we refine the policy
\pi_{\theta}
using GRPO, which improves reasoning by exploring and evaluating multiple candidate plans. In egocentric video understanding, there is rarely a single correct reasoning path, since several plausible interpretations can arise depending on context and camera motion. GRPO addresses this by sampling a group of trajectories per input and updating the policy based on their relative rewards, promoting diverse exploration while keeping learning stable.

3.3.1
GRPO Rollouts, Rewards, and Policy Updates

For each video-prompt pair
(V,X)
, the policy
\pi_{\theta}
generates a group of
k=8
rollouts
\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\}
, where each sequence includes a full
<ego_plan>
,
<exo_verify>
, and
<answer>
block. Each
Y_{i}
is scored using a
composite reward

R
that combines syntactic, semantic, and visual signals to assess overall reasoning quality.

Composite Reward (
R
).

The reward is a weighted sum of four components:

R=w_{f}R_{\mathrm{format}}+w_{a}R_{\mathrm{answer}}\\
+w_{g}R_{\mathrm{ACMG}}+w_{c}R_{\mathrm{confidence}},

(3)

where:

1.

Format Reward (
R_{\mathrm{format}}
)
is a sparse binary reward that checks for correct tag usage and output structure,

2.

Answer Reward (
R_{\mathrm{answer}}
)
is a sparse reward measuring factual alignment between the generated and ground-truth answer choices,

3.

ACMG Reward (
R_{\mathrm{ACMG}}
)
is a dense reward promoting temporal and visual grounding of the predicted plan in future frames (Sec.
3.3.2
), and

4.

Confidence Reward (
R_{\mathrm{confidence}}
)
is a dense reward encouraging coherent and consistent reasoning (Sec.
3.3.3
).

Sparse rewards (
R_{format}
,
R_{answer}
) provide a single pass/fail score for the entire sequence, while our novel dense rewards (
R_{ACMG}
,
R_{confidence}
) provide continuous, stepwise feedback essential for learning complex planning.

GRPO Objective.

Group-level rewards
\{R(Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
are normalized to compute relative advantages:

\hat{A}(Y_{i})=\frac{R(Y_{i})-\mu_{R}}{\sigma_{R}+\epsilon},

(4)

where
\mu_{R}
and
\sigma_{R}
are the mean and standard deviation of the group rewards.
The policy is then updated by minimizing the GRPO objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Big[-\hat{A}(Y)\log\pi_{\theta}(Y\mid V,X)\Big]\\
-\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\theta}(Y\mid V,X)\,||\,\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}(Y\mid V,X)\big),

(5)

where
\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}
limits deviation from the stable SFT policy.

3.3.2
Anticipatory Cross-Modal Grounding (ACMG)

To ensure each generated
<ego_plan>
is temporally predictive and visually grounded, we introduce the
Anticipatory Cross-Modal Grounding (ACMG)
reward. This self-supervised signal measures how well the generated plan anticipates future visual states (Figure
3
).

Mechanism.

Each
<ego_plan>
is divided into semantic clauses
c_{i}
(e.g., “Pick up the knife”). For each clause, we extract its final hidden state
h_{i}
from the LLM and project it into the visual embedding space via a small trainable MLP (
Anticipation Head
) to predict the visual embedding of the next moment
\hat{v}_{i}=\mathrm{MLP}(h_{i}^{\mathrm{text}})
. The Anticipation Head operates on language embeddings already aligned with visual concepts through the MLLM’s pretrained cross-attention layers, enabling direct mapping between textual descriptions (e.g., “knife”) and their corresponding visual representations. We then encode the next
N=16
frames following the current timestamp
t
with the trainable vision encoder to obtain embeddings
\{{v_{t+1},\ldots,v_{t+N}}\}
. The ACMG reward for each clause
c_{i}
measures how closely its predicted feature matches any of these future frames using cosine similarity:

R_{\mathrm{ACMG}}({c_{i}})\;=\;\max_{{n}\in\{{1},\dots,{N}\}}\frac{{\hat{v}_{i}}\cdot{v_{t+n}}}{\|{\hat{v}_{i}}\|\,\|{v_{t+n}}\|}.

(6)

The
\max
operation selects the best-aligned frame (e.g., when the knife enters view), making the reward robust to temporal shifts and irrelevant frames. As shown in Figure
4
, earlier clauses align with near-future frames, while later ones match more distant events.
For a trajectory with
K
clauses
C=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{K}\}
extracted from
<ego_plan>
, the ACMG reward is the mean across all clauses:

R_{\mathrm{ACMG}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\max_{n=1}^{N}\text{sim}(\hat{v}_{i},v_{t+n}).

(7)

3.3.3
Confidence Reward

To improve the logical coherence of
<exo_verify>
, we introduce a
confidence reward
computed from the policy model’s log-probabilities.

Teacher-guided phase.

During the first 200 RL steps, for each sample, each of the
k=8
rollout verifications is compared to a teacher-generated reference verification. This reward guides the model to match the teacher’s reasoning quality:

R_{\mathrm{confidence}}=\beta\cdot\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\text{T}}\mid X)-\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\text{P}}\mid X)\bigr],

(8)

where
y^{\text{T}}
and
y^{\text{P}}
denote teacher and policy outputs, respectively.
We use Qwen2.5-VL-72B
1
as the teacher. This phase helps the model learn stable and coherent verification before self-improvement begins.

Self-ranking phase.

After the warm-up, each of the
k=8
rollout verification is scored using the composite reward (Eq.
3
), and the highest- (
y^{\mathrm{chosen}}
) and lowest-scoring (
y^{\mathrm{rejected}}
) outputs are compared:

R_{\text{confidence}}=\begin{cases}\beta\cdot(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{\text{chosen}}\mid X)\\
\quad-\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{\text{rejected}}\mid X)),&\text{if scores differ;}\\
1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(9)

This adaptive setup first grounds the model in teacher examples, then encourages self-improvement by favoring outputs with higher confidence. The teacher is used only during the early warm-up phase for verification, not for planning.

3.4
Exocentric Regularization

Training solely on egocentric data can lead to
catastrophic forgetting
, degrading the model’s general exocentric understanding. To mitigate this, we periodically interleave GRPO updates with a lightweight exocentric regularization step. Specifically, after every 200 RL iterations, the model is evaluated on a held-out exocentric VideoQA dataset (MSRVTT
39
) using a cross-entropy loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{exo}}
. This loss is combined with the GRPO objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{exo}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{exo}}.

(10)

This multi-task constraint preserves general visual reasoning while allowing specialization to egocentric tasks.

Implementation Details.

We implement EgoVITA using the open-source MS-SWIFT
45
library. In Stage I (SFT), we train on two datasets: EgoProceL
2
(
5k
samples) for planning and HD-EPIC

(
7k
samples) for verification. Chain-of-thought reasoning traces for Stage I (planning and verification) are generated by Qwen2.5-VL-72B
1
using ground-truth annotations. The SFT model is trained for one epoch with a learning rate of
5e-6
. In Stage II (GRPO), we use HD-EPIC and EgoIT (
40k
samples)
40
as rollout sources, setting the policy temperature
\beta=0.1
and the exocentric regularization coefficient
\lambda_{\text{exo}}=0.05
. We apply EgoVITA to three state-of-the-art MLLMs: Qwen2.5-VL-7B
1
, InternVL3.5-8B
36
, and Qwen3-VL-8B. All models are trained on
8
NVIDIA L40S GPUs using DoRA
22
adapters (
r=8
,
\alpha=16
) on the vision encoder, projector, and the LLM. The reward weights are set to
\{w_{f},w_{a},w_{g},w_{c}\}=\{0.1,0.3,0.3,0.3\}
.

(2) the same models after SFT,
(3) the SFT models further trained with GRPO using only final-answer and format rewards, and
(4) state-of-the-art MLLMs for egocentric video understanding.

EgoVITA improves all foundation models.

RQ4: Why does grounding in future frames (ACMG) improve procedural reasoning?

ACMG trains the model to anticipate what will happen next, rather than just describe what is currently visible. This helps the model link each planned action to its future visual outcome, fostering a predictive understanding of how tasks unfold over time.
For comparison,
present-frame grounding
measures similarity between the predicted visual feature and the current frame,
\mathrm{sim}(\hat{v}_{i},v_{t})
, which only encourages the model to describe what is currently visible. In contrast, ACMG connects each plan clause
c_{i}
to future visual states
{v_{t+n}}
by computing
\max_{{n}=1}^{{N}}\mathrm{sim}({\hat{v}_{i},v_{t+n}})
, encouraging the model to predict the visual consequences of its actions and select the best-aligned frame.
As shown in Table
5
, models trained with only present-frame grounding show limited improvement because they focus on describing the current scene rather than learning the causal effects of actions.
ACMG explicitly enforces this prediction; however, visual grounding alone is insufficient: a plan might be predictive but still illogical (e.g., “Grasp the hot pan”). The
<exo_verify>
stage, guided by the confidence reward, corrects such errors by enforcing logical consistency. Together, ACMG and confidence produce plans that are both visually grounded and logically coherent.

5
Conclusion

We presented EgoVITA, a framework that improves egocentric video reasoning by enhancing procedural reasoning. We achieve this by disentangling the task into egocentric planning and exocentric verification. This two-stage approach enables view-invariant learning, yielding strong gains on egocentric benchmarks while maintaining and improving performance on exocentric video tasks. Future work will extend this framework to support streaming, continuous egocentric scenarios.

RQ1: Why Max Instead of Average Pooling for
R_{\mathrm{ACMG}}
?

A valid concern is that
max
pooling (Eq. 6) could reward spurious, high-similarity outliers, whereas a
mean
pooling operation might better capture durational events (e.g., “walk to the bus stop”). In Table
7
, we directly ablate this by replacing
max
pooling with
mean
pooling across the
N=16
future frames. The results show that
mean
pooling is significantly less effective, providing only a fraction of the performance gain (e.g.,
+3.8
vs.
+7.7
on EgoBlind, and
+1.9
vs.
+3.7
on EgoThink).

The
mean
operation’s weakness is that it
dilutes the reward signal
. A correct durational clause like “walk to the bus stop” will have high similarity with
some
, but not necessarily
all
, of the
N=16
future frames. Averaging across the entire window (including the non-matching frames) punishes this correct, but sparse, temporal alignment, resulting in a weak, non-descriptive reward. In contrast,
max
pooling is “robust to temporal shifts” because it finds the
peak evidence
that the predicted event
did
occur within the window, regardless of its exact timing or duration. This provides a much stronger and more stable learning signal for grounding, and the empirical results suggest this benefit outweighs the risk of spurious outliers.

RQ2: Is the teacher-guided warm-up phase necessary for the confidence reward?

Table
8
examines the effect of removing the
200
-step teacher-guided warm-up phase from the confidence reward. Without this initialization, the model relies solely on self-ranking from the start of training. Results show that removing the warm-up phase leads to modest performance drops across all benchmarks:
-1.4
on EgoBlind,
-1.1
on EgoPlan,
-1.4
on EgoThink, and
-1.1
on EOC-Bench. While the model still achieves substantial gains over the baseline without teacher guidance, the warm-up phase provides a useful initialization that helps the model learn stable verification patterns before transitioning to self-improvement.

The teacher-guided phase grounds the model in high-quality reasoning examples, preventing early-stage instability where the model might reinforce suboptimal verification strategies. However, the relatively small performance gap suggests that the self-ranking mechanism is robust enough to learn effective verification even without explicit teacher supervision, indicating that the strong gains stem from the confidence reward structure itself rather than solely from teacher distillation.

N
.

We analyze the sensitivity of
R_{\mathrm{ACMG}}
to
N
, the number of future frames used for grounding, in Table
9
. Performance is clearly sensitive to this hyperparameter. A small window, such as
N=4
or
N=8
, yields lower gains. <ego_plan>
clause. Therefore,
N=16
represents an effective balance, providing a flexible enough window to handle temporal shifts in actions while remaining focused enough to reward precise, causal predictions.
