Relation between two Sinc-collocation methods for Volterra integral equations of the second kind and further improvement Note: This work was supported in part by JSPS KAKENHI Grant-in-Aid for Scientific Research (C), Grant Number JP23K03218.
Tomoaki Okayama

Abstract
Stenger and Rashidinia–Zarebnia independently proposed two Sinc-collocation methods for Volterra integral equations of the second kind, but the relation between the methods has not been clarified. This study reformulates Stenger’s method for general two-variable kernels and rigorously establishes its applicability and convergence. We prove that the approximate functions produced by the two methods are not generally identical, although they coincide at every collocation point. We also provide a rigorous convergence proof for the Rashidinia–Zarebnia method and show that both methods attain the same root-exponential convergence rate. Because Stenger’s method is simpler to implement than the Rashidinia–Zarebnia method, we adopt it as the basis for further improvement. By replacing the tanh transformation with the double-exponential transformation, we develop a new Sinc-collocation method and prove its almost exponential convergence. Numerical examples support the theoretical results and demonstrate the favorable balance between accuracy and computational cost of the proposed method.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with numerical solutions via
Sinc numerical methods
[
19
,
20
]

for Volterra integral equations of the second kind of the form

u(t)-\int_{a}^{t}k(t,s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=g(t),\quad a\leq t\leq b.

(1.1)

Here,
k(t,s)
and
g(t)
are given continuous functions,
and
u(t)
is the solution to be determined.
Equation (
1.1
) is often expressed symbolically
as
(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{V})u=g
by introducing the
Volterra integral operator
\mathcal{V}:C([a,b])\to C([a,b])
defined by

\mathcal{V}[f](t)=\int_{a}^{t}k(t,s)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

One of the powerful tools in Sinc numerical methods,
especially for the target equation (
1.1
),
is the Sinc indefinite integration
[
3
,
6
]
.
It provides an approximation formula for an indefinite integral
of the following form:

\int_{a}^{t}F(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}F(s_{j})\omega_{j}(t),

where the weight
\omega_{j}
depends on
t
, whereas
the sampling point
s_{j}
is
fixed
and independent of
t
,
even though the interval of the integral
(a,\,t)
depends on
t
.
This is a distinctive feature because both
\omega_{j}
and
s_{j}
would
generally depend on
t
if a standard quadrature rule were used to approximate
the indefinite integral.
Another attractive feature is that the Sinc indefinite integration can attain
an
exponential
order of convergence, which is significantly faster
than polynomial-order convergence.
Using these features,
Muhammad et al.
[
7
]

applied the Sinc indefinite integration to approximate
\mathcal{V}
by
\mathcal{V}_{N}

and defined a numerical solution
u_{N}
satisfying

(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{V}_{N})u_{N}=g.

The procedure for obtaining the solution
u_{N}

is called the Sinc-Nyström method,
which is described in Section
3
.
Theoretical analysis has established that the method achieves
a convergence rate of
\Order(\exp(-\sqrt{\piup dN}))

[
11
]
,
where
d
characterizes the size of the domain in which
the solution
u
is analytic.

Another Sinc-based numerical solution for (
1.1
)
was developed by
Rashidinia and Zarebnia
[
17
]
.
They derived their method using a standard collocation procedure
based on the Sinc approximation, a function approximation formula
that also attains an exponential order of convergence.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{N}f
denote the Sinc approximation of
f
.
As shown in this paper, the equation solved by their method
can be written symbolically as

(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}_{N}\mathcal{V}_{N})w_{N}=\mathcal{P}_{N}g.

The procedure for obtaining the solution
w_{N}

is called the Sinc-collocation method,
which is described in Section
4
.
Although a theoretical error analysis of the method was
presented in
[
22
]
,
its convergence was not rigorously proved.

Yet another Sinc-based numerical solution for (
1.1
)
was developed by Stenger
[
18
]
.
Although his method was introduced more than a decade
before the above methods,
it has received considerably less attention.
One possible reason is that his method was originally formulated
for initial value problems

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{k}(t)u(t)+\tilde{g}(t),

\displaystyle u(a)

\displaystyle=u_{a},

which can be reduced to the following Volterra integral equation
of the second kind:

u(t)-\int_{a}^{t}\tilde{k}(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=g(t),

(1.2)

where
g(t)=u_{a}+\int_{a}^{t}\tilde{g}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
.
Because the kernel here (
\tilde{k}
) is a function of a single variable,
Stenger’s original formulation does not appear to cover the general
two-variable kernel in (
1.1
).
Nevertheless, even without a theoretical justification,
his method can be implemented when the kernel is a function of two variables.
Its numerical solution, denoted by
v_{N}
, is determined in two steps:
(i) obtain the Sinc-Nyström solution
u_{N}
,
and (ii) apply the Sinc approximation to
u_{N}
.
Step (i) shows that Stenger’s method is based on the
Sinc-Nyström method; however,
v_{N}
is not equal to
u_{N}
because of
step (ii). The detailed procedure is described in
Section
4
, where an explicit formulation for the general
case (
1.1
) is presented for the first time.
The existing convergence result
[
18
]
assumes that the
kernel is a function of a single variable.

Recent studies show that this diversity of discretization strategies
persists in Sinc-based solvers.
Sinc-Nyström methods have been developed for Fredholm integral equations
on infinite intervals
[
16
]
, whereas a direct
Sinc-collocation approach of the type developed by Rashidinia and Zarebnia
has been applied to Volterra integral equations with proportional
delay
[
4
]
.
Stenger-type Sinc-collocation methods, in which an approximate function is
constructed from nodal values obtained through the corresponding
Sinc-Nyström system,
have also been studied for systems of initial value problems and Fredholm
integral equations
[
13
,
14
]
.
These studies illustrate that different discretizations and basis
constructions continue to be used depending on the target problem.
Although comparisons have been conducted for some problem classes, to the
best of the author’s knowledge, the three formulations have not been
systematically compared for the general Volterra integral
equation (
1.1
).

This lack of a systematic comparison motivates the first objective of this
study. From both theoretical and practical perspectives, we address the
following question:
What are the differences and similarities among
these three methods, and which method is preferable?

We first show that Stenger’s method
can be regarded as another Sinc-collocation method.
We then prove that Stenger’s method and the Rashidinia–Zarebnia method
coincide at the collocation points but are not generally equivalent.
Furthermore, we show that the two Sinc-collocation methods attain exactly
the same convergence rate,

\Order(\sqrt{N}\exp(-\sqrt{\piup d\alpha N}))
,
where
0<\alpha\leq 1
is a Hölder exponent.
From an implementation perspective, Stenger’s method is preferable,
because its implementation is simpler than that of
the Rashidinia–Zarebnia method.

Thus, the remaining comparison is between the Sinc-Nyström method
and Stenger’s Sinc-collocation method.
Although the theoretical convergence rate of Stenger’s method is slightly
slower even when

\alpha=1
, its approximate solution can be evaluated without repeated
evaluations of
k
,
g
, or the sine integral, and the numerical experiments
show lower computational cost for comparable accuracy.
We therefore select Stenger’s formulation as the basis for the improvement
described next.

The second objective of this study is to improve Stenger’s method.
All three methods described above employ the tanh transformation

t=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(x)=\frac{b-a}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+\frac{b+a}{2}

(1.3)

to map
\mathbb{R}
onto the target interval
(a,b)
.
This transformation is required because Sinc numerical methods
are originally defined on the entire real axis
\mathbb{R}
.
For a finite interval, a variable transformation
such as (
1.3
) is therefore necessary.
We aim to improve Stenger’s method by replacing the tanh
transformation with

t=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(x)=\frac{b-a}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\piup}{2}\sinh x\right)+\frac{b+a}{2},

(1.4)

which is called the double-exponential (DE) transformation.
The convergence rates of various Sinc numerical methods have been improved
by replacing the tanh transformation with
the DE transformation
[
5
,
8
,
20
]
.
Specifically, this replacement improves the convergence rate of the
Sinc-Nyström method to

\Order(\log(2dN)\exp(-\piup dN/\log(2dN))/N)

[
11
]
.
Motivated by this result, we develop a new Sinc-collocation method
combined with the DE transformation.
We also analyze the proposed method theoretically and prove that
its convergence rate
is
\Order(\exp(-\piup dN/\log(2dN/\alpha)))
,
which is significantly faster than that of Stenger’s method.
Although this convergence rate is slightly slower than that of the
DE-Sinc-Nyström method, the proposed method avoids sine-integral
evaluation and exhibits a favorable balance between accuracy and
computational cost in the numerical experiments.
The proposed method is developed in two stages: first, Stenger’s formulation
is selected through theoretical and computational comparison; second, its
tanh transformation is replaced by the DE transformation.

The main contributions are fourfold.
First, Stenger’s method is reformulated and rigorously justified for general
two-variable kernels.
Second, its approximate function is shown to coincide with that of the
Rashidinia–Zarebnia method at the collocation points but not in general.
Third, a new RZ approximation operator connects the
Rashidinia–Zarebnia method to the Sinc-Nyström equation and enables a
rigorous convergence proof establishing the same root-exponential rate as
Stenger’s method.
Fourth, the proposed DE-Sinc-collocation method attains almost exponential
convergence without requiring sine-integral evaluation.
The comparison also clarifies the convergence rates, evaluation costs, and
known conditioning properties of the methods, while identifying parameter
selection and the conditioning of the Rashidinia–Zarebnia matrix as issues
requiring further study.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
reviews the Sinc approximation and indefinite
integration, and Section
3
describes the corresponding
Sinc-Nyström methods.
Section
4
compares the existing Sinc-collocation methods,
whereas Section
5
develops the proposed DE method.
Section
6
presents the numerical experiments.
Sections
7
and
8
provide the proofs of the
new theoretical results, and Section
9
concludes the
paper.

2
Preliminaries

This section summarizes the Sinc approximation
and Sinc indefinite integration and their application
with the aid of the tanh or DE transformation.

2.1
Sinc approximation and Sinc indefinite integration

The Sinc numerical methods are generic names of numerical methods
based on the
Sinc approximation
,
expressed as

F(x)\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}F(jh)S(j,h)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1)

where
h
is a mesh size appropriately chosen depending on
N
,
and the basis function
S(j,h)
is the so-called Sinc function
defined by

S(j,h)(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sin(\piup(x-jh)/h)}{\piup(x-jh)/h}&(x\neq jh),\\
1&(x=jh).\end{cases}

Integrating both sides of (
2.1
),
we obtain an approximation formula called
the
Sinc indefinite integration
as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}F(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}F(jh)\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}S(j,h)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{j=-N}^{N}F(jh)J(j,h)(\xi),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R},

(2.2)

where
J(j,h)
is defined by

J(j,h)(x)=h\left\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\piup}\Si\left[\frac{\piup(x-jh)}{h}\right]\right\},

where
\Si(x)
is the sine integral defined by

\Si(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\{(\sin t)/t\}\,\mathrm{d}t
.

2.2
SE-Sinc approximation and SE-Sinc indefinite integration

To use the approximation formulas (
2.1
)
and (
2.2
),
the function
F(x)
must be defined over the entire real line
\mathbb{R}
.
When the function
f(t)
is
defined over the finite interval
(a,b)
,
a variable transformation is required to map
\mathbb{R}

onto
(a,b)
.
For the purpose,
the tanh transformation
t=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(x)
defined in (
1.3
)
is widely employed.
The change of variable (
t=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(x)
) enables us
to apply (
2.1
) by setting
F(x)=f(\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(x))
.
Introducing
t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j}=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(jh)
and
\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)=\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{-1}(t)
,
we express the obtained formula as

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)),\quad t\in(a,b).

(2.3)

This approximation is referred to as the SE-Sinc approximation
in this paper.
Similarly, applying
s=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(x)
and
setting
F(x)=f(\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(x))
in (
2.2
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{-\infty}^{\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)}f(\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(x))\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)J(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)),\quad t\in(a,b),

(2.4)

which is referred to as the SE-Sinc indefinite integration
in this paper.
If
F(x)=f(\psi(x))
is analytic on the strip complex domain

\mathscr{D}_{d}=\left\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|<d\right\}

for a positive constant
d
,
then both approximations performs highly accurately.
In other words,
f(t)
should be analytic on the transformed domain

\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})=\left\{z=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(\zeta):\zeta\in\mathscr{D}_{d}\right\},

which is a simply-connected domain.
Convergence theorems of the two approximations were provided as follows.

Theorem 2.1
(Stenger
[
18
, Theorem 4.2.5]
)

Assume that
f
is analytic on
\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})

for
d
with
0<d<\piup
, and
there exists constants
K
and
\alpha
such that

|f(z)|\leq K|z-a|^{\alpha}|b-z|^{\alpha}

(2.5)

holds for all
z\in\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})
. Let
N
be a positive integer,
and let
h
be selected by the formula

h=\sqrt{\frac{\piup d}{\alpha N}}.

(2.6)

Then, there exists a constant
C
independent of
N
such that

\max_{t\in[a,b]}\left|f(t)-\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t))\right|\leq C\sqrt{N}\rme^{-\sqrt{\piup d\alpha N}}.

Theorem 2.2
(Okayama et al.
[
10
, Theorem 2.9]
)

Assume that
f
is analytic on
\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})

for
d
with
0<d<\piup
, and
there exists constants
K
and
\alpha
such that

|f(z)|\leq K|z-a|^{\alpha-1}|b-z|^{\alpha-1}

(2.7)

holds for all
z\in\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})
. Let
N
be a positive integer,
and let
h
be selected by the formula (
2.6
).
Then, there exists a constant
C
independent of
N
such that

\max_{t\in[a,b]}\left|\int_{a}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)J(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t))\right|\leq C\rme^{-\sqrt{\piup d\alpha N}}.

2.3
DE-Sinc approximation and DE-Sinc indefinite integration

The SE-Sinc approximation (
2.3
)
and SE-Sinc indefinite integration (
2.4
)
employ the tanh transformation (
1.3
) to
map
\mathbb{R}
onto the finite interval
(a,b)
.
The DE transformation (
1.4
) also plays the same role,
and allows for the replacement of
\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
with
\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}
in both formulas.
On the basis of this idea,
introducing
t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{j}=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(jh)
and
\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t)=\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}\}^{-1}(t)
,
we can derive the following formulas

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{j})S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t)),\quad t\in(a,b),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=-N}^{N}f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)J(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t)),\quad t\in(a,b),

(2.9)

which are referred to as the DE-Sinc approximation
and DE-Sinc indefinite integration, respectively.
For the formulas (
2.8
)
and (
2.9
),

f(t)
should be analytic on the transformed domain

\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})=\left\{z=\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(\zeta):\zeta\in\mathscr{D}_{d}\right\},

which forms a Riemann surface.
Convergence theorems of the two approximations were provided as follows.

(Tanaka et al.

)

Assume that
f

\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})

K
and

2.5
)

(Okayama et al.
[

, Theorem 2.16]
)

Assume that
f

\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d})

)

2.4
Generalized SE/DE-Sinc approximation

In the convergence theorems of the SE/DE-Sinc approximation,
the condition (
2.5
) is assumed.
This condition requires
f(t)
to be zero at the endpoints
t=a
and
t=b
,
which seems an impractical condition.
To address this issue,
using auxiliary functions

\omega_{a}(t)=\frac{b-t}{b-a},\quad\omega_{b}(t)=\frac{t-a}{b-a},

and setting

\tilde{f}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N}(t)=f(t)-f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t)-f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t)
,
Stenger
[
18
,
19
]

proposed to apply the SE-Sinc approximation to
\tilde{f}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N}
.
If we define an approximation operator
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}:C([a,b])\to C([a,b])
as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}f)(t)=f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t)+f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t)+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\tilde{f}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)),

(2.11)

then the approximation is expressed as
f\approx\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}f
.
This approximation is referred to as the generalized SE-Sinc approximation
in this paper.
Notably,
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
satisfies
the interpolation property, that is,

f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{i})=(\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}f)(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{i})
(
i=-N,\,\ldots,\,N
).

Similarly, setting

\tilde{f}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{N}(t)=f(t)-f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t)-f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t)
,
we may apply the DE-Sinc approximation to
\tilde{f}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{N}
.
If we define an approximation operator
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}:C([a,b])\to C([a,b])
as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}f)(t)=f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t)+f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t)+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\tilde{f}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{N}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{j})S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t)),

(2.12)

then the approximation is expressed as
f\approx\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}f
.
This approximation is referred to as the generalized DE-Sinc approximation
in this paper.

\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}
also satisfies
the interpolation property, that is,

f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{i})=(\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}f)(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{i})
(
i=-N,\,\ldots,\,N
).

The convergence theorems of the two approximations are described
using the following function spaces.

Definition 2.1

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a bounded and simply-connected domain
(or Riemann surface).
Then,
\mathbf{H}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D})
denotes the family of functions
f

analytic on
\mathscr{D}
such that the norm
\|f\|_{\mathbf{H}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D})}
is finite,
where

\|f\|_{\mathbf{H}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D})}=\sup_{z\in\mathscr{D}}|f(z)|.

Definition 2.2

Let
\alpha
be a positive constant, and
let
\mathscr{D}
be a bounded and simply-connected domain
(or Riemann surface) that satisfies
(a,b)\subset\mathscr{D}
.
Then,
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\mathscr{D})
denotes the family of functions
f\in\mathbf{H}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D})

for which there exists a constant
L
such that for all
z\in\mathscr{D}
,

\displaystyle|f(z)-f(a)|

\displaystyle\leq L|z-a|^{\alpha},

\displaystyle|f(b)-f(z)|

\displaystyle\leq L|b-z|^{\alpha}.

For a two-variable function
k
, we say that
k(\cdot,w)
belongs to

\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\mathscr{D})
uniformly with respect to
w\in\mathscr{D}
if there exists
a constant
L_{k}
independent of
w
such that, for all
z,w\in\mathscr{D}
,

\displaystyle|k(z,w)-k(a,w)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{k}|z-a|^{\alpha},

\displaystyle|k(b,w)-k(z,w)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{k}|b-z|^{\alpha}.

This function space
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\mathscr{D})
only requires
the Hölder continuity at the endpoints instead of
the zero-boundary condition by (
2.5
).
Convergence theorems of the two approximations were provided as follows.
Here,
\|\cdot\|_{C([a,b])}
denotes the usual uniform norm over
[a,b]
.

Theorem 2.5
(Okayama
[
12
, Theorem 3]
)

Assume that
f\in\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
for
d
with
0<d<\piup
.
Let
N
be a positive integer,
and let

4
Existing Sinc-collocation methods

This section describes two different Sinc-collocation methods
developed by
Stenger
[
18
]

and Rashidinia and Zarebnia
[
17
]
.
Both methods employ the tanh transformation (
1.3
)
as a variable transformation,
but their procedures are not identical.

4.1
Sinc-collocation method by Stenger

As explained in Section
1
,
Stenger derived his method for (
1.2
),
where the kernel
\tilde{k}
is a function of a single variable.
However, his method can be easily derived for (
1.1
)
as follows.
His method is closely related to the SE-Sinc-Nyström method,
which is described in the previous section.
First, solve the linear system (
3.3
)
and obtain
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
.
Then, his approximated solution
v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
is expressed as
the generalized SE-Sinc approximation of
u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}](t)

\displaystyle=u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t)+u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t)+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\left\{u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})-u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})-u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\right\}S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)),

(4.1)

where
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
is defined by (
2.11
).

The solution
v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
is also obtained
by the standard collocation procedure as follows.
Set the approximate solution
v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
as (
4.1
),
where
u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})

(j=-N,\,\ldots,\,N)
are
regarded as unknown coefficients.
Substitute
v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
into the given equation (
1.1
),
with approximating the Volterra integral operator
\mathcal{V}
by
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
, where

\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[f](t)=\sum_{j=-N}^{N}k(t,t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})f(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)J(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)),

(4.2)

which is the SE-Sinc indefinite integration of
\mathcal{V}f
.
Then, setting
n=2N+1
sampling points at

(i=-N,\,-N+1,\,\ldots,\,N)

Thus, Stenger’s method can be regarded as a collocation method

4.2
Sinc-collocation method by Rashidinia and Zarebnia

Rashidinia and Zarebnia derived their method
by the standard collocation procedure,
but they considered their approximated solution
w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}

in different manners in the following four cases.

(I)

If
u(a)=u(b)=0
, set
w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
as

w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}(t)=\sum_{j=-N}^{N}c_{j}S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)).

(II)

If
u(a)\neq 0
and
u(b)=0
, set
w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
as

w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}(t)=c_{-N}\omega_{a}(t)+\sum_{j=-N+1}^{N}c_{j}S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t)).

(III)

If
u(a)=0
and
u(b)\neq 0
, set
w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
as

w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}(t)=\sum_{j=-N}^{N-1}c_{j}S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t))+c_{N}\omega_{b}(t).

(IV)

If
u(a)\neq 0
and
u(b)\neq 0
, set
w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
as

w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}(t)=c_{-N}\omega_{a}(t)+\sum_{j=-N+1}^{N-1}c_{j}S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}(t))+c_{N}\omega_{b}(t).

(4.3)

To obtain the unknown coefficients

\boldsymbol{c}_{n}=[c_{-N},\,c_{-N+1},\,\ldots,\,c_{N}]^{\mathrm{T}}
,
where
n=2N+1
,
they substituted
w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
into the given equation (
1.1
),
with approximating the Volterra integral operator
\mathcal{V}
by
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}
.
Then, setting
n
sampling points at
t=t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{i}

(i=-N,\,-N+1,\,\ldots,\,N)
,
they derived a system of linear equations
in each of the four cases: (I)–(IV).
For example, in the case (I), the resulting system is expressed as

(I_{n}-V_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}})\boldsymbol{c}_{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}.

Particularly, in the case (IV), the resulting system is expressed as

(E_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}-V_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}})\boldsymbol{c}_{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}},

(4.4)

where
E_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
and
V_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
are

n\times n
matrices defined by

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{\,}c|ccc|c@{\,}}\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})&0&\cdots&0&\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})\\
\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N+1})&1&&0&\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N+1})\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N-1})&0&&1&\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N-1})\\
\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})&0&\cdots&0&\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle V_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{\,}l|clc|l@{\,}}&\cdots&k(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N},t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)h\delta_{-N-j}^{(-1)}&\cdots&\\
&\cdots&k(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N+1},t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)h\delta_{-N+1-j}^{(-1)}&\cdots&\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\boldsymbol{p}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@intercol&&\lx@intercol\hfil\vdots\hfil\lx@intercol&&\lx@intercol\hfil\boldsymbol{q}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
&\cdots&k(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N-1},t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)h\delta_{N-1-j}^{(-1)}&\cdots&\\
&\cdots&k(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N},t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{j})\{\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}\}^{\prime}(jh)h\delta_{N-j}^{(-1)}&\cdots&\end{array}\right],

where
\boldsymbol{p}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}

and
\boldsymbol{q}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}

are
n
-dimensional vectors defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[\omega_{a}](t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N}),\,\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[\omega_{a}](t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N+1}),\,\ldots,\,\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[\omega_{a}](t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})]^{\mathrm{T}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[\omega_{b}](t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N}),\,\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[\omega_{b}](t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N+1}),\,\ldots,\,\mathcal{V}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}[\omega_{b}](t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})]^{\mathrm{T}}.

4.3

Any relation between Stenger’s method
(v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}})

and Rashidinia–Zarebnia’s method
(w_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}})

Furthermore, convergence of the two methods has not been rigorously proved.
As a first contribution of this paper,
we show the relation between the two methods as follows.
The proof is provided in Section

.

Theorem 4.2

Let

Remark 4.1

In view of Theorems
4.1

and
4.4
,
one might assume that Theorem
4.4

is proved by bounding
\|(I_{n}-V_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}})^{-1}\|_{2}
uniformly for
N
.
However, this is not the case; see Section
7
for details.

Theorems
4.3
and
4.4

reveal that both methods achieve the same convergence rate.
Therefore, users may prefer Stenger’s method
because the implementation of the method by Rashidinia and Zarebnia
is rather complicated.
This complication also causes difficulty in extension to the
system
of
Volterra integral equations.

Regarding numerical stability,
the condition number of the coefficient matrix
(I_{n}-V_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}})

in the infinity norm is known to be uniformly bounded with respect to
N

under the assumptions for the SE-Sinc-Nyström
method
[
11
, Theorem 3.7]
.
Because Stenger’s SE-Sinc-collocation method uses exactly the same
coefficient matrix, it inherits this uniform well-conditioning.
By contrast, the Rashidinia–Zarebnia method uses a different coefficient
matrix
(E_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}-V_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}})
; therefore, the corresponding
boundedness does not follow directly from the existing result.

For this reason, in the next section,
we consider the improvement of Stenger’s method.

5
Sinc-collocation method combined with the DE transformation

The SE-Sinc-collocation method described in the previous section
employs the tanh transformation as a variable transformation.
In this section, a new method is derived
by replacing the tanh transformation with the DE transformation.
Then, its convergence theorem is stated.

5.1
Derivation of the DE-Sinc-collocation method

First, solve the linear system (
3.5
)
and obtain
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}
.
Then, the approximated solution
v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}
is expressed as
the generalized DE-Sinc approximation of
u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle v_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}[u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}](t)

\displaystyle=u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t)+u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t)+\sum_{j=-N}^{N}\left\{u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{j})-u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{j})-u_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{N})\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}_{j})\right\}S(j,h)(\phi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(t)),

(5.1)

where
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}
is defined by (
2.12
).
This procedure is referred to as the DE-Sinc-collocation method.

5.2
Main result 2: Convergence of the DE-Sinc-collocation method

In this paper,
we show the convergence of the DE-Sinc-collocation method
as follows.
The proof is provided in Section
8
.

Theorem 5.1

Let
\alpha
and
d
be positive constants with

\alpha\leq 1
and
d<\piup/2
.
Assume that
the assumptions on
g
and
k
in Theorem
3.2

are satisfied.
Furthermore, assume that
g\in\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))
and that

belongs to
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha}(\psi^{\text{\tiny{\rm{DE}}}}(\mathscr{D}_{d}))

).

the coefficient matrix

Compared to Theorems

and

Remark 7.1

The denominator of

and

\displaystyle\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})-\omega_{b}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{-N})\omega_{a}(t^{\text{\tiny{\rm{SE}}}}_{N})

9
Conclusions

This study clarified the relation between two independently developed
Sinc-collocation methods for Volterra integral equations of the second kind.
Stenger’s method was reformulated and theoretically justified for general
two-variable kernels.
It was then proved that the approximate functions generated by Stenger’s
method and the Rashidinia–Zarebnia method are not generally identical,
even though they coincide at every collocation point.
Furthermore, a rigorous convergence proof was established for the
Rashidinia–Zarebnia method, showing that both methods attain the same
root-exponential convergence rate. The essential step was the introduction
of the projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{\text{\tiny{\rm{RZ}}}}
, which expresses the
Rashidinia–Zarebnia method in operator form and connects it to the
Sinc-Nyström equation.

The comparison provides a practical criterion for selecting a method.
Among the two SE-Sinc-collocation methods, Stenger’s method is preferable
because it is simpler to implement, while having the same
convergence rate as the Rashidinia–Zarebnia method.
The Sinc-Nyström methods have slightly faster theoretical convergence
rates with respect to
N
, but evaluating their approximate solutions
requires repeated evaluations of
k
,
g
, and the sine integral and can
therefore be relatively expensive.
The proposed DE-Sinc-collocation method avoids this evaluation cost and
improves the convergence rate from root-exponential to almost exponential.
Furthermore, Stenger’s SE-Sinc-collocation method and the proposed
DE-Sinc-collocation method inherit the uniform well-conditioning established
for the corresponding Sinc-Nyström coefficient matrices because they use
the same linear systems. The Rashidinia–Zarebnia method uses a different
coefficient matrix, for which an analogous bound is not currently available.

The numerical results support these theoretical and computational
comparisons. The two SE-Sinc-collocation methods exhibit nearly identical
accuracy and computational performance, whereas the collocation methods are
generally more efficient than the corresponding Sinc-Nyström methods when
accuracy is compared against computation time. The oscillatory-kernel
example shows that larger values of
N
are required as the frequency
increases, but the DE-Sinc methods maintain substantially higher accuracy
than the SE-Sinc methods over the tested frequency range. These results
indicate that the proposed method is particularly suitable when high accuracy
and repeated evaluation of the approximate solution are required.

summarizes these characteristics.

The rigorous error estimates rely on analyticity and endpoint regularity in
appropriate transformed domains, and practical implementation requires
estimates of the regularity parameters used to determine the mesh size.
Moreover, the numerical experiments in this study were conducted for three
representative equations using double-precision arithmetic.
Further work should investigate automatic parameter selection and
roundoff-error effects for all the methods, the conditioning of the
Rashidinia–Zarebnia coefficient matrix, and performance for more challenging
problems, including higher-frequency oscillatory kernels and weakly singular
kernels.
