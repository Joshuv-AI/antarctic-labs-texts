Qualitative evidence of service user experiences and perspectives on long-acting injectable buprenorphine for opioid treatment: protocol for a scoping review
Authors: Charles Henderson, Shelley Walker, Zachery Lloyd, Daisy Gibbs, Lorena Romero, Mark A Stoové
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere is substantial literature on opioid pharmacotherapies from a service user perspective, mostly focused on methadone and buprenorphine. However, as a relatively new formulation, much less is known about user experiences and views of Long-Acting Injectable Buprenorphine (LAIB). To date, no published reviews have qualitatively described LAIB service user experience. The objective of this scoping review is to identify and describe the peer-reviewed qualitative studies examining the service user experience of LAIB.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will follow the methodology described by Arksey and O’Malley, further adapted by Levac et al and others. The search strategy will be developed in collaboration with an expert librarian and will include searches of Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus and Web of Science as well as citation chaining. The search dates will range from 2016, when LAIB was first approved, to the date of the final search. Included studies will investigate the perspectives and experiences of LAIB treatment participants with opioid dependence, within an Opioid Treatment Programme (OTP). For service users, the pharmacological formulation of LAIB significantly reshapes both the clinical practice and the social dynamics within OTPs. To effectively capture the depth and nuance of service users’ experiences, this review will be limited to qualitative, mixed-method, case series or case study designs, with a further restriction to peer-reviewed articles published in English. Identified references, with duplicates removed, will be imported into Covidence for title/abstract screening, full-text review and data extraction by two independent reviewers. All decisions on data inclusion or exclusion will be by consensus agreement, using a third reviewer if necessary. A living experience researcher will lead analysis of manuscript content in NVivo using inductive coding to produce a descriptive thematic analysis. The narrative summary will present key study characteristics, quality appraisal and synthesised findings that describe service user experiences and perspectives of LAIB, guided by the Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis for Scoping Reviews checklist.Ethics and disseminationThis scoping review undertakes secondary analysis of data in the public domains only and thus does not require ethics approval given no participants are directly involved. The results of the review will be published in a peer-reviewed journal, presented at relevant academic and community conferences, and discussed and disseminated to/with community organisations, service users and policy-makers.Data statementPrepublication history and additional supplemental material for this paper are available online. Introduction

Opioid dependence

Opioid dependence is a chronic condition in which individuals have difficulty reducing or ceasing opioid use despite a motivation to do so.1 In 2017, an estimated 40.5 million people worldwide were affected by opioid dependence.2 Individuals do not necessarily develop opioid dependence(For the purposes of this review, we have used the ICD-11 (International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision) classification published by the WHO, which determines diagnosis based on substance use (eg, opioids), measures severity based on functional impact criteria, is global and public health oriented and is referenced widely in international healthcare literature.) once they have begun using opioids.3 Those that do develop opioid dependence can experience long-term cycles of use and periods of partial or no use, which can sometimes be supported by opioid dependence treatment.47

The progression from opioid use to dependence is shaped by an interplay of biopsychosocial factors, including genetics, life adversity, mental health and social constructs.8 Wider structural and political factors also play a role, including the impact of drug policy, law enforcement practices, healthcare access and stigma and discrimination.9 The criminalisation of illicit opioid use further complicates this cycle and can lead to arrest and incarceration, which exacerbates harm.3 6 These deeply interconnected environmental determinants influence the extent, opportunity and progression of drug-using behaviours throughout an individual’s life journey, profoundly shaping opportunities and constraints around health and quality of life.8 10

The role of pharmacological opioid treatment

Methadone is a full agonist opioid with a long duration of action.11 Mostly taken orally as a liquid, it has been prescribed for opioid dependence since the 1960s.1214 Buprenorphine is a partial agonist, originally introduced for opioid dependence in France in 1995 primarily as a harm reduction measure.15 It is now available as a sublingual tablet, wafer, film, transdermal patch and in injectable and implant versions.13 16 Both drugs are established, safe, effective treatments for opioid dependence,1720 providing wide-ranging benefits in health and social outcomes.21 Recognised as essential medicines by the WHO, methadone and buprenorphine are considered the gold standard first-line treatment options for opioid dependence.12 2224

Key differences in the pharmacological properties of methadone and buprenorphine influence service user decision-making and acceptability regarding which pharmacotherapy to choose.19 Buprenorphine, unlike methadone, has a ceiling effect,25 whereby increasing the dosage beyond a certain point does not produce additional effects, particularly in terms of euphoria and respiratory depression/risk of overdose.26 27 Buprenorphine has a strong affinity for the mu-opioid receptor, which allows it to displace full agonists such as heroin or methadone, carrying the risk of precipitated withdrawal.28 Despite its tight receptor binding and prolonged action, buprenorphine has lower intrinsic activity and potency compared with full agonists.28 These pharmacological characteristics are significant to service users as buprenorphine is a partial agonist and exerts a weaker opioid effect.19 This can affect service user expectations and their subjective experience of buprenorphine relative to the effects of a full agonist such as methadone.29 30 Additionally, its strong receptor affinity prevents the euphoric effects from other opioids, further influencing user preferences.28 30 Conversely, buprenorphine typically offers a less severe withdrawal experience compared with methadone, another factor service users consider important when selecting a treatment medication.19 27 28 31 32

The effectiveness of Opioid Treatment Programmes (OTPs) is dependent largely on treatment retention. Two Cochrane Reviews have confirmed methadone is superior overall in relation to retention; however, the difference in efficacy is small.19 31 Given the pharmacological differences between the two drugs, and the importance of retention, service user preferences could and should play a crucial role in decision making and treatment selection.33 34 Although service users are seeking more control and flexibility in relation to opioid treatment, they often experience attitudes, policies and practices that minimise their centrality in the treatment journey and diminish their capacity for agency and therapeutic choice.33 35 36 This is further compounded by what Bartoszko refers to as ‘the polluting pharmacological atmosphere’ effect37 whereby the deeply negative social and moral meanings attached to OTP medications come to affect everything about the way treatment is delivered and experienced, including how the medications are provided, how service users are treated, and how people on OTP see themselves.38 In this context, the therapeutic relationship or alliance becomes a key feature in minimising these negative effects through a genuine partnership built on mutual respect, trust, openness and shared decision-making.3944

Evidence indicates that the most effective OTPs offer collaborative, individualised, non-judgemental person-centred care,23 prioritising service user needs, goals and choices to reduce stigma and treatment pessimism.45 Rigid and restrictive policies and frameworks create onerous and alienating conditions and limit success measures such as engagement, retention and sustainability.11 44 46

The introduction of long-acting injectable buprenorphine

The introduction of long-acting injectable buprenorphine (LAIB) has been described as a game changer in opioid treatment,1316 4750 offering an alternative to daily/regular therapeutic dosing which has been identified as a barrier for treatment retention.134749 LAIB is a subcutaneous (depot) injection that delivers a sustained release of buprenorphine over an extended period.5154 Several novel formulations were first approved around the same time in 2016 and 2017, including sustained-release buprenorphine implants (marketed as Probuphine in 2016)—delivered every 6 months; extended-release LAIB formulations in 2017—delivered monthly (marketed as Sublocade); or weekly/monthly (marketed as Buvidal in Australia/Europe and Brixadi in USA).40 55 56

While these injection and implant technologies have been heralded as advancements in ‘addiction treatment’, mostly because they eliminate the need for daily/frequent dispensing, their relative long-term effectiveness remains unclear.40 Maintaining a controlled, steady state, level of buprenorphine51 52 54 removes the burden of frequent clinic or pharmacy visits, potentially improving retention.5661 Conversely, in 2024, a large US study reported service users preferring oral medications and, if it were possible, monthly pick-ups from a pharmacy, eschewing implants and injections despite the seemingly advantageous, more infrequent need for pharmacy dosing or service engagement.36

Some studies suggest LAIB is associated with improved quality of life, increased employment opportunities and higher overall treatment satisfaction.48 56 60 62 63 Users of LAIB describe pragmatic benefits such as increased freedom in their own lives, greater opportunities to decide what to do with their time, the ability to travel unencumbered by dosing (frequency and place), as well as psychological improvements through increased sense of ‘normality’, a reduced sense of stigma and a shift in self-perception.48 62 64 65

Despite these reported benefits, some unintended social and physical effects persist,4563 64 6668 with discordance between service providers and users’ measures of ‘success’ or ‘failure’ often arising from differing treatment expectations and goals.69 Dosing restrictions for methadone lead to life disruptions for many, including impediments to gaining and maintaining employment, travelling and responding to important life events.70 Although LAIB seemingly offers greater freedoms from rigid service requirements, service users also report feeling disconnected, less engaged in their treatment journey and ambivalent towards this treatment option.64 65

Rationale for the scoping review

There is universal acknowledgement in social science literature supporting the need for service user-centred care in OTPs, including addressing stigma and illicit drug use within an inherently criminalised sociopolitical framework.71 Qualitative studies are essential for exploring the complex interplay of sociopolitical, medical, individual and cultural factors that shape people’s experiences and for guiding person-centred models of care.72 73 Furthermore, public health frameworks acknowledge improved opioid treatment adherence and retention outcomes when client agency occurs as part of a connected, participatory therapeutic alliance.20 54 When assessing the emerging option of LAIB within the opioid treatment landscape, the potential benefit for service providers to integrate individualised treatment approaches and service user goals within public health and public policy actions remains important.45

Objective

This paper outlines the protocol for a scoping review that will identify and describe the peer-reviewed qualitative studies examining the service user experience of LAIB.

The objectives of this review are to: 1/systematically describe and synthesise findings from qualitative research on the user experience of LAIB within the broader opioid treatment framework; and 2/describe the breadth and depth of existing qualitative evidence, assess its potential to inform policy and practice, and identify gaps to inform future research priorities.

Methods and analysis

To answer the research question effectively and balance rigour, relevance and feasibility,74 the scoping review will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR),75 along with guidance provided by the Joanna Briggs Institute.7679 These tools build on the original framework by Arksey and O’Malley.80

We have developed this a priori scoping review protocol to guide the direction and conduct of the review process. This protocol outlines key components of the scoping review, including the search strategy, databases, eligibility criteria and methods, to ensure a staged, structured and transparent approach.78 81 82 A priori establishment and adherence to processes described in this protocol will reduce bias, minimise risk of arbitrary decisions, avoid unintended duplication of existing reviews, maximise author collaboration and improve the detection of selective reporting.78 Throughout the review, we will use an iterative and reflexive approach that is flexible and adaptive.83

We will use the PRISMA-ScR Checklist and PRISMA Flowchart best practice84 to supplement the protocol and review development.78 85 Once all database searches have been completed and duplicates removed, identified papers will be uploaded into EndNote (V.X9). Covidence software will be used for screening papers, progressing from titles and abstracts to full-text review and data extraction of key study characteristics.85 Findings from included studies will be extracted using NVivo V.14, coding the data into categories and themes to perform a descriptive qualitative analysis.78 86 An optimised qualitative Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) tool87 in Microsoft Excel, as described by Long et al,72 will be used for quality assessment of the included papers.

An important feature of the review is the first author’s living experience with OTP.88 By integrating the first author’s positionality, we aim to enhance the relevance, validity and potential impact of the review.89 90 There is a growing body of literature by researchers examining topics connected to their own lives, which highlights the value of ‘insider’ perspectives and situated knowledge sourced within that journey.91

The protocol has been registered through Open Science Framework https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/XMHKN.

Stage 1: identifying the research question

The basis of all scoping reviews is to investigate and describe particular concepts or topics by answering research questions (cf. traditional systematic reviews), collating and succinctly describing the evidence and summarising what is discovered.7478 80 9296

Scoping reviews answer broad questions to map the current literature and identify gaps in research or current understanding.81 82 This scoping review will map the current qualitative literature and identify gaps in relation to service user perspectives and experiences of LAIB within the context of OTPs and all the formulations currently offered.

The research question for this review is:

What qualitative evidence exists on the experience of people previously or currently prescribed LAIB as part of their opioid treatment journey that can be used to guide policy and practice, and what key considerations remain unexplored?

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

A search strategy will be developed in collaboration with an expert librarian.86 A Boolean search strategy, tailored for Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus and Web of Science, will be implemented. In addition, citation chain searching will be conducted to identify further relevant studies.97

To develop the search strategy, CH will conduct a preliminary literature search using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and relevant key words. LR and CH will review each iteration of the search results and adjust keywords, subject headings and search strings as needed, checking them against seed articles to ensure the development of a verified and robust search strategy.75 85 86 94 LR will re-run the searches to further refine the strategy and adapt the searches to each database to account for differences in indexing and search functionality. All papers identified and retrieved in the final search strategy will be combined into one Endnote library, with duplicates removed, for screening in Covidence.86

The primary search strategy will be global in scope, designed to capture qualitative studies of LAIB across all settings. Given the variability in how user experiences and perceptions are described, the search strategy will include a broad range of terms to maximise sensitivity and avoid missing relevant evidence in this emerging area.

Stage 3: study selection and eligibility criteria

To guide the study selection, we will follow Peters et al 78 adaptation of Participants-Intervention-Comparator-Outcomes (PICO) to determine the research question and study eligibility criteria by Population-Concept-Context (PCC) and Study Design and Other (SO). This approach will allow for an effective review conducted with appropriate sensitivity and specificity within a resource-limited environment.86 97 Eligibility criteria are summarised in table 1 below.

Stage 4: extracting and charting the data

Two reviewers will independently extract the data. The review team will develop a standardised extraction template based on the scoping review research question and objectives. The extraction tool will be independently piloted by two reviewers.78 86 The non-intervention PCC (scoping) framework will be constructed in Covidence via their customisable extraction tool, and the results will be downloaded into our template data extraction instrument.85 86 Study characteristics will be tabulated to summarise their key features.86 Items to be collected will include author/s, year of publication, origin/country, study design and analysis/methodology, population characteristics and key findings.98 The table will capture the essential characteristics of each study by summarising participant involvement, research methods and conclusions.99

CH will conduct a systematic appraisal guided by the modified CASP qualitative checklist.72 The CASP tool is user-friendly and widely used in the methodological quality appraisal of the qualitative evidence in health-related research.72 87 It facilitates a rigorous and transparent evaluation of qualitative studies, including considering their theoretical, ontological and epistemological foundations,88 along with improving the interpretability of our results.92 Selection of the CASP tool also reflects the review’s objectives, the team’s expertise and available resources.72 97

Stage 5: collating and summarising the results

CH will conduct a qualitative thematic analysis of the results section of the included papers.86 99 NVivo V.14 will be used to manage and organise the data.81 The process will begin with open, inductive coding to identify key descriptive patterns across the studies, which will then be systematically identified, collated and synthesised into higher order themes relevant to the objectives of our scoping review.99 The coding process will be reflexive, recognised as inherently subjective, generated and mediated by the interaction of the data and the researcher, and geared towards the organic and evolving shaping of a reflective development process in the production of themes.100 Aligned with our aims and objectives, we anticipate generating key outcomes of study findings related to LAIB service user values and preferences including experiences with service providers, contextual factors (eg, individual, structural, interpersonal, community, societal) and service model characteristics, all of which influence treatment choice, acceptability and satisfaction. Any disagreements will be addressed through team (MAS, SW, CH, ZL and DG) discussion and consensus.74 99 101

Our thematic synthesis of qualitative study findings will be presented via a narrative summary,102104 providing context, commentary, substance and interpretation in the mapping of the literature.99 103 105 Taking the quality assessment into account, the review will focus on gathering relevant information to answer the research question,102 rather than testing theory or synthesising study findings.104106 A PRISMA checklist (see online supplemental table 1) and PRISMA flowchart and at least one example of the database search strategy will be produced as part of the outputs of the scoping review.75 78

Reporting the results

Findings will be disseminated through journal publication and conference presentations and to the community via appropriate communication pathways (eg, brief accessible summary reports). The final dissemination materials will be determined by consensus and tailored to the needs and interests of each audience, with community being a key focus.

Ethics and dissemination

As this is secondary research in which no data will be drawn from participants, ethics is not necessary. Once the review is nearing completion, CH will engage in consultation with community and peer experts to inform the general and targeted dissemination of these review findings. The results of the review will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at academic and community conferences.
