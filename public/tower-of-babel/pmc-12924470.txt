Salicylic acid enhances photosynthesis in Borago officinalis plants exposed to water deficit by nitric oxide-mediated boosting carbonic anhydrase and antioxidant system
Authors: Rayhaneh Amooaghaie, Zahra Nikram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the role of the interplay between salicylic acid (SA) and nitric oxide (NO) in modulating the antioxidant system of plants is well-documented, their combined effect on carbonic anhydrase (CA) activity has not yet been studied in any plants. To fill this gap, the current study for the first time, explored the impact of interplay between SA and sodium nitroprusside (SNP as a NO donor), on CA activity, photosynthetic efficiency, and the antioxidant defense system in Borago officinalis under water-deficit conditions. Water deficit decreased fresh and dry weight of plants, relative water content, chlorophyll contents, the Fv/Fm ratio and CA activity while increasing H2O2 and malondialdehyde levels in leaves. Foliar spraying with SA (0.5 mM), and SNP (100 µM), alone and particularly in combination, increased endogenous NO levels in leaves. This led to improvements in growth parameters, relative water content, chlorophyll a and b contents, photosynthetic efficiency, and CA activity under water deficit conditions. Additionally, these treatments reduced H2O2 and malondialdehyde levels, by boosting the activities of catalase, superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, and ascorbate peroxidase, and promoting proline accumulation in leaves. The application of a NO scavenger (cPTIO) negated the beneficial effects of SA, underscoring the role of NO in SA-induced enhancements of CA activity, photosynthetic efficiency, and antioxidant defense system. This research provides novel insights into the interplay between SA, NO, and CA activity in modulating photosynthesis in a C3 plant. Furthermore, it underscores the potential of combined SA and SNP application as an effective strategy to enhance the sustainable productivity of medicinal plants in arid regions.Keywords: CA activity, Hormone, Photosynthetic efficiency, Oxidative stress, Proline, Sodium nitroprusside

Introduction

Global warming poses numerous challenges, one of which is the intensification of drought stress. This issue is particularly pronounced in arid and semi-arid regions, where persistent declines in precipitation exacerbate water scarcity [1, 2]. Under limited water conditions, plants experience osmotic and oxidative stresses, which disrupt almost all physiological processes [3, 4]. Water deficit (WD) leads to stomatal closure, reduced chlorophyll content, impaired ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP) activity, and diminished photosystem II (PSII) efficiency and carbon dioxide (CO₂) assimilation, resulting in decreased photosynthesis, and biomass production [1]. Plants respond to WD by stomatal closure, osmotic adjustment, the activation of the antioxidant system, and the modulation of photosynthetic and enzymatic activities [3–5]. Carbonic anhydrase (CAs) are the enzymes that catalyze the reaction of reversible hydration of CO₂ to bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), contributing to photosynthesis [6]. The function of these enzymes enables plants to transport CO₂ from the atmosphere in the form of HCO3− across both the plasma membrane and the chloroplast envelope membrane, supporting the efficient delivery of inorganic carbon into the chloroplast. On the other hand, CAs catalyze the conversion of HCO3− into CO₂ within the chloroplast stroma, ensuring an optimal supply of CO₂ for Rubisco, the key enzyme responsible for carbon fixation during photosynthesis. By supporting these essential processes, CAs are integral to the overall efficiency of photosynthetic carbon assimilation [7]. While much of the research has traditionally focused on the function of CAs in the photosynthetic pathways of C4 plants, recent studies have highlighted the presence of their multiple isoforms including αCAs and βCAs in C3 plants [7, 8]. These isoforms are distributed across different cellular organelles, including the chloroplasts, cytosol, and mitochondria, rendering multifaceted roles in plant physiology and environmental adaptation [9]. It is known that beyond their role in facilitating CO₂ availability to Rubisco in C3 and C4 plants, CAs regulate stomatal function and transpiration rates as well as are integral to plant growth, pH regulation, ion transport, and adaptation to various stresses [6, 7, 10]. He et al. [11] reported that the α-type of CA1 located in chloroplasts plays a crucial role in photosynthesis, regulating water use efficiency, biomass accumulation, and grain yield in rice. They found that the growth and photosynthetic defects observed in the OsαCA1 mutant were due to restricted CO2 availability at the chloroplast. A comparative proteomic analysis has demonstrated that the activity of the βCA1 enzyme was potentially significant for the tolerance of drought stress in Brassica napus [8]. In another study, the transcription levels of βCA genes and the activity of CA enzymes were correlated with maintaining growth and photosynthetic efficiency in Arabidopsis thaliana under drought stress conditions [12]. However, severe stresses can compromise CA activity as noted in two chickpea varieties under salinity. The susceptible variety of Pusa-BGD72 exhibited a more substantial decrease in CA activity which was associated with more pronounced reductions in its growth, photosynthetic pigment levels, and antioxidant responses compared to the Pusa-BG5023 variety [13]. Plants have developed an intricate signaling network, involving reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitric oxide (NO), and phytohormones such as salicylic acid (SA), to fine-tune their physiological processes and bolster defense mechanisms in response to drought stress [14, 15].

Salicylic acid was primarily recognized for its role in inducing systemic acquired resistance (SAR) against pathogens. However, subsequent research demonstrated its potential as a phytohormone involved in enhancing plant resilience under various abiotic stresses [16, 17]. For instance, foliar application of SA improved photosynthetic capacity and the activity of antioxidant enzymes in As-exposed rice [18], salt-stressed sunflower [19], and WD-stressed Conocarpus erectus and Populus deltoides [20]. A study showed that exogenous application of SA improved shoot proliferation in Impatiens walleriana exposed to polyethylene glycol (PEG) by maintaining relative water content (RWC) and photosynthetic pigments, enhancing proline accumulation, adjusting the activity of antioxidant enzymes and protecting cellular membranes from oxidative damage [21]. In another study, foliar SA spraying improved photosynthetic efficiency in maize seedlings subjected to PEG-induced water deficit by repairing the damaged PSII reaction center, upregulating the transcription levels of psbA, Rubisco activase (RCA), and the Rubisco small subunit (rbcS) genes and maintaining chloroplast integrity [22]. While precise mechanism of SA action for regulating CA activity and photosynthesis under drought stress are still being explored, emerging evidence suggests SA’s interaction with NO might be involved in triggering these responses under adverse environmental conditions [23].

Nitric oxide (NO) is a critical gaseous signaling molecule that its synthesis can catalyze either by the activity of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) and nitrate reductase (NR) enzymes or through non-enzymatic pathways [24–26]. Numerous studies documented that exogenous application of sodium nitroprusside (SNP as a NO donor) enhances defensive responses in plants under diverse stresses such as cold stress [27], heavy metal toxicity [28–30], Fe deficiency [31], and water deficit [32, 33]. Foliar application of 100 µM SNP mitigated the adverse effects of irrigation cut-off in Silybum marianum L. plants, resulting in reduced stomatal density on the leaf surface, enhanced RWC, increased proline concentration, and improved grain and biological yields [34]. Foliar spraying SNP improved chlorophyll content, RWC, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), electrolyte leakage, and malondialdehyde (MDA), and enhanced PSII efficiency (Fv/Fm ratio) and plant growth rate in two thyme species (Thymus serpyllum and T. vulgaris) under reduced irrigation [35]. It has been demonstrated that NO reduces water loss through transpiration by regulating ion channels and guard cell turgor pressure [36, 37] while enhancing carbon fixation efficiency by enhancing Rubisco activity and upregulating genes associated with photosystem components [38]. Nevertheless, the role of NO signaling in regulating CA activity and its crosstalk with other signal molecules requires further exploration.

Several studies have highlighted the synergistic effects of NO and SA in boosting antioxidant defense mechanisms and mitigating oxidative damage caused by environmental stresses, including copper toxicity in okra [39], salt stress in cotton seedlings [40], drought stress in wheat [41], and heat stress in Lablab purpureus L [42]. Research has demonstrated SA treatment can increase NO levels through upregulating Nia1 and Nia2 genes [43] and the activation of NOS or NR in plants [44, 45]. Despite these advancements, significant gaps remain particularly concerning their interaction with CA activity in plants. To address this knowledge gap, the present study aims to investigate the interplay between SA and NO in regulating CA activity and defensive responses in the leaves of Borago officinalis under water deficit conditions.

Borago officinalis, commonly referred to as borage or starflower, is an annual herbaceous plant native to the Mediterranean region, renowned for its diverse applications in medicine, cuisine, and sustainable agriculture [46]. Its morphological traits, including deep-root systems and trichome-covered leaves, underscore its adaptability to arid environments and its potential as a resilient crop in water-scarce areas [47]. However, prolonged water stress has been observed to negatively influence its biomass yield and defensive mechanisms [48]. A study has shown the positive effect of SA to improve drought stress tolerance in borage [49], but the impact of SNP and its combination with SA on this medicinal herb remains unexplored. In addition, the impact of the interplay between SA and NO on CA activity has not been studied in any plants so far. Therefore, in this study, we used a NO scavenger to clarify the interactions between SA and SNP for inducing water deficit tolerance in B. officinalis leaves, focusing on the role of CA activity and the antioxidant system in modulating photosynthesis. This study seeks to provide novel insights into the interaction between SA, NO, and CA activity in modulating plant stress tolerance that might support breeding programs and sustainable agriculture.

Materials and methods

Plant growth conditions and performance of treatments

Seeds of Borago officinalis were supplied by the Medicinal Plant Research Center in Isfahan, Iran. Before planting, the seeds underwent surface sterilization using a 5% sodium hypochlorite solution and after washing with water were sown in pots containing a mixture of perlite and cocopeat. These pots were placed in a greenhouse environment with a photoperiod of 13 h light/11 h dark, a temperature cycle of 27 °C during the day and 20 °C at night, and 75% relative humidity. Initially, the seeds were watered every two days for two weeks to support germination. Then, the seedlings received weekly 250 mL of quarter-strength Hoagland’s solution per pot for an additional two weeks, and one-month-old plants were exposed to treatments.

It is worth noting that in another experiment, we selected varying concentrations of salicylic acid (SA) and sodium nitroprusside (SNP as a No donor) based on prior studies indicating that SA concentrations of 0.1–1.1 mM [18–20, 22, 39, 41, 42, 50, 51] and SNP concentrations of 50–200 µM [28, 29, 34, 35, 40] are beneficial for plant stress tolerance. Assessment of the impact of different levels of SA, SNP, and irrigation intervals on B. officinalis seedlings showed that a 4-day irrigation interval led to a 50% reduction in borage growth, while spraying with 0.5 mM SA and 100 µM SNP led to the most significant improvement in growth recovery. Consequently, these treatments were adopted for the current study. SA and SNP were applied via foliar spraying, a method widely recognized for its effectiveness in previous research [18–20, 34, 35, 41, 42, 50, 51]. Foliar application is preferred over soil or solid medium application because it is more affordable and reduces risks associated with interactions of these compounds with soil components and microorganisms. For the performance of water deficit, 30-day-old plants were irrigated at 4-day intervals while control plants were watered every 2 days throughout the experiment. All pots received weekly a half-strength Hoagland’s solution per pot for supplying essential nutrients. Weekly applications of SA and SNP, both individually and in combination, were administered through foliar spraying a total of three times. Additionally, in some pots, plants were sprayed with 100 µM 2-(4-carboxyphenyl)−4,4,5,5-tetramethylimidazoline-1-oxy l-3-oxide (cPTIO as a NO scavenger) two hours post-application of SA and SNP, as reported in earlier studies [38, 50].

Fresh leaves were sampled one day after the final spraying, and after freezing in liquid nitrogen, were kept at − 80 °C for later biochemical analysis. In the end, two-month-old plants were harvested, their fresh weights documented and subsequently dried in an oven at 75 °C for 3 days.

Estimation of NO concentration

The nitric oxide (NO) concentration in fresh leaf samples was determined using the Griess reagent method, as described by Amooaghaie and Roohollahi et al. [31]. Absorbance measurements were taken at a wavelength of 540 nm, and the NO concentration was calculated using a standard sodium nitrite (NaNO2) calibration curve.

The method of Bates et al. [52] was employed to measure proline content in leaf samples. Briefly, leaf tissues were macerated in a 3% sulfosalicylic acid solution, and the resulting supernatant after centrifugation at 4 °C, was combined with ninhydrin reagent glacial and acetic acid, followed by heating for 40 min. After cooling, toluene was added, the mixture was vortexed, and then incubated at room temperature. The absorbance at 520 nm was measured, and proline content was calculated based on a standard curve.

Quantification of chlorophyll content

The fresh leaves were macerated using acetone (80%, v/v), and after filtering and diluting the absorbance of the extract was read at 663, and 645 nm. Then, the content of chlorophyll a and b (Chl a, Chl b) was calculated using following formula suggested by Arnon et al. [53].

Measurement of carbonic anhydrase activity and PSII efficiency

The Fv/Fm ratio, a key indicator of photosystem II efficiency, was evaluated using a portable chlorophyll fluorometer by the method outlined by Vafadar et al. [54]. Following a 20-minute dark adaptation period, the minimum fluorescence (FO) was measured, and the maximum fluorescence (FM) was obtained after imposing a high-intensity saturating flash of 3500 µmol. The variable fluorescence (Fv) was calculated as the difference between FM and FO. Finally, the ratio of Fv/Fm was assessed as an indicator of the plant’s efficiency in utilizing light energy for photosynthesis.

The activity of carbonic anhydrase (CA activity) was assessed following the protocol established by Dwivedi and Randhawa [55]. Leaf samples were prepared by cutting them into small pieces, suspending them in a cysteine hydrochloride solution, and incubating at 40 °C for 20 min. After blotting, the samples were transferred into test tubes containing phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). Then, bromothymol blue and an alkaline bicarbonate solution were added to the mixture. The prepared mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min. Afterward, 0.2 mL of methyl red indicator was introduced, and the reaction mixture was titrated with 0.05 N HCl. The data were reported as µmol CO2 kg⁻¹ FW s⁻¹.

Estimation of hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde contents

To assess lipid peroxidation, malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured using the method developed by Heath and Packer [56]. After extracting leaf samples in trichloroacetic acid and 20 –min centrifugation, 1 ml of the resulting supernatant was combined with trichloroacetic acid and thiobarbituric acid. Then, the mixture was incubated and centrifuged again and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm and 532 nm. Finally, the MDA concentration was computed using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹.

The method of Velikova et al. [57] was applied for quantifying H2O2 concentration using a KI reagent. The H2O2 content was determined by reading the optical absorbance at 390 nm and using a standard curve.

Assaying the antioxidant enzyme activities

For enzyme assay, frozen leaves (0.1 g) were extracted using phosphate buffer containing 0.20 g KH2PO4, 0.14 g Na2HPO4, 0.8 g NaCl, 0.20 g KCl, and 1 g polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) at low temperature and on ice. The extracts were centrifuged (10,000 × g) for 10 min, and enzyme activities were assayed in the supernatants according to the method previously explained by Nabaei and Amooaghaie [28].

The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) was determined using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) method. The reaction solution (3 ml) consisted of 0.3 ml enzyme extract and potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0), 10 µM EDTA, 75 µM NBT, 13 mM methionine, and 2 µM riboflavin. The reaction solution was irradiated under a 40 W fluorescent lamp for 10 min. The reaction mixture without enzyme, which showed maximum color due to maximum reduction of NBT, was considered as the control. The irradiated reaction mixture with enzyme was used as the sample, as it did not develop color. The absorbance was read against the blank (non-irradiated reaction solution for control and sample separately) using a spectrophotometer. The amount of enzyme that inhibited 50% reduction of NBT at 560 nm was defined as one unit of SOD activity and was expressed as units per gram of fresh weight.

To measure catalase activity (CAT), 100 µl enzyme extract was added to 900 µl potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing H2O2, and the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm was monitored for 1 min at 5-sec intervals. One unit of CAT activity was defined as µmoles of H2O2 decomposed per minute.

To determine ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity, the reaction mixture contained 100 µl enzyme extract and 625 µl potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) containing EDTA (0.2 mM), 175 µl ascorbic acid (0.5 mM), 50 µl hydrogen peroxide, and 50 µl bovine serum albumin. The hydrogen peroxide-dependent oxidation of ascorbate was followed by a decrease in the absorbance at 290 nm for 1 min.

To assay peroxidase (POD) activity, 25 µl enzyme extract was added to 2.7 ml potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH = 7.5), containing 0.1 ml guaiacol (4%) and 0.1 ml H2O2. Then, the increase in absorbance at 470 nm (related to the formation of tetraguaiacol) was monitored for 1 min at 5-sec intervals. The enzyme content required for the formation of 1 µM tetraguaiacol per min was considered as one unit of POD activity.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was executed as a completely randomized design with three replications. Statistical analysis was conducted using ANOVA via SAS software, and mean comparisons were performed using Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of P < 0.05. For better comprehension, a Pearson’s correlation, among variables and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) between treatments and variables were performed using R software (ver. 3.5.0, http://www.r-project.org). A principal component analysis (PCA) between treatments and variables was also performed by XLSTAT software (2018).

Results

Interactive effects of SA, SNP, and WD on shoot fresh and dry weight

WD significantly decreased both shoot fresh and dry weight by 40.06% and 45.54%, respectively. However, foliar spraying SA or SNP restored these losses, improving shoot fresh weight by 21.36% and 19.29%, and both increased shoot dry weight approximately by 30%, compared to WD treatment alone. The combined use of SA and SNP had a more pronounced effect, resulting in a notable increase of shoot fresh and dry weight by 34.98% and 41.25%, respectively. Interestingly, these beneficial outcomes were reversed when endogenous NO was diminished using cPTIO (Fig. 1A, B).

Interactive effects of SA, SNP, and WD on NO content

WD significantly increased NO content by 33% and the application of SA and SNP aggravated NO content in the leaves of plants. Foliar spraying SA or SNP and particularly their combination increased NO content by 35%, 50%, and 75% compared to WD treatment alone. These effects of SA and SNP were reversed by applying cPTIO (Fig. 2).

Interactive effects of SA, SNP, and WD on relative water content and proline content

WD significantly reduced relative water content (RWC) by 32.24% while increasing proline content by 3 folds. However, foliar application of SA and SNP improved RWC by 13.36% and 18.14% respectively, and enhanced proline content by 27.95% and 45.85%, compared to WD treatment alone. Discussion

As global climate patterns continue to change and drought events become more frequent, understanding the physiological and biochemical mechanisms that plants utilize to adapt to water scarcity is essential. This study highlights the role of interplay between SA, NO, and CA activity in improving WD tolerance in B. officinalis.

Water deficit (WD) significantly reduced shoot fresh and dry weights of borage plants (Fig. 1), which was consistent with previous findings on B. officinalis [47, 48]. Water scarcity disrupts water uptake and cellular turgidity, reduces cell division and elongation, causes oxidative stress, and impairs photosynthesis, resulting in decreased fresh weight and biomass production in plants [1, 3]. However, foliar application of SA and SNP (a NO donor) significantly improved growth parameters (Fig. 1), suggesting a critical role of these signaling molecules in regulating tolerance to WD stress in borage. The transcriptomic analysis has demonstrated that SA can enhance drought tolerance by regulating the expression of genes associated with carbohydrate metabolism, antioxidant system, osmoregulation, ABA signaling pathways, and proper transcription factors in kenaf [58]. Another study also deciphered that NO’s regulatory effects on the transcription of genes related to the photosynthesis, antioxidative defense system, secondary metabolism, hormonal signal transduction, and carbohydrate metabolism support drought adaption in alfalfa plants [59]. Our study’s results also exhibited that the improved growth of borage plants was attributed to the effectiveness of SA and SNP in alleviating osmotic and oxidative stresses caused by WD conditions. Furthermore, the superior effect of combined treatment (Fig. 1) and the reversal of SA-induced growth improvements by cPTIO as an NO scavenger indicated that NO signaling is involved in SA-mediated adaptation to WD stress. The increased NO content in leaves of SA-treated plants (Fig. 2) further supports this conclusion. Similarly, SA pretreatment increased NO levels and improved fresh and dry weights in maize plants grown under polyethylene glycol (PEG) and these benefits were suppressed by applying cPTIO [60].

The impact of WD on plant growth is profound, primarily by limiting water uptake and inducing osmotic stress in plants [3], as evidenced in borage through a reduction in RWC (Fig. 3A). To counteract this effect, plants increase the biosynthesis and accumulation of osmolytes including proline, which enables plants to water uptake under low water potential in soil [4, 61]. In borage also a reduction in leaf RWC was associated with an increase in proline content (Fig. 3). This was consistent with findings in and Hypericum perforatum L [4]. and Foeniculum vulgare Mill. under WD [5]. The application of SA and SNP, individually and particularly in combination, improved leaf RWC and proline content, suggesting the role of these signal molecules in modulating water and proline contents and safeguarding cellular turgor in plants. However, the decline of proline content and RWC in SA-treated plants by cPTIO suggests a possible role of NO in modulating SA-mediated proline accumulation under WD. Shan and Wang [62] also reported that SA-induced NO improved RWC in drought-stressed maize seedlings by enhancing proline content and root hydraulic conductivity and these effects were inhibited by cPTIO treatment. Xue et al. [63] found that SNP treatment upregulated the transcription of P5CS gene, leading to proline accumulation and improved osmotic adjustment in drought-stressed wheat. Furthermore, it has demonstrated that the involvement of NO in enhancing the transcription of aquaporin genes [36] and the behavior of the stomata [34] play a significant role in maintaining RWC in plants. Therefore, it can be proposed that SA-induced NO likely enhances the gene expression or activity of enzymes associated with proline biosynthesis, degradation, or the function of aquaporin channels and stomata under WD conditions. These assumptions should be further investigated in future studies.

Drought stress also leads to excessive ROS production in chloroplasts, causing photooxidation of chlorophyll (Chl) and dissociation of PSII proteins, which impairs photosynthetic activity [1]. In borage plants also WD reduced Chl a, Chl b contents and maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm ratio). However, SA or SNP application improved these parameters (Figs. 4 and 5A), likely due to their effect on mitigating oxidative stress induced by WD. Numerous studies have demonstrated that SA and NO reduce oxidative stress, thereby protecting chloroplast integrity, minimizing oxidative damage to Chl, and enhancing photosynthetic performance under stressful conditions [17, 26–31]. Similar to our findings (Figs. 4 and 5A), the synergistic effect of SA and NO more efficiently improved Chl content, Fv/Fm ratio, net photosynthesis rate, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate in Henna (Lawsonia inermis) plants under WD [51]. The reversal of SA-induced benefits on Chl content and Fv/Fm ratio by cPTIO in borage points to a critical role of NO in regulating SA effects on these traits. Likewise, the beneficial effects of SA on photosynthetic rate, water-use efficiency, stomatal conductance, Fv/Fm ratio, and also transcription of genes encoding rubisco and rubisco activase (Rbc L, ZmRCAα and ZmRCAβ mRNA) were suppressed by applying cPTIO in maize plants exposed to PEG-induced WD [60]. Li et al. [38] also reported that foliar application of SNP enhanced photosynthesis in salt-stressed tomato seedlings by overcoming stomatal limitations, enhancing the efficiency of photosystem II, and upregulating the transcriptions of genes that encode Rubisco activase, the Rubisco large and small subunits, and activities of key enzymes of the Calvin-Benson cycle. These responses were impaired by cPTIO spraying. Chloroplasts are one of the endogenous sources of NO production and, NO binding sites may exist within Photosystem II (PSII), likely situated between the primary and secondary quinone acceptors [64]. It also has reported that SNP treatment upregulated the transcription of the psbA gene which encoding the D1 protein of PSII, in Triticum aestivum. This facilitated rapid turnover of D1 protein, maintaining the function of PSII reaction centers under drought stress [65]. Thus, it can be supposed that SA-induced NO improves PSII efficiency in borage by activating signaling pathways or the expression of genes related to chlorophyll synthesis and PSII components, which should be assessed in future studies. One of the primary responses of plants to drought stress is the closure of stomata, which prevents excessive water loss and contributes to sustaining leaf water potential. However, this adaptive mechanism also restricts CO₂ uptake, negatively affecting photosynthetic efficiency [1, 3]. Carbonic anhydrase (CA) plays a critical role in regulating photosynthetic efficiency under such conditions by regulating stomatal closure while ensuring sufficient bicarbonate availability into chloroplast [6]. Research have clarified that plant species or varieties with elevated CA activity exhibit higher water use efficiency, and better photosynthesis, highlighting the enzyme’s significance in stress tolerance mechanisms [8, 13]. In this study also there is a positive correlation between CA activity and the Fv/Fm ratio (Fig. 5). However, the gene expression levels or activity of CA proteins are influenced by the intensity of drought stress as noted by Çevik et al. [12]. They observed mild drought stress increased these attributes, while severe stress decreased the transcription of most βCA genes (except βCA-6) and total CA activity. In the present study, the reduced CA activity in WD-exposed B. officinalis plants (Fig. 5A) agreed with the findings in Brassica napus [8]. The decline of the activity of this enzyme might be attributed to excessive oxidative damage induced by WD. The negative correlation between H2O2 and MDA contents with CA activity supports this conclusion (Fig. 8). However, SA and SNP treatments increased CA activity in leaves of borage (Fig. 5B) which was closely associated with improved photosynthetic efficiency (Fig. 9A) in these treatments. Similarly, SNP treatment enhanced photosynthetic efficiency in mustard plants by increasing CA activity and enhancing antioxidant enzymes [66]. The impact of SA on improving CA activity and photosynthetic efficiency has been observed in salt-stressed Brassica juncea [67], and water-stressed tomato plants [68]. To the best of our knowledge, the current study, for the first time, depicted the synergistic effects of SA and SNP on enhancing CA activity under WD conditions. HCA and PCA resultes also showed that the highest levels of NO content, CA activity, and Fv/Fm ratio were associated with WD + SA + SNP (Figs. 9 and 10). These novel findings further support the fact that the combined application of SA and NO stimulated CA activity and that this enzyme supported photosynthesis under WD stress. Research has demonstrated that the CA activity not only enhances CO₂ supply for Rubisco but also contributes to the maintenance of cellular redox which is essential for preserving photosynthesis under stress conditions [6, 10]. The reversal of SA-induced CA activity following cPTIO treatment provides the first evidence that SA mediates CA activity through crosstalk with NO. It can be assumed that SA induces NO generation, and NO modulates CA activity through post-translational modifications. However, the interplay between NO, SA and CA activity might be more complex, involving multiple intermediate components. Research has demonstrated that NO modulates the function of SA-related proteins including NPR1 (NON-EXPRESSER OF PATHOGENESIS RELATED 1) and NRB4 (NON-RECOGNITION OF BTH-4) which have been identified as pivotal components in the SA signaling pathway in plants [23]. NPR1 interacts with SA in the cytosol and after transport to the nucleus and connection with NRB4, regulates the transcription of SA-mediated defensive genes [16]. The function of NPR1 is regulated by post-translational modifications, and its ability to oligomerize or monomerize, which is dependent on the cellular redox state [69]. NPR1 affinity for binding to SA is mediated by redox-dependent agents such as thioredoxin (TRX) and S-nitroso glutathione (SNO is produced by interacting NO and glutathione) that convert the inactive oligomeric form of NPR1 into active monomer form in the cytoplasm [23]. On the other hand, the interactions between βCAs and both NRB4 and NPR1 were detected in yeast and Arabidopsis thaliana by Medina-Puche et al. [70]. They suggested likely SA interacts with βCAs before engaging with NPR1 in the cytosol, and NPR1 facilitates the transport of βCAs to the nucleus. Then, NRB4 localized in the nucleus, may act as a connector between NPR1 (associated with transcription factors and βCAs) and RNA Polymerase II, thereby contributing to the transcriptional regulation of SA-responsive genes. Literature mentioned above proposes that NO and CAs are involved in SA perception in plants through their effect on NRB4 and NPR1. In other words, NO and CAs act upstream of SA. In contrast, our findings suggest that NO functions downstream of SA for modulating CA activity and PSII efficiency under WD conditions. These dual findings suggest the proposal that NO, SA, and CAs may potentially form a self-amplifying feedback loop. Future molecular studies should provide more empirical evidence for this hypothesis.

Disruption of photosynthesis during drought stress leads to ROS generation and lipid peroxidation [3–5]. In this study also WD induced oxidative stress (Fig. 6), and to mitigate it, the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as APX, CAT, POD, and SOD increased (Fig. 7) in the leaves. However, the antioxidant system’s capacity was insufficient to fully counteract oxidative stress, as levels of H2O2 and MDA increased under WD (Fig. 6). The foliar spraying SA and SNP aggravated proline accumulation and the activity of antioxidant enzymes and consequently decreased oxidative stress (Figs. 3B and 7). Proline not only serves as an osmoprotectant but also participates in scavenging free radicals and sustaining cellular redox balance, supporting membrane integrity, and stabilizing enzymes and proteins [61]. The antioxidant enzymes also contribute to ROS scavenging, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and minimizing oxidative damage in cells [4, 5, 17, 30, 54]. Similar to our findings (Figs. 3B and 7), the superior impact of combined treatment with SA and SNP on antioxidant enzymes and proline accumulation has been reported in drought-stressed wheat [41], and salt-subjected cotton. However, SA-induced enhancement of defensive responses corresponded with higher NO levels in these plants and the application of cPTIO diminished these effects (Figs. 3B and 7). SA-induced NO production might directly and indirectly contribute to scavenging ROS. NO can directly interact with superoxide radicals (O2•−), leading to the formation of peroxynitrite (ONOO−), which reduces their cytotoxicity [71]. Additionally, NO signaling can regulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [24, 25, 29]. Supporting evidence from previous studies indicates that single and combined application of SA and NO significantly upregulated the expression of enzymatic antioxidant genes in heat-stressed Lablab purpureus L. plants [72] and drought-subjected safflower plants [73]. A study also showed that several post-transcriptional modifications induced by NO, such as nitration and nitrosylation, are involved in regulating ascorbate peroxidase activity during drought stress [74]. Our results propose that NO functions downstream of SA for motivating antioxidant responses in borage which is in agreement with previous reports in rice [44] and maize [75] under arsenic stress. In contrast, several studies also have clarified that NO treatment increased SA levels in plants and as an upstream signal contributing to the activation of the antioxidant system and proline accumulation [32, 73]. Our findings and above literature support the concept of the “equilateral triangle” between SA, NO, and oxidative stress suggested by Kohli et al. [23]. It is known that ROS, particularly H2O2, exhibit dual roles in plants, and act as signaling molecules at low concentrations while being oxidative agents under adverse conditions [14, 76]. In this study, exposure to WD increased H2O2 and NO contents that were closely correlated to the activation of antioxidant enzymes and proline accumulation in borage plants (Figs. 8, 9, and 10). Likely, WD-induced H2O2 bursts could trigger NO and SA biosynthesis and a functional synergy between them mitigates oxidative stress. Further studies should validate the accuracy of this hypothesis.

Conclusion

This study for the first time clarified that the role of interplay between NO and SA in regulating CA activity was closely linked to regulating photosynthetic performance and enhancing stress adaptation in plants. By modulating CA activity, antioxidant enzyme functions, and proline accumulation, these signaling molecules improved photosynthetic efficiency and enabled plants to better withstand WD conditions. The findings refer to a possible interconnected network involving ROS, NO, SA, and CAs in fine-tuning photosynthesis and defense mechanisms under drought stress. However, the findings presented herein are preliminary, and additional molecular evidence is necessary to validate the proposed hypothesis. Future research should prioritize detailed studies using mutants and incorporate transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to gain deeper insights into these signaling pathways. Understanding the crosstalk between these signal molecules presents promising opportunities for enhancing crop resilience to drought. Genetic engineering strategies targeting NO and SA biosynthesis or other components of signaling pathways such as CAs could potentially lead to the development of drought-tolerant crop varieties.
