Herpes zoster incidence rates in adults aged 18 years or older with immunocompromising or chronic conditions in England: a large retrospective cohort study (Clinical Practice Research Datalink, 2012–2019)

This was a very large, up-to-date study using a data source broadly representative of the UK population to report herpes zoster (HZ) incidence rates (IRs) across a wide range of immunocompromising and chronic conditions. IRs were examined in several subgroups, including age groups and at the condition level.

Introduction

Primary infection with the varicella zoster virus (VZV) usually occurs in childhood and results in chickenpox.1 2 After the chickenpox resolves, VZV becomes latent.1 Subsequent reactivation of the latent VZV results in herpes zoster (HZ, shingles), mainly following a decline in VZV-specific cell-mediated immunity due to ageing, immunosuppressive diseases or therapies.2–9 Complications associated with HZ include post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN), ocular and neurological complications, and disseminated infections.2 10

A systematic review up to 2013 estimated an incidence rate (IR) of HZ ranging between 3 and 5 cases per 1000 person-years (PY) in the general population in North America, Europe and Asia-Pacific.11 A review from 2002 to 2018 estimated global IRs ranging between 5 and 11 cases per 1000 PY in adults ≥50 years of age.3 Between 2000 and 2006 in the UK, the HZ IR was estimated to be 5 cases per 1000 PY in individuals ≥50 years of age, increasing with age up to 84 years.12 The risk of HZ in individuals with several immunocompromising or chronic conditions is higher than in the general population.4–8 13 Yanni et al investigated the incidence of HZ in adults in the UK with at least one of 16 immunocompromising conditions between 2000 and 2012. They reported an overall HZ IR of 7.8 (95% CI 7.7 to 7.9) cases per 1000 PY in adults with immunocompromising conditions, compared with 6.2 (95% CI 6.1 to 6.3) cases per 1000 PY in adults without such conditions.4 Individuals with a haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT), haematological malignancies and polymyalgia rheumatica had the highest HZ IRs.4 Although the study provided important insight into the burden of HZ from 2000 until 2012,4 it is now outdated owing to changes in the UK environment in terms of demographics, clinical services, diagnostic advances, HZ vaccination recommendations and new immunosuppressant therapies.

Vaccination against HZ was first introduced in the UK in 2013, using the live attenuated zoster vaccine (zoster vaccine live (ZVL)).14 In February 2019, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation (JCVI) recommended that severely immunosuppressed individuals aged 70–79 years (who were contraindicated to receive ZVL) should receive the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV), and this recommendation was implemented in 2021.15 Eligibility for severely immunosuppressed individuals was expanded in 2023 to include adults aged 50 years and over.16 In September 2025, eligibility for the RZV programme was expanded to include all severely immunosuppressed adults aged 18 years and over.17 18

Available data on HZ incidence in the UK across a range of immunocompromised (IC) populations are based on the period from 2000 to 2012.4 Up-to-date and robust age group-specific data on HZ incidence in both IC individuals and those with chronic conditions are needed to inform further research, clinical guidelines and immunisation recommendations. The primary objective of the current study was to estimate the annual and overall IRs of HZ in adults with selected immunocompromising conditions, by age and condition in England. Secondary objectives included estimating HZ IRs in adults with selected chronic conditions by age and condition, and HZ incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and HZ complications in the study populations by age. The study also aimed to estimate HZ recurrence in adults with immunocompromising or chronic conditions by age.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational cohort study utilised data from the UK Clinical Practice Research Database (CPRD) Aurum, with linkage to Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) and Office for National Statistics (ONS) data.19 The CPRD is a longitudinal primary care database covering approximately 20% of the UK population. A subset of primary care practices in England is linked to HES and ONS data.19 20 The dataset is considered to be broadly representative of the UK population.21

The study population comprised adults aged ≥18 years who fulfilled CPRD criteria for being acceptable for research, had active registration in the database between 1 January 2012 and 31 December 2019 and ≥12 months of observation time before the index date (figure 1). Individuals with a record of HZ or HZ vaccination (ZVL or RZV) at any time before the index date were not eligible. The 12-month pre-index period allowed identification of pre-existing immunocompromising or chronic conditions or a record of HZ or HZ vaccination.

The index date was the latest date of the study start (1 January 2012), an individual’s 18th birthday, registration with a primary care practice or diagnosis of the first immunocompromising or chronic condition (figure 1). Follow-up continued until the earliest of the following dates: death, transfer out of the primary care practice, last data collection in the primary care practice, ZVL or RZV vaccination or study end (31 December 2019).

Study populations

Four study populations were identified: IC population, chronic disease (CD) population, IC-free population and IC-free/CD-free population. The IC population comprised individuals with at least one CPRD MedCode, International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th revision (ICD-10) code, and/or Classification of Surgical Operations and Procedures 4th revision (OPCS-4) code recorded in their entire medical history for any of the following immunocompromising conditions: haematological malignancy (multiple myeloma, leukaemia, lymphoma), solid organ malignancy, HSCT, solid organ transplant, polymyalgia rheumatica, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, multiple sclerosis, inflammatory bowel disease, HIV and autoimmune thyroiditis.

The CD population comprised individuals with at least one CPRD MedCode, ICD-10 code and/or OPCS code recorded in their entire medical history for any of the following chronic conditions: heart failure, coronary artery disease, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus, chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD) or depression and/or anxiety.

Only the first immunocompromising condition on record was included in the analyses of the IC population, unless there were records of more than one of the above immunocompromising conditions on the same date. In this case, individuals were included in all applicable immunocompromising condition groups. Any subsequent immunocompromising conditions that developed during follow-up were not considered in the analyses. The same principles applied to the CD population.

The IC and CD populations were not mutually exclusive. Individuals were eligible for both populations, if applicable, at the diagnosis date of the first immunocompromising condition on record (IC population) and the first chronic condition on record (CD population). If an individual in the IC population developed a first chronic condition of interest during follow-up, they could also be included in the CD population. Similarly, if an individual in the CD population developed a first immunocompromising condition of interest during follow-up, they could also be included in the IC population.

The IC-free population comprised individuals without any of the above immunocompromising conditions recorded. The IC-free/CD-free population comprised individuals without any of the above immunocompromising or chronic conditions (ie, they were a subgroup of the IC-free population). Individuals in the IC-free population who developed an immunocompromising condition of interest during follow-up were censored from the IC-free population at the diagnosis date of the immunocompromising condition and were eligible for the IC population if they met the relevant eligibility criteria. The same principle applied to the IC-free/CD-free population for individuals who developed an immunocompromising or chronic condition of interest during follow-up.

Study endpoints and analyses

Study endpoints reported here include HZ IRs in each study population, IRRs adjusted by age and sex, complications associated with HZ and recurrence of HZ. Incident HZ and HZ-related complications were identified using MedCodes recorded in the CPRD and ICD-10 codes in HES records. Complications were classified as PHN, neurological, ocular, disseminated and other. Complications were captured for the period from the HZ index date until 365 days after. PHN was analysed over two periods: (1) from the HZ index date to 365 days after the HZ index date; (2) from 90 days post-HZ index until 365 days after the HZ index date. Recurrent HZ was defined as any HZ event that was not PHN and either occurred after a 180-day HZ-free and HZ complications-free period (except PHN) from the initial HZ index date, or occurred at any time after the previous recurrent HZ event (for the second and subsequent recurrences).

For the age-stratified IR calculations, person-time was allocated according to an individual’s attained age group during follow-up (18–49, 50–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–79 and ≥80 years) and first immunocompromising condition (for the IC population) or first chronic condition (for the CD population). Study patients could contribute person-time to subsequent age groups as they aged during the study period (eg, from 18-49 to 50–59 years). The applicable person-time was included in all applicable age groups and the HZ event was reflected in the age group when it developed. However, for the Poisson regression models used to estimate adjusted IRRs, age group was included as a fixed covariate defined at the relevant index date. Therefore, the attained-age group approach was used for the age-stratified IR calculations, but not as a time-varying covariate in the adjusted Poisson IRR models.

Overall HZ IRs and annual IRs (2012–2019) were estimated per 1000 PY with 95% CIs. IRs were calculated in each age group and overall (unadjusted and age-adjusted). IRRs were calculated as the overall HZ IR in the IC or CD populations versus the overall HZ IR in the IC-free or IC-free/CD-free populations. The IRR of each pair was calculated using Poisson regression analysis with 95% CI and adjusted by age group at index date and sex.

Age-adjustment was performed using a model-based approach. Specifically, IRs were estimated using generalised linear models with a Poisson distribution and log link function, with the logarithm of person-years included as an offset to account for differences in follow-up time. Age group was included as a categorical covariate according to the predefined age categorisation described in the study. Adjusted IRs per 1000 person-years and corresponding 95% CIs were derived from model-based predicted rates using predictive margins, producing overall age-adjusted IRs for each relevant analysis population/subpopulation averaged across its observed age distribution. As a model-based estimation was used for age adjustment, no external standard population or external weighting scheme was applied.

The equidispersion assumption of the Poisson regression model was assessed using the Pearson χ2 statistic from the fitted model. Assessment using the Pearson χ2 statistic indicated some evidence of overdispersion in certain fitted Poisson models. Therefore, negative binomial and zero-inflated negative binomial models were explored as alternative modelling approaches. However, these models either failed to converge or yielded an estimated dispersion parameter very close to zero, resulting in estimates that were very similar to those obtained from the Poisson models. Consequently, the Poisson models were retained for the final analysis, and neither an alternative model nor robust variance estimation was applied.

Calculating age-sex specific IRs involved determining the incidence of an HZ event within specific age groups and sex strata. Age and sex categories were created to categorise individuals into specific age groups by sex. The age-sex specific IR of each cohort was calculated by dividing the number of HZ cases in each age-sex category by the corresponding person-time at risk. The proportion of individuals with HZ who developed HZ complications was reported as a percentage with 95% CIs. The rate of HZ recurrence was estimated per 1000 PY with 95% CIs.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software V.9.4 and R V.4.4.1.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study included 1 764 900 individuals in the IC population, 12 867 750 in the IC-free population, 6 423 633 in the CD population and 8 235 858 in the IC-free/CD-free population (table 1, online supplemental figure 1). The proportion of females in each population was 55.78%, 49.71%, 54.30% and 47.27%, respectively (table 1). On average, individuals in the IC-free and IC-free/CD-free populations were younger (mean (SD) 40.75 (17.95) and 37.55 (16.06) years, respectively) compared with the IC and CD populations (mean (SD) 57.70 (18.70) and 48.35 (20.03) years, respectively) (table 1).

Incidence rates of herpes zoster

IC and IC-free populations

HZ IRs were higher in the IC population than in the IC-free population, both overall (unadjusted and age-adjusted) and across all age groups, including adults aged 18–49 years (figure 2; online supplemental table 1). HZ IRs increased with age in both the IC and IC-free populations, specifically from 3.34 (95% CI 3.26 to 3.42) in the 18–49 years group to 12.58 (12.34 to 12.81) in the ≥80 years group in the IC population, and from 1.74 (1.73 to 1.75) in the 18–49 years group to 10.25 (10.11 to 10.39) in the ≥80 years group in the IC-free population (figure 2; online supplemental table 1). The annual IRs showed minor variations in each calendar year (2012–2019) for all age groups in both populations (figure 3).

The HZ IRs observed in adults with each immunocompromising condition were higher compared with IC-free adults in the same age group (figure 4; online supplemental table 1). The HZ IRs varied between immunocompromising conditions, with the highest rates observed in adults with haematological malignancies (18–49 years: 5.70 (95% CI 5.13 to 6.32); ≥80 years: 17.21 (16.01 to 18.46)), polymyalgia rheumatica (18–49 years: 4.91 (CI 3.11 to 7.24); ≥80 years: 13.93 (13.30 to 14.58)), solid organ transplant (18–49 years: 6.21 (5.38 to 7.12); 70–79 years: 17.54 (14.65 to 20.77)) and SLE (18–49 years: 7.98 (6.91 to 9.13); 60–64 years: 14.48 (11.46 to 17.94)). It should be noted that classifying individuals according to the first immunocompromising condition or procedure recorded may have led to underestimation of the HZ IRs in conditions or procedures that typically occur later in the disease course (eg, HZ IRs are likely underestimated in HSCT since this occurs after a haematological malignancy diagnosis, and also in solid organ transplant, since this occurs after a solid organ malignancy diagnosis).

CD and IC-free/CD-free populations

In every age group, higher overall HZ IRs (unadjusted and age-adjusted) were observed in adults with chronic conditions compared with those without immunocompromising or chronic conditions (figure 2; online supplemental table 2). In the CD population, HZ IRs increased with age, from 2.45 (95% CI 2.42 to 2.48) in the 18–49 years group to 11.54 (11.40 to 11.68) in the ≥80 years group; a similar trend was also observed in the IC-free/CD-free population (1.43 (1.41 to 1.44) and 8.57 (8.33 to 8.82), respectively) (figure 2; online supplemental table 2). The annual IRs remained similar each calendar year (2012–2019) for all age groups in both the CD and IC-free/CD-free populations (figure 3).

The HZ IRs varied between chronic conditions, with the highest rates observed in adults with CKD (18–49 years: 3.36 (95% CI 3.07 to 3.66); ≥80 years: 11.37 (11.05 to 11.69)), COPD (18–49 years: 2.50 (2.10 to 2.94); ≥80 years: 12.31 (11.69 to 12.96)), heart failure (18–49 years: 2.41 (1.79 to 3.16); ≥80 years: 11.54 (10.85 to 12.27)) and coronary artery disease (18–49 years: 2.34 (2.02 to 2.70); ≥80 years: 11.80 (11.49 to 12.11)) (figure 5; online supplemental table 2).

Incidence rates of HZ in each CD population versus the IC-free/CD-free population, by age. CD, chronic disease; HZ, herpes zoster; IC, immunocompromised; IR, incidence rate; PY, person-years.

In adults with CKD, higher HZ IRs were observed as CKD stage progressed from stage 1 to stage 5: 4.24 (95% CI 3.48 to 5.10) in stage 1, 6.79 (6.32 to 7.27) in stage 2, 9.35 (9.14 to 9.55) in stage 3, 10.76 (9.87 to 11.69) in stage 4 and 12.07 (10.94 to 13.26) in stage 5. The same pattern was observed across age groups. For example, in adults with CKD aged 18–49 years, HZ IRs were 2.54 (1.74 to 3.54) in stage 1, 2.32 (1.75 to 3.01) in stage 2, 2.95 (2.36 to 3.62) in stage 3, 3.06 (1.58 to 5.19) in stage 4 and 7.72 (6.14 to 9.53) in stage 5. In adults with CKD aged ≥80 years, HZ IRs were 9.95 (4.55 to 18.15) in stage 1, 11.53 (9.72 to 13.54) in stage 2, 11.26 (10.91 to 11.61) in stage 3, 12.73 (11.41 to 14.14) in stage 4 and 11.90 (8.99 to 15.34) in stage 5. Younger adults (18–49 years) with more advanced CKD had higher observed HZ IRs than older adults with less advanced CKD. Specifically, adults aged 18–49 years with CKD stage 5 had an HZ IR of 7.72 (6.14 to 9.53), while adults aged 50–59 years with CKD stage 1, 2, 3 or 4 had HZ IRs of 3.79 (2.38 to 5.64), 4.58 (3.82 to 5.43), 5.32 (4.77 to 5.90) and 6.63 (4.29 to 9.63), and adults aged 60–64 years with CKD stage 1, 2 or 3 had HZ IRs of 4.19 (2.01 to 7.43), 6.79 (5.56 to 8.18) and 6.96 (6.30 to 7.65), respectively.

Herpes zoster incidence rate ratios

The adjusted IRR of HZ in the IC population versus the IC-free/CD-free population was 1.73 (95% CI 1.73 to 1.74), indicating that the observed incidence of HZ was 73% higher in the IC population compared with the IC-free/CD-free population. The adjusted IRR of HZ in the CD population versus the IC-free/CD-free population was 1.47 (95% CI 1.47 to 1.48), indicating that the observed incidence of HZ was 47% higher in the CD population compared with the IC-free/CD-free population.

HZ complications

In all age groups, a higher percentage of each type of HZ complication was reported in IC versus IC-free adults (table 2). In the IC population, within 90–365 days post-HZ index, PHN was reported in 1.16% (95% CI 0.92% to 1.46%) of adults with HZ aged 18–49 years and 7.26% (6.70% to 7.87%) in those aged ≥80 years (table 2). This compared with 0.86% (0.78% to 0.94%) of those aged 18–49 years and 6.55% (6.16% to 6.97%) of those aged ≥80 years in the IC-free population (table 2). When extending the PHN definition to include the first 90 days post-HZ (0–365 days post-HZ index), PHN was reported in 18.89% (17.93% to 19.89%) of IC adults aged 18–49 years and 37.30% (36.21% to 38.39%) of those aged ≥80 years (table 2). Most PHN cases were therefore reported in the first 90 days of the HZ index date. Other HZ complications were less common. Among IC adults with HZ, 0.56% (0.40% to 0.77%) of those aged 18–49 years and 1.53% (1.28% to 1.83%) of those aged ≥80 years reported neurological (excluding PHN) complications within the first 365 days of the HZ index date; corresponding values were 1.84% (1.54% to 2.21%) and 4.36% (3.92% to 4.84%) for ocular complications, 0.08% (0.03% to 0.19%) and 0.00% (0.00% to 0.05%) for disseminated HZ, and 0.16% (0.09% to 0.30%) and 0.36% (0.25% to 0.52%) for other HZ complications (table 2).

The proportion of all HZ complications among adults with HZ was generally higher in the CD population compared with the IC-free and IC-free/CD-free populations (table 2). The proportion of HZ complications increased with age across the populations (table 2). A total of 1.16% (95% CI 1.04% to 1.30%) of adults aged 18–49 years with HZ in the CD population had PHN reported within 90–365 days after HZ, compared with 6.92% (6.55% to 7.31%) in those aged ≥80 years (table 2). When extending the definition to include the first 90 days after the HZ index date, PHN was reported in 16.73% (16.29% to 17.19%) of adults in the CD population with HZ aged 18–49 years and 36.28% (35.56% to 37.00%) of those with HZ aged ≥80 years (table 2). Among adults with HZ in the CD population, 0.45% (0.37% to 0.54%) of those aged 18–49 years and 1.58% (1.40% to 1.78%) of those aged ≥80 years had neurological complications reported within 365 days of the HZ index date; corresponding values were 1.36% (1.23% to 1.51%) and 4.13% (3.84% to 4.43%) for ocular complications, and 0.11% (0.08% to 0.16%) and 0.32% (0.25% to 0.42%) for other HZ complications (table 2). Few disseminated complications were reported (table 2). It should be noted that HZ complications were likely underestimated in this study, as they were only recorded in cases with 365 days of follow-up data available after the HZ index date.

HZ recurrence

In every age group, the rate of HZ recurrence was higher in the IC population compared with the IC-free population (table 3). In IC adults aged 18–49 years, the HZ recurrence rate per 1000 PY was 31.58 (95% CI 29.25 to 34.02) compared with 23.43 (22.70 to 24.17) in IC-free adults in the same age group (table 3). Similarly, IC adults aged ≥80 years had an HZ recurrence rate/1000 PY of 30.25 (28.03 to 32.58) while IC-free adults aged ≥80 years had a rate/1000 PY of 23.99 (22.56 to 25.48). HZ recurrence rates in both the IC and IC-free populations remained relatively constant with slight variations between age groups (table 3).

Discussion

This large observational study supports previous evidence demonstrating that the incidence of HZ and proportion of HZ complications increase with age. The study also reported higher observed HZ incidence and proportion of HZ complications in adults with any immunocompromising or chronic condition examined compared with those of a similar age without such conditions, which aligns with previous findings. In adults with at least one of the immunocompromising conditions of interest (IC population), the highest HZ IRs were observed in those with haematological malignancies, polymyalgia rheumatica, solid organ transplant and SLE. In adults with at least one of the chronic conditions of interest (CD population), the highest HZ IRs were observed in those with CKD, COPD, heart failure and coronary artery disease.

In order to appropriately contextualise these findings, it is important to highlight that the IC and CD populations were not mutually exclusive; thus, the HZ IRs observed following the index of the initial condition of interest might partly reflect multimorbidity rather than only the individual condition under which they are described. Furthermore, differences in the clinical profiles and treatment patterns of patients with specific conditions could explain the variation in HZ IRs observed in this study. In addition, HZ IRs are plausibly underestimated in this study for conditions that develop or procedures that are performed during later stages of an individual’s disease course. HZ IRs may also be underestimated in adults without the conditions of interest compared with those in the IC or CD population who would be more likely to see their general practitioner (GP) for the management of these conditions22–24 and may therefore have had HZ identified opportunistically during a routine GP visit.

This study provides an update of HZ IRs among IC populations in England previously reported by Yanni et al for the period 2000–2012.4 Age and sex distributions of the IC populations are comparable between studies. Both studies used the same age group ranges to stratify outcomes, allowing age group-specific HZ IRs to be appropriately compared. The age group-specific HZ IRs in both the IC population and the IC-free population in the present study were similar to those reported by Yanni et al.4 While the IC and IC-free populations were age-matched in the Yanni et al study, they were adjusted for age in the current study.

The age-specific HZ IRs for each immunocompromising condition in the present study were generally comparable to the Yanni et al study,4 with some exceptions that could be explained by differences in design or analytic approaches. For example, the HZ IRs in the HSCT and HIV groups were notably lower in the present study. A key methodological difference between the studies is that Yanni et al included individuals in all applicable immunocompromising condition groups. In contrast, due to its larger size and scope, the present study considered only the first immunocompromising condition of interest (IC population) or the first chronic condition of interest (CD population). Therefore, the present study may have underestimated immunocompromising or chronic conditions that generally occur later in life (or conditions that tend to occur after another condition), as well as underestimating the HZ IRs in adults with these conditions. Specifically, in the IC population, if a haematological malignancy was recorded first, followed by HSCT on a different date, we included the individual in the haematological malignancy group but not in the HSCT group. This partly explains why the overall HZ IR for HSCT in this study (9.39 (7.11 to 12.08)) is considerably lower than that reported by Yanni et al (41.70 (35.72 to 48.40)).4 A similar principle applies to solid organ malignancies and solid organ transplants. If a patient had an earlier recorded date for a solid organ malignancy diagnosis and a later recorded date for a solid organ transplant procedure, we included them only in the solid organ malignancy group. Consequently, any subsequent HZ was captured only in the solid organ malignancy group. A lower HZ IR in the HIV group was also recorded in the present study (6.49 (5.97 to 7.04)) than reported by Yanni et al (11.78 (9.54 to 14.38)). This could be attributed to the reduced burden of HIV over time resulting from progress in preventive interventions, and rapid linkage to care and treatment to support HIV viral suppression.25

The proportion of adults with observed HZ who experienced complications increased with age and was higher in individuals with immunocompromising or chronic conditions than in those without. The rate of HZ recurrence was higher among IC adults than among IC-free adults. HZ recurrence rates in both populations were similar across age groups.

The increased incidence of HZ across several immunocompromising conditions has been reported previously.4–8 13 The current study supports and updates the existing evidence base. Our understanding is that the current study is the largest to report HZ IRs among adults with chronic conditions in England. It included millions of adults with at least one chronic condition of interest and higher HZ IRs were observed in these adults with at least one chronic condition of interest compared with adults of a similar age without the same and other conditions, particularly at an older age. A higher incidence of HZ was observed in adults with CKD, COPD, heart failure and coronary artery disease. While the unadjusted overall HZ IRs are confounded by age since individuals with CKD, COPD, heart failure and coronary artery disease are older at index than those with other chronic conditions, the age-adjusted HZ IRs show the same pattern. Adults with CKD had the highest overall observed HZ IR among the chronic conditions examined. Younger adults (18–49, 50–59 and 60–64 years) with CKD also had the highest observed HZ IRs compared with adults in the same age group with other chronic conditions. Furthermore, younger adults with more advanced CKD (18–49 years, stage 5) had higher observed HZ IRs than some older adults with less advanced CKD (eg, 50–59 years, stages 1–4; 60–64 years, stages 1–3). Among older adults (70–79 and ≥80 years), the highest HZ IRs were observed for asthma and COPD. The data for specific chronic conditions should be carefully interpreted and account for both known HZ risk factors like age and the specific condition to appropriately contextualise the higher observed incidence of HZ in patients with these conditions.

Previous studies show that individuals with a chronic condition are at higher risk of developing HZ than those without a chronic condition.6–8 A UK case–control study that used data from the CPRD reported a 19%–34% higher risk of HZ in individuals with COPD, asthma, CKD and depression, with the highest risk for COPD.8 The results from the current study, which include HZ IRs specifically, broadly align with these findings.

Given that individuals in the IC or CD populations may have had several immunocompromising or chronic conditions, it is possible that a combination of conditions contributed to the higher observed incidence of HZ seen in individual conditions and populations. A previous retrospective cohort study on the risk of HZ in adults in Spain showed that multimorbidity increased the incidence of HZ, with the highest incidence observed in those with three or more comorbidities of interest (diabetes, COPD, heart disease (ischaemic and other), immunocompromising condition and kidney disease).26 Given that the IC and CD populations in our study were not mutually exclusive, a combination of immunocompromising and chronic conditions could have contributed to the higher incidence of HZ observed in both populations. It is worth noting, however, that there were 1 764 900 adults in the IC population and 6 423 633 adults in the CD population. Even considering potential complete overlap of the IC population with the CD population, this would leave 4 658 733 adults in the CD population who did not have an immunocompromising condition of interest; these adults could plausibly contribute to the higher HZ IRs observed in the CD population.

The data from the present study support the continued need for HZ prevention strategies in IC adults, including consideration of expansion of HZ vaccination programmes to all IC adults. Vaccination against HZ for individuals with severe IC conditions has been part of the UK’s National Immunisation Programme since 2021, and the recommended age for vaccination of these individuals has reduced from 70 years to 18 years over time.2 15 16 18 Furthermore, given the higher incidence of HZ also observed in individuals with chronic conditions in our study, it is important that attention is given to these populations. This knowledge could inform further research, clinical guidelines and immunisation recommendations.

Study strengths

This study has several strengths. Importantly, CPRD Aurum, the primary data source, is large and broadly representative of the UK population.21 Although CPRD-HES linked data are only available for practices that choose to participate, 74% of individuals in the CPRD were included in the CPRD-HES linkage as of March 2024.19 Almost all individuals in the CPRD have linkage to ONS data, minimising selection bias due to the requirement for HES and ONS linkage. Additionally, the study size, both overall and for each population of interest, is a major strength. We included 1 764 900 participants in the IC population, 6 423 633 in the CD population, 12 867 750 in the IC-free population and 8 235 858 in the IC-free/CD-free population. The IC population in this study was almost three times the size of the IC population in the previous study by Yanni et al.4 Not only did we include patients potentially at risk of HZ based on their immunocompromising or chronic condition status, but we also included reference groups of those without the same immunocompromising conditions and a subgroup of individuals without immunocompromising or chronic conditions of interest. This approach supports data interpretation and highlights the non-negligible incidence of HZ, even in populations without immunocompromising or chronic conditions.

Further, this study provides the most recent and robust data on HZ incidence, complications and recurrence in England across a number of immunocompromising and chronic conditions. As such, any impact of recent developments in HZ management is likely to be accounted for in the results. Moreover, the extended follow-up of up to 8 years allowed us to examine HZ, its complications and recurrence over a longer period. The methodology allowed for accurate reporting of HZ IRs in younger age groups by accounting for changing age groups in the analysis of overall (non-age stratified) HZ IRs. For individuals who initially belonged to one age group at index but transitioned to the next age group during follow-up (eg, an individual 48 years old at index and included in the 18–49 years group who turned 50 years old during follow-up before any occurrence of HZ), we censored the follow-up time in the initial age group and began follow-up in the subsequent age group on their applicable birthday. This was important, as individuals could realistically transition between age groups (especially those with narrow ranges) over the 8-year follow-up period.

Limitations

Given that the mean index age varies by condition, data on overall unadjusted HZ IRs in individual conditions should be interpreted with caution as they are confounded by age. For example, at index, adults with haematological malignancies had a mean (SD) age of 62.21 (18.19) years compared with 69.96 (17.05) years for adults with CKD. Examining the overall age-adjusted HZ IRs in each study population is more appropriate, as is comparing age group-specific data between conditions.

We only considered the first immunocompromising or chronic condition, unless two or more conditions of interest occurred on the same date. As a result, HZ IRs are likely underestimated for any subsequent immunocompromising or chronic conditions of interest that developed during the follow-up period. Further research could address this underestimation by including HZ cases in all applicable immunocompromising or chronic condition groups. However, this compromise allowed us to efficiently conduct this large and complex study.

The IC and CD populations were not mutually exclusive in this study as real-world patients often have comorbidities. Furthermore, vaccine recommendations are based on those who have selected conditions rather than those who have selected conditions without other conditions. If we had limited the IC population to patients with an immunocompromising condition but no chronic condition, it would not include all true IC individuals; likewise, a population with a chronic condition but not an immunocompromising condition would not include all true CD individuals. This might have resulted in inaccurate estimation of true HZ incidence.

It could be argued that a CD-free population whereby adults with CDs of interest would not be included would have been a better comparator for the CD population than the IC-free/CD-free population that we used. However, we considered this to be inappropriate as a CD-free population could still have immunocompromising conditions, and given higher HZ IRs have been observed in individuals with immunocompromising conditions,5 13 a CD-free comparator would not necessarily help assess the higher HZ incidence observed in adults with chronic conditions.

Reassuringly, a US-based real-world cohort study examining the HZ IRs in adults with a specified CD (and without specified immunocompromising or autoimmune conditions) reported IRs that were comparable to those in our study.27 For example, adults with CKD aged 50–59 years in our study had an HZ IR of 5.66 (95% CI 5.27 to 6.06) compared with 6.36 (95% CI 5.64 to 7.14) in the US study.27 IRs in adults with CKD aged 70–79 years were 10.29 (95% CI 9.93 to 10.66) in our study versus 10.12 (95% CI 9.29 to 11.00) in the US study, and in those aged ≥80 years were 11.37 (95% CI 11.05 to 11.69) in our study versus 10.44 (9.69 to 11.24) in the US study.27 This indicates that the observed incidence of HZ is comparable in populations with a specified chronic condition, regardless of the presence or absence of immunocompromising or autoimmune conditions. The US study also reported an HZ IR of 3.24 (95% CI 3.17 to 3.30) in the 50–59 years age group among immunocompetent adults without chronic conditions compared with 3.26 (95% CI 3.21 to 3.31) in our study in the analogous IC-free/CD-free population.28 However, the observed HZ IRs were higher in our study versus the US study in the 70–79 years age group (7.76 (95% CI 7.61 to 7.92) vs 4.62 (95% CI 4.31 to 4.94)) and ≥80 years age group (8.57 (95% CI 8.33 to 8.82) vs 4.67 (95% CI 4.26 to 5.11)).28

The HZ IRRs calculated in this study were adjusted for age group and sex only, and did not account for other confounders such as healthcare utilisation, multimorbidity burden, concomitant therapy, smoking, frailty and socioeconomic factors. Therefore, the adjusted IRRs represent only partially adjusted comparisons and should not be interpreted as estimates of an independent association between immunocompromising or chronic conditions and the observed incidence of HZ.

We used diagnostic or procedure codes to identify immunocompromising and chronic conditions or procedures, whereas other studies have used definitions that included treatment codes for these conditions or disease scores,8 13 which might have identified more patients than would be identified using only diagnostic or procedure codes. This might explain why some of the IRs observed in the present study were lower than those found in other studies.13

Use of CPRD Aurum with HES and ONS linkage is both a strength and limitation. Although we utilised patient data deemed acceptable for research, there are limitations inherent in any analysis of data extracted retrospectively from an existing clinical database. CPRD Aurum is populated by GPs for clinical rather than research purposes, and misclassification of the study exposures (immunocompromising or chronic conditions) and outcomes (HZ, HZ complications, HZ recurrence) is possible. We reported HZ IRs and proportions over a fixed follow-up period, which helped reduce bias from differential follow-up between participants. However, this study cannot eliminate biases associated with using routine data sources. The data sources only capture routinely reported conditions and HZ cases, so it was not possible to capture all patients with the conditions of interest and HZ cases in this study.

HZ complications were only examined in HZ cases with 365 days of follow-up data available after the HZ index date, and were therefore underestimated in this study. Specifically, among immunocompromised adults with incident HZ, 75% had 365 days of follow-up data available post-HZ index and 25% were therefore not included in the HZ complications analyses; among IC-free adults with incident HZ, 78% had 365 days of follow-up data post-HZ index and 22% were therefore not included in the HZ complications analyses. Although we considered calculating rates to account for differential follow-up time between HZ patients, we did not do so as some HZ patients had their HZ event confirmed in the study using an HZ complication code, and the time from HZ to complication would therefore have been systematically underestimated in these patients. A fixed follow-up period for the evaluation of HZ complications was preferred, as it minimised bias from differential follow-up between participants and avoided systematically underestimating the time from HZ to complication in patients with an HZ complication code but no HZ code.

Further research

Further research may benefit from a prospective design to enable fuller capture of HZ cases and associated complications. Further research could also explore the incidence of HZ and associated complications in patients with particular chronic conditions to identify further subgroups with higher observed HZ IRs. In a similar study, it would be beneficial to match IC and IC-free populations at index based on age and any other known risk factors for HZ to enhance the comparability of populations. Any subsequent conditions of interest that developed during study follow-up could be included in all applicable condition groups to avoid underestimation of HZ IRs.

Conclusion

This very large, up-to-date, real-world study observed higher HZ IRs in adults in England with immunocompromising conditions compared with those of a similar age without the same conditions, and higher HZ IRs were observed in adults in England with chronic conditions compared with those of a similar age without the same and other conditions. These findings may inform further research, clinical guidelines and immunisation policy discussions. Online supplemental figure 2 summarises the context, outcomes and impact of this study.

The study protocol (24_004090) titled ‘Burden of disease of herpes zoster among adults aged 18 years old and older at increased risk due to an immunocompromising or chronic condition in England: a retrospective cohort study’ was approved via the RDG process on 11 June 2024 (https://www.cprd.com/approved-studies/burden-disease-herpes-zoster-among-adults-aged-18-years-old-and-older-increased).
