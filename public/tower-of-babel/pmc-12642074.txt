Sustainable development goal 3: is Ghana on track with neonatal mortality?
Authors: Zakariya Kuupah Adama, Sabina Abuga, Solomon Opoku, Joel Welbeck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite progress in child health, deaths occurring in the neonatal period remain a major contributor to under-five mortality in Ghana, posing a significant public health challenge. SDG 3.2 aims to reduce neonatal mortality to at least as low as 12 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2030. This study investigates the trends and progress of neonatal mortality rates in Ghana from 1960 to 2022, with an emphasis on assessing the country’s advancement toward achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2. Data were obtained from the World Development Indicators (WDI) and processed using Microsoft Excel and analyzed using R statistical software. The AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) time series model was employed as the statistical model for this study. The results show a consistent decline in neonatal mortality over the study period. In 2023, the rate was estimated at 20.59 deaths per 1,000 live births, and it is projected to decline further to 17.60 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2030. While this downward trend is encouraging, the 95% confidence intervals show considerable uncertainty: the 2030 upper bound remains well above the SDG 3.2 target, while the lower bound is only marginally above it. This indicates that Ghana may come close to the goal under favorable conditions, but is not assured of meeting it unless the rate of progress is significantly accelerated. These findings offer critical insights for policymakers and stakeholders, emphasizing the need for intensified efforts and strategic health interventions to achieve meaningful reductions in neonatal mortality by 2030.

Introduction

The birth of a child is generally considered as a moment of joy and new beginnings. However, for many families in Ghana and across sub-Saharan Africa, this occasion is often clouded by the devastating reality of neonatal loss. Neonatal mortality, defined as the death of a child within the first 28 days of life, remains a significant public health concern and a critical indicator of a country’s healthcare system performance [32]. Despite decades of global and national investment in maternal and child health, neonatal deaths continue to account for a substantial proportion of under-five mortality in most developing countries particularly in Ghana (Ghana Statistical Service, Ghana Health Service, & ICF, 2015). These deaths are often preventable with timely, equitable access to quality care, improved service delivery, and sustained health system strengthening [10].

Globally, progress in reducing neonatal mortality has lagged behind the reduction in post-neonatal and under-five mortality over the past three decades [11, 18]. While the number of neonatal deaths fell from 4.6 million in 1990 to 3.3 million in 2009, the global neonatal mortality rate (NMR) only declined by 28% during that period, with sub-Saharan Africa experiencing a more modest reduction of 17.6% [29]. By 2017, the global NMR had further declined to 18 deaths per 1,000 live births representing a 51% decline since 1990 [18]. Despite this progress, regional disparities remain stark, and projections indicate that millions of neonatal deaths will persist through 2030 without improved interventions [18]. According to World Development Indicators data from the World Bank, in Ghana, the neonatal mortality rate has also declined over the past three decades from 42 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1990 to 21.1 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2022. However, this rate remains above the SDG 3.2 target of 12 per 1,000 by 2030, underscoring the need for accelerated progress.

The country has made measurable efforts in reducing under-five and neonatal, and life expectancy has improved notably between 1995 and 2014 [36]. Yet, neonatal deaths remain disproportionately high. Ghana’s healthcare interventions including the establishment of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), the Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) programme, and the expansion of health infrastructure have improved access but have not consistently translated into equitable outcomes [14]. Systemic inefficiencies, funding shortfalls, high out-of-pocket health expenditures, and disparities in service delivery continue to undermine neonatal health outcomes.

The leading causes of neonatal deaths are well-documented, with preterm birth complications, intrapartum-related events, and neonatal infections such as sepsis and pneumonia dominating the early and late neonatal periods globally [23, 31]. Preterm birth complications alone accounted for nearly 0.99 million neonatal deaths in 2013, followed by intrapartum-related events (0.64 million) and severe infections (0.43 million) [31]. Alarmingly, in regions such as Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, mortality related to preterm birth has continued to rise, highlighting persistent structural vulnerabilities and the need for urgent targeted interventions [7, 30]. In Ghana, similar trends are observed, with preterm birth complications, birth asphyxia, and neonatal sepsis among the top causes of neonatal mortality. According to UNICEF, preterm birth alone contributes to more than 30% of neonatal deaths, followed by complications during delivery and infections [36].

In response to these persistent challenges, global health initiatives such as the Every Newborn Action Plan (ENAP) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) have placed neonatal health at the forefront of international development efforts. ENAP, launched in 2014, emphasized high-impact interventions around birth and care for small and sick newborns [22]. In Ghana, ENAP was adopted and is reflected in key national health policies such as the National Newborn Health Strategy and Action Plan (2019–2023), which outlines interventions including essential newborn care, kangaroo mother care, neonatal resuscitation, and improved referral systems. SDG target 3.2 calls for ending preventable deaths of newborns by 2030, setting a target of reducing neonatal mortality to at least as low as 12 deaths per 1,000 live births [18]. However, over 60 countries including Ghana will need to substantially improve their efforts to meet this target [18]. Even in regions like the Americas, where neonatal mortality declined by 38.3% between 2000 and 2020, significant inequalities remain, driven by broader socioeconomic and systemic health disparities [12]. In addition to clinical interventions, recent studies in Ghana have underscored the role of individual, household, and institutional factors in shaping child health outcomes and supporting government policies aimed at reducing neonatal and child mortality. For instance, Turkson and Ahiabor [35] examined the influence of natal care services and maternity leave policies on child morbidity. The study found that the absence of postnatal care within the first week of delivery leads to a 3% increase in the likelihood of ARI among children under five. Furthermore, children of mothers on unpaid maternity leave faced a 3.9% higher risk of ARI compared to those whose mothers received paid leave.

Achieving further reductions in neonatal mortality will require integrated, equity-focused strategies that address medical, social, and structural determinants of health. These include strengthening health systems, expanding universal health coverage, investing in skilled frontline providers, ensuring quality care during labor, delivery, and the early postnatal period [5, 11, 23]. In Ghana, systemic interventions such as improved insurance coverage and the implementation of predictive, preventive, and personalized medicine (PPPM) approaches are essential to support vulnerable populations.

Against this backdrop, understanding the historical trajectory and forecasting the future course of neonatal mortality in Ghana is crucial. Historical analyses offer valuable insights into the impact of past health interventions, social and political transitions, and economic shifts on neonatal outcomes. From the post-independence expansion of health services to the adoption of primary healthcare strategies and more recent interventions, Ghana’s neonatal health profile reflects a complex relationship of progress and persistent gaps [14, 17]. Therefore, this study undertakes a comprehensive modeling of neonatal mortality trends in Ghana from 1960 to 2025 using robust ARIMA time-series forecasting method. Drawing on longitudinal national data from the World Bank, the study aims to: elucidate long-term patterns in neonatal mortality and generate data-driven forecasts to inform policy and accelerate progress toward SDG 3.2. This study is guided by the research question: Is Ghana on track to meet the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 target of reducing neonatal mortality to 12 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2030?

This study provides a data-driven assessment of Ghana’s progress toward SDG 3.2, offering empirical forecasts of neonatal mortality that reveal whether the country is on track to meet its 2030 target. Unlike previous descriptive analyses, it applies ARIMA time-series modeling to generate evidence-based projections, thereby quantifying the pace of improvement and identifying potential policy gaps. The findings contribute to the discourse on child survival and health system performance by translating statistical evidence into actionable insights for targeted interventions, equitable resource allocation, and continuous monitoring within low- and middle-income settings.

Materials and methods

Data

This study utilized data obtained from the World Development Indicators (WDI), a comprehensive database managed by the World Bank that compiles key economic, social, and health statistics from a range of national and international sources. For Ghana, neonatal mortality data reported in the WDI are primarily derived from the Ghana Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), and estimates from the United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME). These sources draw on household surveys, civil registration systems, and statistical modeling where direct data are limited. Specifically, the dataset used in this study comprises annual neonatal mortality rates from 1960 to 2022, defined as the number of deaths within the first 28 days of life per 1,000 live births. These indicators are essential for evaluating child survival and overall healthcare system performance.

Before conducting the analysis, the dataset downloaded from the World Development Indicators (WDI) was cleaned, edited, and structured in a single Excel sheet. The neonatal mortality data were organized chronologically by year, covering the period from 1960 to 2022. Each entry corresponded to a specific year and the associated neonatal mortality rate for Ghana. During the cleaning process, missing values in the dataset were identified and replaced using the mean imputation method to ensure continuity in the time series. These steps were performed using Microsoft Excel and the R (version 4.3.2) statistical software environment.

Descriptive analysis was carried out to visualize and understand the historical trends of neonatal mortality. This included graphical representation to observe patterns over time. Microsoft Excel was used primarily for initial data structuring and exploratory plots, while R was employed for advanced statistical analysis and modeling.

The ARIMA model

To model and project neonatal mortality rates into the future, specifically for the year 2030, the AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) time series model was employed. ARIMA is widely recognized in public health research for its effectiveness in forecasting based on historical data, particularly when analyzing non-stationary time series. The model leverages past values and lagged moving averages to identify patterns and predict future outcomes, making it well-suited for short-term forecasting.

The ARIMA model is advantageous in that it does not require explanatory variables, relying solely on the internal structure of the time series. This makes it a suitable choice for estimating future neonatal mortality rates, especially in contexts where longitudinal covariate data may be incomplete or inconsistent. Its ability to model trend and seasonality through differencing enables ARIMA to effectively capture the dynamic nature of public health indicators such as neonatal mortality. Previous studies have validated ARIMA’s utility for child health forecasting in Sub-Saharan Africa. For example, Eke and Ewere [15] applied ARIMA (2,1,1) to forecast under-five mortality in Nigeria, while Adeyinka and Muhajarine [2] compared different models and affirmed ARIMA’s robustness for non-stationary child health data. In Tanzania, Mwijalilege et al. [28] found ARIMA effective for short-term mortality trend forecasting.

In the Ghanaian context, Adama et al. [1] applied ARIMA models to predict under-five mortality rates in Ghana, Niger, Nigeria, and Sierra Leone, demonstrating its robustness across countries. However, there is a literature vacuum on the application of ARIMA modeling to neonatal mortality in Ghana. This distinction is important given that neonatal mortality, which refers to deaths within the first 28 days of life, has distinct causes, dynamics, and policy implications compared to broader under-five mortality. Thus, this study contributes uniquely to the literature by focusing exclusively on neonatal mortality using time series forecasting in Ghana, helping to fill a methodological and empirical gap.

Given these empirical precedents, the ARIMA model was selected as the optimal forecasting tool for this study. It enabled the researcher to estimate the future trajectory of neonatal mortality in Ghana, particularly as the country approaches the 2030 target of Sustainable Development Goal 3.2, which calls for ending preventable deaths of newborns and ensuring equitable survival opportunities during the earliest and most vulnerable stage of life.

For the time series forecasting, the AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model was applied to the cleaned data. To ensure robust model selection and validation, the dataset was split into a training set (1960–2009) and a testing set (2010–2022). This allowed for in-sample forecasting and model evaluation by comparing predicted values against actual data in the test period. Once the model was validated for accuracy, out-of-sample forecasting was conducted to project neonatal mortality trends from 2023 to 2030. This forward-looking analysis supports the study’s objective of assessing Ghana’s progress toward achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2.

In univariate ARIMA modeling, it is essential that the time series data be stationary or capable of being transformed into a stationary form. The Box-Jenkins ARIMA methodology involves four key stages: model identification, estimation, diagnostic checking, and forecasting. According to Aue and Horváth [4], a time series is considered stationary when its mean, variance, and covariance remain constant over time. This implies that the statistical properties of the data do not change throughout the observed period. To assess stationarity, several techniques are employed, including visual inspection through time series plots, the use of correlograms displaying the Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF), and the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, which serves as a formal statistical test for stationarity.

The ARIMA model combines three key components: AutoRegressive (AR), Moving Average (MA), and Integration (I). It captures the underlying patterns in a time series by accounting for past values (AR), past forecast errors (MA), and differencing to achieve stationarity (I). An ARIMA model is typically denoted as ARIMA(p, d, q), where p represents the order of the autoregressive terms, d the degree of differencing, and q the order of the moving average terms. Structural break analysis

Given the long time span of the neonatal mortality data (1960–2022) and the likelihood of major policy or economic interventions affecting its trajectory, a Bai-Perron multiple breakpoint test (Bai & Perron, 2003) was applied to detect structural changes in the time series. The test was implemented using the breakpoints() function in the strucchange package in R. This approach identifies multiple breakpoints by minimizing the residual sum of squares (RSS) and selecting the optimal number using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The analysis identified breakpoints in 1974, 1983, 1992, 2001, and 2012. Since the 2001 breakpoint is proximate to the introduction of Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) in 2003, the series was segmented into two sub-periods: pre-NHIS (1960–2002) and post-NHIS (2003–2022). Separate ARIMA models were then fitted to each sub-period to account for potential changes in the underlying data-generating process.

Discussion

The time series analysis of neonatal mortality rates in Ghana reveals a consistent declining trend over time, as shown in Fig. 1. This finding aligns with the results of Kayode et al. [21], who reported that since 1988, the annual average rate of decline in neonatal mortality in Ghana has been approximately 0.6%. Notably, between 1988 and 1989, neonatal mortality declined significantly from 48 to 33 deaths per 1,000 live births. The observed declining trend in neonatal mortality rates in Ghana from 1960 to 2022 aligns with global and regional patterns reported in the literature. According to the World Health Organization (2023), neonatal mortality rates have been steadily declining globally due to improvements in maternal and neonatal health services, expanded immunization coverage, and better management of birth complications. Ghana’s downward trajectory mirrors this global progress and emphasizes the country’s health sector gains, particularly in antenatal care coverage, skilled birth attendance, and early postnatal care.

The trend analysis presented in Fig. 1 indicates that the neonatal mortality data exhibits signs of non-stationarity. In time series modeling, stationarity is a fundamental assumption that underlies the validity of many statistical forecasting techniques, including the ARIMA model. A stationary time series is one whose statistical properties such as mean, variance, and autocorrelation remain constant over time. This ensures that model parameters are stable, allowing for reliable forecasting based on historical patterns [16]. Conversely, non-stationary data may display trends, seasonality, or shifts in variance, potentially leading to misleading results if not appropriately addressed [6].

Ensuring stationarity is crucial, as it allows the forecasting model to capture the underlying stochastic processes without being distorted by deterministic trends [34]. Both the ADF and KPSS tests are widely used tools for diagnosing stationarity and guiding the necessary data transformations prior to model estimation [20]. However, a key limitation of the ARIMA model is its reliance on stationarity—non-stationary data must be differenced or transformed before the model can be applied effectively. Additionally, ARIMA models are purely univariate and do not account for the influence of external explanatory variables, which may be important in public health contexts where socioeconomic, environmental, or policy factors impact outcomes. ARIMA also assumes linear relationships within the time series and may not capture complex nonlinear dynamics or structural breaks. As such, future research could explore alternative or complementary approaches such as ARIMAX models (which incorporate external regressors), structural time series models, or machine learning-based methods like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks that can handle nonlinear patterns and long-range dependencies. These methods may offer enhanced flexibility and predictive power, particularly in dynamic health systems.

The best ARIMA model was selected from the fitted ARIMA models using the AIC, AICC, and BIC values. The model with the smallest AIC is considered to be the best and, in the case, where the AIC values are same for more than one model, we consider the BIC or AICC of the models. These criteria are widely used in time series analysis to balance model fit and complexity [20]. The AIC, developed by Akaike [3], is particularly effective for comparing models based on their goodness of fit while penalizing for the number of estimated parameters. Models with lower AIC values are generally preferred. However, when comparing models fitted to small sample sizes, AICC (a correction of AIC for small samples) provides a more accurate estimate of model quality [19]. Additionally, the BIC, proposed by Schwarz [33], imposes a stronger penalty on model complexity and is often used when model parsimony is emphasized.

Based on these results, the ARIMA (1,1,1) model was selected as the best-fitting model, as it had the smallest AIC value. According to the model selection theory, when the AIC values of multiple models are similar, it is advisable to consider AICC and BIC to determine the more parsimonious model [20]. However, in this case, ARIMA (1,1,1) demonstrated a clear advantage in terms of AIC and did not require further differentiation through AICC or BIC. The selection of the ARIMA (1,1,1) model aligns with findings in existing literature, where similar models have proven effective in capturing and forecasting trends in health-related time series data [9, 25]. This underscores the importance of model selection criteria in ensuring that the forecasting model not only fits the historical data well but also generalizes effectively for predictive purposes.

After selecting the optimal ARIMA (1,1,1) model for forecasting neonatal mortality in Ghana, residual diagnostics were conducted to validate the model’s adequacy. The diagnostic checks focused on the autocorrelation structure of the residuals using the Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and the Ljung-Box Q test at lags 10, 15, and 20. The ACF plot of the residuals revealed no significant autocorrelations, indicating that the residuals approximate white noise. This is a critical condition for a well-specified ARIMA model, as residuals should ideally exhibit no autocorrelation, thereby confirming that all systematic patterns in the data have been captured by the model [6].

The use of the Ljung-Box test is well-established in time series analysis for evaluating the randomness of residuals. According to Ljung and Box [26], a well-fitted model should yield residuals that are not significantly different from white noise, which is indicated when the test’s p-values exceed the conventional significance level of 0.05. This ensures that the model has adequately captured all temporal dependencies in the data and can be confidently used for forecasting purposes [34]. The non-significant Ljung-Box test results thus provide strong evidence that the ARIMA (1,1,1) model does not suffer from residual autocorrelation and is statistically sound for predicting future neonatal mortality trends. The in-sample forecast results of the selected ARIMA (1,1,1) model, as presented in Table 4, provide an assessment of the model’s performance using the available historical data. In-sample forecasting involves generating predictions for a time period within the original dataset used for model estimation. This technique is commonly employed to assess how well a model can replicate known values and is a preliminary indicator of forecasting accuracy [20].

The close alignment between the observed and forecasted neonatal mortality rates suggests that the ARIMA (1,1,1) model captures the underlying data structure effectively. The reduced differences between predicted and actual values demonstrate that the model has strong explanatory power and is likely to perform well in out-of-sample forecasting [34]. This level of agreement implies that the model has minimized forecast errors and has adequately accounted for temporal dependencies in the data. A good in-sample performance often indicates that the residuals are randomly distributed and that the model is neither overfitted nor underfitted. According to Box et al. [6], an appropriate ARIMA model should not only fit past data well but should also yield residuals that exhibit no systematic patterns. The results in this study conform to these expectations and reinforce the reliability of the model for forecasting neonatal mortality trends in Ghana.

As presented in Table 6, the MSE, MAPE, RMSE, and MAE values were 0.244, 1.888%, 0.494, and 0.469 respectively. These relatively low values indicate that the model exhibits high predictive accuracy, with forecast errors that are small and well-contained within acceptable limits. MSE measures the average of the squares of the forecast errors and is particularly sensitive to larger errors, thus emphasizing significant deviations between the forecasted and observed values [27]. A MAPE of 1.888% suggests that the forecasted values deviate from the observed values by less than 2% on average, a level of error generally considered excellent in time series forecasting [24]. MAPE values under 10% are often interpreted as highly accurate [8].

The forecast results reveal a continued downward trend in neonatal mortality rates, declining from 20.59 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2023 to an estimated 17.60 per 1,000 live births by 2030. However, the 95% confidence intervals indicate considerable uncertainty. In particular, for 2030 the upper bound (22.57) remains well above the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 threshold of 12 deaths per 1,000 live births, while the lower bound (12.62) is only marginally higher than the target. This implies that, depending on future developments, Ghana’s neonatal mortality rate could range from near-target achievement to levels significantly above it. This supports the research findings of Adama et al. [1] that there is a continuous decline in under-five mortality in Ghana. These findings are consistent with broader regional and global concerns regarding progress toward SDG 3.2. Hug et al. [18] projected that over 60 countries, including Ghana, are not on track to meet the SDG neonatal mortality target unless urgent and improved interventions are implemented. Similarly, UNICEF (2020) emphasized that despite improvements, many sub-Saharan African countries face systemic health system barriers such as inadequate maternal and newborn care, insufficient skilled birth attendance, and poor access to quality health services, all of which hinder faster progress in reducing neonatal deaths. Ghana, though having made commendable efforts in improving child health outcomes, continues to experience challenges in achieving significant reductions in neonatal mortality. Factors such as rural-urban disparities, maternal education, and access to skilled delivery services have been shown to significantly influence neonatal outcomes [13].

The detection of a structural break around 2001 aligns with the introduction of the NHIS in 2003, suggesting a shift in the underlying mortality trend. The lower variability and improved model fit in the post-NHIS period may indicate that expanded access to healthcare services contributed to a more stable and sustained reduction in neonatal mortality. While causality cannot be definitively established, the temporal correspondence between the structural break and a major health policy reform strengthens the plausibility of a policy-driven impact.

Conclusion and policy recommendations

This study employed the ARIMA time series modeling approach to analyze and forecast neonatal mortality rates in Ghana, with the overarching goal of evaluating the country’s progress toward achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2. The historical trend from 1960 to 2022 revealed a gradual decline in neonatal mortality rates, and projections from 2023 to 2030 suggest a continued downward trajectory. The ARIMA (1,1,1) model was selected as the best-fitting model based on model selection criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), corrected AIC (AICC), and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The model passed all residual diagnostic tests, including the Ljung-Box Q test, which indicated no significant autocorrelation in the residuals, affirming the model’s adequacy for forecasting. Furthermore, performance evaluation metrics such as the Mean Square Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) confirmed the model’s reliability, with low error values indicating high forecast accuracy.

The out-sample forecasts indicate that Ghana’s neonatal mortality rate is on a declining path, falling from 20.59 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2023 to a projected 17.60 in 2030. While this downward trend is encouraging, the 95% confidence interval for 2030 (12.62–22.57) reveals substantial uncertainty. The upper bound remains well above the SDG 3.2 target of 12 deaths per 1,000 live births, while the lower bound is only marginally above it. This suggests that, although Ghana may come close to the target under optimal conditions, there is also a plausible risk of under-performance. Achieving the SDG goal will therefore require not only sustaining current progress but also accelerating the pace of improvement through targeted and effective interventions.

Given the forecast results and the uncertainty ranges, interventions should be prioritized based on their potential for rapid and substantial impact. High-yield strategies include scaling up access to skilled birth attendants and ensuring early postnatal care coverage, as these have been shown to deliver significant reductions in neonatal mortality within short timeframes. Investments in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and community-based newborn care programs should be intensified in high-burden regions, particularly rural and underserved districts where improvements have lagged. Prioritizing these interventions is critical because they directly target the major risk factors for neonatal deaths in Ghana, such as birth asphyxia, sepsis, and complications from preterm birth.

To ensure sustained progress and accountability, a national monitoring and evaluation (M&E) framework is recommended. This framework should incorporate annual neonatal mortality audits at the district and regional levels and integrate real-time surveillance data through the Ghana Health Service’s District Health Information Management System (DHIMS2). It should also include periodic policy reviews linked to key performance indicators such as the coverage of skilled birth attendance, NICU availability, and postnatal care uptake. Additionally, collaboration with research institutions such as the University of Ghana Medical Centre and the Noguchi Memorial Institute is essential to support evidence-based targeting of high-risk populations. With these intensified, data-driven, and well-monitored interventions, Ghana will be better positioned to accelerate reductions in neonatal mortality and achieve the SDG 3.2 target by 2030.
