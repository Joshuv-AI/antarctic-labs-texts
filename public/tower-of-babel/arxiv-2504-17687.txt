The Igusa zeta function of restricted power series over ℚ p \mathbb{Q}_{p}
Leonie Dausy

Abstract
. In this article, we ask whether the Igusa zeta function of a restricted power series over ℚ p \mathbb{Q}_{p} can be determined solely from the terms of degree at most D D . That is, we ask whether the truncated polynomial f D f_{D} , consisting of all terms of f f of degree ≤ D \leq D , yields the same Igusa zeta function as f f for sufficiently large D D . Our main results include a counterexample already in the one-variable case, but also a positive result under the condition that f f is sufficiently non-degenerate with respect to its Newton polyhedron Γ ⁡ ( f ) \Gamma(f) .

1.
Introduction

Igusa introduced the local zeta function in the 1960s as a tool to study Diophantine problems. For a polynomial
f
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the local zeta function is defined by

Z_{f}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|,

where
|dx|
denotes the Haar measure. This integral is directly related to counting the number of solutions of the congruences
f(x)\equiv 0\mod p^{m}
,
m=1,2,3,\dots
, see e.g.
[
4
]
, pp. 97 98. Using resolutions of singularities, Igusa proved that
Z_{f}(s)
is a rational function of
p^{-s}
(see also
[
4
]
). An entirely different proof was obtained ten years later by Denef
[
2
]
using p-adic cell decomposition instead of resolutions of singularities. Despite these theoretical advances, explicitly computing
Z_{f}(s)
for a given function
f
remains a highly nontrivial task. Over the years, a number of results have been established for specific classes of polynomials. In this article, we extend the investigation to the setting of restricted power series over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

Notations.
Fix a prime number
p
and consider a tuple of
M
variables
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{M})
. For
i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{M})
ranging over
\mathbb{N}^{M}
, we use the notation
|i|=i_{1}+\dots+i_{M}
and
X^{i}=X_{1}^{i_{1}}\dots X_{M}^{i_{M}}
.

Definition 1.1
.

The ring
\mathbb{Q}_{p}\{X\}
of restricted power series in
X
over the
p
-adic field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
consists of the formal power series
\sum_{i}a_{i}X^{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[X]]
such that
|a_{i}|_{p}\rightarrow 0
as
|i|\rightarrow\infty
. The subring
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}\{X\}
consists of the series
\sum_{i}a_{i}x^{i}
all of whose coefficients
a_{i}
are in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Let
f=\sum_{i}a_{i}X^{i}
be in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}\{X\}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Then the series
\sum_{i}a_{i}x^{i}
converges to a limit in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

which we denote by
f(x)
. For any such analytic series, we can analogously define the Igusa Zeta functions as for polynomials.

Definition 1.2
.

Consider
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(s)>0
. The Igusa zeta function of
f
is defined as

Z_{f}(s)=Z_{p}(f;s)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

where
|dx|
denotes the Haar measure.

Example 1.1
.

A very standard example is the integral
\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|x^{n}|^{s}|dx|
. Denote
\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\,|\,|x|_{p}=p^{-l}\}
by
A_{l}
and observe that

\displaystyle|dx|(A_{l})

\displaystyle=|dx|\big(\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\,|\,|x|_{p}\leq p^{-l}\}\setminus\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\,|\,|x|_{p}\leq p^{-(l+1)}\}\big)

\displaystyle=p^{-l}-p^{-l-1}=\big(1-\frac{1}{p}\big)p^{-l}.

We can calculate
\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|x^{n}|^{s}|dx|
as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|x^{n}|^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\geq 0}\int_{A_{l}}|x^{n}|^{s}|dx|=\sum_{l\geq 0}p^{-nsl}\int_{A_{l}}|dx|=\sum_{l\geq 0}p^{-nsl}|dx|(A_{l})

\displaystyle=\big(1-\frac{1}{p}\big)\sum_{l\geq 0}p^{-(ns+1)l}.

Main question.
Consider a formal power series
f(X)=\sum_{i}a_{i}X^{i}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
. Does there always exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
D\geq N
, the cut off
f_{D}(X)=\sum_{|i|\leq D}a_{i}X^{i}
of
f
at degree
D
has the same Igusa Zeta function as
f
?

Main result.

A counterexample already exists in the one-variable case. Nonetheless, there is a partial positive result: the answer is yes when
f
and
f^{\prime}
do not vanish simultaneously. This non-degeneracy condition extends to multiple variables, where the condition now becomes: non-degenerate over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with respect to the faces of the Newton polyhedron.

2.
Counterexample to the main question

Consider the series

f(x)=(x-1)^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p^{i}x^{i}.

Since
|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p^{i}x^{i}|_{p}=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we have
\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|(x-1)^{2}|_{p}^{s}|dx|
.
Making a change of variables
x\rightarrow x+1
, we see
\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|x^{2}|_{p}^{s}|dx|
. By example (
1.1
), we get

\displaystyle Z_{f}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\sum_{l\geq 0}p^{-(2s+1)l}.

(1)

Next, an easy calculation shows

\displaystyle f_{2D}(x)=(x-1)^{2}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{2D-2}p^{i}x^{i}\Big)+p^{2D-1}x^{2D-1}(1+(p-2)x).

Observe that if
x\not\equiv 1\mod p
, then
(x-1)^{2}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{2D-2}p^{i}x^{i}\Big)
has valuation zero and
p^{2D-1}x^{2D-1}(1+(p-2)x)
has valuation
\geq 2D-1
, so that
v_{p}(f_{2D}(x))=0
or equivalently
|f_{2D}(x)|_{p}=1
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{1\}}\int_{a+p\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{1\}}\int_{a+p\mathbb{Z}_{p}}1|dx|

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{1\}}|dx|(a+p\mathbb{Z}_{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{1\}}\frac{1}{p}=1-\frac{1}{p}.

Starting from the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\int_{a+p\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|
, it only remains to calculate
\int_{1+p\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|
. Note that if
x\equiv 1\mod p
, then
v_{p}(x)=0
. Therefore

f_{2D}(x)=\underbrace{(x-1)^{2}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{2D-2}p^{i}x^{i}\Big)}_{v_{p}=2v_{p}(x-1)}+\underbrace{p^{2D-1}x^{2D-1}(1+(p-2)x)}_{v_{p}=2D-1}

Since
2v_{p}(x-1)
is even and
2D-1
is odd, one of those two terms will always dominate. More precisely, if we define

B_{l}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\,|\,v_{p}(x-1)=l\},

it holds that

|f_{2D}(x)|_{p}=\begin{cases}p^{-2l}\quad\quad\quad\,\text{if}\,x\in B_{l}\,\,\text{for}\,\,1\leq l<D\\
p^{-(2D-1)}\quad\,\text{if}\,x\in B_{l}\,\,\text{for}\,\,l\geq D.\end{cases}

Because
1+p\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\sqcup_{l=1}^{\infty}B_{l}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{1+p\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\int_{B_{l}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\underbrace{\sum_{l=1}^{D-1}\int_{B_{l}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|}_{(1)}+\underbrace{\sum_{l=D}^{\infty}\int_{B_{l}}|f_{2D}|_{p}^{s}|dx|}_{(2)}.

Then

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{D-1}p^{-2sl}\int_{B_{l}}|dx|

(2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=D}^{\infty}p^{(-2D-1)s}\int_{B_{l}}|dx|

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{D-1}p^{-2sl}|dx|(B_{l})

\displaystyle=p^{(-2D-1)s}\sum_{l=D}^{\infty}\int_{B_{l}}|dx|

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{D-1}p^{-2sl}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)p^{-l}

\displaystyle=p^{(-2D-1)s}\int_{1+p^{D}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|dx|

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\sum_{l=1}^{D-1}p^{(-2s+1)l}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{-(2D+1)s}}{p^{D}}.

All together, we get

Z_{f_{2D}}(s)=\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\sum_{l=0}^{D-1}p^{(-2s+1)l}+\frac{p^{-(2D+1)s}}{p^{D}}.

For no value of
D
is this equal to (
1
) and we are done.

3.
The non-degenerate case in one variable

Let
f(x)=\sum_{i}a_{i}x^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{x\}
be a restricted power series in one variable. We begin by zooming in and considering
p
-adic integrals of the form
\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|
. For these integrals, we can get nice expressions if
m
is sufficiently large. We will distinguish three cases:

(1)

\alpha
such that
f
does not vanish on
\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}

(2)

\alpha
such that
f(\alpha)=0
but
f^{\prime}(\alpha)\neq 0

(3)

\alpha
such that
f(\alpha)=f^{\prime}(\alpha)=0
.

In the first two cases, we indeed have
\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{D}(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|
for all
D
sufficiently large. Hence if
f
and
f^{\prime}
have no common zeros, we will get a positive answer. If an
\alpha
of the third type exists, this conclusion no longer holds. The counterexample discussed in the previous section already illustrates this point. Note that in that case, we have
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=0
.

3.1.
Away from a zero

Consider a neighborhood
\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
f
does not vanish on it. Since closed balls are compact in the
p
-adic topology, the continuous function
|f|_{p}:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
will reach its minimum on the compact set
\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. This cannot be zero by the choice of the neighbourhood, so we denote it with
\epsilon>0
. Choose
D_{\alpha}
large enough such that
|a_{i}|_{p}<\epsilon
for all
i\geq D_{a}
. Consequently, for all
D>D_{\alpha}

\displaystyle|(f-f_{D})(x)|_{p}=|\sum_{i=D+1}^{\infty}a_{i}x^{i}|_{p}<\epsilon.

Fix
x\in\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Then
|f(x)|_{p}\geq\epsilon
. If
|f_{D}(x)|_{p}<\epsilon
, it would follow that
\epsilon\leq|f(x)|_{p}\leq\max(|f_{D}(x)|_{p},|(f-f_{D})(x)|_{p})<\epsilon
. This is a contradiction, so we may conclude
|f_{D}(x)|_{p}\geq\epsilon>|(f-f_{D})(x)|_{p}
. Consequently

\displaystyle|f(x)|_{p}=\max(|f_{D}(x)|_{p},|(f-f_{D})(x)|_{p})=|f_{D}(x)|_{p}.

We have equality of the integrands, so their integrals will be equal as well:

\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{D}(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|.

3.2.
In the neighborhood of a zero with nonzero derivative

The goal is to compute integrals of the form
\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|
. After a change of variables
x\rightarrow\alpha+p^{m}x
, we get

\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=p^{-m}\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(\alpha+p^{m}x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|.

We can formally define the derivative of
f
as
f^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i\geq n}ia_{i}X^{i-1}
. Because
|a_{i}|_{p}\rightarrow 0
as
i\rightarrow\infty
, we definitely have
|ia_{i}|_{p}\rightarrow 0
as
i\rightarrow\infty
and thus
f^{\prime}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
. Inductively we can define any derivative
f^{(k)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
. Then we have the Taylor series

\displaystyle f(\alpha+p^{m}x)

\displaystyle=f(\alpha)+p^{m}f^{(1)}(\alpha)x+p^{2m}\frac{f^{(2)}(\alpha)}{2!}x^{2}+p^{3m}\frac{f^{(3)}(\alpha)}{3!}x^{3}+\dots

Let
n_{0}
be the smallest natural number so that
f^{(n_{0})}(\alpha)\neq 0
. If we choose
m
large enough so that
v_{p}(\frac{f^{(n_{0})}}{n_{0}!}(\alpha))<m
, the coefficient of the term
p^{mn_{0}}\frac{f^{(n_{0})}}{n_{0}!}(\alpha)x^{n_{0}}
will have a uniquely maximal
p
-adic norm among all the coefficients of
f(\alpha+p^{m}x)
. Consequently,

\displaystyle|f(\alpha+p^{m}x)|_{p}=|p^{mn_{0}}\frac{f^{(n_{0})}}{n_{0}!}(\alpha)x^{n_{0}}|_{p}

(2)

for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
For readability, we denote
b=\frac{f^{(n_{0})}}{n_{0}!}(\alpha)
. We can then calculate

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=p^{-m}\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(\alpha+p^{m}x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=p^{-m}\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|p^{mn_{0}}bx^{n_{0}}|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=p^{-m}p^{-mn_{0}s}|b|_{p}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|x^{n_{0}}|_{p}^{s}|dx|.

By Example
1.1
, we know that
\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|x^{n_{0}}|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\big(1-\frac{1}{p}\big)\frac{1}{1-p^{-(n_{0}s+1)}}
and thus

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=(p^{-m}-p^{-m+1})\frac{p^{-mn_{0}s}|b|_{p}^{s}}{1-p^{-(n_{0}s+1)}}.

(3)

Note that this only depends on
n_{0}
and
|b|_{p}=|\frac{f^{(n_{0})}(\alpha)}{n_{0}!}|_{p}
.

For this case, we consider
n_{0}=1
. Denote
e=v_{p}(f^{\prime}(\alpha))
. To use the discussion above, fix
m>e
. We want to use some version of Hensel’s Lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

(Hensel)
Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
be an analytic series in one variable
X
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
f(a)\equiv 0\mod p
and
f^{\prime}(a)\not\equiv 0\mod p
. Then there exists a unique
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that

\displaystyle f(\xi)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\xi\equiv a\mod p.

Proof.

See for example
[
5
]
pp16-17. Note that here the proof is for polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X]
, but the proof also works for
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
.
∎

We have the following variation:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
f(a)\equiv 0\mod p^{2e+k}
and
v_{p}(f^{\prime}(a))=e
. Then there exists a unique
\xi
such that

\displaystyle f(\xi)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\xi\equiv a\mod p^{e+k}

Proof.

Suppose
f
and
a
are such as in the Lemma. Consider
g(y)\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\{y\}
defined by

g(y)=p^{-2e-(k-1)}f(a+p^{e+k-1}y).

Its Taylor expansion at
y=0
yields
h(y)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{y\}
such that

g(y)=p^{-2e-(k-1)}\Big(f(a)+(p^{e+k-1}y)f^{\prime}(a)+(p^{e+k-1}y)^{2}h(y)\Big).

Using
f(a)\equiv 0\mod p^{2e+k}
and
v_{p}(f^{\prime}(a))=e
, we see that actually
g(y)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{y\}
. Moreover
g(0)\equiv 0\mod p
and
g^{\prime}(0)=p^{-e}f(a)\not\equiv 0\mod p
.
So we can apply Hensel’s Lemma to find a unique
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
\eta\equiv 0\mod p
and
g(\eta)=0
. Then
\xi=a+p^{e+k-1}\eta
satisfies
f(\xi)=0
and
\xi\equiv a\mod p^{e+k}
.
∎

Now is the idea to choose
N
large enough such that for all
D\geq N
there exists
\xi_{D}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
satisfying
f_{D}(\xi_{D})=0
,
v_{p}(f_{D}^{\prime}(\xi_{D}))=v_{p}(f^{\prime}(\alpha))=e
and
\xi_{D}+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
Then by (
3
) we can conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{D}(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=\int_{\xi_{D}+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{D}(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=(p^{-m}-p^{-m+1})\frac{p^{-ms}|f_{D}^{\prime}(\xi_{D})|_{p}^{s}}{1-p^{-(s+1)}}

\displaystyle=(p^{-m}-p^{-m+1})\frac{p^{-ms}|f(\alpha)|_{p}^{s}}{1-p^{-(s+1)}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|.

Consider
D
such that
v_{p}(a_{i})>e+m
for all
i\geq D
. Then
f_{D}\equiv f\mod p^{e+m}
and also
f_{D}^{\prime}\equiv f^{\prime}\mod p^{e+1}
. Consequently, it must be that

\displaystyle f_{D}(\alpha)\equiv f(\alpha)\mod p^{e+m}\equiv 0\mod p^{e+m}

\displaystyle v_{p}(f_{D}^{\prime}(\alpha))=e.

Therefore, we can use Lemma
3.2
for
k=m-e
to get
\xi_{D}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that

\displaystyle f_{D}(\xi_{D})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\xi\equiv\alpha\mod p^{m}.

Since
m>e
and thus
\xi_{D}\equiv\alpha\mod p^{e+1}
we have
f_{D}^{\prime}(\xi_{D})\equiv f_{D}(\alpha)\mod p^{e+1}
. We may conclude

v_{p}(f_{D}^{\prime}(\xi_{D}))=e.

This
\xi_{D}
is exactly what we needed, so we are done.

All together we get the following positive result.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{X\}
such that
f
and
f^{\prime}
have no common zeros. Then there exists
N
such that for all
D\geq N
, the truncation
f_{D}
has the same Igusa Zeta function as
f
.

Proof.

From [
[
1
]
, Theorem 5.5.2] we may conclude
f
only has finitely many roots in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. For each root
\alpha
, we can find
m_{\alpha}
and
D_{\alpha}
such that for all
m>m_{\alpha}
and all
D\geq D_{\alpha}

\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\int_{\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{D}(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|.

Fix
m>\max{(m_{\alpha})}
. Denote

\displaystyle V\coloneqq\{a\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\,|\,a+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\alpha+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}\,\text{for some root $\alpha$}\}

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\setminus V

Since
V\cup S=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}
, we have the equality

\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\sum_{a\in V}\int_{a+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|+\sum_{a\in S}\int_{a+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

For
a\in S
, note that
f
does not vanish on
a+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. By the discussion above, there exists
D_{a}
such that for all
D\geq D_{a}

\int_{a+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\int_{a+p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{D}(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|.

So for all
D>\max{(D_{\alpha},D_{a})}
we indeed get

\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}|f_{D}(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|.

∎

4.1.
Definitions and Newton polyhedra

An important class of p-adic analytic functions are the ones that are non-degenerated over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with respect to all the faces of their Newton polyhedron. In order to define this, we first need the following definitions:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f(x)=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\omega\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{\omega}x^{\omega}
be a non-zero analytic series over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
f(0)=0
. Let
\mathbb{R}^{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,x\geq 0\}
and
\text{supp}(f)=\{\omega\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\,|\,a_{\omega}\neq 0\}
.The Newton polyhedron
\Gamma(f)
of
f
is defined as the convex hull in
(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}
of the set

\bigcup_{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)}\omega+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
p
be a prime number. Let
f
be as in Definition
4.1
. For every face
\tau
of the Newton polyhedron
\Gamma(f)
of
f
, we define

f_{\tau}(x)=\sum_{\omega\in\tau}a_{\omega}x^{\omega}

and
\overline{f}_{\tau}(x)
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
by reducing each coefficient
a_{\omega}
of
f_{\tau}
modulo
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Having these notions, we are ready to define what it means to be non-degenerate over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
(or
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
) with respect to all the faces of the Newton polyhedron:

Definition 4.3
.

Let
f
and
f_{\tau}
be as in definitions
4.1
and
4.2
. We say
f
is non-degenerated over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with respect to all the faces of its Newton polyhedron
\Gamma(f)
if for every face
\tau
of
\Gamma(f)
the locus of the polynomial
\overline{f}_{\tau}(x)
has no singularities in
(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times})^{n}
or equivalently, the set of congruences

\begin{cases}f_{\tau}(x)\,\,\equiv 0\mod p\\
\frac{\partial f_{\tau}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\equiv 0\mod p,\quad i=1,\dots,n\end{cases}

has no solution in
(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}
.

Next, we introduce the notion of the cone associated to a face
\tau
, because we will need this later on.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
f
be as in Definition
4.1
. For
a\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}
, we define

4.2.
Why being non-degenerated makes it easier to calculate the Igusa Zeta function

A very useful formula is the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
p
be a prime number and
f(x)=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
. Denote by
\overline{f}
the function over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
obtained by reducing each coefficient modulo
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Let
N
be the number of elements in the set
\{a\in(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times})^{n}\,|\,\overline{f}(a)=0\}
. Suppose that the set of congruences

\begin{cases}f(x)\,\,\,\,\equiv 0\mod p\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\equiv 0\mod p,\quad i=1,\dots,n\end{cases}

has no solution in
(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}
. Then for
s
a complex variable with
\Re(s)>0
, one has that

\int_{(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=p^{-n}\Big((p-1)^{n}-pN\frac{p^{s}-1}{p^{s+1}-1}\Big)

where
|dx|
denotes the Haar measure on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n}
so normalized that
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}
has measure 1

Proof.

A proof of this is given in (
[
3
]
) in case
f
is a polynomial. It is based on Hensel’s Lemma, so since Hensel’s Lemma also holds for analytic functions, this proof can be copied for
f
an analytic series.
∎

Note that if
f
is non-degenerated over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with respect to all the faces of its Newton polyhedron, then for any analytic function
g
, it still holds that for every face
\tau
of
\Gamma(f)
, the set of congruences

\begin{cases}f_{\tau}(x)+pg(x)\,\,\equiv 0\mod p\\
\frac{\partial(f_{\tau}+pg)}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\quad\,\,\equiv 0\mod p,\quad i=1,\dots,n\end{cases}

has no solutions in
(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}
. Hence we can use the previous Proposition to calculate integrals of the form

\int_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}}|f_{\tau}(u)+p\tilde{f}_{\tau}(u)|_{p}^{s}|du|.

The trick is then the following: in the previous section, we showed that for every
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, there is a unique face
\tau
of the Newton polyhedron such that

\tau\cap\text{supp}(f)=\{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)\,|\,k\cdot\omega=\inf_{y\in\Gamma(f)}k\cdot y\},

i.e. for
\omega\in\text{supp}(f)
,
k\cdot\omega
reaches the infimum
m(k)=\inf_{y\in\Gamma(f)}k\cdot y
if and only if
\omega\in\tau
. Now for
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}
with
\text{ord}\,x=k
, we may write
x=p^{k}u
for some unit
u\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}
. Denoting
\sigma(k)=k_{1}+\dots+k_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle|dx|=p^{-\sigma(k)}|du|

\displaystyle x^{\omega}=p^{k\cdot\omega}u^{\omega}.

If
\omega\in\tau
, we get that
v_{p}(x^{\omega})=k\cdot\omega=m(k)
and hence
a_{\omega}x^{\omega}
is certainly divisible by
p^{m(k)}
.
If
\omega\not\in\tau
, we get that
v_{p}(x^{\omega})=k\cdot\omega>m(k)
and hence
a_{\omega}x^{\omega}
is certainly divisible by
p^{m(k)+1}
. Consequently

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)}a_{\omega}x^{\omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)\cap\tau}a_{\omega}x^{\omega}+\sum_{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)\setminus\tau}a_{\omega}x^{\omega}

\displaystyle=p^{m(k)}\Big(\sum_{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)\cap\tau}a_{\omega}u^{\omega}\Big)+p^{m(k)+1}\Big(\sum_{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)\setminus\tau}a_{\omega}p^{k\cdot\omega-m(k)-1}u^{\omega}\Big)

\displaystyle=p^{m(k)}(f_{\tau}(u)+p\tilde{f_{\tau}}(u)).

So we find that

\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{n}\\
v_{p}(x)=k\end{subarray}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=p^{-\sigma(k)-m(k)s}\int_{(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}}|f_{\tau}(u)+p\tilde{f_{\tau}}(u)|_{p}^{s}|du|

and the latter is easily computed by Proposition
4.1
.
Since

\int_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}\\
v_{p}(x)=k\end{subarray}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|,

this should convince you that the Igusa Zeta function of analytic functions that are non-degenerated with respect to their Newton polynomial are easily compute using this method. More specifically, we have the following:
Remember that the cones associated to the faces of
\Gamma(f)
form a partition of
(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}
, see (
4
). Consequently
\mathbb{N}^{n}
is the disjoint union ,

\mathbb{N}^{n}=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\,\text{proper face}\\
\text{of}\,\Gamma(f)\end{subarray}}\mathbb{N}^{n}\cap\Delta_{\tau}.

It follows that

\displaystyle Z_{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}\\
v_{p}(x)=k\end{subarray}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\,\text{proper}\\
\text{face of}\,\Gamma(f)\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\cap\Delta_{\tau}}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}\\
v_{p}(x)=k\end{subarray}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|dx|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\,\text{proper}\\
\text{face of}\,\Gamma(f)\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\cap\Delta_{\tau}}p^{-\sigma(k)-m(k)s}\int_{(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{n}}|f_{\tau}(u)+p\tilde{f_{\tau}}(u)|_{p}^{s}|du|.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
p
be a prime number. Let
f
be as in Definition
4.1
. Suppose that

is non-degenerated over the finite field

You can cut off

Note that the formula from Theorem (

) only depends on
N_{\tau}
, which in turn is determined by
f\mod p
, and the Newton polyhedron
\Gamma(f)
. Therefore we will want to have
f_{D}\equiv f\mod D
and
\Gamma(f_{D})=\Gamma(f)
. Let us now focus on the latter condition.

Let
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\omega\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{\omega}x^{\omega}
be a non-zero analytic function over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
f(0)=0
. Note that supp
(f)
is not necessarily finite. However, we do have the following:

Lemma 4.3
.

There exist
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}\in\text{supp}(f)
such that

\bigcup_{\omega\in\text{supp}(f)}\omega+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}

Proof.

We prove this with induction on
n
. The case
n=1
is trivial, so consider
n>1
. It suffices to show that
\text{supp}(f)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}
. Pick any
\omega_{1}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\text{supp}(f)
. Observe that
\omega_{1}+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}
already contains every
\omega=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\text{supp}(f)
satisfying
w_{i}\geq\alpha_{i}
for all
i
. Now we also want to add the
\omega=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\text{supp}(f)
for which
w_{i}<\alpha_{i}
for some index
i
. Let us start by adding all
\omega\in\text{supp}(f)
of the form
(a,w_{2},\dots,w_{n})
for some
a<\alpha_{1}
. So fix
a\in\{0,1,\dots,\alpha_{1}-1\}
and define

A=\{(w_{2},\dots,w_{n})\,|\,(a,w_{2},\dots,w_{n})\in\text{supp}(f)\}.

Note that this is equal to
\text{supp}(g)
where
g
is defined as follows: collecting all the terms in
f
whose
x_{1}
-exponent is equal to
a
, we can write is as
x_{1}^{a}g(x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
.
By the induction hypothesis, there are
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{l}\in\text{supp}(g)
such that

\bigcup_{\eta\in\text{supp}(g)}\eta+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n-1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{l}\eta_{i}+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n-1}

It is not hard to see that then

\bigcup_{i=1}^{l}(a,\eta_{i})+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}

contains all
\omega\in\text{supp}(f)
of the form
(a,w_{2},\dots,w_{n})
. We can apply this procedure for any
a\in\{0,1,\dots,\alpha_{1}-1\}
and subsequently repeat it finitely many times for
w_{2}\in\{0,1,\dots,\alpha_{2}-1\},\dots,w_{n}\in\{0,1,\dots,\alpha_{n}-1\}
as well. This gives us
\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}\}
such that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{k}+(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}

contains
\text{supp}(f)
. This completes the argument.
∎
