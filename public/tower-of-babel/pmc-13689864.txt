Contemporary stroke management is largely structured around algorithmic frameworks that assume discrete and sequential clinical scenarios, with clear separation between ischemic and hemorrhagic risks. Patients with ischemic stroke continue to experience substantial in-hospital mortality (7.2%), and a subset develop concurrent venous thromboembolism, including pulmonary embolism (PE) in approximately 0.4% of cases, underscoring the systemic and dynamic nature of thrombotic disease.1 At the same time, current recommendations emphasize early anticoagulation for PE but advise caution or delay in the setting of recent intracranial ischemia or hemorrhagic transformation, leaving clinicians with limited guidance when these conditions coexist.2

The optimal timing of anticoagulation initiation after acute ischemic stroke remains an area of active investigation and evolving practice. Traditional guideline recommendations have suggested individualized timing based on infarct size and stroke severity, ranging from day 1 for transient ischemic attack (TIA) to beyond 2 weeks for severe stroke, reflecting concerns about hemorrhagic transformation risk.2,3 However, these strategies were developed primarily in controlled settings and do not fully address the complexity of patients with overlapping thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks, particularly when high-risk PE develops acutely and requires immediate therapeutic anticoagulation despite recent cerebral infarction.

We present a case that illustrates the limitations of guideline-directed care when competing risks evolve simultaneously, requiring dynamic, individualized, and multidisciplinary decision-making beyond conventional algorithms.

Case Summary

A 74-year-old woman with hypertension and obesity, no prior cerebrovascular or cardiac disease, and a negative family history of thrombophilia or venous thromboembolism presented with transient episodes of dysarthria and left facial droop lasting <5 minutes. Initial brain computed tomography (CT) and routine laboratory tests were unremarkable (Figure 1). Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with first-degree atrioventricular block (Figure 2). TIA was diagnosed (age, blood pressure, clinical features, duration, diabetes score of 2), and dual antiplatelet therapy with acetylsalicylic acid and clopidogrel plus a high-intensity statin was initiated.Figure 1Noncontrast Brain Computed Tomography(A to C) Axial computed tomography slices at different levels demonstrating preserved cerebral parenchyma and ventricular morphology. The morning after the TIA, she awoke with persistent dysarthria, left facial weakness, and mild left upper extremity weakness, consistent with a wake-up stroke; her last known well was midnight. On admission, she was hemodynamically stable, with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 4. In-hospital risk stratification showed an Acute Stroke Registry and Analysis of Lausanne (ASTRAL) score of 20 and a Preadmission comorbidities, Level of consciousness, Age, and Neurologic deficit (PLAN) score of 7, indicating an intermediate risk of adverse outcomes.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was not feasible because of claustrophobia and the need for urgent imaging-based reperfusion decision-making. Therefore, a multimodal CT protocol was performed. Initial noncontrast head CT demonstrated no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage, whereas CT perfusion imaging revealed a right parietal ischemic region with perfusion-core mismatch, consistent with potentially salvageable tissue (Figure 3). This imaging-based selection approach, although differing from MRI Diffusion-Weighted Imaging–Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (DWI-FLAIR) mismatch strategies, is supported by evidence from perfusion-based reperfusion trials. Intravenous thrombolysis with tenecteplase (0.25 mg/kg; 25 mg) was administered within 4 hours of awakening, with rapid neurological improvement within 45 minutes.Figure 3Multimodal Computed Tomography Perfusion and Noncontrast Assessment(A to E) Perfusion computed tomography maps demonstrating hemodynamic alteration in the right hemisphere, characterized by increased mean transit time and decreased blood flow, with relatively preserved blood volume. (F to H) Noncontrast computed tomography slices showing focal hypodensity involving the right insular cortex and precentral gyrus, consistent with acute ischemia in the right middle cerebral artery territory (Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score 8) (delineated by a red dashed line). The observed perfusion-volume mismatch confirms the presence of ischemic penumbra with potential for tissue salvage.

Subsequent MRI obtained under sedation confirmed acute right middle cerebral artery territory infarction involving the insula, middle frontal gyrus, and precentral region, with an estimated infarct volume of 15 mL. It also showed focal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), subsequent hemorrhagic transformation (parenchymal hematoma type 1), and chronic microangiopathic changes (Figure 4). No neurological deterioration occurred during the first 24 hours of intensive care monitoring, and antiplatelet therapy was withheld. Subsequent noncontrast head CT confirmed SAH (Figure 5). During hospitalization, the patient developed hypoxemia requiring noninvasive ventilation. CT pulmonary angiography, extended to the lower extremities, demonstrated bilateral segmental PE without evidence of deep venous thrombosis (Figure 7). Transthoracic echocardiography showed right ventricular dilation (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion. 24 mm, estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure 49 mm Hg), and cardiac biomarkers were elevated (N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide 2,632 pg/mL; troponin T 22 ng/L), consistent with intermediate-risk PE.Figure 7Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography(A to C) Axial computed tomography images demonstrating multiple intraluminal filling defects within the pulmonary arterial tree, consistent with pulmonary embolism (red arrow). Following the identification of parenchymal hematoma type 1 hemorrhagic transformation and focal SAH, antiplatelet therapy and pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis had initially been withheld, and the patient was managed with mechanical thromboprophylaxis alone. The patient remained neurologically stable without clinical evidence of hemorrhagic progression. After multidisciplinary discussion involving neurology, cardiology, internal medicine, and critical care teams, therapeutic anticoagulation with weight-based enoxaparin (120 mg subcutaneously every 12 hours) was initiated for the treatment of intermediate-risk PE. Clinical monitoring consisted of serial neurological examinations and follow-up noncontrast head CT demonstrating hemorrhagic stability. Antifactor Xa levels were monitored and prompted dose adjustment of enoxaparin to maintain therapeutic anticoagulation and minimize bleeding risk. No clinical or radiographic evidence of hemorrhage progression was observed following anticoagulation initiation.

Subsequently, she developed new left hemiparesis. Repeat imaging showed right middle cerebral artery occlusion (Figure 8), and mechanical thrombectomy achieved complete reperfusion (thrombolysis in cerebral infarction 3), followed by clinical improvement (Figure 9, Video 1). Further etiologic evaluation with transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated biatrial and right ventricular dilation with an intracardiac shunt on agitated saline contrast. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed an aneurysmal interatrial septum (15-mm excursion), a large right-to-left shunt (>30 microbubbles in the left atrium), and a thrombus straddling a patent foramen ovale (PFO), consistent with paradoxical embolism in transit (Videos 2 to 5). Anticoagulation was continued given the high risk of recurrent embolization.

Based on clinical variables, the Risk of Paradoxical Embolism (RoPE) score was 3 (low), reflecting advanced age and hypertension. However, direct visualization of a thrombus straddling the PFO in the context of concurrent PE provided definitive evidence of paradoxical embolism, independent of probabilistic risk estimation.

Why Beyond the Guidelines

Management extended beyond conventional guideline recommendations because of competing risks of recurrent thrombosis and intracranial hemorrhage in a rapidly evolving clinical course. Key challenges included thrombolysis in a wake-up stroke without MRI-based selection, anticoagulation for PE following hemorrhagic transformation, and mechanical thrombectomy after recent thrombolysis. These overlapping conditions required individualized, sequential decision-making in areas with limited evidence and no definitive guideline direction.

Case Outcome and Follow-Up

After mechanical thrombectomy, the patient remained neurologically stable, with mild residual dysarthria and left facial weakness. Respiratory status improved with supportive care, allowing discontinuation of noninvasive ventilation. After radiological stability was confirmed, antiplatelet therapy with acetylsalicylic acid was reintroduced, followed by anticoagulation with apixaban, without hemorrhagic complications. She was discharged with preserved functional status. Outpatient follow-up was planned for reassessment of the intracardiac thrombus and PFO management. The patient denied any personal history of thrombosis or known thrombophilia. No outpatient work-up for inherited thrombophilia was performed.

Discussion

This case highlights the challenge of managing simultaneous, competing thrombotic and hemorrhagic processes in acute stroke, as clinical priorities may shift rapidly and exceed guideline-directed recommendations. The coexistence of acute ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic transformation, PE, and paradoxical embolism made standard algorithms difficult to apply, requiring individualized, stepwise decision-making.

Intravenous thrombolysis in a wake-up stroke has been increasingly supported by imaging-based selection strategies, including MRI DWI-FLAIR mismatch and CT perfusion approaches.4 The Efficacy and Safety of MRI-Based Thrombolysis in Wake-Up Stroke (WAKE-UP) trial demonstrated that MRI-based selection improves functional outcomes in patients with unknown time of onset,5 whereas the Extending the Time for Thrombolysis in Emergency Neurological Deficits (EXTEND) trial extended these findings using perfusion imaging to identify salvageable tissue beyond conventional time windows.6 In this case, reperfusion therapy was guided by CT perfusion demonstrating a favorable mismatch profile, allowing treatment within an extended time window. Although these approaches have expanded access to reperfusion therapies, they are largely derived from controlled trial populations and do not fully address the management of subsequent complications, such as hemorrhagic transformation or concurrent systemic thromboembolism.4

Current recommendations generally favor delayed initiation, especially after hemorrhagic transformation.3 However, PE introduced a competing priority, as early anticoagulation is essential to prevent hemodynamic deterioration and recurrent embolism. This created a therapeutic dilemma: stroke guidelines prioritize minimizing intracranial bleeding risk,7 whereas PE guidelines emphasize prompt anticoagulation.8 In such cases, management must rely on individualized risk assessment rather than strict disease-specific algorithms.

Despite anticoagulation, the patient developed recurrent intracranial large-vessel occlusion. Mechanical thrombectomy achieved complete reperfusion and a favorable clinical outcome.9 This decision required careful consideration, as recent thrombolysis and hemorrhagic transformation increased procedural and bleeding risk. However, in this case, the risk of ongoing cerebral ischemia and recurrent embolization outweighed the potential bleeding risk, supporting an aggressive reperfusion strategy.9

PE with right ventricular dilation likely increased right-sided pressures, facilitating right-to-left shunting through a PFO. Direct visualization of a thrombus straddling the interatrial septum confirmed paradoxical embolism as the mechanism of recurrent cerebral ischemia.10 Given the high risk of further embolization and the infeasibility of surgical thrombectomy after recent ischemic stroke and intracranial hemorrhage, anticoagulation represented the most appropriate therapeutic strategy despite its hemorrhagic risk. In this context, probabilistic tools such as the RoPE score have limited utility when direct anatomical evidence of thrombus in transit is present. Although a low RoPE score suggests a lower likelihood that a PFO is the cause of stroke at a population level, direct visualization of thrombus traversing the defect provides definitive evidence of paradoxical embolism in an individual patient.10

This case highlights the challenge of managing competing thrombotic and hemorrhagic processes in acute stroke, as clinical priorities may shift rapidly and exceed guideline-directed recommendations.

Conclusions

This case underscores the limitations of current clinical guidelines, which are largely designed to address isolated disease processes and do not fully address complex, rapidly evolving clinical scenarios. When thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks coexist, strict adherence to guideline-based recommendations may be insufficient or even contradictory. Instead, management should be guided by continuous reassessment of competing risks, integration of clinical and imaging data, and multidisciplinary collaboration.
