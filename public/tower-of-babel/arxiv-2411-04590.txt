Singular stochastic delay equations driven by fractional Brownian motion: Dynamics, longtime behaviour, and pathwise stability
M. Ghani Varzaneh, S. Riedel

Abstract
. We study differential equations with a linear, path dependent drift and discrete delay in the diffusion term driven by a γ \gamma -Hölder rough path for γ > 1 3 \gamma>\frac{1}{3} . We prove well-posedness of these systems and establish a priori bounds for their solutions. Applying these results to an equation driven by a multidimensional fractional Brownian motion with Hurst paramter H ∈ ( 1 3 , 1 ) H\in\big(\frac{1}{3},1\big) , we can prove the existence of a Lyapunov spectrum for the linearized system that describes its long-time behaviour. Furthermore, we can deduce the existence of local stable, unstable, and center manifolds for the nonlinear equation. As an application, we can prove that under suitable conditions, the solution exhibits pathwise local exponential stability. En passant, we present new, concise and relatively short proofs for some classical results for 𝒞 0 \mathcal{C}_{0} -semigroups that build on the Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem formulated on Banach spaces.

Introduction

Delay differential equations (DDEs) are used to model complex processes for which the dynamics include aftereffect phenomena. In its most general formulation, they can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}y_{t}=f(t,y_{t},y|_{(-\infty,t]})\,\mathrm{d}t

where the solution
y
is a function taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In many cases, delay equations offer a more accurate description of a real-world phenomenon than a simple ordinary differential equation. Applications of delay differential equations exist in various areas including biology, epidemiology, population dynamics, chemistry, nonlinear optics and lasers, fluid dynamics, economics, and mechanical engineering (cf.
[
Ern09
]
where various applications are discussed in more detail). From a mathematical point of view, a delay differential equation is usually understood as an evolution equation on an infinite dimensional path space (cf.
[
HVL93
]
) which renders its analysis significantly more complicated compared to an ordinary differential equation. Furthermore, effects like oscillatory instabilities are often observed within delayed systems that induce severe challenges regarding their control and observability
[
Bri15
]
or their numerical approximation
[
BZ13
]
.

Stochastic DDEs constitute a natural generalization of the deterministic case. Not surprisingly, these models have numerous applications as well and we refer the reader to
[
Moh84
,
Moh98
,
Buc00
]
and the references therein where examples of stochastic DDEs are discussed in more detail. The noise in a stochastic DDE is typically modelled by a Brownian motion. This is justified in many cases where the noise is supposed to be
white
, i.e. if all time instances are assumed to be uncorrelated. From a mathematical point of view, this is also convenient because standard Itō theory applies in this case
[
Mao08
]
. However, it is not hard to imagine cases (e.g. in financial modelling) where considering memory within the noise is more natural. A typical process that is used for these purposes is the
fractional Brownian motion
(fBm), a generalization of the Brownian motion that was introduced by Mandelbrot and Van Ness
[
MVN68
]
. This process has the advantage that it is still Gaussian and furthermore possesses stationary increments, leading to a stationary noise process. However, on the mathematical side, solving a stochastic differential equation driven by a fractional Brownian motion is significantly more challenging than in the classical Brownian motion case. Indeed, since the fBm is not a semimartingale, Itō’s theory of stochastic integration completely breaks down. Fortunately, since the fBm is a Gaussian process, Malliavin calculus can still be applied
[
Nua05
,
BHOZ08
]
and in some cases, the Skorokhod integral can be used as a substitute for the Itō integral. However, for generic stochastic differential equation, let alone equations with time delay, this theory is not easily applicable. A more elegant solution is provided by Lyons’ theory of
rough paths

[
LQ02
,
LCL07
,
FV10
,
FH20
]
. In fact, rough path theory clearly separates the two problems of solving a differential equation (typically by a fixed point argument) and making sense of a stochastic integral. Without going too much into detail here, rough paths theory requires to make sense of iterated stochastic integrals only which simplifies the problem a lot. In recent years, rough path theory became the standard tool for studying stochastic differential equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion, and the reader may consult
[
FV10
, Chapter 15]
,
[
FH20
, Chapter 10]
, and
[
GVR23a
, Chapter 8]
for an overview of various problems where this theory was successfully applied to. Despite the success of rough path theory to study differential equations driven by an fBm, there are relatively few results that use this approach to study equations containing a time delay. The article that laid the groundwork to do this was written by Neuenkirch, Nourdin, and Tindel
[
NNT08
]
. These authors study equations with finitely many discrete delays, e.g.

(0.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}y_{t}&=b(y_{t},y_{t-r_{1}},\ldots,y_{t-r_{k}})\,\mathrm{d}t+G(y_{t},y_{t-r_{1}},\ldots,y_{t-r_{k}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t};\quad t\geq 0\\
\qquad y_{s}&=\xi_{s};\quad-r_{k}\leq s\leq 0,\ \ \end{split}

where
0<r_{1}<\ldots<r_{k}<\infty
and
\mathbf{X}
is a
\gamma
-Hölder rough path,
\gamma>\frac{1}{3}
, possessing a so-called
delayed Lévy area
(see Definition
1.2
below). They are able to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
0.1
) and can show that an fBm can be enhanced with a delayed Lévy area, resulting in a theory capable to deal with stochastic DDEs with discrete delay driven by an fBm. However, one weakness of the results obtained in
[
NNT08
]
is that the authors have to assume that the drift term
b
in (
0.1
) is globally bounded. In applications, this is hardly ever the case as the reader may see by studying the examples presented in the references above. Furthermore, the theory in
[
NNT08
]
is formulated for discrete delays only, whereas in many applications, the dynamics may be influenced by the whole path of the solution until present time or at least by a path segment. The first main contribution of this work is an extension of the results in
[
NNT08
]
that allows to study a significant larger class of equations. To wit, we will be able to prove existence and uniqueness for equations of the form

(0.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}y_{t}&=A\bigg(y_{t},\int_{-r_{1}}^{0}y_{\theta+t}\,\pi_{1}(\mathrm{d}\theta),\ldots,\int_{-r_{k}}^{0}y_{\theta+t}\,\pi_{k}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}t+b(y_{t},y_{t-r_{1}},\ldots,y_{t-r_{k}})\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+G(y_{t},y_{t-r_{1}},\ldots,y_{t-r_{k}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t};\quad t\geq 0\\
\qquad y_{s}&=\xi_{s};\quad-r_{k}\leq s\leq 0,\ \ \end{split}

where
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times(1+k)}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is linear and
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k}
are finite signed measures, see Theorem
3.2
. We furthermore provide a priori bounds for the solution that are useful in applications.

Having established well-posedness for (
0.2
) makes it possible to study a much more general class of stochastic DDEs driven by an fBm than in
[
NNT08
]
. However, our theorem is even interesting for the Brownian motion case since our result is the first one that gives a
pathwise
meaning to equations of the form (
0.2
). In
[
GVRS22
]
, we observed that in the Brownian motion case, equations of the form (
0.2
) without the linear term
A
induce a stochastic semiflow on random spaces. This, in turn, can be reformulated within L. Arnold’s theory of random dynamical systems (RDS; cf.
[
Arn98
]
) by saying that (
0.2
) induces a
cocycle
on a fiber-type bundle of Banach spaces, called a
field of Banach spaces
(see Definition
4.10
). It turned out that this structure is extremely useful to study the long-time behaviour of stochastic DDEs. Let us mention here that for a long time, it was believed that DDEs of the form (
0.2
) do not generate a stochastic semiflow (or a cocycle), cf. e.g. the introduction in
[
MS03
]
. It was understood in
[
MS03
]
that there is an issue caused by the type of delay in the diffusion term
G
in the equation. A stochastic DDE with a discrete delay in the diffusion term, as it is considered here, was therefore called
singular
in
[
MS03
]
, and we adopt this term. On the other hand, if the diffusion term depends smoothly on the past, the equation was called
regular
in
[
MS03
]
, and in this case, a stochastic semiflow indeed exists on a deterministic space. The articles
[
MS03
,
MS04
]
exclusively study the regular case, whereas this article and its predecessors
[
GVRS22
,
GVR21
]
concentrate on singular equations. Using these terms, the second main contribution of our article is that also the solution to the singular equation (
0.2
) driven by a fractional Brownian motion induces a cocycle on a field of Banach spaces, cf. Theorem
4.8
, and therefore generates a random dynamical system. We emphasize here that fractional Brownian motion introduces additional challenges for this objective.

As we already mentioned above, random dynamical systems are extremely useful when studying the long-time behaviour of a stochastic flow. They also have the great advantage that their usage does not depend on the Markov property which does not hold in the case of a fractional noise
[
BRS17
]
. In the linear case, crucial information about the long time behaviour is encoded in the
Lyapunov spectrum
. The fundamental theorem that assures its existence is the
Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem
(MET), originally proved by Oseledets for matrix cocycles
[
Ose68
]
. Using a version of the MET we proved for RDS defined on fields of Banach spaces
[
GVR23c
]
, we can deduce that linear stochastic DDEs possess a Lyapunov spectrum. For a general nonlinear equation as considered in (
0.2
), we can apply the MET to the linearized equation provided a
stationary point
(which can be seen as a generalized concept of a fixed point for a determinstic equation, cf. Definition
4.16
) exists. The spectrum of the linearized cocycle can be used to prove the existence of invariant manifolds, i.e. stable, unstable, and center manifolds, for the nonlinear equation around a stationary point. These are the next important results in this paper, and we formulate them in Theorem
4.19
, Theorem
4.21
, and Theorem
4.22
. Let us briefly comment on the existing literature in this field now. In the Brownian motion case, the existence of stable and unstable manifolds for regular stochastic DDEs were aready proven in
[
MS04
]
and in the singular case in
[
GVR21
]
. We believe that Theorem
4.22
is the first center manifold theorem formulated for stochastic DDEs even in the Brownian case. In this context, we want to underline the importance of center manifold theory since it is frequently used to study
bifurcation
, a very interesting topic for DDEs due to their unexpected oscillatory instabilities. For a fractional Brownian motion, stable and unstable manifolds for rough ordinary differential equations were recently obtained in
[
GVR23b
]
and center manifolds are studied in
[
NtK21
,
GVR23b
]
. Invariant manifold theorems for stochastic partial differential equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion can be found in
[
GALS10
,
MG22
,
KNt23
,
GVR25
,
LNtZ24
]
. We are not aware of any invariant manifold theorem for stochastic DDEs driven by an fBm up to now.

The last part of our paper deals with the question of pathwise exponential stability for stochastic DDEs, a very interesting topic in the field of delay differential equations. Using the stable manifold theorem, we can formulate several results in this direction both for linear and for nonlinear equations, cf. Section
6
. In this context, we are able to reprove some classical results concerning exponential stability of abstract
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-semigroups that are well-known, yet difficult to obtain. We present new, short, and concise proofs for these theorems by using the MET. Since we believe that this new approach is interesting in its own right, we devoted a whole section to it, cf. Section
5
.

Let us summarize the main contributions of this article:

•

The results in
[
NNT08
]
are substantially extended by establishing the existence and uniqueness of solutions for the rough delay differential equation (
0.2
). Additionally, we derive bounds for the solution (see Theorem
3.2
). To control these bounds and prevent them from becoming excessively large—especially in the presence of an unbounded linear drift—we employ indirect techniques. Standard direct calculations often yield bounds that are too large for our intended future applications.

•

We demonstrate that the solution to (
0.2
) induces a random dynamical system on a continuous (measurable) field of Banach spaces (see Theorem
4.8
). Constructing such a system for delay equations driven by fractional noise is notably more challenging than for those driven by Brownian motion. In this work, we overcome this difficulty through the application of Malliavin calculus.

•

The existence of stable, unstable, and center manifolds around stationary points are deduced for the semiflow induced by (
0.2
) (Theorem
4.19
,
4.21
, and
4.22
). Our calculations enable these results to be derived using certain abstract findings. Direct approaches to constructing invariant manifolds are typically lengthy and complex.

•

Several results regarding almost sure exponential stability for both linear and nonlinear stochastic delay differential equations are established (see Section Section
6
). Proving exponential stability in non-Markovian cases is generally challenging, even in finite-dimensional settings. In our context, these difficulties are compounded by the fact that the equation is formulated in infinite-dimensional Banach spaces, where a classical dynamical system (typically defined on a fixed Banach space) cannot be established. To address these challenges, consistent with our other results, we rely on additional insights into the geometry of the underlying Banach spaces, which we refer to as fields of Banach spaces. In particular, we have removed the parameter restriction that we previously imposed for the special case of the Brownian motion in
[
GVR21
]
and
[
GVRS22
]
.

•

Using the Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem for Banach spaces, we present new and short proofs of classical results about the exponential stability of abstract
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-semigroups (Section
5
).

The article is structured as follows: In Section
1
, we introduce the notation used throughout our work and present some basic notions in rough paths theory and random dynamical systems. Furthermore, we recall the approach to rough delay differential equations introduced in
[
NNT08
]
. Section
2
discusses the delayed Lévy area for the fractional Brownian motion and proves an approximation result with Malliavin calculus techniques. Afterwards, we prove existence, uniqueness, and an a priori bound for solutions to (
0.2
) in Section
3
. We furthermore discuss Fréchet-differentiability of the solution and some technical bounds for it. In Section
4
, we prove that (
0.2
) generates a random dynamical system on a continuous (measurable) field of Banach spaces and prove the invariant manifold theorems. Section
5
contains stability results for abstract
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-semigroups, proven with the MET, that are used in Section
6
to deduce stability results for linear and nonlinear stochastic delay differential equations. In the appendix, we collect some basic facts about Malliavin calculus and fractional calculus, introduce some related stochastic integrals that are used to define the delayed Lévy area in Section
2
and prove a technical Lemma.

1.
Preliminaries

Notation and basic definitions

In this part, we present some notations that will be used throughout the paper.

•

Unless otherwise specified,
U
,
W
will always represent finite-dimensional, normed vector spaces over the real numbers, with the norm denoted by
|\cdot|
. The notation
L(U,W)
refers to the set of linear and continuous functions from
U
to
W
, equipped with the standard operator norm.

•

In estimates, the notation
a\lesssim b
means that there exists a deterministic constant
C
, which may depend on some irrelevant parameters, such that
a\leq Cb
.

•

Let
J
be an interval in
\mathbb{R}
. A map
m:J\to U
is referred to as a
path
. For a path
m
, its increment is denoted by
(\delta m)_{s,t}:=m_{t}-m_{s}
, where
m_{t}
represents
m(t)
. We define the supremum norm of
m
over the interval
J
as

\|m\|_{\infty;J}:=\sup_{s\in J}|m_{t}|.

Additionally, the
\gamma
-Hölder seminorm, for
\gamma\in(0,1]
, is defined by

\|m\|_{\gamma;J}:=\sup_{s,t\in J;s\neq t}\frac{|m_{t}-m_{s}|}{|t-s|^{\gamma}}.

For a general
2
-parameter function
m^{\#}\colon J\times J\to U
, we use similar notation. If the domain is clear from the context, we may omit
J
as a subscript. The space
C^{0}(I,U)
consists of all continuous paths
m\colon J\to U
equipped with the uniform norm, while
C^{\gamma}(J,U)
denotes the space of
\gamma
-Hölder continuous functions, equipped with the norm

\|\cdot\|_{C^{\gamma};J}:=\|\cdot\|_{\infty;J}+\|\cdot\|_{\gamma;J}.

The space
C^{\infty}(J,U)
consists of functions that are infinitely differentiable. If
0\in J
, using
0
as a subscript, such as in
C^{\gamma}_{0}(J,U)
, denotes the subspace of functions for which
x_{0}=0
. An upper index, such as
C^{0,\gamma}(J,U)
, indicates the closure of smooth functions under the corresponding norms.

•

Let
C^{n}_{b}(U,{W})
denote the space of functions
G\colon U\to{W}
that have
n
bounded derivatives, where each derivative up to the
n
-th order is continuous and bounded, with
n\geq 0
. In many cases, we will omit the specific domain and codomain and use the notation
C^{n}_{b}
for simplicity.

We now provide some fundamental concepts from rough paths theory that are essential for this article. For a comprehensive overview, we direct the reader to
[
FH20
]
.

•

Let
X\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a locally
\gamma
-Hölder continuous path, with
\gamma\in(0,1]
. A
Lévy area
associated with
X
is a continuous function
\mathbb{X}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the algebraic identity

\mathbb{X}_{s,t}=\mathbb{X}_{s,u}+\mathbb{X}_{u,t}+(\delta X)_{s,u}\otimes(\delta X)_{u,t}

for every
s,u,t\in\mathbb{R}
and for which
\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\gamma;J}<\infty
holds on every compact interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. If
\gamma\in(1/3,1/2]
and
X
admits a Lévy area
\mathbb{X}
, we refer to the pair
(X,\mathbb{X})
as a
\gamma
-rough path
.

•

Let
I=[a,b]
be a compact interval. A path
m\colon J\to U
is called a
controlled path
based on
X
over the interval
J
if there exists a
\gamma
-Hölder continuous path
m^{\prime}\colon J\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},U)
such that, for all
s,t\in J
, the relation

(\delta m)_{s,t}=m^{\prime}_{s}(\delta X)_{s,t}+m_{s,t}^{\#}

holds, where
m^{\#}\colon J\times J\to U
satisfies
\|m^{\#}\|_{2\gamma;J}<\infty
. The function
m^{\prime}
is referred to as the
Gubinelli derivative
of
m
.
We denote the space of controlled paths based on
X
over the interval
J
by
\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\gamma}(J,U)
. This space is a Banach space with the norm

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|m\|_{\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\gamma}}:=\|(m,m^{\prime})\|_{\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\gamma}}:=|m|_{\infty,J}+|m^{\prime}|_{\infty,J}+\|m^{\prime}\|_{\gamma;J}+\|m^{\#}\|_{2\gamma;J}.

In this paper, when the context makes it clear what
U
represents, we may use the notation
\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\gamma}(J)
instead of
\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\gamma}(J,U)
. Additionally, when we refer to
m_{s}^{\prime}X_{s,t}
, we actually mean
m_{s}^{\prime}\circ X_{s,t}
. When it is clear from the context, we may omit the symbol
\circ
for function composition, in accordance with our conventions.

Finally, we recall the definition of a random dynamical system as introduced by L. Arnold in
[
Arn98
]
.

•

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and
(X,\mathcal{B})
be measurable spaces. Let
\mathbb{T}
be either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}
, equipped with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{I}
, which is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
when
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
and the power set
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z})
when
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{Z}
.

A family
\theta=(\theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
of maps from
\Omega
to itself is called a
measurable dynamical system
if:

(i)

The map
(\omega,t)\mapsto\theta_{t}\omega
is
\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{F}
-measurable,

(ii)

\theta_{0}=\operatorname{Id}
,

(iii)

\theta_{s+t}=\theta_{s}\circ\theta_{t}
, for all
s,t\in\mathbb{T}
.

If
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
that is invariant under each
\theta_{t}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}\circ\theta_{t}^{-1}=\mathbb{P}
for every
t\in\mathbb{T}
, then the tuple
\big(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta\big)
is called a
measurable metric dynamical system
. The system is called
ergodic
if every
\theta
-invariant set has probability
0
or
1
.

•

Let
\mathbb{T}^{+}:=\{t\in\mathbb{T}\,:\,t\geq 0\}
, equipped with the trace
\sigma
-algebra. An
(ergodic) measurable random dynamical system
on
(X,\mathcal{B})
is an (ergodic) measurable metric dynamical system
\big(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta\big)
along with a measurable map
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}^{+}\times\Omega\times X\to X
that satisfies the
cocycle property
, meaning
\varphi(0,\omega,\cdot)=\operatorname{Id}_{X}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, and

\varphi(t+s,\omega,\cdot)=\varphi(t,\theta_{s}\omega,\cdot)\circ\varphi(s,\omega,\cdot),

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{T}^{+}
and
\omega\in\Omega
. The map
\varphi
is referred to as a
cocycle
. If
X
is a topological space with
\mathcal{B}
as its Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and the map
\varphi_{\cdot}(\omega,\cdot)\colon\mathbb{T}^{+}\times X\to X
is continuous for every
\omega\in\Omega
, then
\varphi
is called a
continuous (ergodic) random dynamical system
. Generally, we say that
\varphi
possesses property
P
if and only if
\varphi(t,\omega,\cdot)\colon X\to X
exhibits property
P
for every
t\in\mathbb{T}^{+}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, provided this statement is well-defined. For simplicity and clarity, we will also use the notation
\varphi^{s}_{\omega}(\cdot)
in place of
\varphi(s,\omega,\cdot)
to streamline our presentation.

1.1.
Basic properties of rough delay equations

The main equation that we are interested in for this paper is

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}y_{t}&=A\bigg(y_{t},\int_{-r}^{0}y_{\theta+t}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}t+G\big(y_{t},y_{t-r}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t},\\
y_{s}&=\xi_{s},\quad-r\leq s\leq 0,\end{split}

where
\mathbf{X}
is a
\gamma
-Hölder rough path with
\gamma>\frac{1}{3}
(and later a
multidimensional fractional Brownian motion
with Hurst parameter
H>\frac{1}{3}
). Here,
A
represents a linear drift, and
\pi
is a finite signed measure on
[-r,0]
with
r>0
.
Note that we can add more delay components in the diffusion part in terms of Dirac measures or in the drift part in terms of finite signed measures. However, we do not explore this generality in order to keep the results more focused. The assumptions on
A
,
\pi
,
\mathbf{X}
, and
G
are detailed in Section
3
. We will study several aspects of this equation, including well-posedness and dynamics.
We provide a brief overview of the concept of rough delay equations, along with the fundamental objects and definitions required to solve this class of equations.

Remark 1.1
.

We work with Equation (
1.2
) instead of (
0.2
) to enhance the readability of our results. Note that, for the linear drift term, the drift component of (
0.2
) can be transformed into the drift part of (
1.2
) using an appropriate measure. For the remaining terms in Equation (
0.2
), by assuming
0<r_{1}<\ldots<r_{k}
, we first solve the equation on
[0,r_{1}]
and then extend the solution successively to
[0,r_{2}]
, and so forth, until reaching
[0,r_{k}]
. Moreover, by considering the new path
t\mapsto(t,X_{t})
, we can incorporate
b
into the diffusion term by a new rough path generated by
\mathbf{X}
and the identity path. Since the main idea remains consistent with the case where
k=1
and
b=0
, we will focus on Equation (
1.2
) throughout this work.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\frac{1}{3}<\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and let
X:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
(X,\mathbb{X})
is a
\gamma
-rough path
. Fix
r>0
. We say that
\mathbf{X}
is a
delayed
\gamma
-rough path
if there exists a continuous function
\mathbb{X}(-r)
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}(-r):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that for every interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\mathbb{X}(-r)\|_{2\gamma,[a,b]}<\infty,\\
&\quad\mathbb{X}_{s,t}(-r)=\mathbb{X}_{s,u}(-r)+\mathbb{X}_{u,t}(-r)+X_{s-r,u-r}\otimes X_{u,t},\quad\forall s,u,t\in\mathbb{R},\ s<u<t.\end{split}

We denote a delayed
\gamma
-rough path by
\mathbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}(-r))
. For an interval
J=[a,b]
, we define the norm of
\mathbf{X}
as

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\gamma,J}:=\|X\|_{\gamma,J}+\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\gamma,J}+\|\mathbb{X}(-r)\|_{2\gamma,J}.

We use
\mathscr{C}^{\gamma}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to denote the set of delayed
\gamma
-rough paths.

Next, let’s recall what a delayed controlled path is.

Definition 1.3
.

Assume
J=[a,b]
is a compact interval and
\frac{1}{3}<\beta\leq\gamma
. Let
\mathbf{X}
be a delayed
\gamma
-rough path. We say that a
\beta
-Hölder path
\zeta\colon J\to W
is a
delayed controlled path
based on
\mathbf{X}
over the interval
J
if there exist
\gamma
-Hölder paths
\zeta^{0},\zeta^{1}\colon J\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},W)
such that

(1.4)

\displaystyle\zeta_{s,t}=\zeta^{0}_{s}(\delta X)_{s,t}+\zeta^{1}_{s}(\delta X)_{s-r,t-r}+\zeta^{\#}_{s,t}

for all

, where

The paths
\zeta^{0}
and
\zeta^{1}
are referred to as the

of

. We denote the space of delayed controlled paths based on
\mathbf{X}
over the interval

. A norm on this space is defined as

(1.5)

\displaystyle\|\zeta\|_{\mathcal{D}_{X}^{\beta}(J,W)}:=\|(\zeta,\zeta^{0},\zeta^{1})\|_{\mathcal{D}_{X}^{\beta}(J,W)}:=|\zeta_{a}|+|\zeta^{0}_{a}|+|\zeta^{1}_{a}|+\|\zeta^{0}\|_{\beta;J}+\|\zeta^{1}\|_{\beta;J}+\|\zeta^{\#}\|_{2\beta;J}.

Remark 1.4
.

Assume
J=[a,b]
and let
G:W\times W\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},W)
be a
C^{2}
function. Suppose
\frac{1}{3}<\beta\leq\gamma
,
\xi\in\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([a,b],W)
, and
\tilde{\xi}\in\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([a-r,b-r],W)
. Then, for
\zeta:[a,b]\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},W)
defined by
\zeta_{t}:=G(\xi_{t},\tilde{\xi}_{t-r})
, we have
\zeta\in\mathcal{D}_{X}^{\beta}(J,L(\mathbb{R}^{d},W))
. Furthermore,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zeta^{0}_{s}&=\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s-r})\xi^{\prime}_{s},\\
\zeta^{1}_{s}&=\frac{\partial G}{\partial y}(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s-r})\tilde{\xi}^{\prime}_{s-r},\end{split}

for all

. Additionally, for

, the remainder term of
\zeta
, denoted by
\zeta^{\#}_{s,t}
, is given by

\displaystyle\zeta^{\#}_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{D_{(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s-r})}G\,(\xi^{\#}_{s,t},\tilde{\xi}^{\#}_{s-r,t-r})}_{\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s-r})\xi^{\#}_{s,t}+\frac{\partial G}{\partial y}(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s-r})\tilde{\xi}^{\#}_{s-r,t-r}}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)D^{2}G_{\theta(\xi,\tilde{\xi})(s,t)}\left((\delta(\xi,\tilde{\xi}_{\cdot-r}))_{s,t},(\delta(\xi,\tilde{\xi}_{\cdot-r}))_{s,t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

s,t\in J
, where
(\delta(\xi,\tilde{\xi}_{\cdot-r}))_{s,t}=(\xi_{t},\tilde{\xi}_{t-r})-(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s-r})

\displaystyle G(\xi_{t},\tilde{\xi}_{t})-G(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s})=D_{(\xi_{s},\tilde{\xi}_{s-r})}G(\delta(\xi,\tilde{\xi}_{\cdot-r})_{s,t})+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)D^{2}G_{\theta(\xi,\tilde{\xi})(s,t)}\left((\delta(\xi,\tilde{\xi}_{\cdot-r}))_{s,t},(\delta(\xi,\tilde{\xi}_{\cdot-r}))_{s,t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

2.
Delayed Lévy Area for Fractional Brownian Motion with Smooth Approximations

Recall from Definition
1.2
that a delayed rough path, in comparison to a classical rough path, includes an additional term which we refer to as the
delayed Lévy area
. This extra element must satisfy the condition given in (
1.3
).
Assume that
(B^{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
are independent fractional Brownian motions. While the existence of the Lévy area for this process is well established, the existence of the delayed Lévy area
\mathbb{B}(-r)
is more intricate. Indeed, as outlined in
[
NNT08
]
[Pages 34-37], if we use the symmetric integral (cf. Definition
A.1
) and define

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{s,t}(-r):=\int_{s}^{t}B_{s-r,u-r}\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{u},

then
\mathbb{B}_{s,t}(-r)
is a process that belongs to the second chaos of the fractional Brownian motion
B
. Also

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{s}^{t}B_{s-r,u-r}\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{u}\\
&\quad=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left(\delta^{B^{j}}(B^{i}_{s-r,.-r}\chi_{[s,t]}(.))+\delta_{i=j}\left(-Hr^{2H-1}(t-s)+\frac{1}{2}((t-s+r)^{2H}-r^{2H})\right)\right)e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\end{split}

and

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}|\delta^{B^{i}}(B^{j}_{s-r,.-r}\chi_{[s,t]}(.))|^{2}\lesssim(t-s)^{4H}.

Here,
\delta_{i=j}
denotes the Kronecker delta, which is equal to one if
i=j
and zero otherwise.
By the Kolmogorov continuity theorem, we can demonstrate that
\mathbb{B}(-r)
has a continuous version that satisfies the condition (
1.3
) for every
\gamma<H
. Moreover, for this version, we can establish that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{B}(-r)\|_{2\gamma,[0,r]}\in\bigcap_{p\geq 0}L^{p}(\Omega).

To define a random dynamical system, we need to ensure that
\mathbb{B}_{s,t}(-r)
can be approximated by smooth functions. We will use tools from Malliavin calculus to achieve this. The appendix provides basic background information and definitions for the notations used in this section. For the remainder of this section, we accept the following assumption:

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume that
\frac{1}{3}<H<\frac{1}{2}
and that
B=(B^{1},\ldots,B^{d})\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued two-sided fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H
. This process is defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and is adapted to some filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
, with
B_{0}=0
almost surely.

Let us start with the following definition:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\rho:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,2]
be a smooth function such that
\operatorname{supp}(\rho)\subset[0,1]
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(z)\mathrm{d}z=1
. We set

\displaystyle B^{\epsilon}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{1}B_{-\epsilon z,t-\epsilon z}\rho(z)dz,\quad\epsilon\in(0,1].

It is not hard to see that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|B^{\epsilon}_{s,t}|^{2}\leqslant M(t-s)^{2H}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}|B^{\epsilon}_{s,t}-B_{s,t}|^{2}=0

It is natural to expect that
\int_{s}^{t}B^{\epsilon}_{s-r,u-r}\otimes\mathrm{d}B_{u}^{\epsilon}
converges to
\mathbb{B}_{s,t}(-r)
. We will make this statement precise through several intermediate steps.

Lemma 2.3
.

We have the following pathwise identity on a set of full measure:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}B^{\epsilon}_{s-r,u-r}\otimes\mathrm{d}B_{u}^{\epsilon}=\int_{0}^{1}\rho(z)\int_{s-\epsilon z}^{t-\epsilon z}B^{\epsilon}_{s-r,u+\epsilon z-r}\otimes\mathrm{d}B_{u}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{1}\rho(z)\int_{s-\epsilon z}^{t-\epsilon z}B^{\epsilon}_{s-r,u+\epsilon z-r}\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{u}\mathrm{d}z.

Proof.

Note that the integrals in (
2.6
) are defined pathwise, since
B^{\epsilon}
is smooth and
B
is a Hölder continuous path (almost surely for
\gamma<H
). Using integration by parts, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{s-\epsilon z}^{t-\epsilon z}(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{s-r,u+\epsilon z-r}\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{u}=(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{s-r,t-r}B^{j}_{t-\epsilon z}-\int_{s-r}^{t-r}B^{j}_{u+r-\epsilon z}\mathrm{d}(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{u}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(z)

\displaystyle\int_{s-\epsilon z}^{t-\epsilon z}(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{s-r,u+\epsilon z-r}\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{u}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(z)(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{s-r,t-r}B^{j}_{t-\epsilon z}\mathrm{d}z-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{s-r}^{t-r}\rho(z)B^{j}_{u+r-\epsilon z}\mathrm{d}(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{u}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{s-r,t-r}(B^{\epsilon})^{j}_{t}-\int_{s-r}^{t-r}(B^{\epsilon})^{j}_{u+r}\mathrm{d}(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{u}.

From integration by parts,

\displaystyle(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{s-r,t-r}(B^{\epsilon})^{j}_{t}-\int_{s-r}^{t-r}(B^{\epsilon})^{j}_{u+r}\mathrm{d}(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{u}=\int_{s}^{t}(B^{\epsilon})^{i}_{s-r,u-r}\mathrm{d}(B^{\epsilon})_{u}^{j},

which implies the claim.
∎

Before proceeding, we recall the following simple inequality that holds for every measure space
(Y,\mathcal{Y},\mu)
and every measurable function
f:Y\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{Y}f(y,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\int_{Y}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(f^{2}(y,\omega)\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

The symmetric integral defined in Definition
A.1
is closely related to the Stratonovich integral for Brownian motion. We can then derive the following useful bound, which is crucial for our subsequent objectives.

Lemma 2.4
.

For
\tau\in(-\infty,\sigma)

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\int_{s}^{t}B_{u-\sigma,u-\tau}\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{u}\right|^{2}\right)\lesssim\big(sgn(\sigma)|\sigma|^{2H-1}-sgn(\tau)|\tau|^{2H-1}\big)^{2}(t-s)^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 7}(t-s)^{\frac{i}{2}H}(\sigma-\tau)^{(4-\frac{i}{2})H}.

Proof.

Set

and
\Phi(u)=(\Phi^{j}(u))_{1\leq j\leq d}
. Note that for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\Phi(u)-\Phi(x)\right|^{2}\right)\lesssim(x-u)^{H}(\sigma-\tau)^{H}.

Also,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\Phi(u)\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}{B_{u}}=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\big(\delta^{B^{j}}(\Phi^{i})+Tr_{[s,t]}(D^{B^{j}}\Phi^{i}))e_{i}\otimes e_{j}.

Recall that

We will revisit this result later when we aim to prove that our solutions to Equation (

) generate a random dynamical system.

3.
Existence, uniqueness, and some a priori bounds

In this section, we establish an existence and uniqueness result for Equation (
1.2
) driven by a general delayed rough path. We also obtain an a priori bound for the solution. For our future purposes, this bound must increase polynomially with respect to the initial value and the rough driver. Additionally, we derive several useful estimates for the Fréchet derivative of the solution.
To obtain an a priori bound for our solution, there are several approaches available. We utilize a technique known as the decomposition of flows, which provides more straightforward estimates. Although it is possible to use a semigroup approach, this method is too complex for delay equations, and the bound obtained is difficult to control with a polynomial function. Let us start with the following assumption about the drift part.

Assumption 3.1
.

•

Assume
A\in{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
\pi=(\pi^{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
be a family of finite signed measures on
[-r,0]
. For
y\in C([-r,0],\mathbb{R}^{n})
, assume that
\int_{-r}^{0}y_{\theta+t}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)
denotes the vector

\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\int_{-r}^{0}y^{i}_{\theta+t}\,\pi^{i,j}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\right)_{1\leq j\leq n}.

•

Assume that for
\gamma>\frac{1}{3}
,
\mathbf{X}
is a delayed
\gamma

The following theorem is our main result in this section, which also generalizes
[

Theorem 3.2
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold, and let
\frac{1}{3}<\beta\leq\gamma

. Assume

and

such that for every
0\leq s<t
, we have

\displaystyle(\delta y)_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}A\left(y_{\tau},\int_{-r}^{0}y_{\theta+\tau}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\right)\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{s}^{t}G\left(y_{\tau},y_{\tau-r}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{\tau}.

Here, the last integral is defined in the sense of Theorem
1.5
. If
A=0

G\in C^{3}_{b}
. We denote the solution in both cases by
{\phi}^{t}_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)
. Also, there exists a polynomial
Q
(which only depends on
\|A\|,G,
and
\pi

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\|\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)\right\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([0,r])}\leq Q\left(\left\|\xi\right\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([-r,0])},\left\|\mathbf{X}\right\|_{\gamma,[0,r]}\right),

where
\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)=(\phi_{\mathbf{X}}^{t}(\xi))_{0\leq t\leq r}
. Additionally, considering the map

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi_{\mathbf{X}}:\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([-r,0])&\rightarrow\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([0,r]),\\
\xi&\mapsto\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi),\end{split}

we have that
\phi_{\mathbf{X}}
is continuous.

Proof.

For the case that
A=0

[
GVR21
, Theorem 2.8]
.
To incorporate the case where
A\neq 0
, we employ the technique known as

, as discussed in
[
RS17
]
. Our proof will be divided into several steps and involves establishing well-posedness and obtaining a priori bounds for the solution. During the proof, when we refer to a polynomial bound, we always mean a two-variable polynomial in terms of
\|\xi\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([-r,0])}
and
\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\gamma,[0,r]}
that bounds our term. Our bounds might also depend on
G
,
\pi

\|A\|

Step 1.
In this step, we first estimate

\sup_{t\in[0,r]}\|\phi^{t}_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)\|

3.1

For
0\leq s\leq t\leq r

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}z_{t}=G\big(z_{t},\xi_{t-r}\big)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t},\\
&\quad z_{s}=y,\end{split}

for

Using a standard fixed-point argument for rough differential equations, we can establish both the existence and uniqueness of a solution to the given equation. Additionally, for
0\leq s\leq u\leq t\leq r
, the flow property is satisfied, which means that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}(s,t,y,\xi)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}(u,t,\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}(s,u,y,\xi),\xi).

Applying a standard argument for estimating (linear) rough differential equations, as detailed in
[
FV10

[

and

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max\big(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}\|(\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}}{\partial y}(s,t,y,\xi)-I\|,\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}\|(\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}}{\partial y}(s,t,y,\xi))^{-1}-I\|\big)\leq\\

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}(s,t,y,\xi)

[
GVR21
, Theorem 2.6]

Let
p_{1}:[0,r]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\big(\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}}{\partial y}(0,t,p_{1}(t),\xi)\big)^{-1}A\big(\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t,p_{1}(t),\xi_{t}),\int_{-r}^{0}{\xi}_{\theta+t}^{1}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\big),\\

\displaystyle{\xi}_{t}^{1}:=\begin{cases}\xi_{t}&\text{for }-r\leq t<0,\\
\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t,p_{1}(t),\xi)&\text{for }t\geq 0.\end{cases}\ \

From the chain rule formula, we observe that
\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t,p_{1}(t),\xi)

1.2
).
Let

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&B(\tau,\|\xi\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([-r,0])},\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\gamma,[0,r]})\\

(3.9)

(3.11)

Step 3.
In this step, we show that the remainder of the solution also grows almost polynomially. The remainder term is given by

\displaystyle(\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi))^{\#}_{s,t}:=\phi^{t}_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)-\phi^{s}_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)-G(\phi^{s}_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi),\xi_{s-r})X_{s,t}.

Assume first
s,t\in[(m-1)t_{1},mt_{1}]
, then

\displaystyle\|(\phi_{\mathbf{X}}^{.}(\xi))^{\#}_{s,t}\|

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},t,p_{m}(t),\xi)-\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},s,p_{m}(s),\xi)-G(\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},s,p_{m}(s),\xi),\xi_{s-r})X_{s,t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},t,p_{m}(t),\xi)-\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},t,p_{m}(s),\xi)\|}_{\text{{III}}(s,t)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\|\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},t,p_{m}(s),\xi)-\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},s,p_{m}(s),\xi)-G(\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}((m-1)t_{1},s,p_{m}(s),\xi),\xi_{s-r})X_{s,t}\|}_{\text{{IV}}(s,t)}.

Note that

(3.26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\text{{III}}(s,t)}{(t-s)^{2\beta}}\leq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}}{\partial y}((m-1)t_{1},t,y,\xi)\right|\frac{|p_{m}(t)-p_{m}(s)|}{(t-s)^{2\beta}}\\
&\leq r^{1-2\beta}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{\mathbf{X}}}{\partial y}((m-1)t_{1},t,y,\xi)\right|\sup_{\tau\in[(m-1)t_{1},mt_{1}]}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{m}(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right|.\end{split}

So it is enough to repeat the same argument that we made in the previous step for estimating
\sup_{s,t\in[(m-1)t_{1},mt_{1}]}\frac{\text{I}(s,t)}{(t-s)^{\beta}}
, to obtain a polynomial bound (independent of
m
) for

\sup_{s,t\in[(m-1)t_{1},mt_{1}]}\frac{\text{III}(s,t)}{(t-s)^{\beta}}.

Also, from (
3.5
)

\displaystyle\text{{IV}}(s,t)\leq(t-s)^{2\beta}P_{1}(\|\xi\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\beta}([-r,0])},\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\gamma,[0,r]}).

Therefore, we conclude that the remainder term for
s,t\in[(m-1)t_{1},mt_{1}]
can be bounded polynomially. To obtain the final estimate on
[0,r]
, we proceed as in the previous step. Indeed, since for
s<u<t
, we have

(\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi))^{\#}_{s,t}=(\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi))^{\#}_{s,u}+(\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi))^{\#}_{u,t}+(\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi))^{\#}_{s,u}X_{u,t},

we can easily conclude that

\displaystyle\|\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)^{\#}\|_{2\beta,[0,r]}\leq(1+\|X\|_{\beta,[0,r]})\sum_{1\leq m\leq N}\|\phi_{\mathbf{X}}(\xi)^{\#}\|_{\beta,[(m-1)t_{1},\min(mt_{1},r)]}.

As in the previous step from
t_{1}
, we know that
N
grows polynomially. This completes our argument for estimating the remainder term.

Step 4.

In the previous steps, we proved the well-posedness of the solution and also found polynomial bounds for the elements of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\beta}}
as defined in (
1.1
). As argued, all of them are bounded by a polynomial, which yields (
3.1
).
So far, we have solved the equation up to time

as a new initial value and solve the equation for

∎

Remark 3.5

In Theorem

4.
Random dynamical systems and invariant manifolds

In this section, we establish the connection between stochastic delay equations and Arnold’s concept of a random dynamical system (RDS). Indeed, due to the deterministic nature of rough path theory and the processes involved in our problem, the RDS provides a natural framework for studying the dynamics of the solutions. We specifically address delay equations with fractional Brownian motions, defining integrals using rough path theory, and describing the delayed Levy area as given by (
2.1
). After proving that our equations generate a random dynamical system, we will demonstrate the existence of invariant manifolds. In the next section, we specifically focus on stable manifolds and explore how our theory can yield several insightful stability results. For the remainder of this section, we assume that Assumption
2.1
holds.
We can now prove that our fractional Brownian motion for every
\frac{1}{3}<\gamma<H
can be lifted to a a delayed
\gamma
-rough path. We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

.
For
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
, set

\mathbb{B}_{s,t}(-r):=\int_{s}^{t}B_{s-r,u-r}\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{u}.

Then for every closed interval
J\subset\mathbb{R}
, on a set of full measure
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
, we can obtain a delayed
\gamma
-rough path
\overline{\mathbf{B}}(\omega)=(B(\omega),\mathbb{B}(\omega),\mathbb{B}(-r)(\omega))
, such that
(B(\omega),\mathbb{B}(\omega))
is a geometric rough path for every
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}
.

Proof.

This result already appears in
[
NNT08
]
, but for the reader’s convenience, we repeat the main ideas here. It is known that we can lift
B
to a geometric rough path; cf.
[
FH20
, Corollary 10.10]
. Also, from (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we have

\sup_{s,t\in J}\mathbb{E}|\mathbb{B}_{s,t}(-r)|^{2}\leq C(t-s)^{4H},

where
C>0
is a constant and
H
is the Hurst parameter of the fractional Brownian motion
B
. Since
\mathbb{B}(-r)
is an element in the second Wiener chaos, this bound holds in the
L^{q}
-norm for any
q\geq 1
; see
[
FV10
, Proposition 15.22]
. Thus, we can apply the Kolmogorov–Chentsov theorem; cf.
[
FH20
, Theorem 3.1]
, to conclude that, on a set of full measure
\tilde{\Omega}
, for the process
\mathbb{B}(-r)
and for every closed interval
J
:

\sup_{s,t\in J}\frac{|\mathbb{B}(-r)|}{(t-s)^{2\gamma}}<\infty,

where
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
depends on
H
.
This completes our proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

With a similar argument, we can also define
\mathbb{B}
as a stochastic process like
\mathbb{B}(-r)
using the symmetric integral (cf. Definition
A.1
). Since
\mathbb{B}
is geometric, this definition coincides with the one introduced in

]
[Corollary 10.10]. The proofs are analogous to those we provided for

, the following identity holds:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{B}_{s,t}:=\int_{s}^{t}B_{s,u}\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{u}\\
&\quad=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left(\delta^{B^{i}}(B^{j}_{s,.}\chi_{[s,t]}(.))+\delta_{i=j}\left(\frac{1}{2}((t-s)^{2H})\right)\right)e_{i}\otimes e_{j}.\end{split}

Like

, we can apply the Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem to extend this process to the Lévy area. In addition, from (

), for every

, we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\gamma,[0,r]}\in\bigcap_{p\geq 1}L^{p}(\Omega).

The interesting aspect of our delayed rough path is its ability to generate an ergodic random dynamical system. Two key features that enable us to demonstrate this are: first, the fact that fractional Brownian motions possess stationary increments; and second, the observation that the Lévy area and the delayed Lévy area can be derived by regularizing the path in a limiting process. First, we need some technical definitions.

Definition 4.3
.

Assume

-rough path-valued stochastic process

Definition 4.4
.

\displaystyle\tilde{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\big\{\big(1\oplus(\alpha,\beta)\oplus(\gamma,\theta)\big)\ |\ \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \text{and}\ \gamma,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}\big\}.

Remark 4.9

, can be assumed to be a linear function. Specifically, we can assume that
G\in L(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Under this assumption, our solution exists globally in time for every initial value
\xi\in\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}[-r,0]
, and the cocycle property (
4.7
) holds. We do not provide an explicit proof here, as this result is a straightforward modification of Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
4.8
.

While the collection of spaces
(\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}[-r,0])_{\omega\in\Omega}
allows us to define a generalized cocycle, these spaces are not separable and thus are not suitable for further investigations. To remedy this problem, we need to slightly modify these spaces and define our cocycle on some invariant subspace of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}[-r,0]
(under
\varphi
). This technique was first carry out in
[
GVRS22
]
, for the Brownian motions. Before introducing these modified spaces, we need to recall the following abstract definition:

Definition 4.10
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space. We call a family of Banach spaces
\{E_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
a
measurable field of Banach spaces
if there is a set of sections

\displaystyle\Delta\subset\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}E_{\omega}

with the following properties:

(i)

\Delta
is a linear subspace of
\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}E_{\omega}
.

(ii)

There is a countable subset
\Delta_{0}\subset\Delta
such that for every
\omega\in\Omega
, the set
\{g(\omega)\,:\,g\in\Delta_{0}\}
is dense in
E_{\omega}
.

(iii)

For every
g\in\Delta
, the map
\omega\mapsto\|g(\omega)\|_{E_{\omega}}
is measurable.

Remark 4.11
.

Alternatively, when
\Omega
is a metric space, we can define a stronger concept called
continuous fields of Banach spaces
. In this case, the third condition in Definition
4.10
must be replaced by:

(iii)
′

For every
g\in\Delta
, the map
\omega\mapsto\|g(\omega)\|_{E_{\omega}}
is continuous.

We have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.12
.

Assume
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta)
,
\frac{1}{3}<\beta<\gamma<H
, and let
\mathbf{B}
be the delayed
\gamma
-rough path cocycle constructed in Theorem
4.7
. For every
\omega\in\Omega
, define

\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0]):=\overline{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}([-r,0])}^{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\gamma}([-r,0])},

where
\overline{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}([-r,0])}^{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\gamma}([-r,0])}
denotes the closure of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}([-r,0])
in
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\gamma}([-r,0])
. Then
\{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0])\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
forms a measurable field of Banach spaces.

Proof.

For
0<\epsilon<\gamma
, let
C^{0,1-\epsilon}=C^{0,1-\epsilon}[-r,0]
denote the space obtained by taking the closure of
C^{\infty}
with respect to the
(1-\epsilon)
-Hölder norm. It is not hard to see that
this space is a separable Banach space. Set

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Gamma_{\omega}:=(\prod_{d\times n}C^{0,1-\epsilon})\times\prod_{n}C^{0,1-\epsilon}\longrightarrow\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0]),\\
&\Gamma_{\omega}((f,g))(t):=\int_{-r}^{t}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}+g(\tau):=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\int_{-r}^{t}f_{i}(\tau)\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{\tau}(\omega)+g(\tau),\end{split}

where the integrals are understood in the usual Young’s sense and
f_{i}:[-r,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that since
1-\epsilon+\gamma>1
, the integrals are well-defined. Now we define

\displaystyle\Delta:=\left\{\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\Gamma_{\omega}(h)\big|\ \ h=(f,g)\in(\prod_{d\times n}C^{0,1-\epsilon})\times\prod_{n}C^{0,1-\epsilon}\right\}.

Select a countable dense subset
(\prod_{d\times n}C^{0,1-\epsilon})\times\prod_{n}C^{0,1-\epsilon}
and define

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}:=\left\{\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\Gamma_{\omega}(h)\big|\ \ h\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\right\}.

We claim that by these constructions, we can fulfill the conditions of Definition
4.10
for
\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0])
.

(i)

By definition, it is clear that
\Delta
is a linear subspace of
\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0])
.

(ii)

The density claim for
\Delta_{0}
follows from item (ii) in
[
GVRST22
, Theorem 3.10]
.

(iii)

Let
h\in(\prod_{d\times n}C^{0,1-\epsilon})\times\prod_{n}C^{0,1-\epsilon}
, we must check that the following function is (Borel) measurable

\displaystyle\omega\longrightarrow\|\Gamma_{\omega}(h)\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0])}.

This is clear since, in (
4.8
), the integrals are defined in the Young sense. By the usual partition approximation, the measurability can be easily verified (see
[
GVRS22
, Proposition 3.15]
for more details).

So, our claim is proved.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

Since our probability space is
\Omega:=C_{0}^{0,p\text{-var}}(\mathbb{R},\tilde{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(cf. Theorem

[

, where a much more general statement is proven for the space of branched rough paths.

.

A similar result for the case where

is a Brownian motion is proven in

. However, this result is specific to Brownian motion, and unlike Theorem

). Theorem

not only generalizes this result to fractional Brownian motion but also relaxes the restriction on the choice of

Remark 4.15
.

In the case where
H=\frac{1}{2}
, i.e., the Brownian motion case, we can alternatively define

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{s,t}^{\text{It\={o}}}:=\left(B_{s,t},\mathbb{B}_{s,t}^{\text{It\={o}}},\mathbb{B}_{s,t}^{\text{It\={o}}}(-r)\right):=\left(B_{t}-B_{s},\int_{s}^{t}(B_{u}-B_{s})\otimes\mathrm{d}B_{u},\int_{s}^{t}(B_{u-r}-B_{s-r})\otimes\mathrm{d}B_{u}\right),

where the stochastic integrals are understood in the Itō sense. In this context, the Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem allows us to recover a delayed

-rough path, which is not geometric, as discussed in

]
[Proposition 2.2]. Moreover, as explored in

Recall that our solution to Equation
4.5
defines a cocycle over the family of random Banach spaces
\{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}([-r,0])\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
for every
\frac{1}{3}<\beta\leq\gamma<H
. As previously mentioned, this family of random Banach spaces possesses advantageous properties that enable us to define a generalized cocycle for the solution to Equation
4.5
. However, these Banach spaces are not separable, which presents a technical challenge: it hinders our ability to prove certain measurability conditions necessary for the Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem.
To overcome this obstacle, we consider the parameters
\frac{1}{3}<\beta<\gamma<H
and work with
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0])
instead. By definition, these spaces are invariant under the solution, meaning that the properties (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) hold when we replace
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta}([-r,0])
with
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0])
. We are now ready to present our main results in this chapter regarding the existence of invariant manifolds. Similar to deterministic dynamical systems, we are interested in certain invariant subsets around the equilibrium. In the context of random dynamical systems, the counterpart to an equilibrium point is the stationary point, which will be defined in the following definition.

Definition 4.16
.

Let
\frac{1}{3}<\beta<\gamma<H
be fixed parameters. We define
E_{\omega}:=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{B}(\omega)}^{\beta,\gamma}([-r,0])
. Let
\varphi
be the solution of equation
4.5
, which defines a cocycle with respect to the metric dynamical system
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta)
. A collection of random points
\{Y_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
is called a
stationary point
of
\varphi
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

Y_{\omega}\in E_{\omega}
,

(ii)

\varphi^{m}_{\omega}(Y_{\omega})=Y_{\theta_{mr}\omega}
,

(iii)

\omega\mapsto\|Y_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}}
is measurable.

Remark 4.17
.

A simple example of a stationary point is the zero path when
G(0,0)=0
.

The following result guarantees that when equation
4.5
admits a stationary point, the linearized equation generates certain deterministic values (Lyapunov exponents) that determine the dynamical behavior of our solution.

Proposition 4.18
.

Consider the equation in Theorem
4.8
and assume that
\varphi
is the cocycle defined for this equation (restricted to
(E_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
). Further, assume that
\{Y_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
is a stationary point of this cocycle such that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|Y_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}}\in\bigcap_{p\geq 1}L^{p}(\Omega).

Then
\varphi
is differentiable (in the Fréchet sense), and for
\psi^{m}_{\omega}:=D_{Y_{\omega}}\varphi^{m}
, the linear cocycle property holds. That is, for
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\psi^{p+q}_{\omega}=\psi^{p}_{\theta_{rq}\omega}\circ\psi^{q}_{\omega}.

Furthermore, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
\psi^{m}_{\omega}:E_{\omega}\to E_{\theta_{mr}\omega}
is linear. For
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, set

F_{\mu}(\omega):=\left\{x\in E_{\omega}:\ \limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log\|\psi^{m}_{\omega}(x)\|_{E_{\theta_{mr}\omega}}\leq\mu\right\}.

Then, we have:

•

There exists a measurable
\theta_{r}
-invariant set
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
of full measure, along with a decreasing sequence
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
of Lyapunov exponents,
\mu_{i}\in[-\infty,\infty)
, which have the properties
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}=-\infty
and either
\mu_{i}>\mu_{i+1}
or
\mu_{i}=\mu_{i+1}=-\infty
. For each
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}
, we have
F_{\mu_{1}}(\omega)=E_{\omega}
, and

(4.10)

\displaystyle x\in F_{\mu_{i}}(\omega)\setminus F_{\mu_{i+1}}(\omega)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log\|\psi^{m}_{\omega}(x)\|_{E_{\theta_{mr}\omega}}=\mu_{i}.

Moreover, there exist integers
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots
such that
\operatorname{codim}F_{\mu_{j}}(\omega)=m_{1}+\ldots+m_{j-1}
for each
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}
. Furthermore, for every
i\geq 1
with
\mu_{i}>\mu_{i+1}
and
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}
, there exists an
m_{i}
-dimensional subspace

with the following characteristics:

(i)

(Invariance)

\psi_{\omega}^{k}(H^{i}_{\omega})=H^{i}_{\theta_{kr}\omega}
for each
k\geq 0
.

(ii)

(Splitting)

H_{\omega}^{i}\oplus F_{\mu_{i+1}}(\omega)=F_{\mu_{i}}(\omega)
. Specifically,

\displaystyle E_{\omega}=H^{1}_{\omega}\oplus\cdots\oplus H^{i}_{\omega}\oplus F_{\mu_{i+1}}(\omega).

(iii)

(Fast-growing subspace)
For every
h_{\omega}\in H^{i}_{\omega}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log\|\psi^{m}_{\omega}(h_{\omega})\|_{E_{\theta_{mr}\omega}}=\mu_{i},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log\|(\psi^{m}_{\theta_{-mr}\omega})^{-1}(h_{\omega})\|_{E_{\omega}}=-\mu_{i}.

Proof.

From Theorem
4.12
, the family of random spaces
\{E_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
forms a measurable (continuous) field of Banach spaces. Additionally, Theorem
4.8
establishes that
\varphi
is a nonlinear cocycle, and Theorem
3.3
shows that
\varphi
is also differentiable. By the definition of a stationary point, it is straightforward to see that
\psi_{\omega}^{m}:=D_{Y_{\omega}}\varphi^{m}_{\omega}
is also a cocycle. This can be verified using the fact that
\varphi^{p+q}_{\omega}(x)=\varphi^{p}_{\theta_{qr}\omega}\circ\varphi^{q}_{\omega}(x)
and applying the chain rule (by taking derivatives around the stationary points). Furthermore, Theorem
3.3
ensures that for a polynomial
Q_{1}
, the following holds:

(4.11)

\displaystyle\log^{+}(\|\psi^{1}_{\omega}\|_{L(E_{\omega},E_{\theta_{r}\omega})})\leq Q_{1}(\|Y_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}},\|\mathbf{B}(\omega)\|_{\gamma,[0,r]})\in L^{1}(\Omega),

where
\log^{+}x\coloneqq\max\{0,\log x\}
. Note that the integrability claim in the above equation follows from (
4.9
) and the fact that
\|\mathbf{B}(\omega)\|_{\gamma,[0,r]}\in\bigcap_{p\geq 1}L^{p}(\Omega)
; cf. (
4.2
). Note that, from
[
GVRS22
, Proposition 1.9]
,
\psi^{1}_{\omega}
is also a compact map for every

. Now our claim follows from
[
GVR23c
]
[Theorem 1.21].
∎

Having established the Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem, we can infer the existence of invariant manifolds (stable, center, and unstable) around the stationary points. The existence of stable and unstable manifolds in the case of Brownian motion is already established in
[
GVR21
]
. The contribution of the following results is twofold: first, we establish the same results (existence of stable and unstable manifolds) in more general cases, including fractional Brownian motion; second, we deduce the existence of center manifolds, which has been missing in the literature.
Note that, due to the foundational work we have established, we can prove these results with relative ease by applying some abstract results. The nice part of these three results is that their proofs rely on (almost) the same conditions. The next result guarantees the existence of an invariant stable manifold around the stationary point. This result, in particular, is important because it enables us to conclude local exponential stability when all the Lyapunov exponents are negative.

Theorem 4.19

(Local stable manifolds)
.

Consider the equation in Theorem
4.8
, and assume that
\varphi
is the cocycle defined for this equation, restricted to
(E_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
. Furthermore, suppose that
\{Y_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
is a stationary point of this cocycle, and that the assumptions made in Theorem
3.4
regarding
A
,
\pi
and
G
are also satisfied. Let
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be the Lyapunov exponents corresponding to the linear cocycle

, and define
\mu^{-}:=\max\{\mu_{j}:\mu_{j}<0\}

Then, there exists a
\theta_{r}
-invariant set of full measure
\tilde{\Omega}
and a family of immersed submanifolds
S^{\upsilon}_{\text{loc}}(\omega)
of
E_{\omega}
, where

and
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}
, satisfying the following properties for every
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}

(i)

There exist random variables
\rho_{1}^{\upsilon}(\omega)
and
\rho_{2}^{\upsilon}(\omega)
, which are positive and finite on
\tilde{\Omega}
, such that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\liminf_{p\to\infty}\frac{1}{p}\log\rho_{i}^{\upsilon}(\theta_{pr}\omega)\geq 0,\quad i=1,2,

and the following inclusions hold:

\displaystyle\big\{\xi_{\omega}\in E_{\omega}\,:\,\sup_{m\geqslant 0}\exp(m\upsilon)

(ii)

The tangent space at
Y_{\omega}
satisfies

\displaystyle T_{Y_{\omega}}S^{\upsilon}_{\text{loc}}(\omega)=S_{\omega}:=F_{\mu^{-}}(\omega).

(iii)

For
m\geqslant M(\omega)

\displaystyle\varphi^{m}_{\omega}(S^{\upsilon}_{\text{loc}}(\omega))\subseteq S^{\upsilon}_{\text{loc}}(\theta_{mr}\omega).

(iv)

For

\displaystyle S^{\upsilon_{2}}_{\text{loc}}(\omega)\subseteq S^{\upsilon_{1}}_{\text{loc}}(\omega).

Additionally, for

\displaystyle\varphi^{m}_{\omega}(S^{\upsilon_{1}}_{\text{loc}}(\omega))\subseteq S^{\upsilon_{2}}_{\text{loc}}(\theta_{mr}(\omega)),

(4.13)

\displaystyle\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log\|\varphi^{m}_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega})-Y_{\theta_{mr}\omega}\|_{E_{\theta_{mr}\omega}}\leqslant\mu^{-}.

(v)

The following holds:

\displaystyle\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log\bigg[\sup\bigg\{\frac{\|\varphi^{m}_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega})-\varphi^{m}_{\omega}(\tilde{\xi_{\omega}})\|_{E_{\theta_{mr}\omega}}}{\|\xi_{\omega}-\tilde{\xi_{\omega}}\|_{E_{\omega}}},\ \ \xi_{\omega}\neq\tilde{\xi}_{\omega},\ \xi,\tilde{\xi}_{\omega}\in S^{\upsilon}_{\text{loc}}(\omega)\bigg\}\bigg]\leqslant\mu^{-}.

Proof.

Set

\displaystyle P_{\omega}:E_{\omega}\rightarrow E_{\theta_{r}\omega},

\displaystyle P_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega}):=\varphi^{1}_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega}+Y_{\omega})-\varphi^{1}_{\omega}(Y_{\omega})-\psi^{1}_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega}).

Then from Theorem
3.4

(i)

If
A\neq 0
and
G\in C^{4}_{b}

(4.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|P_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega})-P_{\omega}(\tilde{\xi}_{\omega})\|_{E_{\theta_{r}\omega}}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|(D_{\tau\xi_{\omega}+(1-\tau)\tilde{\xi}_{\omega}+Y_{\omega}}\varphi^{1}_{\omega}-D_{Y_{\omega}}\varphi^{1}_{\omega})[\xi_{\omega}-\tilde{\xi}_{\omega}]\right\|_{E_{\theta_{r}\omega}}\mathrm{d}\tau\\

and

\displaystyle\|P_{\omega}(\xi_{\omega})-P_{\omega}(\tilde{\xi}_{\omega})\|_{E_{\theta_{r}\omega}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\xi_{\omega}-\tilde{\xi}_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}}\left(\|\xi_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}}+\|\tilde{\xi}_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}}\right)\exp\left(R_{0}\left(\|\xi_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}}+\|\tilde{\xi}_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\underbrace{\exp\left(R_{1}\left(\|Y_{\omega}\|_{E_{\omega}},\|\mathbf{B}(\omega)\|_{\gamma,[0,r]}\right)\right)}_{f(\omega)}.

\displaystyle\log^{+}(f(\omega))\in L(\Omega).

So, from the Birkhoff ergodic theorem, we have

Therefore, we have verified the condition of

5.
Intermezzo: An MET-approach to exponential stability of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-semigroups

We have established a general framework for analyzing the dynamics of fractional delay equations. In this section, we discuss several general results related to the spectrum of compact semigroups. Although many of these results have been previously established in other contexts, such as those detailed in
[
HVL93
, Chapter 7]
and
[
MR18
, Chapter 4]
, we offer new proofs by leveraging Lyapunov exponents to provide concise and streamlined arguments. These results will later be integrated into our framework to investigate the exponential stability of rough delay equations. As noted, the generalized version of the MET is crucial for achieving these outcomes. We will first provide some background on the semigroup associated with delay equations.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a real Banach space. A strongly continuous semigroup of linear operators, or a
C_{0}
-semigroup, is a one-parameter family
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{B}
that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

Semigroup Property:
For all
t,s\geq 0
, we have

T_{t+s}=T_{t}\circ T_{s}.

(2)

Identity at Zero:
The operator
T_{0}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{B}
, i.e.,

T_{0}=I.

(3)

Strong Continuity:
For every
x\in\mathcal{B}
, the map
t\mapsto T_{t}x
is continuous from
[0,\infty)
to
\mathcal{B}
.

To every
C_{0}
-semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
on a Banach space
\mathcal{B}
, we can associate an infinitesimal generator
L
defined by

{L}z=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{T(t)z-z}{t},

for all
z\in\mathcal{D}(L)
. The domain of the generator
L
, denoted by
\mathcal{D}(L)
, is given by

\mathcal{D}(L)=\left\{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}\mid\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{T(t)\varphi-\varphi}{t}\text{ exists and is finite}\right\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{D}(L)
consists of all elements
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}
for which the limit above exists. The generator
\mathcal{L}
is a densely defined linear operator mapping
\mathcal{D}(L)
into
\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 5.2
.

The resolvent set
\rho(L)
of an operator
L
is the set of all complex numbers
\lambda
such that the operator
\lambda I-L
has a bounded inverse, and the domain of this inverse is dense in
\mathcal{B}
. This inverse will be denoted by
R(\lambda,L)
. The complement of
\rho(L)
in the complex plane is called the spectrum of
L
and is denoted by
\sigma(L)
. The spectrum of an operator may consist of three different types of points:

(1)

Residual Spectrum

\sigma_{r}(L)
: These are the values
\lambda\in\sigma(L)
for which the resolvent operator
R(\lambda,L)
exists, but its domain
\mathcal{D}(R(\lambda,L))
is not dense in
\mathcal{B}
.

(2)

Continuous Spectrum

\sigma_{c}(L)
: These are the values
\lambda\in\sigma(L)
for which
\lambda I-L
has an unbounded inverse with a dense domain.

(3)

Point Spectrum

\sigma_{p}(L)
: These are the values
\lambda\in\sigma(L)
for which
\lambda I-L
does not have an inverse.

The theory of semigroups is well-established, with many abstract results, particularly for
C_{0}
-semigroups. Since this manuscript focuses on delay equations, we will primarily address this type of equation. Specifically, we aim to demonstrate how classical stability in the deterministic case can be leveraged to obtain path-wise stability for stochastic delay equations. We denote by
C([-r,0],\mathbb{R}^{n})

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\|\xi\|_{\infty,[-r,0]}:=\|\xi\|_{C([-r,0],\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\sup_{\tau\in[-r,0]}|\xi_{\tau}|,

With this norm,

Lemma 5.3

Assume

6.
Pathwise stability results

In this section, we obtained several exponential stability results for both linear and nonlinear cases. It is natural to expect that when the drift part of an equation is exponentially stable, the solution will remain stable under stochastic perturbations from the diffusion term, provided the diffusion is not too large. Demonstrating this stability, especially for non-Markovian equations and even for standard stochastic equations in finite-dimensional settings, is highly non-trivial. For delay equations, the situation is even more complex due to the infinite-dimensional Banach spaces in which we work. When the noise is driven by Brownian motion, we can use classical Itô integration, with many established tools for studying the asymptotic behavior of solutions, such as Lyapunov functions. However, a limitation of the classical approach is its lack of pathwise results. In this article, we explored a pathwise approach, raising the natural question of how these two approaches relate, particularly in the context of Brownian motion. Therefore, before presenting our stability results, we aim to bridge the gap between these approaches. This connection allows us to enlarge the space of initial values and obtain stronger stability results in a pathwise manner.

Assumption 6.1
.

In this section, unless explicitly stated otherwise, the noise

So far, we have considered Brownian motion. In this section, we provide some necessary definitions and theorems from Malliavin’s calculus and fractional integrals. Most of these results, along with their proofs, can be found in
[

Let’s begin with fractional calculus. For
0<\alpha<1

\mathbb{R}
are defined as follows:

(A.1)

\displaystyle(I^{\alpha}_{-}f)(x):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\infty}f(t)(t-x)^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}t,

(A.2)

\displaystyle(I^{\alpha}_{+}f)(x):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)(x-t)^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}t.

receptively.
We say that
f\in D(I^{\alpha}_{-})
, if (
A.1
)is well-defined for a.s
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is known that for

L^{p}(\mathbb{R})\subset D(I^{\alpha}_{-})
.
The right and left-sided Riemann– Liouville fractional derivatives of order
\alpha
, are defined by

\displaystyle(I^{-\alpha}_{-}f)(x):=\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\int_{x}^{\infty}f(t)(t-x)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle(I^{-\alpha}_{+}f)(x):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)(x-t)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}t

repetitively.
For
p\geq 1
, by
I^{\alpha}_{-}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R}))
(

), we mean the class of functions such that

for some
\phi\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
. If
p>1
, then for every
f\in I^{\alpha}_{-}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R}))
(
f\in I^{\alpha}_{+}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R}))
), the right and left-sided Riemann–Liouville derivatives are equal to the Marchaud fractional derivatives, which are defined by:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{-}f)(x):=\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)-f(x+t)}{t^{1+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{+}f)(x):=\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)-f(x-t)}{t^{1+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}t.

It is easy to check that
\langle I^{-\alpha}_{-}f,g\rangle=\langle f,I^{-\alpha}_{+}g\rangle
.
Obviously if
\text{supp}(f)\subset(-\infty,b)
, then
\text{supp}(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{-}f)\subset(-\infty,b)
and

\displaystyle x\in(-\infty,b):\ \ (\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{-}f)(x)=\frac{f(x)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)(b-x)^{\alpha}}+\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{x}^{b}\frac{f(x)-f(t)}{(t-x)^{1+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}t.

For
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}-H
and
C_{H}=\big(\int_{0}^{\infty}((1+s)^{-\alpha}-s^{-\alpha})^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2H}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(H+\frac{1}{2})

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{H}_{-}(f):=C_{H}I^{-\alpha}_{-}(f).

We define
\mathcal{H}=L_{2}^{H}(\mathbb{R}):=\{f:\mathcal{M}^{H}_{-}(f)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\}
. For

, the operator

is an isometric isomorphism from
\mathcal{H}
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In fact,

is a Hilbert space, and it can be characterized as the closure of the set of step functions with respect to the norm induced by

\displaystyle\langle(\chi_{[s_{1},t_{1}]},...,\chi_{[s_{d},t_{d}]}),

\displaystyle(\chi_{[u_{1},v_{1}]},...,\chi_{[u_{d},v_{d}]})\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}\big(R_{H}(s_{i},u_{i})+R_{H}(t_{i},v_{i})-R_{H}(s_{i},v_{i})-R_{H}(t_{i},u_{i})\big).

Where

\displaystyle R_{H}(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H})\ .

We also can define the isonormal stochastic Gaussian process
B=\{B(\phi)=(B^{i}(\phi^{i}))_{1\leq i\leq d},\phi=(\phi^{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}\}
on a complete probability space

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\langle B(\phi),B(\psi)\rangle)=\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\langle\phi^{i},\psi^{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\ \ \phi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{d}.

In particular,
B(\chi_{[s_{1},t_{1}]},...,\chi_{[s_{d},t_{d}]})=(B^{i}(t_{i})-B^{i}(s_{i}))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}
.
Note that for
\phi=(\phi^{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
and
\psi=(\psi^{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}

(A.3)

\displaystyle\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{d}:\ \ \langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\langle D^{\frac{1}{2}-H}_{-}\phi,D^{\frac{1}{2}-H}_{-}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\langle D^{\frac{1}{2}-H}_{-}\phi^{i},D^{\frac{1}{2}-H}_{-}\psi^{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

Consequently, for
\phi\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
with
\text{supp}(\phi)\subset(a,b)

(A.4)

\displaystyle\|\phi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq M\left[\int_{a}^{b}\frac{|\phi(u)|^{2}}{(b-u)^{2\alpha}}\mathrm{d}u+\int_{-\infty}^{a}\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\phi(x)}{(x-u)^{1+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}x\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}u+\int_{a}^{b}\left(\int_{u}^{b}\frac{|\phi(u)-\phi(x)|}{(x-u)^{1+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}u\right].

where
M
is a constant.

A.1.
Stochastic integrals

Suppose
B
is a fractional Brownian motion with
H<\frac{1}{2}
. To define the stochastic integral with respect to
B
, it is more natural to work with the symmetric integral introduced by Russo and Vallois.

Definition A.1
.

For a given process
u=(u^{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}:[s,t]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the symmetric integral is defined as

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}u(x)\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{x}:=L^{2}(\Omega)-\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{s}^{t}u^{i}(x)\frac{B_{r+\epsilon}^{i}-B_{x-\epsilon}^{i}}{2\epsilon}\mathrm{d}r.

when it exists. We also set

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}u(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{x}:=L^{2}(\Omega)-\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{s}^{t}u^{i}(x)\frac{B_{r+\epsilon}^{j}-B_{x-\epsilon}^{j}}{2\epsilon}\mathrm{d}r\ e_{i}\otimes e_{j},

when it exists.

Remark A.2
.

Assume that
u:[s,t]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{1}
function, then it is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}u(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}B_{x}=\int_{s}^{t}u(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}^{\circ}B_{x},\ \ \text{in}\ \ L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Where the left integral is defined pathwise.

The following proposition relates the symmetric integral to the Skorohod integral.
