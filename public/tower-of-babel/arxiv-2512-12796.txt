Optimal design for the planar Skorokhod embedding problem
Maher Boudabra

Abstract
We revisit the planar Skorokhod embedding problem introduced by Gross and developed further by Boudabra-Markowsky, and we place it in a fully variational framework. For a centered probability measure μ \mu with finite second moment, we show that Gross’ μ \mu -domain U μ G U_{\mu}^{G} uniquely minimizes the area among all simply connected μ \mu -domains. Equivalently, U μ G U_{\mu}^{G} minimizes a natural H 1 2 H^{\frac{1}{2}} -type boundary energy, providing an optimal design interpretation of the planar Skorokhod embedding problem. The proof relies on the Fourier characterization of fractional Sobolev spaces on the circle, symmetric decreasing rearrangement of the quantile of μ \mu , and a one-dimensional fractional P ​ ó ​ l ​ y ​ a − S ​ z ​ e ​ g ​ ő P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o} inequality. Within the Schlicht class, we obtain a sharp model case: under a natural normalization of the quantile, the Gross area is uniquely minimized by the shifted arcsine distribution. This identifies the Gross domain of the arcsine law as the extremal Schlicht solution to the planar Skorokhod embedding problem. In the second part of the paper we return to Brownian symmetrization. Using the optimality of U μ G U_{\mu}^{G} , we prove that Brownian symmetrization is area-nonincreasing and, in fact, dominates Steiner symmetrization in this regard. We then quantify the ratio ρ ⁡ ( U ) := 𝒜 ⁡ ( U μ G ) 𝒜 ⁡ ( U ) , \rho(U):=\frac{\mathcal{A}(U_{\mu}^{G})}{\mathcal{A}(U)}, showing that, among simply connected domains of fixed area, every value in ( 0 , 1 ] (0,1] is attained. A family of thin rectangles provides a striking example where the associated one-dimensional laws collapse to a Dirac mass in L 2 L^{2} while the Gross domain does not shrink, illustrating the genuinely fractional nature of the underlying H 1 2 H^{\frac{1}{2}} -geometry. Altogether, our results clarify the geometric content of the planar Skorokhod embedding and open a systematic program of “optimal design” via Brownian symmetrization.

Introduction and results

In a recent paper that appeared in
2019

[
13
]
, the
author R. Gross considered an interesting planar version of the Skorokhod
problem. The one-dimensional version of the Skorokhod problem was
first introduced in
1961
(See
[
20
]
for a concise survey).
The statement of the planar Skorokhod embedding problem is as follows:
Given a distribution
\mu
with zero mean and finite second moment,
is there a simply connected domain
U
containing the origin such
that if
Z_{t}=X_{t}+Y_{t}i
is a standard planar Brownian motion,

has the distribution
\mu
, where

in a prescribed way upon first hitting its boundary.

Figure 1.1:
The real part
X_{\tau}=\Re(Z_{\tau})
samples as
\mu
.

Gross provided a constructive solution to the problem. In addition,
he showed that the exit time
\tau
has a finite average. In
[
8
]
,
Boudabra and Markowsky extended the framework of Gross’ technique
to cover all centered probability measures of finite

-moment

\mathbf{E}(\tau^{\frac{p}{2}})<+\infty.

In terms of geometry of the Gross’ solution, they provided a criterion
to guarantee its uniqueness. Later on, the two authors published a
second paper which generates a new class of domains that solve the
PSEP. As in their first paper, the new solution applies to any distribution
of a finite
p^{th}
-moment (
p>1
). Furthermore, a uniqueness criterion
was given. Following the terminology coined in
[
8
]
,
we shall keep using their terminology “
\mu
-domain” to denote
any simply connected domain that solves the PSEP. Through this manuscript,
we adopt the notation:

•

\mu
-domain.

•

U_{\mu}^{G}
for the Gross solution.

•

U_{\mu}^{BM}
for the Boudabra-Markowsky solution.

•

\mathbb{D}
for the open unit disc in the plane.

•

f_{U}
for any univalent function mapping
\mathbb{D}
onto a domain

The subscript for

may be omitted if the context is clear. Finally,
the term “domain” means a simply connected domain containing the

The planar Skorokhod embedding problem does more than assert existence;
it inaugurates a variational viewpoint. One seeks a
\mu
-domain

U_{\mu}
that is
optimal
among all feasible domains.
Typical objectives include minimizing or maximizing

•

the exit time moments
\mathbf{E}(\tau_{U}^{\delta})

Note that

•

the Dirichlet (Hardy) energy of the conformal map

•

geometric costs such as capacity or perimeter.

In this guise, admissible domains play the role of feasible designs.
The target is an

optimization paradigm that links conformal mapping, stochastic control,

\underset{U:\mu\text{-domain}}{\inf/\sup}\mathcal{J}(U)

where

We recall now the existing results. In

The

As the solutions of the PSEP are simply connected domains, it is natural

A univalent function of the form (

Among all Schlicht probability measures,

2
Tools and Proofs.

In this section we prove our results, and discuss related issues.
The approach combines several tools from the modern theory of nonlocal
function spaces and geometric inequalities. On the analytic side,
we work within the framework of fractional (weak) Sobolev spaces,
which provides the natural setting for energies driven by the fractional
Laplacian. On the geometric side, we exploit symmetrization techniques
for domains and functions, in particular Schwarz symmetrization and
its variants. The bridge between these two ingredients is furnished
by the celebrated
P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o}
inequality, which asserts that
suitable Dirichlet-type energies do not increase under symmetric decreasing
rearrangement. Putting these ingredients together allows us to reduce
the problem to a radial situation and to identify the relevant extremal
configurations.

In this section,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an open set,
1\leq p\leq+\infty

and
0<s<1
. We write
L^{p}(\Omega)
for the usual Lebesgue space.
The symbol
\propto
stands for proportionality.

We begin by recalling a few basic notions from the theory of fractional
Sobolev spaces. For a comprehensive treatment of this subject, including
the various equivalent definitions, embedding theorems and trace results,
we refer the reader to the monograph
[
16
]
and to
the survey
[
12
]
. The limiting connections with
classical Sobolev spaces, as
s\to 1
or
s\to 0
, go back to the
seminal works
[
9
,
19
]
. In the present paper, however, we
only need a small portion of this general theory: essentially the
definition of weak fractional Sobolev spaces via the Gagliardo seminorm,
a few basic properties and the associated form of the fractional Laplacian.
For the convenience of the reader, we briefly recall these ingredients
below and refer to the above references for all further background.

2.1
Weak Sobolev space

For
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
the
\emph{Gagliardoseminorm}
of
u

is defined by the quantity

[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}:=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{N+sp}}\;dx\,dy\bigg)^{1/p},}&1\leq p<\infty,\\[11.99998pt]
{\displaystyle\text{ess sup}_{x,y\in\Omega,\ x\neq y}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{s}},}&p=\infty.\end{cases}

Note that
[\cdot]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}
is a seminorm as it assigns
the magnitude zero to constant functions for example.

Definition 3
.

The
\emph{fractionalSobolevspace}

W^{s,p}(\Omega)
is defined
by

W^{s,p}(\Omega):=\Big\{u\in L^{p}(\Omega),\,\,[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}<+\infty\Big\},

endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}:=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}.

For
p=2
we write
H^{s}(\Omega):=W^{s,2}(\Omega)
.

Remark 4

(Fourier characterization on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
)
.

Note that

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\eta[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}

for some
\eta>0
. Hence,

W^{s,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega).

When
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
p=2
, the space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

admits the equivalent description

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})=\Big\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s/2}\,\widehat{u}(\xi)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\Big\},

with equivalent norm

\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{2}\propto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s}|\widehat{u}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi.

Moreover,

[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\propto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\xi|^{2s}|\widehat{u}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi.

In fact,
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a Hilbert space where the inner
product is given by

(u,v)_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy

Thus, in the Hilbertian case
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
may be interpreted
as the space of functions having “
s
derivatives in
L^{2}
”
in a Fourier sense. The double integral in the Gagliardo seminorm
measures the average size of the difference quotient

\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{N+sp}}

over all pairs
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega
, where the kernel
|x-y|^{-N-sp}

plays the role of a “fractional Jacobian”. In this sense
W^{s,p}(\Omega)

controls both the size of
u
and its oscillations on all scales,
interpolating between
L^{p}(\Omega)
(when
s=0
) and the classical
Sobolev space
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
(when
s=1
). Fractional Sobolev
spaces are naturally connected with the fractional Laplacian, which
is the nonlocal counterpart of the classical Laplace operator
\Delta
.
The fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
is defined as the Fourier
multiplier

\widehat{(-\Delta)^{s}u}(\xi)=|\xi|^{2s}\widehat{u}(\xi).

In particular, weak solutions of nonlocal equations driven by
(-\Delta)^{s}

arise naturally as critical points of energies defined on
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

making fractional Sobolev spaces the canonical functional setting
for the analysis of such problems.

In our setting, the (periodic) fractional Sobolev space is

W^{s,p}(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\{\gamma\in L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R})\mid[\gamma]_{W^{s,p}}<+\infty\}

where

[\gamma]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\iint_{(-\pi,\pi)\times(-\pi,\pi)}\frac{|\gamma(e^{\xi i})-\gamma(e^{\sigma i})|^{p}}{|e^{\xi i}-e^{\sigma i}|^{1+sp}}d\xi d\sigma

More generally, if

u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{u_{k}}e^{\theta i}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{S}^{1})

(periodic distribution) then

(-\Delta)^{s}u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{2s}\widehat{u_{k}}e^{\theta i}

with the convention
(-\Delta)^{0}u=u
. In particular

\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{2s}|\widehat{u_{k}}|^{2}.

The reader may notice that the kernel

\varrho_{s}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2^{1+sp}}\frac{1}{|\sin(\theta)|^{1+sp}}

\frac{1}{|x|^{1+sp}}

Proposition 5

The quantities
[u]_{W^{s,2}}

2.2
Symmetric decreasing rearrangement

Symmetrization is a classical geometric transformation; we refer to
the monographs
[
14
,
15
]
for a
systematic presentation and further references. Roughly speaking,
a symmetrization replaces a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

by a new set
E^{\sharp}
that is more symmetric but has the same
Lebesgue measure as
E
. The prototypical example is
\emph{Schwarzsymmetrization}

(or spherical symmetrization), which associates to
E
the open ball

E^{\ast}
centered at the origin with
|E^{\ast}|=|E|
; in this
process volume is preserved, while geometric quantities such as perimeter
or Dirichlet-type energies typically improve (e.g., decrease), as
quantified by the classical Pólya-Szegő inequality
[
21
]
.
A more anisotropic procedure is
\emph{Steinersymmetrization}
in
a fixed direction: here one replaces each one-dimensional slice of

E
along that direction by a centered interval of the same length,
preserving both the length of each slice and the total volume of the
set, while making
E
symmetric with respect to a hyperplane; see
again
[
14
,
15
]
. On the sphere

\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, one considers
\emph{sphericalcapsymmetrization}
,
which maps a set
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
to a spherical cap centered
at a given pole with the same surface measure
|A|
, again preserving
measure but increasing symmetry (see
[
2
,
10
]
).
Finally,
\emph{polarization}
(or two-point symmetrization) with
respect to a hyperplane replaces
E
by a set obtained by comparing
points and their reflections and keeping them on the side that makes
the set “more balanced”; this operation preserves volume and is often
used as a building block to approximate more regular symmetrizations.
In all cases, the key point is that symmetrizations conserve measure
while enforcing additional symmetry, and they tend to improve isoperimetric
or energy-type quantities associated with the domain
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

The symmetrization procedures described above for sets can be naturally
extended to functions by acting on their level sets; see, for instance,

[
14
,
1
,
15
]
.
Given a nonnegative measurable function
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
one applies a symmetrization to each superlevel set
\{u>t\}
and
then reconstructs a new function
u^{\sharp}
by requiring that
\{u^{\sharp}>t\}

is the symmetrized version of
\{u>t\}
for every
t>0
. In this
way, Schwarz symmetrization of domains leads to the so called symmetric
decreasing rearrangement
u^{\ast}
, Steiner symmetrization induces
a hyperplane-symmetric rearrangement along a fixed direction, spherical
cap symmetrization produces rotationally symmetric functions on the
sphere, and polarization becomes a two-point rearrangement of function
values across a hyperplane. By construction, these function symmetrizations
preserve the distribution function of
u
and hence all its
L^{p}

norms, while often decreasing associated energies such as Dirichlet
or fractional Sobolev seminorms. Thus, symmetrization of domains extends
seamlessly to a powerful rearrangement theory for functions, which
will be a key tool in our variational arguments. In our setting, we
will only use Schwarz symmetrization and the associated symmetric
decreasing rearrangement. For the sake of convenience, we recall the
definition of symmetric decreasing rearrangements and some of their
properties using the same flow as in
[
17
]
.

Definition 6
.

The symmetric decreasing rearrangement of a univariate real valued
function
f
, denoted by
f^{*}
, is defined by

f^{*}(x)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}1_{(-\xi_{t},\xi_{t})}(x)dt

(2.2)

where the set
(-\xi_{t},\xi_{t})
is the Schwarz symmetrization
of the superlevel set
f^{-1}(t,\infty)
. Formula (
2.2
)
shows that the function
f^{*}
is even, i.e
f^{*}(x)=f^{*}(|x|)
.
In particular

f^{*}(|x|)=\sup\{s\mid|x|<\xi_{s}={\textstyle\frac{|f^{-1}(s,+\infty)|}{2}}\}.

(2.3)

Note that in case
f
is bounded below, we can still consider its
symmetric decreasing rearrangement as

f^{*}=g^{*}+\inf(0,\inf f)

with
g=f-\inf(0,\inf f)
. A paramount property shared between
f

and
f^{*}
is equimeasurability. In other words, the level sets

\{x\mid f(x)>t\}
and
\{x\mid f^{*}(x)>t\}
have the same Lebesgue
measure. In particular

\|f\|_{p}=\|f^{*}\|_{p}

From a probabilistic perspective, the equimeasurability property
reads as : A random variable
\vartheta
and its symmetric decreasing
rearrangement
\vartheta^{*}
share the same probability law. In
particular, if
\vartheta,\varrho:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are two
nondecreasing functions with the same distribution then
\vartheta=\varrho

a.e.
[
17
]
. We state the most celebrated result
of symmetrization theory,
P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o}
inequality.

Theorem 7
.

[
15
]
If
f
is in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)

then
f^{*}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
and we have

\|\nabla f^{*}\|_{p}\leq\|\nabla f\|_{p}.

P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o}
inequality states that symmetric decreasing rearrangement
does not increase the average of the magnitude of the
L^{p}
-norm
of
\nabla f
. In particular, the kinetic energy of a function does
not increase under symmetrization. Fortunately,
P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o}

inequality still holds within the frame of fractional Sobolev spaces.
That is

Theorem 8

(Fractional
P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o}
inequality)
.

If
f\in W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
then
f^{*}\in W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
.
Furthermore

[f^{*}]_{W^{s,p}}\leq[f]_{W^{s,p}}.

When it comes to periodic distributions, Fractional
P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o}

inequality extends naturally. That is, if

f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{u_{k}}e^{\theta i},\,f^{*}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{u_{k}^{*}}e^{\theta i}

then

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{2s}|\widehat{u_{k}^{*}}|^{2}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{2s}|\widehat{u_{k}}|^{2}

for
s\in(0,1)
.

then for almost every
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)

\psi^{*}(\theta)=Q(1-{\textstyle\frac{|\theta|}{\pi}}).

(2.6)

The quantile function was the clue for Gross to craft his
\mu
-domain.
First, he expressed
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)\longmapsto Q({\textstyle\frac{|\theta|}{\pi}})

as a Fourier series

Q({\textstyle\frac{|\theta|}{\pi}})=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}a_{n}\cos(n\theta).

(2.7)

Then he showed that analytic function

\Phi(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}a_{n}z^{n}

(2.8)

is univalent in the unit disc. The
\mu
-domain of Gross, which
we denote it by
U_{G}
, is the range of the map
\mathbb{D}
. Of
course, showing that this domain indeed solves the problem requires
some probabilistic tools; however, we have chosen not to include these
arguments so as not to overburden the manuscript with details that
are not central to the main results. Now, we prove our main result.

2.4
Proof of theorem
1

Let
f
be a univalent function mapping the unit disc onto
U
and
fixing the origin. In particular
f
has the form

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}z^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(\alpha_{n}+\beta_{n}i)z^{n}

with
\alpha_{n}=\Re(c_{n}),\beta_{n}=\Im(c_{n})
. Denote by
\varphi(\theta)

the real part of
f(e^{\theta i}).
That is

\varphi(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(\alpha_{n}\cos(n\theta)+\beta_{n}\sin(n\theta)).

By virtue of the conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion, if

\theta
is viewed as a random variable uniformly distributed in

(-\pi,\pi)
then
\varphi(\theta)
samples as
\mu
. The symmetric
decreasing rearrangement of
\varphi
is
Q(1-{\textstyle\frac{|\cdot|}{\pi}})
.
The fractional
P\'{o}lya-Szeg\H{o}
inequality implies that

[Q(1-{\textstyle\frac{|\cdot|}{\pi}})]_{W^{s,2}}\leq[\varphi]_{W^{s,2}}.

As the Fourier coefficients of
Q(1-{\textstyle\frac{|\cdot|}{\pi}})

are the same as those of
Q({\textstyle\frac{|\cdot|}{\pi}})

up to a
(-1)^{n}
factor, we get

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}n^{2s}a_{n}^{2}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}n^{2s}(\alpha_{n}^{2}+\beta_{n}^{2})=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}n^{2s}|c_{n}|^{2}.

Multiplying both sides by
\pi
and taking
s=\frac{1}{2}
gives

\mathcal{A}(U_{G})\leq\mathcal{A}(U)

which ends the proof.

While trying to prove theorem
1
, we initially attempted
to show that the inequality (
1.2
) persists on inner
circles
\{|z|=r\}
, i.e.

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}n^{2}r^{2n}|a_{n}|^{2}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}n^{2}r^{2n}|c_{n}|^{2}

(2.9)

for
r\in(0,1)
. In fact, if (
2.9
) holds, then it
would imply our theorem (
1
). Such a guess was wrong
and here is a counter example. Consider the function

\chi_{N}(\theta)=\cos(N\theta),\,\,\,\theta\in(-\pi,\pi).

The symmetric decreasing rearrangement of
\chi_{N}
is
\chi(\theta)=\cos(\theta)
.
The power series generated from
\chi_{N}
and
\chi
are

\widetilde{\chi}_{N}(z=re^{\theta i})=z^{n},\,\,\text{and}\,\,\widetilde{\chi}(z=re^{\theta i})=z.

Now, fix
0<r<1
. We have

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|{\textstyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\chi}_{N}}{\partial\theta}}|^{2}d\theta=\pi n^{2}r^{2n}\,\,\,\text{and }\,\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}|{\textstyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\chi}}{\partial\theta}}|^{2}d\theta=\pi r^{2}.

If the claim
2.9
was true, then

1\leq n^{2}r^{2n-2}

which is wrong by just taking
r=\frac{1}{2}
and
n=3
for example.

2.5
Proof of Proposition
2

First, note that

\mathcal{A}(U_{\mu}^{G})\geq\pi

with equality if and only if all the coefficients
a_{2},a_{3},...

of the Fourier series of
Q(\frac{|\cdot|}{\pi})
are zero.
Hence,

Q({\textstyle\frac{|\theta|}{\pi}})=-\cos(\theta).

An elementary calculation of the c.d.f. of
Q
shows that the underlying
measure has the density

\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{(-1,1)}

which completes the proof.

Remark 10
.

Proposition
2
can also be interpreted as follows:
Among all the solutions of the PSEP generated by Schlicht probability
measures, the unit disc has exclusively the least area.

3
Brownian symmetrization and Steiner symmetrization

In an expository note
[
6
]
, the authors suggested
to consider the following geometric transformation, which they labeled
Brownian symmetrization : Let
U
be a bounded domain and run a standard
planar Brownian motion
Z_{t}
inside
U
, and let
\mu_{U}
be
the distribution of
\Re(Z_{\tau_{U}})
. The Brownian symmetrization
of
U
is defined by

\mathfrak{B}(U)=U_{\mu_{U}}^{G}.

In other words, the Brownian symmetrization of
U
is the Gross solution
generated by the law of
\Re(Z_{\tau_{U}})
.

Figure 3.1:
Illustration of the Brownian symmetrization process:

1. Start by a (bounded) domain
U
(on the left).

2. Run a Brownian motion
(Z_{t})_{t}
inside
U
and record the
distribution of the real part of
Z_{\tau_{U}}
.

3. Generate
\mathfrak{B}(U)
(on the right) by applying Gross’ method
to
\mu
.

If we denote by
\mathscr{B}
the set of bounded simply connected
domains (containing the origin), the Brownian symmetrization is

\mathfrak{B}:\begin{aligned} \mathscr{B}&\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}\\
U&\longmapsto U_{\mu_{U}}^{G}\end{aligned}

where
\mu
is the law of
\Re(Z_{\tau_{U}})
and
U_{\mu}
is
the domain obtained by Gross’ technique. If
\mathfrak{S}
denotes
the Steiner symmetrization with respect to the real axis, then the
geometry of
U_{\mu}^{G}
(discussed in
[
8
]
)
implies that

\mathfrak{S}\circ\mathfrak{B}=\mathfrak{B}\circ\mathfrak{S}

where
\circ
is the composition symbol. In theorem
3
of
[
6
]
,
the authors showed that Brownian symmetrization and Steiner symmetrization
are two different transformations. They conjectured that the Brownian
symmetrization does not increase the area and hence Brownian symmetrization
does a better job regarding minimizing the area. By virtue of theorem
(
1
), their conjecture turns out to be true. More precisely,
we have

\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))\leq\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{S}(U))=\mathcal{A}(U).

(3.1)

The inequality
3.1
leads to compare the two images through
the dimensionless ratio

\rho(U)=\frac{\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))}{\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{S}(U))}=\frac{\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))}{\mathcal{A}(U)}

This ratio provides a normalized measure of the area distortion produced
by the Brownian symmetrization relative to Steiner symmetrization.
The coefficient
\rho\leq 1
, and equality holds for any bounded domain
symmetric with respect to the real axis and
\Delta
-convex.

Lemma 11
.

If

f=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}z^{n}

is a univalent function mapping the unit disc onto a domain
U
then

\mathbf{E}(\tau_{U})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}|c_{n}|^{2}=\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U}).

(3.2)

Proof.

It follows from combining the conformal invariance principle and the
optional stopping theorem to the martingale
\left(M_{t}=|Z_{t}|^{2}-2t\right)_{t}

at time
t=\tau_{U}
.
∎

The identity
3.2
implies that all
\mu
-domains

U
have a constant
\mathbf{E}(\tau_{U})
. This leads to

Proposition 12
.

The following facts hold.

1.

\mathcal{A}(U)>0\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))>0.

2.

It may happen that
\mathcal{A}(U)=+\infty\,\,\text{and}\,\,\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))<+\infty
.

Proof.

To show the first assertion, it is enough to show the direct implication.
If
\mathcal{A}(U)>0
then
\mathbf{E}(\tau_{U})=\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U})>0
.
In particular,
\mu
is not a point-mass, and hence the underlying
Gross power series
2.8
is nonconstant and then

\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))>0
.

For the second point, let
\mu
be the uniform distribution in
(-1,1)

and let
U
be the
\mu
-domain generated by the method of Boudabra-Markowsky

[
7
]
. In particular
U
has the Cartesian equation

y>-\frac{2}{\pi}\ln(2\cos(\frac{\pi x}{2})).

Hence
U
has an infinite area. The Brownian symmetrization of
U

is the range of the map

f(z)=-\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(2n-1)^{2}}z^{2n-1}

as computed in
[
13
]
. An elementary check shows that
\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))<+\infty
.
More precisely

\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B}(U))=\frac{56}{\pi^{3}}\zeta(3)\approx 2.171185

with
\zeta
the well known Riemann Zeta function.
∎

As

2\pi\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U})\leq\mathcal{A}(U),

we have

\rho(U)\in\left[{\textstyle\frac{2\pi\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U})}{\mathcal{A}(U)}},1\right].

The lower bound
{\textstyle\frac{2\pi\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U})}{\mathcal{A}(U)}}

is attained for
U=\mathbb{D}
. If we allow
U
to have infinite
area, e.g.
U=\{-1<\Re(z)<1\}
, then obviously

\frac{\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U})}{\mathcal{A}(U)}=\frac{\frac{1}{4}}{+\infty}=0.

In particular, if
U
is allowed to be of an infinite area then

\inf_{U}\frac{\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U})}{\mathcal{A}(U)}=0.

(3.3)

It is natural to ask whether (
3.3
) remains valid
when we fix the area of the domains. The next theorem addresses this
question, and in fact establishes a stronger statement.

Theorem 13
.

Let
a>0
and let
\mathscr{B}_{a}

be the set of domains of area
a
. Then

\inf_{U\in\mathbf{B}_{a}}\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{U})=0.

(3.4)

Proof.

Heuristically, the extremal behavior in
3.4
should
occur when
U
is arranged so that
0
almost touches the boundary:
a planar Brownian motion started at
0
then “escapes” to
\partial U
as
fast as possible. To upgrade this picture from intuition to proof,
we introduce a concrete family of domains on which this effect can
be computed explicitly.

First, remark that the scaling property of the area enables us to
give a particular value to
a
, say
a=\pi
. Let
\kappa\in[0,1)

and consider the family of domains

U_{\kappa}=\mathbb{D}-\kappa i=\{\{|z-\kappa i|<1\}\}

and run a planar Brownian motion
Z_{t}
from the origin. The underlying
probability measure
\mu
of
\Re(Z_{\tau_{U}})
is given by

d\mu(x)={\textstyle{\displaystyle\frac{(1-\kappa^{4})dx}{\pi\left((1-\kappa^{2})^{2}+4\kappa^{2}x^{2}\right)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}}}\mathbf{1}_{(-1,1)}.

Clearly
\mu
is centered. A tedious elementary calculation shows
that

\mathbf{Var}(\mu)=\int_{-1}^{1}x^{2}d\mu(x)=\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{2}.

So by letting
\kappa\rightarrow 1^{-}
,
\mathbf{Var}(\mu)
can
be as small as desired, which proves the theorem.
∎

Theorem
3.4
implies

\rho(\mathscr{B}_{a})\subseteq(0,1].

The converse inclusion is natural to investigate. In other words,
is the functional

\rho_{\mid\mathscr{B}_{a}}:\begin{aligned} \mathscr{B}_{a}&\longrightarrow(0,1]\\
U&\longmapsto\rho(U)\end{aligned}

onto? We strongly believe that

Conjecture
: The functional
\rho_{\mid\mathscr{B}_{a}}

is onto.

One may be tempted, as we initially were when investigating the above
conjecture, to believe that if a sequence of probability measures

(\mu_{r})
collapses to a point mass, then the corresponding Gross
domains should also contract to a point. This intuition is, however,
false, as the following example shows. Consider the thin vertical
rectangle

R_{b}\;=\;\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ -\frac{a}{2b}<\Re z<\frac{a}{2b},\;-\frac{b}{2}<\Im z<\frac{b}{2}\right\},\qquad b>0.

By the uniqueness criterion established in
[
8
]
,
the Brownian symmetrization (i.e. the Gross domain) of
R_{b}
is
again
R_{b}
. The area of
R_{b}
is
\mathcal{A}(R_{b})=a
, hence

\rho(R_{b})=1
independently of the parameter
b
. On the other
hand, if we denote by
\mu_{b}
the law of the real part of Brownian
motion started at the origin and stopped upon exiting
R_{b}
, then

\mathbf{Var}(\mu_{b})\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }b\to+\infty.

Thus the underlying one-dimensional distributions
\mu_{b}
collapse
to a point mass in
L^{2}
, while the associated Gross domains do
not shrink. This phenomenon highlights the sharp distinction between
the
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
-control underlying the Gross
construction and the much weaker
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
-behavior
of the corresponding boundary data.
