Hypertriglyceridemia‑Induced Acute Pancreatitis Compared With Non‐Hypertriglyceridemia Acute Pancreatitis: A Retrospective Case‐Control Study
Authors: Zahra Farhangiyan, Leila Moradi, Mehrdad Vaziri, Elena Lak, Ferdos Zaman, Shooka Mohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHypertriglyceridemia‐induced acute pancreatitis (HTGAP) is a distinct clinical subtype of acute pancreatitis (AP), but comparative data remain limited. This study aimed to compare risk factors, disease severity, and clinical outcomes between patients with HTGAP and non‐HTGAP.MethodsA single‐center retrospective case‐control study was conducted using medical records of patients diagnosed with AP who were admitted to Imam Hospital (Ahvaz, Iran) between May 2020 and May 2025. Patients were categorized into HTGAP (n = 150) and non‐HTGAP (n = 150) groups. Demographic data, severity scores, clinical complications, and length of hospital stay were compared between the two groups.ResultsHTGAP was associated with significantly higher rates of complications, including acute respiratory distress syndrome, acute peripancreatic fluid collection, sepsis, acute necrotizing pancreatitis, pancreatic pseudocyst, walled‐off or infected pancreatic necrosis, and renal failure (p < 0.05). HTGAP patients were younger and had higher body mass index than non‐HTGAP patients (p < 0.001). They also had significantly higher mean Ranson (3.18 vs. 2.53), 24‐h Acute Physiology, Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) (9.78 vs. 6.88), and modified computed tomography severity index (MCTSI) scores (7.08 vs. 4.54), as well as longer hospital stays (9.44 vs. 5.00 days), compared with non‐HTGAP patients (p < 0.001). No in‐hospital mortality occurred.ConclusionHTGAP was associated with a more severe clinical profile and higher complication rates than non‐HTGAP, highlighting the need for early recognition and careful management. Interpretation of these findings is limited by the retrospective single‐center design and absence of multivariable adjustment.Keywords: acute pancreatitis, APACHE II, HTGAP, hypertriglyceridemia, MCTSI, non‐HTGAP, Ranson score

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is characterized by inflammation of the pancreas and is one of the most common pancreatic disorders worldwide [1, 2]. It presents a wide clinical spectrum, ranging from mild, self‐limiting inflammation to severe disease associated with organ dysfunction and significant morbidity [3, 4]. The incidence of AP has increased globally, with approximately 20% of patients developing local or systemic complications [5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

Hypertriglyceridemia‐induced acute pancreatitis (HTGAP) occurs when triglyceride (TG) levels exceed 1000 mg/dL, contributing to pancreatic inflammation [10, 11, 12]. Although HTGAP accounts for a relatively small proportion of AP cases, it is recognized as the third most common cause after gallstones and alcohol consumption [13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Several studies have reported that patients with HTGAP more frequently exhibit metabolic risk factors, such as obesity, diabetes, and pronounced inflammatory responses, than those with other etiologies of AP [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. These metabolic and pathophysiological characteristics may contribute to the increased severity observed in HTGAP, including higher complication rates and longer hospital stays [23, 24, 25, 26]. Reducing TG levels decreases the recurrence rate of pancreatitis [26, 27].

HTGAP and non‐HTGAP often present with similar clinical symptoms; however, accurate identification of the underlying etiology is crucial for precise risk assessment, timely intervention, and optimal therapeutic approach [28, 29]. Recent lifestyle and metabolic trends, including obesity and dyslipidemia, have contributed to the increasing incidence of HTGAP in several regions [30, 31]. Despite these trends, few comparative studies have evaluated HTGAP vs. non‐HTGAP [23, 32, 33], and fewer have simultaneously compared multiple validated severity scoring systems in patients with HTGAP and non‐HTGAP. To our knowledge, none have been conducted in Iran. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the clinical characteristics, severity indicators, and complications of HTGAP and non‐HTGAP in hospitalized patients to enhance insight into these diseases and support more effective clinical management. It was hypothesized that HTGAP would be associated with greater disease severity and higher complication rates compared with non‐HTGAP.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This single‐center retrospective case‐control study compared the medical records of patients with HTGAP and those with non‐HTGAP who were admitted to Imam Hospital (Ahvaz, Iran) between May 2020 and May 2025. Cases (HTGAP) and controls (non‐HTGAP) were identified retrospectively from hospital records. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (NO: IR.AJUMS.HGOLESTAN.REC.1402.218). Written consent was not required because this study used de‐identified patient data and employed a retrospective design.

Diagnosis Criteria

The diagnosis of AP followed the 2012 Revised Atlanta Classification (RAC). AP was diagnosed when at least two of the following criteria were met: acute upper abdominal pain consistent with pancreatitis, serum amylase or lipase levels more than 3 times the upper limit of normal, or characteristic imaging findings on computed tomography (CT), ultrasound, or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [6]. Hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) was defined as a fasting serum TG level of ≥ 1000 mg/dL [6, 10, 11, 17]. Serum triglyceride levels were measured at admission before significant fasting or aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation to minimize potential dilutional effects.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible patients were aged 18–85 years, had a confirmed diagnosis of AP, and were hospitalized within 48 h of symptom onset. Patients were excluded if they were under 18 years of age, pregnant, had advanced chronic organ failure (e.g., end‐stage renal disease requiring dialysis, decompensated cirrhosis, or advanced malignancy), lacked essential RAC‐related diagnostic information, or had missing clinical or laboratory data.

Sampling

A total of 450 medical records of patients with AP were reviewed, of which 150 were excluded due to incomplete or inaccurate documentation or failure to meet the eligibility criteria. All eligible HTGAP patients during the study period were included. Non‐HTGAP controls were included consecutively without individual matching to achieve equal group sizes for balanced comparison. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was a retrospective exploratory study. The sample size was based on the number of eligible medical records available during the study period.

Classification of AP

Patients were classified into HTGAP and non‐HTGAP groups based on the etiology of AP. Non‐HTGAP cases included gallstone‐related AP (evidence of cholelithiasis or bile duct obstruction), alcohol‐related AP (significant alcohol consumption meeting the guideline criteria), drug‐induced AP (medication identified as the likely trigger), and idiopathic AP (no identifiable cause despite a standard evaluation) in accordance with the current diagnostic guidelines.

Data Collection

Clinical and demographic data were collected using structured checklists, including presenting symptoms, AP classification, length of stay (LOS) in hospital, severity scores (Ranson, 24‐h acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score, and modified computed tomography severity index (MCTSI) [34], underlying comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension (HTN), and cardiovascular disease (CVD)), and complications such as infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN), acute peripancreatic fluid collection (APFC), walled‐off pancreatic necrosis (WOPN), acute necrotizing pancreatitis (ANP), pancreatic pseudocyst (PPC), organ failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), sepsis, and renal failure.

Severity Scoring Systems

The Ranson score was used to assess the severity of AP using clinical and laboratory parameters measured at admission and within the first 48 h of disease onset [35]. The Ranson score ranged from 0 to 11. A score of ≥ 3 was considered indicative of severe AP.

The APACHE II score was calculated using 12 routinely assessed acute physiological variables, including vital signs (temperature, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and Glasgow Coma Scale), laboratory values (white blood cell count, hematocrit, serum sodium, potassium, and creatinine), and measures of oxygenation and acid–base status (arterial pH and either the partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2) or the alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient, depending on the fraction of inspired oxygen). Additional points were assigned for age and chronic health conditions [36]. Scores ranged from 0 to 71, with higher scores indicating greater disease severity and increased mortality risk. The 24‐h APACHE II score was used because it reflects disease severity after initial resuscitation and has better discriminative power for complications than the admission score.

The MCTSI score was calculated using contrast‐enhanced CT findings, incorporating the degree of pancreatic inflammation, the extent of pancreatic necrosis, and the presence of extra‐pancreatic complications [34]. The scores ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe pancreatic involvement and complications.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data normality, and normality assumptions were met for parametric tests. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent‐samples t‐tests. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the Chi‐square test. Missing data were handled using case‐wise (complete‐case) exclusion, and no data imputation was performed. Analyses were limited to unadjusted group comparisons because of the retrospective exploratory design of the study. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 300 medical records of patients with AP were reviewed, comprising 150 cases of HTGAP and 150 cases of non‐HTGAP. The mean age of the patients was 45.17 ± 9.03 years, and 176 (58.7%) were men. All demographic and clinical baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Patients with HTGAP were younger than those with non‐HTGAP (40.09 ± 7.12 vs. 50.25 ± 7.82 years, p < 0.001) and had higher body mass index (BMI) (28.42 ± 2.15 vs. 24.30 ± 1.91 kg/m2, p < 0.001). Differences in age distribution were observed between the groups. More than half of the patients with HTGAP (52.7%) were 30–40 years old, while this age range accounted for only 7.3% of the non‐HTGAP group. HTN was the most prevalent comorbidity (49%) among the patients, followed by CVD (41.7%) and diabetes (40.3%). All major metabolic risk factors, including HTN, diabetes, CVD, smoking, overweight, and obesity, were more frequent in patients with HTGAP than in those with non‐HTGAP (p < 0.05). The non‐HTGAP group consisted of gallstone‐related AP (49%), alcohol‐related AP (27%), drug‐induced AP (10%), and idiopathic AP (14%). Importantly, no in‐hospital mortality occurred in either group.

Discussion

This case‐control study analyzed 300 medical records and compared the clinical characteristics, severity indices, and complication patterns of patients with HTGAP to those with non‐HTGAP. The results indicate that HTGAP was associated with greater severity, younger age at onset, higher BMI, and a significantly higher prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, including HTN, diabetes, CVD, smoking, overweight, and obesity. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that HTGAP occurs more frequently in younger patients with significant metabolic abnormalities, particularly in regions where obesity and dyslipidemia are increasingly prevalent [23, 32, 33, 37, 38, 39].

This study revealed that HTGAP was associated with a substantially higher frequency of both systemic and local complications than non‐HTGAP cases. The rates of ARDS, APFC, sepsis, septic shock, ANP, WOPN, PPC, IPN, and renal failure were significantly higher in the HTGAP group than in the non‐HTGAP group. These results align with previous studies reporting more severe clinical courses in HTGAP [23, 32, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44]. These complications likely reflect not only more pronounced local inflammation but also an intensified systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), which may explain the disproportionately higher rates of organ dysfunction in this patient group. Patients with HTGAP in this study exhibited higher unadjusted severity scores compared with non‐HTGAP, consistent with prior studies [23, 32, 45, 46]. Higher MCTSI values reflect greater pancreatic necrosis and peripancreatic inflammation, characteristic of HTG‐induced injuries, as previously reported [46, 47, 48, 49]. Importantly, the substantial differences in baseline characteristics between the groups, including younger age, higher BMI, and greater prevalence of diabetes and HTN in the HTGAP group, represent potential confounders. These factors are independently associated with worse outcomes in AP and may partly explain the higher severity observed in HTGAP. In the absence of multivariable adjustment, the findings should be interpreted as unadjusted associations, as the observed differences may reflect confounding rather than a direct causal effect of HTG. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of an independent causal effect of HTG itself.

The more severe clinical profile observed in HTGAP may be related to several proposed pathophysiological mechanisms. Severe hypertriglyceridemia leads to the accumulation of triglyceride‐rich chylomicrons in the pancreatic microcirculation, where hydrolysis by pancreatic lipase generates excessive free fatty acids (FFAs) [13, 14, 21, 32, 50, 51]. At high concentrations, FFAs may induce acinar and endothelial cell injury, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, while chylomicron‐associated hyperviscosity may impair pancreatic microcirculation and contribute to ischemic injury [52, 53, 54]. The combined effects of oxidative injury, impaired microcirculation, and lipotoxicity may contribute to the increased rates of ANP, WOPN, ARDS, and renal failure observed in HTGAP [30, 52].

In this study, no in‐hospital mortality was recorded despite the relatively high proportion of severe cases in the HTGAP group. Several factors may explain these results. Patients with HTGAP were significantly younger, likely providing a greater physiological reserve. All patients presented within 48 h of symptom onset, which enabled timely supportive management. Although severity scores reflect a substantial disease burden, early recognition and standardized treatment may have prevented fatal deterioration. However, the lack of observed mortality should be interpreted with caution, given the single‐center setting and absence of long‐term follow‐up.

Clinical Implications

The outcomes of this study underscore the potential clinical importance of early recognition of HTGAP as a higher‐risk presentation of AP. Prompt reduction of serum TG levels is essential to preventing disease progression. Interventions such as intravenous insulin infusion and therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) have been proposed as potential strategies for early TG reduction, although evidence regarding routine use remains limited [47, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59]. Beyond the acute phase, long‐term lipid control and management of associated metabolic conditions may also be beneficial. These findings may support consideration of closer monitoring and earlier targeted management strategies in patients with HTGAP to potentially reduce complications and length of hospitalization.

The results of this study have significant implications for regions with rising metabolic risk profiles, including the Middle East and other developing countries. Iran, like many middle‐income nations, is experiencing increasing rates of obesity, sedentary behavior, dyslipidemia, and other lifestyle‐related diseases. The relatively high number of young patients with HTGAP in this study may reflect shifting epidemiological trends in the country. Enhancing public health awareness, improving dyslipidemia screening, and expanding lifestyle‐focused prevention programs are essential steps to curb the growing burden of severe HTG.

This study provided a comprehensive analysis of HTGAP in Iran, a Middle Eastern country, and offered valuable regional insights. It included an appropriate sample size and applied strict diagnostic and inclusion criteria. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Its retrospective single‐center design limited causal inference and generalizability. The study design introduced potential selection bias, and reliance on medical records may have resulted in information bias. In addition, multivariable regression analysis was not performed due to limitations inherent to the retrospective dataset and variable distribution. Therefore, baseline differences (e.g., age, BMI, diabetes, and HTN) may have influenced the observed associations, and the findings should be interpreted as unadjusted associations. Residual unmeasured confounding and inability to establish temporal causality cannot be excluded. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were not reported, which may have limited the interpretation of the magnitude and precision of the observed differences. Furthermore, the study population represented a single geographic and ethnic group, which may have limited external validity. Some biochemical indicators, including serial TG levels and inflammatory markers, were not consistently available. Additionally, misclassification of etiology could not be entirely excluded. Moreover, the lack of post‐discharge follow‐up prevented assessment of recurrence, long‐term complications, and mortality beyond hospitalization.

Conclusion

HTGAP was associated with a more severe clinical profile and higher complication rates than non‐HTGAP; however, these results should be interpreted with caution due to baseline differences. These findings suggest the importance of early etiological diagnosis, prompt TG reduction, close hemodynamic monitoring, and long‐term lipid management to potentially mitigate severe complications and improve patient outcomes. Tailored management strategies may help reduce the clinical burden of HTGAP. Future multicenter prospective studies with follow‐up and detailed metabolic profiling are needed to clarify the natural history of HTGAP and guide optimal management strategies for this condition.
