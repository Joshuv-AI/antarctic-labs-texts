Meat quality and muscle fiber comparisons between rapid- and slow-growth broilers
Authors: Choawit Rakangthong, Chaiyapoom Bunchasak, Surasek Tunseng, Savek Kiatsomphob, Phongthorn Kongmun, K. Teepalak Rangubhet, Autchara Kayan, Panatda Bungsrisawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Consumers are concerned about differences in meat quality between rapid-growth (RG) and slow-growth (SG) broilers available commercially. Comparative studies are required to underpin consumer confidence. This study aimed to compare meat quality and muscle fiber traits between RG and SG genotypes and to evaluate the effects of sex and genotype using a 2 × 2 factorial experimental design. A total of 416 birds were used: 13 per replicate and 8 replicates, for a total of 104 per experimental group. RG broilers had significantly greater breast muscle weight, lighter meat color, and higher muscle fiber density, whereas SG broilers exhibited darker meat and thicker connective tissue. RG broilers also showed higher drip loss, indicating reduced water-holding capacity. Correlation analyses revealed distinct relationships between muscle fiber traits and meat quality parameters for the two genotypes: in RG broilers, breast muscle weight was closely associated with pH, drip loss, and color traits, implying a dominant role of metabolic factors in determining meat quality of fast-growing birds; in contrast, SG broilers exhibited stronger associations between muscle structural traits and connective tissue characteristics, suggesting a greater structural contribution to meat quality variation. These findings highlight trade-offs between growth rate and meat quality and suggest that breeding strategies should balance production efficiency with consumer-preferred meat attributes to optimize broiler performance and product value.

Introduction

Thailand is currently the world’s fourth-largest exporter of broiler chicken products and ranks sixth globally in broiler production capacity (USDA, 2024). Therefore, changes in broiler genotypes used in commercial production in Thailand directly affect production costs and competitiveness in the global export market (Klaharn et al., 2024). Broiler production has advanced rapidly through genetic selection and improved management, resulting in modern rapid-growth (RG) genotypes capable of achieving market weight in shorter production cycles (Torrey et al., 2021). This progress has helped meet the global demand for affordable animal protein and improve food security (Abbas et al., 2025).

However, intensive selection of RG broilers for feed efficiency and breast muscle yield is associated with compromised welfare and the development of meat quality defects such as white striping and wooden breast (Huang and Ahn, 2018; Riber and Wurtz, 2024). In response, slow-growth (SG) broiler genotypes have been introduced into alternative and premium production systems, where animal welfare, product differentiation, and sensory quality are prioritized (Weimer et al., 2022). Although these genotypes exhibit different growth patterns, their adoption remains limited by lower production efficiency and higher resource requirements (Rayner et al., 2020; Nicol et al., 2024).

From a production perspective, RG and SG broilers differ substantially in efficiency and sustainability metrics. RG broilers generally grow faster, have superior feed conversion efficiency, and produce lower carbon emissions than SG broilers. In contrast, SG broilers require longer rearing periods and greater feed input, despite differences in carcass composition and their potential value in niche markets (Bungsrisawat et al., 2025; de Jong et al., 2021). These findings indicate a trade-off between production efficiency and evolving consumer demands.

Nevertheless, a comprehensive evaluation of the impacts of these two broiler genotypes is still required. Bungsrisawat et al. (2025) previously reported comparisons between RG and SG broilers regarding growth performance, carcass characteristics, and economic and environmental aspects. However, information concerning meat quality, muscle fiber characteristics, and the relationships between muscle fiber traits and meat quality remains limited. Although previous studies have evaluated meat quality and muscle fiber characteristics in slow-growth chickens, most have focused on either meat quality traits or muscle characteristics separately, and their scope has often been limited to indigenous or specialty breeds (Huo et al., 2022; Selvi et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2021; Weimer et al., 2022; Weng et al., 2022).

Meat quality is influenced by muscle growth, fiber structure, connective tissue, and postmortem biochemical changes. In RG broilers, rapid muscle growth is associated with muscle fiber hypertrophy and metabolic alterations, which can reduce water-holding capacity and overall meat quality (Ismail and Joo, 2017; Lee and Mienaltowski, 2023). In contrast, SG broilers develop muscle under physiological conditions characterized by a more balanced fiber-to-capillary ratio, which may improve oxygenation and nutrient supply to the muscle tissue (Velleman and Clark, 2015). This condition may contribute to improved structural integrity and distinct textural properties (Soglia et al., 2016). Accordingly, evaluating meat quality traits, muscle fiber characteristics, and their relationships in RG and SG broilers would improve understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying differences in meat quality between these broiler genotypes.

This study aimed to integrate meat quality evaluation with detailed muscle fiber analysis to better understand structure–function relationships in broiler breast muscle. Rapid- and slow-growth broilers were compared at a common market weight to determine how differences in growth patterns influence muscle characteristics and key meat quality traits, including pH, color, water-holding capacity, and tenderness. The effect of sex was also evaluated. This approach provides insight into the trade-offs between growth efficiency and meat quality in modern broiler production systems.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and animal care

The protocol for ethical approval and animal care was in accordance the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kasetsart University, Bangkok, Thailand (approval number ACKU66-AGR-003). The animal care procedures were consistent with the guidelines for care and use of agricultural animals in research and teaching by the National Research Council (NRC, 2010).

Animal husbandry

The experiment utilized 416 day-old broiler chicks, consisting of Cobb 500 rapid-growth broilers (Cobb, 2022) and Hubbard redbro slow-growth broilers (Hubbard, 2023), divided equally between males and females in each breed. The 416 birds were assigned randomly to 4 experimental groups based on breed and sex (104 birds from each experimental group with 13 from 8 replications). The chickens were weighed to ensure uniformity across experimental units, resulting in a total of 32 pens. The final weight to market size was set at 2.5 kg. The broilers were raised in a 1.08 m² space per pen, following the standard guidelines of the Department of Livestock Development, Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives (DLD, 2002). The rearing period to reach 2.5 kg varied by breed and sex, with the male and female RG broilers required 33 and 37 days, respectively, while the male and female SG broilers reached the target weight in 40 and 48 days, respectively.

Management

All broilers were raised in a closed housing system with concrete flooring covered with a 5 cm thick layer of husk as bedding material. The housing was constructed to minimize heat radiation, with black curtains along the sides of the housing and an evaporative cooling system, along with a tunnel ventilation system, being used to regulate environmental conditions. The average temperature ± standard deviation was 27.71 ± 2.39 °C and the average relative humidity ± standard deviation was 85.2 ± 3.4% according to the study by Bungsrisawat et al. (2025). A thermostat-controlled system managed the activation of fans and water pumps within the housing. Throughout the experimental period, the temperature and relative humidity were monitored at three locations on the housing (front, middle, rear sections).

During the initial phase of the experiment (0–7 days of age), all the broilers were provided with continuous warmth daily from a brooder lightness-to-darkness ratio of 23:1 h. From day 8 until reaching a body weight of 2.5 kg, the lighting program used 50 W bulbs, with a lightness-to- darkness ratio of 20:4 h. The broilers were fed ad libitum and supplied with drinking water ad libitum via a nipple drinking system. All the broilers were vaccinated against Newcastle disease and infectious bronchitis.

Feed

The feed formulations for the RG and SG broilers consisted primarily of corn and soybean meals as the main ingredients. These formulations had been designed to meet the nutritional requirements of each breed, following Bungsrisawat et al. (2025). For the RG broilers, the feed formulations were divided into 3 phases: 1) starter phase (0–14 days of age) with 21% Crude protein (CP) and 2,975 Kcal/kg, Metabolizable energy(ME); 2) grower phase (15–28 days of age) with 19% CP and 3,025 Kcal/kg, ME; and 3) finisher phase (29–39 days of age) with 17% CP and 3,150 Kcal/kg, ME. For the SG broilers, the feed formulations were divided into 4 phases: 1) starter phase (0–14 days of age) with 22% CP and 2,950 Kcal/kg, ME; 2) grower (15–25 days of age) with 20% CP and 3,000 Kcal/kg, ME; 3) finisher I phase (26 –38 days of age) with 18% CP and 3,050 Kcal/kg, ME; and 4) finisher II phase (39–51 days of age) with 17% CP and 3,100 Kcal/kg, ME.

Meat quality

All broilers were slaughtered at a live weight of 2.5 kg on days 33, 37, 40, and 48 for the RG males, RG females, SG males, and SG females, respectively. After slaughter, 256 birds (64 birds from each experimental group, with 8 birds from each replication) were measured randomly for breast muscle weight (BMW) of the boneless breast plus the fillet and for meat quality traits, namely pH, meat colors, and drip loss (DL) from the left-hand side of the pectoralis major muscle. A random sampling site in the right-hand side of the pectoralis major muscle was used to measure the shear force value (SF) in the 96 birds from the previous slaughter (24 birds from each experimental group, with 3 birds from each replication).

The pH measurement was conducted at two time points: immediately after slaughter at 45 min postmortem (pH45) and 24 h postmortem (pH₂₄). The pH meter (Testo 205, Testo SE & Co., Germany) was calibrated before each measurement with standard buffer solutions of pH 4.0 and 7.0. The pH value was measured at three locations on the left-hand side of the pectoralis major muscle (upper, middle, lower), as described by Tekin et al. (2025) with slight modification.

The meat colors were measured at 45 min and 24 h postmortem using a colorimeter (CR-400 Chroma Meter; Konica Minolta; Japan). The color values were reported according to the Complete International Commission on Illumination system (CIE, 1978), using the parameters lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*). The L* value (lightness to darkness) represents the brightness of the meat, with values in the range 0–100. The a* value (redness to greenness) ranges from -a* to a*, where a higher positive a* value indicates more redness in the meat, and a negative a* value (-a*) indicates greenness. The b* value (yellowness to blueness) ranges from -b* to b*, where a higher b* value indicates more yellowness or fat accumulation in the meat, while a negative b* value (-b*) indicates blueness in the meat. Whereas the measurement of color at 45 min postmortem declared as L*45, a*45, b*45 and for 24 h postmortem as L*24, a*24, b*24, similar to the method of Yaranoğlu and Yaranoğlu (2025).

Drip loss was collected after measuring the pH and meat colors after 24 h. The left-hand side of the pectoralis major muscle was blotted dry and then weighed. The initial weight was recorded before each sample was wrapped tightly with gauze and suspended in a refrigerated storage unit at 4°C for 24 h. After the storage period, the samples were reweighed to determine the weight loss during storage. The percentage of DL was calculated using the formula from Northcutt et al. (1994):Driploss(%)=[(Initialmeatweight−Poststoragemeatweight)Initialmeatweight]×100

For shear force determination, initially, the right-hand side of the pectoralis major muscle was stored at −20 °C until ready for analysis. The meat was removed and thawed by placing it in a sealed plastic bag and then storing at 4 °C for 24 h. After this period, the meat was removed, patted dry, and the weight of the exuded water after thawing loss (TL) was recorded as a percentage. Then, the meat was placed in a heat-resistant plastic bag and cooked until the internal temperature reached 80 °C. After cooking, the meat was allowed to rest at room temperature for 30 minutes. Next, the meat was patted dry again and reweighed to determine the amount of water lost during cooking, which was recorded as the (CL) as a percentage.

The SF measurement followed the method of Saláková et al. (2009). First, the cooked right-hand side of the pectoralis major muscle was cut into 3 samples each 2 cm in width, 1 cm in length, and 1.5 cm in height. Then, these samples were placed in the texture analysis machine (Brookfield Ametek CTX; USA) fitted with a Warner-Bratzler shear blade. The machine was set to a cutting speed of 80 mm/min and a blade travel distance of 20 mm. The average SF value was recorded based on three samples. The SF value from the machine in kilogram force was transformed to newton units using the multiplication factor of 9.8067.

Muscle fiber

In total, 24 samples (6 samples from each experimental group from 6 individual replications) were collected for histopathological muscle fiber analysis. A 4 mm tissue sample was excised from the right-hand side of the Pectoralis major muscle and fixed immediately in 10% formalin. Then, the fixed tissue was wrapped in gauze and rinsed under running water for approximately 30 min to remove any residual formalin. Subsequently, dehydration was performed using a graded series of ethanol concentrations (70%, 80%, 95%, 100%) with a Vacuum Tissue Processor (Leica ASP6025; Germany) to remove water from the tissue. Following dehydration, the samples were embedded in paraffin using a thermo-embedding system (HistoStar Epredia; USA). Then, the paraffin blocks were trimmed and sectioned into 4–5 µm thick slices using a microtome (Microm International GmbH HM 335 E; USA), following the method of Wekin (1981). The tissue sections were processed using a tissue water bath (Leica HI1210; Germany) in 50% ethanol to ensure smooth flattening of the sections. Subsequently, the sections were transferred to a warmer slide (Leica HI1220; Germany) to adhere and dry the tissue on microscope slides, following the method of Humason (1979). The histological staining was performed using hematoxylin and eosin for microscopic examination (Wekin, 1981).

The muscle fiber was analyzed based on 5 parameters, namely fiber diameter, endomysium thickness, perimysium thickness, total number of muscle fibers, and muscle fiber density per area. The process involved using a microscope with a 100 × magnification, utilizing a 20 × objective lens and a 10 × ocular lens, and using the ImageJ software, following the method of Koomkrong et al. (2015), with slight modification. The mean of 500 fibers in 5 random fields for each muscle was measured to estimate the fiber diameter (µm) of individual muscle fibers. The thickness of the endomysium and perimysium were determined on each sample, based on the method of Rahaman et al. (2010), with some modifications. The total number of muscle fibers in each muscle was determined and the average from the samples measured was used to calculate fiber density (number/mm2). The mean cross-sectional area of each fiber was estimated from about 500 fibers, adapted from Huo et al. (2021)

Statistical analysis

The experiment used a 2 × 2 factorial in a completely randomized design with the factors being sex and strain. Analysis of variance was performed on the experimental data from the RG and SG broilers based on the BMW, meat quality parameters (pH, color, drip loss, shear force), and muscle fiber parameters (muscle fiber diameter, endomysium thickness, perimysium thickness, TNF, muscle fiber density per area. Differences between group means were compared using Duncan’s test. A probability value less than 0.05 was considered significant (P < 0.05), and less than 0.01 was considered highly significant (P < 0.01).

Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed on the experimental data from the RG and SG broilers based on the BMW, meat quality parameters (pH, color, drip loss, shear force), and muscle fiber parameters (muscle fiber diameter, endomysium thickness, perimysium thickness, TNF, muscle fiber density per area). The analysis was carried out using the SAS® OnDemand for Academics software (SAS Institute Inc., 2014).

Based on pH measurements, all groups exhibited values within the normal range for broiler meat (6.38–6.54 for pH₄₅ and 5.58–5.80 for pH₂₄; Bungsrisawat et al., 2018). Muscle pH is a key determinant of meat quality, influencing color, water-holding capacity, and overall acceptability (Sristi et al., 2025). In the present study, RG broilers showed lower pH₄₅ than SG broilers, suggesting a more rapid postmortem glycolysis. In addition, there were no pale, soft, exudative (PSE) or dark, firm, dry (DFD) meat characteristics observed in this study. Following slaughter, the cessation of blood circulation and oxygen supply shifts muscle metabolism from aerobic to anaerobic pathways, resulting in glycogen breakdown and lactic acid accumulation, which drives a decline in pH (Bendall, 1973; Lawrie and Ledward, 2006). This process directly affects protein denaturation, water-holding capacity, and meat color development (Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan, 2005).

Higher pH₄₅ values, as observed in SG broilers, are generally associated with a slower glycolytic rate and reduced lactic acid accumulation, which may contribute to improved protein functionality (Fanatico et al., 2007). Ultimate pH (pH₂₄) typically declines to approximately 5.5–5.8 postmortem, depending on muscle glycogen reserves and the rate of glycolysis (Aberle et al., 2001; Kannan et al., 2006). The extent and rate of pH decline are influenced by multiple factors, including glycogen content, carcass temperature, and enzymatic activity (Strobel et al., 2021). Rapid glycolysis at high muscle temperatures can accelerate protein denaturation, leading to reduced water-holding capacity and inferior meat quality (Weng et al., 2022).

Meat color is a key quality attribute influencing consumer acceptance. In the present study, RG broilers exhibited higher L*, a*, and b* than SG broilers, indicating a lighter meat color, whereas SG broilers produced darker meat. This darker coloration in SG broilers may be associated with a higher proportion of oxidative muscle fibers and increased myoglobin content, which are typical of slower-growth genotypes. In addition, strain-related differences in behavior and activity levels may contribute to variation in muscle metabolism, with more active birds developing greater oxidative capacity and darker muscle pigmentation. Genetic background also plays a key role, as SG broilers are less intensively selected for rapid muscle accretion and tend to exhibit muscle characteristics associated with endurance-type activity, resulting in darker meat.

Although RG broilers showed higher pH₂₄ values, their lighter color is likely attributable to genotype-related differences rather than pH alone. Variation in meat color is closely associated with muscle fiber composition and metabolic activity. These differences are consistent with previous reports on muscle fiber composition and meat color variation between fast- and slow-growth broilers (Petracci et al., 2013; Berri et al., 2005; Sirri et al., 2011). Meat color is largely influenced by genotype (Fletcher, 1995) and is strongly associated with consumer perception of freshness and quality, thereby affecting purchasing decisions (Yang and Jiang, 2005; Choo et al., 2014). In addition, production factors such as feeding strategies and rearing systems may further influence final meat color (Castellini et al., 2008). Therefore, meat color should be carefully considered when developing and promoting SG broilers due to its direct impact on market acceptance.

Drip loss, an indicator of water-holding capacity, was higher in RG broilers, indicating reduced water-holding capacity compared with SG broilers. This difference is likely associated with a more rapid postmortem pH decline in RG broilers, where accelerated glycolysis promotes protein denaturation and reduces the ability of muscle proteins to retain water (Bendall, 1973; Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan, 2005; Weng et al., 2022). In addition, structural characteristics of fast-growth muscle, including larger fiber size and reduced extracellular space, may further limit water retention (Dransfield and Sosnicki, 1999). Age at slaughter and muscle maturity may also influence protein functionality and contribute to variation in DL (Aberle et al., 2001; Strobel et al., 2021). The DL values observed in this study exceeded the <3% threshold suggested by Honikel and Hamm (1994) but were within the range reported for commercial broilers. For example, Wang et al. (2017) reported a DL of 2.45% in male Arbor Acres broilers, whereas Gu et al. (2024) reported 3.88% under standard rearing conditions. Similarly, Hammemi et al. (2024) reported DL values of 3.96% and 3.08% in Cobb 500 and Ross 308 broilers, respectively, and Sarsenbek et al. (2013)reported a DL of 4.31% in Arbor Acres, which is comparable to the present findings (3.59–4.32). Although elevated DL is often associated with pale, soft, exudative (PSE)-like conditions resulting from rapid glycolysis and excessive lactic acid accumulation (Warriss, 2010), no direct assessment of PSE was conducted in this study; therefore, this interpretation should be considered with caution. Higher DL values have also been reported in other studies, such as 5.47% in male Arbor Acres broilers (Pu et al., 2025) and in extreme cases may reach up to 10%, negatively affecting meat quality (Zhuang and Savage, 2013). Increased DL is often associated with reduced meat quality traits, including toughness and muscle abnormalities such as wooden breast and white striping (Fanatico et al., 2007).

Shear force, an indicator of meat tenderness, was not affected by strain or sex and remained within the range reported for broiler breast meat (20–30 N). Xiao et al. (2024) reported SF values of 34.29 N in RG (Cobb 500) broilers and 24.48 N in SG (Beijing-You) broilers. Similarly, Gratta et al. (2023) reported SF values equivalent to 21.6–25.9 N in commercial crossbred broilers, supporting the findings of the present study. These findings suggest that meat tenderness was similar between RG and SG broilers under the present conditions. Although rapid growth has been associated with muscle fiber hypertrophy and increased toughness, leading to denser muscle structure (Dransfield and Sosnicki, 1999), tenderness is also influenced by postmortem proteolysis. Reduced activity of proteolytic enzymes responsible for myofibrillar protein degradation may limit tenderization and contribute to higher SF values (Koohmaraie et al., 2002).

The muscle fiber diameter refers to the width of individual muscle cells and is a critical indicator of muscle growth. Typically, larger muscle fiber diameters are associated with the rapid muscle growth seen in RG broiler breeds (Chen et al., 2007). However, excessive enlargement can impaired meat texture and is common in muscle myopathies such as “wooden breast”. Surrounding each muscle fiber are the endomysium and perimysium, connective tissues that can increase in response to muscle damage or fibrosis (Smith, 1963; Chen et al., 2007). This is associated with degenerative conditions, such as white striping and wooden breast, contributing to reduced meat quality through altered texture and water-holding capacity. The thickness of the perimysium is an indicator of connective tissue remodeling, often due to excessive collagen deposition, and has been linked to muscle abnormalities and decreased tenderness in poultry meat (Soglia et al., 2016). Often, this is due to differences in growth dynamics, muscle development, and connective tissue remodeling. The rapid growth of RG broilers was reported to cause muscle fibers to mature and hypertrophy naturally, resulting in larger fiber diameters without the same metabolic stress observed in SG broilers (Jaturasitha et al., 2008).

Smith and Fletcher (1987) studied male and female Arbor Acres broilers and examinined the fiber diameter in the pectoralis major muscle at three locations. They reported muscle fiber diameters in the anterior, middle, and posterior regions of 45.67 µm, 42.67 µm, and 52.33 µm, respectively, in the males and 46.00 µm, 42.67 µm, and 55.67 µm, respectively, in the females. Similarly, the studies of Wattanachant et al. (2004) investigated muscle fiber diameter in a commercial broiler (line CP707) and in Thai indigenous chickens, reporting breed values of 36.36 µm and 33.94 µm, respectively. In addition, de Almeida Assunção et al. (2022) examined Cobb 500 broilers and reported a muscle fiber diameter of 43.08 µm. These muscle fiber diameter values were comparable to those observed in the present study of 41.11 µm in the RG broilers and 40.47 µm in the SG broilers, which represented the range of muscle fiber diameter in the measured broilers.

The growth rate in native breeds has been reported to be lower than 20 g/chick/day, with resultant smaller meat fibers and muscle fiber diameters (Hassen et al., 2006). Native chickens exhibit more active behavior, which produces greater oxidation in their muscle fibers, with a more glycolytic muscle profile having greater potential for meat production (Shao et al., 2022). These muscle fiber attributes not only affect carcass yield but may also influence meat texture, water-holding capacity, and overall consumer acceptability (Kern et al., 2011). The present findings indicated that genetic strain has a pronounced effect on meat fiber characteristics, with SG broilers having a thicker endomysium than RG broilers, whereas RG broilers had higher TNF and fiber density values. These results were consistent with other studies. For example, Fanatico et al. (2007) reported that RG broilers tended to develop larger muscle fibers due to their rapid growth rates, a characteristic that could be attributed to genetic selection aimed at optimizing feed conversion and growth efficiency over those in native chicken breeds. The connective tissue structures are likely a reflection of the differing growth rates between the strains. For example, SG broilers tend to have denser connective tissue due to slower muscle development and more gradual growth (Castellini et al., 2008), suggesting that SG broilers may allocate more energy toward the development of connective tissue, while RG broilers prioritize muscle fiber growth to maximize carcass yield.

Moderate positive correlations (Table 4) were observed between BMW and L*₄₅ (0.55), pH₂₄ (0.51), L*₂₄ (0.51), b*₄₅ (0.41), a*₂₄ (0.39), and b*₂₄ (0.38), indicating that increased breast muscle development was associated with higher ultimate pH and increased lightness values. Drip loss was moderately correlated with L*₄₅ and L*₂₄ (0.49 and 0.47, respectively), suggesting that greater water loss was associated with paler meat. In addition, pH₂₄ was positively correlated with L*₄₅ (0.52), L*₂₄ (0.39), and b*₄₅ (0.31), reflecting the influence of postmortem pH on color development.

Moderate negative correlations (Table 4) were observed between DL and pH₄₅ (−0.56), supporting the role of rapid early postmortem glycolysis in reducing water-holding capacity (Bendall, 1973; Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan, 2005). Endomysium thickness was negatively correlated with pH₂₄ (−0.50) and L*45 (-0.59), and L*24 (-0.55), indicating that increased connective tissue was associated with lower pH and reduced lightness. Structural trade-offs were also evident, as fiber diameter was negatively correlated with fiber count (−0.56) and fiber density (−0.55), while endomysium thickness was negatively associated with fiber count (−0.48) and density (−0.49).

Strain-specific analysis revealed distinct biological patterns. In RG broilers (Table 5), BMW was positively correlated with DL, pH24 and all color trait at both 45 minutes and 24 hours postmortem. DL was negatively correlated with pH45 but positively correlated with pH24 L*45, L*24 and TL, indicating poorer water-holding capacity and paler meat color. These findings suggest that larger BMW maybe associated with alterd postmortem muscle metabolism, thereby affecting both pH decline and water-holding capacity traits, consistent with previous reports in fast-growth broilers (Petracci et al., 2015). Moreover, Oliveira et al. (2021) reported that wooden breast meat commonly exhibits elevated ultimate pH values. In addition. Endomysium thickness was positively correlated with SF, suggesting that increased connective tissue deposition may enhance tructural rigidity and resistance to muscle fiber fragmentation, potentially contributing to tougher meat texture (An et al., 2010). No correlations were observed among muscle fiber traits, except for the strong positive correlation between TNF and fiber density, which likely reflects their mathematical and structural interdependence.

Further comparisons (Table 6) highlighted distinct correlation patterns in SG broilers. BMW was not correlated with DL but was positively correlated with pH24. In contrast, BMW was negatively correlated with a*45, L*24, and a*24, indicating darker breast meat color in SG broilers with heavier BMW. This darker meat appearance may be associated with greater myoglobin content and higher oxidative muscle fiber characteristics in slow-growth genotypes (Weng et al., 2022). In addition, muscle fiber diameter was positively correlated with perimysial thickness, suggesting that longer rearing periods may affect collagen deposition and connective tissue development in SG broilers (Weng et al., 2022).

These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that slow-growth genotypes exhibit greater connective tissue deposition and structural influence on meat quality, whereas fast-growth broilers rely more on metabolic characteristics (Petracci et al., 2013; Soglia et al., 2016; Hassen et al., 2006). Overall, the correlation analysis indicates that postmortem metabolism (pH decline and glycolysis) plays a dominant role in determining meat quality in RG broilers, whereas muscle structure and connective tissue contribute more substantially to meat quality variation in SG broilers (Bendall, 1973; Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan, 2005; Petracci et al., 2013; Weng et al., 2022).

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrated that the interaction between genetic strain and sex is a fundamental determinant of breast muscle development, physicochemical meat quality, and myofiber structure in broilers. Rapid-growth broilers demonstrated superior muscle accretion and fiber density; however, these traits were coupled with a physiological predisposition toward reduced water-holding capacity, higher drip loss, and significantly lighter meat coloration. In contrast, slow-growth broilers produced darker meat and more developed connective tissue matrix, as evidenced by increased endomysium thickness, suggesting differences in connective tissue development that may contribute to textural variation. This study demonstrated significant phenotypic correlations among breast muscle weight, meat quality traits, and muscle fiber characteristics, with distinct patterns between RG and SG broilers. Strong correlations among color traits and between pH and drip loss confirmed the important role of postmortem metabolism in the development of meat quality. In RG broilers, breast muscle weight was closely associated with pH, drip loss, and color traits, indicating that metabolic factors largely influence meat quality in fast-growth birds. In contrast, SG broilers showed stronger relationships between muscle structural traits and connective tissue characteristics, suggesting a greater structural contribution to meat quality variation. Overall, the findings highlight genotype-dependent differences in the biological mechanisms underlying meat quality and provide useful information for breeding strategies aimed at improving both growth performance and meat quality.
