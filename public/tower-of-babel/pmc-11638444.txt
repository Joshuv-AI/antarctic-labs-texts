Unveiling the Potential of Cyclin-Dependent Kinases 4 and 6 Inhibitors Beyond Progression in Hormone Receptor Positive/Human Epidermal Growth Factor Negative Advanced Breast Cancer – A Clinical Review
Authors: Chiara Benvenuti, Thomas Grinda, Elie Rassy, Julia Dixon-Douglas, Joana M. Ribeiro, Alberto Zambelli, Armando Santoro, Barbara Pistilli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Opinion StatementCyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) have revolutionized the management of hormone receptor-positive (HR +) breast cancer. However, resistance to CDK4/6i remains an unavoidable challenge, with limited evidence to guide the choice of subsequent treatments. Continuation of CDK4/6 inhibition raises as a compelling treatment option and is currently an active area of research. This approach encompasses multifaceted strategies regarding CDK4/6i sequence (same or switched agent), endocrine therapy (ET) partner and potential combination with a third drug. Continuing CDK4/6 inhibition while targeting ET resistance in tumours still dependent on ER activity (i.e., ESR1 mutation) through a ctDNA-guided approach has the potential of becoming practice-changing, pending the results of ongoing phase III studies. Conversely, the efficacy of this strategy in cases of radiological progression in a biomarker-unselected population appears to be rather unsatisfactory. While some benefit, albeit modest, has been observed from switching to a different CDK4/6i after progression (e.g. ribociclib after palbociclib in the MAINTAIN trial and abemaciclib after both palbociclib and ribociclib in the postMONARCH trial), the current evidence (mainly with palbociclib) clearly argues against maintaining the same CDK4/6i. Biomarker analyses to optimally identify patients suitable for this approach yielded inconsistent findings that do not apply to daily clinical decision making. Attractive preliminary efficacy has recently emerged from combining a third agent (immunotherapy, AKT/ PIK3CA/mTOR inhibitor, new ET agents, CDK2 inhibitors) to CDK4/6i and ET, but further validation in larger ongoing trials is required to also determine the optimal timing for incorporating these agents into the therapeutic timeline. To date, CDK4/6i after CDK4/6i progression is far from being a standard of care. However, selected patients with indolent disease, prolonged exposure to previous CDK4/6i treatment (especially palbociclib) and without actionable molecular alterations, may be suitable for suchmaintenance strategy beyond progression. In this challenging and rapidly evolving treatment landscape, ongoing studies can refine the optimal approach and identify clinical and molecular factors to select the best treatment for the right patient.

Introduction

Cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) have radically transformed the treatment landscape of hormone receptor-positive (HR +)/Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor-2 Negative (HER2 −) breast cancer (BC). Since 2015, three CDK4/6i, palbociclib, ribociclib and abemaciclib, have become the established treatment for HR + /HER2- advanced breast cancer (ABC) in combination with endocrine therapy (ET) in both endocrine-sensitive and resistant settings (Table 1). All three molecules have shown a consistent and clear progression-free survival (PFS) benefit compared to ET alone [1–7], while conflicting results have been observed for overall survival (OS) [8–14]. Of note, the SONIA trial recently questioned the use of CDK4/6i as an upfront strategy in all HR + /HER2- ABC, showing a lack of difference in survival outcomes with deferred second-line use of CDK4/6i and raising the question of whether some patients might benefit from first-line ET alone [15]. In addition, abemaciclib and ribociclib have shown remarkable efficacy in HR + /HER2- early BC, further changing the treatment algorithm in this subset [16, 17]. However, in metastatic disease these drugs are not curative and resistance eventually occurs, making the identification of effective subsequent treatments an urgent need. Increasing knowledge of the biology of CDK4/6i tumour resistance has opened up an active area of research looking at the continued use of these drugs even after resistance.

This review aims to thoroughly examine the state of the art on the use of CDK4/6i beyond progression. We provide an overview of the mechanism of action and resistance to CDK4/6i and the rationale for pursuing this strategy. We conduct a comprehensive analysis of the clinical evidence and potential future scenarios on this topic, placing this approach in the context of the evolving therapeutic landscape of HR + /HER2- ABC.

CDK4/6 Inhibitors

Pharmacology

CDK4 and CDK6 are key enzymes in the regulation of the cell cycle, specifically the G1-S phase transition and are strictly controlled by cyclins (e.g., cyclin D) and CDK inhibitors (CKIs). In the neoplastic process, growth factors and mitogens upregulate cyclin D, which in turn activates CDK4 and CDK6. These complexes phosphorylate key proteins, namely, the tumour suppressor retinoblastoma (Rb) and weaken the Rb-E2F interaction, enabling gene transcription for DNA replication and cell cycle progression [18, 19]. Up-regulation of the cyclin D-CDK4/6-Rb1 axis plays an important role in tumourigenesis of many cancers, especially HR + /HER2- BC, leading to an altered balance between cell proliferation and cell cycle arrest and resulting in a compelling target [20].

The three CDK4/6i approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) include palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib. Although all three drugs inhibit the activity of CDK4 and CDK6 kinases, there are notable differences in substrate selectivity, pharmacodynamics, and pharmacokinetics, partially explaining some of the observed clinical differences [21]. Palbociclib has an equal suppressive effect on both kinases, abemaciclib and ribociclib show stronger activity against CDK4 than CDK6, with abemaciclib having a fivefold greater potency on CDK4 than the other two agents. Furthermore, palbociclib and ribociclib mainly target CDK4 and CDK6, while abemaciclib inhibits additional kinases (such as CDK1, CDK2, CDK5, CDK9, CDK14, CDKs16-18, GSK3a/b, CAMKIIg/d, and PIM1 kinases), which can lead to cell arrest not only in the G1-S phase, but also in the G2-M phase. Due to this effect on alternative signalling pathways, abemaciclib may be effective in cells resistant to palbociclib and ribociclib and has shown meaningful clinically activity also as a single agent [22, 23]. In addition, CDK4/6i enhance the immune response to tumour cells at multiple levels, providing an intriguing rationale for exploring the synergistic effect of combining CDK4/6i and immunotherapy [24]. Through complex mechanisms, mainly via downregulation of E2F targets, CDK4/6i hinder regulatory T-cell proliferation and elicit tumour antigen presentation by upregulating MHC class I [24, 25]. They also promote an inflamed microenvironment by inducing tumour infiltration and effector T-cell activation [26]. CDK4/6i drive T-cell commitment to the memory phenotype, creating the immunological memory essential for durable antitumour response [27].

Mechanisms of Resistance to CDK4/6i

Despite their long-term effectiveness, in the metastatic setting the development of resistance to CDK4/6i is almost inevitable. In recent years, our understanding of the differing biological mechanisms that drive resistance to CDK4/6i and ET has evolved. Cyclin D-CDK4/6 complexes act as hubs for the transduction of mitogenic signals from membrane receptors into the cell cycle. Overactivation of the cyclin D1-CDK4/6 axis may arise directly through gene amplification or through the increased activity of upstream signals (i.e. tyrosine kinase receptors, such as FGFR and ERBB2, or non-receptors tyrosine kinase signalling, such as the src kinase family) that converge on the MAPK and PIK3CA/AKT/mTOR pathways and ultimately determine cyclin D upregulation [29–31]. In addition, the independent activation of downstream factors, such as the loss of Rb1, overactivation of CDK2 and aberrant cyclin E1 signalling, may lead to the loss of dependence of G1-S progression on the cyclin D-CDK4/6 complex [32]. Interestingly, bi-allelic Rb1 deletions are common in gBRCA2-mutated BC (probably due to the close location of the Rb1 and BRCA2 genes on chromosome 13), which may explain why gBRCA2-mutated patients exhibits poorer outcomes with ET and CDK4/6i treatment [33]. Finally, pathways that stimulate cell cycle progression independently of the cyclin D1-CDK4/6 axis, such as upregulation of Aurora Kinase A (AURKA), which modulates the transition through the G2-M phase, may also contribute to resistance [34]. Nevertheless, prior studies have suggested that ultimate resistance may be the result of a combination of several of the above mechanisms [28]. The dynamics of circulating biomarkers, such as circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) and thymidine kinase enzyme activity (TK1a) determined by liquid biopsy, may offer a promising avenue for early and efficient monitoring of response and resistance to CDK4/6i therapy, as they have demostrated considerable potential as early prognostic and predictive factors, independently and in a combined approach [35, 36].

In parallel, ET resistance is gaining relevance in this scenario, especially when mediated by mutations in the estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) gene, that leads to changes in receptor structure and function and ultimately to ligand-independent activation [37]. These alterations are key players in acquired resistance to aromatase inhibitors (AI), arising in up to 40% of ABC under the pressure of AI exposure [38]. The first and second generation of selective estrogen receptor downregulators (SERDs), such as fulvestrant, elacestrant and camizestrant, have instead been shown to retain efficacy against this specific mutation [39]. Biomarker analyses of various studies have consistently found no significant impact of ESR1 status on CDK4/6i efficacy [40–42]. These findings led to the successful approach of the PADA-1 trial to target endocrine resistance via ESR1 mutation by switching from AI to SERD at the time of its detectability in ctDNA and prior to CDK4/6i resistance and clinical progression [43].

The Treatment Scenario After CDK4/6i Progression

Current guidelines agree that the optimal treatment sequence after CDK4/6i progression remains unclear, mainly due to a lack of knowledge regarding the biological mechanism driving resistance to CDK4/6i in each individual case, preventing a biomarker-driven approach [44, 45].

In the choice of subsequent treatment, key factors in dermining the therapeutic strategy are the duration of previous CDK4/6i treatment, comorbidities, somatic mutations (ESR1, PIK3CA, AKT and PTEN), and germline BRCA1/2 mutation [46].

Single agent ET has shown very limited efficacy in the post-CDK4/6i era, as recent studies with fulvestrant as a control arm have resulted in a median PFS (mPFS) between 1.9 and 4.8 months in this setting [47–51]. A new generation of novel endocrine agents has been developed to overcome the limitations of current ET, including new selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), novel oral selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs), complete estrogen receptor antagonists (CERANs), selective estrogen receptor covalent antagonists (SERCAs) and proteolysis- targeting chimerics (PROTACs) targeting ER [52]. Among the agents currently at the forefront of clinical development, two oral SERDs, elacestrant and camizestrant, showed consistently improved PFS compared to fulvestrant (or AI in the elacestrant study) in patients progressed after CDK4/6i. In the EMERALD trial, elacestrant achieved a mPFS of 2.79 months compared to 1.9 months with standard ET (HR 0.7 [0.55–0.88]) [47]. The elacestrant efficacy is mainly driven by patients with ESR1 mutations who have benefited from CDK4/6i for at least 12 months (mPFS 8.61 [4.14–10.84] months vs 1.91 [1.87–3.68] months, HR 0.41 [0.26–0.63]) [48]. Some clinical benefit of elacestrant over standard ET emerged also in those whose prior treatment with CDK4/6i lasted between 6 and 12 months, but its magnitude is overall modest from a clinical perspective [48]. This suggests that only patients with tumors that maintain dependency on ER activity derive a significant benefit from this treatment. In the SERENA-2 trial, both the 75 mg and 150 mg doses of camizestrant nearly doubled the mPFS to 7.2 months (HR 0.58 [0.41–0.81]) and 7.7 months (HR 0.67 [0.48–0.92]), respectively, compared to 3.7 months with fulvestrant alone [49]. In patients harbouring activating alterations in the PIK3CA-AKT-PTEN pathway, capivasertib plus fulvestrant was recently approved by FDA and is expected to become the preferred option over fulvestrant alone based on results of the Capitello-291 study, where about 70% of patients had received prior CDK4/6i [50]. In the AKT pathway–altered population the mPFS was 7.3 months in the capivasertib–fulvestrant group, as compared with 3.1 months in the placebo–fulvestrant group (HR 0.50 [0.38–0.65]) [50]. The role of this combination in patients without mutations in this pathway remains to be determined. Fulvestrant plus alpelisib represents a viable option for patients with PIK3CA mutations, according to the results of the SOLAR-1 study and, in particular, the BYLieve trial, in which the combination was specifically tested in the post-CDK4/6i setting resulting in a mPFS of 7.3 (5.6–8.3) months [53, 54]. Targeting the same pathway without the need for genomic testing, combining everolimus with fulvestrant or exemestane is another commonly used treatment strategy [55, 56].

For patients with germline BRCA mutations, PARP inhibitors represent a potential therapeutic strategy that offers a targeted approach to this specific genetic alteration [57, 58]. Finally, given the compelling results in pre-treated HR + /HER2- ABC patients, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) are likely to further reshape the therapeutic algorithm, particularly in patients with lower endocrine sensitivity [59–62].

CDK4/6i After CDK4/6i Treatment

Rationale

In this dynamic and complex scenario, continuing or rechallenging treatment with CDK4/6i, which are undoubtedly the most effective drugs for HR + /HER2- patients, has emerged as a potential option to be explored. The rationale for adopting this approach is multi-faceted. First, adaptive resistance is predominantly driven by ET, with tumours being able to adapt to CDK4/6 inhibition if estrogen receptor (ER) signaling is not effectively suppressed [41, 63]. Consequently, modifying the backbone of ET to overcome this resistance mechanism can be a successful option to preserve the inhibitory effect on the CDK4/6-mediated checkpoint and maximise the therapeutic benefit of this drug class. Second, insights from the neoadjuvant setting indicate that the antiproliferative effect induced by CDK4/6 inhibition diminishes after treatment discontinuation, as evidenced by Ki-67 rebound, which can be reversed by prolonged treatment [64]. This finding suggests that continuous exposure is required to maintain the antineoplastic effect. Third, the pharmacological differences between the three agents currently used in clinical practice, especially abemaciclib for its multi-kinase activity, supporting that switching from one agent to another could circumvent certain forms of treatment resistance.

Retrospective Studies

Despite the absence of solid data in favour of continuing CDK4/6i therapy beyond disease progression or reintroduction after interval treatments, numerous retrospective studies have shown that this strategy is not uncommon, representing up to 30% of the therapeutic choices in clinical practice [64]. In these series, palbociclib was the predominant CDK4/6i used in the first-line treatment, while abemaciclib (alone or in combination with switched ET) was often chosen for maintenance/rechallenge. Overall, no notable safety concerns have emerged in these studies. In terms of efficacy, results are heterogeneous and modest overall, with mPFS ranging from 5.3 to 11.8 months [65–71].

Strategies Exploiting Continuous CDK4/6 inhibition in HR + /HER2- ABC. Following the occurrence of radiological disease progression during treatment with CDK4/6i and ET, several approaches focusing on the sustained inhibition of CDK4/6 have been investigated. In the first strategy, the same CDK4/6i is maintained while the ET backbone is switched. The second approach involves switching both agents. The third strategy focuses on the continuation of CDK4/6i and ET (either the same or a switch), while introducing a third agent that targets resistance pathways or exhibits synergistic activity in a combined approach. In parallel, another promising strategy currently under investigation focuses on switching the ET backbone to an agent known to be effective against ESR1mutant tumour cells when an ESR1 mutation is detected by ctDNA analysis prior to radiological progression, while continuing the same CDK4/6i. Maintain the CDK4/6i and Switch ET

The multicentre, phase II BioPER trial evaluated palbociclib in combination with switched ET in patients with HR + /HER2- ABC beyond progression on immediately prior palbociclib-based regimen [72]. The trial met both clinical and biological primary endpoints, although with a modest clinical benefit rate (CBR) of 34.4% [18.6%–53.2%]. The exploratory biomarkers analysis found that loss of Rb, high expression of cyclin E1, or an ESR1 mutation at the time of switch, either independently or combined in a gene signature, were predictive of resistance to palbociclib + ET. In addition, the lower the number of mutations (ESR1, TP53, ERBB2, MET, PIK3CA, and PTEN) at baseline, the better the clinical benefit [72].

The Palbociclib after CDK and Endocrine Therapy (PACE) trial was designed to determine the optimal subsequent treatment for patients with HR + /HER2- ABC who had progressed on prior CDK4/6i combined with AI [73]. The study consisted of three treatment arms: fulvestrant alone, palbociclib plus fulvestrant, and palbociclib plus fulvestrant plus avelumab. The trial did not meet its primary endpoint of improving mPFS with the combination of palbociclib and fulvestrant over fulvestrant alone (4.6 [3.6–5.9]) versus 4.8 [2.1–8.2] months, HR 1.11 [0.79 to 1.55]). A trend towards benefit was observed in the small subgroups who had received interval treatments such as chemotherapy (n = 8; HR 0.45 [0.13–1.59]) or any other regimen (n = 21; HR 0.41 [0.17–1]) between previous CDK4/6i exposure and study treatment. In contrast to the results of the BioPER and MAINTAIN study, translational findings indicated the potential benefit of continuing CDK4/6i in patients with baseline mutation in ESR1 and PIK3CA while no difference between the two arms was found in Rb mutated tumors [51, 73].

In the PALMIRA trial, maintaining palbociclib with different ET did not improve PFS of second-line treatment in patients with HR + /HER2- ABC who progressed on first-line therapy with palbociclib + AI or fulvestrant (mPFS 4.2 [3.5–5.8] versus 3.6 [2.7–4.2] months, HR 0.8 [CI 0.6–1.1]. The 6-month PFS rate (42.1% vs. 29.1%; HR 0.84 [0.66–1.07]) suggests that a subgroup of patients may benefit from continued inhibition of CDK4/6 and currently ongoing biomarker analysis are likely to shed light on how to identify such patients [74].

Recently, the combination of vepedegestrant (ARV-471), a novel oral PROTAC ER degrader, and palbociclib resulted in a remarkable ORR of 42% [24.5%-60.9%] and CBR of 63% [47.5%-76.8%] in heavily pre-treated HR + /HER2- patients who had progressed on previous CDK4/6i (primarily palbociclib) in a Phase 1b study. Efficacy was maintained regardless of ESR1 status, with a sustained clearance of ESR1 mutant allele fractions during treatment in patients carrying this genomic alteration at baseline [75, 76]. The phase 1b/2 TACTIVE-U umbrella study is evaluating vepdegestrant in combination with abemaciclib (NCT05548127) and ribociclib (NCT05573555) in the same setting.

Maintain CDK4/6i and Switch ET at the Time of Detection of ESR1 Mutation

To overcome classical endocrine resistance, such as the occurrence of ESR1 mutation, when sensitivity to CDK4/6i is potentially still retained, the PADA-1 trial introduced a new paradigm in ABC: a ctDNA biomarker-driven approach to guide therapeutic change before radiological progression. This randomized phase III study investigated the efficacy of switching from letrozole to fulvestrant at the time of ESR1 mutation detection in patients receiving first-line palbociclib and letrozole for HR + /HER2- ABC, compared to continuing the initial treatment until radiological progression [43]. In the most recent updated analysis, after a median follow-up of 28.2 months, switching from AI to fulvestrant while continuing palbociclib led to a doubling of both the mPFS with 12.2 [9.3–14.7] versus 5.8 [3.9–7.5] months (HR 0.54 [0.38–0.75]) and mPFS2 with 29.4 months [21.9-NR] and 14 [11–18.6] months (HR 0.37 [0.24–0.56]), respectively [89]. These findings proved the clinical utility of anticipating the molecular diagnosis of a known AI-resistance mutation to guide the change in the ET backbone before radiological progression. The ongoing phase 3 SERENA-6 trial (NCT04964934) with a design similar to that of PADA-1, using camizestrant as the SERD, aims to futher validate this ctDNA-guided early switch strategy [90].

Switching Both CDK4/6i and ET

The randomized phase II MAINTAIN trial investigated the efficacy of ribociclib in combination with modified backbone ET (fulvestrant or exemestane) compared to switched ET plus placebo in HR + /HER2- ABC patients who progressed on prior CDK4/6i. Most enrolled patients (87%) had received palbociclib as prior CDK4/6i treatment [77]. Around two-thirds of patients in both groups benefited from previous CDK4/6i exposure for over 12 months and the switched ET was fulvestrant in more than 80% of patient in both arms. After a median follow-up of 18.2 months, the trial met its primary endpoint showing a nearly doubled mPFS (5.29 [3.02–8.25] months vs 2.76 [2.66–3.25] months), and a 43% (HR 0.57 [0.39–0.85]) reduction in the risk of progression or death in the ribociclib arm compared to the control arm. This benefit was consistent across subgroups selected by the site of disease (visceral and bone only) and companion ET (fulvestrant and exemestane). Hazard ratios were comparable for those with prior exposure to palbociclib (HR, 0.58 [0.38 to 0.89]) and ribociclib (HR, 0.50 [0.15 to 1.70]), although the sample size of the previous ribociclib group was extremely small (N = 14). Greater efficacy of ribociclib was observed in the subgroup of patients whose prior CDK4/6i treatment lasted less than 12 months (HR 0.36 [0.17 to 0.74]). The exploratory translational analysis of ctDNA-based biomarkers showed that the benefit of fulvestrant plus ribociclib was limited to the ESR1 wild-type population (mPFS of 8.32 [5.65–16.63] months versus 2.76 [2.66–5.49] months, HR 0.30 [0.15–0.62]), while no advantage was seen in patients with ESR1 mutations (mPFS of 2.96 [2.66–4.21] months versus 3.02 [2.53–5.62] months, HR 1.22 [0.59–2.49]). The cohort with PIK3CA mutations also did not appear to benefit from adding ribociclib to fulvestrant. However, these seemingly conflicting results should be considered hypothesis-generating only, given the small sample size and variable distribution of concurrent mutations, such as the higher rate of CCND1 and/or FGFR1 amplifications in the ESR1 mutant cohort [77].

The randomized phase III postMONARCH trial, recently presented at the 2024 ASCO Annual Meeting, investigated the efficacy of continued CDK4/6 inhibition with abemaciclib in combination with fulvestrant after progression on a prior CDK4/6i [78]. About 60% of patients had previously received palbociclib and one-third had been treated with ribociclib, with about 75% of patients having received their previous CDK4/6i for at least 12 months. Visceral disease was present in around 60% of patients in both study arms, while bone-only disease accounted for about 20% of patients. According to the investigator assessment, the combination of abemaciclib and fulvestrant resulted in a 27% reduction in the risk of PFS events, with an mPFS of 6 months (5.6–8.6) compared to 5.3 (3.7–5.6) months for fulvestrant alone. Conversely, the study secondary endpoint PFS by blinded independent central review was 12.9 months (9.5-NR) in the abemaciclib arm vs 5.6 months (3.9–7.7), so underlining a high discordance with investigator assessment. The benefit of combination therapy was consistent across all subgroups, although it was clearly less pronounced in patients with visceral metastases and whose prior CDK4/6i treatment lasted less than 12 months. Interestingly, the subgroup of patients previously treated with ribociclib did not appear to derive any clinical benefit from the combination of abemaciclib and fulvestrant (HR 1.01 [0.67–1.51]), although the interaction p- value was not significant and the confidence interval was very wide, suggesting a cautious interpretation of these data. When looking at biomarker subgroups, abemaciclib and fulvestrant showed superior efficacy compared to fulvestrant alone, regardless of the presence of ESR1 and PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN mutations as detected by ctDNA analysis [78].

The phase 2 ELAINE-2 trial showed promising activity of the combination of abemaciclib and lasofoxifene, a new SERM, in HR + /HER2- ABC patients with ESR1 mutations that progressed on prior CDK4/6i (mainly palbociclib) [79]. The mPFS was 56 (31.9-NE) weeks with a CBR of 66% (47.3%-80.1%) and an objective response rate (ORR) of 56% (33.7%-75.4%). Additionally, after only four weeks of treatment, the ESR1-mutated allele fraction clearly decreased in 81% of patients with available ctDNA data, indicating effective engagement with lasofoxifene. Importantly, treatment efficacy was not affected by the presence of other genetic alterations (PIK3CA, CCND1, FGFR1, TP53) [79].

The MORPHEUS BC study is an umbrella trial evaluating giredestrant, a novel oral SERD, in multiple combinations in the post-CDK4/6i setting [80]. The results of the cohorts treated with giredestrant in combination with abemaciclib and ribociclib after previous treatment with another CDK4/6i (mostly palbociclib) were presented at the ASCO 2023 Annual Meeting. The data showed a CBR of 40% for both arms, with an apparently more pronounced efficacy in patients with an ESR1 mutation (accounting for 2 out of the 3 PRs) [80].

Finally, palazestrant (OP-1250) is an oral innovative ET agent that combines the mechanisms of action of the CERAN and SERD classes [91] which have been investigated in combination with palbociclib (NCT05266105) or ribociclib (NCT05508906) in the post-CDK4/6i setting. The results presented at the last SABCS 2023 and ESMO Breast 2024 consistently demonstrated reassuring safety profiles, showing no drug-drug interactions between palazestrant and the two CDK4/6i and no significant reciprocal effect on drug exposure. Encouragingly, both studies documented promising tumour response and disease control [81, 82].

Targeting a Collateral Pathway While Continuing CDK4/6 Inhibition

Preliminary data support the immunomodulatory effect of CDK4/6i and their synergistic activity with immunotherapy. In the PACE study, the combination of palbociclib, fulvestrant, and the anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody avelumab resulted in a notable mPFS and OS of 8.1 [3.2–10.7]months and 42.5 [26.8–46.0] months, respectively [73]. Although statistical significance was not reached, these findings underline the potential for immune modulation in preventing or reversing resistance to CDK4/6i and warrant further research in dedicated studies. Two ongoing umbrella studies (MORPHEUS HR + BC, NCT03280563, and MORPHEUS BC, NCT04802759) are currently evaluating abemaciclib in combination with a SERD (fulvestrant or giredestrant) and immunotherapy (atezoluzamb or avelumab) in the post-CDK4/6i setting.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is linked to the estrogen receptor (ER) and the CDK4/6-cyclin D-Rb-E2F pathways, mediating anti-apoptotic, proliferative, and survival signaling. Increased phosphorylated AKT levels have been associated with continuous CDK2/cyclin E2 expression, allowing cells to circumvent CDK4/6 inhibition and develop resistance to CDK4/6i treatment [92]. Therefore, it has been hypothesized that simultaneous inhibition of the PI3K/AKT and CDK4/6 pathways may delay the onset and overcome resistance to CDK4/6i. The TRINITI-1 trial evaluated the combination of ribociclib, everolimus, and exemestane in 96 patients with HR + /HER2- ABC following progression on a CDK4/6i-based regimen (mainly palbociclib). The triplet regimen confirmed promising efficacy and tolerability with a CBR of 41% [31.1–51.6] and a mPFS of 5.7 [3.6–9-1] months, respectively. ctDNA analysis revealed a trend towards better outcomes in patients with wild-type ESR1 and wild-type PIK3CA, as well as those with a longer duration of initial CDK4/6i therapy [83]. Targeting the same pathway at a different level, the ongoing phase Ib/III CAPITELLO-292 (NCT04862663) study is evaluating the efficacy of the AKT inhibitor capivasertib in combination with fulvestrant and palbociclib or ribociclib in patients who previously progressed on CDK4/6i [93]. Study results on 40 evaluable patients included in the phase Ib part, showed a manageable safety profile, with diarrhoea, neutropenia, fatigue and nausea being the most frequent side effects, and a promising clinical activity: 5/8 patients with measurable disease at baseline had confirmed PRs (ORR: 62.5% [24.5–91.5]), of whom 4 had PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alterations detected either in tumour tissue or ctDNA [93]. In support of this approach, the TAKTIC study demonstrated the feasibility of combining ipatasertib, another AKT inhibitor, with fulvestrant and palbociclib in the post-CDK4/6i setting. This combination proved to be feasible and resulted in an encouraging CBR of 57%, which was numerically higher than in the two arms receiving ipatasertib with ET alone [84]. In this context, gedatolisib, a new dual inhibitor of PI3K/mTOR, has shown promising results in CDK4/6i pre-treated patients in combination with palbociclib and fulvestrant. Remarkable clinical activity was observed in both the overall population and the PIK3CA-mutant cohort (ORR 63% and 73%, 12mo-PFS rate 53% and 60%, respectively) [85]. A phase III trial assessing this combination is currently underway (VIKTORIA-1, NCT05501886).

To hit another putative alteration driving resistance to CDK4/6i and ET, i.e. FGFR1/2 amplification or overexpression, the ROGABREAST trial investigated rogaratinib, a selective FGFR1-4 kinase inhibitor, added to fulvestrant and palbociclib in patients with FGFR1/2 + HR + /HER2- ABC progressing on first-line CDK4/6i plus AI [86]. The resulted presented at the 2023 SABCS congress showed an overall feasibility of the combination in terms of safety profile, but efficacy was overall modest (mPFS: 114 days) and rogaratinib development was discontinued [86]. A similar trial with another FGFR inhibitor, erdafinitib, in combination with fulvestrant and palbociclib in the post-CDK4/6i setting is currently ongoing to further explore this pathway (NCT03238196).

Inhibiting Src kinases has shown promise in overcoming treatment resistance, by restoring sensitivity to CDK4/6i and ET in pre-clinical in vitro and mouse models [87]. The combination of bosutinib, a Src inhibitor, palbociclib and fulvestrant was well-tolerated and led to a robust 50% CBR in 18 HR + /HER2- ABC patients who had progression on previous palbociclib (100%) and fulvestrant (83%) [87].

CDK2 inhibition is gaining increasing interest in this challenging scenario. CDK2 plays a central role in driving resistance of HR + /HER2- ABC and co-targeting of CDK2 and CDK4/6 in addition to ET showed a synergistic effect in models resistant to ET and/or CDK4/6i [94]. These preclinical findings have supported the development and clinical exploration of both selective CDK2 inhibitors and novel inhibitors that simultaneously target CDK2/4/6 as a potential innovative therapeutic approach to restore cell cycle control, even in cases of CDK4/6i resistance. Preliminary results indicate promising activity of such combinations after progression CDK4/6i progression. For instance, the novel CDK2/4/6 inhibitor ebvaciclib (PF-06873600) in combination with fulvestrant exhibited a disease control rate (DCR) of 67% in a small, heavily pre-treated cohort of 9 patients with HR + /HER2- ABC progressing on CDK4/6i [88]. Several trials are currently investigated these agents in the post-CDK4/6i setting (NCT05252416, NCT05735080, NCT05867251, NCT05304962, NCT04553133, NCT06188520).

The evolving understanding of the molecular characteristics of HR + /HER2- ABC coupled with the rapidly growing pipeline of anticancer drugs has led to a remarkable increase in the number of studies addressing this complex and multifaceted issue. Discussion

CDK4/6i have transformed the treatment of HR + /HER2- BC, becoming the established standard of care for ABC and early-stage BC at high risk of relapse. However, the development of resistance to these agents is almost inevitable in metastatic disease and will be an issue in patients presenting with disease recurrence after exposure in the adjuvant setting, for which no data on the best treatment algorithm is yet available. For patients who relapse during or shortly after adjuvant CDK4/6, i.e. endocrine-resistant disease, alternative treatments such as ADCs or chemotherapy appear to be more appropriate. On the other hand, if relapse occurs after a longer interval (at least 12 months post- adjuvant treatment), rechallenging with a different CDK4/6i could be reasonable, although data to support this strategy are lacking and urgently needed. In the postMONARCH trial, patients who had relapsed during or after adjuvant CDK4/6i were eligible for the study, but unfortunately only two patients were enrolled and both were assigned to the placebo arm [78].

Therefore, the compelling question of which therapeutic strategies should be chosen following progression or relapse during or after therapy with CDK4/6i is expanding and takes on new importance for patients treated for both metastatic and early disease. Currently, ET-based regimens, alone or in combination with PIK3CA, AKT, or mTOR inhibitors, are sequentially employed to delay the need for chemotherapy as long as possible. Most of these second-line treatments were approved before the introduction of CDK4/6i as upfront treatment, and recent data show limited efficacy in this new context [46].

While studies on molecular drivers of CDK4/6i resistance have not yet identified such effective targeted therapies post-progression on CDK4/6i, encouraging strategies to prolong CDK4/6i benefit in the form of maintenance are emerging. The PADA-1 and ELAINE-2 studies have shown the feasibility and efficacy of a ctDNA-guided approach in a biologically selected population before and after resistance to CDK4/6i. The results of the SERENA-6 trial are eagerly awaited to confirm the efficacy of this biomarker-guided strategy further in the context of a novel oral SERD (camizestrant). However, the cost and inconsistent availability of ctDNA testing across cancer centers (especially in those with low patient volume), as well as the sensitivity of ctDNA in patients with low disease volume may pose a challenge to the widespread adoption of this potentially pivotal strategy. On the other hand, the lack of benefit from continuing palbociclib in palbociclib pre-treated patients in PACE and PALMIRA trials definitively argues against maintaining the same CDK4/6i outside a ctDNA-guided strategy such as those outlined above.

A different and much more nuanced discussion applies to the switch strategies investigated in the MAINTAIN and postMONARCH trials, both of which showed a significant, albeit modest, efficacy. Despite the comparable absolute benefit of ribociclib (5.3 months) and abemaciclib (5.6 months), the two studies exhibit key differences in terms of design and results. In the MAINTAIN trial, intervening treatments (both chemotherapy and ET-based regimens) between first CDK4/6i and enrollment were allowed and the vast majority of patients previously received palbociclib (only 2 patients abemaciclib). On the other hand, in the postMONARCH study, eligible patients had progression immediately on or after CDK4/6i, that was ribociclib in about one-third of cases and palbociclib in almost all the reminders. Abemaciclib showed a greater benefit in patients without visceral disease and with more than one year of prior CDK4/6i exposure, pointing out low disease burden and endocrine sensitivity as key elements for the success of this strategy. In contrast, the efficacy of switched ribociclib emerged especially in the subgroup of patients with short benefit from initial palbociclib-based therapy (< 12 months), but the limited sample size (39 patients) prevents any solid conclusions. From a molecular standpoint, the MAINTAIN study showed no benefit for continued CDK4/6i in patients with an ESR1 mutation, while in the postMONARCH trial, abemaciclib efficacy was not affected by the presence of any baseline mutation studied (neither ESR1 nor the PIK3CA pathway). A body of evidence suggests that ESR1 mutations do not affect CDK4/6i efficacy, either in the upfront CDK4/6i therapy or in the setting of maintenance (in the ctDNA guided studies) or rechallenge [43, 95]. The apparently conflicting results of the MAINTAIN study may be explained by the small sample size and the higher rate of concurrent alterations involved in resistance mechanisms in the ribociclib-treated ESR1 mutant population. Several ongoing studies exploring the strategy of CDK4/6i switching in patients with ESR1 mutation (ELECTRA and ELAINE-3 trials) will further enrich our knowledges in this critical molecular population. Despite the limitations and slight contradictions mentioned above, these results overall suggest that those patients who are pretreated with palbociclib and have a more indolent disease are the best suitable candidates for a CDK4/6i switch.

In terms of a targeted approach against resistance pathways, available findings (mainly from phase I trials), despite their early and preliminary nature, have the potential to pave the way for innovative treatment approaches that could enrich the overall treatment landscape. An attractive target is the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, which is altered in almost half of HR + /HER2- ABC and plays a well-defined role in resistance to ET and CDK4/6i [96–98]. Combinations of agents targeting this pathway with ET are already used in clinical practice but have shown modest activity following progression on CDK4/6i [54]. The results of the TRINITI-1 trial demonstrated the feasibility of a “triplet” therapy with everolimus, ET, and CDK4/6i; this strategy may benefit from novel inhibitors of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, such as gedatolisib or capivasertib, with improved tolerability and efficacy profiles. Following the same principle, combination regimens of CDK4/6i with ET and agents targeting resistance mechanisms, such as FGFR1 rearrangements, HER2, or the insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling pathway, are currently being tested. Despite the convincing biological rationale and preliminary data suggesting that simultaneous inhibition of the putative resistance pathways outperforms a sequential approach, many unresolved questions remain. The optimal timing for incorporating these agents into the therapeutic continuum remains unclear (i.e., as part of first-line treatment or at the time of disease progression). Furthermore, in the absence of data from comparative studies, whether and to what extent continued inhibition of CDK4/6 is essential for the observed efficacy signals is not fully elucidated. The ongoing trials could potentially address this question.

A novel, compelling strategy currently under active investigation focuses on creating a comprehensive blockade of the cell cycle by combining CDK2i and CDK4/6i, targeting the resistance mechanism involving upregulation of CDK2 activity, a well-described escape route for tumour cells from cell cycle control imposed by CDK4/6i. This synergistic approach, shown in preclinical models and early phase I studies in HR + /HER2- ABC, has led to heightened interest and accelerated development of CDK2i and simultaneous CDK2/4/6i. As a result, clinical trials involving both anti-CDK2 agents alone and novel inhibitors simultaneously targeting CDK2/4/6 are bourgeoned across all lines of treatment, including the post-CDK4/6i setting.

In parallel with refining this CDK4/6i-based strategy, the treatment armamentarium for HR + /HER2- ABC is making groundbreaking progress in several directions. Advances are ongoing to find more potent and smarter ET agents, such as SERMs, oral SERDs, CERANs, PROTACs, and SERCAs [52], and the advent of ADCs adds another layer of complexity to the post-CDK4/6i algorithm. Trastuzumab deruxtecan (TDXd) in pre-treated HR + /HER2low (that is, 1 + or 2 + on HER2 immunohistochemistry (IHC), in the absence of HER2 amplification) and HER2 ultra-low (defined as IHC 0 with membrane staining; IHC > 0 < 1 +) ABC outperformed any second-line treatment of targeted agents with or without ET, with or without maintenance of CDK4/6i [59, 62]. Considering that HR + /HER2low and ultra-low ABC account for approximately 85%-90% of HR + ABC [62] and that the lower limit of HER2 expression demonstrating benefit from TDXd has not strictly been established, TDXd may become an attractive second-line option for this large population without the need for extensive biomarker testing, especially in cases of low endocrine sensitivity [99, 100]. In addition, trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (TROP-2) represents another appealing target for ADCs in HR + /HER2- ABC. Following the positive results of the TROPiCS-02 trial, sacituzumab govitecan has already been approved by the EMA and FDA for HR + /HER2- ABC who have received ET-based treatment and at least two systemic therapies in the advanced setting [60], and datopotamab deruxtecan is also likely to enrich the treatment armamentarium according to the promising efficacy shown in the TROPION-Breast01 trial [61]. ADCs sequences may represent a valuable option for second-line therapy and beyond, especially for patients who become clinically or genomically endocrine-resistant after first-line treatment with CDK4/6i and ET.

Despite the advances in the ADCs field, comprehensive molecular profiling is advocated for tailoring second-line therapies based on well-defined molecular features (e.g., (ESR1, PIK3CA, AKT or PTEN alterations, germline BRCA1/2 mutation) along with clinical factors (patient preference, comorbidities, site of disease and prior duration of CDK4/6i treatment). Indeed, maximizing ET-based options through a tailored, genomics-driven strategy in patients who retain sensitivity to CDK4/6 blockade remains a priority, given the excellent quality of life offered by well-tolerated oral therapies. In this complicated landscape, understanding which patients will benefit from continuing CDK4/6i rather than a complete change in treatment strategy to an ADC-based approach needs to be fulfilled.

Conclusion

Existing clinical evidence on CDK4/6i after CDK4/6i does not support this approach as a standard of care for all patients. Patient selection for such a maintenance strategy will be critical as other novel therapies, such as ADCs, are gaining traction. The advent of ctDNA detection of endocrine-resistant mutations such as ESR1 before radiological progression can become a breakthrough approach in this respect. Maintenance or rechallenge CDK4/6i strategies remain an option for a clinically well-selected still endocrine sensitive population (i.e., absence or low burden of visceral disease and prolonged benefit from prior CDK4/6i treatment) and lacking molecular targets (either due to the absence of targetable alterations or limited access to molecular testing due to cost or availability costraints). Beyond patient selection, the optimal treatment strategy is also yet to be determined: the sequence of CDK4/6i agents to limit cross-resistance, the optimum endocrine partner to overcome endocrine resistance, and the true synergistic value of a third pathway-targeting agent. Results of ongoing trials are required to confirm whether a refined CDK4/6i maintenance strategy will find a place in the expanding treatment scenario of HR + /HER2- ABC.
