From Moore-Penrose to Markov via Gauss
Cole Comfort, Jean-Simon Pacaud Lemay

Abstract
Markov categories are the central framework for categorical probability theory. Many important concepts from probability theory can be formalized in terms of Markov categories. In particular, conditional probability distributions and Bayes’ theorem are captured via the notion of conditionals in a Markov category. Gaussian probability theory gives an example of a Markov category with conditionals, where the conditionals can be computed using the Moore-Penrose inverse. In this paper, we introduce the Gauss construction on a Moore-Penrose dagger additive category, producing a Markov category with conditionals. Applying the Gauss construction to the category of real matrices recaptures the Gaussian probability theory example, while applying it to the category of complex (resp. quaternionic) matrices gives us new Markov categories of proper complex (resp. quaternionic) Gaussian conditional distributions. Moreover, we also characterize all possible conditionals in the Gauss construction.

Introduction

Categorical probability theory, as the name suggests, is the field of research of applying category theory to help study the underlying structures for probability theory. By using categorical tools to provide a synthetic version of probability theory, one can then generalize models and results to more general settings. While categorical probability theory has been around since the works of Lawvere
[
19
]
and Giry
[
14
]
, the field itself really took off in the late 2010s with the landmark paper of Cho and Jacobs
[
5
]
. As such, since the beginning of the 2020s, categorical probability theory is now a well-established field and an active area of research and interest. We invite the reader to see Fritz’ paper
[
13
]
for a lovely introduction to categorical probability theory and its interesting history.

The primary framework for categorical probability theory is Markov categories (Def
2.1
) which are, briefly, symmetric monoidal categories with copy and discard operations, but where only the discard is preserved by maps. Earlier concepts of Markov categories were introduced by Golubtsov
[
15
]
and Fong
[
12
]
, but were fully defined by Cho and Jacobs in
[
5
]
under the name affine copy-discard (CD) categories. In
[
13
]
, Fritz renamed them Markov categories since Markov kernels, a fundamental structure in probability theory, themselves form a Markov category. Of course, Markov categories are named after Russian mathematician Andrey Markov, famous for his foundational works on Markov chains.

Many important concepts and results from probability theory can be cleanly formulated in terms of Markov categories. Of particular importance is the notion of conditionals in a Markov category
[
13
, Sec 11]
which, as the name suggests, captures the notion of a conditional probability distribution. Briefly, given a map of suitable type in a Markov category, a conditional for this map (Def
2.3
), if it exists, is a sort of transpose map which takes one of the outputs of the codomain as an input in the domain. We can also interpret Bayes’ theorem, one of the most well-known theorems from probability theory, in a Markov category with conditionals
[
5
,
15
]
.

Having all conditionals is a highly desirable property for a Markov category to have, since conditionals are highly useful and imply various other interesting properties, see
[
9
,
13
]
for more details. There are many interesting examples of Markov categories with conditionals which capture various frameworks for probability theory. In particular, there is a Markov category with conditionals called
\mathsf{Gauss}
(Ex
2.4
) that captures Gaussian probability theory and whose morphisms are real Gaussian conditional distributions
[
13
, Ex 6]
. Of course, Gaussian distributions are one of the most important concepts in statistics and probability, named after the forever famous German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss. Moreover, conditionals in this Markov category
\mathsf{Gauss}
can be computed using the Moore-Penrose inverse.

For matrices, the Moore-Penrose inverses is a special kind of generalized inverse with many practical applications, see
[
4
]
. While a Moore-Penrose inverse may not exist for matrices over an arbitrary ring, if one does exist, then it is unique. In particular, all matrices over the real, complex, and quaternion numbers have a Moore-Penrose inverse. This kind of generalized inverse is named after American mathematician E. Hastings Moore, who first introduced the concept in terms of orthogonal projectors
[
20
]
, and English mathematician and physicist Roger Penrose, who later (without knowing of Moore’s work) introduced the concept in terms of algebraic identities
[
21
]
. The notion of Moore-Penrose inverses can be generalized to other algebraic settings with an involution such as, in particular, to dagger categories.

Briefly, a dagger category (Def
3.1
) is a category with an involution operation on its maps, which for every map produces a map of dual type. Dagger categories have now been extensively studied, especially during the 2000s and 2010s, and are a fundamental concept for categorical foundations of quantum theory, see
[
17
]
. In any dagger category one can define the notion of a Moore-Penrose inverse for maps (Def
6.1
). Moore-Penrose inverses in dagger categories were first introduced and studied by Puystjens and Robinson in a series of papers in the 1980s, see for example
[
22
]
, though the concept was mostly left untouched for many years. However, with all the significant developments in dagger category theory, Cockett and the second named author picked up the study of Moore-Penrose inverses in dagger categories again in
[
6
]
. This has led to a recent surge of interest in Moore-Penrose inverses in dagger category theory, such as for new applications in categorical quantum foundations
[
11
]
, graphical languages
[
2
]
, developing new generalized inverses in category theory
[
7
,
8
]
, and studying Moore-Penrose inverses in new areas
[
1
]
.

The main objective of this paper is to generalize the category
\mathsf{Gauss}
, of real Gaussian conditional distributions, using the theory of Moore-Penrose dagger categories, providing new examples of Markov categories with conditionals. To do so, we first observe that Gaussian conditional distributions, and thus maps in
\mathsf{Gauss}
, are fully described in terms of real matrices, while composition in
\mathsf{Gauss}
makes use of the transpose and sum. As such, it is natural generalize this to the setting of dagger additive categories (Sec
3
), so dagger categories with compatible biproducts. This is further justified by the fact that maps in an dagger additive category have a canonical matrix representation
[
17
, Sec 2.2.4]
, so composition is given by matrix multiplication and the involution is given by the conjugate transpose of matrices. Therefore, we introduce the Gauss construction (Sec
4
) on an dagger additive category which produces a Markov category (Thm
4.5
). The main result of this paper is that when we apply the Gauss construction to a Moore-Penrose dagger additive category, we obtain a Markov category with conditionals (Thm
6.5
). Applying the Gauss construction to real matrices recaptures
\mathsf{Gauss}
(Ex
4.7
), while applying the Gauss construction to complex matrices gives a new Markov category of proper complex Gaussian conditional distributions (Ex
4.12
). Similarly, applying the Gauss construction to quaternionic matrices gives a new Markov category of proper quaternionic Gaussian conditional distributions (Ex
4.13
).

An important fact about conditionals that we have yet to mention is that they are not necessarily unique, even if a Markov category has all conditionals. So a map in a Markov category can have multiple possible conditionals. As such, the Moore-Penrose inverse gives but one possible way of building a conditional in the Gauss construction. We characterize all possible conditionals in the Gauss construction (Sec
5
) and show that they can be fully described by special maps in the base dagger additive category which we call conditional generators (Def
5.2
). We then show that one can use a weaker version of the Moore-Penrose inverse (Def
7.1
), one that generalizes least squares inverses
[
4
, Fig 6.1]
, to also build conditionals in the Gauss construction (Lemma
7.2
). That said, since (when they exist) Moore-Penrose inverses are always unique, they give a canonical choice of conditionals in the Gauss construction.

Markov Categories with Conditionals

In this background section, we briefly review the basics of the theory of Markov categories, including the notion of conditionals. For an in-depth introduction to Markov categories and categorial probability theory, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
.

We assume that the reader is familiar with the basics of category theory and with symmetric monoidal categories. Briefly, a Markov category is a symmetric monoidal category where each object is a cocommutative comonoid (the dual notion of a commutative monoid) in a coherent way, such that all maps preserve the counit, but importantly not necessarily the comultiplication. For simplicity, in this paper we will work with symmetric
strict
monoidal categories (which is not an issue for Markov category per
[
13
, Thm 10.17]
), so we will assume that the associativity and unital isomorphisms for the monoidal product are identities. An arbitrary category will be denoted by
\mathbb{X}
, with objects denoted by capital letters such as
A,B,C
or
X,Y,Z
, and maps denoted by miniscule letters such as
f,g,h
or
p,q,s
. Homsets will be denoted as
\mathbb{X}(A,B)
and maps
f\in\mathbb{X}(A,B)
as arrows
f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
. Composition will be denoted using the usual
\circ
, while identity maps by
\mathsf{id}_{A}:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
. An arbitrary symmetric monoidal category will be denoted with underlying category
\mathbb{X}
, monoidal product
\otimes
, monoidal unit
I
, and symmetry natural isomorphism
\mathsf{swap}_{A,B}=A\otimes B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\otimes A
. By strictness, we may write
A_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes A_{n}
and
A\otimes I=A=A\otimes I
. We will denote symmetric monoidal categories simply by their underlying category
\mathbb{X}
. For an in-depth introduction to symmetric monoidal categories and their internal comonoids, we refer the reader to
[
17
]
.

Definition 2.1

[
13
, Def 2.1]
A
Markov category
is a symmetric monoidal category
\mathbb{X}
equipped with two families of maps (indexed by objects
A
)
\mathsf{copy}_{A}:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A\otimes A
, called the
copy
or
comultiplication
, and
\mathsf{del}_{A}:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}I
, called the
delete
or
counit
, such that:

(i)

For each object
A
,
(A,\mathsf{copy}_{A},\mathsf{del}_{A})
is a
cocommutative comonoid
, that is, the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle(\mathsf{copy}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{A})\circ\mathsf{copy}_{A}=(\mathsf{id}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{copy}_{A})\circ\mathsf{copy}_{A}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{del}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{A})\circ\mathsf{copy}_{A}=\mathsf{id}_{A}=(\mathsf{id}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{del}_{A})\circ\mathsf{copy}_{A}

\displaystyle\mathsf{swap}_{A,A}\circ\mathsf{copy}_{A}=\mathsf{copy}_{A}

(ii)

\mathsf{copy}
and
\mathsf{del}
are coherent with the monoidal structure, that is, the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle\mathsf{copy}_{A\otimes B}=(\mathsf{id}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{swap}_{A,B}\otimes\mathsf{id}_{B})\circ(\mathsf{copy}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{copy}_{B})

\displaystyle\mathsf{del}_{A\otimes B}=\mathsf{del}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{del}_{B}

(iii)

\mathsf{del}
is a natural transformation, that is, for every map
f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
, the following equality holds:

\displaystyle\mathsf{del}_{B}\circ f=\mathsf{del}_{A}

Readers familiar with comonoids will note that asking for
\mathsf{copy}
and
\mathsf{del}
be coherent with the monoidal structure is precisely asking that the comonoid structure on

be the monoidal product of the comonoid
A
and

in the usual sense
[
17
, Lemma 4.8]
. Moreover, asking that

be a natural transformation is equivalent to saying that the monoidal unit

is a
terminal object

[
17
, Prop 4.15]
, which means that there is a unique map from every object into

. In a Markov categor, this unique map is precisely
\mathsf{del}_{A}
. It is again worth stressing that while maps preserve the counit, maps need not preserve the comultiplication, in other words, we do not ask that
\mathsf{copy}
be natural. Maps that do preserve the comultiplication are called
deterministic
.

Definition 2.2

[
13
, Def 10.1]
In a Markov category
\mathbb{X}
, a map
f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
is
deterministic
if the following equality holds:

\displaystyle\mathsf{copy}_{B}\circ f=(f\otimes f)\circ\mathsf{copy}_{A}

We denote the subcategory of deterministic maps by
\mathsf{DET}[\mathbb{X}]
.

The deterministic maps of a Markov category form a subcategory, and since
\mathsf{copy}
and
\mathsf{del}
are deterministic, the deterministic maps actually form a sub-Markov category
[
13
, Lemma 10.12]
. Moreover, the subcategory of deterministic maps is in fact a
Cartesian
monoidal category
[
13
, Remark 10.13]
, that is, a symmetric monoidal category whose monoidal product is an actual product and whose monoidal unit is a terminal object.

At this point, it may be useful to briefly give some probability theory interpretations for Markov categories – a more in-depth discussion of these interpretations can be found in
[
13
]
. Maps
f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
in a Markov category are interpreted as
Markov kernels
, so “a function with random outcomes,” basically means that
f
assigns to every input value of type
A
a probability distribution over output values of type
B
. Now
\mathsf{del}_{A}:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}I
simply discards the input and produces no output, while
\mathsf{copy}_{A}:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A\otimes A
takes in an input value and outputs two copies of the input, without introducing any randomness. In general, applying a Markov kernel
f
independently to two copies of an input is not equal to applying the Markov kernel first and then copying the result. Thus deterministic maps are those whose random outputs are independent of themselves, thus we can copy them.

A highly desirable property of a Markov category is having
conditionals
. As the name suggests, having conditionals formalizes the notion of conditional probability distributions, allowing for an interpretation of the famous Bayes’ theorem in a Markov category.

Dagger Additive Categories and their Matrix Representation

The main objective of this paper is to generalize the Markov category of Gaussian probability theory by replacing matrices with maps of a
dagger additive category
. So in this background section, we review dagger additive categories and, in particular, their matrix representation. For an in-depth introduction to dagger (additive) categories, we refer the reader to
[

]

Definition 3.1

[
17
, Def 2.34]
A
dagger
on a category
\mathbb{X}
is a contravariant functor
(\_)^{\dagger}:\mathbb{X}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathbb{X}
which is the identity on objects and involutive. More concretely, a dagger
\dagger
associates each map
{f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B}
to a chosen map of dual type
f^{\dagger}:B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
, called the
adjoint
of
f
, such that the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle\mathsf{id}_{A}^{\dagger}=\mathsf{id}_{A}

\displaystyle(g\circ f)^{\dagger}=f^{\dagger}\circ g^{\dagger}

\displaystyle(f^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=f

(3)

A
dagger category
is a pair
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
consisting of a category
\mathbb{X}
equipped with a dagger
\dagger
.

It is important to note that a category can have multiple different daggers. This means that a dagger on a category is structure which must be chosen. Oftentimes, when it is clear from context, dagger categories are referred to by their underlying category. However in this paper, we will encounter categories with multiple daggers, so we will always write dagger categories as pairs. This will be particularly important in Sec
6
, where a category can have Moore-Penrose inverses with respect to one dagger but not with respect to another.

We now add additive structure to our dagger categories. First recall that a

[
3
, Def 1.2.1]

\mathbb{X}
such that each homset

, and inverse
-:\mathbb{X}(A,B)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathbb{X}(A,B)
, such that composition is a group morphism, that is, the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle g\circ(f_{1}+f_{2})\circ h=g\circ f_{1}\circ h+g\circ f_{2}\circ h

\displaystyle g\circ 0\circ f=0

For dagger categories, we also ask that the dagger be a group homomorphism.

Definition 3.2

A
dagger pre-additive category
is a dagger category
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
such that
\mathbb{X}
is a pre-additive category, where additionally
\dagger
is a group homomorphism, that is, the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle(f+g)^{\dagger}=f^{\dagger}+g^{\dagger}

\displaystyle 0^{\dagger}=0

\displaystyle(-f)^{\dagger}=-f^{\dagger}

We can also add biproducts to our dagger categories. First recall that a
zero object

[
3
, Def 1.1.1]
in a category is an object that is both terminal and initial. In a pre-additive category, a zero object can equivalently be defined as an object
\mathsf{0}
such that for every object
A
, the zero maps
0:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathsf{0}
and
0:\mathsf{0}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
are the unique maps to and from
\mathsf{0}

[
3
, Prop 1.2.3]
. Next recall that a
biproduct
is both a product and coproduct satisfying certain compatibility relations. In a pre-additive category, the
biproduct

[
3
, Def 1.1.1]
of a finite family of objects
A_{1}
,…,
A_{n}
, is an object
A_{1}\oplus\ldots A_{n}
with maps (for all
1\leq j\leq n
)
\pi_{j}:A_{1}\oplus\ldots A_{n}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A_{j}
, called the
projections
, and
\iota_{j}:A_{j}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A_{1}\oplus\ldots A_{n}
, called the
injections
, such that the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle\pi_{j}\circ\iota_{i}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{id}_{A_{j}}&\text{if $i=j$}\\
0&\text{if $i\neq j$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\iota_{1}\circ\pi_{1}+\ldots+\iota_{n}\circ\pi_{n}=\mathsf{id}_{A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{n}}

The biproduct of the empty family, if it exists, is precisely a zero object. Then an
additive category

[
3
, Def 1.2.6]
is a pre-additive category with all finite biproducts. For dagger categories,
dagger biproducts
are biproducts whose projections and injections are adjoints of each other.

Definition 3.3

[
17
, Def 2.39]
A
dagger additive category
is a dagger pre-additive category
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
which has all finite
\dagger
-biproducts
, that is, it has all finite biproducts such that the following equality holds (for all finite families of objects and all
j\in\mathbb{N}
):

\displaystyle\pi_{j}^{\dagger}=\iota_{j}

There are many interesting examples of dagger additive categories, such as the category of Hilbert spaces
[
17
, Def 2.31]
or the category of sets and relations
[
17
, Def 2.33]
. Our main examples of interest in this paper are the dagger additive categories of matrices over a ring.

Example 3.4

Let
R
be a (unital and associative, but not necessarily commutative) ring. Define
\mathsf{MAT}(R)
to be the category of
R
-matrices, that is, the category whose objects are the natural numbers
n\in\mathbb{N}
and where a map
{A:n\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}m}
is an
m\times n

R
-matrix
A
, whose coefficients we denote by
A(i,j)
for
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq n
. The identity map of
n
is the
n\times n
identity matrix, while composition is given by matrix multiplication, so
A\circ B=AB
. For an
m\times n
matrix
A
(which is a map of type
n\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}m)
, let
A^{\mathsf{T}}
be its transpose, that is, the
n\times m
matrix (so a map of dual type
m\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}n
) given by
A^{\mathsf{T}}(i,j)=A(j,i)
. Then this makes
(\mathsf{MAT}(R),\mathsf{T})
into a dagger category. Moreover,
(\mathsf{MAT}(R),\mathsf{T})
is a dagger additive category where the sum of maps is given by the usual sum of matrices,
(A+B)(i,j)=A(i,j)+B(i,j)
, the zero map is the zero matrix,
0(i,j)=0
, the negative is the usual negation of a matrix,
(-A)(i,j)=-A(i,j)
, and where the biproduct on objects is given by taking the sum,
n_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus n_{m}=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{m}
. Our main example will be when
R=\mathbb{R}
, the field of real numbers, giving us the dagger category of real matrices
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{R}),\mathsf{T})
.

If the base ring is an involutive ring, then its category of matrices admits another dagger given by conjugate transpose.

Example 3.5

Recall that an involutive ring is a ring
R
equipped with a unary operation
\overline{(\_)}:R\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}R
, called the involution, such that
\overline{x+y}=\overline{x}+\overline{y}
,
\overline{1}=1
,
\overline{xy}=\overline{y}~\overline{x}
, and
\overline{\overline{x}}=x
. Given an
m\times n

R
-matrix
A
, let
A^{\ast}
denote its conjugate transpose, that is, the
n\times m
matrix given by
A^{\ast}(i,j)=\overline{A(j,i)}
. This makes
(\mathsf{MAT}(R),\ast)
into a dagger category. Moreover,
(\mathsf{MAT}(R),\ast)
is also dagger additive category with the same additive structure as in the above example. Our two main examples will be when
R=\mathbb{C}
, the field of complex numbers, whose involution is given by complex conjugation, giving us the dagger of complex matrices
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{C}),\ast)
, or when
R=\mathbb{H}
, the skew field of quaternions, whose involution is given by quaternionic conjugation, giving us the dagger of qauternionic matrices
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{H}),\ast)
.

A very practical fact about additive categories is that maps have
matrix representation

[
17
, Sec 2.2.4]
, which comes from the fact that a biproduct admits the universal properties of both a product and coproduct. In particular, this matrix representation implies that composition in an additive category is essentially given by matrix multiplication. So in an additive category, every map
f:A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{n}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus B_{m}
is uniquely determined by a family of maps
f(i,j):A_{j}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B_{i}
, defined below on the left, such that the equality on the right holds:

\displaystyle f(i,j)=\pi_{i}\circ f\circ\iota_{j}

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i,j}\iota_{i}\circ f(i,j)\circ\pi_{j}

Therefore
f
can be represented as a
m\times n
matrix
[
17
, Lemma 2.26]
where the term in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column is
f(i,j)
, so:

\displaystyle f=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f(1,1)&f(1,2)&\ldots&f(1,n)\\
f(2,1)&f(2,2)&\ldots&f(2,n)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f(m,1)&f(m,2)&\ldots&f(m,n)\end{smallmatrix}\right]

Moreover, two maps in an additive category are equal if and only if they have the same matrix representation
[
17
, Cor 2.27]
. Now given composable maps
f:\bigoplus\limits^{n}_{i=1}A_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bigoplus\limits^{m}_{j=1}B_{j}
and
g:\bigoplus\limits^{m}_{j=1}B_{j}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bigoplus\limits^{p}_{k=1}C_{k}
, the matrix representation of their composition
g\circ f:\bigoplus\limits^{n}_{i=1}A_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bigoplus\limits^{p}_{k=1}C_{k}
is given by matrix multiplication
[
17
, Prop 2.28]
,

\displaystyle g\circ f=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}g(k,l)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\sum\limits^{n}_{j=1}g(j,k)\circ f(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]

while the matrix representation of the identity map
\mathsf{id}_{A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{m}}
is precisely the identity matrix, that is, with identities on the diagonal and zero everywhere else:

\displaystyle\mathsf{id}_{A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{m}}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathsf{id}_{A_{1}}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\mathsf{id}_{A_{2}}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&\mathsf{id}_{A_{m}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]

Now for maps
f:\bigoplus\limits^{n}_{i=1}A_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bigoplus\limits^{m}_{j=1}B_{j}
and
g:\bigoplus\limits^{n}_{i=1}A_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bigoplus\limits^{m}_{j=1}B_{j}
, the matrix representation of their sum
f+g
is given by the usual sum of matrices, so by adding coefficients point-wise, the matrix representation of the negation
-f
is also by taking the negation pointwise, while the matrix representation of the zero map
0:\bigoplus\limits^{n}_{i=1}A_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bigoplus\limits^{m}_{j=1}B_{j}
is of course simply the zero matrix:

\displaystyle f+g=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]+\left[\begin{smallmatrix}g(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f(i,j)+g(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle-f=-\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}-f(i,j)\end{smallmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle 0=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]

In a dagger additive category, for a map
f:A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{m}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus B_{n}
, the matrix representation of its adjoints
f^{\dagger}:B_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus B_{n}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{m}
is the conjugate transpose of the matrix representation of
f

[
17
, Lemma 2.41]
:

\displaystyle f^{\dagger}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f(1,1)^{\dagger}&f(2,1)^{\dagger}&\ldots&f(n,1)^{\dagger}\\
f(1,2)^{\dagger}&f(2,2)^{\dagger}&\ldots&f(n,2)^{\dagger}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f(1,m)^{\dagger}&f(2,m)^{\dagger}&\ldots&f(n,m)^{\dagger}\end{smallmatrix}\right]

Example 3.6

For any ring
R
, notice that in
\mathsf{MAT}(R)
, every object
n
is the biproduct of
n
-copies of
1
, that is,
n=1\oplus\ldots\oplus 1
. Then for a map
A:n\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}m
, which is an
m\times n
matrix
A
, its matrix representation in the additive category sense will be precisely
A
, where in particular
\pi_{i}\circ A\circ\iota_{j}
is precisely
A(i,j)
, the coefficient of
A
in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column, seen as a map
1\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}1
(which is just an element of
R
seen as a
1\times 1
matrix).

4
The Gauss Construction

In this section, we introduce the
Gauss construction
on a dagger additive category which produces a Markov category. The Gauss construction generalizes the Gaussian probability theory example in the sense that when applying the Gauss construction to the category of real matrices, we recover the Markov category
\mathsf{Gauss}
from Ex
2.4
.

Before we jump into giving the definition of the Gauss construction, we need to discuss what the maps might look like. Taking
\mathsf{Gauss}
as our guiding light, it is clear that the objects in the Gauss construction will be the same as the objects of our base dagger additive category, while maps in the Gauss construction will be triples. Now the first component of the triple will be a map in the base category. So we need to address the other two components. In
\mathsf{Gauss}
, recall that the second component was a positive semidefinite square matrix. Now square matrices will correspond to endomorphisms, but what about positive semidefiniteness? It turns out that positive semidefiniteness is captured in a dagger category via the notion of a
positive
map, which is an endomorphism that is the composite of another map and its adjoints.

Definition 4.1

[
17
, Def 2.34]
In a dagger category
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
, a
\dagger
-positive map
is an endomorphism
p:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
such that there exists a map
\phi:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
satisfying
p=\phi^{\dagger}\circ\phi
.

It is important to stress that for a
\dagger
-positive map
p
there may be distinct maps
\phi
such that
p=\phi^{\dagger}\circ\phi
.

Example 4.2

Recall the well-known fact that a real square
n\times n
matrix
A
is positive semidefinite if and only if there exists a real (resp. complex)
m\times n
matrix
B
such that
A=B^{\mathsf{T}}B
. Therefore, in
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{R}),\mathsf{T})
, the
\mathsf{T}
-positive maps correspond precisely to real (resp. complex) positive semidefinite square matrices. The same is true for complex (resp. quaternionic) matrices, thus
\ast
-positive maps in
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{C}),\ast)
(resp.
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{H}),\ast)
), meaning this time that
A=B^{\ast}B
, correspond precisely to complex (resp. quaternionic) positive semidefinite square matrices.

Recall that the third component of a map in
\mathsf{Gauss}
was a column vector, which we may view as a map of type
1\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}m
in our category of matrices. While
1
is indeed a special object in our category of matrices, an arbitrary dagger additive category does not necessarily have such an object. Instead, we need to fix an object of our base category and take maps out of this object for our third components of maps in the Gauss construction.

We are now in a position to give our Gauss construction. So for the remainder of this section, let
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
be a dagger additive category and fix an object
X\in\mathbb{X}
. Then define
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
to be the category where:

(i)

The objects of
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
are the same objects of
\mathbb{X}
;

(ii)

A map
F:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is a triple
F=(f,p,x):A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
consisting of a map
f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
, a
\dagger
-positive map
p:B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
, and a map
x:X\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
;

(iii)

The identity of
A
in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is the triple
\mathsf{Id}_{A}=(\mathsf{id}_{A},0,0):A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
;

(iv)

Composition of
F=(f,p,x):A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
and
G=(g,q,y):B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}C
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle G\circ F=(g,q,y)\circ(f,p,x)=(g\circ f,q+g\circ p\circ g^{\dagger},y+g\circ x):A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}C

Lemma 4.3

\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is category.

Proof:
We first need to explain why composition and identities are well-defined, in particular, we need to address the second component and explain why it is
\dagger
-positive. Now clearly every zero map
0
is
\dagger
-positive since
0=0^{\dagger}\circ 0
, and so identity maps are well-defined in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
. Now for composition, we need to justify why the sum of
\dagger
-positive maps is again
\dagger
-positive. In a general dagger pre-additive category, this need not be the case! That said, in a dagger additive category, dagger biproducts come to the rescue, and in this case the sum of
\dagger
-positive maps is indeed
\dagger
-positive. To see this, consider two
\dagger
-positive maps
\phi:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
and
\psi:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
, where
\phi=\alpha^{\dagger}\circ\alpha
for some
\alpha:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
and
\psi=\beta^{\dagger}\circ\beta
for some
{\beta:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}C}
. So consider the map
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\oplus C
, and we compute that:

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{\dagger}\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{\dagger}&\beta^{\dagger}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\alpha^{\dagger}\circ\alpha+\beta^{\dagger}\circ\beta=\phi+\psi

So in the presence of
\dagger
-biproducts, the sum of
\dagger
-positive maps is again
\dagger
-positive. As such, composition in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is well-defined. Now it is easy to see that composition in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is associative and unital since composition and addition is associative and unital in
\mathbb{X}
,
\dagger
is a contravariant functor, and also that composition and
\dagger
preserves the additive structure. So we conclude that
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is indeed a category.

\Box

Next we define a monoidal structure on
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
. To do so, recall that every additive category is a symmetric monoidal category whose monoidal structure is given its finite biproduct. Explicitly, the monoidal product is the biproduct
\oplus
, which is defined on object as simply
A\oplus B
, while for maps
f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
and
g:C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
, their biproduct
f\oplus g:A\oplus C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\oplus D
is expressed as a diagonal matrix:

f\oplus g=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f&0\\
0&g\end{smallmatrix}\right]

The monoidal unit is the zero object
\mathsf{0}
, while the natural symmetry isomorphism
\mathsf{swap}^{\oplus}_{A,B}:A\oplus B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\oplus A
is the
2\times 2
permutation swap matrix:

\mathsf{swap}^{\oplus}_{A,B}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\mathsf{id}_{B}\\
\mathsf{id}_{A}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]

With this we can now define a symmetric monoidal structure on
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
. So define the monoidal product
\otimes
on objects as
A\otimes B=A\oplus B
and defined on maps
F=(f,p,s):A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
and
G=(g,q,t):C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
as follows:

\displaystyle F\otimes G

\displaystyle=~(f,p,x)\otimes(g,q,y)=(f\oplus g,p\oplus q,\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right])

\displaystyle=~\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f&0\\
0&g\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}p&0\\
0&q\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right):A\otimes B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}C\otimes D

The monoidal unit will be the zero object of
\mathbb{X}
,
I=\mathsf{0}
. The natural symmetry isomorphism
\mathsf{swap}_{A,B}:A\otimes B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\otimes A
is defined as the triple:

\displaystyle\mathsf{swap}_{A,B}=\left(\mathsf{swap}^{\oplus}_{A,B},0,0\right)=\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\mathsf{id}_{B}\\
\mathsf{id}_{A}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right):A\otimes B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\otimes A

Lemma 4.4

\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is a symmetric monoidal category.

Proof:
We first need to explain why the monoidal product of maps is well-defined. This follows from the fact that the
\dagger
-biproduct of
\dagger
-positive maps is indeed
\dagger
-positive. To see this, consider
\dagger
-positive maps
p:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
and
q:C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
, with
p=\alpha^{\dagger}\circ\alpha
for some map
\alpha:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}W
and
q=\beta^{\dagger}\circ\beta
for some map
\beta:C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}V
. Then consider the map
\alpha\oplus\beta:A\oplus C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}W\oplus V
. We easily see that:

\displaystyle(\alpha\oplus\beta)^{\dagger}\circ(\alpha\oplus\beta)

\displaystyle=~\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{\dagger}\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{\dagger}&0\\
0&\beta^{\dagger}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=~\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{\dagger}\circ\alpha&0\\
0&\beta^{\dagger}\circ\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}p&0\\
0&q\end{smallmatrix}\right]=p\oplus q

So
p\oplus q
is
\dagger
-positive. As such, the monoidal product in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is well-defined. Clearly, the natural symmetry isomorphism is also well-defined, so the symmetric monoidal structure on
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is well-defined.

Next we need to prove that
\otimes
is functorial. Since
\mathsf{id}_{A\oplus B}=\mathsf{id}_{A}\oplus\mathsf{id}_{B}
, it is straightforward to see that
\mathsf{Id}_{A\otimes B}=\mathsf{Id}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{Id}_{B}
. To show that
\otimes
also preserves composition, we will need the following identities that hold in any dagger additive category:

\displaystyle(g\oplus k)\circ(f\oplus h)=(g\circ f)\oplus(k\circ h)

\displaystyle(f\oplus g)^{\dagger}=f^{\dagger}\oplus g^{\dagger}

\displaystyle(g\oplus f)+(k\oplus h)=(g+k)\oplus(f+h)

\displaystyle(f\oplus g)\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}h\\
k\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f\circ h\\
g\circ k\end{smallmatrix}\right]

With these identities, we compute that:

\displaystyle(G\otimes K)\circ(F\otimes H)=\left((g,q,y)\otimes(k,u,w)\right)\circ\left((f,p,x)\otimes(h,r,z)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(g\oplus k,q\oplus u,\left[\begin{smallmatrix}y\\
w\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)\circ\left(f\oplus h,p\oplus r,\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
z\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\hbox{\resizebox{}{}{{\hbox{{$\displaystyle\left((g\oplus k)\circ(f\oplus h),(q\oplus u)+(g\oplus k)\circ(p\oplus r)\circ(g\oplus k)^{\dagger},\left[\begin{smallmatrix}y\\
w\end{smallmatrix}\right]+(g\oplus k)\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
z\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)$}}}}}

\displaystyle=\hbox{\resizebox{}{}{{\hbox{{$\displaystyle\left((g\oplus k)\circ(f\oplus h),(q\oplus u)+(g\oplus k)\circ(p\oplus r)\circ(g^{\dagger}\oplus k^{\dagger}),\left[\begin{smallmatrix}y\\
w\end{smallmatrix}\right]+\left[\begin{smallmatrix}g\circ x\\
k\circ z\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)$}}}}}

[
16
, Def 2.3]

\mathcal{F}(-^{\dagger})=\mathcal{F}(-)^{\dagger}
, and is an
additive functor

[
3
, Prop 1.3.4]
, which means it preserves finite biproducts (up to canonical isomorphism)
[
17
, Def 2.22]
or equivalently that it preserves the group structure of the homsets
[

Markov functor

[

[

and on maps as

.

Lemma 4.14

If
\mathcal{F}:(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}(\mathbb{Y},\dagger)
is a dagger additive functor, then for any object
X\in\mathbb{X}
,
\mathfrak{G}[\mathcal{F}]_{X}:\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{\mathcal{F}(X)}
is a Markov functor.

It is straightforward to see that the Gauss construction then gives a
(2-)
functor from the (2-)category of dagger additive categories with a chosen object to the (2-)category of Markov categories. Now if we wish to do away from having to choose an object, since dagger additive functors preserve zero objects (up to isomorphism) and zero maps, it follows that if
\mathcal{F}:(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}(\mathbb{Y},\dagger)
is a dagger additive functor, then
\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{F}]:\mathfrak{P}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathfrak{P}\left[(\mathbb{Y},\dagger)\right]
is a Markov functor, which is defined on objects as
\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{F}](A)=\mathcal{F}(A)
and on maps as
\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{F}](f,p)=\left(\mathcal{F}(f),\mathcal{F}(p)\right)
. Therefore, the
\mathfrak{P}
construction gives a (2-)functor from the actual (2-)category of dagger additive categories to the (2-)category of Markov categories.

5
Conditionals in the Gauss Construction

In this section, we characterize all possible conditionals in the Gauss construction and show that they are fully determined by a map in the base category which is compatible with the positive component of maps in the Gauss construction. We call such a map a
conditional generator
, and we will show that conditionals in the Gauss construction are in bijective correspondence with conditional generators in the base category.

Throughout this section, let
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
be a dagger additive category and fix an object

. Consider first a map in

. Using matrix representation,

\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}s\\
t\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)

On the other hand, a potential conditional of

G:B\otimes A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}C

Definition 5.2

Given a
\dagger
-positive map
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\

(ii)

\delta-m\circ\beta
is
\dagger
-positive.

Lemma 5.3

Let
F=\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f\\

\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Then define the map

in

\displaystyle G_{m}=\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}m&g-m\circ f\end{smallmatrix}\right],\delta-m\circ\beta,t-m\circ s\right)

Then

is a conditional of

Since

is a conditional generator,
\delta-m\circ\beta
is

is a well-defined map in

is

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Id}_{B}\otimes G_{m})\circ(\mathsf{copy}_{B}\otimes\mathsf{Id}_{A})\circ(\mathsf{Id}_{B}\otimes\mathsf{del}_{C}\otimes\mathsf{Id}_{A})\circ(F\otimes\mathsf{Id}_{A})\circ\mathsf{copy}_{A}

Theorem 5.4

For a map
F=\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f\\
g\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}s\\
t\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right):A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\otimes C
in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}

F
are in bijective correspondence with conditional generators of
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Explicitly:

(i)

If
G=\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}m&k\end{smallmatrix}\right],\eta,u\right)
is a conditional of
F
then
m
is a conditional generator of
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
;

(ii)

If
m
is a conditional generator of
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, then
G_{m}
as defined in (
5
) is a conditional of
F
;

and these constructions are inverses of each other.

Proof:
Lemma
5.3
tells us precisely how to construct conditionals from conditional generators. Now we prove the converse direction. If
F
has a conditional

, then by Lemma
5.1
, this implies that

and
\eta=\delta-m\circ\beta

\dagger

\dagger

Therefore a map

in the Gauss construction has a conditional if and only if its

6
Moore-Penrose Inverses

The Markov category
\mathsf{Gauss}
has conditionals; where a canonical choice of conditional can be constructed using the
Moore-Penrose inverse
. In this section, we generalize this result to the setting of
Moore-Penrose dagger categories
showing that the Gauss construction applied to a Moore-Penrose dagger additive category is a Markov category with conditionals, which is the main result of this paper.

Let us begin by reviewing Moore-Penrose inverses in a dagger category, which is a special kind of
generalized inverse
for maps. For a more in-depth introduction to Moore-Penrose dagger (additive) categories, we invite the reader to see
[
6
,
11
]
.

Definition 6.1

[
6
, Def 2.3]
In a dagger category
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
, a
Moore-Penrose inverse
of a map
f:A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B
is a map of dual type
f^{\circ}:B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}A
such that the following equalities hold:

[MP.1]

f\circ f^{\circ}\circ f=f

[MP.2]

f^{\circ}\circ f\circ f^{\circ}=f^{\circ}

[MP.3]

\bigl(f\circ f^{\circ}\bigr)^{\dagger}=f\circ f^{\circ}

[MP.4]

\bigl(f^{\circ}\circ f\bigr)^{\dagger}=f^{\circ}\circ f

If
f
has a Moore-Penrose inverse, we say that
f
is
Moore-Penrose invertible
or simply
Moore-Penrose
. A
Moore-Penrose dagger (additive) category
is a dagger (additive) category such that every map is Moore-Penrose.

In arbitrary dagger category, Moore-Penrose inverses need not necessary exist, but if a Moore-Penrose inverse does exists, then it is
unique

[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
, so we may speak of
the
Moore-Penrose inverse of a map. Thus for a dagger category, being Moore-Penrose is a property rather than structure.

Example 6.2

It is well-known that complex matrices and real matrices have Moore-Penrose inverses. Indeed, given a
n\times m
complex matrix
A
, there exists a unique complex matrix
A^{\circ}
of size
m\times n
such that
AA^{\circ}A=A
,
A^{\circ}AA^{\circ}=A^{\circ}
,
(AA^{\circ})^{\ast}=AA^{\circ}
, and
(A^{\circ}A)^{\ast}=A^{\circ}A
. When
A
is a real matrix, then its Moore-Penrose inverse
A^{\circ}
will also be a real matrix, however this time the last two identities can instead be written as
(AA^{\circ})^{\mathsf{T}}=AA^{\circ}
and
(A^{\circ}A)^{\mathsf{T}}=A^{\circ}A
. See
[
4
]
for various ways of computing the Moore-Penrose inverse. Therefore it follows that
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{C}),\ast)
and
(\mathsf{MAT}(\mathbb{R}),\mathsf{T})

We now turn towards proving our main objective that we can use Moore-Penrose inverses to always construct conditionals in the Gauss construction. Thanks to Theorem

Lemma 6.3

In a Moore-Penrose dagger additive category
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
, a map
m:B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}C
is a conditional generator of a
\dagger
-positive map
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]:B\oplus C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\oplus C
if and only if
m\circ\alpha=\beta^{\dagger}
.

Proof:
The
\Rightarrow
direction is immediate by definition. For the
\Leftarrow
direction, suppose that
m\circ\alpha=\beta^{\dagger}
. Then to show that
m
is a conditional generator we need to show that
\delta-m\circ\beta
is
\dagger
-positive. To do so, first recall that as explained in the previous section, since
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is
\dagger
-positive, this implies there exists a map
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi&\psi\end{smallmatrix}\right]:B\oplus C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
, together with maps
\phi:B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
and
\psi:C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
, such that the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle\alpha=\phi^{\dagger}\circ\phi

\displaystyle\delta=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\psi

\displaystyle\beta^{\dagger}=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi

\displaystyle\beta=\phi^{\dagger}\circ\psi

(6)

Now as we are in a Moore-Penrose dagger category, we also note that by
[
6
, Lemma 2.7.(v)]
, the following equality also holds:

\displaystyle\alpha^{\circ}=\phi^{\circ}\circ{\phi^{\circ}}^{\dagger}

(7)

which will be key to showing that
\delta-m\circ\beta
is
\dagger
-positive. Now since
\alpha
is
\dagger
-positive and
m\circ\alpha=\beta^{\dagger}
, we also get that
\alpha\circ m^{\dagger}=\beta
. Then using the above identity relating the Moore-Penrose inverses of
\alpha
and
\phi
, we compute:

\displaystyle m\circ\beta

\displaystyle=m\circ\alpha\circ m^{\dagger}=m\circ\alpha\circ\alpha^{\circ}\circ\alpha\circ m^{\dagger}=\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{\circ}\circ\beta

\displaystyle=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ{\phi^{\circ}}^{\dagger}\circ\phi^{\dagger}\circ\psi=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ(\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})^{\dagger}\circ\psi

\displaystyle=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ\psi=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ\psi

Thus
m\circ\beta=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ\psi
. Now consider the map
\chi:=(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})\circ\psi
. Then we compute:

\displaystyle\chi^{\dagger}\circ\chi

\displaystyle=~\left((\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})\circ\psi\right)^{\dagger}\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})^{\dagger}\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}^{\dagger}_{B}-(\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})^{\dagger}\right)\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\right)\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}+\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\right)\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}+\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\right)\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\right)\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\psi-\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\delta-m\circ\beta

So
\delta-m\circ\beta
is
\dagger
-positive and thus
m
is a conditional generator.

\Box

m=\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{\circ}

By Lemma
6.3

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi&\psi\end{smallmatrix}\right]:B\oplus C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D

\phi:B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D

Theorem 6.5

Given a Moore-Penrose dagger additive category
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
and any object
X\in\mathbb{X}

\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
is a Markov category with conditionals. In particular, given a map

Lemma 7.2

Let
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]:B\oplus C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B\oplus C
be a
\dagger
-positive map in
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
such that there is a map
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi&\psi\end{smallmatrix}\right]:B\oplus C\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
such that
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi&\psi\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{\dagger}\circ\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi&\psi\end{smallmatrix}\right]
for which
\phi:B\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}D
has a
[MP.1,3]
-inverse
\phi^{\bullet}:D\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.09988pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B

m=\psi^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\bullet}}^{\dagger}
is a conditional generator of
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]

Proof:
The computations in this proof are similar to those in the proof of Lemma
6.3
but there are some subtle differences. Now by assumption, recall that
\phi
and
\psi
satisfy (
6

\phi^{\bullet}
being a
[MP.1,3]
-inverse of
\phi
implies that is satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\phi=\phi

\displaystyle(\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})^{\dagger}=\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}

We need to show that
m=\psi^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\bullet}}^{\dagger}
satisfies the two necessary properties to be a conditional generator. We first compute that:

\displaystyle m\circ\alpha=\psi^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\bullet}}^{\dagger}\circ\phi^{\dagger}\circ\phi=\psi^{\dagger}\circ(\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})^{\dagger}\circ\phi=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\phi=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi=\beta^{\dagger}

So
m\circ\alpha=\beta^{\dagger}
as desired. Next we need to show that
\delta-m\circ\beta
is
\dagger

m\circ\alpha=\beta^{\dagger}

\displaystyle m\circ\beta

\displaystyle=m\circ\alpha\circ m^{\dagger}=m\circ\phi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ m^{\dagger}=\psi^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\bullet}}^{\dagger}\circ\phi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\psi

\displaystyle=\psi^{\dagger}\circ(\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\psi\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\psi=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\psi

m\circ\beta=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\psi

\zeta=(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})\circ\psi

\displaystyle\zeta^{\dagger}\circ\zeta

\displaystyle=~\left((\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})\circ\psi\right)^{\dagger}\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})^{\dagger}\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}^{\dagger}_{B}-(\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})^{\dagger}\right)\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\right)\circ(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet})\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}+\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\right)\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}+\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\right)\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\left(\mathsf{id}_{B}-\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\right)\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\psi^{\dagger}\circ\psi-\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\bullet}\circ\psi

\displaystyle=~\delta-m\circ\beta

So

is indeed

Proposition 7.3

If every map in

Of course every map in a Moore-Penrose dagger additive category admits a
[MP.1,3]
-inverse, namely the Moore-Penrose inverse. However, it is important to again stress that a map could have a

Lemma 7.4

Suppose that
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
is Moore-Penrose. Let
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\

be a
\dagger
-positive map in
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)

\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{\circ}=\psi^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\circ}}^{\dagger}

Proof:
Using
[MP.2]
, (
6

\displaystyle\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\psi^{\dagger}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ}\circ{\phi^{\circ}}^{\dagger}=\psi^{\dagger}\circ(\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\circ}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\psi^{\dagger}\circ(\phi^{\circ}\circ\phi\circ\phi^{\circ})^{\dagger}=\psi^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\circ}}^{\dagger}

Thus
\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{\circ}=\psi^{\dagger}\circ{\phi^{\circ}}^{\dagger}

\Box

We have made a big deal to emphasize that conditional generators and conditionals are not unique in general; we conclude this paper with a class of suitable positive maps which have a unique conditional generator.

Lemma 7.5

Take a Moore-Penrose dagger category
(\mathbb{X},\dagger)
with a †-positive map
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
such that
\alpha
is an isomorphism. Then
\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{-1}
is its unique conditional generator. Therefore, for every object
X\in\mathbb{X}
, if
F=\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}f\\
g\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\dagger}&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}s\\
t\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
is a map in
\mathfrak{G}\left[(\mathbb{X},\dagger)\right]_{X}
, then
F|_{B}
as defined in (
8
) is its unique conditional.

Proof:
Recall that in a Moore-Penrose dagger category, the Moore-Penrose inverse of an isomorphism is its inverse
[
6
, Lemma 2.7.(ii)]
. Thus since
\alpha
is an isomorphism, we have that
\alpha^{\circ}=\alpha^{-1}
. So by Lemma
6.3
, we know that
\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{-1}
is a conditional generator. Now if we had another conditional generator

, then by definition we have that

. However since
\alpha
is an isomorphism, we get that
m=\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{-1}
, and so we conclude that
\beta^{\dagger}\circ\alpha^{-1}
is the unique conditional generator. The rest of the statement then follows as well.

\Box
