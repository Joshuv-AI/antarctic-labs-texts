Catalytic lactate-regulatory nanosystems attenuate cellular senescence for atherosclerosis amelioration
Authors: Chang Wang, Chunmei Wang, Shaozhen Wu, Lanhao Shi, Chen Dai, Ruizhi Hu, Yu Chen, Ruifang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endothelial senescence is a pivotal driver in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (AS), a process accompanied by metabolic reprogramming and increased lactate (LA) production. The accumulated LA stimulates reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, establishing a self-perpetuating inflammation-senescence feedback loop that accelerates disease progression. Therefore, depleting LA to inhibit cellular senescence represents a promising therapeutic strategy against AS. Here, we successfully fabricated a two-dimensional multi-enzyme-mimicking nanozyme (SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme) by incorporating tannic acid-cerium (TA-Ce) frameworks onto the surface of SnSe nanozyme. Leveraging the anti-inflammatory/antioxidative properties of TA-Ce, the SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme effectively alleviates oxidative stress and protects intracellular DNA from ROS damage. Endowed with lactate dehydrogenase-like activity, SnSe nanozyme converts LA to pyruvate, synergistically exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-senescence effects in endothelial cells while blocking endothelial-mesenchymal transition and restoring endothelial barrier function. In vitro and in vivo results confirm that the combination of anti-inflammatory/antioxidant therapy and modulation of LA levels effectively reduces plaque size and alleviates its pro-inflammatory environment. This study presents a pathology-targeted SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme for treating AS, proposing a new paradigm of SnSe-based therapeutics for other age-related diseases.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS), an inflammatory disease characterized by lipid accumulation and plaque formation, could cause clinical complications, such as myocardial infarction and stroke [[1], [2], [3]]. Cellular senescence is a stress response characterized by permanent growth arrest, which is associated with aging and accompanied by the secretion of biologically active molecules, collectively referred to as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Recent evidence linking senescent endothelial cells to AS has shown that these cells are critical drivers of atheroma formation and play a detrimental role throughout the pathogenesis of AS [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. The accumulation of senescent endothelial cells can result in oxidative stress and inflammation at plaque lesions, driving pathology by elevating the expression of key inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which further impair endothelial function, stiffen arteries, induce tissue remodeling and eventually increase risks of diseases [17,18]. The development of models/drugs that can selectively destroy the senescent cells offered additional evidence that senescent cells could serve as a viable therapeutic target [19,20]. Therefore, precise regulation of endothelial cellular senescence represents a promising strategy for treating AS. Senescent endothelial cells are highly secretory and pro-inflammatory [13,[21], [22], [23]]. However, traditional anti-atherosclerotic drugs with antioxidant/anti-inflammatory properties have shown limited efficacy and notable side effects. For example, statins are frequently associated with muscular adverse events, ranging from mild myalgia to rare but severe rhabdomyolysis. This is largely due to the complex molecular and cellular interactions involved in AS [[24], [25], [26]].

Within atherosclerotic plaque, endothelial cells exhibit significant metabolic reprogramming, characterized by heightened glycolytic activity. This increase in glycolysis results in elevated lactate (LA) production, which is closely linked to various pathological processes associated with AS, such as inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and cellular senescence [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Moreover, the acidic microenvironment induced by LA can lead to the loss of endothelial markers and trigger the endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT). This transition facilitates the recruitment and infiltration of inflammation-related immune cells into the arterial intima [31]. EndMT, which often occurs in atherosclerosis-prone regions of arteries, has been identified as a key driver of endothelial dysfunction and AS [32]. Importantly, LA can induce the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via the NOX1 pathway, driving the endothelial senescence-associated secretory phenotype, which exacerbates endothelial cell dysfunction [29]. These senescent cells exhibit increased aerobic glycolysis and enhanced LA generation at plaques while maintaining redox activity and mitochondrial respiration. This metabolic shift represents a unique form of reprogramming during the progression of AS. Therefore, depleting LA to inhibit cellular senescence holds promise as an effective strategy for mitigating the progression of AS.

While natural enzymes such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) hold potential for metabolic intervention, their clinical translation is severely hampered by high cost, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, and rapid loss of activity under variable pH and temperature conditions [33,34]. Nanozymes are nanomaterials that mimic enzyme catalytic activity and provide a robust alternative with superior stability, cost-effectiveness, and resilience under harsh physiological conditions compared to biological enzymes [35,36]. SnSe effectively mimics LDH activity, catalyzing the oxidation of LA to pyruvate (PA) with an efficiency comparable to natural enzymes.

Metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) are formed by the coordination of multivalent metal ions with polyphenols [37]. Tannic acid (TA) is widely employed in MPN construction owing to its potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [38]. Rich in aromatic rings and phenolic hydroxyl groups, TA possesses high-density π-electron clouds that enable π–π stacking interactions with two-dimensional (2D) material surfaces. These phenolic moieties collectively facilitate initial adsorption and interfacial stabilization through diverse non-covalent forces [37,39]. Subsequent introduction of metal ions triggers the formation of robust metal–phenol coordination bonds, thereby establishing stable MPN architectures on the 2D material surfaces. Compared to other metal ions, cerium exhibits exceptional coordination flexibility and favorable biocompatibility. The reversible interconversion between trivalent (Ce3+) and tetravalent (Ce4+) oxidation states confers Ce with multienzyme-mimicking activities [40]. Consequently, these ions effectively eliminate diverse ROS, thereby exhibiting both anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties.

In this work, a 2D SnSe-based multiple-enzyme-mimicking nanozyme (SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme) was constructed for AS remission via ROS scavenging, anti-inflammatory activity, and LA elimination. The SnSe nanozyme modulated LA content in senescent endothelial cells and the acidic plaque microenvironment. To maximize therapeutic efficacy, the SnSe nanozyme surface was further functionalized with metal-phenolic networks (TA-Ce) via self-assembly, imparting additional anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Following accumulation at plaques, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme could effectively scavenge toxic ROS, suppress inflammation-related factor expression, and reduce local LA levels. Furthermore, this nanosystem inhibited EndMT, eventually alleviating endothelial dysfunction and decreasing inflammatory immune cell infiltration. Both in vitro and in vivo experimental results confirmed the synergistic effect between anti-inflammatory/antioxidant therapy and modulation of LA content at plaques. Importantly, transcriptomic analysis of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) revealed that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme influences endothelial cellular senescence and barrier function by regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway. These findings indicate that the pathology-targeted design of the SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme is a promising strategy to enhance anti-atherosclerotic efficiency, offering a nanozyme-enabled and senotherapy-based paradigm for AS management.

Results and discussion

Design, synthesis and characterization of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme

A recent study indicated that LA produced from glycolysis process can trigger ROS generation to drive the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, highlighting the metabolic link between LA generation and cellular senescence [29]. LDH plays a crucial role in regulating LA levels by catalyzing the interconversion of PA and LA. Notably, 2D SnSe nanosheets have been shown to exhibit enzyme-like activity, efficiently converting LA to PA with an efficacy comparable to that of natural LDH [41,42]. To directly identify the products in dehydrogenation reactions, we detected PA, the dehydrogenated product of LA, by liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). As shown in Fig. S1, the LC-MS spectra of the SnSe-catalyzed reaction mixture of LA exhibited a characteristic peak at 1.6 min, displaying nearly identical retention time and MS/MS fragment ions (m/z 87.0 and 43.0) to the PA standard. These findings suggested the generation of PA in the SnSe-catalyzed reactions. To further elucidate the effects of various physiological conditions on the enzymatic activity of SnSe, we conducted systematic assays under acidic pH, NAD+-limited, oxidative stress, and serum-containing environments, with standardized comparisons against natural LDH. At pH 5.3, SnSe retained over 80% of its relative activity, whereas LDH activity decreased significantly, demonstrating the superior tolerance of SnSe toward acidic microenvironments (Fig. S2e). In contrast, natural LDH strictly relied on NAD+ cofactors for catalytic function, while SnSe nanosheets exhibited NAD+-independent catalytic properties (Fig. S2b). More importantly, under oxidative stress conditions simulated by hydrogen peroxide, SnSe maintained high catalytic activity while LDH activity was markedly attenuated, highlighting the resistance of SnSe against redox-mediated inactivation (Fig. S2c). Interestingly, both SnSe and LDH exhibited excellent catalytic stability in environments containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Fig. S2f). Collectively, these results demonstrated that SnSe exhibits superior catalytic stability compared to natural LDH under multiple harsh physiological conditions.

In this study, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was rationally designed and constructed (Fig. 1a). The prepared SnSe nanozyme displayed a planar morphology with a thickness of approximately 1 nm (Figs. S3–S5 and Fig. S6a), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed lateral dimensions ranging from 100 to 200 nm, with an average of approximately 140 nm (Fig. 2a). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) imaging and the accompanying selected area electron diffraction pattern revealed the crystalline lattice of SnSe nanozyme, with lattice fringes measuring 0.23 nm (Fig. 2b and c). To further confirm the nanostructures of the synthesized SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, both TEM and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were conducted. The resulting images indicated that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme largely retained the morphological and size characteristics of SnSe nanozyme (Fig. 2d and e). X-ray energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and elemental distribution mappings of Sn, Se, Ce, N, and O verified the successful fabrication of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme (Fig. 2f, g and Fig. S6b). Fig. 2h–j presents the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of SnSe nanozyme and SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme. The appearance of characteristic peaks for Ce and N in SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, in contrast to SnSe nanozyme, indicated the successful surface modification by the TA-Ce framework. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed the high crystallinity of SnSe, as evidenced by the sharp diffraction peaks. The stability represents a critical parameter in the development of nanomaterials for biomedical applications [43]. The colloidal stability of SnSe and SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was evaluated in various biological media including distilled water, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and DMEM using UV-vis-NIR spectroscopy (Fig. S7). Negligible changes in the absorption spectra over time demonstrated excellent stability of these nanomaterials in biological environments. Furthermore, dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed polydispersity indices (PDI) of 0.28 and 0.31 for SnSe and SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, respectively, indicating homogeneous dispersion states (Fig. S8).

In the acidic environment, the coordination bonds in the TA-Ce network of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme could be cleaved, thereby accelerating the release of Ce, TA, and SnSe from the SnSe@TC MEM nanoplatform. Consequently, we examined the release behavior of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme in both simulated physiological conditions (pH 7.4) and a lysosomal microenvironment (pH 5.3), as shown in Fig. 2l. After 24 h, the release of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was 25% at pH 7.4. In contrast, at pH 5.3, a significantly higher release of 63% was observed after the same time period. TEM images of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme after incubation at pH 5.3 demonstrated that the SnSe nanozymes maintained their structural integrity without significant morphological degradation (Fig. S2d). Furthermore, time-dependent LDH-like activity assays revealed that the SnSe nanozyme retained over 85% of its initial catalytic efficiency toward LA oxidation within the first 2 h at pH 5.3, gradually reaching a stable plateau thereafter (Fig. S2d and e). We also evaluated the LDH-like enzymatic catalytic activity of SnSe nanozyme. As illustrated in Fig. 2m and Fig. S9, SnSe nanozyme exhibited superior catalytic efficiency within a concentration range of 50-200 μg mL−1 compared to LDH at concentrations of 25-100 mU mL−1. Furthermore, to gain deeper insights into the reaction kinetics of SnSe-mediated dehydrogenation, we analyzed the reaction rates at various substrate concentrations. It was found that SnSe nanozyme demonstrated strong catalytic efficacy across a range of LA concentrations starting from 10 mM (Fig. 2n). In summary, these results confirm the successful preparation of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, which functions as LDH-mimicking nanozyme with remarkable catalytic efficiency, holding great potential for LA elimination and anti-senescence effects.

To further elucidate the reaction kinetics of SnSe-mediated dehydrogenations, we measured initial reaction rates at varying substrate concentrations to generate Michaelis-Menten curves (Fig. S10). Furthermore, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations to investigate the energy landscape governing the conversion of LA to PA catalyzed by the SnSe nanozyme. As depicted in Fig. 2o, the most probable reaction pathway derived from our computational analysis comprises four essential steps. Initially, a LA molecule adheres to the SnSe surface (denoted as a’), forming a physisorbed complex (b’). Subsequently, the polar hydroxy hydrogen of LA migrates to a selenium atom on the surface, whilst the hydroxy oxygen coordinates with tin vacancies, leading to the formation of intermediate c’. Next, the α-hydrogen of LA shifts to another selenium atom, thereby facilitating the desorption of PA from the surface. Finally, two chemisorbed hydrogen atoms are transferred from the surface (d’) to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), yielding nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydrogen (NADH) and restoring the active surface. The energy profile corresponding to these reactions is illustrated in Fig. 2p. The overall endothermic reaction has a positive formation energy (ΔE = 3.524 kcal mol−1), consistent with the reported positive standard Gibbs free energy of formation for LDH (ΔG = 6 kcal mol−1).

Cellular internalization and biosafety evaluation of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme

In this work, HUVECs were selected as the typical cell line models to evaluate the antioxidative/anti-inflammatory performance and anti-senescence effect of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme. First, the intracellular uptake of the SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was examined by Bio-TEM and confocal microscopy. SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was taken up after 45 min of culture, and a remarkably enhanced proportion was observed with the prolonged co-incubation duration in a time-dependent manner within the first 4 h of observation, suggesting that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme could be effectively taken up by HUVECs (Fig. S11 and S12).

To guarantee their subsequent safe theranostic utilization, the standard cell-counting kit 8 (CCK-8) assay was applied to evaluate the intrinsic cellular toxicity of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme in HUVECs. It could be found that HUVECs incubated with SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme maintained high relative cell viability (80%) at 24 h even at a high concentration (80 μg mL−1), revealing the high cytocompatibility of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme (Fig. S13). Based on these in vitro findings, the in vivo biosafety of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was further assessed. Kunming mice were administered intravenous injections of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme at doses of 10 and 20 mg kg−1 over four weeks. Throughout the experiment, no significant behavioral changes were observed in the mice. Histological examination of major organs from the treated mice revealed no abnormal structures or significant pathological toxicity (Fig. S14). Additionally, liver and renal function tests as well as hematological parameters showed fluctuations within normal ranges (Figs. S15 and 16), suggesting that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme exhibited no significant toxicity to the mice. These results further confirm the favorable biosafety and biocompatibility of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme.

The biosafety is a top concern in the treatment of metal-based nanomaterials. We injected SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme (10 mg kg−1) into healthy mice and monitored the biodistribution and clearance of cerium ions in the blood at 0, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h post-injection (Fig. S17). The concentration-time curve follows a first-order elimination kinetics with an estimated apparent half-life (t1/2) of 1.79 h, indicating moderate systemic clearance of the nanozyme.

ROS-elimination, inflammation-attenuation and anti-senescence properties of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme

Senescent endothelial cells underwent significant metabolic reprogramming manifested by elevated LA production [44]. Quantitative assessment revealed markedly increased intracellular LA concentrations, which were effectively attenuated upon SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme administration (Fig. S18), confirming the efficacy of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme in eliminating LA accumulations within senescent cells.

Previous studies have shown that the accumulation of senescent endothelial cells can result in oxidative stress and inflammation at plaque lesions, which can further impair the function of endothelial cells [13]. Thus, we assessed the ROS production after co-incubation of SnSe nanozyme and SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme in HUVECs. In the model group, HUVECs were stimulated with H2O2, compared with the fresh medium incubated cells (control group). 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), aminophenyl fluorescein (APF) and dihydroethidium (DHE) were used as fluorescent probes to detect intracellular total ROS, •OH and O2•− after different treatments, respectively. After 4 h of stimulation, the model group exhibited markedly elevated ROS levels. In comparison, when stimulated HUVECs were co-incubated with TA, Ce4+, or SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme for 4 h, the DCFH-DA-derived green fluorescence signals, indicative of ROS levels, decreased greatly, with the most substantial reduction observed in the SnSe@TC MEM-treated group (Fig. 3a). This suggests that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, following TA-Ce modification, possesses potent antioxidative/anti-inflammatory effects and synergistically exerts anti-senescence properties. However, when stimulated HUVECs were co-incubated with SnSe for 4 h, the green fluorescence signal intensity remained largely unchanged. This might be attributed to the affinity of SnSe for hydrogen atoms, which precludes its ROS scavenging ability. Consistent with these observations, separate staining with APF (green fluorescence) and DHE (red fluorescence) further demonstrated that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme could markedly reduce •OH and O2•− production in HUVECs (p < 0.001, Fig. 3b–d). Flow cytometry was further employed to quantify intracellular ROS levels in HUVECs treated with SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, corroborating its potent ROS-scavenging activity (Fig. S19). Owing to their remarkable antioxidative/anti-inflammatory properties, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was anticipated to reverse H2O2-induced apoptosis in HUVECs. Fig. 3e, f and Fig. S20 demonstrated that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme exhibits excellent anti-apoptotic capabilities. Furthermore, we investigated the biological changes in the mitochondria and cytoplasmic membranes of HUVECs. The mitochondrial membrane potential in the SnSe@TC MEM-treated group was notably elevated compared to that in the model group (H2O2-treated) (Fig. 3g and Fig. S21), indicating that reduction of ROS factor level could protect the mitochondria in HUVECs.

DNA damage is a primary driver of cellular senescence, with highly toxic ROS being a major endogenous contributor. After inducing senescence in HUVECs, we observed the expression level of phosphorylated histone H2A family member X (γ-H2AX), a marker of DNA double-strand breaks, using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Senescent cells displayed high γ-H2AX expression, which was markedly reduced following SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme treatment, to a greater extent than in the SnSe, TA, and Ce groups (Fig. 3h and j). These results indicate that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme can effectively protect DNA from ROS-induced damage. In response to external stress, senescent endothelial cells secrete a variety of biologically active molecules collectively termed the SASP. The SASP comprises inflammatory chemokines, growth factors, cytokines, and ROS, all of which contribute to permanent growth arrest. To identify senescent cells and evaluate the anti-senescence efficacy of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, we measured senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity, a well-established senescence marker. Notably, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme treatment resulted in a significant reduction in SA-β-Gal activity in HUVECs compared to other groups (Fig. 3i and k). To rigorously address whether LA clearance is necessary rather than secondary to the anti-senescence effects, we conducted loss-of-function controls and corresponding LA rescue experiments. As shown in Fig. S22–S25, the anti-senescence efficacy of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was significantly attenuated following either thermal inactivation of SnSe or exogenous LA supplementation, indicating that catalytic LA scavenging is indispensable for achieving full therapeutic efficacy.

Given that inflammation activates cellular senescence and drives AS, we further assessed the anti-inflammatory properties of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme. Using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), we found that lipopolysaccharides (LPS) evidently stimulated HUVECs to produce high levels of cell adhesion molecules, including intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) (Fig. 3l and m). Treatment with SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme reduced these cytokine levels, thereby demonstrating their anti-inflammatory effects.

Effect of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme on the barrier function of cytokine activated endothelium

The core responsibility of vascular endothelial cells is to serve as gatekeepers, regulating the movement of molecules and cells across them. Normal, quiescent endothelial monolayers possess intact cellular structures with continuous intercellular adhesion, surrounded by actin filaments at their cellular borders. The actin cytoskeleton plays an important role in maintaining the barrier-regulatory properties of endothelial cell monolayers. However, previous studies indicated that, under specific pathological conditions, elevated cytokine levels in the bloodstream, such as IL-1β, disrupt endothelial integrity and enhance endothelial permeability through the activation of the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) process. The enhanced permeability of endothelial cells provides a shortcut for the trans-endothelial migration of immune cells and lipids, leading to the formation of atherosclerotic diseases. Therefore, how to strengthen the gating role and maintain the integrity of endothelial cells is crucial for treating AS [45,46].

Through fluorescence staining of VE-cadherin and cytoskeletal F-actin (Fig. 4a and b), it was evident that untreated HUVECs exhibited an intact monolayer. The circumferential actin filaments along the cell boundary were anchored by transmembrane VE-cadherin, and together they stabilized endothelial cell-cell contacts and maintained barrier integrity. While IL-1β-treated HUVEC monolayers displayed significant morphological alterations, including discontinuous cell-cell adhesion, the emergence of intercellular gaps, and an increase in actin stress fibers along the cell body. These findings indicated that the activation of IL-1β impaired the morphological integrity of endothelial cells. After co-incubating HUVECs with SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme and IL-1β for 6 h, we observed that the endothelial layer maintained its structural integrity, with no apparent intercellular gaps or increased actin stress fibers. To further validate the efficacy of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, we conducted assays to evaluate the expression levels of CD31 and PAI-1 in endothelial cells. CD31, a typical marker of vascular endothelial cells, plays a key role in angiogenesis, whereas PAI-1 is a mesenchymal marker [47,48]. As seen in Fig. 4c–f, upon induction with IL-1β, the expression of CD31 in endothelial cells decreased, whereas the expression of PAI-1 increased, indicating that under cytokine induction, endothelial cells initiated the mesenchymal transition process. However, the addition of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme significantly inhibited this process.

RNA sequencing of HUVECs after different treatments

To further elucidate the anti-senescence mechanism of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme in HUVECs, we performed high-throughput transcriptome sequencing. The obvious clustering of data from the same treatment groups observed after unsupervised hierarchical clustering indicated high reliability of the RNA-sequencing data (Fig. 5a). Additionally, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further confirmed the correlation between these DEGs and the TGF-β signaling pathway, cell-cell adhesion mediated by cadherin, adherens junction organization, cell-cell junction assembly, and actin cytoskeleton organization (Fig. 5e and h). These findings suggested a critical role for SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme as a regulator of endothelial cell structure in the treatment of AS. The TGF-β signaling pathway is one of the pivotal factors driving EndMT in vascular endothelial cells [50,51]. Upon activation, this pathway initially disrupts the homeostasis of vascular endothelial cells by impairing the bFGF/FGFR1 signaling axis, while concurrently enhancing the expression of adhesion factors, such as VCAM1 and ICAM1, on the endothelial cell surface. Consequently, it facilitates the infiltration, migration, and differentiation of inflammatory cells within the vessel wall. Additionally, TGF-β activation can suppress the expression of proteins essential for maintaining endothelial cell function and promote a direct pathological transition of vascular endothelial cells towards a mesenchymal phenotype. Another crucial aspect is that TGF-β is capable of activating both Smad-dependent and Smad-independent signaling pathways [52,53]. These pathways can subsequently upregulate the expression of cell cycle inhibitors, which can halt cell cycle progression and induce cellular senescence. Moreover, TGF-β can modulate the intracellular redox state by increasing the levels of ROS. This, in turn, activates the relevant signaling pathways and ultimately induces cellular senescence. Based on the protein-protein interaction network constructed from relevant DEGs, the presence of key genes (ID1-4, SMAD6, SMAD9 and AMH) within the TGF-β signaling pathway further validated the regulatory role of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme on this pathway (Fig. 5i).

To rigorously validate the transcriptomic findings, we conducted quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of key TGF-β pathway components, including the transcriptional target ID1, the ligand TGFB1, the canonical downstream effector SERPINE1 (PAI-1), and the endothelial integrity marker CDH5. Consistent with our RNA-seq data, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme treatment significantly downregulated ID1, TGFB1, and SERPINE1, while concomitantly upregulating CDH5. These results collectively demonstrated the inhibitory effect of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme on the TGF-β signaling pathway and EndMT (Fig. S27).

As illustrated in Fig. 7c and g, compared to the model group, the activation levels of caspase-3 in the aortas of ApoE−/− mice treated with SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme were also distinctly down-regulated. This reduction verified that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme can strongly inhibit the caspase-3-mediated apoptotic pathway, which was vital in AS development. Furthermore, previous studies have highlighted that slowing cellular senescence in atherosclerotic lesions is a highly effective strategy for treating AS. Given the potent in vitro anti-senescence effects of the SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme, we further evaluated its in vivo therapeutic efficacy. Immunohistochemical staining with a γ-H2AX antibody showed a marked decrease in γ-H2AX expression in plaque after SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme treatment. Compared to the SnSe, TA, and Ce groups, the SnSe@TC MEM-treated group had much smaller positive areas of γ-H2AX staining, indicating that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme effectively protected DNA from ROS damage (Fig. 7d and h). Moreover, analysis of the cellular senescence marker SA-β-Gal showed that SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme obviously decreased SA-β-Gal expression at plaque sites (Fig. 8c and d). Notably, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme treatment significantly reduced LA levels in aortic plaque tissues (Fig. 6h and i) and decreased the LA/PA ratio compared to the model group (Fig. S29). These metabolic improvements mirror the observed reductions in senescence markers, inflammatory indices, and EndMT indicators, collectively demonstrating the nanozyme's capacity to remodel the local metabolic microenvironment.

We further observed that LA accumulation significantly activated the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway in ApoE−/− mice relative to controls, whereas SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme treatment effectively suppressed this pathway activation (Fig. S30). CDH5, also known as VE-cadherin, is predominantly expressed in endothelial cells and plays a critical role in mediating cell-cell adhesion, angiogenesis, and the maintenance of endothelial barrier integrity. Consistent with our in vitro findings, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme effectively protects endothelial cells against cytokine-induced damage and suppresses their transition from a quiescent phenotype toward a mesenchymal state (Fig. S30).

Conclusions

In this work, we successfully constructed the 2D SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme for the treatment of AS through rational design of incorporating TA and tetravalent Ce to form the metal-TA frameworks onto the surface of SnSe nanozyme. To be specific, metal-TA framework component in the synthesized SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme could effectively scavenge toxic ROS and down-regulate the expression of inflammatory factors, ultimately protecting cellular DNA from ROS attack and reducing the expression of the senescence marker SA-β-Gal. Furthermore, the unique enzyme-like activity of the SnSe component enables these nanozymes to convert LA into PA efficiently, allowing them to synergistically exert anti-inflammation and anti-senescence efficacy in endothelial cells. More importantly, SnSe nanozyme could further reverse the pro-inflammatory acidic condition at plaques, and thereby effectively block EndMT and alleviate endothelial dysfunction. The synergy between anti-inflammatory/antioxidant therapy and LA modulation enabled by SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was validated through systematic in vitro and in vivo experiments, offering a promising paradigm for AS management. Our work not only expands the applications of SnSe-based nanozyme but also provides a high-performance therapeutic nanoplatform for combating AS.

Characterization

TEM images and corresponding EDS spectra were acquired using a JEM-2100 F electron microscope. Particle size and zeta potential measurements were conducted using a Zetasizer Nanoseries (Nano ZS90, Malvern Instrument Ltd.). XPS spectra were obtained with an ESCAlab250 (Thermal Scientific). UV-vis-NIR absorption spectra were recorded using a UV-3600 Shimadzu UV-vis-NIR spectrometer. The concentrations of Ce, Sn, and Se were quantified by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 725, Agilent Technologies, USA).

Synthesis of SnSe NSs

Initially, 1 g of SnSe bulk material was mixed with 50 mL anhydrous ethanol for dispersion by 24 h ultrasonic oscillation (5 s on, 3 s off) with 0.125 inch probe (60 W) in ice-bath. The dispersion was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 22 °C with a SW32 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter, California, USA) to acquire supernatant (s1) and pellet (p1). The supernatant (p1) was further centrifuged at 13000 rpm to get pellets (s2) and supernatant (s2). The resulting pellets (s2) were washed with DI H2O three times and quantified by ICP-OES (iCAP 7000 SERIES, Thermo, USA) to prepare 100 μg mL−1 stock solutions.

Synthesis of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme

SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was synthesized by self-assembly method. Typically, 10 ml SnSe solution (100 μg mL−1), 2 ml TA solution (1.0 mg mL−1) were added to 50 ml deionized water with stirring for 20 min. Then, continue stirring for 3 min after adding 2 ml Ce(NO3)4 solution (1.0 mg mL−1). Following that, the pH of the solution was adjusted to 8.0 by adding NaOH solution. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 h. Finally, the as-synthesized SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was collected by centrifugation and washed with deionized water three times and quantified by ICP-OES (iCAP 7000 SERIES, Thermo, USA) to prepare 100 μg mL−1 stock solutions.

LC-MS/MS analysis

To detect the generation of PA, SnSe (200 μg mL−1) was added to the mixture of LA (20 mM) in 0.5 mL DI H2O and reacted for 16 h at 4 °C. Afterward, the mixtures were ultra-filtrated using centrifugal filters (MWCO of 3 kDa) at 20,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the filtrates for LC-MS analysis. The LC-MS/MS analysis was performed in Hangzhou Yanqu Information Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

The impacts of LA concentration on the dehydrogenase activities of SnSe nanozyme and LDH

LA (10 mM to 10 M), NAD+ (50 mg mL−1), and MTS (0.5 mg mL−1) were reacted in 0.5 mL DI water (pH = 7.0) under the catalysis of 200 μg mL−1 SnSe nanozyme or LDH. The relative amount of PA production was calculated by the absorbance of 490 nm after 2 h incubation.

Analysis of enzyme kinetics

SnSe (200 μg mL−1) was mixed with LA (10 mM to 10 M) as the reactant, 0.5 mg mL−1 MTS as the colorimetric substrate and 50 mg mL−1 NAD + as the coenzyme in 200 μL DI water to react at 37 °C in a 96-well plate. Absorbance at 490 nm was recorded by a microplate reader in scanning kinetics mode at 1 min interval for 30 min. The reaction rates of both SnSe at different LA concentrations were calculated and fitted by Michaelis-Menten equation. All the experiments were independently carried out with at least three replicates.

Analysis of the enzymatic activities

The LDH activity of the SnSe nanozyme was determined using an LDH assay kit. According to the instructions of the manufacturer, reactants and substrates were mixed with SnSe nanozyme at 50-200 μg mL−1 or LDH at 25-100 mU mL−1 and incubated at 37 °C. The absorbance of the chromogenic substrates was recorded using a microplate reader at OD 450 nm for the LDH assay. The relative enzymatic activity was calculated as follows:Relative enzymatic activity (%) = (ODx - ODBL) / (ODm - ODBL)where ODx is the absorbance of tested sample; ODm is the highest absorbance of SnSe or LDH at the optimal condition; ODBL is the absorbance of blank controls.

Assessment of catalytic activities of SnSe at various reaction conditions

Aliquots of SnSe (200 μg mL−1) and LDH (200 μg mL−1) were pretreated in DI H2O at 4-100 °C, 10 mM PBS at pH 5.3, 500 μM H2O2 or serum-containing environments for 2 h. Then the treated LDH and SnSe samples were collected by ultrafiltration (3000 Da) and resuspended in solutions containing MTS for 1 h reactions. After that, the absorbance of substrate at different conditions was examined at 490 nm. All the experiments were independently carried out with at least three replicates.

Drug release kinetic study

Briefly, 100 μg mL−1 of SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme was placed in pH 7.4 and 5.3 media. The supernatant was collected at different time points and was analyzed by the ultraviolet absorption spectrum.

Cell culture

A human umbilical vein endothelial cell line (HUVEC) was cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were grown at 37 °C in an atmosphere with 5% CO2.

In vitro cytotoxicity assessment

First, HUVECs were cultured into 96-well plates overnight. Afterward, the cells were treated with varied concentrations of TA, Ce, SnSe, SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme. After 12 h or 24 h incubation, the CCK-8 assay was applied to evaluate the cell activity. Absorbance values at 450 nm of each well were assayed by a microplate reader in a 30-min incubation.

Cellular uptake

HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the cell culture medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme (200 μg mL−1) for 30 min, 45 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h. The treated cells were subsequently harvested, centrifuged, and suspended in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative overnight. After fixation in an osmium tetroxide solution at room temperature for 1 h, the cells were dehydrated using a gradient ethanol solution and embedded in resin. The prepared sections were then examined using TEM.

In vivo toxicology assessment

Fifteen healthy female Kunming mice (5-6 weeks old) were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 per group): a control group (treated with saline only), and two experimental groups intravenously injected with SnSe@TC MEM-nanozyme at doses of 10 mg kg−1 and 20 mg kg−1, respectively. After one month of treatment, blood and major organs were collected from the mice for comprehensive evaluations, including hematological, histological, and blood biochemical analyses.

Aortas and serial cross-sections, including the aortic root, arch, and brachiocephalic artery, were stained with ORO for lipid deposition analysis. Aortic sinus sections (4 μm) were histologically analyzed using H&E and Masson's trichrome staining, and immunohistochemically with MMP-9 antibodies. Immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against TNF-α, IL-6, CD68, α-SMA, ICAM-1, γ-H2AX, Caspase-3, SA-β-Gal, CDH5, psmad2/3 and TGFβ. Semiquantitative analysis of the histological and immunofluorescent images was conducted using Image J software.

Statistical analyses

All quantitative data in this study were obtained from at least three independent experimental replicates and are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. The sample size (n) specified in figure legends represents biologically independent observations or experimental animals per group. p < 0.05 was used as the significant threshold. Detailed statistical parameters are provided in the corresponding figure legends. ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA), while data visualization and graph preparation were completed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 and Origin 2021 (OriginLab, MA, USA).
