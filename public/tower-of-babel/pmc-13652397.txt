Intravascular bullet embolization: a multicenter three-case series and literature review
Authors: Carlos Andres Muñoz, Adolfo González Hadad, Alejandro Burgos, José Julián Serna, Diego Rivera Arbeláez, Juan Pablo Carbonell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Intravascular bullet embolization (IBE) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of penetrating trauma. It may present as either arterial or venous embolization and is most commonly associated with low-velocity, small-caliber gunshot wounds (GSWs). Diagnosis is often challenging, and management remains controversial because of the limited evidence available to guide therapeutic decision-making.Case presentation:We present a multicenter case series of three patients with venous bullet embolization treated using a hybrid endovascular–surgical approach. In all three cases, the projectile was successfully captured using endovascular techniques; however, definitive retrieval required a venotomy because of the projectile’s diameter. All three patients had favorable clinical outcomes, with no procedure-related complications and complete functional recovery during follow-up.Clinical discussion:IBE should be suspected when the number of entry and exit wounds is inconsistent with the expected ballistic trajectory. Imaging studies play a pivotal role in confirming the diagnosis and in planning the therapeutic strategy. Although management remains controversial and should be individualized, advances in endovascular techniques have expanded the minimally invasive options for foreign-body retrieval. Our case series illustrates that although endovascular capture of the projectile may be technically feasible, definitive retrieval may still require a venotomy when the projectile’s diameter precludes endovascular removal.Conclusion:IBE is a rare complication of penetrating trauma that requires a high index of clinical suspicion for timely diagnosis. When technically feasible and supported by appropriate expertise and resources, an endovascular approach may be considered as an initial retrieval strategy. Management should be individualized according to the type of embolization, projectile characteristics, anatomical location, the patient’s clinical condition, and the anticipated risk–benefit balance of intervention.

Unlike arterial bullet embolization (ABE), which typically presents with early symptoms and generally requires urgent retrieval by endovascular or open surgical techniques because of the risk of tissue or organ ischemia, venous bullet embolization (VBE) is usually asymptomatic. However, its management remains controversial due to the lack of standardized recommendations, as the available literature supports both projectile retrieval and conservative management as acceptable therapeutic strategies in selected patients[1,3].

In this article, we present a multicenter case series of three patients with VBE treated using a hybrid endovascular–surgical approach, achieving successful projectile retrieval with no procedure-related complications. In addition, we provide an updated review of the literature and propose a practical diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm for the management of this rare entity based on the available evidence and multidisciplinary expert consensus. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the PROCESS 2025 guideline[4].

Methods

This multicenter retrospective case series was conducted at two Level I trauma centers in Cali, Colombia. Institutional medical records from January 2008 through January 2025 were retrospectively reviewed.

The inclusion criteria included patients of any age or sex with penetrating firearm-related vascular injuries and confirmed arterial and/or venous IBE, as established by imaging studies, including plain radiography, computed tomography (CT), angiography, or echocardiography, with complete clinical, operative, imaging, and follow-up data available.

The exclusion criteria included incomplete medical records that precluded the retrieval of relevant clinical information, absence of post-treatment clinical follow-up, and embolization due to a foreign body (FB) unrelated to firearm injuries.

Written informed consent for publication of this case series was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians, as appropriate. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. This case series was conducted and reported in accordance with the PROCESS 2025 guideline[4].

Results

A total of three patients with IBE were identified. Their ages ranged from 10 to 63 years. Two patients presented with retrograde VBE, whereas one presented with anterograde VBE. All three patients were treated using a hybrid approach consisting of endovascular projectile capture followed by venotomy for definitive retrieval, with no procedure-related complications. Case presentation

Case 1

A 10-year-old girl was referred to our emergency department (ED) 30 minutes after sustaining a stray gunshot wound (GSW) to the precordial region. On admission, her vital signs were as follows: heart rate (HR), 112 beats/min; respiratory rate (RR), 21 breaths/min; peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), 97%; blood pressure (BP), 86/60 mmHg; mean arterial pressure (MAP), 69 mmHg; and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 14. Physical examination revealed well-ventilated lung fields, diminished heart sounds, and no abdominal tenderness. During the exposure phase of the primary survey, a single entry wound was identified in the left third intercostal space along the left parasternal line, with no exit wound. A chest X-ray (CXR) showed no abnormalities.

Given the suspicion of a penetrating cardiac injury, the patient was immediately transferred to the operating room, where a subxiphoid pericardial window was performed, which revealed hemopericardium. A median sternotomy was subsequently performed, revealing an approximately 7-mm laceration of the right ventricle (RV), which was repaired with a 3-0 nonabsorbable monofilament suture, achieving adequate hemostasis. The posterior surface of the heart and the pericardium were then carefully inspected, and no additional injuries were identified.

During the early postoperative period, an abdominal radiograph demonstrated an FB projecting over the hepatic silhouette. Because the projectile’s location was inconsistent with the expected ballistic trajectory, IBE was suspected. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a metallic projectile lodged within the retrohepatic inferior vena cava (IVC), with no other significant findings. Transesophageal echocardiography and an electrocardiogram (ECG) showed no abnormalities. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a 1 g/dL decrease in hemoglobin concentration, which remained stable on serial measurements. The patient received 24 hours of cefazolin prophylaxis and thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin.

The patient was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit (ICU) for close hemodynamic monitoring. She remained hemodynamically stable throughout this period, with no evidence of active bleeding, arrhythmias, cardiopulmonary compromise, or other complications related to the embolized projectile. Given her stable clinical condition, additional imaging studies were performed, and endovascular retrieval of the projectile was planned. Forty-eight hours after admission, the right common femoral vein (CFV) was accessed, a 6-Fr vascular sheath was inserted, and venography confirmed the presence of the projectile within the retrohepatic IVC. Subsequently, a three-loop endovascular snare (Atrieve®, Argon Medical Devices, Dallas, TX, USA) was advanced and used to mobilize the projectile into the right common iliac vein (CIV). Because of the projectile’s diameter, fully percutaneous retrieval was not feasible. Therefore, a venotomy was performed, allowing complete retrieval of the 0.38-caliber projectile (Fig. 1). The patient tolerated the procedure well and experienced no perioperative complications. She subsequently received thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin until she regained full ambulation and was discharged after 7 days of hospitalization. At the most recent outpatient follow-up, she remained asymptomatic and had returned to her normal daily activities without limitations.

Case 3

A 23-year-old man was transferred from a rural hospital to our emergency department (ED) after sustaining a gunshot wound (GSW) to the chest approximately 40 minutes before admission. On arrival, his vital signs were as follows: HR, 115 beats/min; RR, 25 breaths/min; SpO2, 90%; BP, 90/52 mmHg; MAP, 65 mmHg; and a GCS score of 11. Physical examination revealed severe abdominal tenderness with no abnormal cardiac or pulmonary findings. During the exposure phase of the primary survey, a single 1-cm entry wound was identified at the level of the xiphoid process, with no exit wound.

E-FAST was positive for free fluid in the hepatorenal and splenorenal recesses, whereas the subxiphoid pericardial view was indeterminate for pericardial fluid. Given the patient’s hemodynamic instability and ultrasonographic findings, a blood product transfusion was initiated, and he was immediately transferred to the operating room for an exploratory laparotomy.

Approximately 2000 mL of hemoperitoneum was evacuated. A transdiaphragmatic pericardial window was performed and revealed no evidence of pericardial injury. Abdominal exploration revealed a segment III liver laceration with minimal bleeding, which was controlled with hepatic packing. In addition, an approximately 1-cm duodenal injury was identified and repaired by primary closure using a 3-0 nonabsorbable monofilament suture. A 1-cm laceration involving the anterior wall of the IVC, located immediately above the confluence of the renal veins, was also identified and was actively bleeding. After proximal and distal vascular control with vascular clamps, the injury was repaired using a continuous 5-0 nonabsorbable monofilament suture.

Because the patient presented with metabolic acidosis, hypothermia, massive blood loss, and vasopressor requirements, temporary abdominal closure with vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) therapy was performed as part of a damage-control strategy. The patient was subsequently transferred to the ICU for continued physiologic resuscitation and correction of metabolic and hemodynamic abnormalities. Due to the associated hollow viscus injury, antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated.

During the early postoperative period, CXR showed an FB projecting over the right cardiac silhouette (Fig. 4A and B). Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen subsequently confirmed pneumopericardium, pneumomediastinum, bilateral pleural effusions, and a projectile lodged within the IVC immediately below the right atrium (RA), without evidence of cardiac injury (Fig. 4C). The patient subsequently underwent two additional planned re-look laparotomies before definitive abdominal closure. During the first reoperation, marked bowel edema precluded primary fascial closure, and temporary abdominal closure with VAC therapy was maintained. Forty-eight hours later, a second re-look laparotomy demonstrated significant resolution of the bowel edema, with no evidence of a duodenal leak or residual intra-abdominal contamination, allowing definitive abdominal closure. Discussion

IBE is a rare complication of GSWs, occurring in approximately 0.04%–0.3% of cases[5,6]. It is defined as the migration of an FB, such as a bullet or shotgun pellet, from its site of vascular entry to an abnormal distal location through the bloodstream. Most cases are associated with low-velocity, small-caliber projectiles[7]. As the projectile traverses soft tissues and other anatomical structures, including bone and cartilage, it progressively loses velocity and kinetic energy. Consequently, it may penetrate only one wall of a blood vessel, remain within the vascular lumen, and subsequently migrate to different segments of the same vessel, other vascular territories, or the heart[7,8]. The main factors influencing projectile embolization include blood flow dynamics, projectile size, gravity, body position, and respiratory motion[7].

Most cases of IBE occur in the setting of civilian firearm violence. Published series have reported that 84%–91% of patients are male, with a median age of approximately 27 years, and that the highest frequency is observed during the second and third decades of life[1,9,10]. The thorax is the most common anatomical region of injury associated with IBE, followed by the abdomen, neck, and lower extremities[1,9]. Single-projectile GSWs constitute the most common mechanism of injury, accounting for 48%–74% of reported cases[1,9]. Furthermore, anterior entry wounds predominate, being reported in 54%–79% of patients described in the literature[1,10].

Venous injuries represent the most common source of IBE; in the review by Kuo et al, venous entry accounted for approximately 56% of cases[1]. Among the most frequently reported venous entry sites are the IVC, femoral and iliac veins, and brachiocephalic or subclavian veins (Table 2) [1,9]. Once within the venous circulation, the FB may migrate in an anterograde fashion and become lodged within the right cardiac chambers, where it may be trapped among the endocardial trabeculations and progressively encapsulated by fibrous tissue. Alternatively, it may continue its migration into the pulmonary arteries (PAs), which represent one of the major termination sites. Less frequently, the projectile may terminate within the systemic venous circulation, particularly in the iliac veins, hepatic vein, or IVC (Table 2)[1,9,11,12]. Finally, embolizations secondary to cardiac injury or involvement of the cavoatrial junction account for approximately 15%–22% of cases reported in the major literature reviews. Kuo et al reported that, among cardiac entry sites, 52% involved the left ventricle, followed by the RV (20%), right atrium (16%), and left atrium (9%)[1]. In contrast, Russell et al found a more homogeneous distribution among cases of cardiac or cavoatrial origin, with involvement of the left ventricle in 33%, right atrium or cavoatrial junction in 33%, RV in 27%, and left atrium in 7%[9]. Overall, these findings indicate that the left ventricle is one of the major cardiac sites of origin of IBE, representing the most frequently involved chamber in Kuo et al and sharing the highest frequency with the right atrium or cavoatrial junction in Russell et al.

The migration of an intravascular projectile can be classified into four main patterns. First, anterograde embolization, in which the projectile follows the normal direction of blood flow through a major vessel, is the most common pattern, accounting for 71%–79% of reported cases[1,9,13]. Second, retrograde embolization, observed in approximately 9% of cases, is characterized by projectile migration against the direction of blood flow and appears to occur more frequently within the venous system[1,9,13]. Third, mixed embolization refers to a migration pattern in which the FB follows a trajectory with both anterograde and retrograde components during its passage through the vascular system, without necessarily crossing between the venous and arterial circulations. This pattern accounts for approximately 3%–8% of reported cases[1,9]. Finally, paradoxical embolization, an uncommon migration pattern observed in approximately 3% of cases, occurs when the projectile traverses a communication between the venous and arterial circulations, resulting in embolization to a vascular territory inconsistent with the expected trajectory based on the site of entry[1,9,13]. Mechanisms associated with paradoxical embolization include arteriovenous fistulas, atrioventricular perforations, ventricular septal defects, and patent foramen ovale[14,15].

In the first case, the patient sustained a stray GSW that involved the RV. The FB, rather than remaining within the RV or embolizing to the PAs, migrated retrogradely into the RA and subsequently into the IVC, where it ultimately became lodged within the retrohepatic segment. In the second case, the patient sustained a GSW to the left shoulder. The projectile entered the left subclavian vein and migrated through the left brachiocephalic vein into the superior vena cava (SVC). It subsequently reached the RA; however, instead of remaining within this chamber or continuing anterogradely toward the RV, it followed a retrograde course through the IVC and ultimately lodged within the left IIV.

Finally, in the third case, the patient sustained a GSW at the level of the xiphoid process. The projectile entered the juxtarenal IVC and migrated anterogradely through the vessel until it reached a position immediately below the RA, without entering the cardiac chamber.

The diagnosis of IBE can be challenging and requires a meticulous initial evaluation. Accordingly, a mismatch between the number of entry and exit wounds should raise suspicion for projectile retention and possible intravascular migration[7,16]. Plain radiography is the most commonly used initial imaging modality because of its widespread availability and rapid acquisition. Identification of a projectile outside its expected ballistic trajectory or visualization of a migrating FB on serial radiographs should raise suspicion for IBE and prompt further evaluation with additional imaging studies[7,8].

CT is the imaging modality of choice for hemodynamically stable or asymptomatic patients with suspected IBE because it enables accurate localization of the projectile and assessment of its size. However, metallic artifacts may partially limit image interpretation. In addition, transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography, fluoroscopy, and angiography are valuable complementary imaging modalities for localizing and characterizing intracardiac projectiles, as well as for planning endovascular or surgical retrieval strategies[8,17].

In our case series, the diagnosis of IBE varied according to each patient’s clinical presentation. In the first and third cases, this rare complication was suspected only during the early postoperative period. Both patients presented with hemodynamic instability and required immediate surgical intervention, which limited the initial imaging evaluation because urgent control of traumatic injuries and hemorrhage took priority. Subsequent careful review of the CXRs revealed that the projectiles did not follow the expected ballistic trajectory, raising suspicion for IBE and prompting additional imaging studies to accurately localize the embolized projectiles.

In contrast, the diagnosis was suspected at the time of admission in the second case. The patient was hemodynamically stable and presented with a single GSW to the left shoulder, without an identifiable exit wound. Furthermore, the initial radiograph failed to identify the projectile either within the affected extremity or the thorax, prompting a systematic diagnostic workup with complementary imaging studies. In all three cases, contrast-enhanced CT ultimately confirmed the diagnosis and accurately localized the embolized projectiles.

The complications associated with IBE can be devastating. Arterial emboli are symptomatic in up to 80% of patients, with the most common manifestations including acute limb ischemia, visceral ischemia, myocardial infarction, thrombosis, and stroke[1,3,9,10]. Because of the substantial morbidity and mortality associated with ABE, most authors recommend retrieval of these FBs, even in asymptomatic patients, to prevent delayed complications, particularly ischemic events[1,3,18,19].

In contrast, venous emboli are asymptomatic in approximately 70% of cases. However, they may be associated with potentially serious complications, including pulmonary embolism (PE), valvular insufficiency, endocarditis, abscess formation, venous thrombosis, arrhythmias, interatrial or interventricular communications, tissue erosion, hemorrhage, and cardiac ischemia. Importantly, these complications do not always occur immediately and may develop months or even years after the initial injury[18–20]. In cases of symptomatic VBE, FB retrieval is generally indicated because of the risk of complications. When retrieval is pursued, an endovascular approach may be considered if technically feasible, with open surgery reserved for cases in which percutaneous retrieval fails, is not feasible, or a concomitant surgical indication is present[20].

The optimal management of asymptomatic VBE remains a matter of debate, and the literature describes both FB retrieval and conservative management as valid therapeutic options in selected cases[17]. Published therapeutic recommendations for IBE are heterogeneous and vary according to projectile location, the presence of symptoms, and the feasibility of endovascular retrieval[1,3,9,21,22]. The development and refinement of endovascular techniques in recent years have expanded the therapeutic options for patients with IBE in whom retrieval is indicated. In selected patients, this approach may avoid highly invasive procedures, such as sternotomy, thoracotomy, or cardiopulmonary bypass, and may offer potential benefits in terms of lower morbidity and faster postoperative recovery. These benefits may be particularly relevant in patients with multiple traumatic injuries, in whom minimizing additional surgical trauma is an important goal[3,22]. In ABE, retrieval is generally indicated, whereas the choice between an endovascular and open approach depends on the presence of ischemia, projectile location, and technical feasibility[1,9]. In cases of VBE with projectiles lodged in the vena cava, right cardiac chambers, or systemic veins, an endovascular approach may be technically feasible; however, its utility for projectiles lodged in the PAs appears to be more limited because of higher failure rates. Therefore, observation or open surgery may be considered in selected patients[1,9,21].

The endovascular approach also has limitations. Its feasibility depends on the location, size, shape, and degree of projectile impaction, as well as on the availability of personnel experienced in advanced endovascular retrieval techniques. In addition, secondary projectile migration may occur during retrieval maneuvers, potentially complicating the procedure or causing new complications[1,22]. In a recent systematic review, open or hybrid procedures achieved a higher first-attempt success rate than exclusively endovascular approaches (91% vs. 29%). Moreover, open surgical retrievals accounted for 76% of successful retrievals, compared with 24% for endovascular or percutaneous retrievals[9].

An additional limitation is that endovascular capture and mobilization of the projectile do not always allow fully percutaneous retrieval. In civilian trauma, a substantial proportion of GSWs are caused by low-velocity, intermediate-caliber projectiles, particularly 9-mm and .38-caliber bullets[23–25]. These projectiles can be captured with an endovascular snare and mobilized from the cardiac chambers or great vessels to more accessible vessels, such as the iliac, femoral, or subclavian vessels, thereby facilitating retrieval from a more favorable anatomical location. However, fully percutaneous retrieval may be limited because the projectile diameter often exceeds the internal diameter of large-bore vascular sheaths, typically 12–14 Fr. Consequently, even when endovascular capture is successful, vascular access may need to be enlarged by venotomy or arteriotomy to complete the procedure safely. This technical limitation has been described in a previous review, in which endovascular retrieval achieved an approximate success rate of 63%, although up to 57% of cases required open vascular dissection to complete retrieval because of projectile size[1]. In our series, one patient had embolization of a 9-mm projectile and two had embolization of .38-caliber projectiles; all three required venotomy to complete definitive retrieval after endovascular capture.

Overall, the available evidence suggests that, in patients with IBE in whom retrieval is indicated, an endovascular approach may be considered as an initial strategy when technically feasible and when appropriate expertise and resources are available. In this context, technological advances, the incorporation of hybrid emergency rooms, and the development of multidisciplinary teams may facilitate the integration of diagnostic imaging, endovascular intervention, and surgery, which could reduce patient transfers and delays to definitive treatment[26–30]. Nevertheless, the choice among endovascular, open, or hybrid approaches should be individualized according to projectile characteristics and the patient’s clinical condition.

On the other hand, conservative management has been described in the literature in selected patients with projectiles smaller than 5 mm lodged within the right cardiac chambers, with smooth edges, firmly adherent to the myocardium, and without evidence of contamination, arrhythmias, valvular dysfunction, or intracardiac shunts. Observation has also been proposed in asymptomatic patients with FBs located within the pulmonary arterial tree when endovascular retrieval is not technically feasible or has failed, when the risk associated with open surgery outweighs the potential benefit of retrieval, and when reliable periodic follow-up can be ensured. In these settings, clinical and imaging surveillance has been considered a reasonable alternative[3,6,11,17,18,21,22,31].

Based on this limited evidence, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests considering retrieval of intravascular FBs when technically feasible and when the expected benefit outweighs the procedural risks. Nevertheless, conservative management may represent a reasonable alternative in carefully selected asymptomatic patients, particularly under the following circumstances: fragments or projectiles smaller than 5 mm, with smooth edges and firmly adherent to the vascular endothelium; fragments or projectiles larger than 5 mm in which endovascular retrieval has failed or is not technically feasible, and the morbidity and mortality risk associated with open surgery outweighs the potential benefit of retrieval. In either scenario, the absence of evidence of contamination of the FB or its trajectory, as well as the absence of associated infection, should be considered part of the individualized assessment before conservative management is selected.

Importantly, significant psychological disturbances have been described in patients with FBs lodged within the cardiac chambers. This condition, known as cardiac neurosis, is characterized by intense and persistent anxiety arising from the awareness of harboring an intracardiac FB. Consequently, patients may develop excessive concern, restrict their daily activities, or avoid physical exercise because of fear of FB migration and potentially fatal complications. Although this phenomenon has been described primarily in patients with intracardiac FBs, it could also occur in patients with IBE involving other vascular locations. In such cases, psychological distress and impaired quality of life may constitute additional factors when considering FB retrieval, particularly when anxiety is persistent or disabling[11,31,32].

In addition to the decision between retrieval and conservative management, adjunctive medical treatment for IBE remains a matter of debate. The roles of antibiotic prophylaxis and systemic anticoagulation in IBE are not clearly established; therefore, their use should be individualized according to each patient’s clinical characteristics[8]. The available evidence does not support the routine use of prophylactic antibiotics specifically for IBE. In patients with clinical signs of infection or sepsis attributable to the projectile, antimicrobial treatment rather than prophylaxis should be initiated, particularly when definitive retrieval is planned[6].

However, because of the potential risk of contamination inherent in GSWs and the limited evidence specific to this condition, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests considering antibiotic prophylaxis for the first 24 hours in selected patients after assessing the projectile trajectory, associated injuries, and risk of contamination. In this context, a first-generation cephalosporin such as cefazolin may represent a reasonable option because of its activity against methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) and susceptible streptococci, as well as against some susceptible strains of potentially involved gram-negative bacilli, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Proteus mirabilis[33]. In patients with a significant beta-lactam allergy, a lincosamide such as clindamycin may be considered an alternative[34,35].

In patients with signs of systemic infection or sepsis presumably attributable to the intravascular projectile, early initiation of empiric broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy may be considered, ideally after obtaining blood cultures. The antimicrobial regimen should be individualized according to infection severity, the suspected source, associated injuries, renal function, allergy history, and local antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. In patients with sepsis, septic shock, or risk of resistant organisms, an empiric regimen consisting of vancomycin and cefepime may be considered to provide coverage against gram-positive organisms, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE), as well as a broad spectrum of gram-negative bacilli. When either of these antimicrobial agents is contraindicated, alternatives such as daptomycin to replace the gram-positive coverage provided by vancomycin and/or aztreonam to replace the gram-negative coverage provided by cefepime may be considered. Once culture and antimicrobial susceptibility results become available, therapy should be adjusted and de-escalated according to the identified microorganism and its susceptibility profile.

Additionally, when the projectile trajectory includes hollow viscus perforation, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests considering expansion of antimicrobial coverage to include anaerobic organisms. In this setting, metronidazole may be added to the initial vancomycin–cefepime regimen or, alternatively, cefepime may be replaced with piperacillin–tazobactam, while maintaining coverage against resistant gram-positive organisms when clinically indicated. Given the limited evidence specific to IBE, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests considering a minimum of 2 weeks of antimicrobial therapy in patients with infection or bacteremia attributable to the intravascular projectile, provided that the isolated organism is susceptible to the initial regimen and a favorable clinical response is observed. In patients with persistent bacteremia despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, treatment may be extended for up to 4 weeks, with serial blood cultures and definitive FB retrieval using an endovascular, hybrid, or open approach when technically feasible, as part of source control[34–37] (Table 5). Regarding anticoagulation in patients with IBE, the available evidence is limited, and no standardized regimen has been established. Therapeutic anticoagulation for up to 12 months has been described in asymptomatic patients with pulmonary embolization of bullets or shrapnel fragments[6]. Similarly, systemic anticoagulation for 3 months has been proposed in selected patients with projectiles retained within the cardiac chambers, with the theoretical aim of promoting fibrous encapsulation of partially intracavitary projectiles and reducing the risk of migration or other potential complications[38]. These durations are derived from isolated reports and should not be interpreted as standardized regimens.

In patients with IBE who are hemodynamically stable or have responded adequately to resuscitation, with no evidence of active bleeding or other contraindications to anticoagulation, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests considering systemic anticoagulation during the period preceding definitive FB retrieval, after an individualized assessment of thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks. The proposed objective is to reduce the risk of thrombus formation or propagation associated with the presence of an intravascular FB, although the specific evidence supporting this strategy remains limited.

During hospitalization, unfractionated heparin (UFH) may be considered a preferred option for therapeutic anticoagulation in critically ill patients admitted to the ICU, particularly when urgent surgical or endovascular procedures may be required. Its rapid onset of action when administered intravenously, short half-life, and the ability to neutralize its effect with protamine allow anticoagulation to be rapidly interrupted and adjusted when clinically necessary. The indication for and intensity of anticoagulation should be periodically reassessed according to the patient’s clinical course and bleeding risk.

Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), such as enoxaparin, may be considered a valid alternative in hemodynamically stable patients with a low likelihood of requiring urgent intervention. However, its use has certain limitations in this setting. When a surgical or endovascular procedure is planned, therapeutic anticoagulation with LMWH should generally be discontinued at least 24 hours in advance, although longer intervals may be required depending on renal function, bleeding risk, and procedural characteristics. In addition, its anticoagulant effect can only be partially reversed with protamine, and its use requires caution or dose adjustment in patients with renal impairment[39].

After definitive FB retrieval, once hemostasis has been secured and in the absence of another indication for continued therapeutic anticoagulation, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests discontinuing therapeutic anticoagulation and initiating thromboprophylaxis based on individual thrombotic and bleeding risks. In hemodynamically stable patients with adequate renal function, LMWH, such as enoxaparin, may be considered. UFH may be an alternative for patients with severe renal impairment or when additional urgent procedures may be required. When pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis is temporarily contraindicated, mechanical thromboprophylaxis may be considered until the bleeding risk decreases.

Thromboprophylaxis should be continued while immobility and thrombotic risk factors related to trauma, surgery, or hospitalization persist, with periodic reassessment of its indication. In patients with an independent indication for therapeutic anticoagulation, such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), or atrial fibrillation, among other conditions, treatment should follow the recommendations specific to that condition, taking into account bleeding risk, renal function, and the patient’s clinical characteristics[40,41]. In carefully selected patients for whom conservative management is chosen, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests considering systemic anticoagulation after individually assessing thrombotic and bleeding risks. We suggest initiating overlapping anticoagulation with UFH or LMWH, administered concomitantly with warfarin, for a minimum of 5 days and until the INR has remained within the therapeutic range for at least 2 consecutive days. Both conditions should be met before discontinuing UFH or LMWH.

Given the absence of standardized regimens specific to IBE, our consensus proposes considering warfarin therapy for 6 months in cases of VBE and for up to 12 months in cases of ABE, with outpatient reassessment every 2 months by a trauma or vascular surgeon, in conjunction with an anticoagulation specialist or through an anticoagulation clinic[39,42,43]. The duration of therapy and frequency of follow-up represent a proposal from our multidisciplinary consensus and should be adapted according to projectile location, clinical course, and the individual thrombotic and bleeding risk profile.

We also suggest complementing each follow-up visit with serial imaging studies selected according to the location of the FB to confirm the absence of migration and to allow early detection of thrombosis or other complications. Doppler ultrasonography may be used for FBs located in accessible segments of the peripheral circulation, whereas transthoracic echocardiography may be used for those located within the cardiac chambers. However, when these imaging modalities do not provide an adequate assessment, particularly for FBs located in the pulmonary circulation or the intrathoracic, intra-abdominal, or pelvic great vessels, transesophageal echocardiography or CT angiography may be considered according to FB location and the patient’s clinical characteristics[1,3,9,21,22].

In patients managed conservatively who complete the established period of clinical and imaging follow-up without symptoms or evidence of FB migration, thrombosis, or other FB-related complications, our multidisciplinary consensus suggests discontinuing systemic anticoagulation, provided that no other indication for continued anticoagulation exists. In selected patients with a retained intravascular FB, antiplatelet therapy with acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) may be considered on a long-term or indefinite basis after individually assessing thrombotic and bleeding risks, and provided that no contraindications are present. Given the absence of specific evidence supporting this strategy in IBE, its indication should be periodically reassessed and regarded as an expert-consensus recommendation rather than a universal standard of care.

Conversely, if the patient develops symptoms, thrombosis, infection, or other complications during follow-up, or if FB migration is documented on imaging studies, our consensus suggests reconsidering retrieval, preferably using an endovascular approach. A hybrid or open approach may be used when percutaneous retrieval is not technically feasible, is contraindicated, or has failed, following an individualized risk–benefit assessment and after obtaining informed consent from the patient[39–41].

It is important to emphasize that the diagnostic and therapeutic recommendations proposed in this article were developed by a multidisciplinary expert panel comprising infectious disease specialists, cardiologists, vascular surgeons, and trauma and critical care surgeons from three Level I trauma centers. These recommendations were formulated in response to the limited evidence available for the management of IBE. They were adapted to the local epidemiology, available resources, socioeconomic context, and regional antimicrobial resistance patterns. Consequently, although we believe these recommendations may provide a practical framework to support clinical decision-making, they should be interpreted with caution, individualized for each patient, and adapted to the institutional protocols and available resources of each trauma center.

The main strengths of this study include the presentation of a multicenter case series of three patients with IBE, a comprehensive review of the available literature, and the development of expert consensus–based diagnostic and therapeutic recommendations for the management of this uncommon entity. In addition, we propose a practical diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm based on the best available evidence and the multidisciplinary experience of specialists from three Level I trauma centers.

This study also has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective multicenter case series comprising only three patients, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Second, the rarity of IBE has resulted in a paucity of comparative studies and high-quality evidence. Consequently, several of our recommendations are based on published case reports, case series, literature reviews, and the consensus of our multidisciplinary expert panel. Finally, although all patients achieved favorable clinical outcomes, larger multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods, particularly among patients managed conservatively, are needed to validate the proposed recommendations and the diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm.

Conclusion

IBE is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of penetrating trauma, associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Its diagnosis remains challenging and requires a high index of clinical suspicion. In patients with symptomatic ABE or VBE, projectile retrieval should be considered because of the risk of complications. When retrieval is pursued and technically feasible, an endovascular approach may be considered as an initial strategy. However, hybrid or open surgical approaches may be required to achieve definitive retrieval in selected patients.

In asymptomatic patients, management should be individualized according to the type of embolization, projectile characteristics, anatomical location, the patient’s clinical condition, and the anticipated risk–benefit balance of intervention. Based on the available evidence and our multidisciplinary expert consensus, retrieval may be considered when the anticipated benefits outweigh the procedural risks, whereas conservative management may represent a reasonable alternative in carefully selected patients with appropriate clinical and imaging follow-up.
