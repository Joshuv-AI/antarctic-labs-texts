Trigeminal neuralgia caused by a transfixed arterial loop: illustrative case
Authors: Sydney Kukla, Abdulrahman Albakr, Mohammadmahdi Sabahi, Natalia Jedlinska-Obrzut, Badih Adada, Hamid Borghei-Razavi

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is commonly caused by vascular compression of the trigeminal nerve at the root entry zone (REZ), by an artery or, less frequently, a vein. Other causes include vascular lesions or tumors.1 Among the offending arteries, the superior cerebellar artery (SCA) is the most commonly implicated, with other reported vessels including the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) and the basilar artery.2

Although vascular compression of TN is most often extraneural, few cases have been described in which intraneural vessels are responsible for the symptoms, creating a unique management challenge. Helbig et al. proposed a classification for intraneural vessels in TN.3 Type 1 occurs when the vessel runs between the motor and sensory branch. Type 2 occurs when the vessel runs through the trigeminal nerve between the fibers of the sensory branch.3 We present a unique case of a type 2 intraneural compression of the trigeminal nerve by a loop of the SCA. In addition, we review the relevant literature describing intraneural arterial compression and explore strategies for management of this unusual presentation of TN.

Illustrative Case

A 77-year-old male with a 30-year history of TN in the right V2 and V3 distribution presented for evaluation of worsening and unrelenting pain. The patient’s pain consisted of classic electric shocks, exacerbated by eating, talking, and brushing his teeth. He had tried multiple medical treatments. At the time of initial evaluation, he was unable to speak despite medical management with high doses of gabapentin and baclofen. He had lost significant weight and had a poor quality of life due to the intensity of the pain. MRI with constructive interference in steady-state (CISS) sequencing revealed a right SCA loop bisecting the trigeminal nerve at the REZ with bifurcation of the nerve (Fig. 1).

A right retrosigmoid craniotomy was performed. After freeing the petrosal vein off the shoulder of the trigeminal nerve, we identified the SCA, which was freed from the arachnoid attachment to the brainstem. When following the artery distally, we noticed the loop as it inserted itself into the sensory branch of the trigeminal nerve at the REZ. Further inspection revealed no other arterial compression. We identified the artery proximal and distal to the intraneural loop (Fig. 2). Multiple attempts made to mobilize the vessel and free the intraneural loop from the nerve were unsuccessful. We performed internal neurolysis between the fascicles of the trigeminal nerve at the level of the intraneural loop (Video 1). Given this structural challenge and to avoid numbness, the extent of neurolysis was minimized. We also placed a Teflon pledget between the proximal part of the SCA and medial surface of the trigeminal nerve. A second Teflon pledget was placed between the artery distal to the intraneural loop and the nerve.

The surgery was uneventful, and his postoperative course was unremarkable. The patient woke up completely pain free. At the 7-month follow-up, he had a Barrow Neurological Institute pain intensity score of I. He reported being able to eat, brush his teeth, and talk without any pain and had no facial numbness.

Discussion

TN is most commonly attributed to extraneural vascular compression, but rare instances of intraneural compression have been documented. Helbig’s classification delineates two types of intraneural vascular compression: type 1, where the vessel penetrates between the motor (portio minor) and sensory (portio major) roots, and type 2, where the vessel penetrates between the sensory fibers of the portio major.3 These cases present unique surgical challenges due to the difficulty in isolating/mobilizing the offending vessel along with the risk of nerve injury.

In our case, we observed a loop of the SCA transfixing the trigeminal nerve, an example of a type 2 intraneural vascular compression per Helbig’s classification. Preoperative MRI findings correlated with intraoperative observation. After performing internal neurolysis, we observed that manipulation of the arterial loop embedded in the nerve resulted in movement of the proximal free segment, further indicating continuity of the artery. In addition, we were able to create space between the REZ and the proximal segment of SCA prior to its insertion into the nerve. The placement of the Teflon pledget at this location to minimize contact between the artery proximal to the intraneural loop and the nerve resulted in complete postoperative pain relief without facial numbness. However, complete isolation and mobilization of the transfixing SCA segment was not attempted because its deep intraneural course and strong adherence to the nerve would have required more aggressive internal neurolysis/mobilization and placed the patient at a much higher risk for nerve injury and numbness. Lastly, while the artery did not clearly penetrate through the nerve from one side to the other as in other reported cases, intraoperative visualization showed an arterial loop within the nerve, suggesting a compression from within the nerve and around it.

There have been few reports of intraneural vascular compression in TN. We present a comprehensive review of the literature, focusing specifically on all documented arterial causes of intraneural compression, summarized in Table 1. We identified 13 cases of arterial intraneural compression. Six of the reported cases of intraneural compression were caused by SCA, only 2 of which were type 2 compressions. The AICA is the next most implicated artery, followed by the trigeminocerebellar artery (TCA). The TCA is a variant branch of the basilar artery that supplies both the trigeminal nerve root and the cerebellar hemisphere.1

Reported cases of TN with intraneural arteries

Jito and Nozaki emphasized that decompression at the REZ is the most effective treatment if the compression occurs in this area, and Sindou et al. also identified this region as critical cause for TN pathology.1,4 Jito and Nozaki report that when the REZ is involved, displacing the vessel away from this zone is the most effective method of pain relief. However, in cases in which the REZ is not the sole area of compression, longitudinal dissection of the nerve root or epineurium to move the vessel away from the REZ may not be sufficient to obtain pain relief. Furthermore, they caution that partial transection of the nerve root perpendicular to the nerve axis near the vessel penetration site and separation of the artery from the nerve can result in a postoperative facial sensory deficit. Thus, they propose that in type 1 transfixation, dissection only of the epineurium of the trigeminal nerve branch should be performed to avoid injury to sensory nerve fibers. In type 2 cases, on the other hand, at least a longitudinal dissection of the sensory root may be acceptable to achieve pain relief.1 Further support for the importance of the REZ and adjacent segments comes from Tashiro et al.,5 who studied 384 cases of TN, including 3 cases with intraneural vascular conflict. They proposed that the etiology of TN lies in vascular compression at the Obersteiner-Redlich (OR) zone, which is located approximately 2.2 mm from the trigeminal REZ. In all 3 intraneural cases, the authors observed that the compressing vessel transfixed the nerve within this junctional zone, reinforcing its relevance. Surgical intervention in 2 of these cases included mobilization of the artery out of the nerve by doing a partial rhizotomy perpendicular to the axis of the nerve, which resulted in facial numbness. For the third case, internal neurolysiswith repositioning of the offending vessel distally away from the OR zone was performed, supporting the notion that the anatomical location of the compression strongly informs operative decision-making.5

Another strategy for the management of intraneural vessels was presented by Zheng et al.,2 who hypothesized that merely enlarging the intraneural arterial passage would not suffice in resolving symptoms. In 3 cases of type 1 penetrations with the SCA, they wrapped the artery with a thin piece of Teflon. Interestingly, the authors noted that this intervention seemingly increased the tension on the trigeminal nerve branches, which is against the notion of decompression but rather indicates addition of mechanical compression. This observation led them to propose that insulation may be more critical than decompression in treating TN.2 Similarly, Zhang et al.6 described a type 2 penetration case in which a shredded Teflon implant was placed between the artery and nerve in a way that shifts the offending vessel perpendicular to the nerve fibers. If necessary, they suggest vessel mobilization distally along the nerve by slinging the artery to the bone.6 These theories are opposed by Goto and Inoue,7 who caution against inserting foreign materials, such as Teflon, between the penetrating vessel and the nerve, citing a risk of adhesions and reoccurrence due to reactive changes. Their surgical approach for a type 1 penetration favored dissecting between motor and sensory roots, carefully isolating the offending artery, and then repositioning the artery together with the motor root away from the sensory root. This construct was then suspended to the cerebellar tentorium using a Teflon sling secured with fibrin glue, thereby maintaining vessel separation while minimizing manipulation of the nerve’s sensory components.7

Patrick reported a case in which longitudinal dissection allowed the artery to move freely in its passage through the nerve; however, pain later recurred.8 Three additional cases were reported to achieve complete pain relief. Watanabe et al. performed longitudinal dissection of the epineurium in order to obtain sufficient mobilization of the artery.9 Furuse et al. treated a type 2 penetration with longitudinal dissection, followed by anchoring of the artery to the tentorium and placement of a prosthesis.10 Finally, Nakipuria et al. performed transposition of the artery away from the nerve and Teflon placement.11

Observations

Our case presents a complex anatomical variation of intraneural compression. The SCA appeared to loop deep within the trigeminal nerve, potentially traveling in a direction partially parallel to the fibers. This differs from previously reported cases in which the arteries make clear penetrations transversely or longitudinally through the nerve. This configuration further complicated surgical intervention due to its stiffness/adherence and highlights the need for tailored surgical strategies based on distinct vascular entry pattern.

We propose an approach-specific algorithm for intraneural vascular compression. In a type 1 penetration (between the motor and sensory roots), we suggest splitting the nerve longitudinally and separating the roots. We recommend following this by mobilization of the motor root and artery together away from the sensory root. For type 2 penetrations (through the sensory root), we propose two options. The first is to perform targeted internal neurolysis to separate the fascicles and displace the artery distally, away from the OR zone. Although not recommended, this approach may be considered for patients with refractory TN who are willing to accept the risk of facial numbness. In such cases, a horizontal partial rhizotomy can be performed to mobilize the artery away from the nerve. The latter option, however, should be chosen cautiously and perhaps only if the patient is more concerned about disease recurrence than sensory loss. In both penetration types, placement of Teflon padding can be considered in addition to the proposed surgical techniques. In our patient, achieving complete decompression of the intraneural artery would likely have required more extensive neurolysis in order to achieve full access to the artery. While this approach could have allowed complete decompression of the artery, we would have placed the patient at a much higher risk of facial numbness. We opted for a conservative approach, balancing preservation of function with decompression, focusing on internal neurolysis and placement of Teflon at the REZ. Encouragingly, the patient experienced complete pain relief without neurological deficits and remains highly satisfied with the outcome.

Lessons

This case has deepened our understanding of the surgical management of TN caused by intraneural arterial compression. We learned that treatment must be individualized, depending on the penetration pattern of the vessel relative to the nerve. Decisions about the extent of neurolysis or nerve transection must balance symptom relief with preservation of function. It is essential to discuss the available surgical strategies with patients, outlining both risks and benefits. Understanding patient priorities, such as preferring the risk of numbness over pain reoccurrence, or vice versa, can guide more informed, personalized decision-making.

Although the patient experienced sustained pain relief at 7 months postoperatively, longer-term follow-up is necessary to confirm the durability of this approach. Future studies with extended follow-up and larger cohorts are needed to establish the long-term safety and efficacy of these techniques.
