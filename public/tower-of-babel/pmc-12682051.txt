Looking at bacterial cell poles from a liquid-liquid phase separation of intrinsically disordered proteins perspective
Authors: Raneem Aldadah, Delila Lekic, Kanita Sabanovic, Boris Kovalenko, Muhamed Adilovic, Altijana Hromic-Jahjefendic, Vladimir N Uversky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial cell poles play a fundamental role in several cellular processes, such as cell cycle, chemotaxis, cell differentiation, development, growth, and structure of the bacterial cell, as well as protein localization. Using a set of bioinformatics tools, we evaluated the probability of 19 bacterial cell pole-related proteins to be disordered and undergo spontaneous liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). Our analysis revealed that11 cell pole-related proteins are predicted to be highly disordered, 7 proteins are moderately disordered, and only one protein is expected to be highly ordered. Furthermore, this intrinsic disorder propensity is mostly evolutionary conserved. Most of the analyzed 19 cell pole-related proteins were found to be associated with the LLPS process, with TipN, PopZ, and PBP2A being capable of spontaneous phase separation, RacA, Noc, PBP1A/1B, ParB, PBP3, PBP2B, FtsZ, MipZ, MinD, and MreB being expected to potentially serve as droplet clients, and with the remaining proteins (DivIVA, ComN, Maf, PBP4, MinC, and MinJ) being predicted to be unrelated to LLPS. The results suggested that FtsZ, DivIVA, and MiPZ serve as the main regulatory proteins, being well-known for their role in forming the septum and chromosomal segregation. Furthermore, PopZ and TipN proteins contribute to high stress resistance. Clarifying the function and effects of each mechanism gives insight into the organization of bacterial cells and some strategies for antimicrobial targets.Keywords: Bacterial cell poles, Intrinsically disordered proteins, Liquid-liquid phase separation, Bioinformatics analysis, Structural organization, Cellular functions

Introduction

Bacterial cell poles serve a number of important roles in the structural organization of the cell. They trigger the localization of essential proteins needed for certain sites that regulate cellular processes, cell differentiation, chromosome dynamics, cell motility, and development by regulating the cell cycle using certain proteins that are localized in the area. Previously, it was thought that the bacterial proteins were evenly distributed within the cellular interior. However, it is now understood that they have high spatial organization, where the proteins are asymmetrically localized. Two key discoveries contributed to this discovery, namely that bacterial chemoreceptors and signaling proteins were found to localize at the cell poles in two different bacterial species, and the cell division protein FtsZ was found to localize at the division site (Treuner-Lange and Sogaard-Andersen, 2014). Bacilli, or rod-shaped bacterial cells, have a very distinct feature on the poles of the cell, where they localize different types of proteins based on their biochemical and structural properties. This feature has proven to be quite important for cell division, depending on the exact type of bacterial cell, whether it goes through asymmetrical or symmetrical division, which is how different proteins are localized. Bacterial cell poles also serve as important centers for various sensory systems, acting in signal transduction and environmental navigation; furthermore, they participate in integration of extracellular activities and modulation of intracellular activities. Polar proteins have also found their specific roles in genome segregation, cell elongation, adherence, movement, and even virulence (Chaudhary Mishra et al., 2021).

Bacterial cell division begins with the assembly of the divisome complex at the midpoint, followed by the constriction of the cell wall and the formation of the septum. The positioning of the mid-cell site for septum formation is influenced by the gradient of oscillating Min proteins at the poles (3). One of the regulator proteins, MipZ, located on the pole, was found to have an an important role in negative regulation and inhibition of the FtsZ polymerization (Thanbichler and Shapiro, 2008), which is a cytoskeletal protein found in the mid-cell position. There are other proteins, namely MreB and PBPs, that are crucial in the formation of the poles during septal growth (Chaudhary Mishra et al., 2021).

Peptidoglycan is an essential protein component of every bacterial cell wall; it maintains its shape and prevents turgor (Pazos and Peters, 2019). However, in the study done by Sundararajan et al., FtsZ has been proposed to influence and regulate the peptidoglycan metabolism and, in the end, affect the overall shape of the bacterial cell envelope (Sundararajan Miguel et al., 2015). Studies done on Bacillus subtilis show the correlation between FtsZ and MinC, where a FtsZ ring assembles near the newly formed septum, leading to a reaction from MinC and its ultimate disassembly, so the new cell poles do not form immediately (Gregory Becker et al., 2008).

Since cell proteins tend to be attracted to either highly convex or highly concave membrane curvatures, DivIVA, a component of the Min protein system, has been found to react to the concave surfaces of the septum, therefore making its appearance in the formation of the poles (Chaudhary Mishra et al., 2021). DivIVA homologs have also been found to interact with chromosome segregation in Actinomycetes (Altinoglu Merrifield et al., 2019). The way DivIVA acts is through the recruitment of several other components, including ComN – the competence regulator, Maf – the division inhibitor, MinCD, and MinJ (Laloux and Jacobs-Wagner, 2014). It can also attach itself to the chromosomal origin at the pole and bind a DNA-binding protein RacA (Laloux and Jacobs-Wagner, 2014). In alpha-proteobacteria, a self-organizing cytoplasmic protein PopZ and a membrane-bound protein TipN have crucial roles in chromosome segregation (Altinoglu Merrifield et al., 2019). In a study done by Holmes et al., PopZ was proven to interact with more than eight other proteins involved in chromosome segregation and cell cycle regulation (Holmes Follett et al., 2016). TipN was also mentioned by Vallet et al., due to its role in the bacterial cell membrane, where it builds resistance to the cell envelope-targeting antibiotics, which led to the discovery of TipN having a role in maintaining the cell envelope stress resistance, as well as having intracellular development roles (Vallet Hansen et al., 2020).

This study delves into the role of intrinsically disordered proteins and how they contribute to the formation and organization of the bacterial cell poles and regulate other cellular processes of the cell. A set of bioinformatics tools is used to check the hypothesis that intrinsic disorder and the associated liquid-liquid separation process contribute to the biological roles of proteins related to the bacterial cell. Applying a computational approach, the study seeks to determine different levels of effects of several IDPs on bacterial cell poles' organization.

Methodology

Protein selection

This research specifically highlights the prevalence and functionality of intrinsic disorder in proteins related to bacterial poles. Based on corresponding research, we selected 19 proteins linked to the formation of bacterial poles. The main focus of this study is on the corresponding proteins from B. subtilis, whose structural and functional information was taken from the UniProt database (UniProt, 2025). However, since B. subtilis does not have TipN, PopZ, ParB, and MipZ, these proteins were taken from Brevundimonas subvibrioides, Caulobacter vibrioides, Myxococcus xanthus, and Profundibacterium mesophilum, respectively. The disorder propensity of these proteins was compared to that of the entire B. subtilis proteome (4190 reviewed entries found in UniProt on March 05, 2025 using Taxonomy ID 224308). To check the evolutionary conservation of the intrinsic disorder propensity of the bacterial pole proteins, we also assembled 19 sets of related proteins, which were found in UniProt using the corresponding protein and gene names.

Bioinformatic analysis

There are two different bioinformatics tools that were used in this study to analyze the intrinsic disorder and LLPS predispositions of query proteins. Intrinsic disorder potential was analyzed using the Rapid Intrinsic Disorder Analysis Online (RIDAO) platform (Dayhoff and Uversky, 2022). This tool, which can be used for the analysis of individual proteins and large protein datasets, assembles the outputs of six commonly used per-residue disorder predictors, namely PONDR® VLXT, PONDR® VSL2B, PONDR® VL3, IUPred-Short, IUPred-Long, and PONDR® FIT. It also generates a mean disorder profile (MDP) by averaging the outputs of individual predictors. Outputs of the corresponding per-residue predictors can be used for the classification of proteins based on their intrinsic disorder potential. Here, the outputs of the predictors for individual proteins are first used to calculate the corresponding percent of predicted disordered residues (PPDR) values and the average disorder score (ADS) values. Then, the PPDR values are used to classify proteins as highly ordered, moderately disordered, and highly disordered, if their corresponding PPDR values are below 10 %, between 10 % and 30 %, and above 30 %, respectively (Uversky, 2020). Alternatively, protein ADS values are used for protein classification as highly ordered, moderately disordered/flexible, or highly disordered if their ADS <0.15, 0.15 ≤ ADS <0.5, and ADS ≥0.5 (Uversky, 2020). For each query protein, RIDAO also generates the charge-hydropathy (CH) and cumulative distribution function (CDF) data for construction of the CH-CDF plot that is used for the additional classification of intrinsic disorder status in a query protein based on its position within the CH-CDF phase space. Proteins in Quadrant I can be categorized as ordered (predicted to be ordered by both models), Quadrant II can be categorized as hybrid proteins or putative native molten globules (ordered by CH but disordered by CDF), Quadrant III can be categorized as native coils or pre-molten globules (disordered by both methods), and Quadrant IV can be categorized as proteins that are disordered according to the CH plot but ordered by the CDF plot (Uversky, 2020).

To evaluate the propensity of each protein to undergo spontaneous liquid-liquid phase separation or to serve as liquid droplet client, we utilized the FuzDrop platform that provides the probability of forming a droplet state (pLLPS) (Hardenberg Horvath et al., 2020). Proteins with a pLLPS of ≥0.60 were classified as droplet-drivers, meaning they can independently undergo LLPS. On the other hand, proteins with pLLPS < 0.60 but containing droplet-promoting regions (DPRs) were classified as droplet-clients, with DPR(s) inducing partitioning of such proteins into condensates. Droplet-promoting regions were identified based on the residue-level droplet-promoting probabilities (pDP), as regions containing at least five consecutive residues scoring pDP ≥ 0.60 (Hardenberg Horvath et al., 2020).

3D structural models for 19 bacterial cell pole-related proteins were generated by AlphaFold (Jumper Evans et al., 2021). We have opted to use all structures from AlphaFold, even though some proteins have their structures solved experimentally, to ensure consistency. Furthermore, the experimentally determined 3D structure often lack information about flexible and disordered regions, which are commonly described as regions with missing electron density or removed during preparation of a protein sample for crystallization. Additionally, not all direct homologs were found B. subtilis genome. As a result, we analyzed 15 proteins from B. subtilis, and four from other organisms: TipN protein from B. subvibrioides, PopZ protein from C. vibrioides, ParB protein from M. xanthus, and MipZ protein from P. mesophilum. Importantly, these four proteins (TipN, PopZ, ParB, and MipZ) are well-characterized intrinsically disordered proteins, which are common among bacteria. They have been selected from other model organisms, based on literature evidence that highlights their function. Corresponding models were colored based on the per-residue model confidence (pLDDT), which ranges between 0 and 100. Regions with very high (pLDDT > 90), high (90 > pLDDT > 70), low (70 > pLDDT > 50), and very low model confidence (pLDDT < 50) are shown by blue, cyan, yellow, and orange colors, respectively. Regions with low pLDDT may be unstructured in isolation (Jumper Evans et al., 2021).

Results

Intrinsic disorder status of the 19 bacterial cell pole-related proteins

Table 2 and Fig. 1 summarize the results of the intrinsic disorder predisposition analysis of 19 proteins known to be associated with bacterial cell poles. Therefore, all these proteins contain noticeable levels of intrinsic disorder.

Further support of the abundant presence of intrinsic disorder in 19 proteins known to be associated with the bacterial cell poles is provided by Fig. 2 which shows that most of these proteins have regions whose structure was predicted with low confidence. It is also important to note that some of the modelled structures contain rather unrealistic structural elements, such as long isolated α-helices. It is likely that such long isolated α-helices are artificial and rather represent induced structures originating from the interaction of the corresponding proteins with their binding partners. It is known that being a generative method, AlphaFold can be subjected to hallucinations, which is manifested in the generation of plausible-looking structures in regions, where the real protein is actually unstructured.

Next, we looked for the information on the solved structures of the bacterial cell pole proteins analyzed in this study. No structural information was found for the 855-residue-long TipN, which is not surprising taking into account the highly disordered nature of this protein. Structures of the penicillin-binding protein 1A/1B (PBP1A/1B), penicillin-binding protein 2 (PBP2), penicillin-binding protein 2B (PBP2B), penicillin-binding protein 3 (PBP3), penicillin-binding protein 4 (PBP4), post-transcriptional regulator ComN, division plane positioning ATPase MipZ, septum site-determining protein MinD, cell shape-determining protein MreB, and cell division topological determinant MinJ are not known as well. On the other hand, for highly disordered bacterial polar organizing protein Z, PopZ NMR solution n structure was solved for a C-terminally truncated form of PopZ, PopZ Δ134-177, that does not self-assemble but retains the ability to interact with heterologous proteins (PDB ID: 6XRY) (Nordyke Ahmed et al., 2020). This analysis revealed that the unbound PopZ Δ134-177 is unstructured in solution, with the exception of a small amphipathic α-helix (residues M10-I17), which, together with the adjoining amino acids extending to position E23 served as the core of the binding motif for interaction with RcdA (Nordyke Ahmed et al., 2020). This region is predicted as an α-helix by AlphaFold, which also shows the presence of two α-helices in the C-terminal tail of the protein. Notably, these all these structural elements predicted by AlphaFold do not form a hydrophobic core, being completely isolated.

X-ray crystallographic analysis of the 184-residue-long chromosome-anchoring protein RacA in the complex with a 14mer palindromic centromere DNA revealed that only the DNA-binding domain (residues 1–64) was visible for each RacA subunit (PDB ID: 5I44), with the remaining part of the protein containing a flexible linker region and a coiled-coil domain being spontaneously removed during the extended time required for crystal growth (Schumacher Lee et al., 2016). Curiously, the coiled-coil domain of this protein is predicted by AlphaFold as a long isolated α-helix (see Fig. 2). In the resulting X-ray crystal structure, multiple RacA molecules bind to three DNA duplexes, which are stacked end-to-end to form a pseudocontinuous helix with the protein wrapping it (Schumacher Lee et al., 2016). Each 14mer palindrome site of DNA is contacted by two RacA molecules forming a weak dimer on the DNA through contacts between their two fold related wings (strands β1 (28–32) and β2 (34–38) of each monomer) (Schumacher Lee et al., 2016). The X-ray crystal structure was also solved for the isolated apo-form of this DNA-binding domain (PDB ID: 5I41) (Schumacher Lee et al., 2016).

Nucleoid occlusion protein Noc is evolutionary related to partitioning protein B (ParB, see below). This 283-residue-long, DNA binding protein possesses three functional domains, an N-terminal cytidine triphosphate (CTP) binding domain for protein-protein interactions and for targeting Noc to the cell membrane (residues 1–128), a central DNA-binding domain (DBD, residues 129–228), and a C-terminal dimerization domain (residues 244–283) (Wu and Errington, 2004; Wu Ishikawa et al., 2009). Crystal structure was solved for the DNA-binding domain of Noc (residues 111–242) (PDB ID: 6Y93) (Jalal Tran et al., 2020) and for the C-terminally truncated protein NocΔCTD (residues 1–242) (PDB ID: 7OL9) (Sukhoverkov Jalal et al., 2023) both cocrystallized with the Noc-binding site (NBS) DNA. In the NocΔCTD-NBS structure, two NocΔCTD were found to bind to a single 16-bp NBS DNA. The N-terminal segment (residues 1–27) of each monomer containing the membrane-targeting peptide was poorly resolved indicating its structural flexibility (Sukhoverkov Jalal et al., 2023). Furthermore, in each DNA-bound Noc subunit, the N-terminal CTP-binding domain was disengaged from the DNA-binding domain, likely due to the swapping of helices 4 and 5 at the interface of the two domains (Sukhoverkov Jalal et al., 2023).

Chromosome-partitioning protein ParB is a 296-residue-long component of the chromosome segregation machinery (Iniesta, 2014) that possesses CTP hydrolytic activity (Osorio-Valeriano Altegoer et al., 2019; Osorio-Valeriano Altegoer et al., 2021) and acts as a DNA-sliding clampt loaded at the short palindromic sequence (parS) sites in the centromere of target DNA molecules and spread laterally to form large nucleoprotein complexes serving as docking points for the DNA segregation machinery (Osorio-Valeriano Altegoer et al., 2021). Similar to Noc, ParB is capable of CTP binding via the N-terminally located ParB/Srx nucleotide-binding domain (NBD, residues 39–128). It also contains central helix-turn-helix parS-binding domain (PBD, residues 134–230) connected to the C-terminal dimerization domain (CTD, residues 247–296) by an intrinsically disordered linker (residues 223–249) (Funnell, 2016). A CTP-dependent DNA-sliding clamp is formed due to the homodimerization of ParB via the domain swapping mechanism (Osorio-Valeriano Altegoer et al., 2019). This clamp is transformed into a closed sliding ring at the DNA and CTP binding that spread one-dimensionally over about 33 kb of DNA centred on the parS cluster (Osorio-Valeriano Altegoer et al., 2019). The X-ray crystal structure was solved for the NBD and PBD-containing fragment (residues 34–246) of ParB from Myxococcus xanthus (strain DK1622) (Osorio-Valeriano Altegoer et al., 2021). Residues 34–36 and 230–246 were highly flexible and constituted regions of missing electron density. Furthermore, it was indicated that a long disordered region linking NBD and PBD plays an important role in dimer formation, as the two polypeptide chains cross over at this linker region, so that helix α4 of each chain is placed next to helix α5 of the trans-subunit (Osorio-Valeriano Altegoer et al., 2021).

Septum site-determining protein DivIVA is a 164-residue-long coiled-coil, tropomyosin-like protein, which is a part of the MinCD/DivIVA system responsible for positioning of the division cite at the midcell in vegetative cells (Jacobs and Shapiro, 1999). This protein was also shown to be a moonlighting protein that has a distinct function in chromosome segregation during sporulation being involved in interaction with the chromosome segregation machinery and playing a role in localizing or retaining the oriC region of the chromosome near the pole of the sporulating cell (Thomaides Freeman et al., 2001). Crystal structure of the N-terminal domain (residues 1–57) was determined (PDB ID: 2WUJ) (Oliva Halbedel et al., 2010), in which residues 1–2 and 53–57 formed the N- and C-terminal regions of missing electron density. It was also pointed out that the attempts to crystallize a longer fragment (residues 1–65) failed because the C-terminal residues had greater mobility and were not folded into a rigid structure (Oliva Halbedel et al., 2010). The truncated N-terminal domain (residues 1–57) was shown to form a crossed parallel coiled-coil dimer via the coiled-coil helices (residues 23–52) arranged parallel to each other. The N-terminal region of this domain (residues 3–22) formed a short helical segment and a longer loop, with the loops of the two subunits in the dimer being intertwined and exposing F17 (which is crucial for membrane interaction) and R18 from both chains to the solvent (Oliva Halbedel et al., 2010). In a low-resolution crystal structure of the C-terminal domain (residues 65–164), the protein formed large ring structures with a diameter of ∼280 Å (Oliva Halbedel et al., 2010). A low-resolution model of the C-terminal domain of DivIVA represents a tetramer from two parallel coiled-coil dimers, joined in the middle through an antiparallel four-helix bundle (Oliva Halbedel et al., 2010). This structural information for N- and C-terminal domains (residues 3–52 and 72–150, respectively) was used to reconstruct a structural model of a full-length DivIVA, where the two parallel coiled-coil dimers are joined tail-to-tail via teramerization C-terminal domain, resulting in an extended tetramer that spans around 30 nm (Oliva Halbedel et al., 2010). Structure of monomers within this tetramer is similar to AlphaFold-modelled structure of this protein (see Fig. 2).

Cell division protein FtsZ is a 382-residue-long structural homolog of eukaryotic tubulin that forms a Z-ring (contractile ring structure) at the midcell during bacterial division to orchestrate septum formation and cell constriction (Anderson Gueiros-Filho et al., 2004). By acting as a scaffold, this Z-ring recruits other proteins to create a divisome driving the physical constriction to split the cell into two daughter cells. The Z-ring itself is composed of FtsZ protofilaments that are anchored to the membrane by other proteins and constricts through a force-generating mechanism to facilitate cell division (Du and Lutkenhaus, 2019; Osawa and Erickson, 2011). Contrary to earlier views of a continuous, closed circle, advanced imaging with super-resolution microscopy revealed that the Z-ring represents a dynamic, discontinuous structure. It is composed of clusters or patches of FtsZ protofilaments that are tethered to the inner cell membrane via anchor proteins. These clusters move circumferentially around the cell in a dynamic process known as treadmilling (Whitley Jukes et al., 2021; Holden Pengo et al., 2014). It was shown that FtsZ treadmilling serves two critical roles: first, it helps forming the Z-ring by condensing loose FtsZ filaments into a dense structure, and second, it initiates cell constriction by guiding the synthesis of the new cell wall at the septum. In other words, FtsZ treadmilling organizes the Z-ring and then drives the early stages of division by directing the synthesis of the new cell wall (Whitley Jukes et al., 2021). In a high-resolution crystal structure of FstZ, residues 1–10 and 317–382 are highly flexible and form regions of missing electron density (PDB ID: 2VXY) (Haydon Stokes et al., 2008). This is in agreement with the evaluation of the intrinsic disorder predisposition of FtsZ showing that a disordered C-tail (residues 317–382) represents the longest IDR of this protein. This IDR contains two MoRFs (see Table 2) indicating that C-tail plays a crucial role in disorder-based interactivity of this protein.

Multicopy associated filamentation protein Maf (also known as dTTP/UTP pyrophosphatase) is a 189-residue-long protein that contributes to the cell division arrest (Butler Abhayawardhane et al., 1993) and may prevent the incorporation of modified nucleotides into cellular nucleic acids by hydrolysing CTP and the modified nucleotides pseudo-UTP, 5-methyl-CTP (m5CTP) and 5-methyl-UTP (m5UTP) (Tchigvintsev et al., 2013). Curiously, the high resolution crystal structure of this protein was solved before its functional roles were established (PDB ID: 1EX2) (Minasov Teplova et al., 2000). The key structural characteristic of Maf protein is a large, central, three-stranded antiparallel β-sheet (composed of strands β3, β5, and β6). This core, together with strand β7, acts as a bridge between two primary, mostly α-helical sections: the first lobe (which includes helices α1, α2, α6, α7, and strands β1, β2) and the second lobe (containing helices α3, α4, α5, and strand β4) (Minasov Teplova et al., 2000). In this crystal structure, the four C-terminal residues were undefined in the electron density maps, reflecting structural flexibility of this region. Since Maf is an enzyme, the low level of structural disorder in this protein was expected.

Septum site-determining protein MinC is a 226-residue-long cell division inhibitor that blocks the formation of polar Z-ring septums by preventing FtsZ polymerization (Gregory Becker et al., 2008). MinC is a part of the Min system that includes MinC, MinD, and MinE (in Escherichia coli, DivIVA in B. subtilis) and that spatially regulates the Z-ring formation (Rothfield Taghbalout et al., 2005; Barak and Wilkinson, 2007). Here, MinC acts as the primary division inhibitor, whereas MinD tethers MinC to the membrane (de Boer Crossley et al., 1991). Although Min system functions in many bacterial species, its mechanisms of action is species-dependent. In fact, although in E. coli, the MinC and MinD proteins exhibit a dynamic oscillation between the cell poles with a periodicity of ∼50 s (Hu and Lutkenhaus, 1999; Raskin and de Boer, 1999), in B. subtilis, the MinCD system is static, being localized to the septum late in the cell division process and remaining anchored at the new cell poles through its interaction with the DivIVA protein (Marston Thomaides et al., 1998; Marston and Errington, 1999). In both scenarios, MinCD division inhibitors reach their highest time-averaged concentrations at the cell poles. This effectively prevents cell division from occurring at the poles while still permitting it at mid-cell (Rothfield Taghbalout et al., 2005; Ryan and Shapiro, 2003; Lutkenhaus, 2007). NMR solution structure was solved for the N-terminal domain (residues 1–102) of MinC from B. subtilis (PDB ID: 2M4I) (PDB DOI: https://doi.org/10.2210/pdb2M4I/pdb).

Disorder-based functionality and LLPS potential of the 19 bacterial cell pole-related proteins

Table 2 summarizes the results on the bioinformatics analysis of the potential functional roles of IDRs found in 19 proteins known to be associated with the bacterial cell pole. One of the most common functional roles of IDRs is their involvement in interaction with specific partners. Often, such disorder-based interactions are accompanied by disorder-to-order transitions in corresponding regions, which are defined as molecular recognition features (MoRFs) (Oldfield Cheng et al., 2005). We used the ANCHOR2 facility of the IUPred2A platform (Erdos and Dosztanyi, 2020) to find such disordered binding regions. This analysis revealed that MoRFs are present in TipN, PopZ, PBP1A/1B, PBP2A, PBP2B, and FtsZ (see Table 2) indicating that these proteins might utilize their IDRs for interaction with their partners. Table 2 also illustrates that, based on their high pLLPS values exceeding the 0.6 threshold, TipN, PopZ, and PBP2A are capable of undergoing spontaneous LLPS and can therefore serve as droplet drivers. Although RacA, Noc, PBP1A/1B, ParB, PBP3, PBP2B, FtsZ, MipZ, MinD, and MreB are characterized by pLLPS values below the 0.6 threshold, they contain droplet-promoting regions and can therefore serve as droplet clients. The remaining B. subtilis proteins known to be associated with the bacterial cell pole (DivIVA, ComN, Maf, PBP4, MinC, and MinJ) are not predicted to be associated with LLPS processes. This analysis revealed that although proteins capable of spontaneous LLPS are characterized by high disorder content, not all highly disordered proteins can serve as droplet drivers.

In Caulobacter crescentus, the intrinsically disordered Polar Organizing Protein Z (PopZ) was shown to self-assemble into 200 nm microdomains localized to the cell poles and form a homogeneous membrane-less microdomain that excludes large protein complexes (Lasker Boeynaems et al., 2022). On the other hand, this membrane-less microdomain organized by PopZ at the swarmer-cell pole recruits the cell-cycle regulating proteins and various phospho-signaling proteins to the cell pole (Holmes Follett et al., 2016; Ebersbach Briegel et al., 2008; Bowman Comolli et al., 2008). PopZ was shown to phase separate in vitro as well (Saurabh Chong et al., 2022; Soltys Tarczewska et al., 2022). Although our analysis indicated that LLPS can be related to the functionality of several cell pole-related proteins, this phenomenon has not yet been analyzed in great detail and definitely requires more attention.

Our study emphasizes the importance of IDPs in the organization of the bacterial cell poles and their contribution to several cellular functions, including bacterial cell pole formation via LLPS. The focus was on 19 proteins, mostly from B. subtilis, that were shown to be characterized by different levels of predicted intrinsic disorder. We show that except for the MinJ protein, most of the analyzed proteins are predicted as either highly or moderately disordered. The highly disordered proteins are distinguished by their functions, being involved in organization of cell poles, the process of chromosome segregation (Holmes Follett et al., 2016), and the stability of proteins by being resistant to stress. Moderately disordered proteins contribute to the regulation of many processes, including polymerization of the prokaryotic homolog of tubulin, FtsZ (Erickson, 1995) into a Z ring around midcell during bacterial cell division (Vinella and D'Ari, 1995).

The LLPS analysis suggested that there is a correlation between the disorder level of a protein and its LLPS potential. The greater the level of intrinsic disorder in a protein, the higher its probability to be capable of spontaneous LLPS. In fact, all LLPS drivers identified in this study are characterized by high levels of intrinsic disorder. On the other hand, a high level of intrinsic disorder is not the only factor determining the capability of a given protein to undergo spontaneous LLPS, as many highly disordered proteins analyzed in this study are not droplet drivers, and are instead droplet clients. Interestingly, one of the highly disordered proteins analyzed, DivIVA, is not expected to be involved in LLPS processes.

Although our bioinformatics analysis provides a likely link between the potential of a protein to serve in the bacterial cell poles with its potential to be disordered, further experimental validation of this interesting correlation is needed. Similarly, to confirm the involvement of these proteins in LLPS, corresponding in vitro and in vivo analyses would be required (Alberti Gladfelter et al., 2019). A more detailed characterization of evolutionary conservation of the observed trends would also be useful. We hope that the outputs of our study help pave new roads for the analysis and targeting of specific IDPs that might lead to the development of new antimicrobials.
