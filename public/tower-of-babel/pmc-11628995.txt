Optimal frailty assessment tool for elderly patients with heart failure based on outcome prediction: protocol for a multicentre prospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaolei Wei, Xueli Shen, Qiuchen Yuan, Chen Shen, Tao Liang, Rui Gao, Shuping Li, Lingling Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionFrailty significantly influences the prognosis of elderly patients diagnosed with heart failure. The assessment of frailty is a critical initial step in the management of these patients, as a systematic and precise evaluation facilitates the identification of individuals at high risk. This identification enables timely and targeted interventions, which can subsequently reduce the likelihood of adverse cardiovascular events and improve the quality of life for elderly patients with heart failure. Nevertheless, there exists a notable deficiency in research regarding the most effective frailty assessment tools specifically for elderly patients with heart failure in China. The objective of this study is to identify the frailty assessment tool that demonstrates the highest predictive value for outcomes in this population.Methods and analysisThis study is a multicentre, prospective cohort investigation that commenced in October 2023 across three tertiary hospitals in Beijing, China. Employing a continuous enrolment strategy, the study encompasses all elderly patients diagnosed with heart failure who are undergoing either outpatient or inpatient treatment, continuing until an adequate sample size is achieved. Follow-up evaluations are scheduled every 3 months from the point of patient enrolment, extending until the 12th month post-enrolment. Comprehensive data collection, which includes demographic information, heart failure-related metrics, frailty assessments and significant biochemical test results, is conducted through face-to-face interviews at baseline.Ethics and disseminationParticipant inclusion will depend on obtaining written informed consent from the patient or guardian. The trial protocol was approved by the Central Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital. The approval letter number is 2023BJYYEC-356-01. Outcomes of the study will be published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.Keywords: Heart failure, Frailty, Prognosis

Introduction

Heart failure represents a significant public health concern, characterised by a high prevalence and unfavourable prognosis, particularly among the elderly population. In China, the incidence and prevalence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) are on the rise, driven by the dual challenges of an ageing demographic and the increasing prevalence of metabolic risk factors, ultimately positioning CVD as the leading cause of mortality among the population.1 Heart failure is recognised as the terminal stage of various cardiovascular conditions, impacting nearly 38 million individuals globally.2 In the United States of America, the incidence of heart failure among individuals aged 65 and older is approximately 2.1%.3 In China, there are currently around 8.9 million individuals diagnosed with heart failure, with a mean age of 67±14 years.1 4 Previous research indicates that the prevalence of heart failure escalates with advancing age, reaching a prevalence rate of up to 2.8% among elderly individuals aged 75 years and older residing in the community.5

Heart failure exerts considerable economic pressure on national healthcare systems as well as on individuals. In 2012, the estimated expenditure for heart failure treatment in China reached approximately $5.42 billion, which constituted 5% of the overall healthcare expenditures.6 By 2019, the cumulative hospitalisation costs associated with heart failure in China amounted to ¥13.064 billion, with the average cost per hospital stay being ¥ 9368.51 per patient.1

Elderly individuals diagnosed with heart failure frequently present with comorbid conditions such as hypertension, diabetes and chronic kidney disease. The interplay of advanced age and these multiple comorbidities contributes to a poor prognosis and elevated mortality rates in this demographic. An analysis conducted by the China Heart Failure Registry (China-HF), which included 13 687 patients with heart failure from 132 hospitals across the country between January 2012 and September 2015, revealed a mortality rate of 4.1% among hospitalised patients.7 Furthermore, a report from 2020 assessing the quality of heart failure treatment in China indicated a mortality rate of 2.8% among 33 413 in-hospital patients from January 2017 to October 2020.8 Heart failure considerably affects the quality of life and the prospects for healthy ageing in older adults.

Frailty, a geriatric syndrome marked by a decline in multisystem physiological function due to ageing, encompasses physical, psychological and social dimensions. The prevalence of frailty is notably high among elderly patients with heart failure and is linked to unfavourable outcomes. The presence of heart failure may expedite the onset of frailty. A meta-analysis has indicated that the prevalence of frailty in elderly patients with heart failure ranges from 25.4% to 76%, which is significantly higher than in other elderly populations.9 Additionally, frailty, characterised by diminished physical reserves and reduced stress tolerance, adversely affects disease progression and prognosis, thereby increasing the likelihood of negative events such as hospitalisation and all-cause mortality. Recent meta-analysis suggests that frail elderly patients with heart failure exhibit an HR of 1.54 for all-cause mortality and an HR of 1.56 for hospitalisation when compared with their non-frail counterparts.10 Cross-sectional studies have further demonstrated that frailty adversely influences health-related quality of life (HRQOL) among elderly patients with heart failure. Moreover, frailty may disrupt the ‘risk-benefit’ equilibrium of both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions for heart failure and may impede the utilisation of implantable devices for treatment.11 12 Nevertheless, frailty is a potentially reversible condition, and precise scientific assessment coupled with timely intervention can either reverse or delay its progression, thereby enhancing outcomes for patients with heart failure.13 14 Studies have demonstrated that frailty assessment is superior to traditional heart failure-specific indicators in predicting short-term (30-day) mortality in elderly patients with heart failure, and early intervention in frailty progression can reduce hospitalisation rates, mortality rates and healthcare costs.15 16 Consequently, it is imperative to emphasise the prognostic significance of frailty in elderly patients with heart failure and to refine evaluation and treatment strategies tailored to this population.

Currently, numerous frailty assessment instruments have been used both domestically and internationally, including the Fried frailty phenotype (FP), the FRAIL scale (FRAIL) and the Frailty Index (FI). These instruments vary in their fundamental characteristics, dimensions of assessment and the quantity of questions posed. Expert consensus indicates that the selection of suitable assessment tools should be guided by the specific attributes of the assessment subjects and the intended objectives of the evaluation. The FP and FRAIL scales are particularly recommended for evaluating frailty in elderly populations due to their simplicity, reliability and extensive application in clinical environments. Previous studies on frailty assessment instruments for elderly patients with heart failure have exhibited considerable variability in the selection of tools and outcome measures. Some studies have been characterised by limited follow-up durations and methodologies that require enhancement. Furthermore, almost all studies evaluating the predictive capacity of frailty assessment tools concerning outcomes in elderly patients with heart failure have predominantly involved foreign populations, indicating a notable absence of similar research evidence pertaining to Chinese populations. The majority of the scales employed in these investigations have primarily concentrated on the assessment of physiological frailty, rather than adhering to the consensus definition of ‘functional decline across physical, psychological and social domains’.17 A comprehensive approach to frailty assessment is essential. Notably, certain multidimensional frailty scales, such as the Groningen Frailty Index (GFI), have yet to be validated for their predictive capabilities regarding adverse events in patients with heart failure. Consequently, it is imperative to identify and use currently prevalent or more comprehensive frailty assessment tools within the elderly population in China with heart failure, in order to conduct observational studies and compare the predictive efficacy of various instruments.

In conclusion, we developed a study intended to conduct a preliminary investigation into the frailty assessment tool that demonstrates the highest predictive value for prognosis in elderly patients diagnosed with heart failure. The objective of this research is to enhance the quality of clinical frailty assessments for this demographic, thereby establishing a dependable foundation for treatment and nursing interventions. Ultimately, this endeavour aims to improve patient outcomes and quality of life, thereby contributing to the promotion of healthy ageing.

Study objectives

The objective of this study is to evaluate the efficacy of the FP, the FRAIL scale and the GFI in predicting major adverse cardiovascular events within a 1-year period among elderly patients diagnosed with heart failure. Previous investigations have predominantly employed unidimensional and non-clinically validated assessment instruments. Consequently, this study aims to compare multidimensional scales with widely used clinical measures, thereby enhancing the scientific rigour and practical applicability of tool selection to address clinical requirements. The findings of this study will be anticipated to enhance the quality of frailty assessments in hospitalised elderly patients with heart failure, improve outcomes and facilitate healthy ageing.

Methods

Study design

This study constitutes a multicentre prospective cohort study. The investigators will gather baseline data within 48 hours of patient admission and will employ three distinct frailty scales to evaluate frailty levels. The baseline data will encompass general demographic information, laboratory and imaging test results, as well as the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification of heart function. Patient selection will adhere to rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria. Follow-up assessments will be conducted at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 9 months and 12 months post-enrolment, using outpatient visits or telephone interviews to monitor the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events, management of heart failure symptoms, current therapeutic interventions and HRQOL scores. Subsequently, statistical analyses will be performed to examine the correlation between the initial frailty assessment outcomes and the occurrence of adverse cardiovascular events, while also comparing the prognostic efficacy of the three assessment tools to determine the most effective instrument. Study settings and participants

The primary focus of this research is Beijing Hospital, located in Beijing, China. Elderly patients diagnosed with heart failure in outpatient settings are generally admitted for inpatient care. The selection of participants is conducted by specialists within the cardiology department. For those patients deemed eligible, the researcher will provide comprehensive information regarding the study’s objectives, specific content and instructions for participation. Following the acquisition of informed consent, patients will be formally enrolled in the study.

The study participants will consist of inpatients from three major hospitals in Beijing, China, who have been diagnosed with CVD. Eligibility criteria include patients aged 60 years and older who meet the diagnostic standards outlined in the ‘Chinese Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Heart Failure 2018’. Participants will undergo assessments using FP, the FRAIL scale and GFI, with a follow-up period extending to 1 year. Exclusion criteria encompass patients with significant comorbidities, those undergoing major surgical procedures during the follow-up or individuals with an anticipated survival of less than 6 months. Informed consent was obtained prior to the screening and enrolment process for all participants. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in table 1.

During the hospitalisation period, we will collect comprehensive data, including general information, laboratory test results, imaging examinations and NYHA classification of heart function, for elderly patients with heart failure who fulfil the inclusion criteria. Outcome definitions

The primary objective of this study is to assess the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) occurring within 1 year following the completion of baseline data collection. The MACE considered in this investigation encompass all-cause mortality, the first diagnosis of atrial fibrillation (AF), acute exacerbation of chronic heart failure (AE-CHF), acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and unstable angina (UA). In this context, first-diagnosed AF refers to atrial fibrillation that has not been previously identified prior to the diagnosis of heart failure, irrespective of the presence of symptoms, the temporal pattern or the duration of the condition.18 AMI is characterised as the acute necrosis of myocardial tissue, typically resulting from a sudden obstruction of blood flow in the coronary arteries, and is evidenced by clinical symptoms, changes in the electrocardiogram (ECG) and elevated biomarker levels.19 AE-CHF denotes a rapid worsening of symptoms and signs in patients with chronic heart failure over a brief period. UA is defined as an acute coronary syndrome marked by new-onset or escalating angina, angina occurring at rest or angina that exhibits increasing severity and duration, and is generally associated with a heightened risk of myocardial infarction.20

The secondary endpoints of this study encompass the frequency of hospital readmissions within a 1-year timeframe, HRQOL scores and alterations in NYHA functional classification. Commencing from the completion of baseline data collection, the total number of hospital readmissions will be documented throughout the entire follow-up duration. During follow-up visits at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months, patients will be subjected to an assessment of their NYHA classification. A reduction in heart function classification will be interpreted as a significant change in heart function status.

Measurement

Independent variable

General information questionnaire

The questionnaire employed in this research is developed by the investigator following a comprehensive review of pertinent literature. It encompasses various factors, including gender, age, duration of hospital stay, type of medical insurance, NYHA classification, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, number of comorbidities, body mass index, as well as laboratory and imaging examination results pertinent to the patient’s admission. These results include measurements such as blood sodium, blood potassium, haemoglobin, troponin, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and LVEF, among others.

Fried frailty phenotype

The aforementioned scale represents the most prevalent instrument for evaluating frailty, encompassing five physical indicators: unintentional weight loss, self-reported fatigue, reduced walking speed, diminished grip strength and low levels of physical activity. Assessment can be conducted through self-report questionnaires and observational techniques. The presence of three or more indicators categorises an individual as frail, while the presence of 1–2 indicators classifies them as prefrail. Cohort studies have established that the physical phenotype of frailty possesses significant predictive validity regarding reduced physical activity among community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older.21

FRAIL Scale

The FRAIL Scale was introduced by the International Geriatric Nutrition and Healthy Aging Society in 2008 as a clinical tool for assessing frailty in elderly populations. This scale comprises five key variables: slow gait speed, fatigue, physical decline, feelings of fatigue and unexplained weight loss. A score of 0 indicates a healthy status, while scores ranging from 1 to 2 suggest prefrailty and scores from 3 to 5 indicate frailty. The simplicity and ease of use of the Frail Scale in clinical settings facilitate the early identification of frail individuals. Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated a significant correlation between the application of this scale and patient mortality rates in the context of frailty and prefrailty assessment.22

Groningen Frailty Index

The GFI comprises 15 items categorised into three dimensions: physical (9 items addressing activities of daily living, multiple health issues, physical fatigue and sensory impairments such as vision and hearing), cognitive (1 item pertaining to cognitive functions) and psychosocial (5 items focusing on emotional isolation, depressive symptoms and anxiety). The GFI score ranges from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating a continuum from health to severe frailty. A score exceeding 4 points signifies that a patient is classified as frail and may require intervention. The GFI demonstrates satisfactory reliability, with a Kuder-Richardson 20 (K-R20) coefficient of 0.68.23

Outcomes

In this study, the identification of both major and minor outcome events among the participants will be primarily conducted by cardiologists at the attending level or higher. Nurses will be permitted to evaluate NYHA heart function classification when deemed necessary, which is subsequently reviewed by a physician.

The diagnosis of first-diagnosed AF is predominantly established through comprehensive history-taking and ECG assessment. The specific diagnostic criteria include: (1) the presence of typical symptoms in conjunction with characteristic findings on a standard 12-lead ECG, (2) typical symptoms accompanied by distinctive features observed on single-lead and multi-lead ECG devices, (3) imaging studies revealing structural or functional abnormalities of the atrium, such as those obtained through echocardiography or cardiac MRI and (4) abnormal results from relevant laboratory tests, including those indicating thyroid dysfunction or electrolyte imbalances.18 24

The diagnostic criteria for AE-CHF encompass the following elements: (1) a documented history of chronic heart failure; (2) a swift decline in symptoms and signs associated with heart failure, including but not limited to dyspnoea, fatigue and oedema; (3) the identification of potential precipitating factors for the acute exacerbation, which may include infections, excessive physical exertion and emotional stress; (4) laboratory assessments indicating elevated levels of cardiac injury biomarkers, such as BNP or NT-proBNP and (5) imaging studies, including ECG, that may reveal abnormalities in cardiac structure and function.25

AMI is typically characterised by intense chest pain or discomfort, which frequently radiates to the left arm, neck, jaw or upper abdomen. Additional symptoms may include dyspnoea, excessive sweating, nausea and vomiting. The ECG is an essential diagnostic tool for AMI, with hallmark findings such as ST-segment elevation or the presence of a new left bundle branch block. Non-specific alterations in ST and T-wave morphology may also suggest myocardial ischemia. The assessment of serum cardiac troponins is critical, as elevated levels are indicative of myocardial cell necrosis. Furthermore, other biomarkers, including creatine kinase (CK) and CK-MB, are employed in the diagnostic evaluation of AMI.19

The clinical manifestation of UA encompasses three distinct systems: the emergence of new angina symptoms, the exacerbation or increased frequency and intensity of previously stable angina and the occurrence of chest pain in the absence of physical exertion. Angina episodes are characterised by a duration of at least 10 min, with a notable increase in severity or frequency. Electrocardiographic findings typically reveal the absence of sustained ST-segment elevation, which may suggest transient ST-segment depression, T-wave inversion or no significant changes on the ECG, all occurring without a corresponding elevation in cardiac troponin levels.26

In this study, the evaluation of HRQOL will be performed using the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ). The MLHFQ is a globally recognised and extensively used instrument for assessing the quality of life among individuals with heart failure. It encompasses three dimensions: the physical dimension (8 items), the emotional dimension (5 items) and additional dimensions (8 items). The questionnaire employs a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘no impact’ to ‘very noticeable impact’, with scores assigned from 0 to 5. The total score can vary from 0 to 105, where higher scores reflect diminished quality of life. The Chinese version of the MLHFQ has exhibited commendable psychometric properties, evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88, Kappa coefficients for all items exceeding 0.4 and Spearman correlation coefficients greater than 0.75. These findings indicate exceptional reliability, validity and internal consistency, thereby endorsing its use in clinical assessments.27

Sample size calculation

In this study, we will use MACEs as the primary outcome measure. The sample size will be determined using the single ROC curve calculation method via PASS11.0, with a statistical power set at 1−β=0.8 and a significance level of α=0.05.28 Previous systematic reviews indicate that the average prevalence of frailty among Chinese patients with heart failure is approximately 50%. The variable R represents the required ratio of the negative to the positive group. MACE is defined as a composite event, and based on existing literature regarding the incidence of adverse outcomes in patients with heart failure, it is estimated that around 30% of these patients will experience adverse cardiovascular events within 1-year postdischarge; therefore, R is established at 2. The threshold for ‘predictive value’ is defined as an area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of ≥0.7, and the anticipated minimum AUC is set at 0.65, with all other parameters maintained at their default values.29,31 Taking into account a projected attrition rate of 20%, the minimum required sample size is determined to be at least 356 participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be conducted using R Studio4.3 and MedCalc 5.8. Patient attrition may result in missing data, which will be addressed through methodologies such as multiple imputation or robustness analysis. Continuous variables will initially be assessed for normality; normally distributed data will be reported as mean±SD, while data exhibiting skewed distribution will be described using the median (M) and IQR (P25, P75). Categorical variables will be presented in terms of frequency and composition ratio. To compare differences among categorical data, the Pearson χ2 test, continuity-corrected χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test will be employed, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test will be used for comparing differences in ordinal data. Logistic regression analysis will be employed to investigate the influence of various frailty assessment instruments on postoperative adverse outcomes, while controlling for confounding variables. A subgroup analysis will be performed by categorising participants into distinct groups based on age, gender, pre-existing comorbidities (including diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and dyslipidaemia), as well as LVEF. This subgroup analysis is intended to examine the association between frailty assessment tools and primary outcome indicators across different subpopulations, thereby validating the consistency of findings across these groups. The initial frailty assessment results will be evaluated to determine their effect on all-cause mortality, secondary AF, AE-CHF, AMI and UA. The AUC will be calculated to assess and compare the predictive capabilities of the three frailty assessment scales, with an AUC greater than 0.75 indicating high predictive performance for a given tool. The MedCalc V.5.8 will facilitate pairwise comparisons of the AUCs of the three scales to identify the frailty assessment tool with the most robust predictive performance. Missing data will be addressed through multiple imputation techniques, such as multiple imputation by chained equations and K-nearest neighbours, tailored to the nature of the missing data and the requirements of the statistical analysis. Regarding sensitivity analysis, this study aims to minimise the potential impact of age on the research outcomes. To this end, the study population will be divided into two age groups, with a threshold set at 75 years. The predictive performance of the three frailty assessment tools will then be re-evaluated separately for participants aged 75 years or younger and those older than 75 years. In this investigation, a p value of less than 0.05 will be deemed statistically significant.

Oversight and monitoring

We have composed a study coordinating centre, endpoint adjudication committee and data management team. The department head, deputy head, attending physician and head nurse will be responsible for guiding and coordinating the entire research process. Skilled medical graduate students will be responsible for data collection and collation according to unified standards and will be guided by professional epidemiologists and statisticians in data analysis and processing. Additionally, we have established a quality control group independent from the research team to oversee data custody and review.

Bias

This study acknowledges the potential for various biases that may influence the results.

Selection bias

There exists a possibility of selection bias, indicating that the elderly patients with heart failure included in the study may not accurately represent the broader population of elderly patients with heart failure. For instance, if a particular subset of patients is more likely to use a specific frailty assessment tool, this could result in skewed findings.

Information bias

The data provided by patients may be inconsistent or inaccurate, particularly concerning self-reported information such as lifestyle and dietary habits, which may be subject to memory bias or social desirability effects.

Time bias

The duration of follow-up in the study may be either insufficiently short or excessively long, which could hinder a thorough understanding of the long-term effects of frailty assessment tools on patient outcomes or potentially overlook the critical time frame for patients to experience cardiovascular events.

Confounding bias

During the data analysis phase, confounding bias may arise when additional factors (ie, confounding variables) influence the interpretation of the study’s findings. We will endeavor to rigorously control for a multitude of confounding variables within the analytical framework of our data analysis.

Loss to follow-up bias

Over the 1-year follow-up period, there may be instances of participant attrition, which could lead to biased outcomes, either negatively or positively, depending on the characteristics of the patients who are lost to follow-up.

To mitigate selection bias, standardised and validated versions of assessment tools will be used for data collection. A rigorous dual-check procedure will be implemented on the same day to ensure the accuracy and consistency of all data gathered. A systematically structured interview process will be developed, and assessors will receive standardised training on questioning techniques, evaluation procedures and data recording prior to the initiation of the study.

In light of the comprehensive understanding of the impact of frailty assessment tools on outcomes, a 1-year follow-up period has been established to minimise potential time biases. For any missing data resulting from participant attrition, appropriate imputation methods will be employed during data analysis. Concurrently, the reasons for participant loss will be meticulously documented, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the potential effects of loss to follow-up on the study results.

Furthermore, recognising the considerable clinical heterogeneity among elderly patients with heart failure, which may influence the efficacy of frailty assessment tools, we intend to control for this heterogeneity during the analysis. Confounding factors, such as patient characteristics, will be treated as stratification or adjustment variables to enhance the robustness of our findings.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that the simultaneous presence of frailty and heart failure can exacerbate functional decline, elevate the risk of adverse outcomes among elderly patients with heart failure and serve as a critical predictor of adverse events.32 33 The integration of frailty assessments into the clinical management of elderly patients with heart failure can markedly improve the precision of mortality risk predictions. Timely identification of frail individuals, coupled with the implementation of targeted interventions such as tailored dietary and exercise regimens, has the potential to significantly lower mortality rates and enhance the prognostic outlook for this demographic. Given the impact of comorbidities, advanced age and other contributing factors, elderly patients with heart failure exhibit unique characteristics that differentiate them from the broader elderly population. Furthermore, recent studies have acknowledged the importance of social and psychological dimensions of frailty, which, when considered alongside physical frailty, collectively impacts prognosis.34 35 Consequently, there is a pressing need for further validation of the most appropriate frailty assessment tools specifically for elderly patients with heart failure, particularly within the context of the Chinese elderly population. We propose the development of a prospective observational cohort study with a 1-year follow-up period to identify the frailty assessment tool that offers the most significant prognostic value for Chinese elderly patients with heart failure. This study aims to enhance the understanding of frailty assessment in the clinical management of elderly patients with heart failure, ultimately improving the quality of inpatient care for this population in China.

Frailty is recognised as an independent predictor of negative outcomes in elderly patients with heart failure. Those who present with frailty demonstrate more severe clinical manifestations, which adversely impact their quality of life. This condition is associated with a substantial rise in short-term mortality rates and a significant decline in long-term survival rates.30 36 37 The timely recognition of frailty, coupled with the implementation of tailored exercise and nutritional interventions, has the potential to diminish mortality risk and improve the overall quality of life.38 39 A limited number of studies have investigated the most effective frailty assessment instruments specifically for elderly patients with heart failure. Sze et al40 conducted a longitudinal study comparing six frailty assessment tools, including the Fried FP and the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS). Over a median follow-up period of 554 days, the CFS was identified as potentially the most effective tool for predicting patient mortality and hospitalisation rates. In contrast, Somech et al41 undertook a 3-month longitudinal study involving 215 patients with heart failure, but did not reach a definitive conclusion regarding the most effective tool—whether the Short Physical Performance Battery, Fried phenotype or others—in predicting overall mortality, hospitalisation and quality of life during hospitalisation. This inconclusiveness may be attributed to limitations in sample size and follow-up duration. Additionally, a singular study from China compared the predictive capabilities of the FI, CFS and FRAIL in assessing overall mortality risk among elderly patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). This study suggested that the CFS demonstrated superior performance; however, it faced criticism for biases, including an overrepresentation of male and older patients and reliance on a single outcome measure.42 Currently, various composite-dimension frailty assessment tools have been developed and implemented, among which the GFI stands out as a four-dimensional tool encompassing physical, psychological, cognitive and social aspects of frailty. The Chinese version of the GFI has shown high accuracy and is characterised by ease of use and flexibility, demonstrating good feasibility and validity among elderly individuals residing in Chinese communities. Notably, no existing study has integrated a comprehensive assessment tool that encompasses the physical, psychological and social dimensions of frailty. Therefore, further investigation is warranted to identify the optimal frailty assessment tool for elderly Chinese patients with heart failure, with the aim of evaluating frailty and its associated risks. This research seeks to elucidate the prevalence of social, psychological and cognitive frailty among elderly Chinese patients with heart failure, thereby providing a foundation and guidance for multidisciplinary interventions aimed at enhancing frailty management in this population within China.

Enhancing the treatment and care of elderly patients with chronic diseases, as well as improving their quality of life, holds considerable practical importance. Presently, China possesses the largest elderly population globally, and with the retirement of the second birth peak generation (born between 1962 and 1975) commencing in 2022, the ageing burden in the country is expected to escalate further. The ‘14th Five-Year Plan’ marks the inaugural 5-year period for China to comprehensively advance towards a socialist modern state, representing a critical opportunity to proactively address the challenges associated with population ageing. The promotion of healthy ageing is poised to enter a new developmental phase. To effectively implement the Healthy China strategy and respond to the national demographic ageing strategy, it is essential to continuously address the health needs of the elderly and enhance their overall health status. This necessitates a strengthening of applied research focused on the treatment of chronic diseases and comorbidities in the elderly, as well as advancements in elderly rehabilitation nursing and functional maintenance technologies. Furthermore, improving the prevention and treatment of major diseases affecting the elderly is imperative. Given the implications of chronic illness, elderly patients with heart failure are particularly susceptible to adverse outcomes, including disability. The phenomenon of frailty is closely linked to prognosis and quality of life in this demographic.43 Consequently, the exploration of frailty assessment tools tailored to this population aligns with both domestic realities and national policy requirements.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, it is an observational study, which inherently provides a lower level of evidence compared with a randomised controlled trial (RCT). The evaluation and diagnosis of frailty are predominantly based on scales, which may introduce subjective biases from both the assessors and the participants. Furthermore, conducting an RCT presents numerous challenges, including issues related to randomisation, blinding, control selection, and ensuring that patients are willing and motivated to complete the study. To mitigate these challenges, we endeavoured to select patients with comparable baseline characteristics, control for variations in age and gender, provide standardised training for all assessors involved in frailty assessment and consider employing propensity score matching for data analysis, thereby reducing heterogeneity and controlling for confounding variables. Second, while our study encompassed three centres and did not impose restrictions on patient sources, all participating centres were situated in Beijing. Given the potential influence of varying healthcare standards, economic conditions and sociocultural factors, the generalisability of our findings may require validation in a broader context. Lastly, cohort migration and the duration of follow-up in cohort studies pose significant challenges. One potential strategy to address these issues is to enhance the role of heart failure case managers in facilitating effective follow-up of participants, which may help to alleviate concerns related to cohort migration and ensure adequate follow-up duration in cohort studies.

This proposal delineates a cohort study design intended to investigate the most effective frailty assessment tool for predicting outcomes in elderly Chinese patients diagnosed with heart failure. The study is conducted in accordance with the established protocol specified in the STROBE statement. The STROBE checklist is included as online supplemental file 1. The findings from this research may provide significant evidence to inform the selection of appropriate frailty assessment tools for use in clinical practice and the formulation of relevant guidelines.

Ethics and dissemination

This trial protocol (version 3.0, 19/09/2023; Additional file CRF-new) was assessed and approved by the Central Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital in October 2023 (2023BJYYEC-356-01). All major protocol amendments will only be implemented after approval is gained from the Central Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital and governance committees for participating sites. Amendments will also be notified to the trial registry, and the journal in which the protocol is published.

Following interim and final analyses, trial results will be shared with the investigator group who will have ongoing custody of the data and will be prepared for scientific publication or presentation. The final results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal. If there is an available online platform, a detailed summary of the final test results will be published on it for participants and the public.
