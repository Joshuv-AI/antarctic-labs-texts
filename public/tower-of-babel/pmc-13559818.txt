The TITAN Study: Topical Infliximab for the Treatment of Advanced Corneal Melt and Ocular Surface Inflammation
Authors: Soumaya Bouhout, Dominique Geoffrion, Cristina Bostan, Samir Jabbour, Marie-Claude Robert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSterile corneal melt is a rare but serious condition that may occur secondary to chemical burns, inflammatory disorders, or autoimmune diseases. It is thought to be mediated by cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1. Infliximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting TNF-α, has been used systemically in ocular inflammatory conditions. This study aims to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of topical infliximab in treating sterile corneal melt in humans.DesignProspective, single-center, unmasked, single-arm phase I/II clinical trial.ParticipantsPatients presenting with sterile, culture-negative, corneal melt of any etiology at the Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal.MethodsPatients diagnosed with sterile corneal melt received topical infliximab 10 mg/mL eye drops 4 times daily for 4 weeks, in addition to standard care, and were followed for 12 weeks. Clinical evaluation, anterior segment OCT, and Ocular Surface Disease Index questionnaires were used to assess outcomes.Main outcome measureThe primary outcome was the occurrence of adverse events; secondary outcomes included stabilization of corneal thinning and reepithelialization.ResultsSix patients with etiologies received infliximab. The treatment was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events. One patient developed uveitis after discontinuation, that resolved with topical steroids. Corneal thinning stabilized in all patients, and 66% achieved complete re-epithelialization by 12 weeks. No surgical interventions were required.ConclusionsTopical infliximab appears to be a safe and promising treatment for sterile corneal melt.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Corneal melt, Peripheral ulcerative keratitis, Chemical burn, Biologics, Infliximab

Although sterile corneal melt (keratolysis) is a rare condition, it can lead to disastrous complications, including perforation, permanent vision loss, or even loss of the eye.1 Patients with inflammatory or autoimmune diseases such as chemical burn, rosacea, Sjögren's syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, Stevens–Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, or mucous membrane pemphigoid (among others) are at higher risk of developing this condition.

The pathophysiology of corneal melt is complex but is thought to be mediated by matrix metalloproteinases, which are enzymes that lead to the destruction and loss of the extracellular matrix of the cornea.2,3 Matrix metalloproteinases are produced locally in the cornea by damaged epithelial cells and infiltrating leucocytes.3 Their activity is induced by numerous inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1.4

The standard treatment for corneal melt depends on the underlying etiology but includes optimizing the ocular surface with punctal plugs and lubricants, including ointments and preservative-free artificial tears.5,6 While they may suppress corneal inflammation, topical corticosteroids can impair corneal collagen healing and even worsen the melt.7,8 Systemic immunomodulatory agents are often required to control the inflammation.9,10 When medical treatment fails to stabilize the melt, reconstructive or tectonic corneal surgery is indicated to preserve the integrity of the globe.

Recently, refractory cases of corneal melt associated with Crohn's disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and idiopathic Mooren's ulcers have been successfully treated with biologics, including intravenous infliximab.2,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Infliximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody with a high affinity for both the soluble and transmembrane forms of TNF-α.18 Given the requirement for in-hospital infusion and the association with systemic adverse effects,2 the idea of administering infliximab topically to treat the ocular surface locally could be a more practical and safe approach. It would be particularly useful for localized or limited systemic inflammations where the eye requires significantly more intensive anti-inflammatory treatment than the underlying systemic condition. The safety of infliximab formulated as eye drops has been demonstrated in numerous animal models for corneal scarring, dry eye, and corneal neovascularization.19, 20, 21, 22 However, topical infliximab has never been studied in humans, to the best of our knowledge.19, 20, 21, 22

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of topical infliximab in treating sterile corneal melt in humans.

Methods

We designed a prospective, unmasked, single-arm, phase I/II clinical trial at our institution (Centre Hospitalier de l'Université de Montreal). Approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Board (Comité d'éthique de la recherche du Centre Hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal; Nagano 2017-6803) and a no objection letter was obtained from Health Canada (CTA-HC6-024-C203393). The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02987686). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with the CONSORT guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants included 6 adult patients with sterile corneal melt recruited between April 2017 and August 2024. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Table 1. There was a 1-week delay between eligibility testing and confirmation of eligibility, allowing confirmation of negative cultures and normal complete blood count, liver enzymes, and creatinine.

Intervention

After confirming eligibility, patients received topical infliximab 10 mg/ml 4 times per day for 28 days. The drops were compounded at the hospital research pharmacy. A 100 mg vial of infliximab powder was suspended in 10 mL of a 50:50 mixture of sterile water and an artificial tear solution (Refresh Liquigel 1%, Allergan Inc). To maintain the sterility and stability of this formulation, it can be frozen for a maximum of 45 days and/or refrigerated for a maximum of 9 days, according to USP797. Sterility and stability of infliximab have been previously demonstrated using this schedule.22 Thus, participants were instructed to keep the bottle currently in use refrigerated for 1 week (7 days) and the remaining bottles frozen for a maximum of 28 days. At the first visit, each patient received 4 bottles for treatment weeks 1 to 4. A dosing schedule with a reminder of these instructions was provided and was verified at each clinical visit.

Adjunct therapy included punctal plugs inserted in the upper and lower lid puncta to avoid systemic absorption of infliximab before initiating infliximab. Depending on the patient, additional adjunct therapy may have included topical lubrication, antibiotics, anti-inflammatory medication, oral antivirals, prednisone, mycophenolate mofetil, and doxycycline 100 mg orally or by mouth twice a day (Table 2).

Standard of Care

All our patients underwent standard investigations and treatment for sterile corneal melt at the treating physician's discretion. This included artificial lubricant, topical or oral steroids, topical antibiotics, oral tetracycline, oral vitamin C, or oral immunomodulators. Rheumatological evaluation was performed to exclude systemic autoimmune diseases. To confirm that corneal melts were sterile, all melts were scraped using calcium alginate swabs 1 week before initiation of infliximab eye drops and samples were submitted for Gram and calcofluor white stain, and corneal cultures on blood, chocolate, and Sabouraud agar, and in meat broth. Participants were only included if stain and culture results were negative at 1 week.

Considering the high risk of perforation, patients were followed weekly during the first month of the study until discontinuation of infliximab on day 28. Patients were then seen at weeks 6, 8, and 12 to detect any late complications. The treatment and follow-up calendar is detailed in supplemental materials (Supplementary Material S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) including timing of questionnaires and ancillary testing.

Data Collection

Past ocular and medical history were recorded at baseline. Current systemic and topical medications, best corrected visual acuity, history of present illness and clinical exam were recorded at each visit. Slit lamp evaluation including size and relative thickness (in percentage) of the corneal melt, size of the epithelial defect, and conjunctival hyperemia were specifically noted.

An Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) questionnaire and a side effects questionnaire were administered at each visit. The OSDI questionnaire measured the severity of dry eye and associated vision-related functional impairment, tracked treatment effectiveness, and provided patient-reported outcomes. The total score was calculated for each patient at each time point. Patients' OSDI scores were designated as normal, mild, moderate, or severe for each individual measure. The overall OSDI total score was determined based on a 100-point scale correlated with the severity of symptoms (scores 0–12, normal eyes; 13–22, mild dry eye symptoms; 23–32, moderate dry eye symptoms; and 33–100, severe dry eye symptoms), based on prior reports.23,24

Anterior segment OCT (Cirrus HD-OCT 5000, Carl Zeiss) was performed using the anterior chamber lens adapter (Carl Zeiss) in the wide-angle mode at baseline, week 1, 4, and 12. Measurements of corneal thickness were obtained using AS-OCT. The thinnest point of corneal thickness (μm) at the site of corneal melt was obtained for all patients at the 3 set visits using the review imaging software and performed by an independent observer. Slit lamp photographs of the anterior segment were captured at baseline, week 4, and week 12.

Blood tests were taken at weeks 0, 4, and 12, and included: a complete blood count, liver function tests, creatinine, an autoimmune panel, and beta-HCG levels (when relevant). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and an infectious workup (syphilis, tuberculosis, and hepatitis B screening) were performed only at baseline.

Main Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the occurrence of adverse events related to the administration of topical infliximab (safety outcome).

Adverse events were evaluated through symptom questionnaires, clinical examination (to detect toxicity, increased inflammation, namely conjunctival hyperemia, enlargement of the epithelial defect, worsening of the melt, infection, descemetocele, perforation, and intraocular pressure elevation) and laboratory evaluations.

1.Systemic symptoms assessed by the side effects questionnaire (Supplementary Material S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), which examines potential adverse events secondary to infliximab: unexplained fever, drenching night sweats, and unintentional weight loss, cough, headaches, chest pain, nausea, vomiting, breathing/swallowing difficulty, abdominal pain, diarrhea, dark urine, muscle aches, joint pain, hives, rash, bruising, changes in vision, eyelid swelling, ocular burning/pain/redness, and blurry vision. Statistical Analysis

Means, standard deviations, and ranges are reported for continuous variables. Frequencies and proportions of total (%) are reported for discrete variables.

Results

Six patients were included in this study. Their sterile corneal melts were due to peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK, n = 3), alkali burn with focal limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD, n = 1), keratolysis on a Boston type I keratoprosthesis (KPro, n = 1), and idiopathic central keratitis (n = 1). Their mean age was 68.7 ± 15.4 years (range: 44–87). Baseline and final slit lamp color pictures are presented in Figure 1.

Slit lamp pictures taken of the anterior segment of all patients participating in the study. Pictures were taken at baseline and after infliximab treatment at 12 weeks.

Patient 1 was a 44-year-old former contact lens wearer and remote cocaine user with prior bariatric surgery and abdominoplasty. They had initially been referred with a 2-month history of keratitis, which had been presumed secondary to herpes simplex virus. Visual acuity in the affected eye was 20/80. The patient had central corneal haze with thickened gray epithelium over 3.5 mm of the central cornea. The central lesion stained with fluorescein (0.2 × 0.6 mm). Corneal sensitivity was normal. The preliminary diagnosis was toxic keratopathy due to prolonged (>6 weeks) trifluridine eye drop use. The patient was treated with lesion debridement and cultures as well as prednisolone acetate 1%, moxifloxacin, and preservative-free artificial tear eye drops. Over the following 9 months, the indolent ulcer, with friable epithelium, remained stable yet unhealed. Prophylactic valacyclovir and bandage contact lens did not allow the lesion to heal. Two additional scrapings and cultures were performed and were negative. Given the prior bariatric surgery, the nutritional status was evaluated with vitamin A and E levels, both of which were normal. The ulcer worsened when decreasing topical steroids. With mild thinning of the 3 mm central corneal lesion, the patient was included in the TITAN study. Visual acuity was 20/50 at study inclusion. The patient tolerated the infliximab eye drops for 4 weeks, and progressive improvement in the epithelial defect was noted. Two weeks after cessation of the topical infliximab, the epithelial defect had resolved, but anterior uveitis was noted with 1+ cells (study week 6). The patient was treated with prednisolone 1% four times a day, which was tapered weekly. At study week 8, the epithelial defect reopened, and the uveitis had resolved. At study week 12 (8 weeks after stopping infliximab), the central opacity increased in size (1 mm) and density. Repeat cultures demonstrated the presence of Beauvaria bassiana. The fungal keratitis infection responded to triple topical antifungal therapy (voriconazole, amphotericin B, and caspofungin) and resolved to a paracentral scar and 20/60 visual acuity.

Patient 2 was an 80-year-old with a past medical history of osteoporosis and hypertension. They had LSCD and Salzmann nodules in both eyes due to rosacea and severe meibomian gland dysfunction. They presented with arcuate corneal melting of the temporal cornea which was initially treated with systemic prednisone, doxycycline, and vitamin C as well as conjunctival resection, amniotic membrane overlay, and lateral tarsorrhaphy. Corneal cultures and systemic workup were negative, leading to a diagnosis of PUK secondary to ocular rosacea. The ulceration progressed to involve the nasal cornea, prompting initiation of mycophenolate mofetil 1 g orally twice a day. Corneal ulceration was resistant to standard treatment for over 9 months before inclusion in the study. The patient had an almost circumferential ulcer (sparing the 6 and 12 o'clock positions) that showed progressive improvement with topical infliximab but did not resolve by week 12 of the study (Fig 1). Throughout the study, patient 2 was kept on mycophenolate mofetil 1 g orally twice a day and prednisone 10 mg orally once per day. Visual acuity was 20/60 pretreatment and 20/40 post-treatment at the end of the study.

Patient 3 was an 80-year-old with a past medical history of hypertension and asthma, and previous cataract surgery. They presented with peripheral corneal melting evolving over the previous 2 months. The patient presented with arcuate, nasal peripheral corneal melting (up to 80%). Systemic workup demonstrated high total complement (>20), high uric acid levels (478), anemia, and positive antinuclear antibodies (1/160). Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies was negative, and anti–double-stranded DNA was equivocal. Rheumatologic exam did not find a specific etiology. As a presumed Mooren ulcer, the corneal melt had been treated with topical prednisolone (6 times per day), topical antibiotics and oral doxycycline for 15 days before inclusion in the study without improvement. Infliximab was stopped after 16 days of use because of a positive QuantiFERON tuberculosis test performed as part of the initial workup. Chest radiography was normal. Despite early discontinuation of the study drug, the epithelial defect was healed by week 3. No tuberculosis-related complications occurred. Visual acuity was 20/200 pretreatment and 20/50 at the end of the study.

Patient 4 was a 77-year-old with a past medical history of remote cholecystitis, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, and rheumatoid arthritis since age 4. They had been treated for granulomatous uveitis 5 months prior. They developed PUK secondary to rheumatoid arthritis, presenting with a nasal arcuate corneal melt covering 6 clock hours and 70% depth had been present for 16 days before inclusion in the study. Rheumatoid factor was positive at 16. The patient was also treated with systemic vitamin C, doxycycline 100 mg twice a day, and prednisone 50 mg, tapered by 10 mg weekly. The epithelial defect was closed after the first week of topical infliximab use. Visual acuity was 20/200 pretreatment and 20/50 at the end of the study.

Patient 5 was a 66-year-old known for hypertension and dyslipidemia. They had suffered an acid chemical burn 9 years before the study, with secondary conjunctival deficiency and LSCD. They had undergone 2 failed penetrating keratoplasties for perforating infectious ulcers and implantation of a KPro. The postoperative course after the first KPro was complicated by early postoperative endophthalmitis, glaucoma, and full thickness melt with aqueous leak. They underwent failed focal wound repair and then KPro exchange 3 years (36.8 months) after implantation of the first KPro. The patient presented 81 days after the second KPro with stromal thinning with edema and necrosis of the carrier graft. Corneal cultures were performed and were negative. The patient was treated with topical vancomycin 25 mg/mL and tobramycin 14 mg/mL every 2 hours, topical prednisolone 1% twice per day, vitamin C 1 g by mouth twice daily, and doxycycline 100 mg twice daily but the epithelial defect and stromal necrosis progressed to circle 360 degrees around the KPro stem over the 2 weeks preceding inclusion in the study. After 1 week of topical infliximab, most of the stromal edema had resolved. At study week 4 (termination of infliximab), most of the corneal epithelium had healed, leaving a 2 × 6.5 mm epithelial defect superiorly. The patient missed their visit at week 6 due to a cold. The infiltrate cleared by week 8, improving to a residual area of 1 mm2 epithelial defect at week 12. The epithelial defect only fully and stably resolved 5 months after stopping infliximab. Visual acuity was counting fingers pretreatment and counting fingers post-treatment at the end of the study due to terminal glaucoma.

Patient 6 was a 61-year-old known for hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, asthma, gastroesophageal reflux, and benign prostate hypertrophy. They had undergone myopic LASIK surgery bilaterally 3 years before inclusion in the study. They suffered an alkali burn with persistent corneal epithelial defect, melting of the remote LASIK flap, and 2 clock hours LSCD 4 months before enrollment in the study. The corneal epithelial defect was refractory to 3 amniotic membranes placed over the cornea and a conjunctival–limbal autograft. A tectonic deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty was performed due to progressing corneal melt and descemetocele formation. The corneal epithelial defect persisted in the grafted cornea for 7 weeks before inclusion in the study. After treatment with topical infliximab, the epithelial defect was resolved by study week 8. Visual acuity was hand motion pretreatment and counting fingers post-treatment at the end of the study. The patient later underwent uneventful optical penetrating keratoplasty, improving visual acuity to 20/30.

Safety of Topical Infliximab

Patients reported side effects of topical infliximab through a questionnaire completed at every visit (Supplementary Material S2). All patients experienced blurry vision, which was also present at the initial study visit, before starting infliximab (Table 3). The most commonly reported side effects were bruising (n = 1), fatigue (n = 3), and joint pain (n = 3). None of the reported side effects were severe or required discontinuation of therapy.

Patient 1 developed fungal keratitis 8 weeks after stopping topical infliximab. It is unclear if the fungal infection was the original cause of the keratitis or a complication of long-term epithelial defect and steroid therapy, as Beauveria bassiana infection can have an indolent or protracted course.27

Preliminary Efficacy of Infliximab

We examined the impact on epithelial healing by analyzing the resolution of the epithelial defect (Fig 2). Re-epithelialization was achieved in 50% at 4 weeks and in 66% of patients at 8 weeks. Fifty percent of participants had a stable and complete epithelium at study week 12. Anterior segment OCT performed before and after the start of topical infliximab (Fig 5) showed that treatment stabilized corneal thickness at the site of the corneal melt. However, 5 patients out of 6 had ≥1 missing or unusable (due to poor image quality or centration) AS-OCT during the study. Finally, we evaluated the impact of treatment on visual acuity. Best-corrected visual acuity remained stable or improved while on treatment (Fig 6). None of the patients progressed to descemetocele or perforation, and none required additional tectonic surgery or amniotic membrane. Discussion

Sterile corneal melt is an infrequent but serious condition associated with chemical burns, inflammatory disorders, or autoimmune diseases. Inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, are thought to regulate the pathogenic pathways involved in corneal melt. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha has been shown to be involved in inflammatory and autoimmune pathophysiology. Infliximab, a monoclonal antibody that targets TNF-α, is being used systemically for several autoimmune and inflammatory illnesses, including Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, plaque psoriasis, and juvenile idiopathic arthritis.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35

For ocular conditions and cases of corneal melt, the use of systemic infliximab has also been reported. Its efficacy is thought to occur through the inhibitory mechanism of TNF-α–mediated expression of matrix metalloproteinases, which are responsible for collagen breakdown in corneal melt. In animal models, Zhou et al36 reported that systemic administration of 6.25 mg/kg of TNF-α inhibitor in mice prevented LSCD and facilitated corneal reepithelialization after alkali burn, which may be applicable to other conditions characterized by LSCD.

In humans, Utine et al337 reported that infliximab 5 mg/kg infusion showed drastic improvement in 3 cases of toxic epidermal necrolysis, severe alkaline burn, and Sjögren disease with corneal melts. Dohlman et al38 reported that a patient undergoing a third Boston keratoprosthesis type II implantation appeared to be protected over the course of a year from retraction and necrosis with systemic infusions of infliximab 5 mg/kg. Reported side effects with systemic infliximab may include infusion reactions (characterized by fever, nausea, myalgias, headache, cutaneous manifestations, or swelling) at a rate of 1% to 10% of patients per infusion.39 Abdominal pain, nausea, headache, diarrhea, and infections are other adverse events with such infusions. In comparison to systemic administration, topical infliximab is hypothesized to allow for increased local concentrations and lower systemic side effects.

The effect of local infliximab applied to the eye has been evaluated in few animal models. In rabbits, subconjunctival injection of 4.0 mg adalimumab, a monoclonal antibody against TNF-α usually injected subcutaneously, showed no eye toxicity and excellent neuroprotection from inflammation.40 In another study, topical treatment with topical soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor type I enhanced survival of murine corneal allograft transplants.41 The safety and preliminary efficacy of topical infliximab have never been investigated in ocular conditions in humans.

In this prospective, single-arm, phase I/II clinical trial, patients with sterile corneal melt received topical infliximab 10 mg/mL 4 times daily for 4 weeks, in addition to standard care. Patients were followed for a period of 12 weeks, including 8 weeks after termination of infliximab to monitor for adverse events. The concentration of topical infliximab 10 mg/ml was chosen based on prior reports where this concentration was shown to penetrate the cornea successfully in animal models.19 This study was based on a biological rationale and was not designed to evaluate mechanistic pathways.

Our results have shown that no significant adverse events were reported with topical administration of topical infliximab over 4 weeks. Two patients developed low positive titers of total anti-infliximab antibodies when treated with topical infliximab. Titers >200 ng/mL would be considered high titers.42 Patients who develop anti-infliximab antibodies have been shown to suffer from a significantly increased risk of acute and severe infusion reactions when treated with systemic infliximab.43 However, none of the patients developed instillation site reactions during topical infliximab use. One patient developed anterior uveitis after stopping infliximab. It is unclear if the uveitis was due to an inflammatory rebound phenomenon or to the underlying disease. The patient ultimately developed fungal keratitis due to Beauveria bassiana, a rare, indolent, entomopathogenic fungus, 2 months after stopping infliximab and while on topical steroids, a well-established risk factor for fungal keratitis.27 While the initial cause of the participant's keratitis remained of undetermined etiology, we believe that the fungal infection may have resulted from opportunistic colonization after topical immunosuppression.

Furthermore, re-epithelialization of the cornea was achieved in 50% of patients at 4 weeks and in 50% of patients at 12 weeks. The 3 patients in whom re-epithelialization was not achieved were due to fungal keratitis (patient 1), severe rosacea, PUK and LSCD (patient 2) and recurrent keratolysis following KPro (patient 5). Patient 1 responded well to appropriate antifungal therapy. Patient 5 had a drastic improvement. From 360 degrees of stromal necrosis and epithelial defect around the KPro, a 1 mm2 epithelial defect persisted by 8 weeks. Complete resolution of the epithelial defect was achieved by 5 months. We recognize that concomitant therapies limit attribution of benefit specifically to infliximab. Nevertheless, given the sight- and globe-threatening nature of corneal melt, we deemed it ethically appropriate in this first-in-human study to add topical infliximab to standard care to optimize the chances of healing and visual preservation. Topical infliximab may represent a potential therapeutic route for corneal melt, with preliminary evidence of efficacy and a favorable safety and cost profile. Our results appear to support a cost-effective therapy when compared with other biologics used in ophthalmology, such as intravitreal antivascular growth factor injections and topical nerve growth factor therapy.44 A single vial of infliximab, administered topically over 4 weeks, was able to heal ulcers that had persisted despite months of aggressive ocular surface therapy (patients 1 and 2) as well as stop inflammation and the melting process in other aggressive diseases such as PUK, alkali burn, and KPro-related melt. While rapid response was seen in most of our cases, topical infliximab treatment may need to be continued for longer than 1 month to ensure complete resolution of the corneal melt.

Several strengths make this study rigorous. These include the prospective nature of the study, the strict calendar for all patients with ancillary testing, as well as patient-reported outcomes. Limitations include small sample size, heterogeneous etiologies, early treatment discontinuation in 1 patient due to a positive tuberculosis test, and incomplete AS-OCT data in a minority of patients.

Conclusions

This phase I/II pilot study suggests that topical infliximab may represent a promising therapeutic approach for sterile corneal melt, with preliminary efficacy observed, although its safety profile cannot be definitively established given the limited number of patients. The effect of topical infliximab on corneal melt in human patients ought to be further evaluated over a longer period of time, such as 3 months, to observe the full effect of its efficacy in re-epithelialization and to observe adverse effects over a longer period. Its effect on penetrating keratoplasty and other inflammatory ocular surface pathologies might also be of interest to the cornea field.
