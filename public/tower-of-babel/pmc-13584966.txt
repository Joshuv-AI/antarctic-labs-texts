MicroRNA‐Mediated Placental Maladaptation in Preeclampsia Pathogenesis
Authors: Sarbani Saha, Rupasri Ain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPreeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy‐specific disorder characterized by maternal hypertension and proteinuria, typically manifesting in the late second or third trimester. It affects approximately 5%–8% of pregnancies worldwide and accounts for nearly 10%–15% of maternal deaths. PE is also associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, including preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, and low birth weight, with long‐term cardiovascular and metabolic consequences for both mother and offspring. Although clinical symptoms appear in the latter half of pregnancy, substantial evidence indicates that PE originates earlier during placental development. Impaired trophoblast invasion and defective spiral artery remodeling result in placental insufficiency, compromised uteroplacental perfusion, and a hypoxic placental microenvironment, which are hallmark features of the disease. Despite extensive research, PE remains a heterogeneous disorder lacking reliable early diagnostic markers or effective targeted therapies, with placental delivery remaining the only definitive treatment. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as critical regulators of placental development and function. Owing to their abundance, evolutionary conservation, and ability to modulate multiple gene networks, miRNAs regulate trophoblast proliferation, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and apoptosis, processes that are profoundly disrupted in PE. Genome‐wide expression studies have revealed widespread dysregulation of placental miRNAs in PE, frequently associated with hypoxia and angiogenic imbalance. This review summarizes current advances in miRNA expression and function in the preeclamptic placenta, focusing on their roles in trophoblast dysfunction and disease pathogenesis. We highlight differentially expressed placental miRNAs, their targets, and the regulatory pathways they influence, emphasizing the central role of miRNA‐mediated gene regulation in the development and progression of PE.

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy‐specific syndrome characterized by maternal hypertension and proteinuria, typically diagnosed during the late second or third trimester of pregnancy. It affects approximately 5%–8% of pregnancies worldwide and is recognized as a leading cause of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [1]. Despite extensive investigation, the precise etiology and pathogenesis of PE remain incompletely understood. The placenta is widely regarded as central to disease development, as delivery and removal of the placenta result in rapid resolution of clinical symptoms [2]. Nevertheless, rare cases of PE onset during the postpartum period have also been reported [3, 4].

Genome‐wide gene expression analyses have revealed significant alterations in the placental transcriptome of PE pregnancies compared with normotensive term pregnancies [5, 6]. These changes encompass not only protein‐coding mRNAs but also non‐coding RNAs, particularly microRNAs (miRNAs), which are increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of PE [7]. Importantly, placental hypoxia and a consequent imbalance between pro‐ and anti‐angiogenic factors are now considered hallmark features of PE and are tightly linked to dysregulated gene and miRNA expression.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small (21–25 nt), endogenous, single‐stranded non‐coding RNAs that regulate gene expression at the post‐transcriptional level. They function through imperfect base pairing between a 6–8 nucleotide seed sequence and target mRNAs, leading to recruitment of the RNA‐induced silencing complex (RISC) and subsequent mRNA degradation or translational repression [8]. The biogenesis of miRNAs is illustrated in Figure 1. In mammals, miRNAs are highly conserved across species. A single mRNA can be targeted by multiple miRNAs, while an individual miRNA may regulate numerous mRNAs. It is estimated that nearly one‐third of human genes are regulated by miRNAs, underscoring their broad influence on physiological processes [9]. Many miRNAs reside in the cytoplasm where they bind to their target mRNAs, while others are released into the body fluids (termed as circulating miRNAs) under normal or pathological conditions. Significant presence of these circulating miRNAs in body fluids establishes them as useful biomarkers of several diseases.

Canonical biogenesis of microRNAs. The canonical miRNA biogenesis pathway is linear and conserved in mammals. Primary miRNAs (several hundreds to thousands Kb) are transcribed by RNA polymerase II/III in the nucleus and cleaved by Drosha‐DGCR8 into ~60–80 nt pre‐miRNAs. Pre‐miRNAs are exported to the cytoplasm via Exportin‐5 and processed by Dicer and TRBP into mature miRNA duplexes. The guide strand incorporates into the RISC complex containing Argonaute, directing sequence‐specific silencing of target mRNAs via cleavage, translational repression, or deadenylation.

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that miRNAs play critical roles in fundamental cellular functions, including development, proliferation, metabolism, apoptosis, hormone signaling, and stem cell maintenance and differentiation [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Notably, several miRNAs are expressed in a placenta‐specific or placenta‐enriched manner. More recently, miRNAs have emerged as key regulators of trophoblast proliferation, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and apoptosis [15, 16, 17, 18]. Increasing evidence also supports the involvement of miRNAs in a wide range of human diseases, including pregnancy‐related disorders.

Collectively, these findings highlight the essential role of miRNAs in normal placental development and function, and suggest that their aberrant expression may contribute to the onset and progression of PE. In this review, we summarize recent advances in miRNA research in the context of PE, with a particular focus on differentially expressed miRNAs in preeclamptic placentas. This article also provides current information regarding the potential mechanistic roles of these PE‐related miRNAs, which comprise the miRNA‐mediated effects on placental trophoblast as well as endothelial cells in the context of PE‐pathogenesis. Inclusion of in vitro studies evaluating the miRNA‐mediated maladaptation of placental trophoblast cell line models (HTR8/SVneo, JEG‐3, BeWo, and JAR) further enriches this review with the latest findings that may provide a new promising diagnostic tool for this pregnancy‐related disorder.

Placentation and Preeclampsia Pathogenesis

Preeclampsia (PE) is primarily characterized by maternal hypertension (> 140/90 mmHg) and significant proteinuria (> 300 mg/24 h) during the second half of pregnancy. The resolution of clinical symptoms following placental removal indicates that abnormal placentation is central to disease development. Although the pathogenesis of PE remains incompletely understood, multiple pathological and physiological pathways contribute to its manifestation. A key defect in PE is impaired invasion of extravillous trophoblasts into the maternal vasculature, resulting in defective spiral artery remodeling. In parallel, reduced maternal nitric oxide synthesis disrupts embryo implantation and promotes endothelial dysfunction, leading to vasoconstriction, placental ischemia, and oxidative stress. These abnormalities promote the accumulation of trophoblast debris and increased placental release of harmful factors, including soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase‐1 (sFlt‐1) and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, into the maternal circulation, thereby triggering disease onset. In severe cases, these pathological changes may result in fetal complications such as intrauterine growth restriction and intrauterine fetal death.

Differential Expression of miRNAs in Pregnancy‐Related Complications

To date, several independent array‐based studies have profiled differential miRNA expression in human placentas from normal and complicated pregnancies. These studies have identified both ubiquitously expressed miRNAs and placenta‐specific miRNAs. Recent reports indicate that many of these miRNAs play essential roles in placental development [19, 20, 21], and their dysregulation contributes to pregnancy‐related disorders [22, 23]. Most of these miRNAs, discussed in this review, are not strictly placenta‐specific but are highly expressed in placental tissue and have significant impact in pregnancy‐related complications. Expression profiling studies have identified miRNAs commonly altered in gestational complications. For example, upregulation of miR‐499a‐5p and downregulation of miR‐100‐5p and miR‐199a‐5p are shared features of preeclampsia (PE), gestational hypertension, and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) [24, 25]. Additionally, reduced levels of miR‐146a‐5p, miR‐199a‐5p, and miR‐221‐3p in maternal plasma are associated with pregnancy termination before 34 weeks in PE [24, 25], while downregulation of miR‐26a‐5p, miR‐103a‐3p, and miR‐145‐5p correlates with early termination in both PE and IUGR. Previous studies from the same group identified placenta‐specific miRNAs (miR‐516‐5p, miR‐517a‐3p, miR‐518b, miR‐520a‐5p, miR‐520h, miR‐525‐5p, and miR‐526a‐3p) that normally increase with gestational age but are abnormally elevated in the first trimester of pregnancies that later develop PE and/or IUGR [26]. In contrast, a G>C single nucleotide polymorphism in miR‐146a‐5p was reported to reduce susceptibility to severe PE in Black South African women [27]. A recent study by [28], has also shown a negative correlation between miR‐146a‐5p and SMAD4 in severe PE which affected the proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis of trophoblast cells. Upregulation of the placenta‐specific chromosome 19 microRNA cluster (C19MC) has been observed in the plasma of women with severe PE [29]. C19MC is the largest human miRNA cluster discovered yet, and it is exclusively expressed in the primate placenta. Differential expression of C19MC members, including miR‐512‐3p, miR‐518f‐3p, miR‐520c‐3p, and miR‐520d‐3p, has also been reported at various gestational stages, supporting their involvement in PE development [30]. Furthermore, miR‐520a‐5p, along with miR‐194‐5p and miR‐149‐5p, has been associated with both IUGR and PE [31]. Beyond gestational disorders, miRNAs also regulate implantation. In mouse models, altered expression of miR‐486‐3p and miR‐3074‐5p has been detected at implantation sites, and similar changes have been observed in placental villi from women with recurrent miscarriage [32]. Although differential miRNA expression in pregnancy‐related disorders highlights their potential regulatory roles, further studies are required to identify their target genes. Such investigations will improve understanding of disease pathogenesis and facilitate the development of diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Abnormal Expression of microRNAs in Preeclampsia

Since 2007, numerous studies have investigated placental microRNA expression in preeclampsia (PE) using diverse analytical approaches. Placenta‐specific miRNAs can be released into maternal circulation via exosome‐mediated extracellular vesicles, and placental dysfunction in PE enhances this release [33]. Many of these circulating miRNAs show differential expression in plasma and serum of PE patients compared with normal individuals, highlighting their potential as non‐invasive biomarkers for early disease detection. The roles of circulating miRNAs are extensively reviewed in a recently published review article by [34]. Tables 1 and 2 summarize miRNAs differentially expressed in placental tissue and maternal plasma in PE. Contribution of microRNAs to PE Pathogenesis

Impaired invasion of placental cytotrophoblasts into the maternal vasculature leads to inadequate spiral artery remodeling and reduced placental perfusion. This results in trophoblast injury and the release of cellular debris, microparticles, and other harmful factors into maternal and fetal circulation, ultimately disrupting fetoplacental endothelial function and contributing to PE manifestations. Therefore, abnormal placental development plays a central role in disease pathophysiology. Besides this, angiogenic imbalance, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction also have profound influences on the disease pathogenesis. Multiple studies have demonstrated the importance of miRNAs in placental development and endothelial functioning. Additionally, single nucleotide polymorphism in the miRNA‐processing gene, Drosha, has been associated with increased PE susceptibility [36]. Together, these findings indicate that miRNA dysregulation contributes to PE onset through placental as well as endothelial dysfunction, as depicted in Figure 2. Figure 2 shows that microRNAs, which are up regulated in physiological pregnancies, play important roles in trophoblast proliferation, invasion, and suppress trophoblast apoptosis. They also maintain endothelial function as well as vascular remodeling to meet the requirement of gestational success. Conversely, microRNAs that are increased in PE pregnancies impede trophoblast proliferation, differentiation, and invasion. Moreover, these up‐regulated miRNAs also induce apoptosis and endothelial dysfunction and also inhibit angiogenesis. The impacts of these deregulated miRNAs in PE pathogenesis are discussed in the subsequent sections.

Role of miRNAs in Placental Angiogenesis and Endothelial Dysfunction During PE

Spiral artery remodeling and placental vascularization are critical determinants of successful pregnancy [37]. Deletion of the miRNA‐processing enzyme Dicer causes defective blood vessel formation and embryonic lethality, highlighting the importance of miRNAs in angiogenesis [38]. More recent studies suggest that miRNAs contribute to the pathogenesis of preeclampsia (PE) by impairing placental angiogenesis and vascular remodeling.

Several members of the miR‐17‐92 cluster (miR‐17‐5p, miR‐17‐3p, miR‐20a‐5p, and miR‐20b‐5p) are upregulated in PE placentas compared with normotensive term placentas. miR‐20b‐5p shows strong expression in the villous syncytium and capillary endothelial cells of PE placentas but is absent in endothelial cells of healthy placentas [39]. miR‐20b‐5p directly downregulates the pro‐angiogenic factors ephrin‐B2 and its receptor EPHB4, which regulate VEGF receptor signaling and are significantly reduced in PE [39]. Upregulation of miR‐17‐3p is shown to be correlated with elevated oxidative stress (indicated by increased TOS and decreased SOD/TAC expression) in PE placentas along with an imbalance of trace elements (lower zinc and higher copper concentration) in PE [40]. Another miRNA implicated in endothelial dysfunction in PE is miR‐203‐3p, which is significantly upregulated in PE endothelial cells. miR‐203‐3p promotes inflammation by increasing the expression of ICAM, IL‐6, and IL‐8, leading to enhanced leukocyte adhesion to EC, a hallmark of PE. This effect is mediated through suppression of the anti‐inflammatory molecule SOCS‐3 by miR‐203‐3p, resulting in endothelial inflammation and neutrophil adhesion [41]. A recent study by [42], has also shown that PE‐upregulated miR‐203‐3p impairs the trophoblast vascular remodeling by targeting IGF‐1R. In addition, several anti‐angiogenic and pro‐inflammatory miRNAs (miR‐98‐5p, miR‐222‐3p, miR‐210‐3p, miR‐155‐5p, miR‐296‐3p, miR‐181a‐5p, and miR‐29b‐3p) are elevated in the plasma of women with severe PE, although their direct functional roles remain unclear. Notably, reduced expression of miR‐221‐3p 1 year postpartum suggests persistent inflammation following PE [43].

Another essential criterion for successful placental development is a hypoxic environment, which promotes trophoblast invasion and angiogenesis. Hypoxia induces hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α), which facilitates the overexpression of sFlt‐1. Increased sFlt‐1 sequesters free vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and placental growth factor (PlGF) in the maternal circulation, thereby preventing these two pro‐angiogenic growth factors from binding to their respective membrane receptors. This results in systemic vasoconstriction (hypertension) and glomerular endotheliosis (proteinuria), which are significant features of PE pathogenesis [44]. Concurrently, induction of placental apoptosis leads to the release of trophoblast debris into the maternal circulation, triggering a pro‐inflammatory state that ultimately leads to disease manifestation. Hypoxia can exert both physiological and pathological effects through the regulation of miRNA expression. These hypoxia‐induced miRNAs are often termed hypoxamiRs. For instance, miR‐126‐3p, miR‐195‐5p, miR‐517‐3p, miR‐133b‐3p, miR‐139‐5p, miR‐146a‐5p, miR‐155‐5p, miR‐335‐5p, miR‐652‐3p, and miR‐584‐5p are typically regulated by hypoxia and influence PE pathogenesis, as discussed in the following section. The role of another potent hypoxamiR, miR‐210, which has a major impact on PE pathogenesis, is discussed in a later section of this review. MiR‐126‐3p, a pro‐angiogenic and hypoxia‐induced miRNA highly expressed in endothelial cells, is significantly downregulated in PE. Reduced miR‐126‐3p impairs endothelial progenitor cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation by increasing the anti‐angiogenic factor PIK3R2, a negative regulator of PI3‐kinase signaling [45]. Overexpression of miR‐126‐3p in PE rat models improves placental vascularization, fetal outcomes, and microvessel density. Decreased miR‐126‐3p levels in PE placentas also correlate with reduced VEGF expression, although the underlying mechanism remains to be fully defined. In contrast, circulating miR‐195‐5p is positively correlated with elevated levels of the anti‐angiogenic factor sFlt‐1 in PE. Both miR‐195‐5p and sFlt‐1 are increased in the plasma of preeclamptic women, and PE plasma induces miR‐195‐5p expression and anti‐angiogenic activity in endothelial cells, possibly via sFlt‐1‐mediated pathways [46]. Other miRNAs, including miR‐517a/b‐3p and miR‐517c‐3p, also enhance sFlt‐1 expression in trophoblast cells, whereas miR‐133b‐3p and miR‐139‐5p, which are downregulated in PE, directly suppress sFlt‐1 expression [47, 48, 49].

PlGF, a member of the VEGF family, regulates placental angiogenesis during pregnancy through the modulation of placental endothelial and trophoblast cells. During physiological pregnancy, PlGF is highly expressed by villous cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast cells; however, decreased production of this factor is associated with pregnancy‐related complications, including PE. In PE, the reduced concentration of PlGF, along with increased sFlt‐1 levels, leads to an imbalance in the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio, which is used to predict the risk of developing PE. A recent study by Gusar et al. demonstrated the role of miR‐652‐3p in the regulation of PlGF. PlGF expression is mediated by GCM1 (Glial Cells Missing Transcription Factor 1), which is inhibited by SUMOylation. UBC9 (Ubiquitin‐conjugating enzyme 9) facilitates the SUMOylation of GCM1 and therefore exerts an inhibitory effect on PlGF expression. The researchers demonstrated that, during early‐onset preeclampsia, reduced levels of miR‐652‐3p are associated with increased expression of UBC9, ultimately leading to decreased PlGF expression through GCM1 SUMOylation [50].

Expression of another hypoxia‐regulated miRNA, miR‐155‐5p, is inversely correlated with AT1R levels in PE, and its overexpression reduces AT1R expression in endothelial cells [51]. MiR‐155‐5p also impairs endothelial function by downregulating eNOS, thereby inhibiting vasorelaxation and trophoblast invasion. TNF‐α‐induced upregulation of miR‐155‐5p in PE can be prevented by aspirin, restoring eNOS expression [52]. Additionally, miR‐335‐5p and miR‐584‐5p, which are upregulated in PE, directly target eNOS and inhibit trophoblast migration. Recently, TNF‐α/ NF‐κB mediated up regulation of miR‐214‐3p during PE has been reported, which causes endothelial dysfunction by impairing eNOS expression [53]. In addition, with these hypoxia‐regulated miRNAs, MiR‐29b‐3p, which is not regulated by hypoxia, is aberrantly overexpressed in PE and inhibits trophoblast invasion and angiogenesis while promoting apoptosis by regulating VEGFA, MMP2, MCL1, and integrin β1, suggesting a role in PE pathophysiology [54]. Angiotensin II contributes to PE by promoting inflammation, and miR‐155‐5p negatively regulates its receptor Ang II type 1 receptor (AT1R). Taken together, despite their distinct roles in endothelial dysfunction, miRNAs converge functionally in regulating placental vascularization and spiral artery remodeling, highlighting their importance in understanding the pathogenesis of preeclampsia. Role of MicroRNAs in Trophoblast Migration and Invasion During Preeclampsia

Trophoblast migration and invasion are essential for proper placentation. In preeclampsia (PE), these processes are impaired, leading to shallow trophoblast invasion and contributing to disease development [55, 56]. Several studies have investigated the regulatory roles of miRNAs in trophoblast migration and invasion [57, 58]. Yang et al. reported that miR‐15b‐5p, which is overexpressed in PE, contributes to disease pathogenesis by directly targeting Argonaute 2 (Ago2), a key miRNA effector protein expressed in trophoblast and endothelial cells and required for trophoblast invasion and endothelial tube formation. Overexpression of miR‐15b‐5p suppressed invasion in HTR8/SVneo cells and tube formation in HUVECs. ChIP analysis of LPS‐treated cells showed increased Ago2 occupancy on the miR‐15b promoter, suggesting a regulatory feedback loop between Ago2 and miR‐15b‐5p during placentation [59, 60]. Significant downregulation of miR‐146a‐5p is observed in amniotic fluid‐derived exosomes of PE patients (mentioned in previous section). In vitro administration of miR‐146a‐5p mimic can restore CoCl2 inhibited proliferation and migration of HTR‐8/SVneo and JEG‐3 cells by suppressing HIF‐1α/FLT‐1 expression [61]. HTR‐8/SVneo cells are immortalized trophoblast cell line derived from first trimester placenta (6–12 weeks). This cell line harbors extravillous trophoblast behavior which includes high capacity of proliferation, migration and invasion. Although these cells have limitation of significant heterogeneity (due to mixing of stromal/mesenchymal cells with trophoblast population) but, acts as a valuable in vitro tool for studying placenta development as well as pregnancy related complications, such as, preeclampsia. While JEG‐3 cell line, a human choriocarcinoma derived from Erwin‐Turner tumor, possesses epithelial‐like nature and acts as placental trophoblast model, especially for studying trophoblast differentiation and invasion that have direct impacts on pregnancy disorders. Another microRNA, miR‐193b‐3p, also upregulated in PE, inhibits trophoblast migration and invasion by directly targeting TGF‐β2 in HTR8/SVneo cells [62]. Similarly, aberrant expression of miR‐34a‐5p and miR‐20a‐5p in preeclamptic placentas suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion of JEG‐3 cells by targeting MYC and FOXA1, respectively. In vivo, miR‐20a‐5p reduced JEG‐3 xenograft tumor growth in nude mice [63]. miR‐204‐5p and miR‐491‐5p suppress invasion of JEG‐3, BeWo (another human trophoblast‐derived choriocarcinoma, which, like JEG‐3, are widely used as an in vitro model for studying trophoblast syncytialization) and HTR‐8/SVneo cells by targeting MMP‐9, whereas miR‐519b‐3p inhibits migration and invasion by targeting MMP‐2 [64, 65]. In addition, miR‐93‐5p and miR‐520g‐3p downregulate MMP2 expression in trophoblast cells [66, 67]. These miRNAs are overexpressed in PE and contribute to impaired trophoblast invasion.

Invasion of HTR8/SVneo cells has also been affected by miR‐125b‐1‐3p and miR‐299‐5p, which were found to be elevated in placentas derived from preeclamptic women [68]. MiR‐125b‐1‐3p inhibits invasion by targeting sphingosine‐1‐phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1), an angiogenesis‐regulating factor [69]. Another study by Yang et al. showed that miR‐125b‐5p suppresses SGPL1 expression by targeting its 3′UTR. SGPL1 degrades sphingosine‐1‐phosphate and modulates immune responses, and its deficiency increases proinflammatory cytokine IL‐8. Thus, the negative correlation between miR‐125b‐5p and SGPL1 may contribute to PE pathogenesis [59, 60]. MiR‐299‐5p inhibits invasion by targeting HDAC2 [68].

MiR‐218‐5p, which contributes to PE development, also inhibits trophoblast invasion. HIF‐1α binds to the promoters of SLIT2 and SLIT3, host genes of miR‐218‐5p, inducing its expression and suppressing invasion by targeting cytoskeletal organizing protein, LASP1 [70]. MiR‐137, another miRNA overexpressed in PE, inhibits trophoblast proliferation and migration by downregulating ERRα [71]. The placenta‐specific miR‐141‐3p is overexpressed in PE and regulates trophoblast invasion and proliferation. Elevated miR‐141‐3p is secreted in extracellular vesicles and transported to maternal T cells, suggesting a role in trophoblast–immune cell communication [72].

MiR‐155‐5p, previously implicated in angiogenesis, inhibits HTR8/SVneo cell proliferation and invasion by regulating cell cycle proteins. It induces G1 arrest by targeting Cyclin D1, modulates p27 expression, and suppresses migration by reducing filamin A phosphorylation [73]. LPS treatment increases miR‐155‐5p, AP‐1, and NF‐κB p65 expression in trophoblast cells, and similar upregulation is observed in PE placentas. Binding sites for AP‐1 and NF‐κB are present upstream of the miR‐155‐5p host gene, explaining its transcriptional activation. Aspirin treatment in HUVECs inhibits NF‐κB‐dependent transcription of miR‐155‐5p, leading to endothelial dysfunction [52].

In contrast, several miRNAs that promote trophoblast invasion are downregulated in PE. miR‐18a‐5p enhances invasion of HTR8/SVneo cells by suppressing Smad2 but is reduced in severe PE, accompanied by elevated Smad2 expression in placental tissues [74]. MiR‐18b‐5p is also downregulated in PE and directly targets HIF‐1α. Its overexpression suppresses HIF‐1α and affects trophoblast invasion, migration, and viability, indicating that reduced miR‐18b‐5p contributes to HIF‐1α accumulation in PE [75]. On the other hand, miR‐195‐5p, which promotes the invasion of HTR8/SVneo cells by downregulating the expression of ActRIIA (type II receptor of Activin and Nodal), was appeared to be downregulated in PE‐placentas, whereas ActRIIA level was found to be increased in preeclamptic placentas compared to time‐matched normal placentas [76]. MiR‐675‐5p, derived from the paternally imprinted H19 gene, enhances JEG‐3 cell proliferation by targeting NOMO1 (Nodal Modulator 1). NOMO1 is a transmembrane protein, present in the endoplasmic reticulum and acts as an antagonist of Nodal signaling. H19 expression is reduced in PE, leading to decreased miR‐675‐5p levels. Since Nodal inhibits trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis, reduced miR‐675‐5p may contribute to abnormal trophoblast growth in early‐onset severe PE. Collectively, it is evident that miRNAs act as key regulators of trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion, dysregulation of which leads to the PE pathogenesis.

Role of microRNAs in Trophoblast Cell Apoptosis During Preeclampsia

PE is associated with increased apoptosis and impaired migration and invasion of placental trophoblast cells. These abnormalities contribute significantly to disease pathogenesis. Several microRNAs (miRNAs) regulate trophoblast proliferation and survival by modulating apoptotic pathways. MiR‐378a‐5p promotes trophoblast survival, proliferation, migration, and invasion in HTR8/SVneo cells and enhances extravillous trophoblast (EVT) outgrowth [15]. However, its expression is reduced in preeclamptic placentas. MiR‐378a‐5p directly inhibits Nodal, a TGF‐β superfamily member involved in early embryonic development, which is overexpressed in PE placentas. Although Nodal silencing only partially mimics the effects of miR‐378a‐5p, these findings suggest additional regulatory mechanisms. Another study by [77], has shown the potential role for the miR‐378a‐3p/CMTM3 axis in regulating trophoblast migration by altering the expression of migration‐related proteins (MMP2, MMP9, TIMP1, and TIMP2). Similarly, miR‐376c‐3p and miR‐454‐3p, which are downregulated in PE, suppress trophoblast apoptosis by targeting the Nodal receptor ALK7. Reduced miR‐376c‐3p and miR‐454‐3p expression enhances Nodal signaling, leading to excessive apoptotic cell death [78, 79].

Another downregulated miRNA, miR‐18a‐5p [80], affects both trophoblast invasion and trophoblast apoptosis during PE. In JEG‐3 cells, miR‐18a‐5p inhibits apoptosis by targeting estrogen receptor‐α (ESRα), which is overexpressed in preeclamptic placentas [81]. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress also contributes to trophoblast apoptosis in PE. ERp44, an ER‐resident protein involved in oxidative protein folding, is upregulated in PE placentas. miR‐101‐3p, which targets ERp44, is downregulated in PE and may normally suppress ER stress–induced apoptosis in trophoblast cells [82].

It has been reported that silencing of tumor suppressor, PTEN (Phosphatase and tensin homolog) may lead to the development of many cancers by promoting proliferation and migration of malignant cells. PTEN markedly increased in PE‐affected placentas. The increased expression of this tumor suppressor can promote apoptosis and inhibit the migration of trophoblastic cells which may contribute to the pathogenesis of the disease. Reduced expression of miR‐144‐3p in PE leads to PTEN upregulation. In vitro studies showed that miR‐144‐3p promotes trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion while reducing apoptosis by directly targeting PTEN. In addition, miR‐144‐3p is reduced in PE placentae [83].

In contrast, several pro‐apoptotic miRNAs are upregulated in PE, including miR‐128a‐3p and miR‐34a‐5p [84, 85]. MiR‐128a‐3p induces apoptosis in HTR8/SVneo cells by targeting Bax, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, increased reactive oxygen species, and cytochrome‐C release [84]. MiR‐34a‐5p promotes apoptosis by directly suppressing Bcl‐2 [85]. MiR‐23a‐3p is another apoptosis‐inducing miRNA that is upregulated in PE. Its overexpression promotes trophoblast apoptosis by targeting X‐linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP), and restoration of XIAP reverses this effect, highlighting a potential therapeutic target [86]. MiR‐135b‐5p, which is abnormally upregulated in PE, has been found to induce apoptosis and thus impede trophoblast invasion and migration by targeting ADAM12 [87]. In addition to apoptosis, autophagy is involved in PE pathophysiology. Autophagy‐related proteins such as Atgs and LC3 are elevated in PE placentas. Let‐7i, which directly targets Atg4B, is downregulated in severe PE. Increased Atg4B expression enhances autophagy, whereas let‐7i overexpression suppresses autophagic activity in trophoblast cell lines [88]. Overall, dysregulated miRNA expression in preeclampsia disrupts the balance between trophoblast survival, apoptosis, and autophagy, thereby contributing to abnormal placentation and disease progression.

Role of microRNAs in Regulation of MSCs During Preeclampsia

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are multipotent progenitor cells with self‐renewal and differentiation capacities and are widely studied for their therapeutic potential [89]. MSCs are present in human placenta which serves as a rich and ethical source for these cells. Placenta‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (PMSCs) can be extracted from the maternal side (decidua basalis) and fetal side (chorionic plate) [90, 91]. The immunosuppressive nature of these PMSCs plays a crucial role in protecting the placenta and fetus from the maternal immune system by modulating the maternal immune cell functions at the maternal–fetal interface; these cells help establish maternal immune tolerance toward the semi‐allogeneic fetus (which expresses paternal antigens) [92, 93, 94]. They also play important roles in angiogenesis and tissue regeneration. Decidual MSCs (dMSCs), located at the maternal–fetal interface, contribute to vessel maturation and placental development [95]. Altered cytokine expression in dMSCs from PE patients suggests their involvement in disease pathogenesis [96]. Thus, discussing the role of miRNAs in the regulation of these placenta‐derived MSCs would be beneficial for the better understanding of placental maladaptation that results in PE manifestation.

MicroRNAs critically regulate MSC function during preeclampsia. Expression profiling revealed increased miR‐16‐5p levels in PE‐derived dMSCs. MiR‐16‐5p inhibits cell proliferation and migration by inducing G0/G1 arrest through suppression of cyclin E1 and impairs angiogenesis by directly targeting VEGFA. Both targets are inversely correlated with miR‐16‐5p expression in severe PE [39]. MiR‐136‐5p is also upregulated in PE‐dMSCs and suppresses PPP2R2A, a negative regulator of MSC differentiation [97]. In addition, miR‐136‐5p inhibits proliferation, induces apoptosis by targeting Bcl‐2, and suppresses trophoblast invasion and capillary formation through VEGF inhibition, thereby contributing to disease progression [98]. MiR‐494‐3p, which is elevated in PE placentas, inhibits dMSC proliferation by inducing G1/S arrest through targeting cyclin D1 and CDK6. It also suppresses MSC‐mediated angiogenesis by reducing VEGF expression, thereby impairing trophoblast and endothelial cell migration and tube formation [99]. Similarly, miR‐495‐3p is significantly upregulated in severe PE. Its overexpression induces S‐phase arrest, reduces migration, and promotes apoptosis in MSCs by downregulating the self‐renewal factor Bmi1, leading to cellular senescence [86].

Beyond their regenerative roles, MSCs exert strong immunomodulatory effects through paracrine factors and cell–cell interactions. MiR‐181a‐5p, which is elevated in PE‐derived placental and umbilical cord MSCs, attenuates MSC proliferation and immunosuppressive capacity by inhibiting TGF‐β signaling and activating p38 and JNK pathways. By targeting TGFBR1 and TGFBRAP1, miR‐181a‐5p reduces TGF‐β activity and increases IL‐6 and indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase expression. Consequently, MSC‐mediated suppression of T‐cell proliferation and IFN‐γ production is impaired [100].

Another PE‐associated miRNA, miR‐30a, contributes to immune dysregulation at the maternal–fetal interface. In MSCs, miR‐30a‐3p suppresses IL‐1β‐induced activation of NF‐κB and JNK pathways and inhibits IL‐6, IL‐8, and COX2 production by targeting TABLE 3. It also abolishes the anti‐inflammatory effects of MSCs on LPS‐stimulated macrophages [101].

Collectively, dysregulated miRNA expression in preeclampsia alters MSC proliferation, differentiation, angiogenic capacity, and immunomodulatory functions, thereby contributing to abnormal placentation and immune imbalance. Further studies are required to fully elucidate miRNA‐mediated MSC regulation and to explore their potential as therapeutic targets in preeclampsia.

Other Roles of MicroRNAs in Preeclampsia Pathogenesis

In addition, several miRNAs contribute to PE by disrupting trophoblast differentiation and placental function. MiR‐106a‐5p and miR‐19b‐3p inhibit syncytialization of cytotrophoblasts by suppressing aromatase and GCM1, key regulators of syncytiotrophoblast formation. c‐Myc activates the miR‐17‐92 and miR‐106a‐363 clusters, thereby increasing miR‐19b‐3p and miR‐106a‐5p expression while repressing syncytialization. This regulatory axis is attenuated during normal differentiation but remains active in PE [102].

Similarly, c‐Myc induces miR‐515‐5p by binding to E‐box elements upstream of its precursors. miR‐515‐5p inhibits aromatase, GCM1, and Frizzled‐5, thereby impairing trophoblast fusion. PE placentas exhibit elevated c‐Myc and miR‐515‐5p levels with reduced differentiation markers, supporting its pathogenic role. Hormonal dysregulation in PE is also mediated by miRNAs [103, 104]. During PE, HSD17B3‐induced testosterone elevation promotes miR‐22‐3p expression, which suppresses estrogen receptor‐α and indirectly reduces aromatase activity and estradiol production [105]. Furthermore, miR‐21‐5p downregulates cystathionine γ‐lyase (CSE), an enzyme responsible for hydrogen sulfide production and umbilical artery regulation. Hypoxia‐induced miR‐21‐5p expression reduces CSE levels in PE and intrauterine growth restriction, contributing to vascular dysfunction [106].

MiR‐210‐3p, a key regulator in preeclampsia pathogenesis, a hypoxamiR ubiquitously activated by HIF‐1α and secreted via exosomes [103, 104, 107, 108]. In preeclampsia (PE), miR‐210‐3p levels are significantly elevated in maternal circulation and placental tissues. Increased total and exosomal miR‐210‐3p levels correlate positively with disease severity, highlighting its potential as a biomarker [109]. Placental miR‐210‐3p expression differs between the amnion and chorion [110]. While normal term pregnancies show higher levels in the amnion, PE is associated with an inverse pattern linked to preterm risk, although larger studies are required for validation [110]. In vitro studies using human trophoblast cell lines demonstrate that hypoxia induces miR‐210‐3p expression and suppresses trophoblast migration and invasion. Overexpression of miR‐210‐3p in JAR (human trophoblast‐derived choriocarcinoma), HTR8/SVneo, and primary cytotrophoblast cells reduces invasiveness, mimicking hypoxic effects. Mechanistically, miR‐210‐3p downregulates ephrin‐A3 and HOXA9, genes involved in cell migration and vascular remodeling, both of which are reduced in PE placentas [111]. Beyond HIF‐1α, miR‐210‐3p is also transcriptionally regulated by NF‐κB p50 under hypoxic conditions [111]. NF‐κB activity is elevated in PE, suggesting coordinated induction of miR‐210‐3p by both HIF‐1α and NF‐κB [112, 113]. Luo et al. demonstrated that miR‐210‐3p suppresses trophoblast invasion by directly targeting potassium channel modulatory factor 1 (KCMF1). TNF‐α, an inflammatory mediator elevated in PE, induces miR‐210‐3p and further represses KCMF1 expression, linking inflammation and impaired trophoblast motility [114]. Similarly, thrombospondin type I domain‐containing protein 7A (THSD7A), another invasion‐related gene, is directly targeted by miR‐210‐3p and downregulated in PE [115]. miR‐210‐3p also inhibits protein tyrosine phosphatase PTPN2, potentially affecting cell growth and survival, although its precise role requires further investigation [116].

In addition to impairing trophoblast invasion, miR‐210‐3p disrupts mitochondrial metabolism. It targets iron–sulfur cluster assembly enzyme (ISCU), a key regulator of mitochondrial respiration. Reduced ISCU expression in PE leads to impaired oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial dysfunction [117]. In Swan71 cells, ISCU knockdown reduces invasiveness and increases iron accumulation, consistent with placental hemosiderin deposition in PE [118]. However, further studies are needed to confirm whether miR‐210‐3p overexpression fully recapitulates these effects. miR‐210‐3p also regulates vascular and endocrine pathways. It inhibits TET1, leading to repression of BKCa channel function in uterine arteries and increased vascular tone [47, 48]. Moreover, miR‐210‐3p downregulates hydroxysteroid (17β) dehydrogenase 1 (HSD17B1), a placental enzyme responsible for estradiol synthesis. Reduced HSD17B1 levels precede PE onset and are reproduced in hypoxic trophoblast cells with elevated miR‐210‐3p [119]. Collectively, miR‐210‐3p integrates hypoxic, inflammatory, metabolic, vascular, and hormonal signals to promote abnormal placentation and disease progression in preeclampsia.

Overall, diverse miRNAs regulate trophoblast differentiation, endocrine signaling, vascular homeostasis, and placental metabolism in preeclampsia. Their coordinated dysregulation disrupts placental development and maternal–fetal adaptation, emphasizing their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

A unifying model of miRNA‐mediated placental maladaptation proposes that major placental complications, such as preeclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction, gestational diabetes and preterm birth, share a common network of dysregulated placental miRNAs that impair trophoblast adaptation to the maternal environment. In normal physiological conditions, the placenta continuously adapts to different parameters, such as vascular remodeling, endocrine signaling, oxygen tension, nutrient availability, immune tolerance, oxidative, and inflammatory stress. Each of these parameters is regulated via miRNA‐mediated pathways. Various environmental or maternal stressors, including hypoxia, oxidative stress, metabolic stress, inflammation, along with maternal infection, maternal age, maternal obesity and maternal autoimmunity, can deregulate the miRNA expression. This dysregulation in miRNA expression causes the placenta to shift from a state of adaptive to maladaptive signaling, that ultimately leads to placental dysfunction.

Accumulating evidence demonstrates the indispensable roles of microRNAs in human placental development. The placenta produces and secretes numerous miRNAs that regulate key processes, including trophoblast differentiation, invasion, migration, angiogenesis, and endocrine function. In this review, we have discussed the aberrant expression of critical miRNAs associated with preeclampsia through disruption of fundamental placentation events (Figures 3 and 4). Several miRNAs exhibit altered expression in preeclamptic pregnancies, and recent profiling studies have identified multiple circulating miRNAs in maternal serum (Table 2), highlighting their potential as noninvasive biomarkers for early diagnosis and disease monitoring. For instance, hypoxia‐induced miRNA, miR‐210‐3p, dramatically increased in the placental tissue and serum of PE patients. Moreover, the expression levels of miR‐210‐3p seemed to correlate with disease severity. Presently, the circulating miR‐210‐3p is under clinical trial to establish this particular miRNA as a novel biomarker for the diagnosis of preeclampsia (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT03193554; Official title: Diagnostic Value of microRNA 210 In Preeclampsia). Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo studies have identified miRNAs that directly contribute to preeclampsia by targeting proteins involved in trophoblast proliferation, migration, invasion (Figure 4), angiogenesis, mesenchymal and endothelial cell function (Figure 3). One such miRNA, miR‐126‐3p, which is also under clinical trial phase, acts as bioactive cargo in mesenchymal stem cell‐derived exosomes (MSC‐Exos) to improve endothelial dysfunction in postpartum PE mothers by regenerative therapy (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT07183384; Official title: The Effectiveness of Mesenchymal Stem Cell‐Derived Exosome Therapy in Improving Endothelial Dysfunction in Postpartum Mothers with a History of Preeclampsia).

Despite these advances, significant gaps remain in translating miRNA‐based discoveries into clinical applications. Future research should prioritize well‐designed longitudinal studies and in vivo functional analyses using appropriate animal models to evaluate the effects of miRNA gain‐ and loss‐of‐function on disease initiation and progression. Such approaches will be essential for validating candidate miRNAs as therapeutic targets. Overall, this review highlights the central role of miRNA‐mediated gene regulation in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia and their promise as novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic tools for improving maternal and fetal outcomes.
