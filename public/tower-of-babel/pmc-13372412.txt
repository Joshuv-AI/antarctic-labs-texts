Introduction

Catheter ablation is a cornerstone of therapy for sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) in patients with structural heart disease. Accurate identification of arrhythmogenic substrate is essential to procedural success, yet recurrence rates of 30%–50% are still reported even in experienced centers [1]. Electrogram‐based approaches such as bipolar voltage mapping, late potential (LP) detection, and identification of local abnormal ventricular activity (LAVAs) remain limited by far‐field interference, catheter orientation, and signal attenuation [2]. Visual annotation is time‐consuming and operator‐dependent, which reduces reproducibility.

To overcome these limitations, interest has shifted toward functional mapping techniques that provide objective, physiology‐based metrics from intracardiac electrograms (IEGMs). Decrement‐evoked potential (DEEP) and evoked delayed potential (EDP) mapping are among the most widely studied approaches [3, 4]. These methods identify sites where electrograms show stimulus‐QRS delay or decrement during programmed stimulation, which reflects slow conduction within the VT circuit. Early studies suggest that DEEP or EDP‐guided ablation can improve procedural specificity by targeting functionally critical channels within low‐voltage areas [5]. However, they require multiple pacing protocols, may be influenced by cycle length and pacing site, and depend on patient haemodynamic stability. Their reliance on stimulation‐induced responses also limits use in patients who cannot tolerate pacing or when arrhythmia induction is avoided.

Frequency‐domain analysis of sinus or paced rhythm electrograms offers a stimulus‐independent alternative for probing local conduction properties. Dominant frequency (DF) mapping can be performed rapidly on data acquired during routine mapping and provides a quantitative marker that may highlight both superficial and deep intramural substrate. High‐frequency activity has been linked to midmyocardial fibrosis and other arrhythmogenic regions that may not be apparent on voltage maps [6]. Most previous studies have used offline or partially manual analysis, two‐dimensional visualization and binary lesion overlap, with limited anatomical integration and no standardized method for spatial concordance assessment. DF analysis could be combined with DEEP or EDP mapping to integrate complementary physiological information: temporal delay from pacing and spectral content from intrinsic signals.

Several developments make DF mapping timely. High‐density mapping catheters are now in routine use, computational analysis can be performed in real time in the electrophysiology laboratory, and there is increasing emphasis on advanced analysis tools that can be applied during sinus rhythm or stable pacing. Despite this, no established pipeline exists for DF extraction, projection onto patient‐specific three‐dimensional ventricular models, and quantitative assessment against ablation targets.

In this study, we developed a DF mapping workflow that includes IEGM segmentation, spectral analysis, and three‐dimensional anatomical projection. We applied it retrospectively in patients undergoing VT ablation and evaluated feasibility, applicability with different catheter types, and spatial performance using continuous proximity metrics and discriminative analysis. This represents a first step toward quantitative, spatially resolved assessment of DF activity in relation to procedural ablation targets, providing a foundation for future clinical validation.

Methods

Study Population

We retrospectively analyzed patients with sustained VT who underwent catheter ablation at Amsterdam University Medical Centers. VT diagnosis was confirmed from clinical records and 12‐lead electrocardiography. Both ischemic and non‐ischemic cardiomyopathy were included. Patients with prior cardiac surgery, additional arrhythmias (e.g., atrial fibrillation), or incomplete intracardiac electrogram (IEGM) data were excluded. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all patients had provided written informed consent for the use of their data for research purposes.

Data Acquisition

Electrograms were acquired with the CARTO 3 mapping system (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA, USA) using either an irrigated‐tip contact force catheter (SmartTouch) or a multielectrode mapping catheter (Pentaray), or both, enabling evaluation of the method's performance across differing electrode sizes, interelectrode spacing, and signal bandwidths. Mapping was performed during sinus rhythm and/or right ventricular pacing according to clinical protocol. Data exports included bipolar IEGMs, surface ECGs, three‐dimensional (3D) catheter point coordinates, and patient‐specific ventricular geometries.

Dominant Frequency Analysis

A computational analysis pipeline was developed to extract and spatially map DF activity.

1.Signal segmentation: A region of interest (ROI) was defined around the R wave in lead V2 to account for variability in QRS onset and morphology (Figure 1A). Pre‐ and post‐peak offsets from the R wave were applied uniformly to all mapping channels to standardize temporal alignment (Figure 1B). Lead V2 served solely as a temporal reference. Recordings without a detected surface QRS peak were excluded.

Recordings without a detected surface QRS peak were excluded.

2.Spectral analysis: Segmented electrograms were transformed into the frequency domain using the fast Fourier transform (Figure 1B3). The peak frequency (PF) was defined as the frequency with the maximum spectral magnitude, rounded to the nearest Hz. PF was chosen as a pragmatic and computationally efficient descriptor for three‐dimensional integration.

Anatomical Data Processing

Patient‐specific ventricular meshes were generated from electroanatomic mapping data and processed in 3D Slicer (v5.8.1). Ablation sites were projected onto the nearest mesh surface, and projection distances were recorded for quality control. DF values above the threshold were interpolated over the ventricular surface using inverse‐distance weighting, and sites below the threshold were excluded from interpolation. Color maps were generated to visualize DF distribution, with ablation sites overlaid for qualitative comparison (Figure 2).

Spatial Concordance Analysis

Spatial correspondence between DF activity and ablation targets was quantified using continuous proximity scores derived from the Euclidean distance between DF points and ablation sites, transformed with a Gaussian kernel (standard deviation 5 mm, approximating clinical lesion size) [7, 8, 9]. Ablation sites were used as a procedural reference reflecting operator‐defined targeting rather than definitive functional VT circuit components. Two primary metrics were computed:

Soft recall: mean proximity score at ablation sites, reflecting the proportion of lesions located near DF activity.

Soft precision: mean proximity score at DF points, reflecting the proportion of DF activity near ablation sites.

To assess discriminative performance, proximity scores were used to classify mesh vertices as ablation or non‐ablation points, generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision‐recall (PR) curves. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and average precision (AP) were calculated.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in Python (v3.10) using SciPy and scikit‐learn. Proximity scores at ablation and non‐ablation sites were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. Each case was treated as an independent comparison, with cases including both SmartTouch and Pentaray recordings contributing two comparisons (18 total; p < 0.0028). Overall classification performance was assessed using ROC analysis, with PR analysis used to account for class imbalance.

Results

The cohort included 12 patients (mean age 57.5 ± 18.0 years, 75% male), evenly split between ischemic and non‐ischemic cardiomyopathy. Most patients had an ICD in situ (91.7%) and were receiving beta‐blockers (83.3%), with 58.3% on antiarrhythmic therapy. Baseline clinical and procedural characteristics are summarized in Table S1.

Spatial Correspondence and Discriminative Performance

(I) Soft Score

DF activity showed spatial concordance with operator‐defined ablation targets. Soft recall values were high in nearly all cases (0.776–0.999), indicating close proximity of DF regions to ablation sites. Soft precision values varied more widely (0.025–0.754). The highest soft recall occurred in Case 9 (0.999) and the highest soft precision in Case 11 (0.754). SmartTouch datasets generally achieved higher mean soft recall (~0.89) than Pentaray datasets (~0.86). In all but one case (Case 9, Pentaray), proximity scores at ablation sites were significantly higher than at non‐ablation sites after Bonferroni correction (p = 0.0028; Table 3, Figure 4).

(II) Discriminative Performance

ROC AUC values ranged from 0.622 (Case 8, SmartTouch) to 0.953 (Case 10, Pentaray), reflecting moderate to strong discrimination between ablation and non‐ablation points. Average precision values were low in absolute terms (0.003–0.091) due to class imbalance but exceeded baseline precision in all cases.

(III) Catheter Comparison

SmartTouch recordings generally produced slightly higher soft recall and greater separation between ablation and non‐ablation scores. For example, in Case 1 (SmartTouch), the median proximity score at ablation sites was 0.936 (IQR 0.196) compared with 0.004 (IQR 0.193) at non‐ablation sites. Case 8 (SmartTouch) showed the least separation and the lowest AUC (0.622).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that dominant frequency mapping with 3D anatomical integration is feasible in patients undergoing substrate‐guided VT ablation, and that DF activity shows consistent spatial alignment with operator‐defined ablation targets. Across both catheter recordings, high soft recall values were observed in nearly all cases, indicating that most lesions were delivered within close spatial proximity to DF‐positive regions. Moderate‐to‐strong discriminative performance and reproducibility across catheter types suggest technical feasibility when analyzed using case‐specific spectral scaling.

Relation to the Current Functional Mapping Landscape

The field of VT ablation has moved steadily from purely anatomical approaches toward functional mapping strategies that aim to identify the electrically active components of the substrate [10, 11]. DEEP and EDP mapping apply programmed stimulation to expose conduction slowing, which can reveal diastolic pathways critical to VT maintenance [4, 12, 13]. Early clinical reports suggest that targeting these sites can improve procedural specificity. However, DEEP and EDP mapping require additional pacing protocols, can prolong procedure time, and are dependent on patient haemodynamic stability. They may not be feasible in patients with limited pacing tolerance or in those in whom arrhythmia induction is avoided.

DF mapping offers a complementary, stimulus‐independent approach. By extracting spectral features from routine sinus rhythm or paced data, DF analysis can be performed without altering the activation sequence or provoking arrhythmia. This makes it particularly attractive for use in haemodynamically unstable patients or when procedural strategy emphasizes substrate modification without VT induction. Whereas DEEP and EDP identify inducible conduction delay during programmed stimulation, DF captures spectral complexity related to tissue heterogeneity and thus probes a different physiological property. Other stimulus‐independent approaches, such as ILAM, rely on activation‐ and amplitude‐based mapping. Recent work combining coherent mapping with ILAM‐derived deceleration zones has demonstrated spatial convergence of multiple functional substrate markers, supporting the concept of multiparametric functional substrate assessment [14].

In support of this multiparametric concept, Yalin et al. evaluated coherent mapping‐derived slow conduction zones in 32 patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy undergoing VT ablation, comparing SCZs to traditional substrate markers and ILAM‐derived deceleration zones [14]. SCZs were consistently identified adjacent to low‐voltage areas and colocalized with ILAM DZs in 56.3% of cases, with additional overlap with LPs/LAVAs in selected patients. These findings suggest partial spatial convergence across functional markers while also indicating that each approach may capture distinct substrate properties, supporting a multiparametric strategy.

Within the broader stimulus‐independent space, frequency‐domain mapping has also been implemented in commercial mapping platforms. In EnSite X, OT Near Field derives a peak‐frequency trace using a wavelet‐based approach that tracks high‐frequency components over time to support near‐field annotation [15]. In scar‐related VT mapping, Tonko et al. reported that near‐field detection localized critical circuit segments in 77.7% of cases relative to manually reviewed VT activation maps, but also observed that peak frequency as a stand‐alone substrate feature did not differentiate VT isthmus sites from low‐voltage bystander sites in their cohort [16]. In a multicentre VT ablation cohort, Mayer et al. evaluated substrate‐based PF mapping against activation‐demarcated isthmus zones and highlighted that wavelet‐derived PF can emphasize low‐amplitude, highly fractionated signals (distinct from Fourier‐derived dominant frequency, which reflects the highest‐energy component), and that mapping context, including rhythm and amiodarone exposure, may influence PF‐based performance [8].

Our approach is conceptually aligned with frequency‐domain mapping, but differs in implementation and output. We compute an FFT‐based DF metric from segmented electrograms and integrate DF‐defined regions as 3D projected substrate volumes within the CARTO environment. Rather than proposing catheter‐invariant absolute thresholds, interpretation emphasizes within‐case spatial patterning using a transparent case‐specific thresholding framework, and we add quantitative spatial concordance metrics to relate DF‐defined regions to procedural targeting. Direct head‐to‐head comparison with platform‐specific PF implementations (and their proprietary near‐field detection pipelines) was outside the scope of the present dataset and remains an important direction for future work.

Mechanistic Considerations

The mechanistic link between high DF activity and arrhythmogenic substrate likely reflects a combination of factors. Regions of slow conduction or structural discontinuity may generate complex, fractionated electrograms with increased high‐frequency components [17, 18, 19]. Prior experimental work has shown that high PF values can be associated with midmyocardial fibrosis and deep intramural substrate, which are often difficult to detect using bipolar voltage mapping alone [16, 20, 21]. In practice, sites with high PF often correspond to electrograms that appear visually fractionated or contain rapid deflections, whereas sites with low PF show more organized signals (Figure 5). This difference can often be appreciated directly in the raw intracardiac recordings. DF analysis may therefore help to reveal spatial patterns within or adjacent to scar that are not apparent on conventional voltage maps.

Representative high‐ versus low‐PF electrograms with corresponding spectra. Example of a high‐PF fractionated electrogram (blue, PF = 42.3 Hz) and a low‐PF organized electrogram (red, PF = 23.9 Hz). Fractionated signals display multiple rapid deflections and higher frequency components, whereas organized signals show smoother morphology with lower PF. These distinctions illustrate a potential mechanistic link between spectral content and arrhythmogenic substrate.

The present results support this hypothesis. Soft recall was consistently high, suggesting that DF‐positive regions capture much of the region targeted for ablation, even when voltage or late potential mapping might be less specific. Variation in soft precision likely reflects the broader spatial distribution of DF activity compared with the relatively focal nature of ablation lesions. This could indicate that some DF‐positive areas represent non‐targeted substrate, either because of procedural strategy, anatomical inaccessibility, or substrate located in deeper myocardial layers.

Clinical Implications

This study introduces a DF mapping workflow that integrates case‐specific spectral thresholding with three‐dimensional anatomy and continuous spatial concordance analysis. After manual region‐of‐interest definition and threshold selection, the analysis is performed using a computational pipeline and is applicable across both single‐tip and multielectrode mapping catheters, with spatial correspondence quantified using continuous proximity metrics rather than binary overlap.

This approach enables quantitative evaluation of the spatial relationship between DF‐defined regions and procedural ablation targets using routine mapping data without additional pacing protocols or arrhythmia induction. This may be of interest in contexts where patients are haemodynamically unstable, have diffuse scar, or when procedural strategy emphasizes substrate modification without VT induction. Importantly, DF mapping is not intended as a standalone tool to prospectively define ablation targets, and the observed low soft precision may reflect limited specificity of DF mapping itself, in addition to procedural strategy or deeper myocardial substrate.

Because DF mapping captures spectral characteristics rather than pacing‐induced conduction delay, it probes a different physiological property than functional mapping strategies such as DEEP or EDP. It may therefore serve as a complementary component within multimodal mapping workflows that combine spectral, temporal, and structural information. Prospective studies will be required to distinguish between intrinsic limitations in DF specificity, procedural strategy, and deeper myocardial substrate as contributors to low soft precision, and to determine how DF‐defined spatial patterns relate to clinical endpoints and decision‐making.

Limitations

This study was retrospective, single‐centered, and included a limited number of patients. DF mapping should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. DF thresholds were selected manually from case‐specific frequency histograms by a single analyst in a retrospective manner, which may introduce operator dependency and potential bias. Inter‐operator reproducibility and the robustness of spatial concordance metrics to small variations in threshold selection were not formally assessed. Thresholding was performed in a relative, patient‐specific, and catheter‐dependent manner to account for differences in spectral content. DF mapping is therefore intended as a within‐patient functional marker and is not suitable for absolute or inter‐patient comparison, as cross‐device standardization was not addressed. Spatial concordance analysis relied on operator‐defined ablation sites, which do not represent a functional ground truth for VT circuit and may not capture the full extent of the arrhythmogenic substrate. Procedural data did not consistently distinguish ablation lesions delivered for substrate modification from those targeting critical sites, precluding stratified analysis of precision and recall by ablation type. Because electroanatomic mapping data are spatially autocorrelated due to point proximity and interpolation, ROC‐ and precision‐recall‐based metrics should therefore be interpreted as measures of spatial discrimination rather than diagnostic accuracy and may overestimate apparent performance.

Signal‐processing limitations should also be considered. Fractionated electrograms do not always yield high peak frequencies, and PF estimation may be influenced by region‐of‐interest selection, particularly when using short, non‐periodic signals. The absence of spectral windowing may have increased spectral leakage and inflated high‐frequency components. Moreover, defining dominant frequency as a single peak simplifies complex, multi‐component electrograms and may not fully capture spectral heterogeneity, especially when multiple spectral peaks are present. Comparisons with DEEP or EDP mapping were not performed, and long‐term follow‐up data were unavailable, precluding assessment of the prognostic value of DF mapping. Prospective multicentre studies with predefined thresholding strategies are therefore warranted.

Conclusion

Dominant frequency mapping with three‐dimensional anatomical integration was feasible across different catheter types and showed consistent spatial concordance with operator‐defined ventricular ablation sites. This was achieved using a workflow that combined electrogram segmentation, case‐specific spectral thresholding, and mesh‐based projection. The approach is stimulus‐independent, can be applied to routine mapping data, and can be explored in relation to established pacing‐based mapping strategies. DF values should be interpreted as case‐specific and are not directly comparable across patients, catheter types, or institutions. Prospective multicentre studies are warranted to determine its potential clinical relevance in relation to functional substrate and clinical outcomes.
