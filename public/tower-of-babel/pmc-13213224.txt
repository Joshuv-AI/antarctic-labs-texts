A novel surname-based methodology to improve the disaggregation of Asian American ethnic subgroups in patients with ovarian cancer
Authors: Sarah S. Lee, Heather T. Gold, Lan N. Ðoàn, Simona C. Kwon, Bhavana Pothuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Surname method disaggregated more patients into ethnic subgroups than self-reporting.•Using surname methodology and self-reporting, 91% of patients were disaggregated.•Surname analysis can decrease the number of patients with Other or Unknown race.

Introduction

Disaggregated race and ethnicity data collection and reporting have unmasked disparities and elicited unique insights for Asian American ethnic subgroups in gynecologic oncology. While uterine cancer incidence has increased three times more among Asian Americans than White populations between 2001 and 2017, when examined by ethnic subgroups, Filipino American patients were more likely to present with advanced stages and higher-risk histologies for uterine cancer than other Asian ethnic groups and White patients (Johnson, 2024). Korean women <50 years old had a higher risk of ovarian cancer compared with non-Hispanic White women, while Chinese and Filipina women <50 years old had a greater risk of uterine cancer death when compared to other Asian ethnic groups and non-Hispanic White patients (Johnson, 2024, Karia, 2022). In cervical cancer, Southeast Asian patients were most likely to present with metastatic disease and least likely to receive guideline-concordant care compared with other Asian ethnic groups (Ho, 2025, Lee, 2024). These studies underscore the importance of robust racial and ethnic subgroup data collection to better understand the cancer burden and identify subpopulations at greatest risk. Important to note is the persistent cancer disparities experienced by Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander communities, such as later diagnosis and poorer outcomes, which are obscured by the categorization of Asian Americans and Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islanders together as one monolithic group (Ho, 2025, Lee, 2024, Lee et al., 2024).

Yet, despite calls and efforts to improve the transparency of demographic data in publications, detailed race and ethnic subgroups are not routinely collected or reported (Pothuri, 2023). In oncology clinical trials over the last three decades, only 63% of studies reported race, 29% reported ethnicity, and less than 10% included the major racial or ethnic groups (e.g., White, Asian, Black, Hispanic), with suboptimal improvement over time (Lee, 2024, Loree, 2019). Granular race and ethnicity data collection and reporting can also improve data quality by reducing the number of people categorized as Other or having missing or unknown race or ethnicity data (Webber and Chaparro, 2022). Accurate race and ethnicity data are essential for identifying health disparities and improving data visibility of underrepresented groups.

Thus, there is a critical need for improved reporting of demographic data and for novel methodologies to disaggregate race and ethnicity. This study aimed to retrospectively enhance the racial and ethnic subgroup data for Asian patients using a surname-based methodology. Specifically, our goals were to improve ethnic subgroup disaggregation and reduce the number of Asian American patients classified as Other or Unknown race.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional study of patients with ovarian cancer who received care at a network of academic medical centers between 2011 and 2023. Patients received care at three practice settings within one hospital network: a tertiary academic medical center and two community hospitals. The data were obtained from the electronic health record and the institutional tumor registry. Patients were identified by having diagnoses according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision: ovarian cancer, C56, C56.1, C56.2, C56.3, C56.9; fallopian tube cancer, C57.01, C57.02; and primary peritoneal cancer, C48, C48.01, C48.1, C48.2. In accordance with the journal’s guidelines, we will provide our data for independent analysis by a selected team by the Editorial Team for the purposes of additional data analysis or for the reproducibility of this study in other centers if such is requested.

Self-reported race and ethnic subgroup

We obtained patients’ self-reported race and ethnicity data from electronic health records. In our health system, patients self-report demographic details, including race and ethnicity, when establishing care. Patients can report a racial and ethnic category (e.g., Asian, Black, Hispanic, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, White) and/or specify an ethnic subgroup (e.g., Chinese, Filipino, Indian, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese). Patients can answer as “Other” for any race/ethnicity questions or decline to provide an answer. Patients who reported multiple racial groups were categorized as Multiracial. Patients who selected Asian and one or more racial groups were categorized as Multiracial Asian. The disaggregation analysis focused on those who identified as Asian American, Multiracial Asian, and Other or unknown race.

Surname methodology

A validated surname-based methodology by Lauderdale et al. was used to disaggregate Asian Americans into the six largest Asian ethnic subgroups in the US: Chinese, Filipino, Indian, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese (Lauderdale, 2000, Wong et al., 2010). This approach has been applied in health services and population-based research to infer Asian ethnic subgroups in datasets lacking granular self-reported ethnicity. The surname lists were originally derived from Social Security Administration files of approximately 1.8 million individuals born outside the United States; the country of origin reported in the files was used to approximate ethnic subgroups. Each surname was assigned to an ethnic subgroup based on the strength of its association with the country of origin. Names with low frequency (fewer than five occurrences) or those shared across multiple racial and/or ethnic groups were excluded to improve specificity, as these names were not predictive of a specific ethnic subgroup. This surname list contains over 27,000 surnames and the predicted Asian American ethnic subgroups. According to the 1990 census data, 86% of Asian Americans were born outside the United States, increasing its validity in this population. In our study, patient surnames were programmatically matched to this validated surname list using lookup procedures. For each individual, ethnic subgroup classification was assigned based on surname match. When available, reported race was incorporated to refine classification and improve the predicted probability of the Asian ethnic subgroups.

Data analysis

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients who were disaggregated into an Asian ethnic subgroup using either self-reporting or surname identification. When discrepancies arose between the methodologies, self-reported race or ethnicity was considered the reference. For patients who selected Other or if race or ethnicity was unknown, the surname list was used to minimize the number of patients categorized as Other or Unknown race.

We present descriptive statistics for the overall sample and by racial and ethnic group (i.e., Asian, Hispanic, White) and Asian ethnic subgroup. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous nonparametric variables were conducted. A Spearman correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate the agreement between the surname methodology and the self-reported ethnic subgroups. All statistical analyses were conducted with STATA version 17 (StataCorp, 2021). The NYU Grossman School of Medicine Institutional Review Board approved this study (i22-01090) and a waiver of informed consent was granted in accordance with 45 CFR 46.116.

Results

Overall, 813 patients with ovarian cancer were included. Based on self-reported race and ethnicity in the electronic health record, the cohort consisted of 81 (10%) Asian, 96 (12%) Hispanic, seven (1%) Multiracial, one (<1%) non-Hispanic Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander, 61 (8%) non-Hispanic Black, and 520 (64%) non-Hispanic White patients. Additionally, 47 (6%) were categorized as Other or had unknown race/ethnicity. There were three Multiracial Asian patients: two non-Hispanic White and Asian, and one Hispanic and Asian. The remaining four non-Asian Multiracial patients were diverse and included one non-Hispanic White and American Indian/Alaska Native, one non-Hispanic White and non-Hispanic Black, one non-Hispanic Black and American Indian/Alaska Native, and one Hispanic and Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander.

Among the 47 patients who self-reported their race or ethnicity as Other or Unknown, surname analysis reclassified five (11%) patients from Other or Unknown to Asian race and a specific Asian ethnic subgroup. This was consistent with the proportion of Asian Americans in the overall cohort (89 of 813, 11%), suggesting that the surname analysis was likely able to identify the majority of Asian Americans who were initially classified as Other or Unknown, as it reflected a similar percentage in the overall cohort.

In total, 89 Asian American patients were identified (self-reported Asian American, n = 81; self-reported Multiracial Asian, n = 3; reclassified by surname from Other or Unknown race, n = 5). The surname methodology disaggregated more Asian American patients (74%, n = 66) than self-reported electronic health record (61%, n = 55). Applying the surname methodology in combination with the self-reported data disaggregated an additional 22% (20 of 89) of Asian American patients. Ultimately, using both methods, 91% (81 of 89) of Asian American patients were able to be disaggregated into an ethnic subgroup (Fig. 1). Finally, the concordance between electronic health record data and surname-based disaggregation was high. Among the 37 Asian American patients with identified ethnic subgroups using both electronic health records and surname methodology, 35 (95%) were classified into subgroups consistently across both methodologies, with a Spearman’s Rho coefficient of 0.84, indicating strong agreement.

Discussion

Summary of main results

This study leveraged a novel application of the surname methodology to improve the accuracy of racial and disaggregated ethnic subgroup demographic reporting among Asian American patients in an 813-patient cohort with ovarian cancer. This methodology reduced the number of patients of Other or Unknown race; while 6% were initially categorized as such based on self-reported data, surname analysis reclassified 11% as Asian American, consistent with the overall proportion of Asian American patients in the cohort. Individually, the surname methodology disaggregated 74% and self-reporting disaggregated 61% of Asian ethnic subgroups. Combining both methodologies enabled disaggregation of ethnic subgroups for 91% of Asian American patients, underscoring the value of integrating self-reported data with this surname-based approach to enhance the granularity of race and ethnicity reporting.

Results in the context of published literature

In single-institution or smaller multi-institution studies, the collection and reporting of disaggregated racial and ethnic data may be challenging and inconsistent. This may be due to concerns about patient confidentiality and privacy, lack of infrastructure to collect detailed demographic data, or limited statistical power to detect meaningful effect size differences by racial or ethnic groups (Kader, 2022). However, collecting, analyzing, and reporting disaggregated race and ethnicity data can identify at-risk groups and advance data transparency and health equity to improve patient outcomes for the allocation of resources (Ponce et al., 2023, Lee, 2024). To improve this, we offer a novel methodology using surnames that can be applied retrospectively to disaggregate Asian American race data into specific ethnic subgroups.

Implications for practice and future research

Data disaggregation also allows for the identification of Multiracial individuals, who often have been grouped as Other, categorized as a larger Multiracial group (e.g., Afro-Latino individual combined with Asian-White individual in the same group), combined into the larger racial group (e.g., classified as White for Asian-White individuals), or excluded from analyses (Yi, 2024). The 2020 Census found that the Multiracial population increased by 276% in 10 years, from 9 million to 34 million, the largest increase in a racial or ethnic subgroup (Jones, 2021). In this study, only seven (1%) patients identified as Multiracial, and three of these patients self-identified as Asian and another racial or ethnic group and therefore were disaggregated into specific Asian American ethnic subgroups. Though small, these patients were diverse; caution should be applied in grouping these patients together for analysis. Data collection from Multiracial individuals can be challenging, as the categorical identity can be fluid based on social context and developmental stage of life, and vary from the perceptions of others (Ponce et al., 2023). Yet, invalidating the racial identity of Multiracial individuals can be detrimental to mental health (Franco et al., 2021). Prior studies suggest that selection of “Other,” “Unknown,” or declined responses for race and ethnicity may reflect limitations in standard response categories, preference for more specific ancestry-based identification, confusion between race and ethnicity constructs, and inconsistencies in demographic data collection practices (Magaña López, 2017, Kaneshiro, 2011, Lu, 2022, Polubriaginof, 2019, Nguyen et al., 2022). Efforts to improve data collection and reporting can be improved by including the rationale for race and ethnicity data collection, allowing for “select all that apply,” and “Other” with “specify” options for text, having data collection available in multiple languages, and engaging the local community for data collection design and interpretation (Sharghi, 2024, Kader, 2025).

In 2024, the United States Census Bureau’s Office of Management and Budget updated the requirements for race and ethnicity data collection to include the following groups: American Indian and Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic or Latino, Middle Eastern or North African, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, White, or of multiple groups (Budget, 2024). Efforts should be made to allow for these self-reporting options in data collection by health systems. At the very least, guidelines from JAMA should be considered when reporting demographic data on race and ethnicity, which include an explanation of data collection methodologies for race and ethnicity (e.g., self-reported, investigator-observed), reporting of specific racial and ethnic categories over collective categories; a clear definition of the Other category; and listing of groups in alphabetic order (Flanagin, 2021). The equity rubric proposed by Obstetrics and Gynecology echoes many of these concepts in demographics reporting, acknowledging racism, not race, as the exposure associated with health outcomes, and not just conflating the association of genetics with race (Batman, 2023). These efforts to improve demographic reporting can highlight disparities and enhance the transparency of study recruitment and enrollment. These opportunities will require consistent data collection, education at multiple levels (e.g., patient-level regarding rationale for data collection and reporting, clinicians and data analysts regarding the importance of collecting and analyzing disaggregated data), and data infrastructure changes (Health, 2023, Health, 2023).

Strengths and weaknesses

The strength of this study is the heterogeneous study population, of which a third of the patients were non-White. The study population also included three diverse practice settings within one hospital network, which increases its generalizability. The surname methodology is a novel, simple, and feasible way to improve disaggregated race and ethnicity reporting in oncology literature for smaller datasets. Though other methodologies for disaggregation exist, including expert review, machine learning, and data linkage, the surname list methodology is feasible with minimal technical expertise and can be broadened to large data sets (Chin, 2023). While collecting disaggregated self-reported data for race and ethnicity is the gold standard, the surname methodology offers a reasonable alternative when self-reported data are unavailable or incomplete. A limitation of our study is the diversity of data collection in our electronic health record due to the heterogeneity of practice settings, where race and ethnicity are intended to be self-reported, but the collection methodology cannot be confirmed. In addition, as the surname methodology was only applied to those self-identifying as Asian American or those with Other or Unknown race, the proportion of Multiracial patients may be underreported or misclassified.

The surname methodology has its limitations. The methodology was developed and validated over 20 years ago and does not reflect potential newer trends in surnames. Only six of the most common Asian American groups are represented in this dataset, and the surnames are not comprehensive, as names with five individuals or fewer were excluded from the database and those representing multiple racial or ethnic groups were excluded from the database. Surname disaggregation worked best for those of Chinese descent. The practice of surname designations varies across cultures; surnames can be assigned from paternal or maternal lineages at birth and can be changed with marriage or adoption. Changing surnames upon marriage varies widely across Asian cultures; this practice is uncommon in China and Korea, yet common in Japan. The effect of these practices on those who have immigrated to the United States is unknown. Moreover, the transliteration of surnames from an Asian language to English is not uniform. For instance, the last name “이” (pronounced “ee”) in Korean can be anglicized as Lee, Li, Rhee, Yi, and Yee; these varying romanizations of a singular last name are not captured in the dataset. Patients identifying as multiple Asian ethnicities were not fully captured by this methodology. Finally, the focus of this manuscript was on the disaggregation of Asian Americans only; further work is ongoing to broaden this effort to other racial groups (Nasseri, 2007).

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that integrating self-reported race/ethnicity with surname-based methodologies can substantially improve the predicted accuracy and granularity of demographic reporting. By reducing the proportion of patients classified as Other or Unknown and achieving subgroup disaggregation for 91% of Asian Americans, this method can provide a more nuanced understanding of racial and ethnic diversity within patient populations. The strong concordance between surname-based disaggregation and self-reported electronic health record data highlights the reliability of this approach. Incorporating this novel surname methodology into routine clinical data is feasible, can enhance the precision of demographic reporting, and can enable targeted research efforts to address disparities in outcomes of subgroups across diverse populations.
