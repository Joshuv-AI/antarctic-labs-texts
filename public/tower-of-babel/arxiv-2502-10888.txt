Tensor parametric Hamiltonian operator inference
Arjun Vijaywargiya, Shane A. McQuarrie, Anthony Gruber

Abstract
This work presents a tensorial approach to constructing data-driven reduced-order models corresponding to semi-discrete partial differential equations with canonical Hamiltonian structure. By expressing parameter-varying operators with affine dependence as contractions of a generalized parameter vector against a constant tensor, this method leverages the operator inference framework to capture parametric dependence in the learned reduced-order model via the solution to a convex, least-squares optimization problem. This leads to a concise and straightforward implementation which compactifies previous parametric operator inference approaches and directly extends to learning parametric operators with symmetry constraints—a key feature required for constructing structure-preserving surrogates of Hamiltonian systems. The proposed approach is demonstrated on both a (non-Hamiltonian) heat equation with variable diffusion coefficient as well as a Hamiltonian wave equation with variable wave speed.



Keywords
: parametric model reduction, tensor calculus, Hamiltonian systems, structure preservation, scientific machine learning

Introduction

Mathematical models based on partial differential equations (PDEs) are often formed through the combination of terms describing locally distinct physical processes. For example, an advection-diffusion equation describing the evolution of a scalar quantity
q
on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given by
\partial_{t}q=\nabla\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor D}\nabla q-\mathord{\btensor v}q}}\right)
for appropriate diffusion field
\mathord{\btensor D}
and velocity
\mathord{\btensor v}
, combines the term
\nabla\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor D}\nabla q}}\right)
expressing diffusion with a corresponding term
\mathord{\btensor v}\cdot\nabla q
describing material transport.
The practical utility of PDE-based models typically relies on the calibration of a number of parameters (
\mathord{\btensor D},\mathord{\btensor v}
in the example above), which are critical to the behavior of solutions, hence also to the physical realism of the relevant model. Moreover, these parameters are manifestly application dependent, meaning that any sufficiently general technique for constructing data-driven surrogate models for physical systems must be amenable to parametric variability.
This paper develops a data-driven method of constructing reduced-order models (ROMs)—low-dimensional dynamical systems for approximating the dominant dynamics of a large-scale physical model—which seamlessly encodes parametric dependencies while simultaneously preserving other structural features of interest.

The development of parametric ROMs is straightforward and well understood in the case of intrusive, projection-based methods
[
3
,
4
,
19
,
38
,
50
,
51
]
.
Given a numerical simulator—termed the full-order model (FOM) due to it usually having a large number of degrees of freedom—for a physical process, intrusive methods define a ROM through Galerkin projection onto a low-dimensional manifold which, ideally, captures the dominant state behavior the system.
In this setting, any parametric dependence appearing in the FOM is automatically inherited by the resulting ROM.
However, intrusive methods require direct manipulation of the FOM equations as well as access to the underlying operators in the FOM source code. This may not always be possible, for instance when using proprietary or complex legacy codes whose implementations are inaccessible or not amenable to modifications. Even when codes are available, the direct manipulations required by intrusive model reduction can be highly non-trivial and error prone.

In response to these challenges, many non-intrusive model reduction strategies have recently been developed and successfully applied to a variety of large-scale systems. In contrast to generating ROM equations through the direct projection of FOM equations, non-intrusive model reduction techniques typically rely on the use of a pre-specified model form, along with snapshot data of FOM solutions, to infer a ROM which can be simulated without access to FOM code.
Several approaches, including as dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
[
41
,
42
]
and operator inference (OpInf)
[
17
,
30
,
35
]
, define a convex linear least-squares regression to infer ROM components. In their standard (non-parametric) forms, these approaches tend to “average out” the effects of parameter dependence. To better accommodate parametric problems, extensions have been developed to DMD
[
9
,
26
,
39
]
and OpInf
[
33
,
54
]
that build known parametric structure into the pre-specified model form.
Other strategies use more general regression techniques, such as Gaussian process regression
[
23
,
24
]
or deep learning
[
7
,
11
,
12
]
.

An additional challenge in model reduction generally—and one that can be especially detrimental to data-driven methods in particular if left unaddressed—is to ensure that a ROM possesses certain desired properties of the dynamics being approximated. For instance, this paper focuses on (canonical) Hamiltonian systems, which feature a symplectic structure and conserve the total energy of solutions
[
31
]
. Several strategies exist to guarantee such properties in surrogates learned from data (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
20
,
28
]
); in particular, the OpInf framework has enjoyed several recent extensions specifically catered to gradient flows and Hamiltonian systems
[
15
,
16
,
21
,
44
,
46
,
47
]
. The OpInf formulations that impose structural constraints also tends to negate the need for regularization techniques to stabilize the dynamics of the resulting ROMs
[
32
,
33
,
37
]
.
However, enforcing structure also tends to increase the complexity of the associated inference procedure mathematically, computationally, and in terms of the difficulty of implementation.
Each of these issues are further compounded by the presence of parametric variations.

The purpose of the present work is to develop a simple and general way to incorporate parametric dependence into the OpInf framework and, simultaneously, the preservation of structural properties in the resulting ROM.
The following simple observation is key: when a matrix operator
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu):\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
depends linearly on a known function
\bt@\mu^{\prime}=\bt@\theta(\bt@\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
of the parameter vector
\bt@\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, then
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)=\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}
can be written as the contraction of a
constant
tensor
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times p^{\prime}}
against the transformed parameters
\bt@\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
. Said differently, the adjoint relationship
A\mapsto(B\mapsto C)\cong(A\otimes B)\mapsto C
between continuous linear mappings
\mapsto
of vector spaces and the tensor product
\otimes
yields a description of the “affine” parametric dependence in
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
in terms of the linear action of a constant tensor
\mathbf{T}
on
\bt@\mu^{\prime}
. This notion is general enough to encompass a wide range of parametric behavior and will be shown to enable an OpInf learning problem which does not rely on local approximation techniques such as Taylor expansion
[
10
]
or linear interpolation
[
35
]
.
This approach builds on previous OpInf methods for affine parametric system
[
33
,
55
]
while also enabling a novel extension to parametric Hamiltonian systems, in which the preservation of certain structure information is critical to stability and predictive accuracy. The extension is natural given the mathematical concision and streamlined implementation brought about by the tensorized approach.
To summarize, the contributions of this article are twofold: first, a tensor-based approach to parametric OpInf which is convex, mathematically concise, simple to implement, and recovers previous work as a special case; and second, an extension of parametric OpInf methodology to systems with Hamiltonian structure, leading to non-intrusive parametric ROMs which guarantee symplecticity and the production of conservative Hamiltonian dynamics.

Tensor parametric operator inference

This section focuses on systems of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with affine parametric dependence but without symmetry or conservation properties.
Section

2.1

Section

2.2
, a strategy is presented and analyzed for learning reduced tensorized parametric dynamics from state data.
Section

2.3

Section

.

2.1
Affine parametric linear systems

Consider a linear parametric system of ODEs in which the dynamics depends affinely (i.e., generalized linearly) on a vector
\bt@\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
of parameters:

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor M}\dot{\mathord{\btensor q}}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor q},

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor q}(0,\bt@\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor q}_{0}(\bt@\mu),

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{p^{\prime}}\theta_{x}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor A}_{x},

(2.1)

where
\mathord{\btensor q}=\mathord{\btensor q}(t,\bt@\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the ODE state vector,
\mathord{\btensor q}_{0}(\bt@\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a given initial condition,
\mathord{\btensor M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is symmetric positive definite (e.g., a mass matrix in a finite element method), and
\mathord{\btensor A}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a matrix-valued function defined by the (possibly nonlinear) scalar-valued functions
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p^{\prime}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
and constant matrices
\mathord{\btensor A}_{1},\ldots,\mathord{\btensor A}_{p^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. Such systems frequently arise from a method of lines semi-discretization of time-dependent PDEs (see, e.g.,
[
53
]
).
As the discrete dimension
N
is typically very large,
eq.

2.1
is called the
full-order model
(FOM), which serves as the best available discretization of the dynamics under consideration.

When the matrices
\mathord{\btensor A}_{1},\ldots,\mathord{\btensor A}_{p^{\prime}}
can be accessed intrusively, it is straightforward to construct a computationally efficient
reduced-order model
(ROM) for
eq.

2.1
through projection. This paper considers projection onto
\mathord{\btensor M}
-orthonormal linear reduced bases
\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}(r\ll N)
, though nonlinear approximations such as in
[
2
,
13
,
14
,
27
,
43
]
may also be considered with minor alterations to the approach.
More precisely, given a trial space basis
\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
satisfying
\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor U}=\mathord{\btensor I}
and a low-rank approximation
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor q}}\coloneqq\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}\approx\mathord{\btensor q}
defined in terms of an unknown coefficient vector
\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}(t,\bt@\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, Galerkin projection of the FOM
eq.

2.1
onto the column space of
\mathord{\btensor U}
gives the following reduced ODE system
[
3
]
:

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}=\sum_{x=1}^{p^{\prime}}\theta_{x}(\bt@\mu)\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{x}\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}},\qquad\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}(0,\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor q}_{0}(\bt@\mu),

(2.2)

where
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{x}\coloneqq\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor A}_{x}\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
for each
x=1,\ldots,p^{\prime}
.
The system
eq.

2.2
is a low-dimensional approximation to the FOM
eq.

2.1
which, importantly, exhibits the same affine-parametric structure as the FOM. Provided the trial space basis
\mathord{\btensor U}
is well chosen (e.g.,
L^{2}
-optimal over some data range), the ROM
eq.

2.2
can accurately recover the dominant behavior of the original system at a significantly reduced computational cost, since the matrices
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{p^{\prime}}
can be pre-computed once and reused for multiple values of the parameter
\bt@\mu
. However, computing each
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{x}
involves direct matrix-matrix multiplications with the full-order object
\mathord{\btensor A}_{x}
, an intrusive procedure that requires access to the code implementing the FOM
eq.

2.1
.
The goal of parametric OpInf is to non-intrusively construct a ROM similar to
eq.

2.2
by inferring low-dimensional operators from observations of the state
\mathord{\btensor q}
.

Remark 2.1

(Basis orthonormality)
.

The reduced-order matrix
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}(\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor U}
is symmetric (or skew symmetric) whenever the full-order matrix
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
is.
If a basis
\mathord{\btensor U}
is chosen which is not
\mathord{\btensor M}
-orthonormal, Galerkin projection results in the slightly different ROM

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}}=(\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor U})^{-1}\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}},

which does not inherit (anti-)symmetry from the FOM.
This paper focuses on
\mathord{\btensor M}
-orthonormal bases because the goal of
Section

3
is to construct ROMs which preserve system symmetry without direct access to
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
.

Before proceeding, it is convenient to reformulate
eq.

2.1
and
eq.

2.2
in a way which exposes their tensorial structure. Let
\bt@\mu^{\prime}=\bt@\theta(\bt@\mu)\coloneqq(\theta_{1}(\bt@\mu),\ldots,\theta_{p^{\prime}}(\bt@\mu))^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
denote the vector of scalar coefficients in the affine expansion of
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
.
Then the FOM
eq.

2.1
can be written in the compact form

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor M}\dot{\mathord{\btensor q}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor q},\qquad\mathord{\btensor q}(0,\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor q}_{0}(\bt@\mu),

(2.3)

where
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times p^{\prime}}
is the order-3 tensor satisfying
\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}=\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, obtained from the matrix-valued function
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
by tensor-Hom adjunction (i.e., adjointness).
In a similar fashion, the ROM
eq.

2.2
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}},\qquad\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}(0,\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor q}_{0}(\bt@\mu),

(2.4a)

where
\hat{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r\times p^{\prime}}
is a reduced order-
3
tensor with entries

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{T}}_{abx}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}U_{ia}\mathrm{T}_{ijx}U_{jb}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right)_{ai}(\mathord{\btensor A}_{x})_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor U}}}\right)_{jb}=(\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{x})_{ab},

(2.4b)

where
\mathrm{T}_{ijx}=(\mathord{\btensor A}_{x})_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j,x)
-th entry of
\mathbf{T}
,
U_{ia}
denotes the
(i,a)
-th entry of
\mathord{\btensor U}
, and
(\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{x})_{ab}
denotes the
(a,b)
-th entry of
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{x}
.
Note that
\hat{\mathbf{T}}
can be viewed as the projection of the full-order tensor
\mathbf{T}
or, equivalently, as the tensor-Hom adjoint of the projected matrix-valued function
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}(\bt@\mu)\coloneqq\sum_{x=1}^{p^{\prime}}\theta_{x}(\bt@\mu)\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{x}
. The advantage of the expression
eq.

2.4
lies primarily in its compactness: because the explicit tensor contraction
\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime}
exposes higher-level structure and removes the need for bookkeeping in terms of the matrices
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{1},...,\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{p^{\prime}}
, additional considerations such as operator symmetries can be more easily included in data-driven ROM construction. Besides leading to a streamlined implementation of the method in
[
33
,
55
]
, this approach also enables simplified proofs of established results (e.g.,
Corollary

2.1
) and facilitates an extension which preserves Hamiltonian structure information (c.f.
Section

3
).

2.2
Tensor parametric operator inference

The ROM
eq.

2.2
is fully and uniquely determined by the entries of the reduced-order matrices
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}_{p^{\prime}}
, which in turn depend on the full-order matrices
\mathord{\btensor A}_{1},\ldots,\mathord{\btensor A}_{p^{\prime}}
. When these matrices are not available for computation due to, e.g., proprietary or legacy software implementations, an alternative to direct projection is to choose the matrices that minimize the residual of
eq.

2.2
with respect to a collection of training states. This is the Operator Inference (OpInf) strategy introduced in
[
35
]
and extended to affine-parametric systems in
[
33
,
55
]
.
This section develops a corresponding OpInf procedure for the tensorized ROM
eq.

2.4
and presents some analysis of its basic properties.

Suppose access is granted to fixed-time snapshots of the full-order state—solutions to the FOM
eq.

2.1
—at
N_{s}
training parameter values
\bt@\mu_{1},\ldots,\bt@\mu_{N_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
N_{t}
time instances
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}
, organized into the state snapshot matrices

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathord{\btensor q}(t_{1},\bt@\mu_{s})&\cdots&\mathord{\btensor q}(t_{N_{t}},\bt@\mu_{s})\end{array}}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N_{t}},\quad s=1,\ldots,N_{s}.

Given an
\mathord{\btensor M}
-orthonormal basis matrix
\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
, define the reduced state snapshot matrices
\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}_{s}\coloneqq\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}}
,
s=1,\ldots,N_{s},
as well as corresponding reduced state time derivative matrices
\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}}_{s}=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}D_{t}(\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}}
, which can be estimated, e.g., via finite difference approximation.
Specifically, the
\alpha
-th column of
\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}}_{s}
is an estimate for
\frac{d}{dt}\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor q}(t,\bt@\mu_{s})|_{t=t_{\alpha}}
.
A reduced tensor
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
which approximates the intrusively constructed
\hat{\mathbf{T}}
in
eq.

2.4
can then be inferred by solving the convex minimization problem

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bar{\mathbf{T}}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}}_{s}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu_{s}^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}_{s}}}\right\|^{2},

(2.5)

where
\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|^{2}=\sum_{ij}F_{ij}F_{ij}
denotes the Frobenius matrix norm.

Conveniently, convexity implies that the inference of
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
in
eq.

2.5
is equivalent to a solving a particular linear system.
The precise statement of this result is simplified with the following notation: for an order-
n
tensor
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times...\times N_{n}}
, let
\mathrm{cvec}_{ij}\,\mathbf{B},\mathrm{rvec}_{ij}\,\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times...\times N_{i}N_{j}\times...\times N_{n}}
denote the partial vectorizations that decrement the tensor degree of
\mathbf{B}
by unrolling the ordered indices
i<j
“column-wise” (in the case of
\mathrm{cvec}_{ij}
) or “row-wise” (in the case of
\mathrm{rvec}_{ij}
) into a single index located at the
i
-th position. More precisely, indexing the
m
-th dimension with
k_{m}
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{ij}\,\mathbf{B}}}\right)_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},(k_{j}-1)N_{i}+k_{i},k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{j-1},k_{j+1},\ldots,k_{n}}=\mathrm{B}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{i},\ldots,k_{j},\ldots,k_{n}},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{rvec}_{ij}\,\mathbf{B}}}\right)_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},(k_{i}-1)N_{j}+k_{j},k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{j-1},k_{j+1},\ldots,k_{n}}=\mathrm{B}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{i},\ldots,k_{j},\ldots,k_{n}}.

(2.6b)

Additionally, let
\mathrm{cmat}_{ij}
and
\mathrm{rmat}_{ij}
be the natural inverses of
\mathrm{cvec}_{ij}
and
\mathrm{rvec}_{ij}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{cmat}_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{ij}\,\mathbf{B}}}\right)=\mathrm{rmat}_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{rvec}_{ij}\,\mathbf{B}}}\right)=\mathbf{B},

and let
\otimes
denote the outer product: for an order-
m
tensor
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{m}}
and an order-
n
tensor
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{n}}
,
\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{m}\times N_{1}\cdots\times N_{n}}
is a tensor of order
m+n
with entries

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B})_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m},k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}=\mathrm{A}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}}\mathrm{B}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}.

With this notation in place, the following result formulates the inference of
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
in
eq.

2.5
as a system of linear equations.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s},\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}}
and
\bt@\nu_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
for
s=1,\ldots,N_{s}
. The tensor
\bar{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r\times p^{\prime}}
minimizes the Lagrangian

\displaystyle L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right\|^{2}

(2.7)

if and only if
\bar{\mathord{\btensor T}}\coloneqq\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times rp^{\prime}}
satisfies the linear system

\displaystyle\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}\,\bar{\mathord{\btensor T}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor C}}^{\mathsf{T}},

(2.8)

where
\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{rp^{\prime}\times rp^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\mathord{\btensor C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times rp^{\prime}}
denote the vectorizations

\displaystyle\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{rvec}_{12}\mathrm{cvec}_{34}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\bt@\nu_{s}\otimes\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right),\quad\hat{\mathord{\btensor C}}\coloneqq\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right).

Proof.

The proof is a direct calculation of the first-order optimality conditions for the Lagrangian
eq.

2.7
, appealing to a few minor algebraic results detailed in
Appendix

A
.
Differentiating
L
with respect to
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
and applying the definition of the gradient, it follows that

\displaystyle dL\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d{\bar{\mathbf{T}}}\,\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s},\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle d\bar{\mathbf{T}},\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right]\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle d\bar{\mathbf{T}},\nabla L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)}}\right\rangle,

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\cdot,\cdot}}\right\rangle
denotes the Frobenius inner product and in which the standard properties listed in
Lemma

A.1
have been used. Hence,
the stationarity condition
\nabla L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)=\bt@0
implies the tensorial equation

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}.

(2.9)

From
Lemma

A.2
, the left-hand side can be expressed as the tensor contraction

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}=\bar{\mathbf{T}}:\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\bt@\nu_{s}\otimes\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s},

with the convention that
\mathbf{X}_{1}:\mathbf{X}_{2}
operates over the last two indices of
\mathbf{X}_{1}
and the first two indices of
\mathbf{X}_{2}
in reverse order, i.e.,
\mathbf{X}_{1}:\mathbf{X}_{2}=\sum_{i_{k-1},i_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}}}\right)_{...i_{k-1}i_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{X}_{2}}}\right)_{i_{k}i_{k-1}...}
. By
Lemma

A.3
, vectorizing the contraction column-wise in
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
and similarly vectorizing the last two indices of the result transforms the tensorial equation
eq.

2.9
into the linear system

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)\,\mathrm{rvec}_{12}\mathrm{cvec}_{34}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\bt@\nu_{s}\otimes\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)=\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right),

which, using the definitions in the statement, is the system
\bar{\mathord{\btensor T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor C}}
. Transposition and the symmetry relationship
\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}
then yield the linear system
eq.

2.8
.
∎

Theorem

2.1
suggests a straightforward numerical method for solving the tensor parametric OpInf problem
eq.

2.5
: use the reduced state snapshot matrices to form the linear system
eq.

2.8
, solve for
\bar{\mathord{\btensor T}}
, then set
\bar{\mathbf{T}}=\mathrm{cmat}_{23}\,\bar{\mathord{\btensor T}}
. The resulting non-intrusively obtained OpInf ROM for the approximate state
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor q}}=\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}
is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}},\qquad\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}(0,\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor q}_{0}(\bt@\mu).

(2.10)

This inference procedure can be accomplished with only a few lines of Python code (see

) and
can be carried out even when data for only

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
L:\mathbb{R}^{r\times r\times p^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}
be the Lagrangian function
eq.

2.7
with argument
\bar{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r\times p^{\prime}}
, defined by the matrices
\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s},\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}}
and vectors
\bt@\nu_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
,
s=1,\ldots,N_{s}
.
Consider the tensors
\hat{\mathbf{Y}},\hat{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}\times N_{s}}
which result from stacking these matrices
along the parameter index, i.e.,
\hat{\mathrm{Y}}_{ijk}=(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{k})_{ij}
and
\hat{\mathrm{Z}}_{ijk}=(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{k})_{ij}
.
Writing
\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}=\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\bar{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times rp^{\prime}}
, this Lagrangian has the equivalent expression

\displaystyle L(\bar{\mathbf{T}})=\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}}}\right)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathord{\btensor D}\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\mathord{\btensor R}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right\|^{2},

(2.11)

where
\mathord{\btensor R}=\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\hat{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}N_{s}}
and
\mathord{\btensor D}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{13}\mathrm{cvec}_{24}\,\mathbf{K}}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}N_{s}\times rp^{\prime}}
is defined in terms of the order-4 tensor
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}\times p^{\prime}\times N_{s}}
with components
\mathrm{K}_{b\alpha xs}=(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s})_{b\alpha}(\bt@\nu_{s})_{x}
.
Moreover, the problem of minimizing the vectorized Lagrangian
\ell
decouples over the rows of
\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}
.

Proof.

This can be accomplished by vectorizing the parameter index in
L
(which was previously summed) and is easiest to express with explicit index manipulations. To that end, define the index ranges

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq a,b\leq r,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\leq N_{t},

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq x\leq p^{\prime},

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq s\leq N_{s},

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq I\leq N_{t}N_{s},

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq Y\leq rp^{\prime}.

Adopting the Einstein convention where repeated sub- and super-indices imply a summation and letting
\Sigma
denote the sum over all remaining free indices, the Lagrangian is expressible as

\displaystyle L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s})_{a\alpha}-\bar{\mathrm{T}}_{abx}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)^{x}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s})^{b}_{\alpha}}}\right]^{2}=\sum\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{\mathrm{Z}}_{a\alpha s}-\bar{\mathrm{T}}_{abx}\nu^{x}_{s}\hat{\mathrm{Y}}^{b}_{\alpha s}}}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\hat{\mathbf{Z}}}}\right)_{aI}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)_{aY}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{13}\mathrm{cvec}_{24}\,\mathbf{K}}}\right)^{Y}_{I}}}\right]^{2}=\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}}}\right),

where the first equality is the definition of the Lagrangian, the second uses the definition of the tensors
\hat{\mathbf{Y}},\hat{\mathbf{Z}}
, the third vectorizes the double contraction and its result while applying the definition of
\mathbf{K}
, and the fourth transposes the expression under the norm.
Applying the definitions of
\mathord{\btensor D}
,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}
, and
\mathord{\btensor R}
, it follows that

\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathord{\btensor R}-\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}\mathord{\btensor D}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right\|^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathord{\btensor D}\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\mathord{\btensor R}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right\|^{2},

as desired.
Moreover, the minimization problem decouples over the rows
\bar{\mathord{\btensor o}}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{rp^{\prime}}
of
\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}
and
\mathord{\btensor r}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}N_{s}}
of
\mathord{\btensor R}
,
a=1,\ldots,r
, into
r
independent least-squares problems, i.e.,

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}}\,\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\argmin_{\bar{\mathord{\btensor o}}^{a}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathord{\btensor D}\bar{\mathord{\btensor o}}^{a}-\mathord{\btensor r}^{a}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right\}_{a=1}^{r}.

(2.12)

where each sub-problem has
N_{t}N_{s}
data points and
rp^{\prime}
unknowns.
∎

Remark 2.2

(Regularization)
.

Note that the optimization problems underpinning
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
are solved without regularization. While the selection strategies of
[
32
,
33
]
could be readily incorporated to induce greater numerical stability in the learned operators, our preference is to apply structural constraints when possible, as these are inherently interpretable and implicitly regularize the learning problem through the restriction biases they place on the space of allowable solutions.
Section

3
will provide one example of this approach tailored to operators with symmetry.

The expressions in
Proposition

2.1
involving
\mathord{\btensor D}
,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}
, and
\mathord{\btensor R}
exactly correspond to the state-linear parts of the inference problem (2.13) in previous work
[
33
]
. The advantage of
Algorithm

2
, in addition to the brevity of the implementation, is that the expression for
\mathord{\btensor D}
in
Proposition

2.1
provides insight into when the inference of
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
in
eq.

2.5
has a unique solution.
This is evidenced by the following concise result, analogous to
[
33
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\mathord{\btensor D}
,
\mathord{\btensor R}
, and
\ell(\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}})
be as in
Proposition

2.1
. Let
\bt@\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times p^{\prime}}
be the matrix with entries
\Theta_{sx}=(\bt@\nu_{s})_{x}
. The inference problem
\argmin\,\ell\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}}}\right)
in (
2.12
) has a unique solution
\bar{\mathord{\btensor O}}=\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}
if and only if both
\bt@\Theta
and
(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\hat{\mathbf{Y}})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}N_{s}\times r}
have full column rank.

Proof.

Similar to
Proposition

2.1
, write

\mathord{\btensor D}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{13}\mathrm{cvec}_{24}\,\mathbf{K}}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}N_{s}\times rp^{\prime}}
, where the tensor
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}\times p^{\prime}\times N_{s}}
has components
\mathrm{K}_{b\alpha xs}=(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s})_{b\alpha}\Theta_{sx}
.
Note that
\mathbf{\mathord{\btensor K}}
“factors” as the combination tensor-Hadamard product of
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}
and
\bt@1\otimes\bt@\Theta^{\mathsf{T}}
: it is an outer product in the indices
b,x
and a Hadamard product in the indices
\alpha,s
. This implies that, for any
\mathord{\btensor v}\in\mathbb{R}^{rp^{\prime}}
in the kernel of
\mathord{\btensor D}
, (Einstein summation assumed)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cmat}_{12}\,\mathord{\btensor D}\mathord{\btensor v}}}\right)_{\alpha s}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cmat}_{12}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{cmat}_{34}\,\mathord{\btensor D}:\mathrm{cmat}_{12}\,\mathord{\btensor v}^{\intercal}}}\right]}}\right)_{\alpha s}=\hat{\mathrm{Y}}_{b\alpha s}\Theta_{sx}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cmat}_{12}\,\mathord{\btensor v}}}\right)^{bx}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathrm{Y}}_{b\alpha s}\Theta_{sx}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{v}^{ay}\mathord{\btensor e}_{a}\mathord{\btensor e}_{y}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right)^{bx}=\mathrm{v}^{ay}\hat{\mathrm{Y}}_{b\alpha s}\Theta_{sx}\delta^{b}_{a}\delta^{x}_{y}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathrm{Y}}_{a\alpha s}\mathrm{v}^{ay}}}\right)\Theta_{sy}=\hat{\mathrm{Y}}_{a\alpha s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{v}^{ay}\Theta_{sy}}}\right)=0.

If
\bt@\Theta
has full column rank, the first equality on the last line shows that
(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\hat{\mathbf{Y}})^{\mathsf{T}}
is rank-deficient. Similarly, if
(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\hat{\mathbf{Y}})^{\mathsf{T}}
has full column rank, the second equality on the last line shows that
\bt@\Theta
is rank-deficient. Therefore,
\mathord{\btensor D}
has full column rank whenever these matrices do, while if either is rank deficient, then so is
\mathord{\btensor D}
.
∎

Remark 2.3

(Nonlinear terms)
.

Tensor parametric OpInf can be extended in a straightforward manner to account for quadratic (or higher-order) polynomial terms
\mathbf{H}(\bt@\mu)\!:\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor q}\otimes\mathord{\btensor q}}}\right)
with affine parameter dependence appearing in
eq.

2.1
, such as those seen in
[
33
]
. In this case, a tensor
\mathbf{T}
satisfying
\mathbf{H}(\bt@\mu)=\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}
must have order 4, as
\mathbf{H}
(if not matricized) is of order 3. Since higher-order terms are not necessary for the numerical examples presented later, such extensions are left for future work.

The results of this section demonstrate how solutions to the tensor parametric OpInf problem are global minima and how the tensor reformulation clearly and concisely recovers previous parametric OpInf formulations.
Before discussing how this approach also enables a novel extension to systems with Hamiltonian structure, an example is presented to illustrate its practical utility.

2.3
Example: Heat equation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
d\in\{1,2,3\}
and consider the following initial value problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\phantom{-}\begin{aligned} &\dot{q}(\mathord{\btensor x},t)=\nabla\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c(\mathord{\btensor x},\bt@\mu)\nabla q(\mathord{\btensor x},t)}}\right),&\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega\times(0,t_{f}],\\
&q(\mathord{\btensor x},0)=q_{0}(\mathord{\btensor x}),&\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega,\\
&q(\mathord{\btensor x},t)=0,&\mathord{\btensor x}\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{f}],\end{aligned}}}\right.

(2.13)

where
q:\Omega\times(0,t_{f})\to\mathbb{R}
is the continuous system state,
c:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is the parameterized thermal conductivity coefficient, and
q_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a given initial condition. Note that
q
depends implicitly on the conductivity parameters
\bt@\mu
. Let
\Omega=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}\Omega_{i}
be a non-overlapping decomposition of the spatial domain and consider the piecewise-continuous
c(\mathord{\btensor x},\bt@\mu)
given by

\displaystyle c(\mathord{\btensor x},\bt@\mu)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega_{1}}}\right]\,\mu_{1}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega_{2}}}\right]\,\mu_{2}+\ldots+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega_{p}}}\right]\,\mu_{p},\qquad\bt@\mu=[\mu_{1}\,\,\mu_{2}\,\,\cdots\,\,\mu_{p}]^{\mathsf{T}},

where
[S]
is the Iverson bracket
[
29
]
, i.e., the indicator function of the statement
S
.

A finite-element-based FOM for
eq.

2.13
can be readily constructed using a continuous Galerkin discretization in space.
Let
\Omega_{h}:=\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{E}}
be a conforming triangulation of the domain
\Omega
containing
N_{E}
elements.
The Galerkin weak formulation of
eq.

2.13
with this spatial discretization is the following:
find
\dot{q}_{h}\in V_{h}
such that for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
,

\displaystyle(\dot{q}_{h},v_{h})_{\Omega_{h}}=-(c(\cdot,\bt@\mu)\nabla q_{h},\nabla v_{h})_{\Omega_{h}},

(2.14)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Omega_{h}}
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product on
\Omega_{h}
and
V_{h}
is the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-conforming finite element space

\displaystyle V_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in H^{1}(\Omega):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.v_{h}}}\right|_{K}\in P_{1}(K),\kern 5.0pt\forall K\in\Omega_{h};\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.v_{h}}}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0\},

with
P_{1}(K)
representing the space of linear polynomials on element
K
.
Given a basis
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
for
V_{h}
, inserting the approximation
q(\mathord{\btensor x},t)\approx q_{h}(\mathord{\btensor x},t):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}(t)\phi(\mathord{\btensor x})
into
eq.

2.14
leads to the system of
N
ODEs
\mathord{\btensor M}\dot{\mathord{\btensor q}}=\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor q}
with matrix entries

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\mathord{\btensor M})_{ij}&=(\phi_{i},\phi_{j})_{\Omega_{h}},\\
(\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu))_{ij}&=-(c(\cdot,\bt@\mu)\nabla\phi_{i},\nabla\phi_{j})_{\Omega_{h}}=-\sum_{x=1}^{p}\mu_{x}(\nabla\phi_{i},\nabla\phi_{j})_{\Omega_{x}}.\end{aligned}

(2.15a)

The corresponding tensorized FOM
\mathord{\btensor M}\dot{\mathord{\btensor q}}=(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime})\mathord{\btensor q}
has tensor entries

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}_{ijk}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega_{k}]\nabla\phi_{i},\nabla\phi_{j}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla\phi_{i},\nabla\phi_{j}}}\right)_{\Omega_{k}}.

(2.15b)

Given snapshot matrices
\mathord{\btensor Q}_{1},\ldots,\mathord{\btensor Q}_{N_{s}}
,
Algorithm

1
or
Algorithm

2
can now be used with data
\mathord{\btensor Y}_{s}=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}
and
\mathord{\btensor Z}_{s}=\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}D_{t}(\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s})
to infer a reduced tensor
\bar{\mathbf{T}}\approx\hat{\mathbf{T}}
, producing a parametric OpInf ROM
\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor q}}
.

Remark 2.4

(Tensor symmetry)
.

The full-order tensor
\mathbf{T}
in (
2.15b
) is symmetric in its first two indices, reflecting the fact that the heat equation (
2.13
) is an
L^{2}
-gradient flow. However,
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
do not enforce this symmetry as a constraint, i.e., the learned tensor
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
is not necessarily symmetric in its first two indices.
The next section develops a tensor parametric OpInf algorithm with additional constraints that preserve symmetric (or anti-symmetric) tensor structure.

3
Tensor parametric operator inference for Hamiltonian systems

The concise tensor formulation developed in
Section

2
enables a principled extension to parametric Hamiltonian systems, where preservation of additional algebraic structure in the learned tensor
\bar{\mathbf{T}}\approx\hat{\mathbf{T}}
is necessary in order to enable the energy-conservative and symplectic characteristics of Hamiltonian dynamics.
Section

3.1
summarizes the key aspects of Hamiltonian model reduction, and
Section

3.2
develops an analog to
Algorithm

1
that accounts for the additional desired structure. An application to wave phenomena is outlined in
Section

3.3
.

3.1
Hamiltonian model reduction

Canonical Hamiltonian systems are defined by the joint state variable
\mathord{\btensor y}=[\mathord{\btensor q}^{\mathsf{T}}\kern 5.0pt\mathord{\btensor p}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, a Hamiltonian functional
H:\mathbb{R}^{2N}\to\mathbb{R}
representing a notion of energy, and a skew-adjoint block matrix
\mathord{\btensor J}=-\mathord{\btensor J}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
encoding the symplectic structure:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\{\mathord{\btensor y},H(\mathord{\btensor y})\}=\mathord{\btensor J}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y}),\qquad\mathord{\btensor J}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bt@0&\mathord{\btensor I}\\
-\mathord{\btensor I}&\bt@0\end{array}}}\right].

Here,
\{F,G\}=\nabla F\cdot\mathord{\btensor J}\nabla G
is a particular Lie algebra realization on functions known as the (canonical) Poisson bracket, and notation has been slightly abused by vectorizing this bracket over the components of the state
\mathord{\btensor y}
.
A core feature of Hamiltonian systems is that their solutions conserve energy for all time, which can be seen by computing the rate of change in
H
,

\displaystyle\dot{H}(\mathord{\btensor y})=\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})

\displaystyle=\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor J}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})=-\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor J}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor J}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}=\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor J}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y}),

which shows that
\dot{H}(\mathord{\btensor y})=0
for all
\mathord{\btensor y}
.
This reflects the fact that Hamiltonian systems yield symplectic gradient flows whose solutions are confined to individual level sets of
H

[
31
]
.

When Hamiltonian systems are discretized with the finite element method, it is typical to incorporate a symmetric positive definite mass matrix
\mathord{\btensor M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
, leading to models of the form

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor M}\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\mathord{\btensor L}\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y}),\qquad\mathord{\btensor L}^{\mathsf{T}}=-\mathord{\btensor L}.

(3.3)

To expose the Hamiltonian structure of this system, define
\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor L}
and write

\displaystyle\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor L}\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\nabla H(\mathord{\btensor y})=\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}\,\nabla^{M}\!H(\mathord{\btensor y}),

(3.4)

where
\nabla^{M}\!H=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\nabla H
is the gradient of
H
with respect to the weighted inner product
\mathord{\btensor a}^{*}\mathord{\btensor b}=\mathord{\btensor a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor b}
induced by
\mathord{\btensor M}
(see
Appendix

B
).
The adjoint of
\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}
in this inner product is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}^{*}=(\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor L})^{*}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}(\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor L})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor L}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}^{-\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}=-\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor L}=-\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}.

Therefore,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}
is skew-adjoint with respect to
\mathord{\btensor M}
, hence (
3.4
) is a Hamiltonian system in the
\mathord{\btensor M}
-weighted inner product. Additionally,
H
is conserved along solutions:

\displaystyle\dot{H}(\mathord{\btensor y})=\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}^{*}\nabla^{M}\!H(\mathord{\btensor y})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}\,\nabla^{M}\!H(\mathord{\btensor y})}}\right)^{*}\nabla^{M}\!H(\mathord{\btensor y})=-\nabla^{M}\!H(\mathord{\btensor y})^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}\,\nabla^{M}\!H(\mathord{\btensor y})}}\right)=0.

Remark 3.1

(Block diagonal mass)
.

It is often the case that
\mathord{\btensor L}=\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor M}
and that the mass matrix
\mathord{\btensor M}
can be written as

\mathord{\btensor M}=\mathrm{blockdiag}(\mathord{\btensor M}_{q},\mathord{\btensor M}_{q}),

where
\mathord{\btensor M}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a symmetric positive definite mass matrix corresponding to
\mathord{\btensor q}
and
\mathord{\btensor p}
.
In this case,
\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor M}=\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor J}
and, hence,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor L}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor M}=\mathord{\btensor J}
.
For the remainder of this section,
\mathord{\btensor J}
is written instead of
\bar{\mathord{\btensor J}}
and it is assumed that
\mathord{\btensor J}^{*}=-\mathord{\btensor J}
.

The effectiveness of Hamiltonian systems in modeling non-dissipative phenomena has motivated a significant amount of work into their model reduction

Effective ROMs for Hamiltonian systems—whether intrusive or non-intrusive—must therefore preserve the Hamiltonian structure, meaning that the reduced dynamics should be governed by an appropriate reduced Hamiltonian function
\hat{H}:\mathbb{R}^{2r}\to\mathbb{R}
and reduced skew-adjoint matrix
\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}:\mathbb{R}^{2r\times 2r}
.

1

In fact,
\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}(\mathord{\btensor y})
can generally be a matrix field, and not all skew-adjoint
\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}
define valid Hamiltonian systems. More details can be found in, e.g.,
[
21
,
22
]
.

Structure preservation is equally critical in ROMs for
parametric
Hamiltonian systems, i.e., those governed by a parameterized Hamiltonian
H=H(\mathord{\btensor y},\bt@\mu)
. In this case, the ROM must be defined in terms of the gradient of an appropriate reduced parameterized Hamiltonian.
To this end, consider the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(\mathord{\btensor y},\bt@\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor y}+f(\mathord{\btensor y},\bt@\mu),

(3.5)

defined in terms of a nonlinear function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2N}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
and a matrix field
\mathord{\btensor A}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
with affine parametric dependence.
The (canonical) Hamiltonian system generated by
H
takes the form

\displaystyle\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\mathord{\btensor J}\nabla^{M}\!H(\mathord{\btensor y},\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor J}(\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor y}+\nabla^{M}\!f(\mathord{\btensor y},\bt@\mu)).

(3.6)

It may be assumed that
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
is self-adjoint, that is,
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)^{*}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor M}
, since only the self-adjoint part of
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
contributes to the value of
H
(see
Lemmata

B.1
and
B.4
).
Since
\mathord{\btensor A}
depends affinely on
\bt@\mu
, it follows as before that
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)=\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}
for some constant tensor
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N\times p^{\prime}}
and a vector
\bt@\mu^{\prime}=\bt@\theta(\bt@\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
.
Additionally, because
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
is self-adjoint, it follows that
\mathbf{T}
is self-adjoint in its first two indices, denoted
\mathbf{T}^{*}=\mathbf{T}
.
The key to the tensor parametric Hamiltonian OpInf developed here will be an inference procedure analogous to
Algorithm

1
which explicitly takes this symmetry into account.

To see the constraints that Hamiltonian structure places at the reduced-order level, consider
eq.

3.6
with
f\equiv 0
and a known initial condition:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor y}=\mathord{\btensor J}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor y},\qquad\mathord{\btensor y}(0,\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor y}_{0}(\bt@\mu).

(3.7)

Given a trial space basis
\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2r}
which is metric-compatible in the sense that
\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor U}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor U}=\mathord{\btensor I}
for some
\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2r\times 2r}
defining an inner product on the reduced coordinates (typically
\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}=\mathord{\btensor I}
), making the approximation
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor y}}\coloneqq\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}\approx\mathord{\btensor y}
with
\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}(t,\bt@\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2r}
and performing
\mathord{\btensor M}
-orthogonal projection in
eq.

3.7
results in the following (intrusive) ROM:

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}=\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor J}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\qquad\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}(0,\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor y}_{0}(\bt@\mu).

(3.8)

Unfortunately, the ROM
eq.

3.8
is not Hamiltonian with respect to the reduced inner product
\hat{\mathord{\btensor a}}^{*}\hat{\mathord{\btensor b}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor a}}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor b}}
since
(\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor J})^{*}=\mathord{\btensor J}^{*}\mathord{\btensor U}=-\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor U}\neq-\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor J}
, which implies that the symplectic structure of the original system is lost. Moreover, the natural reduced Hamiltonian functional
\hat{H}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu^{\prime}):=H(\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu)
is not conserved: for any
\bt@\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
,

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{H}}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu^{\prime})=\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}^{*}\nabla^{\hat{M}}\!\hat{H}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor J}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}}\right)^{*}\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}

\displaystyle=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}}\right)^{*}\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor U}\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}\neq 0.

In other words, the structural and conservative properties of the FOM
eq.

3.7
are not automatically inherited by the projection-based ROM
eq.

3.8
, and solutions of
eq.

3.8
are liable to exhibit artificial energetic profiles and stability issues.

There are two primary approaches to circumventing this defect: modifying the reduced basis
\mathord{\btensor U}
, or modifying the equation form
eq.

3.8
. The first approach was initiated by the work
[
36
]
, which introduced the Proper Symplectic Decomposition (PSD) for building Hamiltonian ROMs. This involves constructing a basis
\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2r}
with the desirable
\mathord{\btensor J}
-equivariance property
\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor J}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}
, where
\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}=-\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{2r\times 2r}
.
The advantage of this property is that a variationally consistent and canonical Hamiltonian ROM follows directly from Galerkin projection onto the span of
\mathord{\btensor U}
,

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}=\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor J}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},

(3.9)

where the reduced tensor
\hat{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2r\times 2r\times p^{\prime}}
is (assuming
\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}=\mathord{\btensor I}
) symmetric in its first two indices, written
\hat{\mathbf{T}}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime})\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\nabla\hat{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu}}\right)
is the (Euclidean) gradient of the reduced Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\mathord{\btensor y},\bt@\mu^{\prime})=(1/2)\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime})\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}.

(3.10)

This intrusively constructed ROM exactly preserves the reduced Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, since

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{H}}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu^{\prime})=\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla\hat{H}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu^{\prime})=\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime})\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}=-\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime})\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}=0.

Mirroring this structure, an appropriate OpInf ROM for
eq.

3.7
that incorporates the dynamics induced by the Hamiltonian used above is

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\qquad\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}(0,\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor y}_{0}(\bt@\mu),

(3.11)

as long as the inferred tensor
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
is constrained to satisfy the symmetry property
\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bar{\mathbf{T}}
.
Similar to the previous computation, it is straightforward to verify that the ROM
eq.

3.11
exactly preserves the reduced Hamiltonian
\bar{H}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu)=(1/2)\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime})\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}
, which approximates
\hat{H}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu)
.
The next section extends the work of
Section

2
to infer Hamiltonian ROMs of the form
eq.

3.11
in a non-intrusive fashion.

The alternative to designing the basis matrix
\mathord{\btensor U}
to satisfy the potentially restrictive
\mathord{\btensor J}
-equivariance property is to modify the model form
eq.

3.8
.
An effective way of accomplishing this while retaining variational consistency was presented in

\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor U}

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}^{-\mathsf{T}}\nabla\hat{H}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},\bt@\mu^{\prime})=\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}^{-\mathsf{T}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor y}},

(3.12)

which satisfies
\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}
and is symplectic since
\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}^{-\mathsf{T}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor U}}}\right)^{-\mathsf{T}}=-\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}^{-1}
is constant in the state.

3.2
Enforcing symmetries in tensor parametric operator inference

A key feature of the canonical Hamiltonian ROMs
eq.

3.9
–
eq.

3.12
is the preservation of the symmetry condition
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}(\bt@\mu)^{\mathsf{T}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}(\bt@\mu)
governing the reduced Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
. In the case of affine parameter dependence, this reduces to the symmetry constraint
\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}
on the first two indices of the tensor representation
\hat{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu^{\prime}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}(\bt@\mu)
.
The natural extension of the tensor OpInf problem
eq.

2.10
to this setting is the following constrained regression problem:

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bar{\mathbf{T}}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}}_{s}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor J}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu_{s}^{\prime}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}_{s}}}\right\|^{2}\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bar{\mathbf{T}},

(3.13)

where, as in the setting of
Section

2
,
\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}_{s},\dot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor Q}}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2r\times N_{t}}
are reduced state snapshot matrices and (approximate) reduced state time derivative matrices corresponding to parameter samples
\bt@\mu_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
,
s=1,\ldots,N_{s}
.
The following theorem provides a compact strategy for solving this problem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}},\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{t}}
, and
\bt@\nu_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
for
s=1,\ldots,N_{s}
. Then solutions to the convex minimization problem

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bar{\mathbf{T}}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right\|^{2}\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\eta=\pm\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\eta}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\forall\bt@\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}},

can be computed by solving the linear system

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bt@\nu_{s}\bt@\nu_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes_{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\baroplus_{K}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right)}}\right]\,&\mathrm{vec}\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}\\
=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\bt@\nu_{s}\,\otimes_{K}\,&\mathrm{cvec}_{12}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\pm\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}}}\right),\end{aligned}

(3.14)

where
\mathrm{vec}\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}=\mathrm{cvec}_{12}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{12}\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right)
is a total column-wise flattening of the tensor
\bar{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r\times p^{\prime}}
,
\mathord{\btensor X}\otimes_{K}\mathord{\btensor Y}=\mathrm{rvec}_{13}\mathrm{rvec}_{24}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor X}\otimes\mathord{\btensor Y}}}\right)
denotes the usual Kronecker product of matrices, and
\mathord{\btensor X}\baroplus_{K}\mathord{\btensor Y}\coloneqq\mathord{\btensor X}\otimes_{K}\mathord{\btensor Y}+\mathord{\btensor Y}\otimes_{K}\mathord{\btensor X}
denotes a symmetric Kronecker sum.

Proof.

Let
\bt@\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r\times p^{\prime}}
be a tensor of Lagrange multipliers and define the Lagrangian

\displaystyle L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}},\bt@\Lambda}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right\|^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bt@\Lambda,\bar{\mathbf{T}}\mp\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right\rangle,

where, as before,
\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}
indicates transposition of the first two indices.
Differentiating this expression, it follows that

\displaystyle dL\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}},\bt@\Lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s},\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle d\bt@\Lambda,\bar{\mathbf{T}}\mp\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bt@\Lambda,d\bar{\mathbf{T}}\mp d\bar{\mathbf{T}}}}\right\rangle^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle d\bar{\mathbf{T}},\bt@\Lambda\mp\bt@\Lambda^{\mathsf{T}}-\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle d\hat{\bt@\Lambda},\bar{\mathbf{T}}\mp\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle d\bar{\mathbf{T}},\nabla_{\bar{\mathbf{T}}}L}}\right\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle d\bt@\Lambda,\nabla_{\bt@\Lambda}L}}\right\rangle.

Setting these gradients to zero yields the Euler–Lagrange equations for

,

\displaystyle\bt@\Lambda\mp\bt@\Lambda^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\nu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bt@\nu_{s}

(3.15a)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{T}}\mp\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\bt@0.

(3.15b)

In particular, the Lagrange multiplier
\bt@\Lambda
can be eliminated by symmetrizing/anti-symmetrizing
eq.

3.15a
, leading to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{s}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bt@\mu_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}}}\right)\hat{\mathord{\btensor Y}}_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}}}\right.\hskip 142.26378pt\\

3.3
Example: Wave equation

This section describes an application with canonical Hamiltonian structure to which
Algorithm

1
may be applied.
Consider the following initial boundary value problem for a wave on the bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, with variable wave speed:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\phantom{-}\begin{aligned} &\ddot{y}(\mathord{\btensor x},t)=\nabla\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c(\mathord{\btensor x},\bt@\mu)^{2}\nabla y(\mathord{\btensor x},t)}}\right),&\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega\times(0,t_{f}],\\
&y(\mathord{\btensor x},t)=0,&\mathord{\btensor x}\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{f}],\\
&y(\mathord{\btensor x},0)=y_{0}(\mathord{\btensor x}),&\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega,\\
&\dot{y}(\mathord{\btensor x},0)=0,&\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega.\end{aligned}}}\right.

(3.16)

It is straightforward to check that the system
eq.

3.16
is Hamiltonian with the canonical variables
q=y
,
p=\dot{y}
and Hamiltonian functional

\displaystyle H(q,p,\bt@\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}p^{2}(\mathord{\btensor x})\,d\mathord{\btensor x}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}c^{2}(\mathord{\btensor x},\bt@\mu)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla q(\mathord{\btensor x})}}\right\|^{2}\,d\mathord{\btensor x}.

Suppose
\Omega
can be partitioned into non-overlapping subdomains
\Omega=\cup_{i=1}^{p}\Omega_{i}
representing different materials or media with wave speeds. Then
c^{2}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle c(\mathord{\btensor x},\bt@\mu)^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega_{1}}}\right]\,\mu_{1}^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega_{2}}}\right]\,\mu_{2}^{2}+\ldots+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathord{\btensor x}\in\Omega_{p}}}\right]\,\mu_{p}^{2},\qquad\bt@\mu=[\mu_{1}\,\,\mu_{2}\,\,\cdots\,\,\mu_{p}]^{\mathsf{T}}.

With this choice,
eq.

3.16
is a locally linear, but globally nonlinear, Hamiltonian wave equation whose solution propagates with different speeds in each subdomain.

An appropriate FOM for
eq.

3.16
can be constructed via the Hamiltonian-preserving mixed finite element scheme from
[
49
]
.
Given a triangulation
\Omega_{h}=\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{E}}
of
\Omega
, define the spaces

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W_{h}&\coloneqq\{w_{h}\in L^{2}(\Omega):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.w_{h}}}\right|_{K}=P_{k}(K),\forall K\in\Omega_{h}\},\\
V_{h}&\coloneqq\{\mathord{\btensor v}_{h}\in H(\text{div},\Omega):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\mathord{\btensor v}_{h}}}\right|_{K}=RT_{k}(K),\forall K\in\Omega_{h};\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\mathord{\btensor v}_{h}}}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0\},\end{aligned}

where
RT_{k}(K)
is the order-
k
Raviart–Thomas space
[P_{k}(K)]^{2}\oplus\mathord{\btensor x}P_{k}(K)
on element
K
and
P_{k}(K)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree
k
on element
K
. The semi-discrete weak form can then be stated as follows: find
(\dot{q}_{h},\dot{p}_{h})\in W_{h}\times W_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dot{q}_{h},w_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{h},w_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}\qquad\,\,\,\,\forall w_{h}\in W_{h},

(3.17a)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dot{p}_{h},w_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla\cdot\bt@\sigma_{h},w_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}\quad\forall w_{h}\in W_{h},

(3.17b)

where
\bt@\sigma_{h}\in V_{h}
is a weak representation of
c^{2}\nabla q
and therefore satisfies

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{c(\bt@\mu)^{2}}\bt@\sigma_{h},\bt@\xi_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{h},\nabla\cdot\bt@\xi_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}=0\qquad\forall\bt@\xi_{h}\in V_{h}.

(3.17c)

The discrete Hamiltonian for this scheme is defined in analogy with the continuous case:

\displaystyle H_{h}(q_{h},p_{h})=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{h},p_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{c(\bt@\mu)^{2}}\bt@\sigma_{h},\bt@\sigma_{h}}}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}.

(3.18)

The mixed finite-element scheme
eq.

3.17
can be written in a matrix-vector form suitable for tensorization. It is shown in
Appendix

C
that using
\mathord{\btensor M}_{V}
and
\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}
to denote the mass matrices associated with the spaces
V_{h}
and
W_{h}
, respectively, and collecting
N
degrees of freedom of finite element approximations of
q_{h}
and
p_{h}
into the vector
\mathord{\btensor y}=[\,\mathord{\btensor q}^{\mathsf{T}}\kern 5.0pt\mathord{\btensor p}^{\mathsf{T}}\,]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
,
the system formed from
eq.

3.17
–
eq.

3.17c
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\mathord{\btensor q}}\\
\dot{\mathord{\btensor p}}\end{array}}}\right]&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{ c c }\bt@0&\mathord{\btensor I}\\
-\mathord{\btensor I}&\bt@0\end{array}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{ c c }\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor S}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}_{V}(\bt@\mu)^{-1}\mathord{\btensor S}&\bt@0\\
\bt@0&\mathord{\btensor I}\end{array}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathord{\btensor q}\\
\mathord{\btensor p}\end{array}}}\right]=\mathord{\btensor J}\mathord{\btensor A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bt@\mu}}\right)\mathord{\btensor y}.\end{aligned}

(3.19)

Importantly, the mass matrix
\mathord{\btensor M}_{V}(\bt@\mu)
depends affinely on
\bt@\mu^{-2}=[\,\mu_{1}^{-2}\,\cdots\,\mu_{p}^{-2}\,]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

and
\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)=\mathrm{blockdiag}(\mathord{\btensor A}_{1}(\bt@\mu),\mathord{\btensor I})
where
\mathord{\btensor A}_{1}(\bt@\mu)=\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor S}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}_{V}(\bt@\mu)^{-1}\mathord{\btensor S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
This is a canonical Hamiltonian system of the form
eq.

3.4
or, equivalently,
eq.

3.7
, with respect to the joint mass matrix
\mathord{\btensor M}=\mathrm{blockdiag}(\mathord{\btensor M}_{W},\mathord{\btensor M}_{W})
and the discrete Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} H_{h}(\mathord{\btensor y},\bt@\mu)&=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathord{\btensor p},\mathord{\btensor p}\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathord{\btensor q},\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor S}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}_{V}(\bt@\mu)^{-1}\mathord{\btensor S}\mathord{\btensor q}\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathord{\btensor y},\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)\mathord{\btensor y}}}\right\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}},\end{aligned}

where
\langle\mathord{\btensor a},\mathord{\btensor b}\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}}=\mathord{\btensor a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}_{W}\mathord{\btensor b}
denotes the discrete inner product on
W_{h}
.

Because the affine parametric expansion of
\mathord{\btensor M}_{V}(\bt@\mu)
is in terms of
\bt@\mu^{-2}
, it is reasonable to approximate the parametric dependence of
\mathord{\btensor A}_{1}(\bt@\mu)
as affine in the element-wise square
\bt@\mu^{2}=[\,\mu_{1}^{2}\,\cdots\,\mu_{p}^{2}\,]^{\mathsf{T}}
so that
\mathord{\btensor A}_{1}(\bt@\mu)\approx\mathbf{T}_{1}\bt@\mu^{2}
for some constant tensor
\mathbf{T}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times p}
.
This also defines a tensor
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N\times p}
with
\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime}\approx\mathord{\btensor A}(\bt@\mu)
with
\bt@\mu^{\prime}=[\,(\bt@\mu^{2})^{\mathsf{T}},\,1]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
and entries

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}_{ijk}=[k\neq 1][i\leq N][j\leq N]\,(\mathbf{T}_{1})_{ijk}+[k=1][i>N][j=i],

which amounts to inserting the
N\times N
identity alongside the approximation for
\mathord{\btensor A}_{1}(\bt@\mu)
through the last index of
\mathbf{T}
.
It follows that the model
\dot{\mathord{\btensor y}}=\mathord{\btensor J}(\mathbf{T}\bt@\mu^{\prime})\mathord{\btensor y}
is an affine parametric approximation to the original wave equation
eq.

3.16
which retains its Hamiltonian structure, provided that
\mathbf{T}^{*}=\mathbf{T}
is self-adjoint in its first two indices.

4

This section applies tensor parametric OpInf to the parameterized heat and wave equation models introduced in earlier sections.
Numerical experiments for each problem are conducted in one and two spatial dimensions, comparing both intrusive and non-intrusive ROMs. Accuracy is assessed using the relative
L^{2}
-error in state approximations: if
\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}^{\text{FOM}}
and
\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}^{\text{ROM}}
are matrices of the FOM and ROM solutions, respectively, at the same parameter value and over
N_{t}

\Delta t>0
, the relative error for a collection of
\tilde{N}_{s}

\mathord{\btensor Q}^{\text{ROM}}=\{\mathord{\btensor Q}_{1}^{\text{ROM}},\ldots,\mathord{\btensor Q}_{\tilde{N}_{s}}^{\text{ROM}}\}
compared to the FOM solutions
\mathord{\btensor Q}^{\text{FOM}}=\{\mathord{\btensor Q}_{1}^{\text{FOM}},\ldots,\mathord{\btensor Q}_{\tilde{N}_{s}}^{\text{FOM}}\}
is given by

\displaystyle RL^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor Q}^{\text{FOM}},\mathord{\btensor Q}^{\text{ROM}}}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{s}\|\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}^{\text{FOM}}-\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}^{\text{ROM}}\|_{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{2}}{\sum_{s}\|\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}^{\text{FOM}}\|_{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{2}}},\quad\|\mathord{\btensor Q}\|_{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{2}=\mathrm{trace}(\mathord{\btensor Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor Q}).

(4.1)

For a given basis
\mathord{\btensor U}
, the projection error is defined by replacing
\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}^{\text{ROM}}
with
\mathord{\btensor U}\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}\mathord{\btensor Q}_{s}^{\text{FOM}}
in
eq.

4.1
.
We also examine the signed elementwise errors
(\mathord{\btensor Q}^{\text{FOM}}_{s})_{ij}-(\mathord{\btensor Q}^{\text{ROM}}_{s})_{ij}
and corresponding absolute errors for select trajectories. As discussed in
Remark

2.2
, we have chosen not to explicitly regularize the OpInf learning problems; further performance improvements, such as improvements in numerical conditioning, are possible with a careful choice of regularization parameter.

5
Conclusion

This paper presents a novel, tensorized operator inference procedure for non-intrusively learning reduced-order models corresponding to parametric systems of semi-discrete PDEs. In its most basic form, the proposed method serves as a compact, easy-to-implement replacement for the state-linear component of existing parametric OpInf strategies. Additionally, it is shown that the advantages of this formulation enable an extension to the inference of operators with additional structure information, such as partial symmetries, which appear in the contexts of Hamiltonian systems and gradient flows. Applications to a heat equation with variable diffusion coefficient and a Hamiltonian wave equation with variable wave speed are presented, where the proposed OpInf leads to structure-preserving Hamiltonian ROMs which are energy-conserving and symplectic as required.

The structure-preserving inference procedure of
Section

3
, specifically in
Theorem

3.1
and
Algorithm

1
, is based on solving the normal equations corresponding to a particular minimization problem. It would be interesting to consider future extensions of this approach which avoid the potential for numerical ill-conditioning, i.e., to develop an extension of
Algorithm

2
to the structure-preserving setting. This may have the secondary benefit of removing the need for a large, memory-intensive intermediate tensors that are required for symmetry enforcement in the current approach, as well as the potential for greater parallelism and preconditioning to improve convergence. Future work will also consider incorporating additional constraints on the learned operators beyond symmetry, as well as accounting for nonlinear terms arising from non-quadratic Hamiltonians, i.e., when
f\neq 0
in
eq.

3.5
–
eq.

3.6
.

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}(\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{X})}}\right)_{i,(k-1)r+j}=(\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{X})_{ijk}=\sum_{a=1}^{r}\sum_{b=1}^{p^{\prime}}\mathrm{T}_{iab}\mathrm{X}_{bajk}

for each
1\leq i,j\leq r
and
1\leq k\leq p^{\prime}
.
On the other hand, for
1\leq a\leq r
and
1\leq b\leq p^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\mathbf{T})_{i,(b-1)r+a}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{T}_{iab},

\displaystyle(\mathrm{rvec}_{12}\mathrm{cvec}_{34}\,\mathbf{X})_{(b-1)r+a,(k-1)r+j}=(\mathrm{cvec}_{34}\,\mathbf{X})_{b,a,(k-1)r+j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}_{bajk}.

Hence, the
(i,(k-1)r+j)
-th entry of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\mathbf{T}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{rvec}_{12}\mathrm{cvec}_{34}\,\mathbf{X}}}\right)
is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{r}\sum_{b=1}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathrm{cvec}_{23}\,\mathbf{T})_{i,(b-1)r+a}(\mathrm{rvec}_{12}\mathrm{cvec}_{34}\,\mathbf{X})_{(b-1)r+a,(k-1)r+j}=\sum_{a=1}^{r}\sum_{b=1}^{p^{\prime}}\mathrm{T}_{iab}\mathrm{X}_{bajk},

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.4

(Iterated vectorization and the Kronecker product)
.

Let
\mathord{\btensor A},\mathord{\btensor B},\mathord{\btensor C}
be matrices potentially with different dimensions, and let
\mathrm{rvec}_{135}=\mathrm{rvec}_{13}\mathrm{rvec}_{35}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor A}\otimes_{K}\mathord{\btensor B}\otimes_{K}\mathord{\btensor C}=\mathrm{rvec}_{135}\mathrm{rvec}_{246}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor A}\otimes\mathord{\btensor B}\otimes\mathord{\btensor C}}}\right).

Proof.

By the definition of the Kronecker product along with its associativity, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor A}\otimes_{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor B}\otimes_{K}\mathord{\btensor C}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rvec}_{13}\mathrm{rvec}_{24}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor A}\otimes\mathrm{rvec}_{13}\mathrm{rvec}_{24}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor B}\otimes\mathord{\btensor C}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rvec}_{13}\mathrm{rvec}_{24}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{rvec}_{35}\mathrm{rvec}_{46}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor A}\otimes\mathord{\btensor B}\otimes\mathord{\btensor C}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rvec}_{13}\mathrm{rvec}_{35}\mathrm{rvec}_{24}\mathrm{rvec}_{46}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor A}\otimes\mathord{\btensor B}\otimes\mathord{\btensor C}}}\right),

which immediately implies the conclusion.
∎

Appendix B
Adjoints and gradients

This appendix briefly outlines the relationship between adjoints and gradients with respect to different inner products.

Lemma B.1

(Weighted adjoint)
.

Suppose
\mathord{\btensor A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
\mathord{\btensor x},\mathord{\btensor y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then the adjoint
\mathord{\btensor A}^{*}
of
\mathord{\btensor A}
with respect to the inner product
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathord{\btensor x},\mathord{\btensor y}}}\right\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}}=\mathord{\btensor x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor y}
defined by the symmetric and positive definite matrix
\mathord{\btensor M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is
\mathord{\btensor A}^{*}=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathord{\btensor A}\mathord{\btensor x},\mathord{\btensor y}}}\right\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}}=\mathord{\btensor x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor y}=\mathord{\btensor x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor y}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathord{\btensor x},(\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M})\mathord{\btensor y}}}\right\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}}.

∎

The adjoint of a non-square matrix
\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
is only slightly more delicate, requiring weighted inner products on both
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbb{R}^{r}
.

Lemma B.2

(Weighted non-square adjoint)
.

Let
\mathord{\btensor U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
,
\hat{\mathord{\btensor x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
\mathord{\btensor y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and suppose
\mathord{\btensor M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
are symmetric positive definite. Then the adjoint of
\mathord{\btensor U}
with respect to the
\mathord{\btensor M}
and
\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}
inner products is
\mathord{\btensor U}^{*}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}
.

Proof.

Similar to before, observe that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathord{\btensor U}\hat{\mathord{\btensor x}},\mathord{\btensor y}}}\right\rangle_{\mathord{\btensor M}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor y}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}}}\right)\mathord{\btensor y}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\hat{\mathord{\btensor x}},(\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M})\mathord{\btensor y}}}\right\rangle_{\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}}.

∎

The previous results connect the
\mathord{\btensor M}
-adjoint to the Euclidean adjoint. The next result establishes the same connection for the gradient operator on scalar functions.

Lemma B.3

(Weighted gradient)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function and
\mathord{\btensor M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix. Then the gradient of
f
with respect to the
\mathord{\btensor M}
-weighted inner product is
\nabla^{M}\!f=\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\nabla f
.

Proof.

For any
\mathord{\btensor x},\mathord{\btensor v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it follows from Taylor’s theorem that

\displaystyle df|_{\mathord{\btensor x}}(\mathord{\btensor v})=\mathord{\btensor v}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla f(\mathord{\btensor x})=\mathord{\btensor v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\nabla f(\mathord{\btensor x})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathord{\btensor v},\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\nabla f(\mathord{\btensor x})}}\right\rangle.

Since
\mathord{\btensor v}
is arbitrary, this implies that
\nabla f=\mathord{\btensor M}\nabla^{M}\!f
and the conclusion follows.
∎

The final result shows why affine-parametric state matrices can be assumed to be self-adjoint in the Hamiltonian systems context.

Lemma B.4

(Self-adjointness in quadratic functionals)
.

Let
\mathord{\btensor A},\mathord{\btensor M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and assume that
\mathord{\btensor M}
is symmetric and positive definite.
Only the self-adjoint part of
\mathord{\btensor A}
contributes to the value of the quadratic functional
H:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
H(\mathord{\btensor y})=\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}\mathord{\btensor A}\mathord{\btensor y}=\mathord{\btensor y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor A}\mathord{\btensor y}
.
Specifically,
H(\mathord{\btensor y})
is proportional to
\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}(\mathord{\btensor A}+\mathord{\btensor A}^{*})\mathord{\btensor y}
.

Proof.

Decomposing
\mathord{\btensor A}
into adjoint and skew-adjoint parts,

\displaystyle H(\mathord{\btensor y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}(\mathord{\btensor A}+\mathord{\btensor A}^{*})\mathord{\btensor y}+\frac{1}{2}\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}(\mathord{\btensor A}-\mathord{\btensor A}^{*})\mathord{\btensor y}.

Because
\mathord{\btensor M}
is symmetric,

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}\mathord{\btensor A}\mathord{\btensor y}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathord{\btensor y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor A}\mathord{\btensor y}}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathord{\btensor y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor M}^{-1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathord{\btensor M}\mathord{\btensor y}=\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}\mathord{\btensor A}^{*}\mathord{\btensor y},

and hence
\mathord{\btensor y}^{*}(\mathord{\btensor A}-\mathord{\btensor A}^{*})\mathord{\btensor y}=0
.
∎
