SHORT-TIME BEHAVIOR OF THE AT-THE-MONEY IMPLIED VOLATILITY FOR THE JUMP-DIFFUSION STOCHASTIC VOLATILITY BACHELIER MODEL
Elisa Alòs, Òscar Burés, Josep Vives

Abstract
In this paper we use Malliavin Calculus techniques in order to obtain expressions for the short-time behavior of the at-the-money implied volatility (ATM-IV) level and skew for a jump-diffusion stock price. The diffusion part is assumed to be the stochastic volatility Bachelier model and the jumps are modeled by a pure-jump Lévy process with drift so that the stock price is a martingale. Regarding the level, we show that the short-time behavior of the ATM-IV level is the same for all pure-jump Lévy processes and, regarding the skew, we give conditions on the law of the jumps for the skew to exist. We also give several numerical examples of stochastic volatilities and Lévy processes that confirm the theoretical results found in the paper.



Keywords:
Bachelier model, Stochastic volatility, Jump-Diffusion models, Lévy processes, Malliavin calculus.

MSC Classification:
60H07; 60J76; 91G20; 91G60.

1
Introduction

Historically, quantitative finance has relied heavily on log-normal models such as the celebrated Black-Scholes model and its numerous extensions, including rough and local volatility models. A key feature of these models is that they ensure asset prices remain strictly positive. However, in recent years, instances of asset prices crossing the zero threshold and attaining negative values have been observed. Notably, in April 2020, during the COVID-19 recession, crude oil futures temporarily attained negative values due to the low demand compared to the storage cost. In response, exchanges such as the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) and the Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) adapted their modeling frameworks, transitioning from the Black-Scholes model to the Bachelier model for oil and natural gas options until August 2020.

The Bachelier model, though historically overshadowed by log-normal models, has long played an important role in fixed income markets, where interest rates can take negative values, something poorly captured by log-normal models. For further discussion on the advantages and growing adoption of the Bachelier model, we refer to
Choi et al., (2022)
.

Despite its increasing popularity, analytical results for the Bachelier model remain relatively scarce. Recently,
Alòs et al., (2023)
provided explicit formulas for the short-time behavior of At-The-Money (ATM) implied volatility under the Bachelier model with local and fractional volatility. Although this work fills a significant gap, more generalizations are needed to account for market phenomena that cannot be fully explained by stochastic volatility alone. As discussed in
Cont and Tankov, (2004)
, incorporating jump processes - specifically Lévy processes - into asset price dynamics allows modeling discontinuities and heavy-tailed behaviors, which are prevalent in financial markets. Moreover, combining stochastic volatility with jump-diffusion processes improves the fit of implied volatility surfaces.

In the Black-Scholes framework, short-time asymptotics for the ATM implied volatility level and skew under stochastic volatility and jump-diffusion models have been extensively studied. For example,
Alòs et al., (2007)
examined the short-time behavior of the ATM implied volatility level and skew in the Bates model (see
Bates, (1996)
), while
Rosiński, (2007)
analyzed similar properties when jumps follow a CGMY distribution.

In the present paper, we extend these analyses to the Bachelier setting by considering a jump-diffusion stochastic volatility model, where jumps follow a pure-jump Lévy process with drift. Using techniques from Malliavin calculus and classical results on Lévy processes, we establish that, for any pure-jump Lévy martingale, the ATM implied volatility level coincides with that of the pure stochastic volatility model without jumps (see
Alòs et al., (2023)
for a related result). Furthermore, if the jump process satisfies a stability condition, we derive an explicit formula for the short-time behavior of the implied volatility skew of the ATM, showing that it behaves analogously to the results obtained in
Alòs et al., (2007)
, but in a normal setting rather than a log-normal one.

To validate our theoretical results, we performed numerical simulations under various stochastic volatility models, such as the SABR (see
Hagan et al., (2002)
) and Rough Bergomi (see
Bayer et al., (2016)
) and different pure-jump Lévy processes. Specifically, we analyze cases where jumps follow a compound Poisson process with Gaussian and Laplace jumps (extending the Bates model to normal settings and more general volatility dynamics), as well as cases where jumps follow CGMY and Normal Inverse Gaussian (NIG) distributions.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we define the model we assume for the stock price dynamics. In Section
3
we state the main result of the paper, Theorem
3.2
and the rest of the sections are devoted to prove the theorem and numerically check that the result holds. In fact, in Section
4
, we derive a Hull-White type formula that facilitates the proof of Theorem
3.2
in the compound Poisson case. In Section
5
we prove Equation (
3.2
) for compound Poisson processes, that is, the part of Theorem
3.2
regarding the level before extending it to general pure-jump Lévy processes. Section
6
follows a methodology similar to
Alòs et al., (2007)
to prove equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) of Theorem
3.2
for the compound Poisson case. In the same section, following an approximation argument, we prove Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) for the general Lévy case. The reason why we use an approximation argument is because the strategy of replicating the arguments in
Alòs et al., (2007)
for a general pure-jump Lévy martingale is successful for Lévy processes with infinite activity and finite variation paths, but it fails for Lévy processes with infinite variation paths since the expansions do not give enough information about the leading term. Indeed, the leading order terms of the expansion in the infinite variation case involve a term that depends on an integral of a Greek of the Bachelier function with respect to the Lévy measure whose computation is, in general, not feasible. Finally, in Section
7
, we conduct numerical simulations to verify Theorem
3.2
across various well-established models in the literature.

2
Statement of the model and preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we will assume zero interest rate
r=0
. Not only we assume
r=0
for the sake of simplicity, but also because
r
is assumed to be zero in interest rate models, while the underlying asset in commodity derivatives are future prices. Let
T>0
be a maturity time and let
S=\{S_{t};t\in[0,T]\}
follow the model

S_{t}=S_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s})+L_{t},

(2.1)

where
S_{0}>0
is fixed and
W
and
B
are independent Brownian motions on
[0,T]
defined in the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
\mathbb{F}
is the filtration generated by
W
,
B
and
L
. More precisely, let

\mathbb{F}^{W}=\{\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t};t\in[0,T]\},\quad\mathbb{F}^{B}=\{\mathcal{F}^{B}_{t};t\in[0,T]\}\quad\text{and }\quad\mathbb{F}^{L}=\{\mathcal{F}^{L}_{t};t\in[0,T]\}

denote the complete natural filtrations with respect to
W
,
B
and
L
respectively. Moreover, we define
\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t};t\in[0,T]\}
as the filtration defined by
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t}\vee\mathcal{F}^{B}_{t}\vee\mathcal{F}^{L}_{t}
. We set
\rho\in(-1,1)
, we assume
\sigma
to be an a.s. continuous, square integrable and
\mathbb{F}^{W}
-adapted stochastic process and
L
denotes a pure-jump Lévy process with triplet
(a,0,\nu)
. Because of the Lévy-Itô decomposition (see
Sato, (2013)
or
Cont and Tankov, (2004)
),
L
is of the form

L_{t}=at+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|y|\geq 1}yN(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|y|<1}y\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y),\quad t\in[0,T]

where
N
is a Poisson random measure and
\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y)=N(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y)-\,\mathrm{d}s\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)
denotes the compensated Poisson measure. Notice that, if we demand
S
to be a martingale we need the condition

a=-\int_{|y|\geq 1}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y).

We also define the constants

c_{0}=\nu(\mathbb{R})\in[0,\infty],\quad c_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\in[-\infty,\infty].

We can classify
L
depending on the finiteness of certain constants.

•

In the case
c_{0}=\nu(\mathbb{R})<\infty
,
L
is a compound Poisson process of intensity
\lambda=c_{0}
. Notice that if we let
\sigma
follow the same dynamics as the volatility of the Heston model, then the model (
2.1
) becomes the Bachelier-Bates model.

•

In the case
\nu(\mathbb{R})=\infty
but
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
then
L
has infinite activity with finite variation trajectories, meaning that
L
exhibits an infinitely amount of jumps but the paths of
L
remain of bounded variation.

•

In the case
\nu(\mathbb{R})=\infty
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)=\infty
then
L
has infinite activity with infinite variation trajectories. In that case,
c_{1}
is not well-defined. However, as a consequence of the Lévy-Itô decomposition (see for instance
Sato, (2013)
or
Cont and Tankov, (2004)
), the process

L_{t}^{\varepsilon}=at+\int_{|y|\geq 1}yN(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{\varepsilon<|y|<1}y\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y)

is a compound Poisson process with drift
a
and intensity
c_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}):=\nu(\mathbb{R}\backslash[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon])
. In this situation, we can define the sequence of constants

c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y),\quad\varepsilon>0.

Note that in the compound Poisson case, the infinite activity with finite variation case and some infinite activity and infinite variation cases, the sequence of constants
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}
converge to a real number
c_{1}
as
\varepsilon
tends to zero.

Since
L_{t}
is a pure-jump Lévy martingale, we can also write

L_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y),\quad L_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}y\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y).

By abuse of language, we will refer to stochastic processes with infinite activity with (in)finite variation paths as
infinite activity and (in)finite variation Lévy processes
or
Lévy processes with infinite activity and (in)finite variation
. Regarding technical considerations on the volatility, first notice that when the volatility process is constant and there is no presence of jumps (i.e.
\sigma_{t}\equiv\sigma
and
\nu\equiv 0
) then we recover the classical Bachelier model.

2.1
Malliavin Calculus tools

In this section we will introduce the Malliavin calculus definitions and tools that are needed in order to obtain the key results of this paper. For a complete reference on the Malliavin calculus topics we refer the reader to
Nualart, (2006)
. Let
W=\{W_{t};t\in[0,T]\}
be a standard Brownian motion defined in a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. For a function
h\in L^{2}([0,T])
we define
W(h)
as the Wiener integral of
h
with respect to
W
, i.e.

W(h):=\int_{0}^{T}h(t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

We define
\mathcal{S}
as the class of random variables of
F
the form

F=f(W(h_{1}),\dots,W(h_{n}));\quad h_{i}\in L^{2}([0,T]),\quad f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We say that
\mathcal{S}
is the space of
simple functionals
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{S}
. We define the Malliavin derivative of
F
evaluated at a point
t\in[0,T]
as the stochastic process defined by

D_{t}F=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}f(W(h_{1}),\dots,W(h_{n}))h_{j}(t),\quad\partial_{j}f:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}.

As it has been proved in
Nualart, (2006)
, the operator
D
is closable from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
L^{p}(\Omega\times[0,T])
for all
p\geq 1
. We define
\mathbb{D}^{1,p}_{W}
as the closure of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to the norm

||F||_{1,p}=\left[\mathbb{E}(|F|^{p})+\mathbb{E}\left(||DF||_{L^{2}([0,T])}^{p}\right)\right]^{1/p}.

In the same way we have defined
D
, we can define the iterated derivatives
D^{n}
. We denote
\mathbb{D}^{n,p}_{W}
the space of random variables that are
n
times Malliavin differentiable and
F
, together with its Malliavin derivatives are
p
-integrable.

The adjoint of the Malliavin derivative is the so-called Skorohod integral or divergence operator
\delta
. It is an unbounded and closed operator from
L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
to
L^{2}(\Omega)
. We say that a stochastic process
u
belongs to the domain of
\delta
,
\operatorname{Dom}(\delta)
if there exists a unique element
\delta(u)
satisfying the duality relationship

\mathbb{E}[F\delta(u)]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}D_{t}F\cdot u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right],\quad\forall F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}.

The Skorohod integral
\delta
is an extension of the Itô integral (see
Nualart, (2006)
), in the sense that if
u
is a square-integrable adapted process then
\delta(u)
coincides with the Itô integral
\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
and
\delta(u)
is well defined for a larger class of processes (such as, for instance, square-integrable non-adapted processes). Therefore, all integrals of the form
\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
will be understood in the Skorohod sense. In order to get some insight of which processes belong to
\operatorname{Dom}(\delta)
, we introduce the following family of spaces.

Definition 2.2
.

We define
\mathbb{L}^{k,p}_{W}
as the space of stochastic processes
u
such that for almost every
t\in[0,T]
,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{D}^{k,p}_{W}
.

For all
k\geq 1
,
p\geq 2
the space
\mathbb{L}^{k,p}_{W}
is included in
\operatorname{Dom}(\delta)
. More explicitly, the following inclusions hold.

•

\mathbb{L}^{k,p}_{W}\subset\mathbb{L}^{k^{\prime},p}_{W}
if
k^{\prime}\leq k
.

•

\mathbb{L}^{k,p}_{W}\subset\mathbb{L}^{k,q}_{W}
if
p\geq q
.

•

\mathbb{L}^{k,p}_{W}\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\delta)
if
k\geq 1
,
p\geq 2
.

Again, for a proof of these results we refer the reader to
Nualart, (2006)
.

3
Main result

In this section we state the main result of this paper. This result describes the short-time behavior of the ATM-IV for the jump-diffusion stochastic volatility Bachelier model. In order to state the result, we need some generic technical hypotheses on the volatility process
\sigma
. The set of hypotheses that will be assumed along the paper are the following.

Hypothesis 1
.

There exist
0<\alpha<\beta
such that

\alpha\leq\sigma_{t}\leq\beta

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Hypothesis 2
.

For
p\geq 2
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{L}^{2,p}_{W}
.

Hypothesis 3
.

There exists
H\in(0,1)
and, for all
p\geq 1
there exist constants
\gamma,\delta>0
such that for almost every
0\leq r\leq s\leq u\leq T
and for almost every
t
,

E_{t}\left(|D_{r}\sigma_{u}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq\gamma(u-r)^{H-1/2}

and

E_{t}\left(|D_{s}D_{r}\sigma_{u}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq\delta(u-r)^{H-1/2}(u-s)^{H-1/2},

where
E_{t}(\cdot)
denotes the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}\in\mathbb{F}
.

Remark 3.1
.

The examples we consider in Section
7
don’t satisfy Hypothesis
1
. However, a classical truncation argument performed in the exact same way as in
Alòs et al., (2023)
and
Alòs and Shiraya, (2019)
shows that the main result of the paper (see Theorem
3.2
below) still holds for the considered examples.

Due to the assumption of
r=0
, we can compute the value of a European call option with strike
k
and maturity
T
at time
t
as
V_{t}=E_{t}[(S_{T}-k)_{+}]
. In the case of the classical Bachelier model (i.e.
\sigma_{t}\equiv\sigma
and no jumps) then
V_{t}
can be computed as follows:

V_{t}=\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,\sigma)=(S_{t}-k)\Phi(d(k,\sigma))+\phi(d(k,\sigma))\sigma\sqrt{T-t}

(3.1)

where
\Phi
denotes the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable,
\phi=\Phi^{\prime}
and

d(k,\sigma)=\frac{S_{t}-k}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}.

It is well known that the Bachelier function,
\operatorname{Bac}
, satisfies the following PDE

\mathcal{L}(\sigma)\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma):=\partial_{t}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)=0.

In order to introduce some notation, we define the following objects:

•

The future average volatility
v_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{Y_{t}}{T-t}}
with
Y_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s
.

•

The centered Gaussian kernel,
p(x,\tau)
, with variance
\tau^{2}
. If
\tau^{2}=1
we simply write
p(x)
.

From (
3.1
) we can derive that

\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)=\phi(d(k,\sigma))\sqrt{T-t}>0.

Hence, the function
\operatorname{Bac}
is invertible with respect to
\sigma
. This allows us to define the Bachelier implied volatility
I_{t}(k)
as the unique volatility parameter one should put in the Bachelier function in order to recover the market option price
V_{t}
, that is,

V_{t}=\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k)),\quad I_{t}(k)=\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k,V_{t}).

If
I_{t}(k)
is differentiable at least once with respect to the strike
k
, then Taylor’s formula allows us to approximate
I_{t}(k)
as

I_{t}(k)\approx I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})+\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})(k-k^{*}_{t}),\quad k_{t}^{*}=S_{t}.

Therefore, the terms
I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})
and
\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})
are key in order to get a linear approximation of the implied volatility surface.

In the present work, we apply Malliavin calculus techniques in order to understand the asymptotic behavior of these two terms as the maturity tends to zero depending on the regularity of the jumps. The main result of this paper is encapsulated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
S_{t}
follows the model (
2.1
) with
\sigma
satisfying Hypotheses
1
,
2
and
3
and let
L
an arbitrary pure-jump Lévy martingale. Let
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)
. Then,

\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\sigma_{t}.

(3.2)

If moreover, the sequence
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)
has a finite limit
c_{1}
as
\varepsilon
tends to zero, then

\lim_{T\to t}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{t}}+\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s

(3.3)

if
H\geq 1/2
and

\lim_{T\to t}(T-t)^{1/2-H}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{3/2+H}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s

(3.4)

if
H<1/2
.

Remark 3.3
.

If
L
is a pure-jump Lévy process with finite variation then the condition
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=c_{1}
is always satisfied since the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
ensures that the constant
c_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)
is well defined and therefore
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=c_{1}
. In the infinite variation case, this is no longer true. We will see in future sections that there exist some examples of infinite activity and infinite variation pure-jump Lévy processes for which the constants
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}
diverge as
\varepsilon\to 0
and, performing numerical simulations, we will see this effect in plots of the implied volatility.

Remark 3.4
.

Observe the following facts about Theorem
3.2
.

•

If we assume no jumps (i.e.
\nu\equiv 0
and therefore
c_{1}=0
) the result coincides with the short-time behavior studied in
Alòs et al., (2023)
.

•

The limit obtained in Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) is analogous to the findings in
Alòs et al., (2007)
,
Fukasawa, (2011)
and
Alòs et al., (2008)
where in this last reference the authors assume that the volatility process is also a Lévy process. The difference between Theorem
3.2
and the main result in
Alòs et al., (2007)
is the definition of the constant
c_{1}
. This difference comes from the fact that in
Alòs et al., (2007)
the authors work with a log-normal model while in this article we deal with a non log-normal model.

A study of the short-time behavior of the ATM-IV level and skew when the Lévy process has infinite activity and infinite variation paths has been addressed, for instance, in
Figueroa-López and Ólafsson, (2015)
,
Figueroa-López and Ólafsson, (2016)
and
Gerhold et al., (2016)
, where the authors consider different families of pure-jump Lévy processes with stochastic volatility driven by a Brownian motion and provide an expansion of the ATM-IV considering higher order terms. In the specific case where the process studied in
Figueroa-López and Ólafsson, (2016)
is a CGMY process (see
Rosiński, (2007)
or Section
7
of this paper), the ATM-IV skew expansion up to first order derived by the authors closely resembles Equation (
3.3
). Notably, the cited references primarily focus on log-normal models. In general, the literature about the short-time behavior of the Implied Volatility level and skew is not very extensive. In fact, the literature concerning the jump-diffusion stochastic volatility Bachelier model is even scarcer. In this paper we aim to fill the gap in the literature concerning the ATM-IV level and skew for a very important non log-normal model.

The steps in order to prove Theorem
3.2
are the following:

1.

First, we prove the first part of the theorem in the case where
L
is a compound Poisson process with drift following the same lines as in
Alòs et al., (2007)
and
Alòs et al., (2023)
.

2.

In order to extend the result to the most general case, we use an approximation argument, using the fact that the truncated Lévy process is a compound Poisson process.

3.

Finally, we do the same discussion for the skew using similar arguments.

4
A Hull-White type formula for call options

In order to analyze the asymptotic behavior of the ATM implied volatility level and skew, we will make use of a decomposition formula for the prices of European call options. The first thing we need to do in order to derive a decomposition formula is to provide an anticipating Itô formula for our model.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume
S
follows the model (
2.1
) with
\sigma
satisfying Hypotheses
1
,
2
and
3
. Let
F:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
a
\mathcal{C}^{1,2,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
class function such that there exists a positive constant
C
such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
F
and its partial derivatives evaluated in
(t,S_{t},Y_{t})
are bounded by
C
. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle F(t,S_{t},Y_{t})=

\displaystyle F(0,S_{0},Y_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{y}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r+\rho\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{xy}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})(D^{-}Y)_{r}\sigma_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{xx}^{2}F(r,X_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(F(r,S_{r-}+y,Y_{r})-F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})\right)\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}r,\,\mathrm{d}y),

where

\Delta_{yy}F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})=F(r,S_{r-}+y,Y_{r})-F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})-y\partial_{x}F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})

and, for a process of the form
Y_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}a_{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
where
a
is adapted and belongs to
\mathbb{L}^{1,2}
,
(D^{-}Y)_{t}:=\int_{t}^{T}D_{t}a_{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
. Thus, for the particular case
Y_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s
we have

(D^{-}Y)_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}D_{t}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Remark 4.2
.

An equivalent expression of this anticipating Itô formula is

\displaystyle F(t,S_{t},Y_{t})=

\displaystyle F(0,S_{0},Y_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{y}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r+\rho\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{xy}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{xx}^{2}F(r,X_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(F(r,S_{r-}+y,Y_{r})-F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})\right)\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}r,\,\mathrm{d}y),

where

\Lambda_{r}=\left(\int_{r}^{T}D_{r}\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\sigma_{r}.

Remark 4.3
.

The stock price
S
exhibits at most a countable number of jumps since
L
does so as well. Therefore, the set of times
r\in[0,T]
where
S_{r-}\neq S_{r}
has Lebesgue measure zero, so

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}F(r,S_{r},Y_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Proof.

Consider, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the process

S_{t}^{\varepsilon}=S_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r})+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|>\varepsilon}x\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}x).

Notice that this process has a finite number of jumps and converges to
S_{t}
in
L^{2}
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, we name
T_{i}^{\varepsilon}
the times where
S_{t}
exhibits a jump with the convention of
T_{0}^{\varepsilon}=0
. In the interval
[T_{i}^{\varepsilon},T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon})
,
S_{t}
evolves according to its continuous part
S_{t}^{\varepsilon,c}
, that is,

\,\mathrm{d}S_{t}^{\varepsilon,c}=\sigma_{t}\left(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right)-\left(\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Hence, by applying the anticipating Itô formula (see
Alòs et al., (2023)
,
Alòs and Garcia-Lorite, (2021)
or
Alòs et al., (2007)
) in the interval
[T_{i}^{\varepsilon},T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon})
we get

\displaystyle F(T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}-,S^{\varepsilon}_{T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}-},Y_{T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}})-F(T^{\varepsilon}_{i},S^{\varepsilon}_{T^{\varepsilon}_{i}},Y_{T^{\varepsilon}_{i}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{T^{\varepsilon}_{i}}^{T^{\varepsilon}_{i+1}-}\partial_{r}F(r,S^{\varepsilon}_{r},Y_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r+\int_{T^{\varepsilon}_{i}}^{T^{\varepsilon}_{i+1}-}\partial_{x}F(r,S^{\varepsilon}_{r},Y_{r})\,\mathrm{d}S_{r}^{\varepsilon,c}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{T^{\varepsilon}_{i}}^{T^{\varepsilon}_{i+1}-}\partial_{y}F(r,S^{\varepsilon}_{r},Y_{r})\sigma^{2}_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r+\rho\int_{T^{\varepsilon}_{i}}^{T^{\varepsilon}_{i+1}-}\partial_{y}F(r,S^{\varepsilon}_{r},Y_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{T^{\varepsilon}_{i}}^{T^{\varepsilon}_{i+1}-}\partial^{2}_{xx}F(r,S^{\varepsilon}_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r.

Now, if a jump occurs at time
T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}
, its contribution is

F(T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon},S^{\varepsilon}_{T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}},Y_{T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}})-F(T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon},S^{\varepsilon}_{T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}-},Y_{T_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}}).

Hence, we can write

\displaystyle F(t,S^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y_{t})=

\displaystyle F(0,S_{0},Y_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}F(r,S^{\varepsilon}_{r},Y_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}F(r,S^{\varepsilon}_{r},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}\partial_{x}F(r,S_{r}^{\varepsilon},Y_{r})y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{y}F(r,S_{r}^{\varepsilon},Y_{r})\sigma^{2}_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r+\rho\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{xy}F(r,S_{r}^{\varepsilon},Y_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{xx}F(r,S_{r}^{\varepsilon},Y_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\left[F(T_{i}^{\varepsilon},S^{\varepsilon}_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}},Y_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}})-F(T_{i}^{\varepsilon},S^{\varepsilon}_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}-},Y_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}})\right].

Now, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\left[F(T_{i}^{\varepsilon},S^{\varepsilon}_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}},Y_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}})-F(T_{i}^{\varepsilon},S^{\varepsilon}_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}-},Y_{T_{i}^{\varepsilon}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}\left(F(r,S_{r-}+y,Y_{r})-F(r,S_{r-},Y_{r})\right)N(\,\mathrm{d}y,\,\mathrm{d}r).

Taking into account that
\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y)=N(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}y)-\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}s
and taking
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
we conclude the desired result. The details are omited since the proof follows the same scheme as in
Jafari and Vives, (2013)
.
∎

The following lemma will be useful in order to justify that the integrals in the upcoming results are well defined, and will also be useful in the future to have an idea of the asymptotic behavior of the terms appearing in the decomposition formula.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume
S
follows the model (
2.1
) with
-1<\rho<1
. Let
0\leq t\leq r\leq T
, let
h\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}_{t}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W}\vee\mathcal{F}^{L}_{T}
. Then, there exists a constant
C=C(n,\rho)
such that

\left|E\left(\partial^{n}_{x}G(T,r,S_{r}+h,k,v_{r})|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)\right|\leq C\left(\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}(n+1)}.

where
G(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t}):=\partial^{2}_{xx}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})
.

Proof.

Recall that

\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,k,x,\sigma)=(x-k)\Phi(d(k,\sigma))+\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,\sigma))\sigma\sqrt{T-t},

where

d(k,\sigma)=\frac{x-k}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}.

A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,k,x,\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(d(k,\sigma))+(x-k)\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,\sigma))\partial_{x}d(k,\sigma)+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(d(k,\sigma))\partial_{x}d(k,\sigma)\sigma\sqrt{T-t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(d(k,\sigma))+\frac{x-k}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,\sigma))+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(d(k,\sigma))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(d(k,\sigma))+d(k,\sigma)\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,\sigma))-d(k,\sigma)\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,\sigma))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(d(k,\sigma)).

Hence,

G(T,t,x,k,\sigma)=\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,\sigma))}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}.

This implies that

E\left(\partial^{n}_{x}G(T,r,S_{r}+h,k,v_{r})|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)=(-1)^{n}\partial^{n}_{k}E\left(p(S_{r}+h-k,v_{r}\sqrt{T-r})|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right).

Now, the conditional expectation of
S_{r}+h
given
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is a normal random variable with mean equal to

S_{t}+\rho\int_{t}^{r}\rho\sigma_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}W_{\theta}+(L_{r}-L_{t})+h=:\phi+h

and variance equal to

(1-\rho^{2})\int_{t}^{r}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

This implies that

\displaystyle E\left(p(S_{r}+h-k,v_{r}\sqrt{T-r})|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(y+h-k,v_{r}\sqrt{T-r})p\left(y-\phi-h,\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})\int_{t}^{r}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\left(\phi-k+2h,\sqrt{\int_{r}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta+(1-\rho^{2})\int_{t}^{r}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\left(\phi-k+2h,\sqrt{(1-\rho)^{2}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\rho^{2}\int_{r}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\right).

Finally, taking into account that
-1<\rho<1
we find that

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{n}_{k}p\left(\phi-k+2h,\sqrt{(1-\rho)^{2}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\rho^{2}\int_{r}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left((1-\rho)^{2}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\rho^{2}\int_{r}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}(n+1)}\leq C\left(\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}_{,}

concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Notice that the result still holds if
h=h_{r}
is a stochastic process
\mathbb{F}
-adapted such that
|h_{r}|\leq M
uniformly in
r
. Indeed, in this situation, due to the smoothness of the functions
\partial^{n}_{x}G
and the fact that they are bounded in
x
we know that

\displaystyle|E\left(\partial_{x}^{n}G(T,r,S_{r}+h_{r},k,v_{r})|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)|\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in[-M,M]}|E\left(\partial_{x}^{n}G(T,r,S_{r}+h,k,v_{r})|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)|

and we can apply now Lemma
4.4
to deduce that the result holds. This remark generalizes a bit the similar results found in
Alòs et al., (2007)
and
Alòs and Garcia-Lorite, (2021)
, for instance.

We are now ready to state and proof the Hull-White formula for Model (
2.1
).

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume
S
follows model (
2.1
) with
\rho\in(-1,1)
and
\sigma
satisfying Hypotheses (
1
) – (
3
). Then the value of a call option at time
t
,
V_{t}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle V_{t}=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}H(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right).

where
G=\partial^{2}_{xx}\operatorname{Bac}
,
H=\partial_{x}G
and
\Lambda_{r}=\sigma_{r}\int_{r}^{T}D_{r}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
.

Proof.

We use the anticipating Itô formula to
\operatorname{Bac}(T,T,S_{T},k,v_{T})
to get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}(T,T,S_{T},k,v_{T})=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{r}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\frac{v_{r}^{2}-\sigma_{r}^{2}}{2v_{r}(T-r)}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\rho\int_{t}^{T}\partial^{2}_{x\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\frac{\Lambda_{r}}{2v_{r}(T-r)}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{T}\partial^{2}_{xx}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},v_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},v_{r})\sigma_{r}\left(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r}\right).

We now want to arrange the terms. Using the fact that

\left(\partial_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}\right)\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)=0,

and

\partial^{2}_{xx}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)=\frac{\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)}{\sigma(T-t)}

we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}(T,T,S_{T},k,v_{T})=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}\int_{t}^{T}\partial^{3}_{xxx}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},v_{r})\sigma_{r}\left(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r}\right).

Taking conditional expectations to both sides and using that the stochastic integrals have zero expectation we conclude that

\displaystyle V_{t}=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}(T,T,S_{T},k,v_{T})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}H(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right).

The second term in the right-hand-side of the previous formula is finite thanks to Lemma
4.4
. In order to justify that the third term is well defined, we will follow closely the ideas of
Jafari and Vives, (2013)
. We split the integral in the sum of the integrals over the sets
|y|\geq 1
and
|y|<1
respectively, arguing in each case that the integrals are well defined. Regarding the integral when
|y|\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|\geq 1}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-}+y,k,v_{r})-\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})-y\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\right).

On the one hand we have
|\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)|\leq 1
, so
|\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})y|\leq|y|
. Using the mean value theorem on the first two terms, we see that there exists a random point
\xi
with
|\xi|\leq y
such that

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|\geq 1}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-}+y,k,v_{r})-\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})-y\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|\geq 1}[\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-}+\xi,k,v_{r})-\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})]y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right).

Since

\int_{|y|\geq 1}|y|\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty,

we have that this term is well defined. Regarding the integral when
|y|<1
we proceed as with
|y|\geq 1
, but we apply the mean value theorem one more time to get

E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|<1}G(T,r,S_{r}+\xi^{\prime},k,v_{r})y^{2}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right).

In order to justify that this term is well defined, we apply the tower property of the conditional expectation to get

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|<1}G(T,r,S_{r}+\xi^{\prime},k,v_{r})y^{2}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(E\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|<1}G(T,r,S_{r}+\xi^{\prime},k,v_{r})y^{2}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\bigg|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|<1}E\left(G(T,r,S_{r}+\xi^{\prime},k,v_{r})\big|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)y^{2}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

Finally, applying Lemma
4.4
together with the fact that

\int_{|y|<1}y^{2}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)

we deduce that the term is well defined, concluding the proof.
∎

5
Short-time behavior of the ATM Implied Volatility level

The objective of this section is to study the short-time behavior of the ATM implied volatility. In other words, if we set
k_{t}^{*}=S_{t}
we want to study the asymptotic behavior of
I_{t}(k^{*})
as
T\to t
. As it was outlined in the previous sections, we will first consider the case where
L
is a compound Poisson process with the drift that makes
L
a martingale and then we will argue the other Lévy cases via an approximation argument. Since the result for the compound Poisson process can’t be derived directly for every
\rho
, we will first prove a result for the uncorrelated case and then we will use this result to prove the correlated case.

5.1
The uncorrelated case

Our first analysis is focused on the case where
S
follows Equation (
2.1
) with
\rho=0
. We denote by
I_{t}^{0}(k)
the implied volatility in this case. In this subsection, we prove the following result concerning the asymptotic behavior of the ATM implied volatility level as
T\to t
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
S
follow the model (
2.1
) with
\rho=0
and let
L
be a compound Poisson process with the drift term that makes it a martingale. Then,

\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}^{0}(k^{*}_{t})=\sigma_{t}.

(5.1)

Proof.

Recall that the ATM-IV is defined via the equivalent relationships

V_{t}=\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},I^{0}_{t}(k_{t}^{*})),\quad I_{t}^{0}(k_{t}^{*})=\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}).

The Hull-White formula derived in Section
4
applied for the uncorrelated case (
\rho=0
) tells us that

\displaystyle V_{t}=

\displaystyle E_{t}(\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},v_{t}))+E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)_{k=k^{*}_{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}[T_{1}]+E_{t}[T_{2}].

Hence,

\displaystyle I_{t}^{0}(k^{*}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},E_{t}[T_{1}+T_{2}])\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},E_{t}[T_{1}+T_{2}])-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1}+T_{2})+\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1}+T_{2})\right).

We will analyze first the last term. Observe that we can write

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1}+T_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1}+T_{2})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1})\right)+\mathbb{E}(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1})).

Using the mean value theorem, we observe that

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1}+T_{2})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1})\right)=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\xi)T_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt{T-t}}T_{2}\right).

Now, in order to analyze
T_{2}
, we resort to the mean value theorem in order to claim that exists
\xi_{r}
such that

\displaystyle T_{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,\xi_{r},k,v_{r})-\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r}))y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Using now the facts that
|\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,\sigma)|\leq 1
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
we deduce that

|T_{2}|\leq 2(T-t)||y||_{L^{1}(\nu)}

and therefore,

E_{t}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt{T-t}}T_{2}\right)\to 0,\text{ as }T\to t.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle E_{t}(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1}))=

\displaystyle E_{t}(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},v_{t}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}(v_{t})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\sigma_{t},\text{ as }T\to t.

Finally, using the same arguments as in
Alòs et al., (2023)
for the term

E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},E_{t}[T_{1}+T_{2}])-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},T_{1}+T_{2})\right)

we see that this term goes to zero as
T\to t
, concluding therefore that the identity (
5.1
) holds.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Regarding the properties of
L
, we have relied on the fact that
||y||_{L^{1}(\nu)}<\infty
. Since this hypothesis is also satisfied for the infinite activity and finite variation case, we could have assumed that
L
has infinite activity and finite variation and the result would be the same.

5.1.1
Extending the uncorrelated case to a general pure-jump Lévy process

In order to extend the result to the general pure-jump Lévy case we use an approximation argument with some suitable approximators. Let
S
follow the model (
2.1
) and consider
(a,0,\nu)
the Lévy triplet of
L
. We construct these approximators as follows. Consider
0<\varepsilon<1
. Let
\tilde{S}^{\varepsilon}
be the process resulting of considering the model (
2.1
) with Lévy process
L^{\varepsilon}
defined as the Lévy process with triplet
(a,0,\nu_{\varepsilon})
, where

\nu_{\varepsilon}(\,\mathrm{d}x)=\mathbf{1}_{|x|>\varepsilon}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x).

Notice that, by construction,
L_{t}^{\varepsilon}
has a finite amount of jumps and the magnitude of the jumps is greater than
\varepsilon
. Moreover, from the fact that
L_{t}^{\varepsilon}
converges to
L_{t}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly on
t
, we can assume (by replacing
\varepsilon
by a subsequence if needed) that
|L_{t}^{\varepsilon}-L_{t}|<\varepsilon/2
. We denote by
\tilde{S}^{\varepsilon}
the stock price process that follows the same dynamics as in Equation (
2.1
) with Lévy martingale
L^{\varepsilon}
. The sequence of approximators we will use is

S_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\tilde{S}_{t}^{\varepsilon}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}.

It is easy to see that
S_{t}^{\varepsilon}\to S_{t}
a.s. and in
L^{2}
uniformly on
t
. Moreover,

S_{t}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}=\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}+L_{t}^{\varepsilon}-L_{t}\geq\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon>0.

We now want to proof that if
S
follows the model (
2.1
) with
\rho=0
and
L
being an arbitrary Lévy process with measure
\nu
, then the following result holds.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
S
follow the model (
2.1
) with
\rho=0
and
L
being a general pure-jump Lévy martingale. Then,

\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}^{0}(k^{*}_{t})=\sigma_{t}.

In order to prove the result, we will use a lemma that will help to get to the conclusion via an approximation argument. Consider
S_{t}^{\varepsilon}
the sequence of approximators defined as above. The result that we will use in order to prove Proposition
5.3
is the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
I_{t}^{0}(k_{t}^{*})
be the ATM implied volatility for the price model driven by
S
and let
I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{\varepsilon,*})
be the ATM implied volatility for the price model driven by
S^{\varepsilon}
. Then,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{\varepsilon,*})=I_{t}^{0}(k_{t}^{*})

uniformly in
T-t
.

Proof.

Recall that if
V_{t}^{\varepsilon}
and
V_{t}
stand for the price of a call option with strikes
k_{t}^{*.\varepsilon}=S_{t}^{\varepsilon}
and
k^{*}_{t}=S_{t}
under the stock models
S^{\varepsilon}
and
S
respectively then

I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})=\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon}),\quad I_{t}^{0}(k^{*})=\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},V_{t}).

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})-I_{t}^{0}(k_{t}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-I_{t}^{0}(k_{t}^{*}).

First, notice that by the mean value theorem

I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})=\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon}-k_{t}^{*})

where

S_{t}=k_{t}^{*}\leq\xi\leq k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon}=S_{t}^{\varepsilon}.

(5.2)

On the one hand,

\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})=\frac{-1}{\Phi(d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})))}.

Then, since we got

\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,x,k,*):(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)

whenever
x>k
, we have that

\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})>0

This, together with the fact that (
5.2
) implies
S_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\xi\geq 0
leads us to

|I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})|\leq\frac{1}{\inf_{z\geq 0}\Phi(z)}|k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon}-k_{t}^{*}|.

On the other hand, using that
k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon}=S_{t}^{\varepsilon}
and
k_{t}^{*}=S_{t}
we see that
|I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})|
tends to zero uniformly on
T-t
. We now have to deal with
\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-I_{t}^{0}(k^{*}_{t})
. We can write

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-I_{t}^{0}(k^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k^{*}_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},V_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k^{*}_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},V_{t})+\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},k^{*}_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})\right)+\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},V_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{1}+A_{2}.

Let’s analyze first
A_{1}
. Using the mean value theorem on the third variable we rewrite the first term as

A_{1}=\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,\xi,k_{t}^{*},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})(S_{t}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}).

In the same way as we discussed before, we deduce that
|A_{1}|\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly on
T-t
. Regarding
A_{2}
, by means of the mean value theorem we know that there exists a mid point
\xi_{\varepsilon}
between
V_{t}
and
V_{t}^{\varepsilon}
such that

A_{2}=\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\xi_{\varepsilon})(V_{t}^{\varepsilon}-V_{t}).

Hence,

A_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt{T-t}}(V_{t}^{\varepsilon}-V_{t}).

Observe that

\displaystyle|V_{t}-V_{t}^{\varepsilon}|=|E_{t}[(S_{T}-S_{t})_{+}]-E_{t}[(S_{T}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{+}]|.

Since the function
(\cdot)_{+}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
1
, we can rewrite the last expression as

|V_{t}-V_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq E_{t}\left(|(S_{T}-S_{t})-(S_{T}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}^{\varepsilon})|\right),

from which we deduce that

\displaystyle|V_{t}-V_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(|(S_{T}-S_{t})-(S_{T}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}^{\varepsilon})|\right)\leq E_{t}\left(\left|\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|x|<\varepsilon}x\tilde{N}(\,\mathrm{d}s,\,\mathrm{d}x)\right|\right).

Now, we use the isometry property of the compensated Poisson integral and the fact that it is a martingale with independent increments we have

E_{t}\left[\left|\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|x|<\varepsilon}x\tilde{N}(ds,dx)\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{(T-t)\int_{|x|<\varepsilon}x^{2}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x)}.

This implies that
|A_{2}|\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly on
T-t
.
∎

Using this lemma we can prove the desired result for a general pure-jump Lévy process.

Proof (Proposition
5.3
).

Since the jumps of the approximator
S^{\varepsilon}
are modeled with a compound Poisson process with drift, we can rely on Proposition
5.1
to say that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{T\to t}I^{0,\varepsilon}_{t}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})=\sigma_{t}.

Moreover, Lemma
5.4
gives us that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I^{0,\varepsilon}_{t}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})=I_{t}^{0}(k_{t}^{*})

uniformly on
T-t
. Hence, by the Moore-Osgood theorem we can switch limits and conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}^{0}(k^{*})=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to t}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}^{0,\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}.

∎

With this result, we can now attack the correlated case.

5.2
The correlated case

In the same way as we have proved the uncorrelated case, we can prove that the ATM implied volatility for the correlated satisfies the same asymptotic behavior. The result stating this conclusion is the following.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
S
follow the model (
2.1
) with
L
an arbitrary pure-jump Lévy martingale. Then,

\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\sigma_{t}.

Proof.

We use the same strategy as with the uncorrelated case. We will deduce the result for the case where
L_{t}=-c_{1}t+J_{t}
with
J
a compound Poisson process since the approximation argument performed in the correlated case is identical as with the correlated case and therefore we skip it for the sake of conciseness. The Hull-White formula tells us that

\displaystyle V_{t}=

\displaystyle E_{t}(\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},v_{t}))+\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}H(T,r,S_{r},k^{*}_{t},v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k_{t}^{*},v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right].

Hence, if we let

\Gamma_{t,s}:=E_{t}(\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},v_{t})))+\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{s}H(T,r,S_{r},k^{*}_{t},v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right]

and

A_{t,T}:=E_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k_{t}^{*},v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right]

then we have

\displaystyle I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},V_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T}+A_{t,T})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T}+A_{t,T})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T})+\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T})\right).

Now, using the mean value theorem, we have that there exists
\xi
with

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T}+A_{t,T})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T})\right)

\displaystyle=E_{t}\left(\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},S_{t},\xi)A_{t,T}\right)

which, using the same argument as with the uncorrelated case, tends to zero as
T
tends to
t
. For the remaining term we have

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,t})+\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,t})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{t}^{0}(k^{*}_{t})+E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,t})\right)

because
\Gamma_{t,t}
corresponds to the price of a call option in the uncorrelated case. Using the classical Itô formula, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,T})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,t})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{t,r})H(T,r,S_{r},k^{*}_{t},v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right).

Using the same argument as in
Alòs and Garcia-Lorite, (2021)
and
Alòs et al., (2023)
we can conclude that

E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},\Gamma_{r})H(T,r,S_{r},k^{*},v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\to 0,\quad\text{ as }T\to t.

This implies that

\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}^{0}(k_{t}^{*})=\sigma_{t}.

As mentioned at the beginning of the proof, the extension to the general Lévy case is proved using the same approximation argument as with the uncorrelated case.
∎

Notice that Proposition
5.5
indeed proves the first part of Theorem
3.2
.

6
Short-Time behavior of the ATM Implied Volatility Skew

The objective now is to give an expression for the derivative with respect to the strike of the implied volatility under the Bachelier model in the at-the-money scenario, that is, when the strike equals the price of the stock at time
t
(i.e.
k^{*}_{t}=S_{t}
). The first technical result that we need in order to understand the behavior of the skew as
T\to t
is the following.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume
S
follows the model (
2.1
) with
\sigma
satisfying Hypotheses
1
,
2
and
3
. Then,

\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\frac{E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)_{|k=k_{t}^{*}}}{\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},I_{t}(k^{*}_{t}))}

where

\displaystyle F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}H(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y).

Proof.

Since
V_{t}=\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k))
, we can take partial derivatives with respect to
k
in both sides of the equation to get

\partial_{k}V_{t}(k)=\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k))+\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k))\partial_{k}I_{t}(k).

(6.1)

Furthermore, thanks to the Hull and White formula, we can write

V_{t}(k)=E_{t}(\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})+E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

where

\displaystyle F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}H(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y).

This readily implies that

\partial_{k}V_{t}(k)=E_{t}(\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t}))+E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right).

We have to check that the two terms in the right hand side are well defined. Notice that we have the following relationship:

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Phi(d(k,v_{t}))+(S_{t}-k)\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,v_{t}))\partial_{k}d(k,v_{t})+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(d(k,v_{t}))\partial_{k}d(k,v_{t})v_{t}\sqrt{T-t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Phi(d(k,v_{t}))-\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,v_{t}))d(k,v_{t})-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(d(k,v_{t}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Phi(d(k,v_{t}))-\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,v_{t}))d(k,v_{t})+d(k,v_{t})\Phi^{\prime}(d(k,v_{t}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Phi(d(k,v_{t}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t}).

Since we know that
E_{t}(\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t}))
is well defined, we deduce that
E_{t}(\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t}))
is also well defined. This relationship also implies that

\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})=-\partial_{x}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})

so
E_{t}(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r)
is well defined if and only if
E_{t}(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r)
is well defined. Differentiating
F
with respect to
x
we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{x}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}\partial_{x}H(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y).

Using Lemma
4.4
and similar arguments as in the proof of the Hull-White formula we can show that

E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}H(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

is well defined and finite a.s. We shall now use similar arguments as in the proof of the Hull and White formula to conclude that the last term is well defined. For the term concerning
\nu
, we split the integral when
|y|\geq 1
and when
|y|<1
. If
|y|\geq 1
we can apply the mean value theorem to get

\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-}+y,k,v_{r})-\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})=G(T,r,S_{r-}+\alpha y,k,v_{r})y\quad\text{for some }\alpha\in[0,1].

Hence, applying the tower property of the conditional expectation and Lemma
4.4
to

E\left(G(T,r,S_{r-}+\alpha y,k,v_{r}|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad E\left(G(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r}|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)

we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|\geq 1}\Big[\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-}+y,k,v_{r})\right.

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})-yG(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu\Big](\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2C(T-t)\left(\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{-1/2}E_{t}\left(\int_{|y|\geq 1}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\right).

Using now the Hypothesis
(
3
)
on
\sigma
we conclude that there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that

2C(T-t)\left(\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{-1/2}E_{t}\left(\int_{|y|\geq 1}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)\leq 2C^{\prime}(T-t)^{1/2}\int_{|y|\geq 1}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)

which is well defined because
\int_{|y|\geq 1}|y|\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
. For the second term, we use the mean value theorem twice and Lemma
4.4
to get that

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{|y|<1}\Big[\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-}+y,k,v_{r})\right.

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})-yG(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu\Big](\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2CE_{t}\left(\int_{|y|<1}y^{2}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\right).

Since all terms are well defined we find that the volatility skew satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{k}I_{t}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{k}V_{t}(k)-\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k))}{\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{t}(\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t}))+E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)-\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k))}{\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k,I_{t}(k))}.

Finally, using the fact that

\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},v_{t})=-\frac{1}{2}

we have that, the ATM-IV skew satisfies

\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\frac{E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)_{|k=k_{t}^{*}}}{\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},I_{t}(k^{*}_{t}))}

as desired.
∎

Thanks to this proposition, we will derive the asymptotic behavior of the skew in the case where the driving Lévy process is a compound Poisson process and we will be able to prove Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) for this simplified scenario. In order to conclude the result for general pure-jump Lévy processes we will use an approximation argument similar as the one performed for the ATM-IV level.

6.1
The compound Poisson case

Since the anticipating Itô formula and the Hull-White formula were made for a general Lévy process, making extra assumptions on
L_{t}
will simplify some formulas. Let’s look at the case where
L_{t}
is a compound Poisson process, that is,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)=\nu(\mathbb{R})=\lambda<\infty
. Moreover, the constants

c_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)

are finite. Notice that, in the compound Poisson process case, the terms concerning the Lévy measure can be separated. Indeed,

E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{yy}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

can be rearranged to get

-c_{1}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)+E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{y}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

where
\Delta_{y}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})=\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-}+y,k,v_{r})-\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})
. Hence, by Proposition
6.1
we can write
\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})
as

\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=\frac{E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k_{t}^{*},v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)}{\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},v_{t})}

and therefore, the relationship

\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},v_{t})\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k_{t}^{*},v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

holds. Since we are in the compound Poisson case, we can rewrite
E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k_{t}^{*},v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)
as

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{k}F(T,r,S_{r},k_{t}^{*},v_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)=-

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}H(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\right)_{|k=k_{t}^{*}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{y}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)_{|k=k_{t}^{*}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle c_{1}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}G(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})y\,\mathrm{d}r\right)_{|k=k_{t}^{*}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-T_{1}-T_{2}+T_{3}.

This allows us to deduce the following proposition.

Proposition 6.2
.

Consider
S
following the model (
2.1
) with
L
a compound Poisson process. Then, the skew satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},v_{t})\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=-

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}(\partial_{x}H(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},v_{t})J_{t})+c_{1}E_{t}(G(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},v_{t})(T-t))

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle O(T-t)^{\min(2H,1)}.

Proof.

The proof of this result follows the same ideas as in
Alòs et al., (2007)
. The strategy consists in analyzing the three terms
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
.

Step 1:
The idea to analyze
T_{1}
is applying the anticipating Itô formula (Proposition
4.1
) to
\frac{\rho}{2}\partial_{k}H(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})\int_{t}^{T}\Lambda_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s
. In order to make the notation easier, we define
J_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r
. We will apply the Itô formula to the process
\mathcal{H}(t,S_{t},k,v_{t})J_{t}
where
\mathcal{H}=\partial_{x}H
and then we will deduce the result multiplying everything by
\rho/2
and changing the sign. We have

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\mathcal{H}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\mathcal{H}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})J_{t}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial^{3}_{xxx}\mathcal{H}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})J_{r}\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\rho E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}\mathcal{H}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})(D^{-}J)_{r}\sigma_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{y}\mathcal{H}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})J_{r}\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle c_{1}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial_{x}\mathcal{H}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})J_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\mathcal{H}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})J_{t}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{4}A_{j}.

Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}|\Lambda_{r}|\,\mathrm{d}r\leq

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}|\sigma_{r}|\int_{r}^{T}2|D_{r}\sigma_{u}|\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(T-t)\int_{r}^{T}|D_{r}\sigma_{u}|\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(T-t)\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{r}^{T}(D_{r}\sigma_{u})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}r\right)^{1/2}.

Moreover, using Hölder’s inequality, we have that

\displaystyle|J_{r}|\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{r}^{T}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\left(\int_{r}^{T}\int_{u}^{T}(D_{u}\sigma_{\theta})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2C(T-r)\left(\int_{r}^{T}\int_{u}^{T}(D_{u}\sigma_{\theta})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{1/2}.

Now, using that
|E(\partial_{xxx}^{3}\mathcal{H}(r,S_{r},k,v_{r})|\mathcal{G}_{t})|\leq C(T-t)^{-3}
we have that

\displaystyle|E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\partial^{3}_{xxx}\mathcal{H}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})J_{r}\Lambda_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{T-t}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\int_{r}^{T}(D_{r}\sigma_{\theta})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(T-t)^{2H},

so
A_{1}=O(T-t)^{2H}
. Let’s deal now with
A_{2}
. We have
(D^{-}J)_{r}=\int_{r}^{T}D_{r}\Lambda_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u
. This implies that

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{T}D_{r}\Lambda_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u=

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{T}D_{r}\left(\sigma_{u}\int_{u}^{T}D_{u}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{T}\left[D_{r}\sigma_{u}\int_{u}^{T}D_{u}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\sigma_{u}\int_{u}^{T}D_{r}D_{u}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right]\,\mathrm{d}u.

Now,

D_{r}D_{u}\sigma^{2}_{\theta}=2D_{r}(\sigma_{u}D_{u}\sigma_{\theta})=2(D_{r}\sigma_{u}D_{u}\sigma_{\theta}+\sigma_{u}D_{r}D_{u}\sigma_{\theta}).

Hence,

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\sigma_{u}\int_{u}^{T}D_{r}D_{u}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{u}^{T}(\theta-r)^{H-1/2}(\theta-u)^{H-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(T-r)^{2H}

and

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(D_{r}\sigma_{u}\int_{u}^{T}D_{u}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\leq

\displaystyle CE_{t}\left(D_{r}\sigma_{u}\int_{u}^{T}D_{u}\sigma_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(u-r)^{H-1/2}\int_{u}^{T}(\theta-u)^{H-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(u-r)^{H-1/2}(T-u)^{H+1/2}.

This implies that

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{r}^{T}D_{r}\Lambda_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{r}^{T}(T-r)^{2H}+(u-r)^{H-1/2}(T-u)^{H+1/2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(T-r)^{2H+1}+\int_{r}^{T}(u-r)^{H-1/2}(T-u)^{H+1/2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(T-r)^{2H+1}.

Finally, using again Lemma
4.4
in
A_{2}
we have

\displaystyle A_{2}=

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}E\left[\partial^{3}_{xxx}G(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})|\mathcal{G}_{r}\right](D^{-}J)_{r}\sigma_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CE_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\left(\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)^{-2}\left(\int_{r}^{T}D_{r}\Lambda_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(T-t)^{-2}\int_{t}^{T}C(T-r)^{2H+1}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(T-t)^{2H}.

For
A_{3}
, we use that
|E\left(\Delta_{y}\mathcal{H}(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right)|\leq 2C(T-t)^{-3/2}
and the fact that
\nu(\mathbb{R})<\infty
to conclude that

A_{3}\leq C(T-t)^{-3/2}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}J_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)=O(T-t)^{H+1}.

Since
H>0
, this also implies that

A_{3}=O(T-t).

Regarding
A_{4}
, we can use similar arguments to derive that

A_{4}\leq C(T-t)^{-2}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{T}J_{r}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)=O(T-t)^{H+1/2}.

Now, since for
H>1/2
we have
H+1/2>1
we have

A_{4}=O(T-t)

if
H>1/2
. If
H<1/2
then
H+1/2>2H
, so

A_{4}=O(T-t)^{2H}

whenever
H<1/2
. Therefore,
A_{4}=O(T-t)^{\min(2H,1)}
. Plugging the asymptotics of the terms
A_{j}
,
j=1,2,3,4
together we conclude that

T_{1}=E_{t}\left(\mathcal{H}(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})J_{t}\right)+O(T-t)^{\min(2H,1)}.

Step 2:
Using that
|\Delta_{y}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Bac}(T,r,S_{r-},k,v_{r})|\leq 2
and
\nu(\mathbb{R})<\infty
we can easily see that
T_{2}=O(T-t)
.

Step 3:
Regarding
T_{3}
, we use the anticipating Itô formula to
G(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})
to deduce that

\displaystyle G(T,r,S_{r},k,v_{r})=

\displaystyle G(T,t,S_{t},k,v_{t})+\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{r}\partial^{3}_{xxx}G(T,u,S_{u},k,v_{u})\Lambda_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle c_{1}E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{r}\partial_{x}G(T,u,S_{u},k,v_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E_{t}\left(\int_{t}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{y}G(T,u,S_{u-},k,v_{u})\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}u\right).

Now, using Lemma
4.4
in all terms together with the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
and integrating from
t
to
T
with respect to
r
we find that

T_{3}=c_{1}E_{t}(G(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},v_{t})(T-t))+O(T-t)^{\min(2H,1)}.

Joining the estimates for
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
we obtain the desired result.
∎

Notice that when we are at the ATM case (i.e.
k=k_{t}^{*}=S_{t}
) we can write the Greeks of the Bachelier function as follows:

\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},I_{t}(k^{*}_{t}))=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt{T-t}},

G(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},v_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{v_{t}\sqrt{T-t}},

and

\partial_{k}H(T,t,S_{t},k^{*}_{t},v_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{v_{t}^{3}(T-t)^{3/2}}.

These explicit expressions of the Greeks are very helpful in order to prove the asymptotic of the skew when the Lévy process is a compound Poisson process with drift.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
S
be a stock price with dynamics following model (
2.1
) with
L
a compound Poisson process. Then,

•

If
H\geq 1/2
, then

\lim_{T\to t}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{t}}+\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s.

•

If
H<1/2
, then

\lim_{T\to t}(T-t)^{1/2-H}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{3/2+H}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Using the previous computations of the Greeks, we can write the ATM implied volatility skew as

\displaystyle\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}E_{t}\left[\frac{1}{v_{t}^{3}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\Lambda_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]+c_{1}E_{t}\left[\frac{1}{v_{t}}\right]+O(T-t)^{\min(2H,1)-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{1}+B_{2}+O(T-t)^{\min(2H,1)-1/2}.

Let’s first consider the case where
H\geq 1/2
so that
\min(2H,1)=1
. On the one hand, since
\lim_{T\to t}v_{t}=\sigma_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
is bounded from below uniformly in
t
we have that, due to the dominated convergence theorem,

\lim_{T\to t}c_{1}E_{t}\left[\frac{1}{v_{t}}\right]=\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{t}}.

On the other hand

B_{1}=\frac{\rho}{(T-t)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[v_{t}^{-3}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}\int_{s}^{T}\sigma_{u}D_{s}\sigma_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s\right].

Then, using the dominated convergence theorem again, we find that

\lim_{T\to t}B_{1}=\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s.

Hence,

\lim_{T\to t}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{t}}+\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s,

where this last limit can be computed explicitly depending on the model.

Let’s consider now the case where
H<1/2
. In this case,
O(T-t)^{\min(2H,1)}=O(T-t)^{2H}
we now aim to proceed in a similar way, but we have to take into account that
2H-1/2
is not positive for all
H
. Indeed, if
H<1/4
, then
2H<1/2
. However, since
H>0
, we can use the fact that
(1/2-H)+(2H-1/2)>0
for all
H>0
to conclude that

\lim_{T\to t}(T-t)^{1/2-H}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{3/2+H}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s.

∎

Again, notice that Proposition
6.3
proves
Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) in Theorem
3.2
for the compound Poisson case.

6.2
Extension to more general Lévy processes

Let
S
follow the dynamics given by Equation (
2.1
) and let
S^{\varepsilon}
be the approximator of
S
introduced in Section
5
. Then, the dynamics of
S^{\varepsilon}
follow Equation
(
2.1
)
as well with Lévy process
L^{\varepsilon}
. Recall that
L^{\varepsilon}
is a compound Poisson process with drift and, moreover,
S_{t}^{\varepsilon}\to S_{t}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and a.s. uniformly in
t
in compact sets. We denote by
I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})
the ATM implied volatility for
S
and
I^{\varepsilon}_{t}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})
the ATM implied volatility for
S^{\varepsilon}
. Notice that Proposition
6.3
applies for
S^{\varepsilon}
and, in particular, we know that

\lim_{T\to t}\partial_{k}I^{\varepsilon}_{t}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})=\frac{c_{1}^{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{t}}+\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s

if
H\geq 1/2
and

\lim_{T\to t}(T-t)^{1/2-H}\partial_{k}I^{\varepsilon}_{t}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})=\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{3/2-H}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s

if
H<1/2
, where
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)
. This section then consists in proving a lemma that allow us to deduce a similar result for the infinite variation case. We have to show that, concerning the convergence of the implied volatilities, we can switch limits. The first step in order to be able to justify the exchange of limits is that one of the limits converges. The following result ensures us this property.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})
be the ATM implied volatility associated to the model given by
S^{\varepsilon}
and
I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})
the ATM implied volatility associated to the model given by
S
. Then,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})=\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})

uniformly on
T-t
.

Proof.

We will do a similar strategy as with Lemma
5.4
. First, recall that

I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k^{*,\varepsilon}_{t})=\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t}^{\varepsilon},V_{t}^{\varepsilon}),\quad V_{t}^{\varepsilon}=E_{t}[(S_{T}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{+}]

and

I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},S_{t},V_{t}),\quad V_{t}=E_{t}[(S_{T}-S_{t})_{+}].

Notice also that

I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})=I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon}),

so

\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})=\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})+\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon}).

Let’s first analyze the difference

\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon}).

Using the mean value theorem, we see that there exists a middle point
\xi
between
S_{t}^{\varepsilon}
and
S_{t}
(and due to the construction of
S_{t}^{\varepsilon}
we know that
S_{t}\leq\xi\leq S_{t}^{\varepsilon}
) such that

\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})=\partial^{2}_{kk}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})(S_{t}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}).

As we have seen before,

\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})=\frac{-1}{\Phi(d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))}

so

\displaystyle\partial_{kk}^{2}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Phi(d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S^{\varepsilon}_{t},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})))^{2}}\Phi^{\prime}(d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}d(k,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))

where
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}
denotes the total derivative with respect to
k
. Notice that

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}d(k,\sigma(k))=\partial_{k}d(k,\sigma(k))+\partial_{\sigma}d(k,\sigma(k))\partial_{k}\sigma(k).

Moreover,

\partial_{k}d(k,\sigma(k))=\frac{-1}{\sigma(k)\sqrt{T-t}},\quad\partial_{\sigma}d(k,\sigma(k))=\frac{k-x}{\sigma(k)^{2}\sqrt{T-t}}

so

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}d(k,\sigma(k))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T-t}}\left(\frac{-1}{\sigma(k)}+\frac{(k-x)\partial_{k}\sigma(k)}{\sigma(k)^{2}}\right).

Therefore, applying the previous computations to Bachelier context we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}d(k,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})\sqrt{T-t}}\left(-1+\frac{\xi-S_{t}^{\varepsilon}}{(\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))\Phi(d(k,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})))}\right).

First, using a slight modification of Proposition
5.5
for our context we deduce that
\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})\to I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly on
T-t
. Moreover, by the choice of our approximators,

|\xi-S_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq|S_{t}-S_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq 2\varepsilon.

Finally, using that

1\leq\frac{1}{\Phi(d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})))}\leq 2

we have that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}d(k,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))+\frac{1}{I_{t}(k_{t}^{*}))\sqrt{T-t}}\right|=0

uniformly on
T-t
. We can also interpret this limit as

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sqrt{T-t}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))=\frac{-1}{I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})}

uniformly on
T-t
. For the remaining terms appearing in
\partial_{kk}^{2}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})
, notice that

1\leq\frac{1}{\Phi(d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})))^{2}}\leq 4

and
\Phi^{\prime}(d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})))
is uniformly bounded. In order to conclude this first step of the approximation argument, we derive the following estimate

\displaystyle|\partial^{2}_{k,k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})|\cdot|S_{t}^{\varepsilon}-S_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\sqrt{T-t}\left|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}k}d(\xi,\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon}))\right|\right)|S_{t}-S_{t}^{\varepsilon}|

which tends to zero uniformly on
T-t
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Up to this point, we have that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left[\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})\right]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left[\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})\right].

Now,

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})+\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},S,t,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{1}+A_{2}.

Regarding
A_{1}
, we resort to the mean value theorem to say that there exists
\xi
between
S_{t}
and
S_{t}^{\varepsilon}
such that

|\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})|=|\partial^{2}_{xk}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,\xi,S_{t},V_{t}^{\varepsilon})||S_{t}-S_{t}^{\varepsilon}|.

Using the same arguments as with
|\partial^{2}_{kk}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t}^{\varepsilon},\xi,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})|
, we can see that
A_{1}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly on
T-t
. For
A_{2}
, we use the mean value theorem again to say that there exists a middle point
\xi
between
V_{t}
and
V_{t}^{\varepsilon}
such that

|\partial_{k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},S,t,V_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})|=|\partial^{2}_{\sigma k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,S_{t},S_{t},\xi)||V_{t}-V_{t}^{\varepsilon}|.

Now, from the fact that

\partial^{2}_{\sigma k}\operatorname{Bac}^{-1}(T,t,x,x,\sigma)=0

we see that
A_{2}
vanishes. This proves then that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\partial_{k}I^{\varepsilon}_{t}(k_{t}^{*.\varepsilon})=I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})

uniformly on
T-t
.
∎

This lemma allows us to switch limits in the case where
\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})
is well defined for almost every
\varepsilon
. However, we have proved that when
H<1/2
the skew may exhibit a blowup, and has to be compensated with
(T-t)^{1/2-H}
in order to obtain a finite limit when
T\to t
. Nevertheless, as a consequence of the previous lemma we can deduce the following corollary which deals with the
H<1/2
case.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
S
follow the model (
2.1
) with
\sigma
satisfying Hypotheses
1
,
2
and
3
with
H\in(0,1/2)
. Then,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(T-t)^{1/2-H}\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})=(T-t)^{1/2-H}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})

uniformly on
T-t
.

Now we have all the necessary ingredients in order to prove a result concerning the short-time behavior of the skew when
L
belongs to a wide class of pure-jump Lévy processes.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
S
be a stock price with dynamics following model (
2.1
) with
L
a pure-jump Lévy martingale satisfying that there exists a constant
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=c_{1}
. Then,

•

If
H\geq 1/2
, then

\lim_{T\to t}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{t}}+\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s.

•

If
H<1/2
, then

\lim_{T\to t}(T-t)^{1/2-H}\partial_{k}I_{t}(k^{*}_{t})=\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{3/2+H}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

We first deal with
H>1/2
. Since
L_{t}^{\varepsilon}
is a compound Poisson process and
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
we know that
\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})
has a finite limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly on
T-t
. Moreover, when
H\geq 1/2
we have that the limit as
T
tends to
t
of
\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})
exists for almost every
\varepsilon
. Hence, when
H\geq 1/2
we can directly apply the Moore-Osgood theorem in order to justify that

\lim_{T\to t}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{T\to t}\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon}).

Now, combining this result with Proposition
6.3
we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to t}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to t}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\partial_{k}I_{t}^{\varepsilon}(k_{t}^{*,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to t}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\frac{c_{1}^{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{t}}+\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}(D_{s}\sigma_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{c_{1}^{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{t}}+\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}(D_{s}\sigma_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{t}}+\lim_{T\to t}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{t}(T-t)^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}E_{t}(D_{s}\sigma_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s.

The case
H<1/2
follows the same lines as the case
H\geq 1/2
but using Corollary
6.5
instead of Lemma
6.4
.
∎

The proof of the main result of this paper, Theorem
3.2
, can now be done in one line.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

On the one hand, Equation (
3.2
) is proved in proposition
5.5
. On the other hand, Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are a consequence of Proposition
6.6
.
∎

7
Examples and numerical analysis

The results from the previous sections have provided us with general formulas that we will develop in different particular scenarios. Moreover, we will perform computational experiments to test the veracity of these formulas for particular examples of stochastic volatilities and Lévy processes. Recall that the stock price
S
is assumed to follow the equation

S_{t}=S_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r})+L_{t},

where
L
is a pure jump Lévy process with drift so that
L
is a martingale and
\sigma
satisfies Hypotheses
1
,
2
and
3
.

We will discuss 3 examples in this section.

1.

The first one is the generalized Bachelier-Bates model, that works under the assumption that
\sigma_{t}
is a stochastic process and
L
is a compound Poisson process. We will be looking at
\sigma
following the Fractional Bergomi model, that is the natural generalization of the rough Bergomi model (see
Bayer et al., (2016)
) allowing
H\in(0,1)
and not only
H<1/2
. We will work with different Hurst indices
H
in order to see reflected in the numerics all the factors involved in Theorem
3.2
.

2.

As a second example, we will let
\sigma
follow again the Fractional Bergomi model and we will let
L
be a CGMY Lévy process in order to allow the trajectories of
L
to have infinite variation. In this example we will test some of the experiments we have performed to the Bachelier-Bates model for this scenario. The objective of this second example will be to detail the formulas in Theorem
3.2
for the case of a family of Lévy process with infinite activity and infinite variation paths. Moreover, we will test the veracity of the formulas with numerical experiments.

3.

Finally, we will take a look at the Normal Inverse Gaussian process. This process does not satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
3.2
regarding the skew because
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\infty
(whenever the parameters are not chosen so that the law of the jumps of
L
is symmetric). In such case, we will see that the failure in the applicability of Theorem
3.2
comes from the non-differentiability of the implied volatility surface in the ATM scenario.

For the first two examples, the ATM-IV level will be computed as follows: since
k_{t}^{*}=S_{t}
then

E_{t}\left((S_{T}-S_{t})_{+}\right)=\operatorname{Bac}(T,t,S_{t},k_{t}^{*},I_{t}(k_{t}^{*}))=\frac{I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{T-t}

and therefore

I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=\frac{E_{t}\left((S_{T}-S_{t})_{+}\right)\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt{T-t}}.

In order to simulate the ATM-IV Skew, we will use the formula

\partial_{k}I_{t}(k_{t}^{*})=\frac{1/2-E_{t}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{T}\geq S_{t}\}}\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{T-t}{2\pi}}}.

In order to perform the simulations for the last example, we won’t rely on the ATM-IV level formula because we will explore what happens when we perturb the strike around
k_{t}^{*}
. In that case, we will use the algorithm in
Jäckel, (2017)
in order to simulate options OTM and ITM The code that we have utilized for this section can be found in the GitHub repository
https://github.com/oscarbures01/Bachelier-JD-SV-IV
.

7.1
The generalized Bachelier-Bates model

The generalized Bachelier-Bates model assumes that the stock price process
S
follows the model (
2.1
) with
L
a compound Poisson process with drift. For this case, we can write
L
as the sum of a pure-jump process and a drift term. Hence,
S
can be written as

S_{t}=S_{0}-c_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r})+Z_{t},

(7.1)

where
Z
is a compound Poisson process with Lévy measure
\nu
and
c_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)
. This model is called the generalized Bachelier-Bates model because the dynamics are assumed to drive the price, not the log-price and we let
\sigma
be a general stochastic process unlike the classical Bates model, where the volatility is assumed to follow the Heston model. Usually, the law of the jumps of
L
is assumed to be a Gaussian distribution or a double exponential distribution (see
Cont and Tankov, (2004)
). We will work with both distributions in order to see that the effect of the jumps in the level and the skew of the ATM implied volatility when the time to maturity is small does not depend on the law of the jumps as long as the parameters of the law are adjusted so that
c_{1}
remains the same.

7.1.1
Fractional Bergomi volatility with Gaussian/Laplace jumps

We say that
S
follows the stochastic volatility Fractional Bergomi dynamics if the volatility process
\sigma
satisfies

\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}\exp\left(\alpha W^{H}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}t^{2H}\right)

(7.2)

where
W^{H}
is a fractional Brownian motion of Hurst index
H\in(0,1)
. For the particular case
H<1/2
, the Model (
7.2
) coincides with the rough Bergomi model. Notice that, if we write
W^{H}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t,s)\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
for the kernel described in
Nualart, (2006)
then

D_{s}\sigma_{u}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{u}K_{H}(u,s)\mathbf{1}_{[0,u]}(s).

(7.3)

7.1.2
The case
H\geq 1/2

Regarding the level, we know due to Theorem
3.2
that

\lim_{T\to 0}I_{0}(k^{*})=\sigma_{0}.

(7.4)

Moreover, regarding the skew, we can show as in
Alòs et al., (2023)
that when
H\geq 1/2
, then

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_{0}T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}\mathbb{E}[D_{s}\sigma_{u}]\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s=\begin{cases}0&H>1/2\\
\frac{\rho\alpha}{4}&H=1/2.\end{cases}

In virtue of Theorem
3.2
we have

\lim_{T\to 0}\partial_{k}I_{0}(k^{*})=\begin{cases}\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{0}}&H>1/2\\
\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{0}}+\frac{\rho\alpha}{4}&H=1/2\end{cases}

(7.5)

where
c_{1}
depends on the law of the jumps. For instance, if we assume
Z_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}X_{i}
where
N
is a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda
and
X_{i}
are i.i.d. random variables with
\delta=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
. Then,
c_{1}=\lambda\delta
.

\lambda\delta
is fixed) and we let

and law of the jumps

7.2
Stochastic volatility with CGMY jumps

For this example, we will assume that
S
follows the model (
2.1
), that is,

S_{t}=S_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}(\rho\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r})+L_{t}

where
L
is a pure jump Lévy martingale with Lévy measure

\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)=\begin{cases}C\frac{1}{|y|^{1+Y}}\exp\left(-G|y|\right)\,\mathrm{d}y&y<0\\
C\frac{1}{y^{1+Y}}\exp(-My)\,\mathrm{d}y&y>0\end{cases}

where
C,G,M>0
and
Y\in(-\infty,2)
. This Lévy process is a particular case of a larger class of processes, named Tempered Stable Lévy processes (see
Rosiński, (2007)
for a broader insight on the subject). Since for
Y\in(1,2)
the trajectories of the process are of infinite variation, the application of Theorem
3.2
(and therefore, its usage to compare if the simulated results coincide with the theoretical ones) is subject to the existence of a constant
c_{1}
such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=c_{1}
. As a consequence, a preliminary step to the numerical analysis consists in discussing the existence of
c_{1}
in the case
Y\in(1,2)
. Consider the Lévy measures
\nu_{\varepsilon}(\,\mathrm{d}y)
defined by

\nu_{\varepsilon}(\,\mathrm{d}y)=\begin{cases}C\frac{1}{|y|^{1+Y}}\exp\left(-G|y|\right)\,\mathrm{d}y&y<-\varepsilon\\
C\frac{1}{y^{1+Y}}\exp(-My)\,\mathrm{d}y&y>\varepsilon.\end{cases}

It is clear that
y\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}\backslash[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon])
so the constants
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}
are well defined. In order to compute
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}
notice that

c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}y\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)=I_{1}(\varepsilon)+I_{2}(\varepsilon)

where

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{Y}}\exp(-My)\,\mathrm{d}y

and

I_{2}(\varepsilon)=-\int_{-\infty}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{1}{(-y)^{Y}}\exp(Gy)\,\mathrm{d}y.

For
I_{1}(\varepsilon)
we can perform the cange of variables
t=Mx
in order to get

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\int_{M\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{M^{Y-1}}{t^{Y}}e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t=M^{Y-1}\Gamma(1-Y,M\varepsilon),

where

\Gamma(z,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

denotes the upper incomplete Gamma function. Notice that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}M^{Y-1}\Gamma(1-Y,M\varepsilon)=M^{Y-1}\Gamma(1-Y)

as long as
Y\neq 1
due to the monotone convergence theorem. If we study
I_{2}(\varepsilon)
, we can repeat previous computations in order to get

I_{2}(\varepsilon)=-G^{Y-1}\Gamma(1-Y,G\varepsilon).

Hence, if
Y\neq 1
, then we have that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\left(M^{Y-1}-G^{Y-1}\right)\Gamma(1-Y)=:c_{1}.

Remark 7.3
.

Notice that in the case
Y=1
we can still argue that
c_{1}
exists in the case where
G=M
. Indeed, in this case we have
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=0
for all
\varepsilon>0
and we can define
c_{1}=0
.

We will study this two cases (symmetric
Y=1
and general
Y\in(1,2)
) and we will test them with fractional stochastic volatility models. Both cases are performed using a Monte Carlo method with
2
million paths simulated with antithetic variables.

The symmetric

case

G=M
, we will choose, for instance,
(C,G,M,Y)=(1,5,5,1)
so that

\nu(\,\mathrm{d}y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|y|^{2}}\exp(-5|y|),&y<0\\
\frac{1}{y^{2}}\exp(-5y),&y>0\end{cases}

and
c_{1}^{\varepsilon}=c_{1}=0

8
Conclusions

In this paper we have seen how the Malliavin calculus provides analytic formulas for the short-time behavior for the ATM-IV level and skew for the jump-diffusion stochastic volatility Bachelier model in the case where the Lévy process has finite activity and finite variation. Moreover, since any Lévy process can be approximated by a compound Poisson process, we have shown how a direct approximation argument extends the results for a large class of Lévy processes. The numerical experiments confirm that the presence of the Lévy process does not change the short-time behavior of the ATM-IV level but it has an effect on the ATM-IV skew, acting in an additive way and depending only on the mean of the jumps.
