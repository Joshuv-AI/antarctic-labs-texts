Olanzapine for the Prevention and Treatment of ICU Delirium: Protocol for a Systematic Review With Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Benedikte Liebetrau, Andreas Ruhvald Madsen, Sören Möller, Markus Harboe Olsen, Sebastian Wiberg, Simon Mølstrøm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntensive care unit (ICU) delirium is a multifactorial acute brain dysfunction associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased morbidity and mortality, and higher healthcare costs. Despite these substantial negative impacts of delirium, the optimal prevention and treatment strategies are unclear. Olanzapine, an atypical antipsychotic drug, is commonly used for delirium management. We will perform an updated systematic review and meta‐analysis to assess the benefits and harms of olanzapine for the prevention and treatment of ICU delirium.MethodsThis protocol is developed in accordance with the recommendations of The Cochrane Collaboration and is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis Protocols (PRISMA‐P) guidelines. We will include all randomized clinical trials (RCTs) evaluating prophylactic or therapeutic olanzapine versus any comparator, including pharmacological, non‐pharmacological, placebo, or routine care/no specific delirium‐directed intervention (treated as a single comparator) in ICU patients. Observational studies will be included solely to address potential harms. We will systematically search the following databases: Embase, MEDLINE, The Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, Scopus, and PsycINFO, as well as clinical trial registries. We will perform meta‐analysis and trial sequential analysis (TSA) to investigate the risk of both Type I and II errors. We will assess the risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB‐2). The quality of trials will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach. The review is registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420261474813).OutcomesThe primary outcome is days alive and out of the hospital. The secondary outcomes include all‐cause mortality at 90 days, number of CAM‐ICU (Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit) negative days at 30 days, number of days without pharmacological treatment for delirium other than study drug during the intervention period and serious intervention‐related harms.DiscussionThis protocol outlines the methodology we will use in a systematic review with meta‐analysis designed to evaluate the beneficial and harmful effects of olanzapine for prevention and treatment of delirium in ICU patients. The findings of the systematic review will be disseminated through peer‐reviewed publication. We hope this review will inform future RCTs and improve clinical practice.

Introduction

Delirium

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating, and reversible syndrome characterized by impaired cognition and/or consciousness accompanied by troubles maintaining attention and potential visual and auditory hallucinations [1]. Although prevalence estimates vary, delirium is expected to occur in up to 70% of patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs), with even higher rates observed in those requiring mechanical ventilation [2, 3]. Delirium is classified into three motoric subtypes: (1) hypoactive delirium; (2) hyperactive delirium; and (3) mixed form delirium. In ICU patients, hypoactive and mixed delirium are most common [4, 5]. Most commonly, the etiology of delirium is multifactorial, with the development of delirium involving a complex relationship between a vulnerable patient with multiple predisposing factors and exposure to precipitating factors [6]. Predisposing factors that increase susceptibility to delirium include pre‐existing cognitive impairment, older age, and comorbidity burden. Precipitating factors or noxious insults include polypharmacy, psychoactive medication, infection, and surgery, among others [6, 7].

ICU delirium is associated with a higher risk of mortality and morbidity, prolonged ICU and hospital stay, a longer duration of mechanical ventilation, and increasing healthcare costs [8, 9, 10]. Additionally, patients who develop delirium may experience long‐term sequelae after hospital discharge, including reduced health‐related quality of life, functional decline, and cognitive impairment, which may further increase healthcare costs [11, 12, 13]. Because delirium has a significant adverse effect on outcomes in ICU patients and places a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems, its prevention and management have become a major priority in critical care medicine.

Delirium Management

Both pharmacological and non‐pharmacological interventions have been used to prevent and treat ICU delirium [14]. Pharmacological interventions may include avoidance of benzodiazepines, use of dexmedetomidine for sedation, antipsychotics, and melatonin. Non‐pharmacological interventions may include repeated reorientation of patients, mobilization, sleep protocols, and the use of a scheduled pain management tool [2]. Regarding antipsychotic medications, these are often used for the treatment of ICU delirium, although their therapeutic effect is unclear [15]. It has been hypothesized that the effect of antipsychotic medication may be mediated by a reduction in psychotic symptoms and by sedation. There are two types of antipsychotic drugs: First‐generation or typical antipsychotics (such as haloperidol and chlorpromazine), and second‐generation or atypical antipsychotics (such as olanzapine, quetiapine, and risperidone) [8]. Dopamine receptor pathways in the brain are blocked by both types of antipsychotics, while atypical antipsychotics also affect serotonin receptors [8].

Typical antipsychotics may cause a variety of adverse reactions in clinical practice, including extrapyramidal symptoms, arrhythmias, QT‐interval prolongation, and dystonia. These adverse reactions may be even more pronounced in older patients and critically ill patients [16]. Conversely, atypical antipsychotics are characterized by fewer extrapyramidal symptoms and other adverse reactions [17]. Consequently, there is an increased focus on prevention and treatment of delirium with atypical antipsychotics [18, 19].

Description of the Intervention

As an atypical antipsychotic drug, olanzapine is one of the most used drugs to treat delirium [20, 21]. It belongs to the thienobenzodiazepine class that blocks multiple receptors including dopaminergic, serotonergic, adrenergic, histaminic, and muscarinic. The affinity of olanzapine for multiple receptors has led to its identification as an important agent in the treatment of delirium, nausea, and vomiting. The adverse effects of olanzapine include somnolence, postural hypotension, constipation, dizziness, restlessness, and weight gain [22]. A benefit of olanzapine is its multiple routes of administration, including oral administration (tablets, disintegrating tablets, and solution) and injectable administration (short‐acting intramuscular or long‐acting depot). Olanzapine is extensively metabolized by the liver by glucuronidation and the cytochrome P450 system. The half‐life of olanzapine is approximately 30 h (21–54 h), and it is excreted primarily via the renal route [23].

Relevance of This Review

We identified one previous systematic review with meta‐analysis by Liu et al. from 2023 evaluating the efficacy and safety of olanzapine for the treatment of ICU delirium [24]. This review did not find that olanzapine was significantly more efficacious at relieving delirium symptoms in ICU adults. Additionally, the duration of delirium as well as ICU and hospital stays were not shortened, and it showed no effect in reducing in‐hospital mortality. In patients treated with olanzapine, extrapyramidal reactions, QTc‐interval prolongation, and overall adverse reactions did not increase.

However, several important limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the systematic review by Liu et al. [24]. In the protocol for their systematic review, Liu et al. stated that trials investigating olanzapine interventions for both the prevention and treatment of delirium in ICU patients would be included [25]. However, the final published review included only studies evaluating olanzapine for the treatment of manifest delirium in the ICU [24]. Liu et al. included four Randomized Clinical Trials (RCTs) comprising a total of 278 participants across the intervention and control groups. Considerable clinical heterogeneity was present, including variation in intervention regiments (e.g., olanzapine dose and administration frequency) as well as comparator groups (haloperidol, routine therapy, and Taohe Chengqi Decoction, a traditional Chinese medicine). The heterogeneity in intervention regiments limits meaningful evaluation of potential dose–response relationships, while the absence of placebo‐controlled comparators constitutes an additional methodological limitation. In their primary meta‐analysis, Liu et al. combined data from both RCTs and retrospective cohort studies using a random‐effects model, which combines investigation of causality with that of associations. The use of only random‐effects models might further inflate the risk of Type I error [26]. Furthermore, the review did not employ trial sequential analysis (TSA) to assess imprecision and account for the risk of random errors, nor did it evaluate the certainty of evidence using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach. Finally, in their review Liu et al. included studies identified up to October 2022; therefore, an updated systematic review is required to incorporate subsequent published evidence.

Taken together, these limitations underscore the need for an updated systematic review with rigorous methodology to assess the benefits and harms of olanzapine for the prevention and treatment of ICU delirium. If possible, we will include studies of both prophylactic and therapeutic olanzapine interventions for delirium in the ICU but also analyze them separately as subgroups.

We will minimize the risk of random error by using TSA, assess the risk of bias using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomized trials (RoB‐2), the Risk Of Bias In Non‐randomized Studies—Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool, and the Risk of Bias due to Missing Evidence (ROB‐ME) tool [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Finally, we will evaluate the certainty of the evidence using the GRADE approach [32, 33]. This protocol is intended to provide a transparent and reproducible methodological framework that may facilitate future updates of the systematic review as new evidence and studies on olanzapine for the management of ICU delirium emerge.

Objective

The purpose of this systematic review is to assess the benefits and harms of olanzapine versus any comparator for prevention and treatment of delirium in adult ICU patients.

We hypothesize that, compared with any comparator, prophylactic and therapeutic olanzapine have beneficial effects on patient‐relevant and delirium‐related outcomes, without increasing the risk of serious harms.

Methods

This protocol has been developed based on the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook and is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) Protocols (PRISMA‐P) [32, 33]. The review has been registered in the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the following registration number: CRD420261474813 (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261474813). A completed PRISMA‐P 2015 checklist is provided in Supporting Information 1. The final systematic review will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [34].

Criteria for Considering Studies for This Review

Types of Studies

For the assessment of benefits and harms, we will include all RCTs, including both individually randomized and cluster‐randomized trials. While RCTs provide the most robust evidence on benefits, relying solely on these studies may limit the detection of harms, particularly rare or delayed adverse events. Therefore, for the assessment of harms only, observational studies, including prospective and retrospective cohort studies, nested case–control studies, and case–control studies will also be included, as they are more likely to capture such events [35, 36]. Case reports and case series will not be included in the primary evidence synthesis because, in the absence of a comparator group, they cannot provide comparative effect estimates and cannot be incorporated into the quantitative synthesis. However, they may be used to identify rare or unexpected adverse events.

Eligible studies will be included regardless of publication status, publication type, year, or language. For studies published in languages other than English, we will initially seek assistance with translation through our professional network. If adequate human translation support is unavailable, AI‐based translation tools may be used with caution to facilitate interpretation of non‐English studies. Any AI‐assisted translation will be critically evaluated, and only translations deemed sufficiently accurate and valid will be used in the review.

Types of Participants

Adult participants (≥ 18 years) who are treated in an ICU of any specialty (e.g., medical, cardiac, surgical, neurological, trauma, burn and mixed). We will exclude studies conducted in wards and intermediate care settings (e.g., coronary care units and respiratory units). Studies including both ICU and non‐ICU participants will be excluded.

Types of Interventions

Pharmacological prevention or treatment of delirium with olanzapine at any dose, formulation, and of any frequency and duration. We will differentiate between trials of preventative interventions and those of treatment interventions and perform subgroup analyses of both.

Types of Comparators

Comparison with any intervention, including pharmacological interventions, non‐pharmacological interventions, placebo, or routine care/no specific delirium‐directed intervention (treated as a single comparator). Studies comparing different doses of olanzapine will be identified but not included in the primary analyses. These studies may be considered descriptively to explore a potential dose–response relationship.

Outcomes

Primary Outcome

Days alive and out of the hospital (DAOH)

The primary endpoint is determined as follows: A day (≥ 24 h) spent at health care facility is counted as time in hospital. Outpatient visits and brief hospitalizations for less than 24 h are not counted as days in hospital. If DAOH are not reported, the corresponding authors of the included studies will be contacted to obtain the relevant data.

We will preferentially use DAOH assessed at 90 days, or the nearest comparable timepoint if 90‐day data are not reported. Days admitted to hospital or as deceased within 90 days from initial hospitalization are subtracted from 90 days and the result is rounded down to the nearest whole number of days. For patients dying during initial 90 days, the time spent alive and out of the hospital is retained.

We will use the International Council for Harmonization's Good Clinical Practice (ICH‐GCP) definition for a serious adverse event [37]. However, we will also include events classified as serious adverse events (or equivalent) by the study authors, recognizing that the definitions used may differ between studies. We will consider all other adverse events as non‐serious. Where studies report individual serious adverse events rather than the number of participants with one or more serious adverse events, we will use the number of participants with one or more serious adverse events as reported by the study authors, where available. We will not sum individual events, as this may result in double‐counting participants who experience more than one event. If the number of participants with one or more serious adverse events cannot be determined, the available data will be reported descriptively rather than used to derive a composite estimate. In addition, we will explicitly record the occurrence of adverse events of particular clinical interest for olanzapine, including extrapyramidal symptoms, arrhythmias, QT‐interval prolongation, and dystonia.

Search Methods for Identification of Studies

Electronic Searches

We will conduct a systematic search in Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Cochrane Library (Wiley), CINAHL (EBSCO), Web of Science (Clarivate), Scopus (Elsevier), and PsycInfo (Ovid) to identify relevant studies. We will search all databases from inception to present day. The Embase search strategy will serve as the primary search strategy and will be translated for each additional database, with appropriate modifications to account for differences in indexing terms and search syntax. The complete search strategy used for the Embase database is provided in Supporting Information 2. The detailed search strategy is developed in collaboration with an experienced information specialist and will be provided in the final systematic review. The search strategy will be peer reviewed. By a research librarian according to Evidence‐based guideline for Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) for systematic reviews (SRs) [39] and will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA Statement for Reporting Literature Searches in Systematic Reviews (PRISMA‐S) [40].

Searching for Other Resources

In accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions we will search for unpublished and ongoing trials through WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) [27]. Clinicaltrials.gov will be searched through Embase (Ovid). In addition, conference abstracts, conference proceedings, and relevant preprint servers will be screened to identify potentially eligible studies. To identify additional studies, we will perform forward and backward citation searches in Scopus (Elsevier) using included studies as seed articles.

For all included studies, we will search for retraction notices, expressions of concern, or published corrections through PubMed and the Retraction Watch Database [41].

Selection Process

Two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts identified through the systematic search using Covidence systematic review software (Covidence, Melbourne, Australia) [42]. Duplicates will be removed in Covidence. Potentially relevant studies will be assessed through full‐text reading by two review authors. Any disagreement between the reviewers regarding title/abstract screening will be discussed and if uncertainties remain, the trial will be included for full‐text screening. Two authors will screen full‐text papers and record reasons for the exclusion of ineligible studies. Any disagreement regarding inclusion or exclusion will be resolved through discussion by the same two authors, and if required, they will consult a third author. The systematic review will include a PRISMA flow‐diagram showing the reasons for excluding full‐text articles as well as the number of studies remaining after each stage of the selection process [34].

Data Extraction and Management

Two reviewers will extract data independently from included studies on predefined variables using the Covidence software. Papers from the same main trial will be reviewed together to ensure all available data are considered and to maximize data extraction. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion by the same authors and, if necessary, through discussion with a third author. The trials will be named after the first author and year of the primary publication. Regarding missing data, we will contact the corresponding authors and, where relevant, principal investigators of unpublished or ongoing trials to obtain additional information and data that are insufficiently reported or unavailable in published records, conference abstracts or proceedings, trial registry records, or other study documentation. We will make up to three contact attempts by email, with approximately 2 weeks between each attempt. If no response is received after the final attempt, we will proceed using the available information and report the missing data accordingly.

Outcomes

From each included study, we will extract all outcomes as defined above and by data availability. We will identify if outcomes are missing, inappropriately measured, or selectively reported in accordance with the criteria outlined below regarding assessment of risk of bias. If any of the count or continuous outcomes are measured using different scales or time horizons, we will use the Proportion of Maximum Scaling (POMS) methodology to standardize the values [44]. When appropriate, this methodology will be applied for both primary, secondary, and exploratory outcomes.

Assessment of Risk of Bias

We will base our risk of bias assessment on the RoB‐2 as recommended in The Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews of Interventions [27]. Two reviewers will independently conduct the risk of bias assessment separately for the primary outcome and each secondary outcome within each trial using the RoB‐2 tool. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Risk of bias in each domain will be judged as low, high, or some concerns. Only if all components are rated as having a low risk of bias, the overall classification will be considered as low risk of bias.

Observational studies are included solely to assess serious intervention‐related harms as information on rare or late occurring adverse events is more likely to be reported [35, 36]. Non‐randomized studies will undergo risk of bias assessment using the ROBINS I‐tool [28]. If data necessary for the assessment of risk of bias are missing or insufficiently reported, we will contact the corresponding authors to obtain the relevant information.

Measurement of Treatment Effect

We will use intention‐to‐treat data if it is provided by the authors of the included studies. We will not impute missing values for any outcomes in our primary analysis. For continuous outcomes, data will be extracted as means with standard deviations. Mean differences will be calculated at the study level, and if appropriate pooled to calculate a weighted mean difference (WMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). For dichotomous outcomes, data will be extracted as the number of patients and events per group. Study‐level risk ratios (RR) with corresponding 95% CI will be estimated and pooled when feasible.

Statistical Methods

Assessment of Heterogeneity

Data will be synthesized in accordance with the methodology outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [27]. We will assess clinical diversity, methodological diversity, and statistical heterogeneity across studies. Statistical heterogeneity will be quantified using the I 2 statistic with 95% CIs, the Cochran's χ 2 test, and the between‐study variance (τ 2) [45, 46]. Potential sources of heterogeneity will be explored through subgroup and sensitivity analyses where sufficient data are available.

Assessment of Publication Bias

If at least 10 studies are included in a meta‐analysis, we will assess the potential for reporting biases and small‐study effects using funnel plots [27]. Funnel plot asymmetry will be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect factors other than reporting bias, including clinical, methodological, and statistical heterogeneity. Where appropriate, we will assess the risk of bias due to missing results using the RoB‐ME framework [29]. The findings from these assessments will be considered when interpreting the certainty and robustness of the review findings.

Meta‐Analysis

We will perform a meta‐analysis in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews [27] and use the five‐step approach proposed by Jakobsen et al. in order to assess the intervention effect [47]. Meta‐analysis will only be performed when effect measures from two or more trials are comparable. Intervention effects will be evaluated using both fixed‐effect and random‐effects meta‐analytic models, applying the Mantel–Haenszel method for dichotomous outcomes and the inverse‐variance method for continuous outcomes. If the two approaches yield differing conclusions, the more conservative estimate will be adopted as the primary analysis [48]. Specifically, if statistical significance is observed under only one model, the primary intervention will be based on the model yielding the non‐significant estimate.

Since DAOH is typically reported as median (IQR) rather than mean (SD), reflecting its left‐skewed, zero‐inflated distribution, we will follow the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook and use the method proposed by Wan et al. to estimate means and standard deviations (SDs) from reported medians and interquartile ranges where appropriate [27, 49]. Conventional meta‐analysis based on means and SDs will be limited by the non‐normal distribution of DAOH, the uncertainty with conversion from medians and interquartile ranges to means and SDs, and the ceiling effect inherent to DAOH.

Thus, as a sensitivity analysis, we will first calculate the percentage of maximum possible scale (POMS) to make studies with different follow‐up windows (e.g., DAOH within 30 days and DAOH within 90 days) comparable [44]. The POMS‐transformed medians and IQRs will then be pooled using the quantile estimation (QE) method, which meta‐analyzes the difference of medians directly from reported quantiles and sample size, without assuming normality or converting medians and IQRs to means and SDs [50]. The POMS‐transformed median differences estimated using the QE method will be pooled using standard inverse‐variance random‐effects meta‐analysis. In simulation studies, the median‐based methods outperformed mean/SD‐transformation methods, particularly for skewed outcomes such as DAOH [50].

For cluster‐randomized trials, we will follow the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook and account for clustering using the Rao and Scott method, where appropriate [27, 51]. We will investigate possible heterogeneity through subgroup analyses. We may ultimately decide that a meta‐analysis should be avoided if heterogeneity is considerable, and the results will be reported narratively.

TSA

TSA is a method developed by Copenhagen Trial Unit (CTU). It combines information on the meta‐analytic sample size with the threshold for statistical significance and the level of power [30, 31]. When performing meta‐analysis, there is a risk of random errors due to limited data and repeated testing of the data. We will perform TSA to control the risk of Type I and Type II errors [30]. We will use the TSA to calculate the required information size (the participant number needed in a meta‐analysis to detect or reject a certain intervention effect) and the cumulative Z‐curve's breach of relevant trial sequential monitoring boundaries [31, 52, 53]. The TSA manual provides a detailed description of TSA [54].

To ensure a TSA as unbiased as possible, we have predefined the settings [48]. Having only one primary outcome, we will use an alpha of 0.05 and a beta of 0.10 (i.e., a power of 90%). In terms of heterogeneity, we will calculate both random‐effects models and fixed‐effects models and report the most conservative estimate as outlined in the above paragraph. Proportion of events in the control group (Pc) is ideally taken from previous systematic reviews, RCTs at low risk of bias, or from the meta‐analysis itself. Here, we will estimate the Pc from the control groups in the meta‐analysis. For continuous outcomes, the calculation of the required information size requires an estimate of the variance of treatment effect in the population at hand; here, we will use the variance identified in the meta‐analysis. For dichotomous outcomes, relative risk reduction (RRR) is the difference between the Pc minus the proportion of events in the experimental group (Pe) divided by the Pc. We will use an RRR of 10%. For continuous outcomes, the minimally relevant difference (MIREDIF) is the smallest effect that would be clinically meaningful for the individual participant. As the MIREDIF is uncertain, we will use sensitivity analyses to estimate the MIREDIF as 0.2 and 0.8 SD units, with the broad MIREDIF as the primary analysis [48, 55].

For imprecision we will use the TSA. If the accrued information size is between 50% and 100% of the required information size, we will downgrade one level and two levels if it is lower than 50%.

Discussion

Delirium is a frequent complication in ICU patients and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality and longer hospitalization. Despite its substantial consequences for both patients and the healthcare system, the optimal pharmacologic prevention and treatment strategies for delirium are unknown. However, there is an increased focus on prevention and treatment of delirium with atypical antipsychotics such as olanzapine. One previous systematic review has evaluated the use of olanzapine for the treatment of ICU delirium, but this review did not apply TSA to assess the risk of random errors or evaluate the quality of the evidence using the GRADE approach.

This protocol has several strengths. We have followed the methodological guidance provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and based the protocol on the PRISMA‐P checklist [27, 33]. We will use RoB‐2, ROBINS‐I, and RoB‐ME to assess the risk of bias, TSA to assess the risk of random errors, and GRADE to assess the certainty of the evidence. Furthermore, the inclusion of observational studies to assess the possible harms of olanzapine will strengthen the study. To ensure maximal clinical relevance, we aim to include both prophylactic and therapeutic interventions of olanzapine for the prevention and treatment of ICU delirium.

The protocol also has several limitations. We will include patients treated in ICUs of any specialty, which may introduce clinical heterogeneity. In addition, we will pool data from all randomized trials regardless of route, dose, frequency, and duration of olanzapine administration, which may contribute to both clinical and statistical heterogeneity. If heterogeneity is identified, or if only a limited number of RCTs are available, we will assess whether a meta‐analysis is appropriate or whether a narrative synthesis would be more suitable. Further, the inclusion of studies irrespective of comparator type may also introduce additional heterogeneity. While no language restrictions will be applied, the possible use of AI‐assisted translation for non‐English publications may introduce a small risk of translation‐related misinterpretation.

This protocol outlines a systematic review with meta‐analysis designed to evaluate the beneficial and harmful effects of olanzapine for the prevention and treatment of delirium in ICU patients. We hope that findings from this review may help guide the design of future RCTs and improve clinical practice.
