Physical Inactivity Syndrome: A Risk Factor and Target for Intervention—A Narrative Review
Authors: Masahiro Kohzuki,
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aging of the world’s population has caused a dramatic change in the overall health of patients, with many suffering from disuse syndrome—a condition caused by immobility and bed rest. Even among people who are able to live independently, newly defined issues such as frailty, sarcopenia, locomotive syndrome, and hospitalization-associated disability have rapidly emerged, and it has been found that physical inactivity is a key cause of these issues. Disuse syndrome is a term that is mainly used in Japan but not widely accepted elsewhere because of its negative connotations. Moreover, although the term physical inactivity is widely used, it has a strong connotation of simply referring to reduced physical activity and does not emphasize that it also poses systemic and multi-organ risks. To solve these problems, in this narrative review, I propose a new academic term—physical inactivity syndrome (PIS)— to describe the diverse physical, mental, and social symptoms and disorders caused by bed rest and decreased physical activity. PIS serves as an umbrella term for disuse syndrome, physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and related diseases. Furthermore, the effects of rehabilitation and exercise therapy required to prevent and treat PIS are introduced. It is necessary for medical professionals to be fully aware of the risks of PIS, properly acquire the skills needed to improve patient activity, and carry out prevention and treatment with sufficient confidence.

INTRODUCTION

The world is aging. Japan has become the world’s most rapidly aging society,1) and the condition of patients seen by general practitioners has changed dramatically in recent years. The physical strength of many patients is steadily declining even though their visceral organ function can be maintained through medical treatment. Some patients suffer from multiple disabilities because of musculoskeletal diseases such as osteoarthritis in addition to internal diseases, whereas others are forced to transfer to a care facility because of the increased burden of care on their families. We are also now in an age where many patients suffer from disuse syndrome, which encompasses a variety of symptoms throughout the body caused by immobility and bed rest.2)

Disuse syndrome is a term that is used mainly in Japan3,4,5,6,7) but is not widely accepted elsewhere. In addition to disuse syndrome, other academic terms related to reduced physical activity include physical inactivity,8) frailty,9) sarcopenia,10) locomotive syndrome,11) and hospitalization-associated disability.12)

There are various definitions of physical inactivity, with the most commonly used definition being “a state in which one does not engage in moderate-intensity exercise for 30 min or more per day, 5 days or more per week, or vigorous-intensity exercise for 20 min or more per day, 3 days or more per week.”8) Frailty is defined as a clinical syndrome in which three or more of the following criteria are present: unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, weakness (grip strength), slow walking speed, and low physical activity.9) Sarcopenia is a muscle disease (muscle failure) rooted in adverse muscle changes that accrue across a lifetime. The updated consensus paper on sarcopenia published by the Writing Group for the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) focuses on low muscle strength as a key characteristic of sarcopenia, uses the detection of low muscle quantity and quality to confirm the sarcopenia diagnosis, and identifies poor physical performance as an indicator of severe sarcopenia.10) The consensus paper also updates the clinical algorithm that can be used for sarcopenia case-finding, diagnosis and confirmation, and severity determination, and it provides clear cut-off points for measurements of variables that identify and characterize sarcopenia.10) Locomotive syndrome is a condition of reduced mobility caused by the impairment of locomotive organs.11) In the USA, Covinsky et al.12) reported that more than 30% of hospitalized patients aged 70 or older have new disabilities when they are discharged that were not present at the time of admission. This is called “hospitalization-associated disability,” and is considered to be a difficult problem to prevent or improve.12)

In this study, the frequency with which these terms are used in academic articles is investigated. As a result, I propose a new academic term—physical inactivity syndrome (PIS)— to describe the diverse physical, mental, and social symptoms and disorders caused by bed rest and decreased physical activity.13)

METHODS AND RESULTS

Literature searches were conducted on 31 March 2025 to identify studies that covered a wide range of systemic symptoms and disabilities resulting from physical inactivity. Four electronic databases (Cochrane, PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus) were used. Publication dates ranged from 1970 to December 2024. The medical subject terms “disuse syndrome,” “physical inactivity,” “frailty,” “sarcopenia,” “locomotive syndrome,” and “hospitalization-associated disability” were used. The inclusion criterion was that the article was a study or review article, and the exclusion criterion was that it did not have an abstract.

For “disuse syndrome,” the following numbers of studies were retrieved from each database: Cochrane, 243; PubMed, 93; PsycINFO, 243; and Scopus, 150. For “physical inactivity,” the numbers were Cochrane, 31,383; PubMed, 12,330; PsycINFO, 31,383; and Scopus, 20,169. For “frailty,” the numbers were Cochrane, 55,374; PubMed, 33,968; PsycINFO, 55,374; and Scopus, 31,154. For “sarcopenia,” the numbers were Cochrane, 27,379; PubMed, 23,532; PsycINFO, 27,379; and Scopus, 24,164. For “locomotive syndrome,” the numbers were Cochrane, 175; PubMed, 451; PsycINFO, 175; and Scopus, 285. For “hospitalization-associated disability,” the numbers were Cochrane, 60; PubMed, 37; PsycINFO, 60; and Scopus, 28. No studies were retrieved from any of the databases for “physical inactivity syndrome (PIS).”

The term “disuse syndrome” is not used often, including in Japan. The reasons for this are as follows: (1) the word “disuse” brings to mind words such as “out of business, disabled person, waste,” which can be viewed negatively; (2) there is a risk of misunderstanding because it does not accurately express the fact that the problem is not simply the complete disuse or non-use of a certain function, but rather that it occurs because of insufficient use.14)

Although the term physical inactivity is widely used,15,16,17,18) it has a strong connotation of simply decreased physical activity and does not make clear the systemic and multi-organ risks that disuse syndrome poses. Moreover, recently defined issues such as frailty, sarcopenia, and locomotive syndrome can occur in rapid succession, even for people who are able to live independently. All of these concepts are linked by the danger of physical inactivity and bed rest, and the importance of exercise therapies such as aerobic exercise and resistance training.19,20,21,22)

In a meta-analysis that investigated the degree of association between frailty and various physical fitness variables, daily walking speed was the most strongly associated with frailty, followed by aerobic capacity, maximum walking speed, lower body strength, and grip strength.23) The clinical practice guidelines of the International Conference on Frailty and Sarcopenia Research (ICFSR) recommend the management of sarcopenia, the treatable causes of weight loss, and the causes of exhaustion (depression, anemia, hypotension, hypothyroidism, and B12 deficiency) (strong recommendation).24) Moreover, all persons with frailty should receive social support as needed to address unmet needs and encourage adherence to a comprehensive care plan (strong recommendation).24) First-line therapy for the management of frailty should include a multicomponent physical activity program with a resistance-based training component (strong recommendation).24) Protein/caloric supplementation is recommended when weight loss or undernutrition are present (conditional recommendation).24)

A meta-analysis examining the effectiveness of exercise interventions on patient-important outcomes in older adults with sarcopenia showed that resistance exercise with or without nutritional intervention and a combination of resistance exercise and aerobic exercise/balance training were the most effective interventions for improving quality of life.25) To treat sarcopenia, the ICFSR clinical practice guidelines strongly recommend the prescription of resistance-based physical activity, and they conditionally recommend protein supplementation or a protein-rich diet.26)

A meta-analysis found that the prevalence of hospitalization-associated disability in older adults receiving acute care was 30%, and this rate remained constant over time despite the reduction in length of hospital stay.27) Hospitalization in acute care poses a significant risk to the functional independence of older adults, and this risk has not changed despite the reduction in length of hospital stay. This evidence supports the continued need for hospital-based programs to assess the functional capacity of older adults and identify those at risk for better treatment and prevention of hospitalization-associated disability.27) A meta-analysis of risk factors for hospitalization-associated disability in elderly patients revealed significant associations with various risk factors in these patients.28) Specifically, older age, female sex, white race, burden of comorbidities, improved activities of daily living at the time of admission, diagnosis of dementia, and greater length of hospital stay were significant risk factors for hospitalization-associated disability.28) Furthermore, frailty, poor physical function, immobility, and delirium were identified as distinct risk factors for hospitalization-associated disability in elderly patients.28) No meta-analyses of interventions for disuse syndrome or locomotive syndrome were reported.

PHYSICAL INACTIVITY SYNDROME

I propose a new academic term—physical inactivity syndrome (PIS)— to describe the diverse physical, mental, and social symptoms and disorders caused by bed rest and decreased physical activity as an umbrella term for disuse syndrome, physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and related diseases. Resting in bed during hospitalization is comfortable. However, long periods of bed rest can lead to serious problems. It is not rare for elderly people or those who are already frail to be unable to walk after just 1 week of bed rest. These people already have weakened basic physical and muscular strength, and resting further weakens them, eventually making it difficult for them to live independently. In the past, when drugs and surgery were less advanced, “rest” was undoubtedly a major part of treatment. However, it is now understood that “rest” is no longer considered a treatment, because it can be a harmful approach that affects the independence of patients.29)

Table 1 lists the symptoms and diseases in PIS; these are the types of harmful effects that bed rest can have on the whole body.2) Of course, the appearance of symptoms and signs varies from person to person and is influenced not only by the underlying disease that necessitated bed rest, but also by age, original physical strength, duration of bed rest, degree of bed rest, nutritional status, inflammatory state, and details of treatment. Despite the variability in patient conditions and morbidity, the path toward PIS typically begins as an abnormality in one function or organ that gradually leads to abnormalities in others, eventually resulting in serious abnormalities in multiple functions and organs, the nerve and mental system, and social functioning.2)

Effects of physical inactivity syndrome (PIS) on organs and systems

PIS makes patients susceptible to dementia, hallucinations, delusions, anxiety, insomnia, and depression. It also promotes obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, which, in turn, accelerates arteriosclerosis, leads to cardiovascular disease, and shortens a patient’s lifespan.2) PIS also makes patients susceptible to social isolation and social withdrawal. Moreover, it takes significantly longer to restore impaired function through rehabilitation than bed rest.

Maximum oxygen intake (VO2max) is a representative index of exercise tolerance that indicates endurance, and it is a surrogate marker for life prognosis. As the severity of PIS increases, VO2max decreases. VO2max is determined by five factors: the heart, lungs, muscles, blood, and kidneys.30) In 1966, five 20-year-old American college students were subjected to 3 weeks of forced bed rest followed by 8 weeks of exercise therapy to monitor how much their VO2max changed. Their VO2max was also investigated at age 50 (30 years later) and age 60 (40 years later). The results showed that the VO2max at age 60 was the same as the VO2max at age 20 after 3 weeks of forced bed rest (Fig. 1).31) Similarly, in the group of healthy older adults, there was a large loss of skeletal muscle from the lower extremities (15.6%) as a result of bed rest (10 days).32) A systematic narrative review of the effects of bed rest in athletes found that horizontal bed rest significantly reduced muscle strength in athletes within 5 days.33) A meta-analysis of currently available transcriptomic datasets on inactivity and acute and chronic exercise responses showed that inactivity was associated with reduced expression of genes involved in mitochondrial processes and ATP production and increased expression of genes associated with ubiquitination.34) Conversely, aerobic training increased the expression of genes related to mitochondrial function and metabolic pathways.34)

Covinsky et al.12) found that upon discharge, more than 30% of hospitalized patients aged 70 or older had new disabilities that were not present at the time of admission. This is called “hospitalization-associated disability.”12) However, given that the same phenomenon would occur if the patient were to stay at home, PIS is a more appropriate term to describe the various harmful effects that bed rest has on the whole body.29) In fact, because people were forced to stay at home during the COVID-19 pandemic, PIS was likely to occur not only in hospitals, but also at home. It is also likely that, under these circumstances, the level of care required actually increases and cognitive decline progresses in affected individuals.35,36)

Wen and Wu8) reported that eight in ten adolescents (aged 13–15) and one in three adults worldwide are physically inactive. Surprisingly, physical inactivity increases the mortality rate to the same extent as smoking (Fig. 2).8) However, in Japan—perhaps related to the large number of smokers—physical inactivity ranked third after smoking and hypertension in terms of the contribution of various risk factors to the number of deaths from non-communicable diseases and external causes in 2007 (Fig. 3).37) It is important to be aware that PIS is more dangerous than hyperglycemia, obesity, or excess salt intake.

THE NEED FOR THE TERM PIS

There have been numerous studies on physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and hospital-associated disability, and the various effects of a lack of physical activity on muscle strength, exercise tolerance, life prognosis, and organ function have already been described. However, there are three lines of reasoning that justify the proposal of PIS.

First, despite the fact that numerous studies have been conducted on physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and hospital-associated disability, the effects of physical inactivity spread rapidly and strongly. Therefore, all measures that can help change the lifestyle of people with physical inactivity are justified. Second, although physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and hospital-associated disability are familiar to those involved in rehabilitation, geriatrics, and sports medicine, some medical professionals, patients, and members of the public may not be interested in exercise or rehabilitation and may have little awareness that these terms refer to various problems throughout the body. For example, the definitions of frailty and sarcopenia highlight the importance of muscle strength and motor function, but do not directly evoke the idea that problems will arise throughout the body. The definition of physical inactivity refers only to the duration of exercise. Furthermore, the term “locomotive syndrome” means little to those who are not interested in musculoskeletal diseases. Third, despite widespread research into physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and hospital-associated disability, the reality is that there is insufficient use of increased physical activity, early mobilization, rehabilitation, and exercise therapy.

PIS is a term that encompasses the various adverse effects that physical inactivity has on the entire body. It also evokes the idea that the problem is not physical inactivity itself, but the various disadvantages brought about by physical inactivity. The use of the term PIS is extremely significant in the sense that it makes the harm of bed rest and the importance of exercise easier to understand for medical professionals, patients, and the public.

The definitions of physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and hospital-associated disability each have a history of being determined through extensive research and debate, and by proposing PIS, there is no intention to change any of these definitions, methods of operation, or diagnoses. The intention behind the use of the term PIS is to increase awareness that physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and hospital-associated disability have an impact on the entire body.

RECENT PROGRESS IN REHABILITATION MEDICINE

It can be dangerous to force a bedridden person to move. In the past, rest was considered one of the treatments for cerebrovascular disorders, chronic heart failure, chronic respiratory failure, and chronic renal failure. However, attitudes toward the treatment of these conditions have changed somewhat. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a guideline-recommended, multidisciplinary program of exercise training, risk factor management, and psychosocial counseling for people with cardiovascular disease.38,39,40,41,42,43) CR reduces rates of hospitalization and mortality and improves quality of life.38,39) In the 2021 guidelines for rehabilitation in patients with cardiovascular disease (issued by the Japanese Circulation Society and Japanese Association of Cardiac Rehabilitation), CR for acute coronary syndrome, stable angina, chronic heart failure, post-cardiac surgery, peripheral arterial disease with claudication, and post-heart transplant is categorized as “Class I, Evidence Level A,” making it the highest level of medical treatment.44) However, only an estimated one in four eligible patients enroll in CR, and there are persistent disparities in participation based on sex, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and geographic location.39) Of the patients that enroll in CR, fewer still complete the full CR program.39)

It is well known that rehabilitation for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is effective in increasing exercise tolerance and balance, improving dyspnea, improving health-related quality of life, and reducing medical resource utilization in terms of hospitalization days.45,46) Furthermore, despite the use of rest as a traditional treatment for pulmonary hypertension, advances in the treatment of pulmonary arterial hypertension and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension have led to improvements in hemodynamics and life prognosis.47,48) Subsequently, rehabilitation designed to improve exercise tolerance and quality of life has become recognized as standard treatment for these conditions (Class IIa, Evidence Level B).44)

In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), rest was a basic element of treatment because exercise leads to orthostatic proteinuria and increased proteinuria. Recent findings from basic and clinical research have revealed that renal rehabilitation improves exercise tolerance, improves protein energy wasting, inhibits protein catabolism, and improves quality of life in dialysis patients.30) Furthermore, renal rehabilitation in conservative CKD patients has been highlighted as a potential new treatment for improving renal function and preventing transition to dialysis, and CKD treatment has made a Copernican shift from exercise restriction to exercise therapy.30) In the Diabetes Treatment Guide published by the Japan Diabetes Society,49) the word “restriction” has been removed from the description of exercise for stage 3 and stage 4 diabetic nephropathy; instead, there has been a dramatic shift over the past few years to recommending exercise. According to the Renal Association clinical practice guidelines in the United Kingdom,50) CKD patients should maintain or improve their physical function by undertaking activities aimed at improving or maintaining muscle strength, balance, and flexibility at least 2 days a week. CKD patients should aim to accumulate 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, building up gradually from current levels. Those who are already regularly active can achieve these benefits through 75 min of vigorous activity per week, or a combination of moderate and vigorous activity.50) Similarly, a statement from the Physical Exercise Working Group of the Italian Society of Nephrology affirms that regular physical activity and exercise training offer numerous benefits to CKD patients, including improved physical function, enhanced cardiometabolic and neuromuscular function, cognitive benefits, and an overall improvement in quality of life.51) Furthermore, exercise may provide nephroprotection and reduce mortality.51)

In the past, various rehabilitation guidelines have been limited to evidence for the rehabilitation of a single disease, and evidence for the rehabilitation of multiple disorders has not been adequately presented. However, in actual clinical practice, there are more patients with multimorbidity and multiple disabilities than patients with a single disease or disorder, so rehabilitation plans should be made and implemented under circumstances not shown in the guidelines.52) In reality, when patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) have renal dysfunction, the subsequent total mortality rate and cardiovascular-related mortality rate increase.53) However, it has been reported that the rehabilitation of CKD dialysis patients who underwent coronary artery bypass surgery for ischemic heart disease reduced both total and cardiac mortality by more than 30%.54) In addition, although the mortality and rehospitalization rates of heart failure patients are closely related to renal dysfunction,55) it has been reported that the mortality rate is actually influenced by exercise tolerance (maximum oxygen intake).56) Furthermore, in a CKD patient with AMI, renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate—eGFR) was reported to improve after convalescent CR.57) In an intervention study conducted by the author, after CR for AMI patients and helping them understand the importance of exercise therapy, the patients were transferred to late convalescent CR at home. It was found that maintaining a high number of steps per day at home also led to the suppression of renal function decline.58) Therefore, there is considerable hope that rehabilitation for multiple disabilities may be effective.

A PARADIGM SHIFT IN REHABILITATION MEDICINE

The Kōjien—a well-known Japanese dictionary—defines rehabilitation as “the provision of medical and psychological guidance and functional training to people who have completed the treatment stage and suffer from the aftereffects of illness or injury, in order to restore their functions and return to society.”59) Whereas the main purpose of medical care is to extend life expectancy (adding years to life), the primary goal of rehabilitation is to improve daily and motor functions and improve the quality of life (adding life to years) (Fig. 4).60) However, in rehabilitation—especially in visceral rehabilitation for cardiovascular disease, COPD, and CKD—it has become clear that, after acute rehabilitation in hospital, the second goal of rehabilitation (adding years to life) can be achieved through an outpatient rehabilitation program and the patient’s own self-rehabilitation at home.60)

A paradigm shift in rehabilitation goals. The primary goal of rehabilitation is to improve daily and motor functions and improve the quality of life (QOL) (adding life to years). However, in rehabilitation, especially in visceral rehabilitation for cardiovascular disease, COPD, and CKD, it has become clear that the second goal of rehabilitation (adding years to life) can be achieved by performing late recovery rehabilitation. Therefore, for patients who are able to walk, the second goal of adding years to life should also be aimed for. Ideal medical care would be rehabilitation and exercise therapy that results in improvements in daily living, motor functions, and quality of life, as well as the extension of life. ADL, activities of daily living.

For patients who are able to walk, the second goal of adding years to life should also be targeted. If rehabilitation and exercise therapy can result in improvements in daily living and motor functions, improved quality of life, and extension of life (adding life to years and years to life), this scenario would emerge as an ideal form of medical care (Fig. 4).60) As a result, there is a significant need to actively promote preventive measures against PIS and to refine its treatment.

PREVENTION AND TREATMENT OF PIS DEPEND ON THE ATTENDING PHYSICIAN

If a doctor simply instructs a patient to “exercise,” it is unlikely that the patient will exercise at the intended intensity or frequency, and the doctor will not be able to respond to the patient’s declining physical strength. If the patient is already in a situation where they are unable to exercise or rehabilitate well on their own, the doctor must assist them or refer them to specialized rehabilitation staff. The potential ability of the patient to undertake rehabilitation must be assessed by a doctor, because rehabilitation staff are not permitted to conduct rehabilitation without a doctor’s instructions. As with drug prescriptions, rehabilitation begins with the doctor’s “exercise prescription.”

Given the requirements for a doctor to coordinate a patient’s rehabilitation, it seems obvious that a patient should be referred to a rehabilitation hospital or department to achieve the desired outcome. However, specialized rehabilitation hospitals are limited in number and may not be in close proximity to many patients in need of rehabilitation therapy. For cases in which symptoms are not serious enough for the patient to be admitted to a rehabilitation hospital, the family physician must prescribe the rehabilitation themselves, and, to achieve the desired outcomes, the physician must also have basic knowledge of rehabilitation.

PREVENTING AND IMPROVING PIS

As a measure against PIS, although it is very important to increase daily physical activity and to start and continue rehabilitation early, there are several points to consider. First, there are potential risks associated with early mobilization. For safe early mobilization, the patient’s condition must be stable, vital signs must be monitored (e.g., electrocardiogram, blood pressure, blood oxygen saturation, depending on the patient’s disease and condition), and the mobilization must be performed by experienced rehabilitation therapists. Second, there are situations in which bed rest is appropriate, such as when the patient’s condition is unstable, their level of consciousness is low, or when they have a fever. Third, there are differences in culture and medical systems in the implementation of measures. For example, early mobilization will be difficult without experienced rehabilitation therapists. In addition, rehabilitation is accessible if it is covered by insurance for patients with heart failure, as in the USA and Japan, but active rehabilitation is difficult in countries where this is not the case.

However, even with these points in mind, it is still necessary to fully inform people that prolonged bed rest and reduced physical activity can lead to systemic problems, such as those shown in Table 1. Moreover, people should be encouraged to increase their physical activity, even if only slightly, from the current level and to start and continue rehabilitation early.

LIMITATIONS

This narrative review has several limitations. First, this is not a scoping review or systematic review. Second, no consensus statement or expert opinion survey has been conducted for the introduction of PIS as a clinical term. Third, although PIS is called a syndrome, there are no specific criteria for its diagnosis, as in metabolic syndrome. However, the impact of PIS may be influenced by the patient’s age, basic physical strength, rest period, rest level, comorbid diseases, and nutritional status, making it difficult to rate PIS severity using a simplified scoring system. Although it is conceptually understood that physical inactivity causes various adverse events, the fact that it is a truly serious problem as population aging progresses is not yet fully understood. How to define PIS and its severity using a number or score remains a topic of future work.

CONCLUSION

In this narrative review, PIS was introduced as an umbrella term for disuse syndrome, physical inactivity, frailty, sarcopenia, and related diseases. It must be emphasized that physical inactivity causes problems in many areas of healthcare and can occur in hospitals, homes, and communities. It is crucial that people are made aware of PIS and the need to avoid it. If we can understand PIS as a risk factor, target it for intervention, and incorporate it into medical care—even if only incrementally—early mobilization, rehabilitation, and exercise therapy will be more easily disseminated. Such changes will allow patients to live longer and more vibrantly—that is, with extended life prognosis and improved quality of life. It will also be possible to develop a seamless rehabilitation system for use in hospitals and local communities all over the world.

In the future, research studies should investigate the manifestation of PIS and how it differs depending on the patient’s own basic physical strength, age, duration and degree of physical inactivity, nutritional status, and the severity of disease. Studies should also investigate the efficacy of prevention and treatment approaches.
