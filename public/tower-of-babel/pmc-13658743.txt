Mass Gatherings as Amplifiers of Meningococcal Disease: A Modeling Study of Hajj and Umrah
Authors: Laurent Coudeville, Musallam Abuhasan, Abrar Alasmari, Anas Khan, Saber Yezli, Refaat Abdelaziz, Florence Coste, Alp Dogu, Angie Upegui, George Wahba, Abdullah Assiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) hosts millions of Hajj and Umrah pilgrims annually, and it aims to welcome 30 million external (international) Umrah pilgrims annually by 2030 (Saudi Vision 2030). These mass gatherings pose significant risks for infectious disease transmission. We extended a previous meta-population model of Neisseria meningitidis transmission during Hajj to include Umrah pilgrimage and assessed the impact of varying vaccination coverage rates (VCRs) among pilgrims on meningococcal meningitis (MM) cases.MethodsThe model population was divided into five clusters: residents of the pilgrimage sites (Makkah/Madinah); the broader KSA population; and non-KSA populations from high-, medium-, and low-transmission settings. The model incorporated age, pilgrim movement patterns, and VCRs. The model was calibrated with data from 1995 to 2011 and validated with data from 2012 to 2024. MM cases under various vaccination and pilgrim scenarios were simulated for the 2025–2034 period.ResultsUmrah accounted for approximately 90% of pilgrimage days in 2023–2024, with external pilgrims accounting for > 90% of these days. High VCRs (90%) for external and internal pilgrims reduced the number of expected MM cases across the KSA by > 80% compared with no vaccination.ConclusionOur analysis highlights the amplifying effect of mass gatherings on disease transmission dynamics and confirms vaccination as a highly effective intervention. Introduction

The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) hosts millions of internal (domestic) and external (international) Hajj and Umrah pilgrims annually, necessitating robust public health preparedness and surveillance systems [1]. Hajj and Umrah are large religious gatherings centered on pilgrimage to holy sites in the KSA and are characterized by high population density, close interpersonal contact, and increasing international participation, all of which increase the risk of infectious disease transmission [2, 3]. However, unlike Hajj, which occurs during a fixed period in which millions of pilgrims gather over a few days, Umrah can take place year-round and can be performed multiple times in a given year. Although crowd sizes are more variable during Umrah, high concentrations of pilgrims can still occur during certain months, and the growing visitor numbers in recent years may contribute to substantial public health risks over a more prolonged period.

The number of Umrah pilgrims has increased considerably over the last 2 years [4], especially during the months of Rajab, Sha’ban, and Ramadan (the 7th, 8th, and 9th months of the Islamic calendar, respectively) [5]. In 2024, the total number of pilgrims from the KSA and abroad performing Umrah reached more than 35 million [4]. In peak months, Umrah increasingly resembles a mass gathering similar to Hajj, with associated elevated risks of infectious disease transmission [6].

In a previous modeling analysis, we highlighted the role of the Hajj pilgrimage in the transmission of Neisseria meningitidis and the importance of vaccination in reducing spread among pilgrims and the entire KSA population [7]. Although vaccination coverage rates (VCRs) are high for Hajj pilgrims, particularly among external pilgrims [8], this is not the case for Umrah pilgrims [9]. Consequently, clusters of meningococcal meningitis (MM) or other invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) cases associated with travel to the KSA for Umrah have been reported among pilgrims and their secondary contacts in their countries of origin [3, 10]. In this study, we expanded our meta-population model of N. meningitidis transmission [7] by incorporating Umrah pilgrimage data.

Here, we aimed to quantify the impact of Umrah on N. meningitidis transmission dynamics and to evaluate the effects of different VCRs among Umrah pilgrims on disease spread.

Methods

Model Overview

We expanded a previously developed meta-population, age-structured transmission model initially designed to assess N. meningitidis transmission dynamics during the Hajj pilgrimage [7] to include Umrah pilgrimage. Although the model aimed to represent overall dynamics of disease transmission, it was mainly focused on Hajj and Umrah, the two major mass gatherings in the KSA (Fig. 1a). The model used data on MM cases, pilgrim movement patterns, arrival timing and volume entering the KSA, duration of stay, and VCRs. The model was calibrated using data from 1995 to 2011, validated with data from 2012 to 2024, and then used to simulate the effects of potential changes in vaccine policy and endemic conditions for 2025–2034. Model Components

The world population, including both pilgrims and non-pilgrims, was modeled in homogeneous groups called clusters. Five clusters were determined: the pilgrimage sites (the KSA cities of Makkah and Madinah); the rest of the KSA; and external pilgrims’ countries of origin divided into high-, medium-, and low-meningococcal transmission settings (Table 1). In the model, pilgrimage corresponds to the transition from non-pilgrim to associated pilgrim clusters (Fig. 1b). This transition is based on pilgrimage statistics and accounts for the seasonality of Hajj and Umrah pilgrimages, the age of pilgrims, and the duration of stay for internal pilgrims (KSA clusters) and external pilgrims (non-KSA clusters). Pilgrimage is based on the Islamic calendar. The correspondence between the Islamic and Gregorian calendars considered in the model is shown in Supplementary File 2 (Table S1). Hajj occurs in the 12th month (Dhu al-Hijjah) of the Islamic calendar between the 8th and 13th days of the month. Umrah can take place year-round (except during Hajj), although there are increased numbers of pilgrims during the months of Rajab, Sha’ban, and Ramadan (the 7th, 8th, and 9th months of the Islamic calendar, respectively; Table S2) [5].

Each cluster shared the same representation of infection and demographic processes. Five clusters were considered for non-vaccinated pilgrims: susceptible (S); short-term carrier (C); asymptomatic carrier (A); diseased (D); and recovered (R). In line with the previous publication, the distinction between short-term and long-term carriage is meant for enabling a better representation of the time between infection and disease occurrence [7]. Six additional compartments were used for vaccinated pilgrims: vaccinated and protected against infection (V); vaccinated but susceptible to meningococcal infection (SV); vaccinated and short-term carrier (CV); vaccinated and asymptomatic carrier (AV); vaccinated and diseased (DV); and vaccinated and immune following infection (RV). For non-pilgrims, transmission occurs only within clusters. For pilgrims, transmission occurs among all pilgrims and among non-pilgrim residents at the pilgrimage sites, thereby corresponding to the exposure effect associated with the mass gathering effect. We also accounted for increased contact between pilgrims, notably due to the density effect, which is the second major component of the mass gathering effect.

Several vaccination programs were considered, including cluster-specific routine or catch-up vaccination programs, and Hajj and Umrah vaccination for internal and external pilgrims occurring before the start of the pilgrimage. We also considered the use of two types of quadrivalent meningococcal vaccines (polysaccharide and conjugate) and the switch from one vaccine to another at the cluster level.

Data Sources

Disease and vaccine parameters are provided in Supplementary File 2 (Table S3). Data sources for the number of internal and external pilgrims for Hajj and Umrah are shown in Supplementary File 2 (Table S4), with a breakdown of external Umrah pilgrims categorized into transmission clusters shown in Supplementary File 2 (Table S5). The N. meningitidis carriage rates among Hajj and Umrah pilgrims used are summarized in Supplementary File 2 (Tables S6 and S7). Demographic data for the KSA clusters are based on Saudi Arabia statistics and those for the other clusters are based on United Nations data (Supplementary File 2 [Table S8]).

To fit the model to observed MM cases, we used surveillance data covering the 1995–2024 period. Surveillance data for 2025 were not available at the time of our analysis. In line with our previous analysis [7], we used data pertaining to cases observed among pilgrims (internal and external) and in the entire KSA population for the 1995–2011 period. However, for the 2012–2024 period, we used data reported by the KSA surveillance system for the KSA population only (Supplementary File 2 [Table S9]). Annual reports during that period did not systematically indicate whether reported cases occurred in the context of pilgrimage. Such information requires specific data that were only available to us for the 1995–2011 period. Thus, for the 2012–2024 period, which we used as the validation period in our analysis, we only considered cases reported for the entire KSA population. In addition, given that the cases reported by the KSA surveillance system correspond to MM rather than to the broader IMD case definition, we therefore reported calibration and simulation results for MM cases.

Use of the quadrivalent meningococcal vaccine started in the KSA in 2002 with meningococcal polysaccharide vaccines (MPSV) and included a catch-up program for Saudi residents in Makkah and Madinah [11]. Vaccine requirements for pilgrims have been maintained since that initial program and current recommendations state that all individuals from all countries intending to perform Hajj or Umrah are required to have received a quadrivalent meningococcal vaccine in the last 3 years for MPSV or 5 years for the conjugate vaccine [12, 13]. The quadrivalent ACWY meningococcal conjugate vaccine (MCV) was officially introduced in 2010 and was recommended in 2013 for infants as a two-dose series at 9 and 12 months of age. In 2020, vaccination was also recommended at 18 years of age, but this has not been fully implemented [14]. We assumed that, for vaccinations outside the KSA, MPSV was used in the high-transmission setting cluster, whereas a switch from MPSV to MCV occurred in 2010 for pilgrims from medium- and low-transmission settings. Type of vaccine used, recommendations, and VCRs considered in the model are detailed in Supplementary File 2 (Table S10).

Calibration Methods

Two periods were considered for calibrating the model. A calibration period from 1995 to 2011 was used for adjusting model parameters to observed data, and a validation period from 2011 to 2024 was used to check if model outputs after calibration were able to reproduce observed data. Data used for calibrating the model were the number of MM cases among pilgrims and in the KSA population. The calibration methods are presented in more detail in Supplementary File 3.

Simulation Methods

Model simulations were conducted to estimate the number of MM cases among pilgrims and in the KSA during the 2025–2034 period, and to examine the effect of the evolution of the pilgrim population and the effect of different Umrah pilgrim VCRs. The effect of seasonality was also examined.

Two scenarios for the 2025–2034 period were simulated: a reference scenario in line with Saudi Vision 2030 [15], which assumes a large increase to 30 million external Umrah pilgrims annually by 2030; and an alternative, more conservative scenario that assumes about 17 million external pilgrims annually by 2030. This second scenario, which can be seen as a lower bound for the evolution of the number of Umrah pilgrims, assumes steady growth, namely that the increase in the number of pilgrims from 2025 onwards follows the increase of the population size in the different clusters (see Table S8 for data on population growth).

Ethical Approval

This modeling analysis is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Analyzing case numbers by Islamic month indicates that Ramadan would be associated with the highest expected number of MM cases among both pilgrim groups (Fig. 5a) and in the KSA population (Fig. 5b), which highlights that this period would have the highest risk of an outbreak. Ramadan accounts for about 23% and 16% of MM cases among pilgrims and in the KSA population, respectively, despite comprising only 8% of the days in the Islamic year. This suggests that the risk of MM during Ramadan compared with the other months of the year is on average more than three times higher for pilgrims and more than two times higher for the KSA population.

In the alternative, more conservative scenario, the trend in the evolution of the number of MM cases according to VCRs would be similar to that of the reference scenario, although the risk of a major outbreak at low VCRs would be reduced. The predicted probability of observing more than 100 MM cases a year is 28% if the VCR among both internal and external pilgrims is 10% (Supplementary File 4 [Fig. S5]).

The evolution of the average number of MM cases over time for Umrah pilgrims and for the KSA population in the reference and alternate scenarios are shown in Supplementary File 4 (Figs. S6 and S7, respectively). There was a large decrease in MM cases during the 2016–2021 period compared to the pre-vaccination period (1995–2001). However, an increase in MM cases was observed during 2022–2024 compared with the 2016–2021 period, with the increase projected to continue during the 2025–2034 period in the two scenarios. The latter increase was projected to be smaller in the alternative, more conservative scenario than in the reference scenario.

Discussion

Our analysis highlights the amplifying effect of the Umrah mass gatherings on N. meningitidis transmission and confirms vaccination as a highly effective preventive measure for disease caused by this bacterium. However, relying solely on vaccinating external pilgrims will not adequately control MM in the KSA, and, conversely, relying solely on vaccinating internal pilgrims will not adequately control MM among pilgrims. Adequate control of MM in the KSA requires a high internal pilgrim VCR, irrespective of the external pilgrim VCR. To ensure a safe pilgrim experience, public health measures in KSA that complement vaccination are also already in place and include: outbreak preparedness through crowd management and control strategies; rapid diagnosis; contact tracking, tracing, and surveillance; sufficiently resourced outbreak response capacity; and risk communication, health promotion, and international coordination [17, 18]. These measures are also essential in assessing the need for continued improvement in vaccination initiatives.

Although mandatory quadrivalent meningococcal ACWY vaccination for pilgrims was introduced in 2002 as part of efforts to prevent major outbreaks [10], vaccination coverage among Umrah pilgrims has not been closely monitored. We were only able to use data published in 2025, which had some limitations that are detailed below [19]. The uncertainty around current and future vaccination coverage led us to consider a wide range of possible VCRs for the 2025–2034 period. Nevertheless, all available evidence indicates that VCRs are lower for Umrah than for Hajj, in part due to the suspension of the vaccination requirement caused by vaccine shortage so as to allow pilgrims to travel without being vaccinated [20]. Coverage among internal pilgrims tends to be lower than among external pilgrims [8], mainly due to a lack of awareness and proper control of the mandatory vaccination requirement for internal pilgrims, though recent data are lacking. Enforcement of meningococcal ACWY vaccination requirements for internal and external pilgrims performing Umrah should be strengthened, as should vaccination requirements for all residents of the holy sites (i.e., those living in Makkah and Medina, and in surrounding areas) [21]. High vaccination coverage could realistically be achieved through multiple initiatives, including digital certificate verification, visa-linked requirements, domestic permit systems, and targeted vaccination awareness campaigns [22]. Umrah-related MM outbreak prevention should be recognized as a critical global and national public health priority since it has the potential to lead to international outbreaks and spread of antimicrobial resistance. Beyond the immediate health risks, repeated MM outbreaks could jeopardize the full realization of the pilgrim experience project, a key component of Saudi Vision 2030 [23].

Our analysis also highlighted the importance of vaccinating internal Umrah pilgrims for limiting meningococcal disease occurrence in the KSA population. Nearly 50% of Umrah pilgrimages were performed by internal pilgrims, accounting for about 18 million pilgrimages in 2024 (Fig 3a). Therefore, a high VCR for Umrah internal pilgrims directly translates into a high VCR in the KSA population, especially in the adult population, which limits disease transmission for the whole country. Finally, our analysis can be placed in the context of other studies that leverage disease transmission modeling to identify relevant control measures that can mitigate disease spread during religious pilgrimages or other types of mass gathering events [24, 25].

Although we leveraged detailed pilgrimage statistics and accounted for the transmission risk associated with polysaccharide and conjugate vaccines, our analysis has several limitations. First, we relied on MM cases that were reported to the KSA health authorities and on published sources using KSA health statistics; notably, no direct information about cases among pilgrims has been reported since 2011. In addition, the reported cases do not include all clinical forms of IMD. IMD commonly manifests as meningitis or sepsis, especially in children and young adults; other forms, such as meningococcal pneumonia, and less frequent, non-meningeal presentations (gastrointestinal symptoms, septic arthritis, and bacteremia-linked epiglottitis) are more common in older adults [26]. Our projected number of meningitis cases can therefore be seen as conservative estimates of the potential number of IMD cases. For instance, a large French database study of patients admitted to hospital with IMD between 2014 and 2016 found that, across all age groups, 48–67% of cases presented as meningitis alone, 17–34% as septicemia alone, and 5–18% as combined meningitis plus septicemia [27].

Nevertheless, vaccination provides protection against all IMD forms, not only MM, which makes the estimated percentage of prevented cases a reliable indicator of overall vaccine benefit. Another limitation was the input data on meningococcal VCRs among Umrah external pilgrims, which the KSA Ministry of Health reported as 54%. This VCR was ascertained through assessing vaccination certificates provided by pilgrims at the port of entry during Ramadan 1446 (March 2025). In a paper that focused on Umrah pilgrimage, Alshahrani et al. [6] noted that this VCR might exceed the actual coverage in recent years. Given the large attendance for Umrah throughout the year, VCRs are likely to be much lower for pilgrims from many countries.

Our analysis allowed us to estimate an overall increased contact rate for pilgrims during their pilgrimage (14.3, range 13.2–15.5). Although this enabled us to identify the main drivers such as VCR for specific populations, seasonality, or the evolution in the number of cases, this remains a simplified representation of the complex mechanisms at play in disease transmission. We notably combined Hajj and Umrah when assessing this parameter, leading to a more diluted pilgrimage effect compared with our previous analysis, which specifically focused on Hajj [7]. We also measured this effect for the entire pilgrimage period without distinguishing between time dedicated to religious rituals and other activities during pilgrimage. A more granular representation would have strengthened our analysis but goes beyond the level of complexity supported by available information.

The meta-population used in the analysis represents N. meningitidis transmission among pilgrims and in the entire KSA population, as well as in the country of origin for external pilgrims. However, we focused our analysis on the implications for pilgrims and for the KSA population, and external pilgrims were grouped into three transmission clusters (high, medium, and low) depending on their country of origin to maintain model tractability. These three settings are heterogeneous and correspond to very large populations, which limits the robustness of the specific conclusions that could be drawn for each setting. A more in-depth analysis of the consequences for returning pilgrims would have required a substantial expansion in the number of clusters in our model, informed by country-specific information, but this was beyond the scope of the current analysis.

In addition, our model does not account for serogroup specificities. The distribution of MM cases published by Memish et al. in 2013 reported that serogroup A was the most frequent between 1995 and 1999 [11]. Serogroup W became predominant during the 2000–2001 outbreak, with serogroups W and A being predominant between 2002 and 2011. Most of the recent cases reported among Umrah pilgrims, their close contacts, and individuals in their home countries without direct contact with the pilgrims were caused by serogroup W [28–30]. However, we considered that the MenACWY vaccines would still prevent the majority of MM cases. This consideration was supported by studies assessing the epidemic potential of the serogroups or lineages that caused epidemics during Hajj/Umrah (serogroup A and serogroup W). If a given serogroup or lineage has a high disease-to-carriage ratio, it is considered as hyperinvasive and has higher potential to cause disease and thus epidemics. An 8-year longitudinal study of meningococcal colonization and disease patterns in the African meningitis belt, specifically focusing on northern Ghana [31], found that serogroup A (sequence type [ST] 7, clonal complex [CC] 5) exhibited a significantly higher disease-to-carriage ratio (16.8–42.3 per 1,000 population) than the “low-virulence” serogroup X (ST-751, CC181) (0.1–0.3 per 1,000), reflecting a marked difference in invasiveness. This disparity meant that serogroup A caused larger meningitis outbreaks, despite reaching lower population colonization rates, than the more widespread but less virulent serogroup X. Similarly, in the late 1990s, serogroup C (ST-11, CC11) was estimated to be over 12 times more invasive than serogroup B among teenagers in the UK [32]. The risk of disease given carriage for serogroup C was 110.9 per 10,000 population, compared with only 8.7 per 10,000 for serogroup B. This highlights the significant impact of serogroup C on IMD during that period. The serogroup W ST-11 lineage that caused epidemics during Hajj in 2000 and 2001 is believed to have diverged from an ancestral serogroup C ST-11 strain [33].

Although there have been previous beliefs that meningococcal vaccines needed to be evaluated in older adults [34], most available quadrivalent MCVs are now licensed for use from age 2 months (or 6 weeks of age) with no upper age limit [35]. Quadrivalent MCVs have been shown to be immunogenic and well tolerated in older adults (aged ≥ 56 years), including those very much older [36–39]. Modeling studies predict favorable antibody persistence at 10 years post-vaccination in older adults [35]. Additionally, compared with MPSVs, MCVs provide enhanced immunogenicity, reduce nasopharyngeal carriage of targeted serogroups, and provide indirect protection to those unvaccinated in the community [40, 41].

The complexity, variability, and evolving nature of the KSA’s vaccination requirements for pilgrims suggest an ongoing need for clearer, more accessible guidance for external Umrah pilgrims, as well as guidance from their countries of origin. The KSA faces challenges in checking whether pilgrims are vaccinated because differences by visa type, country of origin, and travel arrangements complicate monitoring of vaccination requirements, especially among those traveling independently or at short notice [6]. In addition, a substantial proportion of internal pilgrims remain unvaccinated [10]. The recent clusters of IMD cases associated with travel to the KSA among pilgrims and their secondary contacts in their countries of origin highlight the urgency of the situation [3, 10].

Conclusions

Despite some limitations, our analysis, which aimed to leverage all available evidence on the topic, highlights that Umrah pilgrimage is a major driver for N. meningitidis transmission both for pilgrims and the KSA population. This underscores the importance of vaccinating internal and external pilgrims as a key preventive strategy to reduce the risk of MM outbreaks and benefit the entire KSA population, as well as the pilgrims, their relatives, and their communities in their countries of origin. Our observations could also be generalized to other mass gatherings with global attendance similar to that observed during Hajj and Umrah.
