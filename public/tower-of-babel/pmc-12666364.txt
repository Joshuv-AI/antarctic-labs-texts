Physical activity patterns and environmental influences: Findings from the Kuwait diabetes epidemiology program
Authors: Victor M. Oguoma, Mark Daniel, Ralf-D Schroers, Mohamed Abu-Farha, Faisal H. Al-Refaei, Fahd Al-Mulla, Neil T. Coffee, Saad Alsharrah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsufficient physical activity (PA) is the fourth leading risk factor for cardiometabolic disease. This study assessed in Kuwait adults the spatial clustering of PA; relationships between PA, climatic factors and built environmental (BE) indicators; and ethnic group differences in PA.MethodsA population-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 2011 to 2014 targeting adults in Kuwait ≥18 years old, using the World Health Organisation (WHO) Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) to assess five domains of physical activity. 2530 participants were classified as physically active or not physically active based on accruing at least 600 MET (min/week). Objectively measured residential-area BE indicators were extracted using the street address location of study participants. Climatic variables expressed as monthly aggregates were acquired from Kuwait's climate stations. Random effects logit analysis was used to assess the relationships between PA, climatic and BE variables.ResultsThe proportion of physically active respondents was 51.9 % (95 % CI: 49.9, 53.8 %). Insufficient PA was greater in women than men. Suburb-level PA ranged from 21.4 % to 81.9 %. Covariate-adjusted analyses of PA revealed a weak positive association between average temperature aOR = 1.03 (95 %CI: 1.00, 1.06), open space sports ground with synthetic grass (300 m) aOR = 1.28 (95 %CI: 0.88, 1.86), and weighted area road density (100 m) aOR = 1.06 (95 %CI: 0.96, 1.17). A lower odds of PA was apparent for respondents who were currently married (vs never married) aOR = 0.68 (95 %CI: 0.46, 0.99), and for women (vs men) aOR = 0.57 (95 %CI: 0.46, 0.72). A greater odds of PA was apparent for the South/Southeast Asian relative to Arab ethnic group aOR = 1.32 (95 %CI: 1.04, 1.67).ConclusionThis study indicates that half the population in Kuwait did not meet the WHO recommended minimum PA level of at least 600 MET (min/week). Efforts to promote PA in Kuwait should consider addressing gender and ethnic disparities, local environmental factors, and demographic influences such as marital status.

Introduction

Insufficient physical activity (PA) is the fourth leading risk factor for cardiometabolic disease, accounting for 3.2 million deaths worldwide, with one in five adults estimated to acrue insufficient PA [1,2]. Rapid urbanisation, now a global phenomenon, has shaped negative lifestyle changes including physical inactivity and unhealthful diet which contribute to the clustering of cardiometabolic risk factors in both high-and low-middle-income countries [3,4].

Kuwait, a country in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC), has the highest age-standardised prevalence of insufficient PA 67 % (95 %CI: 59–74 %) in the world [2,5]. Surging economic development in Kuwait since the 1960s has led to massive changes in socio-economic conditions and in the urban landscape [6]. Ongoing changes in urban areas conditioning lesser PA and calorie-dense, nutrient-poor diet have heralded the emergence in Kuwait of common noncommunicable diseases, including hypertension, heart disease, obesity and diabetes [7].

Characteristics of built environments (BE) are considered major indirect determinants of health, influencing mobility (especially travel mode, such as walking) and lifestyle behaviours (shopping, socialising, sports, etc.) and thence health. BE features can also directly impact health in ways either harmful (e.g., noise and pollution) or beneficial (e.g., parks). BE features such as streets, parks, open spaces, and transport infrastructure play important roles in determining individuals' total PA behaviour and extent of PA accrued during leisure-time, transport-related, or occupational activities [8]. There is increasing evidence, notably from developed countries in Europe, North America, and Australia, of associations between BE features and PA [[9], [10], [11]]. For instance, studies have shown BE indicators to exhibit consistently moderate effects in associations with transport-related PA [12,13]. However, such evidence has not yet been elucidated for countries in the GCC such as Kuwait where the BE and patterns of lifestyle are considerably different from those in the global North.

Climatic factors shape how time is allocated to sedentary behaviour and PA across the course of a typical day [14]. Extremes of weather (very hot, cold, windy, or wet) can create undesirable, unsafe outdoor environments for exercise, and thus increase time spent indoors engaged in typical sedentary behaviours such as sitting, watching TV, using computers and mobile devices [15]. A perceived barrier to PA in Kuwait is the region's hot weather [16]. In the Gulf region, the temperature rises substantially during Spring and in Summer can reach beyond 50 °C, restricting outdoor activities and discouraging even travelling to climate-controlled gyms and exercise studios [17].

Given the strong link between PA and cardiometabolic risk factor clustering and the need for better primary prevention and management of cardiometabolic diseases, World Health Organisation (WHO) member states including Kuwait have ratified a commitment to achieve a 10 % reduction in the prevalence of insufficient PA by 2025 [2,18]. The need for low cost, reliable and standardised tools to validly measure health-enhancing PA in countries where patterns of energy expenditure differ from developed countries, led to the development of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [19]. Despite such efforts in measurement development, application by research relevant to the prevention of cardiometabolic disease risk factors is still underdeveloped in the GCC region. Most epidemiological studies on PA in the GCC region are small-scale and lacking in the use of standardised measures [20]. A recent systematic review identified only three studies that reported adult PA levels in Kuwait between 2009 and 2015 [21], each lacking information on the role of the BE and climatic factors on PA. The objectives of this study were to assess in Kuwait adults PA patterns; the relationship between PA, climatic factors and built environmental (BE) indicators; and ethnic group differences in PA.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Kuwait national population-based cross-sectional survey of diabetes and obesity (n = 5291), part of the Kuwait Adult Diabetes Epidemiology (KADEM) Program conducted between 2011 and 2014 for Arab and South/Southeast Asian adults aged 18–82 years residing in Kuwait, provided data for this study. A random sample of participants stratified by nationality and governorate (similar to municipality, in which are nested suburbs) from the computerised register of the Public Authority for Civil Information (PACI) was used, as previously reported [22]. A total of 2673 (51 %) of survey respondents from 87 suburbs provided complete records for physical activity, gender, age, and BMI. For statistical reasons (inadequate suburb-level information due to inadequate numbers of respondents), suburbs with less than 10 respondents (n = 39 suburbs [n = 125 participants]) were excluded (Table S3). Analytical sample of 2530 participants residing across 48 suburbs remained for analysis after excluding additional 18 participants having ethnic group coded as “others”.

Physical activity

The WHO GPAQ was used to collect five domains of PA (vigorous-intensity work, moderate-intensity work, travel from place to place, vigorous-intensity recreation, and moderate-intensity recreation) [23]. The GPAQ has been shown to have a good-excellent test-retest reliability in countries where energy expenditure patterns differ from those in the global north [19]. Trained research personnel engaged each study participant in a face-to-face interview to document physical activity status. Questionnaires were administered in either Arabic or English languages, according to participant preference.

The frequency of PA for the five domains was recorded in days per week. The duration of each session of PA was recorded in minutes per day. Metabolic equivalents (MET) recorded in minutes per week, a continuous indicator of activity-related energy expenditure, was used to express PA status. The frequency (days/week) and duration (min/day) for PA per domain was multiplied by its corresponding MET value and summed to derive total PA per week [23]. Participants were dichotomised as physically active (≥600 MET min/week) or physically inactive (<600 MET min/week) per the GPAQ analysis guide [23]. The GPAQ does not register the location(s) of PA for the five domains of PA it assesses and, unfortunately, the KADEM study did not supplement the GPAQ in asking follow-up questions regarding the domain-specific locations in which PA was accrued.

Anthropometric measurements

The anthropometric protocol for this study has been previously reported [22,24]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared [25]. Participants were classified according to BMI as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), or obese (≥30 kg/m2) [25]. Central obesity was defined according to waist circumference (WC); cut-offs were ≥ 94 cm for Arab men, ≥80 cm for Arab women, ≥90 cm for South Asian men, and ≥ 80 cm for South Asian women [26].

Built environmental and climatic exposure variables

Participants' residential addresses were geocoded. Six sets of network buffers and Euclidean buffers between 100 m and 3000 m were created around the address geocoordinates (at 100 m, 300 m, 500 m, 1000 m, 1600 m and 3000 m). These distances centred on the widely used 1 km buffer radius by which resident activities are characterised in geospatial research in international studies [27,28].

Network buffers were created around walkable roads and walking paths (excluding highways, ramps and other non-walkable road segments), thus accounting for accessible walkways usable for utilitarian purposes of peoples' daily living. Exposure measures of length, density, connectivity and presence or absence was computed for walking paths alone and for walking paths and walkable roads together. A weighted road density measure for buffers was derived based on major and minor road type classes, representing traffic intensity and a proxy for noise and pollution. Similarly, measures of area size of open-space features and supermalls were derived. Lastly, environmental climatic indicators were acquired from Kuwait's weather and climate stations for the period of the survey (2011–2014). These included air temperature, wind speed, rainfall, relative humidity, and derived heat index computed as monthly averages for each Euclidean buffer. Euclidean buffers were used to aggregate raster-based interpolation results modelling climate conditions and their continuous propagation through space for buffer areas around the point of residence. Detailed descriptions of environmental exposure variables is provided in the included Supplementary File.

Ethical clearance

This study was conducted at Dasman Diabetes Institute and approved by the Ethical Review Committee (ERC – RA2010–004). The study protocol was consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants signed the consent form, in writing, prior to enrolment in the study. Details are provided in a supplementary file (Appendix A).

Statistical analysis

Built Environmental covariates selection

As noted, different buffer types of different sizes were constructed for walking paths, walkable roads and paths, weighted road density, open-space, and supermalls. We first centred the variables at their mean (standardisation) and conducted a bivariate evaluation of the different built environmental covariates with their buffer sizes on the PA outcome variable, using a logit model. We considered a set of 19 covariates based on individual associations with the PA outcome variable at a 10 % level probability (Table S1). Adaptive Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (aLASSO) on a training sample (70 % of the total sample) was used to guide variable selection. Five covariates with non-zero coefficients and no multicollinearity issues were selected by aLASSO (Fig. S2). These covariates, used for multivariable analysis, included count of open space sports ground/synthetic grass for 100 m and 300 m sausage buffers, count of open space sports ground/natural grass for 500 m network buffers, network area of road density for 1000 m Euclidean buffers, and weighted area road density for 100 m Euclidean buffers.

Descriptive statistics

Continuous data were presented as means and standard deviation, or median and interquartile range as appropriate, while categorical data were presented as counts and percentages according to sex and ethnic group.

Cluster and outlier analysis

The local Moran's I statistic was used to measure spatial autocorrelation across study suburbs. The Moran's I statistic and Z-score were calculated for each suburb to assess the statistical significance and geographic variation of PA. These statistics evaluate a localised measure of spatial clustering of similar PA levels around each suburb, as compared to a reference random distribution. Given statistical significance, areas with high Z-scores signify hot spots (clusters of areas with high PA levels) and low Z-scores signify cold spots (clusters of areas with low PA levels). High-High and Low-Low clusters expose study suburbs adjoining suburbs with similar PA levels, while High-Low and Low-High suburbs denote dissimilar, non-random PA patterns. First-order neighbours were used in constructing the spatial binary weight matrix. Areas that share both vertex (corner) and border (line segment) were set to 1.

Random-effects logistic regression

A random-effects logistic regression model accounting for suburb-level clustering was used to assess relationships between PA (more or less than MET 600 (min/week)) and predictors of PA. Model 1 assessed the relationship between individual/socio-economic factors and PA. Model 2 assessed the relationship between built environmental factors and PA, while Model 3 incorporated both individual/socio-economic factors and built environmental variables to constitute a fully adjusted model. The log-odds coefficients of the regression model were exponentiated to represent the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of PA and the 95 % confidence interval. As the random-effects model is not a spatially explicit model, the geographical coordinates of the observed data were included in models to account for the potential effects of spatial heterogeneity for study locations. Further, an interaction model was fitted to explore relationships between environmental variables with each sociodemographic variable. In this model, environmental variables with p ≤ 0.25 in the fully adjusted model were considered. Statistical significance was set at alpha = 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed in Stata 16.1 (StataCorp, TX, USA). Estimates were mapped using ArcGIS Pro (ESRI ArcPro 2.7.1, CA, USA).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of physical activity and its associations with built environmental features and climatic variables in Kuwait. Slightly more than half the sample, 51.9 % (95 % CI: 49.9, 53.8 %) had insufficient physical activity, achieving less than the WHO recommendation of 600 METs (min/week) of physical activity.

Our study found that men were more physically active than women for domains of physical activity including work, study/training, farming-related and recreational activities. Time (mins/day) spent travelling to and from work, shopping, market, or places of worship was similar across sex and ethnic categories, and the frequency of physical activity (days/week) was greater in men than in women. This finding is consistent with evidence from most Middle Eastern and Mediterranean countries that more men than women engage in physical activity [21]. Strong and distinct socio-cultural gender roles for men and women in Kuwait likely underpin the observed difference [29]. Knowledge of gender-based barriers and enablers of physical activity in Kuwait is likely to be essential in the development of culturally- and gender-appropriate physical activity interventions. To some degree, ethnocultural differences in physical activity are already accounted for in Kuwait with women-only gyms and fitness classes being widespread, but less is known as to why women are less inclined to partake in physical activity even if enabled by the presence of women-only opportunities.

Overall, half of the population in Kuwait was physically inactive, the Arab population fraction being less active than the South/Southeast Asian fraction. A previous study using the same measure as used here reported a 41 % prevalence of sufficient physical activity in Kuwait [21]. South/Southeast Asians had a lower median sitting and sleeping time than did Arabs. Kuwait has a significant population of expatriates due to its extensive oil industry, with 67 % of the population being of expatriate, and predominantly, Asian origin [30]. This finding implies that Asians are likely to be involved in more work-related physical activity that limits sedentary time than their Arab counterparts who widely have white-collar jobs associated with sedentary behaviour. Research on working adults shows that occupational sitting time contributes substantially toward daily sitting time, reflecting work-saving but diminished movement and health-compromising technological advances and process automation [31,32]. Additionally, cultural norms and built environmental challenges may have large influences on levels of physical activity. For Arab communities, especially among women, social expectations can create barriers to engaging in public physical activity, despite such norms slowly lessening. High car dependence and low-density suburban living, particularly among Kuwaiti nationals, also limit opportunities for walking or incidental physical activity. Non-Kuwaiti Arabs show greater socioeconomic diversity, some relying on cars but lower-income groups depending on public transport requiring more walking. Asians, often residing in higher-density areas, may engage in more physical activity given a greater reliance on walking for daily mobility. While walkability challenges affect all groups in Kuwait, systemic factors like housing policies, transportation reliance, and cultural attitudes contribute to disparities in physical activity levels. Our finding that 32 % of South/Southeast Asians have a greater odds of being physically active relative to Arabs suggests an understanding of determinants of physical activity among racial groups in Kuwait will be key to the planning of racial−/ethnic-appropriate physical activity interventions.

This study also revealed variations in physical activity at the suburb level. The suburbs Sharq, Bnaid Al-Qar, and Dasman were identified as hotspots of physical activity predominantly undertaken by South/Southeast Asian ethnic populations, especially Sharq and Bnaid Al-Qar. This finding highlights the likely occupational options for South/Southeast Asians in these suburbs which drive physical activity, especially given these suburbs are devoid of open-space synthetic grass at the 100 m network buffer that overall, across the full sample, was positively associated with physical activity. Knowledge of the overall magnitude of physical activity, and sub-national variations in physical activity status, is important to the development of physical activity interventions in Kuwait.

Individual-level socioeconomic status is well documented as positively associated with PA [33,34]. However, the association between income and PA is complex because of utility maximisation [35]. It is argued that time and budget constraints often influence how individuals allocate their time to maximise physical versus leisure activities [36]. Such selective behaviour is observed where higher income leads to more opportunities for physical activity (income effect) and may increase the opportunity cost of leisure time, and as a result, decrease the amount of time spent on physical activity (substitution effect). We found that individuals with at least 1500KD monthly household income had a 37 % greater odds of being physically active compared to those who earn less, aligning with other studies reporting a positive association between income and physical activity [34,37].

Marital status was an important determinant of physical activity. Our findings show, even accounting for age, that married individuals, especially men, have lower odds of being physically active than those who have never been married. Financial and social responsibilities associated with marriage have been suggested as factors that could help explain lesser physical activity in married men [38]. Equally, evidence shows that married women engage in less physical activity because of their tendency, supported by prevailing cultural traditions, to attend to family responsibilities more than pursue physical activities enabling weight loss or sustaining healthful body weight [39,40]. We infer that targeted physical activity interventions to encourage physical activity specifically among married men and women are needed to achieve improved health in this group.

Evidence from North America suggests that the roadenvironment can shape physical activity. Our results illustrate that the weighted area density of roads (100 m buffer) was positively associated with physical activity. This positive association was weakened after accounting for socio-demographic factors. Weighted road density integrates into a single variable the maximum amount of information related to traffic without a requirement to obtain traffic counts for the area being considered [41]. The weighted road density is a road environment indicator that can be used as a proxy for traffic-related pollution exposure [42]. Higher values are considered to denote greater traffic-related pollution exposure. Residents of Kuwait are thus likely prone to be exposed to traffic-related pollution, given physical activity was positively associated with greater weighted road density. Usage of local walkways for physical activity often running parallel to highways and major roads could explain this finding. Further research accounting for the locations of physical activity is needed to better explain this finding. As noted (Methods) the GPAQ does not ask about the domain-specific locations of physical activity, and the KADEM survey unfortunately did not seek to expand the GPAQ to capture such information.

A strong positive association was observed in univariable analyses between the presence of open space (synthetic grass) and physical activity. This relationship was weakened once covariates were accounted for. The presence of open space areas is an important characteristic of built environments and community design that has previously been shown to facilitate active transportation [8]. For example, having a variety of nearby shopping, education, work, and recreation options is positively associated with walking and bicycling for transportation [10,42,43]. However, not all studies on this topic have reported positive results [44]. Mytton, Townsend [45] found no association between green space and domains of physical activity often associated with green space, despite observing that green space was positively related to overall physical activity. This contrary result might be explained by a limited validity of the measure used to assess physical activity (false negative), or it could be real. A weak association between open space and physical activity in our study could perhaps reflect the low number of participants who reported participation in physical activity domains (Table 1) related to open space. It may also reflect challenges inherent in meaningfully operationalising features of the built environment as they relate to physical activity.

Our study revealed a positive association between mean air temperature and physical activity. Temperature has been shown to have a strong impact on physical activity, with extreme temperatures often leading to reduced activity, and moderate temperatures encouraging it [46]. Seasonal and geographical variations, accessibility of and the availability of indoor facilities, and exposure to public health interventions, all play key roles in mitigating the impact of temperature on physical activity level. In regions with extreme temperatures, people often adapt by engaging in indoor physical activities. As noted, the physical activity measure used here did not differentiate indoor versus outdoor physical activity, nor for any of the domains assessed. It is unclear whether a positive relationship between air temperature and physical activity in our study is driven by people engaging in indoor activity as an adaptation to high outdoor temperatures. Future studies exploring the impact of climatic influences on indoor versus outdoor physical activity in Kuwait are needed to build an evidence base for targeted, contextually-specific public health interventions.

Strengths and limitations of the study

An assessment of physical activity according to ethnic category with estimation of its relationship with objectively measured built environmental indicators and spatial clustering is unique and has not previously been reported for Kuwait. Key limitations include the cross-sectional design precluding causal inference, use of bias-prone self-reported measures of the frequency, intensity, and duration of physical activity, and lack of data on domain-specific locations of physical activity. The overall Moran's I being close to 0 for the study area indicates a randomly distributed physical activity pattern across suburbs. This may indicate that the scale of the applied aggregation units (suburbs) is too coarse–aggregation of physical activity data might be better performed on a more refined spatial unit (administrative or spatially defined) with well-characterised urban features, using a larger participant sample size (not possible for this study).

Conclusion

This study indicates that half of the population in Kuwait does not meet the WHO-recommendation of at least 600 METs (min/week) of physical activity. Arabs were more inactive than South/Southeast Asians. Weak covariate-adjusted positive associations were observed between physical activity and mean air temperature and open space sports ground, and a weak inverse association with weighted area road density. We would not anticipate more than weak strength associations in multi-level or random effects analysis of ‘upstream’ or ‘distal’ built environmental influences and physical activity [47]. The ‘prevention paradox’ holds that environmental influences and supports can greatly impact a population yet still offer little to each participating individual [48]. Only when populations are contrasted do environmental influences become clear in their role differentially shaping behaviour through the reciprocal interaction of cognitive, behavioural, and environmental determinants [49,50]. Given the high prevalence of insufficient physical activity and corresponding high burden of overweight/obesity and related chronic disease in Kuwait [22], it is important to foster physical activity-supportive local-area built environments concomitant with greater attention to the health benefits of physical activity behaviour to improve population health outcomes.
