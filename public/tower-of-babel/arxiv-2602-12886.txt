Well-posedness and mean-field limit estimate of a consensus-based algorithm for min-max problems
Hui Huang, Jethro Warnett

Abstract




Abstract.
The recent work
[
5
]
proposes a derivative-free consensus-based particle method that computes global solutions to nonconvex–nonconcave min–max problems and establishes global exponential convergence in the sense of the mean-field law.
This paper aims to address the theoretical gaps in
[
5
]
, specifically by providing a quantitative estimate of the mean-field limit with respect to the number of particles, as well as establishing the well-posedness of both the finite particle model and the corresponding mean-field dynamics.

Keywords:
Mean-field limits, Consensus-based optimization, Interacting particle systems, Coupling methods, Wasserstein stability estimates

AMS subject classifications:
65C35; 65K05; 90C56; 35Q90; 35Q83

1
Introduction

Min-max problems are a central class of optimization problems. They describe two competing agents, where one tries to minimize an objective function, whilst the other tries to maximize it. The min-max problem was first introduced in Nash’s seminal paper
[
33
]
, in which he established conditions for competing agents to reach an equilibria. The min-max problem has also found substantial applications in machine learning. Specifically, they have become a central research question for Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)
[
21
]
. Here, two neural networks compete with each other, the first tries to generate data, whilst the other tries to discriminate between the generated data and the real-world data. For a broader perspective of the history and application of the min-max problem, we refer the reader to
[
37
]
and the references therein.

Recently, a consensus-based optimization (CBO) algorithm has been proposed to find the global optimisers of nonconvex-nonconcave min-max problems
[
5
]
. The CBO algorithm has been originally proposed in
[
34
,
8
]
, which is inspired by collective behavior in nature, such as flocking birds or swarming fish. It is used to solve optimization problems by simulating the interaction of particles that collectively and stochastically move toward an optimal solution. The agents share information and adjust their positions based on a consensus mechanism, which drives the system toward a global optimum. The CBO algorithm offers several advantages, including its derivative-free nature and amenability to mathematical analysis. It has since been extended and adapted to a wide range of optimization settings, including such as saddle-point problems
[
28
]
, multi-level optimization
[
24
,
17
]
, multi-objective optimization
[
4
]
, constrained optimization
[
2
,
6
]
, and multi-player games
[
10
]
. Rather than attempting to include a necessarily incomplete
account of this very fast-growing field, we refer the interested reader to the review papers
[
35
]
and
[
36
]
for a more recent and
relatively comprehensive report.

The authors in
[
5
]
consider a two-agent CBO algorithm. We are given a cost function
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
. The first agent picks a decision, aiming to make the objective as small as possible under the second agent’s maximizing response. This means

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\;\mathcal{E}(x,y).

(Min-Max)

In order to define a global optimal solution to this problem, we introduce the definition of the upper envelope function

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{E}}(x):=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\mathcal{E}(x,y)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},

where this function may take the value
+\infty
. Now, we say that a point
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
is a gobal min-max solution to (
Min-Max
) if

\displaystyle x^{\ast}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\bar{\mathcal{E}}(x)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003pty^{\ast}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\mathcal{E}(x^{\ast},y).

Each agent is attributed a collection of
N
particles, which are denoted by the vectors
X^{1},\ldots,X^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
for the first one and
Y^{1},\ldots,Y^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
for the second one. Both agents have their respective consensus point that they drift towards, namely

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X,N},\rho_{t}^{Y,N})&:=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\;\omega^{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y,N},x))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{X,N}(x)}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega^{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y,N},x))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{X,N}(x)},\\
\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y,N},X_{t}^{i})&:=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\omega^{\mathcal{E}}_{-\beta}(X_{t}^{i},y)\,\mathrm{d}\rho^{Y,N}_{t}(y)}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega^{\mathcal{E}}_{-\beta}(X_{t}^{i},y)\,\mathrm{d}\rho^{Y,N}_{t}(y)},\end{split}

(1)

where for any value
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
we define the weights and the empirical approximations by

\displaystyle\omega_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}(x,y):=e^{-\gamma\mathcal{E}(x,y)},\hskip 9.24994pt\rho_{t}^{X,N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}},\hskip 9.24994pt\rho_{t}^{Y,N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Y_{t}^{i}}.

The continuous CBO dynamics is expressed by an interacting particle evolution system represented by the following stochastic differential equations, for every index
i\in[N]
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \,\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{i}=&-\lambda(X_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X,N},\rho_{t}^{Y,N}))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma D(X_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X,N},\rho_{t}^{Y,N}))\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{X,i},\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} \,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}^{i}=&-\lambda(Y_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y,N},X_{t}^{i}))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma D(Y_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y,N},X_{t}^{i}))\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{Y,i},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(CBO)

where
\lambda,\sigma>0
are drift and diffusion parameters respectively and
\{B^{m,i}\}_{m\in\{X,Y\},i\in[N]}
are independent Brownian motions. Here
D:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a (possibly agent-dependent) matrix-valued map that is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the Frobenius norm. For simplicity, we take either the anisotropic choice
D(x)=\mathrm{diag}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
or the isotropic choice
D(x)=|x|\,\mathrm{id}
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The initial law satisfies

\displaystyle\law(X_{0}^{1},Y_{0}^{1},\dots,X_{0}^{N},Y_{0}^{N})=(\rho_{0}^{X}\otimes\rho_{0}^{Y})^{\otimes N},

given initial probability distributions
\rho_{0}^{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\rho_{0}^{Y}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. The paper
[
5
]
formally states that the mean-field limit of the above particle dynamics is dictated by the following stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \,\mathrm{d}\overline{X}_{t}=&-\lambda(\overline{X}_{t}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{X},\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y}))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma D(\overline{X}_{t}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{X},\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y}))\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{X},\end{aligned}\\
\begin{aligned} \,\mathrm{d}\overline{Y}_{t}=&-\lambda(\overline{Y}_{t}-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma D(\overline{Y}_{t}-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{Y},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(MF CBO)

where we have the law of the two agents

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{t}^{X}:=\law\big(\,\overline{X}_{t}\big),\hskip 18.49988pt\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y}:=\law\big(\,\overline{Y}_{t}\big).

The initial law satisfies
\law(\overline{X}_{0},\overline{Y}_{0})=\overline{\rho}_{0}^{X}\otimes\overline{\rho}_{0}^{Y},

given initial probability distributions
\overline{\rho}_{0}^{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\overline{\rho}_{0}^{Y}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

1.1
Motivation

The authors
[
5
]
rigorously prove global convergence in the large particle limit. Specifically, they establish the convergence of the mean-field dynamics
(\overline{X}_{t},\overline{Y}_{t})
in (
MF CBO
) to the global min-max solution
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})
as
t
approaches infinity. To achieve this, they introduce the error functions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&V^{X}(t):=\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t}-x^{\ast}|^{2},\hskip 9.24994ptV^{Y}(t):=\mathbb{E}|\overline{Y}_{t}-y^{\ast}|^{2},\\
&V(t):=V^{X}(t)+V^{Y}(t).\end{split}

(2)

By analyzing the decay behavior of the (cumulative) error function
V(t)
, they demonstrate that
V(t)
decays exponentially at a finite time
T_{*}>0
, with a decay rate that can be controlled through the parameters of the CBO method. Specifically, it holds that

\displaystyle V(t)\leq V(0)\exp\left(-\frac{2\lambda-\sigma^{2}}{2}t\right)

for
t\in[0,T_{*}]
, and
V(T_{*})\leq\varepsilon
for any given accuracy
\varepsilon>0
. It is worth noting that one can also establish particle-level convergence of CBO methods directly, using a variance-based analysis; see, e.g.,
[
7
,
31
,
22
]
.

However, the justification for passing to the mean-field limit from the particle system (
CBO
) to the mean-field dynamics (
MF CBO
) remains only formal, and a rigorous mathematical analysis is absent in
[
5
]
. Establishing a rigorous proof of the CBO models has been a challenging task, primarily due to the fact that the consensus point defined in (
1
) is only locally Lipschitz.

Recent years have seen substantial progress on the mean-field limit analysis for CBO. For example,
[
14
,
23
]
derived mean-field limit estimates for CBO variants on compact manifolds by assuming a globally Lipschitz consensus point. Later,
[
26
]
proved the mean-field limit for the standard CBO model via a compactness argument based on Prokhorov’s theorem; however, this method did not yield an explicit convergence rate in terms of the particle number
N
. Subsequent work
[
15
,
27
]
established a probabilistic mean-field approximation, showing that the limit estimate holds with high probability. The high-probability restriction was removed in
[
19
]
through an improved stability bound for the consensus point, which was further extended to the multi-species setting in
[
29
]
.
Finally, uniform-in-time type of mean-field estimates have been recently obtained
[
25
,
18
,
1
,
11
]
.

The primary objective of this paper is to address some theoretical gaps in
[
5
]
by providing a quantitative estimate of the mean-field limit with respect to the number
N
of particles. Additionally, we establish the well-posedness of both the finite particle model (
CBO
) and the mean-field dynamics (
MF CBO
). We extend the results of
[
19
]
to settings where the dynamics depend on several distributions with inter-agent dependencies, rather than a single distribution.

For this reason we cannot take this extension for granted, as it is not clear that the same mean-field argument still holds and if the convergence rate remains unchanged. This extension requires several non-trivial steps, such as the measure–variable stability result in Lemma
2.2
, the coupled stability result in Lemma
2.3
, the weighted moment bound in Lemma
2.6
, the convergence established in Lemma
2.9
that is uniform with respect to the state of the agent
X
, or the coupled convergence in Lemma
2.10
. Our proof for the well-posedness of (
MF CBO
) (See Section
5
) is fundamentally different due to the agent–dependent nature of the consensus function
\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})
for agent
Y
. Consequently, the results presented in
[
8
]
cannot be directly applied. Finally, in the proof of Theorem
1.3
for the mean-field limit, we need to take the mean-field limit in two measures simultaneously, and the mean-field limit of the agent
Y
must happen uniformly among all possible states of the agent
X
. Our approach therefore both leverages and advances the methodology of
[
19
]
.

1.2
Main Results

At first we ferment the notation that we use throughout the paper. Given any radius
R>0
, we define the closed ball of radius
R
centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
by
B_{R}
. We denote by
|x|
and
|A|
the absolute value of a vector
x
the Lebesgue measure of a Borel measurable Euclidean subset
A
. For any positive values
1<p<\infty
and
R>0
we let
\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(or
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) denote the space of probability measures with
p
-moment bounded by
R
(or finite
p
-moment), that means

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq R,\hskip 9.24994pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)<\infty,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The Wasserstein distance between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the minimal transportation cost required to move one distribution to the other, given by

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\right)^{1/p},

where we take the infimum over all
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying the constraint
\pi(A\times\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mu(A)
and
\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A)=\nu(A)
for all Borel measurable
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We denote the set of all admissible
\gamma
, called a coupling, by
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
. We define the expected value of a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mu]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

For any random vector
X
and value
p\geq 1
we write
\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}
as a shorthand for
\mathbb{E}\big[|X|^{p}\big]
. Finally, for any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we let
\delta_{x}
denote the Dirac measure at
x
.

Throughout this paper we assume that the cost function is bounded and that the Lipschitz constant has polynomial growth.

(A1)

There exists a constant
c>0
such that we have boundedness

\displaystyle-c\leq\mathcal{E}(x,y)\leq c.

(A2)

There exists constants
C>0
and
s\geq 0
such that for
z_{0},z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
, where we set the vector
z_{k}=(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
for
k\in\{0,1\}
, we have the polynomial-growth Lipschitz property

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(z_{0})-\mathcal{E}(z_{1})|\leq C(1+|z_{0}|+|z_{1}|)^{s}\,|z_{0}-z_{1}|.

We develop a novel existence theory for the nonlinear coupled setting by significantly adapting the strategy of
[
19
, Theorems 2.2–2.3]
, overcoming key challenges arising from the coupling that are absent in the original uncoupled framework.

Theorem 1.1

(Existence and uniqueness for (
CBO
))
.

Let Assumptions
(A2)
hold. Then the SDEs (
CBO
) posses unique strong solutions
\{(X_{t}^{i},Y_{t}^{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
for any initial conditions
\{(X_{0}^{i},Y_{0}^{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
that are independent of the Brownian motions
\{(B_{t}^{X,i},B_{t}^{Y,i})\}_{i\in[N]}
. The solutions are almost surely continuous.

Theorem 1.2

(Existence and uniqueness for (
MF CBO
))
.

Let Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold, let
p>2
if
s=0
, otherwise let
p\geq 2+s
. Then, for all
T>0
,
\overline{\rho}_{0}^{X}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and
\overline{\rho}_{0}^{Y}\in\mathcal{P}_{p+\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
, there exists unique processes
\overline{X}:\Omega\to C^{0}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and
\overline{Y}:\Omega\to C^{0}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
satisfying (
MF CBO
) in the strong sense with initial condition
\law(\overline{X}_{0},\overline{Y}_{0})=\overline{\rho}_{0}^{X}\otimes\overline{\rho}_{0}^{Y}
. Furthermore, we have the bounds

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[0,T]\end{subarray}}\;|\overline{X}_{t}|^{p}+|\overline{Y}_{t}|^{p}+|\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{X},\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y})|+|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t})|\right]<\infty,\\
&\overline{\rho}_{t}^{X}=\law\big(\,\overline{X}_{t}\big),\hskip 9.24994pt\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y}=\law\big(\,\overline{Y}_{t}\big)\end{split}

and the function
t\mapsto(\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{X},\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y}),\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t}^{Y},\overline{X}_{t}))
is continuous over
[0,T]
.

Furthermore, we also close a key gap in
[
5
]
, by providing the first quantitative convergence rate for the mean-field limit.

Theorem 1.3

(Mean-field limit of (
CBO
))
.

Let Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold with
q\geq 2(2+s)
,
p\in(0,\frac{q}{2}]
,
\rho_{0}^{X}\in\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and
\rho_{0}^{Y}\in\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
. We assume the particles
\{(X^{i},Y^{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
satisfy (
CBO
) and
\{(\overline{X}^{i},\overline{Y}^{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
are
N
i.i.d. samples for each player from (

). They both use the same standard Brownian motions

T>0
, there exists a positive constant

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}\;\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\;\big|X_{t}^{i}-\overline{X}_{t}^{i}\big|^{p}+\big|Y_{t}^{i}-\overline{Y}_{t}^{i}\big|^{p}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq CN^{-\gamma},

2.1
Wasserstein stability estimate for weighted mean

Lemma 2.1

(Estimate on weighted mean)
.

Let
(A1)
hold and let
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then, for every
\mu^{X}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
,
\mu^{Y}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
and
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
there exists constants
C_{0},C_{1}>0
such that

(C1)

We can bound the weights from below

\displaystyle C_{0}\leq\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptC_{0}\leq\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(U,\cdot)].

(C2)

We can bound the weighted means from above

\displaystyle\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

\displaystyle\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(U,\cdot)]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|y|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(C3)

We can bound the consensus from above

\displaystyle|\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu^{X},\mu^{Y})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{0}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

\displaystyle|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},U)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{0}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|y|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Proof.

The lower bound
(C1)
is clear by
(A1)
. For the upper bound
(C2)
we use
(A1)
and the Jensen inequality. Finally,
(C3)
is an immediate consequence of
(C1)
and
(C2)
.
∎

Lemma 2.2

(Basic stability estimate for
\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}
)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold. Then, for all
R>0
and
p\geq s+2
, there exists a constant
C>0
depending on
R
and
p
such that the following assertions hold true:

(B1)

For all
\mu^{Y}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
and
U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},U)-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},V)|\leq C\,(1+|U|+|V|)^{s}\;|U-V|.

(B2)

For all
\mu^{Y},\nu^{Y}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},X)-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},X)|\leq C\,(1+|X|)^{s}\;W_{p}(\mu^{Y},\nu^{Y}).

Proof.

First we prove
(B1)
. Thus, we estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},U)-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},V)|\\
&\leq\frac{|\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(U,y)]-\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(V,y)]|}{\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(U,y)]}+\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(V,y)]\,\frac{|\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(U,y)]-\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(V,y)]|}{\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(U,y)]\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(V,y)]}.\end{split}

(3)

We can bound all terms except for the differences in (
3
) by using
(C1)
and
(C2)
of Lemma
2.1
respectively. Hence, we only need to bound the differences. To that end, we compute

\displaystyle|\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(U,y)]-\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(V,y)]|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|y|\,|\omega_{-\beta}(U,y)-\omega_{-\beta}(V,y)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}(y).

We use
(A2)
and Jensen’s inequality and find

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|y|\,|\omega_{-\beta}(U,y)-\omega_{-\beta}(V,y)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|y|\,(1+|U|+|V|+|y|)^{s}\,|U-V|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|y|^{s+1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|y|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}(y)\,(1+|U|+|V|)^{s}\right)\,|U-V|

\displaystyle\leq C\,(1+|U|+|V|)^{s}\;|U-V|.

Here
C>0
is a constant that depends on
p
and
R
and changes line from line. We bound the other numerator in (
3
) similarly. By combining all inequalities we see that
(B1)
holds.

Next, we prove
(B2)
. Thus, we estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},X)-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},X)|\\
&\leq\frac{|\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]-\nu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]|}{\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]}+\nu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]\,\frac{|\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]-\nu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]|}{\mu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]\nu^{Y}[\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]}.\end{split}

(4)

We can bound all the terms apart from the differences in (
4
) by using
(C1)
and
(C2)
of Lemma
2.1
respectively. Hence, we only need to bound the numerators. To that end, for any coupling
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu^{Y},\nu^{Y})
we compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|\mu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]-\nu^{Y}[y\,\omega_{-\beta}(X,y)]|\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|u\,\omega_{-\beta}(X,u)-v\,\omega_{-\beta}(X,v)|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\omega_{-\beta}(X,u)\,|u-v|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|v|\,|\omega_{-\beta}(X,u)-\omega_{-\beta}(X,v)|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v).\end{split}

(5)

For the first term on the RHS in (
5
), we use
(A1)
and Jensen’s inequality to derive the bound

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\omega_{-\beta}(X,u)\,|u-v|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)\leq e^{\beta c}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|u-v|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

For the second term on the RHS in (
5
), we use
(A2)
, the Hölder inequality, and that
\frac{(s+1)p}{p-1}\leq p

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|v|\,|\omega_{-\beta}(X,u)-\omega_{-\beta}(X,v)|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}(1+|u|+|v|)^{s+1}|u-v|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)+C|X|^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|v|\,|u-v|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+|X|^{s}\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|u-v|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(u,v)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Here
C>0
is a constant that depends on
p
and
R
and changes from line to line. We derive a similar estimate for the other difference in (
4
). Thus, by taking the infimum over all couplings
\gamma
, we see that
(B2)
holds.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(Basic Stability Estimate for
\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}
)
.

Let Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold. Let
R>0
and
p\geq 2(s+1)
. Then, there exists
C>0
depending on
R
and
p
, such that for any pairs
\mu^{X},\nu^{X}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and
\mu^{Y},\nu^{Y}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
the following inequality holds

\displaystyle|\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu^{X},\mu^{Y})-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\nu^{X},\nu^{Y})|\leq C(W_{p}(\mu^{X},\nu^{X})+W_{p}(\mu^{Y},\nu^{Y}))\,.

Proof.

The goal of this proof is to derive estimates to the following four terms

\displaystyle|\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu^{X},\mu^{Y})-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\nu^{X},\nu^{Y})|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}-\frac{\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}\right|

(I)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\left|\frac{\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}-\frac{\nu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}\right|

(II)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\left|\frac{\nu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}-\frac{\nu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}\right|

(III)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\left|\frac{\nu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}-\frac{\nu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}{\nu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]}\right|.

(IV)

Step 1: Bounding (
I
):

We need to devise an estimate of the form

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:conx_est_bound_one}\leq C\;W_{p}(\mu^{Y},\nu^{Y})

(6)

for some constant

depending on
p
and
R
. Thus, we first devise the representation

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:conx_est_bound_one}=\frac{|\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]-\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]|}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}.

We can bound the denominator using
(C1)
of Lemma
2.1
. Then, for the numerator we compute using
(A1)
and
(A2)

\displaystyle|\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]-\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|x\,\omega_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},x))-x\,\omega_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x))|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|x|\,(1+|x|+|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x)|+|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},x)|)^{s}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}}\times|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},x)-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}(x).

We use
(C3)
of Lemma
2.1

of Lemma
2.2

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|x|\,(1+|x|+|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x))|+|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},x))|)^{s}

\displaystyle\phantom{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}}\times|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},x)-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\;W_{p}(\mu^{Y},\nu^{Y})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}(1+|x|)^{2s+1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}(x).

By using the Jensen inequality and the fact that
2s+1\leq p
, we find that the inequality (
6
) holds true.

Step 2: Bounding (
II
):

We need to devise an estimate of the form

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:conx_est_bound_two}\leq C\;W_{p}(\mu^{X},\nu^{X})

(7)

for some constant

depending on
p
and
R

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:conx_est_bound_two}=\frac{|\mu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]-\nu^{X}[x\,\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},\cdot))]|}{\mu^{X}[\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\mu^{Y},\cdot))]}.

We can bound the denominator using
(C1)
of Lemma

&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|x-y|\,\omega_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x))\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\\
&\phantom{\leq}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|y|\,|\omega_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x))-\omega_{\alpha}(y,\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},y))|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y).\end{split}

\displaystyle\phantom{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}}\times\big(|x-y|+|\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},x)-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\nu^{Y},y)|\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)

(Stability estimate for
\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}
)
.

(A1)
and
(A2)

and

Lemma 2.6

(Moment estimates for the empirical measures)
.

Let Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold and let
p\geq 2
,
\bar{\rho}_{0}^{X}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
,
\bar{\rho}_{0}^{Y}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})

be solutions to the SDEs (

\kappa>0
that does not depend on

such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[N]\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\;|X_{t}^{i}|^{p}+|Y_{t}^{i}|^{p}\right]\leq\kappa\,\big(\mathbb{E}|X_{0}^{1}|^{p}+\mathbb{E}|Y_{0}^{1}|^{p}\big).

Proof.

First, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
N
such that we can estimate the drift and diffusion terms by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|X_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X,N},\rho_{t}^{Y,N})|^{p}\vee\|D(X_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{t}^{X,N},\rho_{t}^{Y,N}))\|^{p}\leq C\left(|X_{t}^{i}|^{p}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{X,N}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\end{split}

(11)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|Y_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y,N},X_{t}^{i})|^{p}\vee\|D(Y_{t}^{i}-\mathbb{Y}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}^{Y,N},X_{t}^{i}))\|^{p}\leq C\left(|Y_{t}^{i}|^{p}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{Y,N}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\end{split}

(12)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Frobenius norm, and we have used the consensus estimate
(C3)
of Lemma
2.1
. Next we apply the Burkholder-Davis-Grundy (BDG) inequality
[
32
, Chapter 1, Theorem 7.3]
and the bounds (
11
) and (
12
) to find for all
t\in[0,T]
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3^{p-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|X_{s}^{i}\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|X_{0}^{i}|^{p}+\lambda^{p}T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left|X_{s}^{i}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{s}^{X,N},\rho_{s}^{Y,N})\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+T^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\sigma^{p}C_{\mathrm{DBG}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left|D(X_{s}^{i}-\mathbb{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho_{s}^{X,N},\rho_{s}^{Y,N}))\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|X_{0}^{i}|^{p}+C\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}^{i}|^{p}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}^{X,N}(x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

As the particles
X^{1},\ldots,X^{N}
are exchangeable, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{X,N}(x)\right]=\mathbb{E}|X_{t}^{i}|^{p}.

Hence, for some constant
C>0
, we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|X_{s}^{i}\right|^{p}\right]\leq C\left(\mathbb{E}|X_{0}^{i}|^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,s]}\left|X_{r}^{i}\right|^{p}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Similarly we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|Y_{s}^{i}\right|^{p}\right]\leq C\left(\mathbb{E}|Y_{0}^{i}|^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,s]}\left|Y_{r}^{i}\right|^{p}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

The result now follows from the Grönwall inequality.
∎
