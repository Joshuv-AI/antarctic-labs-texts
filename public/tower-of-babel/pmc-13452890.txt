Clinical Outcomes and Mechanical Complications After Adult Spinal Deformity Surgery in Patients Aged 80 Years or Older: A Propensity Score-Matched Comparative Study
Authors: Kai Inoue, Atsuyuki Kawabata, Jun Hashimoto, Takayuki Motoyoshi, Kurando Utagawa, Yu Matsukura, Takuya Oyaizu, Takashi Hirai, Hiroaki Onuma, Satoru Egawa, Kentaro Yamada, Shingo Morishita, Kazuo Kusano, Kazuyuki Otani, Shigeo Shindo, Toshitaka Yoshii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignRetrospective cohort study.ObjectivesTo compare outcomes after adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery between patients aged 80 years or older and those younger than 80 years.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients aged 65 years or older with ASD who underwent long-segment posterior corrective fusion. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to age at surgery (<80 years and ≥80 years). Propensity score matching was performed to adjust for baseline differences between groups. Because PJFx was more frequent in the super-elderly group, logistic regression analysis was additionally performed to identify factors associated with PJFx in patients aged 80 years or older.ResultsA total of 386 patients were included, of whom 90 were aged 80 years or older. Before matching, the super-elderly group had significantly higher rates of PJFx and mechanical failure. After matching, 60 pairs were analyzed, and no significant differences in mechanical complications were observed between the groups. In patients aged 80 years or older, lower upper instrumented vertebra Hounsfield unit value and lower postoperative sacral slope were independently associated with PJFx.ConclusionsAfter adjustment for baseline characteristics, postoperative mechanical complication rates were comparable between age groups. Risk stratification in super-elderly patients should incorporate bone quality and the postoperative mechanical environment rather than chronological age alone.Keywords: adult spinal deformity, super-elderly, octogenarian, proximal junctional fracture, proximal junctional kyphosis, mechanical complication, frailty

Background

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) is becoming increasingly prevalent in aging societies and is associated with substantial impairment in health-related quality of life as a consequence of chronic axial pain, spinal imbalance, and neurological symptoms.1,2 Recent studies have demonstrated that pain relief and functional improvement are superior after surgical correction than with nonoperative treatment, even in elderly patients, leading to an expansion of surgical indications in this population.3-5 However, ASD surgery is highly invasive, and elderly patients are particularly vulnerable to postoperative complications because of reduced physiological reserve.6,7 Higher rates of both medical complications and mechanical failure, including proximal junctional kyphosis and rod-related complications, have been reported in older patients, often necessitating revision surgery and adversely affecting clinical outcomes.8-12 Most previous studies have analyzed patients aged ≥65 or ≥70 years as a single “elderly” cohort, whereas evidence focusing specifically on super-elderly patients aged ≥80 years remains limited.4,7,13 This evidence gap is clinically important because spine surgeons are increasingly required to determine whether corrective surgery should be offered to octogenarian patients despite the potential risk of postoperative complications. In particular, it remains unclear whether the higher rate of postoperative mechanical failure observed in very elderly patients is attributable to chronological age itself or to differences in baseline frailty, bone quality, spinal alignment, and surgical factors. Although chronological age has traditionally been considered an independent risk factor for postoperative complications, emerging evidence suggests that physiological factors may play a more critical role in determining surgical outcomes.10,11,14,15 Nevertheless, it remains unclear to what extent age itself influences postoperative outcomes when these background factors are taken into account in patients aged ≥80 years.16,17 This study compared the clinical and radiographic outcomes after corrective surgery for ASD, as well as postoperative complication rates, between patients aged ≥80 years and those aged <80 years after adjustment for baseline characteristics. The aim was to determine whether postoperative outcomes in super-elderly patients are attributable to chronological age alone, and to provide evidence to support surgical decision-making and preoperative risk stratification in this growing patient population (Figure 1).

Methods

We retrospectively reviewed all patients who underwent surgery for ASD at our hospital between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2023. The study period was limited to this interval because, after 2023, modern proximal junctional prophylaxis strategies were increasingly adopted at our institution. To maintain a relatively homogeneous cohort without the confounding influence of these prophylactic techniques, patients treated after 2023 were not included. Institutional review board approval was secured, permitting patient enrollment and data collection (approval number 2019-5). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board due to the retrospective design and use of de-identified data; patients were provided the opportunity to opt out via institutional public disclosure. The inclusion criteria were age ≥65 years at the time of surgery, a minimum postoperative follow-up of 1 year, surgery that involved posterior instrumentation spanning 5 or more levels, and complete radiographic data available. The underlying causes identified included degenerative kyphosis/kyphoscoliosis, complications following lumbar surgery, and vertebral fractures. Deformities secondary to a tumor or spinal infection were excluded. Three-column osteotomy entailed use of pedicle subtraction osteotomy or vertebral column resection. Surgical procedures included multiple transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), staged oblique lateral interbody fusion (OLIF) with posterior fusion, and osteotomy, selected according to the surgeon’s preference. UIV level was categorized as T6 or above or T7 or below. Proximal fixation was uniform across the cohort, with pedicle screws used in all cases without hooks, ligament augmentation/tethering, or cement augmentation. All constructs consisted of two titanium rods. The general alignment goal was to achieve a postoperative PI-LL mismatch of less than 10°, although the final correction target was determined at the surgeon’s discretion. Postoperative mechanical complications included proximal junctional fracture (PJFx), rod fracture, and distal junctional failure (DJF). Any mechanical complication was defined as the occurrence of PJFx, rod fracture, or DJF. Proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) was defined as kyphosis exceeding 10° between the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) and the two vertebra above, 18 with an increase of at least 10° compared with the preoperative measurement. Because PJK is a broad radiographic entity, the present analysis focused on PJFx as fracture-type PJK, which represents structural bony failure at the proximal junction and was considered a more severe and clinically relevant mechanical endpoint in this elderly cohort. PJFx requiring reoperation was defined as PJFx necessitating revision surgery because of neurological deterioration, progressive deformity, mechanical instability, or intractable pain. Rod fracture was defined as radiographic breakage of the posterior instrumentation rod at any level. Rod fracture requiring reoperation was defined as rod fracture necessitating revision surgery. DJF was defined as clinically significant distal adjacent segment pathology necessitating revision surgery, including neurological deterioration, segmental instability, collapse, or other distal junctional disorders, regardless of the presence of kyphotic deformity. Because DJF is often difficult to define consistently on radiographs alone and is usually identified as symptomatic distal adjacent segment pathology requiring revision surgery, DJF was defined as a reoperation-defined event in the present study. Postoperative radiographic surveillance was conducted as part of routine clinical follow-up and varied slightly according to the surgeon’s practice. In general, standing radiographs were obtained weekly during the initial postoperative hospitalization and subsequently at approximately 1,3,6, and 12 months after surgery. Thereafter, radiographs were obtained at least annually. Computed tomography was not routinely performed for all patients, but was obtained when mechanical complications, including fracture at or around the UIV, rod fracture, distal junctional failure, or other structural failure, were suspected on the basis of clinical symptoms or radiographic findings. Mechanical failure was defined as PJFx requiring reoperation, rod fracture requiring reoperation, or DJF. Surgical site infections and all-cause mortality during the follow-up period were also recorded. Overall survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences between age groups were assessed using the log-rank test, and 5-year overall survival rates were estimated from the Kaplan–Meier curves. Patient-reported outcomes were available in a subset of patients and were analyzed as an exploratory supplementary analysis. These outcomes included preoperative and 1-year postoperative visual analog scale scores for low back pain, leg pain, and leg numbness, as well as Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SRS-22) domain and total scores. In the present scoring system, higher SRS-22 scores indicated worse status.

We collected data for age, sex, body mass index, and bone mineral density at the femoral neck (T-score). Frailty status was assessed using the 11-item modified frailty index (mFI-11), 19 and nutritional status was evaluated using the Controlling Nutritional Status score.20,21 Radiographic measurements included the sagittal vertical axis, thoracic kyphosis from T4 to T10, lumbar lordosis from L1 to S1, sacral slope, pelvic incidence, and pelvic tilt. Global tilt was defined as the sum of the pelvic tilt and the C7 vertical tilt (i.e., the angle created by the intersection of two lines); the first drawn from the center of the C7 vertebra to the center of the sacral endplate and the second drawn from the center of the femoral head to the center of the sacral endplate.22,23 The positioning of the lower instrumented vertebra, whether fixed to the sacrum or not, was also examined. These parameters were assessed in a standing position before and 2 weeks after surgery. Ideal spinal alignment was defined as a pelvic incidence–lumbar lordosis mismatch angle within –10° to +10° according to the Scoliosis Research Society–Schwab ASD classification.24,25 The Hounsfield unit (HU) value at the UIV was assessed in the midsagittal view on preoperative computed tomography images using a circular region of interest placed within the cancellous portion of the vertebral body while avoiding the cortical margins.10,11 Preoperative comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, renal disease, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, and Parkinson disease, were recorded. Medication-related variables included steroid use and preoperative and postoperative osteoporosis medication. Surgical invasiveness variables included operative time and blood loss, which were analyzed as continuous variables using available data.

Patients were divided according to age at surgery into those aged <80 years and those aged ≥80 years. First, univariate analyses of all clinical, surgical, radiographic, and outcome variables were performed to evaluate age-related differences in baseline characteristics and postoperative outcomes. To minimize measured baseline differences between groups, propensity score matching was performed using sex, body mass index, mFI-11 score, preoperative lumbar lordosis, preoperative sagittal vertical axis, and HU values at the UIV. Nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed with a caliper width of 0.4 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. After propensity score matching, the balance between groups was assessed using standardized mean differences. As a sensitivity analysis for potential overadjustment, additional propensity score matching was performed after excluding mFI-11 and UIV HU from the matching model.

After the age-based comparisons and propensity score-matched analyses, further risk factor analyses were conducted in the patients aged ≥80 years. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for PJFx in this super-elderly cohort. PJFx was selected as the primary endpoint for regression analysis because it represents the most frequent and clinically relevant mechanical complication and is considered the key determinant of postoperative neurological deterioration and need for reoperation in elderly patients.

Univariable analysis was performed for selected candidate variables considered relevant to the study objective. As a sensitivity analysis, patients aged 78–82 years were excluded to reduce the potential influence of the arbitrary age cutoff, and the comparison between patients aged <80 and ≥80 years was repeated (Supplementary Table 1).

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test and categorical variables using the chi-squared test. Variables with a p-value of <0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate logistic regression model. Missing data were minimal for demographic, surgical, radiographic, and complication variables; therefore, the analyses were performed using a complete-case approach, and no imputation was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 386 patients aged ≥65 years who underwent corrective surgery for ASD were included in the study. Table 1 shows the baseline patient characteristics and postoperative outcomes. The mean age at surgery was 75.3 ± 5.4 years, and 327 patients were female (84.7%) and 59 (15.3%) were male. The mean body mass index (calculated as kg/m2) was 22.5 ± 3.7. The mean number of fused levels was 7.3 ± 1.6. Three-column osteotomy was performed in 176 patients (45.6%), with fixation to the sacrum performed in 314 (81.3%). In terms of postoperative mechanical complications, PJFx occurred in 123 patients (31.9%), 38 (9.8%) of whom required reoperation for PJFx. Rod fracture occurred in 36 patients (9.3%), with 20 (5.2%) requiring reoperation. DJF was observed in 22 patients (5.7%). Overall, any mechanical complication occurred in 162 patients (42.0%), and mechanical failure occurred in 71 patients (18.3%).

Among the 386 patients, 296 were younger than 80 years, and 90 were aged ≥80 years. Table 2 shows the baseline demographic, surgical, and radiographic characteristics according to whether patient age was <80 years or ≥80 years. Compared with patients aged <80 years, those aged ≥80 years had a significantly lower body mass index and a significantly lower T-score as well as a significantly higher mFI-11 score and lower UIV HU value. Preoperative sagittal vertical axis and global tilt were also significantly greater in the group aged ≥80 years, whereas surgical variables, including number of fused levels, frequency of three-column osteotomy, and frequency of fixation to the sacrum did not differ significantly between age groups. Diabetes mellitus, renal disease, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, Parkinson disease, steroid use, preoperative osteoporosis medication use, and blood loss did not show major between-group imbalances, whereas operative time was shorter in the ≥80-year group. Table 3 shows the postoperative complications in patients aged <80 and ≥80 years. Postoperatively, the incidence of any mechanical complication was significantly higher in patients aged ≥80 years (54.4% vs 38.2%, p=0.006). The incidence of PJFx was also significantly higher in patients aged ≥80 years (44.4% vs 28.0%, p=0.003), as was the incidence of PJFx requiring reoperation (16.7% vs 7.8%, p=0.012). The incidence of mechanical failure was also significantly higher in the group aged ≥80 years (27.8% vs 15.5%, p=0.005). Rates of rod fracture, rod fracture requiring reoperation, DJF, and surgical site infection did not differ significantly between groups. In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients aged 78–82 years, patients aged ≥80 years still showed significantly higher rates of PJFx, PJFx requiring reoperation, and mechanical failure than those aged <80 years (Supplementary Table 1). The 5-year overall survival rate was 97.3% in patients aged <80 years and 88.9% in those aged ≥80 years. Kaplan–Meier curves differed significantly between groups (χ2=20.659, p<0.001, log-rank test). Median survival was not reached in either group. One-year patient-reported outcome (PRO) data were available in a subset of the cohort. Although most preoperative PRO measures were similar between groups, the ≥80-year group had worse postoperative VAS low back pain and a smaller improvement in SRS-22 total score at 1 year. These exploratory findings are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

After propensity score matching, 60 matched pairs of patients aged <80 years and ≥80 years were analyzed (Table 4). All baseline demographic, clinical, and radiographic variables were well balanced between the groups, with standardized mean differences of ≤0.1. After matching, there was no significant between-group difference in the incidence of PJFx, PJFx requiring reoperation, rod fracture, rod fracture requiring reoperation, DJF, any mechanical complication, or mechanical failure. Radiographic parameters before and after surgery were also comparable between the two groups. In the sensitivity propensity score-matched analysis excluding mFI-11 and UIV HU from the matching model, 60 matched pairs were analyzed (Supplementary Table 3). PJFx remained numerically higher in patients aged ≥80 years with a borderline between-group difference (56.7% vs 36.7%, p=0.050), whereas any mechanical complication (66.7% vs 50.0%, p=0.110) and mechanical failure (36.7% vs 28.3%, p=0.359) did not differ significantly between groups.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed in patients aged ≥80 years to identify risk factors for PJFx in super-elderly patients (Table 5). In univariate analysis, a lower serum albumin level, a lower UIV HU value, and a lower postoperative sacral slope were significantly associated with PJFx. In multivariate analysis, a lower UIV HU value (odds ratio [OR] 0.984; 95% confidence interval 0.971–0.998; p=0.028) and a lower postoperative sacral slope (OR 0.922; 95% confidence interval 0.865–0.981; p=0.011) remained independently associated with PJFx. Nutritional indices, including serum albumin and the Controlling Nutritional Status score, were not independently associated with PJFx after adjustment.

Discussion

Advanced chronological age has traditionally been considered to be an independent risk factor for complications after ASD.7,13 However, accumulating evidence highlights a potential dissociation between chronological age and physiological age, suggesting that carefully selected older patients can tolerate more invasive reconstruction and still achieve acceptable outcomes.16,26

In our unmatched cohort, patients aged ≥80 years had a significantly higher frailty burden (mFI-11 score, 1.6 vs 1.1) and a poorer skeletal substrate, including a lower UIV HU value (110.6 vs 120.1) and a lower T-score for bone mineral density (−2.2 vs −1.8). Consistent with these baseline differences, the group aged ≥80 years had higher rates of PJFx and overall mechanical failure before adjustment. However, rod fracture and DJF, which were also included in the composite mechanical endpoints, were less frequent than PJFx and showed similar rates between age groups. Supplementary analysis of available PRO data suggested that improvement in PRO may have been smaller in octogenarians, particularly for residual low back pain and SRS-22 total score. Importantly, after balancing key physiological and skeletal factors, including UIV HU, bone mineral density, and frailty, these between-group differences in complication rates were no longer statistically significant. However, in the sensitivity propensity score-matched analysis excluding mFI-11 and UIV HU from the matching model, PJFx remained numerically higher in patients aged ≥80 years with a borderline between-group difference. This suggests that the primary matched analysis may attenuate part of the total age-related risk signal by adjusting for age-related skeletal and physiological factors. Collectively, these findings suggest that chronological age alone may not fully explain postoperative risk; rather, a composite of local bone quality (as reflected by UIV HU) and physiological reserve likely contributes substantially to mechanical vulnerability in the “oldest-old” ASD population.

In the subgroup analysis limited to patients aged ≥80 years, frailty was not a significant predictor of PJFx (p=0.67), whereas a lower UIV HU value (OR 0.984) and postoperative sacral slope (OR 0.922) emerged as independent predictors. Frailty reflects global physiological reserve, but its range within the “super-elderly” stratum may be insufficient to stratify junctional mechanical complications. In contrast, the construct-supporting substrate (UIV bone quality) and postoperative mechanical environment (spinopelvic alignment) better captured vulnerability to PJFx.

In terms of alignment, the finding that a higher postoperative sacral slope was associated with a lower risk of PJFx (OR 0.922) requires careful interpretation. Given that the sacral slope is a postoperative parameter and does not directly translate into a specific corrective maneuver, we interpret a lower postoperative sacral slope as a marker of residual pelvic compensation and a potentially unfavorable mechanical environment at the proximal junction.27,28 Therefore, rather than supporting age-driven realignment, our results suggest that octogenarians with a low postoperative sacral slope, especially those with a low UIV HU value, may warrant closer surveillance and intensified management for osteoporosis (e.g., consideration of anabolic therapy) to mitigate the risk of junctional failure.

A key next step is to translate the computed tomography-derived HU value into a clinically usable risk stratification tool. Although HU-based thresholds are not universally transferable across scanners and protocols,10,11 our findings suggest that a low UIV HU value in octogenarians captures structural vulnerability that is more informative about mechanical risk than chronological age alone. Rather than treating a single cutoff as a definitive treatment threshold, the HU value may be used to define a “higher-risk” range that triggers consideration of junctional prophylaxis and intensified management of osteoporosis, particularly when additional mechanical risk factors are present. In our octogenarian cohort, the receiver-operating characteristic-derived discriminatory range clustered around 102 HU; however, this value should be viewed as cohort-specific and hypothesis-generating rather than a universal intervention threshold.

When preoperative assessment suggests compromised local bone quality (e.g., a low UIV HU value and/or low bone mineral density), it may be reasonable to consider individualized proximal junctional prophylaxis as part of a bundled strategy, while acknowledging that the evidence base remains heterogeneous and patient selection is key. Reported options include (i) posterior ligamentous/tether augmentation to create a more gradual transition zone and limit excessive junctional motion,29,30 (ii) prophylactic vertebral cement augmentation at the UIV and/or the level immediately above, which has been associated with lower rates of proximal junctional complications in some cohorts and meta-analyses, albeit with variability in indications and technique,31,32 and (iii) hook-based “soft-landing” constructs at the proximal end, intended to create a less abrupt transition in stiffness and to preserve posterior soft tissue attachments.33,34 However, supporting evidence continues to evolve and techniques vary across studies.

Taken together, the present findings suggest that mechanical risk after ASD surgery in octogenarians should not be judged by chronological age alone. Instead, risk stratification should incorporate physiological reserve, local bone quality, postoperative mechanical environment, and the limitations of reoperation-defined endpoints. Because PRO data were available only in a subset of patients, they were analyzed as an exploratory unmatched comparison. Therefore, the present data cannot determine whether the less favorable PRO findings in patients aged ≥80 years were independent of mechanical complications, baseline differences, frailty, or selection bias. Nevertheless, these exploratory findings suggest that postoperative symptoms and perceived recovery should be considered alongside radiographic and mechanical endpoints when evaluating surgical outcomes in octogenarians.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design introduces the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding despite propensity score matching and multivariable adjustment. In particular, because surgical indications were determined clinically, the octogenarian cohort may have represented a relatively selected population with better physiological reserve than the general super-elderly ASD population. In addition, the predominance of female patients and relatively low body mass index may reflect the patient population treated at our institution. Therefore, the present findings may not be fully generalizable to broader ASD populations with different demographic profiles, higher body mass index, or a greater burden of comorbidities. Second, follow-up in patients aged 80 years or older may have been influenced by death, frailty progression, or loss to follow-up, which could have affected the observed rates of complications and reoperations. Third, although PJFx itself was assessed as an objective radiographic endpoint, reoperation-defined endpoints may have introduced endpoint-related bias. PJFx requiring reoperation and mechanical failure reflect clinically important events, but they also depend on the decision to perform revision surgery. This decision may be influenced by chronological age, patient preference, frailty, comorbidities, perceived surgical risk, surgeon judgment, and institutional treatment strategy. Therefore, reoperation-defined endpoints may underestimate clinically relevant junctional complications, particularly in octogenarians, who may be less likely to undergo revision surgery despite radiographic or symptomatic complications. The propensity score model included variables such as UIV HU and mFI-11, which may be age-related mediators rather than conventional confounders; therefore, the matched analysis should not be interpreted as estimating the total causal effect of chronological age. Residual confounding from comorbidities, medication exposure, and surgical invasiveness should also be considered. Although diabetes mellitus, renal disease, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, Parkinson disease, steroid use, osteoporosis medication, operative time, and blood loss were collected, the severity of these conditions, medication dose and duration, adherence to osteoporosis treatment, and other unmeasured comorbidities could not be fully accounted for.

Fourth, the study period spanned approximately 10 years, during which surgical decision-making, perioperative management, and reconstructive strategies may have evolved, potentially introducing temporal heterogeneity. In addition, because this cohort was treated before the routine implementation of modern proximal junctional prophylaxis at our institution, the findings may not be directly generalizable to contemporary cohorts treated with prophylactic strategies, including tethers, hooks, ligament augmentation, or cement augmentation. Fifth, Hounsfield unit values and postoperative radiographic parameters may vary depending on imaging and measurement conditions, and their absolute values should therefore be interpreted with caution. Sixth, postoperative sacral slope should not be interpreted as a purely preoperative predictive factor, but rather as a parameter reflecting the achieved postoperative mechanical environment. Finally, although a general alignment goal of postoperative PI-LL mismatch <10° was commonly considered, the actual correction target was adjusted according to surgeon judgment, and the discrepancy between idealized alignment goals and real-world correction strategies should be taken into account when interpreting the present findings. External validation in independent cohorts is required to confirm the reproducibility and generalizability of these results.

Conclusion

In this cohort of patients undergoing ASD surgery, patients aged ≥80 years had higher rates of PJFx and mechanical failure before adjustment, but these differences were no longer significant after adjustment for measured physiological, skeletal, and radiographic factors. Chronological age alone may be insufficient for risk stratification, and age-related factors such as frailty and bone quality may mediate mechanical risk. In patients aged ≥80 years, lower UIV HU and lower postoperative sacral slope were associated with PJFx. These findings may help spine surgeons refine risk stratification for octogenarian patients with ASD by integrating physiological reserve, local bone quality, and the postoperative mechanical environment rather than relying on chronological age alone.
