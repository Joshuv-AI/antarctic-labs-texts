Comparison between operative management versus non-operative management in patients with solid organ blunt abdominal injury in the National Trauma Registry of Iran
Authors: Sara Mirzamohamadi, Mohammad Navid HajiAbbasi, Somayeh Ghorbani, Payman Salamati, Mohammadreza Zafarghandi, Vafa Rahimi-Movaghar, Esmaeil Fakharian, Seyed Houssein Saeed-Banadaky, Vahid Hoseinpour, Mehdi Nasr Isfahani, Amir Ghadiphasha, Sobhan Pourmasjedi, Seyed Mohammad Piri, Khatereh Naghdi, Seyed Amir Miratashi Yazdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Trauma is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. Severe abdominal injuries cause one-fifth of injury mortality. Non-operative management (NOM) or operative management (OM) is a primary challenge in abdominal injuries. In this study, we aimed to compare NOM and OM outcomes in patients with solid organ blunt abdominal injuries in the National Trauma Registry of Iran (NTRI).Method:This cross-sectional study was conducted with data from the NTRI from 24 July 2016 to 21 May 2023. All patients with a solid organ abdominal injury by road traffic crash, falls, hit or bodily force, and assault with blunt objects were admitted to trauma registry member hospitals across the country, according to the NTRI criteria included in this study.Results:Among 358 patients, liver, spleen, and kidney single-organ injuries were managed non-operative more than operative. Among multi-organ abdomen injuries, kidney injuries alongside spleen or liver injuries are managed non-operative more than operative. In the multiple regression model on the matched cohort (102 NOM with 102 OM), moderate or severe Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) had 16.79 times more chance of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission than mild GCS [OR: 16.79, 95% CI (2.06, 137.22), P = 0.008]. Also, hypovolemia increased the chance of death 12.9 times [OR: 12.90, 95% CI (2.20, 75.53), P = 0.005]. In addition, for every 1-year increase in age, the odds of the death occurring increased 1.07 [OR: 1.07, 95% CI (1.02, 1.12), P = 0.005].Conclusion:We observed the non-inferiority of NOM than OM due to the outcomes. Although the liver, spleen, and kidney are mainly managed by non-operatives.

Introduction

Trauma is one of the leading causes of death worldwide[1–3] and the leading cause of death in people under 44 years of age [4,5]. According to a World Health Organization (WHO) report, in 2019, injuries accounted for 8% of all deaths, including approximately 4.4 million deaths[6]. Additionally, every day, 14 000 deaths are recorded due to injuries, a figure that is expected to increase by 40% by 2030[7].

Severe abdominal injuries cause one-fifth of injury-related mortalities[8]. The most common regions of the body that suffer from trauma include the head, extremities, and abdomen [9]. Worldwide, the abdominal injury mortality rate is between 1% and 20%[3]. More than 75% of abdominal traumas are caused by motor vehicle accidents or falls from height. The mechanisms of abdominal injuries include (1) crushing: direct collision of abdominal components with fixed structures of the body, such as the spine, which often damages solid visceral components; (2) compression: a sudden increase in pressure inside the abdominal cavity, which causes rupture of the hollow viscera; and (3) deceleration: shearing forces at the attachment points of abdominal structures cause injury to solid and hollow viscera and to vessels[10]. Solid organ injuries in the abdomen can be categorized in two ways: parenchymal injuries, which include laceration, hematoma, and contusion, and vascular injuries, which include contained vascular injury, non-contained vascular injury, or active bleeding[10].

In the past, laparotomy was used for blunt abdominal injuries to identify damaged organs. With advances in imaging methods, knowledge of injury patterns, and patient selection criteria, a new strategy has been implemented. Non-operative management (NOM) is intended for hemodynamically stable patients and can also be used for blunt abdominal injuries[11].

The initial assessment of blunt abdominal injury, in line with Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS), assesses patient stability according to ABCDE (airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure); physical examination, simple radiography, and focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) are primary tools to identify life-threatening injuries and to determine treatment for unstable patients quickly. At the same time, more evaluations are required in stable patients[12].

The management of abdominal injuries has improved with radiological interventions – such as angiography and splenic artery embolization – and non-operative treatment (NOT), which includes follow-up of injuries without surgical intervention[2,13]. Surgery is indicated in patients whose hemodynamic status remains continuously unstable despite active resuscitation measures[13]. Approximately 70% of injuries to the liver, spleen, and kidneys can be controlled by conservative treatment. At the same time, hollow organs, such as the intestines, mostly require surgical intervention and laparotomy[5].

In this study, we aimed to compare the outcomes of NOM and operative management (OM) among patients with solid organ blunt abdominal injuries in the National Trauma Registry of Iran (NTRI).

Method

Study Design

This cross-sectional study, based on the STROCSS criteria, was conducted using hospital-based registry data (NTRI) from 24 July 2016 to 21 May 2023, across six hospitals in the cities of Tehran, Saveh, Yazd, Urmia, Isfahan, and Kashan.

Study population

All patients with solid-organ abdominal injuries resulting from road traffic crashes (RTCs), falls, being struck or other bodily force, or assaults with blunt objects who were admitted to member hospitals of the trauma registry across the country, according to NTRI criteria, were included in this study. Data collection

In this study, our variables included gender, age, Injury Severity Score (ISS), hospitalization in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), length of stay (LOS), organ injury, systolic blood pressure (SBP), heart rate (HR), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and death. Demographic data and outcomes, including gender, age, LOS, ICU admission, injured organs, and in-hospital death, were collected by trained nurses through the hospital information system (HIS) and interviews with patients. Vital signs, including SBP, HR, and GCS, were measured by physicians during visits to the emergency room. Finally, a physician serving as a quality reviewer verified the accuracy of all data and calculated the ISS as the sum of the squares of the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) values for the three most severe injuries in each body region, based on ICD-10 codes[14].

This study categorized ISS as 1-8, 9–15, and ≥16. We considered patients with SBP <90 and/or a pulse rate > 120 as hypovolemic. The shock index is calculated by dividing heart rate by systolic blood pressure. Victims with a shock index ≥1 are considered to be in hypovolemic shock. GCS was categorized as mild (13–15), moderate (9–12), and severe (3–8). Multi-organ abdominal injuries are defined as injuries in more than one abdominal organ. Victims with injuries in other parts of the body alongside an abdominal injury are considered multiple-trauma (MT) patients.

Statistical analysis

Mean and standard deviation (SD) were used to describe quantitative variables, and percentages were used to describe qualitative variables. Accordingly, t-tests and chi-square tests were used to assess relationships. This study used a significance level of p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26 and R.

This study compares two treatment approaches (OM and NOM) for treating patients and their outcomes. Because this is a cross-sectional study, it is not possible to investigate the causal effect of the treatment approach or other factors on the study outcomes –death (yes/no) and ICU hospitalization (yes/no). However, given the nature of the disease, a clinical trial is not feasible. Therefore, in this study, patients’ essential baseline characteristics in the two treatment approaches were compared using the propensity score method to control for confounding factors. The MatchIt package in R version 4.1.3 was used to calculate propensity scores. Three hundred fifty eight patients (102 under OM and 256 under NOM) whose data were complete were included in the study, and one person was excluded due to unknown GCS before matching. The qualitative variables–transfusion, multiple trauma, and hypovolemia–and the quantitative variables–GCS, age, and ISS–with p-values less than 0.2 that were potential confounders of the treatment approach were included in the propensity score model using a logistic regression model. This method is commonly used in observational studies to identify potential confounders, thereby minimizing the risk of omitting variables that may contribute to residual confounding despite not reaching the conventional significance level of 0.05 in primary analyses. At the time of matching, 102 people from the NOM group were matched with 102 people from the OM group, and the other 154 people who were not matched were excluded from the study. Analyses to estimate causal effects were performed on 204 matched participants. After obtaining matched treatment groups and ensuring control of confounders, simple and multivariable logistic regression was used to investigate the effect of each factor on the occurrence of death (yes/no) and ICU hospitalization (yes/no) separately.

All analyses were interpreted at a significance level of 0.05. The multiple logistic regression model included all variables with a p-value of less than 0.2 in the simple model.

Results

Of 358 patients with solid organ blunt abdominal injury, 256 (71.5%) were managed non-operatively and 102 (28.4%) were managed surgically. Patients with OM had higher blood transfusion rates than the NOM group [32 (31.4%) vs. 49 (19.1%), P = 0.013] before matching. Patients with multiple traumas were more likely to undergo OM than NOM before matching [76 (74.5%) vs. 157 (61.3%), P = 0.018]. Patients who were managed operatively had a higher rate of shock index ≥1 before matching [23 (22.5%) in OM vs. 33 (12.9%) in NOM, P = 0.023]. In addition, patients with OM had higher ISS than those with NOM before matching [13 (4,17) in OM vs. 8 (4,14) in NOM, P = 0.018]. After the two groups were matched (102 OM with 102 NOM), there were no statistically significant differences. In a multiple regression model of the matched data, management type was not associated with the odds of death, but NOM showed a trend toward increased ICU admission, with an OR of 1.85 (P = 0.066). Only the GCS was significantly associated with the odds of ICU admission. Holding other variables in the model constant, the odds of ICU admission for a person with moderate or severe GCS were 16.79 times higher than for those with mild GCS. Hypovolemia and age were significantly associated with the patient’s chance of death. The chance of death for a person with hypovolemia was 12.9 times higher than that for a person without hypovolemia. Additionally, for every 1-year increase in the patient’s age, the chance of death increased by a factor of 1.07. Discussion

Our findings showed that approximately two-thirds of patients with solid organ blunt abdominal trauma were treated with observation. The percentage managed with NOM was lower than in other studies and countries because our results came from a multicenter study involving level II and III trauma hospitals in the capital and in smaller cities. Liver, spleen, and kidney injuries are mainly managed nonoperatively. Moderate and severe GCS scores were predictors of ICU admission, while NOM showed a trend toward a higher chance of ICU admission compared to OM after matching. Additionally, hypovolemia and age were predictors of mortality after propensity score matching, and treatment options were not predictors of death.

Initially, observation of abdominal injuries without increased mortality and morbidity was described by Shaftan[15]. The choice between NOM and OM is a primary challenge in abdominal injuries. In our study, two-thirds of patients with solid organ blunt abdominal trauma were managed nonoperatively. After propensity score matching, we observed that management type did not affect patient outcomes; therefore, NOM did not have worse mortality outcomes than OM, but it showed an increasing trend toward ICU admission. Patients undergoing NOM require close monitoring and observation so that, if NOM fails, they can be operated on; therefore, ICU admission of NOM cases can be higher than that of OM cases. However, our study sample was small, and to support this conclusion we need a study with a larger cohort. Studies have reported that blunt injuries are managed with NOM as the first step in hemodynamically stable patients [16,17]. At the same time, 11.7% of our patients who were treated nonoperatively had hypovolemia on admission. Eight deaths were recorded in the NOM group, and five of them had hypovolemia on admission. Given the low mortality rate, we cannot conclude that hypovolemic patients can be treated with NOM, and a larger sample is needed. We observed 16.6% mortality in hypovolemic patients who were managed nonoperatively, while this percentage was 35% in the OM group before matching. Because hypovolemia increases the risk of mortality, the management strategy should be selected carefully. OM candidates had more blood transfusions and more frequent multiple trauma before matching. In line with this, other studies have demonstrated that OM groups required more blood transfusions[2,18].

In our study, we observed that splenic injuries were the most frequent abdominal blunt trauma and underwent NOM more often than OM, similar to other populations[2]. Studies have mentioned that NOM is a prevalent strategy for splenic injuries that can be used in all patients with stable hemodynamics and without signs of peritonitis because of the low mortality rate, low LOS, and low complication rates. However, some factors, including higher ISS and AIS and injuries above-grade III, increased the probability of operative management [19–21]. According to studies, the age of the victim, high-degree splenic injury (III–V), ISS, hemoperitoneum volume, vascular abnormalities, need for transfusions, associated liver and brain injuries, systolic hypotension, and hematimetric levels are factors associated with NOM failure in blunt splenic injuries[22].

The liver was another organ with a higher rate of NOM than OM. NOM criteria for liver injury included stable hemodynamics, hepatic-related blood transfusion limited to four units, and the absence of other abdominal injuries requiring surgical exploration of the abdomen[23]. One-fourth of splenic injuries were concomitant with liver injury, and one-fourth of them were managed non-operatively. In line with this, a study from Italy reported that the most commonly associated injury in blunt liver trauma was the spleen[18]. They also reported that blood transfusion requirements, LOS, and mortality in surgical cases were higher than in NOM[18].

In this study, kidney injuries are managed mainly non-operatively. Renal blunt injuries grades I-II are managed nonoperatively; grade III injuries are treated with NOM with monitoring and angioembolization if needed, and grades IV–V with stable hemodynamics may undergo NOM[24]. Patient selection for NOM in kidney injuries includes low blood transfusion requirements during the first 24 hours, smaller peri-renal hematomas, and reduced depth of renal laceration[11].

The rate of NOM for multi-organ abdominal injuries was 39.3%. Our results showed that neither MT nor treatment options were predictors of mortality. However, we need a larger study population to decide on NOM in multi-organ injuries and in patients undergoing MT.

Our study had limitations; it may have biases because this is a registry-based study, not a randomized, double-blind, controlled clinical trial. The CT scan and sonography results were not recorded in our database; therefore, we could not investigate the sensitivity and specificity of FAST and CT scan results. Failed NOM cases that led to surgery were not recorded in our database; therefore, we could not report the number of failed NOM cases and their outcomes. Our registry did not follow patients after discharge for complications, so we did not have information about their complications.

Conclusion

The treatment option was not a predictor of patient outcomes, including ICU admission, LOS, and mortality. On the other hand, NOM for solid-organ multi-organ injuries did not show higher mortality or worse outcomes compared with OM. Based on our observations, we suggest examining NOM in multi-organ injuries to assess whether it can reduce adverse outcomes.
