Marketplace coverage during pregnancy: what can we learn from state-level variation?
Authors: Julia C P Eddelbuettel, Alison A Galbraith, Sarah H Gordon, Anjali Kaimal, Laura F Garabedian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLittle is known about the role of the Affordable Care Act Marketplaces in providing health insurance coverage for pregnant women.MethodsUsing PRAMS survey data from 2018–2019 from 36 states and Washington, DC, we estimated the percentage of women with a pregnancy resulting in live birth who had Marketplace coverage during pregnancy, overall, by state, by federal poverty level (FPL) categories, and by state-based vs federal Marketplace status. Introduction

Health insurance coverage in the perinatal period is associated with increased access to prenatal care and improved child and maternal health outcomes.1-5 The Affordable Care Act (ACA) created health insurance Marketplaces, which were designed to offer affordable and comprehensive health insurance plans for individuals and families.6 Marketplace plans are a potential source of coverage for pregnant individuals who have an income above the Medicaid eligibility threshold during pregnancy (ranging from 138% of the federal poverty line [FPL] in Idaho, Louisiana, and South Dakota, to 380% of the FPL in Iowa; Appendix Table S1)7 and do not have another source of coverage (eg, employer-sponsored insurance [ESI]). Prior research has shown that continuous Marketplace coverage from preconception through to the postpartum period is associated with an increase in timely, high-quality prenatal care.1

However, little is known about the role of the Marketplaces in providing insurance coverage for pregnant and birthing people in the United States. To date, there have been no estimates of the percentage of pregnant people covered by Marketplace plans, and whether this rate differs by state. State-level policy decisions may influence Marketplace enrollment during pregnancy. States with state-based Marketplaces (SBMs) have flexibility to relax enrollment and eligibility criteria (eg, extend annual open enrollment periods [OEPs], or make pregnancy a special enrollment period [SEP] qualifying event8,9) in ways that states that use the federal Marketplace platform (healthcare.gov) do not. The SBMs also invest more in marketing of their Marketplace platforms, including having a greater emphasis on culturally and linguistically appropriate outreach, and on enrollment assistance services, which have been associated with increased enrollment and coverage.10-12 For these reasons, we hypothesized that SBMs may facilitate broader coverage of pregnant people. Conversely, states with a federally-run or federally-facilitated (ie, “federal”) Marketplace use the healthcare.gov platform and must follow federal enrollment rules.13 Marketplace enrollment in the general population also varies based on household income, with higher rates of coverage among those with lower incomes (100%–400% of the FPL), as the ACA made those below 400% of the FPL eligible for Marketplace premium tax credits (PTCs).14 Among parents specifically, Marketplace coverage rates are significantly higher among individuals with a household income below 250% of the FPL vs above (8.8% vs 3.9%).15 States also have varying Medicaid income eligibility thresholds during pregnancy, which could affect available coverage options.7

In this study, we used Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) survey data to examine the proportion of women with pregnancies resulting in live birth who had Marketplace coverage during pregnancy, overall, and by state, state SBM vs federal Marketplace status, and individual FPL levels. Among women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy, we examined insurance coverage in the preconception and postpartum periods, concurrent Medicaid coverage during pregnancy, and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.

We focus on data from 2018–2019, the years before COVID-19 pandemic–era Marketplace policies,16,17 which have sunset, and the most recent complete years of PRAMS available that were not impacted by pandemic-era policies.18 This study provides an estimate of enrollment and characteristics of birthing Marketplace enrollees under pre-pandemic ACA policies, which have again become the status quo policy environment, to provide a baseline that informs the potential scale to which more recent policies may impact coverage for this population.

Data and methods

Data

We used individual-level data from the PRAMS survey from the 35 states available in the PRAMS Automated Research File (ARF) in 2018–2019, Washington, DC, and Connecticut. PRAMS combines birth certificate data with a survey instrument to provide data on indicators such as maternal and infant health status, health behaviors, health care utilization, and insurance status.19 A key advantage of PRAMS over other data sources is the ability to identify Marketplace coverage during pregnancy across multiple states.

Study population

Our sample included PRAMS phase 8 respondents, who are women with a live birth (hereafter referred to as “pregnant women”) between January 2018 to December 2019 aged 18 years and older with non-missing insurance status during pregnancy. During our study period, 11 states and Washington, DC had SBMs (CA, CO, CT, ID, MD, MA, MN, NY, RI, VT, WA; the PRAMS ARF for 2018–2019 includes all of these states except CA, ID, and CT, and we accessed CT data through a data-sharing agreement with the Connecticut Department of Health).20 We examined the full population and stratified respondents by whether they resided in a state with an SBM vs federal Marketplace (Appendix Table S1).

We also stratified respondents by FPL status as four mutually exclusive categories based on self-reported income in the 12 months before pregnancy: (1) individuals below the pregnancy-specific Medicaid eligibility FPL threshold in their state; (2) individuals above their state′s pregnancy-specific Medicaid eligibility threshold through 400% of the FPL, the upper limit for eligibility for Marketplace premium tax credits based on ACA policy in the study period (and the current policy again in 2026 with the expiration of the enhanced PTCs [ePTCs]); (3) individuals above 400% of the FPL; and (4) individuals with missing FPL status, due to missing household income information in the PRAMS survey. PRAMS surveys women after birth but asks about household size and household income in ranges in the year before the new infant is born. Given that PRAMS asks about household income in ranges, respondent-level FPL was calculated using the median value of the respondent's selected household income range and self-reported household size in the 12 months prior to pregnancy in conjunction with 2018–2019 annual poverty guidelines.21,22 As a sensitivity analysis, we calculated FPL more conservatively by requiring both the upper and lower bound of a person's income range to be within a given FPL category.23

Outcomes and covariates

The primary outcome of interest was the percentage of pregnant women who had Marketplace coverage during pregnancy. This outcome is derived from the PRAMS question that asks respondents what type of insurance coverage covered their prenatal care (Appendix Table S2). Respondents who stated that their type of insurance coverage was private health insurance from their state's health insurance Marketplace, state-specific marketplace website, or healthcare.gov were coded as having Marketplace coverage during pregnancy. PRAMS also includes information on insurance status in the month prior to becoming pregnant (preconception coverage), insurance status at the time of survey (ie, 2–6 months postpartum), and maternal sociodemographic and clinical variables such as maternal age, race and ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, household income, number of previous live births, body mass index (BMI), and chronic condition status prior to pregnancy. See Appendix Table S2 for full categorization of the PRAMS insurance status questions and response options. We assigned each respondent to state-specific policies (ie, SBM vs federal Marketplace and Medicaid income eligibility threshold during pregnancy) based on their state of residence (Appendix Table S1).7,20

Statistical analyses

We calculated the survey-weighted percentage of women who had Marketplace coverage during pregnancy, overall and by state. We used Wald tests to examine outcomes in states with state-based vs federal Marketplace status, overall, and stratified by FPL categories.

To describe the population of pregnant women with Marketplace coverage, we examined individual-level characteristics. First, we examined the distribution of preconception coverage and postpartum coverage among individuals with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy, as well as the percentage with concurrent Medicaid coverage during pregnancy (ie, reported both Medicaid and Marketplace coverage in the prenatal period, although we are unable to distinguish between overlapping and non-overlapping coverage during this period), overall and by FPL category. We also examined sociodemographic (ie, age, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, household income) and clinical (ie, number of previous live births, BMI, and chronic conditions prior to pregnancy [diabetes, high blood pressure/hypertension, depression]) characteristics among respondents with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy compared with other coverage types. We report survey-weighted means and 95% CIs.

Analyses were conducted in Stata/MP version 18.0 and R version 4.3.1, and PRAMS's complex survey weights were applied. The Harvard Pilgrim Health Care Institute Institutional Review Board deemed this research as exempt.

Limitations

PRAMS is an ideal data source to estimate cross-state and state-specific percentages of women who had live births with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy, since it includes indicators for pregnancy and Marketplace enrollment and data from most US states. However, PRAMS has several limitations. PRAMS data to examine current Marketplace coverage for pregnant women after the sunset of COVID-19–era policies are not yet available, so we relied on data from 2018–2019, pre–COVID-19 pandemic. PRAMS data are also self-reported and may be prone to misclassification bias, both in household income and insurance status reporting. Since we rely on the median of a given income range to calculate FPL, we may be misclassifying FPL and therefore conducted sensitivity analyses to estimate FPL more conservatively. Further, a large proportion of respondents with Marketplace coverage reported income levels that would have made them eligible for Medicaid. Finally, we only had PRAMS data from 36 states and Washington, DC, and are missing data from states that have both large Marketplace-covered populations and a high proportion of births, such as California and Florida.

Results

Our study population included 77 242 PRAMS respondents from 2018–2019, which corresponds to 3 652 990 survey-weighted women over the 2-year period who had a pregnancy that resulted in live birth. This represents approximately 49% of live births in the United States annually.

Prevalence of Marketplace coverage during pregnancy

On average, across 36 states and Washington, DC in 2018-19, 4.9% (95% CI: 4.7%–5.2%) of women who had a pregnancy that ended in live birth had Marketplace coverage during pregnancy. This percentage varied greatly by state, from 1.6% (0.8%–2.5%) in Iowa to 13.1% (11.2%–15.1%) in Maryland (Figure 1, Appendix Table S3). Overall, the percentage of women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy was higher in state-based (8.0% [7.4%–8.6%]) vs federal (3.5% [3.3%–3.8%]) Marketplaces (P < .001) (Figure 2, Appendix Table S4). Among women with an FPL above the pregnancy-specific Medicaid threshold, but below the 400% FPL eligibility limit for PTCs, rates of Marketplace coverage during pregnancy were significantly higher in SBMs vs federal Marketplace states (5.4% [4.5%–6.3%] vs 3.7% [3.3%–4.1%]; P = .001) (Figure 2, Appendix Table S4). Marketplace coverage during pregnancy among women with FPLs above the PTC eligibility limit (400%) was lower (2.0% [1.6%–2.3%]), with no significant difference by SBM vs federal Marketplace status (P = .978) (Appendix Table S4). These findings are robust to more conservative FPL coding (Appendix Figure S1). Pregnant women with FPLs below their state-specific threshold for Medicaid eligibility during pregnancy had the highest rates of Marketplace coverage, especially in SBMs, where almost 1 in 8 (12.1% [11.0%–13.3%]) reported Marketplace coverage during pregnancy (Figure 2, Appendix Table S4).

Coverage characteristics among of pregnant Marketplace enrollees

Preconception coverage

The majority (73.4%) of women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy had Marketplace coverage prior to becoming pregnant, and this was similar across FPL categories (71.2%–77.4%) and in SBMs vs federal Marketplaces (72.5%–74.4%) (Table 1, Appendix Table S5). One in 10 (10.9%) pregnant women with Marketplace coverage were uninsured in the preconception period, with higher rates of preconception uninsurance among respondents in the lower FPL thresholds (Table 1). The prevalence of private insurance prior to pregnancy was more common at higher FPL categories (15.3% for >400% FPL), and the prevalence of preconception Medicaid coverage was higher among the lower-income FPL categories (2.4% among enrollees with an FPL between the threshold for Medicaid coverage during pregnancy and 400% FPL and 6.5% among enrollees with an FPL below the Medicaid pregnancy threshold) (Table 1).

Concurrent coverage during pregnancy

Overall, almost one-third (30.4%) of women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy also reported Medicaid coverage during pregnancy, and this was highest among those with FPLs that would make them eligible for Medicaid coverage during pregnancy in their state (38.7%) (Table 1). Women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy were more likely to also report Medicaid coverage during pregnancy in SBMs (37.1%) than in federal Marketplace states (23.5%) (Appendix Table S5), potentially because SBMs have higher FPL thresholds for Medicaid coverage during pregnancy (253% FPL in SBMs in our sample vs 209% in federal Marketplaces in our sample; Appendix Table S1).

Postpartum coverage

Most (70.3%) women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy maintained their coverage in the postpartum period, and this was consistent across FPL categories. On average, 9.3% transitioned from Marketplace coverage during pregnancy to being uninsured in the postpartum period, with higher rates of postpartum uninsurance among lower-income women (10.3% among those with an FPL below the pregnancy-specific Medicaid threshold and 7.4% among those with an FPL between the Medicaid pregnancy threshold and 400% FPL) vs women with an FPL of more than 400% (3.6%) (Table 1). Women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy were more likely to retain their Marketplace coverage in the postpartum period in SBMs vs federal Marketplace states (73.4% vs 67.0%), with a greater percentage of pregnant women in federal Marketplace states transitioning to ESI or Medicaid coverage in the postpartum period (Appendix Table S5).

Demographic and health characteristics of pregnant Marketplace enrollees

Among women with a live birth who had Marketplace coverage during pregnancy, 29.7% were 25–29 years old, 50.1% identified as non-Hispanic White, 24.9% had a college degree, and 55.0% were married (Table 2). Compared with individuals with other types of coverage during pregnancy, those with Marketplace coverage were younger than those with ESI but older than those with Medicaid coverage. The birthing population with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy was more similar to the birthing population with Medicaid than with ESI in terms of self-identified race/ethnicity, with 21.3% of Marketplace enrollees identifying as Hispanic and 18.8% identifying as non-Hispanic Black (Table 2). Marketplace enrollees were more likely than individuals with ESI and less likely than individuals with Medicaid to have obesity and depression prior to pregnancy.

Discussion

Affordable Care Act Marketplace plans were a modest but meaningful source of coverage for pregnant women who had a live birth in the United States in 2018-19. The percentage of women with a live birth who were covered by the Marketplace during their pregnancy varied greatly by state (1.6% to 13.1%) and FPL category (2.0% to 6.4% overall). Marketplace coverage during pregnancy was higher for individuals with incomes less than 400% of the FPL, especially those below the state-specific threshold for Medicaid coverage during pregnancy. Most women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy also reported Marketplace coverage prior to pregnancy and postpartum. However, about 1 in 10 women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy reported being uninsured in the preconception and/or postpartum periods. Women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy generally had sociodemographic and clinical characteristics (ie, age, self-reported race/ethnicity, educational attainment, and preconception clinical predictors of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as obesity and depression) more akin to populations with Medicaid coverage during pregnancy than ESI coverage during pregnancy, particularly in SBMs. It is possible that this state-level variation is partly reflective of broader state-level trends in Marketplace enrollment among women of reproductive age. However, the correlation between the state-level percentage of women with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy from PRAMS and the percentage of women aged 19–44 years with direct pay coverage from the 2019 American Community Survey is weak, so other factors likely play a role (see Appendix Table S6 for comparison).

Overall, the prevalence of Marketplace coverage during pregnancy was significantly higher in states with SBMs than in states that rely on the federal Marketplace. States with SBMs have flexibility to extend the annual OEP, create SEPs for pregnancy or low-income status,9,24,25 provide additional state-funded subsidies for premiums and/or cost-sharing reductions beyond standard federal subsidies,26 and devote resources to marketing, outreach, and patient navigation to increase Marketplace enrollment.10,11 Notably, we observed significantly higher Marketplace coverage during pregnancy in SBMs, even though SBMs also have higher pregnancy-specific Medicaid eligibility thresholds. More research is needed to examine the impact of specific SBM policies on enrollment for pregnant women. Almost three-quarters (73.4%) of people who were covered by the Marketplace during pregnancy had Marketplace coverage before pregnancy, suggesting that policies that facilitate Marketplace enrollment for all populations (eg, ePTCs and longer OEPs) may particularly benefit maternal health by increasing preconception and inter-conception coverage.

We estimate that two-thirds (66.2%) of our sample with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy had a self-reported income level in the year before pregnancy that would make them eligible for Medicaid coverage during pregnancy in their state (Table 2). When looking at the denominator of all PRAMS respondents with a reported FPL that would make them eligible for Medicaid during pregnancy, 6.4% overall and 12.1% in SBMs reported Marketplace coverage during pregnancy (Figure 2). There are several potential explanations for this observed pattern. First, PRAMS asks respondents about their household income and household size in the 12 months prior to pregnancy, but income often fluctuates in the Marketplace and Medicaid-eligible populations and may have changed from preconception to pregnancy.27 In future research, it will be important to distinguish whether these Marketplace enrollees were indeed eligible for Medicaid but were enrolled in Marketplace plans, or if they were categorically ineligible due to their income verification. Second, past research has shown that surveys are prone to insurance-type misreporting, particularly for Medicaid managed care and Marketplace plans that often have names of private insurance carriers.28 Misclassification of insurance type could lead us to overestimate (ie, if the person actually had Medicaid coverage) or underestimate (ie, if a person with Marketplace coverage reported other private coverage) the percentage of pregnant women who have Marketplace coverage. Third, it is possible that individuals have multiple sources of coverage throughout their pregnancy and transition coverage types throughout pregnancy (eg, Marketplace early in the pregnancy and Medicaid in the third trimester and/or delivery episode).29 Over a third (38.7%) of pregnant women with Medicaid-eligible incomes and Marketplace coverage during pregnancy also reported Medicaid coverage in the prenatal period (Table 1). More research is needed in datasets such as all-payer claims data to examine timing of coverage transitions during pregnancy. Fourth, Medicaid-eligible pregnant women with preconception Marketplace coverage may be retaining Marketplace coverage during pregnancy to facilitate continuity of care (ie, if different provider networks are covered by Marketplace and Medicaid plans). However, Medicaid-eligible pregnant women who retain or enroll in Marketplace coverage may be incurring higher than necessary financial burden, since Marketplace plans have higher premiums and cost-sharing than Medicaid plans.1,30

It will be important to research the impact of COVID-19–era and current policies on Marketplace coverage among the pregnant population. However due to the halt on the collection and availability of PRAMS data, the ability to conduct this research with PRAMS is limited.31 During the COVID-19 pandemic and its aftermath, there were a number of policy changes that contributed to a doubling of Marketplace enrollment from 2020-2025.32 COVID-19 SEPs from 2020–2022 facilitated year-long open enrollment;16 ePTCs from 2021–2025, instituted by the American Rescue Plan Act and extended by the Inflation Reduction Act, increased subsidies for lower-income Marketplace enrollees and allowed people over 400% of the FPL to qualify for tax credits;17 and Medicaid unwinding from 2023–2024 facilitated Marketplace coverage for people who lost Medicaid coverage at the end of the COVID-19 Public Health Emergency.32 In 2026, the pandemic-era ePTCs expired, returning eligibility for premium subsidies to pre-pandemic levels. Additionally, increasing premiums of Marketplace plans and direct changes to Marketplace policies from H.R.1 2025, such as increased administrative burdens to enroll,33,34 are predicted to dramatically decrease enrollment35 and will likely make it more challenging to enroll in a Marketplace plan early in pregnancy or prior to becoming pregnant.

Conclusion

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the ACA Marketplaces were a small but meaningful source of coverage during pregnancy among women who had live births, insuring 1 in 20 women in our PRAMS sample of 36 states and Washington, DC. The percentage of women who had live births with Marketplace coverage during pregnancy was higher in states with SBMs, which have flexibility to set eligibility and enrollment criteria and prioritize marketing and enrollment assistance, and was higher among women with lower FPLs who are potentially eligible for Medicaid during pregnancy. Future research is needed to examine the exact timing of coverage transitions during pregnancy and how pandemic-era policies, which increased Marketplace enrollment, and more recent Marketplace policies, which are expected to reduce Marketplace enrollment, have affected coverage and care for this population.
