Effect of a School‐Based Yoga Intervention on Behavioral Intention and Yoga Practice Among Secondary School Students: A Quasi‐Experimental Study in Kirtipur, Kathmandu, Nepal
Authors: Savyata Panthi, Rahi Bikram Thapa, Khem Bahadur Karki, Ramhari Regmi, Chet Kant Bhusal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSchool‐based yoga interventions may provide an opportunity to promote healthy behaviors among adolescents by improving yoga‐related knowledge, motivation, and engagement in regular practice. However, evidence regarding changes in yoga‐related behavioral outcomes following school‐based interventions among Nepalese secondary school students remains unexplored. So, this study aims to evaluate whether a 4‐week structured school‐based yoga intervention changes yoga‐related knowledge, behavioral intention to practice yoga, and self‐reported yoga practice among secondary school students, compared with a non‐intervention group.MethodsA quasi‐experimental pre‐post study used multistage random sampling to select one intervention school (n = 102) and two comparison schools (n = 204; total N = 306, grades 9–12) in Kirtipur Municipality, Nepal. The intervention comprised 4 weekly 45 min sessions of asanas, pranayama, and meditation, delivered by a certified trainer. Data were collected via a pretested, researcher‐developed questionnaire and analyzed in SPSS v25 using chi‐square and McNemar's tests.ResultsGroups were comparable at baseline on ever‐practiced status and intention (p ≥ 0.139), despite widespread awareness of yoga (96.4%), but limited knowledge of specific types (33.2%). Post‐intervention, the intervention group showed significant gains in knowledge of yoga types (19.6% → 89.2%), contraindications (12.7% → 41.2%), ever‐practiced status (52.9% → 95.1%), and intention (22.5% → 70.6%; all p < 0.001), while the comparison group improved only in knowledge, with no significant change in practice (53.4% → 57.4%, p = 0.484) or intention (31.4% → 30.4%, p = 0.912).ConclusionA structured, instructor‐led school yoga program can shift short‐term yoga knowledge and intention in school settings; whether this translates into sustained practice or broader health benefits requires further study.Keywords: Nepal, non‐communicable diseases, physical activity, school‐based intervention, secondary school students, Yoga

Introduction

Yoga, an ancient practice that combines physical postures, breath control, and meditation, has long been celebrated for its numerous health benefits [1]. Over recent decades, it has gained global recognition as an effective tool for enhancing physical fitness, reducing stress, and promoting overall well‐being. The World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Office for South‐East Asia recognizes yoga as a valuable approach for increasing physical activity and contributing to the prevention of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) as part of a comprehensive health promotion strategy [2]. As the health challenges facing the younger population continue to rise, school‐based interventions such as yoga have become a promising strategy to address these concerns, encouraging students to adopt healthy habits early in life [3]. Schools are a logical setting for such programs, not because yoga alone uniquely ensures early practice, but because they offer structured, repeated access to adolescents during a developmentally important period for habit formation, making any well‐designed structured physical activity, including yoga, more feasible to deliver and sustain than activity promoted outside institutional settings [4].

Two additional lines of evidence support examining yoga specifically in Nepalese schools rather than physical activity in general. First, Nepalese classroom research shows that digital devices are a significant source of distraction and reduced attention among students [5], underscoring competing demands on adolescent time and attention that a structured, device‐free practice like yoga may help offset. Second, emotional intelligence (the capacity to recognize and regulate emotion) has been linked to stronger affective commitment (emotional attachment to one's institution and studies) among Nepalese undergraduates, with self‐regulation identified as a key mechanism [6]. Insofar as yoga is theorized to build self‐regulation and emotional awareness through breathing and meditation practices, it may plausibly support this same pathway among younger students.

International evidence supports the feasibility and benefits of school‐based yoga, though it is concentrated in high‐income settings. A randomized controlled trial by Khalsa et al. in a US secondary school found that participation in yoga was associated with improved mood and reduced anxiety relative to a physical education control group [7]. A systematic review and narrative synthesis of randomized controlled trials by Khunti et al., covering school‐aged children, found small‐to‐moderate improvements in mental health outcomes, although intervention duration and delivery varied considerably across the studies [8]. A more recent qualitative study by Sumner et al., conducted in a disadvantaged UK school, found that students and staff perceived yoga and mindfulness sessions as improving self‐regulation and coping with everyday stressors [3].

Behavior change theory offers a framework for interpreting how a school‐based intervention might translate into practice. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) proposes that behavioral intention (the most proximal predictor of action) is shaped by three factors: attitude toward the behavior, subjective norms (perceived social support, such as from parents or peers), and perceived behavioral control (confidence in one's ability to perform the behavior) [9]. This is directly relevant to yoga uptake: enabling factors such as parental practice and peer encouragement plausibly operate through subjective norms, while a commonly cited barrier such as “not knowing how to do yoga” reflects low perceived behavioral control. Intention itself should be distinguished from durable habit: computational models of habit formation indicate that behavior persistence depends on habit strength built through repeated performance in a stable context over time, not on intention alone [10], a distinction relevant given this study's short 4‐week window. Self‐Determination Theory [11] and the Transtheoretical Model [12] offer complementary perspectives on sustained versus brief behavior change.

The evidence gap in Nepal is not that yoga is unfamiliar, given the shared cultural context with India; general awareness may already be comparable to other settings, but no published Nepalese study has tested whether a structured, school‐delivered program measurably shifts adolescents' behavioral intention and actual reported practice, as distinct from documenting awareness alone. Existing Nepalese data are themselves inconsistent: a Birgunj medical‐student study found high awareness (96.6%) but only 14% current practice [13]; a Kathmandu cross‐sectional study documented benefits and barriers without testing an intervention [14]; and a more recent Kathmandu high‐school study (n = 226) found markedly lower awareness (49.8% poor) and minimal practice (90.5% reporting no habit) [15]. Therefore, requiring setting‐specific intervention evidence, rather than assuming that Nepalese adolescents are uniformly under‐exposed to yoga relative to adolescents elsewhere.

This study addresses that gap by evaluating a structured, 4‐week school‐based yoga intervention among secondary school students (grades 9–12) in Kirtipur Municipality, Nepal, using a quasi‐experimental pre‐post design with a non‐intervention comparison group. It examines change in three explicitly defined outcomes: yoga‐related knowledge (awareness of yoga types, benefits, and contraindications), behavioral intention (the self‐reported likelihood), and self‐reported yoga practice. This study assesses specifically whether a brief intervention can move students beyond awareness toward intention and short‐term reported action, providing Nepal‐specific, setting‐bounded evidence for educators and policymakers considering yoga's role in school health programming.

Methods

Study Design

A quasi‐experimental, pre‐post design with a non‐equivalent comparison group was used to evaluate the effect of a 4‐week school‐based yoga intervention on yoga‐related knowledge, behavioral intention, and yoga practice among secondary school students in Kirtipur Municipality, Nepal. This design was chosen because randomizing individual students to intervention and comparison conditions was not feasible within an intact school‐class structure, and school‐level assignment avoided contamination between students who share classrooms and social networks. At baseline, the intervention and comparison groups were compared on socio‐demographic characteristics and yoga‐related knowledge, perception, and practice to assess comparability; no statistically significant baseline difference was found between groups on ever‐practiced status or behavioral intention (both p ≥ 0.139), supporting the appropriateness of the comparison despite the absence of individual‐level randomization. The study was conducted over a 6‐month period from October 2022 to March 2023.

Study Area and School Selection

Kirtipur Municipality was purposively selected because it had not yet implemented a school‐based yoga program, unlike some other Nepalese municipalities that have adopted yoga as an extracurricular activity in collaboration with WHO and the Government of Nepal. A list of public secondary schools was obtained from the Kirtipur Municipality Education Office; 10 public secondary schools were identified. Of these, only three offered classes through grade 12 (Mangal Secondary School, Shree Taudaha Rastriya Secondary School, and Shree Janasewa Secondary School) and were eligible, since the study population was defined as students of grades 9–12. Public schools were used throughout to maintain relative socio‐economic homogeneity, since private schools in Nepal typically serve families of different socio‐economic status. Among the three eligible schools, one was randomly assigned to the yoga intervention by lottery; the remaining two served as the non‐intervention comparison group.

Sampling Technique

A multistage sampling approach was used. At the school level, one intervention school was selected from the three eligible schools by lottery. Within each of the three schools, one section from each grade (9–12) was then selected by lottery, drawn by the researcher in the presence of the class teacher; all students in the selected sections who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled.

Study Population, Sample Size, and Sample Characteristics

The study population comprised students in grades 9–12 at the selected public secondary schools. Students who verbally reported a disease condition in which yoga is contraindicated (e.g., acute or serious illness, fractures or musculoskeletal injury, or a condition for which specific yoga postures are medically inadvisable) at the time of the intervention were excluded from the physical sessions, based on self‐report at eligibility screening.

The sample size was calculated in two stages. For the baseline prevalence estimate, Cochran's formula (n = Z2pq/d2) was used with a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error [16], and an expected baseline yoga‐practice prevalence of 14% (based on a study among preclinical medical students in Birgunj, Nepal) [13], giving a minimum sample of 185; applying a design effect of 1.5 (n = 278) and adjusting for 10% non‐response yielded a target of 306. For the intervention‐comparison objective, the two‐proportion formula [17] [n = 2(Zα/2 + Zβ)2P(1 − P)/(P1 − P2)2] was used with 95% confidence, 80% power, an expected pre‐intervention prevalence of 14% [13], and an expected post‐intervention prevalence of 24.5%, giving a required sample of 232, adjusted for 30% attrition to 302. The overall target of 306 was allocated between arms in a 1:2 ratio (intervention: comparison), yielding 102 students from the intervention school and 204 from the two comparison schools.

Study Phases

The study was conducted in two phases. In Phase I, a cross‐sectional baseline survey was administered concurrently to all three schools to characterize and compare the intervention and comparison groups on socio‐demographic factors and baseline yoga‐related knowledge, perception, and practice. In Phase II, the intervention school received the 4‐week yoga program, after which post‐intervention data were collected from the same intervention‐school cohort (n = 102) and compared against their own baseline responses, and against the post‐study responses of the same comparison cohort (n = 204) at their own two time points. Both groups were therefore assessed longitudinally (baseline vs. post).

Intervention

The yoga intervention package was adapted from the “Module for Ayurveda and Yoga Education in School” and accompanying school yoga video, developed by Nepal's Department of Ayurveda and Alternative Medicine (DoAA) in collaboration with WHO (Accessed from: https://doaa.gov.np/content/8769/8769-school-ayurveda-and-yoga-educa). [18] 4 weekly sessions, each approximately 45 min, were delivered on a fixed weekday over four consecutive weeks in the intervention school, after regular class hours, in a carpeted hall provided by the school. Each forty‐5‐min session followed the same structured sequence: (1) a 12‐step Surya Namaskar (sun salutation) cycle [9 min]; (2) pranayama practices (Anulom‐Vilom and Kapalbhati) [9 min]; (3) Bhramari Dhyaan (humming‐bee breath meditation) [9 min]; (4) asanas (Trikonasana, Vakrasana, Ustrasana, Mandukasana, Naukasana, Makarasana) [9 min]; and (5) closing relaxation (Shalabhasana, Dhanurasana, Sarvangasana, Shavasana) [9 min]. Sessions were delivered by a certified yoga trainer and two Ayurveda physicians, with the principal researcher present throughout; co‐trainers received 2 days of standardized training beforehand.

Data Collection Tools

Data were collected using a structured, self‐administered questionnaire covering socio‐demographic characteristics, knowledge/perception/beliefs about yoga, enabling factors and barriers to practice, behavioral intention, and current yoga practice. The tool (Supplementary File 1) was developed by the principal researcher with the study supervisor, co‐supervisor, and yoga experts, based on a literature review; content validity was established through expert review, with the final item set confirmed by a third independent yoga expert. The English‐language instrument was translated into Nepali and back‐translated (English–Nepali–English) to check semantic equivalence. Face validity and clarity were assessed through pretesting (Supplementary File 2) among 18 secondary‐level students at a school not involved in the main study, followed by a focus group discussion; item wording was revised accordingly.

Data Collection Process

Baseline data were collected from all three schools within a 2‐day window prior to the intervention. Post‐intervention data were collected from the intervention group 25 days after the final session; post‐study data collection in the two comparison schools was conducted 45 days after their baseline survey, because the intervention school's academic calendar (mid‐term examinations followed by winter vacation) delayed completion of the intervention sessions and the subsequent post‐test.

Statistical Analysis

Questionnaires were reviewed for completeness and entered into EpiData 3.1; analysis was done on SPSS v25. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages) summarized socio‐demographic and baseline knowledge/practice variables. Pearson's chi‐square compared the intervention and comparison groups at baseline and post‐intervention on each outcome, while McNemar's test assessed within‐group pre‐post change for the same outcomes. Chi‐square tests of association examined whether ever‐practiced status was related to demographic and yoga‐related factors, and whether baseline knowledge was related to demographic sub‐groups. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC), Institute of Medicine, Tribhuvan University (Ref. no: 281). Permission was obtained from the Kirtipur Municipality Office and each participating school. Written informed consent was obtained from students aged 18 or older, and parental/guardian consent for students under 18, prior to data collection and participation in the yoga sessions. Confidentiality was maintained; no names of students or schools were recorded, and participants were identified only by ID number. Participation was voluntary, and students could withdraw at any stage without consequence.

Results

Data were collected from a total of 306 students, with 102 participants in the intervention group and 204 in the non‐intervention group. The socio‐demographic characteristics, knowledge, and perception about yoga, as well as the status of yoga practice before and after the intervention, are summarized below.

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

The participants’ ages ranged from 13 to 21 years, with a mean age of 16.28 ± 1.63 years. Of the total participants, 57.5% were male. The ethnic composition of the sample showed that 51.6% were Brahmin/Chhetri. Regarding religion, 80.1% of participants were Hindu. Additionally, 55.2% of participants came from joint families, and 44.8% from nuclear families. The educational status of the parents indicated that 57.8% of mothers and 85.3% of fathers had formal education. Detailed information is shown in Table 1.

Effect of the Intervention

The intervention and comparison groups were statistically comparable at baseline on ever‐practiced status and behavioral intention (both p ≥ 0.139), but diverged significantly by post‐test (both p < 0.001; Table 3). Within the intervention group, all four outcomes, including knowledge of yoga types, knowledge of contraindications, prior yoga practice, and behavioral intention, improved significantly following the intervention (McNemar's p < 0.001 for all outcomes). In the comparison group, knowledge of yoga types and contraindications also rose significantly over the same period, but neither ever‐practiced status (p = 0.484) nor behavioral intention (p = 0.912) changed significantly, indicating the intervention specifically drove the gains in practice and intention.

Discussion

This study examined whether a 4‐week structured, school‐based yoga intervention could shift yoga‐related knowledge, behavioral intention, and self‐reported practice among secondary school students in Kirtipur Municipality, Nepal, relative to a non‐intervention comparison group.

The intervention group showed significant gains across all four measured outcomes: knowledge of yoga types, knowledge of contraindications, ever‐practiced status, and behavioral intention. The comparison group showed significant change only in the two knowledge outcomes, not in practice or intention. Three design features of the intervention plausibly explain this pattern, rather than the change being an artifact of repeated testing or general maturation over the study period. First, the intervention was structured and sequential: each of the four sessions followed a fixed protocol (Surya Namaskar, pranayama, Bhramari meditation, asanas, and closing relaxation), repeating the same content across 4 weeks rather than a single exposure, a dosage the comparison group never received. Second, sessions were delivered by a certified yoga trainer with support from an Ayurvedic physician, which plausibly improved both the technical accuracy of instruction and students’ confidence in attempting the practices themselves. Third, the intervention was embedded in the school day and setting, which likely lowered the practical barriers to trial that dominated the baseline barriers, chiefly not knowing how to do yoga (43.2%) and difficulty managing time (38.6%), by supplying both instruction and a scheduled opportunity to practice within an already‐structured environment. The comparison group's improvement in knowledge alone, without a corresponding rise in practice, is consistent with this interpretation: incidental exposure through media, school conversations, or peer discussions can plausibly raise awareness passively but appears insufficient on its own to translate into the skill‐building and structured opportunities that practice requires.

To be specific about which knowledge domains improved, overall awareness of contraindications rose in both groups, but the composition of that knowledge shifted specifically among intervention‐group students. Recognition of severe illness and fracture‐related contraindications increased post‐intervention, from 51.7% to 59.3% and from 39.7% to 56.9%, respectively, while the very high pre‐intervention emphasis on condition‐specific restrictions alone (91.4% among the few aware of any contraindication at baseline) fell to 52.8% post‐intervention. This pattern indicates the intervention broadened a narrow, single‐category understanding of contraindications into a more complete one, rather than uniformly reinforcing whatever students already believed.

The direction of change in knowledge and intention observed here is broadly consistent with prior school‐based yoga literature [19, 20], but this study differs from that literature in scope and measurement in ways that matter for interpretation. Khalsa et al. evaluated a randomized controlled trial that measured students’ mood and anxiety directly, using validated psychological instruments, and found improvements in those outcomes [7]. The present study administered no mental‐health or mood measure, only self‐reported knowledge, intention, and practice, so while our findings are broadly compatible with the idea that structured yoga programs can benefit students, they cannot be taken as evidence of improved mental health or psychological well‐being. Similarly, Khunti et al.‘s systematic review discusses sustained participation in school‐based yoga programs across studies with longer follow‐up periods [8], but sustained participation was never assessed in the present study: the intervention lasted 4 weeks, intervention‐group follow‐up occurred only 25 days after the final session, and no question in the instrument captured whether practice continued beyond the study period. Our results, therefore, speak only to short‐term change immediately following a brief intervention, not to durability, a distinction this study cannot resolve given its design.

Where our findings diverge more clearly from assumptions in earlier literature is on the demographic correlates of practice. Some published work associates joint‐family structure and being male with higher psychological experiences, reasoning that more adult household members and traditional gender norms create more opportunity or encouragement for practice [21]. Our chi‐square analysis of the current dataset found no significant association between ever having practiced yoga and sex, caste, religion, or parental education, and only a borderline association with family type (p = 0.050), favoring nuclear‐family students (59.9%), the opposite direction from what some earlier literature. Given that this association is only borderline significant, we treat it as tentative and hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmed. In contrast, three yoga‐specific factors were strongly associated with practice: knowledge of yoga types, parental yoga practice, and encouragement from others, consistent with a broader pattern in the literature connecting social modeling, encouragement, and knowledge transfer to youth health behavior adoption, and with the qualitative finding of Sumner et al. that peer and family support shaped adolescents’ engagement with school‐based yoga and mindfulness [3]. A parallel sub‐group analysis found that baseline knowledge itself was not concentrated in any particular demographic segment. Knowledge gaps at baseline appear broadly distributed across sex, family type, and parental education, suggesting a universal, school‐wide intervention approach is appropriate rather than one targeted at a specific demographic subgroup.

This study also adds a specific, setting‐bounded data point to the small and inconsistent existing body of Nepalese yoga research. A study among preclinical medical students in Birgunj found much higher awareness of yoga's benefits than we found awareness of yoga's types or contraindications here [13], while a more recent study of Kathmandu‐area high school students found considerably lower awareness (49.8% poor) and minimal baseline practice than our sample [15]. These are not directly comparable populations or measures, but the spread across them illustrates that yoga awareness and practice vary meaningfully even within urban Nepal, and reinforces that this study's findings describe Kirtipur specifically, not Nepal as a whole.

The pattern of results is consistent with the staged behavior‐change pathway, moving from knowledge, to attitude and perceived capability, to behavioral intention, to reported action. At baseline, most students already held favorable general beliefs about yoga (84.3% believed it improves health), but specific, actionable knowledge of yoga types and contraindications was comparatively low, and few had translated belief into practice (21.2% current practice). Following the intervention, knowledge rose sharply in the intervention group, accompanied by a large rise in behavioral intention (22.5%–70.6%) and in self‐reported practice. Read through the TPB, this sequence, knowledge, and skill‐building translating into intention and then reported action, is consistent with the intervention operating primarily by increasing perceived behavioral control, students’ confidence that they know how to do yoga, given that not knowing how to do yoga was the single most commonly cited barrier at baseline (43.2%), and structured instruction directly targets this specific barrier. The strong association between encouragement from others and practice is similarly consistent with the TPB's subjective norms pathway, in which perceived social support for a behavior increases the likelihood of its adoption. We note, however, that this study measured intention and short‐term self‐reported practice, not the more durable construct of habit strength; habit formation is understood to depend on repeated behavior in a stable context over a period longer than 4 weeks, so these findings should be read as evidence of successful short‐term intention and initial‐practice change, a meaningful first step along the behavior‐change pathway, rather than as evidence that a lasting habit was established.

Strengths and Limitations

The study's quasi‐experimental design with both intervention and control groups strengthens the findings by allowing for a comparison of the yoga intervention's effects. The pre‐ and post‐intervention data collection method provided insights into changes in students’ knowledge, perceptions, and practice of yoga. The large sample size (306 students) adds to the study's representativeness, and the intervention package developed by the DoAA ensured credibility. Certified yoga instructors conducted the sessions, which likely contributed to the observed positive outcomes.

This study has several limitations. Individual‐level randomization was not feasible within an intact school‐class structure, given the risk of contamination among students sharing classrooms and social networks. Because only one school was assigned to the intervention arm, school‐level factors (leadership, scheduling, unmeasured context) cannot be fully separated from the intervention's effect, and the design does not allow adjustment for school‐level clustering. Formal psychometric testing (e.g., Cronbach's alpha) of the data collection tool was not conducted, given its largely categorical and multiple‐response item structure. Relatedly, the study relied entirely on self‐report, with no objective measures of participation: attendance, frequency, duration, and adherence to yoga sessions were not independently verified, so reported gains in practice and intention reflect what students said rather than confirmed behavior. Treatment fidelity during intervention sessions was also not quantified through a formal checklist or an independent observer. Follow‐up intervals differed between groups (25 days for the intervention group vs. 45 days for the comparison group), and this unequal exposure time could itself have affected the comparability of the two groups’ change scores. Finally, the short intervention duration (4 weeks) and the single‐municipality setting (Kirtipur) limit both the durability and generalizability of these findings, as local cultural attitudes toward yoga in Kirtipur may differ from those in other regions of Nepal.

Conclusion

This 4‐week school‐based yoga intervention was associated with significant improvements in yoga‐related knowledge, behavioral intention, and self‐reported practice among secondary school students in Kirtipur Municipality, relative to a non‐intervention comparison group. The intervention appeared most effective at addressing the barriers students themselves identified at baseline, chiefly not knowing how to practice yoga and difficulty finding time, suggesting that structured, instructor‐led sessions embedded within the school day can meaningfully lower the practical obstacles to yoga uptake among adolescents. Given the short intervention period and single‐municipality setting, these findings point to feasibility and short‐term change rather than confirmed long‐term habit formation, warranting further study with longer follow‐up and broader geographic scope.
