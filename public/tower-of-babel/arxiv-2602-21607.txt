Direction-of-arrival estimation of a gravitational wave by correlations between quadrupole moments of pulsar timings
Hideki Asada

Abstract
Can we estimate the direction of arrival (DOA) of a gravitational wave (GW) signal from pulsar timing array observations? The present paper addresses the inverse problem, for which we consider quadrupole moments of pulsar timings due to GWs from a dominant isolated source such as a binary of supermassive black holes over an isotropic stochastic background. Correlations between the quadrupole moments are discussed, where the correlations between pulsar pairs over the full sky are taken into account. The correlations turn out to be in the form of a three-dimensional traceless matrix with rank 2 that can be closely related with a projection tensor for the GW. Thereby, we demonstrate that the rank-2 matrix allows to estimate the DOA of the GW. In expectation of the forthcoming Square Kilometer Array, angular resolutions as well as DOA estimation errors are also examined.

Yet, it is expected that the near future Square Kilometer Array (SKA)

Can there exist a method of estimating the DOA of GWs

2.2
Correlations of quadrupole moments of pulsar timings

A quadrupole and traceless part of the redshifts over the sky can be defined as

\displaystyle I^{ij}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}z_{a}(t)q_{a}^{ij},

(2.6)

where
\oint d\Omega_{a}
is an integral over the full sky,
and

\displaystyle q_{a}^{ij}\equiv\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{i}\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{j}-\frac{1}{3}\delta^{ij}.

(2.7)

The present paper employs the pulsar averaging
[
33
]
.

A naive intuition may mislead to a candidate for
correlations of quadrupole moments in pulsar timings as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{obs}}\int_{0}^{T_{obs}}dt\left[\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}\Bigl(s_{a}(t)q_{a}^{ij}\Bigr)\right)\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{b}\left(s_{b}(t+\tau)q_{b}^{ij}\right)\right)\right],

(2.8)

where
the correlation among pulsar timings is taken over the full sky
as well as the whole observation period
T_{obs}
,
and
\tau
denotes a time lag in the autocorrelation.
Here, the subscripts
i
and
j
are doubly written but not contracted with each other.
As a result,

q_{a}^{ij}q_{b}^{ij}
is not a tensor but ill-defined
in the simultaneous limit as
\tau\to 0
.
Hence, the present paper does not employ the form of Eq. (
2.8
).

Therefore, we define another form as

\displaystyle Q^{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{T_{obs}}\int_{0}^{T_{obs}}dt\left[\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}\left(s_{a}(t)q_{a}^{ik}\right)\right)\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{b}\left(s_{b}(t+\tau)q_{b}^{kj}\right)\right)\right],

(2.9)

where the summation is only for
k
as
\sum_{k=1}^{3}
.
Note that
q_{a}^{ik}q_{b}^{kj}
is a tensor
in the simultaneous limit as
\tau\to 0
.

We suppose that noises are random in space and time to follow

\displaystyle\oint d\Omega_{a}n_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\oint d\Omega_{a}n_{a}(t)\hat{\Omega}_{a}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\oint d\Omega_{a}n_{a}(t)q_{a}^{ij}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{obs}}dt\>n_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=0.

(2.10)

The second and third equations in Eq. (
2.10
) mean that
there are no dipole and quadrupole moments of noises.
By using Eqs. (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and (
2.10
),
Eq. (
2.9
) becomes

\displaystyle Q^{ij}=\frac{1}{T_{obs}}\int_{0}^{T_{obs}}dt\left[\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}\left(z_{a}(t)q_{a}^{ik}\right)\right)\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{b}\left(z_{b}(t+\tau)q_{b}^{kj}\right)\right)\right],

(2.11)

The directional average of
F^{A}_{a}q_{a}^{ij}
for
A=+,\times

becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}F_{a}^{A}q_{a}^{ij}=C_{A}e_{A}^{ij}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW}),

(2.12)

where the sum over
A
is not taken and

C_{A}=1/6
.
See Appendix A for a derivation of Eq. (
2.12
).

By using Eqs. (
2.3
), (
2.4
) and (
2.12
),
Eq. (
2.11
) is calculated as

\displaystyle Q^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{36T_{obs}}\int_{0}^{T_{obs}}dt\Bigl((h^{+})^{2}\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega(t+\tau))e_{+}^{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e_{+}^{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+(h^{\times})^{2}\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega(t+\tau))e_{\times}^{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e_{\times}^{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+h^{+}h^{\times}\Bigl[\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega(t+\tau))e_{+}^{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e_{\times}^{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega(t+\tau))e_{\times}^{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e_{+}^{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})\Bigr]\Bigr).

(2.13)

We use

\displaystyle e_{+}^{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e_{+}^{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})

\displaystyle=P^{ij},

\displaystyle e_{\times}^{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e_{\times}^{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})

\displaystyle=P^{ij},

\displaystyle e_{+}^{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e_{\times}^{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{ijk}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{k},

(2.14)

where

\epsilon^{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita symbol,
and
the projection tensor with respect to
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}

is defined as

[
5
,
6
]

\displaystyle P^{ij}\equiv\delta^{ij}-\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{i}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{j}.

(2.15)

See Appendix B for the derivation of Eq. (
2.14
).

Substituting Eq. (
2.14
) into Eq. (
2.13
) leads to

\displaystyle Q^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{36T_{obs}}\int_{0}^{T_{obs}}dt\Bigl((h^{+})^{2}\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega(t+\tau))P^{ij}+(h^{\times})^{2}\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega(t+\tau))P^{ij}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~-h^{+}h^{\times}\Bigl[\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega(t+\tau))-\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega(t+\tau))\Bigr]\epsilon^{ijk}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{k}\Bigr).

(2.16)

Furthermore, we take the limit as
T_{obs}\to\infty
,
which leads to

\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega(t+\tau))\to\cos(\omega\tau)/2
,

\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega(t+\tau))\to\cos(\omega\tau)/2
,

\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega(t+\tau))\to\sin(\omega\tau)/2
,
and

\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega(t+\tau))\to\sin(\omega\tau)/2
.
From Eq. (
2.16
),
we thus arrive at

\displaystyle Q^{ij}

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{72}\left(\Bigl[(h^{+})^{2}+(h^{\times})^{2}\Bigr]\cos(\omega\tau)P^{ij}-h^{+}h^{\times}\sin(\omega\tau)\epsilon^{ijk}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{k}\right).

(2.17)

In addition, we suppose that
the time lag is much shorter than the period of GWs
(denoted as
T_{GW}
),
namely
2\pi(\tau/T_{GW})=\omega\tau\ll 1
.
As an example, we can imagine

\tau=2
weeks, and
T_{GW}=12
months.
So, we take the limit as
\omega\tau\to 0
,
which leads to
\cos(\omega\tau)\to 1
and
\sin(\omega\tau)\to 0
.
Eq. (
2.17
) thus becomes

\displaystyle Q^{ij}

\displaystyle\to KP^{ij},

(2.18)

where

\displaystyle K\equiv\frac{1}{72}(h^{tot})^{2},

(2.19)

and

h^{tot}\equiv[(h^{+})^{2}+(h^{\times})^{2}]^{1/2}
is the GW intensity,

Some noise might contribute via the trace of
Q^{ij}
.
In order to avoid such an error contamination from noises,
it is convenient to consider the traceless part of
Q^{ij}
,
which can be defined as

\displaystyle R^{ij}\equiv Q^{ij}-\frac{1}{3}Q\delta^{ij},

(2.20)

where
Q
denotes the trace of
Q^{ij}
.
From Eqs. (
2.18
) and (
2.20
),

\displaystyle R^{ij}

\displaystyle\to K\left(P^{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\delta^{ij}P^{kk}\right)

\displaystyle=-Kq_{GW}^{ij},

(2.21)

where

\displaystyle q_{GW}^{ij}\equiv\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{i}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{j}-\frac{1}{3}\delta^{ij}.

(2.22)

By calculating the traceless part of Eq. (
2.9
),

R^{ij}
is expressed in terms of
the observed signals
s_{a}(t)
and
s_{b}(t+\tau)
as

\displaystyle R^{ij}=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}T_{obs}}\int_{0}^{T_{obs}}dt\oint d\Omega_{a}\oint d\Omega_{b}\left[s_{a}(t)s_{b}(t+\tau)\left(q_{a}^{ik}q_{b}^{kj}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigl[(\cos\gamma_{ab})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigr]\delta^{ij}\right)\right],

(2.23)

where

\gamma_{ab}
is a separation angle between the
a
-th and
b
-th pulsars,
namely
\cos\gamma_{ab}\equiv\hat{\Omega}_{a}\cdot\hat{\Omega}_{b}
,
and we use
q_{a}^{ik}q_{b}^{ki}=(\cos\gamma_{ab})^{2}-1/3
.

3
From quadrupole-moment correlations to DOA estimations

In this section,
we shall discuss how to estimate
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}

from the matrix
R^{ij}
.
From Eq. (
2.21
), we obtain
\det(R)=-2K^{3}/27
,
where
the determinant of
R^{ij}
is denoted as
\det(R)
,
and

\det(q_{GW}^{ij})=2/27
is used.
This equation is solved for
K
as

\displaystyle K=\left(-\frac{27\det(R)}{2}\right)^{1/3}.

(3.1)

For
i=x
and
j=x
, Eq. (
2.21
) becomes

R^{xx}=-K[(\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x})^{2}-1/3]
,
which is solved for
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{R^{xx}}{K}}.

(3.2)

There exist both signs of
\pm
, because

P^{ij}
does not distinguish
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}
from
-\hat{\Omega}_{GW}
.

For
i=x
and
j=y
, Eq. (
2.21
) reads

R^{xy}=-K\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}
,
which is rearranged as

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}=-\frac{R^{xy}}{K\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}},

(3.3)

where
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}\neq 0
is assumed.
Similarly, Eq. (
2.21
) for
i=z
and
j=x
leads to

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{z}=-\frac{R^{zx}}{K\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}}.

(3.4)

From PTA signals, we can estimate
R^{ij}
.
The components of
R^{ij}
are substituted into Eqs. (
3.2
) - (
3.4
)
to obtain the GW source direction
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}
.

For a unit vector
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}
,
at least one of its components,

\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}
,
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}
and
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{z}

never vanishes.
In a case of
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}=0
,
we should use counterparts of Eq. (
3.2
)-(
3.4
).
If
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{R^{yy}}{K}}.

(3.5)

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{R^{yz}}{K\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{R^{xy}}{K\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}}.

(3.7)

If
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{z}\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{z}

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{R^{zz}}{K}}.

(3.8)

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{R^{zx}}{K\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{z}},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{y}

\displaystyle=-\frac{R^{yz}}{K\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{z}}.

(3.10)

Therefore, the present method can be used in principle for any DOA.

4
Discreteness, noises and estimation errors

4.1
From a continuous limit to a discrete distribution of pulsars

In this section,
we take account that
the number of the observed pulsars as well
as that of the observations are finite.
We suppose that a finite number (
N_{p}
) of pulsars are observed
for a whole observation time
T_{obs}
with a cadence
P
.
Each of the pulsars is observed
N_{obs}\equiv[T_{obs}/P]+1
times,
where
[\quad]
denotes a floor function.

Eq. (
2.23
) with Eq. (
2.11
) can be rewritten in terms of discrete sums as

\displaystyle R^{ij}=\frac{1}{{\cal K}_{max}(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\left[s_{a}(t_{K})s_{b}(t_{K}+\tau)\left(q_{a}^{ik}q_{b}^{kj}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigl[(\cos\gamma_{ab})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigr]\delta^{ij}\right)\right],

(4.1)

where

K
is a positive integer denoting the
K
-th observation,

t_{K}\equiv P(K-1)
denotes the
K
-th observation epoch,
and

{\cal K}_{max}\equiv[(T_{obs}-\tau+P)/P]

means the number of the neighboring pair of

t=t_{K}
and
t=t_{K}+\tau
from
t=0
to
t=T_{obs}
.
In the rest of this paper,
we adopt
\tau=P
for its simplicity.
This leads to
{\cal K}_{max}=N_{obs}-1
.

4.2
Angular resolution limited by the number of pulsars

Eq. (
4.1
) is a discretized version of Eq. (
2.23
) with Eq. (
2.11
).
Let us imagine a random distribution of pulsars on the celestial sphere,

\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{i}=\Omega_{aBG}^{i}+\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}
,
where

\Omega_{aBG}^{i}
corresponds to a homogeneous distribution,
and the perturbation induced by a pulsar distribution is denoted by
\delta_{p}
.
Namely,
\Omega_{aBG}^{i}
and
\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}

mean a background quantity and a perturbation, respectively.
The random perturbation follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}\Bigr)\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{b}^{j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}\Bigr)\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{b}^{j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=b}\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}\Bigr)\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{b}^{j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}\Bigr)\Bigl(\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{N_{p}}\right),

(4.2)

where
the second equation is used to show the first equality of the third equation,
and

|\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}|=O(1)
is used in the last line.
In a random distribution,
the typical size of
\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}
is suppressed statistically
by a factor
O(1/\sqrt{N_{p}})
.

From Eq. (
4.1
),
a linear perturbation of
R^{ij}
induced by
\delta_{p}\Omega_{a}^{i}
,
denoted as
\delta_{p}R^{ij}
,
is expressed as

\displaystyle\delta_{p}R^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\cal K}_{max}(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\delta_{p}\left[z_{a}(t_{K})z_{b}(t_{K}+P)\left(q_{a}^{ik}q_{b}^{kj}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigl[(\cos\gamma_{ab})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigr]\delta^{ij}\right)\right].

(4.3)

The fluctuation in the pulsar direction is not dependent on time
during the PTA observation.
Hence, the perturbation does not affect the time average procedure defined as

(1/{\cal K}_{max})\sum_{K}
when
we calculate the autocorrelation.
Namely,
(1/{\cal K}_{max})\sum_{K}\sim O(1)
.
Hence, Eq. (
4.3
) becomes

\displaystyle\delta_{p}R^{ij}\sim\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\delta_{p}\left[z_{a}z_{b}\left(q_{a}^{ik}q_{b}^{kj}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigl[(\cos\gamma_{ab})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\Bigr]\delta^{ij}\right)\right],

(4.4)

where
z_{a}
or
z_{b}
simply denote a typical value of

z_{a}(t_{K})
or
z_{a}(t_{K}+\tau)
.

For the later convenience, first
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}z_{a}z_{b}

\displaystyle\sim(N_{p}h^{tot})^{2},

(4.5)

where we use

z_{a}\sim z_{b}\sim O(h^{tot})
.

The contribution of the
\delta^{ij}
term in Eq. (
4.4
)
is a fraction
\sim 5/6
,
because
R^{ij}
is a matrix with six independent components
in the limit of
\omega\tau\to 0
.
The factor
\sim 5/6
originating from the
\delta^{ij}
term
is
O(1)
in the following order-of-magnitude estimation.
Hence, we focus on the trace part of Eq. (
4.4
) as

\displaystyle\delta_{p}R^{ij}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\delta_{p}\left[z_{a}z_{b}q_{a}^{ik}q_{b}^{kj}\right]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}z_{a}q_{a}^{ik}\delta_{p}(z_{b}q_{b}^{kj}).

(4.6)

Note that the mean of
\delta_{p}R^{ij}
almost vanishes
owing to the first equation of Eq. (
4.2
).
By using Eq. (
4.6
),
the variance of
\delta_{p}R^{ij}
is thus calculated as

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{p}R^{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{4}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{d=1}^{N_{p}}\left(z_{a}q_{a}^{ik}\delta_{p}(z_{b}q_{b}^{kj})\right)\times\left(z_{c}q_{c}^{ik}\delta_{p}(z_{d}q_{d}^{kj})\right),

(4.7)

where the summation is taken only for
k
but not for
i,j

(also in the rest of this subsection).

Next,
we calculate

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{p}}\left(z_{a}q_{a}^{ik}\times z_{c}q_{c}^{ik}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{p}}z_{a}z_{c}

\displaystyle\sim O\left((N_{p}h^{tot})^{2}\right),

(4.8)

where we use Eq. (
4.5
),
and

|q_{a}^{ik}|\sim|q_{c}^{ik}|\sim O(1)
.

By using Eqs. (
2.3
), (
2.4
) and (
2.5
),
we find

\delta_{p}(z_{b}q_{b}^{ik})\sim O(z_{b}q_{b}^{ik})\times\delta_{p}\Omega_{b}^{i}\sim O(h^{tot})\times O(\delta_{p}\Omega_{b}^{i})
,
where
O(z_{a}q_{a}^{ik})\sim O(h^{tot})
is used.
By using
\delta_{p}(z_{b}q_{b}^{ik})\sim O(h^{tot})\times O(\delta_{p}\Omega_{b}^{i})
,
we thus obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{d=1}^{N_{p}}\left(\delta_{p}(z_{b}q_{b}^{kj})\times\delta_{p}(z_{d}q_{d}^{kj})\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle O\left((h^{tot})^{2}\right)\times\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{d=1}^{N_{p}}O\left(\delta_{p}\Omega_{b}^{j}\times\delta_{p}\Omega_{d}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle O\left(N_{p}(h^{tot})^{2}\right),

(4.9)

where Eq. (
4.2
) is used in the last line.

By using Eqs. (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) in Eq. (
4.7
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{p}R^{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(h^{tot})^{4}}{N_{p}},

(4.10)

where
|q_{a}^{ij}|=O(1)
is used.
Therefore,
the size of
\delta_{p}R^{ij}
is obtained as

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{p}R^{ij}\right|\sim\frac{(h^{tot})^{2}}{\sqrt{N_{p}}}.

(4.11)

We should note that
h^{tot}
cancels out from the ratio of
R^{ij}/K
,
because
R^{ij}\propto(h^{tot})^{2}
,
and

K\propto(h^{tot})^{2}
.
By using Eq. (
4.11
), therefore,
an accuracy in the components of the GW directional vector is

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{p}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{i}\right|

\displaystyle\sim\left|\frac{\delta_{p}R^{ij}}{K}\right|

\displaystyle\sim O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{p}}}\right),

(4.12)

where Eqs. (
3.2
) - (
3.10
) are used in the first line.
This leads to the angular resolution limited by the number of pulsars,
which is obtained as

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{p}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left|\delta_{p}\hat{\Omega}^{x}_{GW}\right|^{2}+\left|\delta_{p}\hat{\Omega}^{y}_{GW}\right|^{2}+\left|\delta_{p}\hat{\Omega}^{z}_{GW}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{\left|\delta_{p}\hat{\Omega}^{i}_{GW}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{N_{p}}}.

(4.13)

Eq. (
4.13
) is the angular resolution limited by the number of pulsars in the present method.
Roughly speaking, the angular resolution is

\sim 0.2
radian
(corresponding to
\sim 10
degrees) for
N_{p}\sim 100
for instance.

4.3
Estimation error from a random noise

In this subsection, next,
we shall discuss a typical size of the estimation error due to random noises,
where we suppose
n_{a}(t_{K})
and
n_{b}(t_{K^{\prime}})
are white random noises
with the zero mean and the common standard deviation
\sigma
for its simplicity.
Let us write down relations between
n_{a}(t_{K})
and
n_{b}(t_{K^{\prime}})
for the later convenience.
The zero mean for averaging over pulsar directions or over the whole observation time
is expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}n_{a}(t_{K})

\displaystyle=0,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}n_{a}(t_{K})

\displaystyle=0.

(4.15)

We assume that
there are no correlations between different directions or between different observation epochs.
They are written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a\neq b}n_{a}(t_{K})n_{b}(t_{K})

\displaystyle=0,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{({\cal K}_{max})^{2}}\sum_{K\neq K^{\prime}}n_{a}(t_{K})n_{a}(t_{K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=0.

(4.17)

For its mathematical simplicity,
we suppose the noises with the common variance
\sigma^{2}
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\left(n_{a}(t_{K})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2},

(4.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\left(n_{a}(t_{K})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}.

(4.19)

For a correlation between noises observed at a simultaneous time,
we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}n_{a}(t_{K})n_{b}(t_{K})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\left(n_{a}(t_{K})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N_{p}},

(4.20)

where Eqs. (
4.16
) and (
4.18
) are used in the first and second lines, respectively.
Similarly, we find that noises for the same pulsar satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{({\cal K}_{max})^{2}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{K^{\prime}=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}n_{a}(t_{K})n_{a}(t_{K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{({\cal K}_{max})^{2}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\left(n_{a}(t_{K})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}_{max}},

(4.21)

where Eqs. (
4.17
) and (
4.19
) are used in the first and second lines, respectively.

Let us discuss the perturbation of
R^{ij}
owing to the noises, denoted as

\delta_{n}R^{ij}
,
which is roughly

\displaystyle\delta_{n}R^{ij}\sim\left(\frac{1}{{\cal K}_{max}(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}n_{a}(t_{K})n_{b}(t_{K}+P)\right).

(4.22)

Here, we focus on the quadratic terms in noises
in Eq. (
4.1
)
by ignoring the coupling terms of GWs and noises,
because
the coupling terms are
\sim h^{tot}\times\sigma
,
the quadratic noise terms are
\sigma^{2}
,
and

h^{tot}\ll\sigma
is expected for the current PTA observations.

Note that the mean of
\delta_{n}R^{ij}
vanishes
owing to Eq. (
4.15
).
Therefore, the variance of
R^{ij}

is thus calculated as

\displaystyle(\delta_{n}R^{ij})^{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{{\cal K}_{max}(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}n_{a}(t_{K})n_{b}(t_{K}+P)\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{1}{{\cal K}_{max}(N_{p})^{2}}\sum_{K^{\prime}=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{d=1}^{N_{p}}n_{c}(t_{K^{\prime}})n_{d}(t_{K^{\prime}}+P)\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{({\cal K}_{max})^{2}(N_{p})^{4}}\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{p}}n_{a}(t_{K})n_{c}(t_{K})\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\left(\sum_{b=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{d=1}^{N_{p}}n_{b}(t_{K}+P)n_{d}(t_{K}+P)\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{({\cal K}_{max})^{2}(N_{p})^{4}}\times\sum_{K=1}^{{\cal K}_{max}}\left(N_{p}\times\sigma^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{4}}{{\cal K}_{max}(N_{p})^{2}},

(4.23)

where
Eqs. (
4.15
) and (
4.17
) are used in the fourth and fifth lines,
respectively, and
Eq. (
4.18
) is used in the sixth line.
Note that the summation is not taken for
i,j

By using Eq. (
4.23
),
the size of
\delta_{n}R^{ij}
is obtained as

\displaystyle|\delta_{n}R^{ij}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\delta_{n}R^{ij}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N_{p}\sqrt{N_{obs}}},

(4.24)

where we use
{\cal K}_{max}\approx N_{obs}

for
N_{obs}\gg 1
.

The ratio of
\delta_{n}R^{ij}
to
R^{ij}

gives us a typical size of the estimation error for a component of the GW directional vector.
It is

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{n}\hat{\Omega}^{i}_{GW}\right|

\displaystyle\sim\left|\frac{\delta_{n}R^{ij}}{R_{GW}^{ij}}\right|

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{N_{p}\sqrt{N_{obs}}}\times\left(\frac{\sigma}{h^{tot}}\right)^{2},

(4.25)

where
R_{GW}^{ij}
denotes the purely GW part of
R^{ij}
,
and
we use
R_{GW}^{ij}\sim(h^{tot})^{2}

from Eqs. (
2.19
) and (
2.21
).
This leads to the typical size of an estimation error in the GW direction as

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{n}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left|\delta_{n}\hat{\Omega}^{x}_{GW}\right|^{2}+\left|\delta_{n}\hat{\Omega}^{y}_{GW}\right|^{2}+\left|\delta_{n}\hat{\Omega}^{z}_{GW}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{\left|\delta_{n}\hat{\Omega}^{i}_{GW}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\sqrt{3}}{N_{p}\sqrt{N_{obs}}}\times\left(\frac{\sigma}{h^{tot}}\right)^{2},

(4.26)

This is evaluated as

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{n}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}\right|

\displaystyle\sim 0.1\Bigg(\frac{128}{N_{p}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\frac{24}{N_{obs}}\Bigg)^{1/2}\Bigg(\frac{\sigma}{10}\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{h^{tot}}\Bigg)^{2}.

(4.27)

Roughly speaking, the estimation error is

\sim 0.1
radian (corresponding to
\sim 6
degrees)
for e.g.
N_{p}\sim 100
,
N_{obs}\sim 24
,

\sigma/h^{+}\sim\sigma/h^{\times}\sim 10
.

4.4
Single dominant source over an isotropic stochastic background

Finally, let us mention a possible way to make the scenario more realistic.
We suppose a single SMBHB that dominates over a stochastic background
in a certain frequency bin of PTA observations,
where the signal can be expressed as

s_{a}(t)=z_{a}(t)+z_{aBG}(t)+n_{a}(t)

for
z_{aBG}
denoting the redshift due to the stochastic background.

For its simplicity, we assume the isotropic stochastic background
possibly generated by inflation,
for which there is no quadrupole moment of the redshifts
due to the background GW,
namely
\oint d\Omega_{a}z_{aBG}(t)q_{a}^{ij}=0
.
From Eq. (
2.9
), we can thus arrive at Eq. (
2.18
).
Therefore, the present formulation and method stand also in this situation.

5
Summary

We discussed correlations between the quadrupole moments of
pulsar timings due to GWs,
where we supposed that an isolated source such as a SMBHB
dominates over the isotropic stochastic background
in a certain bin of the PTA frequency domain.
This situation was formulated to demonstrate that a DOA of a GW can be estimated in principle.
The angular resolution as well as estimation error in the present method was studied.
According to the analytical and numerical calculations,
the forthcoming SKA will make the DOA estimation possible.

Is the present method applicable also in the presence of a sub-dominant GW component
in the same frequency bin?
Do higher multipole moments such as octupole moments play any role
in improving the DOA estimation?
These issues are left for future.

Appendix A
Derivation of Eq. (

In this appendix,
we shall derive Eq. (

Let us begin with the angular average of PTA antenna pattern functions
in Eq. (
2.5
),
which is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}F_{a}^{A}=\frac{1}{8\pi}e_{ij}^{A}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})\oint d\Omega_{a}\frac{\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{i}\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{j}}{1+\hat{\Omega}_{GW}\cdot\hat{\Omega}_{a}}.

(A.1)

The integral in the right-hand side of Eq. (

i
and

\hat{\Omega}_{GW}

Secondly, we consider the angular average of a dipole part of the antenna pattern,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}F_{a}^{A}\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{k}=\frac{1}{8\pi}e_{ij}^{A}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})\oint d\Omega_{a}\frac{\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{i}\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{j}\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{k}}{1+\hat{\Omega}_{GW}\cdot\hat{\Omega}_{a}}.

i,j,k
,
and it depends on
\hat{\Omega}_{GW}
.
Hence, the integral is proportional

\delta^{ij}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{k}+\delta^{jk}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{i}+\delta^{ki}\hat{\Omega}_{GW}^{j}
.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint d\Omega_{a}F_{a}^{A}\hat{\Omega}_{a}^{k}=0,

(A.4)

where we use

Thirdly, we examine the angular average of a quadrupole part of the antenna pattern,

\hat{\Omega}_{GW}
.

From Eqs. (

\displaystyle e^{+}_{ik}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})e^{\times}_{kj}(\hat{\Omega}_{GW})

In the left-handed system,
