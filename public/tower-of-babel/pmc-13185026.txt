Adaptations to the Chronic Disease Self-Management Program for diverse clinical and cultural populations: a scoping review protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Chronic Disease Self-Management Program (CDSMP) is a widely used peer-led patient education intervention that imparts disease knowledge and self-management skills to people with chronic diseases. While the CDSMP was developed to be accessible to people with diverse chronic diseases, chronic disease self-management needs and beliefs can vary by clinical or cultural populations. Questions remain regarding the range of populations it has been adapted for and the methods that have been used to make these modifications. This scoping review explores the following research questions: For which clinical and cultural populations has the CDSMP been adapted? Which methods, frameworks and/or processes have been used to inform CDSMP adaptations?MethodsWe are conducting a scoping review with guidance from the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis and Arksey and O’Malley’s framework. To extract information on adaptations, we will use an adapted version of the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications.Ethics and disseminationThis study does not involve the participation of human subjects. Introduction

Chronic diseases such as diabetes, heart disease, stroke and cancer are some of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the USA and globally.1 Chronic diseases, referred to as ‘non-communicable diseases’ by the WHO,2 typically result from and progress due to a combination of genetic, physiological, environmental and modifiable behavioural factors such as smoking, physical activity, stress and dietary habits.2 According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), chronic diseases are defined broadly as conditions that last 1 year or more and require ongoing medical attention or limit activities of daily living or both.3 There is also variation in the use of the term chronic disease within and across professional communities (eg, public health, academic research, social policy). For example, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services list Alzheimer’s disease, depression and HIV as chronic conditions.4

While precise definitions can vary, there is consensus that chronic diseases require day-to-day management,4 but developing the skills needed to self-manage a chronic disease can be challenging. The Chronic Disease Self-Management Program (CDSMP) is a peer-led patient education intervention that imparts disease knowledge and self-management skills to individuals with a wide range of chronic diseases.5 6 As described by Lorig et al,7 the CDSMP is based on three main assumptions. First, people with different chronic diseases share many self-management challenges (such as feelings of anxiety or overwhelm) and disease-related tasks (such as adhering to medications and communicating with health providers). Second, people with chronic diseases can learn skills for managing their health conditions. Third, individuals that are skilled and empowered to manage their own chronic diseases will both demonstrate improved health and consume fewer healthcare resources.

The CDSMP consists of six weekly group sessions and can be delivered either in person or virtually. The programme is based on self-efficacy theory8 and incorporates elements of skills mastery, modelling, problem solving and social persuasion into each session.5 6 During each weekly session, study participants learn different skills needed to self-manage any chronic condition. Example session topics include fatigue and getting good night’s sleep, dealing with difficult emotions, making decisions, making an action plan, medication usage, communication skills and working with your healthcare team. Another core feature of the CDSMP is the assumption that peer health providers (eg, trained lay persons with chronic conditions) are effective programme facilitators, and that patients and other professionals are willing to work with peer facilitators7

The CDSMP has been implemented worldwide, and a body of research has documented its effectiveness for improving disease self-management, behavioural activation and clinical outcomes in general medical populations.7 9 10 Effectiveness studies have reported CDSMP participant improvements in weekly minutes of exercise, communication with healthcare providers and self-reported health, along with fewer days in the hospital, among other outcomes.7 Participating in the CDSMP, relative to usual care, can also have a lasting impact on programme participants. A 1-year and 2-year longitudinal follow-up to a randomised clinical trial of the CDSMP reported that, compared with baseline, emergency room/outpatient visits and health distress were reduced while self-efficacy (eg, perceived ability to manage different aspects of chronic disease) was improved. Study authors concluded that the CDSMP can improve elements of health status while reducing healthcare costs in populations with diverse chronic diseases.11

The CDSMP includes content related to specific self-management strategies and allows ample space for participants to apply these strategies to their specific concerns. This is what makes the CDSMP useful to people with diverse chronic diseases and health conditions. However, programme participants’ chronic disease self-management needs and beliefs can vary by clinical or cultural population, as can the self-management of co-occurring social and mental health challenges. Thus, the CDSMP has been adapted across multiple studies for a range of clinical, cultural and global populations. For example, the CDSMP has been adapted for persons with serious mental illness,12 13 for homeless men14 and for people who are deaf or hard of hearing.15 Various intervention adaptation frameworks have been used to modify the CDSMP for these populations. One author used the ADAPT-ITT13 framework to better reflect the needs of persons with HIV, such as adding a self-management record to track participant medications, and increasing the number of group sessions from 6 sessions to 12.

The CDSMP has also been adapted to meet the needs of diverse cultural populations, including people of African American16 and Latinx17 heritage. Adaptations have included modifications to the programme’s name, inclusion of culturally familiar logos and references towards culturally familiar foods. Often these adaptations are made using Community-Based Participatory Research frameworks.18

While the CDSMP has been adapted and implemented across diverse populations, questions remain regarding the range of populations it has been adapted for and the methods that have been used to make these modifications. Understanding who the CDSMP has been adapted for and how adaptations were made can accelerate its implementation among patient populations with highly specific needs. Methods

We will conduct our scoping review with guidance from the latest version of the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis.19 We will use the scoping review methodological framework as outlined by Arksey and O’Malley20 to conduct our scoping review with Arksey’s five stages: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data and (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results. We will adhere to the PRISMA reporting guidelines for scoping reviews and searches onlinesupplemental appendix A B.21 22 We will use Box (box.com) for data management, EndNote (Clarivate Analytics) for citation management and Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation) licensed to the University of Utah for screening, selection and extraction data management. We began title-abstract screening on 10 November 2025. We plan to begin extracting articles in December 2026. We plan to finish our study and submit manuscript for publication in late 2027.

Literature searching

A librarian (TC) will develop the search strategies using a combination of keywords and database subject headings for the primary database, Medline (Ovid), from sentinel studies and team feedback, then translate the strategy to the other selected databases. A librarian colleague (MMM) will peer review the strategy according to Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines.23 Citation management and duplicate detection and removal will be accomplished with EndNote (Clarivate Analytics).

Databases will include Medline (Ovid) 1946–2025, Embase (Elsevier) 1974–2025, CINAHL Complete (Ebscohost) 1937–2025, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) 1898–2025 (Wiley), Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate) 1900–2025, Sociological Abstracts (ProQuest) 1952–2025, Dissertations and Theses Global (ProQuest) 1861–2025 and Dimensions (Digital Science) coverage varies. Conference abstracts will be excluded from Embase, Web of Science, Sociological Abstracts and Dimensions. Study selection

Two reviewers from a pool of five reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts and will independently review full text for inclusion. When no consensus can be reached between the two reviewers, a third reviewer (ES) will be the deciding vote. We will use Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation), an online systematic reviewing platform, to screen and select studies. We will use the AI tools integrated within Covidence for (1) prioritising records with most relevant sorting in title/abstract screening and (2) tagging references reporting on randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to assist in the screening, although all records will be screened by human reviewers. The tool will be used with no customisation, training or parameter changes applied.

Missing data

In cases where there is missing data, we will contact the corresponding author (two times over a 3-week period) to supplement information for the participants, concept or context missing from the primary source. If no response, we will include the study and state the missing data elements for the participants, concept or context.

Quality assessment

Given that the goal of this scoping review is to rapidly map the literature, the study team will not conduct quality assessment of included studies, which complies with scoping review methodology. Further, it is difficult to assess study quality across the multiple study designs that are likely to emerge in our study results.

Analysis of evidence

We will analyse our extracted data descriptively. Two researchers (E.S and a research assistant) will organise study findings into categories (eg, target population; method, framework or process used for adaptation; adaptations made to the CDSMP, outcomes and reported study limitations). In the case of missing data, we will document and report missing data, or author-supplied missing data, for elements for the participants, concept or context.

Presentation of results

In our final written report, we will organise results from included studies into a narrative and tabular descriptions by conceptual categories and subthemes. The final report’s first table will report on characteristics of the articles that meet inclusion criteria. This table will include the study citation, aims and purpose, and target population. The final report’s second table will include adaptation goals, the adaptation framework or method used and description of the adaptations made to the CDSMP. The final report’s third table will include outcomes measured and key findings. The final report’s fourth table will include the FRAME process elements.

Ethics and dissemination of results

This protocol does not involve human subjects and thus does not have approval from an institutional board (irb@hsc.utah.edu). Results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal for publication and disseminated at conference presentations. Any changes from the scoping protocol methodology will be acknowledged and defined in the manuscript.
