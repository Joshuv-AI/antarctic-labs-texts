Bidirectional relations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems in community adults: Findings from a longitudinal observational cohort study
Authors: Sophie G Coelho, Kyla L Belisario, Matthew T Keough, James MacKillop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBehavioral economic theory posits that the relative reinforcing value of alcohol (i.e., demand) prospectively predicts alcohol use behaviors, although this has rarely been examined longitudinally. This study examined bidirectional associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems over time. Community adults (N = 1,395; M age = 34.32, 59.93% female) completed measures of alcohol demand, alcohol use, and alcohol‐related problems in three assessments, spaced 6 months apart. Random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel analyses modeled time‐invariant and reciprocal time‐varying associations of alcohol demand indices with alcohol use and problems. Between participants, we observed large, positive associations of demand intensity (i.e., consumption at zero cost), demand elasticity (i.e., price sensitivity), O max, (i.e., maximum expenditure), and breakpoint (i.e., price suppressing consumption to zero) with alcohol use and problems. Within participants, lower elasticity forecasted greater alcohol use and problems, with the latter also forecasted by higher O max; reciprocally, lower elasticity and higher O max were each forecasted by greater alcohol use. Breakpoint exhibited inconsistent within‐person associations with alcohol use and problems, and intensity was only forecasted by changes in alcohol use. Results support an etiological role of alcohol demand in changes in alcohol use and problems over time, albeit principally for demand indices capturing the elastic portion of the demand curve.Keywords: alcohol demand, alcohol use, behavioral economics, reinforcer pathology, repeated assessments

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol is among the most widely used psychoactive substances in North America, with more than two thirds of adults reporting at least annual use (Government of Canada, 2021; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services, 2024). Among drinkers, a sizable subset report frequent alcohol use, heavy episodic drinking, and alcohol use disorder (Grant et al., 2017; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services, 2024), which substantively increase risk for morbidity and mortality (Abdul‐Rahman et al., 2018; Kendler et al., 2016; MacKillop et al., 2022). These risks underscore the importance of understanding the determinants of alcohol misuse to inform prevention and intervention.

Behavioral economic models draw from operant learning theory and microeconomics to understand the motivational determinants of substance use and have been applied extensively to the study of alcohol use and problems (Bickel et al., 2007, 2014; Hursh et al., 2005; MacKillop, 2016). According to behavioral economic theory, the reinforcing value of alcohol, or the extent to which an individual finds alcohol positively and negatively reinforcing, is an important proximal determinant of its use (Bickel et al., 2007, 2014; Hursh et al., 2005). The relative reinforcing value of alcohol can be operationalized as alcohol demand, describing the relation between the consumption of alcohol and its financial cost (Hursh, 1980; Hursh et al., 2005; Hursh & Silberberg, 2008). Alcohol demand is most commonly assessed using hypothetical purchase tasks, which were originally developed by Dr. Warren Bickel for measuring opioid and tobacco reinforcing value (Jacobs & Bickel, 1999) and subsequently adapted for alcohol, cannabis, and other commodities (Murphy & MacKillop, 2006). The Alcohol Purchase Task asks individuals to report how much alcohol they would consume across escalating prices (Kaplan et al., 2018; Murphy & MacKillop, 2006). Demand curve analyses then quantify the consumption‐cost relation, yielding multiple indices of strength of motivation to consume alcohol, such as consumption when alcohol is free (intensity), maximum expenditure (O max), the price at which consumption is suppressed to zero (breakpoint), and the sensitivity of consumption to increasing price (rate of change in elasticity, or the aggregate slope of the demand curve). These indices, which are conceptually related but distinct, collectively characterize individual differences in the reinforcing value of alcohol (Hursh et al., 2005; MacKillop, 2016). Warren Bickel championed the importance of using demand curves as a comprehensive framework for quantifying reinforcing value and reconciling divergent findings across the field (Bickel et al., 2000), and the use of self‐reported consumption preferences in purchase tasks provides an efficient and versatile methodology for diverse research designs.

Prior research has demonstrated robust cross‐sectional associations of alcohol demand indices, and especially intensity, O max, and elasticity, with alcohol use behaviors. Indeed, meta‐analyses indicate that elevated alcohol demand is cross‐sectionally associated with greater alcohol consumption, alcohol‐related problems, and hazardous drinking, generally with moderate effect sizes (Martínez‐Loredo et al., 2021; Zvorsky et al., 2019). However, cross‐sectional data preclude the disaggregation of within‐ and between‐person associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use behaviors and obscure the directions of these associations. This is a significant limitation because although theory predicts that alcohol demand is a determinant of drinking, an alternative hypothesis is that elevated reinforcing value is a feature or expression of alcohol involvement—more epiphenomenon than psychological mechanism. Moreover, as hypothesized by molar behavioral models of substance use (Tucker et al., 2023), within‐person shifts in alcohol demand confer within‐person shifts in alcohol use behaviors and vice versa, although this has been examined in only a small number of longitudinal studies.

One prospective study examined bidirectional associations of alcohol demand with heavy drinking status among adults initiating attempts at natural recovery from problem drinking, observing bidirectional associations of demand intensity with heavy drinking and inconsistent results for other demand indices (Cheong et al., 2023). However, this study's analyses did not disaggregate within‐ from between‐person associations, and thus the extent to which results reflect time‐varying (versus time‐invariant) associations is unclear. Two recent studies employed analytic approaches that disaggregated within‐ from between‐person associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems, specifically among emerging adults reporting heavy drinking. Acuff et al. (2024) observed prospective associations of increased intensity with subsequently elevated alcohol‐related problems, but only among male and racially minoritized participants. Reciprocal associations whereby greater alcohol‐related problems predicted subsequently elevated intensity were also observed in these subgroups. Greater O max also forecasted subsequent increases in heavy drinking at select measurement points, but associations in the opposite direction were more consistently observed. Bird et al. (2024) similarly observed reciprocal associations of greater O max and breakpoint with greater alcohol‐related problems as well as prospective associations of lower elasticity with subsequently elevated alcohol‐related problems, but only at select measurement points. More consistent across measurement points were reciprocal associations between lower elasticity and heavier drinking as well as associations from greater O max to subsequently heavier drinking and from heavier drinking to subsequently increased intensity. In each of these studies, between‐person associations of alcohol demand indices with alcohol use and problems were statistically significant and of large magnitude, consistent with prior literature, and the within‐person cross‐lagged associations were of smaller magnitude, as expected.

These studies provide initial evidence for time‐varying, bidirectional associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems, but important knowledge gaps remain. First, previous samples consisted entirely of individuals reporting past or current heavy drinking (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024; Cheong et al., 2023). Thus, the generalizability of findings to individuals reporting light or moderate alcohol use is unclear. This is an important research gap given that these individuals comprise the majority of adults who use alcohol (Grant et al., 2017; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services, 2024). Furthermore, alcohol‐related harm is not exclusive to individuals who drink heavily, and rather, there exists a “prevention paradox” wherein individuals who drink at light or moderate levels, in constituting the majority of the drinking population, account for the majority of alcohol‐related harms at the population level (Kreitman, 1986; Poikolainen et al., 2007). Thus, examining whether prospective associations between alcohol demand and alcohol use and problems extend to general community samples that include individuals reporting light or moderate drinking, in addition to individuals reporting heavy drinking, is needed to establish the generality of behavioral economic models of alcohol use, which could inform prevention efforts targeting the wider drinking population.

The two studies of longitudinal relations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems that disaggregated within‐ from between‐person associations also focused exclusively on emerging adults (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024). A focus on emerging adults is understandable insofar as emerging adulthood is the developmental period during which heavy drinking and alcohol‐related problems are most prevalent across the lifespan (Government of Canada, 2021; Hingson et al., 2017; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services, 2024; White & Hingson, 2014). The transition out of this developmental period is associated with natural reductions in drinking (i.e., “maturing out;” Lee & Sher, 2018; Maggs & Schulenberg, 2004), but whether this transition is accompanied by concomitant shifts in demand–use associations remains unknown. Thus, there is a need to investigate the time‐varying relations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems in samples spanning a wider age range and to examine invariance across emerging adults and older adults to elucidate the developmental specificity or generality of these relations. In addition to age, well‐documented sex differences in alcohol use behaviors (Grant et al., 2012; Salvatore et al., 2017) also suggest a need to examine the invariance of time‐varying associations between alcohol demand and alcohol use and problems across sexes. Acuff et al. (2024) observed sex differences in the time‐varying associations of select demand indices with alcohol use and problems but, as noted, in a sample limited to heavy‐drinking emerging adults. Thus, there remains a need to investigate potential sex differences in the extent to which alcohol demand forecasts alcohol use and problems and vice versa in general community samples.

The current study

The current study examined bidirectional associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems surveyed over a 1‐year period in a large sample of general community adults, with the goal of disaggregating between‐person (i.e., time‐variant) and within‐person (i.e., time‐varying) associations. We hypothesized that at the between‐person level, greater alcohol demand would be associated with heavier and more hazardous alcohol use, aggregating across waves. Furthermore, we hypothesized that at the within‐person level, changes in alcohol demand would prospectively predict changes in alcohol use and problems, which would reciprocally prospectively predict changes in alcohol demand. These hypotheses were not preregistered but reflect long‐standing predictions in the field (Bickel et al. 2014, MacKillop, 2016) and findings from comparable longitudinal models among young adults (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024). As exploratory objectives, we also examined the extent to which time‐varying associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems were invariant by age and sex, although without a priori hypotheses.

METHOD

Participants and study design

Data were from a longitudinal observational cohort study of health behavior in general community adults, for which all study procedures were approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (protocol #4699). The initial cohort of 1,502 participants was recruited from an existing research registry of community adults in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada. Eligibility criteria for inclusion in the research registry included (a) age between 18 and 65 years, (b) minimum ninth‐grade education; (c) no extant terminal illness, and (d) willingness to consider participation in research studies. Enrollment in the research registry included a single extended in‐person assessment.

This analysis used data from the first three study waves, which took place in September of 2018 (T1), April of 2019 (T2), and October of 2019 (T3). Retention rates at T2 and T3 were 94.67% and 92.61%, respectively. Assessments were administered via the web‐based Research Electronic Data Capture software (Harris et al., 2019), and participants received a $40 (Canadian dollars) online gift card upon completion of each assessment. Five quality control items with unambiguously correct responses (e.g., “for this question, select ‘strongly agree’”) were administered throughout each assessment to ensure adequate attention and effort, and data were retained only when three or more of these items were answered correctly.

Of the 1,502 participants enrolled in the larger cohort study, we excluded n = 4 (2.66%) participants whose data did not meet the quality control criteria for any of their completed assessments across the first three waves. As the current analysis was focused on associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems, we also excluded the n = 103 (6.86%) participants who did not report past‐6‐month alcohol use in at least one of the three assessments (i.e., who were abstinent from alcohol across the entire study period). The remaining n = 1,395 constituted the final analytic sample. Baseline characteristics of participants who were included and excluded in the analytic sample are presented in Table S1. Participants who were excluded from the analytic sample were more likely to report a high school diploma or GED as their highest level of education relative to participants who were included in the analytic sample; no other statistically significant group differences were observed.

Sample demographic characteristics are reported in Table 1, and descriptive statistics on alcohol use and demand variables at each study wave are reported in Table 2. Briefly, at baseline, participants in the analytic sample were an average of 34.32 years of age (SD = 13.92). The sample was majority female (59.93%) and White (80.14%). The sample consisted primarily of individuals reporting light or moderate alcohol use; participants reported an average of six standard drinks and two drinking days per week across waves (see Table 2), and a minority of participants met a hazardous alcohol use threshold across waves (14%–18%). Among participants included in the analytic sample, n = 1,380 (98.92%), n = 1,314 (94.19%), and n = 1,291 (92.54%) provided data that met the quality control criteria at T1, T2, and T3, respectively. Data meeting the quality control criteria were available at all three waves for n = 1,239 (88.82% of the analytic sample), at two waves for n = 112 (8.03%), and at one wave for n = 44 (3.15%). Participants who contributed data at only one or two waves were retained in the analytic sample, as our use of full‐information maximum‐likelihood estimation to handle missing data (see Data analysis) allowed all participants to contribute to the estimation of model parameters regardless of the number of waves at which data were available.

Measures

Alcohol use

Participants completed the Daily Drinking Questionnaire (Collins et al., 1985) at each wave, on which they reported the total number of standard drinks (1.5 fl. oz. of distilled spirits, 5 fl. oz. of wine, or 12 fl. oz. of beer) that they consumed on each day during a typical week in the past month. For the analyses, we calculated the number of standard drinks and number of drinking days per typical week in the past month. Participants also completed the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT; Saunders et al., 1993) at each wave, which was used to assess hazardous alcohol use. The AUDIT consists of 10 self‐report items assessing past‐6‐month alcohol consumption and alcohol‐use‐related behaviors and problems. Items are rated on 5‐point ordinal scales from 0 to 4 and are summed to obtain a total score, with cut‐off scores of 8 or higher indicating hazardous alcohol use. The AUDIT exhibited acceptable internal consistency in our sample at each wave (ωT1 = 0.83; ωT2 = 0.84; ωT3 = 0.84). For analyses, we modeled indices of consumption and alcohol‐related problems separately (Reinert & Allen, 2007), with the former obtained by summing the first three items (ωT1 = 0.72; ωT2 = 0.75; ωT3 = 0.71) and the latter obtained by summing the remaining seven items (ωT1 = 0.79; ωT2 = 0.80; ωT3 = 0.81).

Alcohol demand

At each wave, the Alcohol Purchase Task (APT; Murphy & MacKillop, 2006) was administered when past‐6‐month alcohol use was reported to assess behavioral economic demand for alcohol. Participants reported the number of standard drinks that they would consume in a typical drinking scenario (i.e., where they typically drink; what they typically drink; and who, if anyone, they typically drink with) across 30 prices, ranging from $0 to $40 per drink. Responses were capped at 99 standard drinks to avoid extreme outliers. Task instructions outlined several assumptions including that no drinking would occur before or after the drinking scenario, they would personally consume all drinks during the drinking scenario, and no drinks could be stockpiled for later consumption. Instructions and price structure are shown in the Supplemental Materials. Demand indices used in analyses were maximum alcohol expenditure (O max), consumption when alcohol was free (intensity), the price at which alcohol consumption was suppressed to zero (breakpoint), and sensitivity of consumption to price (i.e., aggregate slope of the demand curve; elasticity).

Data analysis

APT data processing and calculation of demand indices

APT data were processed and demand indices were calculated in R (R Core Team, 2022), using the beezdemand package (Kaplan et al., 2019). APT data were treated as missing if exhibiting one or more of signs of nonsystematic responding (Kaplan et al., 2019; Stein et al., 2015), including the following: (a) constant demand, describing less than a 0.025 log‐unit reduction in consumption per log‐unit range in price that is not attributable to zero consumption across all prices (T1: n = 2; T2: n = 3; T3: n = 2); (b) bounce violations, describing price‐to‐price increases in consumption, at more than 10% of price increments (T1: n = 2; T2: n = 1; T3: n = 2); and (c) more than one reversal from zero, describing nonzero consumption values following zero consumption at two or more consecutive prices (T1: n = 0; T2: n = 0; T3: n = 0). In keeping with common practices in the purchase task literature (Kaplan et al., 2018), price‐level outliers in the raw APT data, defined as consumption values more than |3.99| standard deviations from the mean, were winsorized to one unit higher than the next non‐outlying consumption value.

Consistent with prior work (Kaplan et al., 2018), estimates of O max, intensity, and breakpoint were empirical, obtained by directly examining APT responses. Breakpoint was the first price at which consumption was suppressed to zero, or when consumption never reached zero, $45 was used as breakpoint (i.e., the preceding price increment [$5] higher than the highest price [$40]). Estimates of elasticity (i.e., price sensitivity) were derived from individual demand curves, fit using the exponentiated demand curve model (Koffarnus et al., 2015): Q=Q0×10ke−a×Q0×C−1+u, where Q = consumption at each price; Q 0 = derived intensity, or consumption at a price of zero; k = a constant across individuals denoting the range of consumption in logarithmic units, calculated by subtracting the log10‐transformed mean consumption at the highest price from the log10‐transformed mean consumption at the lowest price and adding 0.50 (Kaplan et al., 2019); C = the cost of the commodity; a = elasticity, or the rate constant representing the rate of decline in consumption as price increases; and u = residuals. As two points of demand (i.e., consumption beyond a price of zero) are required to calculate elasticity, elasticity was not calculated for participants reporting zero consumption across all prices (T1: n = 26; T2: n = 28; T3: n = 23) or consumption only when alcohol was free (T1: n = 10; T2: n = 5; T3: n = 12). Individual R 2 values for the estimated demand curves at each wave suggested acceptable fit (T1: median R 2 = .89 [range = .30–1.00]; T2: median R 2 = .90 [range = .31–.99]; T3: median R 2 = .90 [range = .05–.99]).

Extreme outliers in demand indices, defined as values more than |3.99| standard deviations from the mean, were winsorized to one unit (O max, intensity, breakpoint) or 0.001 units (elasticity, given its scale) higher than the next non‐outlying value. The APT was administered only when participants reported past‐6‐month alcohol use, as alcohol demand was otherwise presumed to be zero. Thus, in cases when the APT was not administered due to the absence of past‐6‐month alcohol use, O max, intensity, and breakpoint were each imputed as zero. In these cases, as well as when participants reported zero consumption across all prices or consumption only when alcohol was free, elasticity was imputed as 0.001 units higher than the maximum elasticity value across all waves (after Winsorizing extreme index‐level outliers) to represent highly elastic alcohol demand.

Descriptive analyses of temporal trends in alcohol demand, alcohol use, and alcohol‐related problems

For descriptive purposes, linear mixed models (LMMs) with waves (Level 1) nested within participants (Level 2) were used to examine temporal trends in alcohol demand (intensity, elasticity, O max, breakpoint), alcohol use (number of standard drinks per typical week, number of drinking days per typical week, AUDIT consumption scores), and alcohol‐related problems (AUDIT problems scores) across the study period. Separate models were specified with each of the aforementioned variables as the dependent variable. Wave (T1 coded as 0, T2 coded as 1, T3 coded as 2) was the independent variable in each model and was person‐mean centered to disaggregate within‐ from between‐person variance (Curran & Bauer, 2011; Enders & Tofighi, 2007) given that participants varied in their numbers of waves. Models included random intercepts and random slopes for wave. LMMs were fit using maximum‐likelihood estimation, and the Satterthwaite degrees of freedom correction was used to obtain robust standard errors and significance tests. These analyses were conducted using the lme4 package in R (Bates et al., 2015; R Core Team, 2022).

Random intercept cross‐lagged panel models

Bivariate random intercept cross‐lagged panel models (RI‐CLPMs) were used to examine time‐invariant and reciprocal time‐varying associations of alcohol demand indices with alcohol use and problems. The RI‐CLPM is an extension of the traditional cross‐lagged panel model that decomposes longitudinal data into stable between‐person differences and time‐varying within‐person differences (Hamaker et al., 2015; Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). The RI‐CLPM includes random intercepts, modeled as latent factors, with factor loadings constrained to 1, for each time‐varying variable. Random intercepts represent individuals' trait‐like deviations from the grand means such that lagged effects pertain exclusively to within‐person processes (Hamaker et al., 2015; Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). In the RI‐CLPM, autoregressive effects represent within‐person carryover effects from one measurement point to the next and cross‐lagged effects represent the extent to which an individual's deviation from their expected score (i.e., person‐specific mean) on one variable predicts their proceeding deviation from their expected score on another variable (controlling for within‐person carryover effects; Hamaker et al., 2015).

Each RI‐CLPM examined time‐invariant and time‐varying associations of one of the alcohol demand indices (O max, intensity, breakpoint, elasticity) with one of the alcohol use variables (number of standard drinks per typical week, number of drinking days per typical week, AUDIT consumption score, AUDIT problems score), yielding 16 primary models from which conclusions were drawn. We estimated separate models for each demand index combined with each alcohol use outcome to avoid multicollinearity, as demand indices and alcohol use outcomes were highly intercorrelated. This approach was consistent with prior longitudinal modeling of associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024). Extreme outliers (i.e., values more than |3.99| standard deviations from the mean) in number of standard drinks per typical week were Winsorized to one unit higher than the next non‐outlying value prior to estimating models. Furthermore, elasticity was log10 transformed and other demand indices and alcohol use variables were left untransformed. For each pair of variables, we estimated three RI‐CLPMs: (1) an unconstrained model, wherein both autoregressive and cross‐lagged effects were time‐varying; (2) a partially constrained model, wherein autoregressive effects were time invariant but cross‐lagged effects were time‐varying; and (3) a fully constrained model, wherein both autoregressive and cross‐lagged effects were time invariant. The optimal model specification for each pair of variables, from which parameter estimates were ultimately drawn, was determined using model fit indices; specifically, we examined the Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample‐size‐adjusted BIC (SSA‐BIC), comparative fit index, and Tucker–Lewis index, and we sequentially compared increasingly constrained models using the Satorra–Bentler scaled chi‐square difference test. In all models, we regressed random intercepts for both the alcohol demand and alcohol use variables on baseline annual pretax household income to control for its potential confounding effects. Income was selected as a covariate given its ostensible influence on monetary expenditure on the APT and established associations with alcohol use and problems (Huckle et al., 2010; Karriker‐Jaffe et al., 2013; Keyes & Hasin, 2008). Although age and sex also relate to alcohol use and problems (Grant et al., 2012; Nicolai et al., 2012; Salvatore et al., 2017), these were not included in the main RI‐CLPMs as covariates, as our research questions required their later use as grouping variables in multiple‐group RI‐CLPMs.

Next, the aforementioned RI‐CLPMs were extended to multiple‐group RI‐CLPMs to test whether lagged effects were invariant by age and sex. For these models, participants' ages were categorized as ≤29 years versus ≥30 years to align with a common definition of emerging adulthood as spanning ages 18 to 29 years (Arnett et al., 2014). We created just two age categories to maximize power retained to detect within‐person cross‐lagged effects within subgroups, and the aforementioned categorization yielded age groups similar in size (n = 724 and n = 671, respectively). We compared multiple‐group models with no equality constraints with multiple‐group models with lagged effects constrained to be equal across groups (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021), using the Satorra–Bentler scaled chi‐square difference test.

RI‐CLPMs were estimated using Mplus Version 8.8 (Muthén & Muthén, 2021), and the MplusAutomation and SBSDiff packages in R (Hallquist et al., 2024; Mann, 2018; R Core Team, 2022) were respectively used to summarize models and compute Satorra–Bentler scaled chi‐square difference tests. RI‐CLPMs were fit using maximum‐likelihood estimation with robust standard errors. Missing data were handled using full‐information maximum‐likelihood estimation, which uses all available data from each participant to estimate model parameters without requiring complete cases. Thus, all n = 1,395 participants were retained in analyses regardless of the number of waves at which data were available or whether APT data were missing at a given wave due to nonsystematic responding. Results are reported using standardized coefficients to facilitate interpretability, and per Orth et al. (2024), cross‐lagged effect sizes of ~0.03, ~0.07, and ~0.12 were, respectively, considered small, medium, and large.

RESULTS

Descriptive analyses of temporal trends in alcohol demand, alcohol use, and alcohol‐related problems

LMMs showed that, on average, participants significantly decreased in their demand intensity (B = −0.14, SE = 0.04, p < .001), number of standard drinks per typical week (B = −0.29, SE = 0.07, p < .001), number of drinking days per typical week (B = −0.04, SE = 0.02, p = .041), AUDIT consumption scores (B = −0.14, SE = 0.02, p < .001), and AUDIT problems scores (B = −0.08, SE = 0.03, p = .004) across the study period. We did not observe statistically significant changes in elasticity (i.e., price sensitivity; B = 0.0005, SE = 0.0004, p = .226), O max (B = −0.20, SE = 0.16, p = .230), or breakpoint (B = 0.02, SE = 0.09, p = .816) across the study period. Cross‐lagged associations from each primary RI‐CLPM are in Table 3. Across all models, multiple‐group analyses indicated invariance of lagged‐paths age groups and sexes. Fit indices for the models with and without lagged paths constrained across sexes and age groups are presented in Tables S4 and S5, respectively.

In models examining associations of alcohol demand indices with number of standard drinks per week, no cross‐lagged associations were statistically significant.

In models examining associations of alcohol demand indices with number of drinking days per week, lower elasticity prospectively predicted more frequent alcohol use from both T1 to T2 and T2 to T3, with large effect sizes. Reciprocally, more frequent alcohol use prospectively predicted lower elasticity from both T1 to T2 and T2 to T3, with large effect sizes. More frequent alcohol use also predicted higher O max and higher intensity from both T1 to T2 and T2 to T3, all with large effect sizes, although no cross‐lagged paths from O max and intensity to number of drinking days per week were statistically significant. No cross‐lagged associations of breakpoint with number of drinking days per typical week were statistically significant.

In models examining associations of alcohol demand indices with AUDIT consumption scores, from T2 to T3, lower elasticity and higher breakpoint each prospectively predicted heavier alcohol consumption, both with large effect sizes, whereas the reciprocal cross‐lagged paths from AUDIT consumption scores to elasticity and breakpoint were not statistically significant. By contrast, from T1 to T2, heavier alcohol consumption prospectively predicted lower elasticity and higher breakpoint, both with large effect sizes, whereas the reciprocal cross‐lagged paths from elasticity and breakpoint to AUDIT consumption scores were not statistically significant. Heavier alcohol consumption prospectively predicted higher O max from both T1 to T2 and T2 to T3, with large effect sizes, although the reciprocal associations from O max to AUDIT consumption scores were not statistically significant. No cross‐lagged associations of intensity with level of alcohol consumption were statistically significant.

In models examining associations of alcohol demand indices with AUDIT problems scores, greater O max and lower elasticity each prospectively predicted greater alcohol‐related problems from both T1 to T2 and T2 to T3, all with large effect sizes. No cross‐lagged associations in the opposite direction (i.e., from alcohol‐related problems to alcohol demand) or involving intensity or breakpoint were statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The current study sought to extend the sizeable cross‐sectional literature (Martínez‐Loredo et al., 2021; Zvorsky et al., 2019) and emerging longitudinal literature (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024; Cheong et al., 2023) linking alcohol demand with alcohol misuse by examining the bidirectional associations of multiple indices of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems over a 1‐year period among community adults. Consistent with an etiological role of alcohol demand in alcohol misuse (Bickel et al. 2014; MacKillop, 2016), we hypothesized that alcohol demand indices would exhibit both between‐person and bidirectional within‐person associations with alcohol use and problems. As hypothesized and consistent with prior cross‐sectional and longitudinal investigations (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024; Martínez‐Loredo et al., 2021), we observed large between‐person associations of each alcohol demand index with each alcohol use outcome such that individuals who tended to have greater alcohol demand across waves reported heavier alcohol use and more alcohol‐related problems overall across waves. Within persons, we observed several cross‐lagged associations of alcohol demand indices with alcohol use variables, although only some specifically supported alcohol demand as a determinant of future alcohol use and problems. Specifically, lower elasticity (i.e., lower price sensitivity) forecasted heavier alcohol use, consistent with prior longitudinal research among high‐risk young adults (Bird et al., 2024). Greater breakpoint also forecasted heavier alcohol use, but only as indexed by AUDIT consumption scores (and not number of drinking days per week) and between one pair of successive waves. Furthermore, both lower elasticity and greater O max forecasted more alcohol‐related problems, also aligning with prior longitudinal research (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024). These findings collectively suggest that as posited by behavioral economic theory (Bickel et al. 2014; MacKillop, 2016), within‐person shifts in select demand indices predict subsequent shifts in alcohol use and problems among community adults.

In addition to prospective associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems, we observed several within‐person associations wherein shifts in alcohol use and problems predicted subsequent shifts in alcohol demand. Specifically, increases in alcohol use forecasted subsequently lower elasticity and higher O max and intensity, consistent with prior research (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024). Whereas elasticity and O max additionally forecasted future alcohol use and problems, intensity did not; this pattern of results, considered alongside the large between‐person associations observed between intensity and all alcohol use variables, suggests that intensity may represent an epiphenomenon of heavy alcohol use rather than a determinant of future alcohol use. This aligns with prior brief intervention research wherein despite its large cross‐sectional associations with baseline alcohol use, intensity exhibited limited utility in predicting postintervention alcohol use (Mackillop & Murphy, 2007). Elasticity, by contrast, exhibited bidirectional within‐person associations with alcohol use, suggesting a vicious cycle wherein relatively inelastic alcohol demand confers heavier alcohol use, which in turn further diminishes elasticity. Thus, increasing sensitivity to the cost of alcohol may be an essential target of interventions for alcohol misuse.

The results of this study demonstrate that indices of alcohol demand selectively forecast and are forecasted by changes in alcohol use and problems. Notably, these associations were observed in a general community sample that included participants reporting light, moderate, and heavy alcohol use, extending prior longitudinal work conducted exclusively in heavy‐drinking samples (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024; Cheong et al., 2023) and supporting the generality of alcohol reinforcing value as a determinant of drinking behavior over time. As in prior research (Bird et al., 2024), elasticity and O max were most consistently supported as determinants of future alcohol use and problems, perhaps attributable to the degree of cost–benefit decision making that these indices reflect. That is, elasticity and O max capture the elastic portion of the demand curve as opposed to its start (intensity) or end (breakpoint; Hursh et al., 2005). Functional magnetic resonance imaging studies have shown this elastic portion of the demand curve to represent the most salient period of cost–benefit decision making (Amlung et al., 2024; MacKillop, 2016; MacKillop et al., 2014). Thus, our findings suggest that the value of alcohol when participants are putatively most conflicted may be most relevant in predicting future drinking. It is also notable that whereas intensity and breakpoint are available to conscious cognitive processing, O max and elasticity are cognitively implicit, not readily calculable by participants; the latter indices' consequent lower susceptibility to bias may additionally have contributed to the more consistent effects observed.

We also examined the extent to which time‐varying associations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems varied across age groups and sexes. Across all models, all time‐varying associations were found to be invariant by age and sex, indicating that within‐person associations of alcohol demand indices with alcohol use and problems differed significantly neither between emerging adult and older adult participants nor between male and female participants. The invariance of associations across age groups suggests that within‐person demand‐use associations observed in prior research (Acuff et al., 2024; Bird et al., 2024) are not specific to emerging adulthood, instead extending across the adult lifespan. It should be noted, however, that our age categories distinguished emerging adults from other adults to approximate the transition out of peak risk for heavy drinking but that further research with larger samples should explore invariance across more granular age categories. The invariance of associations across sexes contrasts with Acuff et al.'s (2024) findings, wherein bidirectional within‐person associations of intensity with alcohol‐related problems were statistically significant only among male (and not female) participants; the current study did not observe such associations in either sex. This discordance may be due to sample differences or to differences in measures used to assess alcohol‐related problems, although replication is needed to clarify. Nonetheless, our findings preliminary suggest that alcohol demand has similar utility in forecasting within‐person changes in alcohol use and problems across male and female community adults.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its large sample size, high participant retention, and repeated assessment of alcohol demand and alcohol use and problems over time. Still, several limitations should be considered. First, assessments covered only a 1‐year period, and thus future studies employing longer assessment periods should replicate analyses. Second, alcohol use and problems were relatively stable over the course of the 1‐year period, limiting the extent of change in these variables to be forecasted by alcohol demand. Third, all measures used in the current study relied on self‐reports, with the APT involving hypothetical rather than actual purchasing and consumption. Although APT performance has previously been shown to be similar for hypothetical and actual rewards (Amlung et al., 2012; Amlung & MacKillop, 2015), replication of findings using behavioral measures would support robustness. Fourth, as the current study was a secondary analysis with a predetermined sample size, power to detect within‐person cross‐lagged effects may have been limited; that some statistically nonsignificant within‐person cross‐lagged associations reflect insufficient power to detect smaller effects cannot be ruled out. Fifth, sociodemographic variables beyond age, sex, and income were not included as covariates in models in the interest of parsimony, and their potential confounding influence thus represents a limitation of the current study. Furthermore, most of the participants were White, and thus further research is needed to determine whether findings generalize to more racially diverse populations. The extent to which findings from our community sample may generalize to clinical populations also warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, this study extends understanding of role of behavioral economic demand in alcohol use and problems over time in providing further evidence that elevated alcohol demand is a feature of alcohol misuse yet also has longitudinal significance. Specifically, alcohol demand forecasted within‐person shifts in alcohol use and problems, albeit principally as indexed by elasticity and O max and in relation to specific alcohol use outcomes. Future research should further examine the bidirectional relations of alcohol demand with alcohol use and problems across longer periods and in different samples, including in both community and clinical settings.
