Health-related quality of life in palliative care: determinants identified in a hospital-based observational study
Authors: Iveth Marcela Urbano Chamorro, Juan Carlos Miangolarra Page
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care enhances the health-related quality of life of patients with advanced diseases. Therefore, it is essential to identify which factors contribute most to the perception of well-being to develop timely intervention strategies. The aim of this study was to examine factors related with health-related quality of life in adults with advanced disease of any etiology according NECPAL tool.MethodsAn observational cross-sectional study was conducted using a questionnaire based on the assessment of health-related quality of life with the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy – Palliative Care Spiritual Well-Being scale (FACIT-Sp).ResultsForty-two patients with life-threatening illnesses of 71.8 ± 11.29 years, 64.3% diagnosed with oncological disease. The mean Barthel Index was 59.5 ± 24.34, and an estimated survival prognosis of 50–60 days based on the Palliative Performance Scale. The mean FACIT-Sp was 87.9 ± 22.1, the highest scores observed in physical well-being dimension. Patients who engaged in some degree of physical activity the year prior to the study reported higher scores (103.51 ± 15.35 vs. 83.73 ± 21.88 [p = 0.015]), also, physical activity remained the only independent predictor of better HRQoL (β = − 17.96; p = 0.030). Additionally, participants with a primary caregiver—59.4% of whom were female—had higher scores on FACIT-G subscale (64.09 ± 13.48 vs. 53.38 ± 18.38 [p = 0.05]). The FACIT-Sp scale and its subscales demonstrated high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.889).ConclusionsEngaging in some degree of physical activity and maintaining functional performance may be associated with a better perception of health-related quality of life in palliative care patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01974-1.Keywords: Palliative care, Quality of life, Terminal care, Exercise, Physical functional performance, Spain, Caregivers

Background

According to the World Health Organization, palliative care aims to improve the quality of life of patients and their families confronting life-threatening illnesses [1]. While all chronic diseases may benefit from palliative interventions, in practice they are commonly categorized into groups: oncological conditions, which currently represent the largest share of advanced diseases; chronic organ diseases such as pulmonary, cardiac, and renal failure; dementias; neurodegenerative disorders such as multiple sclerosis or Parkinson’s disease; and certain infectious diseases, including HIV. Each condition follows a distinct trajectory [2]. Although they generally share common physical symptoms such as pain, dyspnea, and fatigue, certain differences exist [3, 4]. For example, neurological diseases are characterized by predominant functional decline and cognitive manifestations, including insomnia. Psychosocial and advance care planning needs are generally comparable across groups [5].

Advanced disease is well established to negatively affect multiple dimensions of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) simultaneously [4, 6]. The philosophy of palliative care emphasizes enhancing quality of life for patients and families, as medical interventions cannot alter the underlying disease. Strong evidence supports the benefits of early palliative interventions on HRQoL [7, 8], shaping the current model of care, which begins at diagnosis and extends throughout the disease trajectory, with particular attention to the final stages [9].

HRQoL measurement tools for palliative populations should be in line with their care goals and frailty. Ideally, they should be multidimensional, thoug no single tool is universally optimal for all patients [10]. Most available scales are multidimensional, but many omit the spiritual domain, which extends beyond religion and is fundamental in end-of-life care [11, 12]. Nonetheless, several HRQoL scales have been validated in this setting, with no major differences in outcomes across instruments [13, 14].

In Spain, it is estimated that 42–79% of individuals at the end of life require palliative care, yet only about 40% receive it [15, 16]. Despite the country’s strong contribution to palliative care research, there remains a pressing need to expand investigations, particularly in advanced non-cancer conditions [17]. Such efforts are essential to meet the demands of an aging population and the rising prevalence of non-communicable chronic diseases. Furthermore, identifying the factors that influence HRQoL in palliative care patients—especially those amenable to intervention—is critical to improving current care strategies. Against this background, the present observational study was designed to explore factors associated with HRQoL and functional status in adult patients with advanced disease of any etiology admitted to a hospital-based palliative care unit.

Methods

Desing

An observational, cross-sectional study was conducted between February 1, 2023, and February 29, 2024, among patients with life-threatening illnesses of any etiology admitted to a hospital-based palliative care unit in Spain. Data were collected through a questionnaire designed for this research and administered during the previously mentioned period to identify the factors (independent variables) associated with better HRQoL, which was the main dependent variable of the study.

A pilot study with five participants (three patients, one volunteer, and one palliative care professional) was conducted to refine the measurement instrument. None of the validated instruments included in the questionnaire were modified in form or content at any stage. Also, a self-administration was discarded due to the condition of the patients, and the questionnaire was completed at the bedside by the principal investigator. These preliminary results were not included in the final analysis.

The STROBE Statement checklist for cross-sectional studies was used for this work [18].

Setting/participants

Medical records of patient admissions to the palliative care unit over a 12-month period were reviewed. Patients ≥ 18 years or older, diagnosed with an advanced disease of any etiology, and identified as needing palliative care based on the NECPAL tool were included [19].

The following exclusion criteria were, participants with moderate to severe cognitive impairment assessed by Pfeiffer Short Portable Mental State Questionnaire (SPMSQ) ≥ 5 errors [20], also, patients with advanced dementia or Global Deterioration Scale (GDS) ≥ 6, as well as those in the end-of-life stage or Palliative Performance Status (PPS) ≤ 20. Given the vulnerable nature of the study population, efforts were made to keep data collection brief and eligible participants were already aware of their diagnosis and prognosis; to ensure confidentiality, each questionnaire was coded numerically. Therefore, the final sample was defined using a non-probabilistic design.

Data collection was carried out after a brief explanation of the investigation and the consent of each participant. A study-specific questionnaire was designed, consisting of two sections:

The first section collected sociodemographic and clinical variables, including cognitive status assessed by Pfeiffer SPMSQ and functional status evaluated by the Palliative Performance Scale (PPS), a validated tool in palliative care, estimates survival based on functional performance, ranging from 100% (fully ambulatory and healthy) to 0% (death) [21] and Barthel Index a widely validated instrument in palliative care, was used to assess functional ability in daily activities [22].

The second section contains the FACIT-Sp (Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy Palliative Care - Spiritual Well-Being version), a 39-item Likert-type scale scored from 0 to 4, that assess HRQoL through two subscales, the general questionnaire (FACIT-G) which is composed of key domains include physical, functional, emotional, and social/family well-being. And the specific questionnaire of palliative care and spiritual Well-Being, (FACIT-Sp-12). The total score can range from 0 to 156, where a higher score indicates a better HRQoL [23]. This scale has been internationally validated and is available for use in Spanish [13, 24, 25]. For this study, a license for the scale was obtained.

The questionnaire was not administered to participants experiencing moderate or severe pain according to the Visual Analog Scale (VAS ≥ 4) or any other uncontrolled symptoms. In such cases, data collection was divided into two or more sessions until adequate symptom control was achieved.

The final dataset was compiled and stored in a digital database using Microsoft Excel.

Data analysis

The analysis of the database was performed using IBM Corp. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Released 2020 (SPSS) for Windows, Version 29.0. Initially, an exploratory evaluation of the data was carried out, no missing values ​​ were found.

A descriptive analysis was performed using measures of central tendency and dispersion for quantitative variables and categorical variables were presented as percentages, graphs, and frequency tables. The results of FACIT-SP scale, as a dependent variable, were compared across different groups. The magnitude of the association was assessed based on the type of independent variable. A t-test was used for quantitative variables in two independent groups, while an ANOVA test was applied for categorical variables with more than two groups. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A multiple linear regression model was constructed using the enter method to examine the effect of the independent variables—physical activity, presence of a primary caregiver, and functional status measured by the Barthel Index—on health-related quality of life. The total FACIT-Sp score was used as the dependent variable.

Additionally, a descriptive analysis was performed for each item of the FACIT-Sp scale, along with a reliability analysis of the scale and its main components (FACIT-Sp-12 and FACIT-G) using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 283 patients were admitted to the palliative care unit between February 1, 2023, and February 29, 2024. After reviewing the medical records of each admission, 76 patients met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1), of these, 34 were excluded, due to cognitive impairment (Pfeiffer ≥ 5 errors) or clinical deterioration (PPS ≤ 20). A final sample of 42 participants answered the questionnaire. During the interview, only 14.28% of participants reported pain ≤ 3 on the VAS; the rest had a VAS score of 0. During the survey process, 4 patients completed the questionnaire in two sessions and 3 patients in three sessions, until adequate symptom control was achieved, mainly for pain (VAS ≥ 4) or dyspnea. The time elapsed between the start and completion of the questionnaire did not exceed 3 days.

Patient flow chart

The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Most patients were male (61.9%), with a median age of 71.8 years [IQR 51–96]. The main place of medical care was the urban area. Prior to admission to the palliative care unit, 52.4% of patients lived alone and 40.5% lived with their families; nevertheless, most participants had a caregiver, who was female in 59.4% of cases.

Clinical characteristics

In our sample, most patients were admitted for palliative care due to oncological disease (64.3%), mainly of lung origin, followed by colorectal and kidney cancer; 43.3% of these had metastatic disease. The second most common diagnosis was neurodegenerative disease (11.9%), primarily amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and multiple sclerosis. Except for advanced chronic kidney disease, male patients predominated across all groups. Pfeiffer test was normal (≤ 2) in 76.2% of patients, while 23.8% exhibited mild cognitive impairment.

Performance status

The functional ability to perform activities of daily living according to the Barthel Index (BI) was 59.5 ± 24.34. A total of 54.8% exhibited mild dependence (BI ≥ 60), whereas 14.3% had severe or total dependence (BI ≤ 30).

The mean BI was 60.65 ± 21.35 for male participants and 57.56 ± 29.2 for females; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.69). Regarding etiology, patients with advanced cardiac disease had the highest BI (67 ± 18.39), while those diagnosed with neurodegenerative disease had the lowest (43.4 ± 27.4), with no statistically significant differences (p = 0.6). The group of patients who engaged in some degree of physical activity had a mean BI of 71.67 ± 25.98, compared with 56.15 ± 23.18 in those with no physical activity, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.9).

Respecting the Palliative Performance Scale (PPS), the mean score was 52.14 ± 11.37. Most patients (69%) had a PPS score ≥ 50, which corresponds to an estimated survival prognosis of approximately 50–60 days. No statistically significant differences were found in PPS scores by etiology, although patients with neurodegenerative disease had the lowest values (48 ± 4.47, p = 0.6). Within this subscale, only the Social Well-Being (SWB)dimension showed a significantly higher score among participants with a caregiver compared with those without (SWB with caregiver: 17.72 ± 4.30 vs. SWB without caregiver: 8.28 ± 5.95; t = 5.51, p < 0.001).

Participants with neurodegenerative disease exhibited the lowest HRQoL across all outcomes. However, participants with heart disease, the spiritual dimension had an even lower value, with a statistically significant difference (FACIT Sp-12 subscale: 17 ± 1.41 p = 0.08).

Within the FACIT Sp-12 subscale, the Meaning dimension showed the highest mean score compared with the other components (Table 3). Among participants who engaged in some degree of physical activity, the Peace dimension obtained the highest score, with a statistically significant difference (some degree: 13.22 ± 2.28 vs. none: 8.58 ± 3.10; t = 4.18, p < 0.001). When analyzed by etiology, the Meaning dimension demonstrated significant differences: the group with advanced renal disease had the highest mean score 12.75 ± 3.40, whereas the group with neurodegenerative disease had the lowest 7.60 ± 3.65 (F = 2.87, p = 0.036). No significant differences were found across the remaining groups for the dimensions within this subscale.

According to the multiple linear regression analysis, the model yielded a coefficient of determination of R² = 0.212, indicating that 21.2% of the variability in the total FACIT-Sp score was explained by the independent variables included. The overall regression equation was statistically significant (F(3,38) = 3.40, p = 0.027), demonstrating that at least one predictor had a significant effect on health-related quality of life. Table 5 presents the coefficients of the regression model. Physical activity was the only variable that showed a statistically significant association with health-related quality of life. Its coefficient (β = − 17.96, t = − 2.261, p = 0.030) indicates that the absence of physical activity is associated with an average reduction of 17.96 points in the total FACIT-Sp score, reflecting a clinically relevant worsening of HRQoL. The absence of a primary caregiver was also associated with a decrease of 13.15 points in the FACIT-Sp total score; however, this worsening of HRQoL did not reach statistical significance (β = − 13.15, t = − 1.777, p = 0.084).

Discussion

This descriptive study aims to address the growing need to expand knowledge about the perception of well-being in palliative care, including patients with life-threatening non-oncological illnesses, while acknowledging the inherent fragility of the life cycle in which they find themselves. This perspective may explain the relatively small final sample despite the high number of eligible patients after applying selection criteria, largely because many were admitted to the unit as actively dying patients. Original studies involving palliative care patients, particularly those including individuals with a life expectancy of three months or less, often have small sample sizes. A review of palliative care research characteristics reported a median sample size of 53.5 patients, which was even lower in studies focused on the last month of life (mean of 20 patients) [26]. Likewise, a multinational cohort study of 7,220 palliative care patients, with a mean survival of approximately 66 days, found that only 11% were considered eligible, and of these, roughly half agreed to participate [27]. Our sample comprised inpatients with different diagnoses, admitted to the palliative care unit, most of whom had an estimated survival of approximately two months according to PPS values. Consistent with this, previous descriptive studies in palliative care units and hospices have shown that survival prognosis is generally limited, with a median survival of only a few weeks or less than three months, and somewhat longer among non-oncological patients [28, 29].

The overall HRQoL according to the FACIT-SP Total Score scale in our participants was 87.9 ± 22.1. Other studies have reported values for this scale; however, most of them focus on patients with specific diagnoses. For instance, 285 outpatients with advanced cancer reported a mean FACIT-SP Total Score of 99.8 [30], while in 150 patients with advanced heart failure a mean of 120.6 was recorded [31]. When analysing the results based on the diagnosis of advanced disease, patients with neurodegenerative disease had the lowest HRQoL. This aligns with existing literature, which highlights the negative impact of neurological diseases on HRQoL from the early stages of diagnosis, reinforcing the need for the timely integration of palliative care [32, 33]. Oncological disease was the predominant diagnosis in our sample, aligning with European palliative care data, where up to 94% of patients had this diagnosis [34]. Similarly, a Spanish study reported 92.1% of 63 patients from a palliative care unit had oncological diagnoses [35]. This pattern may reflect the historical development of palliative care programs in our setting, which were originally designed to primarily serve patients with advanced cancer, as well as the current institutional prioritization of identifying palliative needs in oncological populations. Therefore, our results may limit the representativeness of individuals with non-oncological diseases.

Although an established minimally important difference (MID) has not been defined for the FACIT-Sp scale, published MIDs from other FACIT instruments used in advanced disease, such as 4 points for the FACIT-G subscale and 2 to 4 points for the FACIT-F (fatigue scale) [36, 37], may offer a useful reference for interpreting the differences observed across comparison groups in our sample. These values provide helpful contextual information; however, this clinical interpretation should be considered exploratory.

The principal finding in our study was that participants who had engaged in any level of physical activity during the year prior to the study reported higher HRQoL scores. These differences were statistically significant across all scales. It is important to note, however, that this same group also showed higher functional levels compared with patients without a history of physical activity. Since better baseline functional ability is associated with fewer physical symptoms and higher HRQoL in patients with advanced disease, particularly in oncological populations [38], this may partially explain the observed differences. Nevertheless, in the multivariate linear regression model, physical activity was the only variable in our sample that was independently associated with better HRQoL.

As mentioned, physical activity is a well-established non-pharmacological intervention with demonstrated benefits for quality of life and symptom control in palliative care, provided it is adapted to individual needs and limitations. Most existing evidence derives from physiotherapy-guided interventions and rehabilitation groups [39], whereas our study examined the impact of any degree of activity, whether regular or irregular. Although no universal dose has been defined, in advanced disease the mere practice of physical activity represents a significant achievement given the symptom burden [40]. Greater benefits are likely to result from individualised prescriptions that account for functional status, symptom profile, and patient preferences [41]. Furthermore, physical activity plays a crucial role in mitigating or reversing the progressive loss of functionality associated with advanced diseases [35, 39], and reducing the caregiving burden [42, 43]. Additionally, in our sample, higher HRQoL values were found in patients with lower levels of functional impairment according to BI, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Even so, this finding remains clinically relevant, as functional status, together with morbidity and mortality, is a key indicator for evaluating healthcare strategies, including palliative care [44].

In our results, HRQoL scores across all scales were higher among participants with a primary caregiver; however, the statistically significant difference was observed in the general subscale and specifically within its social well-being domain. Caregivers play a crucial role in supporting patients with life-threatening illnesses, often assuming complex responsibilities that extend beyond basic assistance [45]. For this reason, the incorporation of caregivers into periodic assessments of patient needs is recommended [46]. Previous studies in advanced cancer populations highlight social support as a key contributor to improved HRQoL [47], which aligns with our findings. The literature consistently reports a predominance of female caregivers, a pattern associated with increased levels of stress, anxiety, and fatigue [48, 49]. In our sample, most primary caregivers were also women, which is consistent with previous reports. A local study involving 77 caregivers found that 66.2% were women and emphasizing the need for strategies to mitigate the adverse consequences of caregiving in advanced illness contexts [50]. Despite this, evidence regarding how caregivers specifically influence patients’ HRQoL, as well as their own, remains limited due to the scarcity and heterogeneity of available research [51].

Among all the dimensions, Physical Well-Being (PWB) recorded the highest score. This result may be attributed to the extensive research on managing physical symptoms prevalent in all advanced diseases, such as pain, dyspnoea, fatigue, nausea, and vomiting, in palliative care [52]; consequently, these interventions are widely implemented, and the management of physical symptoms can be considered key to assessing the effectiveness of palliative care [8, 49]. Emotional Well-Being (EWB) ranked second. Emotional support enhances HRQoL in patients and their families, alleviates depression and anxiety, and facilitate in disease understanding and end-of-life planning [53, 54]. In Spain, palliative care units adopt an interdisciplinary approach, including psychologists [55], which may explain our findings and highlights their crucial role in palliative care teams.

Regarding the FACIT-Sp-12 subscale, our results indicate an acceptable level of spiritual well-being, scores above 40 suggest good spiritual well-being consistent with the literature [56]. Within this subscale, the Peace and Meaning dimensions showed some significant differences, particularly among participants who engaged in physical activity. Previous studies have demonstrated that these two dimensions are associated with better psychological adjustment and lower levels of depression and anxiety [57], whereas the Faith dimension is more closely related to religious or cultural factors [58]. However, as previously mentioned, other dimensions received higher scores and and showed stronger associations in the overall HRQoL assessment. Although the medical literature suggests that spiritual care can improve quality of life in palliative care patients, the magnitude of this effect is generally small and not sustained over the long term [59]. Based on our experience during the administration of the questionnaire, we conclude that spirituality is a highly variable dimension that is difficult to quantify using a closed-question questionnaire. Therefore, we agree with other reports emphasizing the need for studies using other methodological approaches, such as qualitative designs, to better understand the effect of spiritual care interventions [60].

It is well established that the integration of palliative care into the treatment of life-threatening illnesses has a positive effect on HRQoL [61, 62]. However, the magnitude of this effect could be greater if palliative care were guided by the identification of patient-reported outcomes in terms of well-being, which may differ across populations. Additionally, such an approach could serve as a valuable tool for self-assessing the effectiveness of palliative care units.Observational studies such as this one can provide a foundation for intervention research exploring safe and feasible modalities of physical activity in palliative care.

Although Spain ranks among the leading countries in palliative care research [17], further studies, particularly those focusing on advanced non-oncological diseases, are needed to address the growing demands associated with increased life expectancy and the rising prevalence of chronic non-communicable conditions.

Finally, the high reliability values of the FACIT-Sp scale observed in this study are consistent with findings from previous research [25, 63], supporting its potential application in clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Spain to describe the most relevant aspects of HRQoL in palliative care patients with oncologic and no oncologic conditions, using validated tools to measure outcomes in terms of HRQoL and functional status.

The present work provides original observational data supporting a care approach centered on the perceived well-being of patients with life-threatening illnesses. Despite its methodological rigor, certain aspects warrant consideration.

The main limitation of this study is the small final sample size. Despite a high number of annual admissions to the palliative care unit, only a limited number of patients met the inclusion criteria, primarily because many were admitted in an actively dying state, while others were excluded due to severe cognitive impairment or rapid clinical deterioration occurring between screening and the planned assessment. These factors may have reduced the statistical power of the associations observed in the quality-of-life outcomes. For this reason, no further comparisons were performed between the dimension scores within each subscale, as the available data were deemed insufficient to support analyses with adequate power.

Because the objective was to obtain data specifically from patients admitted to the palliative care unit within a defined period, the study followed a non-probabilistic sampling design, for which establishing clear selection criteria was essential. Larger samples in future studies may allow a more detailed and robust exploration of these components. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader population with palliative care needs. Nevertheless, owing to its rigorous methodology, this study remains reproducible, its limitations are clearly identified for refinement in future research, and it contributes to the advancement of high-quality evidence in the field.

Although this study aimed to include both oncological and non-oncological patients, oncological diagnoses constituted by far the predominant etiological group in our sample. This should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results, as it may limit the representativeness of individuals with non-oncological diseases. Nonetheless, this work underscores the need to expand palliative care research to include, ideally in a more balanced manner, patients with advanced non-oncological conditions.

A potential source of confounding should be acknowledged, as the higher HRQoL observed in participants who reported physical activity in the previous year may reflect better baseline functional or a lower symptom burden rather than a true effect of physical activity itself. Therefore, these associations should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the results of the multiple linear regression analysis help mitigate this concern.

A potential performance bias is also recognized, as the principal investigator was part of the palliative care medical team. Still, to minimize this, patients were informed that their responses would not influence their treatment.

The magnitude of the associations observed directed the focus of this study toward physical activity and functionality, and to a lesser extent toward the spiritual dimension. The team considers that, to adequately analyze the benefits of this dimension on HRQoL, a different approach and tools other than closed-ended questionnaires would be necessary.

We acknowledge that, in a small subset of participants, the questionnaire had to be administered in two or three separate sessions to allow for adequate symptom relief. Given that patients with advanced illness commonly experience multiple symptoms, particularly pain, this situation was considered unavoidable. Allowing time for symptom control before continuing the assessment likely contributed to obtaining more reliable responses.

Conclusion

According to our observations, within our sample, engaging in some level of physical activity was associated with better perceived HRQoL among patients with life-threatening illnesses. In palliative care, functional capacity is particularly significant, as patients prioritize maintaining independence beyond their illness. This study also provides insight into factors associated with HRQoL in both oncological and non-oncological populations. Although some results did not reach statistical significance, the potential clinical relevance of these observational associations underscores the importance of a comprehensive, patient-centered approach that also recognizes the crucial role of the family or primary caregiver, as well as the continuous evaluation and adaptation of existing care programs.

Higher scores in physical well-being may reflect the effectiveness of current interventions targeting symptom control, a cornerstone of palliative care. By contrast, the spiritual dimension, widely recognized as essential in clinical practice, remains challenging to quantify, underscoring the need for complementary methodological approaches to assess its contribution.

Despite its limitations, we expect that the patient-reported outcomes of this study will contribute to enhancing the integrated approach to palliative care and encourage further research in this field, particularly among populations with advanced non-cancer diseases, while acknowledging their vulnerability and specific needs.
