Triple M overlap syndrome in the intensive care unit: differential diagnosis and management
Authors: Chail Shah, Rakesh Shetty Rajalbandi, Vikram Seshadri, Alvin Chandra, Oluseyi Abidoye, Joshua Battley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are increasingly used as first-line therapy for various malignancies and are associated with a growing spectrum of immune-related adverse events. While many toxicities are managed in the outpatient setting, a clinically significant subset progresses to life-threatening multisystem disease requiring intensive care. Among these, the Triple M overlap syndrome, characterized by concurrent myasthenia gravis-like disease, inflammatory myositis, and myocarditis, represents the most severe and lethal phenotype encountered in critical care.Main textTriple M overlap syndrome presents unique diagnostic and management challenges in critically ill patients. Clinical features frequently overlap with sepsis, pneumonia, thromboembolism, and cardiopulmonary disease, creating significant diagnostic uncertainty. In addition, traditional diagnostic tools such as myasthenia gravis antibody testing and early cardiac imaging may be normal or nondiagnostic early in the disease course. This review focuses on the recognition and management of Triple M overlap syndrome in the intensive care unit. We highlight key diagnostic pitfalls, including the frequent under-recognition of occult myocarditis and the limitations of electrophysiologic and serologic testing. A structured ICU-oriented approach is proposed, emphasizing early multisystem evaluation with creatine kinase, serial troponins, electrocardiography, and cardiac imaging, alongside concurrent assessment for infection. Management strategies include prompt discontinuation of ICIs, early initiation of corticosteroids, and escalation to intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange for severe neuromuscular involvement. Cardiac complications require close monitoring and early immunosuppression. Targeted therapies such as abatacept are discussed in the context of myocarditis, with current evidence limited primarily to cardiac involvement. This review emphasizes a practical ICU-oriented framework incorporating parallel evaluation for immune-mediated toxicity and infection in critically ill patients. ConclusionTriple M overlap syndrome represents a high-risk and often underrecognized manifestation of ICI toxicity in critically ill patients. Early recognition, parallel evaluation for infection and immune-mediated disease, and coordinated multisystem management are essential to improving outcomes. Background

The widespread adoption of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has fundamentally transformed cancer therapy over the past decade. Agents targeting cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), and, more recently, lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) are now used across a broad range of solid and hematologic malignancies [1–4]. ICIs are increasingly used as first-line or early-line therapy in cancers such as melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), urothelial carcinoma, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). As the use of immunotherapy continues to expand, critically ill patients receiving or recently exposed to ICI therapy are being encountered with increasing frequency in modern intensive care units (ICU) [5–8].

By enhancing antitumor immunity through disruption of immune checkpoint pathways, ICIs promote sustained T-cell activation and tumor destruction but may also lead to immune-related adverse events affecting nearly every organ system [3, 9]. While dermatologic, gastrointestinal, endocrine, and pulmonary toxicities are most commonly observed, neurologic and cardiac complications are comparatively rare but disproportionately severe, with high rates of hospitalization, intensive care admission, and mortality [5, 9–13].

Recognizing the growing burden of immune-related toxicities, major oncology societies including the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO), and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) have published guidelines for early recognition and management, including corticosteroid initiation and immunotherapy interruption [5, 9, 14]. Despite these efforts, a subset of patients continues to experience rapid clinical deterioration requiring escalation to intensive care.

In critically ill patients, immune-related toxicities frequently present with nonspecific features such as respiratory failure, hemodynamic instability, and multiorgan dysfunction, often mimicking common ICU syndromes including sepsis, pneumonia, thromboembolism, and acute cardiopulmonary disease [6–9]. This challenge is further compounded by the increased susceptibility to infection in this population.

Among the spectrum of ICI-associated toxicities, concurrent involvement of the neuromuscular junction, skeletal muscle, and myocardium referred to as the Triple M overlap syndrome (myasthenia gravis–like disease, inflammatory myositis, and myocarditis) represents the most severe and clinically challenging phenotype encountered in critical care [Fig. 1]. This syndrome is associated with high rates of respiratory failure, malignant arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, and mortality, often exceeding 38–60% in reported series despite aggressive immunosuppressive therapy [15–18].

Immune checkpoint inhibitor toxicities encountered in critical care

Prior reviews have summarized ICI–associated toxicities across organ systems and clinical settings, including recent narrative reviews in anesthesiology and intensive care literature [19, 20]. However, these studies primarily focus on individual organ involvement or provide broad overviews without emphasizing high-risk multisystem overlap syndromes encountered in critically ill patients. In contrast, this review focuses specifically on Triple M overlap syndrome as a high-risk ICU phenotype and emphasizes a practical diagnostic framework integrating parallel evaluation for immune-mediated toxicity and infection in critically ill patients.

Literature search strategy

A focused narrative literature review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar to identify studies related to ICI–associated Triple M overlap syndrome. Search terms included combinations of “immune checkpoint inhibitor,” “myocarditis,” “myositis,” “myasthenia gravis,” “Triple M syndrome,” “overlap syndrome,” and “critical care.” Priority was given to systematic reviews, pharmacovigilance analyses, registry-based studies, and guideline statements from major oncology societies, including ASCO, ESMO, and NCCN, as well as studies relevant to ICU-level management and emerging immunomodulatory therapies.

Pathophysiology of triple M overlap syndrome

The pathophysiology of Triple M overlap syndrome is incompletely understood but is believed to result from dysregulated T-cell activation following immune checkpoint inhibition, leading to simultaneous immune-mediated injury involving skeletal muscle, the neuromuscular junction, and myocardium [3, 9, 15]. Shared antigenic targets between cardiac and skeletal muscle may contribute to the frequent coexistence of myocarditis and inflammatory myositis observed in overlap syndromes [Fig. 2].

Pathophysiology of triple M overlap syndrome

Histopathologic studies have demonstrated lymphocytic infiltration predominantly composed of CD8 + T cells and macrophages within affected myocardium and skeletal muscle, supporting a T cell–mediated inflammatory process [21, 22]. Increased expression of PD-L1 on injured myocytes and skeletal muscle fibers may further contribute to uncontrolled immune activation following checkpoint blockade.

Unlike idiopathic myasthenia gravis, ICI-associated neuromuscular disease is frequently seronegative and often occurs concurrently with inflammatory myositis and myocarditis, suggesting a broader systemic inflammatory phenotype rather than isolated neuromuscular junction dysfunction [23–25]. This overlap likely contributes to the rapid progression of respiratory failure, malignant arrhythmias, and cardiogenic shock commonly observed in critically ill patients.

The triple M overlap syndrome

Multisystem immune-related adverse events represent a distinct and high-risk subset of ICI toxicities. Concurrent involvement of multiple organ systems, particularly the neuromuscular junction, skeletal muscle, and myocardium, has been increasingly recognized in critically ill patients [15, 16]. Among these, the Triple M overlap syndrome characterized by myasthenia gravis–like disease, inflammatory myositis, and myocarditis represents the most severe and clinically significant phenotype, associated with rapid clinical deterioration, respiratory failure, malignant arrhythmias, and high mortality [15–18].

Unlike isolated organ-specific toxicities, Triple M overlap syndrome reflects a shared immunopathologic process affecting striated muscle tissues. The coexistence of cardiac, skeletal muscle, and neuromuscular involvement contributes to rapid clinical deterioration and complicates early diagnosis in critically ill patients.

Epidemiology and mortality

Triple M overlap syndrome is a rare but highly morbid manifestation of ICI toxicity, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.1–0.3% among patients receiving ICIs [15, 16, 26]. Despite its low frequency, its clinical impact is disproportionate due to the high rates of respiratory failure, cardiac complications, and death.

The largest pharmacovigilance analysis to date evaluated 1,726 cases of ICI–associated myositis and myasthenia gravis from an international safety database, identifying 103 patients with Triple M overlap syndrome. Patients with Triple M syndrome demonstrated significantly higher mortality compared with myocarditis occurring alongside either myositis or myasthenia gravis alone (43.8% vs. 29.8%), reinforcing the particularly severe nature of this overlap phenotype [15–17, 26]. Cardiac toxicity remains the strongest predictor of adverse outcomes, with fulminant myocarditis associated with malignant arrhythmias, conduction abnormalities, and cardiogenic shock.

Triple M overlap syndrome most commonly occurs in patients treated for melanoma, reflecting both disease prevalence and the frequent use of combination immunotherapy. Combination regimens, particularly PD-1 plus CTLA-4 blockade (e.g., nivolumab plus ipilimumab) and PD-1 plus LAG-3 inhibition (e.g., nivolumab plus relatlimab), are associated with higher rates of severe immune-related adverse events compared with monotherapy [3, 15, 16, 18]. Multisystem toxicity has been reported in approximately 30–40% of patients receiving combination therapy versus 10–20% with monotherapy.

The syndrome typically presents early in the treatment course, most commonly within 2–6 weeks after initiation of ICIs, with a median onset of approximately 2–3 weeks [9, 15, 16]. However, delayed presentations have been reported weeks to months after therapy, and in some cases more than one year after the last dose, underscoring the need to consider this diagnosis even in the absence of recent exposure.

Clinical presentation

Triple M overlap syndrome typically presents with a combination of neuromuscular and cardiac manifestations that evolve over days to weeks. Early symptoms are often nonspecific and may include fatigue, dyspnea, and myalgias, contributing to delays in recognition.

Neuromuscular involvement frequently manifests with ocular and bulbar symptoms, including ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia, and dysarthria, and may rapidly progress to respiratory muscle weakness and respiratory failure [23–25, 27]. Concurrent inflammatory myositis is common and may present with proximal muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels [28–30].

Cardiac involvement ranges from asymptomatic troponin elevation to fulminant myocarditis with ventricular dysfunction, conduction abnormalities, and malignant arrhythmias [10, 21–33]. Notably, myocardial involvement may be clinically silent early in the disease course.

The coexistence of neuromuscular and cardiac involvement results in complex multisystem presentations, which may be difficult to recognize early in critically ill patients.

Case 1

A 77-year-old man with scalp melanoma treated with radiation therapy (last session two months prior) and pembrolizumab (last dose two weeks prior) presented with six days of progressive generalized weakness, dyspnea at rest, gait instability, and proximal lower-extremity myalgias. Laboratory evaluation revealed a markedly elevated creatine kinase level of 18,000 U/L and an elevated troponin level of 574 ng/L. Given recent ICI exposure and concurrent respiratory symptoms with elevated cardiac and muscle biomarkers, ICI–associated myocarditis and myositis were suspected. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) demonstrated focal areas of elevated native T1 and T2 values consistent with acute myocarditis, and high-dose corticosteroids were initiated promptly. Neurologic examination revealed fatigable bilateral ptosis and impaired ocular motility without focal limb weakness. Respiratory function was closely monitored with serial negative inspiratory force and maximal expiratory pressure measurements every six hours. Despite corticosteroid therapy, respiratory parameters continued to decline, prompting initiation of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and transitioning to a gradual oral steroid taper. Further evaluation demonstrated elevated acetylcholine receptor–modulating antibodies (55%), supporting the diagnosis of ICI–associated Triple M overlap syndrome, encompassing myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis. The patient was managed in the neuro ICU with noninvasive ventilatory support and enrolled in the ATRIUM trial evaluating abatacept for ICI–associated myocarditis [27]. Following immunomodulatory therapy, respiratory function improved, and the patient was discharged home on nocturnal bilevel positive airway pressure therapy and methotrexate for long-term immunosuppression.

Case 2

A 38-year-old woman with metastatic mandibular squamous cell carcinoma presented with two days of dyspnea, chest discomfort, and tachycardia. She had recently initiated ICI therapy with pembrolizumab and anti-EGFR agent cetuximab, with the most recent dose administered six days prior. She had no prior history of coronary artery disease, heart failure, or arrhythmia. On presentation, she was tachycardic but initially hemodynamically stable. Computed tomography angiography excluded pulmonary embolism but demonstrated myocardial and septal thickening. High-sensitivity troponin was markedly elevated at 5,898 ng/L, with elevated NT-proBNP at 3837 pg/ml. Emergent coronary angiography revealed normal coronary arteries [Fig. 3]. Transthoracic echocardiography showed mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction with global hypokinesis and mildly reduced right ventricular systolic function. Given recent ICI exposure and severe biomarker elevation, ICI –associated myocarditis was suspected, and high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 gram/day) was initiated. cMRI demonstrated subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement with prolonged native T1 and T2 values, meeting Lake Louise criteria for acute myocarditis with right ventricular involvement [Fig. 4]. The course was complicated by malignant ventricular arrhythmias and cardiogenic shock requiring ICU, antiarrhythmic therapy, escalation of immunosuppression including abatacept, plasma exchange, adjunctive ruxolitinib, and temporary Veno arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) for circulatory support. Cardiac biomarkers and ventricular function steadily improved over days to weeks, and mechanical support was successfully discontinued. At the outpatient follow-up, troponin normalized, immunosuppression was de-escalated, and repeat cMRI demonstrated normalized biventricular systolic function with resolution of inflammatory changes and minimal residual enhancement.

Key clinical insights

These cases illustrate the variable and often overlapping presentations of Triple M overlap syndrome, including early neuromuscular symptoms, rapidly progressive respiratory failure, and evolving cardiac involvement. These cases highlight the importance of early multisystem evaluation in critically ill patients receiving ICIs.

Diagnostic pitfalls in the intensive care unit

Diagnosis of Triple M overlap syndrome is frequently delayed due to nonspecific early symptoms and overlap with common causes of critical illness. Presentations often mimic sepsis, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, acute coronary syndromes, or isolated neuromuscular disorders, leading to single-organ diagnostic anchoring [6–9].

A key pitfall is failure to recognize concurrent multisystem involvement. Neuromuscular weakness may be attributed to isolated myasthenia gravis or critical illness myopathy, while myocardial injury may be overlooked in the absence of chest pain or overt heart failure. Importantly, even modest elevations in troponin may precede malignant arrhythmias or cardiogenic shock [10, 21–33].

Traditional diagnostic tools may be normal in this setting. Autoantibody testing for myasthenia gravis is frequently negative, and electrophysiologic studies may be nondiagnostic early in the disease course [23–25]. Similarly, early cardiac imaging may be normal despite active myocardial inflammation, and reliance on a single modality may delay diagnosis.

Failure to obtain a detailed treatment history, including recent or remote exposure to ICI, remains a common contributor to delayed recognition. Importantly, Triple M overlap syndrome may occur weeks to months after therapy initiation or even after treatment discontinuation.

A critical challenge in the ICU is distinguishing immune-mediated toxicity from infection. Patients receiving ICIs are often immunocompromised due to underlying malignancy, prior therapies, and corticosteroid exposure, increasing susceptibility to opportunistic infections. Initiation of high-dose corticosteroids and immunosuppressive therapies may worsen outcomes if underlying infection is present.

Accordingly, a parallel diagnostic approach is essential, incorporating microbiologic testing, imaging, and early empiric antimicrobial therapy when clinically indicated, while simultaneously evaluating for immune-mediated toxicity. Avoiding diagnostic anchoring and maintaining a high index of suspicion for multisystem involvement are essential for timely recognition.

ICI –associated myasthenia gravis versus idiopathic myasthenia gravis

ICI-associated myasthenia gravis differs fundamentally from idiopathic myasthenia gravis in its clinical presentation, diagnostic profile, and underlying pathophysiology. Unlike classic disease, a significant proportion of patients with ICI-associated myasthenia gravis lack detectable autoantibodies, including acetylcholine receptor (AChR), muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), and low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein 4 (LRP4) antibodies, and are frequently seronegative [23–25, 34–36].

Electrophysiologic studies may also be atypical. Repetitive nerve stimulation and electromyography (EMG) may not demonstrate classic neuromuscular junction transmission failure and may instead show nonspecific or myopathic patterns. These findings limit the reliability of traditional diagnostic criteria in critically ill patients.

Increasing evidence suggests that ICI-associated myasthenia gravis often represents a myositis-dominant inflammatory process involving ocular, bulbar, diaphragmatic, and proximal limb muscles, rather than an isolated disorder of neuromuscular transmission. This paradigm explains the frequent coexistence of myositis and myocarditis and the early onset of respiratory failure observed in many patients.

Recognition of these differences is essential, as reliance on classic diagnostic frameworks may delay diagnosis and appropriate management in patients with suspected Triple M overlap syndrome.

Cardiac involvement in triple m overlap syndrome

Cardiac involvement is a major determinant of morbidity and mortality in Triple M overlap syndrome. ICI-associated myocarditis is characterized by T cell–mediated myocardial inflammation and may present with a wide spectrum of disease severity, ranging from asymptomatic troponin elevation to fulminant myocarditis with cardiogenic shock, malignant arrhythmias, and high-grade conduction abnormalities [10, 21–33, 37].

Electrocardiographic abnormalities are common and may include nonspecific ST-T wave changes, atrioventricular conduction delays, bundle branch blocks, or ventricular arrhythmias. Serial measurement of cardiac biomarkers, particularly troponin, is essential, as even modest elevations may precede rapid clinical deterioration [10, 21, 22].

Transthoracic echocardiography may demonstrate reduced left ventricular ejection fraction or regional wall motion abnormalities; however, findings may be normal early in the disease course. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) may detect subclinical myocardial dysfunction and can provide additional diagnostic value when conventional echocardiographic parameters are preserved [38].

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) is a key noninvasive diagnostic modality and may demonstrate myocardial edema and fibrosis. Typical findings include increased native T1 and T2 relaxation times, reflecting myocardial inflammation and edema, as well as late gadolinium enhancement in a nonischemic distribution. However, early CMR may be normal, and a negative study does not exclude myocarditis.

Coronary angiography should be considered when acute coronary syndrome cannot be reasonably excluded, particularly in patients presenting with chest pain, dynamic electrocardiographic changes, marked troponin elevation or hemodynamic instability. In ICI-associated myocarditis, coronary angiography most often demonstrates normal or nonobstructive coronary arteries and serves primarily to exclude ischemic causes rather than confirm the diagnosis [21, 22].

Endomyocardial biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard in selected cases and may be particularly useful in patients with diagnostic uncertainty or refractory shock. Histopathologic findings typically demonstrate lymphocytic infiltration of the myocardium with associated myocyte injury. However, due to its invasive nature and potential for sampling error, biopsy should not delay initiation of immunosuppressive therapy in critically ill patients [21, 33].

Diagnostic approach to suspected triple M overlap syndrome in the intensive care unit

Diagnosis of the Triple M overlap syndrome relies on early multisystem evaluation initiated at the time of clinical suspicion. Careful history taking is essential to identify current or prior ICI exposure, as presentations most commonly occur within 2–6 weeks of therapy initiation but may be delayed for up to 68 weeks after the last dose.

Initial assessment should prioritize recognition of concurrent neuromuscular and cardiac involvement. Ocular or bulbar symptoms, proximal muscle weakness, dyspnea disproportionate to radiographic findings, new arrhythmias, syncope, or unexplained hypotension should prompt immediate evaluation for overlap disease. Rapid clinical deterioration is common, and progression to respiratory failure or cardiogenic shock may occur despite early corticosteroid exposure.

All patients with suspected ICI–associated neuromuscular toxicity should undergo early screening for myositis and myocarditis, including creatine kinase, serial troponins, EKG, echocardiography, cMRI, and basic laboratory assessment when feasible [Fig. 5]. Elevation of creatine kinase may precede overt limb weakness and supports an inflammatory myopathy phenotype, while troponin elevation may represent the earliest marker of myocardial involvement, even in the absence of chest pain or overt heart failure. Normal initial biomarkers do not exclude disease, and repeat testing is warranted when clinical suspicion remains high.

Diagnostic pathway for triple M overlap syndrome in the ICU

Neuromuscular evaluation should focus on bulbar function, airway protection, and respiratory muscle strength, with early measurement and trending of negative inspiratory force and forced vital capacity when feasible. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies may demonstrate myopathic or mixed findings but are frequently atypical and should not delay treatment initiation. MG antibody testing may assist with phenotyping but is often negative and should not be used to exclude ICI–associated disease.

Given the high lethality of ICI–associated myocarditis, cardiac evaluation should be systematic and aggressive. Continuous telemetry is recommended to detect atrioventricular blocks, ventricular arrhythmias, and new conduction abnormalities. Transthoracic echocardiography should be performed early to assess left and right ventricular systolic function, regional wall motion abnormalities, and pericardial effusion. Importantly, preserved ejection fraction does not exclude myocarditis.

Coronary angiography may be necessary when acute coronary syndrome cannot be excluded. In ICI–associated myocarditis, angiography most often demonstrates normal or nonobstructive coronary arteries and primarily serves to exclude ischemia rather than establish the diagnosis.

cMRI is the preferred noninvasive diagnostic modality in hemodynamically stable patients, and can provide supportive diagnostic information. Typical findings include myocardial edema on T2-weighted imaging, increased native T1 values, and late gadolinium enhancement in a nonischemic distribution consistent with inflammatory myocardial injury [Fig. 4]. Importantly, cMRI may be normal early in the disease course and should not be used to exclude myocarditis when clinical suspicion is high.

Endomyocardial biopsy remains the histopathologic gold standard for myocarditis and plays an important complementary role, particularly in cases with diagnostic uncertainty, atypical imaging findings, refractory cardiogenic shock, or when alternative diagnoses such as infiltrative or infectious myocarditis are being considered [39]. Although the sensitivity of endomyocardial biopsy may be lower than cMRI due to the patchy nature of ICI–associated myocardial inflammation, biopsy can provide definitive evidence of immune-mediated myocardial injury and exclude competing etiologies. Importantly, biopsy should be pursued in experienced centers and should not delay initiation of immunosuppressive therapy in critically ill patients with high clinical suspicion [39].

Adjunctive echocardiographic assessment with global longitudinal strain may detect early myocardial dysfunction even when left ventricular ejection fraction is preserved and has been associated with adverse outcomes in ICI–associated myocarditis [38]. Recently proposed risk stratification tools incorporating troponin burden, cardiac and muscular symptoms, electrocardiographic abnormalities, and ventricular dysfunction may further aid early prognostication and escalation of care in the ICU setting [40] [Fig. 6].

Risk stratification of immune checkpoint inhibitor – associated myocarditis: prognostic tool

Because respiratory failure is frequently multifactorial, parallel evaluation for immune-mediated pneumonitis, infection, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, neuromuscular respiratory failure, and thromboembolic disease is essential. Patients receiving ICIs are often immunocompromised due to underlying malignancy, prior chemotherapy, or corticosteroid exposure, placing them at increased risk for severe infections. Because high-dose corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive therapies used to treat immune-related toxicities may worsen untreated infection, evaluation for infectious etiologies should occur in parallel with evaluation for immune-mediated toxicity [Fig. 5].

Overall, the diagnosis of Triple M overlap syndrome is clinical, supported by laboratory and imaging findings. For intensivists, the key diagnostic principles are early recognition, routine screening for cardiac and skeletal muscle involvement, avoidance of single-organ anchoring, and early multidisciplinary collaboration between intensivists, oncologists, neurologists, and cardiologists. Prompt diagnosis enables timely escalation of immunomodulatory therapy and may reduce the high morbidity and mortality associated with this syndrome.

Management of triple M overlap syndrome

Triple M overlap syndrome constitutes a medical emergency requiring prompt recognition, early immunosuppression, and intensive care monitoring because delayed treatment may result in rapid progression to respiratory failure, malignant arrhythmias, or cardiogenic shock. ICI therapy should be discontinued immediately, and critically ill patients should undergo continuous cardiac monitoring, frequent neurologic and respiratory assessment, and early multidisciplinary evaluation involving intensivists, oncologists, neurologists, and cardiologists. Because immunosuppressive therapy may worsen untreated infection, evaluation for infectious etiologies should occur concurrently with initiation of immunosuppressive treatment in critically ill patients [Fig. 7].

ICU management approach to triple M overlap syndrome

Initial management and corticosteroid therapy

High-dose systemic corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of initial treatment for severe immune-related toxicities and are recommended by major oncology guidelines including ASCO, ESMO, and NCCN. In patients with life-threatening neurologic, neuromuscular, cardiac, pulmonary, or gastrointestinal involvement, intravenous methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day should be initiated promptly. In patients with suspected or confirmed myocarditis, rapidly progressive neuromuscular weakness, or multisystem involvement, pulse-dose methylprednisolone 1,000 mg daily for 3–5 days is appropriate, followed by transition to high-dose oral prednisone (1–2 mg/kg/day) once clinical stabilization is achieved [5, 9, 14].

Early corticosteroid initiation is associated with improved outcomes, particularly in ICI–associated myocarditis. However, corticosteroid monotherapy is frequently insufficient in severe neurologic and cardiac toxicities. Transient clinical worsening after steroid initiation may occur and should not be misinterpreted as treatment failure. Steroid tapering should be slow and prolonged, typically over at least 4–6 weeks, and often longer in patients with cardiac or neuromuscular involvement, as rapid tapering is associated with relapse [5, 9, 14].

Escalation beyond corticosteroids

Early escalation beyond corticosteroids should be considered in patients with Triple M overlap syndrome who develop fulminant myocarditis, malignant arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, rapidly progressive bulbar dysfunction, diaphragmatic weakness, or inadequate clinical improvement despite initial corticosteroid therapy [5, 9, 14, 41]. Current NCCN, ASCO, and ESMO recommendations support early introduction of additional immunomodulatory therapies in severe or steroid-refractory immune-related toxicities, particularly in the setting of concurrent myocarditis and neuromuscular involvement [5, 9, 14].

For patients with significant bulbar symptoms or impending respiratory failure, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or plasma exchange (PLEX) should be initiated early and should not be delayed until corticosteroid failure is established. IVIG is commonly administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg divided over 2–5 days and may be favored in hemodynamically unstable patients or in those with concern for concurrent infection. PLEX may be preferred in fulminant neuromuscular presentations because of its rapid removal of circulating antibodies and inflammatory mediators [14, 23, 41].

Abatacept, a CTLA-4 agonist that counteracts T-cell activation induced by immune checkpoint inhibition, has emerged as a promising targeted therapy for severe or steroid-refractory ICI-associated myocarditis and overlap syndromes [33, 41, 42]. Increasing observational data suggest potential benefits in patients with fulminant cardiac involvement, particularly when combined with aggressive supportive care and additional immunomodulatory therapies.

Ruxolitinib, a Janus kinase (JAK) 1/2 inhibitor, suppresses downstream inflammatory cytokine signaling involved in T-cell activation and macrophage-mediated inflammation. Although not routinely encountered in general intensive care practice, it has been used adjunctively in severe steroid-refractory myocarditis and cytokine-driven overlap syndromes, particularly in combination with abatacept [33, 42].

Other immunomodulatory agents have been reported in refractory disease. Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, may be beneficial in patients with persistent or antibody-mediated myasthenic features, while tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 receptor antagonist, has been used in selected hyperinflammatory phenotypes and severe inflammatory myositis [14, 22, 41]. Efgartigimod, a neonatal Fc receptor antagonist approved for generalized myasthenia gravis, has also emerged as a potential option for refractory ICI-associated myasthenia gravis–like disease, although evidence remains limited to case reports and early clinical experience [43].

Given the absence of prospective randomized data, selection of second-line immunomodulatory therapy should be individualized according to the dominant clinical phenotype, severity of myocarditis, degree of respiratory compromise, infectious risk, and institutional expertise.

Organ-specific and supportive critical care management

Respiratory failure in Triple M overlap syndrome is frequently multifactorial and may result from neuromuscular weakness, myocarditis-related pulmonary edema, immune-mediated pneumonitis, infection, or thromboembolic disease. While noninvasive ventilation may be appropriate in select patients, early endotracheal intubation should be favored in those with bulbar dysfunction, declining respiratory mechanics, or cardiac instability, as delayed airway protection is associated with worse outcomes.

Given the immunocompromised state of many patients due to underlying malignancy, concomitant chemotherapy, or corticosteroid exposure, broad infectious evaluation and empiric antimicrobial therapy should be pursued early in the presence of shock, fever, or pulmonary infiltrates. Infection and immune-mediated toxicity frequently coexist and should be evaluated in parallel rather than sequentially. Standard venous thromboembolism prophylaxis is recommended, as patients with malignancy and critical illness are at increased thrombotic risk.

Rechallenge with immune checkpoint inhibitors

Current NCCN and ASCO guidelines generally discourage rechallenge following severe ICI-associated myocarditis, neurologic toxicity, or Triple M overlap syndrome due to the high risk of recurrent and potentially fatal immune-related adverse events [9, 15, 16]. Available registry and case-series data suggest substantial risk of recurrence and severe outcomes following re-exposure, particularly in patients with prior myocarditis or multisystem involvement [9].

Given the life-threatening nature of Triple M overlap syndrome and the availability of alternative oncologic strategies in many patients, rechallenge should generally be avoided and considered only in highly selected cases following multidisciplinary discussion involving oncology, cardiology, neurology, and critical care teams. Careful risk-benefit assessment and informed patient counseling are essential when considering re-exposure to immunotherapy.

Reported mortality rates for Triple M overlap syndrome remain high, and recurrent episodes following rechallenge may be more severe than the initial presentation. Additional prospective data are needed to better define long-term oncologic outcomes, recurrence risk, and patient selection strategies in this setting.

Conclusion and future directions

Triple M overlap syndrome represents one of the most severe and life-threatening immune-related adverse events encountered in the ICU as immunotherapy becomes a cornerstone of cancer treatment across multiple malignancies. High mortality, rapid clinical deterioration, and frequent diagnostic delay underscore the need for early recognition and decisive management.

This review emphasizes the importance of a multisystem, ICU-centered approach to ICI–associated critical illness. Early identification of prior immunotherapy exposure, routine screening for cardiac and skeletal muscle involvement, avoidance of single organ anchoring, and prompt initiation of high-dose corticosteroids with early escalation are central to improving outcomes. This article provides a practical framework for intensivists managing Triple M overlap syndrome and other associated multisystem immune-related complications.

Looking forward, several priorities warrant focused investigation. Prospective studies are needed to define risk stratification strategies and predictive biomarkers that identify patients at the highest risk for severe neurologic and cardiac toxicity, particularly those receiving combination ICI regimens. The role of routine surveillance with creatine kinase and troponin measurements in high-risk populations requires further validation.

Importantly, while abatacept has emerged as a promising targeted immunomodulatory therapy, its use to date has been studied primarily in patients with ICI–associated myocarditis, most notably in the context of the ATRIUM and ACHLYS trial [21, 27, 41, 44, 45]. At present, abatacept has not been systematically studied in patients with ICI–associated MG, inflammatory myositis, or other immune-mediated toxicities requiring ICU admission. Whether abatacept has a role beyond myocarditis, including overlap syndromes or isolated neuromuscular disease, remains unknown and warrants prospective investigation [9, 27, 41].

From a critical care perspective, development of ICU-specific guidelines for ICI toxicity is essential, as oncology-focused recommendations may not fully address the complexity of multisystem failure, diagnostic uncertainty, and rapid progression encountered in critically ill patients. Future research should also evaluate whether earlier recognition, standardized ICU algorithms, and targeted immunomodulatory strategies can reduce length of stay, hospital costs, resource utilization, and mortality in this growing patient population. Multicenter registries and harmonized reporting of immune-related adverse events will be critical to advancing understanding of rare but lethal syndromes such as the Triple M overlap phenotype and improving outcomes for patients requiring intensive care.
