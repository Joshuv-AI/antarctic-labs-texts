The Heat equation and independence of the spectrum of the Hodge Laplacian on ℓ p \ell^{p}
Philipp Bartmann, Matthias Keller

Abstract
. We study the heat equation associated to the Hodge Laplacian on simplicial complexes. Using recently developed techniques for magnetic Schrödinger operators, we prove Davies-Gaffney-Grigoryan type estimates for the kernel of the heat semigroup on ℓ 2 , \ell^{2}, which we then use to extend the semigroup to ℓ p \ell^{p} for p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] under suitable curvature and volume growth conditions. Furthermore, we establish p p -independence of the Hodge Laplacian spectrum under the assumption of form bounded curvature and uniform subexponential volume growth. While the main focus of the paper is the Hodge Laplacian on simplicial complexes, the results are indeed proven for general positive magnetic Schrödinger operators on graphs.

Introduction

In Riemannian geometry, the Hodge Laplacian is most famously known for its role in the celebrated Hodge theorem, which identifies its kernel with the de Rham cohomology of compact manifolds. In addition, its spectrum encodes geometric information, and so in the past century it has been extensively studied and defined the field of spectral geometry. In recent years there has been a growing interest in Hodge theory for various discrete spaces
[

,

,

[

]
. One natural way to understand how geometry interacts with the analytic properties of the Hodge Laplacian is through the associated heat equation, by examining how heat evolves and diffuses over time on a given space. The
L^{2}
-theory of the heat equation is classical and well-understood via the spectral theorem both in the continuous
[

,

,

,

,
18
,
50
]
and the discrete setting for graphs
[

,
5
,

,
31
,
52
]
, see also recent work on simplicial complexes and hypergraphs
[
36
,
45
,
47
]
. Especially, the works of Dodziuk bridged the gap between the continuum and the discrete setting, see e.g.
[
21
,
20
]
. For manifolds there is a substantial body of literature on more general

-spaces
[

,
11
,
10
,

,

]
as well, which reveal even more interesting and subtle phenomena such as

-cohomology,

-Hodge decompositions and boundedness of Riesz-transforms in

as well as their connection to the heat semigroup. In particular, the behavior of spectra of semigroup generators in

and their dependence on

,

and forms as well
[

For discrete spaces such as simplicial complexes, the

In this paper, we take a step towards filling this gap by exploring the
\ell^{p}
-theory of the heat semigroup as well as the spectral theory of the
\ell^{p}
-generators and aim to lay the groundwork for further developments. To do so, we first establish a Davies-Gaffney-Grigoryan type estimate for the kernel of the heat semigroup. This generalizes the result for bounded Laplacians in

Besides that, we will explore two more criteria that allow to extend semigroups to

Building on these results, we establish the

but also hold under rather weak assumptions. In particular, we do not need to assume uniform lower curvature bounds but only form bounded curvature nor do we need any apriori heat kernel bounds beyond the Davies-Gaffney-Grigory’an bound, which is proven in this paper and always holds for a given intrinsic metric. Furthermore, in the combinatorial setting, which we discuss below, we do not even need the curvature assumption at all.

To set the stage, we consider the Hodge Laplacian on a combinatorial simplicial complex
\Sigma
with standard weights, which is a natural instance applicable to our more general results. For details on the general setting of weighted simplicial complexes we refer to Section
5
recalling the setting from
[
3
]

So, let
\Sigma
be a combinatorial simplicial complex with standard weights
and consider the Hodge Laplacian acting on a direct sum over all

k

\omega=\oplus_{k}\omega_{k}

\Delta^{H}=\delta\partial+\partial\delta,

where
\delta
and
\partial
are the coboundary and boundary operator respectively.
In
[
3

, it was shown that the quadratic form

Q^{H}(\omega)=\|\delta\omega\|^{2}_{2}+\|\partial\omega\|^{2}_{2}

defined on compactly supported forms is closable and thus gives rise to a positive self-adjoint operator
\Delta^{H}_{2}
on
\ell^{2}
.

We are interested in two geometric concepts: curvature bounds and volume growth bounds. For curvature, we consider the Forman curvature
c^{H},
which arises naturally as a potential from the discrete Bochner-Weitzenböck formula for
\Delta^{H}
,
[

]

for

with

, we have , cf.

\displaystyle c^{H}(\tau)=2(k+1)+(k+2)\#\{\sigma\succ\tau\}-\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\#\{\tau^{\prime}\succ\rho\},

where
\prec
and

refer to the incidence relation on the complex.
Even though

is a positive operator,

may take negative values. We consider the situation where the negative part

is form bounded with respect to

, i.e., where one has
M,C\geq 0
such that for all compactly supported
\varphi\in C_{c}(\Sigma)

\|\sqrt{c_{-}^{H}}\varphi\|_{2}^{2}\leq M{Q}^{H}(\varphi)+C\|\varphi\|^{2}_{2}.

In this case, we say that
\Sigma
has
form bounded curvature
with
bound
M
. This assumption is natural, as it generalizes uniform lower curvature bounds while maintaining control over the analytic properties of
\Delta^{H}_{2}
.

Regarding volume growth, we consider the growth of balls with respect to the combinatorial graph metric on the
1

\Sigma
given by

d(v,w)=\min\{n\mid\mbox{there is a path }{v=v_{0}\sim\ldots\sim v_{n}=w}\}.

Here

v

We assume that the

One may wonder why the combinatorial distance is sufficient for our purposes here. The reason is that exponential volume bounds automatically imply boundedness in the combinatorial setting, see Proposition

Our goal is to study the heat equation for

For

We say one can

Moreover, we say that (

We prove the following special case of our main results.

Theorem 1.1

(Heat equation)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a combinatorial simplicial complex.

(a)

Assume
\Sigma
has form bounded curvature with bound
M\geq 0
. Then, (
HE
) is solved by a strongly continuous semigroup on
\ell^{p}
for all

in the interval

(b)

) is solved by a strongly continuous semigroup on

.

Indeed, the heat equation (

at the boundary of the interval

We denote the generator of this semigroup by

and the spectrum by

Using these results about the heat equation, we establish

Theorem 1.2

(

Let

\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{p})=\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{2}).

In

More precisely, we will prove the results above in the more general setting of positive magnetic Schrödinger operators whose negative part of the potential is form bounded. 2.1.
Magnetic Schrödinger Operators on Graphs

Let
X
be a discrete set and denote by
C(X)
the space of all complex valued functions on
X.
Respectively, denote by
C_{c}(X)
the space of all finitely supported functions on
X.
For a discrete set
A
and an absolutely summable or positive function
f\in C(A)
, we fix the notation

\sum_{A}f=\sum_{x\in A}f(x).

A function
m:X\rightarrow(0,\infty)
extends to a measure on
X
of full support by setting
m(A)=\sum_{A}m
for
A\subseteq X.
We call
(X,m)
a
discrete measure space
. The
\ell^{p}
-spaces,
p\in[1,\infty)
associated to
(X,m)
are defined as usual by

\displaystyle\ell^{p}=\ell^{p}(X,m)=\Big\{f\in C(X)\mid\|\omega\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{X}m|f|^{p}<\infty\Big\}

and

\displaystyle\ell^{\infty}=\ell^{\infty}(X)=\Big\{f\in C(X)\mid\|\omega\|_{\infty}=\sup_{X}|f|<\infty\Big\}.

The space
\ell^{2}
is a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{X}mf\overline{g}.

A graph
b
over
(X,m)
, is a symmetric function
b:X\times X\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with zero diagonal satisfying

\sum_{y\in X}b(x,y)<\infty

for all
x\in X
. Moreover, we let
o:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid|z|=1\}
be a function satisfying
o(x,y)=\overline{o(y,x)}
for all
x,y\in X.
We call
o
a
magnetic potential
on
X.
Finally, let
c:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an
(electric) potential
. We call the triple
(b,o,c)
over
(X,m)
a
magnetic Schrödinger graph
. We say that the graph is
locally finite
if, for all
x\in X
, we have
\#\{y\in X\mid b(x,y)>0\}<\infty.

The
magnetic Schrödinger operator

\mathcal{H}
associated to the triple
(b,o,c)
over
(X,m)
is defined on

\displaystyle F=\{f\in C(X)\mid\sum_{y\in X}b(x,y)|f(y)|<\infty~\text{for all}~x\in X\}

and is acting as

\mathcal{H}f(x)=\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in X}b(x,y)\left(f(x)-o(x,y)f(y)\right)+\frac{1}{m(x)}c(x)f(x).

To introduce a self-adjoint restriction on
\ell^{2}
, we define an associated sesquilinear form on
C_{c}(X)
. For a function
f\in C(X)
, we denote

\displaystyle\nabla_{o}f(x,y)=f(x)-o(x,y)f(y)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla f(x,y)=f(x)-f(y).

Green’s formula
[
32
, Lemma 2.1.]
then states that for
\varphi\in C_{c}(X)
and
f\in F
one has

\displaystyle\sum_{X}m\mathcal{H}f\overline{\varphi}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{X\times X}b\nabla_{o}f\overline{\nabla_{o}\varphi}+\sum_{X}cf\overline{\varphi}=\sum_{X}mf\overline{\mathcal{H}\varphi}.

For
\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}(X)
, we let

\displaystyle Q(\varphi,\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{X\times X}b\nabla_{o}\varphi\overline{\nabla_{o}\psi}+\sum_{X}c\varphi\overline{\psi}.

We assume that
Q
is
positive
, i.e., for all
\varphi\in C_{c}(X)

\displaystyle Q(\varphi):=Q(\varphi,\varphi)\geq 0.

Whenever
Q
is closable on
\ell^{2},
we denote its closure again by
Q
with domain
D(Q).
The associated positive self-adjoint operator on
\ell^{2}
is denoted by
H=H_{2}.

Assumption.
We assume
(b,o,c)
is a magnetic Schrödinger graph over
(X,m)
such that
Q
is positive and closable on
\ell^{2}
.

For criteria for closability of
Q
, we refer to
[
32
]
. One particular instance relevant to this paper are forms with form bounded potential. We say that the potential
c
is
form bounded
with respect to
Q

with bounds

M,C\geq 0
if for all
\varphi\in C_{c}(X)

\|\sqrt{c_{-}/m}\varphi\|_{2}^{2}\leq MQ(\varphi)+C\|\varphi\|^{2}_{2},

where
c_{-}=-(c\wedge 0)
is the negative part of
c.
In
[
32
, Proposition 2.8.]
it is shown that if
c
is form bounded, then
Q
is closable in
\ell^{2}
. Note that a form bounded potential corresponds to form bounded curvature for the Hodge Laplacian as indicated in the introduction. However, positivity and closability of the form of the Hodge Laplacian are always satisfied, even without any assumption on the curvature, as discussed in
[
3
]
.

2.2.
The Magnetic Schrödinger Semigroup on
\ell^{2}

The spectral theorem gives rise to a strongly continuous contraction semigroup
S_{2}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2})
,
S_{2}(t)f=\mathrm{e}^{-tH}f,
where
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2})
are the bounded operators on
\ell^{2}
, which is characterized for
t\geq 0
by its integral kernel

p_{t}:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad p_{t}(x,y)=\frac{\langle\mathrm{e}^{-tH}1_{x},1_{y}\rangle}{m(x)m(y)}.

Let
K\subseteq X
be finite and
\ell^{2}(K,m)=C(K).

We have the canonical inclusion
\iota_{K}:\ell^{2}(K,m)\rightarrow\ell^{2},
which is obtained by extending functions from
K
to
X
by zero. Its adjoint
\iota^{*}_{K}
is given by the restriction of functions in
\ell^{2}
to
K.
We define the operator
H_{K}
acting on
\ell^{2}(K,m)
as

H_{K}f(x)=\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in K}b(x,y)\left(f(x)-o(x,y)f(y)\right)+\frac{1}{m(x)}c_{K}(x)f(x),

where
c_{K}(x)=c(x)+\sum_{y\in X\backslash K}b(x,y).

By construction,
H_{K}
is the self-adjoint positive operator associated to the quadratic form
Q_{K}=Q\mid_{\ell^{2}(K,m)}
and gives rise to the semigroup
\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{K}}.

Now let
K_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be an exhaustion of
X
by finite subsets, i.e., finite subsets such that
K_{n}\subseteq K_{n+1}
and
\bigcup_{n}K_{n}=X.
Then we have the following strong convergence of semigroups, shown in
[
32
, Proposition 2.20]
.

Proposition 2.1

(Semigoup convergence)
.

Assume that
Q
is positive and closable on
\ell^{2}.

Let
t\geq 0
and
K_{n}
be an exhaustion. Then,

\iota_{K_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-t{H}_{K_{n}}}\iota_{K_{n}}^{*}\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{-tH}~\text{strongly in}~\ell^{2}~\text{as}~n\rightarrow\infty.

2.3.
Davies-Gaffney-Grigoryan Lemma

In this subsection, we prove a Davies-Gaffney-Grigoryan type lemma for magnetic Schrödinger operators. This extends the result of
[
5
,
6
]
.

Let us consider an
intrinsic metric

d
with respect to the graph
b
over
(X,m),
that is, a pseudo metric
d
such that for all
x\in X

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in X}b(x,y)d(x,y)^{2}\leq 1.

We define the
jump size

s
of
d
as

s=\sup_{x\sim y}d(x,y),

where
x\sim y
means that the supremum runs over all pairs
x,y\in X
with
b(x,y)>0.

Assumption.
We fix an intrinsic metric
d
with finite jump size
s>0.

Let
\sigma(H_{2})
denote the
\ell^{2}
-spectrum of
H=H_{2}.
Let

\lambda_{2}=\inf\sigma(H_{2}).

Theorem 2.2

(Davies-Gaffney-Grigoryan)
.

For all
f,g\in\ell^{2}
,
A=\mathrm{supp}\ f
,
B=\mathrm{supp}\ g
one has

|\langle\mathrm{e}^{-tH}f,g\rangle|\leq\mathrm{exp}\left(-\lambda_{2}t-\frac{t}{s^{2}}\zeta\left(\frac{sd(A,B)}{t}\right)\right)\|f\|_{2}\|g\|_{2},

where

\zeta(r)=\left(r\mathrm{arcsinh}\left({r}\right)-\sqrt{1+r^{2}}+1\right).

By choosing characteristic functions
1_{x},1_{y},
of vertices
x,y\in X
, Theorem
2.2
yields the estimate

|p_{t}(x,y)|\leq\frac{\mathrm{exp}(-\lambda_{2}t-(t/s^{2})\zeta(sd(x,y)/t))}{\sqrt{m(x)m(y)}}.

The proof consists of two main steps. First, we prove a decay estimate of an energy functional for finite subsets
K\subseteq X,
which extends the result in
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
to magnetic Schrödinger operators. Secondly, we use the semigroup convergence from Proposition
2.1
to extend the result to the full space
X
.

The next lemma was shown for graphs in
[
5
,
6
]
, for simplicial complexes with bounded Laplacians in
[
36
]
and ultimately goes back to results on manifolds
[
16
,
28
]
. We extend it to magnetic Schrödinger operators on finite subsets. We say that a function
\omega:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
\kappa>0
with respect to
d
if

|\omega(x)-\omega(y)|\leq\kappa d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
. Furthermore, for
U\subseteq X
, a function
u:[0,\infty)\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a
solution to the parabolic equation on
U
if
t\mapsto u_{t}(x)
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
, differentiable on
(0,\infty)
and satisfies

-\mathcal{H}u_{t}(x)=u_{t}^{\prime}(x)

for all
t>0
and
x\in U
, where
u_{t}
is extended by
0
to
X\setminus U
and
u_{t}^{\prime}
denotes the time derivative of
u_{t}.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
K\subseteq X
be a finite subset. Let
\omega
be a bounded real valued Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant
\kappa>0
and let
u
be a solution to the parabolic equation on
K
. Then,

t\mapsto\exp{\left(2\lambda_{2}(K)t-\frac{2t}{s^{2}}(\cosh{\frac{\kappa s}{2}}-1)\right)}E_{u}(t)

is nonincreasing in
t\geq 0,
where
\lambda_{2}(K)
is the smallest eigenvalue of
H_{K}
and

E_{u}(t)=\sum_{K}m|u_{t}|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\omega}.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
closely. Write
(f\otimes g)(x,y)=f(x)g(y)
,
x,y\in X
.
We take the time derivative of
t\mapsto E_{u}(t)
and use the parabolic equation, as well as Green’s formula to obtain

\frac{d}{dt}E_{u}(t)=2\sum_{K}m\mathrm{Re}(\overline{u_{t}}u_{t}^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\omega}=-2\sum_{K}m\mathrm{Re}(\overline{u_{t}}H_{K}u_{t})\mathrm{e}^{\omega}=-2\mathrm{Re}\,Q(u_{t},\mathrm{e}^{\omega}u_{t})\\
=-2Q(u_{t}\mathrm{e}^{{\omega}/{2}})-2\sum_{K\times K}b\mathrm{Re}\left[\overline{o}\cdot(\mathrm{e}^{\omega/\penalty 2}u_{t}\otimes\mathrm{e}^{\omega/\penalty 2}\overline{u}_{t})\right]\left(1-\cosh\left(\nabla\mathrm{e}^{\omega/2}\right)\right)\\
\leq-2\lambda_{2}(K)E_{u}(t)+\frac{2}{s^{2}}(\cosh{\frac{\kappa s}{2}}-1)E_{u}(t),

where in the last step we used the Lipschitz property of
\omega
and that
d
is an intrinsic metric with jump size
s
(cf.
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
for details of the specific estimates). This implies the lemma.
∎

With this preparation, we can now prove Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

We will only prove that the function

t\mapsto\exp{\left(2\lambda_{2}t-\frac{2t}{s^{2}}(\cosh{\frac{\kappa s}{2}-1})\right)}E(t),\qquad t\geq 0,

is non-increasing, where

E(t)=\|\mathrm{e}^{\omega/\penalty 2}S_{2}(t)u\|^{2}_{2}

for any initial condition
u\in\ell^{2}
and
\omega
any real Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant
\kappa>0.
Once we have achieved that, the rest of the proof follows exactly as in
[
36
, Theorem 1.2]
or
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
.

First, take an exhaustion
K_{n}\subseteq X
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of finite sets. Because

\lambda_{2}=\inf_{\varphi\in C_{c}(X),\|\varphi\|=1}{Q(\varphi)}

it easily follows that
\lambda_{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{2,n}
with
\lambda_{2,n}=\lambda_{2}(K_{n})
being the smallest eigenvalue of
H_{K_{n}}
. Furthermore, by Proposition
2.1
we have that

u_{n,t}=\iota_{K_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{K_{n}}}\iota_{K_{n}}^{*}u\rightarrow S_{2}(t)u\quad\text{in}~\ell^{2}.

Since
\mathrm{e}^{\omega}
is bounded, we conclude that

\exp{\left(2\lambda_{2}t-\frac{2t}{s^{2}}(\cosh{\frac{\kappa s}{2}}-1)\right)}E(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\exp{\left(2\lambda_{2,n}t-\frac{2t}{s^{2}}(\cosh{\frac{\kappa s}{2}}-1)\right)}E_{u_{n}}(t).

The desired monotonicity now follows from Lemma
2.3
.

∎

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.2
and provides explicit heat kernel estimates. It is inspired by
[
4
, Lemma 3.7]
but with a sharper constant
C_{\beta}
. Specifically, the constant
C_{\beta}
becomes arbitrarily small for small enough
\beta>0.
This will be useful in the upcoming section.

Corollary 2.4
.

For all
\beta>0
there exists a constant
C_{\beta}=s^{-2}(\cosh(\beta s)-1)
such that for all
t\geq 0
,
x,y\in X
,

|p_{t}(x,y)|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m(x)m(y)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta d(x,y)+C_{\beta}t-\lambda_{2}t}.

Proof.

Consider the function

f(r)=s^{-2}\left(rs\mathrm{arcsinh}(sr)-\sqrt{1+r^{2}s^{2}}+1\right)-\beta r

for
r\geq 0.
Direct calculation shows that
f^{\prime}(r)=s^{-1}\mathrm{arcsinh}(rs)-\beta
and
f^{\prime\prime}(r)>0.
Consequently,
f
achieves its minimum in
r_{0}=\sinh{(s\beta)}/\penalty s.
Thus,

f(r)\geq f(r_{0})=\frac{1-\cosh(\beta s)}{s^{2}}.

The statement follows from Theorem
2.2
after observing, that
(t/s^{2})\zeta(sd(x,y)/t)=t(f(d(x,y)/\penalty t)+\beta d(x,y)/\penalty t).

∎

3.
Magnetic Schrödinger Semigroups on
\ell^{p}

In this section we want to investigate conditions under which the semigroup
S_{2}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{2}}
consistently extends to strongly continuous semigroups
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}
for
p\in[1,\infty].
Here, consistently means that
S_{2}(t)=S_{p}(t)
on
\ell^{2}\cap\ell^{p}
for all
t\geq 0.
For graph Laplacians, which arise from Dirichlet forms, this is a well-known consequence of the Markov property
[
17
,
40
]
. However, for magnetic Schrödinger operators, this is in general not clear.

Let us recall some general facts about semigroups obtained in this way. Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
-H_{2}
and
-H_{p}
be the generators of
S_{2}
and
S_{p}
respectively. If
p>1,
then we also obtain the strongly continuous semigroup
S_{q}
on
\ell^{q}
for
q
being the Hölder dual of
p.
This follows from the symmetry of
S_{2},
duality of
\ell^{p}
and
\ell^{q}
and density of
\ell^{p}\cap\ell^{q}
in
\ell^{q}.
Moreover, one has for all
t\geq 0
that
S_{p}(t)=(S_{q}(t))^{*}
and
H_{p}=(H_{q})^{*}
where
\ast
indicates the Banach space adjoint of an operator. This also implies the relation of the spectra
\sigma(H_{p})=\sigma(H_{q})^{*},
where
\ast
indicates complex conjugation, i.e., the spectra get flipped along the real axis. Whenever
S_{1}
exists, we direcly define
S_{\infty}(t)=(S_{1}(t))^{*}
on
\ell^{\infty}
and
H_{\infty}=(H_{1})^{*}.
Here, we should note that
S_{\infty}
will in general not be strongly continuous but only weak-
\ast
-continuous due to the fact that
\ell^{1}\cap\ell^{\infty}
is not dense in
\ell^{\infty}.
The semigroups turn out to be mutually consistent, i.e., for all
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
one has
S_{p}=S_{q}
on
\ell^{p}\cap\ell^{q}.

We present two criteria that ensure that
S_{2}
consistently extends to
\ell^{p}.
The first criterion, which can be viewed as a curvature condition in case of the Hodge Laplacian, is to assume a form bound on the negative part of the potential
c
. The second one is an assumption on the volume growth of distance balls with respect to an intrinsic metric
d
of finite jump size.

Again, throughout this section, we assume that
(b,o,c)
is a magnetic Schrödinger graph over
(X,m)
such that
Q
is closable in
\ell^{2}
and positive.

3.1.
The Case of Form Bounded Potentials

The following result is the main theorem of this subsection. Recall that
\lambda_{2}
denotes the bottom of the
\ell^{2}
-spectrum of
H_{2}
and
c
is said to be form bounded with bounds
M>0
and
C\geq 0
if
\|\sqrt{c_{-}/m}\varphi\|_{2}^{2}\leq MQ(\varphi)+C\|\varphi\|^{2}_{2}
for all
\varphi\in C_{c}(X)
. We denote the operator norm of an operator
T:\ell^{p}\rightarrow\ell^{q}
by
\|T\|_{p,q}
for
p,q\in[1,\infty].

Theorem 3.1

(Form bounded potentials)
.

Assume
c
is form bounded with bounds
M>0
and
C\geq 0
.
For all
p\in I=[2\frac{M+1}{M}-2\frac{\sqrt{M+1}}{M},2\frac{M+1}{M}+2\frac{\sqrt{M+1}}{M}]
and all
t\geq 0
, one has that
S_{2}(t)
is bounded as an operator on
\ell^{p}
and

\|S_{2}(t)\|_{p,p}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-D_{p}t},

where
D_{p}=\left(C_{p}-M(1-C_{p})\right)\lambda_{2}-C(1-C_{p}),

C_{p}=4\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}
. In particular,
S_{2}
extends consistently to strongly continuous semigroups
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}.

Theorem
3.1
is inspired by
[
43
, Proposition 3.3]
, which itself relies on ideas from
[
41
]
.
Observe that
C_{p}=4\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}\in[0,1]
and, for
p\in I
, we have
C_{p}-M(1-C_{p})\geq 0
.
For the proof, let us denote the positive part of the quadratic form
Q
on
C_{c}(X)
by

q={Q}+\frac{c_{-}}{m}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f\in C_{c}(X)
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then,

\mathrm{Re}\,q(f,f|f|^{p-2})\geq 4\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}q(f|f|^{(p-2)/\penalty 2}).

Proof.

We deduce the statement from the following pointwise estimate.
Fix
x,y\in X
and let
|f(x)|\geq|f(y)|.
Assume that
f(x)\neq 0.
Let
C_{p}=4\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}.
Note that
1-C_{p}=(\frac{p-2}{p})^{2}.
Recall that
\nabla_{o}f=f(x)-o(x,y)f(y)
and
\nabla f=f(x)-f(y)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(\nabla_{o}f

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla_{o}(f|f|^{p-2})})-C_{p}\left|\nabla_{o}(f|f|^{\frac{p-2}{2}})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(1-C_{p})\left(|f(x)|^{p}+|f(y)|^{p}\right)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left(o(x,y)f(y)\overline{f(x)}\right)\left(|f(x)|^{p-2}+|f(y)|^{p-2}-2C_{p}|f(x)f(y)|^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,(1-C_{p})\left(|f(x)|^{p}+|f(y)|^{p}\right)

\displaystyle-\left|f(x)f(y)\right|\left((\nabla|f|^{\frac{p-2}{2}})^{2}+2(1-C_{p})|f(x)f(y)|^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(1-C_{p})\left(\nabla|f|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{2}-|f(x)||f(y)|\left(\nabla|f|^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,|f(x)|^{p}\left(\left(\frac{p-2}{p}\right)^{2}\left(1-\left(\frac{|f(y)|}{|f(x)|}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{|f(y)|}{|f(x)|}\left(1-\left(\frac{|f(y)|}{|f(x)|}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,0,

where we conclude the positivity in the last step from the inequality

\left(\frac{p-2}{p}\right)^{2}(1-t^{p/\penalty 2})^{2}\geq t(1-t^{\frac{p-2}{2}})^{2}

for all
t\in[0,1]
,
p\in[1,\infty),
which follows from
[
41
, Lemma 2.1, inequality (2.4)]
.

The case
f(x)=0
is trivial. Multiplying with
b(x,y)
, adding the positive part of the potential on both sides and summing over
x,y
yields the statement.
∎

Remark
.

One may prove in a very similar fashion to Lemma
3.2
the estimate

\kappa(p)q(f|f|^{(p-2)/\penalty 2})\geq\mathrm{Re}\,q(f,f|f|^{p-2})

for all
f\in C_{c}(X)
and
p\in[1,\infty),
where

\kappa(p)=\sup_{t\in(0,1)}\frac{(1+t)(1+t^{p-1})}{(1+t^{p/\penalty 2})^{2}}.

The proof uses the estimate

t(1-t^{\frac{p-2}{2}})^{2}\leq(\kappa(p)-1)(1+t^{p/\penalty 2})^{2}

for all
t\in[0,1],
which one deduces from
[
41
, Lemma 2.1, inequality (2.5)]
.

We also note the following extension lemma for strongly continuous semigroups on
\ell^{2}
which is of independent interest.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(T({t}))
be a strongly continuous semigroup on
\ell^{2}
such that

\|T({t})f\|_{p}\leq e^{-At}\|f\|_{p}

for all
f\in C_{c}(X)
and some
A\in\mathbb{R}
and some
1\leq p<\infty
. Then
(T({t}))
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup on
\ell^{p}
with the same bound.

Proof.

By density of
C_{c}(X)
in
\ell^{p}
we can extend
T(t)
for all
t\geq 0
to a bounded operator
T_{p}(t)
on
\ell^{p}.
The family of operators
(T_{p}(t))
then satisfies the semigroup property
T_{p}(s+t)=T_{p}(s)T_{p}(t)
trivially, as it holds on the dense subspace
C_{c}(X).

For the strong continuity, it is sufficient to show that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\|T_{p}(t)f-f\|_{p}=0
for all
f\in C_{c}(X)
since
C_{c}(X)
is dense in
\ell^{p}
and we already know that
T_{p}
is exponentially bounded. Let
f\in C_{c}(X)
and
K=\mathrm{supp}\,f.
As
T_{p}=T
on
C_{c}(X)
and
T
is strongly continuous, we conclude from finiteness of
K
and pointwise convergence that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\sum_{K}m|T_{p}(t)f-f|^{p}=\sum_{K}m\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}|T(t)f-f|^{p}=0.

By the same reasoning, we also have that

\sum_{K}m|T_{p}(t)f|^{p}\to\sum_{K}m|f|^{p}
as
{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}
. So, for the remaining part of the sum, we use the triangle inequality and the bound on
T_{p}(t)
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{X\setminus K}m|T_{p}(t)f-f|^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\setminus K}m|T(t)f|^{p}=\|T(t)f\|_{p}^{p}-\|f\|_{p}^{p}-\!\sum_{K}m(|T_{p}(t)f|^{p}-|f|^{p})

\displaystyle\leq(\mathrm{e}^{-At}-1)\|f\|_{p}^{p}-\sum_{K}m(|T_{p}(t)f|^{p}-|f|^{p}).

By the argument above, the right hand side tends to zero for
t\to 0.

∎

We are now in the position to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
f\in C_{c}(X)
and
\mathrm{supp}\,f\subseteq K
for some finite set
K\subseteq X
and let
f_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{K}}f,

t\geq 0
, with
H_{K}
defined above Proposition
2.1
. Note that
f_{t}^{\prime}=-H_{K}f_{t}.

We identify
f_{t}\in\ell^{p}(K,m)
as elements in
\ell^{p}
via extension by zero to
X.
Then, for all
p\in I=[2\frac{M+1}{M}-2\frac{\sqrt{M+1}}{M},2\frac{M+1}{M}+2\frac{\sqrt{M+1}}{M}],
we have
p\neq 1,\infty
since
M>0
and we obtain

-\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\|f_{t}\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{K}m|f_{t}|^{p-2}\mathrm{Re}(\bar{f}_{t}H_{K}f_{t})=\mathrm{Re}\,{Q}(f_{t},f_{t}|f_{t}|^{p-2})\\
\geq\left[\left(C_{p}-M(1-C_{p})\right)\lambda_{2}-C(1-C_{p})\right]\|f_{t}\|_{p}^{p}=D_{p}\|f_{t}\|_{p}^{p},

where
D_{p}=\left[\left(C_{p}-M(1-C_{p})\right)\lambda_{2}-C(1-C_{p})\right]
,
C_{p}=4\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}
and
\lambda_{2}
is the bottom of the
\ell^{2}
-spectrum of the operator
H_{2}.

Here, the inequality follows from Lemma
3.2
, the form bound of
c_{-}
and the fact that

\left(C_{p}-M(1-C_{p})\right)\geq 0
for all
p\in I.

What we have shown is equivalent to

\frac{d}{dt}\ln({\|f_{t}\|}_{p}^{p})\leq-{p}D_{p}.

Integrating both sides from
0
to
t
and taking exponentials yields

\|f_{t}\|^{p}_{p}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-tpD_{p}}\|f\|^{p}_{p}.

Now let
K_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, be an exhaustion of
X
with finite subsets. We infer from Proposition
2.1
that
\mathrm{e}^{-t{H}_{K_{n}}}f
converges pointwise for all
t\geq 0
to
S_{2}(t)f
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
Thus, by Fatou’s lemma we get

\|S_{2}(t)f\|_{p}^{p}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\mathrm{e}^{-t{H}_{K_{n}}}f\|_{p}^{p}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-tpD_{p}}\|f\|_{p}^{p}.

The fact that
S_{2}
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup on
\ell^{p}
follows from density of
C_{c}(X)
in
\ell^{p}
and Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Theorem
3.1
excludes the case where
p=1.
However, using Kato’s inequality, we can show that the statement still holds under the stronger assumption of a lower bounded potential, which corresponds to a form bound
M=0.

Theorem 3.4

(Bounded potentials)
.

Suppose the potential
c/\penalty m
is bounded from below, i.e., there exists
K\in\mathbb{R}
such that
c/\penalty m\geq-K.
Then, for all
p\in[1,\infty]
and
t\geq 0
,

\|S_{2}(t)\|_{p,p}\leq\mathrm{e}^{Kt}.

In particular,
S_{2}
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup for
1\leq p<\infty
and a weak-
\ast
-continuous semigroup for
p=\infty.

Proof.

Under the given assumptions, we may apply
[
32
, Theorem 5.2 (a)]
to obtain for all
x,y\in X
,
t\geq 0
, the estimate

|p_{t}(x,y)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{Kt}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y),

where
\tilde{p}_{t}
is the kernel of the semigroup on
\ell^{2}
generated by
-\Delta,
the Laplacian associated to the graph
(b,1,0)
over
(X,m)
. However,
-\Delta
generates a Markov semigroup
[
40
, Proposition 2.6]
and so for all
x\in X

\sum_{y\in X}m(y)\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\leq 1.

Thus, for
f\in C_{c}(X)
,

\|S_{2}(t)f\|_{1}\leq\mathrm{e}^{Kt}\sum_{y\in X}m(y)|f(y)|\sum_{x\in X}m(x)\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\leq\mathrm{e}^{Kt}\|f\|_{1}.

By density of
C_{c}(X)
in
\ell^{1}
, we get that
S_{2}
extends consistently to a semigroup
S_{1}
on
\ell^{1}.

The statement follows now for
p=1
from Lemma
3.3
. By duality, we obtain a weak-
\ast
-continuous semigroup
S_{\infty}
on
\ell^{\infty}
with the same bound which is consistent with
S_{2}
on
\ell^{1}\cap\ell^{\infty}
. Interpolation then yields the statement for all
p\in[1,\infty].

∎

If the graph
b
is locally finite, then the statement of Theorem
3.4
becomes, in fact, an equivalence. The argument for this is essentially the one from
[
38
, Theorem 2.1]
. In particular, for locally finite simplicial complexes, this characterizes lower boundedness of the Forman curvature in terms of an exponential bound of the semigroup generated by the Hodge Laplacian.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that
b
is locally finite and that
S_{2}
extends consistently to a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{1}
on
\ell^{1}.
Assume there is
K\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
\omega\in\ell^{1}
and
t\geq 0
, we have

\|S_{1}(t)\omega\|_{1}\leq\mathrm{e}^{tK}\|\omega\|_{1}.

Then
c/\penalty m
is uniformly bounded from below, i.e., for all
x\in\Sigma
, we have

\frac{c(x)}{m(x)}\geq-K.

Proof.

We fix
x\in X
and define the function
\varphi_{x}
as

\varphi_{x}(y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\quad y=x.\\
\overline{o(x,y)},&\text{if}\quad b(x,y)>0.\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Local finiteness implies
\varphi_{x}\in C_{c}(X)\subseteq D(H_{2})
due to
[
32
, Lemma 2.11]
and Green’s formula,
[
32
, Lemma 2.1]
. Moreover, we get by construction that

H_{2}\varphi_{x}(x)=\frac{c(x)}{m(x)}.

Then, by consistency of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
on
C_{c}(X),
we have

S_{2}(t)\varphi_{x}(x)=\langle S_{2}(t)\varphi_{x},1_{x}/\penalty m\rangle=\langle\varphi_{x},S_{1}(t)1_{x}/\penalty m\rangle

and so

\mathrm{Re}(S_{2}(t)\varphi_{x}(x))\leq\mathrm{e}^{tK}\|\varphi_{x}\|_{\infty}\|1_{x}/\penalty m\|_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{tK}

as
\|\varphi_{x}\|_{\infty}\|1_{x}/\penalty m\|_{1}=1
.
Since
\varphi_{x}\in D(H_{2})
, we get

\displaystyle-\frac{c(x)}{m(x)}

\displaystyle=-H_{2}\varphi_{x}(x)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{Re}(S_{2}(t)\varphi_{x}(x))-\varphi_{x}(x)}{t}\leq\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{tK}-1}{t}=K.

This finishes the proof.
∎

3.2.
The Case of Exponential Volume Growth

Let
b
be a graph over
X
and
d
be an intrinsic metric with finite jump size.
We say that
b
has (uniform)
exponential volume growth
with
growth rate

\nu>0
, if there is
C\geq 0
such that for all
x\in X
,
r>0

m(B_{r}(x))\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\nu r}m(x),

where
B_{r}(x)
is the metric ball with radius
r
centered at
x
with respect to
d.
We say that
b
has (uniform)
subexponential volume growth
, if for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for all
x\in X
,
r>0

m(B_{r}(x))<C_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon r}m(x).

The main result of this subsection is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6

(Exponential volume growth)
.

Assume the graph has exponential volume growth with growth rate
\nu>0
. Then, for all
\beta>3\nu/\penalty 2
there exist constants

C_{\beta}=s^{-2}(\cosh(\beta s)-1)
and
M=M_{\beta}>0
such that for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\|S_{2}(t)\|_{1,1}\leq M\mathrm{e}^{(C_{\beta}-\lambda_{2})t}.

In particular,
the semigroup
S_{2}
extends consistently to a strongly continuous semigroup on
\ell^{p}
for
1\leq p<\infty
and to a weak-
\ast
-continuous semigroup on
\ell^{\infty}.

We start with the observation that exponential volume growth implies a bounded combinatorial degree and a bound on the number of vertices in a ball.

Lemma 3.7

(Exponential volume growth implies bounded degree)
.

Assume the graph has exponential volume growth with growth rate
\nu>0
. Then, there is
C>0
such that for all
x\in X
,
r>0

\#B_{r}(x)\leq C^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\nu r}.

In particular, the combinatorial vertex degree is bounded by
C^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\nu s}
, where
s>0
is the jump size of
d.

Proof.

First, observe that exponential volume growth allows to estimate
m(x)
by
m(y)
for
x,y\in X.
More precisely,

m(x)\leq m(B_{d(x,y)}(y))\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\nu d(x,y)}m(y).

Next, we observe for
r>0

\#B_{r}(x)=\sum_{y\in B_{r}(x)}\frac{m(y)}{m(y)}\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\nu r}\frac{m(B_{r}(x))}{m(x)}\leq C^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\nu r}.

The statement about the combinatorial vertex degree follows by noting that neighbors of a vertex
x
are contained in
B_{s}(x)
.∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume the graph has exponential volume growth with growth rate
\nu>0
. For all
\beta_{0}>\frac{3}{2}\nu
there is
C=C_{\beta_{0}}>0
such that, for all
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
,

\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in X}\sqrt{\frac{m(y)}{m(x)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta d(x,y)}<C.

In particular, if the graph has subexponential volume growth, then such a bound holds for all
\beta>0
with
C=C_{\beta}>0
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and employing the Lemma
3.7
, we get for arbitrary
x\in X
and
\beta>\frac{3}{2}\nu

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\sqrt{\frac{m(y)}{m(x)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta d(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m(x)}}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{r\leq d(x,y)<r+1}\sqrt{{m(y)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta d(x,y)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m(x)}}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}m(B_{r+1}(x))^{1/\penalty 2}\left(\sum_{r\leq d(x,y)<r+1}\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta d(x,y)}\right)^{1/\penalty 2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\nu r/\penalty 2-\beta r}\#B_{r}(x)^{1/\penalty 2}\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{3\nu r/\penalty 2-\beta r}<\infty.

The “in particular” statement follows by noting that, for subexponential volume growth, we can choose
\nu
arbitrarily small.
∎

The lemma above allows us to extend the semigroup
S_{2}
to
\ell^{p}.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let
f\in C_{c}(X)
and
t\geq 0.
Then, choose
\beta>3\nu/\penalty 2
so that

\displaystyle\sum_{X}m|S_{2}(t)f|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}m(x)\Big|\sum_{y\in X}m(y)p_{t}(x,y)f(y)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{(C_{\beta}-\lambda_{2})t}\sum_{y\in X}m(y)|f(y)|\sum_{x\in X}\sqrt{\frac{m(x)}{m(y)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta d(x,y)}\leq C\mathrm{e}^{(C_{\beta}-\lambda_{2})t}\|f\|_{1}.

Here, we used the triangle inequality, Corollary
2.4
and Lemma
3.8
. Thus,
S_{2}(t)
extends uniquely to a bounded operator
S_{1}(t)
on
\ell^{1}.
From the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem, we conclude that
S_{2}(t)
also extends uniquely to bounded operators
S_{p}(t)
on
\ell^{p}
for all
1\leq p\leq 2.
The fact that
S_{p}
is again a semigroup, i.e., for
t,s\geq 0
, the identity
S_{p}(t)S_{p}(s)=S_{p}(t+s)
holds, follows as
S_{2}
is a semigroup and
\ell^{2}\cap\ell^{p}
is dense in
\ell^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
. By noting that
S_{2}(t)
is self-adjoint, we conclude by a standard duality argument that the same holds for
2\leq p\leq\infty.
It remains to show that
S_{p}
is strongly continuous for
\ell^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
.

Let
p=1.
We established that
S_{1}
is exponentially bounded. Thus, as
C_{c}(X)
is dense in
\ell^{1},
it is sufficient to show that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\|S_{1}(t)f-f\|_{1}=0
for all
f\in C_{c}(X).
Let
K=\mathrm{supp}\,f
and fix arbitrary
\varepsilon>0.
Let

K_{1}=\{y\in X\mid\text{ there is}~x\in K~\text{such that}~d(x,y)\leq 1\}.

Since we assume exponential volume growth, we find that

m(K_{1})\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\nu}m(K)<\infty.

Thus, as
S_{2}
is strongly continuous, we conclude for
t
small enough, that

\sum_{K_{1}}m|S_{1}(t)f-f|=\sum_{K_{1}}m|S_{2}(t)f-f|\leq m(K_{1})^{1/\penalty 2}\|S_{2}(t)f-f\|_{2}<\varepsilon/\penalty 2.

Moreover, by Lemma
3.8
we may choose
\beta>3\nu
large enough, such that

\|f\|_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta/\penalty 2}\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in X}\sqrt{\frac{m(y)}{m(x)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}d(x,y)}<\varepsilon/\penalty 3.

Finally, let
C_{\beta}
be the constant from Corollary
2.4
and choose
t
small enough, such that
\mathrm{e}^{C_{\beta}t}\varepsilon/\penalty 3<\varepsilon/\penalty 2.
Then, with Corollary
2.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{X\setminus K_{1}}m|S_{1}(t)f-f|

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\setminus K_{1}}m|S_{2}(t)f|\leq\sum_{x\in X\setminus K_{1}}m(x)\sum_{y\in K}m(y)|f(y)||p_{t}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{C_{\beta}t}\sum_{y\in K}m(y)|f(y)|\sum_{x\in X\setminus K_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{m(x)}{m(y)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta d(x,y)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{C_{\beta}t}\sum_{y\in K}m(y)|f(y)|\sum_{x\in X}\sqrt{\frac{m(x)}{m(y)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}d(x,y)}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\mathrm{e}^{C_{\beta}t}/\penalty 3<\varepsilon/\penalty 2.

All in all, we find that for
t
small enough
\|S_{1}(t)f-f\|_{1}<\varepsilon.
We obtain the strong continuity for
1\leq p\leq 2
by interpolation and for
2\leq p<\infty
by a standard duality argument.∎

3.3.
The Action of the Generator

In this section, we give a criterion when the generator
H_{p}
is a rectriction of the formal Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{H}
.
So, we assume that
-H_{p}
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}
for some
1\leq p<\infty
that is consistent with the semigroup
S_{2}
. Moreover,
H_{\infty}=(H_{1})^{*}
whenever
S_{1}
exists and is strongly continuous. By general theory about semigroups (
[
24
,
40
]
) such generators exist. Clearly,
H=H_{2}.
Here we briefly discuss the action of
H_{p}
in case when
X
is locally finite.

Theorem 3.9

(Action of
H_{p}
)
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
and
q
the respective Hölder dual. Assume
\mathcal{H}C_{c}(X)\subseteq\ell^{q}\cap\ell^{2},
which holds in particular if
b
is locally finite. Then

H_{p}=\mathcal{H}\quad\text{on}~D(H_{p})\quad\text{and}\quad C_{c}(X)\subseteq D(H_{q}).

Proof.

First, observe that
(H_{p}+\alpha)^{-1}=(H_{2}+\alpha)^{-1}
on
\ell^{p}\cap\ell^{2}
and
(H_{q}+\alpha)^{-1}=(H_{2}+\alpha)^{-1}
on
\ell^{q}\cap\ell^{2}
for large enough
\alpha>0.
This follows for
p,q<\infty
by consistency of the semigroups and the Laplace transform,
[
24
, Theorem 1.10]
and for
p=\infty
or
q=\infty
by duality and self-adjointness of
(H_{2}+\alpha)^{-1}
. Now, let
f\in D(H_{p})
and
\varphi\in C_{c}(X).

We have
\mathcal{H}C_{c}(X)\subseteq\ell^{2}
and so it follows from Green’s formula,
[
32
, Lemma 2.1]
that
C_{c}(X)\subseteq D(H_{2})
and
H_{2}=\mathcal{H}
on
D(H_{2}).
Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{X}m(H_{p}+\alpha)f\overline{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{X}m(H_{p}+\alpha)f\overline{(H_{2}+\alpha)^{-1}(\mathcal{H}+\alpha)\varphi}=\sum_{X}mf\overline{(\mathcal{H}+\alpha)\varphi}.

It follows that
\varphi\in D(H_{p}^{*})
which gives the second statement for
q>1
as
H_{p}^{*}=H_{q}
. The case
q=1
follows from the identity

\varphi=(H_{2}+\alpha)^{-1}(\mathcal{H}+\alpha)\varphi=(H_{1}+\alpha)^{-1}(\mathcal{H}+\alpha)\varphi\in D(H_{1}).

Moreover, by choosing
x\in X
and
\varphi=1_{x}/\penalty m,
we see that

H_{p}f(x)=\sum_{X}mf\overline{\mathcal{H}\varphi}=\mathcal{H}f(x),

where the last equality is due to the Green’s formula for
\mathcal{H}
,
[
32
, Lemma 2.1]
. The inclusion
\ell^{p}\subseteq F
follows from
\mathcal{H}C_{c}(X)\subseteq\ell^{q}
and Hölder’s inequality.
∎

3.4.
Analyticity and the Parabolic Equation

Here, we briefly discuss the analyticity of the semigroup
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}
and the solution of the parabolic equation associated to
H_{p}

(PE)

-H_{p}\omega_{t}(x)=\frac{d}{dt}\omega_{t}(x),

for all
t>0
,
x\in X
and initial conditions
\omega_{0}\in\ell^{p}.
In the previous section, we discussed when
H_{p}
is a restriction of the formal Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{H}
and in this case (
PE
) is indeed the parabolic equation associated to
\mathcal{H}
.

While
S_{p}
itself does not need to be analytic in the strict sense of
[
24
, Chapter II Definition 4.5]
, it is after a spectral shift due to the exponential bound on
S_{p}
. In other words, we find constants
A\in\mathbb{R}
such that the semigroup
T(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tA}S_{p}(t)
is analytic and bounded. Thus,
T
maps
\ell^{p}
into the domain of its generator
H_{p}-A
according to
[
24
, Chapter II Theorem 4.6]
. Consequently,
T
solves the
modified parabolic equation

-(H_{p}-A)\omega_{t}(x)=\frac{d}{dt}\omega_{t}(x)

for all
t>0,~x\in X
and initial conditions
\omega_{0}\in\ell^{p}
which is true iff
S_{p}
solves (
PE
) for all
\omega_{0}\in\ell^{p}.

Proposition 3.10
.

(a)

If the potential is form
bounded with constant
M>0
, then
\omega_{t}=S_{p}(t)\omega
solves (
PE
) on
X
for all initial conditions
\omega\in\ell^{p}
with

p\in\left(2\frac{M+1}{M}-2\frac{\sqrt{M+1}}{M},2\frac{M+1}{M}+2\frac{\sqrt{M+1}}{M}\right).

If the potential is bounded from below, then this is true for all
1<p<\infty.

(b)

If the graph has exponential volume growth, then
\omega_{t}=S_{p}(t)\omega
solves (
PE
) on
X
for all initial conditions
\omega\in\ell^{p}
and all
1<p<\infty.

Proof.

By the spectral theorem the semigroup
S_{2}
is analytic and contracting. By Theorem
3.1
,
3.4
and
3.6
, we get that
\|S_{2}(t)\|_{p,p}\leq Ce^{At}
for respective
p
and constants
A,C\geq 0.
Then the rescaled semigroup
T(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-tA}S_{2}(t)
is still contracting and analytic on
\ell^{2}
and bounded on
\ell^{p}.
A standard application of Stein’s interpolation theorem (see
[
49
, Theorem 1 of Chaper III §2]
or
[
42
, Theorem 6.6]
) yields that
T
extends to an analytic semigroup on
\ell^{r}
for all
r
strictly between
2
and
p.
Thus,
T
solves the modified parabolic equation from above for all initial conditions in
\ell^{r}
and, therefore,
S_{r}
solves (
PE
) for all initial conditions in
\ell^{r}.

∎

4.
Independence of the
\ell^{p}
-Spectrum

In the previous section, we discussed when the
\ell^{2}
semigroup
S_{2}
of a magnetic Schrödinger graph
(b,o,c)
over
(X,m)
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}.
Now, we study the stability of the spectrum
\sigma(H_{p})
under variation of
p.

Assume that
S_{2}
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}
for some
1\leq p<\infty
. Then
S_{p}
is
exponentially bounded
, i.e.,

\|S_{p}(t)\|_{p}\leq Me^{At}

for some
M\geq 1
,
A\in\mathbb{R}
, see
[
24
, Proposition 1.4]
.
This gives us the representation of the resolvents via the
Laplace transform

(H_{p}-\lambda)^{-1}f=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}S_{p}(t)f\mathrm{d}t

for all
\mathrm{Re}\lambda<-A
(see
[
24
, Theorem 1.10]
), where the integral is understood as an improper Riemann integral. In particular, we see that those
\lambda
are in the resolvent set denoted by
\rho(H_{p})=\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H_{p}).
Let us discuss this in the case of (sub-)exponential volume growth.

Example 4.1

((Sub-)exponential volume growth)
.

According to the previous section, for graphs with exponential volume growth with exponent
\nu>0
, the semigroup
S_{1}
is strongly continuous and exponentially bounded, i.e., for
\beta>3\nu/\penalty 2
, we have
\|S_{1}(t)\|_{1,1}\leq M_{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{(C_{\beta}-\lambda_{2})t}
for a constant
M_{\beta}
and
C_{\beta}=s^{-2}(\cosh(\beta s)-1)
as in Corollary
2.4
.
As
S_{2}
is a contraction semigroup with
\|S_{2}(t)\|_{2,2}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}t}
, we conclude from the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem and duality arguments that

\|S_{p}(t)\|_{p}\leq M_{\beta}^{\frac{2-r}{r}}\mathrm{e}^{\left(\frac{2-r}{r}C_{\beta}-\lambda_{2}\right)t},

where
r=\min\{p,p/\penalty(p-1)\}.

By the discussion above, we have for all
\mathrm{Re}\lambda<-\frac{2-r}{r}C_{\beta}+\lambda_{2}
that
\lambda\in\rho(H_{p}).
Specifically, since
C_{\beta}=s^{-2}(\cosh(\beta s)-1)
and
\beta>3\nu/2
, we obtain the estimate

\inf\sigma(H_{2})\leq\inf\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma(H_{p})+\frac{2-r}{rs^{2}}(\cosh(3\nu s/2)-1),

where
s
is the jump size of the intrinsic metric
d.

As we noted in the remark after Theorem
2.2
, we find that the constant
C_{\beta}
gets arbitrarily small for small
\beta>0.
Hence, in case of uniform subexponential volume growth, we may deduce that for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
\varepsilon>0
there is
M=M_{\varepsilon,p}>0
such that

\|S_{p}(t)\|_{p}\leq M\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon t}

and

\inf\sigma(H_{2})\leq\inf\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma(H_{p}).

This is a first glimpse at the stability of the
\ell^{p}
-spectrum.

4.1.
Uniformly Positive Measure and Spectral Inclusion

In this section we discuss the spectral inclusion of the
\ell^{2}
-spectrum in the
\ell^{p}
-spectrum in case of a uniformly positive measure, i.e.,
\inf_{x}m(x)>0.
This assumption implies that
\ell^{p}\subseteq\ell^{q}
for all
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Assume that
S_{2}
extends consistently to a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, and

\inf_{x\in X}m(x)>0.

Then,

\sigma(H_{2})\subseteq\sigma(H_{p}).

Proof.

Note that if
S_{2}
extends consistently to a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{p}
on
\ell^{p}
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, by duality we also have that
S_{2}
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{q}
on
\ell^{q},
where
q
is the respective Hölder dual of
p\neq 1
and to a weak-
\ast
-continuous semigroup on
\ell^{\infty}
if
p=1
. In either case, we have
\sigma(H_{p})\cap\mathbb{R}=\sigma(H_{q})\cap\mathbb{R}.
Thus, as the
\ell^{2}
-spectrum is real, we can assume without loss of generality
1\leq p\leq 2
and let
q
be its Hölder dual. We make the following claim:

Claim:

H_{p}\subseteq H_{2}\subseteq H_{q}.

If the claim holds, then the resolvents
(H_{p}-z)^{-1}
and
(H_{q}-z)^{-1}
are consistent for all
z\in\rho(H_{p})\cap\rho(H_{q})
because for all
f\in\ell^{p}\subseteq\ell^{q}

(H_{q}-z)(H_{p}-z)^{-1}f=(H_{p}-z)(H_{p}-z)^{-1}f=f=(H_{q}-z)(H_{q}-z)^{-1}f

and since
(H_{q}-z)
is injective.

Now, take
z\in\rho(H_{p})\cap{\rho(H_{q})}.
Then we conclude by consistency of the resolvents and interpolation, that
(H_{q}-z)^{-1}
is bounded as an operator on
\ell^{2}.
Moreover, it is an inverse of
H_{2}-z
and, therefore,
z\in\rho(H_{2})
: To see this, take
f\in D(H_{2}).
It follows from the claim that

(H_{q}-z)^{-1}(H_{2}-z)f=(H_{q}-z)^{-1}(H_{q}-z)f=f.

On the other hand, for
f\in\ell^{2}
choose
f_{n}\in\ell^{p},n\geq 0
, such that
f_{n}\rightarrow f
in
\ell^{2}.
Then
(H_{q}-z)^{-1}f_{n}=(H_{p}-z)^{-1}f_{n}\in D(H_{p})\subseteq D(H_{2})
and by the claim

(H_{2}-z)(H_{q}-z)^{-1}f_{n}=(H_{q}-z)(H_{q}-z)^{-1}f_{n}=f_{n}\rightarrow f.

Since
(H_{q}-z)^{-1}
is bounded on
\ell^{2}
and
H_{2}
is closed, we conclude that
(H_{q}-z)^{-1}f\in D(H_{2})
and

(H_{2}-z)(H_{q}-z)^{-1}f=f.

Hence,
\rho(H_{p})\cap\rho(H_{q})\subseteq\rho(H_{2})
and therefore
\sigma(H_{2})\subseteq\sigma(H_{p})\cup\sigma(H_{q}).
Since
\sigma(H_{2})
is real and
\sigma(H_{p})\cap\mathbb{R}=\sigma(H_{q})\cap\mathbb{R},
the assertion follows.

It remains to show the claim. By assumption, the semigroup
S_{p}
is exponentially bounded and consistent with
S_{2}
. Thus, we infer that the resolvents
(H_{p}+\alpha)^{-1}
and
(H_{2}+\alpha)^{-1}
exist and are consistent for large
\alpha>0,
which is a simple consequence of the Laplace transform. Let
f\in D(H_{p}).
Then, as
\ell^{p}\subseteq\ell^{2},

f=(H_{p}+\alpha)^{-1}(H_{p}+\alpha)f=(H_{2}+\alpha)^{-1}(H_{p}+\alpha)f\in D(H_{2}).

Applying
H_{2}+\alpha
to both sides yields

\displaystyle(H_{2}+\alpha)f

\displaystyle=(H_{p}+\alpha)f,

which implies the first inclusion of the claim. The second inclusion follows by duality: Let
f\in D(H_{2}).
Then, for all
g\in D(H_{p})\subseteq D(H_{2})
,
1\leq p\leq 2
, we have by self-adjointness of
H_{2}

\langle H_{2}f,g\rangle=\langle f,H_{2}g\rangle=\langle f,H_{p}g\rangle.

Hence,
f\in D((H_{p})^{*})=D(H_{q})
and
H_{2}f=H_{q}f.
This finishes the proof of the claim and, thus, of the theorem.
∎

Remark
.

If
S_{2}
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup on
\ell^{1}
, then by duality, we also have

\sigma(H_{2})\subseteq\sigma(H_{1})\cap\mathbb{R}=\sigma(H_{\infty})\cap\mathbb{R}

and, therefore,
\sigma(H_{2})\subseteq\sigma(H_{\infty})
as well.

The theorem above has the following immediate consequence in case of subexponential volume growth.

Corollary 4.3
.

If the graph has uniform subexponential volume growth and uniformly positive measure
\inf_{x}m(x)>0,
then, for all
1\leq p\leq\infty,

\inf\sigma(H_{2})=\inf\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma(H_{p}).

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorem
4.2
and Example
4.1
prior to it.
∎

4.2.
Independence of the
\ell^{p}
-Spectrum

In this section we study the independence of the
\ell^{p}
-spectrum in the case of form bounded potentials and subexponential volume growth.

Theorem 4.4

(Independence of
\ell^{p}
-spectrum)
.

If the potential is form bounded and the graph has uniform subexponential volume growth, then, for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
,

\sigma(H_{2})=\sigma(H_{p}).

The proof of the theorem above is similar to the graph case,
[
4
]
, and the case of manifolds
[
51
]
.
The key estimate we need is of the form

\|\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}(H_{2}-z)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\psi}\|_{2,2}<C

on compact subsets of
\rho(H_{2}),
where
\psi
is an arbitrary real bounded Lipschitz function with respect to the metric
d
and fixed Lipschitz constant
\varepsilon>0.
But first, we need a technical lemma which is found for Dirichlet forms in
[
29
, Lemma 3.5]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume the potential
c
is form bounded.
Let
f\in D(Q)
and
\psi
be a bounded Lipschitz function. Then,
\psi f\in D(Q).

Proof.

Let
f_{n}\in C_{c}(X),n>0
, be a Cauchy sequence with respect to the form norm
\|\cdot\|_{{Q}}=(Q(\cdot)+\|\cdot\|^{2}_{2})^{1/2}.
We need to show that
\psi f_{n}
is such a Cauchy sequence as well. Obviously,
\psi f_{n}
is an
\ell^{2}
-Cauchy sequence. Thus, all that remains to show is that for
\varepsilon>0
and all
n,m
large enough

Q(\psi(f_{n}-f_{m}))<\varepsilon.

Let
\kappa
be the Lipschitz constant of
\psi
. For arbitrary
f\in C_{c}(X)
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq Q(\psi f)\leq\sum_{X\times X}b|\nabla_{o}\psi|^{2}+\sum_{X}(c\vee 0)|\psi f|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in X}b(x,y)|f(x)(\psi(x)-\psi(y))+\psi(y)(f(x)-o(x,y)f(y))|^{2}+\sum_{X}(c\vee 0)|\psi f|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa^{2}\sum_{x,y\in X}b(x,y)|f(x)|^{2}d(x,y)^{2}+2\|\psi\|^{2}_{\infty}\left(Q(f)-\sum_{X}(c\wedge 0)|f|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2(\kappa^{2}+C\|\psi\|^{2}_{\infty})\|f\|_{2}^{2}+2(M+1)\|\psi\|^{2}_{\infty}Q(f).

Here, we used that
d
is intrinsic and that
c
is form bounded with bounds
M,C
. Applying this estimate to
f=f_{n}-f_{m}
finishes the proof.
∎

The following lemma is the key estimate for the proof of Theorem
4.4
and a variation of
[
4
, Lemma 4.1]
on graphs.

Lemma 4.6
.

Assume the potential is form bounded.
Let
K\subseteq\rho(H_{2})
be compact. Then there are constants
\varepsilon,C>0
such that, for all
z\in K
and all bounded real Lipschitz functions
\psi

with Lipschitz constant
\varepsilon
,
one has

\|\mathrm{e}^{\psi}(H_{2}-z)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\|_{2,2}<C.

Proof.

Since
\psi
is a bounded Lipschitz function, so are
\mathrm{e}^{\pm\psi}.

Thus, with Lemma
4.5
, we can define the quadratic form

Q_{\psi}
on
D(Q_{\psi})=D(Q)
as

Q_{\psi}(f,g)=Q(\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}f,\mathrm{e}^{\psi}g)-Q(f,g).

By doing an analogous calculation as in
[
4
, Lemma 4.1]
one finds that there is
C=C(s)>0
such that, for
\delta>0
and
0<\varepsilon<1
arbitrary,

|Q_{\psi}(f)|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\|f\|^{2}_{2}+\delta\left(Q(f)-\sum_{X}(c\wedge 0)|f|^{2}\right).

Now, since
c
is form bounded, we see that for
f\in D(Q)

Q(f)-\sum_{X}(c\wedge 0)|f|^{2}\leq C(Q(f)+\|f\|^{2}_{2})

for some
C>0.
Combining this with the estimate above yields

|Q_{\psi}(f)|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\|f\|^{2}_{2}+C\delta\|f\|^{2}_{2}+C\delta Q(f).

We conclude from
[
39
, Theorem VI 3.9]
that
Q_{\psi}+Q
is closed and sectorial and, therefore, is represented by a sectorial operator
H_{\psi}.
Moreover, by the same reference, we find that for
\delta
and
\varepsilon
small enough
K\subseteq\rho(H_{\psi})
and

\|(H_{\psi}-z)^{-1}\|_{2,2}<C

for some constant
C=C(\varepsilon,\delta,K).
The fact that
C
may only depend on
K
but not on
z
is due to compactness of
K.

A direct calculation shows that
H_{\psi}=\mathrm{e}^{\psi}H_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}
on
D(H_{\psi})=\mathrm{e}^{\psi}D(H_{2}).

Therefore,

\|\mathrm{e}^{\psi}(H_{2}-z)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\|_{2,2}=\|(\mathrm{e}^{\psi}H_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}-z)^{-1}\|_{2,2}=\|(H_{\psi}-z)^{-1}\|_{2,2}<C.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Under the assumption of subexponential volume growth, we already established in Example
4.1
that the semigroups on
\ell^{p}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, are exponentially bounded. Therefore, the resolvents
(H_{p}-\lambda)^{-1}
can be expressed via the Laplace transform as

(H_{p}-\lambda)^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}S_{p}(t)\mathrm{d}t

for all
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda<0
, (where the formula holds strongly for
1\leq p<\infty
and in a weak-
\ast
-sense for
p=\infty
). Since the semigroups
S_{p}
are consistent for
1\leq p<\infty,
we conclude the same for the resolvents on
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda<0\}
due to density arguments.
In particular, the kernel
g_{\alpha}
of the resolvent
G_{\alpha}=(H_{p}-\alpha)^{-1}
,
\alpha<0
, is independent of
1\leq p<\infty.

The following bounds for
g_{\alpha}
are a variation of
[
4
, Lemma 4.2]
on graphs and provide the necessary estimates for the proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Assume the potential
c
is form bounded and the graph
b
has subexponential volume growth. For every
\varepsilon>0
, there are
\alpha<0<C<\infty
such that, for all bounded Lipschitz functions
\psi
with Lipschitz constant
\varepsilon
,

(a)

|g_{\alpha}(x,y)|\leq C(m(x)m(y))^{-1/\penalty 2}\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon d(x,y)}
for all
x,y\in X.

(b)

\|\mathrm{e}^{\psi}G_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}m^{1/\penalty 2}\|_{1,2}\leq C.

(c)

\|m^{1/\penalty 2}\mathrm{e}^{\psi}G_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\|_{2,\infty}\leq C.

Proof.

Assertion (a) follows from Corollary
2.4

Assertions (b) and (c) follow verbatim as in

With the estimates from Lemma

The proof follows again the graph setting

Lemma 4.8
.

Assume the potential

is form bounded and the graph

extends to a bounded operator on

Moreover, for every compact

such that for all

\|(H_{2}-z)^{-2}\|_{p,p}\leq C.

Proof.

Let
g_{z}^{(2)}
be the kernel of
G_{z}^{2}=(H_{2}-z)^{-2}.

For given compact

, we choose
\varepsilon>0

according to Lemma
4.6
and, then
\alpha<0
according to Lemma
4.7
. By using the resolvent identity twice, we obtain for

G_{z}^{2}=(G_{\alpha}+(z-\alpha)G_{\alpha}G_{z})(G_{\alpha}+(z-\alpha)G_{z}G_{\alpha})=G_{\alpha}(I+(z-\alpha)G_{z})^{2}G_{\alpha}.

Let

be a bounded Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant

Then

and, therefore,

\|m^{1/\penalty 2}\mathrm{e}^{\psi}G_{z}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}m^{1/\penalty 2}\|_{1,\infty}\\
\leq\|m^{1/\penalty 2}\mathrm{e}^{\psi}G_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\|_{2,\infty}\|(I+\mathrm{e}^{\psi/\penalty 2}G_{z}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi/\penalty 2})^{2}\|_{2,2}\|\mathrm{e}^{\psi}G_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}m^{1/\penalty 2}\|_{1,2}\leq C<\infty,

by the calculation above and the estimates in Lemma
4.6
and Lemma

with

. Put

Then, we have shown that

is a bounded operator from
\ell^{1}
to

and as such admits a kernel

By the Hölder inequality, cf.
[

, so that,
for all

If we fix

Let

Then, we get

\displaystyle\|G^{2}_{z}f\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in X}m(x)|f(x)|\sum_{y\in X}m(y)|g^{(2)}_{z}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{x\in X}m(x)|f(x)|\sum_{y\in X}\sqrt{\frac{m(y)}{m(x)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon d(x,y)}\leq C^{\prime}\|f\|_{1}.

The last inequality is due to Lemma
3.8
. This proves the statement for

The case

via interpolation and duality arguments.
∎

following

]

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

First, we show the inclusion
\sigma(H_{p})\subseteq\sigma(H_{2}).
For

, let

be the kernel of

Then for any fixed

we conclude that

(see

is contained in the maximal domain of analyticity of

which is

Now, let

and

be the respective Hölder conjugate. From the inclusion

Moreover, it follows from the Laplace transform (which holds for

\ast

Weighted Simplicial Complexes

In this section, we introduce the fundamental concepts and notation for weighted simplicial complexes and their associated Laplacians, following the framework developed in
[
3
]
.

5.1.
Laplacians on Weighted Simplicial Complexes

Let
V
be a countable set and
\hat{\Sigma}
be a subset of the power set
\mathcal{P}(V)
consisting of finite subsets of
V.
We call
\hat{\Sigma}
a
simplicial complex
if
\sigma\in\hat{\Sigma}
and
\tau\subseteq\sigma
implies
\tau\in\hat{\Sigma}.
The set of elements of cardinality
k+1
(called
k
-
simplices
) is denoted by
\hat{\Sigma}_{k}
.

Let
m:\hat{\Sigma}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a function. We define
\Sigma=\hat{\Sigma}
if
\sum_{v\in\hat{\Sigma}_{0}}m(v)<\infty
and
\Sigma=\hat{\Sigma}\backslash\{\varnothing\}
otherwise and call
(\Sigma,m)
a
weighted simplicial complex
.
We assume throughout that the weight
m
is
locally summable
, i.e., for all
\tau\in\Sigma

\gamma(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)<\infty,

where for
\tau,\sigma\in\Sigma
with
\tau\subset\sigma
and
|\sigma\backslash\tau|=1
, we write
\tau\prec\sigma
and say
\tau
is a
face
of
\sigma
or
\sigma
is a
coface
of
\tau
. The
dimension
of a simplex is
\dim(\tau)=|\tau|-1
and the dimension of the complex is
\dim(\Sigma)=\sup_{\tau\in\Sigma}\dim(\tau)
.

We define the function space

\displaystyle{F}

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in C(\Sigma)\mid\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)|\omega(\sigma)|<\infty~\text{for all}~\tau\in\Sigma\}.

A linear map
\delta:D(\delta)=C(\Sigma)\rightarrow C(\Sigma)
is called a
coboundary operator
if, for all
\tau,\sigma\in\Sigma
, we have
\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma)\in\{-1,0,1\}
,
\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma)\neq 0
iff
\tau\prec\sigma
, and

\delta\delta=0.

On
D(\partial)={F}
, we define the
boundary operator

\partial:{F}\rightarrow C(\Sigma)
as

\partial\omega(\rho)=\frac{1}{m(\rho)}\sum_{\tau\succ\rho}m(\tau)\omega(\tau)\theta(\rho,\tau),

where
\theta(\tau,\sigma)=\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma)
.
Here we define the coboundary operator on functions rather than alternating forms, as traditionally done in the literature. However,
\delta
stands in a one-to-one correspondence to the respective coboundary operator acting on alternating forms on the oriented simplicial complex. This was shown in
[
3
, Subsection 2.2]
. The operator
\delta
acting on functions is for our purposes more convenient and allows for a direct interpretation of the Laplacian as a signed Schrödinger operator,
[
3
, Theorem 3.12]
. The “orientation” is encoded in the sign
\theta(\tau,\sigma).

We consider the Banach spaces
\ell^{p}=\ell^{p}(\Sigma,m)
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, defined as above with norms
\|\cdot\|_{p}
, where again
\ell^{2}
is a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\omega,\eta\rangle=\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega\overline{\eta}
.

We define the quadratic forms

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{+}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\|\delta\omega\|^{2}_{2},

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{Q}^{+})

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in C(\Sigma)\mid\|\delta\omega\|^{2}_{2}<\infty\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{-}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\|\partial\omega\|^{2}_{2},

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{Q}^{-})

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in{F}\mid\|\partial\omega\|^{2}_{2}<\infty\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{H}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\|\delta\omega+\partial\omega\|^{2},

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{Q}^{H})

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in{F}\mid\|\delta\omega+\partial\omega\|^{2}_{2}<\infty\}.

By
[
3
, Section 3.1]
, these quadratic forms are densely defined, non-negative and closed when restricted to the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}.

For
\circ\in\{\pm,H\}
, the corresponding Dirichlet Laplacians are defined as the self-adjoint operators associated to the closed quadratic form

\displaystyle Q^{\circ}=\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}\mid_{D(Q^{\circ})},\qquad D(Q^{\circ})=\overline{C_{c}(\Sigma)}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}}},\qquad\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}}=\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}+\|\cdot\|^{2}_{2}.

We denote by
\Delta^{\circ}=\Delta^{\circ}_{2}
the positive self-adjoint operator arising from the closed quadratic form
Q^{\circ}
. In
[
3
, Theorem 3.4]
it is shown that these operators are restrictions of the formal Laplacians

\displaystyle\Delta^{+}\omega=\partial\delta\omega,\qquad\Delta^{-}\omega=\delta\partial\omega,\qquad\Delta^{H}\omega=(\delta\partial+\partial\delta)\omega,

for
\omega
in the respective domains.

A key perspective developed in
[
3
]
is the representation of these Laplacians as signed Schrödinger operators, i.e.,
there exist explicit edge weights
b^{\circ}
, magnetic potentials
o^{\circ}
taking values in
\{\pm 1\}
, and potentials
c^{\circ}
such that the Laplacians can be expressed as Schrödinger operators

\Delta^{\circ}=\mathcal{H}^{\circ}

on appropriate domains
[
3
, Theorem 3.19]
. This allows us to leverage techniques from the theory of Schrödinger operators developed above in our analysis of these Laplacians. The term
c^{H}/m,
given by

\frac{c^{H}(\tau)}{m(\tau)}=\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{m(\tau)}{m(\rho)}+\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}\frac{m(\sigma)}{m(\tau)}-\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\sum_{\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}\succ\rho}\Big|\frac{m(\tau^{\prime})}{m(\rho)}-\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\frac{m(\sigma)}{m(\tau)}\Big|,

is known in the literature as
Forman curvature

[
27
,
38
]
. Hence, we say that the weighted simplicial complex has
form bounded curvature
if there exists
M,C>0
such that for all
\omega\in C_{c}(\Sigma)

\|\sqrt{c^{H}_{-}/\penalty m}\omega\|^{2}_{2}\leq M\mathcal{Q}^{H}(\omega)+C\|\omega\|^{2}_{2}.

We fix an intrinsic metric
d^{\circ}
for
b^{\circ}
over
(\Sigma,m)
with finite jump size (see
[
3
, Section 4 and Section 6]
for various possible choices) for the rest of this section and define the volume of balls as in Subsection
3.2
. In the next subsections, we discuss applications of the results developed above to the Laplacians on weighted simplicial complexes.

(Boundedness)

-skeleton of
\Sigma,
i.e., the collection of vertices and
1
-simplices, that we identify as a graph.
In order to apply the more abstract results about weighted simplicial complexes, we need to construct an intrinsic metric on all of
\Sigma
just from
d.
The remarkable fact here is that this is possible by considering the
1
-dimensional data of the complex only.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a weighted simplicial complex with combinatorial weights. If the volume of balls grows at most (sub)exponentially with respect to the combinatorial metric
d
, then
\Sigma
is finite dimensional and there is an intrinsic metric
\rho
with finite jump size on
\Sigma
such that the volume of balls grows at most (sub)exponentially with respect to
\rho
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
the combinatorial vertex degree is uniformly bounded by some
D>0.
This also implies that
\Sigma
is finite dimensional. Therefore, the combinatorial metric divided by
\sqrt{D}
is intrinsic with finite jump size, and we denote this metric by
\rho
. We extend
\rho
to all of
\Sigma
by

\rho(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=(\dim(\Sigma)+1)^{-1/2}\inf_{\sigma=\sigma_{0}\sim\ldots\sim\sigma_{n}=\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\max_{v\in\sigma_{k}\cap\sigma_{k-1}}\deg(v)^{-1/2}

for all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
of the same dimension and by infinity for simplices of different dimension. In
[
3
, Proof of Theorem 6.1]
, we have shown that
\rho
is intrinsic on
\Sigma_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq\dim(\Sigma).
Clearly, it has finite jump size as well. Furthermore, it was shown there that for all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{k}
, we find
v\in\sigma,v^{\prime}\in\sigma^{\prime}
such that

d(v,v^{\prime})/\penalty\sqrt{D}=\rho(v,v^{\prime})\leq(\dim(\Sigma)+1)^{1/2}\rho(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Thus, we can conclude the (sub-)exponential volume growth of balls with respect to
\rho
from that of
d
since every
\sigma
contains exactly
\dim(\sigma)+1
vertices.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be a weighted simplicial complex with combinatorial weights. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The volume of balls grows at most exponentially with respect to some intrinsic metric with finite jump size on the
1
-skeleton.

(ii)

The volume of balls grows at most exponentially with respect to the combinatorial metric on the
1
-skeleton.

(iii)

The combinatorial degree on the
1
-skeleton is bounded.

(iv)

The coboundary operator
\delta
is bounded on
\ell^{p}
for some (all)
1\leq p<\infty.

In that case there exists an intrinsic metric of finite jump size on
\Sigma
such that the volume of balls grows at most exponentially. Moreover,
\Delta^{\circ}
is bounded on
\ell^{p}
for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
\circ\in\{\pm,H\}.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii): This follows from Lemma
3.7
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii): If the combinatorial degree is bounded by
D\geq 1,
then the combinatorial metric divided by
\sqrt{D}
is intrinsic with finite jump size. Hence, the balls grow at most exponentially with respect to this metric, i.e.,
\#B_{r}(x)\leq D^{r\sqrt{D}}
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): In the proof of Lemma
5.6
we have seen that the combinatorial metric is intrinsic after normalization with some constant that depends on the dimension of
\Sigma
only.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv):
Let the combinatorial degree be bounded by
D.
This already forces
\Sigma
to be finite dimensional as well as
\gamma\leq D.
Thus, we get for every
\tau\in\Sigma
the estimate

\gamma(\tau)(\dim(\tau)+2)\leq D(\dim\Sigma+2)<\infty.

We now conclude (iv) from Proposition
5.2
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Let
\delta
be bounded on
\ell^{p}
for some
1\leq p<\infty
and choose an arbitrary vertex
v.
Since the combinatorial degree of
v
is precisely given by
\|\delta 1_{v}\|_{p}^{p},
we conclude (iii).

The last part of the statement follows from Lemma
5.6
and Proposition
5.2
.
∎

We finish the paper with the proof of Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
from the introduction.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Theorem
3.9
and
[
3
, Theorem 3.12]
(see also the remark after Proposition
5.2
) we have that the generator
\Delta_{p}^{H}
acts as the formal Laplacian
\partial\delta+\delta\partial
on
D(\Delta_{p}^{H})
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
in case the
\ell^{2}
semigroups extend to
\ell^{p}
.
Assertion (a) follows directly from Theorem
5.3
(a).
For assertion (b) in the case of expontial volume growth, we get from the lemma above that the combinatorial degree on the
1
-skeleton is bounded by some
D>0.
This implies that
\Sigma
is finite dimensional and
\gamma\leq D.
We conclude the statement now from Theorem
5.3
(c).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By Lemma
5.6
, there exists an intrinsic metric on
\Sigma
with finite jump size such that the volume of balls grows at most subexponentially. Furthermore, by Lemma
5.7
, we obtain that
\Delta^{H}
is bounded on
\ell^{1}.
Therefore,
S_{2}^{H}
extends to a strongly continuous semigroup
S_{1}^{H}
on
\ell^{1}
with
\|S_{1}^{H}(t)\|_{1,1}\leq\mathrm{e}^{t\|\Delta^{H}\|_{1,1}}.
Hence, by Proposition
3.5
, the curvature is bounded from below and, in particular, form bounded. Thus, the result is a consequence of Theorem
5.5
.
∎
