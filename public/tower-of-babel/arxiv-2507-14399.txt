Understanding radial flow fluctuations in the quark-gluon plasma via event-by-event momentum rescaling approach
Jiangyong Jia

Abstract
The transverse momentum ( p T p_{\mathrm{T}} ) differential radial flow fluctuation, v 0 ​ ( p T ) v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}) , has emerged as a new probe of the quark-gluon plasma in heavy-ion collisions. I present a framework that recasts v 0 ​ ( p T ) v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}) as a p T p_{\mathrm{T}} -dependent momentum rescaling function g ⁡ ( p T ) g(p_{\mathrm{T}}) . This transformation reveals that constant rescaling ( g ⁡ ( p T ) = 1 g(p_{\mathrm{T}})=1 ) captures the main features of v 0 ​ ( p T ) v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}) , including its characteristic rise-and-fall pattern that emerges naturally from the particle spectral shape. Analysis of ATLAS data at s N ​ N = 5.02 \sqrt{s_{NN}}=5.02 TeV reveals small but significant deviations of g ⁡ ( p T ) g(p_{\mathrm{T}}) from unity with clear centrality dependence. This transformation converts v 0 ​ ( p T ) v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}) into a transparent probe of p T p_{\mathrm{T}} -dependent dynamics: g ⁡ ( p T ) < 1 g(p_{\mathrm{T}})<1 signals suppressed fluctuations while g ⁡ ( p T ) > 1 g(p_{\mathrm{T}})>1 indicates enhanced fluctuations relative to global radial flow. Predictions for lower energies reveal spectral shape differences alone could generate substantial variations in v 0 ​ ( p T ) v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}) . This framework enables direct connections between theoretical predictions and underlying physics mechanisms.



Introduction.

Heavy ion collisions at RHIC and the LHC create the quark-gluon plasma (QGP), a state of matter composed of deconfined quarks and gluons. A fundamental aspect of these collisions is radial flow, representing the isotropic component of collective expansion driven by pressure gradients established in the initial stages of the collision
Heinz and Snellings 2013
. The magnitude of radial flow is directly influenced by the initial geometry of the collision: events with smaller initial sizes tend to be denser, leading to stronger radial flow
Bozek and Broniowski 2012
;
Giacalone et al. 2021
;
ATLAS Collaboration 2024
. This manifests as flatter transverse momentum (
p_{\mathrm{T}}
) spectra, denoted as
n(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, and higher average transverse momentum,
[p_{\mathrm{T}}]
(see Fig.
1
a). Conversely, larger initial sizes result in weaker radial flow, steeper spectra, and lower
[p_{\mathrm{T}}]
values.

Radial flow fluctuations can be quantitatively assessed through event-by-event (EbE) variations in either
[p_{\mathrm{T}}]
, denoted as
\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}]
, or
n(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, denoted by
\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, where
\delta X=X-\left\langle X\right\rangle
represents the deviation from the event-averaged quantity. These fluctuations are quantified by a
p_{\mathrm{T}}
integrated observable,
v_{0}\equiv\sqrt{\left\langle(\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}])^{2}\right\rangle}/\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle
and a
p_{\mathrm{T}}
-differential observable
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})

Mazeliauskas and Teaney 2016
;
Schenke et al. 2020
. Both
v_{0}
and
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
are sensitive to initial conditions, as well as medium properties such as the equation of state and viscosities of the QGP. While integrated
v_{0}
measurements have provided valuable insights across various collision energies and systems, the differential nature of
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
offers additional diagnostic power. It can differentiate between distinct initial density profiles that might otherwise yield identical
[p_{\mathrm{T}}]

Mazeliauskas and Teaney 2016
, and it offers a unique avenue to probe
p_{\mathrm{T}}
-dependent influence of transport coefficients like bulk viscosity
Samanta et al. 2024
.

\displaystyle v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})=\rho(n(p_{\mathrm{T}}),[p_{\mathrm{T}}])\frac{\sqrt{\left\langle(\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}}))^{2}\right\rangle}}{\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}\;,

(1)

where

is the Pearson correlation coefficient between variables

v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})

that correlates with
[p_{\mathrm{T}}]

\displaystyle\frac{\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}=\frac{v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{v_{0}}\frac{\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}]}{\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle}\;.

(2)

The first measurement of
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
by the ATLAS experiment

p_{\mathrm{T}}
(see Fig.
1

v_{n}(p_{\mathrm{T}})

ATLAS Collaboration 2012
, where the rising portion has been attributed to anisotropic expansion and the falling portion to jet quenching. By analogy, a similar interpretation has been tentatively proposed for
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})

Aad et al. 2025
. However, this interpretation obscures a fundamental question: how much of the observed
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
shape arises from the kinematic effect of the varying spectral slope, and how much represents genuine
p_{\mathrm{T}}
-dependent dynamics beyond global radial flow?

This work addresses this question by introducing a momentum rescaling framework that transforms
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
into a more physically transparent observable. The key insight is that radial flow fundamentally boosts particles from lower to higher momentum, as captured in models like the blast-wave parametrization
Schnedermann et al. 1993
;
Retiere and Lisa 2004
. Event-by-event fluctuations in radial flow can therefore be viewed as a momentum rescaling of the final-state particle spectrum (see Fig.
1
c). In this picture,
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
factorizes into two distinct components: a kinematic factor determined by the spectral shape, and a dynamical factor
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
that encodes deviations from global momentum rescaling.

This transformation has important implications. We demonstrate that a constant momentum rescaling (
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})=1
) already reproduces most features of the measured
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, including the rise-and-fall pattern. Deviations of
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
from unity then directly signal additional physics. Most importantly, this framework provides a universal language for theoretical models. Any dynamical model can now make specific predictions for
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
that directly connect to the underlying physics mechanisms.

Momentum rescaling model.

We start with the normalized particle spectrum in a single event,
n(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, with
\int n(p_{\mathrm{T}})dp_{\mathrm{T}}=1
. Drawing from by hydrodynamic principles, we assume that fluctuations in
n(p_{\mathrm{T}})
around the event-averaged spectrum
f(p_{\mathrm{T}})\equiv\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle
are driven by variations in
[p_{\mathrm{T}}]
around its mean. This scenario corresponds to a rescaling of the momentum axis:
p_{\mathrm{T}}\rightarrow p_{\mathrm{T}}(1+\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}]/\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle)
, leading to a transformed spectrum,

\displaystyle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})=\frac{\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle}{[p_{\mathrm{T}}]}f\left(\frac{p_{\mathrm{T}}}{[p_{\mathrm{T}}]}\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle\right)\;,

(3)

where the prefactor
\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle/[p_{\mathrm{T}}]
ensures particle number conservation.

For small fluctuations, the spectral variation
\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}})=n(p_{\mathrm{T}})-\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle
can be obtained through a first-order Taylor expansion:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}\approx\left.\frac{\partial\ln\left\langle n\right\rangle}{\partial\ln[p_{\mathrm{T}}]}\right|_{[p_{\mathrm{T}}]=\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle}\frac{\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}]}{\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle}\;.

(4)

Comparing with Eq. (
2
) yields our central relation:

\displaystyle\frac{v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{v_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial\ln\left\langle n\right\rangle}{\partial\ln[p_{\mathrm{T}}]}\right|_{[p_{\mathrm{T}}]=\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle}=-\frac{d\ln\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}-1\;.

(5)

The additional
-1
term originates from the normalization prefactor in Eq. (
3
).

This framework implies that under constant fractional momentum rescaling,
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
is entirely determined by the spectral shape. For an exponential spectrum characteristic of thermal emission,
\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle\propto p_{\mathrm{T}}\exp(-2p_{\mathrm{T}}/[p_{\mathrm{T}}])
, we recover
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})/v_{0}=2(p_{\mathrm{T}}/\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle-1)

Schenke et al. 2020
. For a power-law spectrum expected at high
p_{\mathrm{T}}
region,
\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle\propto(p_{\mathrm{T}}+p_{0})^{-m}
, the result asymptotes to a constant
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})/v_{0}=\frac{mp_{\mathrm{T}}}{(p_{\mathrm{T}}+p_{0})}-1\underset{p_{\mathrm{T}}\to\infty}{=\!=}m-1
. The transition of the spectra between these regimes at around
p_{\mathrm{T}}\approx 3
–4 GeV naturally produces a rise-and-fall shape for
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
.

To capture deviations from global rescaling, we introduce a momentum-dependent factor:

\displaystyle\frac{v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{v_{0}}=-g(p_{\mathrm{T}})\left(\frac{d\ln\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}+1\right)\;,

(6)

where
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})=1
corresponds to pure global momentum rescaling, and deviations signal additional
p_{\mathrm{T}}
-dependent dynamics.

Model implementation.
The extraction of
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
from experimental measurements proceeds through the following steps:

1.

Baseline calculation:
The first step is to compute the expected
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
assuming pure global rescaling using published spectra:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{v_{0}}\right)_{\text{model}}=-\frac{d\ln\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}-1\;.

(7)

2.

Acceptance correction:
ATLAS measurements use reference particles within
p_{\mathrm{T}}\in A=[0.5,2]
GeV, yielding
v_{0}^{A}

Aad et al. 2025
. The relationship to full-acceptance
v_{0}
is given by
Schenke et al. 2020
;
Parida et al. 2024
:

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092ptC_{A}=\frac{v_{0}^{A}}{v_{0}}=\frac{\int_{A}(p_{\mathrm{T}}-\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle_{A})\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})/v_{0}dp_{\mathrm{T}}}{\int_{A}p_{\mathrm{T}}\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle dp_{\mathrm{T}}}\;,

(8)

where
\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle_{A}
denotes the mean
p_{\mathrm{T}}
within range
A
. This yields:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{v_{0}^{A}}\right)_{\text{model}}=-\left(\frac{d\ln\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}+1\right)/C_{A}\;.

(9)

3.

Incorporate normalization offset:
As demonstrated in previous work, the choice of the reference momentum range
A
can introduce a global offset in the measured
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})

Bhatta et al. 2025
. To account for this, a shift is applied to align the zero-crossing points of the model with the experimental data for each centrality class:

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pts=\left(\frac{v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}^{0})}{v_{0}^{A}}\right)_{\text{model}}-\left(\frac{v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}^{0})}{v_{0}^{A}}\right)_{\text{data}}\!,

(10)

where
p_{\mathrm{T}}^{0}
denotes the
p_{\mathrm{T}}
value at which the ATLAS data crosses zero, i.e.
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}}^{0})=0
.

4.

Extract
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
:
Finally,
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
is determined by taking the ratio of the measured
v_{0}^{A}
and
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
data from ATLAS
Aad et al. 2025
to the offset-corrected model prediction:

\displaystyle g(p_{\mathrm{T}})=\frac{(v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})/v_{0}^{A})_{\text{data}}}{-(d\ln\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle/d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}+1)/C_{A}-s}\;.

(11)

This allows for the calculation of the scaled variance of the momentum fluctuation as a function of
p_{\mathrm{T}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{p_{\mathrm{T}}}(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{p_{\mathrm{T}}}\equiv g(p_{\mathrm{T}})v_{0}=g(p_{\mathrm{T}})\frac{v_{0,\textrm{data}}^{A}}{C_{A}}\;.

(12)

Future extension.
The momentum rescaling framework introduced in this work provides a useful tool for characterizing radial flow fluctuations and theirs connection to QGP dynamics. Two promising extensions for future research are outlined here.

Two-particle radial flow fluctuations.
According to Ref.
Schenke et al. 2020
,
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
can also be measured by correlating multiplicity fluctuations in two different transverse momentum ranges,
p_{\mathrm{T1}}
and
p_{\mathrm{T2}}
,

\displaystyle V_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T1}},p_{\mathrm{T2}})=\frac{\left\langle\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T1}})\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T2}})\right\rangle}{\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T1}})\right\rangle\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T2}})\right\rangle}\;.

(13)

The collective nature of radial flow implies a factorization:
V_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T1}},p_{\mathrm{T2}})=v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T1}})v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T2}})
. Within the momentum rescaling model, this leads to the prediction (Eq. (
4
)):

\displaystyle V_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T1}},p_{\mathrm{T2}})\approx\left(\frac{d\ln\left\langle n\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}(p_{\mathrm{T1}})+1\right)\left(\frac{d\ln\left\langle n\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}(p_{\mathrm{T2}})+1\right)\;.

(14)

Deviations from this prediction would signal momentum-dependent decorrelation effects arising from subleading flow
Mazeliauskas and Teaney 2016
.

High-order radial flow fluctuations.
The momentum rescaling model can be extended to investigate higher-order spectral fluctuations, such as the skewness,
v_{0}\{3\}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, and kurtosis
v_{0}\{4\}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
of the spectra fluctuations
Bhatta et al. 2025
:

\displaystyle v_{0}\{3\}(p_{\mathrm{T}})^{3}=\left\langle(\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}}))^{3}\right\rangle/\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle^{3}\;,

\displaystyle v_{0}\{4\}(p_{\mathrm{T}})^{4}=\frac{\left\langle(\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}}))^{4}\right\rangle-3\left\langle(\delta n(p_{\mathrm{T}}))^{2}\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle^{4}}\;,

\displaystyle...

(15)

Based on Eq, (
4
), in the limit of global rescaling, all high-order fluctuations are expected to exhibit the same
p_{\mathrm{T}}
dependence as the variance:

\displaystyle\frac{v_{0}\{k\}(p_{\mathrm{T}})}{v_{0}\{k\}}\approx-\frac{d\ln\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}-1\;.

(16)

This can be further extended to the case where each particle in a
k
-particle multiplet is selected from a different
p_{\mathrm{T}}
range:

\displaystyle V_{0}\{k\}\left(p_{\mathrm{T}1},p_{\mathrm{T}2,...}\right)\approx\overset{k}{\underset{i=1}{\prod}}\left(-\frac{d\ln\left\langle n\right\rangle}{d\ln p_{\mathrm{T}}}(p_{\mathrm{T}i})-1\right)\;.

(17)

Summary and outlook.

We have introduced a new approach to understanding radial flow fluctuations by recasting
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
as a momentum rescaling function
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
. This framework reveals that the characteristic rise-and-fall pattern of
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
is primarily a kinematic effect from the varying spectral slope: a constant rescaling (
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})=1
) already captures the essential features.

The transformation
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})\rightarrow g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
separates kinematic from dynamical effects. Analysis of ATLAS data at
\mbox{$\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{NN}}}$}=5.02
TeV reveals that
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
deviates from unity by about 20–40%: suppression at low
p_{\mathrm{T}}
(
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})\sim 0.8
), enhancement at intermediate
p_{\mathrm{T}}
(
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})\sim 1.4
), and gradual decrease at high
p_{\mathrm{T}}
. These deviations are largest in central collisions and systematically decrease toward peripheral collisions, revealing their connection to specific effects in the QGP.

The
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
is an observable more situable for theoretical interpretation: any dynamical model can now make specific predictions for
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
that directly encode its physics content. Our predictions for RHIC energies suggest that spectral shape differences alone can generate significant energy-dependent features, providing a clear baseline for future measurements to identify genuine dynamical effects.

This momentum rescaling principle opens new avenues for experiment and theory. Future measurements of
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
for identified particles will test flow universality and reveal mass effects. Calculating
g(p_{\mathrm{T}})
within blast-wave parameterizations, viscous hydrodynamics, and models including jet quenching will provide quantitative tests of QGP dynamics. The framework extends naturally to higher-order fluctuations, where deviations from factorization signal
p_{\mathrm{T}}
-dependent non-Gaussian dynamics. This approach may also apply to anisotropic flow
v_{n}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, where
v_{n}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
could potentially be understood through azimuthally-dependent momentum rescaling. This approach could then be used to explore the correlation between anistropic flow and radial flow
\left\langle v_{n}(p_{\mathrm{T}})^{2}v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle

Parida et al. 2025
within the momentum rescaling framework. This connection between isotropic and anisotropic collective phenomena warrants future investigation.

The experimental determination of
v_{0}=\sqrt{\left\langle(\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}])^{2}\right\rangle}/\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle
varies across experiments due to different detector acceptances and analysis strategies. STAR and ALICE typically use
p_{\mathrm{T}}>0.2
GeV, CMS employs
p_{\mathrm{T}}>0.3
GeV, while ATLAS uses
p_{\mathrm{T}}>0.5
GeV. Though these measurements probe the same underlying radial flow fluctuations, their numerical values differ substantially due to the
p_{\mathrm{T}}
-range dependence.

To quantify this effect, we employ the acceptance factor
C_{A}
introduced in Ref.
Parida et al. 2024
, which relates measurements within a specific range
p_{\mathrm{T}}\in A=[p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{min}},p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{max}}]
to the full-acceptance value through Eq. (
8
). This factor encodes how the interplay between spectral shape and
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
weights different momentum regions.

Figure
5
presents our predictions for
C_{A}
as a function of acceptance boundaries. Several key features emerge: 1)
C_{A}
decreases in peripheral collisions for larger
p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{max}}
, reflecting their flatter spectra. 2) RHIC shows systematically larger
C_{A}
than LHC due to steeper spectra at lower energies. 3) The acceptance factor saturates for
p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{max}}>5
–6 GeV at LHC but earlier (
\sim
3–4 GeV) at RHIC.

These trends arise because
C_{A}
weights contributions from both the local yield
\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle
and the local
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
value (Eq. (
8
)). In peripheral collisions, the flatter spectrum reduces the relative contribution from low-
p_{\mathrm{T}}
particles where
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
is negative, thereby decreasing
C_{A}
.

A disadvantage of
v_{0}
is its strong dependence on
\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle
, which itself varies considerably with the choice of
p_{\mathrm{T}}
range. Hence, we introduce a complementary observable that removes the
\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle
dependence:

\displaystyle F_{A}=C_{A}\frac{\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle}{\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle_{A}}=\frac{\sqrt{\left\langle(\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}])^{2}\right\rangle_{A}}}{\sqrt{\left\langle(\delta[p_{\mathrm{T}}])^{2}\right\rangle}}\;.

(18)

This quantity directly measures the fraction of total momentum variance contained within range
A
.

Figure
6
reveals that
F_{A}
is substantially larger than
C_{A}
and and is also greater at lower
\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{NN}}}
. The reference range
0.5<p_{\mathrm{T}}<2
GeV captures approximately 40% of total fluctuations at LHC but 55% at RHIC, reflecting the concentration of particle production at lower momenta for smaller
\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{NN}}}
. The saturation behavior of
F_{A}
with
p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{max}}
provides a direct measure of the momentum reach of collective radial flow. This influence extends to 5–6 GeV at LHC but only 3–4 GeV at RHIC.

The formula used to predict
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
, Eq. (
5
), is derived under the assumption of small momentum fluctuations with leading-order Taylor expansion. This approximation may break down for large values of
v_{0}
, which are relevant for peripheral collisions and small systems. To illustrate this limitation and establish the validity range of our approach, we consider an exponential spectrum:

\displaystyle\left\langle n(p_{\mathrm{T}})\right\rangle\propto\frac{p_{\mathrm{T}}}{[p_{\mathrm{T}}]^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2p_{\mathrm{T}}}{[p_{\mathrm{T}}]}\right)\;.

(19)

For this spectrum, the exact result for small
v_{0}
is
Schenke et al. 2020
:

\displaystyle v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})=2v_{0}\left(\frac{p_{\mathrm{T}}}{\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle}-1\right).

(20)

Experimental measurements span a wide range of
v_{0}
values. In Au+Au (RHIC)
Adams et al. 2005
;
Adam et al. 2019
;
Abdulhamid et al. 2024
and Pb+Pb (LHC)
ALICE Collaboration 2014
;
ATLAS Collaboration 2024
,
v_{0}
ranges from
\sim
0.01 (central) to
>
0.05 (peripheral). Small systems show even larger values: 0.05–0.14 in
pp
collisions
ALICE Collaboration 2014
. These values are generally larger at RHIC energies
Adam et al. 2019
, primarily due to their smaller
\left\langle[p_{\mathrm{T}}]\right\rangle
values. Crucially, most measurements use restricted
p_{\mathrm{T}}
ranges (typically 0.2–2 GeV). Full-acceptance values could be factor
\sim
2 larger (see Fig.
5
). This implies that the total
v_{0}
in Au+Au or Pb+Pb collisions could reach values as high as 0.02 in central, 0.05 in mid-central, and greater than 0.1 in peripheral collisions.

Figure
7
quantifies deviations from the linear approximation (Eq. (
20
)) for different magnitudes of
v_{0}
. Key findings are:

•

For
v_{0}\lesssim 0.02
(typical of central collisions): deviations remain below 5%.

•

For
v_{0}=0.05
(mid-peripheral): 10% deviations appear at
p_{\mathrm{T}}\sim 4
GeV.

•

For
v_{0}=0.1
(peripheral/small systems): deviations reach 40% at high
p_{\mathrm{T}}
.

•

The zero-crossing point shifts systematically to larger
p_{\mathrm{T}}
with increasing
v_{0}
.

These results have important implications:

•

The linear framework remains accurate for central and mid-central collisions (
v_{0}\lesssim 0.05
).

•

Peripheral collisions require nonlinear corrections, particularly at high
p_{\mathrm{T}}
.

•

In extreme cases (
v_{0}>0.1
), the same spectrum can produce qualitatively different
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})/v_{0}
shapes purely from the magnitude of fluctuations.

To handle large-fluctuation regimes accurately, future work should develop systematic expansions or numerical approaches for a proper interpretation of
v_{0}(p_{\mathrm{T}})
. This is particularly important for small systems where collective behavior remains debated.
