Everything all at once: On choosing an estimand for multi-component environmental exposures
Kara E. Rudolph, Shodai Inose, Nicholas Williams, Ivan Diaz, Lucia Calderon, Jacqueline M. Torres, Marianthi-Anna Kioumourtzoglou

Abstract
Many research questions—particularly those in environmental health—do not involve binary exposures. In environmental epidemiology, this includes multivariate exposure mixtures with nondiscrete components. Causal inference estimands and estimators to quantify the relationship between an exposure mixture and an outcome are relatively few. We propose an approach to quantify a relationship between a shift in the exposure mixture and the outcome—either in the single timepoint or longitudinal setting. The shift in the exposure mixture can be defined flexibly in terms of shifting one or more components, including examining interaction between mixture components, and in terms of shifting the same or different amounts across components. The estimand we discuss has a similar interpretation as a main effect regression coefficient. First, we focus on choosing a shift in the exposure mixture supported by observed data. We demonstrate how to assess extrapolation and modify the shift to minimize reliance on extrapolation. Second, we propose estimating the relationship between the exposure mixture shift and outcome completely nonparametrically, using machine learning in model-fitting. This is in contrast to other current approaches, which employ parametric modeling for at least some relationships, which we would like to avoid because parametric modeling assumptions in complex, nonrandomized settings are tenuous at best. We are motivated by longitudinal data on pesticide exposures among participants in the CHAMACOS Maternal Cognition cohort. We examine the relationship between longitudinal exposure to agricultural pesticides and risk of hypertension. We provide step-by-step code to facilitate the easy replication and adaptation of the approaches we use.

1.1
Estimand that quantifies the relationship between a shift in the exposure mixture and the outcome

Recall the interpretation of the regression coefficient given above. We can formulate a statistical estimand that has a similar interpretation:
a shift in some or all components of
\mathbf{A}
, defined by
\mathrm{d}
(we discuss this definition next),
is associated with an X-unit increase in outcome

, holding all other unshifted exposure components (if any) at their observed levels and controlling for covariates.
We more formally define this type of estimand, which is called a modified treatment policy in the causal inference literature (see Supplement S2).

110

111

The particular shift,
\mathrm{d}

For simplicity, we first consider an exposure mixture at a single timepoint—baseline. We denote the shift applied to
\mathbf{A}_{0}
as
\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{A}_{0})
. We can define an additive shift as:

\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{A}_{0})=(A_{1,0}+\gamma_{1},A_{2,0}+\gamma_{2},...,A_{7,0}+\gamma_{7})

for an increase in each pesticide
A_{i}
of

. For example, we could shift each pesticide up by 10 units; in this case,
\gamma_{i}=10
for each
i\in\{1,..,7\}
. These shifted values are shown in Figure

Panel A.
We can define a multiplicative shift as

\mathrm{d}^{\text{all}}(\mathbf{A}_{0})=(\delta_{1}A_{1,0},\delta_{2}A_{2,0},...\delta_{7}A_{7,0}).

(1)

For example, we could shift each pesticide down by 20%; in this case,

for each

3
Question: How can we evaluate the extent to which shifts in the exposure mixture,
\mathrm{d}
, have support in our data?

In Section
1.1
, we showed how one could flexibly define many different shifts—of varying magnitudes—across one or more members of the exposure mixture and across one or more timepoints. However, not all shifts will be well-supported by our observed data. If the shift we first consider is not well-supported, meaning that it is based on regions where there are no real-world data, is it still of interest? Many would answer no, as such a shift would not reflect the real world. Consequently, we view choosing a shift as an iterative process: evaluating the extent to which a scientifically interesting shift is supported by real data, and if it is poorly supported, modifying the shift to be one that is better supported (though we acknowledge that standard uncertainty quantification would not reflect such iteration).

3.1
Positivity

What does it mean to be “in support" of our observed data? Formally, this means satisfying the positivity assumption defined in Section
1.1
. Informally, positivity means that we do not condition on zero-probability events. In the case of the estimands we consider (Eq
5
and
6
), in the single timepoint case, it means that for every exposure-covariate combination observed in our data, there is a nonzero probability of observing the shifted exposure value conditional on the covariates; and in the longitudinal case, it means that for every observed value of the exposure mixture and history at time
t
, the exposure mixture’s shifted value must also be in the support of the data. We can formalize this assumption using notation as: if
(\mathbf{a}_{t},h_{t})\in supp\{\mathbf{A}_{t},H_{t}\},
then
(\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{a}_{t},h_{t}),h_{t})\in supp\{\mathbf{A}_{t},H_{t}\}
for each
t\in\{0,..,T\}
,
[
112
]
where
H_{t}
denotes the history at time
t
.

Fortunately, positivity is a function only of observed data—not counterfactual data; consequently, we can evaluate it and the extent to which there may be near-positivity violations, which occur when the exposure conditional probabilities are not strictly zero but are close to zero.
[
100
]
In the mixture setting of multiple continuous exposures and numerous covariates, satisfying positivity is exceptionally challenging,
[
120
]
made even more so when considering longitudinal estimands. Bao and Schomaker propose examining multidimensional density ratios to assess positivity, which we discuss in Section
E
.
[
120
]
However, here, we explore how one can use an alternative, cruder measure to evaluate extrapolation—the convex hull.

3.2
Using a convex hull to explore extrapolation

A
convex hull
of observed data across
n
variables is an n-dimensional polygon that is the smallest convex set that contains those data points.
[
101
]

In the exposure mixture setting, it is “the smallest polygon that contains all of the
observed exposure values…[i.e.,] the
region where exposure values are actually observed" (page 1394).
[
77
]
The convex hull is the border between observed or interpolated data and—beyond the border—extrapolation. Thus, regions inside the convex hull may have poor or no support in the observed data, but such interpolation is typically more palatable than extrapolation.
[
101
]

Statistical software that outputs a convex hull that is a function of the conditional exposure densities (instead of the marginal exposure values) does not yet exist and is an area of current work. Consequently, we use the marginal observed pesticide exposure values to define a 7-dimensional convex hull at each time
t
and assess the extent to which various shifts,
\mathrm{d}
, would lie inside or outside of this polygon.
[
77
]
Because the convex hull of the marginal exposure values represents a less restrictive space than the convex hull of the conditional exposure values,
[
77
]
using the marginal values would identify some but not all of the extrapolation issues. Although visualizing a 7-dimensional object is a near-impossible task, Figure
5
shows the convex hull of three of the seven pesticides for simplicity. Note that we calculate the 7-dimensional convex hull in practice.

Figure 5
:
Convex hull of 3 of the 7 pesticides at baseline: pyrethroids (X-axis), neonicotinoids (Z-axis), and manganese-containing fungicides (Y-axis).

We also examine, albeit crudely, the extent to which a shifted point is outside the convex hull. We consider two metrics: 1) the fraction proposed in Antonelli and Zigler,
[
77
]
called R, that represents the proportion of the shifted distance that lies outside the convex hull (
\frac{\text{distance[closest point on the convex hull to the desired shifted point, shifted point]}}{\text{distance[observed point, shifted point]}}
); and 2) the numerator of R. R is a relative measure; its numerator is an absolute measure. We add the second metric, because many individuals have low levels of exposure to several pesticides (Figure
2
). Consequently, a 20% reduction of a number close to zero would be another number close to zero, making the denominator of R close to zero. We may not care about a large R value in such cases, as the absolute value of the extrapolation would also be close to zero. The degree of extrapolation that someone is comfortable with is likely subjective and problem-specific. Antonelli and Zigler set R
<0.1
to represent a minimal amount of extrapolation.
[
77
]
We consider this same cut-off and use
<0.1
standardized units (exposure levels standardized to the [0,1] scale) for the absolute measure to represent a minimal amount of extrapolation, which translates to about half of a standard deviation of each pesticide.

We make available a Julia module that takes as input the exposure mixture and outputs the convex hull of its marginal distribution:
github.com/nt-williams/MTPConvexHull.jl
.

3.3
A warning about assessing extrapolation based only on the correlation matrix; how one can use the multidimensional convex hull of the exposure mixture instead

For example, consider a 20% reduction of glyphosate and paraquat at baseline:
\mathrm{d}^{A6,A7}(\mathbf{A}_{0})
, defined in Eq
8
. In our dataset, these two components are not very correlated with each other or other pesticide classes (Figure

), so such a shift seems unlikely to result in extrapolation.
However, examining the convex hull,
over 16% of the shifted exposure values lie outside the convex hull. Most of these—over 14% of all the shifted exposure values—have an R value
>0.1
.
But the absolute amount of extrapolation for many of these observations is low; only a single observation would extrapolate beyond 0.1 standardized units.

Consider a shift that may be more ill-advised. Say we would like to estimate the association between a 20% reduction in neonicotinoid exoposure at baseline and hypertension risk, i.e., a shift of
\mathrm{d}^{A4}(\mathbf{A}_{0})=(A_{1,0},A_{2,0},A_{3,0},0.8\times A_{4,0},A_{5,0},A_{6,0},A_{7,0})
. This would be ill-advised based on Figure

A_{1,0},A_{2,0},A_{3,0},A_{5,0}

over 36% of the shifted exposure values lying outside of the convex hull.

We can use the convex hull to define a shift in the exposure distribution that may be better supported by the data. Considering our example shift reduction in glyphosate and paraquat by 20% at each timepoint, we can redefine

If this is the case, we could redefine

\mathrm{d}^{\bar{A6},\bar{A7}}(\mathbf{A}_{t}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{d}^{\bar{A6},\bar{A7}}}_{t-1}))=\begin{cases}(A_{1,t}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{d}^{\bar{A6},\bar{A7}}}_{t-1}),...,0.8\times A_{6,t}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{d}^{\bar{A6},\bar{A7}}}_{t-1}),0.8\times A_{7,t}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{d}^{\bar{A6},\bar{A7}}}_{t-1}))&\text{ if }\\

7
Discussion

There remains much additional work to define real-world-relevant causal estimands whose estimates are based on real data. This includes formulating a convex hull that is a function of both the exposure mixture and relevant covariates, extending such a formulation to the longitudinal setting and natural values of treatment, as well as step-by-step guidance for how to use such diagnostics to redefine the causal estimand of interest. In addition, defining causal estimands and developing estimators that accommodate other study designs, like case-control designs, and accommodate interference or spatial dependence are other areas for future work that are especially relevant to environmental mixtures.

A.1
Cohort

Data come from the Center for the Health Assessment of Mothers and Children of Salinas (CHAMACOS) Maternal Cognition Study. This study builds on a longitudinal birth cohort of low-income, Mexican American families in the Salinas Valley, a California agricultural community.
[
102

Initial enrollment occurred in two waves. In 1999-2000, pregnant women attending prenatal care visits at one of six local clinics were screened for eligibility (
\geq
18 years old;
\leq
20 weeks gestation; English- or Spanish-speaking; and eligible for California’s Medicaid program, MediCal) and 1,130 women met eligibility criteria. Of these, 597 unique women enrolled into a prospective pregnancy cohort and 525 unique women continued participating with their liveborn index child(ren) at or after delivery. When index children turned 9, 348 unique women were still engaged in the study.
In 2009-2011, to refresh the cohort, additional mother-child dyads were recruited through primary care clinics, schools, churches, community service agencies, and by referral from existing participants. Women were eligible to enroll in CHAM2 if they were English- or Spanish-speaking and had a 9-year-old child for whom they had received prenatal care in the Salinas Valley. In addition, women needed to have qualified for MediCal during pregnancy and to have been at least 18 when their index child was born. At this point, 287 unique new women enrolled in the cohort, for a total of 635 participants. From that point, families proceeded to complete study roughly every two years between child ages 9 and 18, and to participate in COVID-specific data collection from 2020-2021.

This specific tutorial focuses on maternal participants with available pesticide exposure measures, blood pressure outcomes, and covariate information starting from the 2010-2013 visit (when index children were roughly age 10.5 years). 621 unique women attended any study visit between the 2010-2013 visit to the 2022-2024 visit. We excluded patients with baseline cancer, patients who never had sufficient exposure measurements (i.e. we excluded patients without at least one instance in which exposures were measured for at least 1 year due to insufficient data), and patients who experienced the outcome before the first exposure measurement. Our final analytic cohort contained N=407 unique women. We set each mother’s first observation period as the first period in which their exposures were measured for at least 1 year, allowing for mothers to be followed for up to 5 distinct periods. We treated the outcome as a survival-type outcome such that once a woman experiences hypertension, they were considered to have it for any subsequent outcome measurement periods.

A.2
Exposure

We used participant-reported addresses and California’s Pesticide Use Reporting (PUR) database to estimate average use (kg/year) of agricultural pesticides within 1 km of residences in the 2 years preceding each study visit. When index children were age 16 (2016-2018), all women were asked to recall their residential history in detail, starting at the date of conception of their index child, including move in and move out dates. Thereafter, residential history was collected prospectively at each follow-up visit.

The California Department of Pesticide Regulation’s PUR database contains application information including active ingredient, amount, date, and location,
[
103

which allows for the creation of geospatial measures of chronic pesticide exposure that have been found to correlate with community air samples.
[
104

105

We selected exposure periods of 2 years to estimate recent chronic exposure while minimizing potential temporal effects on the amount or type of pesticides used (e.g., seasonal or climatic changes, industry or regulatory changes). We selected a radius of 1 km because studies comparing measured pesticide concentrations in house dust or ambient air with proximity to agricultural pesticide use have observed the strongest correlations for pesticide use within 1 km.
[
104
,
105
,
106

The methods used to calculate residential proximity to agricultural pesticide use have been published previously.
[

Briefly, we geocoded residential addresses to obtain latitude and longitude coordinates, and we used a geographic information system to create a 1-km buffer radius around each residence. Modified treatment policies

A so-called
modified treatment policy
(MTP) is a type of hypothetical intervention on the exposure that can be a function of the
natural value of the exposure
, which is defined as the value an exposure would take at time-point
t
if an intervention on the exposure was discontinued right before time
t
.
[
110
,
111
,
112
,
113
]

Importantly, the modified treatment policy may affect a multi-component exposure in flexible way, possibly affecting each of the exposure components differently and may affect some not at all.

Consider the case of a single time-point, where exposure,
\mathbf{A}
is only measured at baseline
t=0
. In this case, the natural value of exposure is just the observed value of exposure. We denote a hypothetical intervention on
\mathbf{A}_{0}
that is only a function of the natural value of the exposure as
\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{A}_{0})
. For any discrete components of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
, we can intervene to set each component to a particular value, e.g.,
A_{j,0}=a_{j,0}
. Hypothetically intervening on non-discrete components of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
is more complex. An appealing strategy is to intervene on continuous
A_{j,0}

\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{A}_{0})=(A_{1,0}-\gamma_{1},A_{2,0}-\gamma_{2},\ldots A_{7,0}-\gamma_{7})

for an additive reduction in each pesticide
A_{i}
of
\gamma_{i}
, e.g., each
\gamma_{i}=10
units; and

\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{A}_{0})=(\delta_{1}A_{1,0},\delta_{2}A_{2,0},\ldots,\delta_{7}A_{7,0})

for a multiplicative reduction of
A_{i}
by
\delta_{i}=0.8
(20% reduction).

Now, consider the more complex scenario where exposure is time-varying and measured at multiple time-points:
\mathbf{A}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{\tau}
. Using notation, we can denote the natural value of exposure at time
t
as
\mathbf{A}_{t}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{d}}_{t-1})
where
\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{d}}_{t}
is recursively defined as
\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{A}_{t}({\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{d}}}))
and the overbar is used to denote the history of
\mathbf{A}
up to and including
t
. As an illustrative example, consider the scenario where
\mathbf{A}
is a single pesticide and
\tau=2
. When
t=0
, the natural value of the pesticide is simply the observed value:
\mathbf{A}_{0}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{d}}_{-1})=\mathbf{A}_{0}
. Then, an MTP that reduces the natural value of the pesticide by 20% would yield
\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{d}}_{0}=0.8\times\mathbf{A}_{0}
. Moving to time

Instead, it’s the hypothetical value we would have observed in a world where the observed value of the pesticide at the first time-point was replaced with replaced with the observed value of the first time-point reduced by 20%. That is,
\mathbf{A}_{1}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{d}}_{0})=\mathbf{A}_{1}(0.8\times\mathbf{A}_{0})
. Now,
\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{d}}_{1}=0.8\times\mathbf{A}_{1}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{d}}_{0})=0.8\times\mathbf{A}_{1}(0.8\times\mathbf{A}_{0})

Importantly, under the identification assumptions given in the main text, the distribution of the natural value of treatment at each time

Question: Over what population or subpopulation should we be estimating the relationship between the exposure mixture and outcome?

Recall the causal and statistical estimands from Eq
5

6

A fundamental principle of epidemiologic analysis involves restricting the analysis to those individuals who could plausibly have the exposure or treatment of interest and who are “at-risk" of the outcome. We estimate small effects of reducing pesticides at a single timepoint on hypertension risk at the final followup point, but estimate slightly larger effects of reducing pesticide exposure over 10 years on hypertension risk. In the single timepoint setting, we estimate that reducing all pesticide classes by 20% at baseline would be expected to reduce risk of hypertension by the last follow-up visit by 2.2 percentage points (95% CI: -1.0, 5.4) (see Figure
S2
. Reducing just the first 5 pesticide classes (risk reduction of 0.6 percentage points, 95% CI: -2.7%, 4.0%), the last 2 pesticide classes (risk reduction of 1.4 percentage points, 95% CI: -1.0%, 3.7%), or paraquat only (risk reduction of 0.4 percentage points, 95% CI: -1.4%, 2.3%) at baseline yielded smaller reductions in risk of hypertension to varying extents.
