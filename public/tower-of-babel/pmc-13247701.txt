What works to improve health and digital health literacy in disadvantaged groups: A policy-focused evidence brief
Authors: Sashika Harasgama, Amy Dehn Lunn, Danielle Lamb, Anna Gkiouleka, Helena Painter, Adnaan Ghanchi, John Ford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe policy challengeLow health literacy disproportionately affects disadvantaged populations and is associated with poorer health outcomes and reduced engagement with health services. Functional literacy skills, such as reading, writing, and numeracy, underpin health literacy and is shaped by cultural and socioeconomic factors. As digital health technologies become more widespread, digital health literacy is increasingly important; however, both low digital health literacy and digital exclusion can limit access to and engagement with health information and services, potentially exacerbating existing inequalities. Here, we review key policy-relevant evidence of what works to improve health and digital literacy for disadvantaged communities.Key evidence to inform policyThis evidence brief synthesises findings across four key population groups: people from low socioeconomic backgrounds, ethnic minority communities, older people with low digital literacy, and people with mental health conditions. Across populations, successful literacy interventions tailored information to the cultural and social needs of their target users, engaged and co-produced with communities in real-world settings, used audiovisual information to improve accessibility and focused on empowering users. Effectiveness in the context of intensity, i.e. contact hours, or intervention type was dependent on population groups studied. Evidence on digital health literacy interventions highlights the importance of usability, skills development and integration with existing health services, although consideration of digital health literacy in intervention design remains inconsistent. There is moderate evidence that health literacy interventions improve knowledge and skills, with mixed evidence for effects on behaviour and service use, and limited evidence for sustained improvements in clinical outcomes.Further considerations and implicationsHealth and digital health literacy are important drivers of health inequalities, influencing access to, engagement with and experience of health and care services. Interventions are most effective when delivered across individual, organisational and system levels. Policymakers should prioritise culturally tailored, accessible and community-based approaches, while also addressing structural barriers such as digital exclusion. Further research is needed to strengthen the evidence base, particularly on long-term clinical outcomes and implementation in diverse contexts.Keywords: Health literacy, Digital health literacy, Health inequalities

Current policy challenges

Low health literacy is associated with poor health outcomes [1]. Gradients in population health literacy are driven by functional literacy (i.e. reading, writing and numeracy skills), which in turn are influenced by socioeconomic factors such as education or social capital, and different cultural and linguistic backgrounds [2]. Recent OECD data revealed literacy inequity in countries like Denmark, England and Finland, which had larger gaps between its highest and lowest achievers in literacy, numeracy and adaptive problem-solving skills [3].

The advent of digital health technologies has also unveiled further health inequity, driven in part by poor access and understanding of digital tools across disadvantaged groups [4]. Digital literacy is frequently noted in policy and evidence as a driver of inequality, with barriers to digital engagement including design complexity, poor usability, language barriers, and fear of making mistakes [5,6].

Good health literacy usually refers to individuals’ ability to seek, access, critically understand and use health information in ways which promote and maintain good health for themselves and their communities [7]. Digital literacy is often defined as the ability to use technological tools to appropriately find, evaluate and communicate information [8]. The intersection between digital and health literacy (see Fig. 1) is digital health literacy i.e. having the skills to navigate and appraise health information available on the internet and social media platforms, as well as comfortably use digital health interventions [9].

Intersection between health and digital literacy.

Digital literacy is dependent on access to both the internet and technological devices, as well as digital interfaces that facilitate use for people with additional physical, cognitive and language needs. It is important to appreciate how both the digital divide as well as data poverty can affect effectiveness and feasibility of digital health literacy interventions.

Improving health and digital health literacy among disadvantaged groups has potential to narrow gaps in health outcomes and improve self-efficacy and self-management. Here we review the policy-relevant evidence for interventions which aim to improve health and digital health literacy.

Approach to collating evidence

The purpose of this evidence brief is to draw key lessons for policymakers from the most relevant and robust evidence, rather than reviewing all published literature on this broad topic. We drew evidence from three sources: 1) HEEC Living Evidence Maps, hosted in EPPI-Reviewer (n = 23), 2) additional evidence drawn from searches conducted in August 2024 for other briefs in this series (n = 59) and 3) snowball citation tracking of key reviews (n = 34).

Studies were eligible if they were evidence syntheses evaluating interventions to improve health and/or digital health literacy in disadvantaged groups. We excluded descriptive studies and digital health intervention studies without a literacy focus, studies from low- or middle-income countries, and non-English publications.

From 116 reviewed studies, 22 were prioritised based on recency, methodological quality, relevance to disadvantaged populations, and relevance to the UK policy context, with preference for studies published from 2014 onwards. Findings were synthesised narratively by population group, focusing on transferable principles of what works. Quantitative data were prioritised, with qualitative findings used to contextualise results. Where evidence permitted, outcomes are distinguished as proximal (knowledge and skills), intermediate (self-efficacy, service utilisation), or distal (e.g. HbA1c, blood pressure). Recommendations are graded by certainty of evidence using the GRADE framework.

Key evidence to inform policy

The evidence base consistently drew on health literacy as a social determinant of health [1], which explains why low health literacy is concentrated in disadvantaged groups and why interventions must address social and structural context alongside individual skills; and the eHealth Literacy framework [9], which identifies the skills needed to navigate digital health information. Together, they explain why effective interventions operate across individual, organisational and system levels, and why those addressing only one level are unlikely to achieve sustained impact.

Overall, there was consistent evidence that health literacy interventions improve proximal outcomes like patients' knowledge and skills, though evidence on intermediate and distal (clinical) outcomes such as improvements in self-efficacy, behaviour changes, weight, HbA1c and blood pressure remains limited and inconsistent. More successful interventions tailored information to users' needs, used multimedia tools, and focused on upskilling and empowering patients. The effectiveness of intervention format, delivery and intensity varied by population group.

Evidence is presented across four groups: people from low socioeconomic backgrounds, ethnic minority groups, older people with low digital literacy, and people with mental health conditions, with a further section on upskilling healthcare staff.

Interventions for people from low socioeconomic backgrounds

Several studies examined ways to increase health literacy for people with behavioural risk factors that lend to chronic diseases. While often not presenting data disaggregated to socioeconomic status, the burden of chronic disease and associated risk factors is often higher in lower socioeconomic groups [10]. Evidence also suggests the low health literacy is associated with higher deprivation [11], and that individuals from low socioeconomic groups are most likely to benefit from health literacy interventions [12,13].

A landmark review in 2012 reviewed 52 studies looking at health literacy interventions in primary care to improve chronic disease risk factors [14]. They found that group education was the most common intervention (n = 15), with nutrition (n = 34) and physical activity (n = 32) the most common risk factors targeted. The authors found that 73% of interventions improved health literacy, and 75% improved outcomes related to smoking, nutrition, alcohol consumption, physical activity, or weight. No single intervention type was found to be effective for all risk factors. For example, individual counselling may be more effective for smoking-related health literacy while multicomponent literacy interventions with written materials may be more effective for nutrition. Effectiveness was also not dependent on intensity of interventions, with less intensive interventions (<3 h of contact) being as successful in achieving health literacy outcomes as more intensive interventions (>8 h of contact), though the authors could not find reason for this. Health literacy interventions for smoking cessation were more likely to be effective in primary care settings, whereas diet and physical activity interventions were more likely to be successful in the community settings such as churches or neighbourhood centres.

A 2023 meta-analysis of 18 studies examined health literacy interventions for people with chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease; and found that interventions ranged from group education sessions, social media, educational videos, one-on-one education and mobile apps [15]. The authors found improvements in health literacy, mental well-being and self-efficacy. They did not find statistically significant improvements in Hb1Ac but found improvements in blood pressure control. Another review also found evidence that health literacy interventions can optimise blood pressure, including web-based interventions, skills training, interactive workshops, and counselling [16].

A 2020 review assessed the efficacy of health literacy interventions to improve modifiable health behaviours across various populations [17]. Based on 22 studies focusing on particularly women and ethnic minorities, they found consistent evidence for improvements in smoking prevention, physical activity, cancer screening, nutrition, and diet-related behaviours. However, it was unclear what the impact of interventions were across different socioeconomic groups, and the authors urged interventions be underpinned by behaviour change theoretical frameworks to be more effective.

Healthcare-based literacy interventions

A 2023 umbrella review of 16 systematic reviews explored the use of brochures, visual aids, digital tools, multimedia resources (videos), group and personalised counselling sessions for improving health literacy in hospitals [18]. The authors found that using pictograms reduced medication errors by caregivers significantly. Presenting information in multiple formats (i.e. verbal, written, digital), video-based educational interventions and face-to-face visits by health professionals led to greater knowledge retention.

A 2022 review mapped evidence on health literacy interventions to optimise emergency department and primary care use [19]. Based on 64 studies, the authors examined seven interventions: navigation tools directing patients to services (n = 7); written information about managing minor health problems (n = 17); person-delivered education (n = 5); written education complimented by person-delivered education (n = 17); multicomponent of written education, person-delivered education and mass media campaign (n = 5); self-triage (n = 9); and other interventions (n = 7). 19 out of 30 articles showed a reduction in emergency department attendances, and 16 out of 27 a reduction in general practice consultations, with the rest revealing no change in utilisation.

There is evidence of improved patient outcomes when existing services are tailored for people with low health literacy. A 2017 review of nine trials examined the impact of tailored health information such as reducing the grade level reading of material, online health literacy training, and support from a community health worker [20]. Five of nine studies showed improvements in areas such as hypertension knowledge, HbA1c, and depression severity, but three found no impact on knowledge or clinical outcomes. Visscher and colleagues included three studies in their review which explored tailoring information to different health literacy levels [21]. The authors found that narrated animations, illustrated text, and audio-visual information led to better knowledge retention.

Community-based literacy interventions

A comprehensive 2015 realist review synthesised 570 papers to examine how peer support through existing social networks and self-help groups can improve health literacy, of which 39 papers were directly attributable to a UK context [22]. The review found that peer support improves self-perception in people with low literacy, empowering them to adopt healthy behaviours and self-management attitudes [22].

The role of librarians to support health literacy was also studied. A review containing 55 studies found that of 31 described evidence of public librarians supporting group-based interventions in the community, and hospital librarians assisting clinical teams to understand the health literacy evidence, supporting the co-design of health literacy interventions and aiding research [23].

Digital health literacy interventions

Despite the proliferation of digital health interventions (DHIs), few have considered digital health literacy in their design. A 2020 review of 51 studies evaluating DHIs for different population groups including low-income, low literacy, and rural communities found that none explicitly reported consideration of digital health literacy in their development [24]. User co-production was minimal, and DHIs tended to solely focus on providing health information, rather than tailoring information to the cognitive and functional needs of patients to optimise engagement.

A 2023 review of 16 trials examined online self-management training, electronic health record training and remote patient monitoring for people with chronic conditions through a digital health literacy lens [25]. The authors found that interventions augmented with patients' electronic health records were most effective at improving digital health literacy.

Interventions for ethnic minority groups

We found limited evidence of health literacy interventions focusing on ethnic minority groups. A 2024 review of 55 health literacy interventions found that the most effective interventions for ethnic minority groups assessed the needs of participants prior to implementation through focus groups, used bilingual materials, and included professionals fluent in the native languages of patients [26]. The review highlighted the importance of distinguishing literacy needs from language proficiency.

A 2024 review of 50 studies examining health literacy interventions for disadvantaged groups included 11 focused on ethnic or racial minorities [27]. Interventions ranged from culturally relevant written material with or without videos, face-to-face teaching sessions led by peers or community leaders, Spanish comics known ‘fotonovelas’ or games, and church-based sermons. Six of 11 interventions were co-designed with members of the community. Most studies reported improvements in health literacy and had effects on decreasing stigma around certain conditions, but none reported on clinical outcomes.

A US review of health literacy interventions among Spanish-speaking populations examined 62 studies across both community and clinical settings [28]. Most studies (89%) reported improvements in health literacy, as well as improvements in clinical outcomes such as weight, HbA1c, and blood pressure. It found that the most successful strategies included professional liaison support, such as community health workers, and multimedia such as linguistically and culturally adapted fotonovelas.

Two reviews examined the role of DHIs for South Asian communities [29,30]. A scoping review of 15 articles found consistent evidence that older South Asian people particularly struggled to use digital interventions because of low English language proficiency, mistrust in digital technology, and gendered roles (with men more likely to adopt technology than women) [29]. Few studies quantitatively examined the impact of digital health literacy interventions. However, authors identified principles more likely to be associated with success, such as multilingual and culturally sensitive interventions in trusted community locations, for example faith organisations, and digital skill development sessions. Similarly, a realist review of DHIs to prevent cardiovascular disease and diabetes in South Asians and black minority ethnic groups, found barriers including personal beliefs, social networks, education and socioeconomic circumstances [30]. The authors found that user interfaces with pictographic instructions rather than text, and integration into existing health services, were key.

Interventions for older people with low digital literacy

A 2022 systematic review of 34 studies focusing on digital health literacy interventions in older people found that intervention effectiveness was limited by personal and environmental barriers, such as anxiety around technology and access to digital tools themselves. Successful interventions ideally were tailored to older peoples’ needs regardless of format or type, and addressed these obstacles by empowering them to navigate and understand online health information and technology [31].

A 2023 meta-analysis of seven studies, predominantly from the United States, explored digital health literacy interventions (three in-person and four online) in older people [32]. Overall, the authors found the interventions to have a positive effect on digital health literacy outcomes, but those with face-to-face teaching, based in a conceptual framework, and delivered over four weeks or more were more likely to be successful.

Interventions for people with mental health conditions

Digital mental health literacy (DMHL) interventions aim to enhance mental health through improved recognition, management and prevention [33]. DHML interventions have evolved to include more interactive platforms like mobile apps and social media. A 2024 meta-analysis of 144 studies found that DHML interventions had a large statistically significant effect on digital health literacy measures; and a small but statistically significant effect on mental health outcomes [33]. The authors found that DHML interventions were as effective as traditional face-to-face mental HL interventions. Moreover, DHML interventions augmented with another intervention (such as cognitive behavioural therapy) proved more effective than those delivered alone. The digital platform chosen, and frequency of use, did not appear to affect results.

Health literacy training for healthcare staff

A 2019 review of 28 studies explored health literacy training interventions delivered to healthcare students to help them communicate better with patients with low health literacy [34]. Interventions ranged from classroom-based teaching to community placements. While studies were generally of low quality, the most successful interventions offered multiple training sessions, developed within real-world settings, and used integrative approaches to health literacy knowledge and skills development with consideration for cultural competency. A further review of 17 studies examined health literacy champions to increase organisational awareness or influence strategic planning [35]. Only five described how they trained champions, with two high-quality studies finding positive impacts, such as more patients and family receiving information about their health condition.

Limitations and further equity considerations

The rapid review methodology of this brief means that relevant studies may have been missed, and streamlined search and screening processes increase the risk of selection and publication bias. While formal risk-of-bias assessments were not undertaken, we aimed to mitigate them by drawing on existing living evidence maps, conducting snowball citation tracking, and prioritising reviews with the best quality.

Comparing across studies was challenging due to methodological heterogeneity, variability in intervention design, and inconsistent definitions of health literacy. Most evidence addressed proximal outcomes (knowledge and skills) with limited and inconsistent evidence on long-term clinical outcomes or sustained behaviour change.

Most evidence comes from high-income settings in North America, Europe and Australasia, limiting generalisability to lower-income settings, though having some relevance to the UK context. While digital interventions may improve access for some, digital exclusion risks widening inequalities for others; policymakers should consider local digital access, language needs and community capacity, and offer non-digital or blended delivery options alongside digital tools.
