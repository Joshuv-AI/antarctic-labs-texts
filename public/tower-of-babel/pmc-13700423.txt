ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophage subset drives intraplaque hemorrhage via the SPP1-ITGB1-mediated angiogenic axis in carotid atherosclerosis
Authors: Ning Wang, Le Zhang, Chenqin Xu, Can Hu, Yudong Fang, Jian Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntraplaque hemorrhage (IPH) is a key driver of carotid plaque rupture and ischemic events. However, the molecular mechanisms linking immune infiltration to pathological neovascularization in the IPH microenvironment remain poorly defined. This study aimed to elucidate the critical role of the ADAM8-SPP1-ITGB1 signaling axis in driving this ischemic phenotype.MethodsTranscriptomic analyses were performed on bulk (n = 230) and single-cell (n = 15) RNA-seq datasets and included weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA), survival analysis, cell clustering, virtual knockout, and cell–cell communication. In vivo, ApoE knockout mice fed a high-fat diet were evaluated using ultrasound, H&E staining, immunofluorescence, and ELISA. In vitro, following siRNA-mediated ADAM8 knockdown, the lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis of ox-LDL-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages were assessed. HUVECs were subjected to lentivirus-mediated ITGB1 knockdown and treated with recombinant SPP1 to assess cellular proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis.ResultsADAM8 was identified as a robust diagnostic (AUC = 0.935) and prognostic (HR = 2.659) biomarker for the ischemic phenotype of IPH. Single-cell clustering and metabolic analysis revealed a distinct ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophage subset characterized by active glycolysis and a hypoxic state. Virtual knockout analyses, corroborated by in vitro knockdown experiments, confirmed that ADAM8 drives SPP1 expression. Cell-cell communication analysis indicated that this macrophage subpopulation is linked to angiogenic endothelial cells via the SPP1-ITGB1 axis. In vivo, carotid atherosclerotic mice exhibited enrichment of ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophages and elevated serum SPP1 levels. In vitro, ADAM8 downregulation in RAW264.7 macrophages alleviated lipid accumulation, ROS generation, apoptosis, and inflammation. Furthermore, recombinant SPP1 significantly promoted HUVEC proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. Importantly, these proangiogenic effects were completely abolished by lentivirus-mediated ITGB1 knockdown.ConclusionsADAM8+SPP1+ macrophages promote IPH and plaque instability via the SPP1-ITGB1 angiogenic axis and are potential therapeutic targets for high-risk atherosclerosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08627-8.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on society and health care systems [1]. Epidemiological evidence highlights carotid atherosclerosis as a significant etiology of ischemic stroke, with approximately 20% of ischemic events originating directly from distal embolization caused by carotid plaque rupture [2, 3].Consequently, the discovery of novel molecular targets to stabilize these vulnerable plaques and the development of precision therapeutic interventions have become urgent needs for effective stroke prevention.

In recent decades, significant progress in medicinal chemistry, targeted drug design, and advanced nanocarrier delivery systems have revolutionized treatment paradigms across various complex conditions, offering profound insights into modulating pathological microenvironments [4–7]. Currently, the clinical pharmacological management of atherosclerosis relies heavily on systemic lipid-lowering agents and antithrombotic therapies [8, 9]. While these foundational medicinal therapies effectively reduce systemic lipid burdens and overall mortality, a significant residual risk of ischemic events persists. This effect occurs primarily because current systemic drugs often fail to specifically halt or reverse localized structural deterioration, including pathological angiogenesis and intraplaque hemorrhage, within established high-risk atherosclerotic lesions [10]. Therefore, directly targeting the localized pathological features of the plaque itself is essential.

Vulnerable plaques prone to rupture typically exhibit specific pathological features, including a thinned fibrous cap, significant inflammatory leukocyte infiltration, depletion of smooth muscle cells, enhanced proteolytic activity, an enlarged lipid necrotic core [11], and prominent intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH). Among these characteristics, IPH is regarded as one of the most critical predictors of plaque instability [12, 13]. The formation of IPH primarily stems from the microrupture of intraplaque neovessels. These neovessels, generated to meet the increasing metabolic demands of the plaque, are often structurally compromised and characterized by fragile basement membranes and loose endothelial junctions, rendering them highly prone to leakage [14, 15]. Crucially, IPH is not merely a consequence of vascular fragility but also a potent driver that accelerates plaque progression [16]. Hemoglobin and iron released from the lysis of erythrocytes that infiltrate the plaque core serve as strong oxidants and proinflammatory mediators, activating resident macrophages [17]. Upon extensive uptake of lipids and heme, these macrophages differentiate into foam cells and secrete copious amounts of inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and leading to apoptosis and tissue destruction [11]. This self-amplifying “hemorrhage–inflammation–apoptosis” cycle continuously undermines structural stability, ultimately precipitating plaque rupture and clinical events [17].

Within this complex microenvironment, the crosstalk between inflammatory cells and angiogenesis plays a decisive role [18]. Breakthroughs in oncology have provided critical insights: glycolysis-activated macrophages can promote pathological tumor angiogenesis through the secretion of SPP1 [19]. SPP1 is a multifunctional matricellular protein and proinflammatory cytokine widely involved in immune cell migration, tissue remodeling, and angiogenesis and act as a key molecular bridge between chronic inflammation and pathological repair [20]. We hypothesized that within the hypoxic and inflammatory microenvironment of atherosclerotic plaques, specific macrophage subsets may actively promote the formation of fragile neovessels through similar mechanisms [21]. Therefore, elucidating the core role of this “macrophage‒endothelial interaction” in driving IPH is of paramount importance for understanding the mechanisms of plaque instability [22].To validate this hypothesis and identify key regulatory molecules, we integrated bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to systematically analyze transcriptomic changes in carotid IPH tissues [23] and cellular heterogeneity in symptomatic plaques. Through integrative multidimensional data analysis, we identified a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 8 (ADAM8) as a key gene associated with IPH and identified a potential core pathogenic cell population: ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophages. ADAM8 is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein with proteolytic activity [24]. While previous studies have confirmed its integral involvement in tumor cell invasion, neutrophil transendothelial migration, and inflammatory regulation [25, 26], its specific role in maintaining plaque stability remains undefined.

In the present study, we explored the function of ADAM8 in IPH, focusing on the proinflammatory and proangiogenic effects of the ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophage subset. Our results demonstrate that ADAM8 inhibition effectively reduces the release of inflammatory cytokines, decreases macrophage SPP1 secretion, and promotes apoptosis. Crucially, we found that macrophage-derived SPP1 directly promotes neovascularization by binding to the integrin receptor complex ITGB1 on endothelial cells; conversely, inhibition of ITGB1 effectively blocks this pathological angiogenic process. These findings reveal a novel ADAM8-SPP1-ITGB1-mediated macrophage‒endothelial communication axis. Targeting this signaling axis holds promise for disrupting the vicious “hemorrhage–inflammation–apoptosis” cycle, offering a prospective therapeutic strategy for stabilizing plaques and preventing ischemic stroke.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

Four transcriptomic datasets related to carotid atherosclerosis were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. GSE163154 comprises 43 carotid plaque transcriptomes, including 27 IPH samples and 16 non-IPH samples [27]. GSE21545 provides the transcriptomic profiles of 126 carotid plaques along with a mean follow-up of 44 months, and 25 ischemic events were recorded [28]. GSE28829 includes 13 early-stage and 16 advanced-stage plaque samples [29]. GSE43292 contains 32 paired plaque cap (PC) and adjacent plaque (AC) samples from the same patients [30].

For single-cell analysis, two scRNA-seq datasets were included. GSE253903 contains 12 carotid plaque samples, which are classified into 6 symptomatic and 6 asymptomatic plaques on the basis of the occurrence of stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) within 6 months prior to surgery [31]. GSE155512 includes 3 plaque samples (1 symptomatic and 2 asymptomatic). Samples from both datasets were merged and categorized into symptomatic and asymptomatic groups for downstream integration and analysis [32].

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis of the GSE163154 dataset was performed using the limma R package. A threshold of |log2Fold Change (FC)| > 1 was applied to define biologically significant expression changes. Genes whose log2FC was > 1 were classified as upregulated, whereas those whose log2FC was < -1 were classified as downregulated. The genes identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between IPH and non-IPH samples were visualized using a volcano plot.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA)

To identify gene modules associated with the IPH phenotype, a weighted gene coexpression network was constructed using the WGCNA R package [33]. Briefly, the expression matrix of IPH and non-IPH samples from GSE163154 was extracted, filtered for genes with low expression, and normalized. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between gene pairs. A hierarchical clustering dendrogram was constructed based on the adjacency matrix and topological overlap matrix (TOM). Gene modules were identified using the dynamic tree cut method. Highly correlated modules were merged based on the basis of module eigengene (ME) dissimilarity threshold of 0.25. Finally, the Pearson correlation between MEs and the IPH clinical trait was calculated to identify key modules significantly associated with intraplaque hemorrhage. The intersection between module genes and DEGs was utilized to pinpoint key phenotype-related genes.

Functional enrichment analysis

Genes from the key coexpression modules significantly associated with clinical traits were extracted for functional annotation. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package [34]. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Survival analysis

To evaluate the prognostic value of the key IPH-related genes identified in the previous step, we utilized the GSE21545 cohort, which includes the transcriptomic profiles of 126 patients with a mean follow-up of 44 months. During the observation period, a total of 25 ischemic events, comprising 18 ischemic strokes and 7 myocardial infarctions, were recorded. Kaplan‒Meier analysis was performed to determine any association between the key genes and the incidence of these clinical events.

Diagnostic efficacy evaluation (ROC analysis)

To assess the diagnostic potential of ADAM8 in distinguishing unstable plaque phenotypes, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted across three independent datasets. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify the discriminatory ability of ADAM8.

Immune infiltration analysis

Samples were stratified into ADAM8-high and ADAM8-low-expression groups on the basis of the median expression level of ADAM8. The CIBERSORT [35] algorithm was applied to quantify the relative proportions of 22 infiltrating immune cell types in each sample. Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis were subsequently performed between the two groups to elucidate the effects of ADAM8 levels on the immune microenvironment and associated biological pathways.

Single-cell quality control and integration

scRNA-seq data were analyzed using the Seurat package (v4.0) [36]. Strict quality control was applied to filter out low-quality cells on the basis of the following criteria: (1) 200 < nFeature_RNA < 4500; (2) mitochondrial content < 10%; and (3) hemoglobin content < 3%. Genes detected in fewer than 3 cells were also excluded. Following filtration, the top 4500 highly variable genes were identified for normalization. The datasets were then integrated using standard Seurat anchors to correct for batch effects, followed by principal component analysis and SNN-based clustering.

Cell clustering and annotation

Cell clustering was performed using the graph-based clustering approach implemented in Seurat. The SNN graph was used as the input for FindClusters (resolution = 0.8), utilizing the Louvain algorithm. Nonlinear dimensionality reduction was performed using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) via RunUMAP to visualize cell clusters in two-dimensional space [37]. Cell types were manually annotated on the basis of canonical marker genes and referenced to a published human atherosclerotic plaque single-cell atlas [38]. Major cell types were defined by the following canonical gene markers: macrophage (CD68, CD163, C1QA, C1QB, and C1QC), monocyte (CD14, FCGR3A, and FCN1), neutrophil (CSF3R, S100A8, and S100A9), B cells (CD79A, CD79B, MS4A1, and CD37), plasma cells (JCHAIN, XBP1, IGHG1, and IGHG3), mast cells (CPA3, TPSAB1, and TPSB2), dendritic cells (CD1C and CLEC10A), T cells (CD247, CD3E, and CD3D), natural killer cells (NKG7, KLRD1, GNLY, and GZMK), endothelial cells (PECAM1 and VWF), smooth muscle cells (MYH11, TAGLN, MYL9, and ACTA2), fibroblast (COL1A1, LUM, and DCN), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (LILRA4 and IRF8), and cycling cells (MKI67 and TOP2A).

Macrophage subclustering and cell‒cell communication

To investigate the specific role of ADAM8 in macrophages, the macrophage cluster was subsetted from the global population for further analysis. Macrophage subclusters were identified on the basis of gene expression similarity without enforcing functional subclassification a priori. Intercellular communication was analyzed using the CellChat R package [39]. A signaling network was constructed on the basis of the integrated expression matrix to infer and visualize potential ligand‒receptor interactions between macrophage subpopulations and other cell types.

Trajectory and differentiation potential analysis

To reconstruct the developmental dynamics of cell populations, two complementary computational strategies were used. First, single-cell trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle 2 (v2.30.1) [40] Dimensionality reduction was conducted using the DDRTree method, and cells were ordered along a pseudotime trajectory using orderCells. Differential gene expression along the trajectory branches was visualized using plot_genes_branched_heatmap. In addition, the cellular differentiation potential was quantified using CytoTRACE (v1.1.0) [41]. CytoTRACE scores were calculated on the basis of gene count signatures, where higher scores indicate a less differentiated state and lower scores indicate a more differentiated state. CytoTRACE scores were projected onto UMAP and Monocle trajectory plots to independently validate the direction of differentiation.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

To evaluate functional differences between cell subsets, GSVA was performed using the GSVA R package. Pathway enrichment scores were calculated for each single cell using gene sets retrieved from MSigDB. GSVA scores were compared between groups to assess differential pathway activity.

High-dimensional WGCNA (hdWGCNA)

The hdWGCNA R package was used to construct a gene coexpression network. Functional enrichment analysis of the identified modules was performed using Enrichr.

Virtual knockout analysis (scTenifoldKnk)

To mechanistically explore the regulatory role of ADAM8, we performed in silico gene perturbation analysis using the scTenifoldKnk R package [42]. A virtual knockout of the target gene was simulated, and the top 50 genes with the most significant perturbations in regulatory relationships were identified. Functional enrichment analysis was subsequently performed on these perturbed genes.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Approval ID: 2025234). Male Apoe−/− mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under standard specific pathogen-free conditions at 25 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice were randomly assigned to a control group (standard diet, n = 6) or a model group (high-fat diet, n = 6) and maintained on their respective diets for 8 weeks. Carotid artery tissues were collected, fixed, and processed for paraffin embedding, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and immunofluorescence (IF). Serum levels of SPP1 were analyzed using an ELISA kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Ultrasound imaging

Carotid morphology and hemodynamics were assessed via high-frequency ultrasound. B-mode and color Doppler imaging were used to quantify lumen diameter and blood flow disturbances.

Cell culture

The murine macrophage line RAW264.7 and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from ATCC. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. HUVECs were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM) supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS). All the cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Macrophage transfection and model construction

Small interfering RNAs targeting Adam8 (siAdam8) and negative control siRNA (siNC) were synthesized by GenePharma (Suzhou, China). RAW264.7 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the cells were treated with 80 µg /mL oxidized LDL (ox-LDL) for 24 h [43]. The cells were divided into four groups: siNC, siNC + ox-LDL, siAdam8, and siAdam8 + ox-LDL.

Nile red staining assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 105 cells per well. After adhering, the cells were treated with ox-LDL for 24 h to induce foam cell formation. The cells were subsequently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Following fixation, the cells were stained using a Nile Red Staining Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the stained cells were visualized and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometry for apoptosis

The apoptosis of RAW264.7 cells was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells were harvested, resuspended in binding buffer, and incubated with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI). The percentage of apoptotic cells was analyzed using a flow cytometer.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

Intracellular ROS levels in RAW264.7 cells were measured using a DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After treatment, the cells were incubated with DCFH-DA, and the fluorescence intensity, which reflects the degree of oxidative stress, was detected using flow cytometry.

Endothelial transfection and treatment

HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. To establish stable ITGB1-knockdown cell lines, cells were infected with lentiviral vectors encoding short hairpin RNA targeting ITGB1 (shITGB1) or a negative control sequence (shNC), which were constructed by and purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Lentiviral infection was performed at a multiplicity of infection of 100 in the presence of polybrene (5 µg/mL, GeneChem) to increase transduction efficiency. After 24 h of infection, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium. At 48 h post-infection, stable transfectants were selected by culturing cells in medium supplemented with 1 µg/mL [44] puromycin for approximately one week, until noninfected control cells were completely eliminated. The knockdown efficiency was subsequently verified by qRT‒PCR. The cells were subsequently treated with recombinant SPP1 (1 µg/mL) protein (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The HUVECs were divided into four groups: shNC, shNC + SPP1, shITGB1, shITGB1 + SPP1.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates. The grouped cells were subsequently subjected to the indicated treatments for 24 h. The cell viability was then evaluated by adding CCK-8 reagent and measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

EdU proliferation assay

HUVEC proliferation was assessed using a 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). DNA synthesis was visualized by fluorescence staining with EdU, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and the proportion of proliferating cells was determined by calculating the ratio of EdU-positive cells to total DAPI-positive nuclei using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs were seeded onto Matrigel-coated (Corning) 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 6 h to allow tube formation. Capillary-like structures were imaged, and total tube length and branching points were quantified using ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

HUVECs were grown to confluence in 6-well plates. A scratch wound was created using a 200 µL pipette tip. Images were captured at 0 h, 12 h and 24 h. Cell migration was quantified by calculating the wound closure area using ImageJ.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from harvested cells using TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression was subsequently analyzed by qRT‒PCR using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). In murine RAW264.7 cells, the mRNA levels of Adam8, Spp1, Il-1β, Il6, Tnf-α, Jak2 and Stat3 were measured and normalized to those of β-actin. For HUVECs, the expression of ITGB1 was quantified, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The specific sequences of primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10). Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

To identify key genes, we intersected the module-specific genes with the DEGs. Venn diagrams illustrated the overlap between the blue module and upregulated DEGs (Fig. 2E) as well as downregulated DEGs (Fig. 2F). The union of these overlapping genes was defined as the key IPH-related gene signature. Identification of ADAM8 as a critical prognostic risk factor and diagnostic biomarker

To determine the clinical relevance of the identified key gene signature, we performed survival analysis using follow-up data from the GSE21545 dataset. This analysis revealed 12 candidate genes (Table S5) significantly associated with the incidence of ischemic events (P < 0.05). Representative survival curves for ADAM8, CRISPLD1, and SFRP1 are shown in Fig. 3A–C, while the results for the remaining 9 genes are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Crucially, while genes such as CRISPLD1 and SFRP1 appeared to be protective, ADAM8 exhibited a hazard ratio (HR = 2.659) > 1, indicating that it as a prominent risk factor for plaque rupture and cerebrovascular events. Consequently, ADAM8 was selected as the core target for further investigation.

To validate the expression pattern of ADAM8, we analyzed its transcriptomic levels across multiple datasets representing diverse plaque phenotypes (GSE163154, GSE28829 and GSE43292). ADAM8 expression was consistently and significantly upregulated in high-risk plaque samples (including IPH, advanced, or symptomatic plaques) compared with control tissue samples across all cohorts (Fig. 3D–F). Furthermore, ROC curve analysis was conducted to assess its diagnostic value. ADAM8 demonstrated high discriminatory power in distinguishing unstable plaques, with AUC values of 0.9352, 0.8173, and 0.7832 in the respective datasets (Fig. 3G-I). These results strongly suggest that ADAM8 is not only a predictor of poor prognosis but also a robust diagnostic marker for high-risk plaques.

ADAM8 remodels the plaque immune microenvironment and activates inflammatory pathways

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying ADAM8-mediated plaque instability, we stratified the plaque samples into ADAM8-high and ADAM8-low expression groups on the basis of the median expression value. Differential expression analysis revealed DEGs between the two groups, as shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 4A) and heatmap (Fig. 4B). Functional enrichment analysis of these ADAM8-related DEGs revealed significant enrichment in biological processes associated with the positive regulation of the response to external stimuli, leukocyte migration, and external encapsulating structure organization (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that high ADAM8 expression triggers a transcriptomic program that favors inflammation and tissue remodeling.

Single-cell data integration, quality control, and batch effect correction

To ensure the reliability of the downstream analyses, we performed rigorous quality control on the merged scRNA-seq datasets. Initially, the distribution of nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, and percent.mt varied across samples (Figure S2A). After filtering criteria was applied, low-quality cells were excluded, resulting in a uniform distribution of quality metrics across all the retained cells (Figure S2B). Additionally, the proportion of RBCs was assessed and minimized to prevent contamination (Figure S2C). Significant batch effects were observed between different samples and datasets prior to integration. We used the Harmony algorithm to correct for these technical variations. As shown in the UMAP projections, cells from different samples were well integrated after correction, confirming the effective removal of batch effects while preserving biological heterogeneity (Figure S2D). The dimensionality of the dataset was determined using an elbow plot, and the top 10 principal components (PCs) were selected for clustering (Figure S2E). Unsupervised clustering with a resolution of 0.8 identified 23 distinct cell clusters. These clusters were annotated on the basis of the expression of canonical marker genes, as shown in the dot plot (Figure S2F). In vivo validation: accumulation of ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophages in atherosclerotic plaques

To validate the clinical relevance of our bioinformatic findings in vivo, we established a carotid atherosclerosis mouse model. High-frequency Doppler ultrasound imaging was used to assess vascular morphology and hemodynamics. Compared with the control group, the model group exhibited significant vascular occlusion and hemodynamic disturbances in the carotid artery (Fig. 9A-B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a marked reduction in the lumen diameter in the model group (Fig. 9C). Histological assessment via H&E staining further corroborated the imaging findings. While the control arteries displayed a smooth and thin intima (Fig. 9D), the model group showed extensive neointimal hyperplasia, inflammatory cell infiltration, and significant plaque deposition (Fig. 9E). Statistical quantification revealed a significantly greater plaque area ratio in the model group than in the control group (Fig. 9F). Crucially, to verify the presence of the specific ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophage subset identified by our single-cell analysis, we performed triple-immunofluorescence staining on plaque sections. In the model group, we observed robust colocalization of CD68, ADAM8, and SPP1 within the plaque lesions (Fig. 9H), whereas this specific costaining was negligible in the control group (Fig. 9G). These findings provide direct in vivo evidence that ADAM8+SPP1+ macrophages are abundantly enriched in atherosclerotic lesions. Furthermore, ELISA demonstrated that serum SPP1 levels were significantly elevated in the model group (Fig. 9I), suggesting that local plaque inflammation is associated with systemic upregulation of SPP1.

Discussion

IPH represents a defining feature of atherosclerotic plaque instability and acts as a critical trigger for ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction [12]. Despite significant advances in lipid-lowering therapies, specific interventions targeting IPH and its associated pathological microenvironmental remodeling are currently lacking. In this study, by integrating large-scale bulk transcriptomics data with single-cell resolution data, we mapped the high-resolution immune-vascular microenvironment of IPH and revealed a novel macrophage‒endothelial interaction axis driven by ADAM8. To explicitly highlight the mechanistic and translational novelties of our study, our core findings are threefold: First, ADAM8 serves as a critical upstream driver and a robust prognostic biomarker of IPH. Second, single-cell analysis revealed a distinct, highly proinflammatory, and metabolically active ADAM8⁺SPP1⁺ macrophage subset that acts as a central destabilizer of the plaque microenvironment; and third, this specific macrophage subset orchestrates structural failure by activating the ITGB1 receptor on local structural cells via paracrine SPP1 signaling, thereby inducing pathological angiogenesis and biomechanical plaque instability.

ADAM8 is an upstream regulator of plaque instability

Members of the ADAM family typically function as molecular scissors involved in membrane protein cleavage and cell adhesion [45]; however, the specific role of ADAM8 in atherosclerosis remains unknown. Our study establishes ADAM8 as a critical upstream regulator of IPH. Unlike previous studies that focused primarily on matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that degrade the fibrous caps [11], we found that high ADAM8 expression is significantly associated with the infiltration of immune cells, particularly macrophages. In vitro experiments confirmed that ADAM8 knockdown significantly inhibited macrophage inflammatory responses and foam cell formation. These findings suggest that ADAM8 may facilitate the recruitment and retention of inflammatory cells in plaques, thereby initiating a local inflammatory cascade. This finding aligns with its known function in cleaving adhesion molecules to facilitate leukocyte extravasation [46]. Importantly, survival analysis revealed ADAM8 as the only independent risk factor with a hazard ratio > 1 among the candidate genes, and ROC analysis confirmed its high diagnostic accuracy. These results indicate that ADAM8 is not only a mechanistic driver but also a promising sentinel biomarker for high-risk plaques.

The ADAM8 + SPP1+ macrophages functions as destabilizers of the plaque microenvironment

The application of single-cell sequencing allowed us to move beyond traditional M1/M2 classification and investigate macrophage heterogeneity in greater detail. We identified a distinct macrophage subpopulation characterized by the specific coexpression of ADAM8 and SPP1. Metabolic pathway analysis revealed pronounced activation of glycolysis and hypoxia-responsive programs, which is consistent with the “Warburg effect” metabolic reprogramming characteristic of inflammatory macrophages [47], allowing us to functionally define this cluster as SPP1⁺ inflammatory foam macrophages. This subset is mechanistically linked to established risk factors for IPH as these macrophages function as integrators of systemic and microenvironmental stress. Elevated circulating ox-LDL drives excessive lipid uptake and foam cell formation, promoting the initial polarization of SPP1⁺ macrophages [48]. Concurrently, progressive plaque growth and microvascular insufficiency generate a hypoxic niche that stabilizes HIF-1α and markedly amplifies SPP1 transcription [19, 49]. Intracellularly, our virtual knockout analysis suggests that ADAM8 acts as a master upstream regulator rather than a passive coexpression marker. By orchestrating a tissue-remodeling transcriptional network, ADAM8 sustains high SPP1 expression, likely via activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway [50].Once established, this subset engages in extensive intercellular crosstalk, functioning as a central communication hub within the plaque. On one hand, SPP1 secretion promotes the recruitment and retention of T lymphocytes, reinforcing a chronic inflammatory circuit [51]. On the other hand, macrophage-derived SPP1 profoundly alters adjacent vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), suppressing cholesterol efflux and promoting VSMC-derived foam cell formation while simultaneously inducing phenotypic switching and maladaptive collagen matrix remodeling [52]. This coordinated multicellular interaction provides a mechanistic basis for how the ADAM8⁺SPP1⁺ subset precipitates IPH. The dual-expression phenotype renders this population uniquely destabilizing as secreted SPP1 drives aberrant angiogenesis by stimulating endothelial proliferation and migration, leading to the formation of structurally fragile and leaky neovessels, whereas membrane-bound ADAM8 facilitates extracellular matrix degradation and impairs effective efferocytosis [53]. The combined effects expand the necrotic core [54] and weaken the structural scaffolding supporting newly formed vasa vasorum, predisposing them to rupture. Microvascular rupture results in erythrocyte extravasation and iron deposition within the plaque, intensifying oxidative stress and apoptotic cell death [55]. The ensuing tissue injury exacerbates hypoxia, further stabilizing HIF-1α and amplifying SPP1 expression, thereby perpetuating pathological angiogenesis. Collectively, these processes establish a self-amplifying circuit linking lipid overload, hypoxia, angiogenesis, and hemorrhage, positioning the ADAM8⁺SPP1⁺ macrophage subset as a central metabolic–inflammatory amplifier of plaque destabilization.

The SPP1-ITGB1 axis: a molecular bridge for pathological angiogenesis

Neovessels within plaques are typically composed of immature endothelial cells lacking pericyte coverage and possessing loose endothelial junctions, making them highly prone to leakage and subsequent hemorrhage [56, 57]. Our cell–cell communication analysis and in vitro HUVEC assays confirmed that macrophage-derived SPP1 directly engages endothelial ITGB1 receptors to promote rapid migration and tube formation. In terms of the specific downstream mechanisms of this axis, SPP1 binding to ITGB1 typically triggers canonical mechanotransduction cascades. These cascades, such as the FAK/Src or PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, subsequently drive actin cytoskeletal reorganization and aggressive endothelial sprouting [58]. However, because this robust angiogenic response occurs within a highly inflammatory and ADAM8-degraded matrix milieu, the resulting neovessels fail to establish mature VE-cadherin tight junctions and lack essential pericyte recruitment, rendering them intrinsically leaky [59].

Furthermore, the downstream consequences of ITGB1 activation extend profoundly into biomechanical vulnerability. As previously discussed, parallel SPP1-ITGB1 signaling in adjacent VSMCs suppresses structural matrix production. The ultimate downstream effect of this axis is therefore a lethal dual structural failure leading to attenuation of the fibrous cap combined with the breakdown of the defective endothelial barrier. Together, this process drastically lowers the mechanical threshold of the entire lesion, leading to catastrophic microvascular rupture under hemodynamic shear stress [60]. Crucially, our single-cell analysis revealed a specific endothelial subset (E2) characterized by high ITGB1 expression and enriched oxidative stress signatures. This result highlights how the SPP1-ITGB1 axis acts not only as a pro-angiogenic stimulus but also as a targeted molecular bridge that translates immune infiltration into ultimately vascular structural failure.

Clinical Implications and limitations

This study has significant translational value, particularly compared with current reports that focus largely on late-stage lipid reduction or broad-spectrum antiangiogenesis effects. First, from a diagnostic perspective, the extracellular domain of ADAM8 can be shed into the circulation. This aligns with our observation of elevated serum SPP1 levels in our animal model, suggesting the promising potential for liquid biopsy applications. While traditional inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP lack plaque-specific diagnostic power, soluble ADAM8 and SPP1 could serve as specific sentinels for vulnerable plaques [61]. Second, therapeutically, blocking peptides or neutralizing antibodies targeting the SPP1-ITGB1 binding site could represent a novel, precise strategy. Compared with broad anti-VEGF therapies, which have shown limited efficacy in cardiovascular trials [62, 63], targeting this specific axis may suppress pathological neovascularization within the inflammatory core while sparing physiological endothelial maintenance.

However, our study has several limitations. First, regarding the control of confounding variables, although our bioinformatic pipelines utilized rigorous normalization and batch-effect correction algorithms, the retrospective nature of the transcriptomic datasets inherently limits our ability to fully control for clinical confounders, such as variations in patient age, sex, metabolic comorbidities, and concurrent medication histories. Future large-scale, propensity-score matched prospective cohorts are necessary to validate these findings. Second, while we validated the mechanism in animal models and in vitro, mouse atherosclerotic plaques rarely undergo spontaneous rupture or hemorrhage, limiting their ability to fully mimic the complex pathology of human IPH. Third, regarding the intracellular mechanisms, although our PCR data suggest that ADAM8 may regulate SPP1 expression via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, this hypothesis relies on transcriptional evidence and lacks further validation through protein-level assays or inhibitor studies. While we identified ITGB1 as a receptor, the specific downstream kinase signaling cascades triggered in endothelial cells were not fully elucidated in this study. Finally, our study focused primarily on the effect of SPP1 on endothelial cells; however, SPP1 may also act on VSMCs to promote aberrant proliferation and migration, an interaction network that warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified an ADAM8⁺SPP1⁺ macrophage subset that promotes intraplaque hemorrhage by activating pathological endothelial angiogenesis through the SPP1–ITGB1 signaling axis. These findings reveal an immune–vascular coupling mechanism that links macrophage activation to pathological neovascularization within atherosclerotic plaques. In addition to serving as a diagnostic and prognostic marker, ADAM8 may represent a promising therapeutic target for modulating macrophage-driven angiogenesis and reducing plaque vulnerability.
