The role of radiotherapy in extensive-stage small cell lung cancer: insights from treatment failure patterns in the era of immunotherapy
Authors: Ya Zhang, Ya Zeng, Yipengchen Yin, Wenqing Zhang, Tianyu Li, Tiaoyan Jiang, Xiaojun Zheng, Zhongdan Yu, Xuwei Cai, Qin Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe therapeutic advantage of radiotherapy (RT) as an adjunct to first-line immunotherapy and chemotherapy in patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the value of RT based on the first failure pattern of ES-SCLC.MethodsIn this study, we retrospectively analyzed ES-SCLC patients treated with first-line chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) at Shanghai Chest Hospital from August 2018 to October 2023. Our study recorded the first failure pattern in ES-SCLC, analyzed the main sites of disease progression, explored factors that may affect prognosis and estimated the value of RT in extending patient survival in the immunotherapy era. Key endpoints included the first failure pattern, progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).ResultsAmong 344 patients, 70 (20%) had local failure, 105 (31%) had distant failure, 69 (20%) experienced both types of failure, and 100 (29%) showed no disease progression. In the non-pre-progression RT group, 55 patients (30%) had local failure, 72 patients (39%) had distant failure, and 56 patients (31%) had both. Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) constitutes about 15% of all lung cancers and is notorious for its rapid progression and poor prognosis [1]. In the case of extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC), the cancer has spread beyond a localized area, unlike limited-stage small cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC), where the disease remains confined to a specific region and can be more effectively treated with radiotherapy (RT) [2]. Unfortunately, nearly two-thirds of SCLC patients are diagnosed with ES-SCLC at the time of initial diagnosis, leading to a poorer prognosis [3]. For the past 30 years, etoposide plus either cisplatin (EP) or carboplatin (EC) has been the standard-of-care first-line treatment in ES-SCLC. Despite this, the survival rate for these patients is alarmingly low, with fewer than 7% surviving beyond five years [4]. Combing with other therapies or exploring new drugs and methods to improve patient survival is our goal.

Historically, adding RT to chemotherapy has been shown to enhance overall survival (OS) for patients with ES-SCLC. The phase III trial (CREST) showed that first-line chemotherapy followed by sequential thoracic RT improved two-year OS [5]. Likewise, the outcomes of a phase II trial (RTOG0937) indicated that prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) together with thoracic radiotherapy (TRT) effectively halted disease progression in patients with ES-SCLC [6]. These results prompted further investigation of the role of RT in ES-SCLC.

With the advent of immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors have remarkably prolonged patient survival in terms of progression-free survival (PFS) and OS in patients with ES-SCLC, as evidenced by the IMpower133 and CASPIAN randomized trials. The CASPIAN trial showed that the combination of durvalumab, etoposide and cisplatin prolonged median overall survival (mOS) of patients with ES-SCLC to 13 months, demonstrating a long-term benefit of immunotherapy compared with EP alone. The IMpower133 study, which used atezolizumab instead of durvalumab, showed similar findings. Unfortunately, survival data on RT relevant are insufficient. In both the CASPIAN and IMpower133 trials, some of the patients received PCI and TRT. However, data on the effectiveness of these treatments are not available, as in the IMpower133 study, only 11% of patients in each arm received PCI, whereas in the CASPIAN study, only the control group was permitted to receive PCI, and conventional TRT was not permitted in the IMpower133 and CASPIAN trials [7–9]. Only a limited portion of patients receiving PCI in CAPSTONE-1 and ASTRUM-005 [10, 11].

Previous studies have shown that the combination of RT and chemotherapy improved OS in the chemotherapy era, but the role of RT in the era of immunotherapy is unknown. The guideline notes that for patients with ES-SCLC who achieve complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) in the early stages of treatment, TRT may be an option, while PCI requires careful consideration. Patients with local symptoms may benefit from individualized palliative RT and chemotherapy, while those with brain metastases receive whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT) in combination with immunotherapy and chemotherapy [12].

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with ES-SCLC receiving first-line chemotherapy and immunotherapy at Shanghai Chest Hospital. Our study recorded the first failure pattern in ES-SCLC, analyzed the main sites of disease progression, explored factors that may affect prognosis and estimated the value of RT in extending patient survival in the immunotherapy era.

Methods

Data collection

We investigated the first failure pattern and RT for prognostic impact in patients with ES-SCLC in this single-center retrospective study. ES-SCLC patients who received first-line treatment with chemotherapy and ICIs at Shanghai Chest Hospital from August 2018 to October 2023 were verified (Clinical trial number: not applicable). All data were obtained from clinical medical records. The patient's clinical and demographic features included age, gender, smoking status, T stage, clinical disease stage, tumor pathology type, PD-1/PD-L1 expression status, metastasis at diagnosis and the RT participation. This study was authorized by the ethics committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital [IS24011] according to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are as follows: 1. Confirmed histological or cytological diagnosis of SCLC; 2. Diagnosis of extensive-stage disease based on the 9th TNM staging system and VALG two-stage staging method; 3. Initial treatment includes EP/EC-based chemotherapy with immunotherapy 4. Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance-status (PS) score of 0 or 1(on a 5-point scale, with higher numbers reflecting greater disability); 5. Presence of at least one measurable lesion in accordance with Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1.

The exclusion criteria are as follows: 1. Incomplete clinical data; 2. Active or past autoimmune disease; 3. No available imaging data to evaluate the efficacy of the treatment 4. Interruption of the treatment for any reason.

Pathology and biology markers

Tumor specimens removed either surgically or by biopsy were analyzed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) to confirm the pathological diagnosis of SCLC. PD-L1 status was also assessed by IHC staining. The PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) determined the ratio of tumor cells exhibiting complete or partial cell membrane staining. A PD-L1 expression below 1% was negative, while an expression beyond 1% and less than 50% was positive, and an expression of 50% or greater was strongly positive.

Efficacy assessment

Tumor response was assessed by RECIST version 1.1 before starting chemoimmunotherapy. Baseline imaging was obtained in all patients before initiating chemoimmunotherapy. Imaging scans were done every 8 weeks during treatment until either disease progression or death. All patients with ES-SCLC who completed first-line immunotherapy and chemotherapy underwent a thorough evaluation to determine their eligibility for RT. The treatment planning process also took into account factors like age, comorbidities, and patient preferences. The attending radiation oncologists made decisions regarding treatment techniques, target delineation, planned target volume (PTV) margin adjustments, dose prescription, and fractionation. Radiation doses were determined through a thorough evaluation of each patient's specific medical situation.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were PFS and OS. PFS was defined as the interval from the start of chemoimmunotherapy until disease progression or death from any cause, while OS was defined as the period from the start of chemoimmunotherapy until death from any cause. Local failure was defined as progression limited to the lung and regional lymph node, while distant metastasis referred to progress in locations outside the local area. The first failure pattern was designated as failure that occurred from the treatment to the disease progression debut. RECIST version 1.1 was used to assess tumor response in our study. Adverse effects were assessed based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. The patients were followed up by consulting outpatient records, hospitalization medical records, and telephone inquiries. The latest follow-up was August 2024.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by SPSS25.0, R version 4.3.3 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentiles. Survival curves were plotted and PFS and OS were calculated by the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank tests. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate the hazard ratio (HR) and related 95% confidence interval (CI) and to explore factors associated with OS. Differences in categorical variables were assessed by the chi-squared test. A two-sided p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Disease progression occurred in 244 patients (71%). Regarding the number of progression sites, 157 patients (64.3%) had only one site of progression, 61 patients (25%) had two sites, and 26 patients (10.7%) had three or more sites (S1, supporting information).

Assessment of efficacy

We identified first failure pattern and analyzed survival outcomes in our study of ES-SCLC treated with first-line chemoimmunotherapy. Before the cut-off date, 194 patients (56%) died, the median progression-free survival (mPFS) of the enrolled patients was 6.8 months (95% CI: 6.1–7.4 months; Fig. 2A), and the mOS was 17.1 months (95% CI: 14.4–19.6 months; Fig. 2B). The mOS was 19.1 months (95% CI: 13.6–24.6 months) for patients with the local failure; 20.6 months (95% CI:16.5–24.6 months) for patients for distant failure and 13.4 months (95% CI: 10.7–16.0 months) for both failures.

First failure pattern of progression patients

To exclude the impact of pre-progression RT on the first failure pattern, 183 patients were divided into a non-pre-progression RT group, and 61 patients were classified into a pre-progression RT group. In the pre-progression RT group, the first failure pattern comprised 15 patients (25%) with local failure, 33 patients (54%) with distant failure, and 13 patients (21%) with both (Fig. 3A). In the non-pre-progression RT group, the first failure pattern comprised 55 patients (30%) with local failure, 72 patients (39%) with distant failure, and 56 patients (31%) with both (Fig. 3B). Regarding progression sites in the pre-progression RT group, the most common were the lungs and regional lymph nodes (28 cases, 33.8%), followed by the brain (25 cases, 30.1%), bone (6 cases, 7.2%), liver (7 cases, 8.4%), adrenal glands (5 cases, 6.0%), pleural effusion (1 case, 1.2%) and others (11 cases,13.3%; Fig. 3C); Regarding progression sites in the non-pre-progression RT group, the most common were the lungs and regional lymph nodes (111 cases, 40.3%), followed by the brain (63 cases, 22.9%), bone (34 cases, 12.4%), liver (31 cases, 11.3%), adrenal glands (9 cases, 3.3%), pleura (12 cases, 4.4%), pleural effusion (7 cases, 2.5%), pericardial effusion (2 cases, 0.7%), and others (6 cases, 2.2%; Fig. 3D).

Based on the differences in non-pre-progression RT failure patterns and pre-progression RT failure patterns, we observed a reduction in local failure rates from 30 to 25%; in lungs and regional lymph nodes from 40.3% to 33.8%. We further explored the impact of RT on local control and patient survival.

Factors of effectiveness

We selected several variables based on the database and clinical practice, including gender, age, smoking status, T stage, total stage, histological type, brain metastasis, liver metastasis, bone metastasis, other metastasis and participation in RT, to conduct univariate analyses to investigate potential factors affecting OS. The number of patients with detected PD-L1 status was limited, so this factor was excluded from the analysis. The results indicated that factors such as gender, liver metastasis, bone metastasis and participation in RT were likely related to patient prognosis (p < 0.2, Table 1). We selected factors with p < 0.2 from the univariate analysis for multivariate analysis. The multivariate analysis identified RT (p = 0.010) as an independent predictor of OS (Table 4).

Discussion

Previous data regarding efficacy of RT in immunotherapy to ES-SCLC is scarce. In our retrospective study of ES-SCLC patients treated with first-line chemotherapy and immunotherapy, we analyzed first failure pattern and explored the value of RT. Failure pattern analysis showed sites of progression. The lung, with its regional lymph nodes and brain are the primary sites of progression in patients with ES-SCLC receiving first-line chemotherapy with ICIs. To eliminate the confounding effect of RT prior to progression on the first failure pattern, we divided the patients into two groups based on whether they received RT before progression. It is noteworthy that both the rate of local failure and proportion of lung and regional lymph node were lower in the pre-progression RT group. The differences in failure patterns prompted further exploration of the value of RT. Univariate and multivariate analysis explored factors influencing patient survival. Confirming RT (p = 0.010) as an independent predictor of OS. RT improved OS in ES-SCLC patients undergoing first-line chemoimmunotherapy and pre-progression RT improved PFS further. Pre-progression RT shows a trend toward prolonged OS. No adverse events (AEs) of grade 3 or above occurred in the RT group. Results indicated the survival benefit of RT but its further local control of early involvement remains unclear.

Chemotherapy has long been the mainstay of treatment for ES-SCLC, with the combination of EP/EC serving as the standard first-line regimen. In recent years, the use of ICIs targeting PD-1 and PD-L1 has shown clinical effectiveness in the treatment of SCLC. The CASPIAN and IMpower133 trials marked the beginning of the immunotherapy era for ES-SCLC treatment and immunotherapy was introduced as a first-line option. The IMpower133 study, a Phase III trial, compared the efficacy and safety of atezolizumab in combination with EC versus a placebo plus EC as first-line treatment for ES-SCLC. The study reported longer mOS (12.3 vs. 10.3 months; 51.9% vs. 30.9% of 1-year os, p = 0.0154) and longer mPFS (5.2 vs. 4.3 months; 12.6% vs. 5.4% of 1-year PFS, p = 0.002) with the atezolizumab regimen. The incidence of severe (grade 3/4) adverse events was comparable between the two groups. The CASPIAN study, which evaluated durvalumab (a PD-L1 inhibitor) in combination with EC/EP as first-line treatment for ES-SCLC, found that the durvalumab plus chemotherapy group had a significantly longer mOS than the chemotherapy-only group (13.0 vs. 10.3 months; 54% vs. 40% of 1-year os, p = 0.0047), with a 27% reduction in the risk of death (HR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.59–0.91). The occurrence of AEs was nearly identical in both groups (98.1% vs. 97%) [7–9].

There are innate mechanisms of RT in combination with immunotherapy. RT can enhance immunotherapy efficacy by converting unproductive immune responses into productive and lasting ones [13]. Combining RT and immunotherapy can increase the “abscopal effect” and overcome drug resistance [14]. Previous studies showed that RT associated with chemotherapy can significantly improve OS in the chemotherapy era, but the value of RT in the immunotherapy era needs more evidence. Notably, results from the phase II clinical trial (TREASURE, NCT04462276) indicated that following first-line atezolizumab combined with EC, sequential TRT of 30 Gy/10f concurrent with maintenance atezolizumab was terminated due to significantly increased toxicity. In our retrospective study, there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of grade 3–4 AEs between the two groups, suggesting that RT did not lead to a notable increase in safety toxicity in our patient cohort. However, in another similar phase II study, first-line durvalumab combined with the EP/EC regimen followed by TRT ≥ 30 Gy/10 fractions or ≥ 50 Gy/25 fractions concurrent with maintenance durvalumab showed acceptable treatment toxicity, with an ORR of 74.5% and mPFS of 7.0 months, which is superior to previous data from first-line immunochemotherapy in ES-SCLC [15, 16]. In addition, the RAPTOR/NGRLU007 trial is a phase II/III randomized study (NCT04402788) that combined 4 to 6 cycles of platinum-containing plus etoposide with or without atezolizumab. Non-progressing patients were randomly assigned to either the atezolizumab maintenance group or the atezolizumab plus RT group. Stratification of patients is based on the number of radiation lesions, first-line systemic therapy treatment response, and PS score. These trails will provide more data on how to best utilize RT based on the standards of care in the chemoimmunotherapy era [17].

Previously prospective and retrospective analyses of ES-SCLC patients treated with first-line chemoimmunotherapy showed lung and regional lymph node were the most common sites of progression, while the brain was the most common site of distant metastasis. Our study is consistent with previous literature reports [18]. The mPFS of enrolled patients was 6.8 months (95% CI: 6.1 to 7.4) and the mOS was 17.1 months (95% CI: 14.4 to 19.6), which were similar to previous data [19–21]. RT can improve OS of patients (HR, 0.694; 95% CI: 0.523–0.921; P = 0.0108). Pre-progression RT significantly improved PFS (HR, 0.715; 95% CI: 0.549–0.930; P = 0.0119). Prior small sample size analyses demonstrated safety and survival benefits of RT [22–24]. These outcomes suggest that immunotherapy combined with RT has considerable potential in ES-SCLC.

Compared to previous retrospective studies, our research presents several advantages. First, we incorporated a larger sample size. Unlike other studies, our research takes failure patterns as the starting point, further exploring it based on whether RT was administered prior to progression. The observed differences in these patterns prompt us to delve deeper into the factors influencing prognosis. Furthermore, through subgroup analysis, we further validated the therapeutic impact of RT. After identifying RT as a favorable prognostic factor, we further investigated the timing of RT and found that administering RT prior to progression can significantly enhance PFS. The impact of RT on OS remains unclear, though the trend suggesting potential benefits can be observed. Further prospective studies are needed to validate this. However, as a retrospective study, there are inevitable limitations. First of all, this study was retrospective and selected patient limitedly. For example, 87% patients were male. It also had retrospective bias, group heterogeneity, and selection bias. Second, data were collected from a single center. Third, the treatments were not uniform and may affect the interpretation of the result, including different radiation timing, and dose/fraction. Despite these limitations, we ensured accuracy of patient information, treatment processes and therapeutic evaluation. The findings revealed that synergistic RT potentially brought survival advantages, and we plan to conduct further prospective studies to validate these effects.

Conclusions

Standard platinum-etoposide chemotherapy in conjunction with immunotherapy was given to 344 patients in this retrospective analysis of the first-line ES-SCLC group. According to our findings, the main areas of progression are the lung, with its regional lymph nodes and brain. RT was associated with improved OS in these patients, and pre-progression RT improved PFS further. Pre-progression RT indicated a trend toward prolonged OS; however, the optimal timing of integrating RT into treatment regimen remains unclear and requires further exploration.
