Whole and refined grains consumption and social interaction in children: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammadreza Aliakbari, Mohammad Mehdi Haghighat Lari, Pania Nouri, Maryam Mollaei, Zahra Kermanizadeh Jamkarani, Faezeh Nematolahi, Alireza Milajerdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial interaction is fundamental to a child’s mental health and development. While diet is a known modulator of brain function, the specific roles of refined versus whole grain intake on social behaviors in children are not well understood. This study aimed to investigate association between refined and whole grain consumption and social interaction scores among Iranian children.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 313 children aged 7–13 years from Kashan, Iran. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 168-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Social interaction was evaluated using the 40-item Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ), where higher scores indicate greater difficulties with communication. Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess association between grain intake and SCQ scores, adjusting for a comprehensive set of demographics, socioeconomic, anthropometric, and dietary confounders.ResultsIn the fully adjusted model, total refined grains intake was significantly associated with higher SCQ scores (β‌: 0.007, 95% CI: 0.005, 0.009; P < 0.001). Conversely, total whole grain was associated with lower SCQ scores (β‌: -0.006, 95% CI: -0.009, -0.002; P < 0.001).ConclusionIn this study, higher intake of refined grains was associated with poorer social interaction, while greater consumption of whole grains was associated with more favorable social behaviors in Iranian children. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are required to clarify the causal relationship between grain quality and social interaction in children of different countries.

Introduction

Social Interaction (SI) is a basic human need that plays a vital role in mental health and personality development during childhood and adolescence [1]. Children with higher social skills tend to perform better in learning, and they manage their emotions to reduce aggressive behaviors. Deficits in SI are considered significant risk factors for depression and anxiety in the future [2, 3].

Nutritional status and dietary intakes can influence SI during childhood. Diet can alter the composition of the gut microbiota, and this change affects brain function [1]. In addition, unhealthy dietary patterns due to high amounts of saturated fats and refined grains can induce a low-grade systemic inflammation [4]. This inflammation increases the risk of depression and other mood disorders by an adverse effect on serotonin production [2]. Zhao et al. reported that a dietary pattern rich in sweet foods and beverages significantly increased the risk of children’s mental disorders [5]. A meta-analysis, which was published in 2021, indicated that children with unhealthy diets showed more depression and internalizing symptoms [2]. In contrast, Jonsson et al. found that a healthier eating behavior among adolescents improved their mental health [6].

High intake of refined grains and a lower intake of whole grains is one of the hallmarks of an unhealthy eating pattern. This imbalance causes dysbiosis [7, 8], inflammation [9], and overweight or obesity [10]. A systematic review by Ross et al. on adults concluded that higher intake of whole grains was associated with better mood and anxiety-related scores [11]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Yang et al. reported that greater intake of dietary fiber was linked with lower odds of depression symptoms among children and adolescents [12]. While the link between general dietary patterns and mental health is established, the specific, independent roles of refined versus whole grain consumption on social communication skills in children remain largely unexplored, particularly in Middle Eastern populations with distinct dietary sources of grains [13, 14]. Given the scarcity of studies in this area, further research is warranted. Additionally, childhood is a crucial period in shaping the social personality of individuals. In this cross-sectional study, we aimed to investigate the association between whole and refined grain intake and the level of SI among children aged 7–13 years in Kashan, Iran.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study in Kashan, Iran. All stages of the study have been approved by the Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences and registered with the code IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1403.117. It is also worth noting that both the child and their parents were informed about the study’s aims and procedures, and a written consent form was completed by all parents. At this stage, the children answered our questions with the help of their parents.

Study participants

This study was conducted in Kashan, Iran, between June and December 2024. To ensure representation across different socio-economic regions of the city, stratified sampling was employed, and participants were recruited through convenience non-random sampling from health centers. After obtaining ethical approval, a list of health centers under the supervision of Kashan University of Medical Sciences was prepared. Kashan was divided into seven regions based on socio-economic diversity, with each considered a distinct cluster. One health center was randomly selected from each region, except for regions 4 and 5, where two centers were chosen due to higher population density. We included all children aged 7 to 13 who met the inclusion criteria during the sampling period. Finally, we included 313 children from the different areas of Kashan in this study.

Eligibility criteria

Children aged 7–13 years who spoke Persian and had no history of underlying diseases (such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or epilepsy) or other conditions affecting brain function were deemed eligible. In contrast, children who had a history of using specific medications or supplements (at least twice per week), parental substance abuse, adherence to special diets within the past year, or unwillingness to participate were excluded.

Data collection

The telephone numbers of children who met the inclusion criteria were obtained from their electronic records. They were then invited to the research center to answer the researchers’ questions along with their parents. General demographic data, including age, gender, ethnicity, parental marital status, physical activity status, and medical history, were collected at the meeting through an interview.

Dietary intake assessment

We used a validated Persian 168-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [15]. This tool helps us measure the frequency of intake of various foods over the past year. Participants were able to determine their frequency of intakes in daily, weekly, and monthly choices. We converted all measurements into grams per day, and then calculated the amounts of macro- and micronutrients using the Nut4 software. In addition, grains were classified into two groups, refined or whole, based on their processing and fiber content as defined by Modern Nutrition in Health and Disease [16]. The classification of mixed-grain products was based on the predominant grain type as defined in previous relevant studies, ensuring consistency with established dietary assessment methodologies. Refined grains included refined breads, cooked rice, cooked pasta, noodles/vermicelli, wheat flour, crackers, biscuits, cakes, and sweets. Whole grains included whole breads, bulgur, barley, and popcorn. In addition to the primary analyses, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which all ultra-processed items originally classified under refined grains (including crackers, yazdi cake, cream-filled cakes, other cakes, dry and creamy sweets, biscuits, homemade halva, piroshki, pizza, snacks, and puffed corn products) were excluded from this food group. Refined grain intake was recalculated after removing these items, and all analyses were repeated accordingly. We categorized participants into quartiles based on their intakes of refined and whole grains.

Social interaction assessment

To assess the levels of SI, we used a validated Persian format of the 40-item Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) [17, 18]. This questionnaire is a widely used tool for screening individuals for autism spectrum disorders. Each question can be answered as either yes or no, and the final score ranges from 0 to 39, with higher scores indicating lower levels of SI.

Physical activity assessment

The Physical Activity Questionnaire for Children (PAQ-C), developed by Kowalski et al. [19] and validated by Zarei et al. [20] was used to assess participants’ physical activity. The PAQ-C includes 10 questions that assess various aspects of physical activity over the past week. Each question consists of 5 choices, ranked from 1 to 5, with higher scores reflecting higher physical activity levels.

Statistical analyses

Misreporting of energy intake was evaluated using the Goldberg cut-off or EI: BMR ratio. The dataset was examined for outliers and unrealistic energy intake values. The outliers were excluded. The grain intakes were adjusted for total energy using the Residual method before analysis. Subsequently, participants were classified into quartiles according to their intakes of refined and whole grains. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage (n, %). One-way ANOVA and the Chi-square (χ²) test were applied for continuous and categorical variables to assess these characteristics across the quartiles of grain consumption, respectively. Multivariable linear regression models were developed to examine the relationship between grain consumption and SCQ scores. The SCQ score was designated as the dependent variable. The crude model represented the unadjusted analysis; Model 1 was adjusted for age, gender, and energy intake, Model 2 was further adjusted for ethnicity, marital status, father’s education, mother’s education, father’s job, mother’s job, economic situation, tobacco use, family number, family history of mental illness, family history of autism, and weight, Model 3, the fully adjusted model, which accounted for all adjustments of Model 2 plus BMI z-score, and physical activity status. The findings were expressed as unstandardized beta coefficients (B) accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CI). The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (Version 27.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 313 children were included in the analysis (mean age: 9.12 ± 1.80 years; 41.9% boys). Table 1 shows the distribution of sociodemographic and anthropometric characteristics of participants across quartiles of refined and whole-grain consumption. Significant differences were detected between the refined grain quartiles in terms of participants’ age, height, BMI z-score, and SCQ score (all P < 0.05). Height, weight, BMI z-score, and SCQ score also significantly differed across quartiles of whole grains (P < 0.05). The proportions of Fars ethnicity, higher parental education, better economic status, and family tobacco use showed statistically significant differences across quartiles of both refined and whole grains (P < 0.05).

Table 2 shows the dietary intakes of participants across quartiles of refined and whole-grain consumption. Higher refined grain intake was significantly associated with an increasing trend in energy, protein, carbohydrate, and fat consumption (all P < 0.001). For whole grains, carbohydrate intake increased gradually across quartiles (P < 0.001), while fat intake was significantly lower among those at the highest consuming category than those at the lowest intake (P = 0.003). Total dietary fiber intake did not differ significantly across refined or whole grain quartiles (P > 0.05 for both).

Table 3 summarizes the findings from our multivariable regression. Linear regression in the crude model revealed a clear relationship: total refined grain intake was associated with higher SCQ scores (β: 0.008, 95% CI: 0.006, 0.01; P < 0.001). This direct association remained statistically significant even after we adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic, anthropometric, and lifestyle factors. Regarding whole grains, a higher intake of whole grains was associated with lower SCQ scores in the crude model. Children in Q4 had a significantly lower SCI score (β = -0.005; 95% CI: -0.008, -0.001) compared to those in Q1. In the fully adjusted model, this association remained significant (β: -0.006, 95% CI: −0.009, − 0.002; P < 0.001).

To further evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted sex-stratified sensitivity analyses which did not fundamentally alter our main conclusions. While the association between refined grains and higher SCQ scores remained consistent across both sexes (girls: β: 0.007, 95% CI: 0.005, 0.010; P < 0.001; boys: β: 0.007, 95% CI: 0.005, 0.01; P < 0.001), we observed sex-specific differences regarding whole grains. Specifically, an association between whole grain intake and lower SCQ scores was evident in girls (β: -0.005, 95% CI: −0.010, 0.000; P < 0.034) whereas this relationship was not statistically significant among boys (β: -0.005, 95% CI: −0.010, − 0.000; P < 0.068). Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the relationship between whole and refined grain intake and SI among Iranian children aged 7–13 years. Our findings indicated that higher intake of refined grains was directly associated with higher SCQ scores, indicating lower levels of SI. In contrast, greater consumption of whole grains showed an inverse relationship with SCQ scores, indicating more favorable SI outcomes. These relationships remained statistically significant after controlling for a comprehensive set of demographics, socioeconomic, anthropometric, and lifestyle variables. Considering Iranian nutritional patterns, which are usually rich in dietary refined grains, the results of the present study highlight again the importance of public health strategies for healthier dietary patterns in Iranian children.

Our findings align with a growing body of evidence indicating that diet quality is related to psychosocial functioning in children and adolescents. Meta-analytic evidence has shown that unhealthy dietary patterns, often characterized by energy-dense and nutrient-poor foods, are related to worse symptoms of depression and anxiety in youth [2]. More specifically, studies have linked diets high in sweets and processed foods to a greater prevalence of emotional and behavioral problems [5]. While a systematic review by Ross et al. concluded that higher whole grain intake was associated with significant improvement in mood among adults, but they did not include studies about children [11].

It should be emphasized that SI has an important role in mental health during childhood. Additionally, childhood is a fundamental period in shaping the social personality of persons [1]. Moreover, Children with grater social skills do better in learning. Scarcities in SI are significant risk factors for anxiety and depression [2, 3]. Some factors such as Dietary intakes including grains can influence SI significantly during childhood. Although the association between general dietary patterns and mental health was studied, the independent roles of refined and whole grain intake on SI skills in children remain unexplored before this study, particularly in Middle Eastern populations [13, 14]. Given the insufficiency of studies in this area, further research was necessary. Therefore, a sample of this particular population of children was considered valuable for studying this research gap. In the current study, we examined the link between whole and refined grain intake and the level of SI among children in Kashan, Iran.

The observed sex-specific associations warrant further discussion. The significant positive correlation between whole grain consumption and lower SCQ scores in girls, contrasted with its absence in boys, suggests a potential gender-specific protective effect of whole grains. One hypothesis for this difference could be related to the earlier onset of puberty in girls. Pubertal development is a period of significant hormonal and physiological changes, often accompanied by increased social pressures and cognitive development. It is plausible that the nutrients and fiber provided by whole grains may offer a more pronounced advantage in buffering against or supporting adaptive responses to these pubertal stressors in girls. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that our study did not collect data on the pubertal status of the children included. Therefore, while this remains an intriguing avenue for future research, it is a limitation of the current investigation. Future studies incorporating measures of pubertal development would be essential to explore this hypothesis rigorously.

Our sensitivity analyses, which involved re-categorizing refined grains to exclude highly processed items like crackers and pastries, confirmed the overall robustness of our primary findings. While the general associations remained consistent, we observed minor fluctuations in the regression coefficients. This indicates that while the broader pattern of association is stable, the precise magnitude of the effect may be influenced by the inclusion or exclusion of specific ultra-processed foods within the refined grains category. The modest changes in coefficients suggest that the overall dietary pattern might play a more significant role than isolated components, or that our adjustment factors adequately capture confounding variables. Further research could explore the impact of ultra-processed foods as a distinct dietary pattern.

Several plausible mechanisms may explain the observed associations. Evidence from interventional studies supports some potential biological pathways; for instance, a randomized crossover trial in Danish children reported that replacing refined grains with whole grains improved cardiometabolic risk markers and favorably altered gut microbiota composition [7]. This is aligned with the hypothesis that refined grains, which are low in fiber and high in rapidly absorbable carbohydrates, may contribute to glycemic fluctuations, systemic low-grade inflammation, and gut dysbiosis [4, 8, 9]. Such pathways are known to negatively affect serotonin regulation and mood, which can in turn influence social interaction levels [2]. Conversely, whole grains are rich in fiber, resistant starch, and bioactive compounds that are known to improve metabolic health, reduce inflammation, and support a healthier microbiome profile [7, 9]. This healthier microbiome, in turn, has been associated with improved mental health outcomes and a lower severity of autism-related symptoms [21, 22]. Furthermore, diets rich in refined grains are often associated with a higher BMI and overweight [23], a condition that can negatively influence self-esteem and social participation in children [3].

It should be taken into consideration that some unique factors might influence the observed associations such as parenting styles, intrafamilial dynamics, and recent stressful life events (e.g., bereavement). However, we fully acknowledge that due to its cross-sectional nature and practical limitations, our study was unable to directly measure and control for all such potentially confounding variables. This represents an inherent limitation of the present research. Furthermore, as mentioned previously, the absence of data pertaining to the pubertal status of the participating children is another limitation that should be recognized when interpreting our findings.

This study has several notable strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to specifically investigate independent associations of refined and whole-grain intake with social communication skills in a Middle Eastern pediatric population, which has its unique dietary patterns. Furthermore, we utilized validated questionnaires for assessing dietary intake, physical activity, and social communication. Finally, the validity of our findings was strengthened by the comprehensive adjustment for a wide range of potential demographic, anthropometric, and dietary confounders. Despite these strengths, our study had some limitations that should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; therefore, we cannot determine whether dietary habits influence social interaction or vice versa. Second, dietary intake was assessed using an FFQ, which may be subject to recall bias and misreporting [24]. Third, although the SCQ is a validated and widely used tool for screening autism spectrum disorders, it is not a comprehensive diagnostic measure of social interaction abilities [17, 18]. Finally, the study population was limited to children from a single city in Iran, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Future well-designed longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to confirm these findings and elucidate the causal relationship between grain quality and social interaction in children.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study among Iranian children, a greater intake of refined grains was independently associated with poorer social interaction. In contrast, greater intake of whole grains was associated with more favorable outcomes. Considering Iranian nutritional patterns, which are typically rich in refined grains, these findings highlight again the importance of public health strategies for healthier dietary patterns in children. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are required to clarify the causal relationship between grain quality and social interaction in children of different countries in the world.
