Molecular Dynamics of Catalyst-Free Edge Elongation of Boron Nitride Nanotubes Coaxially Grown on Single-Walled Carbon Nanotubes
Authors: Kaoru Hisama, Ksenia V Bets, Nitant Gupta, Ryo Yoshikawa, Yongjia Zheng, Shuhui Wang, Ming Liu, Rong Xiang, Keigo Otsuka, Shohei Chiashi, Boris I Yakobson, Shigeo Maruyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in low-dimensional materials have enabled the synthesis of single-walled carbon nanotubes encapsulated in hexagonal boron nitride (BN) nanotubes (SWCNT@BNNT), creating one-dimensional van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures. However, controlling the quality and crystallinity of BNNT on the surface of SWCNTs using chemical vapor deposition (CVD) remains a challenge. To better understand the growth mechanism of the BNNT in SWCNT@BNNT, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using empirical potentials. The simulation results suggest that spontaneous BN nucleation is unlikely to occur on the outer surface of the SWCNT when we assume only vdW interaction between the BN and SWCNT layers. However, we observe the elongation of the BNNT when a short BNNT is provided as a seed nucleus on the SWCNT. This grown BNNT structure, with its sharply cut edges, aligns with experimental observations made using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Moreover, the edge-reconstruction process favors zigzag B edges, which exhibit low edge energy according to the ReaxFF potential. Our simulation successfully provides insights into the catalyst-free growth process of this one-dimensional vdW heterostructure.Keywords: boron nitride nanotube, carbon nanotube, heterostructure, molecular dynamics, ReaxFF

Introduction

Single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) are among the most famous nanomaterials known for their interesting physical properties that have motivated researchers for the past 30 years.1−3 Their covalent honeycomb structure, which is composed of sp2 bonds of C atoms, forms rolled-up cylindrical graphene sheets. This structure provides SWCNTs with mechanical strength4,5 and high thermal conductivity.6,7 They also possess unusual electronic properties, which can be either metallic or semiconducting, depending on their chiral indices (n,m).8−10 These properties make them potentially useful in various electronic applications, such as transistors, integrated circuits,11−13 transparent/flexible films,14,15 thermoelectric devices,16,17 and photovoltaic cells.18 It is also worth noting that the formation of tubular structures is possible with other two-dimensional materials, not just graphene. Hexagonal boron nitride (BN) and transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), such as MoS2 and WS2, are known as 2D materials. These can form boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs)19 and transition metal dichalcogenide nanotubes (TMDNTs),20,21 respectively. BNNTs are insulators,22 while MoS2NT and WS2NT are semiconductors,23,24 inheriting the properties of their 2D counterparts. BNNTs also exhibit high mechanical strength and thermal conductivity, making them promising materials for various applications. They are particularly useful for composites with polymers and can be applied as dielectrics or nanoscale insulators.25

The potential and applications of nanotubes (NTs) can be further expanded by forming host–guest compounds with other nanomaterials, such as nanotubes and nanowires,26−33 resulting in a heterostructure. Recent advances in chemical vapor deposition (CVD) techniques for low-dimensional materials have enabled the experimental synthesis of van der Waals heterostructures. In these structures, SWCNTs are encapsulated by BNNTs and TMDNTs.26 This is recognized as the one-dimensional version of two-dimensional van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures with graphene, BN, and TMDs, which exhibit various physical properties and applications.34−37 The weak interlayer interaction allows for infinite combinations of constituent materials and stacking configurations, regardless of their elements or lattice matching. In particular, the inert surface of BNNT can preserve the electronic structure of semiconductor nanomaterials, thereby maintaining the electrical and optical properties of CNTs26 and TMDNTs.27

Among these coaxial heteronanotubes, SWCNT encapsulated in BNNT (SWCNT@BNNT) is one of the simplest heterocompounds, with similar layers of carbon and BN. This structure is fascinating because the SWCNTs are functionalized and protected by dielectric BNNT. It maintains the electronic structure of SWCNT near the Fermi level and is expected to serve as the ultimate SWCNT channel with a nonbonded surrounding dielectric layer.38 This makes it more resistant to oxidation26 and enhance its thermal conductance.39 Consequently, its applications in field-effect transistors have been noted.40,41 Furthermore, the BN layer can be functionalized with amorphous carbon, resulting in a memristive device.42

Despite the promising results from past studies of SWCNT@BNNT, controlling the quality and crystallinity of the BNNT coating around a SWCNT using CVD remains a challenge, although these factors are critical for device applications. Previous experimental studies have shown that BNNTs with various chiral angles can grow around SWCNTs, and that the grown BNNTs have zigzag-edge structures.28 However, the dynamics of constructing these edge structures remain unclear, requiring a deeper exploration of the growth mechanism of BNNTs in SWCNT@BNNTs. Since in situ observation of BNNT growth around SWCNTs is difficult in experiments, theoretical modeling of the construction dynamics of the zigzag-edge structure provides an efficient way to understand the growth mechanism of BNNTs on SWCNTs. In this study, we performed a molecular dynamics (MD) simulation using ReaxFF potentials to reveal the dynamics during the growth process of BNNT on the atomic scale. Our simulation did not reveal BNNT nucleation, indicating that it was a process that was too slow to be captured within reasonable simulation times. However, it successfully revealed the elongation process. Our simulation also demonstrated that edge-reconstruction processes, during which the edge grows unevenly, favor the boron-terminated zigzag edge, and form sharp-cut or flat-cut partial walls. This finding is consistent with the sharp-cut edge observed in experimental TEM studies.

Results/Discussion

In line with previous experimental observations, we developed a simulation protocol to capture the growth of the outer BN layer on the surface of the CNT. Because the formation of the CNT is beyond the scope of this investigation, the fully formed SWCNT was placed in the center of the simulation cell prior to the introduction of the feedstock of boron and nitrogen atoms in the form of B3N3 molecules. We used B3N3 as the precursor and excluded the dehydrogenation step of the borazine precursor decomposed from ammonia borane in experiments, from the simulation to expedite the target process of BNNT growth. Additional feedstock molecules were periodically introduced on the periphery of the simulation cell (20 ≤ |x|,|z| ≤ 25), see details in the Figure 7 and Methods/Experimental, with randomized velocities that would carry them toward the center, facilitating natural participation in the growth process. Any unreacted molecules or fragments that approached the cell boundary were removed from the simulation to prevent irrelevant precursor reactions. While previous experimental results considered SWCNTs of various chiralities, there were no obvious correlations between SWCNT and BNNT chirality in all cases.28 This indicates that the interlayer interactions are weak. This observation allowed us to simplify the model by including only van der Waals interactions between the layers of the heterostructures. The B–N interaction was modeled using the empirical ReaxFF potential, while Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential is used for the interlayer BN-C interaction, which allows for prolonged simulation times necessary for growth observations.

In our initial simulations, we mimicked the experimental setup using bare CNTs as templates. Unfortunately, despite a long simulation time of 9 ns, this setup did not allow the simulation of the nucleation process. This is likely due to the weak interaction between BN units and the CNT layer, analogous to terrace nucleation on the steps of the substrate in the growth of vdW materials.43 To simulate the nucleation process, further extension of the simulation for the introduction of a starting point for nucleation would be desirable or there should be a strong interaction between BN fragments and carbon structures. While defects and amorphous regions of carbon structure promote the BN yields in the experiment,28 our modeling of the combination of the ReaxFF and the LJ potential lacks the reactive interaction between the BN and CNT layers. We performed another simulation for the nucleation using a machine-learning interatomic potential, Preferred potential (PFP), which is a pretrained model available for various materials.44

Figure 1a–c shows the result of the nucleation simulation using PFP by 8 ns sequential snapshots at 1300, 1600, and 1900 K, respectively. At the highest temperature 1900 K, a fullerene-like network of BN broke the CNT structures, and this was also observed at lower temperatures. However, the BN network interruption into CNT is less abundant at 1300 or 1600 K than at 1900 K, and hBN-like fragments adsorbed by the edge on the CNT surface are predominantly observed. These edge-attaching BN structures can play a role as nuclei for the BNNT structures, although the formation of the seed BNNT from the fragment on CNT was not observed in the simulation. The seed BNNT may form accidentally from these fragments over a longer timescale or may be led by some steric effects at the bundles or junctions of CNTs. The hBN fragment retains its weak interaction with the CNT on its planar surface, even when using PFP, as the elongation simulation produced consistent results for BNNT growth with the ReaxFF/LJ simulation, as shown in Figure S1. These results prove that the covalent bonds between B–C or N–C are much stronger than the hBN–graphene interlayer interactions. It seems that the contact on the planar sides of the layers is not stable, whereas the edges easily stick together. This seems to be a key factor in the difficulty of growing hBN on nonmetal substrates, which is important for the semiconductor applications of hBN.45

Sequential snapshots (2, 4, 6, and 8 ns) of the MD simulation of BN structure growth without initial seed using PFP at (a) 1300 K, (b) 1600 K, and (c) 1900 K.

As far as our current experimental observation including the previous literature,26,28 the penetration of the BN network into the CNT was not observed, which is not consistent with Figure 1a–c. Thus, our simulation using PFP overestimates the reactive interaction of BN fragments with carbon. Hydrogen termination may be the factor for this overestimation, which was omitted in the simulation. Therefore, we conclude that our elongation simulation using the ReaxFF and the LJ modeling is effective with a weak interwall interaction after the nucleation process, which was partly observed in the simulation using PFP. This observation prompted us to introduce the BNNT seed on the surface of the CNT to allow for the study of the dynamic growth process.

In the MD simulation using ReaxFF/LJ, which supplied B3N3 molecules, we observed elongation of the BNNT from the BNNT seed. Figure 2a shows a snapshot of the grown BNNT structure during a 9.0 ns simulation from (20,0) seeds at 1900 K. The unrolled structure of the grown BNNT reveals a defectless side wall, although there are some amorphous structures on the edges (Figure 2b). The simulated TEM image shown in Figure 2c and derived from the same structure as that in Figure 2b resembles the experimental TEM observation (Figure 2d). This indicated that both the simulated and experimental CNT@BNNT had sharp-cut edge structures and some amorphous structures on the edges.

BNNT on SWCNT has been considered to be a rolled-up zigzag trapezoidal flake associated with the triangular flake of a 2D BN structure in a previous experiment.28 The rolled-up BN from an ideal zigzag-edge flake forms two shapes of tube edges, depending on the chirality of the BNNT (Figure S2). For a zigzag (n, 0) NT, it has a sharp-cut edge and a flat-cut edge. The edges observed in the MD snapshot in Figure 1a,b resemble these flat-cut and sharp-cut edges, showing the contribution of the zigzag edge to the tube edge in the simulation. Note that although our MD starts from asymmetric flat-cut edges, it grew to have both flat-cut-like and sharp-cut-like edges.

The addition of the precursor leads to the dissociation of B–N bonds and the formation of new hexagonal BN rings, resulting in this catalyst-free elongation. Figure 2e shows the sequential snapshots of the dissociation dynamics when a new adsorbed precursor made a new defect. (The simulation condition is the same as Figure 2a.) The defect resulted in an unstable structure and initiated the reconstruction of the bonds. These defects occasionally formed a new hexagonal ring, as shown in Figure 2f. The BN chains or large deformed rings were observed as common intermediate structures, occasionally retaining defects for hundreds of picoseconds on the timescale. See the Supplemental video that shows the growth from (20,0) BNNT seed at 1900 K during 470–710 ps, including the event depicted in Figure 2e). The formation and annihilation of the defects is rather a shorter process with a few picoseconds than the reconstruction by those defective structures, as shown in the simulation time in Figure 2e,f.

The boron or nitrogen (B/N) atoms in our MD simulation can be categorized into two segments according to the observation of the obtained structures in which one class of B/N atoms formed into BNNT (“tube BN”) and the other included all B/N atoms in the simulation box (“total BN”). The B/N atoms created three bonds (within 1.6 Å, regarded as sp2 bonds) and within 9.5 Å from the CNT center are distinguished as tube BN. Otherwise, the BN structure had amorphous structures (see Figure S3 for the histogram of B/N atoms from the center of CNT) outside this threshold radius.

Growth at 1600, 1700, 1800, and 1900 K was simulated from each seed BNNT. Figure 3a presents the number of B/N atoms in the tube BN and the total number of B/N atoms, which depends on the simulation time and temperature for growth from the (20,0) seed. The case without the seed nucleus at 1600 K is also presented in the figure, which indicates that the BN structure does not grow on the CNT after 9.0 ns of MD simulation. It indicates that the nucleation process on the inert surface of CNTs should be much slower than the elongation of BNNTs from initial seeds. The number of tube B/N atoms increases as the temperature increases, because the thermodynamic defect healing process becomes more active at a higher temperature and it promotes the deposited B/N atoms on the edge to form a new part of BNNT. This temperature dependence is maintained for growth from other BN seeds (see Figure S4 for details), although fluctuations in the number of BN tubes during the simulation do not make this relationship deterministic. Figure 3b illustrates the average ratio of tube BN to total BN during the simulations times of 3–6 ns and 6–9 ns for all the initial BN seeds investigated: (20,0), (19,0), (15,5), (14,7), and (11,11). The ratio of the tube BN increases with an increasing temperature. The ratio is similar for each time average, which implies that the steady growth of BNNT is simulated at the highest temperature of 1900 K.

However, some seeds show bond dissociation during edge reconstruction during the B3N3 supply. As a result, we observed that the seed breaks apart, especially at higher temperatures (e.g., shown in Figure S5). The breaking of the seed BNNT at higher temperatures is observed for the (11,11) seed at 1800 and 1900 K, as well as for the (14,7) seed at 1700, 1800, and 1900 K. The initial structures for the (14, 7) BNNT and (11,11) BNNT simulations are shown in Figure 7d,f in the Methods section, respectively. While this phenomenon is common under B3N3 supply, we confirm that the (11,11) BNNT at 1900 K without B3N3 supply remained stable after a 9.0 ns simulation. The result shows that short seeds may break by the edge reconstruction led by the supply of precursor atoms. For these seeds with chiralities (14,7) and (11,11), the simulations starting from seeds with doubled initial length are performed at 1800 and 1900 K (see the Methods section and Figure 7d–g for details) since the defect formed by the adsorbed precursor broke the tube structure. The growth of BNNT is successful from these larger seeds and that higher temperature growth should require a larger initial nucleus of BNNT. The instability of the short BNNT seeds implies that the BNNTs may originate from large fragments of hBN. Very small fragments cannot form tubular structure because of the instability with its high aspect ratio. The BN fragment that is adsorbed on CNT with its edge as shown in Figure 1a can accidentally form BNNTs, and it is long enough to start elongation. This hypothesis of the origin of seed BNNT is consistent with our results of the nucleation simulation and breaking of small seeds.

The simulation indicates that a higher growth rate of BNNT at higher temperatures implies a higher yield. In fact, the experimental yield increased with the increased temperature. Figure 3c shows the optical absorption spectra of CNT@BNNT grown from SWCNT films at 1075 °C, 1250 °C, and 1300 °C. There is an increase in the peak at about 205 nm, indicating that the yield of BN increases with increasing temperature. A similar trend is observed in the FT-IR spectra shown in Figure 3d. Note that the highest temperature condition at 1300 °C in the experiment may have resulted in an excessive yield, leading to a thicker structure with less crystallinity (see TEM images in Figure S6). However, the result at 1250 °C has a sharp peak compared to the result at 1075 °C, indicating the high crystallinity growth of the BN structure. The temperature gap between the simulation and experiment depends on the interatomic potential and simulation modeling. While the ReaxFF growth simulations usually gave good results at the highest temperature of 1900 K, the nucleation simulation using PFP indicated that the network of BN network broke the CNT structures, which is not observed in experiments at the higher temperatures of 1600 and 1900 K.

The final snapshot of growth after 9 ns of MD from (20,0) seed is shown in Figure 2a,b indicating that the edge does not grow uniformly. Sequential unrolled snapshots of the temperature conditions with the best tube BN qualities for each seed (1700 K for (19,0), 1900 K for the other) are shown in Figure 4, to confirm this tendency. The tube BN quality was estimated by the sharpness of the peak in the histogram of BN atoms along the radial position (Figure S3) and the number of tube BN atoms (Figures 3a and S4). The results show that the grown BNNT did not have a uniform length within the angular direction of the tube, although it started from seeds with uniform flat-cut edges. Therefore, we conclude that sharply cut edge structures often appear from seeds with any of the simulated chiral indices.

The BN flakes exhibited triangular shapes, suggesting a preference for zigzag B/N edge structures, as observed in previous studies on the large crystalline growth of two-dimensional hBN on graphite.46 This zigzag-edge preference is also proposed in the growth of CNT@BNNT, where a similar open edge growth process governs the reaction, resulting in sharp, flat-cut edges.28 Therefore, we investigated the edge preference of the grown BNNT structure in our MD simulations by classifying the bonds into four categories: “zigzag B”, “zigzag N″, “armchair”, and “unclassified”, according to the connectivity rules of the atoms, with detailed explanations provided in SI Section 7. Snapshots in Figure 4 also show the bond classification through color coding. These structures imply that some edges are preferable to others because the zigzag N edge bond, colored yellow-green, is significantly less frequent in the 4.5 and 9 ns snapshots compared to the 0 ns snapshot. Figure 5 presents the time-dependent data and accumulated histograms showing the number of bonds for each edge class. The zigzag B edge appears most frequently across all chiral indices simulated, except in the case of unclassified bonds. This preference for zigzag boundary edges in our MD simulations implies a driving force behind the formation of sharp-cut or flat-cut edges observed in experiments. The zigzag B edge is not always the majority due to fluctuations, although it appears most frequently when counted over the entire simulation time. This fluctuation is partly due to the structure of hBN, where a kink in the growing zigzag edge forms an armchair edge, as shown in Figure 4c,d with the 0 ns snapshots of the (15,5) and (14,7) seeds. The zigzag B edges are accompanied by kink armchair edges. That contributes to the fact that sometimes the number of zigzag B edges decreases and that of the armchair edges increases. This may explain why the width of the distribution of edge frequency is ordered as zigzag B, armchair, and zigzag N edges. Note that the appearance of the zigzag B edge is more moderate for the armchair BNNT (11,11) growth in Figure 5e compared to the zigzag (20,0) and (19,0) BNNTs shown in Figure 5a,b, respectively. Since the possible shortest edge configurations are limited for a fixed chirality nanotube, the formation of a purely zigzag B edge would involve an energetic penalty due to the increased number of edge atoms. This energetic trade-off between zigzag B edge and armchair edge coincides with the fact that the short (14,7) and (11,11) BNNT seed with large chiral angles broke in higher temperatures, as shown in Figure S5. Those edge preferences can contribute to the chirality preference of the BNNT, because the stable zigzag edge can appear more frequently in near-zigzag BNNTs. There have been experimental discussions about the chirality dependence of the growth of the BNNTs. Although previous experiments on CNT@BNNT did not show a clear tendency,26 there was a slightly higher count of near-zigzag BNNT reported compared to the others, although the count is not precise due to the limitations of TEM observation.28 Pristine SWBNNT growth is also reported to have a zigzag preference.47 The edge distribution, depending on the chirality, suggests that the near-zigzag BNNTs can grow with stable zigzag edges, which is consistent with the suspected near-zigzag SWBNNT preference in the experiments.

Edge preference seems to be revealed through a simple observation of the energetic stability of each edge type. However, this has been difficult for hBN and its derivatives, such as BNNT, because the lower symmetry of their structure makes it difficult to define edge energy for zigzag B or zigzag N.48,49 The edge energy is usually defined using the energy of the slab (Eslab) and the bulk (Ebulk) with the same number of atoms as ϵ = (Eslab – Ebulk)/L, where L denotes the length of the edge. For the zigzag nanoribbon of hBN, both edges always appear on each side (e.g., shown in Figure 4a). Thus, triangular flakes have been used as a slab with uniform zigzag edge, but the numbers of B and N atoms are not equal in this case. The bulk energy for the flakes requires a per-atom breakdown of bulk energy for each element, , where are the number of atoms of element i with j dangling bonds, and is the per-atom energy for each i and j. Because this disassembly to obtain the per-atom energy has been difficult, there remains condition-dependent parameter such as chemical potential to consider the energetic stability of hBN edges.49−51 We estimated the edge energy through the per-atom energy fitting as a function of the number of dangling bonds for each element. This per-atom energy approximation, which depends on the number of bonds, describes the total energy of ideal triangular flakes of BN structures with zigzag B, zigzag N, and armchair edges (Figure S7a–d). Figure 6 presents the edge energy of the ideal triangle BN flakes, which depends on the edge length L. The edge energy is defined as following the discussion in SI Section 8 according to the method by Fu and Zhang,52 where i represents the element (B or N). The energy parameter for each index was measured by linear regression for i and j (see Table S1 and Figure S7e). The zigzag B edge has a lower edge energy compared to other types under the interatomic potential, which indicates a structural preference driven by this energetic stability. This corresponds to the zigzag B edge preference of BNNT during growth in our MD simulation. Note that in the original study of this method by Fu and Zhang,52 the definition of edge energy is different, and we have followed the conventional discussion outlined above.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the BNNT growth on the CNT surface as a heteronanotube CNT@BNNT by molecular dynamics simulation using the ReaxFF potential. As a result, the BNNT nucleation process on CNTs was not observed in the 9 ns MD simulation with only an LJ interaction between BNNTs and CNTs, whereas the elongation from seed BNNTs was successfully observed during the MD simulations from the BN seeds with various chiral indices. The defect formation by the adsorbed precursor on the edge resulted in the construction of new hexagonal rings and a continual growth. The grown BNNT structure had sharply cut edges similar to those obtained by an experimental TEM observation, and the zigzag B edge most frequently appeared except for the unclassified edge in the grown BNNT structure. The edge energy of the clean zigzag B edge was smaller than that of the zigzag N and armchair edges, which indicated that the edge preference is driven by the thermodynamic stability of the edge. These results reveal the atomistic process of the open-edge, catalyst-free growth of CNT@BNNT.

Methods/Experimental

Computational Method of MD Simulation

In this study, atomic interactions among boron (B) and nitrogen (N) atoms are described using ReaxFF parameter sets,53 while Lennard–Jones parameters54 are utilized for interactions between B or N atoms and C atoms in SWCNT structures. The simulations were performed using the LAMMPS package55 with its ReaxFF implementation.56 Initially, BN fragments are supplied as B3N3 molecules in a rectangular cell (50 × 50 × 62.5 Å) with periodic boundary conditions, where a (6,6) CNT lies at the center (0,0,0) of the cell along the y axis, and unattached clusters or isolated B and N atoms are systematically removed. This setup emulates the conditions inside a CVD reactor. The supply rate of B3N3 molecules is one molecule per 50 ps. BN fragments straying near the boundaries of the x or z axes (20 ≤ |x|,|z| ≤ 25) are removed at the same rate, with six atoms deleted every 50 ps.

We investigated the growth of BNNT from initial seeds consisting of (6,6) CNT@(20,0) BNNT (3 unit cells), (6,6) CNT@(19,0) BNNT (3 unit cells), (5,5) CNT@(15,5) BNNT (1 unit cell), (6,6) CNT@(14,7) BNNT (1 unit cell), (6,6) CNT@(14,7) BNNT (2 unit cells) (T ≥ 1800 K), (6,6) CNT@(11,11) BNNT (3 unit cells), (6,6) CNT@(11,11) BNNT (6 unit cells) (T ≥ 1800 K) as is shown in Figure 7a,b,c,d,e,f,g, respectively, where they have appropriate intertube spacing of approximately 0.32–0.38 nm. Note that there is no obvious correlation of the chiralities between inner CNT and outer BNNT in previous experimental studies.26,28

For the simulations of BN nucleation using PFP shown in Figure 1 and Section 1 in SI, we employed PFP (v5.0.0, CRYSTAL_U0_PLUS_D3) model, which include D3 dispersion correction with Becke–Johnson damping function.57 All calculations using PFP are performed by Matlantis environment,58 which is a web service providing PFP calculation implemented in the LAMMPS package. The thermostat settings, simulation cell, and (6,6) CNT placed at the center are the same as those of the other simulations. The time step is 2 fs, and the B3N3 supply rate is one molecule every 2.0 ps.

Experimental Method

CNT@BNNT heterostructures were prepared through a low-pressure thermal CVD process using a boron–ammonia complex (H3NBH3) introduced as the BN precursor. The setup for the synthesis is similar to the apparatus shown in the previous works.26,28 The pristine SWCNT was placed in the center of the furnace. A 30-mg BN precursor was located upstream and heated to a temperature between 70 and 90 °C. The BN precursor vapor was then carried by a 300 sccm Ar flow (containing 3% H2) into the hot zone, where BNNTs were created on the surface of the SWCNTs. The reaction occurred at temperatures ranging from 1000 to 1300 °C and a chamber pressure of 300 Pa. The coating process varied between 30 min and 6 h. After 3 h of CVD, BNNTs had grown on the surface of most SWCNTs.

The conditions for the TEM measurements were as follows: HR-TEM images were captured using a JEM-2010F or JEM-2800 instrument at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. Selected area electron diffraction patterns of the entire film were obtained with the same TEMs, using a typical selected area aperture diameter of a few micrometers and a camera length of 60 cm. We obtained individual CNT@BNNT heterostructure images using a JEM-ARM200F instrument with a cold field-emission gun operating at 80 kV. Additionally, the samples were heated at 300 °C during the measurement to prevent carbon contamination using a heating holder (EM-31670SHTH) and a controller unit (EM-08170HCU).
