Societal costs associated with mothers of children with major congenital anomalies: a population-based matched cohort study in Denmark
Authors: Kyung Mi Kim, Erzsébet Horváth-Puhó, Aditya Narayan, Venus Wong, Niels Skipper, Nirav R Shah, Eyal Cohen, Henrik Toft Sørensen, Arnold Milstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the 10-year economic burden experienced by mothers of children with major congenital anomalies in Denmark.DesignPopulation-based matched retrospective cohort study.SettingDenmark, 1 January 2007 to 31 December 2017.ParticipantsAll women who gave birth to a singleton child with a major congenital anomaly during the study period (n=12 513). A comparison cohort of mothers with singleton births of children without major congenital anomalies was included, matched 1:10 by maternal age, parity and delivery year (n=125 008).Main outcome measuresSocietal costs associated with mothers of children with major congenital anomalies. Costs were quantified as direct healthcare costs (primary care, outpatient, inpatient, psychiatric services and prescriptions) and indirect costs (productivity losses estimated using the human capital approach, including income, labour market contributions, supplementary pensions and fringe benefits).ResultsDirect costs peaked in the delivery year with a mean attributable cost of more than €1731 (95% CI €1634 to €1824). Indirect costs increased over time, reaching a peak of €2251 (95% CI €978 to €3442) at 9 years after delivery. Over a median follow-up of 6.3 years, attributable indirect costs were two and a half times higher than direct costs. Total societal costs associated with the 12 513 mothers of children with major congenital anomalies in our cohort were estimated at €152 million (95% CI €92 million to €219 million).ConclusionsMothers of children with major congenital anomalies experienced substantial and persistent economic burdens, with indirect costs increasing over time and exceeding direct healthcare costs. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health and policy initiatives to support affected families and address long-term societal costs.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Prior research has documented the financial strain on mothers of children with chronic illnesses, including higher healthcare utilisation and disruptions to employment. However, the long-term societal costs borne by these mothers remain poorly understood. Most studies have focused on direct healthcare costs, been cross-sectional or relied on self-reported data and few have examined indirect costs such as income loss, thus risking underestimation of total societal costs.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We examined the societal costs, including both direct healthcare costs and indirect costs (ie, productivity losses), among mothers of children with major congenital anomalies in Denmark over a 10-year period. We found that indirect costs were two and a half times higher than direct costs, with total societal costs reaching €152 million. The financial burden was not shared equally; unmarried mothers and those with higher pre-pregnancy body mass index experienced a disproportionate share.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Addressing healthcare costs alone would not be sufficient to alleviate the economic burden on these mothers. Support needs change over time, from financial assistance during delivery and early postpartum to employment policies that may reduce longer-term indirect costs. Future research should continue to incorporate indirect costs in economic evaluations, examine these costs across different healthcare systems and evaluate the effectiveness of interventions for vulnerable groups, such as unmarried mothers.

Introduction

Mothers of children with major congenital anomalies (MCAs) experience extensive physical, emotional and financial stress.14 MCAs are structural or functional abnormalities identified prenatally, at birth or during infancy, with significant medical or surgical consequences and include conditions such as congenital heart defects, neural tube defects and chromosomal abnormalities.5 6 These mothers are more likely to experience poorer physical health, including higher rates of cardiac disease7 and mortality,8 increased healthcare use9 and weaker attachment to the labour market.10 These outcomes likely reflect the cumulative impact of having and raising a child with an MCA, including financial and emotional strain,1115 social isolation and stigma14 16 and feelings of guilt.17 18 This strain affects a considerable number of mothers,1922 given that children with MCAs are estimated to affect 2–3% of all live births in the USA and Europe.1 3 23 The care these mothers provided is vital. However, the increased and sustained demands associated with their children’s conditions may contribute to substantial costs for the mothers and society.9 13 22 While social support mechanisms exist, many of these costs remain only partially recognised or compensated.

Unfortunately, insufficient attention and effort have been directed towards supporting these mothers at the societal level.24 A comprehensive understanding of the economic burdens faced by mothers of children with MCA (MCMCA) could offer crucial insights to policymakers, aiding development of a supportive public health infrastructure and raising awareness about the urgent need for societal-level interventions. However, current economic evaluations of these costs often fall short. They primarily focus on immediate healthcare utilisation while neglecting longer-term costs,25 and rely on one-time measures or self-reported data subject to biases.22 26 As well, few evaluations examine indirect costs, thus risking underestimation of total societal costs. We therefore aimed to estimate the societal costs of maternal caregiving for children with MCAs over a 10-year period. We considered both direct healthcare costs and indirect costs, including impacts on wages, pensions and fringe benefits.

Methods

Study design, setting, data

We conducted a population-based matched cohort study in Denmark using data obtained between 2007 and 2017 from several linked national registries. Denmark provides free tax supported healthcare within a long-established welfare and social support system. We used the Danish Civil Registration System to create study cohorts with access to extensive demographic data and individual-level data linkage across various registries.27 Linked registries included the Danish Medical Birth Registry,28 permitting identification of all women who gave birth to a liveborn singleton, and the Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR),29 which encompasses care at all Danish hospitals. The DNPR made it possible to identify cohorts of children with major congenital anomalies and to collect information on maternal medical history. To estimate societal costs, we used five national registries providing individual-level data: the DNPR (for inpatient and outpatient care costs),29 the Danish National Health Service Registry (for primary and dental care costs),30 the Danish National Prescription Registry (for costs of redeemed prescriptions),31 the Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register (for mental health costs)32 and the Integrated Database for Labour Market Research database from Statistics Denmark (for indirect costs).33

The study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (online supplemental table 1).

Study cohorts

The analytical sample comprised 12 513 women who gave birth to a live singleton infant with MCA from 1 January 2007 to 31 December 2017. Infants with MCA were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes aligned with the European Surveillance of Congenital Anomalies Classification System.34 The ICD-10 codes used in the study are provided in the online supplemental table 2. For mothers who had multiple children with an MCA, the first affected pregnancy was considered the index pregnancy. We identified a comparison cohort of 125 008 women, using 10:1 random sampling with replacement, matching for maternal age at delivery, parity (1, 2 or ≥3 children) and infant’s year of birth. These variables are associated with both healthcare utilisation and labour market participation, and are well-established confounders in prior studies.810 Women in the two cohorts were followed until one of the following events occurred: reaching 31 December 2017; emigration; loss to follow-up; or death. Once a censoring event occurred, the individual was excluded from the analysis from that point forward. We excluded mothers with fewer than 2 years of data prior to giving birth, and those who had missing values for matching or key study variables. Online supplemental figure 1 shows the cohort selection process. Online supplemental table 3 presents the yearly number of observations for each cohort.

Outcome measures

MCMCA-associated societal cost was the primary outcome. Societal cost was quantified as the economic burden that included direct and indirect costs, but excluded intangible costs, such as the subjective burden (eg, emotional and psychological consequences) experienced by mothers. Each year, we calculated both the total costs and mean costs per person for each cohort. To determine the annual mean costs per cohort, we divided the sum of all individual total costs by the number of individuals in each cohort for each year.

Measurement of direct costs incorporated healthcare costs from primary, outpatient, inpatient and psychiatric care, as well as prescriptions all of which are captured with near-complete coverage by the Danish national registries. As Denmark has universal health coverage and no cost sharing for primary care or maternity care,35 we treated mothers without cost values in a specific year as having zero costs that year. For mothers observed and alive in Denmark, these zero-cost years reflect genuinely low or no healthcare utilisation rather than missing data.

To measure indirect costs, we followed the human capital approach.36 37 We defined indirect costs as productivity losses, measured as the difference in gross earned income between the MCMCA and comparison cohorts. Gross earned income was obtained from the Danish Integrated Database for Labour Market Research, which encompasses labour market contributions, labour market supplementary pensions and fringe benefits as a single composite measure (online supplemental table 4). The human capital approach was selected as the most widely used method for estimating indirect costs in cost-of-illness studies, and is consistent with the recommendations of Drummond et al (2015) for analyses adopting a societal perspective. This approach values productivity losses as foregone earnings over the full period of reduced or absent work and was therefore appropriate for our analysis of long-term (10-year) economic burden.

We focused on maternal costs because children with MCAs often require ongoing complex care, which disproportionately falls on mothers. This study extends our prior work examining healthcare utilisation and employment outcomes in MCMCA using the same Danish registry data.9 10

To account for inflation, we adjusted all costs to the 2018 DKK (Danish krone), using the Consumer Price Index from Statistics Denmark,38 and converted these costs to euro using the DKK to € exchange rate of 7.44.

Covariates

The following covariates were defined a priori: maternal age and marital status at the time of the index birth; individual-level income quartile and level of education in the year prior to the index birth; immigration status; pregnancy history (parity and pregnancy-related health conditions); and pre-birth health conditions (Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, mental illness) and body mass index (BMI) at the time of index birth. A list of ICD-8 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes used to determine the CCI score and the WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification codes used to identify prescriptions for treating pre-birth mental illness is provided in the online supplement.

Statistical analysis

We summarised the characteristics of the MCMCA and comparison cohorts descriptively. Standardised mean differences were used to compare groups, as they are less influenced by large sample sizes than significance tests.39 A standardised mean difference of 0.2 was used as the threshold for identifying meaningful differences between groups.

To estimate the economic burden associated with MCMCAs, we conducted a cost-of-illness analysis using a societal perspective,36 37 examining both direct and indirect costs annually. We first examined unadjusted annual mean direct costs and mean incomes in each cohort and by care setting. We then conducted covariate-adjusted analyses using a generalised linear model (GLM) with a gamma distribution and log link. SEs and 95% CIs were derived from 1000 bootstrap replications with person-level resampling. We used a one-part GLM to examine direct costs, given the near-universal prevalence of positive direct healthcare costs (98.2–100% depending on the year). Attributable direct costs were calculated as the adjusted difference in mean healthcare costs between the MCMCA and comparison cohorts. For indirect costs (productivity losses, measured as the difference in income between the MCMCA and comparison cohorts), we employed a two-part model to account for the substantial proportion of zero values in income data.4042 The first part used logistic regression to estimate the probability of having positive income. The second part predicted income conditional on positive values, using a GLM with gamma distribution and log link.

Additional analyses

We performed covariate-adjusted analyses of attributable direct costs and indirect costs by child age categories (0 to 1 year, >1 to 6 years (pre-school) and 7 to 10 years (primary education)). These categories align with Denmark’s policy of 52 weeks of paid maternal leave and the age cut-offs implemented by the Danish educational system, consistent with our previous study.9 10 For age-category analyses, we used ordinary least squares regression with log-transformed outcomes to estimate percentage differences in costs between cohorts. We chose relative rather than absolute measures for these additional analyses because the analyses aggregate costs over periods of varying duration (1, 5 and 4 years, respectively), and absolute cost differences would largely reflect differences in the length of each period rather than the relative economic burden. Percentage differences allow more intuitive comparisons of the magnitude of economic burden across age categories, complementing the year-by-year results from the main analyses. We then conducted stratified analyses, contrasting the MCMCA cohort with the comparison cohort within strata of marital status, maternal health, maternal mental health diagnoses and maternal BMI. To examine a potential dose-response relation between exposure and outcome, we stratified these additional analyses for attributable direct costs and indirect costs by the number of hospitalisations within the first year following birth (0, 1–3 or 4+), infant death within the first year and number of affected organ systems in the child (ie, single organ vs multiple organ congenital anomalies). Stratified analyses were conducted for the analyses by age-category rather than the year-by-year analysis to ensure adequate sample sizes within each stratum. We also performed a sensitivity analysis excluding mothers in the top 1% of costs, to address concerns regarding outliers.

Analyses were performed between January 2022 and April 2026 using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public directly participated in this retrospective cohort study using registry data. Although the study was initiated before patient and public involvement became common practice, the registry data reflect population-level information, representing an indirect involvement of patients and the public.

Results

Characteristics of study cohorts

In both the MCMCA and comparison cohorts, the majority of mothers were married or in a registered partnership (50.7% vs 51.0%) and employed (75.0% vs 77.7%), with a median age of 31 years at the time of the index delivery (IQR 27–35 years) (table 1). The only meaningful difference between cohorts was marital status (standardised mean difference=0.25): mothers in the MCMCA cohort tended to be single, divorced, widowed or living alone (49.2% vs 45.8%). Although mothers in the MCMCA cohort, in comparison to their matched counterparts, had lower education levels (primary or secondary education as the highest level of education, 30.8% vs 28.7%), a higher prevalence of comorbidities (CCI score 2+, 2.2% vs 1.5%) and a more prevalent pre-birth history of mental health diagnoses (24.9% vs 21.4%), these differences were small.

Direct costs

The highest mean direct costs per person were incurred during the year of delivery, both in the MCMCA and comparison cohorts (€8081 and €6353, respectively) (online supplemental figure 2). These costs decreased substantially in the first year after delivery, then slightly increased in the second and third years compared with the first year after delivery. Over time, the comparison cohort showed a continued decline in these costs. In contrast, the MCMCA cohort experienced a rebound in mean direct costs 7–8 years after delivery. Unadjusted mean attributable direct costs per person were highest in the year of delivery (€1727), declined sharply in the first year after delivery (€533) and remained relatively stable in subsequent years (online supplemental figure 3). By care setting, outpatient care had the highest mean attributable direct costs per person during the year of delivery (€933), followed by inpatient settings (€720) (online supplemental figure 4). Total direct costs by care setting are presented in online supplemental table 5. Direct costs decreased in the following years. However, the MCMCA cohort showed a resurgence in direct costs during the 7th and 8th years post-delivery, particularly within inpatient and psychiatric settings. Total direct costs attributed to the MCMCA cohort were highest in outpatient (€11.7 million) and inpatient (€9 million) settings during the delivery year. Mental healthcare costs peaked at €0.6 million 4 years post-delivery, declined thereafter and rose again to €0.4 million in the 8th year post-delivery.

After covariate adjustment, the mean attributable direct costs per person in the MCMCA cohort peaked during the year of delivery at €1731 (95% CI €1634 to €1824), followed by a substantial decrease in subsequent years (figure 1). 8 years post-delivery, the costs rose again to €470 (95% CI €228 to €713), approaching the level observed 1 year after delivery. The total attributable direct costs were €44.2 million (95% CI €35.0 million to €53.9 million) over a median follow-up of 6.3 years (IQR 3.6–9.1 years), varying by post-delivery year. The total attributable direct costs peaked during the delivery year at €21.6 million (95% CI €20.4 million to €22.8 million), followed by a decline with greater year-to-year variability (online supplemental figure 5).

Indirect costs

The MCMCA cohort had consistently lower mean gross earned income than the comparison cohort. The gross earned income gap started widening 2 years post-delivery, continued to expand until 7 years post-delivery and then stabilised (online supplemental figure 6). Unadjusted mean attributable indirect costs per person increased progressively from €1155 in the year of delivery to €2275 by 10 years after delivery, reflecting a steady widening of the gross earned income gap between the MCMCA and comparison cohorts over time (online supplemental figure 7).

After covariate adjustment, the mean indirect costs per person were €1151 (95% CI €726 to €1593) during the year of delivery. These costs generally increased over the follow-up period, reaching a peak of €2251 (95% CI €978 to €3442) at 9 years after delivery and remained at similar levels at 10 years after delivery (figure 1). The total indirect costs were €108.1 million (95% CI €56.5 million to €164.7 million) over a median follow-up of 6.3 years. These costs were the highest during the year of delivery at €14.4 million (95% CI €9.1 million to €19.9 million) and remained above €10 million annually through 5 years after delivery, declining thereafter (online supplemental figure 8).

Results from the covariate-adjusted analysis by child age groups show that indirect costs were higher during the 1–6 years post-delivery in the MCMCA cohort than in the comparison cohort, indicating a productivity loss of 4% (95% CI 1% to 7%) (figure 2). Indirect costs did not differ between MCMCA and the comparison cohorts in the year of delivery and during the 7–10 years post-delivery period.

Societal costs associated with MCMCAs

The mean societal cost associated with MCMCAs ranged from €1649 per person in the first year after delivery to €2882 per person during the year of delivery, totaling €21 522 (95% CI €5289 to €38 122) per person during the study years. The annual total societal costs fluctuated between €36.1 million (95% CI €29.5 to €42.8 million) during the year of delivery and €2.6 million (95% CI €0.6 to €4.6 million) 10 years post-delivery, averaging €13.8 million per year (table 2). Over median follow-up of 6.3 years, the total societal costs associated with MCMCAs in Denmark were €152.2 million (95% CI €91.5 to €218.6 million). These costs were largely driven by indirect expenses related to lost income and productivity among MCMCAs, accounting for up to 92% of total societal costs (table 3). Attributable indirect costs exceeded direct costs in every year except the year of delivery, ranging from approximately 2 to 12 times greater (tables2 3). The total attributable indirect costs (€108.1 million) were two and a half times greater than the total attributable direct costs (€44.1 million).

Stratified analysis

Direct costs were higher in the MCMCA cohort than in the comparison cohort for both mothers who were married or in a registered partnership and those who were not. Among mothers who were married or in a registered partnership, indirect costs did not differ between the MCMCA and comparison cohorts. However, for unmarried mothers, MCMCAs had 6% greater indirect costs during the 1–6 years post-delivery (95% CI 1% to 10%). Additionally, MCMCAs with a pre-birth BMI greater than 30 experienced 9% greater indirect costs during the 1–6 years following delivery than members of the comparison cohort (95% CI 0% to 16%).

Direct costs were similarly increased among MCMCAs regardless of maternal mental health condition (online supplemental table 6). No difference in gross earned income was observed for either group (online supplemental table 7). MCMCAs whose children had zero hospitalisations had the largest direct cost difference in the delivery year (26%; 95% CI 24% to 28%) compared with those with 1–3 and 4+ hospitalisations (9% (95% CI 8% to 10%) and 7% (95% CI 4% to 11%), respectively) (online supplemental table 8). No difference in gross earned income was observed across hospitalisation groups (online supplemental table 9). We did not find a difference in direct costs or gross earned income between cohorts based on infant mortality within the first year (online supplemental tables 10 and 11). Mothers of children with multiple MCAs had higher direct costs than those with a single MCA in the delivery year (41% (95% CI 37% to 44%) vs 19% (95% CI 18% to 20%)), with both remaining elevated during years 1–6 (online supplemental table 12). Gross earned income reductions during years 1–6 were observed only among mothers of children with a single MCA (3%; 95% CI 0% to 6%), with no difference among those with multiple MCAs (online supplemental table 13).

Sensitivity analysis

When we excluded the mothers with the top 1% of costs, our findings remained consistent. During both the year of delivery and the subsequent 1–6 years, the MCMCA cohort faced higher direct costs (online supplemental table 14). Furthermore, the MCMCA cohort also experienced greater indirect costs in the 1–6 years post-delivery than did the comparison cohort (online supplemental table 15).

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive examination of the societal costs associated with MCMCAs in Denmark. By examining both direct healthcare costs and indirect costs, we captured healthcare expenses as well as losses in gross earned income and employment-related benefits (ie, labour market supplementary pensions, fringe benefits) over a decade. MCMCAs incurred higher direct costs, which decreased after delivery, though mental healthcare expenses rose in later years. Indirect costs, reflecting these income and benefit losses, increased progressively over time and notably surpassed direct healthcare costs.

Meaning of results

These findings demonstrate the substantial economic burden associated with MCMCAs. Between 2007 and 2017, the cohort of 12 513 children with MCAs, representing approximately 0.2% of Denmark’s population,43 accounted for societal costs of €152.2 million over a median follow-up of 6.3 years. Although these mothers represent a small fraction of the population, the attributable societal costs represent approximately 0.5% of Denmark’s annual healthcare expenditure in 2018,44 highlighting the substantial financial burden borne by affected families and society. While this figure may appear modest at first glance, it likely underestimates the true societal burden. Our analysis did not include maternal quality of life, direct costs for children with MCA, or costs for fathers of these children, factors that would further raise the total cost. This potential underestimation is particularly relevant in the context of Denmark’s extensive health and social benefits, which are not matched in most other countries, such as the USA. Although our estimates were specific to Denmark, prompting caution about generalisability, it is likely that societal costs would be higher in nations with fewer welfare supports.

We found that direct healthcare costs varied by care settings and child age. Physical health-related costs peaked within the first year post partum, likely reflecting the prenatal and postpartum care demands, then declined before rising again 5–6 years later. Mental health-related costs increased steadily, peaking at 4–8 years after delivery, likely reflecting the ongoing psychological and emotional challenges faced as children grow. These shifting cost patterns underscore the need for tailored interventions that address the evolving needs of MCMCAs.

Comparison with other studies

Our societal costs estimates align with earlier research highlighting the economic implications associated with providing support to individuals with complex health needs, such as for individuals with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementia,45 stroke46 or cancer.47 48 However, this is the first study to quantify the financial impact associated with raising a child with an MCA from a societal perspective. The economic strain, manifested through heightened maternal healthcare utilisation, considerable income reduction and reduced productivity, echoes findings from previous studies in different caregiving domains.

Our findings highlight the long-term financial implications often overlooked in prior research focused on short-term costs.9 13 Importantly, indirect costs, such as gross earned income loss, greatly exceed direct healthcare costs. This points to the need for support mechanisms and policies that address both direct medical and broader economic challenges faced by families.

The finding that mothers whose children with MCAs had zero hospitalisations in the first year had the largest direct costs compared with those whose children had 1–3 or 4+ hospitalisations may seem paradoxical. This may reflect that mothers of sicker children requiring more hospitalisations have less time for their own healthcare utilisation, or the heterogeneity of MCAs that may systematically differ in terms of child hospitalisations. Gross earned income reductions during years 1–6 were observed among mothers of children with a single MCA but not among those with multiple MCAs, which likely reflects limited statistical power in the multiple MCA subgroup rather than an absence of effect.

Our study underscores health equity concerns. We observed greater productivity loss among unmarried mothers of children with MCAs and those with a pre-birth BMI exceeding 30, suggesting intensified economic strain in the absence of partner support and in the presence of compounding health and socioeconomic factors. The finding among unmarried mothers warrants further exploration. While one might expect that unmarried mothers would be less able to reduce paid work as sole income providers, our findings suggest the opposite. Several factors may explain this apparent paradox. Unmarried MCMCA may lack the partner support for children with MCA to maintain continuous employment, leading to involuntary workforce exits or reduced hours despite financial need, as demonstrated in our prior work.10 Without a co-parent to share the responsibilities of raising a child with a chronic health condition, those mothers may face conflicts that are particularly incompatible with employment, especially in occupations with limited flexibility. Moreover, unmarried mothers in our cohort had lower baseline education and income levels than the comparison cohort, despite the difference being small, which may be related to occupations offering fewer accommodations, such as flexible work arrangements. These findings are consistent with the concept of the ‘triple bind’ facing single-parent families, in which inadequate resources, constrained employment and insufficient policy support interact to produce greater economic hardship and poorer employment outcomes.49 Similar patterns of increased poverty risk among single mothers have been documented across affluent countries, including in Europe, the UK and the USA, even after accounting for individual-level characteristics.50 Unpaid care burdens often disproportionately fall on women and single-parent mothers,49 51 reflecting societal expectations and limited alternative supports. These results align with existing evidence showing greater burdens on single parents of children with chronic illnesses.52 Recognising these frequently overlooked economic pressures is crucial for designing targeted, equitable strategies. National initiatives, such as the special care allowance in Finland, offer an example of such support.53

Policy implications

Our findings have several policy implications. The substantial societal costs associated with MCMCAs point to the importance of support measures. Economic support policies, such as caregiver allowances that partially offset income loss, may be essential in countries where such mechanisms are not yet established. While our findings are specific to the Danish context, they may offer insights for other policymakers in identifying priority populations and allocating resources. Ensuring access to affordable and quality healthcare with a long-term focus can mitigate the direct healthcare costs faced by these mothers. Importantly, support needs evolve over time, requiring different types of assistance at various stages. For instance, financial assistance during the delivery and early post partum, followed by support for workforce participation and income security later on. Employment policies, such as flexible hours, telework, job protection or vocational training, could help reduce long-term indirect costs. We observed an escalating productivity loss among MCMCAs, which outpaced direct healthcare costs. Healthcare demands and children’s evolving needs may further contribute to reduced maternal productivity. Finally, our findings emphasise the importance of considering indirect costs in economic evaluations. Doing so can inform more effective resource allocation and help design policies that better support families raising children with MCAs.

We were unable to capture additional factors that may influence stress or health-related outcomes, such as access to social support or caregiving responsibilities for siblings or elderly family members. Nor were we able to quantify the costs associated with reductions in quality of life among affected mothers. Costs related to increased mortality and the associated long-term productivity losses from premature death were also not included. Some additional cost categories may not be fully captured by the registries, including private healthcare expenditures (eg, dental care, physiotherapy or psychotherapy services not covered by public insurance), over-the-counter medications and informal care provided by family members. Importantly, our cost estimates do not account for public expenditures incurred by the Danish welfare system (ie, public transfer payments), including health-related benefits (eg, sick leave, disability pensions), educational support benefits (eg, educational leave) or other forms of social assistance (eg, cost of job training for immigrant mothers). In some categories, analytical limitations reflect the scope of our dataset, which captures duration but not the amount of associated expenditures. In other categories, limitations were self-imposed based on theoretical considerations. For example, in keeping with traditional economic analysis, health-related public expenditures other than payments for healthcare are not regarded as direct economic costs, since they reflect a redistribution of resources.54

Mothers of children with MCAs may themselves have a higher prevalence of genetic conditions or shared environmental exposures that contribute both to their child’s anomaly and to their own health and economic outcomes. Additionally, the MCMCA cohort had higher rates of mental health diagnoses and lower educational attainment at baseline, which could partially account for the observed differences. To address these concerns, we adjusted our analyses for maternal health conditions, including pregnancy history, comorbidities, BMI and pre-birth mental health diagnoses. Nonetheless, we were unable to account for potential confounders such as family structure and environmental exposures, which may contribute to maternal health and economic burden. Our study is limited to liveborn children, as terminated pregnancies are not captured. In Denmark, prenatal screening is routine and termination for fetal anomalies is permitted. Mothers who continued a pregnancy with a known anomaly may differ systematically from those who did not, potentially introducing selection bias that we are unable to address.

Our use of individual-level gross earned income may not fully capture household economic dynamics, as changes in a partner’s income or household composition could influence the overall financial impact on these families. Moreover, differences in gross earned income may reflect not only productivity losses but also voluntary parental decisions to adjust labour market participation associated with having a child with complex health needs. Additionally, the human capital approach may overestimate indirect costs relative to the friction cost approach, which restricts productivity losses to the period required to replace a worker. However, the friction cost approach may be less suitable for capturing the long-term individual earnings differences associated with having a child with an MCA when reduced labour market participation persists over several years. Given the 10-year follow-up and our focus on maternal earnings, we considered the human capital approach the more appropriate primary method. Future studies could employ the friction cost method as a complementary analysis to provide a range of indirect cost estimates.

This study focused on costs incurred by mothers. While the economic burden associated with raising a child with an MCA may extend to fathers, examining paternal costs was beyond the scope of this study. Future studies could examine costs incurred by both parents to provide a more comprehensive picture of the household-level economic burden. We estimated the overall societal costs associated with MCMCAs as a group rather than by specific anomaly subtype. Examining cost differences across organ system categories could help determine which anomaly types contribute most to the economic burden and inform targeted resource allocation.

Our cost data extend through 2017, the most recent data accessible at the time of analysis. While more recent data would be valuable, the rigour of our study design and analytical methods supports the relevance of the findings and patterns observed for current policy and practice. Finally, while our cost estimates are specific to the Danish context, where universal healthcare and extensive social welfare benefits are provided, several findings may apply to other high-income settings. The temporal patterns we observed—direct costs peaking in the delivery year and declining thereafter, with indirect costs rising progressively over time—are likely common to mothers of children with MCAs across settings, even if the absolute magnitudes differ. In countries without universal healthcare, families may face greater out-of-pocket direct costs, potentially narrowing the gap between direct and indirect costs. In countries with less generous parental leave and workplace policies, productivity losses may be greater. Even so, indirect costs are likely to remain the larger share, as observed in our cohort. The greater productivity loss observed among unmarried mothers may also be amplified in settings with fewer publicly funded childcare options. Our absolute cost estimates are Denmark-specific, but the patterns we observed and their policy implications are relevant to other high-income contexts. Future research in other settings is warranted.

Conclusion

This study refines estimates of the societal costs associated with mothers of children with MCAs, emphasising that indirect costs increase over time and exceed direct healthcare costs. The significant economic burden unveiled by our study calls for a comprehensive public health approach that addresses both healthcare and economic dimensions to lessen the strain on affected mothers and society. Our findings may direct policy and programme prioritisation across healthcare and economic systems to more effectively respond to these challenges.
