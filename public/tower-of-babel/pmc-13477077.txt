Outcomes following the first 100 cases of a new robotic mitral valve program
Authors: Omar Toubat, Fotios Pitoulis, Jonathan Szeto, Michael Catalano, Max Shin, Amit Iyengar, Paul N Fiorilli, Matthew A Jolley, Michael A Acker, Wilson Y Szeto, Michael E Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWidespread adoption of robotic mitral surgery has been limited owing to challenges associated with initiating a robotic program. We report outcomes from the first 100 consecutive patients undergoing robotic mitral surgery at our institution.MethodsData were collected retrospectively and analyzed for 100 consecutive patients who underwent robotic mitral valve surgery between March 2022 and February 2025. Categorical variables are reported as count and percentage; continuous data, as median and interquartile range (IQR).ResultsThe median patient age was 63.6 years (IQR, 55-71 years) and Society of Thoracic Surgeons–predicted mortality was 0.55% (range, 0.1%-9.2%). The median body mass index was 26 (IQR, 24-31), and 4% of the patients had a severe pectus deformity. Most patients had isolated posterior prolapse (67%), followed by bileaflet prolapse (27%) and isolated anterior prolapse (3%). Ten patients (10%) underwent 11 prior cardiac interventions, including coronary artery bypass grafting in 2, mitral valve repair/replacement in 5, atrial septal defect/patent foramen ovale repair in 2, mechanical root/ascending/hemi-arch in repair in 1, and mitral balloon valvuloplasty in 1. Cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp times were 172 (IQR, 138-202) minutes and 111 (IQR, 90-132) minutes, respectively. There were no sternotomy conversions or failed repair attempts in the last 40 cases. Of 92 attempted repairs, 91 (99%) were successful with mild or less mitral regurgitation. The median intensive care unit length of stay was 1.3 (IQR, 1-2) days, and the median hospital stay was 5.3 (IQR, 4-6) days, and there were no strokes, unplanned coronary interventions, or 30-day deaths.ConclusionsRobotic mitral surgery can yield satisfactory outcomes early in a program's experience. High repair rates and low morbidity rates are attainable in patients with conventional relative contraindications to robotic approaches, including those with previous cardiac surgery, complex mitral valve pathology, and severe chest wall deformities.Key Words: mitral valve disease, robotic mitral valve surgery, mitral regurgitation, degenerative mitral valve disease, minimally invasive cardiac surgery

Central Message

Robotic mitral valve surgery can be performed with acceptable repair rates and low morbidity rates early in a program’s experience even in patients with relative contraindications to robotic surgery.

Perspective

There has been increasing interest in minimally invasive and robotic approaches to mitral valve surgery. This series reflects the first 100 robotic mitral valve operations performed at our institution and demonstrates the safety, feasibility, and technical learning curve of robotic mitral surgery even in a relatively high percentage of patients with relative contraindications to a robotic approach.

Robotic-assisted mitral valve repair (MVr) is a well-established approach to surgical correction of degenerative mitral regurgitation (MR).1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 However, in a recent analysis of the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Adult Cardiac Surgical Database from 2015 to 2021, Mori and colleagues8 found that robotic-assisted approaches were used in only 14.6% of all isolated MVr cases in the United States, with only a 3.7% increase in volume over the study period. By comparison, a minimally invasive thoracotomy approach was pursued in 27.2% of isolated MVr operations, with a 7.8% increase over the same period. Despite evidence suggesting shorter hospital stays, fewer postoperative complications, lower blood transfusion requirements, and improved cosmesis following robotic-assisted MVr compared to sternotomy,8 there remain significant challenges limiting its uptake. An understanding of the technical and programmatic challenges associated with initiating a new robotic mitral program is an important barrier to facilitating continued uptake of this approach.

The University of Pennsylvania made the strategic decision to launch a dedicated robotic MV surgery program in 2022, with the goal of offering the full spectrum of surgical approaches to MV patients.9 The primary objective of the present study was to evaluate surgical outcomes, repair rate, intraoperative complication events, and trends in operative times associated with the first 100 consecutive robotic MV operations performed at our institution. Secondary objectives included characterizing programmatic evolution in case volume, patient selection, and clinical outcomes with the goal of identifying key barriers experienced when scaling a new robotic mitral program.

Methods

Patient Selection

From March 2022 to February 2025, the first 100 consecutive patients undergoing robotic MV surgery by a single surgeon (MEI) at Pennsylvania Presbyterian Hospital were included in this study. The study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania (850427, January 25, 2022), with a waiver of informed consent. Baseline characteristics were collected retrospectively from chart review. Additional preoperative variables included echocardiographic parameters of MV pathology, other concomitant valvular abnormalities, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). All patients included in our robotic MV program undergo multidisciplinary team review and a standardized preoperative workup that includes coronary and carotid assessment, as well as computed tomography angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis to rule out significant peripheral vascular disease or tortuosity. During the first 50 cases of this series, patients were carefully selected to include those undergoing first-time heart surgery with less complex mitral pathology. After the first 50 cases were performed, our program gradually expanded to include patients with a history of cardiac surgery, more complex mitral pathology (ie, isolated anterior or bileaflet/Barlow pathology), and severe chest wall deformities.

Operative Technique

Following induction and intubation with a double-lumen tube with anesthesia, patients receive bilateral upper extremity arterial lines. Ports are placed in standard fashion, with the left robotic arm in the second or third interspace, the right arm in the sixth interspace, the service and camera ports in the fourth interspace, and the left atrial retractor in the fifth interspace (Figure E1). Initially, the service port incision was 3 to 4 cm long, but with increasing experience, this incision has been shortened to 1.5 to 2 cm. Routinely, our team implements peripheral nerve blocks and cryoablation of intercostal nerves for additional pain control. All patients in this series underwent percutaneous cannulation for peripheral cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) via the femoral artery and vein. For patients undergoing aortic occlusion with the endoballoon, this is advanced and positioned under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance. Our technique for positioning the endoballoon has been described previously, and we have found this method to be particularly helpful for redo cases.10 A superior vena cava cannula is placed by the anesthesia team via percutaneous access of the right internal jugular vein. All patients in this series were cooled to 32 °C for isolated mitral surgery and received antegrade cardioplegia. After careful dissection and entry into the left atrium, a comprehensive valve analysis is performed in each patient to determine repairability.

Operative Details

Intraoperative details included CPB time, aortic occlusion time, MVr technique, and concomitant procedures. In addition, intraoperative complications, including the need for conversion to mini-sternotomy or sternotomy, unanticipated coronary intervention, new aortic valve insufficiency or need for aortic valve intervention, and conversion to MV replacement (MVR), were recorded. Intraoperative TEE was used to record postintervention MR severity, valve gradients, clinically significant systolic anterior motion of the MV, and LVEF. Postoperative outcomes included mortality, major morbidity and complication events, reinterventions, intensive care unit and hospital length of stay, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) assessments.

Statistical Analysis

Preoperative demographic and clinical characteristics, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical data were reported as frequency and percentage and compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were reported as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data normality. Time-to-event analyses were performed to evaluate overall survival and freedom from moderate or greater MR after MVr. The technical learning curve was evaluated by comparing CPB and aortic cross-clamp times throughout the series. All statistical tests were performed in SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute) and were 2-sided, with P < .05 serving as the threshold for significance.

Results

Study Cohort

Between March 2022 and February 2025, 100 consecutive patients underwent robotic-assisted MV surgery at our institution. Annual robotic case volume increased significantly over the study period (Figure 1). The study cohort had a median age of 63.6 (IQR, 54.4-70.6) years at operation, a median BMI of 26.3 (IQR, 23.8-31.2), and a median Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) of 0.55% (range, 0.1-9.2%) (Table 1). Most patients presented with New York Heart Association class II heart failure symptoms (n = 83, 83%) and relatively preserved LVEF (60%; SD 55-65%). MV pathology included isolated posterior prolapse in 67 patients (67%), bileaflet prolapse in 27 (27%), and isolated anterior leaflet prolapse in 3 (3%) (Table 1).

Ten patients (10%) in our series had a history of 11 previous cardiac interventions. These included prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with bilateral internal mammary arteries (n = 2), MVr/MVR (n = 5), atrial septal defect/patent foramen ovale (PFO) repair (n = 2), mechanical root/ascending/hemi-arch reconstruction (n = 1), and MV balloon valvuloplasty (n = 1). Of note, 2 patients in this series had a history of MVr and underwent successful re-repair robotically.

Intraoperative Outcomes

Of the 92 patients in this series who underwent intended MVr, 91 were successfully repaired (repair rate, 99%). The median total CPB time was 172 (IQR, 138-202) minutes and median total aortic cross-clamp/occlusion time was 111 (IQR, 90-132) minutes. Concomitant procedures included the maze procedure (n = 17; 17%), left atrial appendage ligation (n = 16; 16%), and PFO closure (n = 15; 15%). Postintervention TEE parameters are shown in Table 2.

Overall, 4 patients underwent a conversion to sternotomy or partial sternotomy, with no conversions occurring in the final 40 cases. Reasons for conversion included chest wall bleeding (n = 2) and aortic valve repair for new-onset aortic insufficiency (n = 2). One patient underwent an unplanned MVR for the presence of continued residual MR after 2 attempts were made to repair the valve. Two patients were found to have systolic anterior motion physiology requiring a second cross-clamp period, both of whom ultimately achieved a successful repair.

To evaluate the technical learning curve in this series, the total CPB and aortic cross-clamp/occlusion times were compared by quartile. Postoperative Outcomes

The median length of stay was 1.3 (IQR, 1.0-2.1) days in the intensive care unit and 5.3 (IQR, 4.3-6.6) days in the hospital. Postoperative complications are shown in Table 3. Twenty-seven patients (27%) had new-onset atrial fibrillation, 6 (6%) had pleural effusions necessitating thoracentesis, and 4 (4%) had pericarditis. Three patients required surgical reintervention for bleeding during the index hospitalization, including 2 mediastinal explorations and 1 pericardial window. No patients experienced a stroke with clinical/residual deficits in the postoperative period. Thirty-day mortality was 0%. Over a median follow-up of 8.0 (IQR, 2.1-18.3) months, 2 patients underwent robotic reintervention for recurrent MR, and 1 patient died due to hemorrhagic complications associated with therapeutic anticoagulation following mechanical MVR (Table 4). Figure 3 shows the overall survival and freedom from moderate or greater MR at follow-up. At their most recent TTE assessment, all patients continued to have mild or less MR (Figure E2).

Discussion

Robotic MV surgery has emerged as a proven approach to surgically address degenerative MR.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7,11 Despite reported patient and surgeon benefits, there remains limited uptake of robotic MV surgery nationally.8 Widespread adoption has been limited by the perceived challenges associated with robotic surgery and the institution of a robotic valve program.12 In the present study, we demonstrate that a robotic program can be safely initiated and scaled with acceptable outcomes early in the programmatic learning curve. Our data demonstrate high repair rates, low complication rates, and iterative improvements in operative times despite a gradual increase in patient complexity and the inclusion of patients with relative contraindications to a robotic approach.

In recent decades, several large center series have reported excellent repair rates with robotic MV surgery for degenerative MR, ranging from 97% to 100%.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 In addition to the enhanced visualization of the entire MV complex and technical precision afforded through a robotic approach, such repair rates likely are also due to the significant experience at these centers of excellence.8 The present study demonstrates similar success with robotic repair compared to prior studies, with a 99% repair rate in the first 100 cases of our program. While efforts to preferentially select simpler degenerative MR pathologies were pursued early in our series, there was a gradual expansion to correct more complex pathologies with increasing surgeon and program experience.

In the single case of repair failure, the valve was noted to have significant myxomatous degeneration with bileaflet involvement with multisegment scallop and multiple regurgitant jets. After initial repair with a 34-mm annuloplasty band, 4 neochordae to the P2 segment, and 3 interrupted sutures for cleft closure, the patient had residual MR when the clamp was removed. A second attempt at repair was made by closing residual clefts along P2-P3, but this was unsuccessful, and the decision was made to replace the valve.

Despite this repair failure, overall, ∼30% of patients in our series had bileaflet/Barlow pathology or isolated anterior leaflet disease, with comparable rates of repairability. In addition to MV complexity, there was iterative expansion to offer robotic surgery to patients with greater comorbidity burden, higher STS-PROM score, severe chest wall deformities, and the need for reoperative cardiac surgery. This differentiates the present series from other series with similar case volumes in that it is not highly restrictive with patient selection, reflecting the general evolution and standardization of robotic MV surgery in recent years. Although our robotic MV program is new, our institution has served as a high-volume center for MV surgery through both sternotomy (∼500 cases annually) and minimally invasive approaches (∼80 cases annually). We believe that such institutional experience has been critically important in building the requisite understanding of MV pathology and advanced surgical repair techniques that can be translated to the robotic platform. In addition, the operating surgeon in this series completed a dedicated 3-month robotic fellowship after residency training to gain specific expertise with this approach. Overall, the outcomes and trends in this study underscore that after gaining sufficient surgeon and team experience, successful MVr can be achieved for a diverse spectrum of patients with the full complement of degenerative MR pathologies through a robotic approach.

An important and unique aspect of this early institutional series is the relatively high percentage of reoperative cardiac surgical patients. Traditionally, redo cardiac surgery has been viewed as a relative contraindication for a robotic approach.13,14 To this end, rates of redo robotic MV surgery have ranged from 0% to 8.4% in published series.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 By comparison, in the present series, patients undergoing reoperative cardiac surgery composed 10% of our entire cohort. As expected, most of these cases (70%) occurred in the second half of the series. Among the first 50 cases, reoperative patients included 2 patients who underwent open atrial septal defect/PFO repair and 1 patient who underwent prior MVr. In the latter 50 cases, reoperative patients included 2 patients who underwent prior MVr, 1 patient who underwent MVR, 1 patient who underwent prior minimally invasive MVr followed by redo MVR, 2 patients who underwent bilateral internal mammary artery CABG, and 1 patient who underwent mechanical root with ascending/hemiarch reconstruction. In our experience, we have found that the robotic approach confers many benefits in the reoperative setting, as it provides the opportunity for safe entry into a chest that otherwise is complicated by dense adhesions or high risk of cardiac injury during sternal reentry, allows for excellent exposure and visualization of the MV with limited dissection, and facilitates quick postoperative recovery.

One unique benefit of the robotic approach is the use of the endoballoon for aortic occlusion, which limits the extent of dissection required in redo cases. For this reason, the endoballoon was used in all redo cases (as well as 91% of our cohort overall). Other groups have described similar advantages for minimally invasive thoracotomy MV surgery compared to sternotomy in the reoperative setting.15 In addition, the combination of peripheral cannulation and use of the endoballoon for aortic occlusion allows for CPB and cardioplegic arrest without the need to extensively dissect out cardiac structures, further reducing the risks associated with reoperative cardiac surgery. Taken together, these outcomes suggest that a robotic approach is both safe and feasible in reoperative patients after a standardized approach has been established.

One of the main objectives of the present study was to evaluate the technical learning curve in this series. While average CPB times were not found to be statistically significantly improved between quartile 1 (cases 1-25, 189 minutes) and quartile 4 (cases 75-100, 166 minutes), there was a significant improvement in aortic occlusion time (cases 1-25, 148 minutes; cases 75-100, 106 minutes; P = .010). Notably, this improvement in aortic occlusion/cross-clamp times occurred despite the gradually increasing complexity of cases and selection of patients as described above.

While the exact number of robotic cases required to achieve technical and programmatic proficiency is variable, similar findings regarding the number of cases needed to demonstrate technical proficiency (∼50 cases) have been described by others.16, 17, 18 Some studies suggest that these improvements continue to extend well into the first 200 to 400 cases in a robotic series.17,19 Our institutional data support this and demonstrate continued technical improvements associated with cases 125 to 150. In addition to operative times, our series also showed a concomitant reduction in the frequency of intraoperative complications with no unplanned replacements or intraoperative conversions in the final 40 cases. Moreover, the 2 cases of early repair failure also occurred early in this series. One case was due to ruptured posterior leaflet neochordae leading to flail leaflet, while the other was due to progressive anterior leaflet prolapse in a patient who underwent anterior leaflet neochordae. Taken together, these data underscore the importance of surgeon and operating room team experience for these patients.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center, single-surgeon experience with robotic MV surgery, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Second, the results presented here reflect a follow-up period of only 8 months. While this period is informative for evaluating early technical results and complications with the robotic approach, it precludes the ability to assess longer-term endpoints in repair durability and survival. Third, although the patient cohort reflects consecutive cases at a single center, there has been an evolution in the patient selection criteria during this series. Additional evaluation of outcomes with increased case complexity and greater frequency of reoperative surgery is warranted. Finally, these data reflect a single cohort assessment and lack comparisons to a contemporaneous cohort of open or minimally invasive mitral patients.

In conclusion, our early experience demonstrates that a robotic MV program can be safely established and scaled at a high-volume center. Early patient selection is important, but gradual and deliberate expansion to more complex patients and MV pathologies can occur with experience. Our experience suggests that early, iterative increases in complexity may be necessary to obtain adequate volume to support program growth and evolution of the learning curve. The data demonstrate that high repair rates and low morbidity rates are attainable even in patients with relative contraindications to a robotic approach early in the series, including those with higher STS-PROM scores, chest wall deformities, complex MV disease, and reoperative cardiac surgery (Figure 4).
