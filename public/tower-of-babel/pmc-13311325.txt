Traumatic appendicitis: A case report and literature review
Authors: Visarat Palitnonkiat, Sirote Wongwaisayawan, Tharin Thampongsa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The primary cause of acute appendicitis is obstruction of the proximal lumen of the appendix, although the underlying etiology remains unclear in many cases. Trauma is a rare but documented cause of appendicitis.We present the case of a 38-year-old man who developed right lower quadrant abdominal pain four days after jumping from the second floor of a department store. He reported no prior episode of lower abdominal pain. Abdominal computer tomography (CT) confirmed the diagnosis of acute appendicitis, and the patient subsequently underwent an emergency appendectomy.This case report and literature review highlight the importance of promptly considering appendicitis in patients presenting with acute abdominal pain following abdominal trauma and underscore the need for timely evaluation and appropriate management.

Introduction

Acute appendicitis (AA) is the most common cause of acute abdomen, often prompting visits to the emergency department [1]. Its peak incidence occurs between ages 10 and 30, with a lifetime risk of 9% in the USA, 8% in Europe, and 2% in Africa [2], [3], [4]. The primary cause of AA is obstruction of the proximal lumen of the appendix, although the underlying etiology remains unclear in many cases. Trauma is a rare but documented cause of appendicitis [5].

Several clinical and laboratory scoring systems aid in predicting the likelihood of AA. The classic presentation included right lower quadrant abdominal pain, anorexia, and nausea/vomiting. However, some patients may exhibit atypical symptoms such as abdominal distension, constipation, or dysuria. Various imaging modalities, including ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are commonly used to enhance diagnostic accuracy [1].

Case presentation

A 38-year-old man was referred to this tertiary hospital. Four days prior to admission, he experienced a delusional episode and jumped from the second floor of a department store. At the primary hospital, evaluation by a trauma surgery consultant revealed no significant injuries. An Extended Focus Assessment Sonography of Trauma (E-FAST) showed no intraabdominal free fluid, and computed tomography (CT) of the brain and cervical spine demonstrated no intracranial hemorrhage, skull fracture, or cervical spine fracture. He was subsequently transferred to this hospital for further management of his schizophrenia.

Two days after admission, the patient developed right lower quadrant abdominal pain. He denied any prior episodes of lower abdominal pain or symptoms suggestive of chronic appendicitis. The trauma surgeon team was consulted for evaluation. On examination, his vital signs were stable: temperature of 37.5 °C, heart rate of 80 beats per minute, blood pressure of 120/60 mmHg, and respiratory rate of 16 breaths per minute. Abdominal examination revealed localized guarding and rebound tenderness in the right lower quadrant. Discussion

Traumatic appendicitis is rare, with clinical presentation typically including right lower quadrant abdominal pain, anorexia, and nausea/vomiting. However, some patients may present with atypical symptoms such as abdominal distension, constipation, or dysuria. Various imaging modalities, including ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are commonly used to enhance diagnostic accuracy [1]. Surgery is the treatment of choice for this condition, while antibiotic therapy may also be considered, as reported by Albalawi et al. [6]. The exact relationship between appendicitis and trauma remains unclear. In our case, the patient was diagnosed with appendicitis four days after falling from a height. He developed localized abdominal pain in the right lower quadrant and denied any prior episodes of lower abdominal pain or symptoms suggestive of chronic appendicitis. A CT scan of the abdomen revealed an enlarged appendix with decreased mucosal enhancement at the tip, surrounding fat inflammation, and an adjacent fluid collection. The patient was scheduled for emergency laparoscopic surgery. However, due to intraoperative findings of hemoperitoneum suspected of active bleeding from the appendiceal vessel, the procedure was converted to a laparotomy. In this patient, the acute appendicitis appears to be associated with abdominal trauma, as the CT scan also showed a contusion in the right lower quadrant of the abdominal wall. Interestingly, the patient did not exhibit abdominal pain immediately after the trauma. It developed on the fourth day post-accident. Based on the patient's history, clinical presentation, and imaging findings, the diagnosis of acute appendicitis following trauma was confirmed.

Conclusion

Traumatic appendicitis is rare; However, it should be considered in patients presenting with acute abdominal pain following abdominal trauma. Imaging plays a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis and enhancing diagnostic accuracy.
