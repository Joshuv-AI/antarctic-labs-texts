Circulating lysophosphatidylethanolamine species are associated with pericoronary adipose tissue inflammation and coronary atherosclerotic burden in type 2 diabetes
Authors: Daopeng Dai, Shuai Chen, Qingrun Li, Chengshuang Chu, Feifei Li, Yipaerguli Maimaiti, Xinrui Wu, Wenli Zhang, Qiujing Chen, Yong Wang, Fenghua Ding, Lin Lu, Rong Zeng, Jinzhou Zhu, Yang Dai

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) involves alterations in lipid metabolism beyond conventional cholesterol profiles. We examined whether specific plasma lipidomic signatures are related to pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) inflammation and coronary artery disease (CAD) burden.MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis included 176 T2DM participants from the GADA study. PCAT inflammation was measured by the fat attenuation index (FAI), CAD burden by Gensini/SYNTAX scores and plasma lipid species by targeted LC-MS. Multivariable regression were used to identify lipid species associated with FAI and CAD burden. For lipid species linked to both, mediation analyses were performed.ResultsWe quantified 660 lipid molecular species across 20 classes. Five species were associated with FAI: sphingomyelin (SM) (34:4), phosphatidylcholines (PC) (16:0/20:4), Lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) (18:1), LPE (20:4), and LPE (18:2). The three LPE species remained independently associated with both Gensini and SYNTAX scores after adjustment for clinical risk factors, conventional lipid measures, triglycerides, and hsCRP. Mediation analyses indicated that FAI accounted for part of the associations between these LPE species and CAD burden.ConclusionsIn T2DM patients, circulating LPE (18:1), LPE (20:4), and LPE (18:2) were associated with both PCAT inflammation and CAD burden. These findings point to altered LPE metabolism as a lipid-inflammatory signature linked to residual atherosclerotic burden beyond conventional lipid and glycemic measures.Keywords: lysophosphatidylethanolamine, pericoronary adipose tissue, fat attenuation index, coronary atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes mellitus

Background

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains frequent in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), even when conventional risk factors are treated. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a well-established causal factor in atherosclerosis, and LDL-C lowering remains central to cardiovascular prevention.1–3 Yet in clinical practice, patients with T2DM may still have substantial coronary atherosclerotic burden despite lipid- and glucose-lowering therapy. This residual burden suggests that conventional cholesterol and glycemic indices do not fully capture the metabolic risk profile of diabetic atherosclerosis.4,5

Lipid metabolism in T2DM is altered at multiple levels. Beyond LDL-C and triglycerides, diabetes is associated with changes in triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and their remnants, lipoprotein(a), sphingolipids, glycerophospholipids, and lysophospholipids.6,7 These lipid species are not merely structural components of cell membranes; some also serve as bioactive mediators implicated in inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and immune cell signaling, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.8–12 Advances in lipidomics now allow detailed profiling of circulating lipid molecular species and provide a way to examine non-cholesterol lipid signatures that are not captured by routine lipid measurements. 13

Atherosclerosis is also shaped by local inflammatory signals around the coronary vessel wall. Pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) is a metabolically active endocrine and paracrine tissue that communicates with the adjacent coronary artery through adipokines, cytokines, and lipid mediators.14–16 The coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA)-derived fat attenuation index (FAI) provides a non-invasive measure of inflammation-related changes in PCAT and has been linked to coronary plaque burden, high-risk plaque features, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes.17–19

How circulating lipidomic alterations relate to local coronary inflammation in T2DM is still unclear. Lysophospholipids are of particular interest because they arise from phospholipid remodeling and have been implicated in membrane turnover, inflammatory signaling, and vascular dysfunction.10–12 Among them, lysophosphatidylethanolamines (LPEs) remain less well characterized in diabetic coronary atherosclerosis. However, data linking circulating LPE species to PCAT inflammation and angiographic CAD burden in diabetic patients are limited.

In this study, we examined plasma lipidomic profiles in patients with T2DM who underwent both coronary CTA and invasive coronary angiography (CAG). Using targeted liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry-based lipidomics, FAI assessment, and Gensini and SYNTAX scoring, we aimed to identify circulating lipid molecular species associated with PCAT inflammation and coronary atherosclerotic burden.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data and biospecimens from the GADA (Glycation of ApoA-I and Diabetic Atherosclerosis) study. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The parent study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Rui Jin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The GADA study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05659043).

Between January 2022 and December 2022, 514 patients with T2DM who underwent both coronary CTA and invasive CAG at Rui Jin Hospital were screened. The main exclusion criteria were acute coronary syndrome (n = 55), prior coronary revascularization (n = 79), chronic heart failure (n = 48), significant structural heart disease, including valvular disease (n = 26), pulmonary disease (n = 24), congenital heart disease (n = 15), or cardiomyopathy (n = 21), significant liver dysfunction (n = 17), chronic kidney disease (n = 18), active malignancy (n = 11), blood-borne infectious disease (n = 21), and type 1 diabetes mellitus (n = 12). These criteria were not mutually exclusive; after removing all patients who met any exclusion criterion, 176 participants with available coronary CTA, CAG, clinical data, and plasma lipidomic profiles were included in the final analysis.

T2DM was diagnosed according to the American Diabetes Association criteria: fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L on at least two separate measurements, 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L after a 75-g oral glucose load, random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, or current treatment with oral glucose-lowering agents or insulin. 20

Coronary angiography and analysis

CAG was performed via radial or femoral access according to standard clinical practice. Quantitative CAG was performed using the Cardiovascular Angiography Analysis System version 3.0 (Terra, GE, USA). Significant CAD was defined as luminal diameter stenosis ≥50% in any major epicardial coronary artery. 21

Angiographic CAD burden was assessed using the SYNTAX score and the Gensini score. The SYNTAX score was calculated for each lesion with ≥50% diameter stenosis in vessels ≥1.5 mm in diameter, and total per-patient scores were derived using the online SYNTAX score calculator. 1 The Gensini score was calculated by combining the severity of luminal stenosis with a weighting factor according to the coronary segment involved. 22

All angiograms were reviewed retrospectively by two experienced interventional cardiologists who were blinded to clinical data, FAI measurements, and lipidomic results. SYNTAX and Gensini scores were assessed independently. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third observer until consensus was reached.

FAI assessment

Pericoronary FAI was assessed on coronary CTA images according to previously validated methods.18,19 FAI was measured in the left anterior descending artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery (RCA). PCAT was defined as adipose tissue located within a radial distance from the outer vessel wall equal to the vessel diameter. The proximal 40-mm segments of the left anterior descending artery and left circumflex artery, and the 10–50-mm segment of the RCA, were segmented. PCAT voxels were identified within an attenuation range of −190 to −30 HU, and FAI was calculated from the attenuation profile of these voxels. In the present analysis, RCA-derived FAI was used as the primary measure of pericoronary inflammation because the RCA has relatively low anatomical variability and has been widely used in previous prognostic studies of CAD. Elevated pericoronary inflammation was defined as FAI ≥ −70.1 HU.18,19 FAI analyses were performed by investigators blinded to clinical data, angiographic scores, and lipidomic results.

Lipidomic analysis

Plasma lipidomic profiling was performed using a high-throughput targeted liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) platform with electrospray ionization in both positive and negative ion modes, as previously described.23,24 Briefly, lipids were extracted from plasma using a methyl tert-butyl ether-based protocol. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Nexera X2 UHPLC system (LC-30AD; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled to a 6500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Foster City, CA, USA). Data were acquired using Analyst software version 1.6.3 (SCIEX).

Samples were analyzed in randomized order, and a pooled quality-control sample was injected after every 23 study samples to monitor analytical stability. Lipid peak areas were normalized to corresponding internal standards. In total, 802 lipid molecular species were measured. Lipid species with missing values in more than 20% of samples or with a relative standard deviation greater than 30% in quality-control samples were excluded. Remaining missing values were imputed using k-nearest-neighbor imputation. The normalized lipid abundances were then log2-transformed, and batch effects were corrected using the ComBat method. After quality control and preprocessing, 660 lipid molecular species across 20 lipid classes were retained for the final analyses.

The lipidomic data used in regression analyses are reported as log2-transformed and ComBat-corrected normalized lipid abundances, rather than absolute concentrations. Lipid nomenclature followed the LIPID MAPS shorthand notation, with examples provided in Table S1.

Clinical data and biochemical measurements

Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records, including age, sex, height, weight, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, duration of diabetes, hypertension history, smoking status, and medication use. Medication data included lipid-lowering therapy, glucose-lowering therapy, antiplatelet therapy, and antihypertensive treatment when available. Fasting blood samples were collected after an overnight fast. Serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein A, apolipoprotein B, lipoprotein(a), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein were measured using standard laboratory methods in the hospital clinical laboratory. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Non–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol was calculated as total cholesterol minus high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the 2021 race-free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation. 25

Statistical analyses

To evaluate associations between individual lipid molecular species and continuous FAI, Gensini score, or SYNTAX score, separate multivariable linear regression models were constructed for each lipid species. The prespecified covariates included age, sex, BMI, smoking status, hypertension history, eGFR, HbA1c, non–HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and log2-transformed hsCRP.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify lipid species associated with elevated pericoronary inflammation, defined as FAI ≥ −70.1 HU. The same covariate set was used in these models. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF < 5 indicating no significant collinearity among the lipid species included in the same regression models. Results from logistic regression are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess whether the associations between the five FAI-associated lipid species and elevated pericoronary inflammation were influenced by statin use. For these analyses, statin use was additionally included in the primary multivariable logistic regression models, while keeping the original covariate set unchanged.

Bootstrap internal validation was performed for the five lipid species consistently associated with FAI. Bootstrap analyses were conducted using 1,000 resamples with replacement, and in each iteration the fully adjusted logistic regression model (with and without additional adjustment for statin use) was refit. For each lipid species, the bootstrap median odds ratio was derived from the distribution of bootstrap estimates, with 95% confidence intervals obtained from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The percentage of resamples with an odds ratio greater than 1 was also calculated as an indicator of the stability of the association direction.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to examine the consistency of the associations between the three LPE species and angiographic CAD burden across clinically relevant strata, including age, sex, statin use, and hsCRP level. hsCRP was dichotomized at 2 mg/L. Multivariable linear regression models were repeated within each subgroup using the same covariate set as in the primary analyses, with the stratifying variable omitted when appropriate.

Exploratory mediation analyses were performed to estimate whether FAI statistically accounted for part of the associations between selected lipid species and angiographic CAD burden. Lipid species were selected for mediation analyses if they were associated with both continuous FAI and angiographic CAD burden in covariate-adjusted models. For each lipid species, linear models were fitted for the mediator, FAI, and for the outcome, Gensini or SYNTAX score, with adjustment for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, hypertension history, eGFR, HbA1c, non–HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and log2-transformed hsCRP. Mediation estimates were obtained using the R package mediation version 4.5.1 with 5,000 nonparametric bootstrap iterations. The indirect effect, direct effect, and proportion mediated were reported.

A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; for lipidomic association analyses, the false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and an FDR-adjusted P (q-value) < 0.05 was used to denote significance after multiple testing correction.

Patients were stratified into two groups based on pericoronary FAI: an FAI ≥ –70.1 HU group (indicating higher pericoronary inflammation, n = 44) and an FAI < –70.1 HU group (indicating lower inflammation, n = 132). As shown in Table 1, patients with higher FAI values had significantly higher BMI and hsCRP levels. A higher prevalence of hypertension and a greater proportion of current or former smokers were also observed in the high FAI group, although these differences were not statistically significant. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of gender, age, systolic or diastolic blood pressure, eGFR, TG, TC, HDL cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, ApoA, ApoB, Lp(a), FBG, or HbA1c levels.

Identification of five lipid molecular species associated with pericoronary inflammation

Targeted lipidomic analysis via LC-MS identified 20 lipid classes, 37 fatty acids within lipids, and 660 distinct lipid molecular species in the study cohort (Figure 1(A)). The most abundant lipid classes based on normalized relative abundance were free fatty acids, cholesteryl esters, sphingomyelins, phosphatidylcholines, and triglycerides (Figure 1(B)). At the fatty acid level, the most prevalent species were FA (18:1), FA (18:2), FA (16:0), FA (16:1), and FA (18:3) (Figure 1(C)).

In a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted for statin use, the associations of these five FAI-associated lipid species with elevated FAI remained directionally consistent, with odds ratios greater than 1 for each lipid species (Figure S1). Bootstrap internal validation further showed stable positive associations between the three LPE species and elevated FAI. Across 1,000 resamples, bootstrap median ORs for the three LPE species ranged from 1.58 to 1.79 in the primary model and from 1.60 to 1.80 after additional adjustment for statin use. The direction of association was positive in 98.9%–99.7% of resamples for the three LPE species, whereas PC(16:0/20:4) showed estimates close to the null (Figure S2, Table S2). These findings supported the stability of the LPE–FAI associations and indicated that they were not substantially changed by statin adjustment.

Clinical association of FAI-associated lipids with CAD severity

To assess the clinical relevance of these FAI-associated lipids, we examined their associations with established measures of angiographic CAD burden. Consistent with previous reports, 19 patients in the high FAI group had significantly higher Gensini and SYNTAX scores, and FAI values showed positive correlations with both scores (Figure 3(A)–(D)). We then evaluated the associations between the five FAI-associated lipid species and angiographic CAD burden using multivariable linear regression. LPE (18:1), LPE (20:4), and LPE (18:2) were associated with both Gensini and SYNTAX scores after adjustment for clinical and biochemical covariates. In contrast, SM(34:4) and PC(16:0/20:4) did not show consistent associations with both angiographic scores; SM(34:4) showed only a nominal association with SYNTAX score, whereas PC(16:0/20:4) was not significantly associated with either score (Figure 3(E) and (F); Tables 2 and 3). In exploratory subgroup analyses, the associations of the three LPE species with CAD burden were generally consistent across age and sex strata and were most evident among participants receiving statin therapy or with hsCRP <2 mg/L (Table 4).

Exploratory mediation analysis of FAI in the associations between LPE species and CAD burden

To assess whether FAI statistically accounted for part of the associations between the identified LPE species and coronary atherosclerotic burden, we performed exploratory mediation analyses for LPE (18:1), LPE (18:2), and LPE (20:4). Across both outcomes, FAI accounted for part of the associations between each LPE species and angiographic CAD burden. For Gensini score, the indirect effects were 1.61 (95% CI, 0.22–3.14; P=0.024), 1.68 (0.36–3.14; P=0.016), and 1.82 (0.71–3.70; P=0.003), with proportions mediated of 17.83%, 22.01%, and 22.06%, respectively. For SYNTAX score, the indirect effects were 0.69 (0.10–1.36; P=0.024), 0.72 (0.15–1.40; P=0.016), and 0.78 (0.30–1.61; P=0.003), with proportions mediated of 19.62%, 24.98%, and 23.83%, respectively. Direct associations remained significant in all models, indicating partial rather than complete statistical mediation (Figure 4(A)–(F)). Collectively, FAI accounted for approximately one-fifth to one-quarter of the LPE–CAD burden associations, while residual direct associations suggested additional pathways not captured by FAI.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of patients with T2DM undergoing coronary CTA, invasive CAG, and targeted plasma lipidomic profiling, we found that circulating lipidomic alterations were associated with PCAT inflammation and angiographic coronary atherosclerotic burden. Our study yielded four key findings. First, right coronary artery (RCA)-derived FAI was positively associated with Gensini and SYNTAX scores, supporting the relevance of PCAT inflammation to coronary disease burden in T2DM. Second, lipidome-wide screening identified five lipid molecular species associated with FAI in both logistic and linear regression analyses: SM (34:4), PC (16:0/20:4), LPE (18:1), LPE (20:4), and LPE (18:2). Third, among these FAI-associated lipid species, the three LPE species showed the most consistent associations with both angiographic scores after adjustment for clinical risk factors, conventional lipid measures, triglycerides, and hsCRP. Fourth, exploratory mediation analyses suggested that FAI statistically accounted for part of the associations between LPE species and CAD burden. Together, these findings point to an LPE-related lipid-inflammatory signature linked to residual atherosclerotic burden in T2DM.

PCAT has emerged as an important local inflammatory compartment surrounding the coronary arteries. Unlike systemic inflammatory markers such as hsCRP, PCAT-derived imaging signals may reflect inflammatory activity in the immediate coronary microenvironment. Previous studies have shown that FAI is associated with coronary plaque burden, high-risk plaque features, and future adverse cardiovascular events.17–19 In line with these reports, we observed higher Gensini and SYNTAX scores among patients with elevated FAI and positive correlations between FAI and angiographic disease burden. These findings support the use of FAI as a non-invasive marker of coronary inflammatory burden in patients with T2DM. Of note, the lipid associations observed in our study persisted after adjustment for hsCRP, suggesting that selected lipidomic signals may capture information not fully reflected by systemic inflammation alone.

Given the association between lipids and adipocyte inflammation, we investigated whether specific circulating lipid species were correlated with PCAT inflammation. Beyond conventional cholesterol measures, lipidomic profiling provides a more detailed view of lipid remodeling in metabolic disease. Previous studies have linked triglycerides, ceramides, sphingomyelins, phosphatidylcholines, and lysophospholipids to atherosclerosis, plaque vulnerability, inflammation, and cardiometabolic risk.8–13,26–28 In the present study, several lipid species were associated with FAI, but not all showed consistent relationships with angiographic CAD burden. SM (34:4) and PC (16:0/20:4) were associated with FAI, yet their associations with Gensini and SYNTAX scores were weaker or inconsistent after multivariable adjustment. This pattern suggests that PCAT inflammation and coronary lesion burden may be related to partially overlapping yet distinct lipidomic signatures.

The most consistent signal in our data involved three LPE species: LPE (18:1), LPE (20:4), and LPE (18:2). These species were associated with FAI and with both Gensini and SYNTAX scores, independent of conventional lipid parameters and hsCRP. The VIF-based assessment supported their independent statistical contributions, despite their structural similarities. And their associations with elevated FAI were directionally consistent after additional adjustment for statin use, and bootstrap internal validation supported stable positive associations, particularly for the three LPE species. These results suggest that LPE-related alterations may be part of a residual lipid-inflammatory signature in T2DM, rather than simply reflecting LDL-C, triglycerides, or systemic hsCRP levels.

LPEs are generated mainly through phospholipid remodeling, including hydrolysis of phosphatidylethanolamines by phospholipase A1 or A2, and through reacylation-related pathways in tissues such as the liver and lung.29,30 In metabolic disease, altered phospholipase activity and adipose tissue dysfunction may reshape lysophospholipid metabolism.31,32 Experimental studies have also suggested that inflammatory stimuli, including C-reactive protein, can promote LPE release from activated or inflamed cells. 33 These observations provide biological plausibility for the association between circulating LPE species and pericoronary inflammation in T2DM. However, our data do not establish the tissue source of circulating LPEs or prove that LPEs directly drive PCAT inflammation.

Several potential mechanisms may underlie the link between LPE species and coronary inflammatory burden. Lysophospholipids can participate in membrane remodeling, cell signaling, oxidative stress responses, and immune-cell activation.30,34–36 In a diabetic metabolic environment characterized by insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and altered adipose tissue biology, LPE accumulation may reflect enhanced phospholipid turnover or inflammatory lipid remodeling. These LPE species may also interact with vascular, adipose, or immune-cell pathways within the coronary microenvironment. Such mechanisms remain hypothetical in the present study and require validation in cellular, tissue-based, and animal models.

Among the three LPE species we identified, LPE (20:4), which contains arachidonic acid at its sn-2 position, is of particular interest. Arachidonic acid is a well-established precursor of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids, including prostaglandins and leukotrienes, via the cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways. Phospholipase A2-mediated cleavage of LPE (20:4) could potentially liberate arachidonic acid, fueling local inflammatory cascades within the vascular wall and pericoronary adipose tissue.37,38 This raises the hypothesis that LPE (20:4) may not merely be a marker of membrane turnover, but could actively contribute to the pro-inflammatory milieu associated with diabetic coronary atherosclerosis. Future mechanistic studies are warranted to explore whether LPE (20:4)-derived arachidonic acid contributes to PCAT inflammation and plaque progression.

The mediation findings are compatible with, but do not confirm, a model in which altered LPE metabolism is linked to coronary atherosclerotic burden partly through local pericoronary inflammation. The remaining direct association suggests additional LPE-related mechanisms, such as systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, plaque lipid remodeling, or unmeasured metabolic factors. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, these mediation estimates should be interpreted only as statistical evidence for a plausible indirect pathway, rather than definitive evidence of causal mediation. Prospective longitudinal studies are therefore required to rigorously establish the temporal ordering of these relationships. Above all, our findings carry several implications for future research and potential clinical translation. First, the results imply that specific LPE species may assist in characterizing residual atherosclerotic burden in T2DM beyond routine lipid and glycemic measures. Second, our data support a closer link between lysophospholipid metabolism and local coronary inflammatory activity. Third, they suggest that future studies should examine whether LPE species are markers, mediators, or downstream consequences of PCAT inflammation and coronary atherosclerosis. Prospective cohorts and mechanistic studies will be needed before these lipid species can be considered for clinical translation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal sequence or causality. Second, this was a single-center study with a moderate sample size, and the findings require validation in larger and independent cohorts. Third, the study focused exclusively on patients with T2DM, which improves relevance to diabetic atherosclerosis but limits generalizability to non-diabetic populations. Fourth, the stringent exclusion criteria applied to the original cohort, while necessary to minimize confounding from major comorbidities, may limit the generalizability of our findings to unselected T2DM populations with multiple concomitant conditions, and validation in broader community-based diabetic cohorts is warranted. Finally, although we adjusted for major clinical and biochemical covariates, some residual confounding from unmeasured inflammatory markers, lifestyle factors, medication intensity, and treatment duration cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In patients with T2DM, circulating LPE (18:1), LPE (20:4), and LPE (18:2) were associated with both PCAT inflammation and angiographic CAD burden. Exploratory mediation analyses suggested that FAI may partly explain these associations. These findings point to altered LPE metabolism as a lipid-inflammatory signature of residual atherosclerotic burden beyond conventional lipid and glycemic measures. Prospective and mechanistic studies are needed to validate these observations and clarify the role of LPE species in diabetic coronary atherosclerosis.
