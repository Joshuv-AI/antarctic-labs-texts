Preliminary estimates of genetic parameters for bone geometry, strength, and metabolism traits and their relationship with walking ability in turkeys (Meleagris gallopavo)
Authors: Rick O. Hernandez, Marisa A. Erasmus, Nathan A. Griffith, Gideon S. Ajibola, Hayley L. Sutherland, Stephanie Kulbacki, Leticia F. de Oliveira, Ryley Vanderhout, Jani de Vos, Prafulla Regmi, Luiz F. Brito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Impaired walking ability is a persistent concern in commercial turkeys, affecting both animal welfare and productivity. Although bone geometry, strength, and metabolism contribute to overall walking ability, the genetic background of these traits remains poorly characterized. Therefore, the main objectives of this study were to estimate genomic-based variance components and genetic parameters for bone geometry, strength to fracture, and collagen turnover metabolism, and to evaluate their relationship with walking ability in domestic turkeys (Meleagris gallopavo). Phenotypic data were collected from 722 male turkeys raised under experimental conditions across three repetitions. Birds were sampled at either 8, 12, and 18–19 weeks of age, and traits related to bone geometry and bone strength were measured. Bone geometry traits included weight, length, midpoint, anterior-posterior diameter, and medial-lateral diameter; and bone strength traits included peak force and work to fracture, which were recorded for the femur (n = 339), tibia (n = 177), and humerus (n = 167). Additionally, a collagen turn-over marker, pyridinoline (PYD) and welfare-related traits with repeated records, including gait score (GS; n = 2,215), footpad lesions (n = 2,216), head lesions (n = 2,216), and body weight (BW; n = 704) were also collected. All 722 birds were genotyped using a SNP chip panel containing 13,491 SNPs after quality control. Variance components were estimated using Bayesian animal models via Gibbs sampling in the GIBBSF90+ software. Heritability estimates across bone traits ranged from 0.11 ± 0.08 to 0.48 ± 0.27 for bone geometry traits and from 0.09 ± 0.08 to 0.29 ± 0.25 for bone strength traits. PYD presented a heritability of 0.17 ± 0.10. Our preliminary results suggest that bone geometry is moderately heritable with variable genetic correlations with bone strength, suggesting that geometry-based selection alone could be insufficient for improving skeletal health in turkeys. The preliminary estimates of heritability and phenotypic correlations observed for PYD suggest its potential as a phenotype to enhance selection for skeletal integrity in turkeys. However, future research using larger sample sizes and diverse turkey populations is recommended to validate these findings.

Introduction

Impaired walking ability is a persistent concern in commercial turkeys (Meleagris gallopavo), affecting both animal welfare and production (as reviewed by Erasmus, 2018). Rapid growth and increased breast muscle development, achieved through advances in nutrition, management, and genetic selection, have inadvertently contributed to a higher prevalence of locomotor problems and altered walking patterns, with more than 50% of flocks reported to exhibit some degree of abnormal gait (da Costa et al., 2014; Olschewsky et al., 2021). Walking impairment in turkeys is multifactorial, often resulting from both skeletal and soft-tissue alterations (Erasmus, 2018; Oviedo-Rondon et al., 2018). Valgus and varus deformities, hip joint degeneration, footpad dermatitis, imbalance between bone formation and resorption, and biomechanical stress caused by an altered center of gravity in heavy toms can compromise femur and tibia integrity, leading to abnormal gait, particularly at later growth stages (Oviedo-Rondón et al., 2017). These gait abnormalities contribute to substantial economic losses through early culling, increased mortality, reduced productivity, and carcass condemnation, and remain among the primary welfare concerns for turkey producers (Erasmus, 2018; Van Staaveren et al., 2020).

Various strategies have been explored to mitigate walking ability problems in turkeys, including environmental enrichment (Dong et al., 2023), modifications in stocking density (Beaulac and Schwean-Lardner, 2018), targeted nutritional interventions (Hocking et al., 2002; Tatara et al., 2011), and forced locomotor activity (Hahn et al., 2020). Environmental enrichment, such as platforms, straw bales, or pecking objects, has been shown to improve welfare indicators; however, the effects on walking ability are not conclusive (Dong et al., 2023). Reducing stocking density can decrease leg stress and promote better locomotion, although commercial feasibility is often limited by space and economic constraints (Beaulac and Schwean-Lardner, 2018). Nutritional strategies, including optimized calcium and phosphorus levels, can influence bone strength, particularly in young turkeys and broilers (Tatara et al., 2011); however, their effects on skeletal integrity at later growth stages are limited, and such interventions may involve trade-offs with growth rate or feed efficiency (Tatara et al., 2011). Forced locomotor activity has produced measurable improvements in leg posture and walking ability, with a reduction of up to 30% of severe lameness under controlled experimental conditions but require training of turkeys (Hahn et al., 2020).

While management and nutritional interventions can address some aspects of poor walking ability, genomic selection directly targets the genetic determinants of skeletal integrity and locomotion, providing a long-term improvement strategy. Previous studies provided anecdotal evidence that bone traits were considered relevant to genetic improvement. For example, Nestor et al. (1985) reported that selection for increased shank width affected body weight (BW), walking ability, and aspects of skeletal structure, while Emmerson et al. (1991) observed associations between selection for leg muscle and shank diameter with walking performance. Noble et al. (1996) highlighted the influence of genetic line and rearing system on growth, gait traits, and skeletal development. Although these studies suggest that skeletal health traits were of interest for improving locomotor performance, they were conducted decades ago and their results may not fully represent modern commercial turkey populations. More recent studies in contemporary populations, such as Mulim et al. (2024) and Soyalp et al. (2023), which estimated heritability and variance components for production and walking ability traits in domestic turkeys, provided a framework to support further exploration of the genetic architecture of locomotor and skeletal traits in modern commercial lines. Consequently, further research is needed to characterize these traits in contemporary populations and to enable more effective genomic selection programs. In addition, genomic selection may offer clear advantages by improving gait performance but maintaining BW and other production traits (Kapell et al., 2017; Mulim et al., 2024).

Walking ability, commonly evaluated using gait score (GS), is heritable with substantial additive genetic variance, indicating the feasibility of improving it through genetic selection (Abdalla et al., 2019; Mulim et al., 2024). However, whereas GS is simple and cost-effective to record, it presents limitations related to observer accuracy and its practical use under commercial conditions (Wurtz and Riber, 2024). Accordingly, there is growing interest in assessing traits that capture more specific skeletal or metabolic traits to complement GS assessment that may be more heritable (Mulim et al., 2024). Traits related to skeletal integrity may provide detailed information on the structural integrity and metabolism of turkey locomotion (Oviedo-Rondón et al., 2017; Zhong et al., 2012). These include bone geometry (weight, length, and midpoint), anterior-posterior diameter (APD; the distance between surfaces at the midpoint), and medial-lateral diameter (MLD). Additionally, mechanical strength traits, such as peak force (PF; maximum force to fracture) and work to fracture (U; total energy absorbed), along with biochemical markers like circulating Pyridinoline (PYD; a marker of collagen turnover) offer deep insight into bone health. Integrating these traits with GS measurements can facilitate the identification of novel heritable traits to be included in multi-trait selection models for improving walking ability, skeletal health, and related production and welfare traits in turkey populations. However, the information on the genetic background of these traits is limited and genetic parameters are population specific. Therefore, the main objectives of this study were to estimate variance components and genetic parameters for various traits related to bone geometry and strength assessed via direct bone evaluation, and bone collagen matrix metabolism and assess their relationship with walking ability, production, and welfare indicators in commercial turkeys raised in experimental conditions.

Material and methods

Animals and housing

The Purdue University Animal Care and Use Committee approved all procedures involving live animals (protocol #2111002216). A detailed description of the experimental units, management practices, and housing conditions provided to the animals was previously described by Griffith (2025) and methods were similar to Dong et al. (2023). In summary, a total of 1,200 one-day-old male crossbred turkey poults (Nicholas Select, Aviagen Turkeys Inc. Lewisburg, WV, USA), raised across three study repetitions (n = 400/repetition), were obtained from a commercial hatchery and transported to the Purdue Animal Science Research and Education Center (ASREC, West Lafayette, IN, USA). Upon arrival, poults from each repetition were housed in four pens (3.05 m x 2.44 m), with wood shavings bedding, heat lamps, and light schedule following commercial standards. At 3 weeks of age, the birds were redistributed to 24 littered pens, equally divided between two rooms (12 pens/room). Allocation was based on the BW of turkeys to ensure that the average BW per pen was balanced across treatments. Each pen contained two bell drinkers and one feeder, providing ad libitum access to feed and water. Birds were fed a standard corn-soybean meal diet formulated to meet or exceed the National Research Council (NRC) requirements (NRC, 1994). Room temperature, humidity, and ventilation were controlled to emulate commercial conditions as described by Griffith (2025).

At 5 weeks of age, each pen was randomly assigned within room to one of five environmental enrichment treatments (platform, pecking block, robot, straw bale, tunnel) and the control treatment had no environmental enrichment (n = 4 pens/treatment). The platform, pecking block, straw bale, and tunnel enrichments were present continuously in the pens until slaughter, whereas the robot was introduced for 20 min every two days until slaughter. Enrichment types are described in detail in Griffith (2025).

Data collection

Production and welfare traits

For every repetition, at weeks 8, 12, and slaughter age (18-19 weeks), turkeys were individually weighed using a digital scale (SRV415 Multi-Purpose Mobile Vet Scale, SR Instruments, Tonawanda, NY, USA) and three categories of BW were determined for further analyses: weight in kg at week 8 (BW1, n = 704), week 12 (BW2, n = 704), and slaughter age (BW3, n = 708). After being weighed, birds were evaluated for gait score (GS, n = 722) on a 0-5 scale, where 0 indicates normal walking and 5 indicates complete lameness (Dong et al., 2023). Footpad lesions (FP, n = 722) were scored by direct visualization on a 0-2 scale where 0 represents no lesions and 2 severe deep lesions or ulcers and scabs (Dong et al., 2023). Head lesions (HL, n = 722) were scored on a 0-2 scale where 0 represents no lesions and 2 indicates an open wound (Dong et al., 2023). All phenotypic recordings were performed by the same trained team of observers, who were regularly evaluated for intra- and inter-observer concordance during the three repetitions to ensure consistency across measurements (Griffith, 2025).

Pyridinoline (PYD)

Blood samples from 330 birds across the three repetitions were collected from the brachial vein at week 8 and slaughter age (18-19 weeks). Samples were collected into vacutainer tubes, centrifuged at 1900 x g for 15 min, and the serum was stored at −20 °C until posterior analyses. Serum samples were shipped to the University of Georgia (Athens, GA, USA) for plasma PYD quantification using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (MicroVue™ PYD EIA, Quidel, San Diego, CA, USA). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 7.2% and 14.2%, respectively.

Bone traits

Bone traits were measured at weeks 8, 12, and slaughter age (18–19 weeks), where 2 birds/pen were randomly selected at 8 weeks and 12 weeks, while the remaining were obtained at slaughter age. Descriptive statistics for the selected birds are presented in Supplementary Table S1Prior to bone collection, turkeys were humanely euthanized according to the American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines (AVMA, 2020). Right tibia (n = 339), femur (n = 177), and humerus (n = 167) bones across all three repetitions were collected. Bones were dissected, placed in zip-lock bags, and stored at –20 °C until analyses. Posteriorly, to perform bone cleaning, carcasses were thawed in a refrigerator at 4 °C for 48–72 h depending on bird age and size. Cleaning involved removal of ligaments, tendons, muscle, fibula (for tibia), and the periosteum, following procedures reported by Santos et al. (2022). Bones were wrapped in tissue paper, sprayed with 10% phosphate buffered saline solution (PBS), returned to –20 °C and shipped to the University of Georgia (Athens, GA, UGA) for analyses.

Prior to the analyses, bones were thawed at 4 °C for 24 h and weight (g), length (mm), midpoint diameter (mm), APD (mm), and MLD (mm) were measured. Length was measured from the lateral intercondylar tubercle to the inferior articular surface, and APD and MLD were measured at the midpoint of the diaphysis using vernier calipers. A 3-point bending test was performed to measure the PF (g) and U (g·mm). Each bone was placed with the posterior surface facing upward and supported in a cradle (30 mm apart for tibiae, 15 mm apart for humerus). Depending on the age of the birds and the size of the bone samples, either a 250 or 750 kg load cell was used to apply force at 1 mm/s at the midpoint until fracture (Williams et al., 2004).

Phenotypic data quality control

All phenotypic records were subjected to quality control (QC) procedures. Continuously distributed traits (e.g., BW, bone measurements, PYD concentration) were inspected for extreme values, and records exceeding ± 3.5 standard deviations from the mean were excluded from further analyses. Categorical traits (e.g., gait score, footpad lesions, head lesions) were checked to ensure all values fell within the defined levels described before; any values outside the expected range were treated as missing. Missing or out-of-range observations were excluded from downstream analyses. For tibia, femur, and humerus PF and U traits, values were standardized to facilitate bivariate analyses and subsequently transformed back to the original scale for reporting. Genotypic data

Following bone sample collection, approximately 3 cm2 of skin were excised from the neck (caruncles) or snood of 770 turkeys across all repetitions using a scalpel for genomic DNA extraction and subsequent genotyping. Skin samples were immediately refrigerated to −20 °C until further preparation. Subsequently, samples were placed into 10 mL of high-grade ethanol (90%) in 20 mL labeled tubes and were stored at room temperature protected from direct sunlight until shipment to the Hendrix® Genetics Genomics laboratory (Ploufragan, France).

Individuals were genotyped using a proprietary custom 15 K single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) chip panel developed by Hendrix® Genetics and containing 13,835 SNPs. The SNP chip panel was developed based on the turkey 5.1 reference genome assembly GCF_000146605.3 by the Turkey Genome Consortium (NCBI, 2019). Only samples with sufficient DNA quantity and quality, as assessed by the laboratory, were genotyped. A total of 722 individuals across all three repetitions were successfully genotyped and retained for subsequent analyses. All data processing, quality control, and statistical modeling were conducted independently by the researchers at Purdue University (West Lafayette, IN, USA).

Before genotype imputation, 78.2% (n = 564), 18.9% (n = 137), and 2.9% (n = 21) of the individuals had less than 5%, more than 10%, and between 5% and 10% missing genotypes, respectively. At the SNP level, 5.8% (800 SNPs) presented more than 10% missing data, 56.5% (7,823 SNPs) had between 5% and 10% missing values, and 37.7% (5,212 SNPs) had less than 5% missing values. Only SNPs from autosomal chromosomes were included in the analyses. To ensure the integrity of the results despite the technical variation in the raw genotyping platform, the subset of markers with the highest call rates (> 0.95, n = 5,212) was used for haplotype phasing.

Haplotype phasing was performed using the Eagle software (version 2.4.1) (Loh et al., 2016). Of the 30 chromosomes available, chromosome 18 was excluded at the phasing stage because it contained only one SNP. Genotype imputation was conducted using Minimac (version 4.1.6), with all genotyped individuals serving as reference and target populations. Genotype imputation was carried out on each chromosome independently following the method described by Druet et al. (2010). Physical positions of SNPs were converted to a genetic map assuming 1 Mb = 1 cM, and any SNPs not uniquely mapped were excluded. Imputation quality was evaluated using Minimac® accuracy (r²) per SNP and variants with poor imputation quality (r² < 0.30) were excluded. Overall, the dataset initially included 722 individuals and 13,835 SNPs with an SNP and individual call rate of 93.96%. After imputation, the number of SNPs was reduced to 13,690, but both SNP and individual call rates reached 100%. In total, 722 samples and 13,690 SNPs remained for subsequent analyses (Supplementary Table S2). Post-imputation, genotyping quality control was performed using the PLINK software (version 1.9.0; Chang et al., 2015). SNPs located in non-autosomal regions or with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 were removed. After QC and genotype imputation, a total of 722 individuals and 13,491 SNPs remained for further analyses.

Statistical model definition and variance component estimation

For the estimation of non-genetic sources of variation, we evaluated the effects of the birds’ age at measurement (8, 12, and 18-19 weeks), repetition (1, 2, and 3), room (1 and 2), and pen with environmental enrichments (1–12) and their interaction on the response variables using linear models. Variables significantly (P < 0.05) influencing the traits were retained in the final models. Based on this criterion, age (as a categorical effect) and contemporary group (combined effect of repetition, room, and pen) were fitted as systematic effects in all subsequent analyses.

Single-trait analyses – single-record animal model

To estimate variance components and narrow-sense heritability (h2) for single-record traits (femur, tibia, and humerus weight, length, midpoint diameter, APD, MLD, PF, U, BW1, BW2, and BW3), we fitted the following linear mixed model:y=Xb+Za+ewhere y is the vector of phenotypic values for each trait; b is the vector of systematic effects of age and contemporary group; a is the vector of animal additive genetic effects with a∼N(0,Gσa2), where σa2 is the additive genetic variance and Gis the genomic relationship matrix constructed based on the first method of VanRaden (2008); and e is the vector of residuals, assuming e∼N(0,Iσe2) where σe2 is the residual variance and I is an identity matrix. X and Z are the incidence matrices associated with the systematic and additive genetic effects, respectively. Variance and covariance components were estimated using a Bayesian approach via Gibbs sampling algorithm implemented on GIBBSF90+ and POSTGIBBSF90, which are part of the BLUPF90+ software family (Misztal et al., 2014). Each analysis was run for 2,000,000 iterations, with a burn-in period of 100,000 and thinning of 20 iterations. Convergence and posterior inference were evaluated using the “boa” R package (Smith, 2007), applying the Geweke diagnostic criteria and visual inspection. Additionally, Monte Carlo Standard Error (MCSE, <5% of the posterior standard deviation) and lag-1 autocorrelation (<0.3) were evaluated from the POSTGIBBSF90 output as complementary measures. The h2 estimates were calculated as:h2^=σa2^σ^a2+σ^e2

Single-trait analyses – repeated-records animal model

For traits with repeated records (i.e., PYD, GS, HL, BD, and FP), the model fitted was:y=Xb+Za+Wpe+ewhere pe is the random vector of permanent environmental effects with pe∼N(0,Iσpe2) where σpe2 is the permanent environmental variance and I is an identity matrix; and W is the incidence matrix linking permanent environmental estimates to the observations. All the other model parameters were previously defined. The h2 estimates were calculated as:h2^=σa2^σ^a2+σ^pe2+σ^e2

Bivariate models

The following bivariate animal models were applied to estimate (co)variance components. The general repeatability model can be defined as:[y1y2]=[X100X2][b1b2]+[Z100Z2][a1a2]+[W100W2][pe1pe2]+[e1e2]where yi are the vectors of phenotypes, bi, ai, pei, and ei are vectors of fixed, additive genetic, permanent environmental, and residual effects, respectively; and X, Z, and W are their corresponding incidence matrices. For traits with single records, the permanent environmental effect (pe) and its incidence matrix (W) were omitted. The (co)variance structure was defined as:Var[apee]=[G⊗Ga000I⊗Gpe000I⊗R]where G is the genomic relationship matrix; I is an identity matrix, and Ga, Gpe, and R are the (co)variance matrices for additive genetic, permanent environmental, and residual effects between traits, respectively. For bivariate analyses involving one repeated-measure trait (e.g., GS) and one single-measure trait (e.g., femur weight), the permanent environmental effect was included only for the repeated trait, and residual covariance between traits was fixed to zero. Genetic (rg) and phenotypic (rp) correlations were calculated as:rg=σa12σa1σa2,rp=σp12σp1σp2where σa12 and σp12 are the genetic and phenotypic covariance between traits 1 and 2, respectively. Theoretical accuracies of genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) were calculated as:accuracy=1−PEVσa2where PEV is the prediction error variance from the mixed model analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Overall, most birds exhibited low prevalence of severe lesions or impaired gait problems (Table 1). Most birds (78.16%) presented a GS of 0, whereas mild problems (score 1) were observed in 13.15% and only 8.76% showed moderate lameness (score ≥ 2). HL were absent (score 0) in 86.11% of birds, with 13.23% presenting mild injuries (score 1) and 0.66% showing severe lesions (score 2). Regarding FP, 91.41% of birds did not present lesions (score 0), whereas 8.35% showed mild cases (score 1), and only 0.25% presented severe lesions (score 2; Table 1).

Descriptive statistics for tibia, femur, and humerus traits are also presented in Table 1. Among the three bones, the tibia presented the highest average weight (86.6 ± 31.49 g) and length (217 ± 34.89 mm), on the other hand the femur presented the highest PF (79,432 ± 29,864 g) and U (197,349 ± 135,149 g x mm) (Table 1). BW increased with age, averaging 3.14 ± 0.57 kg for BW1, 8.21 ± 1.11 kg for BW2, and 15.68 ± 2.20 kg for BW3 (Table 1). Plasma PYD concentrations presented a mean of 128.3 ± 36.80 nmol/L (Table 1).

Heritability estimates

Heritability estimates for tibial traits ranged from 0.09 ± 0.08 to 0.36 ± 0.18 (Table 2). Midpoint distance showed the highest estimate (0.36 ± 0.18), conversely PF (0.09 ± 0.08) and weight (0.11 ± 0.08) were the lowest ones. Femoral traits followed a similar pattern, with heritability estimates ranging from 0.12 ± 0.10 (femur U) to 0.42 ± 0.21 (femur midpoint). Phenotypic and genetic correlation estimates

Phenotypic correlations among bone geometry traits were consistently positive and moderate to high, ranging from 0.12 ± 0.39 (between tibia APD and humerus APD) to 0.94 ± 0.04 (between tibia length and tibia midpoint), with tibia and femur weights, lengths, and diameters showing strong phenotypic associations (Table 4). In contrast, correlations between bone geometry and strength traits were smaller, generally between −0.15 ± 0.43 (between femur MLD and humerus PF) and 0.52 ± 0.17 (between humerus weight and humerus PF) (Table 4). On the other hand, GS presented weak to moderate negative correlations with most bone geometry traits, ranging from −0.24 ± 0.08 (between GS and femur MLD) to 0.11 ± 0.10 (between GS and humerus midpoint), and with bone strength traits from −0.12 ± 0.09 between (GS and femur U) to 0.21 ± 0.10 (between GS and femur PF) (Table 5). BW showed positive correlations with bone geometry traits, ranging from −0.02 ± 0.16 (between BW2 and femur midpoint) to 0.68 ± 0.03 (between BW2 and tibia weight), and moderate associations with bone strength traits, ranging from 0.09 ± 0.06 (between BW2 and tibia PF) to 0.65 ± 0.05 (between BW 2 and tibia U; Table 5). PYD was mainly negatively correlated with several bone geometry and strength traits, ranging from −0.35 ± 0.09 (between PYD and tibia length) to 0.38 ± 0.42 (between PYD and tibia U; Table 5). FP and HL were only weakly correlated with bone traits, ranging between −0.09 ± 0.07 (between FP and femur midpoint; Table 5) and 0.09 ± 0.05 (between FP and Tibia MLD). Regarding animal welfare traits GS was positively associated with BW (0.24 ± 0.08), FP (0.39 ± 0.02) and HL (0.29 ± 0.02) (Table 6). Likewise, FP presented a positive correlation with HL (0.30 ± 0.02). Genetic correlations between bone and welfare traits generally followed similar patterns as the phenotypic correlations. However, most estimates showed high posterior standard deviations (PSD), limiting their interpretability. Discussion

Walking ability is a major concern in commercial turkeys, affecting both productivity and welfare. Impaired locomotion can reduce feed and water access, limit expression of natural behaviors, increase aggression, and elevate the risk of injuries or condemnations at processing (Erasmus, 2018). Bone characteristics, including geometry (size and shape) and mechanical strength, provide structural support to carry the rapid body growth typical of modern turkey lines (Oviedo-Rondon et al., 2017, 2018), while indicators of bone turnover, such as plasma PYD, reflect bone metabolism (Van Wyhe et al., 2014). Together, these traits are directly related to locomotor performance. Despite their importance, few studies have quantified genetic variation for these skeletal traits in commercial turkeys, limiting opportunities for selection strategies aimed at improving walking ability and skeletal robustness.

In the present study, we estimated variance components for skeletal integrity traits of the tibia, femur, and humerus, including bone geometry traits (weight, length, midpoint, APD, and MLD) and bone strength traits (PF and U). A biomarker of bone metabolism (PYD), as well as production and welfare traits (BW, HL, FP, and GS), were also evaluated. A total of 21.9% of the evaluated turkeys displayed some degree of impaired walking based on GS. This prevalence is comparable to values reported in the same commercial line (Dong et al., 2023), but lower than those described in other turkey populations more than ten years ago (Da Costa et al., 2014). Such variation among studies may reflect differences in genetic background and the result of recent selection processes to improve GS (Kapell et al., 2017). Additionally, mean final BW in our study was consistent with values reported for commercial turkey lines at market age, raised under experimental conditions (Dong et al., 2023), suggesting that the turkeys evaluated in this study are representative of current performance standards and genetic background. Consequently, our results likely reflect the genetic and phenotypic landscape of modern commercial turkey lines.

Heritability estimates for bone geometry traits (weight, length, midpoint, APD, and MLD) were generally low to moderate. Although there are no published reports of heritability for these traits in domestic turkey populations, these estimates are consistent with those reported in broilers, where morphological traits of the tibia ranged between 0.23 and 0.54 (Gonzales-Ceron et al., 2015). In this study, length and midpoint showed the highest heritability estimates of all bone geometry traits across the tibia, femur, and humerus. Notably, bone length and midpoint are closely associated with cortical thickness and cross-sectional geometry, which in turn influence overall mechanical stability and the capacity to efficiently support and mobilize increasing body weight (Van Wyhe et al., 2014; Oviedo-Rondon et al., 2017). Furthermore, APD and MLD define the area of weight distribution within the bone. While fast-growing birds often develop thicker bones, a lack of proportionality between bone diameter and increasing body mass can negatively impact locomotion. Notably, the heritability estimates for these indicators were lower than those reported in broilers (0.36 ± 0.06) under a random breeding scheme (Gonzales-Ceron et al., 2015). This may be attributable to the high phenotypic and genetic homogeneity of the commercial population evaluated in this study. These findings highlight the potential for bone length and midpoint measurements to be integrated into selection programs, as has been demonstrated in broiler populations (Gonzales-Ceron et al., 2015).

In contrast, traits related to bone strength (PF and U) presented lower heritability estimates and larger PSDs. These results suggest that bone strength is heavily influenced by non-genetic factors such as mineralization, bone microarchitecture, nutrient absorption capacity, and locomotor activity (Kapell et al., 2017; Van Wyhe et al., 2014). A similar phenomenon has been observed in broilers, where tibia breaking strength exhibits the lowest heritability among all skeletal traits (Gonzales-Ceron et al., 2015). To reduce this variability in future research, accounting for bone mineral density as a covariate in ex vivo studies could help isolate the additive genetic component from environmental noise. Additionally, future studies would benefit from increased sample sizes to obtain more precise estimates for genomic selection. Implementing repeated measures via non-invasive methods, such as computed tomography (CT) scans, could provide detailed longitudinal data on bone density and cortical thickness (Charuta et al., 2012) as well detailed information for bone growth curves, allowing for the evaluation of bone conditions throughout the bird’s life. Finally, the inclusion of correlated, higher-heritability geometric or metabolic traits in multi-trait genetic evaluations may improve the overall accuracy of selection for bone strength in turkeys. The presence of additive genetic variance for bone geometry suggests that even within a highly homogeneous commercial population, sufficient genetic diversity remains to enable genetic selection for improved turkey welfare.

Regarding animal welfare traits, the heritability estimates observed for GS, FP, and HL were low. While heritability estimates for visual score assessments are well documented (Abdalla et al., 2019; Mulim et al., 2024), the heritability estimate found for GS in the current study is significantly lower than estimates reported in purebred turkey lines, such as 0.23 ± 0.01 (Mulim et al., 2024). While these results may initially suggest limited potential for direct genetic improvement, they likely reflect the high phenotypic and genotypic homogeneity of the commercial turkey population evaluated in this study. Furthermore, GS is a multifactorial trait heavily influenced by environmental factors, where inter-observer reliability and training are determinant (Wurtz and Riber, 2024). These challenges are amplified in more homogeneous commercial populations, reinforcing the need for more sophisticated indicator traits that can capture physiological details not visible to the human eye. With regards to HL and FP, heritability was even lower and nearly negligible. While the homogeneity of the population contributed to this effect, the high environmental influence and the role of behavioral interactions of injurious pecking (Haug et al., 2023) suggest that future evaluations of these traits would benefit from including social interaction models when estimating variance components.

In this study, bone geometry traits exhibited strong phenotypic associations among themselves, reflecting a coordinated morphological development. These findings align with previous reports in ducks, broilers, and laying hens, where robust covariance among bone dimensions suggests a shared developmental architecture and pleiotropic loci affecting skeletal frame (Duggan et al., 2017; Gonzalez-Ceron et al., 2015; Hocking et al., 2009). Similarly, BW across all ages was favorably associated with bone geometry traits, reinforcing the expected mechanical scaling of bone dimensions with increasing size and weight load, similar to reports in broilers (Gonzalez-Ceron et al., 2015). However, the correlations between bone geometry and mechanical strength traits were notably weaker and more variable. This dissociation suggests that increasing bone size does not guarantee a proportional improvement in mechanical integrity (Crespo et al., 2000; Oviedo-Rondon et al., 2018). While larger birds possess a larger skeletal frame, the internal quality of mineralization and composition may not keep pace with rapid muscle deposition (Williams et al., 2004). This phenomenon is supported by observations in wild turkey lines with no selection pressure for BW where they exhibit fewer leg deformities when compared to intensively selected turkey populations (Stover et al., 2018) and by comparative studies that show a faster maturation and bone growth rate of broilers compared to laying hens (Hocking et al., 2009). From a biomechanical perspective, a longer tibia bone increases the torque applied to joints during locomotion (Oviedo-Rondon et al., 2018). If bone diameter (APD/MLD) or strength does not increase proportionally, the risk of fractures and gait degradation rise. Consequently, supporting the argument that selecting for improved geometry alone is insufficient and allometric balance is needed.

Regarding welfare indicators, we observed that poorer gait (higher GS) tended to be associated with smaller or weaker bone phenotypes. Although GS is multifactorial and heavily influenced by environmental factors such as flooring and litter quality (Dong et al., 2023), the relationships observed represent a biological relationship between skeletal structure and walking ability (Bradshaw et al., 2002). Furthermore, the strong unfavorable associations between BW and GS highlight the well supported trade-off between rapid growth and leg health in modern poultry systems (Kapell et al., 2017). The discordance between bone geometry, strength, and gait scores further emphasizes that selection for improved welfare cannot rely solely on external bone measurements. Environmental stressors may exacerbate the underlying genetic predisposition for leg issues, requiring multi-trait selection strategies that balance production with the intrinsic mechanical requirements of the turkey skeleton.

PYD is a collagen cross-link that functions as a biomarker of bone resorption, offering insight into the remodeling processes underlying skeletal metabolism (Fujimoto and Moriguchi, 1978; Van Wyhe et al., 2014). In this study, we observed a heritability of 0.17 ± 0.08 for PYD, which, to our knowledge, represents the first heritability estimate reported for this biomarker in domestic turkeys. The use of repeated measures and age adjustments was essential, as PYD levels follow a non-linear trajectory during growth, particularly up to 17 weeks, which complicates the accuracy of single-point estimates (Rath et al., 2000). Due to this non-linearity, a single measurement may miss the most physiologically relevant period for PYD signaling. Furthermore, in commercial settings, birds exhibiting severe lameness or impaired gait are often culled before reaching market age. This practice may inadvertently remove individuals during their most sensitive metabolic windows, potentially reducing the observable variation in PYD levels. While comparable heritability estimates in poultry are currently unavailable, research in turkeys has shown that PYD reflects the dynamics of matrix maturation and resorption as processes that fundamentally influence bone strength (Zhong et al., 2012; Van Wyhe et al., 2014). Notably, systemic bone resorption in turkeys near market age is unusually high compared to other species, suggesting a state of rapid and continuous turnover (Van Wyhe et al., 2014). While larger datasets with longitudinal sampling are required to improve parameter precision, future experimental studies characterizing the transition between peak growth and skeletal maturity are necessary to fully realize the potential of PYD as a selection criteria.

The phenotypic correlations observed in this study suggest that collagen turnover, as measured by PYD, could be an indicator of skeletal health during rapid growth in turkeys. Elevated PYD levels were associated with a reduction in bone strength, reinforcing the biological principle that increased collagen turnover correlates with reduced bone structural integrity (Fujimoto and Moriguchi, 1978; Van Wyhe et al., 2014). This pattern was mirrored by unfavorable directional trends in the genetic correlations, particularly between PYD and body weight as the birds aged. However, due to the large PSD obtained for the genetic correlation estimates, further studies are necessary to validate this hypothesis. Nevertheless, these findings suggest a growth-skeletal imbalance; as birds rapidly accumulate mass, there is a simultaneous increase in bone remodeling and resorption to accommodate the mechanical strain. This metabolic cost of rapid growth is a challenge of modern poultry lines, where the rate of mineral deposition often lags behind the rate of collagen turnover (Van Wyhe et al., 2014). Currently, the three point fracture test as the gold standard for bone strength measurement can only be post-mortem, meaning that selection must rely on sibling testing. PYD, however, is a minimally invasive blood biomarker that can be measured in vivo during the bird’s peak growth phase. This could allow for the identification of individuals with high resorption rates before they are selected as breeding stock, while bone size provides a snapshot of the skeletal frame, PYD captures the dynamic state of the bone matrix. The inclusion of metabolic markers like PYD into selection models could therefore enhance the detection of individuals prone to excessive resorption, offering a more nuanced approach to mitigating locomotor problems in commercial turkey populations.

In summary, the genetic parameters of the traits evaluated in this study indicates that (1) bone geometry is moderately heritable, making it a practical target for genomic selection; (2) mechanical bone strength traits show lower heritability and are less correlated with geometry, emphasizing the potential influence of non-additive genetic effects, measurement limitations, and the need for larger reference populations when implementing genomic selection for these traits; and (3) PYD is genetically informative for bone strength and may serve as a complementary metabolic indicator for breeding purposes. Heritability of welfare-related traits was low to near zero, which may reflect limited additive genetic variance, low prevalence of extreme phenotypes, or challenges in trait measurement. Consequently, direct selection for these welfare traits alone is likely to be inefficient. These findings support multi-trait genomic selection approaches for improving turkey skeletal health in breeding programs. By integrating high-heritability geometry metrics, physiological biomarkers (e.g., PYD), and welfare indicators, breeders can improve skeletal robustness and locomotor health while maintaining productivity.

Limitations and future directions

To our knowledge, this is the first study to present heritability estimates, genetic and phenotypic correlations between bone geometry and strength traits in tibia, femur and humerus, and PYD as a bone metabolism marker in turkeys. However, this study has some limitations. First, the number of animals with both phenotypic and genotypic records was limited, reducing the precision of the variance component estimates. Therefore, these estimates should be considered as preliminary evidences. However, this dataset provides an essential foundation for identifying candidate traits and guiding future large-scale studies. Second, due to the logistical constraints of large-scale commercial field sampling and DNA degradation during international transit, the raw genotyping performance was lower than expected. However, genotype imputation and quality control has contributed to mitigating the missing data and low-quality markers. Third, the analyses were conducted using only data from a commercial turkey crossbred population without pedigree links to nucleus purebred animals, further reinforcing the exploratory nature of the variance components reported. However, heritability estimates in crossbred animals provide vital insights into the genetic architecture of traits as they are expressed in commercial production environments. Although the variance components may differ, if there is additive genetic variability in a crossbred population, it is likely that these traits are also heritable in purebred populations. Future studies should inspect the traits identified here in nucleus purebred populations to ensure their direct applicability to turkey breeding programs. Fourth, bone traits were measured at slaughter, and animals were sampled at different ages. This design restricted the availability of repeated phenotypic records across the lifespan of individuals. As a result, longitudinal changes in bone development and integrity could not be evaluated. Advanced imaging technologies such as computed tomography in further research might provide opportunities for non-destructive and repeated assessments of bone morphology over time, although they do not directly capture biomechanical strength as evaluated here. In this context, biomarkers such as PYD could serve as auxiliary traits, due to correlations between bone metabolism and bone strength traits to be explored without requiring slaughter of selection candidates.

Conclusions

Our preliminary findings provide insights into the genetic background of bone geometry, strength, and metabolism in domestic turkeys. While bone geometry traits were moderately heritable, their variable phenotypic correlations with bone strength suggest that bone geometry alone is insufficient to improve skeletal integrity. This relationship indicates that the environmental and management driven variance in bone strength remains a challenge for breeding programs. However, the moderately heritability of PYD and its correlations suggest it as a potential phenotype that could complement traditional measurements by reflecting matrix collagen turnover, a characteristic that is correlated to bone strength and is not captured by bone geometry alone. Furthermore, the low heritability observed indicate the need for genomic selection with large reference populations or correlated traits. Yet, while these results are preliminary, they provide a foundational step toward identifying a multi-trait approach to understand bone integrity and improve locomotor health and welfare in turkey breeding programs.
