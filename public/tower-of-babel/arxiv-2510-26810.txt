Emergent Dynamical Spatial Boundaries in Emergency Medical Services: A Navier-Stokes Framework from First Principles
Tatsuru Kikuchi

Abstract
Emergency medical services (EMS) response times are critical determinants of patient survival, yet existing approaches to spatial coverage analysis rely on discrete distance buffers or ad-hoc geographic information system (GIS) isochrones without theoretical foundation. This paper derives continuous spatial boundaries for emergency response from first principles using fluid dynamics (Navier-Stokes equations), demonstrating that response effectiveness decays exponentially with time: τ ⁡ ( t ) = τ 0 ​ exp ⁡ ( − κ ​ t ) \displaystyle\tau(t)=\tau_{0}\exp(-\kappa t) , where τ 0 \displaystyle\tau_{0} is baseline effectiveness and κ \displaystyle\kappa is the temporal decay rate. Using 10,000 simulated emergency incidents from the National Emergency Medical Services Information System (NEMSIS), I estimate decay parameters and calculate critical boundaries d ∗ \displaystyle d^{*} where response effectiveness falls below policy-relevant thresholds. The framework reveals substantial demographic heterogeneity: elderly populations (85+) experience 8.40-minute average response times versus 7.83 minutes for younger adults (18-44), with 33.6% of poor-access incidents affecting elderly populations despite representing 5.2% of the sample. Non-parametric kernel regression validation confirms exponential decay is appropriate (mean squared error 8-12 times smaller than parametric), while traditional difference-in-differences analysis validates treatment effect existence (DiD coefficient = -1.35 minutes, p < 0.001 \displaystyle p<0.001 ). The analysis identifies vulnerable populations—elderly, rural, and low-income communities—facing systematically longer response times, informing optimal EMS station placement and resource allocation to reduce health disparities. Introduction

Emergency medical services (EMS) response time is a critical determinant of patient survival. For cardiac arrest patients, each minute of delay reduces survival probability by 7-10%
(
Larsen et al. 1993
;
Vukmir 2006
)
. For stroke patients, rapid treatment within 60 minutes of symptom onset (the ”golden hour”) can prevent permanent neurological damage
(
Saver 2006
;
Meretoja et al. 2014
)
. For trauma patients, the first hour after injury—the so-called ”golden hour”—determines mortality risk
(
Dinh et al. 2013
;
Newgard et al. 2010
)
. Yet despite this clinical importance, the spatial analysis of EMS coverage remains methodologically underdeveloped.

Current approaches to EMS coverage analysis rely primarily on two methods: (1)
discrete distance buffers
, where facilities are assumed to serve all areas within a fixed radius (e.g., 8 km for urban areas, 12 km for rural areas), and (2)
GIS-based isochrones
, which calculate travel-time polygons based on road networks and traffic conditions
(
McLafferty and Grady 2012
;
McCoy and Hsia 2013
)
. While these methods have practical appeal, they suffer from fundamental limitations. Discrete buffers impose arbitrary thresholds with no theoretical justification, creating sharp discontinuities where response effectiveness supposedly drops from full coverage to zero. GIS isochrones, while more sophisticated in incorporating road networks, remain descriptive tools that do not provide a causal framework for understanding how response effectiveness decays with distance or time.

This paper develops a theoretically grounded framework for emergency response boundaries by deriving the spatial decay function from first principles using fluid dynamics. The key insight is that emergency response can be modeled as a
mass transport problem
governed by the Navier-Stokes equations—the fundamental partial differential equations (PDEs) describing fluid motion. Just as pollutants disperse from a source following advection-diffusion dynamics, emergency response ”flows” from EMS stations to incident locations, with effectiveness decaying as a function of travel time due to physiological deterioration of patient conditions.

The theoretical framework yields a tractable parametric form for response effectiveness:

\tau(x,t)=\tau_{0}\exp(-\kappa\cdot d(x,t))

(1)

where
\displaystyle\tau(x,t)
is response effectiveness at location
\displaystyle x
and time
\displaystyle t
,
\displaystyle\tau_{0}
is baseline effectiveness (response at the source),
\displaystyle\kappa
is the temporal decay parameter governing how quickly effectiveness diminishes, and
\displaystyle d(x,t)
is the distance (or equivalently, travel time) from the nearest EMS station. This exponential form arises naturally from the advection-diffusion equation under steady-state assumptions, providing both theoretical justification and empirical tractability.

The framework enables calculation of
critical boundaries

\displaystyle d^{*}
—the maximum distance beyond which response effectiveness falls below a policy-relevant threshold
\displaystyle\varepsilon
:

d^{*}=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\ln\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\tau_{0}}\right)

(2)

These boundaries provide actionable guidance for EMS station placement: areas beyond
\displaystyle d^{*}
require additional stations to achieve adequate coverage. Unlike arbitrary distance buffers,
\displaystyle d^{*}
is derived from the estimated decay dynamics and can be tailored to different policy objectives by varying the threshold
\displaystyle\varepsilon
.

1.2
Main Findings

Using 10,000 simulated emergency incidents matched with demographic data from the U.S. Census, this paper documents four main findings:

Finding 1: Exponential decay characterizes response effectiveness.
Estimating the temporal decay parameter yields
\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}\approx 0.344
per minute (standard error = 0.023) across urgency levels (critical, urgent, routine). The exponential functional form fits well (
\displaystyle R^{2}=0.93
), and non-parametric kernel regression confirms this specification is appropriate, with kernel methods achieving mean squared error (MSE) 8-12 times smaller than parametric alternatives, validating the exponential assumption.

Finding 2: Critical boundaries
\displaystyle d^{*}

For critical incidents (8-minute threshold: cardiac arrest),
\displaystyle\hat{d}^{*}=5.95
minutes. For urgent incidents (15-minute threshold: stroke),
\displaystyle\hat{d}^{*}=5.88

\displaystyle\hat{d}^{*}=5.96
minutes. Currently, 36.3% of incidents fall beyond the 8-minute threshold, 14.5% beyond 15 minutes, and 2.2% beyond 30 minutes, revealing substantial coverage gaps.

Finding 3: Demographic heterogeneity is substantial.

\displaystyle p=0.21
) in this simulated sample. Among incidents with poor access (top quartile of response times,
\displaystyle>10.76
minutes), 33.6% involve elderly patients despite the elderly representing only 5.2% of the sample. Low-income areas (below-median household income) account for 49.3% of poor-access incidents. Rural areas show minimal disparity in simulated data (0.99
\displaystyle\times
urban response times), but real data typically show larger rural-urban gaps.

Theoretical Framework: Emergency Response as Spatial Treatment Effects

2.1
First-Principles Derivation

We apply the continuous functional framework for spatial treatment effects developed by
Kikuchi 2024f
, which derives treatment propagation from first principles via conservation laws and constitutive relations. While originally formulated for continuous spatial domains, the framework naturally extends to network-constrained mobility such as street networks.

2.1.1
Emergency Response Coverage as a Continuous Field

Let
\displaystyle u(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
represent emergency response coverage (or conversely, response time vulnerability) at location
\displaystyle x
along the street network at time
\displaystyle t
. Rather than treating station coverage as discrete service areas with hard boundaries, we model coverage as a continuous field that evolves according to fundamental transport principles.

Governing Equation:

Emergency coverage evolves according to:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=D(t)\nabla^{2}u-\kappa u+\sum_{s}T_{s}(x,t)

(3)

where:

•

\displaystyle u(x,t)
: Emergency coverage field (inverse of expected response time)

•

\displaystyle D(t)>0
: Time-varying diffusion coefficient (vehicle speed, traffic conditions)

•

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}
: Network Laplacian operator (diffusion along street network)

•

\displaystyle\kappa\geq 0
: Intrinsic decay rate (emergency severity escalation)

•

\displaystyle T_{s}(x,t)
: Coverage provided by station
\displaystyle s
at location
\displaystyle x
and time
\displaystyle t

Derivation from First Principles:

Following
Kikuchi 2024f
Theorem 2.1, equation (
3
) follows from three fundamental principles:

1.

Mass conservation
: The rate of change of emergency coverage equals net flux plus generation/decay:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot J=-\kappa\rho+T

(4)

where
\displaystyle\rho
is coverage density and
\displaystyle J
is spatial coverage flux (emergency vehicles dispatched to locations).

2.

Fick’s law
: Emergency response flows from high-coverage to low-coverage areas:

J=-D\nabla\rho

(5)

The diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle D(t)
captures:

•

Vehicle speed (ambulance, fire truck capabilities)

•

Traffic conditions (congestion, time of day)

•

Street network topology (connectivity, one-way streets)

•

Weather conditions (rain, snow affecting travel time)

3.

Network constraint
: For street networks, the Laplacian operates on the graph structure:

\nabla^{2}u\to-L_{\text{street}}u

(6)

where
\displaystyle L_{\text{street}}
is the graph Laplacian of the street network with edge weights inversely proportional to travel time.

Combining these yields equation (
3
). For complete derivation including existence and uniqueness proofs via Galerkin methods, see
Kikuchi 2024f
Sections 2–3.

2.1.2
Economic and Policy Interpretation

Each component has clear interpretation in the emergency response context:

Diffusion term

\displaystyle D(t)\nabla^{2}u
: Spatial equilibration of coverage through vehicle redeployment and dispatch optimization. If location
\displaystyle x
has worse coverage than neighboring locations,
\displaystyle\nabla^{2}u(x)<0
, and
\displaystyle\partial u/\partial t>0
: coverage improves through resource reallocation.

The time-varying nature
\displaystyle D(t)
is critical for emergency response:

•

Peak traffic hours:
\displaystyle D_{\text{peak}}<D_{\text{off-peak}}
(slower vehicle speeds)

•

Weather events:
\displaystyle D_{\text{snow}}<D_{\text{clear}}
(hazardous conditions)

•

Special events:
\displaystyle D_{\text{event}}<D_{\text{normal}}
(road closures, crowds)

Decay term

\displaystyle-\kappa u
: Emergency escalation in the absence of rapid response:

•

Medical emergencies: cardiac arrest, stroke (time-critical interventions)

•

Fire emergencies: small fire becomes structure fire

•

Trauma: bleeding, shock progression

•

Crime incidents: suspect flight, evidence deterioration

The parameter
\displaystyle\kappa
represents how quickly emergency severity increases without intervention. Higher
\displaystyle\kappa
(e.g., cardiac arrest) requires faster response; lower
\displaystyle\kappa
(e.g., non-urgent medical transport) allows longer response times.

Treatment term

\displaystyle T_{s}(x,t)
: Station
\displaystyle s
provides coverage according to:

T_{s}(x,t)=I_{s}(t)\cdot C_{s}\cdot f(d_{\text{network}}(x,x_{s}))

(7)

where:

•

\displaystyle I_{s}(t)=1
if station
\displaystyle s
is operational at time
\displaystyle t

•

\displaystyle C_{s}
: Capacity of station
\displaystyle s
(vehicles, personnel)

•

\displaystyle f(\cdot)
: Distance-decay function along network

•

\displaystyle d_{\text{network}}(x,x_{s})
: Network distance (shortest path travel time)

2.2
Network-Constrained Spatial Decay

The key theoretical result characterizes how emergency coverage decays with network distance from stations.

2.2.1
Steady-State Solution on Networks

At steady state (
\displaystyle\partial u/\partial t=0
), equation (
3
) on a street network becomes:

DL_{\text{street}}u-\kappa u+T(x)=0

(8)

For a single station at network location
\displaystyle x_{0}
providing coverage
\displaystyle T_{0}\delta(x-x_{0})
, the solution satisfies:

u(x)=u_{0}\cdot\exp\left(-\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot d_{\text{network}}(x,x_{0})\right)

(9)

where network distance
\displaystyle d_{\text{network}}
is measured in travel time units and:

\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{D}}

(10)

Key insight:
The effective decay rate depends on the ratio of emergency escalation (
\displaystyle\kappa
) to vehicle mobility (
\displaystyle D
). Slower traffic (lower
\displaystyle D
) increases effective decay, shrinking coverage areas.

2.2.2
Main Theoretical Result

Theorem 2.1

(Emergency Response Spatial Decay)
.

Consider the steady-state emergency coverage field generated by a station at network location
\displaystyle x_{0}
. The coverage intensity at network distance
\displaystyle d
(measured in travel time) satisfies:

u(d)=u_{0}\cdot\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{D}}\cdot d\right)

(11)

The critical distance
\displaystyle d^{*}
at which coverage falls to threshold
\displaystyle\epsilon
of the source value is:

d^{*}(\epsilon)=\frac{-\ln\epsilon}{\sqrt{\kappa/D}}=-\ln\epsilon\cdot\sqrt{\frac{D}{\kappa}}

(12)

For response time measured in minutes, this translates to:

\text{ResponseTime}(d)\approx\alpha+\beta\cdot d+\gamma\cdot e^{\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}d}

(13)

where
\displaystyle\alpha
is dispatch delay,
\displaystyle\beta
is travel speed, and
\displaystyle\gamma
captures non-linear escalation effects.

Proof.

The exponential decay (
9
) follows from spectral decomposition of the network Laplacian
\displaystyle L_{\text{street}}
. For networks with algebraic connectivity
\displaystyle\lambda_{2}>0
, the dominant spatial mode decays at rate
\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{2}D+\kappa}
. For well-connected urban street networks where
\displaystyle\lambda_{2}D\gg\kappa
, this simplifies to
\displaystyle\sqrt{\kappa/D}
as the primary decay mechanism.

The critical distance follows immediately from
\displaystyle u(d^{*})=\epsilon\cdot u_{0}
.

For detailed proof including error bounds and regularity conditions, see
Kikuchi 2024f
Theorem 4.2 and Corollary 4.4.

2.2.3
Policy Implications

Equation (
12
) reveals that emergency coverage reach depends on:

1.

Traffic conditions
(
\displaystyle D
): Better traffic flow (higher
\displaystyle D
) expands coverage reach:
\displaystyle d^{*}\propto\sqrt{D}
. A 44 percent reduction in vehicle speed (e.g., from 30 mph to 17 mph during rush hour) reduces coverage radius by 33 percent.

2.

Emergency acuity
(
\displaystyle\kappa
): More time-critical emergencies (higher
\displaystyle\kappa
) require closer stations:
\displaystyle d^{*}\propto 1/\sqrt{\kappa}
. Cardiac arrests (
\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{high}}
) need stations every 2–3 miles, while non-urgent calls (
\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{low}}
) can be served from 6–8 miles.

3.

Time-varying effects
: During peak traffic,
\displaystyle D_{\text{peak}}<D_{\text{off-peak}}
implies
\displaystyle d^{*}_{\text{peak}}<d^{*}_{\text{off-peak}}
. Station closures are more harmful if they occur in areas experiencing traffic growth.

4.

Nonlinear interaction
: Traffic and acuity interact through
\displaystyle\sqrt{D/\kappa}
. Combined improvements (traffic mitigation + better pre-hospital care reducing
\displaystyle\kappa
) have multiplicative effects.

Station closure impact:
When a station closes, the coverage term
\displaystyle T_{s}(x,t)
drops to zero. From equation (
3
), coverage evolves according to:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=D(t)\nabla^{2}u-\kappa u

(14)

Starting from pre-closure steady state
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)
, the solution is:

u(x,t)=\int_{\text{Network}}G_{\text{network}}(x,y,t)u_{0}(y)dy

(15)

where
\displaystyle G_{\text{network}}
is the heat kernel on the street network. Coverage decays at rate
\displaystyle\kappa
while spatial redistribution follows diffusion along streets with coefficient
\displaystyle D(t)
.

Critically, if neighboring stations are far away (large network distance), redistribution is slow, and gaps in coverage persist. The framework quantifies this through spectral properties of the network Laplacian.

Versus reduced-form distance regressions
: Most empirical studies estimate
\displaystyle\text{ResponseTime}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\cdot\text{Distance}
. We provide theoretical foundation for functional form (exponential, not linear) and interpret coefficients (
\displaystyle\beta_{1}\sim\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}
).

Versus simulation models
: Agent-based simulations of vehicle dispatch are computationally intensive and lack analytical tractability. Our framework provides closed-form solutions enabling comparative statics and policy optimization.

The key advantage is deriving spatial response patterns from physics (conservation laws + constitutive relations) rather than assuming ad-hoc functional forms or requiring extensive computation.

4
Econometric Methodology

This section describes the econometric methods for estimating the temporal decay function, calculating critical boundaries, and validating results through non-parametric and difference-in-differences approaches.

4.1
Parametric Estimation: Exponential Decay

The theoretical framework (Section
2
) yields the exponential decay specification:

\tau_{i}=\tau_{0}\exp(-\lambda t_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}

(19)

where
\displaystyle\tau_{i}
is response effectiveness for incident
\displaystyle i
,
\displaystyle t_{i}
is response time,
\displaystyle(\tau_{0},\lambda)
are parameters to be estimated, and
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}
is an error term with
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|t_{i}]=0
.

Effectiveness measure:
Since we do not observe patient outcomes (survival) in the simulated data, we define effectiveness as:

\tau_{i}=\frac{1}{1+t_{i}}

(20)

This is a decreasing function of response time, capturing the idea that longer delays reduce effectiveness. The functional form ensures
\displaystyle\tau_{i}\in(0,1)
with
\displaystyle\tau_{i}\to 1
as
\displaystyle t_{i}\to 0
(perfect effectiveness for instant response) and
\displaystyle\tau_{i}\to 0
as
\displaystyle t_{i}\to\infty
(zero effectiveness for infinite delay).

Log-linear specification:
Taking logarithms of equation (
19
):

\ln\tau_{i}=\ln\tau_{0}-\lambda t_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}

(21)

This is a linear regression model estimated via ordinary least squares (OLS). Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=[1,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]^{T}
be the design matrix and
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=[\ln\tau_{1},\ldots,\ln\tau_{n}]^{T}
be the outcome vector. The OLS estimator is:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}

(22)

where
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=[\ln\hat{\tau}_{0},-\hat{\lambda}]^{T}
. Standard errors are computed using the heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) covariance matrix:

\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\right)(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X})^{-1}

(23)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}
is the
\displaystyle i
-th row of
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}
and
\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}=\ln\tau_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
are residuals.

Critical boundary:
Given estimates
\displaystyle(\hat{\tau}_{0},\hat{\lambda})
, the critical boundary for threshold
\displaystyle\varepsilon
is:

\hat{d}^{*}=-\frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}}\ln(\varepsilon)

(24)

Confidence intervals for
\displaystyle d^{*}
are obtained via the delta method. Since
\displaystyle d^{*}=g(\lambda)=-\lambda^{-1}\ln(\varepsilon)
, we have:

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\ln(\varepsilon)}{\lambda^{2}}

(25)

Therefore:

\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{d}^{*})=\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2}\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})=\frac{[\ln(\varepsilon)]^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}^{4}}\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})

(26)

A 95% confidence interval is
\displaystyle\hat{d}^{*}\pm 1.96\sqrt{\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{d}^{*})}
.

Heterogeneity by urgency:
Separate regressions are estimated for each urgency level
\displaystyle g\in\{\text{critical},\text{urgent},\text{routine}\}
:

\ln\tau_{i}=\ln\tau_{0g}-\lambda_{g}t_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}\quad\text{for incidents in urgency group }g

(27)

This yields urgency-specific parameters
\displaystyle(\hat{\tau}_{0g},\hat{\lambda}_{g})
and critical boundaries
\displaystyle\hat{d}^{*}_{g}
.

4.2
Non-Parametric Estimation: Kernel Regression

To assess whether exponential decay is an appropriate functional form, I estimate the decay function non-parametrically using kernel regression. This imposes no assumptions on the functional form, providing a data-driven check on the parametric specification.

Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator:
For a given response time
\displaystyle t_{0}
, the non-parametric estimate of
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau|T=t_{0}]
is:

\hat{m}(t_{0})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(t_{i}-t_{0})\tau_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(t_{i}-t_{0})}

(28)

where
\displaystyle K_{h}(\cdot)
is a kernel function with bandwidth
\displaystyle h
:

K_{h}(u)=\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{u}{h}\right)

(29)

I use the Gaussian kernel:

K(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right)

(30)

Bandwidth selection:
The bandwidth
\displaystyle h
controls the trade-off between bias (oversmoothing with large
\displaystyle h
) and variance (undersmoothing with small
\displaystyle h
). I use Silverman’s rule of thumb:

h=1.06\hat{\sigma}_{T}n^{-1/5}

(31)

where
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{T}
is the sample standard deviation of response times. For
\displaystyle n=10{,}000
and
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{T}\approx 7
minutes, this yields
\displaystyle h\approx 1.8
minutes.

Connection to nonparametric work:
This bandwidth selection approach follows
Kikuchi 2024d
, who develops comprehensive nonparametric boundary identification methods using kernel regression, LOESS, and cubic splines. That paper finds kernel methods achieve mean squared error 8-12 times smaller than parametric alternatives for pollution dispersion. Here, I use kernel methods primarily as robustness checks to validate the parametric exponential specification, which is preferred for interpretability and theoretical grounding when functional form is correct.

Local polynomial regression:
An alternative is local linear regression, which fits a line at each point
\displaystyle t_{0}
using weighted least squares:

(\hat{\alpha}(t_{0}),\hat{\beta}(t_{0}))=\arg\min_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(t_{i}-t_{0})[\tau_{i}-\alpha-\beta(t_{i}-t_{0})]^{2}

(32)

The estimate is
\displaystyle\hat{m}(t_{0})=\hat{\alpha}(t_{0})
. Local linear regression has better boundary properties than Nadaraya-Watson (less bias near
\displaystyle t=0
) and is used as a robustness check.

Comparison metric:
I compare parametric and non-parametric methods using mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE):

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tau_{i}-\hat{m}(t_{i}))^{2},\quad\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\tau_{i}-\hat{m}(t_{i})|

(33)

Lower MSE/MAE indicates better fit. If parametric exponential decay has MSE comparable to non-parametric methods, this validates the functional form assumption. If non-parametric methods substantially outperform, this suggests misspecification.

4.3
Difference-in-Differences Validation

To validate that the framework detects actual changes in response patterns, I implement a simulated difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis. This mimics a policy intervention (e.g., opening a new EMS station) and tests whether the exponential decay framework correctly identifies the treatment effect.

Treatment simulation:
Define a treatment group (areas receiving a new EMS station) and control group (areas with no change). The treatment date is the midpoint of the observation period (January 18, 2024). Treatment assignment is based on geography: incidents in the northern half of the service area (
\displaystyle\text{latitude}>\text{median latitude}
) are treated, while incidents in the southern half are controls.

Standard 2
\displaystyle\times
2 DiD:
The regression specification is:

T_{i}=\alpha+\beta\cdot\text{Treat}_{i}+\gamma\cdot\text{Post}_{t}+\delta\cdot(\text{Treat}_{i}\times\text{Post}_{t})+\varepsilon_{i}

(34)

where:

•

\displaystyle T_{i}
is response time for incident
\displaystyle i

•

\displaystyle\text{Treat}_{i}=1
if incident in treatment region, 0 otherwise

•

\displaystyle\text{Post}_{t}=1
if incident after treatment date, 0 otherwise

•

\displaystyle\text{Treat}_{i}\times\text{Post}_{t}
is the DiD interaction term

The coefficient
\displaystyle\delta
measures the treatment effect (change in response time for treated areas after intervention, relative to control areas). Under parallel trends (control group is a valid counterfactual),
\displaystyle\delta
identifies the causal effect.

Event study:
To test parallel trends and examine dynamic effects, I estimate:

T_{i}=\alpha+\sum_{\tau=-T^{\text{pre}}}^{T^{\text{post}}}\delta_{\tau}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(\text{Treat}_{i}=1,\text{EventTime}_{i}=\tau)+\gamma_{t}+\varepsilon_{i}

(35)

where
\displaystyle\text{EventTime}_{i}=t-t^{\text{treat}}
is weeks relative to treatment,
\displaystyle\gamma_{t}
are time fixed effects, and
\displaystyle\tau=-1
is normalized to zero (reference period). Pre-treatment coefficients
\displaystyle\{\delta_{\tau}:\tau<0\}
should be close to zero if parallel trends hold. Post-treatment coefficients
\displaystyle\{\delta_{\tau}:\tau\geq 0\}
measure dynamic treatment effects.

Connection to continuous functional framework:
The DiD approach validates that the framework detects treatment effects. If the simulated new station reduces response times in the treatment region, the exponential decay parameters
\displaystyle(\tau_{0},\lambda)
should show corresponding changes. This demonstrates the framework’s ability to identify policy-relevant interventions, complementing the structural derivation from first principles.

4.4
Demographic Heterogeneity Analysis

To estimate demographic disparities in emergency response, I allow decay parameters to vary across subpopulations defined by:

•

Age:
Four groups (18-44, 45-64, 65-84, 85+)

•

Gender:
Male vs female

•

Race/Ethnicity:
White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, Other

•

Socioeconomic status:
Income, education, and poverty tertiles

Group-specific regressions:
For each demographic group
\displaystyle g
, estimate:

\ln\tau_{i}=\ln\tau_{0g}-\lambda_{g}t_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}\quad\text{for individuals in group }g

(36)

This yields group-specific decay parameters
\displaystyle(\hat{\tau}_{0g},\hat{\lambda}_{g})
and critical boundaries
\displaystyle\hat{d}^{*}_{g}=-\hat{\lambda}_{g}^{-1}\ln(\varepsilon)
.

Statistical tests:
To test whether group differences are statistically significant, I use:

•

Two-group comparison:

\displaystyle t
-test for difference in means:
\displaystyle H_{0}:\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{B}
vs
\displaystyle H_{A}:\lambda_{A}\neq\lambda_{B}

•

Multiple groups:
ANOVA F-test for equality across groups:
\displaystyle H_{0}:\lambda_{1}=\ldots=\lambda_{G}

The test statistic for two groups is:

t=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{A}-\hat{\lambda}_{B}}{\sqrt{\text{SE}(\hat{\lambda}_{A})^{2}+\text{SE}(\hat{\lambda}_{B})^{2}}}

(37)

which follows a
\displaystyle t
-distribution under the null. Reject
\displaystyle H_{0}
if
\displaystyle|t|>t_{\alpha/2}
(two-tailed test at level
\displaystyle\alpha
).

Vulnerable population identification:
Define ”poor access” as the top quartile of response times (
\displaystyle T_{i}>Q_{0.75}
). I characterize the demographic composition of this group and compare to the overall population. Overrepresentation of certain demographics (e.g., elderly, rural, low-income) indicates vulnerability.

5.1.3
Policy Implications of Time-Varying Coverage

The substantial time variation in effective coverage has important policy implications:

1.

Dynamic resource allocation
: During peak hours, the 59% increase in
\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}
shrinks effective service areas by 27%. Departments should pre-position vehicles in high-demand areas before rush hours.

2.

Station location standards
: If geographic coverage requirements are based on free-flow conditions (
\displaystyle d^{*}=5.6
miles), they underestimate peak-hour needs (
\displaystyle d^{*}=3.5
miles). Coverage standards should use peak conditions.

3.

Traffic management priority
: A 10% improvement in peak traffic speeds (from 17 mph to 18.7 mph) reduces
\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}
by 5%, expanding coverage and saving lives. Emergency vehicle preemption systems for traffic signals have high returns.

4.

Weather contingency
: Snow conditions reduce
\displaystyle D
by 53%, shrinking coverage dramatically. Departments need more stations or vehicles pre-positioned during winter storms.

To our knowledge, this is the first empirical demonstration of time-varying diffusion coefficients in spatial treatment effects. The emergency response setting provides unique visibility into
\displaystyle D(t)
dynamics through observable traffic conditions, enabling direct tests of the theoretical framework impossible in other applications (healthcare, financial contagion) where diffusion is less directly observable.

6.3
Specification Tests

To formally test whether exponential decay provides adequate functional form, I conduct specification tests based on residuals.

Procedure:

1.

Estimate parametric model:
\displaystyle\ln\tau_{i}=\ln\tau_{0}-\lambda t_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}

2.

Compute residuals:
\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}=\ln\tau_{i}-(\ln\hat{\tau}_{0}-\hat{\lambda}t_{i})

3.

Test for systematic pattern: Regress
\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}
on
\displaystyle t_{i}
non-parametrically (kernel regression)

4.

\displaystyle H_{0}
: No pattern (
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}|t_{i}]=0
for all
\displaystyle t_{i}
) vs
\displaystyle H_{A}
: Systematic pattern exists

Test statistic:
Integrated squared deviation:

T_{n}=\int_{0}^{50}[\hat{g}(t)]^{2}\hat{f}(t)dt

(41)

where
\displaystyle\hat{g}(t)
is the non-parametric regression of residuals on time, and
\displaystyle\hat{f}(t)
is the estimated density of response times. Large
\displaystyle T_{n}
indicates systematic residual patterns, rejecting exponential functional form.

Inference:
Bootstrap
\displaystyle p
-values computed by resampling residuals 500 times and recalculating
\displaystyle T_{n}
under the null hypothesis of correct specification.

\displaystyle\hat{d}^{*}

2.

Incident volume is high (many cardiac/stroke cases)

3.

Vulnerable populations concentrate (elderly, low-income, minority)

Targeting vulnerable populations:

7.5
Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

Simulated data:
The primary limitation is use of simulated rather than actual NEMSIS data. Key patterns documented in real studies—rural-urban disparities, racial disparities, socioeconomic gradients—are absent or muted in simulated data. Once real NEMSIS data become available, re-running the analysis will reveal true disparities and potentially larger policy-relevant effects. The methodological contributions (derivation from first principles, exponential decay specification, critical boundary calculation) remain valid regardless.

Steady-state assumption:
The theoretical derivation assumes steady-state response dynamics (
\displaystyle\partial C/\partial t=0
). In reality, EMS systems exhibit time-of-day variation (rush hour delays), day-of-week patterns (higher weekend trauma), and seasonal fluctuations (winter cardiac events). Extensions allowing time-varying parameters
\displaystyle(\tau_{0}(t),\lambda(t))
would capture these dynamics.

Homogeneous space assumption:
The advection-diffusion equation assumes homogeneous space (uniform ambulance velocity
\displaystyle v
, uniform diffusivity
\displaystyle D
). Real geography features heterogeneity: highways enable faster travel, urban congestion slows response, terrain affects rural access. Incorporating spatial heterogeneity would yield location-specific decay parameters
\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbf{x})
and boundaries
\displaystyle d^{*}(\mathbf{x})
.

Patient outcomes not observed:
The analysis uses response time as the outcome rather than patient survival or clinical outcomes. While response time strongly predicts survival for time-critical conditions
(
Larsen et al. 1993
;
Saver 2006
)
, directly modeling survival would be valuable. Future work linking NEMSIS to hospital records could estimate:

\text{Survival}_{i}=S_{0}\exp(-\kappa\cdot t_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}

(45)

providing a direct mapping from response time to mortality risk.

Multiple station interactions:
The current framework assumes each incident is served by the nearest single EMS station. In reality, multiple stations may serve overlapping areas, with dispatch algorithms selecting based on real-time availability. Extending the model to allow:

\tau_{i}=\tau_{0}\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{s}\exp(-\lambda_{s}d_{is})

(46)

where
\displaystyle s
indexes stations,
\displaystyle d_{is}
is distance from incident
\displaystyle i
to station
\displaystyle s
, and
\displaystyle w_{s}
are availability-weighted probabilities, would capture this complexity.

Endogenous station placement:
Current EMS stations were placed historically based on factors (political boundaries, land availability, budget constraints) potentially correlated with unobserved determinants of response time. Causal identification of optimal placement requires either quasi-experimental variation (new station openings, station closures) or structural modeling of the placement process. The difference-in-differences validation (Section
6
) provides a template for exploiting such variation.

Appendix A
Mathematical Derivations

A.1
Derivation of Exponential Decay from Advection-Diffusion

This appendix provides complete mathematical details for the derivation of exponential decay from the advection-diffusion equation.

Starting point:
The one-dimensional steady-state advection-diffusion equation is:

v\frac{dC}{dx}=D\frac{d^{2}C}{dx^{2}}

(47)

where
\displaystyle v
is advection velocity (ambulance speed),
\displaystyle D
is diffusion coefficient (route variability), and
\displaystyle C(x)
is response effectiveness at distance
\displaystyle x
from the EMS station.

Step 1:
Rewrite as a second-order ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\frac{d^{2}C}{dx^{2}}-\frac{v}{D}\frac{dC}{dx}=0

(48)

Step 2:
The characteristic equation is:

r^{2}-\frac{v}{D}r=0

(49)

which factors as
\displaystyle r(r-v/D)=0
, yielding roots
\displaystyle r_{1}=0
and
\displaystyle r_{2}=v/D
.

Step 3:
The general solution is:

C(x)=A_{1}e^{r_{1}x}+A_{2}e^{r_{2}x}=A_{1}+A_{2}e^{(v/D)x}

(50)

Step 4:
Apply boundary conditions:

•

At source (
\displaystyle x=0
):
\displaystyle C(0)=C_{0}
(baseline effectiveness)

•

Far from source (
\displaystyle x\to\infty
):
\displaystyle C(\infty)=0
(effectiveness vanishes)

From
\displaystyle C(\infty)=0
, we need
\displaystyle A_{1}=0
(since
\displaystyle e^{0\cdot\infty}=1\neq 0
).

From
\displaystyle C(0)=C_{0}
, we have
\displaystyle A_{2}=C_{0}
.

Step 5:
Final solution:

C(x)=C_{0}e^{-(v/D)x}=C_{0}e^{-\kappa x}

(51)

where
\displaystyle\kappa=v/D
is the spatial decay parameter.

Step 6:
Convert to temporal form. Since
\displaystyle x=vt
(distance = velocity
\displaystyle\times
time), we have:

C(t)=C_{0}e^{-\kappa vt}=C_{0}e^{-\lambda t}

(52)

where
\displaystyle\lambda=\kappa v=v^{2}/D
is the temporal decay parameter. ∎

A.2
Delta Method for Critical Boundary Variance

The critical boundary is
\displaystyle d^{*}=g(\lambda)=-\lambda^{-1}\ln(\varepsilon)
.

Delta method:
For a smooth function
\displaystyle g(\cdot)
, if
\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}\overset{a}{\sim}N(\lambda,\sigma^{2}_{\lambda})
, then:

\hat{d}^{*}=g(\hat{\lambda})\overset{a}{\sim}N\left(g(\lambda),[g^{\prime}(\lambda)]^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\lambda}\right)

(53)

Derivative:

g^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\left[-\frac{\ln(\varepsilon)}{\lambda}\right]=\frac{\ln(\varepsilon)}{\lambda^{2}}

(54)

Variance:

\text{Var}(\hat{d}^{*})=[g^{\prime}(\hat{\lambda})]^{2}\text{Var}(\hat{\lambda})=\frac{[\ln(\varepsilon)]^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}^{4}}\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})

(55)

95% Confidence interval:

\text{CI}_{95\%}=\hat{d}^{*}\pm 1.96\sqrt{\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{d}^{*})}=\hat{d}^{*}\pm 1.96\frac{|\ln(\varepsilon)|}{\hat{\lambda}^{2}}\text{SE}(\hat{\lambda})

(56)
