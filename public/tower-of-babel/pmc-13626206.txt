CaMKIIβ Signaling drives expression of metabolic and stress response genes in skeletal muscle, and its loss contributes to the LGMDR1 phenotype
Authors: Irina Kramerova, Frieda Anastopulos, Diana Becerra, Ruby Goldstein de Salazar, Alexander R Keeble, Jeffrey S Chamberlain, Steven A Moore, Elizabeth M McNally, Melissa J Spencer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcium calmodulin kinase II (CaMKII) signaling is activated by muscle use and drives gene expression that promotes slow oxidative muscle phenotype. Previously, we showed that the calpain 3 knockout (C3KO) mouse model of limb girdle muscular dystrophy R1 (LGMDR1) exhibits impaired slow-oxidative gene expression and these deficits were associated with reduced levels of the CaMKIIβ isoform and attenuated CaMKII signaling in C3KO muscles. To investigate the contribution of CaMKIIβ signaling to the calpain 3-deficient phenotype, we generated both loss-of-function (muscle-specific conditional knockout, Camk2b cKO) and gain-of-function (muscle-specific overexpression of a constitutively active CaMK2b) models. Camk2b cKO muscles exhibited myopathic features and weakness and their muscles failed to upregulate genes that promote oxidative metabolism and stress-responses following endurance exercise, similarly to C3KO muscles. Mitochondrial respiration showed reduced activity of complex I, complex II and fatty acid oxidation. RNA sequencing of LGMDR1 patient biopsies revealed a similar reduction in genes involved in oxidative metabolism, aligning human and mouse findings. Overexpression of a constitutively active Camk2b in C3KO muscles enhanced oxidative metabolism and improved functional performance. Our results support the hypothesis that blunted CaMKIIβ signaling contributes to the failed upregulation of genes involved in oxidative metabolism and stress-responses in C3KO muscles. These studies highlight metabolic insufficiency as a central contributor to LGMDR1 pathogenesis and support the therapeutic potential of targeting CaMKII signaling to ameliorate disease features in LGMDR1.

Introduction

Skeletal muscles can sense functional demands and adapt to them by altering gene expression patterns that facilitate muscle remodeling. Depending on the type and duration of muscle activity, various signaling pathways are activated that result in adaptive transcriptional responses and lead to changes in muscle fiber phenotype [1–3]. Skeletal muscle myofibers are classified based on their contractile and metabolic properties into three main types: slow-oxidative (SO), fast-glycolytic (FG), and fast-oxidative glycolytic (FOG) [4]. Slow-oxidative fibers (Type I) rely primarily on oxidative metabolism and are slow to fatigue. Fast-glycolytic fibers (Type IIB) depend mainly on glycolytic metabolism, and fatigue quickly. Fast-oxidative glycolytic fibers (Type IIA and IIX) have intermediate properties, combining aspects of both oxidative and glycolytic metabolism. The type of myosin heavy chain expressed in a fiber (fast or slow) determines whether a fiber is Type I or Type II and dictates its twitch speed (slow or fast) and force generating capacity. In healthy muscle, the metabolic profile generally correlates with its myosin type, though FOG fibers show intermediate characteristics [4]. It is well established that different types of exercise training can change the fiber type and metabolic composition of a muscle. For example, endurance training results in a higher abundance of slow-twitch oxidative type fibers while resistance power exercise increases the content of fast-twitch glycolytic type IIA and IIX fibers [5]. These phenotypic changes affect the muscle’s myofibrillar protein composition, mitochondrial content and metabolic profile.

Ca2+ plays a vital role in muscle contraction and in activation of signaling pathways that regulate muscle adaptation [1]. Three major Ca2+-dependent signaling pathways are present in skeletal muscle that operate through either protein kinase C, calcineurin-NFAT and Ca2+-calmodulin dependent kinases II and IV (CaMKII and CaMKIV). All three pathways ultimately control the activity of transcription factors that promote transcription of genes of the slow oxidative phenotype. Such transcription factors include MEF2 and the transcriptional co-activator PGC1α [2].

In our previous studies we demonstrated that muscles from a mouse model of limb girdle muscular dystrophy R1 (LGMDR1, formerly known as LGMD2A) exhibit blunted Ca2+ release and reduced CaMKIIβ signaling [6–9]. LGMDR1 is one of the most common subtypes of LGMD and it is caused by mutations in the CAPN3 gene encoding calpain 3 proteinase [10]. Clinically, symptoms typically begin in the second decade of life, and most patients lose ambulation within a decade of diagnosis [11]. Muscle biopsies from LGMDR1 patients show a mild dystrophic picture and mitochondrial abnormalities [12–14].

CAPN3 is a member of a large family of Ca2+-activated proteinases (calpains) that include both ubiquitously expressed and tissue-specific members. Pathogenic CAPN3 mutations can impact both proteolytic and non-proteolytic functions of CAPN3 [15, 16]. CAPN3 is primarily expressed in skeletal muscle with low expression in the brain and no expression in the heart [17]. We previously demonstrated that Capn3 knockout mice (C3KO) show a very mild dystrophic phenotype and that they replicate many features of LGMDR1 such as decreased myofiber size, abnormal cytoarchitecture of myofibers, and mitochondrial dysfunction [7, 18, 19].

Cell fractionation studies have revealed that CAPN3 is present in multiple subcellular compartments, suggesting that it plays different roles depending on its subcellular location [7, 20]. The majority of CAPN3 is found in the myofibrillar fraction, where it is anchored on titin at the N2 line [21]. Mutations disrupting this interaction are pathogenic, suggesting its vital importance for regulating calpain 3 activity and stability [18, 22, 23]. Within the sarcomere CAPN3 is believed to facilitate sarcomeric protein turnover during muscle remodeling [20, 24]. CAPN3 is also present in the membrane fraction where it is enriched at skeletal muscle triads, which are specialized structures where excitation-dependent Ca2+ release occurs [7, 25]. The triad invaginates at the sarcomere N2 line, which is also the site of CAPN3 binding on titin. Loss of CAPN3 in mouse muscle leads to decreased Ca2+ release, reduced abundance of triad-associated proteins, including Ryanodine Receptor I, and reductions in a specific isoform of CaMKIIβ that localizes to the triad complex [7, 26]. In subsequent studies we observed markedly reduced levels and activation of CaMKIIβ in C3KO muscles. By contrast, other signaling pathways, including AKT and AMPK, remain unaffected [8, 27]. The reduction in CaMKIIβ signaling in response to mechanical loading is associated with failure to upregulate slow oxidative and stress-response genes, impaired lipid metabolism, and a diminished capacity for endurance exercise [6, 8, 28].

While the reduction in CaMKII signaling aligns with abnormal changes in muscle remodeling and gene expression, the precise contribution of deficient CaMKII signaling, specifically mediated by the CaMKIIβ isoform, remains unclear. To investigate how loss of CaMKIIβ in skeletal muscle contributes to the LGMDR1 phenotype, we generated a loss-of-function model of Camk2b in skeletal muscle (Camk2b cKO). We examined how CaMKIIβ deficiency affects muscle morphology, gene expression profiles, and mitochondrial respiration to understand the contribution of CaMKIIβ signaling to the LGMDR1 phenotype. To determine the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway, we overexpressed constitutively active CaMKIIβ in skeletal muscle and demonstrated that key features of the LGMDR1 phenotype were ameliorated. Together, these findings indicate that metabolic dysfunction is an under-recognized contributor to LGMDR1 pathogenesis, and that enhancing CaMKIIβ signaling is a potential therapeutic strategy for this disease.

Results

Muscle specific Camk2b cKO mice show a mild myopathy and muscle weakness

To generate Camk2b cKO mice, Camk2bfl/fl mice [29] were crossed to mice carrying Cre recombinase under control of the human skeletal actin promoter (HSA). As shown in Fig. 1A, Cre expression led to specific and efficient knockout of the Camk2b gene in skeletal muscles. Levels of CaMKIIβ proteins were undetectable by western blot analysis of Camk2b cKO skeletal muscles (Fig. 1B, Supplemental Fig. 1). Of note, western blot analysis was performed for all mice used in experiments to ensure efficient knockout of CaMK2b (Supplemental Fig. 1 shows an example of such analysis in mice used for gene expression studies).

We next examined the effect of Camk2b ablation on muscle functional performance. The performance of Camk2b cKO mice was compared with their Cre-negative littermates in three functional tests, forelimb grip strength, wire mesh hang test and run to exhaustion. These tests evaluate the strength and fatigability of different muscle groups. As shown in Fig. 1C–E, Camk2b cKO mice showed significantly impaired performance in all three tests. These data suggest that loss of CaMKIIβ in skeletal muscle leads to muscle weakness and that the beta isoform has unique and essential functions in skeletal muscle that cannot be fully compensated for by other CaMKII isoforms.

Histological analysis of Camk2b cKO and control muscles was performed at 4 weeks, 2 months, 4 months and 6 months of age (Fig. 1F and G and Supplemental Fig. 2) using hematoxylin and eosin staining. A mild dystrophic phenotype was evident, characterized by a low frequency of fibers with internalized nuclei. On average, the percent of mislocalized nuclei did not exceed 1%; however, on rare occasions we observed clusters of fibers with mislocalized nuclei (an example is shown in Fig. 1G left panel). We immunostained muscles at 3 different ages (4 weeks, 2 months, and 6 months) for the macrophage marker CD68 and for developmental MyHC as a marker of regeneration (an example of such staining is shown of Supplemental Fig. 2B). We did not detect significant infiltration of the CD68-positive immune cells nor the presence of developmental MyHC-positive regenerating fibers. Dystrophin-deficient mdx muscles served as a positive control for staining (Supplemental Fig. 2C). These observations reflects the mild pathology observed in Camk2b cKO muscles, which is similar to C3KO muscles [18] and pre-sympotmatic LGMDR1 patients [30].

Since CaMK2b cKO mice showed significantly reduced performance (Fig. 1C–E) without any severe histological abnormalities, we hypothesized that reduced muscle performance could be due to metabolic deficiencies. Mitochondrial respiration was evaluated by Seahorse, and it was determined that the maximal oxidative rate was significantly reduced in CaMK2b cKO plantaris muscles compared to controls (Fig. 2A). Fiber type analysis based on the expression of different myosin heavy chain (MyHC) isoforms in plantaris of the same animals, did not reveal significant differences; however, a trend toward an increased percent of fast glycolytic type IIb fibers was observed in mice lacking CaMKIIβ (Fig. 2B). These results support a role for CaMKIIβ in promotion of oxidative metabolism in skeletal muscle and suggest that reduced functional performance of CaMK2b cKO mice could be due to energy deficits.

Muscles of Camk2b cKO mice show impaired transcription of genes involved in oxidative metabolism

Prior studies in Capn3 knockout mice (C3KO) demonstrated impaired muscle remodeling in C3KO after treadmill exercise or hindlimb unloading and reloading [8, 28]. In both models these changes coincided with blunted CaMKIIβ signaling and failure to activate a normal transcriptional response. Three categories of genes were not properly induced in C3KO mice after treadmill exercise: 1) genes encoding slow isoforms of myofibrillar proteins, 2) genes involved in mitochondrial and lipid metabolism and 3) stress response genes [8, 28].

In the current experiments, we used the identical 5 day-running protocol [8] to determine which impaired transcriptional programs could be specifically attributed to aberrant CaMKIIβ signaling. Male Camk2b cKO mice and their control littermates (n = 6 per group) were subjected to 1 h a day of treadmill running for 5 days. This protocol involved running at a moderate speed and was well tolerated by all mice, ensuring consistent exercise exposure across all animals. On the final day of running, gene expression was evaluated by bulk RNA-seq in plantaris muscles of 4 different groups of mice (Camk2b cKO sedentary, Camk2b cKO exercised, Cre− control littermates sedentary and Cre− control littermates exercised). Plantaris muscle was used to enable comparison with our prior studies. Histological assessments of plantaris muscles did not reveal significant morphological changes that would suggest that muscle damage occurred in response to running exercise (Supplemental Fig. 3).

To identify changes in gene expression in response to exercise, we compared transcriptomes of exercised vs sedentary muscles for each genotype. These comparisons revealed a similar pattern of failed upregulation of genes involved in oxidative metabolism in Camk2b cKO and C3KO mice (RNA-seq data for C3KO mice were previously reported in [8]). As shown in Fig. 3A, four of the ten top exercise-induced canonical pathways in control mice were associated with oxidative metabolism, including PPAR signaling, Apelin signaling, adipogenesis and protein kinase A signaling. In exercised Camk2b cKO mice, three of these four pathways failed to be upregulated, and although adipogenesis appeared increased, the magnitude of induction was substantially lower than in WT mice. A heatmap (Fig. 3B) further illustrates this pattern: many of the affected genes encode proteins central to lipid metabolism [31] such as lipoprotein lipase, (Lpl), a key regulator of free fatty acid release, proteins associated with lipid droplets (perilipins Plin3 and Plin4), and enzymes involved in intracellular lipid metabolism such as diacylglycerol acyltransferase, Dgat2. Figure 3C provides an example of this blunted transcriptional response, highlighting reduced induction of select lipid metabolism genes in the cKO muscles compared to controls. Table 1 lists metabolic genes that are similarly dysregulated in both Camk2b cKO and C3KO muscle following exercise. Together, these data show that impaired induction of oxidative-metabolism pathways previously observed in C3KO mice can be at least partially attributed to blunted CaMKIIβ signaling.

In healthy muscles, endurance exercise up-regulates genes that encode slow muscle-specific isoforms of sarcomeric proteins. In our previous studies in C3KO mice, we showed that several sarcomeric genes were not properly up-regulated with exercise [8]. In contrast, these changes were not observed in exercised Camk2b cKO muscles. For example, in C3KO plantaris muscles, expression of Myl2, Ckmt2 and Myom3 were not induced by exercise [8] while the same genes were induced in exercised muscles from Camk2b cKO mice (Supplemental Fig. 4). These data suggest that the observed failure to upregulate slow sarcomeric genes in C3KO cannot be attributed to blunted CaMKIIβ signaling.

Increased muscle loading places mechanical stresses on the muscle and an adequate systemic response is required to prevent muscle damage and orchestrate regeneration. One of the important stress-induced molecules that can trigger a systemic response is a secreted form of the heat shock protein HSP70 [32, 33]. In our previous studies we found that secreted HSP70 (sHSP70), encoded by Hspa1a and Hspa1b genes, was highly up-regulated in WT mice in two different muscle adaptation protocols (running exercise and muscle reloading after a period of disuse). At the same time, no such up-regulation occurred in C3KO mice [28]. We also provided evidence that CaMKII signaling controls expression of sHSP70 in response to oxidative stress in C2C12 muscle cells [28].

Camk2b cKO muscles failed to upregulate both Hspa1a and Hspa1b following exercise. Hspa1a and Hspa1b transcript levels were 2.4 and 5.7 times higher in exercised control muscles compared to exercised Camk2b cKO muscles. It is noteworthy that several other stress response genes also failed to induce in Camk2b cKO mice compared to control littermates in response to exercise (Fig. 4A). These observations were confirmed by western blot analysis of sHSP70 and HSP90 in plantaris muscles from sedentary controls and exercised mice (Fig. 4 B-D). These data strongly suggest that CaMKIIβ-mediated signaling is necessary for induction of stress response genes in skeletal muscles and this may be one way in which mechanical loading is translated to biochemical signals.

Downregulation of genes involved in oxidative metabolism in LGMDR1 biopsies

To determine whether human LGMDR1 muscle biopsies exhibit gene expression changes similar to those observed in our mouse model, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on quadriceps biopsies from young LGMDR1 patients (17–22 years old) and control subjects (17–34 years old). This age range was selected to minimize confounding effects of advanced disease progression. RNA-seq analysis showed no major differences between LGMDR1 and healthy subjects in the expression of myofibrillar genes. In contrast, transcripts involved in oxidative metabolism and cellular stress responses were downregulated in LGMDR1 biopsies compared to healthy subjects (Tables 2 and 3 and Fig. 5). Metascape analysis further highlighted significant enrichment of pathways governing oxidative and lipid metabolism, all of which were reduced in LGMDR1 muscles. Notably, expression of PPARGC1A and PPARGC1B, encoding the metabolic transcriptional co-activators PGC1α and β, respectively, was diminished in patient samples. PGC1α is a principal regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis that promotes remodeling of muscle fibers towards an oxidative phenotype [2]. Consistent with our findings in the C3KO mouse model, several lipid-handling genes (PLIN2, PLIN5, FABP3) were less abundant in LGMDR1 biopsies compared to healthy controls. Together, these findings identify impaired oxidative and lipid metabolic programs as a key molecular feature of LGMDR1 and support the hypothesis that these metabolic defects contribute to disease pathogenesis.

Overexpression of constitutively active Camk2b increases mitochondrial respiration in C3KO muscles and improves functional performance

We next investigated whether increasing levels of active CaMKIIβ in skeletal muscle can improve the C3KO phenotype. To achieve this goal, the cDNA encoding a constitutively active mutant of Camk2b (CaMKII-T287D) under the control of the human skeletal actin promoter (HSA) was systemically delivered to skeletal muscle using adeno associate virus AAV serotype Rh74. This promoter is restricted to skeletal muscle and is not active in the heart [34]. To identify the dose for the study, we injected 2-month-old C3KO mice retro-orbitally with 5E13 vg/kg or 2E14 vg/kg of Rh74-CaMK2b-T287D and assessed levels of CaMKIIβ in various skeletal muscles 6 weeks post injection. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 5, a substantial increase in CaMKIIβ levels was observed in C3KO skeletal muscles injected with 2E14 vg/kg Rh74- CaMK2b-T287D compared to C3KO mice injected with PBS or 5E13vg/kg. This treatment was well tolerated by the mice; no lethality or serious health problems were observed and suggested a dose of 2E14 vg/kg for the subsequent study.

For the long-term studies, 10-week-old C3KO mice (n = 8 males per group) were injected with either 2E14 vg/kg of Rh74-CaMK2b-T287D or the same dose of empty Rh74 as a control. Mice were tested every 5 weeks after the injection to assess functional performance using the wire mesh test. As shown in Fig. 6, C3KO injected with Rh74-Camk2b-T287D showed a trend towards improved functional performance starting 5 weeks post dosing compared to C3KO mice injected with empty Rh74. Significant strength increases were observed in treated mice from 20 to 35 weeks post dosing. Histological examination of skeletal muscles and hearts from Rh74-Camk2b-T287D—injected mice did not reveal any pathological changes (Supplemental Fig. 6).

C3KO mice have decreased muscle weight (muscle atrophy) and reduced slow myofiber phenotype and both features likely contribute to the impaired muscle performance observed in this model of LGMDR1 [18]. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 7, overexpression of constitutively active CaMK2β had no effect on muscle weight or fiber cross-sectional area despite the improved muscle performance.

Since CaMKII signaling promotes the oxidative phenotype in skeletal muscle, we assessed the oxidative capacity of soleus and plantaris muscles from dosed C3KO mice by Seahorse analysis or NADH staining. These data revealed that the maximal oxidative rate was significantly higher in the Rh74-CaMK2b-T287D injected group compared to the control group for mitochondrial complex II and fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 7A).

The muscle oxidative capacity in Rh74-Camk2b-T287D injected C3KO mice was also evaluated by NADH staining of the plantaris muscles (Fig. 7B and C) showing that the percent of highly oxidative slow muscle fibers (stained dark blue) and the ratio of fast oxidative/glycolytic (FOG) to fast glycolytic fiber (FG) fibers were significantly higher in mice injected with Rh74-Camkb-T287D compared to controls. We performed fiber analysis in plantaris muscles of Rh74-Camk2b-T287D injected and control mice (Fig. 7E and F). This assessment revealed an increase in the percent of the type IIA (FOG) fibers with a concomitant reduction in the percent of type IIB (FG) fibers after dosing C3KO mice with Rh74-Camk2b-T287D. This effect was somewhat unexpected, as we did not observe changes in MyHC isoform expression in CaMK2b cKO mice (Fig. 2B). It is possible that constitutively active CaMKIIβ can activate other forms of CaMKII due to ability of CaMKII to form hetero-oligomeric complexes [35] which may have enhanced the effect of CaMKIIβ overexpression. These results strongly suggest that restoring CaMKIIβ signaling can increases oxidative capacity and improve muscle function in the mouse model of LGMDR1.

Taken together, these data show that CaMKIIβ-mediating signaling promotes oxidative metabolism in skeletal muscle and is important for endurance exercise adaptation. Downregulation of this pathway in C3KO mice significantly contributes to compromised slow oxidative gene expression and metabolism. RNA-seq data also revealed a deficiency in metabolic gene expression in LGMDR1 patients compared to age-matched healthy control subjects, providing support for the hypothesis that metabolic insufficiency is an underlying feature of LGMDR1. Finally, the data suggest that increasing CaMKIIβ signaling in C3KO mice can reverse several aspects of the calpainopathy phenotype making it a possible target for therapeutic intervention to treat LGMDR1.

Discussion

The goal of these studies was to define the contribution of blunted CaMKIIβ signaling to the calpainopathy phenotype described in previous publications [6, 8]. CaMKIIβ-mediated signaling has been shown to regulate several processes that control skeletal muscle gene expression. Early studies showed that CaMKII signaling promotes expression of genes encoding slow isoforms by increasing activation of the transcription factor MEF2 [1, 36, 37] and that CaMKII activation is required for efficient muscle regeneration following injury [38]. All four CaMKII isoforms are expressed in skeletal muscles; however, the specific function of each individual isoform and the impact on gene expression in response to loading have not been investigated.

Our RNA-seq analysis identified two CaMKII isoforms, encoded by Cam2a and Camk2b genes, as being expressed at relatively high levels in murine skeletal muscle, with Camk2b emerging as the dominant isoform in soleus (unpublished data). This is noteworthy because the soleus is one of the few mouse muscles with a substantial proportion of slow-twitch fibers, in contrast to human skeletal muscle, which generally exhibits a more balanced distribution of fast and slow fibers. The prominence of Camk2b in soleus, coupled with the fact that soleus is among the most severely affected muscles in C3KO mice [18], highlights the possibility that CaMKIIβ activity may be especially important for maintaining the integrity and metabolic function of slow fibers.

Prior studies have suggested that slow fibers are disproportionately affected in LGMDR1, although direct correlations between fiber type and disease indices remain limited in human biopsy datasets. Nonetheless, a consistent feature across multiple reports is the enrichment of lobulated fibers in LGMDR1 biopsies and mitochondrial dysfunction [6, 39–41]. The observation that lobulated fibers appear to be predominantly in slow muscles lends support to the notion that slow fibers are more affected than fast in LGMDR1 [6, 39–41]. The predominance of lobulated fibers in slow muscles has two important implications. First, it suggests that the pathological process in LGMDR1 is not uniformly distributed across fiber types but instead reflects a selective vulnerability of slow fibers. Second, it underscores the likelihood that metabolic defects, rather than gross structural abnormalities alone, contribute significantly to disease progression. Taken together with our findings in C3KO mice, these observations strengthen the argument that impaired CaMKIIβ signaling and downstream metabolic regulation disproportionately impact slow oxidative fibers, thereby shaping the muscle-specific phenotype of LGMDR1.

Our previous studies demonstrated that blunted CaMKII-mediated signaling in C3KO muscles coincided with impaired muscle adaptation [6, 8, 28]. In those studies, we utilized two experimental models of muscle adaptation: endurance running exercise and muscle regrowth following a period of disuse [8, 28]. In wild-type muscles, CaMKII activation was strongly correlated with muscle loading in both experimental conditions. However, in C3KO muscles, CaMKII activation was severely blunted, which was associated with defective gene expression and impaired muscle adaptation [6, 8, 28]. During endurance running, three major gene groups failed to be induced in C3KO mice: 1) metabolic genes involved in oxidative metabolism, particularly those regulating β-oxidation of fatty acids; 2) genes encoding slow isoforms of myofibrillar proteins and 3) stress response genes. Although these deficits were strongly associated with blunted CaMKII signaling, it remained unclear whether all the observed impairments were directly caused by CaMKII dysfunction or if additional factors were involved. While Camk2b knockout mice were previously generated, phenotypic studies were mostly limited to studies of brain function [29]. Interestingly, it was shown that besides a neuronal defect, Camk2b knockout mice also demonstrated decreased forelimb grip strength [29]. These data prompted us to generate a muscle-specific conditional knockout of Camk2b to investigate the unique role of this isoform in skeletal muscles.

Data presented here show that CaMKIIβ is indispensable for normal muscle function and its absence can’t be fully compensated by other CaMKII isoforms. Camk2b cKO mice performed significantly worse than their control littermates in all three functional tests that we used. However, histological evaluation revealed relatively mild morphological changes with a variable, but higher than normal presence of centrally nucleated fibers while no significant degeneration and regeneration was observed in muscles of young (1–2 months of age) or old (6–7 months of age) mice. The same phenotypic features have been reported in C3KO mice and have been observed to differ from skeletal muscle phenotypes for other forms of muscular dystrophy such as the mdx model of Duchenne muscular dystrophy, which shows extensive degeneration and regeneration [18].

Even though CaMKII signaling has been shown to promote the slow phenotype, we did not observe statistically significant changes in the percent of slow or FOG fibers in Camk2b cKO muscles compared to control mice (Fig. 2). Furthermore, we did not observe abnormalities in transcription of sarcomeric genes induced by running exercise in the CamkIIb cKO, suggesting that other isoforms of CaMKII may have compensated for the lack of CaMKIIβ to induce slow isoforms of myofibrillar genes. This hypothesis is indirectly supported by the fact that overexpression of constitutively active CaMKIIβ that can activate other CaMKII isoforms, promotes a shift from glycolytic type IIB fibers toward more oxidative type IIA in C3KO mice (Fig. 7F) Other Ca2+-mediated signaling pathways may also contribute to regulation of sarcomeric genes. Identifying these pathways, including their downstream transcription factors, as well as further characterizing the functional consequences of CaMKIIβ overexpression (such as by assessing Ca2+ handling and directly measuring muscle force and fatigability) will be important goals for future studies.

Our data showed that CaMKIIβ-mediated signaling promotes expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation, suggesting that the blunted CaMKIIβ signaling likely underlies the lipid metabolism defect previously observed in C3KO. In skeletal muscle, fatty acid oxidation plays an important role as a source of energy especially during prolonged muscle activity [42]. Even though gene signatures of Camk2b cKO and C3KO shared many similarities, the deviations from the controls were stronger in C3KO mice compared to Camk2b cKO mice. These data suggest that CAPN3 acts upstream of CaMKIIβ and affects other pathways. Data from our lab and from others indicate that CAPN3 is required for normal Ca2+ trafficking and homeostasis [7, 9, 43]. We found that CAPN3 is enriched in the triad fraction and that the lack of CAPN3 is associated with destabilization of the triad protein complex, reduced levels of triad-associated CaMKII and decreased Ca2+ release upon excitation in young (2 month) presymptomatic and old (1 year) C3KO mice [7, 9]. Given the multiple Ca2+-dependent pathways that operate in muscle, all of which could potentially be affected by the observed Ca2+ abnormalities, we cannot rule out involvement of these pathways in LGMDR1 pathogenesis. In our previous studies we showed that calcineurin levels, AKT signaling and AMPK activation were not changed in C3KO muscles [8, 24, 27]; however, more in depth studies are required to elucidate how the absence of CAPN3 impacts other Ca2+ signaling pathways, gene expression and muscle adaptation.

Our previous studies in C2C12 myoblast cell cultures and in C3KO mice suggested that CaMKII signaling controls expression of stress response genes, particularly Hspa1a and Hspa1b [28]. Both genes encode an inducible HSP70 that acts not only intracellularly as a molecular chaperone but can also be secreted as a Damage Associated Molecular Pattern (DAMP) molecule [33]. DAMPs play an important protective role in cells by inducing an appropriate systemic response to stressful conditions such as exercise or muscle reloading after a period of disuse. Previously, we found that induction of Hsp1a and Hsp1b as well as other stress response genes was significantly reduced in C3KO muscles compared to WT muscles in both experimental settings [28]. We also showed that the systemic inflammatory response that normally accompanies muscle recovery after a period of disuse, was compromised in C3KO mice affecting recruitment of inflammatory cells needed for muscle regeneration and ultimately affecting muscle growth in these mice. Here we found that similar to C3KO muscle, stress response genes were not upregulated in Camk2b cKO to the same extent as controls. These data corroborate our previous observations and demonstrate that CaMKIIβ-mediated signaling activates inducible HSP70 genes in the setting of muscle remodeling.

The RNA sequencing data from human biopsies presented here provide the first evidence that genes involved in oxidative metabolism are downregulated in young LGMDR1 patients. While the metabolic aspects of LGMDR1 have not been systematically studied, previous reports have documented mitochondrial abnormalities and reduced activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes in LGMDR1 muscle biopsies [14, 44, 45]. Additionally, we and others have demonstrated mitochondrial abnormalities and dysfunction in C3KO cells and mice [19, 46]. These data suggest that metabolic insufficiency could be a primary pathological feature underlying calpainopathy, leading to inadequate energy production and the inability of muscle to meet functional requirements. Further studies are necessary to corroborate this hypothesis and to identify molecular pathways involved in this process.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experimental protocols and the use of animals were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the UCLA Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Camk2bfl/fl mouse line was generated at the Institut Clinique de la Souris (France) and was described previously [29]. Frozen embryos for this line were obtained from the Centre European de Recherche en Biologie et Medecine (France). HSA-cre transgenic mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratories. C3KO mice were previously described [18]. Both Camk2b cKO and C3KO mice are congenic to C57/BL6. AAV Rh74-HSA-CaMK2b-T287D and empty control AAV Rh74 (5E13 vg/kg or 2E14 vg/kg for the sort-term studies and 2E14 vg/kg for the long-term experiment) were delivered to C3KO mice by retro-orbital vein injection (n = 8 for each group). AAV was packaged, purified and tittered by qPCR by Virovek.

Treadmill exercise and other functional tests

Treadmill running for gene expression studies

Male mice (n = 6 for each genotype, 6-month old) were accustomed to the treadmill (Columbus Instruments) with a 15 min run once per day at 10 m/min for 3 days. For the next 5 days, mice were subjected to forced running exercise performed on a 10% positive incline at 10 m/min for the first 20 min followed by 40 min at 15 m/min. Mice were sacrificed two hours after the last run. Age-matched males (n = 6 for each genotype) that were not subjected to exercise were used as sedentary controls.

Run to exhaustion

Mice were run on a treadmill on a 15% positive incline at a starting speed of 1 m/min. Speed was increased by 1 m/min every minute until the mice were unable to perform. All mice used for these experiments were males (n = 11–12 for each genotype), aged 6–7 months.

Wire mesh

Male mice were placed on the wire mesh pulled over a wooden frame on a swivel (custom built). Once a mouse was in position, the wire mesh was rotated 180°, resulting in the mouse hanging upside down from the mesh, using all four limbs. Five trials were conducted with 1 min rest between the trials. The average of all 5 attempted was used for statistical analysis. All mice used for these experiments were males (n = 12–13 for each genotype), aged 6–7 months.

Forelimb grip strength

Grip strength was measured using a digital force gauge (DFIS2, Chatillon CE). Five trials were performed with 30 sec of rest between the trials. Data were presented as an average of 5 trials. All mice used for these experiments were males (n = 12–13 for each genotype), aged 6–7 months.

Histological analysis

All histological studies were performed on 10 μm frozen muscle sections. Muscle sections from 3–6 mice per group were used for histological studies. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was used to evaluate overall pathological changes including centrally nucleated fibers. For immunohistochemistry, muscle sections were thawed at room temperature and fixed with cold acetone for 10 min. Sections were blocked first with a non-specific blocking buffer (0.2% gelatin, 0.5% Tween-20 and 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS) followed by M.O.M. blocking solution (Vector Laboratories). Rat anti-mouse CD68 antibody (BD Biosciences) was used to stain inflammatory cells, mouse anti-developmental myosin heavy chain (devMyHC) (Leica Biosystems) was used to stain newly formed myofibers. Images were acquired, processed and analyzed using Axio Imager M1 microscope and Axiovision software (Zeiss).

For NADH staining, frozen sections of plantaris were air-dried at room temperature and then incubated with the staining solution (1 mg/ml Nitro Blue Tatrazolium and 1 mg/ml reduced NADH in 50 mM Tris–HCL, pH 7.4). All reagents were obtained from Sigma. After incubation, slides were washed quickly in sequential acetone solutions of 30%, 60%, 90%, 60%, 30% and in water. NADH histochemistry leads to three distinguishable levels of staining that we identified as low, intermediate and high. The three levels of staining were analyzed by a blinded evaluator to ensure objectivity. We refer to the low stained fibers as fast glycolytic (FG), and the intermediate plus high together as fast glycolytic/oxidative (FOG).

For fiber type analysis, frozen sections were fixed for 5 min in ice-cold acetone and blocked for 1 h in 1% BSA prior to overnight incubation at 4°C in-anti lama2 (rat IgG1, 1:100, Sigma L0663), myosin heavy chain Type 1 (mouse IgG2b, 1:10; BA-F8, DSHB), myosin heavy chain Type 2a (mouse IgG1, 1:30; SC.71, DSHB), and myosin heavy chain Type 2b (mouse IgM, 1:30; 6-H1, DHSB) in blocking reagent. The following day, slides were incubated for 1 h with goat anti-mouse IgG2b AF350 (A21140, Thermo Fisher Scientific), goat anti-mouse IgG1 AF488 (A21121, Thermo Fisher Scientific), goat anti-mouse IgM AF594 (A21044, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and donkey anti-rat AF647 (A78947, Thermo Fisher Scientific) before mounting with ProLong Gold Antifade mountant (P36930, Thermo Fisher Scientific). and All sections were imaged on a Keyence microscope at 20× magnification. Type 2B, type 2A, and type 1 fibers were manually quantified and expressed as a percentage of total myofibers per muscle section.

Measurement of oxygen consumption rates(OCR) in mitochondrial fractions

For OCR analysis, freshly dissected soleus muscles were homogenized on ice in a Dounce homogenizer in Chappell-Perry buffer (100 mM KCl, 50 mM Tri-HCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 12 mM EGTA, adjusted to pH 7.4 with KOH) supplemented with 1 mM ATP and 0.5% fatty acid-free (Fraction V) BSA immediately before use. Muscle homogenates were centrifuged at 600 x g for 10 min at 4°C. Supernatants were transferred to new tubes and then centrifuged at 10 000 g for 10 min at 4°C to pellet mitochondria. Mitochondria were resuspended in SHE buffer (250 mM sucrose, 5 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, adjusted to pH 7.2 with KOH). Protein concentrations were measured using BCA protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples (4 μg/well) were analyzed in the UCLA Mitochondrial and Metabolism Core using a Seahorse XF96 Analyzer (Agilent). Pyruvate plus malate were used to assess Complex I-dependent respiration, succinate plus rotenone were used to assess Complex II-dependent respiration, and palmitoyl-carnitine was used to assess fatty acid oxidation. Data were normalized to total protein. Seahorse analysis was carried out according to Acin-Perez et al. [47].

Western blot analysis

Muscles were homogenized by hand in a Dounce homogenizer in reducing sample buffer (80 mM Tris, pH 6.8, 10% β-mercaptoethanol, 2% SDS and 10% glycerol) with Halt phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An equal amount of total protein was loaded on SDS-PAGE gels followed by transfer to nitrocellulose membrane. Ponceau red S was used to verify the transfer onto the nitrocellulose. The following primary antibodies were used for western blotting: anti-CaMKII (1:500, Cell Signaling, D11A10), anti-phospho-CaMKII alpha/beta (Thr286) (1:1000, ThermoFisher PA1-4614), anti-CaMKII beta (1:500, ThermoFisher 13-9800) anti-hsp70 (StressMarq), anti-hsp90 (BD Transduction Laboratories). Secondary antibodies conjugated with HRP were from Sigma-Aldrich (used 1:10000). Specific signals were developed using ChemiGlow chemiluminescent substrate for HRP (Protein Simple). Images of the blots were acquired using FluorChem FC2 Imager (Alpha Innotech). Specific signals were developed using ChemiGlow chemiluminescent substrate for HRP (Protein Simple). Images of the blots were acquired using FluorChem FC2 Imager (Alpha Innotech). Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

RNA-Seq

RNA-seq was performed at the UCLA Technology Center for Genomics & Bioinformatics.

Libraries for RNA-Seq were prepared with KAPA mRNA-Seq Hyper Prep Kit. The workflow consists of mRNA enrichment and fragmentation, first strand cDNA synthesis using random priming followed by second strand synthesis converting cDNA:RNA hybrid to double-stranded cDNA (dscDNA) to incorporate dUTP into the second cDNA strand. cDNA generation is followed by end repair to generate blunt ends, A-tailing, adaptor ligation and PCR amplification. Different adaptors were used for multiplexing samples in one lane. Sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq6000 for a PE 2 x 50 run. Data quality check was done on Illumina SAV. Demultiplexing was performed with Illumina Bcl2fastq v2.19.1.403 software. The reads were mapped by STAR 2.7.9a [48] and read counts per gene were quantified using the mouse genome mm10.102 or human genome GRCh38. In Partek Flow (Partek® Flow® software, v7.0 Copyright ©. 2019 Partek Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA), read counts were normalized by median ratio for use in DESeq2 [49]. All results of differential gene expression analysis utilized the DESeq2 statistical analysis tool [49]. P-value (< 0.05) and fold change (> 2×) filters were applied for differentially expressed gene lists prior to downstream analysis.

Statistical analysis

For all studies, results from quantitative experiments were expressed as means ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was calculated by Student’s t test (for two group comparisons), paired t test (for repeat measures in two groups), one-way ANOVA (for more than two groups comparison); p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
