Organelle-mimetic nanoreactors for scalable solar H2 and pyruvic acid co-production
Authors: Xiao-hong Wang, Xu-jia Liu, Yun-biao Wang, Yi-lei Li, Shao-jia Liu, Hui-ying Mu, Fa-tang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Revealing the mechanism of photocatalytic organic transformation in aqueous solutions is crucial for photocatalytic processes, yet precisely regulating complex interfacial electron transfer and the microenvironment of reaction molecules remains challenging. Inspired by cellular structures and natural metalloproteins, we construct a ZIF-67@CoS/CdS nanoreactor mimicking organelle architecture. This system enables directional charge transport and hydrogen-bond microenvironment regulation, allowing efficient co-production of H2 and pyruvic acid under light irradiation. Characterizations and calculations reveal that the interfacial electric field accelerates charge migration, while the catalyst reduces the energy barriers for water dissociation and hydrogen formation by modulating hydrogen bonds. The optimized catalyst delivers a molar-level H2 activity of 1457.1 mmol m−2 (5 h, 1,000 cm2) under sunlight, with a pyruvic acid selectivity of 91.2%. In this work, we propose a design strategy for an organelle-mimetic nanoreactor for scalable sunlight-driven H2 production and selective pyruvic acid synthesis.

Introduction

With the increasing global greenhouse effect and the continued consumption of fossil energy, artificial photocatalytic technology that mimics natural photosynthesis has become a crucial direction for the efficient conversion of solar energy to hydrogen (H2) energy1–4. However, although sacrificial agents and precious metals are currently used to enhance hydrogen production activity, these methods come at the cost of sacrificing the oxidative half-reaction or are relatively expensive, and the hydrogen production rate of most developed photocatalysts still remains below 10.0 mmol h−1 m−25–9. Thus, utilizing photogenerated holes to achieve the selective oxidation of organic compounds and promoting the generation of hydrogen gas is extremely attractive5–7. To maintain the strong redox capability of materials, inspired by the Z-scheme electron transfer pathway in natural photosynthesis, researchers have developed various artificial Z-scheme photocatalytic systems to enhance photocatalytic activity10–12. However, the current artificial photosynthesis systems generally suffer from the problem of disorder in their material composition13, which directly leads to the disorder of charge transfer pathways in multi-component materials, increasing the possibility of electron-hole recombination14,15 and exacerbating the reverse reaction16.

In natural photosynthetic systems, the core advantage of efficient energy conversion is the structural features of organelles (e.g., the ordered hierarchical structure of thylakoid membranes in chloroplasts), and their ordered distribution facilitates directional charge transfer13,17. Therefore, simulating the regulation of oxidation and reduction sites on the thylakoid membrane of chloroplasts to achieve an ordered arrangement of these sites in semiconductor materials can enable the directional movement of charge carriers and achieve the spatial separation of oxidation products and reduction products18. Most currently designed ordered structures generally lack electric field-driven long-range directional charge carrier transport, which makes the realization of artificial photosynthesis under natural light highly challenging19–21. More importantly, the absence of effective means of regulating reaction energy barriers hinders water splitting and hydrogen production. In the process of photosynthesis in plants, enzymes (such as water-splitting enzymes, FBPase enzymes, and Rubisco enzymes) can precisely regulate the micro-environment to lower the energy barrier of the reaction, while artificial photosynthesis systems usually lack effective means to precisely control the reaction micro-environment22–24. Currently, researchers are using inorganic-biological hybrid systems and attempting to introduce enzymes into catalysts to regulate the catalytic reaction microenvironment25. However, enzymes require harsh conditions to function; some systems rely on the high selectivity of natural enzymes but are limited by their oxygen sensitivity and pH dependence, which severely affects the activity and stability of the materials26,27. Happily, similar to enzymes, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are composed of metal sites and ligands, and their abundant active sites have been widely used in the field of catalysis28. Incorporating MOFs (such as ZIF-67, UiO-67) into traditional semiconductor catalysts to mimic the role of enzymes in regulating electron transfer and the reaction microenvironment can promote the progress of photocatalytic reactions24. Thus, combining enzyme-mimetic MOFs with ordered structures to construct a sequential electric field can not only promote the long-range directional transfer of charge carriers but also regulate the microenvironment, achieving synergistic optimization of kinetics and thermodynamics. However, how to realize the multifunctional structural design and research on the relevant reaction mechanisms has rarely been reported.

In this work, we address photocatalytic limitations by drawing inspiration from the characteristics of natural photosynthetic cells and propose the concept of “organelle-mimetic sequential nanoreactors”. A ZIF-67@CoS/CdS sequential reactor is designed via precise assembly through continuous anion-cation exchange, where CoS and CdS act as cell wall-like layers to encapsulate the enzyme-like ZIF-67 for stability protection (Supplementary Fig. 1). ZIF-67 serves as the inner layer, CoS as the intermediate charge transport layer, and CdS as the outer layer, enabling sequential dual-electric-field long-distance directional charge transport and regulation of the hydrogen-bond microenvironment. Satisfactorily, the system exhibits an outdoor photocatalytic hydrogen production activity of 1457.1 mmol m⁻2 (5 h) and achieves conversion of lactic acid (LA) to pyruvic acid (PA). The enlarged catalytic area (1000 cm2) exhibits favorable performance under natural sunlight. The design of the simulated cell sequence nano-reactor demonstrates its potential for outdoor photocatalytic applications.

To verify electron migration between ZIF-67, CoS and CdS, surface work functions (WFs) of the material were obtained by Kelvin probe testing. The Kelvin probe detects the contact potential difference (CPD) between the sample and the Au probe, as shown in Fig. 2d. The CPD values of ZIF-67, CoS and CdS are −690 mV, −580 mV, and −40 mV, respectively. Their WFs can be calculated by the following Eq. The WFs of ZIF-67, CoS, and CdS were calculated as 4.41 eV, 4.52 eV, and 5.06 eV, respectively (Fig. 2e). Due to the differences in the WFs of each component, when ZIF-67, CoS and CdS contact, between CoS and CdS, free electrons migrate from CoS to CdS through the contact interface under dark conditions, and the energy level of CdS with the lowest Fermi energy level moves upward until it reaches equilibrium with the Fermi energy level of CoS. In the equilibrium state, electrons accumulate on the CdS interface, while the electron density decreases on the CoS interface. Thus, the positively charged CoS and the negatively charged CdS create a built-in electric field that is directed from CoS to the CdS at the interface. When the electrons in CoS are repelled by the internal electric field, the potential energy increases and the energy band bends upward. Conversely, the potential energy of the electrons in CdS decreases, the energy band bends downward, and an S-Scheme heterojunction is formed between CoS and CdS34,35. Similarly, for the contact between ZIF-67 and CoS, ZIF-67 has a higher Fermi level. In the process of Fermi capacity flattening, free electrons from ZIF-67 move towards CoS through a dense boundary, forming an internal electric field from ZIF-67 to CoS at the interface, resulting in the band bending36. Forming an ohm heterojunction, the CoS band edges bend down, allowing the photogenerated electrons to flow unhindered into ZIF-6737,38. The results of the DFT differential charge density simulation indicate that at the interface between ZIF 67 and CoS, the blue area representing the decrease in charge density is mainly located on the ZIF 67 side (Supplementary Fig. 14), while electrons are significantly enriched on the CoS side. At the interface between CoS and CdS, the CoS side shows a feature of charge depletion, while the CdS side exhibits charge accumulation. These results suggest that after ZIF 67 comes into contact with CoS, electrons can transfer from ZIF 67 to CoS and further migrate to CdS.

The interface electronic interaction and the formation of the built-in electric field were further verified by high-resolution XPS spectroscopy. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 15a, compared with ZIF-67, the Co 2p peak of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-2 shifted in the positive direction; and compared with CoS (Supplementary Fig. 15b), the Co 2p peak of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-2 also showed a positive shift. These positive shifts indicate that the electron density around the Co atoms decreases39, which is consistent with the results of the DFT simulation showing the depletion of charges at the interface between ZIF-67 and CoS. At the same time, the Cd 3 D peak of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-2 showed a significant negative shift relative to CdS (Supplementary Fig. 15c). This negative shift confirms the increase in the electron density around the Cd atoms40, which is consistent with the results of the DFT showing the accumulation of charges on the side of CdS. Combined with the differential charge density simulation of density functional theory (Supplementary Fig. 14), the change in binding energy directly indicates that electrons transfer from ZIF-67 to CoS, and then further migrate to the CdS at the heterojunction. This directional electron flow forms an ordered built-in electric field from the interior of the material to the exterior, which is expected to significantly improve the separation and migration efficiency of photogenerated carriers.

ZIF-67@CoS/CdS has a homogeneous three-layer sequential structure with two built-in electric fields in the same direction, resulting in an efficient sequential heterojunction. Under sunlight, the construction of a sequential electric field greatly promotes the directional transfer of electrons from the outer CdS layer by layer to the interior. The ZIF-67@CoS/CdS nanocube structure with sequential electric field not only promotes the efficient separation of internal e−–h+ pairs, but also effectively avoids the self-canceling of multiple electric fields between disordered materials41. The electric field can be stable for a long time inside the material36, which greatly promotes the forward progress of the reduction process. At the same time, the holes gathered in the outer layer of the material promoted the oxidation half reaction of the high-energy barrier, which was conducive to the oxidation of LA into high-value organic matter and improved the economic value of the overall reaction. The holes are consumed by LA in the outermost layer, effectively avoiding the oxidation of the sulfur of the outer layer CdS itself, so the material also has good photocorrosion resistance.

In situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to monitor the migration of photoexcited electrons of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-2. Two peaks appear in the high-resolution XPS spectrum of S 2p (Fig. 2f), belonging to S 2p 1/2 and S 2p 3/2. The first two peaks of illumination are located at 162.12 and 161.01 eV respectively, and the peaks of S 2p 1/2 and S 2p 3/2 after illumination are located at 162.27 and 161.11 eV respectively. After illumination, the two peaks shift towards higher binding energy. The shift of S to a higher energy level indicates a decrease in the density of electronic states, because the outer CdS loses electrons under illumination, and the electrons transfer to the inner CoS and ZIF-67. Six peaks appear in the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Co 2p (Fig. 2g), with two peaks at 798.33 and 782.54 eV before illumination attributable to Co 2p 1/2 and Co 2p 3/2 of Co2+, and two peaks at 796.87 and 780.86 eV attributable to Co of Co3+ 2p 1/2 and Co 2p 3/2, plus two satellite peaks at 802.32 and 786.39 eV. After illumination, the two peaks of Co2+ are located at 798.25 and 782.46 eV, and the two peaks of Co3+ are located at 796.54 and 780.76 eV, both of which shift to low binding energy after illumination. A large number of Co ions exist in ZIF-67 inside the material, indicating that the internal electron state density of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS is increased42. Similarly, before illumination, the two peaks of the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Cd 3 d are 411.53 and 404.76 eV, which are attributed to Cd 3 d 3/2 and Cd 3 d 5/2 (Fig. 2h). After illumination, the two peaks are 411.61 and 404.88 eV, respectively, and the binding energy also shows a positive shift. The outer layer of the material is CdS, indicating that the density of electronic states there is reduced. The binding energy changes of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS before and after irradiation indicate that photoexcited carriers transfer from the outer layer of CdS to the inner CoS and ZIF-67 layers successively, and prove the successful preparation of the sequential electric field three-layer heterojunction.

In situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was performed with 5, 5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin trap agent. The types of hydroxyl (·OH) and superoxide (·O2-) radicals were investigated, and the charge-transfer pathway at the interface was further verified. No signal of DMPO ·O2- and DMMPO ·OH was observed under dark conditions, indicating that no effective electrons or holes were produced under dark conditions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16a, before measurement, the photocatalyst was illuminated for 300 s. For CdS and CoS/CdS alone, a weaker EPR signal of DMPO-·O2- was observed, while a significantly enhanced DMPO-·O2- signal of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS was observed. The results show that the construction of the sequential electric field makes the ZIF-67@CoS/CdS three-layer heterojunction exhibit strong reducing ability, which promotes the inner layer ZIF-67 to reduce O2 to ·O2− at the CB position. For the detection of ·OH, pure CdS and CoS/CdS have weak EPR signals, while the DMPO-·OH signals of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS are significantly enhanced after illumination (Supplementary Fig. 16b). The EPR results further indicate that the photogenerated carriers in ZIF-67@CoS/CdS are well separated and transferred to participate in oxidation and reduction reactions.

Transient fluorescence spectroscopy (TRPL) was employed to investigate the photogenerated carrier recombination kinetics in CdS, CoS/CdS, and ZIF-67@CoS/CdS composite materials. The double-exponential fitting results indicated that the fluorescence decay of all samples could be described by two characteristic lifetime components (τ1 and τ2) in Supplementary Table 2. For pure CdS, τ1 (0.0514 ns, accounting for 96.79%) corresponded to the ultrafast non-radiative recombination process of photogenerated electrons being rapidly captured by shallow defect states, while τ2 (2.9293 ns, accounting for 3.21%) reflected the slower kinetics of intrinsic band-to-band radiative recombination and deep-level trap recombination45. When CoS catalyst was introduced, the average carrier lifetime of CoS/CdS (τₐᵥₑ = 2.589 ns) slightly increased, indicating that the presence of CoS promoted charge separation to a certain extent (Fig. 3c). In the ZIF-67@CoS/CdS ternary composite material, τ₂ significantly extended to 9.1901 ns, resulting in an average carrier lifetime of 7.053 ns, which was much higher than pure CdS and CoS/CdS. This significantly prolonged fluorescence lifetime strongly demonstrated that the synergistic effect of ZIF-67 and CoS effectively inhibited the recombination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, greatly promoting the separation and transfer of interface charges, thereby providing more active carriers for photocatalytic reactions46. In contrast, the average lifetime of the ground ZIF-67@CoS/CdS sample after grinding (τₐᵥₑ = 2.845 ns) dropped significantly, indicating that its regular core-shell structure is crucial for efficient charge separation (Supplementary Fig. 20). To further investigate the influence of constructing heterojunction pairs on the carrier separation efficiency of the samples, Femtosecond Transient Absorption (fs-TAS) tests were conducted, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 21. It can be observed that ZIF-67@CoS/CdS has a longer electron lifetime, which is beneficial for the improvement of activity.

It can be seen from Supplementary Fig. 22 that the adsorption-desorption curve of the sample is type IV. The specific surface area of CdS and ZIF-67@CoS/CdS is 159.4 m2 g-1 and 176.2 m2 g-1, respectively, while that of the ZIF-67@CoS/CdS grounding is 147.3 m2 g-1, respectively. ZIF-67@CoS/CdS shows a higher specific surface area than CdS alone, which facilitates exposure of the reactive active site.

The Photo-irradiated Kelvin probe force microscopy (Photo-KPFM) test further revealed the spatial separation of charges in the three layers of heterogeneous materials. Figure 3d–f shows the representative topographic map of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS and its corresponding surface potential (SP) under dark and light conditions. In the scan at ZIF-67@CoS/CdS, the surface potential difference (△SP) of the material is about 153 mV (Fig. 3g). In order to investigate the evolution of SP with structural transformation, we also conducted Photo-KPFM analysis of CdS and CoS/CdS (Supplementary Fig. 23 and Supplementary Fig. 24). Figure 3h shows the calculated △SP for three samples. It can be used to study the photoresponse of catalysts and photogenerated e−– h+ pairs. The ΔSP of pure CdS is relatively small at -2 mV. The ΔSP value of CoS/CdS heterojunction is 43 mV, which is significantly higher than that of pure CdS, indicating that the introduction of CoS can promote electron transport. It is inferred that a built-in electric field is formed between the CoS/CdS interfaces that can act as a driving force for photogenerated charge migration34. Similarly, in ZIF-67@CoS/CdS, ΔSP is higher than the CoS/CdS heterojunction, indicating that there is also a built-in electric field between CoS and the inner ZIF-67 layer.

The photoinduced surface potential (phtoto-SP) of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS (4.592 V) shows a positive shift compared with CoS/CdS (4.482 V) and pure CdS (4.469 V), indicating that the electron density on the outer surface of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS decreases. The layer-by-layer transfer of photogenerated charge to ZIF-67 greatly facilitates the orderly separation of electron-hole pairs47. As shown in Fig. 3i, the local structural configuration and electron migration direction of the cubic composite are deduced. The reduction half-reaction proceeds on the inner ZIF-67 component, while the oxidation half-reaction takes place on the outer surface of the cubic material. The ordered nanostructure of the ground ZIF-67@CoS/CdS sample is destroyed. According to the photoelectric chemical test results before and after grinding ZIF-67@CoS/CdS (Supplementary Figs. 18–20) and the BET test results (Supplementary Fig. 22), it can be seen that the ordered heterostructure accelerates the electron hole pair recombination. ZIF-67@CoS/CdS uniform three-layer cubic nanostructure and the sequential transfer of electrons within the material effectively avoid the chaotic recombination of e−– h+ pairs between particles, which greatly improves the oxidation and reduction efficiency48.

Exploration of oxidation half-reaction

The reaction was carried out in a liquid phase system containing 1% LA, the liquid-phase products were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 35, LA was oxidized to pyruvic acid (PA). LA consumption and PA increase of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-2 sample with time were calculated (Fig. 4e). Exposure to light for 4 h (from 11:00 to 15:00) resulted in the consumption of LA and its conversion to PA. The selectivity of PA was approximately 91.2%. The price of LA is typically set at 1.36 USD/kg (the average transaction price for Chinese exports in February 2026, Tridge), and the price of PA is set at 6.00 USD/kg (the bulk price quoted by Chinese suppliers, ChemicalBook). Based on the conversion results of this experiment (1351 µmol of LA → 1232 µmol of PA, with a molar yield of 91.2%), the product has increased in value by 3.92 times. In order to explore the mechanism of oxidation semi-reaction, oxidative dehydrogenation of LA to PA can be started by removing α-H or removing the hydrogen in α-OH. The ZIF-67@CoS/CdS signals before and after the addition of LA were analyzed by EPR. As shown in Fig. 4f, in the sample without LA, light irradiation induced the generation of ·OH, which derives from water oxidation by photogenerated holes or the conversion of other reactive oxygen species51,52. When LA is present, a distinct new signal is detected. After the addition of the hydroxyl radical shielding agent, the new signal is still retained, and it is consistent with the analog signal of carbon center free radical53. This signal indicates that the photoinduced activation of LA molecules is initially triggered by the cleavage of the α-C(sp3)-H bond and the elimination of α-hydrogen. At the same time, the signal of ·OH was weakened after the addition of LA, indicating that ·OH combined with the removed α-H to form water.

Based on the existence of carbon radicals observed in EPR, the interaction between LA and ZIF-67@CoS/CdS was simulated through DFT calculations, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 36. Further, the formation energy barriers of different intermediates were investigated to determine the reaction path with the highest energy efficiency. Figure 4g delineates the competing reaction channels of LA. PA is yielded through dehydrogenation along Paths 1 and 2, while ethanol is generated via decarboxylation following Path 3. The two dehydrogenation modes differ in hydrogen-extraction sites: Path 1 strips the hydrogen bonded to the α-carbon, and Path 2 removes the hydrogen from the α-hydroxyl group54. The free energy curve indicates that path 1 is the most feasible reaction pathway, as it sequentially removes the hydrogen atoms in α-H and α-OH to form PA. In contrast, the energy barriers of paths 2 and 3 are significantly higher, so the priority of path 1 makes PA highly selective. The reaction energy barrier for the R· radical pathway is the lowest during pyruvic acid formation, indicating that pyruvic acid is most readily generated via the R· radical intermediate. This finding is consistent with the aforementioned EPR analysis.

Thus, the intermediates and products of organic acid oxidation were determined, and the possible photocatalytic mechanism was proposed. Under the illumination of light, ZIF-67@CoS/CdS effectively separates the e--h+ pairs through sequential electric fields. The electrons transfer to the ZIF-67 inside the material, reducing H+ to hydrogen gas. The holes migrate to the outside of the material, oxidizing α-C(sp3)-H in LA to dissociate the α-H, forming carbon-centered radicals, or oxidizing water to ·OH. Then the holes continue to react with the H atom in the carbon-centered radical α-OH to form a C-O bond and generate PA. The released H+ can be reduced to hydrogen gas. Adding EDTA as a h+ scavenger and CCl4 as an electron scavenger55, respectively, conducted activity tests on ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-2. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 37, it can be seen that the yield of PA significantly decreased after adding the h+ scavenger, indicating that the formation of oxidation products mainly depends on h+. And H2 also decreased accordingly because H2 originates from the H+ in H2O and the dehydrogenation of LA. Since the conversion of LA is restricted after the addition of hole masking agents, the generation of H2 also decreases. When the CCl4 electron mask agent was added, the yield of H2 decreased significantly, indicating that the generation of H2 was mainly due to the effect of e-56. By adding p-benzoquinone (PBQ) and tert-butanol (TBA) to capture superoxide free radicals and hydroxyl free radicals (Supplementary Fig. 38), it can be observed that the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution activity did not show significant changes after the introduction of p-benzoquinone, because the system was carried out in an oxygen-free environment, so O₂⁻ did not have an impact. After adding tert-butanol, the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution activity decreased, indicating that the participation of ·OH was the key step in this reaction pathway, which is consistent with the radical test results shown in Fig. 4f. The evolution of reaction intermediates during the selective oxidation of lactic acid to pyruvate over the ZIF-67@CoS/CdS catalyst was investigated using in-situ Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (in-situ FTIR). As shown in Fig. 4h, under light conditions, the vibrations at 1677–1726 cm-1 correspond to the carbon-oxygen double bond (C = O)56 in the pyruvate molecule, 1506 cm-1 and 1398 cm-1 belong to the carboxyl group (COO⁻)56,57 in the pyruvate molecule, 1353 cm-1 and 1454 cm-1 belong to the methyl (-CH3)57 of the pyruvate molecule, and the characteristic CH3-C = O57 group of pyruvate (1160 cm-1) detected simultaneously indicates the formation of the pyruvate (PA) product. As the duration of light exposure increases, the intensities of these characteristic upward peaks gradually increase, indicating that the pyruvate product accumulates continuously on the catalyst surface. The in-situ FTIR results confirmed that ZIF-67@CoS/CdS can achieve efficient and highly selective conversion of lactic acid to pyruvate through a selective dehydrogenation process involving the selective cleavage of C-H and O-H bonds. Furthermore, in-situ infrared spectroscopy measurements of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS and CoS/CdS were performed in aqueous solution before and after lactic acid (LA) addition, as displayed in Supplementary Fig. 39. A more evident shift of the characteristic hydroxyl peak of water was observed for ZIF-67@CoS/CdS upon lactic acid introduction, revealing that the incorporation of ZIF-67 can tune the hydrogen bond strength. The high-value-added oxidation product makes the designed ZIF-67@CoS/CdS material containing a sequential electric field have commercial application potential. The built-in ordered electric field drives directional transport of charge carriers. Photogenerated electrons and holes migrate along distinct pathways, resulting in spatially separated oxidation and reduction half-reactions. This effect suppresses the recombination of photogenerated charge carriers and inhibits reverse reactions, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 40.

In order to further investigate the source of hydrogen and the reaction mechanism, isotope experiments were conducted. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 41, using D2O as the reactant, it was found that hydrogen has a mixture of products including H2, D2 and HD. This further indicates that water acts as the reaction medium in this process. By observing changes in water and lactic acid volumes, it can be seen that the activity at low lactic acid concentrations is higher, and it decreases as the lactic acid concentration increases. This indicates that water plays the role of a reaction medium in the process (Supplementary Fig. 42). In addition, when using pure lactic acid as the reactant and allowing it to react for several hours, no hydrogen gas is produced, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 42. In order to investigate the effect of sacrificial agents on activity, methanol, triethanolamine, and Na2SO3/Na2S aqueous solutions were selected as sacrificial agents to test the activity of the ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-2 sample (Supplementary Figs. 43–45). It is found that the activity is much lower than when lactate is used as the sacrificial agent. This may be attributed to the hydrogen bonds formed between lactate and water, which are more conducive to hydrogen evolution. At the same time, the activity test was also conducted in pure water, and it was found that the activity was extremely low.

DFT calculation

In order to study the effect of adding LA on the photocatalytic water splitting, the mechanism of action of LA and H2O was illustrated through DFT calculations (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Table 5). The side and top views of the optimized structure of the corresponding reaction intermediates and transition states are obtained (Supplementary Fig. 46 and Supplementary Fig. 47). H2O is distributed in the catalyst, the Co site adsorbs H2O molecules, and then the H2O molecules overcome the barrier to complete the decomposition (Fig. 5a–d). According to the calculated results, when LA environment is not added to the catalytic system, the adsorption energy of H2O molecules at the Co site is -0.62 eV, and the bond length between Co-O(H2O) is 2.240 Å (Fig. 5b). Subsequently, H2O is decomposed, and the dissociation barrier is calculated to be 1.26 eV. When LA is added to the system (Fig. 5e–h), through DFT, it is known that the regulation of the hydrogen bond between water and lactic acid by ZIF-67 mainly stems from the dominant role of its coordinatively unsaturated Co active site. The Co site preferentially forms stronger Co-O coordination bonds with the lactate molecule. By competing for hydrogen bond donor/receptor sites, it directly disrupts the original hydrogen bond network between lactate and water. At the same time, the polarization of the O-H bond weakens the hydrogen bond association ability. The spatial confinement of the imidazole framework and weak hydrogen-bond interactions with the N atom rearrange the molecules' hydrogen-bond orientation. When LA is present in the system, the adsorption energy of H2O molecules is -0.81 eV, and the bond length between Co-O (H2O) is 2.142 Å (Fig. 5f). Compared with the system without lactic acid, the Co-O bond length is shorter. At the same time, H of H2O forms a hydrogen bond with LA, the hydrogen bond length is 1.815 Å, and the adsorption strength is increased. The information of key lengths is in Supplementary Table 6. Furthermore, when LA is present in the system, the decomposition energy barrier of H2O decreases, and the calculated water decomposition energy barrier is 0.70 eV. At this time, the *OH obtained from the decomposition of H2O forms a hydrogen bond with the O of LA molecule, which helps to fix the intermediate and reduce the energy barrier. At the same time, we also studied HER reactions of two systems (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Table 7). In the process of photocatalytic hydrogen production, the reactants need to be adsorbed and desorbed on the surface of the material58. This means that the Gibbs free energy (ΔGH*) of the hydrogen-adsorbing catalyst cannot be too positive or too negative, otherwise it will lead to insufficient adsorption of the reactants or difficult desorption, thus affecting the catalytic activity. The optimal value of | ΔGH*| should be zero59. For ZIF-67@CoS/CdS system, HER energy barrier is 0.70 eV, and for ZIF-67@CoS/CdS+LA system, HER energy barrier is 0.52 eV. When lactic acid is present in the system, the energy barrier of HER reaction decreases, which is in line with the expected design of the material. The above calculation results further support the mechanism of the enhancement in catalytic activity. Since the catalytic reaction is dynamic, theoretical calculations can only assist in explaining the reaction mechanism.

Methods

Materials

Anhydrous ethanol (C2H6O) was purchased from Tianjin Yongda Reagent Co., LTD. Cd(CH3COO)2 2H2O (99%), Co(NO3)26H2O (99%), thioacetamide (TAA, 98%), 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM, C4H6N2, 98%), cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB, 98%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., LTD. The chemical reagents were purchased without further processing.

Synthesis of ZIF-67 nanoparticles

A total of 4.5 g of 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM) was weighed and dissolved in 60 mL of deionized water. In addition, 10 mL of CTAB solution with a concentration of 0.002 g/mL was measured, and 0.3 g of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O) was added into it and stirred until completely dissolved. The as-prepared cobalt nitrate solution was rapidly poured into the 2-MIM solution, followed by continuous stirring for 30 min. The obtained purple mixture was centrifuged at about 4000 x g for 5 min, then washed three times by centrifugation with anhydrous ethanol. The product was dried at 60 °C for 6 h, ground into powder, and preserved for further use.

Synthesis of ZIF-67@CoS and pure CoS nanoparticles

A total of 80 mg of ZIF‑67 and 120 mg of thioacetamide (TAA, Aladdin) were dissolved in 50 mL of ethanol. The mixture was stirred in an oil bath at 70 °C for 20 min, then allowed to stand in cold water at room temperature. After centrifugation, the product was washed with anhydrous ethanol three times and dried at 60 °C for 6 h, yielding a dark purple sulfided product ZIF‑67@CoS. When the sulfidation time was prolonged to 1 h, black pure-phase CoS nanoparticles could be obtained.

Synthesis of ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-x by partial cation exchange60

ZIF-67@CoS/CdS is obtained by partial cation exchange of ZIF-67@CoS by the hydrothermal method. 80 mg ZIF-67@CoS nanoparticles, 35 mL ethanol and 8, 12, 16, 20 or 24 mg of Cd(CH3COO)2·2H2O was added into the polyteflon lining, stirred with magnetic force for 20 min, hydrothermal at 120 °C for 6 h, cooled and centrifuged, washed with ethanol three times, dried at 60 °C for 6 h, to obtain brown ZIF-67@CoS/CdS product, recorded as ZIF-67@CoS/CdS-x (x = 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3), (x is the product of the mass ratio of cadmium acetate and ZIF-67@CoS multiplied by 10, and this value is used as the sample name). Excess Cd(CH3COO)2·2H2O (200 mg) reacting with CoS (20 mg) can yield the CdS sample.

Femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectra measurements

Femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) measurements were performed on a custom-built femtosecond pump-probe experimental system. A regenerative amplified Ti: sapphire laser (Astrella-Tunable-USP, Coherent, USA) was utilized as the light source, which generated ultrashort laser pulses at 800 nm with a pulse duration of 35 fs and a repetition rate of 1 kHz. The fundamental laser beam emitted from the laser device was split into two independent beams, namely the pump beam and the probe beam, by a beam splitter. In the experiment, 90% of the 800 nm fundamental laser beam was frequency-doubled via a 0.5 mm-thick type-I β-barium borate crystal to produce 320 nm pump pulses with a single-pulse energy of approximately 0.1 μJ. The probe beam was precisely adjusted in optical path using a computer-controlled optical delay line, before being focused onto a thin sapphire plate to generate broadband white-light supercontinuum. The produced supercontinuum was further divided into signal and reference beams by a broadband 50/50 beam splitter, with the valid spectral detection range covering 430–750 nm. During the test, the focused pump and probe beams were spatially overlapped and irradiated on liquid samples in a cuvette or solid film samples. A half-wave plate was installed in the pump optical path to tune the relative polarization angle between the pump and probe beams to the magic angle of 54.7°, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of experimental data61.

Characterizations

A series of characterization techniques was adopted to investigate the microstructure, optical and electrochemical properties of the as-prepared samples. A Hitachi S4800 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) integrated with a Bruker Quantax energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS) was used to characterize the micromorphology and elemental distribution of materials. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were recorded via an Edinburgh Instruments FS5 fluorescence spectrometer. The crystal structure of the samples was analyzed using a Rigaku D/MAX 2500 X-ray diffractometer, while the fine-scale microstructure was observed using a JEOL JEM-2010 high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM). The optical absorption ability of photocatalysts was evaluated by UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) with a Thermo Scientific Evolution 220 spectrophotometer. An Agilent 720 inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) was employed to quantify the residual ion concentration. The photoelectrochemical performance of the samples was evaluated on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation. For electrochemical measurements, the working electrode was fabricated by dispersing 50 mg of the sample into DMF solvent, followed by uniformly coating the obtained suspension onto FTO conductive glass with a size of 1.0 × 1.5 cm. In the test system, Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) and platinum wire served as the reference electrode and counter electrode, respectively, and 50 mL of 0.2 M sodium sulfate aqueous solution was applied as the supporting electrolyte. Furthermore, Mott-Schottky curves were acquired at a frequency of 1200 Hz on the electrochemical workstation to determine the band position of the photocatalyst.

Light irradiation Kelvin Probe Force microscopy (KPFM) (SPM-9700, Shimadzu, Japan) was used to detect the surface potential of the samples (The light source used is an Xe lamp, with a wavelength ranging from 360 to 800 nm; the probe model is SCM-PIT-V2; the substrate is high-order thermal decomposition graphite (HOPG), and the substrate potential is 4.4 V). In situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was obtained by an electron spectrometer (ESCALAB 210, VG, UK) under A-365 nm light irradiation.

Photocatalytic activity experiment

The catalytic activity was evaluated by loading a specific quantity (50 mg) of the sample onto filter paper with a diameter of 4 cm. Then the sample was transferred to a quartz reactor, and subsequently, a solution (30 mL) of lactic acid was added (1, 3, 5, 10, 30, 70, 100 vol%). Subsequently, the reactor system was purged with argon gas for 30 min to remove residual air. A 300 W xenon lamp (~100 mW/cm2) was employed as the light source. Samples were collected and injected into a gas chromatograph (GC) for H2 and CO2 analysis, while liquid products were characterized by High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). HPLC analysis was performed on an Agilent LC1220 Infinity system from the United States fitted with a UV-Vis detector. The chromatographic parameters were set with 10 mM monopotassium phosphate aqueous solution as the mobile phase at a flow velocity of 0.5 mL per minute62. For the scale-up experiment under natural sunlight, the amount and loading area of the catalyst were scaled up proportionally, and the catalytic performance was then tested outdoors under direct sunlight.
