The social and clinical profile of patients with ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma in Tanzania
Authors: Godfrey Malangwa, Mathias O Banzi, Glory Makupa, Godwin Nnko, Leila Mwakipunda, Eulade Rugengemanzi, Angela Karia, Aisha Ramadhan, Getruda Mashashi, Cepheline Idrisa, Mwitasrobert Gisiri, Jesse Kashabano, Nur Antar Mabruk, Milpah Moturi, Beatrice Mushi, Jerry Ndumbalo, Crispin Kahesa, Khamza Maunda, Rebecca J DeBoer, Elia Mmbaga, Emmanuel L Lugina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOcular surface squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the most common ocular malignancy and occurs disproportionately in Sub-Saharan Africa, where high ultraviolet exposure and HIV prevalence coexist. Data describing the social and clinical profile of ocular surface SCC in Tanzania remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of adults with histologically confirmed ocular surface SCC treated at Ocean Road Cancer Institute and Muhimbili National Hospital between January 2016 and December 2019. Demographic, clinical, radiological, and staging data were extracted from medical records and summarized descriptively.ResultsNinety-nine patients were included, with a median age of 43 years; 61.6% were female, and 80.8% were subsistence farmers. HIV prevalence was 70.7%, and among HIV-positive patients, 67.1% were receiving antiretroviral therapy at diagnosis among HIV positive patients. Documentation of CD4 count and viral load was limited. Most patients presented with locally advanced disease (T3 or T4), with 61.6% staged as T4 and 16.2% as T3, while distant metastasis was not observed. Computed tomography (CT) imaging, available in a subset of patients, showed low frequencies of intracranial and contralateral orbital extension.ConclusionOcular surface SCC in Tanzania predominantly affects young, HIV-positive individuals and commonly presents at an advanced local stage, with documentation gaps in immunological and staging documentation. Improved integration of HIV and cancer care, enhanced diagnostic capacity, and targeted prevention strategies are essential to promote earlier diagnosis and optimize outcomes in high-burden settings.Keywords: Carcinoma, Clinical, Social, HIV, Ocular surface, Tanzania

Introduction

Ocular surface SCC is the most common ocular malignancy of the eye [1]. Ocular surface SCC often arises in the bulbar conjunctiva and limbus within the interpalpebral zone, the area of the globe that is exposed to the sun [1, 2]. Ocular SCC involves the presence of dysplastic squamous epithelial cells breaching the basement membrane with invasion into the underlying substantia propria or stromal tissue [3] Lesions confined to the epithelium without stromal invasion are conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) grades I–III. and are classified as pre-invasive disease [4]. Ocular SCC can spread to the orbit, globe, cornea, and nasolacrimal system. It is a slow-growing tumor that occurs most often in middle age and the elderly [1]. The main risk factor is solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation [2]. Prolonged exposure to solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation increases the risk of ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma through cumulative DNA damage to ocular surface epithelial cells. People who spend more than 50% of their time outdoors in the first 6 years of life or live within 30 degrees of the equator are at greater risk for ocular surface SCC [1].The other risk factors are human papillomavirus types 16 and 18 and immunodeficiency, such as immunodeficiency secondary to HIV infection or organ transplant [5]. Xeroderma pigmentosum, which is a hereditary genetic condition characterized by extreme sun sensitivity, has also been associated with carcinoma of the conjunctiva in young patients [1].

Ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN) encompasses a spectrum of epithelial lesions, including conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia and invasive ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma. The association between HIV infection and OSSN has been explained through several biological mechanisms. HIV weakens immune surveillance, increasing the rate of OSSN because the immune system is unable to attack cancer cells [6]. HIV also potentiates the effects of oncogenic viruses such as Human Papillomavirus by facilitating their transmission to target cells [7, 8]. The last postulated mechanism is that HIV causes a state of persistent inflammation, which leads to tumor-promoting effects through the overexpression of microRNA-155, which increases the chance of mutation [6, 9].

Ocular surface SCC is an important public health problem in equatorial Africa, including Tanzania, where there are both high levels of UV radiation and a high prevalence of HIV. The HIV prevalence in Tanzania is high (4.9%) [10] while the worldwide prevalence is 0.7 among adults aged 15–45 years of age [11]. In SSA, the pattern of ocular surface SCC occurs predominantly in females and younger people and is associated with HIV, whereas in developed countries, it occurs mostly in older, predominantly male individuals and is not associated with HIV.

HIV-associated ocular SCC is a public health challenge in SSA because of late presentation, diagnostic difficulties, and high recurrence rate after treatment. Ocular surface SCC can lead to loss of vision and, in severe cases, death [12]. In a previous study done in Tanzania, most patients with ocular SCC in Tanzania presented with advanced disease and were treated with exenteration or enucleation followed by adjuvant radiotherapy [13].

Data on the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma in Tanzania remain limited. This study aimed to describe the social demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with ocular surface SCC in Tanzania.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study at Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI) and Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH). ORCI is the largest cancer center in Tanzania, and it offers comprehensive cancer management, including prevention, screening, diagnostics, and treatment. MNH is the national referral hospital, research center, and teaching hospital in Tanzania. MNH has an ophthalmology department, which offers screening, diagnosis, and surgical management of ocular malignancies. This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We obtained ethical clearance from the institutional review board (IRB) of MUHAS (MUHAS-REC-09-2022-1385), and we obtained permission to conduct the study from MNH and ORCI. Since the study used anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

The study included all patients aged 18 years or older with a confirmed pathologic diagnosis of ocular surface SCC at MNH or ORCI from 1st January 2016 to 31st December 2019. We identified eligible participants by reviewing/searching International Classification of Diseases code (ICD code) C69.0, C44.101, C44.102, and C44.109 in the electronic medical record. We confirmed the diagnosis by the presence of a histological report.

We extracted data from the patients’ electronic and paper medical records using structured data collection forms. Demographic variables included age, sex, and occupation. Clinical variables included HIV status, ART use, and CD4 count at diagnosis, tumor size, nodal status, and metastatic status, perineural invasion on imaging, contralateral involvement, and stage of disease at diagnosis. The staging was done by physical examination supplemented by imaging and pathological results. The post-exenteration or enucleation pathological staging and CT scan of the head and orbit, and physical examination are used to determine the tumor size. CT scan of the head and orbit, physical examination, and fine needle aspiration cytology, or results of nodal biopsy, are used to determine the nodal status. Distance metastasis was assessed using chest x-ray and abdominal pelvic ultrasound. Assessment for distant metastasis was performed using chest X-ray as part of the routine baseline evaluation. Cross-sectional imaging and bone evaluation were not routinely performed and were reserved for patients with clinical features suggestive of metastatic disease due to cost constraints.

Data were analyzed using Stata version 15 StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages and compared between groups using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and graphical inspection. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation) and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Social demographic characteristics

The study included 99 patient records. The median age of patients with ocular surface SCC was 43.0. Most patients with ocular SCC were females (61.6%), subsistence farmers (80.8%), and married (54.6%). The prevalence of HIV among patients with ocular surface SCC was 70.7%.

Age was not normally distributed; therefore, it was summarized using the median and interquartile range. There was no statistically significant difference in median age between HIV-positive and HIV-negative patients (Mann -Whitney U test, p = 0.306).

Discussion

Ocular surface SCC is a vision-threatening malignancy that can become life-threatening when locally advanced, making an understanding of its social and clinical profile essential for improving early recognition and management. In SSA, HIV infection is a key risk factor in the development of ocular surface SCC through impaired immune surveillance and interaction with other oncogenic exposures. In this study, we describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with ocular surface SCC in Tanzania, highlighting the high prevalence of HIV infection and the frequent presentation with advanced local disease. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating HIV and cancer services and strengthening strategies for earlier diagnosis in high-burden, resource-limited settings.

Our study population was largely young patients with a median age of 43. This is consistent with other studies done in SSA, where younger patients presented with ocular SCC [13–17]. The median age of this study was lower compared to other parts of the world, such as in the Middle East (Jordan), with a median age of 58 years [18], India was 55 years [19], Italy was 68.2 years [20] and 65.5 years in the USA [21]. Although the median age of patients with ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma varies across regions, this variation is more plausibly explained by differences in the prevalence of key risk factors, most notably HIV infection and ultraviolet radiation exposure, rather than by geographical location per se. This study was conducted in SSA (near the equatorial region), where there is a high intensity of UV radiation, which increases the risk of ocular surface SCC compared to studies done in Asia, Europe, and the USA.

This study reported a female predominance (61.6%), which differs from many studies in high-income countries that often report male predominance [18, 21]. Similar female dominance has been noted in other SSA reports [15] and may be attributed to higher HIV prevalence among women in Tanzania compared to males (6.3% vs. 3.6%) [10]. Greater exposure to environmental risk factors, such as indoor air pollution from biomass fuel used for cooking, may also be a contributing factor [22], as women are more often involved in cooking in most African settings [23].

Our study showed that the majority (80.8%) were subsistence farmers. Subsistence farmers spent most of their time in outdoor occupations. The findings of this study are supported by the Ugandan study, which showed people doing outdoor occupations have a 1.7 times risk of developing ocular surface SCC [24]. However, the finding of this study differs from the study in India, which showed 70% of patients with ocular surface SCC had indoor occupation, but all patients with ocular surface SCC who had indoor occupation reported a history of prolonged sun exposure [19].

CT imaging findings were available for a limited subset of patients and are therefore presented descriptively. As a result, no comparative inference regarding the association between ART use and CT-based clinical features can be drawn. In this cohort, the clinical profile of ocular surface SCC was characterized predominantly by locally advanced disease (T4 and T3) at presentation, with more than half of patients staged as T4 and over half having unassessed regional nodal status (Nx). This pattern is consistent with reports from SSA, where delayed presentation, limited access to specialist ophthalmic services, and constrained diagnostic capacity contribute to advanced-stage disease at diagnosis [12, 13]. The low proportion of patients with documented nodal evaluation likely reflects reliance on clinical examination in the absence of routine cross-sectional imaging or pathological nodal assessment, a challenge previously highlighted in resource-limited settings [25].

Radiological findings, available in a subset of patients, demonstrated relatively low frequencies of intracranial extension, contralateral orbital involvement, and bone invasion, suggesting that while primary tumors were frequently advanced locally, extensive craniofacial spread was uncommon at initial imaging. Similar observations have been reported in regional studies, where orbital invasion is more frequent than intracranial extension, the latter usually representing very late disease [18, 20]. The limited availability of CT imaging in this study, largely due to financial and logistical barriers, underscores an important diagnostic gap that may lead to underestimation of radiologic disease extent.

Notably, all patients in this cohort were classified as M0 at diagnosis, consistent with existing evidence that distant metastases in ocular surface CC are rare and typically occur late in the disease course [12, 17, 21]. Overall, the clinical profile observed in this study reflects the typical presentation of ocular surface SCC in high HIV-burden, resource-limited settings, dominated by advanced local disease, incomplete staging documentation, and limited radiologic assessment, highlighting the need for earlier detection and improved integration of diagnostic services.

Our study showed that 70.7% of patients with ocular surface SCC were HIV positive. This result is approximately similar to other studies conducted in Africa, such as in Nigeria, with a 75% prevalence of HIV among patients with ocular surface SCC [26], 71% in South Africa [27], and 77% in Uganda [28]. However, our study had a lower prevalence of HIV among patients with ocular SCC compared to the study done in Zimbabwe, which showed 92.3% of patients with ocular surface SCC were HIV positive [29]. This difference might be due to the reason that Zimbabwe has a high prevalence (12.9%) of HIV in the general population among adults [30] compared to Tanzania, with HIV prevalence of 4.9% [10].

The study showed a low proportion (67.1%) of patients who are HIV positive were on ART at diagnosis, compared to the general WHO recommendation of at least 90% of all patients with HIV to be on ART [31]. The study showed only 3 (4.3%) had documented CD4 count at and during diagnosis. This is low compared to the recommendation that all patients with HIV should have their CD4 count checked every 3–6 months for the first 2 years [10]. The low proportion of patients who have documented CD4 count may result from the lack of integration between HIV management and cancer management, thereby causing poor documentation of CD4 count and viral load. Patients with HIV and cancer have HIV clinics and cancer clinics in different hospitals, and there is no electronic file link between one hospital and the other.

The high prevalence of HIV infection and the advanced stage at presentation observed in this cohort underscore the importance of strengthening primary prevention strategies in high-risk populations. In settings such as Tanzania, where prolonged outdoor occupational exposure is common, public health measures promoting reduction of ultraviolet (UV) exposure, such as use of protective eyewear, wide-brimmed hats, and behavioral modification, may play a role in reducing ocular surface carcinogenesis. In addition, early HIV diagnosis and sustained access to antiretroviral therapy may mitigate immunosuppression-related oncogenic risk and potentially delay disease progression. Although the role of human papillomavirus (HPV) in ocular surface SCC remains incompletely defined, emerging evidence suggests a contributory role in a subset of cases, highlighting the potential long-term relevance of HPV vaccination as part of comprehensive cancer prevention strategies. Future studies integrating preventive exposures and immunological markers are needed to better inform targeted prevention programs in high-burden settings.

This study has several limitations. The clinical profile could not be compared by HIV status due to incomplete documentation of key immunological variables, including CD4 cell counts and viral load. In addition, not all patients underwent CT imaging of the head and orbit because of financial and logistical constraints, which limited detailed radiologic characterization. The retrospective design further restricted control over data completeness and precluded causal inference.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that ocular surface SCC in Tanzania predominantly affects relatively young adults, with a high burden among females and individuals engaged in outdoor occupations, particularly subsistence farming. A substantial proportion of patients were living with HIV, yet only two-thirds were on antiretroviral therapy at the time of cancer diagnosis, and documentation of key immunological markers such as CD4 count and viral load was notably limited. Clinically, most patients presented with locally advanced disease, while distant metastasis was absent, and radiological assessment was available in only a subset of cases, reflecting existing diagnostic constraints.

These findings highlight persistent gaps in early detection, staging completeness, and integration of HIV and cancer care. Strengthening coordinated HIV oncology services, improving access to diagnostic imaging, and promoting targeted prevention strategies for high-risk populations may contribute to earlier diagnosis and improved clinical outcomes for ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma in resource-limited settings.
