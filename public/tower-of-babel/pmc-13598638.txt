Clinical Evidence and Mechanistic Plausibility of Chinese Patent Medicines for Sepsis: An Evidence Map
Authors: Qingsong Liu, Mengyao Zheng, Zichen Wang, Yifei He, Jiayue Xu, Wen Wang, Xin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a life-threatening syndrome with limited specific therapeutic options. Chinese patent medicines (CPMs) are widely used as adjunctive therapies in China, although the available clinical evidence, predominantly from injectable formulations, remains fragmented. This evidence map integrated review-level clinical evidence with in vivo findings to assess evidence certainty and mechanistic plausibility. Eighteen meta-analyses and 337 eligible animal studies were identified; 237 animal studies corresponding to interventions with at least one moderate-certainty clinical outcome entered the focused mechanistic analysis. No review–outcome estimate was rated as high certainty, and 27 of 152 review–outcome estimates (17.8%) were rated as moderate certainty. Moderate-certainty evidence was concentrated mainly in Xuebijing and Shenfu, with fewer moderate-certainty signals for Shengmai, Shenmai, Yiqifumai, Shuxuetong, and Xiyanping. In animal models, Xuebijing and Shenfu were associated with inflammatory and immune regulation, endothelial or barrier protection, mitochondrial function, and programmed cell death, while Shengmai, Shenmai, and Yiqifumai showed more limited pathway-level associations involving oxidative stress, cardiovascular protection, and ferroptosis. Overall, the animal evidence suggested overlapping biological pathways, but risk of bias was generally unclear or high and did not establish causal mechanisms in humans. This evidence map provides a structured framework linking clinical evidence certainty with mechanistic plausibility and highlights important evidence gaps. Given the limited certainty of the clinical evidence, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in rigorous clinical trials, systematic safety evaluations, and methodologically robust translational studies.

Introduction

Sepsis remains a life-threatening syndrome associated with substantial mortality and global disease burden.1 In addition to uncontrolled inflammation, sepsis involves profound immune dysregulation, endothelial injury, microcirculatory disturbance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and progressive multi-organ failure.2–4 Despite advances in supportive care, it remains a leading cause of mortality in critically ill patients and a major global health burden.5,6

In China, several Chinese patent medicines (CPMs), particularly injectable formulations, have been recommended and used as adjunctive therapies for sepsis.7,8 Unlike individualized herbal prescriptions, CPMs are marketed products with fixed compositions and standardized manufacturing processes, making them more amenable to evaluation as herbal medicinal products. Their use in intensive care settings has been documented in observational studies,9 with Xuebijing (XBJ) and Shenfu injection (SFI) being among the most commonly prescribed.10

However, the clinical evidence on CPMs for sepsis remains fragmented, and the evidence for individual CPMs are not well characterized. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined individual CPMs, but these reviews often focus on a narrow range of interventions, include overlapping primary studies, and differ in scope and conclusions.11–14 Consequently, a comprehensive overview of which CPMs and outcomes have been evaluated or of the certainty of the available evidence is still lacking. An evidence-map approach is therefore appropriate for describing the distribution and certainty of this evidence and identifying important evidence gaps. Interpretation is further complicated by heterogeneity in sepsis populations, treatment regimens, background care, and outcome definitions, while safety outcomes remain incompletely reported.

A further challenge concerns the translational relevance of mechanistic evidence. Although some studies have explored the potential biological mechanisms of CPMs in sepsis, these mechanistic insights are often limited to individual formulations, and the connection between biological findings and clinical outcomes remains unclear.10,15,16 Therefore, understanding whether the observed clinical signals of CPMs are biologically plausible remains an important issue.

To address these gaps, this evidence map was designed to answer three questions: Which CPMs and outcomes have been evaluated in existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and what is the certainty of the resulting evidence? For CPMs with at least one clinical outcome supported by moderate-certainty evidence, what biological pathways have been reported in eligible in vivo studies, and do these findings support mechanistic plausibility? Where do the clinical and preclinical evidence converge, and what evidence gaps should be prioritized in future clinical and translational research?

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

For the clinical evidence map, we included: (1) systematic reviews with meta-analyses. (2) patients with a definitive diagnosis of sepsis. (3) the intervention group received the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA)-approved CPM, alone or in addition to a background treatment, whereas the comparator received the same background treatment without the CPM, including standard care alone where applicable. Exclusion criteria were: (1) duplicate publications; (2) reviews focusing on pediatric sepsis; and (3) non-peer-reviewed or non-full-text records, including preprints, protocols, and conference abstracts.

For the mechanistic review, we included: (1) in vivo animal experiments. (2) animal models of sepsis. (3) the experimental group received a CPM, with or without an identical background treatment, whereas the comparator received no CPM, vehicle, placebo, or the same background treatment alone. Exclusion criteria were: (1) duplicate publications; (2) comments, reviews, case reports, and conference abstracts; (3) studies with incomplete data or inaccessible full texts; (4) studies in which the sepsis model, intervention, comparator, or outcome data could not be adequately ascertained.

Search Strategy

We systematically searched the following nine databases from inception to December 31, 2025: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, and SinoMed. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for “sepsis” and “Chinese patent medicine”. The search was restricted to publications in Chinese or English. The complete database-specific search strategies and search yields are provided in Supplementary Table S1. This review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. Two reviewers independently screened the literature, extracted data, and cross-checked the results. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer.

Data Extraction

For the clinical evidence map, two reviewers independently extracted study characteristics, interventions, outcome definitions, summary effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals where available, statistical conclusions, and source references. All eligible review–outcome estimates from the retained reviews were tabulated and classified according to GRADE certainty to characterize the distribution and certainty of the available evidence. During full-text assessment, review-level overlap was evaluated qualitatively on the basis of PICO domains and reported primary-study coverage. For multiple reviews addressing the same or substantially overlapping clinical question, selection was performed sequentially according to: (1) closest correspondence to the intervention–outcome question; (2) higher AMSTAR 2 confidence; (3) greater completeness of the eligible evidence base; and (4) a more recent literature search. Excluded overlapping reviews and the reasons for exclusion are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

All in vivo sepsis studies meeting the prespecified eligibility criteria were retained as eligible animal studies. After construction of the clinical evidence map, the focused mechanistic analysis was restricted to interventions with at least one clinical outcome supported by moderate-certainty evidence. Animal studies were matched to the corresponding interventions and analyzed according to biological domains relevant to sepsis pathophysiology, including inflammation, immune regulation, endothelial and barrier protection, microcirculation, mitochondrial function, and programmed cell death. Eligible animal studies of interventions that did not meet this clinical-certainty criterion were not included in the focused mechanistic analysis.

Quality Assessment and Certainty of the Evidence

Methodological quality of the included systematic reviews was assessed using AMSTAR 2.17 The certainty of evidence for each review–outcome estimate was reassessed using the GRADE framework,18 considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, based on the information available in the source reviews. Reported statistical heterogeneity and relevant clinical and methodological differences across populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and follow-up periods were considered when assessing inconsistency. Because this study was designed as an evidence map rather than a de novo meta-analysis, primary-study data were not re-pooled, and no new heterogeneity statistics or conventional sensitivity analyses were generated. Risk of bias in animal studies was assessed using the SYRCLE risk-of-bias tool.19 Two reviewers independently performed the assessments, with disagreements resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Outcome-level GRADE ratings are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Summary of Clinical and Mechanistic Evidence

Clinical evidence was synthesized descriptively at the intervention–outcome level, and bubble plots were used to visualize the distribution and certainty of evidence across CPMs and outcome domains. Interventions with at least one moderate-certainty clinical outcome were prioritized for focused mechanistic analysis. For these interventions, eligible in vivo studies were synthesized to summarize reported biological pathway associations relevant to sepsis pathophysiology. Clinical and in vivo evidence were summarized in parallel to assess mechanistic plausibility and identify areas of concordance and evidence gaps.

Results

The initial search identified 33,366 records. After removal of duplicates and screening of titles and abstracts, 1229 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 355 studies were ultimately included. These included 18 systematic reviews and meta-analyses and 337 animal studies. The study selection process and reasons for exclusion are presented in Figure 1. Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias

According to AMSTAR 2, the methodological quality of the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses was generally suboptimal. No review achieved high confidence; three were rated as moderate confidence, and the remainder were rated as low or critically low confidence (Figure 2A). The main critical weaknesses included lack of protocol registration, incomplete reporting of search strategies, inadequate reporting of funding sources for the primary trials, and limited consideration of trial-level risk of bias in the interpretation of pooled estimates. According to the SYRCLE tool, risk of bias in the included animal studies was generally unclear or high across domains, largely because of insufficient reporting (Figure 2B). Allocation concealment and blinding were rarely reported, and blinding of outcome assessment was infrequently described. These findings indicate that, although mechanistic evidence was abundant, its internal validity remains uncertain.

Clinical Evidence

Across the 18 included meta-analyses,11–14,20–33 152 review–outcome estimates were available for multiple CPMs used in sepsis. Of these, 27 (17.8%) were rated as moderate certainty, 66 (43.4%) as low certainty, 53 (34.9%) as very low certainty, and 6 (3.9%) were not assessable; none reached high certainty (Figure 3). The available evidence was unevenly distributed across CPMs. XBJ and SFI were the most extensively studied, with 7 and 6 meta-analyses contributing 51 and 40 review–outcome estimates, respectively, whereas most other CPMs were supported by much less evidence.

Safety outcomes were infrequently reported. Only 7 of 18 meta-analyses (38.9%) reported adverse events. One adverse-event estimate was rated as very low certainty, whereas the remaining six were not assessable. The only numerical estimate was imprecise and not statistically significant (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.10 to 1.99); therefore, no firm conclusion regarding safety can be drawn.

XBJ was associated with reduced 28-day mortality (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.71) and overall mortality (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.62), as well as lower APACHE II and SOFA scores (WMD −2.65, 95% CI −3.23 to −2.08; and MD −2.41, 95% CI −2.79 to −2.03, respectively). For the other CPMs, moderate-certainty evidence mainly related to mortality, shock-related outcomes, cardiac function, and inflammatory markers.

Mechanistic Evidence from Animal Studies

A total of 337 animal studies met the basic eligibility criteria. Based on the clinical evidence map, 237 studies corresponding to interventions with at least one moderate-certainty clinical outcome were included in the focused mechanistic analysis: Xuebijing injection (n = 130), Shenfu injection (n = 78), Shenmai injection (n = 17), Shengmai injection (SMI; n = 8), and Yiqifumai injection (YQFM; n = 4). The remaining 100 eligible animal studies involved interventions that did not meet this clinical-certainty criterion and were therefore not included in the focused mechanistic analysis. No eligible in vivo sepsis-model studies meeting the prespecified eligibility criteria were identified for SXT or XYP. Accordingly, these interventions were retained in the clinical evidence synthesis, but no focused mechanistic analysis was performed for them. Their moderate-certainty clinical signals were therefore not accompanied by eligible in vivo mechanistic evidence within the scope of this review.

Xuebijing Injection

XBJ was associated with favorable clinical signals for mortality, disease-severity scores, hemodynamic measures, platelet count, inflammatory markers, and hospital stay. The reported animal findings clustered into four broad biological domains: inflammatory signaling, immune regulation, endothelial and microcirculatory function, and programmed cell-death-related pathways (Figure 5). These findings provide mechanistic plausibility for the clinical signals but do not establish causal mechanisms in humans. Specifically, XBJ and its constituents reduced pro-inflammatory mediator release and attenuated inflammatory organ injury through diverse signaling pathways.34–41 It also showed endothelial, coagulation, and microcirculatory effects, including changes in leukocyte adhesion, ICAM-1/VCAM-1 expression, coagulation indices, and tissue perfusion.42–52 In addition, XBJ appeared to influence immune regulation, organ function, and survival across experimental models,53–58 while other studies implicated cell-death and redox-related processes.34,53,59 These findings provide a relatively broad body of animal evidence supporting the mechanistic plausibility of XBJ.

Shenfu Injection

Moderate-certainty signals for SFI included lower 28-day mortality and SOFA scores, increased CD4+ T-cell counts, and shorter ICU stay. Lower-certainty estimates also suggested changes in cardiac function and mean arterial pressure. The reported animal findings were concentrated mainly in anti-inflammatory signaling, cardioprotection, immunomodulation, and barrier protection (Figure 6). The anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects of SFI were associated with NF-κB/MAPK-related signaling, apoptosis, and mitochondrial function,60–62 and reduced HMGB1 release,63 with additional involvement of IRAK1/TRAF6/NF-κB, p38 MAPK, PPARγ, HMGB1, and cholinergic pathways.64–68 A prominent feature of the mechanistic evidence was cardiometabolic and organ protection. Shengmai Injection

SMI was associated with reduced shock treatment failure in patients with sepsis. The corresponding animal studies indicated that its potential mechanisms were mainly related to endothelial and microcirculatory protection, anti-inflammation, cardioprotection, and barrier protection.

SMI improved hemorheological indices, including blood viscosity and hematocrit.90 Across complementary models, SMI was associated with vascular-endothelial, inflammatory, intestinal-barrier, and cardiac effects involving TNF-α, IL-6, vWF, TM, NF-κB/MLCK/occludin-related signaling, and cardiac function.91–93 In addition, SMI appeared to alleviate myocardial injury and improve cardiac function,93 while also protecting the intestinal mucosal barrier through regulation of MLCK and occludin expression.92 Metabolomic evidence further suggested an antioxidant effect involving taurine, glutathione, and amino acid metabolism.94

Shenmai Injection

Clinical evidence suggested that Shenmai was associated with reduced 28-day mortality, lower APACHE II scores, and improved left ventricular ejection fraction in patients with sepsis. The corresponding animal studies indicated that these effects may be related mainly to antioxidation, anti-inflammation, immunomodulation, cardioprotection, and regulation of programmed cell death.

Shenmai injection exerted antioxidant effects by scavenging oxygen free radicals, enhancing superoxide dismutase activity, and reducing malondialdehyde levels.95 It also suppressed the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-8, mainly through inhibition of NF-κB signaling, with additional reductions in plasma endotoxin and C-reactive protein levels.96,97 Studies also reported renal protection with CRRT plus Shenmai injection and immunomodulatory effects in experimental sepsis models.98–100 In addition, Shenmai injection was associated with pulmonary protection and cardiomyocyte apoptosis-related effects.101–103 More recent evidence further suggested that Shenmai injection may attenuate sepsis-associated lung injury through the gut-lung axis by remodeling gut microbiota and restoring steroid hormone biosynthesis.101

Yiqifumai Injection

YQFM was associated with reduced 28-day mortality in patients with sepsis. The corresponding animal studies indicated that its potential mechanisms mainly involved cardioprotection, anti-inflammation, endothelial and microcirculatory protection, and regulation of programmed cell death.

Available experimental evidence suggested that YQFM alleviated sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy by inhibiting ferroptosis through activation of the xCT/GPX4 axis, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation, and myocardial injury markers.104 It was also reported to improve cardiac function in septic shock by increasing blood pressure and reducing NT-proANP, BNP, and myocardial enzyme levels.105 In addition, YQFM appeared to attenuate endothelial dysfunction and microcirculatory disturbance through inhibition of NF-κB signaling, downregulation of adhesion molecules such as CD11b/CD18 and ICAM-1, and reduction of leukocyte adhesion and vascular leakage.106

Among the CPMs for which animal evidence was available, the alignment between moderate-certainty clinical signals and mechanistic findings appeared to be most consistent for XBJ and SFI. For XBJ, the observed clinical signals in mortality, disease severity, hemodynamics, platelet count, inflammatory markers, and hospital stay were broadly qualitatively concordant with reported animal findings indicating anti-inflammation, immunomodulation, endothelial and microcirculatory protection, and regulation of programmed cell death. For SFI, the clinical improvements in mortality, organ function, immune parameters, and ICU stay were broadly consistent with mechanistic findings related to anti-inflammation, cardioprotection, immunomodulation, and barrier protection.

The alignment was less comprehensive, but still biologically plausible, for SMI, Shenmai, and YQFM. For SMI, the shock-related clinical signal was qualitatively aligned with animal findings involving endothelial and microcirculatory protection, anti-inflammatory regulation, cardioprotection, and barrier protection. For Shenmai, the clinical signals for mortality and cardiac function were qualitatively aligned with reported animal findings involving antioxidation, inflammatory and immune regulation, cardioprotection, and programmed cell death. For YQFM, the 28-day mortality signal was qualitatively aligned with animal findings involving cardioprotection, inflammatory regulation, endothelial and microcirculatory protection, and ferroptosis-related pathways.

In contrast, although SXT and XYP showed favorable moderate-certainty clinical signals, no eligible in vivo sepsis-model studies were identified, and mechanistic alignment could therefore not be assessed.

Discussion

This evidence map extends previous formulation-specific and pharmacological reviews by providing a structured assessment of evidence certainty and methodological quality across multiple CPMs. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were evaluated using AMSTAR 2,17 clinical outcomes were characterized according to GRADE certainty, and eligible in vivo studies were synthesized separately to assess mechanistic plausibility. This framework highlights the distribution and limitations of the available clinical evidence, together with areas of concordance and gaps between clinical and preclinical evidence.

The evidence map should be interpreted cautiously. No clinical outcome reached high certainty; 119 of 152 outcomes (78.3%) were rated as low or very low certainty, while 6 outcomes (3.9%) could not be assigned a GRADE certainty rating. Twenty-two outcomes showed no statistically significant difference, and eight could not be evaluated from the available effect estimates. No included review achieved high confidence according to AMSTAR 2, and only three were rated as moderate confidence. Safety outcomes were reported in only seven of 18 reviews; six adverse-event estimates could not be assigned a certainty rating, and one was rated as very low certainty. The available data are therefore insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding safety.

Moderate-certainty clinical evidence and eligible animal studies were concentrated mainly on XBJ and SFI; however, these findings should not be interpreted as establishing clinical efficacy or causal mechanisms in humans. Estimates across reviews may also involve overlapping primary trials and should not be regarded as independent replication.107 In addition, heterogeneity in sepsis populations, treatment regimens, background care, outcome definitions, and follow-up limits direct comparisons across interventions.108 The generally unclear or high risk of bias in animal studies further limits the translation of these findings to human sepsis.

These findings have several implications for future research. Given the comparatively stronger alignment of clinical and mechanistic evidence, Xuebijing and Shenfu, along with other CPMs showing mechanistic plausibility, warrant further investigation in adequately powered, well-designed clinical trials. Future studies should also incorporate mechanism-informed biomarkers, such as indicators of endothelial injury, immune dysfunction, and coagulation abnormalities, to assess whether experimentally identified pathways are reflected in patients.109 More broadly, greater methodological rigor is needed across both clinical and preclinical research, including prospective protocol registration, adherence to reporting guidelines such as PRISMA, and more systematic collection and reporting of safety outcomes.110–112 The absence of eligible in vivo sepsis-model studies for SXT and XYP, despite moderate-certainty clinical evidence for these interventions, highlights a gap in the available preclinical evidence. This finding does not invalidate the observed clinical signals; rather, it indicates that their mechanistic plausibility could not be evaluated using eligible in vivo evidence within the scope of this review. Further well-designed preclinical and translational studies are needed to investigate the biological pathways potentially associated with these clinical findings.

This study has several limitations. First, the evidence-map approach was useful for characterizing the overall evidence landscape but did not permit formal quantitative comparisons across all CPMs and outcomes. Although overlapping reviews were identified and were not treated as independent evidence, differences in review eligibility criteria, search dates, included primary studies, and analytical methods may still affect the comparability of review-level estimates. Second, the mechanistic synthesis was restricted to animal studies and did not include in vitro evidence or human mechanistic data, which may also contribute to understanding biological plausibility. Moreover, animal models of sepsis may not fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity of human sepsis, which limits direct translational inference. Third, for CPMs supported by relatively few studies, including SMI, Shenmai, and YQFM, the observed alignment between clinical and mechanistic evidence should be considered preliminary.

Conclusion

The available evidence suggests that several CPMs may be associated with favorable signals for selected outcomes in patients with sepsis, although the clinical evidence is predominantly derived from injectable formulations. However, no outcome was supported by high-certainty evidence, most included reviews had important methodological limitations, and safety evidence was insufficient. The eligible in vivo findings support mechanistic plausibility rather than established mechanisms in humans. These findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. Rigorously designed clinical trials, systematic safety assessments, and methodologically robust translational studies are needed to clarify the potential role of CPMs in sepsis management.
