Advancing carbon neutrality in post-COP28 in Ghana: Examining the impact of foreign direct investment and governance indicators
Authors: Clare Teroviel Tergu, Jin Zhang, Jinkai Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper investigates the efficacy of foreign direct investment (FDI) and governance quality in reducing the adverse impact of CO2 emissions in Ghana. To achieve this aim, panel data for the independent and control variables spanning from 2000 to 2022 were obtained from World Development Indicators database and World Governance Indicators. The data was analysed using the Fixed Generalised Least Squares (FGLS) and the Method of Moment Quantile Regression (MMQR) methods. The findings of the study reveal that FDI aids in lowering the level of CO2 emissions in Ghana and therefore can be achieved through political stability, voice and accountability, controlled corruption, and enforcement of rule of law. It further reveals that the level of governance effectiveness and regulation quality is low in attracting FDI to aid in lowering CO2 emissions in Ghana. This study suggests that policymakers in Ghana should strengthen the system and structure governing the environmental management process and improve the quality of environmental policies to encourage environmentally friendly FDI flows.Keywords: CO2 emissions, Foreign investment, Governance quality, FGLS, MMQR, Ghana

Introduction

Foreign direct investment (FDI) is now recognised as an important source of private external financing for developing nations and a key factor in driving their economic growth [1]. Foreign investment flows have the potential to stimulate economic development, but they also have the capacity to negatively impact the environment of the nations where they are received [2]. Climate change is an urgent and challenging environmental issue globally [3]. In 2021, Ghana received FDI worth 1.88 billion U.S. dollars, equivalent to around 3.3 percent of the country's GDP. The FDI inflow in 2023 declined by 730 million U.S. dollars compared to 2020, according to UNCTAD. Ghana's rapid economic expansion has been facilitated by the inflow of FDI, which is attributed to the country's strong trade openness and political stability [4]. The 2022 World Bank report affirms that Ghana's per capita contribution accompanied by its economic development adds to global greenhouse gas emissions by 24 % of the global average. In 2023, World Bank Group's Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) for Ghana predicts that if immediate climate mitigation measures are not implemented, the country might witness an increase of at least one million individuals falling below the poverty line as a result of climate-related disasters [5]. Poor households may experience a decrease in income of up to 40 % by the year 2050. The report recommends following a developmental strategy that enhances the ability to withstand the impacts of climate change and promotes a shift towards economic growth with reduced CO2 emissions [5]. This may be achieved by implementing a mix of policies and making both public and private investments. The 2023 CCDR reports that Ghana's economic and human growth is susceptible to the impacts of climate change. Annually, around 45,000 individuals in Ghana are impacted by floods, and half of Ghana's coastal areas are susceptible to erosion and flooding due to the rise in sea levels [5]. Human capital and productivity will be hindered by land degradation, water insecurity, and local air pollution [6]. The report further recommends a climate resilient and low-carbon approach as a potential to transform difficulties into advantageous circumstances.

According to a 2020 report by the World Bank, air pollution is identified as the primary environmental hazard to public health in Ghana and ranks as the sixth highest risk of mortality among 19 total risks. The whole population of Ghana is subject to particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5) levels that surpass the guidelines set by the World Health Organisation [5]. The Ghana Tourism Authority has affirmed tourism as Ghana's fastest growing industry in recent years. Though international tourism receipts increase the country's GDP, it has negative repercussions on the environment. The travel and tourism industry in Ghana made a significant contribution of approximately 3.4 billion U.S. dollars to the country's Gross Domestic Product in 2022. This is a growth in comparison to the 3.1 billion U.S. dollars recorded in the previous year. According to Statista's prediction for 2023, the share is expected to exceed 3.6 billion U.S. dollars, driven by a yearly growth in international tourist arrivals. Tourism significantly contributes to CO2 emissions through various activities such as air travel [7]. Civil aviation has made substantial contributions to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and local air pollution [8]. The civil aviation industry predominantly depends on fossil fuels, making it appear unachievable to reduce carbon emissions in poorer nations [9]. In 2018, the estimated time series of worldwide aviation emissions amounted to 1.04 billion tonnes of CO2 [10]. A report from the International Air Transport Association (IATA) stated that the distribution of aviation CO2 emissions is determined by the relative weight of passengers and freight. However, the International Civil Aviation Organisation aims to achieve a 50 % global reduction in carbon emissions by the year 2050. The many pollutants discharged from aircraft engines have an impact on the overall composition of the Earth's atmosphere [8].

In order to mitigate CO2 emissions, the Ghanaian government has enacted many environmental regulations. One of such policy is the Emissions levy Act, 2023 (Act 1112), which was implemented in February 2024. This act puts a fee on the CO2 equivalent emissions produced by internal combustion engine cars. The Emissions Levy aligns with the government's endeavours to address greenhouse gas emissions by promoting the use of eco-friendly technology and sustainable energy sources. It aims to enhance environmental stewardship while regulating overall levels of air pollution. The primary obstacle facing the environmental management process in Ghana is inadequate implementation of policies and legislation to accomplish the intended outcome. Thus, the Environmental Protection Act of Ghana lacks comprehensive legislation and methods to adequately tackle the existing environmental concerns. These obstacles can be categorised as either substantive (such as lack of funds or awareness) or procedural (such as limited involvement, inadequate sanctioning, or insufficient monitoring). The factors of ineffectiveness in environmental policy in Ghana are indicative of a significant failure in governance. There is a crucial study vacuum in comprehending the intricate governance complexity associated with these drivers. Within the realm of environmental conservation, the significance of governance cannot be underestimated. The pollution-haven theory has shown that relaxed environmental regulations in the recipient country might incentivize more FDI from companies seeking to evade expensive regulatory obligations in their home countries [11]. Strong governance and FDI have a vital role in mitigating ecological harm and fostering sustainable long-term growth [12]. The 2023 CCDR report emphasises the significant contribution of FDI in harnessing prospects for green and blue bonds, insurance, and other financial protection mechanisms to mitigate climate and disaster risks in Ghana [5]. A study conducted by Athari [13] affirms that the inflow of FDIs promote the use of renewable energy, which aids to lower CO2 emissions.

The nexus between economic expansion and environmental degradation in different nations has produced varied outcomes. However, notwithstanding these empirical findings, there is a widely accepted agreement that in order to achieve green economic growth, it is essential to have robust environmental legislation and high-quality governance. Acheampong [14] in recent times has examined the effect of de facto economic, political, and social globalization on Ghana's CO2 emissions. In understanding the factors that influence CO2 emissions in Ghana, Gyimah and Yao [15] used GMM and DOLS to investigate the influence of globalisation, and renewable energy on CO2 emissions, and the results indicate that trade openness and population reduce CO2 emission. Kwakwa [16] assessed the CO2 emissions effect of industrialization, government expenditure, and militarization in Ghana as a path to attain lower CO2 emissions. These studies failed to include the crucial variable of FDI along with indices of good governance, which could contribute to more reliable results. Climate neutrality can be attained alongside robust economic expansion if policymakers effectively manage governance efficiency concerns. The quality of governance is ensured when corruption is minimal, the rule of law is consistently and effectively enforced without discrimination, public institutions operate independently from political influences, and are competent and trustworthy in implementing policies. Therefore, this paper will bridge the gaps in prior studies by investigating on how FDI and good governance positively impact national environmental pollution policies in Ghana, which frequently goes unnoticed as a significant aspect in the discourse on environmental quality in Ghana. The conclusions of few research conducted on the nexus between FDI and CO2 emissions have likewise been inconsistent [17] in Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) countries [18]. Nevertheless, proposals to enhance funding structures and foster financial collaboration in order to attain the financial objectives for sustainable green energy initiatives persistently inspire the endeavours of developing nations [19]. Another aspect of the argument posits that the rise in economic expansion has resulted in a rise in the amount of energy that is consumed, hence giving rise to environmental concerns [12].

To our knowledge, no research has been undertaken on the impact of governance quality and FDI to control the level of CO2 emissions in Ghana. This demonstrates the necessity of doing this study to establish and enforce green environmental regulations within the country while fostering economic growth. Based on the reasoning described above, this paper has three motivations: (i) the strong need to achieving carbon neutrality in Ghana, (ii) the relevance of the impact of FDI in Ghana on emissions reduction, and (iii) the need for Ghana to formulate strong environmental policies to reduce emission by carefully examining government's involvement in different policy options. This study aims to reduce the disparity by examining how good governance and FDI in achieving carbon neutrality, either by lessening its negative effects or by enhancing its positive effects. To further explore the moderation impact, the study employs specific subsets of governance indicators that provide more insightful information for policy involvement, as advocated by Omri and Mabrouk [20].

The innovations of the study are as follows; a common limitation in the literature is the broad focus of the inquiry, predominantly centred on developed countries forgetting that most developing countries suffer the consequences from developed countries use of highly pollutant technologies through the effects of governance and economic openness or cross countries research ignoring the degree of pollution in one country may be different in another and therefore results may be ineffective in another. Nevertheless, this study addresses this disparity by examining the situation from the perspective of a country specific research, specifically Ghana. It also showcases the efficacy of governance quality in reducing the adverse impact of emissions while throwing light on the air travel contribution to CO2 emissions in the country. Therefore, this study represents a significant advancement in bridging the existing funding gap between mitigation and adaptation projects. The paper is structured as follows: section 2 contains the literature review; section 3 explains the methodology; section 4 analyses the study's findings and their significance; and section 5 ends with policy recommendations.

Literature review

The nexus between foreign direct investments (FDI) and carbon emissions

Existing research have classified the correlation between FDI and the promotion of low-carbon economic development in host nations. Several researchers contend that FDI inflows have detrimental impacts on developing host nations, as per the Pollution Haven Hypothesis (PHH), commonly referred to as “pollution havens”. The theory, initially formulated by Walter and Ugelow [21] and later improved upon by Baumol and Oates [22], aims to investigate the potential transfer of pollution-intensive businesses to the host nation through FDI, resulting in a rise in local carbon emissions. The Pollution Haven Hypothesis posits that firms that generate high levels of pollution will relocate from nations that internalise the costs of environmental damage to countries with lower degrees of internalisation. Consequently, countries with less stringent environmental requirements become havens for pollution-intensive industries. Another cohort of scholars contend that FDI inflows yield beneficial outcomes for developing countries hosting such investments, including the phenomenon known as the “pollution halo”. The concept of the “pollution halo” effect was first proposed by Birdsall and Wheeler [23]. It specifically examines the positive impact of FDI on the local environment. The “pollution halo” hypothesis posits that FDI introduces state-of-the-art equipment and cutting-edge technology to the recipient nation, leading to resource and factor input savings through technology spill overs and enhancing environmental quality [24]. An alternative perspective posits that FDI does not add to the carbon emissions of the country when it is invested in renewable energy [13], but instead mitigates or reduces them through economic openness [25]. Nie and Liu [26] employed a systematic Generalised Method of Moments (GMM) approach to illustrate that the presence of foreign companies leads to a “pollution halo” effect. They found that cities with less stringent environmental regulations tend to attract a greater number of high-polluting foreign corporations into China. Yu and Xu [27] employed a Panel Corrected Standard Errors (PCSE) model to analyse data from China spanning from 2000 to 2017. Their findings indicate that FDI helps lower CO2 emissions nationwide and in various parts of China. An alternative perspective suggests that the influence of FDI on carbon emissions in the recipient nation is indeterminate, given the multitude of variables that might influence the impact of FDI [28]. These studies failed to focus on developing countries since the primary focus of these research was on the economies of the BRICS and OECD countries.

The nexus between good governance/institutions and carbon emissions

The developmental objectives of a nation, such as achieving a cleaner environment, can be influenced by the quality of its governance [29]. The overarching principle in the literature is that achieving better environmental conditions, such as lower CO2 emissions, necessitates the presence of more effective institutions [30]. High-quality institutions ensure the efficient allocation of public resources by upholding law and order, combating corruption, and safeguarding freedom of speech. The economy experiences an improvement in value for money and a decrease in resource wastage as a consequence [30]. Well-established institutions such as the rule of law, laws, and effective governance compel companies and industries to adhere to environmentally sustainable practices [31] and effectively promote sustainable development [32] leading to a subsequent reduction in CO2 emissions. Zhang, Ozturk [33] report that institutions typically develop policies aimed at mitigating CO2 emissions.

Robust institutions attract investment and greatly facilitate economic expansion. Hence, robust governance, intricately linked to the calibre of institutions, is vital for effectively attracting FDI. The importance of good governance noticeably increased in discussions and studies on global development. Lack of openness is associated with corrupt conduct, which in turn, signifies inadequate leadership. Research has demonstrated the beneficial relationship between good governance and FDI, highlighting the catalytic function of good governance in attracting FDI [34]. Safdar, Khan [35] examined the influence of institutional quality on FDI. The study was conducted using data from 29 developing nations spanning the years 2000–2018. The multiple regression analysis findings indicate that government effectiveness, the rule of law, regulatory quality, and control of corruption have positive and substantial effects on FDI among the six institutional indicators. A subsequent investigation was carried out encompassing 46 African nations spanning the years 2010–2019. The GMM model's findings indicate that economic governance institutions have a substantial beneficial effect on FDI inflows in Africa [36]. Overall, high-quality institutions have the capacity to decrease production costs, foster innovation and incentives, and safeguard state resources in order to achieve a high-quality environment [37].

To summarise, FDI and good governance can boost a country's economic development and help alleviate the impacts of extreme poverty and economic disparity. However, it is crucial to examine FDI's potential negative impact on environmental quality [38] and the role of good governance in achieving carbon neutrality especially on a developing country. Therefore, our work aims to comprehend this correlation in order to enhance the accuracy of policy-making in Ghana. The study delves into the debate that surrounds the development of the financial sector as well as the absence of empirical evidence regarding the important nexus between financial inclusion, governance indicators, and environmental quality. This study investigates how financial inclusion affects climate change and the need of efficient governance in mitigating its consequences. Ultimately, the analysis provides significant policy recommendations for upholding environmental sustainability and attaining financial prosperity in Ghana.

Methodology

In Simon Stiell's speech during the COP28 summit in Dubai, he mentioned that “Developed nations allocated a sum of $83.3 billion, with merely 8 percent of the funds directed towards low-income countries and approximately one-fourth towards Africa, despite the fact that both regions are significantly susceptible to the impacts of climate change”. The largest funding category consisted of loans, primarily allocated to middle-income countries. Amidst the challenges of high public debt burdens, developing countries are confronted with difficult decisions, such as allocating resources between climate adaption and enhancing vital public services, while simultaneously seeing a rise in investment costs. This study aims to provide empirical evidence for occurrences that occurred before and after COP28.

Dependent variable

Within governmental and scholarly spheres, there is growing emphasis placed on the issue of achieving carbon neutrality, particularly in light of the COP28 reports which seeks to achieve a global reduction in CO2 emissions due to its detrimental impact on the earth's atmosphere, which is crucial to the expansion of policy development. Therefore, our research employs CO2 emissions as an indicator of environmental degradation in order to gain a deeper knowledge on how FDI and governance quality in Ghana can potentially reduce this problem.

Independent variables

As stated in the initial section, the analysis incorporates two independent variables to determine the impact of CO2 emissions. First, FDI served as a proxy for economic openness [44]. This is because, FDI is considered a crucial component of economic growth of a country [45,46]. The information on these indicators is gathered from the WDI database. According to the available literature, we anticipate that FDI can lower the level of CO2 emissions [18]. Second, the model incorporates six governance indicators, namely voice and accountability (VA), political stability (PS), governance effectiveness (GE), regulatory quality (RQ), rule of law (RL), and control of corruption (CC), as policy variables. These indicators work alongside foreign direct investment to effectively decrease CO2 emissions. According to Ofori, Onifade [12], these indicators can be categorised into three classes: political governance (voice and accountability, political stability), economic governance (governance effectiveness, regulatory quality), and institutional governance (control of corruption, rule of law). Grouping of these WGI aids to investigate its individual effects on the dependent variable. The information on these indicators were gathered from the WGI web database. According to Omri and Hadj [47], good governance is anticipated to reduce CO2 emissions.

Control variables

The model incorporates additional factors that influence CO2 emissions, such as GDP per capita (GDP), as well as air travel passengers and freights (ATP and ATF) correspondingly. The measurement of GDP per capita is denoted in constant 2010 US dollars. The information on these indicators were gathered from the WDI web database. These variables are anticipated to contribute to the escalation of CO2 emissions [48].

Econometric modelling and procedures

We present a model that assesses the influence of several elements connected to FDI and governance quality, alongside other variables that serve as controls. We used the time series model that was proposed by Omri et al. [49] and then convert it into a panel data model so that it can be relevant to our investigation. Initial tests were carried out in order to guarantee the longevity of our data and the framework of our model systems. In order to ascertain if there was enough ground for analysis in the chosen data, we utilized a sample adequacy test to assess their reliability and applicability. We used a scree plot to visually evaluate the normal distribution of the data. We performed a CD test to determine if there was any cross-sectional dependence among the variables. The CD test examines the spill-over effect. We utilized second-generation unit root testing. Using the first-generation panel unit root technique can lead to imprecise predictions. The Westerlund cointegration approach was used as a result. The latter intended to assess the reliability of long-term predictions. Cointegration examines the possibility of a sustained connection between variables over time. If a high correlation coefficient exists between the independent and dependent variables, multicollinearity issues may arise in the equation and typically, when the variance inflation factor (VIF) exceeds 10, it indicates a significant presence of multicollinearity [50]. Consequently, we created a correlation matrix to examine this inadequacy. Following thorough preliminary testing, we conducted long-term parameter calculations. The Feasible Generalised Least Square (FGLS) technique was subsequently employed to evaluate the central hypothesis. FGLS technique was undertaken with the recognition that it addresses heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation in panel data and is suitable for running long run analysis if the time dimension is greater than the number of cross sections. We utilized the Method of Moments Quantile Regression (MMQR). This enables the identification of the conditional heterogeneous covariance effects of the factors influencing CO2 emissions by allowing individual variables to impact the entire distribution. The MMQR estimation technique is especially applicable in situations where the panel data model includes individual effects and when the model has endogenous explanatory variables, which provides non-crossing estimates of the regression quantiles.

Long run empirical analysis

The study analysed the correlation between FDI, governance quality, and CO2 emissions using eleven (11) groups (thus, CO2, FDI, GE, RQ, RL, CC, VA, PS, GDP, ATP, ATF), which are log-transformed. The values can be seen as elasticities or percentages as shown in Table 7. Examining the influence of governance indicators on environmental pollution involves breaking down the indicators into economic, institutional, and political components.

Table 7 presents several intriguing discoveries. Initial findings indicate that FDI has a negative impact on the environmental pollution. It suggests that a rise in FDI by 0.05%–0.07 % with a significant threshold of 1 % decreases the level of CO2 emissions. This strand of evidence supports previous studies [52,53], affirming that FDI does not lead to an increase in CO2 emissions in the host country; instead, it reduces them. Li, Liu [54] also discovered that FDI has a “pollution halo” in developing countries. The evidence threads corroborate our findings. We argue that FDI results in more efficient use of resources and better environmental quality as a result of technological spill-overs, leading to resource savings and improved environmental quality through technology spill-overs. This evidence contradicts the pollution haven hypothesis and previous findings [12,49], indicating that FDI will contribute to the escalation of environmental pollution.

The second set of empirical data shows that economic governance worsens the environmental quality. It reveals that an increase in poor regulations by 0.03 %–0.04 % at a 5 % significance level leads to environmental pollution. Similarly, ineffective governance leads to environmental pollution by 0.01 %–0.02 % at 5 % significance level. This evidence reveals that in Ghana, the level of governance effectiveness and regulation quality is low to have a significant influence to attract FDI from foreign investors to aid in reducing CO2 emissions and possibly the reason behind the decline in FDI inflow in 2023 as compared to 2020. This study corroborates the research by Samimi, Ahmadpour [55] which establishes a connection between environmental degradation and inadequate economic governance as factors leading to ecological mismanagement and its associated calamities. The result contradicts with works of Omri and Hadj [47] affirming that economic governance decreases CO2 emissions through FDI inflows.

The third set of empirical data shows that institutional governance helps in reducing CO2 emissions by 0.032 %–0.033 % at a significance level of 1 %, through the enforcement of rule of law and controlling corruption by 0.04 %–0.14 % at a significance level of 5 %. This implies that in Ghana, FDI can aid in lowering CO2 emissions if rule of law is enforced and corruption is controlled in Ghana. This evidence aligns with Omri and Hadj [47] and Wang [56], indicating that improving institutional quality can reduce CO2 emissions and enhance environmental quality by establishing robust regulations and legislation to combat corruption [12]. This study does not support evidence by Sabir, Qayyum [57] through the use of ARDL technique, affirming that the presence of strong legal frameworks, as an indicator of institutional quality, does not lower the level of CO2 emissions in both the short and long run.

The fourth strand of empirical data indicates that political governance reduces CO2 emissions when voice and accountability and political stability are prioritized in a country's governance structure. Similarly, inclusion of voice and accountability in a country's governance structure by 0.20%–0.22 % at a 1 % significance level decrease the level of CO2 emissions, however, political stability did not have a meaningful impact. This evidence shows that in Ghana, FDI can help to lower CO2 emissions when voice and accountability are upheld in Ghana. This outcome aligns with research indicating that political stability [58] and voice and accountability [12] mechanisms in a country can reduce emissions levels.

The results for the control variables show that air travel and GDP have a positive impact on CO2 emissions. An increase in air travel by 0.015 %–0.019 % at a significance level of 5 %, and GDP by 0.050 %–0.054 % at a significance level of 1 % are projected to result in a rise in CO2 emissions. This discovery highlights the negative environmental impact of air travel in Ghana, a fact that has been confirmed by numerous existing empirical research [59,60]. The results of GDP align with research conducted by Ali, Shayanmehr [61] and Radmehr, Shayanmehr [62]. Furthermore, the results of air transport freight align with the research by Johansson, Vierth [63], whereas the findings related to air transport passengers are in line with the study conducted by Woldeyohannes Yirgu and Kim [64]. These results reveal that Ghana places more emphasis on its economic growth with less drive to improve on its ecological quality.

This relationship is significant across the 1st to 9th quantiles, but is particularly pronounced at the lower quantiles (1st, 2nd, and 3rd). However, the effect is particularly pronounced at higher quantiles, specifically the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd quantiles. Conclusions

This study examines the interplay between FDI and good governance in Ghana, specifically focusing on environmental factors. The environmental effects were analysed using robust methods applied to a panel dataset spanning from 2000 to 2022. The findings of the study are summarised into four primary elements. The first set of evidence from this study suggest that FDI contributes to the mitigation of CO2 emissions in Ghana. The second set of evidence confirms that the level of governance effectiveness and regulation quality is low to have a significant influence to attract FDI from foreign investors to aid in reducing CO2 emissions. The third set of evidence demonstrates that FDI can lower CO2 emissions through a strict enforcement of the rule of law and effective control of corruption making it difficult for companies to introduce highly polluting technologies in Ghana through bribery and influencing environmental regulatory bodies. The fourth line of evidence also suggests that FDI can lower CO2 emissions in Ghana, when there is a system in place to ensure voice and accountability, thus, individuals and companies that contribute to the rise in CO2 emissions will be held responsible and subjected to appropriate sanctions. Finally, the findings of this study align with existing scientific literature that confirms that Ghana's GDP and air travel sector are rapidly increasing while exerting negative impacts on the country's ecological well-being. These results add up to an important contribution in achieving carbon neutrality in Ghana, and by extension, to other developing countries through a significant impact of FDI, strengthening of the institutional system, implementing standards, empowering supervision agencies, and creating an effective regulatory environment.

Practical policy recommendations

The results clearly indicate pertinent recommendations for policymakers in Ghana. It is imperative to acknowledge that the influence of FDI and effective governance carries significant importance in policy. FDIs from Ghana's leading foreign investors like China and the United Kingdom are not only necessary for promoting Ghana's economic development, but also for mitigating CO2 emissions by adopting environmentally friendly technologies. Hence, it is imperative to maintain political stability, voice and accountability, controlled corruption, and enforced rule of law to attract FDI in order to achieve carbon neutrality through the implementation of green environmental policies and standards that are advantageous to both countries, while simultaneously enhancing Ghana's ecological quality. This will serve to ultimately pave the way for Ghana to achieve carbon neutrality. Hence, it is imperative for policymakers to prioritise the integration of FDI projects into national environmental policies.

Furthermore, the absence of stringent environmental regulations in the country promotes the establishment of investment havens that have detrimental effects on FDI to aid in lowering CO2 emissions in the country. Hence, it is imperative for policymakers to reassess Ghana's trade regulations and Emissions levy Act of Ghana, 2023 (Act 1112) in order to establish green policies that prohibit developed nations and individuals from introducing highly polluting technologies into the country. In addition, the 2024 national budget and economic policy presented by the Ministry of Finance of Ghana intend to boost FDI but throws more light on the economic development order than achieving carbon neutrality through FDI. Therefore, individuals with the right and requisite knowledge on green policy making and its related issues should be put in office to help Ghana achieve its carbon neutrality through financial inclusion. Several economic programmes like “The Year of Return” and “Beyond the Return” tourism initiatives have been implemented to boost Ghana's economy, there is a pressing requirement to establish ecological quality campaigns and initiatives. The government should allocate funds for research works aimed at examining the most effective ways in which air travel may simultaneously enhance the ecological quality and economic development of the country.

Study limitations and future recommendations

This study seeks to analyse several sustainability variables; however, it faces challenges. This analysis employs data from the period 2000 to 2020. Subsequent studies may broaden its reach if additional data becomes accessible. This study aims to fill a gap in knowledge by investigating control variables such as air travel and GDP. Future research could consider exploring additional control variables, such as trade openness, to broaden the scope of the study. This study aims to fill a knowledge gap by investigating the impact of FDI. Future research could further explore other independent variables, such as domestic credit to the private sector and private credit provided by deposit money banks and other financial institutions, to address issues related to CO2 emissions.
