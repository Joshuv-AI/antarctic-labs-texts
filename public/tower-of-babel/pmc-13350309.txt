Acute pain transfusion reaction in a patient with thalassemia: In‐depth characterization of short‐ and long‐term phenotypes
Authors: Georgia Tzafa, Sophia Delicou, Ioanna Barla, Konstantina Theocharaki, Aggeliki Kondi, Athanassios D Velentzas, Nikolaos Simantiris, Elli‐Anna Stylianaki, George Mpekoulis, Efthymia Pavlou, Niki Vassilaki, Ioannis V Kostopoulos, Aikaterini Xydaki, Efrosyni Nomikou, Ioanna‐Katerina Aggeli, Nikolaos Thomaidis, Evangelos Gikas, Martina Samiotaki, Jerome Zoidakis, Constantina Politis, Marianna H Antonelou

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pain transfusion reaction (APTR) is a rare, under‐recognized condition of unknown etiology. It can cause significant distress in recipients, necessitating symptomatic management and, occasionally, hospitalization.Study Design and MethodsHere, we present an APTR event in an adult subject with transfusion‐dependent thalassemia (TDT). Patient and returned red blood cell (RBC) unit samples from this one, and additionally from preceding and subsequent transfusions, were compared with patient and unit reference groups, matched for genetic profile and storage duration, respectively, using laboratory, morphological, and multi‐omics approaches.ResultsThe patient had a pre‐transfusion hemoglobin concentration of 10.7 g/dL and evidence of coagulation and fibrinolysis. Following APTR, the patient RBCs showed acute oxidative stress, increased adhesiveness, phosphatidylserine exposure, and membrane‐specific proteostatic failure. Multi‐omics analyses revealed overexpression of biological response modifiers, like gamma‐aminobutyric acid derivatives, RANTES/DARC, and ongoing ephrin‐B reverse signaling. The implicated RBC unit displayed advanced storage lesions with overexpression of ophthalmate and STEAP3, among others, metabolic aging not matching its “chronological age”, and differential expression of potential immune mediators and neuromodulators. Functional enrichment analysis linked the differential proteome profile of the unit with RBC dysfunction and neuropathy. APTR‐related pathologies showed only partial resolution over a two‐week period, indicating sustained disturbances in proteostasis and hypercoagulability, as evidenced, among other markers, by thrombospondin binding to patient RBCs.DiscussionThis study identified a series of patient and RBC unit stress markers and conditions potentially linked to APTR. These hypothesis‐generating variables and molecular signatures may facilitate prospective evaluation and mechanistic investigation of suspected APTR cases.Keywords: acute pain transfusion reaction, biological response modifiers, RBC storage lesion, transfusion‐associated adverse reactions, transfusion‐dependent thalassemia

INTRODUCTION

Blood transfusion is a cornerstone of care, and mainstay therapy for thalassemia patients with severe anemia. Despite advances in storage strategies, extended red blood cell (RBC) typing, 1 and patient blood management, transfusion still carries cumulative risks in patients with transfusion‐dependent thalassemia (TDT). 2 The most serious is secondary iron overload 3 which can cause organ damage to the heart, liver, bones and endocrine system. 4 Apart from this, alloimmunization, allergic and febrile reactions, and other transfusion‐associated adverse reactions (TAARs) occur in nearly half of patients with TDT. 5 TAARs are related to transfusion of whole blood or blood components, 5 and can range in severity from asymptomatic to life‐threatening. They may be infectious or noninfectious, immunologic or non‐immunologic, and either acute (during or shortly after transfusion) or delayed. In the field of TDT, the most common TAARs are unspecified allergic reactions (52%) and febrile events (16%), while anaphylactic, hypotensive, and hemolytic reactions each occur in fewer than 5% of cases. 6

First described in 2001, acute pain transfusion reactions (APTRs) have gained growing attention in recent years. 7 They are characterized by sudden onset of mild to severe pain in the back, chest, joints, or trunk, 8 typically arising during or shortly after transfusion. 9 The existing literature reports no APTRs occurring with/after another TAAR, making APTR an isolated condition. 7 Reported incidence rates range from 0.02% of RBC units 10 to 0.04% of transfusions and account for approximately 8.2% of all TAARs. 11 However, actual frequency is likely underestimated due to underreporting. 7 , 12

Despite their rarity, APTRs have been observed across diverse patient populations (with hemoglobinopathies, solid malignancies, etc.) and in multiple transfusion settings (leukoreduced, 13 non‐leukoreduced, 14 irradiated, nonirradiated blood components 15 ). Interestingly, some APTRs have been linked specifically to platelet transfusions, suggesting a potential component‐specific pathophysiology. 13 The underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, although pain is likely multifactorial, involving donor, component, and recipient‐related factors, such as disease context, comorbidities, and medication. APTRs have been reported more frequently in outpatients, following preventive premedication, and after transfusion of leukoreduced components prepared using specific filters, 10 suggesting potential component‐related risk factors, such as cytokines or other immunomodulators. 10 , 13 , 15 Hemovigilance systems are responsible for monitoring TAARs irrespective of severity. 16 Nevertheless, APTRs are often reported as “unclassified” once other TAARs are excluded, making APTR a diagnosis of exclusion. 7 It has been proposed that incorporating respiratory rate and pain assessment into routine vital signs monitoring could improve recognition and reporting. 17

Here, we report an APTR event in the TDT context, investigated by electron microscopy, multi‐omics, and other approaches. This exploratory, though unique in the field of APTR research study intended to establish a broad framework of potential candidate risk factors or correlations for APTR.

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

Patient profile, APTR event, and study design

The subject was an adult female splenectomized patient with TDT, with secondary hemochromatosis, hypothyroidism, osteoporosis, atrial fibrillation, and depression, but no history of TAARs. She was routinely transfused with complete phenotype‐matched RBC units and had chronically increased D‐dimers (T − 1, Table 1). On APTR day, she received an RBC unit without incident; however, shortly after initiation of a second unit, moderate pain appeared in the neck and lower back. The transfusion was paused, and the pain resolved shortly thereafter. Corticosteroids and intravenous fluids were administered during the acute phase of the reaction; however, systemic corticosteroid therapy was not continued in the following days. Posttransfusion clinical/laboratory analysis ruled out other TAARs. Fifteen days later, she was transfused with two units that were washed precautionarily.

Three transfusion events were studied for this patient: T − 1 (preceding APTR), T0 (APTR), and T + 1 (15 days later) (Figure 1). Pre/posttransfusion samples were analyzed in comparison with a reference group of 10 patients matched for sex, age (48.8 ± 7.3 years), spleen status (splenectomized), HBB mutations (IVS‐I‐110/IVS‐I‐110), and transfusion/chelation regimes. RBC units were analyzed alongside a matched reference group of 14 units, identical in processing, leukoreduction filter, and storage duration (16.9 ± 5.0 days) to the APTR unit. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the competent Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No 65/081121). Additional data for the methodology are provided in the Supporting Information.

Study design. Three transfusion events were studied for the same patient: T − 1 (preceding the acute pain transfusion reaction [APTR] event), T0 (during which the APTR occurred), and T + 1 (15 days after APTR). Pre‐ and posttransfusion blood samples were collected from the patient at T − 1 and T + 1; only posttransfusion samples were available at T0. The returned RBC units were also studied. Both patient and RBC unit samples were analyzed compared to matching reference groups.

Biochemical and laboratory analyses

Clinical laboratory analyses and hemolysis (baseline, osmotic, oxidative) were performed as previously reported. 18 , 19 For scanning electron microscopy, purified RBCs were fixed (2% glutaraldehyde, 1% osmium tetroxide; Serva, Heidelberg, Germany) and dehydrated in ascending ethanol series, before coating with gold–palladium. 20 Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS; 10 μM 5‐ (and‐6)‐chloromethyl‐2′,7′‐dichloro‐dihydro‐fluorescein diacetate; Invitrogen, Molecular Probes), 21 proteolytic proteasome activity (PPA), and phosphatidylserine (PS) exposure (PE‐annexin V, FITC‐anti‐CD235, BD Biosciences) 20 were detected by fluorometry and flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II Clinical Flow Cytometry System). PPA was estimated by using fluorogenic substrates specific for chymotrypsin‐like (CH‐like), caspase‐like (CASP‐like), and trypsin‐like (TR‐like) activities. 19 , 22 Plasma extracellular vesicles (EVs) (Exo‐Prep, Hansa BioMed, Estonia) were measured at a Nanosight NS300 (Panalytical, Malvern, UK). 19 Blood leukoreduction was performed in cellulose columns, 23 and membrane/cytosol fractions were isolated by hypotonic lysis (19,000xg at 4oC), in the presence of protease inhibitors. Membrane‐bound hemichromes were assessed by the method of Ferru et al. 24

For the proteomic analysis, the samples were digested using a modified SP3 clean‐up protocol. 25 Peptides were analyzed by LC–MS/MS on a Dionex Ultimate RSLC system coupled to a Thermo Q Exactive/HF‐X Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Orbitrap raw data were processed with DIA‐NN (Data‐Independent Acquisition by Neural Networks) against the canonical UniProt Human databases. Metabolites were extracted using a standard protocol, 26 and analyzed on a timsTOF‐Pro‐2 mass spectrometer coupled to an Elute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a VIP‐HESI ionization source operated in positive ion mode. Data were acquired in data‐dependent acquisition mode. Metabolites were identified using an in‐house MS/MS spectral library (MSMLS, IROA Technologies), and data processing was performed with TASQ 2025 software (Bruker Daltonics). 27

Statistics, bioinformatics, and reference groups

All biological measurements were performed in triplicate. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. APTR‐related samples were compared to the mean values of reference TDT patients and RBC units using one‐sample t‐test and Wilcoxon rank sum test, as appropriate. For metabolomics, fold changes were calculated relative to the patient's own T − 1 (pre‐ and posttransfusion) samples (3 technical replicates per time point) and three matched RBC units of the same processing and storage duration. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (ΙΒΜ Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA accessed on NKUA), GraphPad Prism 6, MATLAB R2021a, and Metascape (functional gene enrichment). 28

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

APTR patient

Subjects with TDT are pain sensitized. 29 While transfusion alleviates pain, the pre‐transfusion period represents a particularly vulnerable window. 30 Age > 30 and high pre‐transfusion Hb further increase pain risk/severity. 29 , 30 The 50‐year‐old patient under evaluation presented with a higher pre‐transfusion Hb concentration compared to the usual 19 and reference group (Table 1). On the day before the APTR, bilirubin and hsCRP (2.39 mg/L) were normal, in contrast to red cell distribution width (RDW), plasma fibrinogen, D‐dimer, and clotting times (Table 1).

Immediately following the APTR she showed a marked Hb increase due to transfusion of the first unit (Figure 2B). RDW and NRBC also increased, but fibrinogen dropped sharply (Table 1). Elevated cell‐free Hb and RBC osmotic hemolysis were observed, along with marked oxidative stress (ROS, d 23.21; hemichromes), and PS exposure (Figure 2), suggesting eryptosis. Membrane‐associated CH‐like proteasome activity was low (d 1.20), despite normal cytosolic levels, reflecting limited access to the membrane or oxidation‐induced inhibition. Poor proteasome activity could compromise not only the degradation of excess globin chains, 31 but also the production of Hb‐derived hemorphins 32 and other RBC‐based morphinomimetic analgesic peptides that act as opioid receptor ligands or GABA release inhibitors. 33 Importantly, pain and peripheral neuropathy are known side effects in cancer patients receiving proteasome inhibitors. 34

At the morphological level, there was echinocytosis and aggregation (Figure 3A). RBC membrane proteomics showed several biological response modifiers (BRMs) (Figure 3B) involved in coagulation, fatty acid metabolism, and protein activation (Figure 3C), including the chemokine RANTES (Figure 3D), FAS receptor (associated with eryptosis 21 and NF‐kB activation), and plasma‐derived ADAM9 (a stress‐responsive peptidase that enhances chemotaxis 35 ). RBCs can bind and release soluble immune mediators, 36 including those contributing to hyperalgesia. 37 RANTES interacts with the receptor DARC (ACKR1), 38 and both proteins were upregulated in the APTR (T0) (Figure 3B) and T + 1 RBC proteomes (Figures S2 and S4). Conversely, chaperones, proteasomal subunits, fatty acid synthase, immunoglobulins, and fibrinogen chains were downregulated—consistent with plasma levels (Table 1). Following enrichment analysis, ephrin‐B reverse signaling, that is linked to neuronal excitability, emerged as the top pathway (Figure 3E).

Targeted metabolomics analysis (Figure S1A) identified intracellular differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) absent in T − 1 metabolomes, including γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) derivatives (eg, 4‐acetamidobutanoate) previously linked to neuropathic pain. Other DEMs are also potentially neuroactive through modulation of ischemia or inhibitory tone in the neuro‐immune crosstalk (eg, methyl‐GABA). Moreover, the low citicoline (CDP‐choline, FC <0.35) levels in APTR RBCs imply altered cholinergic anti‐inflammatory signaling, mirroring defects observed in the transfused APTR RBCs (Figure 5A). RBCs respond to/interfere with the metabolism of stimulatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters. 39 It is worth noting that RBC cholinergic receptors share structural features with brain muscarinic receptors, 40 and that, although GABA receptors are not present, RBC band 3 shows significant structural similarities to them. 41 The APTR RBCs were further depleted in metabolites involved in NO regulation, methyl donation, and energy/redox metabolism.

Plasma DEMs (Figure S1B) included amino acids, neurotropic B vitamins (eg, thiamine), and metabolites related to energy imbalance (e.g., inosine monophosphate—IMP; carnitines), immune activation (e.g., kynurenine‐like purine breakdown profile, elevated deoxyadenosine and IMP), and neurotransmission (glutamine/glutamate, normetanephrine, and arginine implicated in glutamatergic and catecholaminergic pain signaling).

Characteristics of the RBC unit involved in the APTR event

The 16‐day old APTR‐implicated unit was donated by a young female. Compared to reference, it showed low Hb and elevated RDW (Table 2). Despite low storage hemolysis, it had increased storage lesions (e.g., osmotic/mechanical hemolysis, intracellular ROS, PS exposure, low membrane‐located CASP‐like PPA). Advanced echinocytosis (grade IV) and sphero‐echinocytosis were present—atypical for this storage duration (Figure 4A).

A total of 259 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified by proteomics (top shown in Figure 4B), including the “unique” chemokine receptor DARC and the ferrireductase STEAP3, a promoter of ferroptosis‐like phenotypes in stored cells. 42 Interestingly, STEAP3 polymorphisms in blood donors have been linked to poor RBC storage quality with lipid peroxidation, 43 consistent with the redox imbalance phenotypes of APTR units. Moreover, GPX4, a detoxifier of phospholipid hydroperoxides and negative regulator of storage lesions and ferroptosis 44 , 45 was decreased (d − 0.81; p 0.02), accompanying the ferroptosis‐like signatures in these cells and predisposing to adverse transfusion outcomes. Variations in GPX4, STEAP3, and ACKR1 have been associated with osmotic hemolysis (or genetic regulators of it) in stored RBCs, 43 , 45 as observed in APTR units, in which the increased levels of GPX1 (d 1.62; p 0.01; Figure 4C) might be compensatory. 45

Adenosine monophosphate deaminase AMPD3 (another storage lesion marker 46 ), alpha synuclein, and ICAM4 were also differentially expressed (Figure 4C), in addition to DEPs absorbed from plasma, such as ADAM9 and alpha‐2‐macroglobulin (A2M), a protease and potential cytokine transporter. 47 The overexpressed CD36 and CD38 interact with platelets and endothelium, 48 and the PIP5K1C kinase is involved in neutrophil chemotaxis. Molecular chaperones and glycophorin C were downregulated, reflecting the increased mechanical hemolysis (Table 2). DisGeNET analysis revealed neuropathy‐associated gene sets significantly enriched among the DEPs (Figure 4D).

Following metabolomics analysis (Figure 5A), the set of intracellular DEMs pointed to (1) severe storage lesion and aging (e.g., adenine, carnitine, glutamine, 5‐oxoproline 49 ; ophthalmate); (2) impaired membrane and nucleotide repair (depletion of cytidine, citicoline); and (3) immune‐ and neuro‐modulation, including the unique presence of 2‐quinolinecarboxylate.

Both ophthalmate and carnitine are metabolic clocks for RBC aging and biomarkers of impaired redox buffering. 50 , 51 Genetically regulated low carnitine levels in old stored RBCs have been related to worse storability with membrane vesiculation and lipid peroxidation, as a result of defected repair of oxidatively damaged lipids, 51 linking thus disturbances in carnitine metabolism with the ferroptosis‐like phenotypes of the APTR unit. Whether carnitine supplementation (in either unit additives or TDT patient) may affect APTR susceptibility is a worth testing hypothesis for future investigation. Excess adenine points to impaired nucleotide salvage or utilization and ongoing nucleotide breakdown, that is a hallmark of late‐stage storage lesion, 52 consistent with the morphological profile of these cells. 2‐quinolinecarboxylate is a kynurenine pathway intermediate and potential NMDA receptor antagonist, while kynurenine is a donor‐specific marker of osmotic hemolysis in stored RBCs 53 (see Table 2) and a pain sensitizer. Together with the depletion of anti‐inflammatory, neuroprotective factors (e.g., GABA, trigonelline 54 ) these DEMs may contribute to pain sensitization. Indeed, RBC absorb tryptophan and kynurenine from plasma, 53 and there is evidence that alterations in kynurenine metabolism in RBC mediate excitatory pathophysiological processes in neurodegenerative disorders. 55 These findings suggest that the transfused RBCs were in a metabolically “primed” state, which may be clinically relevant, as activation of signaling pathways may require only a small fraction of efficiently primed cells.

The extracellular (supernatant) BRMs (Figure 5B) also reflected advanced storage lesion (depletion of carnitine, etc.), 49 with additional overexpression of BRMs, such as adenine (FC >3.50; a potential activator of purinergic receptors 56 ), 3‐methoxytyramine (dopamine receptor agonist), and methyl 4‐aminobutyrate (potential disruptor of GABAergic inhibitory signaling 57 ). In contrast, epinephrine—a known anti‐inflammatory mediator 58 —was at low levels, potentially contributing to recipient sensitization and neuroinflammation posttransfusion. Of note, several metabolites of the APTR‐related unit (eg, intracellular ophthalmate; supernatant adenine, deoxyadenosine) were present at concentrations substantially higher (>10 fold) than those in the patient's RBCs or plasma, thereby partially mitigating dilution effects.

The subsequent (T + 1) transfusion

15 days later, the patient showed a sharp rise in D‐Dimers compared to T0 (Table 1). While basal hemolysis, ROS, and PS exposure returned to reference, osmotic and oxidative hemolysis, in addition to hemichrome burden, remained elevated or worsened, whereas plasma EV levels rose (Figure 2). These findings suggest that despite partial recovery, the patient remained in a stress‐primed state.

Nearly one‐third of the identified proteins (230/680) exhibited significant variation from the reference group. ADAM9 was no longer detected in RBCs but RANTES/DARC persisted at elevated levels (Figure S2B). Unlike T − 1 (Figure S2A,C), most DEPs are now related to protein degradation (including proteasome) pathways (Figure S2D). Downregulated chaperones and proteasome subunits aligned with the PPA levels (Figure 2) and suggested proteostasis disruption as a long‐term APTR effect. Additionally, the presence of the thrombospondin (THBS1)—absent in T0 RBCs—reflects emerging hypercoagulability, in accordance with the observed increase in EVs (Figure 2) and D‐Dimers (Table 1). Binding of thrombospondin to the RBC membrane—is not unexpected. There is evidence that under thrombo‐inflammatory conditions RBCs bind fibrinogen, thrombospondin, and complement, acting as procoagulant scaffolds. 59 Thrombospondin specifically interacts with CD47 and band 3, influencing RBC deformability, adhesion, and NO signaling. 60

RBCs and plasma metabolite profiles (Figure S1A) differed markedly from T − 1, retaining several APTR‐like features in amino acids, hexosamine, and purine metabolism. Intracellular methyl‐GABA rose sharply, however, indicating recovery of GABAergic inhibitory signaling, which is important for pain suppression. Plasma arginine increased, and normetanephrine returned to T − 1 pre‐transfusion range (Figure S1B).

RBC units delivered in T + 1 transfusion

While storage, osmotic, and mechanical hemolysis were within reference ranges in T + 1 units, oxidative hemolysis, membrane‐bound hemichromes, PS+ RBCs, and PPA were elevated (Table 2). These distortions likely resulted from the combined impact of 16‐day storage and washing, which can induce mechanical stress and depletion of antioxidants. 61 With the exception of ADAM9 binding, APTR unit‐associated proteomic storage lesion markers (e.g. STEAP3) and DARC were absent (Figure S3A). MFSD2B transporter that exports sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P) to plasma was upregulated. 62 Enrichment analysis linked DEPs to purine/pyridine metabolism, neutrophil degranulation, and hemostasis (Figure S3B).

Metabolic disruptions in these units (e.g. low 5‐aminopentanoate extracellularly) (Figure 5) were consistent with the metabolic reprogramming at this storage age, and the mildly elevated clearance signaling and oxidative hemolysis (Table 2). However, the metabolic storage lesion was also less severe compared to the APTR unit (eg, reference or reduced levels of ophthalmate and adenine 49 ) while potentially anti‐inflammatory or inhibitory neurotransmission modulators (e.g., GABA) that were depleted in APTR unit RBCs were at reference or elevated levels. Intracellular carnitine was mildly reduced but less than in APTR RBCs, in line with improved hemolysis and ROS levels (Table 2). Epinephrine was now reduced (Figure 5A), implying lower catecholaminergic signaling interactions between these RBCs and vascular endothelium or immune cells. The supernatant (Figure 5B) also showed reference‐ or reduced levels of potential BRMs compared to the APTR unit.

Patient response to T + 1 transfusion

Posttransfusion, a typical ΔHb 63 was observed (Figure 2). NRBCs increased compared with baseline, and D‐Dimers remained elevated (Table 1). Oxidative hemolysis normalized, but hemichrome burden persisted, and membrane PPA increased above reference (Figure 2). The updated membrane proteome (Figure S4B) shared APTR features (e.g., DARC); however, peroxiredoxin‐2 increased, and potential pro‐inflammatory components (eg, S100A protein 64 ) and hypoxanthine transporter tended to normal. Thrombospondin binding persisted, as seen pre‐transfusion. Functional enrichment analysis linked DEPs to membrane organization and 20S proteasome activities (Figure S4D).

Metabolomics analysis (Figure S1A) revealed restoration of several RBC DEMs including GABA‐related compounds, glutamine, and normetanephrine in RBCs. Finally, patient plasma (Figure S1B) resembled the reference profile more closely, showing improved arginine levels, reduced oxidative stress markers, and normalized components involved in immune reactions or neurotransmission (eg, normetanephrine, glutamine, IMP).

CONCLUSIONS

Higher than typical 63 pre‐transfusion Hb, erythropoietic stress, a procoagulant state, and RBC defects (e.g., removal signaling, aggregation) define the context of this APTR. The overexpressed or downregulated BRMs were functionally enriched in cell activation and neuronal excitability pathways, while disturbances in proteostasis, redox state, and coagulation (as evidenced by thrombospondin binding to patient RBCs) were also observed 2 weeks post‐APTR, suggesting a prolonged thrombo‐inflammatory state. It is tempting to suggest that defects in hemostasis mirror neurovascular coupling, where the nervous and vascular systems interact (during development and beyond) through shared signaling molecules like semaphorins and ephrins. 65

The implicated RBC unit showed severe storage lesions, metabolic aging and ferroptosis‐like phenotypes that did not match its “chronological age”. It is worth noting that, despite guidelines for the management of patients with TDT (namely, prioritization of RBC units better matched to the recipient over storage duration, and first‐in first‐out principle), many thalassemia centers often prefer units that are less than 14 days old. Thus, in a cohort practically receiving younger units, the presence of primed RBCs may be relevant to transfusion‐related changes and reactions, despite dilution. 66 , 67 The NIH‐funded REDS‐IV‐P (RBC‐IMPACT) study 68 is expected to address this issue in chronically transfused patients.

However, the low‐Hb, APTR‐implicated unit appeared to be in a metabolically “primed” state beyond the typical storage lesion, potentially contributing to recipient neuroexcitability. Indeed, it overexpressed stress‐related DEPs/DEMs (e.g., ADAM9, alpha‐2‐macroglobulin, ICAM4), as well as immune or neuromodulatory factors, while downregulating anti‐inflammatory or inhibitory neurotransmission modulators. Accordingly, DisGeNET analysis revealed significant enrichment of DEPs in gene sets related to neuropathy, in contrast to DEPs identified in RBC units from the flanking transfusions and reference events.

More likely, TAARs (like transfusion effectiveness 19 , 63 ) represent the overall outcome of the biological interaction between the donor/blood product and the recipient's biology, and combination of conditionally relevant risk factors, which, in the case of APTR, may contribute to posttransfusion pain sensitization in the recipient. While causality cannot be inferred from a single case, these candidate mediators are hypothesis‐generating and merit further investigation as correlates of APTR risk and severity, potential predictive factors, or contributors to APTR pathophysiology (Table 3). Given evidence that female donor units may offer benefits (e.g., rheology, storability) but also carry immunomodulatory and thrombo‐inflammatory activity 69 , 70 , 71 —and sex‐based differences in thalassemia phenotypes 19 , 72 and transfusion outcomes 63 —the impact of donor–recipient sex matching on APTR warrants further investigation in thalassemia context, particularly under specific product‐related risk factors.

Elucidating the mechanistic basis of APTRs associated with RBC transfusions likely requires recognizing RBCs as active signaling entities with diverse systemic roles beyond oxygen transport, including vasodilation, thrombosis, 59 purinergic signaling, neurotransmitter interaction, and immunomodulation. At first, several RBC components can act as danger signals and may influence nervous system signaling, contributing to persistent pain and neuroinflammation. For example, cell‐free heme activates spinal glia and neural pain circuits via TLR4 and oxidative stress, causing acute and chronic pain, as shown in animal models of SCD. 73 Similarly, ATP released from RBCs (as in many other cells) acts on purinergic receptors on nociceptors to trigger pain signals. 74

Apart from this, a growing body of evidence indicates that circulating RBCs can bind, uptake, and release a range of BRMs and potential neuroactive molecules (e.g., cytokines, 36 nucleic acids, 75 kynurenine, heme catabolism products 39 ). Exposure or release of these BRMs via RBC surface receptors (adrenergic, cholinergic, glutamatergic), membrane transporters (e.g., for dopamine), hemolysis, erythrocrine activity or membrane exovesiculation may underly complex pathophysiological events in TAARs. Finally, other aspects of RBC activities may also indirectly influence pain perception and sensitization. These include arginase/NO signaling (affecting vascular tone, neuronal signaling, and tissue oxygenation/ischemia), release of EVs (modulating endothelial function, inflammation, and NO bioavailability), and ROS (sensitizing nociceptors and promoting neuroinflammation).

This study has certain limitations, including the unavailability of the first unit used in T0 transfusion, and lack of inflammatory marker profiling or donor screening for subclinical inflammation. Additionally, the single‐case design limited the findings to exploratory observations and did not allow for biomarker discovery. Given, however, the limited mechanistic understanding of the APTR, this study is the first to closely examine an APTR patient and the implicated RBC unit, across clinical, laboratory, and omics data from iterative transfusions, using well‐matched controls. Instead of being recorded as a diagnosis of inclusion, this APTR case was framed as a biologically interpretable interaction between a stress‐primed recipient and a metabolically primed RBC unit. As such, it may provide valuable insights—even without direct evidence of causality—and offer a foundation for future research into APTR mechanisms and prevention strategies.
