Capsid inclusion in a CHIKV mRNA vaccine impairs adaptive immune responses
Authors: Hongyu Chen, Longhai Yuan, Hao Yang, Qing Huang, Yun Yang, Cong Tang, Junbin Wang, Yanan Zhou, Wenhai Yu, Haixuan Wang, Wenqi Quan, Yuxia Yuan, Youchun Wang, Shuaiyao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTwo mRNA vaccines (V1 and V2) from our previous study, encoding different CHIKV structural proteins, exhibit distinct immune effects and protective efficacy. However, the immune mechanisms underlying these differences remain unclear. In this study, we use an integrated multi-omics approach (single-cell RNA sequencing, immune repertoire sequencing, and Olink cytokine profiling) to elucidate the differential immune cell activation states induced by the two vaccines and the potential structural basis of the antigens that may account for these differences. We find that V2 induces sustained B cell activation, predominantly IgG-type memory antibodies, and a robust recall response following viral challenge. By contrast, V1 elicits only transient B cell activation and relatively weak T cell responses. These findings delineate a mechanistic pathway linking mRNA antigen structure to immune activation, functional differentiation, immunological memory, and protective efficacy. This work enhances our understanding of the immunological mechanisms underlying CHIKV mRNA vaccination and offers insights for rational vaccine antigen design.

Introduction

Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) is an alphavirus, transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, that causes acute febrile illness with severe arthralgia, whereby a substantial proportion of patients progress to chronic or recurrent arthritis, imposing a considerable public-health and economic burden.1 Driven by global warming, urbanization and vector expansion, the geographic range of CHIKV continues to widen.2,3 According to the latest update from the World Health Organization (WHO), as of the end of 2024, local transmission has been reported in 119 countries and territories, indicating a persistent risk of global outbreaks and re-emergence.4 In 2025, the southern Chinese megacity Guangzhou experienced the largest CHIKV outbreak in recent years, with more than 5,000 cases reported to date and rising.5,6 Accordingly, the WHO recognized the risk of global spread associated with this event, while at the same time evaluating and updating recommendations for CHIKV vaccine use. However, global access and coverage remain limited.7

Over the past decade, substantial efforts have been devoted to the development of vaccines against CHIKV, leading to the development of multiple technological platforms. Live-attenuated vaccine candidates have demonstrated protective efficacy in preclinical models, where they elicited both humoral and cellular immune responses.8 Virus-vectored vaccines, including measles-vectored (MV-CHIK) and adenoviral vector platforms such as ChAdOx1 Chik, have shown promising safety and immunogenicity profiles in early- and mid-phase clinical trials.9,10 In addition, virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines were demonstrated to elicit robust neutralizing antibody responses in large phase 3 trials across different age groups.11,12 More recently, mRNA-based CHIKV vaccines have entered both preclinical and early clinical evaluation, showing the capacity to elicit potent humoral and cellular immunity, including cross-lineage protection in animal models.13,14,15 However, in spite of this rapid progress in vaccine development, only a single recombinant VLP vaccine (Vimkunya [PXVX0317]) has received emergency-use approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).16 Moreover, no specific antiviral therapy is currently available.17 Although multiple platforms have demonstrated immunogenicity, important mechanistic questions remain unresolved. In particular, the mechanisms by which these vaccines induce adaptive immune responses and shape long-term immune memory formation remain incompletely understood. Clarifying these processes is essential for optimizing CHIKV vaccine design and for informing broader antigen engineering strategies in next-generation mRNA vaccines.

The CHIKV genome is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA of 11.8 kb18 comprising two open reading frames (ORFs) encoding four non-structural proteins (nsP1–nsP4) and a structural polyprotein (capsid-p62-6K/TF-E1). E1 and p62 are co-translated and inserted into the endoplasmic reticulum, where they are glycosylated.19 In the secretory pathway, p62 is cleaved by furin into mature E2 and E3 glycoproteins.20 E3 remains associated with the mature E2–E1 heterodimer, yielding a stable lattice of 80 trimeric spikes. During entry, the class II fusion protein E1, which comprises domains I, II, and III, mediates membrane fusion, while E2 engages the host receptor.21 The capsid protein (C/CP) self-assembles around genomic RNA in the cytoplasm to form the nucleocapsid. E2 and E1 are the principal targets of neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), a key component of protective immunity.21,22 Notably, domain B of E2 and the adjacent domain A harbor dominant neutralization-susceptible epitopes that drive cross-neutralization and memory B cell differentiation, conferring the highest immunogenicity.18,23

Multiple design strategies have been explored for CHIKV vaccine development across VLP23 and mRNA platforms,24 generally based on full-length or major structural proteins.14 In our previous work, 769 conserved sequences from CHIKV isolates spanning four lineages were analyzed to generate two mRNA vaccines expressing either the full structural polyprotein or key structural components, i.e., V1 (capsid-E3-E2-6K-E1) and V2 (E3-E2-6K-E1), after which we evaluated their protective efficacy in infection models.13 Notably, although both vaccines conferred protection, significant differences were observed in rhesus macaques, whereby V2 elicited higher neutralizing-antibody titers, stronger cellular immune responses, and reduced post-infection organ injury relative to V1. However, the molecular basis underlying these divergent humoral and cellular outcomes remained unclear. Furthermore, this outcome contradicted our initial expectation, because the full-length structural antigen in V1 was theoretically expected to have stronger immunogenicity.24 To address this discrepancy, we performed an integrated multi-omics analysis that combined single-cell transcriptomics, immune-repertoire sequencing (assessed antibody usage frequencies) and Olink cytokine secretomics, with the aim to compare peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) immune-cell dynamics before and after vaccination, as well as in a vaccination-plus-challenge scenario vs. direct infection. Taken together, these findings advance our understanding of the mechanisms through which vaccines elicit adaptive immune responses and establish durable immunological memory.

Results

The single-cell atlas revealed differential immune activation by two CHIKV mRNA vaccines

To define systemic immune landscapes after vaccination with V1 and V2, we used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to profile PBMCs collected before (V1BG and V2BG) and after vaccination (V1 and V2). UMAP clustering at a resolution of 0.8 identified 30 clusters among all PBMCs (Figure S1A). Subsequently, we used validated rhesus macaque single-cell markers to achieve robust annotation, defining these clusters as pDC cells, T cells, natural killer (NK) T cells, B cells, monocytes, NK cells, and megakaryocytes (contamination) (Figure S1B). The spatial arrangement on UMAP further supported these definitions (Figure 1B). Lineage-defining genes of key immune cells showed specific expression and clear segregation, including the B cell markers CD79A, CD79B, MS4A1 (CD20), CD19, PAX5, IGKC, and BANK1 as well as the T cell markers CD3D, CD3E, CD3G, and CD2 (Figures 1C and 1D). Further comparisons of cell composition before and after vaccination using the two vaccines showed that the major PBMC lineages were consistent across time points but abundance patterns differed. Compared with V2, V1 induced a greater increase in overall T cell abundance, whereas V2 preferentially expanded B cells (Figures 1E and 1F). Subsequently, we further compared immune-cell features between the vaccination-infection groups and the direct infection group (C) to understand potential protective mechanisms of vaccination. After infection, the V2 vaccination-infection group (CV2) exhibited an immune-cell landscape more similar to authentic viral infection, characterized by a high abundance of B cells (Figure S1D). Interestingly, although V1 induced stronger T cell expansion after vaccination, the V1 vaccination-infection group (CV1) displayed constrained or non-sustained T cell levels under infection pressure. Furthermore, B cells in CV2 underwent secondary activation after infection, whereas those in CV1 remained at post-vaccination levels without evident gain (Figure 1G). To determine whether the differential immune responses elicited by the two vaccine constructs could be attributed to differences in antigen expression levels, we compared the abundance of key antigenic expressions both in vitro and in vivo. Firstly, HEK293T and RAW264.7 cells were transfected with V1 and V2 constructs, respectively, and the expression of the encoded structural proteins (E1, E2, and capsid) was assessed by western blot analysis. The results showed that the expression levels of the shared antigens E1 and E2 were comparable between the two constructs (Figure S2A). Subsequently, the injection-site muscle, inguinal draining lymph nodes, and spleen were collected from mice following primary and booster immunizations to further characterize the distribution and expression of vaccine-derived antigens. Consistent with the in vitro results, the antigen expression levels in all tissues were similar between the two vaccine groups. V2 did not exhibit higher antigen expression compared with V1 (Figure S2B). Together, these findings indicate that the differences in immune responses observed between the two vaccine constructs are unlikely to be explained by differences in antigen expression levels.

Overall, V2 induced coordinated and sustained humoral and cellular immune responses from vaccination through challenge and showed immune features resembling natural infection. By contrast, V1 induced a transient expansion of T cells after vaccination but failed to maintain or expand these responses following infection.

V2 vaccination induced a continuous progression of B cells from activation to effector differentiation and humoral immune profile that better mimics authentic viral infection

Because B cells are the primary mediators of vaccine-induced humoral immunity, we compared gene expression in bulk B cells before and after administering the two vaccines and performed functional enrichment analysis. The results first confirmed that both vaccines broadly enhanced immune components and processes related to antigen presentation and humoral effects, including the MHC protein complex, peptide antigen binding, as well as antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigens. However, the V2 vaccine showed higher enrichment significance. Furthermore, B cells from both vaccine groups showed enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) categories related to membrane-bound organelles and molecular functional pathways (Figure S3A), reflecting activation-associated biosynthetic expansion and functional reprogramming necessary for antibody production and differentiation. We hypothesized that differences in B cell abundance and activation state after V2 vaccination might contribute to the observed differences of protection between the two vaccines. Accordingly, subcluster definition of B cells was further conducted to resolve B cell dynamics after vaccination and to identify key differential B cell types. UMAP partitioned B cells from all groups into 14 subclusters (Figure 2A). Based on highly expressed marker genes, the subcluster B cells were further defined as atypical memory B cells (atMBCs, SLC9A9+MS4A1+DLGAP1+SPAG16+SOX5+MS4A1+FCRL5+CD80+CD86+CD27−), plasmablast-like B cells (S100A4+S100A6+MS4A1+IGKC+JCHAIN+), cycling-activated B cells (STMN1+ITGA4+ADGRB3+NEGR1+CDK6+VIM+RAPGEF2+), pre-plasmablasts (MS4A1+IRF4+CXCR4+), early-activated B cells (RAPGEF2+FCRL1+CD69+CXCR4+CD83+S100A4+), primed B cells (ESR2+HOPX+TANC2+RPS27A+PTPRO+PLEKHA5+FOS+JUN+),+ and virus-activated naive-like B cells (ESR2+RPS27A+PTPRO+FOS+JUN+) (Figures 2B and 2C). Notably, subcluster 4 showed specific enrichment of PF4, NRGN, and PPBP, which are canonical platelet-associated markers, suggesting platelet contamination. Given that these marker profiles were inconsistent with B cell identity, subclusters 4 and 13 were excluded from subsequent B cell analyses to avoid potential confounding effects arising from contaminating platelet or progenitor cell populations. Next, comparative analysis of B cell subcluster frequencies revealed that pre-plasmablast abundance increased following both V1 and V2 immunization. Notably, the proportion of early-activated B cells was significantly increased in the V2 group compared with V1 and the pre-immunization baseline (V2BG). Statistical analysis confirmed significant enrichment of both pre-plasmablasts and early-activated B cells in the V2 group (Figures 2D and 2E). To assess the cell maturation trajectories of these subsets, we performed pseudotime ordering analysis (Figure 3A). Early-activated B cells primarily occupied intermediate differentiation states, while pre-plasmablasts were enriched at late-stage pseudotime. Furthermore, pre-plasmablasts showed a predicted transition trajectory toward the virus-activated naive-like B cell cluster, suggesting progression toward an effector fate (Figure 3B). To further explore the mechanisms underlying the differential protective effects of the two vaccines, we compared B cell subset profiles across vaccine-challenge groups (CV1 and CV2) and direct CHIKV infection (C). Virus-activated naive-like B cells were selectively expanded in response to infection and were significantly more abundant in the CV2 and C groups compared with CV1 (Figures S3B and S3C). These findings were further supported by multiplex immunofluorescence staining of spleen tissues (Figure S3D). Pseudotime reconstruction revealed that V2 vaccination led to the accumulation of late-differentiation B cells that could be readily reactivated upon viral challenge (Figure S4A). Moreover, functional enrichment across the pseudotemporal axis supported these findings. Genes that were highly expressed in early to intermediate states were associated with biosynthetic and metabolic pathways (e.g., phosphorylation, steroid, and phospholipid metabolic process) and early antigen presentation processes (regulation of cellular localization, transport, and lipid hydroperoxide transport), consistent with a shift from resting to activated states. At the same time, genes expressed in late pseudotime showed strong enrichment for components of membrane-bound organelles (e.g., ARF1, CDC42, and MACF1), spliceosomal machinery (e.g., SFPQ, hnRNPs, and SRRM1), and proteasome core complex components (e.g., YTHDF2, AGO1, TUT4, RPL5, EIF3I, and PSMB2/PSMA5) (Figure 3C), indicating clonal expansion and enhanced antigen processing capacity. Further functional enrichment analysis of B cell DEGs between V1 and V2 revealed stronger induction of the B cell receptor signaling pathway in the V2 group (Figure 4A). Finally, to evaluate whether the baseline plasma cytokine landscape may have influenced subsequent immune responses, we compared key plasma cytokines between the V1 and V2 groups at baseline and after vaccination, including immunoregulatory cytokines, inflammatory mediators, and chemokines. However, no significant differences were observed between two groups (V1 vs. V2), while longitudinal comparisons within each group (V1 vs. V1BG and V2 vs. V2BG) did not reveal significant cytokine changes following vaccination (Figure S4B). Furthermore, Spearman correlation analyses revealed that several baseline cytokines were associated with subsets related to B cell differentiation. The results showed that most baseline cytokines did not display significant correlations with immune cell subsets. Moreover, only a limited number of cytokines showed significant associations with subsets related to B cell differentiation. Specifically, LIF-R, TNFRSF9, CXCL6, TWEAK, and CCL23 were significantly correlated with pre-plasmablast abundance (p < 0.05, R > 0.5). In addition, CCL3 and CCL4 were significantly positively correlated with the abundance of virus-activated naive-like B cells (p < 0.05, R > 0.5; Table S1). Next, we examined changes in key pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines before and after immunization. The results showed modestly elevated levels of several inflammatory mediators following vaccination, including IFN-γ, IL-1α, IL-18, and CXCL5. However, these increases did not reach the level of statistical significance. Importantly, a direct comparison between the V1 and V2 groups revealed no significant differences in overall post-vaccination inflammation intensity (Figure S4B).

Collectively, these findings indicate that both vaccine constructs and LNP formulations induced comparable and limited systemic inflammatory responses, and that baseline cytokine profiles had minimal predictive value for downstream immune trajectories. While inflammatory mediators may modestly influence B cell activation, they are unlikely to explain the observed differences in immune outcomes. Instead, V2 induced a more sustained and differentiated humoral response, characterized by enhanced transitions from activation to effector states and stronger antigen presentation and phagocytic signatures, whereas V1 elicited more transient antibody responses.

CHIKV infection induced acute IgM expansion, whereas V2 vaccination elicited an IgG-dominated antibody repertoire

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed that V2 vaccination induced robust early B cell activation, clonal expansion, and sustained differentiation toward plasmablasts. These features were associated with stronger enrichment of BCR signaling and downstream effector pathways, suggesting a more durable and functionally mature humoral immune response. Accordingly, B cell immune repertoire sequencing (BCR-seq) was conducted to compare the antibody isotype composition and clonal diversity across groups. The results showed that, during vaccination, both vaccines induced increases in IgM abundance and diversity, which further increased after viral challenge (Figures S5A and S5B). However, V2 maintained significantly higher IgM diversity than both V1 and C (Figure 4C). Notably, in the infection group (C), CHIKV viremia induced a prominent IgM-biased response characterized by acute clonal expansion and reduced diversity of IgM, consistent with an oligoclonal primary humoral response. By contrast, vaccine-primed groups exhibited lower overall IgM levels after infection compared with the infection group (C), particularly the CV2 group. Furthermore, the IgM repertoire of CV2 displayed significantly higher diversity than those of CV1 and C (Figure 4D). Statistical comparisons at baseline confirmed that these differences were independent of immunization background (Figure S5C). These results indicate that infection did not drive oligoclonal IgM expansion in the presence of V2-induced immunological memory.

To further clarify the relationship between antibody responses and vaccine-elicited immune cell states, particularly B cell differentiation, Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to link the abundance of BCR isotypes (IgM, IgG, and IgA) with the frequencies of key B cell subsets identified by single-cell transcriptomics. The results confirmed that the increased frequency of pre-plasmablasts observed after immunization was positively correlated with the abundance of both IgG and IgM, wherein the correlation for IgG was statistically significant. Notably, virus-activated naive-like B cells also exhibited significant positive correlations with the proportions of both IgM and IgG (Figure S5D). To further characterize the differentiation trajectories underlying these associations, we calculated a plasmablast differentiation/secretory program score based on genes associated with antibody secretion and plasma cell commitment by using the AddModuleScore function across B cell subsets. The results showed that virus-activated naive-like B cells and pre-plasmablasts exhibited elevated differentiation scores, supporting the presence of a progressive activation-to-terminal differentiation continuum within the B cell compartment (Figure 4E). Collectively, these analyses linked B cell activation and differentiation states with quantitative features of the antibody repertoire.

Analysis of variable, diversity, and joining gene segments (VDJ) usage revealed that the IGHV4-2-J4 combination was significantly enriched in both CV1 and the infected group C compared with CV2, showing the highest frequency among all IgM clones, potentially representing a dominant virus-induced clonotype (Figure 4F). Notably, IGHV4-2-J4 was the major IgM clonotype induced after vaccination, with significantly increased abundance following both V1 and V2 immunization, although the activation level was higher in V2. This pattern was independent of the immunization background (Figure S5E). Furthermore, while the total IgG abundance did not differ significantly between the groups (Figures 4C and 4D), analysis of the top 10 high-frequency IgG clones showed that IgG IGHV4-2-J4 was the predominant IgG clonotype induced by vaccination (Figure S5F) and continued to expand after infection. particularly in V2 (V2 and CV2 showed significantly higher frequencies than V1 and CV1, respectively). Interestingly, IGHV4-2-J4 was also the high-preference IgG in the CHIKV infection group (C). In addition, the IGHV1-1-J4 clonotype showed infection-specific expansion in the infected group (Figures S6A and S6B). Finally, given that IgA constituted a substantial portion of the antibody pool in all groups, we compared the abundance of high-frequency IgA clones across groups. Despite the overall prominence of IgA, no statistically significant differences were observed among the top 10 most frequently used IgA clonotypes across all groups, suggesting that this may be unrelated to immunity or infection (Figure S6C).

Taken together, these findings indicate that antibody repertoire output scales with the magnitude of B cell activation and differentiation. V2 vaccination established a humoral immune repertoire characterized by a pre-established pool of memory B cells capable of mounting rapid and efficient IgG responses. During viral challenge, this pre-existing memory pool enabled rapid IgG mobilization without reliance on acute-phase IgM expansion. By contrast, the V1 and the direct infection groups exhibited predominantly IgM-biased responses with reduced repertoire diversity, consistent with features of primary immune activation. These findings further support that V2 vaccination promoted the establishment of a humoral immune profile with greater durability and potential for affinity maturation.

T cell subpopulation analysis revealed distinct transcriptional activation patterns induced by different CHIKV mRNA vaccines

In our global immunophenotyping analysis, substantial differences in overall T cell abundance were observed across the different vaccinated groups. However, the functional states and subset composition of these T cells remained unresolved. Given the essential role of T cells in orchestrating vaccine-induced cellular immune responses, high-resolution subclustering of T cells among the identified PBMCs was conducted to dissect the phenotypic and functional heterogeneity of immune responses elicited by the two mRNA vaccines. Unsupervised UMAP clustering revealed 12 transcriptionally distinct T cell subsets (Figure 5A), while the specific expression of canonical markers CD3E and CD3D confirmed the robustness of T cell identity assignment (Figure 5B). Spatial separation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was further delineated by CD4 and CD8A expression, respectively (Figure 5C). Based on subcluster-specific gene expression profiles (Figure 5D), the following populations were identified: (1) pre-regulatory memory-like CD4+ T cells (TOX+CTLA4+BACH2+TIGIT+CCR7+), (2) central memory-like CD4+ T cells (GPHN+BACH2+RASGRF2+CERS6+PLCL1+ICOS+IL10+CD40LG PRDM1+), (3) CD4 memory-like T cells (AHR+ICOS+CD40LG+IL10+PRDM1+), (4) Th17-like CD4+ T cells (IFNG+RORA+CCR6+), (5) pre-effector CD4+ T cells (S100A4+ICOS+IL4R+SOCS3+RPS24+RPL30+), (6) memory-like CD8+ T cells (CD96+MAML3+THEMIS+PDE3B+GZMB−IFNG-PRDM1−PDCD1-), (7) fully differentiated cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (KLRG1+GZMBGZMK+PRF1+), and (8) pre-effector CD8+ T cells (GZMM+CD8A+RPL+) (Figure 5E). Comparative analysis of subset abundance between groups revealed that vaccination with V1 significantly increased both pre-effector CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations compared with the pre-vaccination baseline (V1BG) (Figure 5F). Moreover, the corresponding gene sets were enriched for ribosomal gene expression (RPS and RPL), suggesting they may represent metabolically active transitional states. Notably, pre-effector CD4+ T cells were also enriched in the direct infection group (C), but their abundance did not further increase in V1-immunized animals after challenge (CV1). By contrast, although V2 vaccination alone did not significantly alter pre-effector CD4+ T cell frequencies compared with the baseline (V2BG), viral challenge significantly expanded this population in CV2, generating a cell distribution that more closely mirrored natural infection. V2 vaccination induced an infection-like T cell response with stronger Th1-associated activation

To further compare the cellular immune responses elicited by the two vaccines, we performed T cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq) to systematically analyze TCR usage profiles and diversity, which reflects T cell clonal expansion and functional activation. Analysis of TCR diversity indices revealed that both the infection group (C) and the CV2 group exhibited a higher clonality and lower Pielou index, suggesting a contraction of the overall TCR repertoire accompanied by an increased dominance of expanded clones. Notably, the global TCR diversity metrics of the infection group (C) were more similar to those of CV2 than to CV1 (Figure 6B). TCR clonotype overlap analysis further demonstrated that CV2 shared more TCR clones with infection group (C), indicating higher intergroup similarity (Figure 6C). A further comparison of the top 10 most prevalent TCR clonotypes showed that TRAV22-3-J27 was the predominantly utilized VDJ type in the infection group (C) and was significantly more frequent in CV2 than in CV1. Furthermore, several TCR types from CV2 exhibited usage trends similar to infection group (C), such as TRAV22-3-J27, TRAV22-3-J32, and RAV26-3-J23 (Figure 6D). Among them, TRAV22-3-J27 was already significantly increased in the V2 group compared with V1 following vaccination, suggesting antigen-induced activation as the primary driver (Figure 6E). Notably, longitudinal analyses (repeated measures) performed separately for the two vaccine groups showed that both V1 and V2 immunizations induced a significant expansion of TRAV22-3-J27, with a stronger effect observed in V2. However, after infection, the abundance of TRAV22-3-J27 was not maintained in the V1 group, whereas it continued to expand in the CV2 group (Figure 6F). Statistical comparisons of TCR abundance between the V1 and V2 background groups further demonstrated that this differential expansion was not attributable to individual baseline differences (Figure 6G). Furthermore, the Olink multiplex cytokine panel was used to assess cytokines associated with T cell immunity (e.g., interleukins and chemokines) and inflammatory regulation to validate the differences of T cell activation between the two vaccines. Firstly, the key T cell cytokines IFN-γ, CXCL9/10/11, IL-12B, IL-18, IL-18R1, TNFRSF9, TRAIL, CD40, CX3CL1, and CD8A were used as the basis to calculate the equally weighted mean Z score as the T cell activation score (TAS), which, in turn, was used to assess T cell activation levels. The results showed that both vaccines led to an increase of the TAS upon infection (Figure S7B). However, V2 elicited stronger T cell activation post-infection (Figure S7C), with higher expression levels of CD40, IL-12B, IL-18, the CXCR3 axis (CXCL11/9), and CD8A in V2/CV2 compared with CV1. This indicated that V2 vaccination induced stronger antigen-presenting cell signaling and Th1 immune responses (Figures 6H and 6I; Figures S7D and S7E).

Taken together, these findings indicate that V2 vaccination and subsequent challenge induced more focused TCR clonal expansion that extensively overlapped with the repertoire induced by infection in naive animals. This was accompanied by stronger activation of Th1-associated cytokines, suggesting a more effective induction of functional T cell responses.

Capsid protein may attenuate the antigenicity of the V1 vaccine by competition or interference of key E2 epitopes

To investigate the mechanistic basis for the observed differences in immunogenicity, encompassing both cellular and humoral responses, we conducted a structural analysis of the encoded antigens. Immunodominant epitopes of the capsid and E proteins were first predicted using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) algorithm, identifying key B cell epitopes (Figures 7A and S8A) and MHC-binding epitopes (Figure 7B; Figures S8B and S8C) within the full-length structural proteins (E3, E2, 6K, and E1) and the capsid protein. The predictions indicated a higher density of immunodominant epitopes on E protein, largely in the E2 and E1 regions, consistent with prior reports. We then modeled the antigens encoded by CV1 and CV2 using AlphaFold3, and visualized the predicted immunodominant epitopes. The results indicate that the capsid protein contains both B cell and MHC epitopes (Figures 7C and S8A), and previous studies have shown that the V1 vaccine undergoes proteolytic processing after immunization to form a monomeric protein. We speculate that the capsid of V1 (capsid-E3-E2-6K-E1) may have impacted critical E2/E1 epitope recognition through antigenic competition or interference, potentially dampening antigen presentation and exposure of neutralization-susceptible epitopes. Discussion

CHIKV has now spread to over 100 countries and territories worldwide, resulting in substantial economic burden on endemic regions.1 Its mosquito-borne transmission makes effective control particularly challenging.25 A recent outbreak (July 2025) in China further underscores the global epidemic potential of this virus.5,6 Currently, only a limited number of vaccines have been approved,16 and their accessibility remains restricted.7 Accordingly, more diverse and innovative vaccines are needed. Previously, we developed two mRNA vaccines, V1 (encoding capsid-E3-E2-6K-E1) and V2 (encoding E3-E2-6K-E1), based on comparative analysis of 769 CHIKV genomic sequences. Both vaccines elicited systemic immune responses and protected non-human primates (NHPs) against CHIKV challenge.13 However, direct comparison showed that V1 induced weaker humoral and cellular immunity and provided less effective pathological control than V2, while the immunological basis for these differences remained unclear.

In this study, we leveraged an established CHIKV infection model in NHPs and two rationally designed CHIKV mRNA vaccines, on the basis of which we implemented a multi-omics approach combining single-cell transcriptomics, immune repertoire sequencing, as well as the Olink cytokine and chemokine panel. This allowed us to comprehensively map the dynamics of humoral and cellular immunity elicited by the two vaccine constructs during both the immunization and post-challenge phases. Notably, our prior immunogenicity evaluation in rhesus macaques demonstrated that neutralizing antibodies were detectable only after booster immunization, indicating that effective functional humoral immunity was primarily established following the second dose rather than during the initial priming phase. Accordingly, the 42 days post-immunization (dpi; 21 days after booster immunization) time point was selected to represent stabilized functional immunity, while 50 dpi (7 days post-challenge) time point was chosen to capture early recall responses in the multi-omics analysis. Our findings indicate that differences of antigenic composition critically shape immunological protection. Compared with V1, V2 induced more sustained B cell differentiation, with enhanced activation spanning early-activated B cells, pre-plasmablasts, and virus-activated naive-like B cells upon challenge. Furthermore, viral challenge in V2-immunized animals robustly reconstituted a Th1-biased T cell landscape dominated by pre-effector CD4+ T cells, accompanied by high clonal overlap of the TCR repertoire with that elicited by direct infection in naive animals, indicative of superior cellular plasticity and memory responsiveness. Structural modeling and epitope masking analysis further suggested that the additional capsid protein encoded by V1 may have obscured key B and T cell epitopes on the E2 glycoprotein, thereby impairing antigen recognition and immunogenicity.

Evaluation of protective efficacy revealed distinct levels of humoral and cellular immune responses between the two vaccine candidates in rhesus macaques. In particular, V1 immunization failed to induce a significant antibody response in terms of either binding or neutralizing antibodies. We found that both V1 and V2 immunizations induced an expansion of the pre-plasmablast subset within the B cell compartment. However, V2 vaccination induced stronger activation across both the intermediate-stage early-activated B cell subset and the fully differentiated pre-plasmablasts. Pre-plasmablasts were characterized by high expression of IRF4, CD69, and CXCR4. Notably, IRF4 has been demonstrated as a crucial transcriptional “switch” in the generation of functionally competent plasma cells. Klein et al. demonstrated that in IRF4-deficient mice, germinal center memory B cells failed to differentiate into plasma cells.26 Furthermore, CD69 has been identified as a hallmark of B cell activation.27 Increased expression of CD69 and CXCR4 represent key molecular signatures that enhance the differentiation of B cells into plasma cells, driving mature B cell phenotypes.28,29 Our pseudotime trajectory analysis also positioned pre-plasmablasts at the terminal end of the B cell differentiation axis, further suggesting that these cells serve as critical progenitors of effector plasma cells. Clinical studies by Powers et al. have demonstrated that protective immunity against CHIKV is strongly associated with the durable maintenance of memory B cells. CHIKV infection induces long-lived memory B cell responses that can persist for decades, indicating that effective memory differentiation rather than a transient antibody peak is a key correlate of sustained immunity.30 Mechanistically, B cell receptor (BCR) signaling strength critically determines germinal center selection and differentiation, with properly integrated signals promoting plasmablast output and shaping durable memory B cell formation.31 In this study, V2 vaccination not only enhanced BCR signaling and lowered the threshold for effector commitment but also more effectively orchestrated the spatial progression of B cells from activation to terminal differentiation. Interestingly, we observed that viral infection activated a subset of antigen-specific B cells that underwent oligoclonal expansion, with significantly higher abundance detected in CV2 compared with CV1. By further integrating the BCR sequencing results, we inferred that these B cells may represent a limited number of expanded IgM clones bearing the V4-2-J4 rearrangement, which were activated in an antigen-specific manner by the viral challenge. However, compared with direct viral infection in naive animals, CV2 did not exhibit significantly elevated IgM expression. Instead, the corresponding V4-2-J4 IgG clones displayed a significant increase of abundance, which may be attributed to isotype class switching of memory B cells during secondary antigenic stimulation.32,33 Although V1 immunization also induced significant expansion of pre-plasmablasts, the memory cell response following antigenic re-stimulation was relatively weak compared with V2. This response was predominantly characterized by a limited number of IgM clones, which is indicative of a transient, acute-phase protection.34 In addition, the T cell landscape revealed systematic differences in T cell induction and functionality between the two vaccine groups. V1 vaccination significantly expanded metabolically active but functionally immature pre-effector CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, characterized by elevated ribosomal gene expression and a lack of effector molecules such as IFNG and PRF1,35 suggesting that these cells predominantly remained in an intermediate activation state. By contrast, activation of pre-effector CD4+ cells in the V2 group occurred primarily after viral challenge, resulting in T cell lineages that closely resembled the repertoire observed during direct infection in naive animals. These results may indicate that V1 vaccination resulted in suboptimal recall efficiency, limiting the ability to rapidly mount a high-quality cellular immune response following actual viral exposure. The high similarity of TCR repertoires between the V2 and the direct infection groups supports the notion that V2 possesses a more optimal epitope composition allowing efficient clonal selection. Accordingly, the antigenic epitopes of V2 appear to more faithfully mimic those of the natural virus.36 Furthermore, our findings are consistent with clinical observations reported by Agarwal et al., who demonstrated that CHIKV infection is associated with sustained expansion of CHIKV-specific CD4+ T cells predominantly targeting the E2 protein, especially in chronic cases. Notably, elevated proportions of CHIKV-specific memory CD4+ T cells persisted for years after infection, underscoring the importance of durable helper T cell memory in long-term immune control. By contrast, CD8+ T cell activation was not prominent in those patients, a pattern that aligns with our observation that protective efficacy in the vaccination-challenge setting was more closely associated with coordinated CD4+ recall responses rather than sustained CD8+ activation.37 Among the full-length structural proteins of CHIKV, E2 and E1 were shown to be highly immunogenic.38,39 The E2 protein serves as the primary target for neutralizing antibodies, while the E1 protein of alphaviruses is more conserved and holds the potential to elicit cross-protective neutralizing antibodies.40,41,42 Notably, although the C protein is not the principal structural target of neutralizing antibodies in CHIKV infection, its antigenicity has been well documented. Arriaza et al. demonstrated that the C protein contains immunogenic epitopes capable of inducing CHIKV-specific CD8+ effector and memory T cells.43 Furthermore, recently, human studies have confirmed that the CHIKV capsid protein is immunogenic; natural infection induces antibody responses targeting capsid-derived epitopes, and capsid-specific CD4+ T cell epitopes have been identified in infected individuals.44 Together, these findings indicate that capsid-derived peptides can participate in shaping adaptive immune responses. Here, we integrated actual immune responses, immunodominant epitopes, and structural prediction results, finding that although the antigen encoded by the V1 vaccine contains more epitopes than V2 due to the inclusion of the capsid sequence, incorporation of the capsid protein may partly redistribute epitope presentation toward capsid-derived peptides, which may reduce the relative presentation efficiency or immunodominance of key neutralizing epitopes within E2 and E1.18,45 The resulting antigenic competition or interference may, therefore, diminish the antigenicity of E2 and E1. It was previously demonstrated that when multiple antigenic determinants are simultaneously present, epitopes may compete for antigen-processing, MHC loading, and helper T cell signals. In particular, B cells targeting distinct epitopes compete for limited helper T cells, which can suppress responses to other epitopes.46 Moreover, even subtle alterations in antigen structure can modify epitope processing and presentation hierarchies, thereby shifting adaptive immune focusing.47 Accordingly, we hypothesized that this interference may impair the activation of virus-specific effector and memory B cells, resulting in weaker virus recognition and secondary differentiation following CHIKV challenge.48 As a result, strong immune memory was not recalled upon subsequent infection. Therefore, V1 elicited weaker humoral as well as cellular immunity, which ultimately led to less effective protection compared with V2. Accordingly, the exposed epitopes on the capsid and E proteins in V1 nevertheless permitted a certain degree of immune-cell activation, conferring partial protection in rhesus macaques.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the systematic investigation of the mechanistic continuum spanning from mRNA antigen architecture, immune activation, functional differentiation, memory formation, and protective efficacy in the context of CHIKV vaccination. These insights offer a conceptual framework for rational vaccine design and mechanistic dissection of antigen-induced immune protection.

Limitations of the study

In spite of its findings, this study also has several limitations. First, the overall sample size was relatively limited, and the animal experiments were conducted using single-sex cohorts, which precluded a dedicated assessment of sex-associated effects. In addition, immune profiling was primarily based on PBMCs, which may not fully capture tissue-specific immune responses. Second, immune dynamics were profiled only to day 7 post-challenge, so the durability of immunological memory, long-term protection, and stability of cellular or repertoire states beyond this acute phase remain undetermined. In addition, the viral challenge experiments were performed using a single CHIKV strain, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to other circulating or emerging strains. Third, high-frequency antibody VDJ rearrangements were identified but not functionally validated. Accordingly, future work should reconstruct representative monoclonal antibodies in vitro to test their binding, neutralization breadth and affinity, and Fc-effector functions. Finally, the structure-epitope mechanism was deduced solely from computational modeling. Although the proposed capsid-mediated epitope masking is supported by in silico analyses, direct confirmation will require high-resolution structural and accessibility assays (e.g., cryo-EM, hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry , and targeted epitope knockin or knockout experiments).

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

HEK-293 T cells (Procell, CL-0005) and RAW264.7 cells (Procell, CL-0190) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and used for in vitro antigen-expression analysis. HEK-293 T cells are a highly transfectable derivative of the human embryonic kidney 293 cell line generated by insertion of a temperature-sensitive SV40 large T-antigen gene. RAW264.7 cells are a murine monocyte/macrophage leukemia cell line derived from BALB/c mice. Both cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Cells were used at low passage numbers after recovery and were passaged according to the supplier’s instructions. Before use, both cell lines were tested and confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma contamination using a colorimetric mycoplasma detection assay.

Animals

Two animal models were used in this study: rhesus macaques for vaccination and viral challenge experiments, and mice for in vivo antigen-expression analysis.

Nine healthy, clean-grade male rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta), 4–8 years of age, were obtained from the non-human primate breeding facility of the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College. Animals were confirmed to be clinically healthy and free of CHIKV infection before enrollment and had not previously undergone any experimental procedures. Rhesus macaques were individually housed under standard husbandry conditions with a commercial primate diet, free access to drinking water, and a normal light/dark cycle throughout the study (Approval Nos. DWSP20240309).

For in vivo antigen-expression analysis, all mice were specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade female BALB/c mice, 6–8 weeks of age, purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). Mice had not been subjected to any previous experimental procedures before enrollment. Animals were maintained under SPF conditions with a commercial rodent diet, free access to drinking water, and a normal light/dark cycle throughout the study. (Approval Nos. DWLL202508001).

Biosafety

All experiments involving live CHIKV and animals were performed in the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facility at the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, Kunming, China. The use of live CHIKV was approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Biosafety Approval No. SWAQ20250323). All procedures involving infectious materials were performed in accordance with institutional biosafety guidelines and national regulations governing research involving highly pathogenic microorganisms.

CHIKV strains and animal experimental procedures

An Asian lineage CHIKV strain (GenBank accession: MH670649.1), originally isolated from an imported clinical case in Ruili, Yunnan, China in 2019, was used for experimental infection. Animals were intramuscularly inoculated in the hindlimb with 2 × 107 plaque-forming units (PFU) of CHIKV. All animals were anesthetized to harvest blood. A schematic of the experimental workflow is shown in Figure 1A.

Method details

Preparation of V1/V2 mRNA-LNPs

Plasmids encoding the V1 and V2 constructs were linearized using BsaI (cat. no. #GMP4303PC-01, Vazyme, China) and purified with DNA magnetic beads (cat. no. N411, Vazyme, China) to obtain high-quality linear DNA templates. In vitro transcription was subsequently performed using a co-transcription kit (cat. no. DD4203, Vazyme, China), and the resulting mRNA was purified using RNA magnetic beads (cat. no. #N412, Vazyme, China). The purified mRNA was diluted to a concentration of 108 ng/μL in citrate–sodium citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 4.0). Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) were formulated by mixing SM102, DSPC, cholesterol, and PEG2000 at a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5 in ethanol to generate the lipid phase. mRNA-LNPs were prepared using a microfluidic mixing system, followed by concentration and buffer exchange into Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4) using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration unit (cat. no. UFC9100, Merck, USA). The resulting mRNA-LNPs were characterized by measuring encapsulation efficiency and particle size to assess their physicochemical properties.

Cell transfection and antigen expression analysis

HEK293T and RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at appropriate densities one day prior to transfection. For transfection, V1 or V2 mRNA-LNPs were added dropwise to the cells and evenly distributed (Each sample was transfected three times as three repetition, and using untransfected cells as negative controls). At 36 h post-transfection, culture supernatants were collected, and cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (cat. no. 89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with protease inhibitor (cat. no. K1007, APExBIO, USA) to extract total cellular proteins. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer (cat. no. G2075, Servicebio, China) and denatured at 95°C for 10 min. Protein expression of C, E2, and E1 was analyzed by Western blot using the following primary antibodies: anti-C (cat. no. GTX135183, GeneTex, USA, 1:4000), anti-E2 (cat. no. VK572014, Abinscience, China, 1:1000), and anti-E1 (cat. no. VK425014, Abinscience, China, 1:1000). β-actin (cat. no. ab49900, Abcam, UK, 1:1000) was used as an internal loading control. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software to obtain relative protein expression levels.

In vivo antigen expression analysis

Thirty mice were randomly divided into two cohorts: a prime immunization group (V1-1[n = 5], V2-1[n = 5], and NC-1[n = 5]) and a boost immunization group (V1-2[n = 5], V2-2[n = 5], and NC-2[n = 5]). Mice were intramuscularly injected with V1 or V2 mRNA-LNPs at a dose of 10 μg/per animal, while control groups received 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For the prime immunization group, tissues were collected 12 h post-immunization, including muscle (injection site), inguinal lymph nodes (draining lymph nodes) and spleen (a primary immune organ). For the boost immunization group, mice received a second immunization 14 days after the primary dose, and tissues were collected 12 h after the booster injection. The same set of tissues was harvested as described above. All collected tissues were subsequently subjected to immunofluorescence analysis to assess CHIKV antigen expression (IACUC approval no. DWLL202608001).

Immunofluorescence staining and analysis

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded mouse tissue samples were sectioned into 3 μm-thick slices. Tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies against C (cat. no. GTX135183, GeneTex, USA), E2 (cat. no. VK572014, Abinscience, China), and E1 (cat. no. VK425014, Abinscience, China). After incubation with corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and sections were subjected to immunofluorescence analysis. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded spleen tissues from rhesus macaques were also sectioned into 3 μm-thick slices and subjected to multiplex immunofluorescence staining using antibodies against CD79A (cat. no. CSB-PA004957LA01HU, CUSABIO, China; 1:200), IRF4 (cat. no. CSB-PA004957LA01HU, CUSABIO, China; 1:200), and CD69 (at. no. A00529-2, BOSTER, China; 1:100). Stained sections were scanned using a 3D HISTECH slide scanner, and positive cells were identified and quantified using HALO software (Indica Labs, USA). Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0).

Vaccine immunization for rhesus macaques

A total of nine rhesus macaques (4–8 years old, male) were used in this study and randomly assigned to a negative control group (n = 3, mock treatment with phosphate-buffered saline), a V1 group (n = 3, immunization with mRNA vaccine 1), and a V2 group (n = 3, immunized with mRNA vaccine 2). Vaccine design and immunization procedures were consistent with those previously described (Xiaoming et al., 2025). Briefly, rhesus macaques were either immunized with vaccine or mock-immunized with PBS twice at 21-day intervals, followed by viral challenge on day 43 and sacrifice 7 days post-challenge. PBMCs and serum were collected before immunization (V1BG, V2BG, and CBG), after immunization (V1 and V2), and after infection (CV1, CV2, and C) for single-cell transcriptome sequencing, B cell receptor (BCR) and T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing, as well as the Olink cytokine panel (Figure 1A).

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from anticoagulated whole blood using the Ficoll-Paque Plus kit (cat. no. 17144003; Cytiva, Sweden). Briefly, 5 mL of whole blood was collected into a vacutainer tube containing anticoagulant (cat. no. 17-1440-026, GE, USA). The sample was diluted 1:1 with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and carefully layered onto 5 mL of Ficoll-Paque Plus. Density gradient centrifugation was performed at 200 g for 20 min at room temperature (RT), using maximum acceleration and minimum deceleration. The mononuclear cell layer (appearing as a thin, white band at the interface) was collected and transferred into a 15 mL conical polypropylene tube. Cells were washed twice with 5 mL of PBS, each followed by centrifugation at 200g for 5 min at room temperature (RT). The resulting PBMCs were then resuspended and prepared for downstream applications.

Cell suspensions were filtered using a 40 μm nylon cell strainer (352340; Corning Falcon, USA) and red blood cells were removed using 1× Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (Cat. no. 00-4333-57; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The Chromium Single-Cell Gene Expression solution (Chromium Controller; 10× Genomics, USA) was used for single-cell capture and library construction. The cell suspension (10 000 live cells/sample) was loaded for droplet formation using Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit (10× Genomics, USA). Libraries were constructed using Single-cell 3′ Library and Gel Bead Kit V3 (cat. no. 1000075; 10× Genomics, USA). Sequencing libraries were quantified using a High Sensitivity DNA Chip (High Sensitivity DNA Kit for Bioanalyzer 2100; Agilent, USA) on a Bioanalyzer 2100 and the Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, USA) was used to sequence the libraries. The resulting reads were processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (v7.1.0) with default parameters. FASTQ files were aligned to the Mmul10 Macaca mulatta reference genome using STAR (v 2.7.11 b), and gene-barcode matrices were generated by counting UMIs and removing non-cell barcodes. These matrices were imported into Seurat (v4.1.1), an R package specifically developed for single-cell RNA-seq data analysis and visualization, for quality control and downstream analyses. Low-quality cells were excluded based on three criteria: number of UMIs, number of detected genes, and the percentage of mitochondrial transcripts (outside mean ±2 SD). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction and visualization of high-dimensional single-cell RNA-seq data, enabling the identification and comparison of cell clusters. The mitochondrial fraction was calculated using the PercentageFeatureSet function in Seurat package, and data were normalized using the NormalizeData function. DEGs between groups or clusters were identified using Seurat’s FindMarkers function (likelihood ratio test), with significance thresholds of |log2FC| > 0.25 and q ≤ 0.05. Functional enrichment of GO terms and KEGG pathways was performed on DEGs using GOATOOLS (https://github.com/tanghaibao/Goatools) and Python SciPy. For multiple hypothesis testing, statistical significance was determined using the Bonferroni correction (adjusted p < 0.05). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 3.16.1). Gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Genes were ranked using the Signal2Noise metric based on their expression differences between groups. Pathway enrichment scores across different cell clusters were calculated using the limma package (version 3.44.3). Significantly enriched pathways were identified based on the normalized enrichment scores (NES) and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Considering the species differences between humans and rhesus macaques, marker genes for cell identification in this study were derived from a previously validated rhesus macaque single-cell transcriptomic study (Han et al., 2020). To reconstruct the dynamic process of cell activation and differentiation, pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle2 (R package, v2.22.0). Highly variable genes identified among cell subsets were used as ordering genes (min_expr = 0.1, min_cell_expr = 10). Dimensionality reduction was conducted using the DDRTree algorithm, and pseudotime smoothing was implemented using the smoothData function. Cells were subsequently ordered along the inferred trajectory using the orderCells function. Pseudotime values represent the relative progression of cells along the inferred differentiation trajectory. Cells were divided into early and late pseudotime stages using the median pseudotime value as the threshold.

BCR/TCR immune repertoire

Total RNA was extracted from whole blood using the PAXgene Blood RNA Kit (cat. no. 762174; Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Post-extraction treatment with DNase I (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. EN0521) was performed to remove residual genomic DNA. RNA purity was assessed by measuring the A260/A280 ratio using a NanoDrop OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. ND-ONEC-W), and integrity was verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Quantification of qualified RNA samples was performed on a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, cat. no. Q33216) using the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, cat. no. Q32855).

Approximately 2 μg of total RNA per sample was used to construct B cell receptor and T cell receptor (BCR and TCR) repertoire libraries using the KC-Digital Stranded BCR-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina 150 (Seqhealth Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; cat. no. DT0815-02), following the manufacturer’s protocol. To mitigate PCR- and sequencing-derived duplication, pre-amplified cDNA molecules were tagged with an 8-nt unique molecular identifier (UMI). Libraries were size-selected to 250–500 bp, quantified, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw FASTQ files were quality-filtered using SOAPnuke (v1.6.0) to remove reads containing adapter sequences, excessive ambiguous bases, or low average Phred scores. The resulting clean reads were then processed using custom scripts for UMI-guided error correction and de-duplication. Briefly, reads sharing an identical 8-nt UMI were grouped, pairwise alignments were computed within each UMI group, and reads with ≥95% sequence identity were aggregated into sub-clusters. For each sub-cluster, a multiple-sequence alignment was generated to infer a single consensus sequence, yielding one de-duplicated, error-corrected consensus read per sub-cluster. These consensus reads were used for downstream BCR- and TCR-seq analysis. V(D)J assignment and CDR3 extraction were performed using MiXCR (v3.0.3) by searching against the IMGT reference database (Lefranc et al., Nucleic Acids Res., 2015; https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gku1056), using default settings unless otherwise specified.

Olink multiplex cytokine assay

To maximize the detection of cytokines, serum cytokine levels were measured using the Olink Target 96 Inflammation panel (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden). Briefly, we employed a customized panel comprising key inflammatory proteins and immunoregulatory cytokines, including interleukins, chemokines, and immunomodulators. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, 95 μL of serum was pre-treated with 5 μL of 1% Triton X-100. The accuracy of this method has been previously validated (Sun et al., 2025). The rhesus macaque cytokine panel is designed with pairs of oligonucleotide-labeled antibody probes that specifically recognize each of the target proteins. When the two probes bind in close proximity on the same protein, their conjugated oligonucleotides hybridize, enabling a DNA polymerase–mediated proximity-dependent extension reaction that generates a unique PCR-amplifiable DNA sequence. The resulting DNA fragments were amplified and quantified on a microfluidic real-time PCR instrument (Olink Signature Q100). Protein concentrations were normalized against internal and inter-plate controls and reported as Normalized Protein Expression (NPX) values on a log2 scale, with higher NPX values reflecting greater protein abundance. All cytokine assays were performed by Maiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) using the Olink Signature Q100 platform. Protein abundance was measured using the Olink proximity extension assay platform and reported as normalized protein expression (NPX) values on a log2 scale. Initial quality control was performed according to Olink standard procedures using internal incubation controls, extension controls, amplification controls, and external inter-plate controls to ensure compliant assay performance and normalization across samples. The limit of detection (LOD) for each protein was defined based on assay-specific background levels derived from negative controls in the Olink platform. Prior to downstream analyses, proteins with 30% of samples below the LOD threshold were excluded to ensure reliable quantification. For the remaining proteins, values below the LOD were replaced with half of the corresponding LOD value to account for left-censored measurements, following commonly used practices in Olink data analysis.

Because no missing values (NA) were observed, the filtered NPX dataset was used directly for all subsequent statistical analyses (Cui et al., 2025).

Antigen structure and epitope prediction

The antigen structures of CV1 and CV2, as well as the E and C proteins, were predicted using the AlphaFold3 online platform (https://alphafoldserver.com). Epitope prediction was performed using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB, https://www.iedb.org/). Linear B-cell epitopes were predicted using the Bepipred Linear Epitope Prediction algorithm, and predictions with scores≥0.9 were considered high-confidence candidate epitopes. For T cell epitope prediction, the NetMHCpan 4.1 EL algorithm was used to identify potential MHC class I and class II epitopes. Peptide lengths were set to 9 for MHC class I and 15 for MHC class II, and the human 27-allele panel was selected for both analyses. Predicted epitopes with a percentile rank≤0.01 were considered high-confidence candidates. To further evaluate the reliability of predicted MHC-binding epitopes, molecular docking was performed using the HDOCK server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/). Predicted peptide epitopes were docked with MHC-I (PDB ID: 2XPG) and MHC-II (PDB ID: 1DLH) structures obtained from the Protein DataBank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Docking simulations were conducted using default parameters, which employ a hybrid strategy combining template-based modeling and ab initio free docking. Template information was enabled, while symmetric multimer docking was not applied. No experimental restraints were used, and no binding site residues or inter-residue distance constraints were specified. A total of 100 docking models were generated and ranked according to docking score, where more negative values indicate higher binding likelihood. The model with the lowest docking score was selected as the final predicted complex for downstream analysis. Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of proportional cell subsets, BCR, TCR and cytokines (NPX value) were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0; Graph Pad Software Inc, USA) and R (version 3.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Temporal comparisons across the three time points (VGB, V, and CV) were conducted separately for the V1 and V2 groups using repeated-measures analyses (Friedman test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test), thereby capturing within-subject longitudinal dynamics. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, whereas comparisons involving more than two independent groups were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. For analyses involving multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR, ∗q < 0.05), and statistical significance was reported as adjusted q-values, with a threshold for significance of q < 0.05. The Olink multiplex cytokine assay was used to assess cytokines associated with T-cell-mediated immunity (e.g., interleukins, chemokines) and inflammatory regulation to validate the differences in T cell activation between the two vaccines. The T-cell activation score (TAS) was defined as the equally weighted average of Z-scored NPX values for IFN-γ, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, IL-12 B, IL-18, IL-18R1, TNFRSF9, TRAIL, CD40, CX3CL1, and CD8A. The Th1 score was defined as the equally weighted average of Z-scored NPX values for CXCL11, CXCL10, CXCL9, IL-12 B, and IFN-γ. The Th17 score was defined as the equally weighted average of Z-scored NPX values for CCL20, IL-17C, and IL-17 A. Statistical analyses (Kruskal-Wallis H test [FDR, Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, CI: 0.95]) were performed with q < 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance.
