A Unified Formal Theory on the Logical Limits of Symbol Grounding
Zhangchi Liu

Abstract
This paper synthesizes a series of formal proofs to construct a unified theory on the logical limits of the Symbol Grounding Problem. We demonstrate through a four-stage argument that meaning within a formal system must arise from a process that is external, dynamic, and non-algorithmic. First, we prove that any purely symbolic system, devoid of external connections, cannot internally establish a consistent foundation for meaning due to self-referential paradoxes. Second, we extend this limitation to systems with any finite, static set of pre-established meanings, proving they are inherently incomplete. Third, we demonstrate that the very "act" of connecting an internal symbol to an external meaning cannot be a product of logical inference within the system but must be an axiomatic, meta-level update. Finally, we prove that any attempt to automate this update process using a fixed, external "judgment" algorithm will inevitably construct a larger, yet equally incomplete, symbolic system. Together, these conclusions formally establish that the grounding of meaning is a necessarily open-ended, non-algorithmic process, revealing a fundamental, Gödel-style limitation for any self-contained intelligent system.



Keywords:
Symbol Grounding Problem, Formal Proof, Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems, Self-Reference, Dynamic Semantics, Embodied Cognition, Metalogic, Limitations of Computationalism.

1
Introduction

The Symbol Grounding Problem, famously articulated by Harnad
[
2
]
, questions how symbols within a formal system (like a dictionary or a computer program) can acquire intrinsic meaning, rather than being endlessly defined by other ungrounded symbols. This paper aims to provide a single, unified, and formally rigorous answer by demonstrating that the problem’s solution cannot lie within any closed formal system. We will prove that grounding is not a property or state that a system can achieve and finalize, a continuous, open-ended, and non-algorithmic process.

Our argument unfolds in a logical progression over four main sections, synthesizing a series of core theorems.

First, in Section 2, we establish the foundational formal framework and prove that a
purely symbolic system
—one with no external connections—is logically incapable of self-grounding. By constructing a self-referential sentence about "groundability," we show that any such system with sufficient expressive power falls into inconsistency or incompleteness, akin to Gödel’s and Tarski’s limitative theorems
[
1
,
6
]
.

Second, in Section 3, we address the seemingly obvious solution: providing the system with an initial, finite set of grounded symbols derived from experience. We prove that any
statically grounded system
is also incomplete. A system can always formulate new, provable truths about its own limitations that cannot be grounded in its original, static experiential base. This establishes the necessity for grounding to be a
dynamic process
.

Third, in Section 4, we investigate the nature of this dynamic process. What is the "grounding act" that expands the system’s connection to the world? We prove that this act is
non-inferable
. No internal, condition-triggered logical rule can deduce a command to ground a new symbol without risking a contradiction where the system’s inference invalidates its own proven theorems. This suggests the grounding act is an axiomatic, meta-level update, not a logical deduction.

Finally, in Section 5, we confront the ultimate computationalist question: can this meta-level update process be simulated by a fixed, external, algorithmic "judgment system"? We prove that any such attempt is futile. The combination of the original system and the judgment system forms a larger, but still closed, "super-system" which is itself subject to the same incompleteness. This proves that the grounding process is fundamentally
non-algorithmic
.

Together, these four proofs paint a complete picture: a system of meaning must, by logical necessity, be an infinitely open universe. Any attempt to draw a final boundary around it or to prescribe a fixed set of rules for its growth will fail, as it cannot account for the meaning of the boundary or the rules themselves.

2
The Formal Framework and the Impossibility of Self-Grounding

We begin by defining a formal framework to precisely discuss symbols, definitions, and grounding.

Definition 2.1

(Symbolic Language)
.

A symbolic language
\mathcal{L}
is a triple
\langle S,D,G\rangle
, where:

•

S
is a non-empty, countable set of symbols.

•

D
is a
Definition Function
,
D:S\to\mathcal{P}(S)
, which maps each symbol
s\in S
to its direct definitional set.

•

G
is the
Grounding Set
,
G\subseteq S
. Symbols in
G
are postulated to be directly grounded, and for all
g\in G
, we require
D(g)=\emptyset
.

Definition 2.2

(Definitional Closure)
.

For a symbol
s\in S
, its
Definitional Closure

C(s)
is the smallest set satisfying: (1)
s\in C(s)
, and (2) if
s^{\prime}\in C(s)
and
s^{\prime\prime}\in D(s^{\prime})
, then
s^{\prime\prime}\in C(s)
.
C(s)
contains all symbols used directly or indirectly to define
s
.

Definition 2.3

(Groundability)
.

A symbol
s\in S
is
groundable
if and only if its definitional closure intersects with the grounding set:
\text{isGroundable}(s)\iff C(s)\cap G\neq\emptyset
.

Definitional closure
C(s)=\{s,s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},g\}
. Since
C(s)\cap G\neq\emptyset
,
s
is groundable. A purely symbolic system would lack the node
g
.

Our first investigation concerns a system that is entirely self-contained.

Definition 2.4

(Purely Symbolic System)
.

A symbolic language
\mathcal{L}
is a
Purely Symbolic System
if its grounding set is empty, i.e.,
G=\emptyset
.

To prove our main theorem, we assume the system
\mathcal{L}
has sufficient expressive power to encompass Peano Arithmetic, allowing for Gödel numbering and self-reference
[
1
,
5
]
.

Axiom 2.1

(Arithmetization & Expressibility)
.

There exists a Gödel numbering function
\ulcorner\cdot\urcorner
for all symbols and formulas. Consequently, there exists a predicate
Groundable(n)
in
\mathcal{L}
which is true if and only if "the number
n
is the Gödel number of a groundable formula."

Axiom 2.2

(Diagonal Lemma)
.

For any predicate
P(x)
in
\mathcal{L}
, there exists a sentence
\psi
such that the system can prove
\psi\leftrightarrow P(\ulcorner\psi\urcorner)
.

Theorem 2.1

(Impossibility of Self-Grounding)
.

Any purely symbolic system that satisfies Axioms 2.5 and 2.6 cannot internally provide a complete and consistent determination of groundability for all its propositions.

Proof.

1.

In a purely symbolic system,
G=\emptyset
. Thus, for any symbol
s
,
C(s)\cap G=\emptyset
. By Definition 2.3, no symbol is groundable.

2.

Now, assume for contradiction that the system can define a predicate
Groundable(x)
for its own propositions. Define a new predicate
Ungr(x)\equiv\neg Groundable(x)
.

3.

By the Diagonal Lemma (Axiom 2.6), there exists a sentence
P_{\text{ungroundable}}
such that the system proves:

P_{\text{ungroundable}}\leftrightarrow Ungr(\ulcorner P_{\text{ungroundable}}\urcorner)\quad\text{which is}\quad P_{\text{ungroundable}}\leftrightarrow\neg Groundable(\ulcorner P_{\text{ungroundable}}\urcorner)

This sentence asserts its own ungroundability.

4.

We analyze the groundability of
P_{\text{ungroundable}}
.

•

Case A: Assume
P_{\text{ungroundable}}
is groundable.
Then
Groundable(\ulcorner P_{\text{ungroundable}}\urcorner)
is true. The equivalence implies the system proves
\neg P_{\text{ungroundable}}
. A consistent system cannot have a true, groundable proposition whose negation it proves. This is a contradiction.

•

Case B: Assume
P_{\text{ungroundable}}
is ungroundable.
Then
\neg Groundable(\ulcorner P_{\text{ungroundable}}\urcorner)
is true. The equivalence implies the system proves
P_{\text{ungroundable}}
. Here, the system proves a sentence whose content asserts its own lack of a semantic foundation. This creates a rupture between syntactic provability and semantic groundability, as a consistent system should not prove sentences that are, by its own definitions, meaningless.

5.

Both cases lead to a contradiction or paradox. Therefore, a purely symbolic system cannot consistently and completely define its own groundability predicate.

∎

This theorem establishes that meaning cannot arise from a closed loop of definitions, a conclusion that echoes the foundational crises addressed by Tarski and Kripke
[
6
,
3
]
. It necessitates an external element: a non-empty grounding set
G
.

3
The Incompleteness of Statically Grounded Systems

The natural solution is to provide the system with a non-empty, but finite, grounding set
G
based on a set of initial experiences. We call such a system "statically grounded."

Definition 3.1

(Statically Grounded System)
.

A symbolic language
\mathcal{L}=\langle S,D,G\rangle
is a
Statically Grounded System
if its grounding set
G
is non-empty and finite, i.e.,
0<|G|<\infty
.

However, we will now prove that even this is insufficient. Any static foundation will eventually prove to be incomplete.

Theorem 3.1

(Limitation of Static Grounding)
.

For any consistent, statically grounded system
\mathcal{L}
, there always exists a sentence
P_{new}
that is provable in
\mathcal{L}
, but
P_{new}
itself cannot be grounded by the set
G
.

Proof.

1.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a statically grounded system with its finite grounding set
G
.

2.

Since
G
is finite, the property of "being groundable by
G
" is recursive. Therefore, the system can express a specific predicate
Groundable_{G}(x)
, which is true if and only if the formula with Gödel number
x
is groundable by the set
G
.

3.

We apply the Diagonal Lemma to the predicate
\neg Groundable_{G}(x)
. This yields a sentence
P_{new}
such that the system proves:

P_{new}\leftrightarrow\neg Groundable_{G}(\ulcorner P_{new}\urcorner)

The informal reading of
P_{new}
is: "
This sentence is not groundable by the set G
".

4.

We analyze this sentence in the metalanguage:

•

Case A: Assume
P_{new}
is groundable by
G
.
Then
Groundable_{G}(\ulcorner P_{new}\urcorner)
is true. The equivalence implies the system proves
\neg P_{new}
. This contradicts the system’s consistency. Thus,
P_{new}
cannot be groundable by
G
.

•

Case B: Assume
P_{new}
is not groundable by
G
.
Then
\neg Groundable_{G}(\ulcorner P_{new}\urcorner)
is true. The equivalence implies the system proves
P_{new}
.

5.

From the assumption of consistency, we conclude two things: (1) The system proves
P_{new}
, and (2)
P_{new}
is not groundable by
G
.

6.

This completes the proof. We have constructed a sentence that is true within the system’s logic, yet its meaning transcends the system’s entire original experiential basis
G
.

∎

This theorem reveals that grounding cannot be a one-time event. To understand the new truth
P_{new}
, the system must expand its grounding set, for example, by acquiring a new experience related to its own cognitive limitations. This implies that the grounding set
G
must be
dynamically expanding
. The argument’s structure is a direct application of Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem to the problem of semantics
[
1
,
5
]
.

4
The Non-Inferable Nature of the Grounding Act

If the grounding set must expand, what is the nature of this expansion? Is the "grounding act"—the connection of a new symbol to an external experience—a logical deduction? We will now prove that it is not.

Definition 4.1

(Deductive Symbolic System)
.

A deductive symbolic system
\mathcal{S}
is a quadruple
\langle S,D,G,\vdash\rangle
, where
\vdash
is a provability relation defined by axioms and rules of inference.

Definition 4.2

(The Grounding Act)
.

A
Grounding Act

A(s,g)
is a meta-level operation that updates the system’s definition function, adding a new grounded symbol
g
to the definitional set of a previously ungrounded symbol
s
.

Theorem 4.1

(The Non-Inferability of the Grounding Act)
.

In a consistent deductive symbolic system
\mathcal{S}
, there exists no general, internal, and condition-triggered logical rule that can deduce a command to ground any of its provable but ungrounded sentences.

Proof.

1.

From Theorem 3.2, we know we can construct a sentence
P_{new}
such that (a)
\mathcal{S}\vdash P_{new}
(it is provable) and (b)
P_{new}
is ungrounded by the current set
G
.

2.

Assume for contradiction that there exists an internal "Grounding Principle" in
\mathcal{S}
. This principle would state: "For any provable sentence
\phi
, if
\phi
is ungrounded, then the system must deduce a command to execute the grounding act
A(\phi,g)
."

3.

Applying this assumed principle to
P_{new}
, the system would deduce a command to ground
P_{new}
. Let’s say it executes
A(P_{new},g)
.

4.

This leads to a direct contradiction. After the grounding act, the system is in a new state
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

•

In
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, the sentence
P_{new}
has, by definition, become
groundable
.

•

However, the
content
of the sentence
P_{new}
is "This sentence is not groundable by the set G."

•

This assertion, which was true in
\mathcal{S}
, has now become
false
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

5.

The contradiction is as follows: The system
\mathcal{S}
used its rules of inference to prove a theorem (
P_{new}
) and then used another rule of inference (our assumed Grounding Principle) to deduce an action that directly falsified its own theorem. A consistent logical system cannot have rules that invalidate its own conclusions.

6.

Therefore, the initial assumption must be false. The grounding act cannot be the result of logical inference within the system.

∎

This proves that grounding is not an inference but an
update
. It is not a conclusion reached within the system’s existing rules but is analogous to adding a new axiom from outside. This provides a formal basis for the "Aha!" moments or paradigm shifts in cognition, which are not deductions but restructurings of the axiomatic foundation itself.

5
The Incompleteness of Algorithmic Judgment

The final question remains: if the grounding act is a meta-level update, can this update process be governed by a separate, more powerful, but still algorithmic, "judgment system"?

Definition 5.1

(Cognitive Super-System)
.

A cognitive super-system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
consists of:

•

An
Object System

\mathcal{S}_{1}=\langle S,D,G,\vdash_{1}\rangle
, representing the agent’s knowledge.

•

A
Judgment System

\mathcal{S}_{2}=\langle M,R,\vdash_{2}\rangle
, an external system with a set of
fixed, algorithmic judgment rules

R
. The rules in
R
take the state of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
as input and output grounding commands.

Theorem 5.1

(Limitation of Logical Judgment Systems)
.

Any cognitive super-system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
with a fixed, algorithmic judgment system is itself an incomplete deductive system.

Proof.

1.

The core insight is that because the judgment rules
R
of
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are fixed and algorithmic, the behavior of the entire super-system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
(including all its future state updates) is completely determined by a fixed set of initial axioms and rules.

2.

Therefore, the super-system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is itself a larger, more complex, but ultimately
static deductive symbolic system
.

3.

We can now apply Theorem 3.2 (the limitation of static grounding) directly to this super-system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

4.

For the system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
, we can construct a Gödel-style sentence
P^{*}
with the content: "
This sentence cannot be grounded by the rules of the super-system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
."

5.

As per the proof of Theorem 3.2, we know that if
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is consistent, then (a)
\mathcal{S}^{*}\vdash P^{*}
(it is provable within the super-system’s logic) and (b)
P^{*}
is not groundable by the rules of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
(which includes the judgment rules of
\mathcal{S}_{2}
).

6.

This means the judgment system
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, which was designed to handle all necessary grounding acts, has failed. It is faced with a truth
P^{*}
that its own rules cannot ground. To ground
P^{*}
, one would need a yet higher-order judgment system
\mathcal{S}_{3}
, leading to an infinite regress.

7.

This hierarchical paradox is analogous to arguments against strong AI, such as Searle’s Chinese Room, where adding more layers of symbolic processing fails to bridge the gap to semantics
[
4
]
. Therefore, any attempt to fully simulate the open, dynamic grounding process with a fixed, algorithmic system is logically bound to fail.

∎

6
Conclusion

This paper has constructed a unified, four-part formal proof demonstrating the fundamental logical limits of symbol grounding. Our chain of reasoning leads to a single, powerful conclusion:
a system of meaning must, by logical necessity, be an infinitely open universe.

We have shown that:

1.

Meaning cannot be generated internally within a closed symbolic loop (
impossibility of self-grounding
). It requires an external connection.

2.

This external connection cannot be a one-time, fixed set of experiences. Any static foundation is inherently incomplete, necessitating a
dynamic process
of continuous grounding.

3.

The act of expanding this connection the grounding act is not a logical deduction but a
non-inferable, axiomatic update
to the system’s foundation.

4.

This update process cannot be fully captured by any fixed, external algorithm. The meta-system responsible for grounding must itself be open and
non-algorithmic
.

This result places a Gödelian limit on strong computationalism
[
1
,
4
]
, suggesting that core aspects of intelligence, such as understanding and the "Aha!" moment of insight, are not reducible to a fixed set of computations. The essence of meaning lies not in the symbols themselves or even in a static map to the world, but in the open-ended, non-algorithmic process of continuously and creatively updating that map.

Appendix A
Formal Construction of the Groundability Predicate

This appendix provides a more detailed formal construction of the
Groundable(n)
predicate within a system containing Peano Arithmetic, demonstrating that Axiom 2.5 is well-founded.

1.

Arithmetization of Syntax:
We assume standard Gödel numbering. The definition function
D
is assumed to be recursive, so the relation "
m\in D(n)
" can be represented by a recursive arithmetic predicate
Def(\ulcorner m\urcorner,\ulcorner n\urcorner)
. The predicate for membership in the grounding set,
is\_in\_G(\ulcorner g\urcorner)
, is syntactically well-formed, even if its extension is empty (as in a purely symbolic system).

2.

Arithmetization of Definitional Closure:
The definitional closure
C(s)
is the transitive closure of the relation defined by
D
. The transitive closure of a recursive relation is itself recursive. We can define a predicate
InClosure(m,n)
which is true iff
\ulcorner m\urcorner\in C(\ulcorner n\urcorner)
. This is expressible by stating that there exists a finite sequence (coded as a single number)
s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
such that
\ulcorner s_{0}\urcorner=n
,
\ulcorner s_{k}\urcorner=m
, and for all
i<k
,
Def(\ulcorner s_{i+1}\urcorner,\ulcorner s_{i}\urcorner)
holds.

3.

Definition of Groundable(n):
With the above components, we can formally define the
Groundable(n)
predicate as:

Groundable(n)\equiv\exists g(is\_in\_G(g)\land InClosure(g,n))

Since all components of this formula are recursive, the predicate
Groundable(n)
is itself recursive and thus expressible within the system.
