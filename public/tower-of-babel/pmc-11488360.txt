The role of uric acid in the risk of hypertension developed from prehypertension: a five-year Chinese urban cohort study
Authors: Jun Zhu, Lingyu Shen, Shifen Jia, Wei Wang, Yaqing Xiong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUric acid as a prominent causal factor in the pathogenesis of hypertension is well recognized. Nevertheless, the influence of uric acid on the transition from prehypertension to hypertension within the Chinese population remains understudied.MethodsA cohort of 1,516 prehypertensive individuals, aged 35 to 84 years, underwent recruitment following a comprehensive health assessment in 2017 and subsequent re-evaluation in 2022. Baseline characteristics and relevant clinical data were collected. The analytical approach encompassed multiple logistic regression and propensity score matching.ResultsOver 5 years, the cumulative incidence of hypertension amounted to 35.1%, with 33.9% in males and 37.3% in females, respectively. Notably, prehypertensive subjects concomitant with hyperuricemia exhibited a higher cumulative incidence of hypertension in comparison to the non-hyperuricemic counterparts (40.7% vs. 34.0%, p = 0.041). Multiple logistic regression unveiled a significant association between hyperuricemia and heightened hypertension risk (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 1.44; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.05–1.98; p = 0.022). Nonetheless, this association did not reach statistical significance when examining female subjects (adjusted OR = 1.10; 95% CI, 0.58–2.09; p = 0.781) or participants aged ≥ 60 years (adjusted OR = 1.07; 95% CI, 0.61–1.88; p = 0.814). Further validation through propensity score matching affirmed that subjects afflicted by hyperuricemia experienced a substantially elevated risk of transitioning from prehypertension to hypertension over the course of five years compared with the non-hyperuricemic counterparts (41.3% vs. 32.3%, p = 0.045), after adjusting for 12 covariates including age and gender. Hyperuricemia emerged as an independent risk factor predisposing individuals to the development of hypertension from a prehypertensive state.ConclusionThis observation prompted the formulation of a hypothesis suggesting that ameliorating elevated uric acid levels may potentially mitigate the progression from prehypertension to hypertension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-024-01421-2.Keywords: Serum uric acid, Hypertension, Prehypertension

Normotensive subjects with hyperuricemia have a higher incidence of hypertension than those with normouricemia. Pilot clinical trials fail to show the consistent beneficial effect of lowering serum urate in hypertensive individuals with hyperuricemia.• Hyperuricemia remains an independent risk factor for hypertension developed from prehypertension in Chinese urban population.• Subsequent clinical trials of uric acid intervention might focus on the prehypertensive population. Background

Hyperuricemia has been identified as a risk factor for gout and kidney stones, coupled with considerable evidence linking elevated uric acid levels to diabetes, coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, and renal dysfunction, leading to widespread attention to the hypothesis that hyperuricemia as a risk factor mediates the cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. Recent studies have highlighted that uric acid, once considered merely a clinical marker, may indeed play a substantial etiological role in the genesis of hypertension, independently from traditional risk factors [3–5]. The potential for uric acid reduction to mitigate blood pressure or avert the onset of hypertension has consequently gained recognition [6, 7].

Until now, the majority of studies have focused on observing the morbidity of hypertension in normotensive subjects with hyperuricemia within longitudinal follow-up cohorts, or on investigating the antihypertensive efficacy of uric acid -lowering therapies among individuals suffering from hypertension. Scant attention has been directed towards scrutinizing the impact of uric acid on the progression to transition from prehypertension to hypertension [8–11]. In comparison to normotensive individuals, those with prehypertension may possess a more exigent need to modify pertinent risk factors, thereby retarding the progression of their blood pressure disorders [12]. Furthermore, considering that prehypertension holds a greater possibility of advancing to hypertension compared to normotension, conducting clinical observations or trials to explore the influence of uric acid on the development of hypertension within this demographic may require diminished sample sizes and abbreviated follow-up periods. Additionally, individuals suffering from stable hypertension are more prone to possess anomalies in renal structure and/or may have undergone treatment involving angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, either of which could potentially dampen the therapeutic effectiveness on blood pressure of uric acid-lowering intervention. As a result, prehypertensive individuals may emerge as an appropriate target cohort for interventions centered around uric acid.

In this context, we conducted a retrospective cohort study aimed at evaluating the risk of hypertension following a 5-year exposure to hyperuricemia among urban Chinese adults diagnosed with prehypertension. This may provide relevant evidence for the subsequent study on uric acid intervention preventing hypertension within the prehypertensive cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design and study subjects

The present retrospective, single-center cohort study was conducted in the center of chronic disease and health management, geriatric hospital of Nanjing medical university, China. A website-based practitioner-patient interaction platform (www.js-jk.com) has been used to record the archives of the participants who underwent annual medical examinations since 2017. The platform has two login options: one for medical staff to enter or review medical records and communicate with participants, and another for patients to access their own case data, sign the informed consent, and communicate with health administrators. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the geriatric hospital of Nanjing medical university (Permit Number: NJMU-GH-2022011), and electronic informed consent was authorized by all subjects.

A total of 10,974 subjects aged between 35 and 84 years old, with the complete physical examination data in 2017, was enrolled in the initial assessment. Prehypertensive subjects were identified if their systolic blood pressure was between 120 and 139 mm Hg or their diastolic blood pressure was between 80 and 89 mm Hg. After excluding those with histories of cardiovascular disease (CVD), tumors, cognitive impairment, and received uric acid–lowering medications at baseline, 1,516 prehypertensive subjects with useful physical examination data in 2022 were pooled for the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the study enrollment. This study eventually enrolled 1516 subjects with prehypertension in the center of chronic disease and health management from 2017 to 2022

Data collection

Baseline characteristics and clinical data were collected by the well-trained center staffs when the subjects participated in the health physical examination at the center. Data on gender, age, smoking status, and medical history were directly inputted in the web-based electronic questionnaire during a face-to-face consultation. Physical examinations, including body weight, height and blood pressure measurement were carried out by the nurses. Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast (≥ 12 h). Clinical indicators such as fasting blood glucose (FBG), renal/liver functions, a routine blood test, lipid profiles, and uric acid were measured by the hospital central laboratory within 2 h after collection.

Terms and definitions

Hypertension was defined as a condition when the subjects had a history of hypertension, received anti-hypertensive medication, or systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg. Blood pressure readings were measured using an automatic oscillometric device (HEM-5001; Omron, Tokyo, Japan) with an appropriate cuff size. After participants were seated for 5 min with keeping quiet, measurements were taken in triplicate on the same arm by an interval of 1 min, and the mean of the last two BP readings was calculated and used in for the subsequent analyses. Prehypertension was defined as a condition when subjects have a systolic blood pressure between 120- and - 139 mm Hg and/or a diastolic blood pressure between 80- and - 89 mm Hg [13].

Hyperuricemia was defined as serum uric acid level > 7.0 mg/dL in men and > 6.0 mg/dL in women [14, 15].

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the abbreviated Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula [16] (eGFR = 186 × [SCR×0.011] −1.154× [age] −0.203× [0.742 if female]; SCR was expressed as µmol/L.

Anthropometric measurements were taken with subjects in light clothing and without foot covering. The height and weight of the subjects were measured to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively, by using a measuring instrument. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m2).

Statistical analysis

Data for continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical data were expressed as percentages. Differences in continuous variables between two groups were estimated by the unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test according to whether the data fitted a normal Gaussian distribution. Chi-square test was used to compare the differences between two categorical variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were carried out to assess the strength of association between uric acid and the risk of hypertension by adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A propensity score matching model was established to assess the rate of hypertension development between subjects with hyperuricemia and without hyperuricemia, adjusting for age, gender, BMI, smoking status, baseline blood pressure, FBG, lipid profile, and eGFR. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, USA), and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of subjects with prehypertension are shown in Table 1. Among the 1,516 subjects eventually enrolled, the mean age of the total was 55.0 ± 10.1 years (range 35–84 years), including 966 (63.7%) male and 550 (36.3%) female, and 243 (16.0%) subjects were found to have hyperuricemia in 2017. Males with prehypertension were more likely to be younger, with a smoking history, and higher clinical measures for BMI, diastolic blood pressure, FBG, total triglyceride (TG), serum creatinine (Scr), urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin and globulin ratio (A/G), red blood cell counts, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), white blood cell counts, hemoglobin, and serum uric acid levels compared with female subjects (p < 0.05). The levels of systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol (TC), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and eGFR were higher in females than those in males (p < 0.05). There was not significantly difference in the concentrations of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) by gender (p > 0.05).

Cumulative incidences of hypertension over five years

The overall cumulative incidence of hypertension over five years was 35.1%, and gender showed no significant difference to the development of hypertension (males 33.9% vs. females 37.3%, p = 0.180). As shown in Fig. 2, subjects with hyperuricemia had a higher cumulative incidence of hypertension over 5 years compared to those with normouricemia (40.7% vs. 34.0%, p = 0.041), and after stratifying by gender, the cumulative incidence of hypertension in male subjects with hyperuricemia was significantly higher than that in normouricemic subjects (40.3% vs. 32.3%, p = 0.033). However, there was no significant difference observed in female subjects (42.3% vs. 36.8%, p = 0.418).

Multivariate analysis of the risk factors for hypertension from prehypertension

A multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusting for age, gender, BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, smoking status, FBG, TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, eGFR, and hyperuricemia, was performed to evaluate the risk of hypertension from prehypertension. As presented in Fig. 3, age (adjusted OR = 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02–1.05; p < 0.001), female (adjusted OR = 1.35; 95% CI, 1.03–1.78; p = 0.031), higher baseline systolic blood pressure (adjusted OR = 1.09; 95% CI, 1.06–1.10; p < 0.001) and diastolic blood pressure (adjusted OR = 1.04; 95% CI 1.02–1.06; p < 0.001), smoking status (adjusted OR = 1.36; 95%CI, 1.02–1.81; p = 0.037), and hyperuricemia (adjusted OR = 1.44; 95% CI, 1.05–1.98; p = 0.022) were significantly associated with the risk for developing hypertension. However, serum uric acid levels per 1 mg/dl increase (adjusted OR = 1.10; 95% CI, 0.98–1.24; p = 0.097) had no positive relationship with the development of hypertension after adjustment.

Discussion

Uric acid represents the terminal product of purine metabolism within the human body. Its inherent physiological functions encompass pivotal roles such as scavenging oxidant radicals, upholding the stability of endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity, regulating blood pressure in individuals with restricted dietary salt intake, and facilitating heightened adipose storage and triglyceride levels [17, 18]. However, excessive uric acid production is intricately linked to the development of cardiovascular diseases. This pathological progression involves not only the elevation of intracellular urate levels but also an increased generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during uric acid synthesis. This process can trigger inflammation, oxidative stress, and damage to endothelial function [19, 20].

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have unveiled a noteworthy association between uric acid levels and the risk of hypertension. For instance, a comprehensive meta-analysis, comprising 25 studies and a participant cohort of 97,824 individuals, highlighted a dose-response relationship between uric acid levels and the likelihood of incident hypertension within various normotensive populations [9]. Similarly, Beckera et al. proposed that maternal hyperuricemia during pregnancy, as well as hyperuricemia in early life, independently contributed to the predisposition for hypertension [10]. In this context, the use of xanthine oxidase inhibitors, which block uric acid production, has been studied as a way to reduce hypertension in both animal models and human trials. However, the results have been inconsistent [6, 21, 22]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the inclusion of subjects of varying ages, which could affect their susceptibility to uric acid-related damage. It may also stem from the proposed pathophysiological mechanism through which uric acid contributes to hypertension development. In the early stages of hypertension, uric acid is thought to exert its influence by activating the renal renin-angiotensin system and promoting inflammation. However, over time, the significance of uric acid’s contribution might pivot towards the renal role in maintaining a salt-sensitive hypertensive state. As a result, its impact may diminish or change across different phases of hypertension progression [2]. It is still unclear at what stage individuals with prehypertension stand, and whether uric acid remains an independent risk factor for the progression from prehypertension to hypertension.

Our investigation yielded compelling evidence revealing that prehypertensive individuals with hyperuricemia have an elevated risk of transitioning to hypertension within a span of five years compared to their normouricemic counterparts. Employing a multiple regression model, hyperuricemia retained its status as an independent risk factor for the progression from prehypertension to hypertension. To fortify the veracity of our findings, we employed a propensity score model utilizing a 1:1 matching strategy involving 235 hyperuricemic participants, effectively accounting for 96.7% of the total 243 participants. This approach was underpinned by comprehensive adjustment for 12 crucial baseline characteristics. The outcome of this analysis unequivocally revealed that prehypertensive individuals with hyperuricemia exhibited a substantial 27.9% higher incidence of hypertension over the course of five years compared to their counterparts with non-hyperuricemia. However, an increase of per 1 mg/dL in serum uric acid levels did not show a positive relationship with the development of hypertension. Through the spline regression model analysis, we identified a threshold effect in the association between serum uric acid levels and hypertension progression. As shown in Supplement Fig. 1, the significant association emerged when serum uric acid levels exceeded 5.5 mg/dL.

After conducting additional subgroup analysis stratified by age grouping, a clear trend emerged: a significant positive association between hyperuricemia and the incidence of hypertension was observed exclusively among prehypertensive subjects under the age of 60, while no such association was found among those aged 60 and above. These findings showed an interactive effect between age grouping - elderly and non-elderly- and serum uric acid levels in influencing the risk of hypertension. This aligned with prior research investigating the transition from normal blood pressure to hypertension. For instance, a cross-sectional study involving 45,098 Korean individuals indicated that blood pressure exhibited a significant positive association with uric acid solely within the demographic of adults under the age of 55, while this association was conspicuously absent in those aged 55 and above [23]. In the context of the Chinese population, Cheng et al. also underscored a similar trend, affirming that the potency of the correlation between uric acid concentration and hypertension exhibited greater magnitude among individuals below the age of 50 [24]. This observation may be elucidated by age-related pathophysiological transformations. If serum uric acid indeed triggers premature vascular degeneration, its effect may be attenuated in elderly individuals, who may already exhibit such changes due to aging, including premature vascular degeneration and endothelial dysfunction [25]. Additionally, elderly-related alterations in renal architecture might diminish the influence of uric acid on blood pressure dynamics.

An intriguing finding in our study was the tendency for males with hyperuricemia to be more likely to progress from prehypertension to hypertension compared to females with hyperuricemia. However, we did not observe a significant interaction between gender and serum uric acid concentration in predicting the risk of developing hypertension. This result appears to contradict previous studies, which have suggested that uric acid has a more pronounced impact on blood pressure in women than in men [4, 26]. The influence of hyperuricemia on hypertension seems to be more pronounced in women, potentially due to increased xanthine oxidase activity, which is enhanced by estrogen’s role in promoting uric acid excretion [27]. This implies that for women to reach the same serum uric acid levels as men, xanthine oxidase activity, which drives uric acid production, would need to remain elevated. Xanthine oxidase also promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species, which may contribute to higher blood pressure [28]. Our study cohort included female participants with an average age of 56.5 ± 10.1 years, which is significantly higher than in similar studies where the mean age was under 50 years [26, 29]. This age difference suggests that most women in our study were postmenopausal, likely reducing the variation in estrogen levels between men and women. Additionally, we noted a substantial age gap between male and female participants in our cohort (54.1 ± 9.9 vs. 56.5 ± 10.1, p < 0.001), contrasting with age distributions in other studies where males tended to be older than females [26, 29]. This age difference may contribute to the observed discrepancies, as previous research suggests that uric acid has a more prominent effect on blood pressure dynamics in younger individuals [4, 11].

Several limitations inherent to this study warrant consideration. Firstly, the participants included in this investigation were sourced from a singular center within a specific geographical region, which could potentially introduce selection bias. Secondly, potential population selection bias may be present in our study, as we were unable to ascertain whether subjects with hyperuricemia and prehypertension had these conditions prior to 2017. This may have resulted in enrolling elderly participants who are less sensitive to the harmful effects of hyperuricemia. Thirdly, the data collection was confined to physical examinations conducted solely in 2017 and 2022, thereby precluding access to continuous monitoring data spanning the entire duration of the study period. Finally, despite adhering rigorously to the standardized blood pressure measurement protocols within the clinical setting, the potential influences of the white coat effect or masked hypertension cannot be definitively excluded.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that uric acid plays a critical role in the progression from prehypertension to hypertension. This highlights the potential appropriateness of targeting prehypertensive individuals for interventions aimed at reducing uric acid levels. However, these conclusions require validation through meticulous randomized controlled trials that are explicitly designed to assess the impacts of interventions targeting uric acid.
