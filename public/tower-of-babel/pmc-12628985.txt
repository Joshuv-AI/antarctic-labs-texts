Increasing measles vaccination coverage through supplementation with an SQ-LNS incentive in children aged 6 to 23 months: study protocol of NutriVax-Measles, a superiority pragmatic parallel cluster-randomized controlled trial in Yobe State, Northern Nigeria
Authors: Cécile Cazes, Baba Waru Goni, Nuru Suleiman Muhammad, Delphine Gabillard, Pierrot Kalubi Balonda, Loic Feuzeu, Irine Atanga, Brice Yapi, Gemma Guylène Habiyambere, Serge Danho, Eric Balestre, Katherine P Adams, Mélanie Plazy, Kevin P Q Phelan, Renaud Becquet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUndernutrition and infectious diseases form a vicious cycle that poses a dual threat to vulnerable children in low- and middle-income countries. Integrating small-quantity lipid-based nutrient supplements (SQ-LNS) distribution into routine immunization services in children 6–23 months may act as both a nutritional intervention and as an incentive for increased vaccine uptake.MethodsWe will conduct a two-arm superiority pragmatic parallel cluster-randomized controlled trial with baseline measures in 20 wards of Nguru and Karasuwa Local Government Areas, Yobe State, Northeast Nigeria. Wards will be randomly allocated 1:1 to either the standard National Program on Immunization (NPI) arm or the NutriVax arm, which combines NPI with SQ-LNS distribution. After up to 12 months of implementation, measles immunization coverage will be assessed at two levels: population-level coverage in children aged 12–23 months (primary outcome, endline cross-sectional survey) and individual-level coverage in children aged 6–12 months (key secondary outcome, longitudinal survey). Each cross-sectional survey (baseline and endline) will include 1560 participants. The longitudinal survey will enroll 600 participants. Sampling will use a multi-stage design with probability proportional to size and systematic random household selection. All outcomes will be analyzed by intention-to-treat. Qualitative interviews will assess feasibility and acceptability. Cost-efficiency differences between arms will be estimated using an incremental cost-efficiency ratio.DiscussionTo our knowledge, this is the first trial to evaluate whether a preventive nutrition supplement can also serve as an incentive to increase vaccination coverage. Findings will provide evidence to guide governments and donors in settings where high rates of undernutrition and low immunization coverage persist as major public health challenges.Trial registrationProtocol Number NCT06387511 (clinicaltrials.gov).Keywords: Immunization coverage, Small-quantity lipid-based nutrient supplements SQ-LNS, Immunization-Nutrition Integration, Undernutrition Malnutrition, Children, Nigeria, Africa, Low-middle income countries

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Undernutrition and infectious disease form a vicious cycle that poses a double threat to vulnerable children in low-income countries. Undernutrition, which drives 45% of deaths among young children [1], makes children more susceptible to infectious diseases, while vaccine-preventable diseases increase their chances of becoming undernourished. When trapped in this cycle, children are significantly more likely to die or suffer damaging health and developmental effects [2–5]. High burden of wasting (i.e., 45 million) [6] and low vaccination coverage is also a debilitating double burden for already fragile health systems. Every year in these contexts, hundreds of thousands of children are treated for wasting while experiencing repeated outbreaks of vaccine-preventable illnesses, most notably measles, which affected more than 10 million children in 2023 [7].

Small-quantity lipid-based nutrient supplements (SQ-LNS) are highly nutrient-dense and ready-to-use food-based supplements designed for preventing malnutrition and improving child survival, growth, and development [8]. Feeding a child just one sachet of SQ-LNS a day up to a year can reduce the risk of all-cause mortality by 27% [9], and has shown significant reductions of iron-deficiency anemia by 64% [10], severe wasting by 31%, stunting by 17% [11], and developmental delays by 16–19% [12]. This body of evidence led experts to call for scaling up SQ-LNS distribution in vulnerable populations [13], and release implementation operational guidance [8, 14, 15]. The recent WHO Guideline for complementary feeding of infants and young children 6–23 months of age recommended SQ-LNS for certain food-insecure contexts, with evidence judged to be of “high certainty” [16].

Vaccination prevents 3.5 to 5 million deaths each year [17]. Despite this, 15·7 million (95% UI 14·6–17·0) children worldwide are zero-dose (i.e., have not received the first dose of the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis vaccine) [18] and 22.2 million children are missing the measles vaccination [7]. In 2021, Nigeria accounted for 18% of the global burden of zero-dose children, with over 2.2 million children unvaccinated [19], contributing to an under-five mortality rate of 105 per 1000 live births—approximately 768,000 childhood deaths annually [20]. Nigeria also experiences significant measles outbreaks, with notable peaks in in conflict-affected northeastern states [21], and accounts for half of the children unvaccinated for measles and 75% of the measles deaths worldwide [22].

Substantial research exists on effective interventions to prevent undernutrition, including SQ-LNS for optimizing health and growth in children [23]. A recent Cochrane meta-analysis examined interventions to improve childhood vaccine coverage in low- and middle-income countries [24] but, to our knowledge, no study has robustly tested combining these approaches. Among the 41 randomized trials included in the Cochrane meta-analysis, only one used family food to incentivize attendance at vaccination clinics [25] while an earlier modeling simulation suggests that combining SQ-LNS with measles vaccines could significantly reduce measles morbidity and mortality [26]. Concomitantly, Gavi and the Eleanor Crook Foundation published a “Literature review and proposed learning agenda on Immunization-Nutrition Integration (INI)” in 2023, highlighting the need for formal, evidence-based evaluation of INI approaches. Integration of childhood nutrition and immunization makes sense in that services are often co-located, delivered in close succession by the same health worker, and target the same age groups [27]. Providing free SQ-LNS at health facilities could help overcome barriers such as distance and vaccine hesitancy by increasing caregiver attendance and, consequently, opportunities for vaccination and to additional health services. To reverse the dynamic of the vicious cycle between undernutrition and preventable vaccine disease, ALIMA (The Alliance for International Medical Action), a Dakar-based African medical alliance connecting NGOs and research institutes, has proposed a research agenda to assess the potential impact of jointly programming monthly supplementation with SQ-LNS, of which NutriVax-Measles trial in Nigeria is the first under this initiative.

The NutriVax-Measles study tests the hypothesis that integrating SQ-LNS distribution into routine immunization services for children 6–23 months can serve as an incentive for caregivers to seek immunization services, thereby increasing vaccine uptake. Specifically, we aim to determine whether this strategy increases measles vaccination coverage compared to routine immunization alone, while being acceptable to communities and cost-efficient for the health system. This is the first randomized controlled trial to assess whether a SQ-LNS distribution program at health facilities can also improve immunization coverage, thereby addressing two major child health challenges—undernutrition and low vaccine uptake—through a combined, scalable approach.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective of the NutriVax-Measles study aims to evaluate whether the NutriVax strategy—coupling SQ-LNS supplementation with the standard National Program on Immunization (NPI)—improves measles vaccination coverage in children aged 12–23 months, at a population level, after up to 12 months of implementation, compared to NPI alone. The main secondary objective is to determine whether the NutriVax strategy improved measles vaccination in children aged 6–12 months, compared to NPI alone, at an individual level, after up to 12 months of implementation. Other secondary objectives are to assess whether the NutriVax strategy compared to NPI alone improves uptake of all other childhood vaccines and anthropometric status at population and individual level, and increases the number of visits to healthcare facilities, notably of pediatric consultations; to identify the barriers and facilitators influencing the implementation, including acceptability and feasibility, of the SQ-LNS supplementation program within routine immunization; and to assess the cost-efficiency of the SQ-LNS supplementation program measured in terms of cost per child supplemented and vaccinated.

Trial design {8}

The NutriVax-Measles study is a superiority pragmatic parallel cluster randomized trial with baseline measurements. The study design embeds multiple populations and data collection methods: (1) a baseline and an endline cross-sectional household surveys of children aged 12–23 months conducted respectively, before and up to 12 months after the intervention implementation; (2) a longitudinal 12-month follow-up household survey of children aged 6–12 months at inclusion with last participant included before the intervention implementation; (3) two cross-sectional qualitative feasibility and acceptability surveys including parents/legal guardian of children aged 6–23 months, community representatives, community health workers, and health care providers, one after 3–4 months of the intervention implementation and one during the last 2 months of the study; (4) a caregiver cost survey of the caregivers of children from the longitudinal survey (population 2); and (5) a health facility cost survey of a randomized sub-sample of health facilities. The unit of randomization (i.e., cluster) is a ward and its associated population catchment area. Wards are sub-divisions of local government areas (LGAs)—the third administrative level in Nigeria, after the state level—and represent the smallest administrative unit used for planning and implementing health activities.

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The study will take place in the 20 wards of Karasuwa and Nguru LGAs in Northern Yobe State, Northeast Nigeria, areas severely impacted by the vicious cycle of undernutrition and infections. Undernutrition among children aged 0–59 months is high, with 30.2% stunting and 14.2% acute malnutrition (3.7% severe) [28], surpassing the regional African prevalence (6). Measles vaccination coverage is low, with only 29.9% receiving the first dose, and 39.6% of children aged 12–23 months are zero-dose for pentavalent vaccine [28]. Nutritional and immunization deficiencies are known to be exacerbated by food insecurity with only 1% of children under 2 years of age consuming a minimally acceptable diet [28]. Based on projections from Nigeria’s last official census in 2006, the estimated population is approximately 150,000 people including 7000 infants 0–24 months in Karasuwa LGA and 260,000 people including 12,000 infants 0–24 months in Nguru LGA, with an annual birth rate around 4%. Both LGAs are predominantly rural, with some peri-urban centers. In each ward, health services are provided by primary health care centers (PHCCs) and health posts (HPs) which offer basic services, including malnutrition treatment, health promotion, and immunizations.

Eligibility criteria {10}

The NutriVax-Measles study will include participants who meet specific eligibility criteria for each of the different data collection components. For the baseline and the endline cross-sectional household surveys, participants are children aged 12–23 months at the time of each survey, living in the study area, with oral informed consent obtained from the parent or legal guardian. Being already included in the longitudinal follow-up survey cohort is an exclusion criterion for the endline cross-sectional survey. The longitudinal follow-up survey targets infants aged 6–12 months at the time of inclusion, living in the study area, with written informed consent obtained from the parent or legal guardian. Presenting acute malnutrition based on WHO criteria (i.e., Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) < 125 mm, Weight-for-Height Z-score (WHZ) < − 2, or nutritional edema), known medical complications requiring referral for hospitalization, known allergies to SQ-LNS or other contraindications to SQ-LNS use, and any other condition that may interfere with protocol adherence or the ability to give informed consent, as judged by the site investigator (e.g., conditions making routine home visits and evaluation difficult or compromising retention in the study), are exclusion criteria. The qualitative feasibility and acceptability surveys will include purposely various profiles of parents or legal guardians of children aged 6–23 months, community representatives, community health workers and health care workers, all residing or working in the study area, and after obtaining written informed consent. The caregiver cost survey will consist of a representative sample of parent or legal guardian who will give their informed consent for their infant to participate in the longitudinal survey and the health facility cost survey will include a representative sample of the 20 PHCCs included in the study.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Research assistants, trained in good clinical practices, will explain the study to all eligible participants for the quantitative or qualitative surveys. They are responsible for obtaining informed consent from all participants. Trial monitor and study manager will supervise research assistants to ensure compliance with GCP standards and correct implementation of consent procedures. Participation will be voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time without affecting the quality of care provided. Refusal to participate will not impact medical treatment or healthcare. Consent forms (or fingerprints in case of illiteracy) will be presented only after all questions will be answered. If illiterate, a witness will read the consent form, sign it, and the participant will provide a fingerprint. For the baseline and cross-sectional surveys, parents or guardians of eligible children will provide oral consent, as recommended by the WHO reference manual on vaccination coverage cluster surveys and in accordance with Nigerian and French ethical authorities [29].

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Information notes and consent forms for each survey type were ethically approved by the Yobe State Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC) and Inserm’s international ethics committee. These documents provided detailed information on the research objectives, personal data processing, and participants’ rights. To ensure full understanding, the documents were translated into both the local language and English.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The NutriVax-Measles study will compare two interventions randomly assigned to each cluster (i.e., ward and its catchment area) to either (1) the standard NPI in children aged 6–23 months delivered according to the Ministry of Health’s routine plans in the community and at PHCCs and HPs, named the standard NPI arm; or (2) the NutriVax program combining the NPI in children aged 6–23 months according to the Ministry of Health’s routine plans in the community and at PHCCs and HPs associated with SQ-LNS mass supplementation integrated into pre-existing services delivered at PHCCs for children 6–23 months of age, named the NutriVax arm. In addition, sensitization sessions on vaccination and recommended Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) practices in the community and at PHCCs will be part of routine activities regardless of the study arm.

Intervention description {11a}

In Yobe state, the NPI corresponds to the Nigerian vaccination schedule in use (see Table 1), with catch-up of early childhood vaccines (e.g., pentavalent 1 or 3) possible at any visit to the PHCCs or HPs in children aged beyond 14 weeks. The first dose of measles vaccine can be administered routinely at the age of 9 months and the second dose from the age of 9 months after a minimum of 4 weeks after receiving the first dose. ALIMA will support the Ministry of Health (MoH) to maintain a buffer stock of measles vaccines throughout the study in the event of a stock shortage.

The NutriVax program consists of implementing SQ-LNS distribution for up to 12 consecutive months in the clusters randomized in the NutriVax arm. SQ-LNS distribution will take place at the PHCC of each ward after the child’s vaccination status will be checked and updated either at the PHCC or at the HPs, depending on the health structure’s population catchment area. For children living in catchment areas of HPs in the NutriVax clusters, this means receiving vaccines at the HP and then going to the PHCC of their ward to receive SQ-LNS. The eligibility criteria for admission to the SQ-LNS program will be as follows: to be aged between 6 and 22 months at the time of presentation to the PHCC; to reside in a cluster in the NutriVax arm; absence of known allergies to SQ-LNS or other contraindications to SQ-LNS use; absence of acute malnutrition criteria as defined by Nigeria’s malnutrition treatment protocol and the WHO (i.e., MUAC < 125 mm or WHZ < − 2 or nutritional edema). Children with acute malnutrition will be referred to the Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition program (CMAM) in their area for treatment and resumed SQ-LNS supplementation once recovered if still eligible. If no functioning CMAM available or in case of prolonged stock-outs of nutritional treatment, SQ-LNS will be provided if the child is otherwise eligible. Caregivers will receive a monthly ration of 28 SQ-LNS sachets, with adjustments to three months’ worth in cases of limited access to health centers until 23 months of age or after having received a maximum of 12 consecutive months of rations, depending on enrollment timing. For example, a 6-month old enrolling in month 2 can receive 11 months of rations, while a 12-month old enrolling in month 8 received 4 months of ration.

Community health workers will deliver sensitization sessions on vaccination and on IYCF practices in the community, PHCCs, and HPs across the 20 clusters, by messages aligned with the MoH’s routine plans in Nigeria (i.e., Nigeria Strategy for Immunization and PHC System Strengthening 2018–2022). Key messages about vaccination will include vaccines given and remaining doses needed for full protection, return schedules for the next immunization, and managing expected side effects. Key messages about IYCF Practices, based on UNICEF-validated materials [30], will point the importance of nurturing care and recommended feeding through the first 2 years, consuming a diverse, nutritious diet and continuing breastfeeding, acute malnutrition detection using MUAC. In the NutriVax clusters only, key messages on SQ-LNS [31] will point eligibility criteria and proper usage. Standard operating procedures and specific advice sheets will be developed to harmonize awareness-raising activities between all clusters at community and health facility level.

NPI and sensitization activities will be integrated into existing services without adding staff, maintaining alignment with available human resources but financial incentives will be provided to compensate MoH staff, including 10 community mobilizers per clusters for their additional workload. In addition, two positions of SQ-LNS distributors will be funded at each PHCC of the NutriVax arm because these positions do not exist in the MoH plans.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

All parents or guardians of eligible children for the intervention will be free to attend or to discontinue proposed activities in each arm, as explained and specified during the informed consent procedure and in the study information notice.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Community engagement and adherence were promoted through meetings with community representatives and local MoH nutrition and immunization actors to explain the study’s objectives, eligibility, and procedures, ensuring alignment with local customs since the preparation phase. Ward randomization will be conducted publicly in each LGA in the local languages in the presence of MoH and community stakeholders. During the trial, joint supervisions by representatives and Nigerian co-investigators will ensure protocol adherence and context-specific implementation, supporting results uptake. An intermediate qualitative study will be conducted 3 months after the intervention implementation, for understanding barriers and facilitators and revealing any issues requiring some adaptation to ensure successful implementation and adherence to the study protocols by all stakeholders in the study.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

In both arms of the study, participants or beneficiaries of the research intervention will receive all other routine preventive and curative care, as well as follow-up procedures in accordance with national guidelines, both at community and health facility level.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Non-applicable. This study allows to reinforce preventive activity procedures part of national guidelines.

Outcomes {12}

The primary outcome will be the measles 1 vaccine coverage, defined as the proportion of children aged 12–23 months in the endline cross-sectional survey who have received at least one dose of measles vaccine, as reported on their vaccination card. The key secondary outcome will be one additional dose of measles vaccine coverage, defined as the proportion of children aged 6–12 months at inclusion in the longitudinal follow-up survey, who have received at least one additional dose of measles vaccine, as reported on their vaccination card, administered between inclusion and the end of follow-up. The qualitative survey will describe the facilitators and challenges relating to access to healthcare, the community’s knowledge, perceptions, and influences toward vaccination, the specific barriers, or facilitators to measles vaccination, in both arms. In the NutriVax group, outcomes will include acceptability, adherence, practices, trust, and scalability of SQ-LNS distributions. Between the final survey and the interim survey, changes in certain results could be observed.

The main outcomes of the cost study will be to (1) estimate the incremental cost per additional child age 12–23 months who receives at least one dose of the measles vaccine after up to 12 months of implementation of the NutriVax program, and (2) estimate the incremental cost per additional dose of measles vaccine received among children age 12–23 months after up to 12 months of implementation of the NutriVax program. Other cost study outcomes will include the incremental cost per additional fully vaccinated, the incremental cost of integrating SQ-LNS into the standard NPI per child who receives SQ-LNS, the incremental cost per zero dose child avoided, and incremental cost per case of acute malnutrition, stunting, and underweight averted.

Sample size {14}

For both the main and the key secondary outcome, we determined the sample size using an expecting baseline measles vaccination coverage of 30% (based on vaccination cards) [28], a conservative intra-cluster correlation of 0.2 [32], a 5% alpha risk, and a power of 90% for the primary outcome, 80% for the key secondary outcome and unusable/missing data of 5%. To detect at least a 10 percentage-point difference in measles vaccination coverage between arms, 78 children per cluster are required at baseline and endline, totaling ~ 1560 children per cross-sectional survey and 30 children per cluster, totaling ~ 600 children for the longitudinal survey. Recruitment, conducted offline across villages, may slightly exceed targets, with totals finalized daily. Sample sizes were calculated using the Shiny calculator [33].

Recruitment {15}

The participants will be included at community level for baseline, endline cross-sectional, and longitudinal surveys. Household and participants will be selected through a multi-stage sampling method following the WHO reference manual for vaccination coverage cluster surveys [32] and using the UNICEF tools used for Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) [34]. The sampling will be carried out in three stages in each randomized cluster, namely, (1) random selection of settlements in each cluster using the probability proportional to size method; (2) systematic random selection of household based on population estimates provided by the MoH, ensuring alignment with local health plans; and (3) child selection according to eligibility criteria specific to each survey criteria with oldest one included for cross-sectional surveys and youngest for longitudinal surveys if multiple children are eligible in a household. Households will be defined as individuals or groups sharing food, livelihood, and recognizing one head [28]. For the baseline and endline cross-sectional survey, we will prepare specific sampling plan stage 1 and 2 for each survey, allowing selecting 5 settlements per cluster (i.e., 50 per arm), for including a maximum of 16 children aged 12–23 months per settlement meeting eligibility criteria (i.e., 78 per cluster or 780 per arm). For the longitudinal survey, the sampling plan stage 1 will allow selecting 4 settlements per cluster (i.e., 40 per arm), for including a maximum of 8 babies aged 6–12 months per settlement meeting eligibility criteria (i.e., 32 per cluster or 320 per arm). For the stage 1, we will use the sampling frame listing all settlements per ward, per LGA with population estimates provided by the MoH, in order to apply the WHO probability proportional to size method for selecting settlements in each cluster. In large settlements randomly selected during stage 1, where the number of households exceeds 240, household selection can be logistically challenging due to extensive walking distances and time constraints, limiting the feasibility of completing data collection within a single day. To address this, large settlements will be segmented with the help to CHWs and community representatives, as follows: those with over 900 households will be divided into four segments, those with more than 480 into two segments. For the stage 2, we will use the template of MICS survey for systematic selection of households for surveys oversampling households with children under age 5. It will specify the number of segments, the number of households per segment (after segmentation), the number of households to be selected per segment, the interval per segment, and the random start in each segment. After a household selection, the trained research staff will check whether a child meet the eligibility criteria and may proceed for an inclusion after informed consent. Detailed standardized operational procedures and training modules, based on the MICS manual and MICS tools for mapping and selecting household, will be developed, so as to ensure consistent application of the sampling plan across all clusters by trained research teams and for each type survey. A planning for recruitment will be developed specifically for each type of survey.

For the qualitative surveys, 12 clusters (six in each LGA: three in the NutriVax arm and three in the standard NPI arm) will be selected to ensure representation of both well-covered wards by PHCCs, PHCs, and health posts and less-covered wards in each arm and each LGA. The selection of eligible participants will be based to ensure a diversity of profiles according to the following characteristics: (1) location, child’s age, family socioeconomic status, household composition and access or not to PHCC, PHC, or health post for parents or guardians of children aged 6–23 months; (2) sex, years of experiences, health area, type of health structure for healthcare workers; (3) years of experiences to reflect varied levels of engagement with community members and proximity to health facilities to understand access-related challenges for CHWs; and (4) roles and perspectives by selecting representatives from different sections or neighborhoods within the catchment area and PHCC coverage for community representative. The inclusion location will be both in PHCCs or at community level for the parents/guardians of children 6–23 months and CWHs; both in PHCCs and health posts for healthcare workers; in local councils, religious organization for community representatives.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Data will be collected by trained research assistants on sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, family size, and parental literacy level: MUAC, nutritional edema and breastfeeding practices; vaccination status based on vaccination cards or maternal recall using WHO case report form [32], morbidity and hospitalization data, during the baseline and endline cross-sectional surveys. In the longitudinal survey, similar variables as the cross-sectional surveys, with additional data on minimum dietary diversity [35], including the Household Hunger Scale [36]: travel, lodging, and opportunity costs to inform the caregiver cost survey, will be collected at baseline and every 4 months for 1 year or four home visits in total. Vaccination cards will be anonymously photographed for verification during data cleaning as recommended [32]. The aggregated monthly data will be collected from the 20 PHCCs that include vaccination activities, preventive and curative pediatric consultations, and SQ-LNS distribution activities. Qualitative data will be collected through semi-structured interviews and focus groups conducted by a trained qualitative team under national and international researcher in social sciences supervision. Interviews will explore perceptions and experiences of vaccination, SQ-LNS, access to healthcare, and integration of SQ-LNS into routine activities. Interview guides will be developed using established frameworks (e.g., Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [37], Andersen’s model [38], Levesque framework [39]) as well as including a gender-related barriers approach. In the costs survey, trained data collectors will collect data on facility-level costs in each arm, including the cost of vaccines and SQ-LNS, training, labor, supplies, equipment, and utilities for incremental cost-efficiency ratio (ICER) analysis. Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

Data from both cross-sectional and longitudinal surveys will be entered into offline-capable REDCap software by trained research assistants using tablets. An international monitor will oversee the study’s compliance with good clinical practices, ensuring data accuracy, completeness, and adherence to the protocol and regulatory requirements, through both remote and onsite monitoring. All study data will be securely stored and managed to protect participant confidentiality, with participants identified only by a unique identifier. Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The full statistical analysis plan for the study is publicly available as of September 10, 2024 [40]. Data will be analyzed as intention to treat. All randomized clusters and individuals with available endpoint data will be included in the analysis. Variables will be described using appropriate summary statistics, including means, standard deviations, medians, and confidence intervals (CIs) for quantitative data, and absolute numbers and percentages for qualitative data. Statistical testing between arms or subgroups will be conducted using appropriate tests depending on the observed frequencies (χ2 or Fisher’s exact test), or distributions (Student’s t test, or Kruskal–Wallis test) with a significance level of p < 0.05. At first intention, the primary and key secondary analysis will be conducted at the individual level, by using logistic regression models with random effects: with a binomial distribution if the events/trials data syntax is used or if necessary with a binary distribution, if an individual-level data syntax is used, depending on adjustments variables if the individual level is necessary or due to problematic of boundary effects with the binomial distribution [41, 42]; a logit link function; study arm as fixed effect; adjustment by cluster level baseline coverage estimate; ward (cluster) as random intercept term. The intervention effect will be estimated as the cluster-specific odds ratio (with 95% CI) for vaccine coverage between the intervention and control arms [43–45]. If the model does not converge due to low numbers of clusters, a cluster-level analysis will be performed as a secondary approach. In this analysis, individual data will be aggregated by cluster and summarized. A Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test will be applied, depending on the distribution of the endpoint, to assess the intervention effect. A two-sided type I error α of 5% will be used to compare endpoints between arms, without correction for multiple comparisons. For the primary outcome, we will perform a sensitive analysis, using a difference-in-difference (DiD) analysis as a robustness check, comparing the average change in the outcome variable over time between the intervention and control groups. The DiD estimate will be calculated using logistic regression, adjusting for selected covariates and including the period, the arm, and the interaction between period and arm as covariates. All estimates will be reported in percentage points and relative odds ratios (with 95% CI). Consistent findings from both the primary analysis and DiD analysis will increase confidence in the conclusions. Secondary analyses include mean changes at each visit and absolute changes compared to baseline in anthropometric parameters (MUAC, MUAC-for-age Z-scores, WHZ, weight/length-for-age) in children from the longitudinal follow-up survey. The analysis will primarily focus on individual-level data, adjusting for clustering, using mixed-effects linear regression. For secondary endpoints, all estimates will be reported with 95% confidence intervals and interpreted primarily in terms of clinical consistency and plausibility. p-values will be adjusted for multiplicity using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate at 5% [46]. Adjusted p-values below 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. For all surveys, the intra-cluster correlation will be reported at both baseline and endline, stratified by study arm. The analysis will be performed using SAS or R software.

Interim analyses {21b}

The primary and key secondary outcomes will be measured at the endline cross-sectional survey and at the final visit of the longitudinal survey, respectively; therefore, no interim analysis is planned.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

For both the primary and the key secondary outcomes, additional models will be considered for sensitivity analyses such as re-running the same model but without using the Kenward-Roger correction, using between-within method for DDF [44], model with an additional nested random effect term (LGA, cluster within LGA), or adjusted on LGA as fixed effect.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Missing data will be described for all outcomes. We anticipate a low proportion of missing data (< 5%). For the primary outcome, the only anticipated missing data is children without a vaccination card. In this case, a conservative imputation rule will be applied: absence of a vaccination card will be classified as non-vaccinated (failure). For the key secondary outcome, the two anticipated missing data are children without a vaccination card and children lost to follow-up. The primary analysis will use all available data with no imputation, i.e., without loss-to-follow-up. In addition, we will perform sensitivity analyses to assess the potential impact of missing data (for children loss-to-follow-up before additional vaccination) on the study conclusions, considering two extreme scenarios [47]: (1) all children with missing data in the NutriVax arm will be classified as not receiving an additional measles vaccine dose (failure), while those in the standard NPI arm will be classified as having received at least one additional dose (success); 2) all children with missing data in the NutriVax arm will be classified as having received at least one additional measles vaccine dose (success), while those in the standard NPI arm will be classified as not receiving an additional dose (failure). If the primary conclusions differ under extreme assumptions, a tipping point analysis will be conducted to identify the conditions under which statistical significance changes; if conclusions remain the same, no further analysis will be performed [48].

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data, and statistical code {31c}

The data will be made available on request to research teams for use in subsequent collaboration (e.g., for meta-analysis).

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

NutriVax-Measles strategy encompasses only preventive “routine care” according to national guidelines and procedures. The intervention tested may make it possible to reinforce national guidelines for the preventive monitoring of children aged between 6 and 23 months and national action plan for nutrition [49]. However, given that adverse events or unanticipated problems can occur in routine preventive care or at home in daily life in the domestic context, these data will be collected among participants of the longitudinal survey at each home follow-up visits in the database and in a registry by the field investigator, and reported to the principal’s investigators within 48 h. Participants requiring curative care will be referred to health facilities by research assistants and managed according to national guidelines by Ministry of Health healthcare workers.

Joint supervision missions of the study sites will be organized regularly between state health authorities and national main investigators.

If substantial modifications affecting participant protection, study validity, or procedures occur, written amendments will be submitted to the ethics committees for approval.

The results, whether positive or negative, will be shared through peer-reviewed publications, conferences, and clinical networks targeting academics, policymakers, clinicians, and caregivers.

Discussion

To our knowledge, NutriVax-Measles is the first study to evaluate if a supplement designed to prevent undernutrition can simultaneously incentivize vaccine uptake and thereby increase vaccination coverage among children under 2 years of age. The literature review and proposed learning agenda on Immunization-Nutrition Integration released by ECF and GAVI in November 2023 [27] combined with an additional search on PubMed in June 2025 for publications in English using the search terms “Small-quantity lipid based nutrient supplements” AND “coverage” with no date restrictions reported no studies on this topic among the ten studies referenced. NutriVax-Measles will be conducted in Northeastern Nigeria where the needs for addressing the vicious cycle between malnutrition and infectious disease are high, and where protracted armed conflict and insecurity has disrupted healthcare services. The factors underlying low vaccination coverage, including the presence of zero-dose and missed-dose children, as well as the interventions needed to address the challenges faced by immunization service delivery, are well documented and have been extensively studied in the Nigerian context [50]. Integrating SQ-LNS distributions with routine immunization activities could help address, and potentially overcome, key barriers to vaccination—such as distance to health facilities and vaccine hesitancy—by providing additional motivation for caregivers to bring their children for routine immunization.

The NutriVax-Measles study has several strengths. First, it uses a superiority pragmatic cluster randomized trial design to generate real-world evidence scientifically rigorous and relevant for policy and implementation. This superiority pragmatic approach combines the rigor of randomized controlled trials with external validity, allowing findings to be generalized to routine practice settings and similar contexts [51, 52]. Also, it supports assessing the NutriVax program within a large-scale, routine implementation, enhancing the study’s relevance for health policy and practice while retaining methodological rigor. Second, the multi-modal data collection approach allows for a comprehensive measurement of measles vaccination coverage as main endpoint. Measles is a highly contagious airborne viral disease, with unacceptable mortality particularly among under-5 children. Measles cases have been rising worldwide recently, with 57 countries experiencing large or disruptive outbreaks in 2023, whereas 95% or greater coverage with two doses of measles-containing vaccine is needed to protect communities from outbreaks [53, 54]. Secondary endpoints will allow us to assess the effect of the strategy regarding all others vaccines of the NPI, including on the proportion of children who are zero dose. Impact analysis will further enhance the study’s validity. Third, the embedded implementation research component, including cost-efficiency analysis, will provide valuable insights for program implementers, public health policy-makers, and donors. It will identify factors that facilitate or hinder the delivery of SQ-LNS in combination with immunization services, especially in underserved communities facing geographic, socioeconomic, or gender-related barriers. While the implementation of SQ-LNS distribution programs for children aged 6–23 months have been documented in various countries [55, 56], NutriVax-Measles study is the first to assess SQ-LNS as an incentive within routine immunization activities, offering new evidence on the feasibility of integrated delivery. Although SQ-LNS is provided as a public health intervention, we acknowledge the potential for unexpected behavioral or social effects when used as an incentive to increase health facilities’ attendance and vaccine uptake. Participation in both immunization services and SQ-LNS distribution is entirely voluntary. Possible unintended consequences, such as misinterpretation of eligibility criteria or program intent, will be monitored through community engagement and dialogue with local health authorities. Additionally, the midline qualitative survey will help identify any risk of negative effects or areas for improvement.

The study has several limitations. First, it will be implemented in a single country and within a specific region (northeastern Nigeria), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings with different health system capacities, cultural context, or security situation. However, this real-world and particularly complex (rural and under a tensed security situation) setting strengthens the relevance of the results and offers valuable insights into the potential for scale-up in comparable complex settings. Second, the SQ-LNS and immunization activities rely on routine service platforms and health facility-based distribution, which may limit reach in populations with restricted access to health services. Third, while the sample size and design are powered for primary and the key secondary outcome, the detection of effects on certain secondary outcomes may be constrained.

In conclusion, NutriVax-Measles will generate critical evidence on Immunization and Nutrition Integration—an emerging priority on the global health agenda [57]. Findings may inform policy and investment decisions by governments and donors in settings where undernutrition and low vaccination coverage remain intertwined public health challenges. If successful, this integrated strategy could help break the vicious cycle of malnutrition and infectious disease, fostering better health and growth for vulnerable children. The study also aligns with the WHO-endorsed Global Immunization Agenda 2030, which aims to reduce zero-dose children by 50% reduction, achieve 90% global coverage for key vaccines, and prevent 50 million deaths through vaccination by 2023 [18, 58] while simultaneously addressing global undernutrition.
