From cytoreduction to precision oncology: evolution of ovarian cancer therapy
Authors: Ghassan M. Saed, Laura Detti, Dina Massad, Asma Basha, Robert T. Morris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer remains the most lethal gynecologic malignancy due to its asymptomatic progression, limited screening modalities, and late-stage diagnosis. Although initial responses to platinum-based chemotherapy are common, recurrence and multidrug resistance are nearly universal, necessitating a shift toward biomarker-guided, precision-based interventions. This review synthesizes current evidence on the evolving landscape of ovarian cancer treatment, with emphasis on molecular pathogenesis, histotype-specific vulnerabilities, and advances in therapeutic strategies. Focus is placed on the clinical integration of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-targeting agents, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and antibody–drug conjugates, alongside emerging platforms such as liquid biopsy, artificial intelligence–enhanced diagnostics, and histotype-driven targeted therapies. Translational insights from other malignancies were utilized to highlight future directions for ovarian cancer, including dual pathway targeting, immune and epigenetic modulation, and receptor-guided cytotoxic delivery. Collectively, these advances demonstrate how precision oncology is advancing ovarian cancer treatment, with the potential to improve survival outcomes while preserving quality of life.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer remains the most lethal gynecologic malignancy worldwide, largely due to asymptomatic progression, lack of effective screening strategies, and late-stage diagnosis [1]. Although many patients initially respond to platinum-based chemotherapy, recurrence and multidrug resistance are common [2]. These limitations have driven a shift from conventional cytotoxic therapy toward biomarker-guided, precision-based treatment strategies [3].

Ovarian cancer is characterized by marked molecular heterogeneity, with distinct genomic alterations influencing tumor behavior and therapeutic response [4]. Inherited germline mutations, including BRCA1, BRCA2, and other homologous recombination repair genes, act as genetic drivers that promote tumor development by impairing DNA repair pathways. Most ovarian cancers occur at older age (> 50), with risk increasing as age increases. Additionally, family and personal medical history, such as prior breast cancer diagnosis, especially when associated with BRCA mutations, further elevates risk.

Survival outcomes vary globally and reflect disparities in healthcare access, diagnostic infrastructure, and availability of targeted therapies [5]. Over recent decades, ovarian cancer mortality trends have shown a striking divergence between high-income and low- and middle-income regions, reflecting differences in healthcare access, medical technology, and implementation of preventive strategies [5, 6]. High-income countries have experienced declining mortality over recent decades due to advances in surgical cytoreduction, platinum-based chemotherapy, genetic testing, and incorporation of targeted agents such as PARPi and anti-angiogenic therapies [6, 7]. In contrast, many low- and middle-income regions continue to experience poorer outcomes due to delayed diagnosis and limited access to specialized oncology care [5] (Fig. 1).

In high-income countries such as the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and much of Western Europe, ovarian cancer mortality has steadily declined over the past five decades. This decline is largely attributed to the introduction of oral contraceptives, which confer long-term protective effects; improvements in surgical cytoreduction and platinum-based chemotherapy; and, more recently, the implementation of targeted therapies such as poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and anti-angiogenic agents [8, 9]. Enhanced diagnostic imaging, multidisciplinary care models, and increased genetic testing for BRCA1/2 and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) have also contributed to earlier detection and more personalized treatment approaches [10, 11]. In contrast, low- and middle-income countries in regions such as South Asia, the Middle East, Sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Latin America have experienced little change or even an increase in ovarian cancer mortality over the same period. Limited access to specialized oncology care, late-stage diagnosis, inadequate surgical infrastructure, and the absence of national cancer screening or genetic counseling programs have perpetuated poor outcomes. Consequently, the global gap in ovarian cancer survival has widened: while five-year survival in some high-income nations now exceeds 45–50%, it remains below 30% in many resource-limited settings. These patterns underscore the urgent need for global health equity initiatives, emphasizing early detection, access to molecular diagnostics, and affordable targeted treatments to reduce the persistent mortality disparities in ovarian cancer [8–11].

Clinically, ovarian cancer is staged according to the FIGO system, ranging from localized disease (stage I) to distant metastasis (stage IV) [12]. Despite improvements in imaging and molecular diagnostics, most patients present with advanced-stage disease characterized by peritoneal dissemination and malignant ascites, underscoring the need for improved early detection.

Molecular biomarkers now play a central role in diagnosis and treatment selection. Serum markers such as CA125 and HE4 remain widely used for monitoring [13], while genomic profiling has identified recurrent alterations in TP53, BRCA1/2, KRAS, ARID1A, PIK3CA, and mismatch repair genes that inform prognosis and therapeutic response [3, 4, 14–16]. These findings underscore that ovarian cancer represents a heterogeneous group of malignancies with distinct biological behavior.

High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC), the most common subtype, is characterized by near-universal TP53 mutations and frequent homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), conferring sensitivity to platinum chemotherapy and PARP inhibition. In contrast, low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC) is commonly driven by MAPK pathway mutations and demonstrates relative resistance to conventional chemotherapy but responsiveness to hormonal and MEK-targeted therapies [17]. Other subtypes, including endometrioid, clear cell, and mucinous carcinomas, exhibit distinct molecular alterations that influence therapeutic vulnerability [18–21] (Fig. 2).

Early detection remains a major challenge, as current screening approaches lack sufficient sensitivity [22]. Emerging strategies, including liquid biopsy and molecular profiling, may improve early diagnosis and disease monitoring. Liquid biopsy technologies, including ctDNA profiling and DNA methylation assays, have demonstrated promise in detecting minimal residual disease and monitoring recurrence with greater sensitivity than conventional imaging modalities [23–25].

Artificial intelligence–based models integrating imaging and molecular data further enhance risk stratification [26, 27], while population genomic screening for BRCA1/2 and related mutations support preventive strategies [28].

Emerging biotechnology and AI-driven approaches are reshaping ovarian cancer screening, risk stratification, and therapeutic decision-making [29].

This review is based on a comprehensive analysis of literature identified through PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, with emphasis on randomized trials, biomarker-driven studies, and histotype-specific investigations. In summary, this review highlights the emerging precision oncology strategies and their implications for individualized ovarian cancer therapy.

Traditional treatment approaches

Cytoreductive surgery

Surgical cytoreduction remains a cornerstone of treatment for ovarian cancer and a key determinant of outcome. The extent of residual disease after surgery is the strongest prognostic factor for overall and progression-free survival [1–4]. The goal is complete resection of all visible disease. Optimal cytoreduction (≤ 1 cm residual disease), and especially complete resection (R0), is consistently associated with improved survival, particularly in HGSC [4, 16, 30].

Surgical management varies by stage and patient factors. In selected reproductive-age patients with stage I disease, fertility-sparing surgery with unilateral oophorectomy and comprehensive staging may be considered. Standard staging otherwise includes total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, peritoneal biopsies, and lymphadenectomy [17].

In advanced-stage disease, extensive procedures such as diaphragmatic stripping, splenectomy, and bowel resection may be required to achieve maximal cytoreduction [31, 32]. These aggressive surgical approaches are often required to achieve maximal tumor clearance and improve survival outcomes. When complete resection is unlikely due to tumor burden or performance status, neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval debulking surgery offers comparable survival in appropriately selected patients [33]. These findings underscore the central role of surgical completeness in improving long-term disease control.

Chemotherapy

Systemic chemotherapy remains essential for eliminating microscopic residual disease following cytoreductive surgery (Fig. 3).

The introduction of platinum-based therapy established the backbone of treatment, with carboplatin largely replacing cisplatin due to comparable efficacy and improved tolerability [34]. The pivotal GOG-111 trial established the superiority of combining a platinum agent with paclitaxel, defining the platinum–taxane doublet as the standard first-line backbone [35]. Currently, carboplatin and paclitaxel administered every three weeks for six cycles remains the standard of care [18, 19, 36]. These approaches are applied on an individualized basis depending on toxicity and disease characteristics [3]. Clinically, platinum-based chemotherapy remains the backbone of treatment, while the platinum-free interval guides therapeutic decisions in recurrent disease.

For recurrent disease, treatment selection is guided by the platinum-free interval (PFI), which reflects tumor sensitivity to prior platinum therapy. Platinum-sensitive recurrence (PFI ≥ 6 months) is typically managed with platinum-based combinations, whereas platinum-resistant disease (PFI < 6 months) is treated with non-platinum agents such as pegylated liposomal doxorubicin, topotecan, or gemcitabine [37, 38].

Despite initial responses, most patients with advanced ovarian cancer eventually relapse and develop chemoresistance, which remains a major therapeutic challenge [28]. A major mechanism of resistance involves increased drug efflux mediated by ATP-binding cassette transporters, such as P-glycoprotein (ABCB1), which actively export chemotherapeutic agents out of tumor cells [6].Restoration of homologous recombination through BRCA reversion mutations allows tumor cells to overcome platinum-induced DNA damage and regain genomic stability. Resistance is also reinforced through evasion of apoptosis, frequently driven by TP53 mutations that impair DNA damage-induced cell death and by overexpression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins that enhance tumor cell survival under therapeutic stress [6]. The tumor microenvironment actively promotes resistance through angiogenic, inflammatory, and hypoxia-mediated mechanisms [6].

Predictive and response biomarkers in chemotherapy

In addition to traditional clinical metrics such as the platinum-free interval, emerging biomarkers provide a more precise assessment of treatment response and chemosensitivity. The kinetics of CA125 decline during chemotherapy have been formalized into the KELIM (elimination rate constant K) score, a validated dynamic biomarker that reflects tumor chemosensitivity to platinum-based therapy [39]. A favorable KELIM score, indicating rapid CA125 decline, is associated with improved progression-free and overall survival and has been shown to predict benefit from maintenance strategies, including anti-angiogenic therapy [39]. Conversely, an unfavorable KELIM score identifies patients with intrinsically chemoresistant disease who may benefit from alternative or intensified therapeutic approaches [39].

Pathological assessment following neoadjuvant chemotherapy also provides critical prognostic information. The Chemotherapy Response Score (CRS), a standardized histopathologic grading system applied to omental specimens at interval debulking surgery, has emerged as a robust predictor of treatment response [40]. CRS3 (complete or near-complete response) is strongly associated with improved survival outcomes, whereas CRS1–2 reflects partial or minimal response and poorer prognosis. The CRS system is now widely incorporated into clinical trials and increasingly used to stratify patients in translational research [40].

Biomarker development continues to evolve, with HRD, TIL density, ctDNA, and CRS emerging as key predictors of therapeutic response [41].

Together, these biomarkers highlight a shift toward integrating dynamic and tissue-based response assessments into ovarian cancer management, enabling more individualized therapeutic strategies and improved prognostic stratification.

Emerging strategies in platinum-resistant disease

Recent phase III clinical evidence has expanded therapeutic strategies in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer, a setting historically associated with poor outcomes and limited treatment options. The ROSELLA trial evaluated the selective glucocorticoid receptor antagonist relacorilantin in combination with nab-paclitaxel, targeting glucocorticoid receptor–mediated tumor survival pathways implicated in chemotherapy resistance [42]. Activation of the glucocorticoid receptor has been shown to promote anti-apoptotic signaling and reduce cytotoxic efficacy, thereby contributing to treatment resistance.

In this context, relacorilant enhances chemotherapy sensitivity by inhibiting glucocorticoid receptor signaling, thereby restoring apoptotic responses to cytotoxic therapy. Clinical findings from the ROSELLA trial demonstrated improved progression-free survival and objective response rates with the combination compared to chemotherapy alone, supporting glucocorticoid receptor modulation as a novel therapeutic strategy in platinum-resistant disease [42].

These results underscore a broader shift toward targeting adaptive resistance mechanisms in ovarian cancer, complementing existing approaches such as anti-angiogenic therapy and antibody–drug conjugates, and further advancing precision oncology strategies in the recurrent setting.

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy has historically served as an adjunct modality in ovarian cancer management. Earlier approaches, including whole abdominal irradiation, were used following surgery to target microscopic disease but declined with the adoption of platinum-based chemotherapy due to toxicity and limited survival benefit [43]. In contemporary practice, radiotherapy is primarily used for palliation, providing symptom relief in metastatic or recurrent disease, including control of bone pain, bleeding, and localized symptomatic lesions [44].

Advances in radiation delivery have expanded its role in selected patients with oligometastatic disease. Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) allows precise, high-dose treatment of isolated lesions and has demonstrated high local control rates with acceptable toxicity in prospective studies [45, 46].

Technological advances continue to improve the precision of radiotherapy. MRI-guided adaptive radiotherapy (SMART) enables real-time visualization and adjustment of treatment delivery to better spare surrounding organs, particularly the bowel [47].

Although radiotherapy is rarely curative in advanced ovarian cancer, modern techniques can provide durable local control and complement systemic therapy within a multidisciplinary treatment strategy [47].

Hormonal therapy

Hormonal therapy provides a biologically targeted and comparatively low-toxicity option for selected ovarian cancer subtypes, particularly LGSC and endometrioid carcinomas, where tumor growth is often driven by estrogen receptor signaling [19, 48].

Selective estrogen receptor modulators, such as tamoxifen, and aromatase inhibitors, including letrozole and anastrozole, have demonstrated clinical activity, particularly in recurrent LGSC, a subtype characterized by relative resistance to cytotoxic chemotherapy [20, 49]. These agents are primarily associated with disease stabilization and may prolong progression-free survival in selected patients.

Recent advances in precision oncology have reinforced the importance of biomarker-guided patient selection in hormonal therapy. Estrogen and progesterone receptor expression may help predict therapeutic response, and molecular profiling approaches are being explored to better characterize hormone pathway activity [50]. Ongoing studies are evaluating combination strategies, including aromatase inhibitors with CDK4/6 or MEK inhibitors, particularly in hormone receptor–positive and LGSC [50].

Compared with conventional chemotherapy, hormonal therapy offers favorable tolerability and quality-of-life benefits, supporting its role in recurrent and chemoresistant settings when guided by appropriate biomarker selection. These developments underscore the expanding role of endocrine-based strategies within precision ovarian cancer management. Clinically, hormonal therapy offers a well-tolerated option for selected patients, particularly in low-grade serous carcinoma [19, 48].

Modern targeted therapy

Building upon the limitations of conventional treatment modalities and an improved understanding of ovarian cancer biology, the therapeutic landscape has undergone a profound transformation over the past two decades. Treatment strategies have shifted from traditional cytotoxic approaches toward biomarker-driven therapies that exploit specific molecular vulnerabilities within tumor cells (Fig. 5).

These advances have focused on precision oncology, in which treatment selection is increasingly guided by genomic alterations, pathway dependencies, and tumor-specific biomarkers.

Current therapeutic approaches now include the use of PARPi inhibitors, VEGF-targeting agents, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), each of which has significantly improved disease control and survival outcomes in appropriately selected patient populations [51]. Collectively, these therapies reflect a shift from nonspecific cytotoxicity toward mechanism-based interventions designed to disrupt critical oncogenic pathways, enhance antitumor immunity, and overcome resistance mechanisms that limit the efficacy of traditional treatments.

PARP inhibitors

Among the most significant advances in ovarian cancer precision oncology has been the development of PARPi, which exploits synthetic lethality in tumors with HRD, particularly those harboring BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations. Landmark trials established their clinical benefit and expanded their role into the first-line maintenance setting. The SOLO-1 trial demonstrated substantial progression-free survival benefit with olaparib in newly diagnosed BRCA-mutated advanced ovarian cancer, while PRIMA showed that niraparib prolonged progression-free survival in HRD-positive tumors beyond germline BRCA-mutated populations. These findings shifted management toward biomarker-driven maintenance strategies [3, 18, 52–57].

Despite these advances, resistance frequently emerges. Mechanisms include restoration of homologous recombination, replication fork stabilization, and alterations in DNA repair signaling, limiting the durability of response. Ongoing studies are evaluating rational combination approaches to enhance treatment efficacy and overcome resistance [22, 57–60]. Clinically, PARPi have redefined first-line management, with use guided by homologous recombination deficiency and BRCA mutation status [52–57].

A key advance in first-line maintenance therapy is the combination of PARP inhibition with anti-angiogenic therapy, as demonstrated in the PAOLA-1 trial. This phase III study evaluated the addition of olaparib to bevacizumab maintenance therapy in patients with newly diagnosed advanced ovarian cancer who had responded to platinum-based chemotherapy [51]. The combination significantly improved progression-free survival compared with bevacizumab alone, with the greatest benefit observed in patients with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), including both BRCA1/BRCA2-mutated and HRD-positive/BRCA-wild-type tumors [51].

Importantly, PAOLA-1 established that patients without HRD derive minimal benefit from this combination, reinforcing the central role of biomarker-driven patient selection in optimizing PARP inhibitor use [51]. These findings expanded the therapeutic landscape beyond PARP inhibitor monotherapy and support a dual-targeting strategy that simultaneously exploits defects in DNA repair and tumor angiogenesis. Clinically, the PAOLA-1 results have informed current first-line maintenance strategies, particularly in HRD-positive populations, where combination therapy represents a preferred approach in appropriately selected patients [51].

Recent data from the DUO-O and ATHENA-MONO trials have further refined the role of PARP inhibition in the first-line setting. DUO-O demonstrated that adding durvalumab and olaparib to bevacizumab-based maintenance improved progression-free survival in HRD-positive disease, while ATHENA-MONO confirmed rucaparib benefit across biomarker-defined subgroups. These findings highlight ongoing efforts to optimize PARP inhibitor–based combinations and refine biomarker-guided patient selection [61, 62].

Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy represents an evolving therapeutic strategy in ovarian cancer. Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 have demonstrated durable responses in a small subset of patients; however, overall activity remains modest compared with other solid tumors. Large, randomized trials, including JAVELIN Ovarian 200, failed to show superiority over chemotherapy, reflecting the relatively immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment [58]. This limited responsiveness is driven by low tumor mutational burden, inadequate antigen presentation, and the presence of immunosuppressive stromal and immune cell populations. The ovarian cancer immune microenvironment profoundly influences immunotherapy response and resistance [63].

Multiple mechanisms of immune evasion, including impaired antigen presentation, T-cell exclusion, and immunosuppressive cytokine signaling, further limit the efficacy of checkpoint blockade and are now key targets for emerging therapeutic strategies [64].

Recent phase III data have begun to redefine the role of immunotherapy in ovarian cancer, particularly in the platinum-resistant setting. The ENGOT-ov65/KEYNOTE-B96 trial evaluated pembrolizumab in combination with weekly paclitaxel, with or without bevacizumab, in patients with platinum-resistant ovarian cancer [65]. This study demonstrated a significant improvement in progression-free and overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone, with the greatest benefit observed in patients with PD-L1–positive tumors (combined positive score [CPS] ≥ 1) [65].These findings suggest that immune checkpoint inhibition may have a more substantial clinical role when combined with chemotherapy and/or anti-angiogenic therapy, particularly in biomarker-selected populations. Mechanistically, chemotherapy may enhance tumor antigen release and immune priming, while VEGF inhibition can modulate the tumor microenvironment to improve immune cell infiltration, thereby augmenting the efficacy of PD-1 blockade [65].

Collectively, these results represent an important shift from earlier studies demonstrating limited single-agent activity and support a more integrated, combination-based approach to immunotherapy in ovarian cancer, with patient selection guided by PD-L1 expression and other emerging biomarkers. In 2026, pembrolizumab plus paclitaxel ± bevacizumab received regulatory approval for PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 platinum-resistant recurrent epithelial ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal carcinoma after one or two prior systemic regimens [65].

Current strategies therefore emphasize rational combination approaches, integrating checkpoint inhibitors with PARPi, anti-angiogenic agents, or chemotherapy to enhance immune responsiveness [66]. Despite ongoing investigation, the absence of reliable predictive biomarkers and significant interpatient heterogeneity remain major barriers to effective patient selection [67].

Anti-angiogenic therapy

Angiogenesis is a key driver of ovarian cancer progression, making VEGF inhibition an important therapeutic strategy. Bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting VEGF-A, demonstrated progression-free survival benefit in the phase III GOG-0218 and ICON7 trials when added to platinum-based chemotherapy and continued as maintenance, particularly in patients with high-risk disease [68–71].

VEGF inhibition also has a role in platinum-resistant recurrence, where bevacizumab improves response rates when combined with chemotherapy [72]. However, its broader use is limited by the absence of predictive biomarkers, cumulative toxicity, and modest overall survival benefit. These considerations highlight the need for improved patient selection within precision oncology frameworks.

Antibody–Drug Conjugates (ADCs)

Building upon advances in targeted therapy and immunologically directed treatment, ADCs represent a major innovation in precision oncology for ovarian cancer [73]. ADCs combine tumor-specific antigen recognition via monoclonal antibodies with highly potent cytotoxic payloads, enabling selective intracellular drug delivery while minimizing systemic toxicity, a critical limitation of conventional chemotherapy (Fig. 6). By leveraging antigen-specific targeting, ADCs allow for enhanced therapeutic efficacy with improved tolerability [73].

Among emerging ADCs, mirvetuximab soravtansine, which targets folate receptor alpha (FRα), has demonstrated the most advanced clinical efficacy and represents a milestone in biomarker-guided therapy [74]. FRα is highly expressed in a substantial subset of epithelial ovarian cancers, particularly in platinum-resistant disease, making it an attractive therapeutic target. In the phase III MIRASOL trial, mirvetuximab soravtansine significantly improved objective response rates and overall survival compared with investigator’s choice chemotherapy in patients with platinum-resistant ovarian cancer exhibiting high FRα expression [75]. These findings led to regulatory approval and established ADC therapy as a new standard of care for biomarker-selected patients with limited treatment options. Clinically, these results validate receptor-guided cytotoxic delivery as an effective strategy for overcoming resistance and improving outcomes in advanced disease.

Beyond FRα-targeted therapy, next-generation ADCs such as upifitamab rilsodotin (NaPi2b), tisotumab vedotin (tissue factor), and B7-H4–directed agents are advancing through phase II/III evaluation, reflecting rapid expansion of receptor-guided cytotoxic platforms in ovarian cancer [76–79].

Beyond treatment of active recurrence, ADCs are now being explored in earlier treatment settings, including maintenance therapy and combination regimes designed to prolong disease control and delay subsequent chemotherapy exposure [73]. Ongoing clinical trials, such as the GLORIOSA study, are evaluating mirvetuximab and soravtansine in combination with bevacizumab as maintenance therapy following response to platinum-based treatment in patients with FRα-positive recurrent disease [80] (Table 1).

These combination approaches aim to integrate the therapeutic benefit of ADCs earlier in the disease course and improve long-term disease management.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. Tumor antigen heterogeneity, variability in FRα expression levels, and the development of resistance mechanisms, including antigen downregulation, impaired intracellular trafficking, and reduced payload release, may limit the durability of clinical responses. Additionally, treatment-associated toxicities, particularly ocular adverse effects such as keratopathy, require careful monitoring and management. Continued refinement of biomarker thresholds, optimization of dosing strategies, and rational combination approaches will be essential to fully realize the therapeutic potential of ADCs and integrate them effectively into personalized ovarian cancer treatments [81–85].

Next-wave DNA damage response / anti-replicative stress combinations

Drug resistance remains a defining challenge, driven by genomic instability, metabolic rewiring, and microenvironmental adaptation [86].

Building upon the clinical success and subsequent resistance observed with PARP inhibition, next-generation DNA damage response (DDR)-targeting agents represent a promising shift in precision oncology for ovarian cancer. These therapies exploit tumor-specific vulnerabilities in DNA repair and replication stress pathways, enabling selective targeting of malignancies that have developed resistance to conventional cytotoxic and PARP-directed therapies. ATR, WEE1, and PI3K–AKT inhibitors are being evaluated in early-phase clinical trials, either as monotherapies or in rational combinations with PARPi or chemotherapy, to overcome acquired PARP resistance [87].

An additional emerging strategy within the DNA damage response landscape involves targeting polymerase theta (POLQ), a key mediator of the microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) pathway. POLQ is frequently upregulated in homologous recombination–deficient (HRD) tumors and provides an alternative DNA repair mechanism that supports tumor survival under conditions of genomic instability [88, 89]. Importantly, POLQ dependency is enhanced in tumors with defects in homologous recombination, including those harboring BRCA1/BRCA2 alterations [89].

Preclinical studies have demonstrated that inhibition of POLQ induces synthetic lethality in HRD tumors and may restore sensitivity in cancers that have developed resistance to PARPi through restoration of homologous recombination or replication fork stabilization. By disrupting this compensatory repair pathway, POLQ inhibitors selectively target tumor cells reliant on error-prone DNA repair mechanisms while sparing normal cells with intact homologous recombination [88, 89].

Early-phase clinical trials are currently evaluating POLQ inhibitors as monotherapy and in combination with PARPi or other DDR-targeting agents. These strategies aim to exploit dual vulnerabilities in DNA repair and represent a promising approach to overcoming acquired resistance and extending the clinical benefit of precision oncology therapies in ovarian cancer [88].

Early-phase clinical trials are currently evaluating ATR inhibitors (e.g., ceralasertib), WEE1 inhibitors (e.g., adavosertib), and PI3K–AKT pathway inhibitors, both as monotherapies and in rational combinations with PARPi, chemotherapy, or anti-angiogenic agents [90–92]. These combination strategies aim to re-sensitize PARP-refractory tumors by re-inducing replication stress and overwhelming adaptive DNA repair mechanisms. Importantly, these approaches are increasingly guided by biomarker-driven patient selection, incorporating genomic profiling, replication stress signatures, and functional DNA repair assays to identify patients most likely to benefit [90, 91].

Collectively, DDR-targeted therapies represent a critical next wave of precision oncology, offering a mechanistically rational strategy to overcome acquired resistance and extend the therapeutic durability of DNA repair–directed treatment paradigms in ovarian cancer.

Lessons from other cancers: translational insights for ovarian cancer

The continued evolution of ovarian cancer treatment has been profoundly influenced by therapeutic breakthroughs achieved in other malignancies, underscoring the importance of cross-disease translation oncology in accelerating innovation. Advances in immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and cellular therapy across diverse tumor types have provided mechanistic insights and therapeutic frameworks now being actively adapted for ovarian cancer.

One of the most influential examples is the transformative success of immune checkpoint inhibitors in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer, where blockade of PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 pathways has produced durable responses and long-term survival benefits. These successes have catalyzed extensive investigation of immune checkpoint inhibition in ovarian cancer, both as monotherapy and in rational combinations with PARPi, anti-angiogenic agents, and chemotherapy. These combination approaches aim to overcome the immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment characteristic of ovarian cancer by enhancing T-cell infiltration and reversing immune suppression [93, 94]. Ongoing trials are evaluating predictive biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, and interferon signatures, to refine patient selection and improve clinical outcomes [95].

Similarly, the efficacy of ADCs in breast cancer, particularly trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER2-positive disease, has accelerated the development of receptor-targeted ADCs in ovarian cancer [96–98]. Mirvetuximab soravtansine, targeting FRα, exemplifies this translational success and has shown significant clinical activity in platinum resistant ovarian cancer with high FRα expression, ultimately leading to regulatory approval in biomarker-selected populations. These findings validate antigen-directed cytotoxic delivery, as an effective strategy for improving therapeutic precision while minimizing systemic toxicity [99].

Lessons from hematologic malignancies have similarly informed the development of cellular immunotherapies in ovarian cancer. The success of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy in leukemias and lymphomas has inspired efforts to develop analogous cellular platforms targeting ovarian-cancer antigens such as MUC16, mesothelin, and FRα [100].

Additionally, the rapid clinical integration of KRAS G12C inhibitors in lung cancer highlights the potential for targeting oncogenic drivers previously considered undruggable. These advances have important implications for rare ovarian cancer subtypes, particularly mucinous carcinoma, which frequently harbors KRAS mutations and may benefit from analogous molecularly targeted therapies as such inhibitors continue to expand in clinical availability [101].

These advances support a shift toward biomarker-driven precision oncology in ovarian cancer. Leveraging therapeutic insights from other malignancies will be essential for accelerating drug development, overcoming resistance, and improving long-term outcomes for patients with ovarian cancer.

Fertility implications for ovarian cancer therapy and options for preservation

As therapeutic advances continue to improve survival outcomes in ovarian cancer, increasing attention has been directed toward the long-term reproductive and endocrine consequences of treatment, particularly in women of reproductive age. In this population, treatment planning requires careful consideration of both oncologic efficacy and preservation of future fertility and hormonal function [102].

Fertility-sparing surgery may be considered in carefully selected patients with early-stage, low-grade tumors confined to a single ovary, where unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy with comprehensive staging can preserve reproductive potential without compromising oncologic safety. However, definitive treatment, including bilateral oophorectomy and systemic therapy, is required in most patients, resulting in irreversible loss of ovarian function and treatment-induced menopause [103]. Systemic chemotherapy may further impair ovarian reserve and reproductive potential. Similarly, pelvic irradiation can cause irreversible ovarian injury, depending on cumulative dose and patient age at exposure [104].

Although targeted therapies, including PARPi, anti-angiogenic agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, have transformed ovarian cancer treatment, their reproductive and endocrine safety profiles remain incompletely characterized, with limited data on long-term fertility outcomes.

Iatrogenic menopause in younger women is associated with adverse cardiovascular, cognitive, and quality-of-life outcomes, underscoring the importance of early fertility counseling [105, 106].

Fertility preservation options for ovarian cancer remain limited. Fertility-sparing surgery is feasible only in carefully selected early-stage cases, and established approaches such as oocyte or embryo cryopreservation are often constrained by treatment urgency or ovarian involvement. Ovarian tissue cryopreservation remains investigational due to concerns about malignant cell reintroduction, and the role of gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists remains controversial, with inconsistent evidence supporting routine use [107–109].

As survival improves with modern therapies, preservation of reproductive and endocrine health remains a key issue. Women undergoing cancer treatment will have the option to pursue fertility preservation strategies, including oocyte or embryo cryopreservation prior to initiating gonadotoxic therapy. Continued efforts are needed to expand access to safe fertility-preserving interventions and long-term reproductive monitoring ([110, 111].

Combination therapies

Rationale for combination therapies

Combination strategies in ovarian cancer are designed to target complementary biological pathways that contribute to tumor survival, immune evasion, angiogenesis, and therapeutic resistance. Unlike single-agent approaches, combination regimens aim to exploit synergistic interactions between DNA repair inhibition, immune modulation, and angiogenesis blockade to enhance antitumor activity and overcome adaptive resistance mechanisms. These strategies reflect a precision-oncology framework in which treatment selection is guided by molecular vulnerabilities, tumor microenvironmental features, and histotype-specific biology [112] (Fig. 7).

PARPi + anti-angiogenic therapy

PARP inhibition induces accumulation of DNA damage in homologous recombination–deficient tumors, while VEGF-driven angiogenesis promotes hypoxia, genomic instability, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Hypoxia can induce an HRD-like phenotype, increasing tumor sensitivity to PARP inhibition. Conversely, VEGF blockade normalizes tumor vasculature, improves drug delivery, and reduces hypoxia-mediated resistance pathways. This dual targeting of DNA repair and angiogenesis forms the biological basis for combining PARPi with anti-angiogenic agents [112].

PARPi + immune checkpoint inhibitors

PARP inhibition increases cytosolic DNA fragments and activates the cGAS–STING pathway, leading to type I interferon signaling, enhanced antigen presentation, and recruitment of cytotoxic T cells. PARP inhibition also upregulates PD-L1 expression, creating a mechanistic rationale for synergy with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. By simultaneously inducing DNA damage and enhancing immune recognition, PARPi may sensitize tumors to immune checkpoint inhibition, particularly in HRD-positive or BRCA-mutated disease [112].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors + anti-angiogenic therapy

VEGF promotes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by inhibiting dendritic cell maturation, reducing T-cell infiltration, and expanding regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages. VEGF blockade reverses these effects, facilitating immune cell trafficking and improving the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. This complementary targeting of angiogenesis and immune evasion provides a strong biological rationale for combining anti-angiogenic agents with immune checkpoint inhibitors [112].

Chemotherapy + immunotherapy

Chemotherapy induces immunogenic cell death, increases tumor antigen release, and reduces immunosuppressive myeloid populations. These effects enhance dendritic cell activation and T-cell priming, thereby potentiating the activity of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Weekly paclitaxel has demonstrated the ability to modulate the tumor microenvironment in ways that enhance responsiveness to PD-1 blockade [112].

ADC-based combinations (emerging)

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) deliver cytotoxic payloads directly to tumor cells and can induce immunogenic cell death, increasing antigen release and promoting T-cell activation. These immunomodulatory effects provide a rationale for combining ADCs with immune checkpoint inhibitors or anti-angiogenic agents. Ongoing trials are evaluating ADC-based combinations in folate receptor-α–positive ovarian cancer and other molecularly defined subgroups [112].

Frontline chemo–immunotherapy-targeted combinations

Recent advances in ovarian cancer therapeutics have increasingly focused on rational combination strategies designed to enhance treatment efficacy by simultaneously targeting DNA repair pathways, angiogenesis, and immune evasion mechanisms. These multimodal approaches aim to overcome intrinsic tumor resistance and improve durable clinical outcomes through synergistic interactions between targeted therapies and immunomodulatory agents.

The phase III FIRST/ENGOT-OV44/GOG-3069 trial represents a landmark effort evaluating the integration of the PARP inhibitor niraparib and the PD-1 inhibitor dostarlimab in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy followed by maintenance therapy. This study evaluates the therapeutic synergy between PARP inhibition and immune checkpoint blockade, particularly un homologous recombination-deficient (HRD) and BRCA-mutated populations, where impaired DNA repair mechanisms may enhance tumor immunogenicity and susceptibility to immune-mediated cytotoxicity [113].

Similarly, multi-arm platform trials such as NRG-GY023 are evaluating combination strategies incorporating the PD-L1 inhibitor durvalumab, the PARP inhibitor olaparib, and the VEGF inhibitor cediranib in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer [114]. These approaches aim to target complementary pathways in DNA repair, angiogenesis, and immune evasion, reflecting a shift toward integrated, biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies.

Emerging immunotherapy modalities

Cellular immunotherapy is being explored as a strategy to enhance antitumor immune responses in ovarian cancer. The presence of intraepithelial CD8⁺ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes is associated with improved survival, supporting the biological rationale for immune-based interventions [115]. Early-phase studies of adoptive cellular therapies, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and engineered T cell approaches, have demonstrated feasibility, though clinical efficacy remains under investigation [116].

Therapeutic cancer vaccines are also being evaluated to induce antigen-specific immune responses; however, these strategies remain investigational and require further validation [117]. Preventive vaccine strategies are also being explored, particularly in high-risk populations such as individuals with BRCA1/2 mutations. Early efforts aim to prime immune recognition of tumor-associated antigens expressed in precursor or early-stage disease, including the use of mRNA-based platforms for personalized prevention approaches [118].

Therapeutic vaccine strategies are also under investigation. Dendritic cell–based and peptide vaccines targeting tumor-associated antigens have demonstrated acceptable safety and preliminary clinical activity in early-phase studies [119] (Fig. 8).

Beyond checkpoint blockade, antigen-directed immunotherapy has re-emerged as a promising approach. Future directions and precision oncology

Evolving paradigms in precision oncology

The therapeutic landscape of ovarian cancer is increasingly defined by precision oncology, driven by advances in molecular profiling, biomarker discovery, and rational combination strategies. Treatment selection is now guided by tumor-specific features such as HRD, BRCA1/2 mutation status, folate receptor-α expression, and characteristics of the tumor immune microenvironment. These biomarkers have enabled the integration of targeted therapies, including PARPi, anti-angiogenic agents, and antibody–drug conjugates, representing a shift from empiric cytotoxic therapy toward biologically informed treatment strategies.

Overcoming therapeutic resistance

Despite these advances, ovarian cancer remains characterized by frequent relapse and the development of therapeutic resistance. Mechanisms of resistance to PARPi, anti-angiogenic agents, and chemotherapy include restoration of homologous recombination repair, replication fork stabilization, immune evasion, and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Accordingly, current research increasingly focuses on rational combination strategies aimed at overcoming these resistance pathways. Promising approaches include inhibitors targeting the DNA damage response (e.g., ATR, WEE1, and CHK1 inhibitors), combinations of PARPi with immune checkpoint blockade, and anti-angiogenic immunotherapy platforms designed to enhance treatment durability [125].

Advances in cancer immunology

Advances in cancer immunology are further expanding therapeutic opportunities. Cellular therapies, therapeutic cancer vaccines, and antigen-targeted immunotherapies are under active investigation to enhance anti-tumor immune responses [120]. Concurrently, emerging technologies, including proteogenomic profiling, single-cell sequencing, and characterization of tumor immunopeptidomes, are improving understanding of tumor heterogeneity and immune escape, facilitating the development of predictive biomarkers and individualized treatment strategies [126, 127].

Innovations in early detection and disease monitoring

Improving early detection and optimizing treatment sequencing remain critical priorities. Integration of circulating tumor DNA, minimal residual disease monitoring, and liquid biopsy technologies may enable earlier identification of recurrence and allow more adaptive therapeutic interventions.

Survivorship and long-term outcomes

As therapeutic options expand and survival outcomes improve, increasing attention must also be directed toward survivorship considerations, including quality of life, long-term toxicity, and reproductive health. Incorporating patient-centered outcomes into treatment decision making will be essential for optimizing long-term care.

Outlook

Collectively, these advances highlight a transition toward integrated precision oncology in ovarian cancer, in which treatment selection and monitoring are guided by tumor biology and patient-specific factors. Continued progress will depend on translating advances in molecular diagnostics, tumor biology, and immunology into actionable clinical strategies while ensuring equitable access to emerging therapies.

Additional contemporary literature and integrative perspectives

Recent comprehensive reviews highlight the rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape in ovarian cancer, emphasizing advances in biotechnology and artificial intelligence–based approaches that are reshaping screening, risk stratification, and treatment paradigms [29]. Increasing attention has been directed toward the ovarian cancer immune microenvironment and mechanisms of immune evasion, which critically influence response to checkpoint inhibitors and inform next-generation immunotherapy strategies [64, 86]. Drug resistance remains a major barrier to durable outcomes, with recent analyses detailing genomic, metabolic, and microenvironmental drivers of treatment failure and ongoing clinical efforts to overcome them [86]. Parallel progress in biomarker development, including HRD, TIL density, ctDNA, and CRS, continues to refine patient selection and therapeutic monitoring [41]. Finally, updated reviews of antibody–drug conjugates underscore the expanding pipeline of FRα-independent targets such as NaPi2b, tissue factor, and B7-H4, which are reshaping treatment options for platinum-resistant disease [64].

Limitation

This review provides a broad synthesis of current and emerging therapeutic strategies in ovarian cancer; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a narrative review rather than a systematic review, and study selection may introduce inherent bias despite efforts to include high-quality and clinically relevant evidence. Second, the therapeutic landscape in ovarian cancer is rapidly evolving, and newly reported clinical trial data—particularly for next-generation DNA damage response inhibitors, immunotherapy combinations, and antibody-drug conjugates—may not yet be fully captured. Third, heterogeneity across study designs, patient populations, histologic subtypes, biomarker definitions, and endpoints limits direct comparison of outcomes across trials. Fourth, long-term survival and toxicity data remain limited for many recently approved or investigational agents, restricting the ability to assess durability of benefit. Finally, access to molecular diagnostics and targeted therapies varies globally, which may affect the generalizability of some findings to resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

Ovarian cancer management has shifted from a reliance on cytoreductive surgery and cytotoxic chemotherapy toward a precision-based framework guided by molecular vulnerabilities, histotype-specific biology, and biomarker-driven treatment selection. Advances in PARP inhibition, anti-angiogenic therapy, immunotherapy, and antibody-drug conjugates have expanded therapeutic options and improved outcomes for selected patient populations. Emerging strategies such as next-generation DNA damage response inhibitors, rational combination regimens, and liquid biopsy–based monitoring continue to refine individualized care. Despite these gains, challenges remain, including therapeutic resistance, limited benefit of immunotherapy in unselected patients, and disparities in access to molecular diagnostics and targeted agents. Continued integration of genomic profiling, dynamic biomarkers, and translational insights will be essential to optimize treatment sequencing, overcome resistance mechanisms, and broaden the impact of precision oncology. As the field evolves, multidisciplinary collaboration and equitable access to advanced therapies will remain central to improving survival and quality of life for individuals with ovarian cancer.
