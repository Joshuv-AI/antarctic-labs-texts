Factors associated with mother-to-infant bonding difficulties without prior postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months after childbirth: the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS)
Authors: Hitomi Inano, Kenta Matsumura, Akiko Tsuchida, Hidekuni Inadera, Kanako Shimada, Tomomi Hasegawa, the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS) Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo identify factors associated with mother-to-infant bonding difficulties (MIBD) in mothers without prior postnatal depression and to elucidate the prevalence.MethodsA total of 64,938 mother–child pairs who had registered between 2011 and 2014 in a nationwide birth cohort study called the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS) were analysed. MIBD was assessed using the Mother-to-Infant Bonding Scale Japanese version (MIBS-J). Prior postnatal depression was defined as an Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale score ≥ 9 at both 1 month and 6 months postpartum, and mothers who met this criterion were excluded. Mothers with a MIBS-J score ≥ 5 were considered to have MIBD. Cut-offs of ≥ 3 were set for MIBS-J subscale scores for lack of affection (LA)and anger/rejection (AR). We set 30 factors as exposures, and data were subjected to logistic regression analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of MIBD, LA, and AR among those without prior postpartum depression was 7.7%, 3.9%, and 11.7%, respectively. The most strongly associated factors were ‘feeling difficulty in holding the baby when they were cranky or arching their back’ (AOR: 3.45 [2.96–4.03]) and ‘negative feelings towards the pregnancy’ (AOR: 2.42 [2.20–2.65]). ‘High social support’ (AOR: 0.45 [0.41–0.49]) was strongly associated with the absence of MIBD without prior postnatal depression.ConclusionsIn this study, the prevalence of MIBD among women without prior postnatal depression was 7.7%. MIBD without prior postnatal depression was strongly associated with ‘difficulty holding the baby when they were cranky or arching their back’, ‘low social support’, and ‘negative feelings towards the pregnancy’.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-026-01726-x.

Introduction

Mother-to-infant bonding is the formation of an emotional bond between a mother and her child through their interactions (Kinsey and Hupcey 2013), and it plays an important role in the performance of a mother’s role. The essence of mother-to-infant bonding difficulties (MIBD) lies in the mother’s emotional response to her child (Brockington et al. 2006). Kumar reported that characteristic features of MIBD include feelings of detachment from the baby, coldness, indifference, anger or hatred, and even a desire to harm the baby (Kumar 1997). It has been reported that insufficient mother-to-infant bonding can contribute to postnatal depression (Ohara et al. 2017) as well as increase the risk of inappropriate child-rearing (Chen et al. 2024) and child developmental delay (Faisal-Cury et al. 2021; de Waal et al. 2023).

Many studies have shown that postnatal depression and MIBD often occur together (Kerstis et al. 2016; Slomian et al. 2019; Taylor et al. 2005). However, MIBD and postnatal depression have independent mechanisms of occurrence (Brockington and Guedeney 1999), and it is also thought that they are confounding factors for each other (Kerstis et al. 2016; Kitamura 2007).

Postnatal depression has long been considered one of the causes of developmental delays in children (Walker et al. 2007). However, it has been suggested that it is not postnatal depression itself but rather the MIBD accompanying it that contributes to developmental delays in children (Faisal-Cury et al. 2021). Furthermore, MIBD are strongly suspected to be a factor in child abuse (Ohashi et al. 2016), and postnatal depression may actually be a protective factor against child abuse because, among parents who had experienced high levels of abuse, those who also exhibited high levels of depression and posttraumatic stress disorder were less likely to be abusive than those who had low levels of depression and posttraumatic stress disorder (Pears and Capaldi 2001).

It has been reported that bonding disorders occur in approximately 1% of the general population and in 32% of depressed women (Brockington et al. 2006). The prevalence of MIBD is reported to be 11%–12% (Edhborg et al. 2011; Thamby et al. 2024), but to our knowledge, there are few reports on the prevalence of MIBD without postnatal depression.

According to the Perinatal Mental Health Consensus Guide, risk factors for MIBD can be broadly categorised into three groups: (1) environmental factors, (2) maternal factors, and (3) child-related factors (Japanese Society of Perinatal Mental Health 2023). Environmental factors include mother-infant separation, lack of social support, low socioeconomic status, single motherhood, marital discord, and spousal abuse. Maternal factors include antenatal and postnatal depression, a difficult pregnancy, a difficult birth, an unwanted pregnancy, the death of one twin, a previous stillbirth, the mother’s perception of her own upbringing, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive tendencies, and immature personality. Child-related factors include premature birth, illness, disability, unwanted sex, and constitutional or temperamental issues in the child, such as irritability or poor responsiveness. However, these are all risk factors; the guidelines do not mention any protective factors for MIBD (Japanese Society of Perinatal Mental Health 2023). Furthermore, although many risk factors for depressive symptoms overlap with those for MIBD, there is little existing research on risk factors specific to MIBD in the absence of depression.

The true nature of MIBD remains unclear (Kitamura 2019). Factor analysis of the Mother-to-Infant Bonding Scale (MIBS), a measurement tool for MIBD, identified two subfactors: lack of affection (LA) and anger and rejection (AR) (Kitamura et al. 2015; Yoshida et al. 2012). These two factors, at the very least, underpin the observed emotional responses towards children in MIBD. To our knowledge, few studies have investigated factors associated with LA and AR; however, insights into these factors are crucial for understanding the true nature of MIBD.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify factors associated with LA and AR, as well as to determine their respective prevalence. Identifying factors associated with MIBD would provide an important foundation for safeguarding the health of mothers and children, tailoring support to individual needs, and reducing long-term developmental risks for the child. Identifying factors associated with MIBD in the absence of postnatal depression is important for preventing inappropriate parenting and developmental delays.

Materials and methods

Study design

The JECS is an ongoing nationwide government-funded birth cohort study investigating the association of various environmental factors with children’s health and development. The JECS has been registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN-CTR), Japan. The registration number is UMIN000030786. Trial details are publicly available at the UMIN-CTR website. Details of the design and baseline characteristics of the JECS are available elsewhere (Kawamoto et al. 2014; Michikawa et al. 2018). Briefly, recruitment took place between January 2011 and March 2014 at public facilities, including obstetrics and gynaecology departments and health centres, across 15 regional centres throughout Japan, including rural and urban locations. Follow-up occurred in mid/late pregnancy and at childbirth. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires distributed to the participants at two time points during their pregnancy (early, mid/late pregnancy) or transcribed from the medical records by physicians, midwives/nurses, and/or research coordinators.

The JECS protocol was reviewed and approved by the Japanese Ministry of the Environment’s Institutional Review Board on Epidemiological Studies (100910001, September 2010) and the ethics committees of all participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures contributing to this work complied with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for experiments involving humans. This study was also approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Toyama (R2024129, October 2024).

The present analysis adhered to the JECS data usage policy, and the manuscript was reviewed and approved by the JECS Group prior to submission.

The hypotheses and analysis plan were specified prior to data analysis, and no post hoc modifications were made based on the results.

Study data

The present study analysed the jecs-ta-20,190,930 and jecs-qa-20,210,401 datasets, which were released in October 2019 and April 2021, respectively. The combined dataset contains 104,059 records, of which 5,689 multiple participations and 3,620 miscarriages/still births were excluded to derive unique mothers. Among the remaining 94,750 mothers, 13,958 were excluded due to lack of responses or missing data on the Mother-to-Infant Bonding Scale Japanese version (MIBS-J) at 1 year after childbirth. Of the remaining 80,792 mothers, 15,854 were excluded because of postnatal depression (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale [EPDS] score ≥ 9), leaving 64,938 mothers for analysis (Fig. 1).

Measures

All variables were collected by a self-report questionnaire that was administered to mothers during their first trimester, second/third trimester of pregnancy, 1 month after childbirth, 6 months after childbirth, and 1 year after childbirth.

Exposure

The explanatory variables were the 30 items listed below. ‘[ ]’ indicates the time of the response.

Child factors

Sex of the baby (male, female) [after childbirth]; gestational age (< 37 weeks, 37–41 weeks, ≥ 42 weeks) [after childbirth]; single or multiple pregnancy (single, twin, more) [after childbirth]; Have you experienced crying spells that persist no matter what you do? (yes or no) [1 month after childbirth]; birth defects (yes or no) [after childbirth].

Maternal physical factors

Maternal age (< 25 years, < 35 years, < 45 years, ≥ 45 years) [mid-pregnancy]; body mass index (< 18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–<25 kg/m2, ≥ 25 kg/m2) [early pregnancy]; parity (primipara, multipara) [early pregnancy]; hyperemesis gravidarum (vomiting, nausea, asymptomatic) [mid-pregnancy]; obstetric complications (yes or no) [after childbirth].

Maternal lifestyle factors

Smoking habits (never, stopped before pregnancy, stopped after discovering pregnancy, continued during pregnancy) [1 month after childbirth]; drinking habits (never, used to drink but stopped, 1–3 times a month, 1–3 times a week or more) [1 month after childbirth]; omega-3 fatty acid intake (0–1.04 g, 1.05–1.41 g, 1.42–1.79 g, 1.80–2.34 g, ≥ 2.35 g) [mid-pregnancy]; nutrition methods (breastfeeding, formula, mixed feeding) [6 months after childbirth]; employment status (employed, unemployed) [mid-pregnancy]; Do you take your baby with you when you go out for walks, shopping, etc.? (yes, no) [1 month after childbirth].

Maternal psychological factors

Emotions towards pregnancy (unintended and confused/upset/ambivalent/other, unintended but happy, very happy) [early pregnancy]; maternal history of mental illness (yes or no) [early pregnancy]; Do you find it difficult to hold your baby, such as when they are cranky or arching their back? (often, sometimes, rarely, never) [1 month after childbirth].

Maternal social and economic factors

Education level (junior high/high school graduate, vocational school/community college graduate, college/university graduate or higher) [mid-pregnancy]; household income (< JPY 4 million, JPY 4–<6 million, ≥JPY 6 million) [mid-pregnancy]; marital status (married, unmarried/divorced/widowed) [6 months after childbirth]; physical/psychological intimate partner violence (yes or no) [mid-pregnancy]; social support (low, 3–9; medium-low, 10–12; medium-high, 13–14; high, 15) [mid-pregnancy]. Social support was assessed using a three-item social support scale derived from the short (five-item) version of the ENRICHD Social Support Inventory (Mitchell et al. 2003) with some modifications, which was included in the mid-pregnancy questionnaire (Matsumura et al. 2023). The items asked about someone who shows you love and affection (Q1), emotional support (Q2) and frequency of contact with someone you trust (Q3). The total scores for Q1 to Q3 were divided into four quartiles (high, medium‑high, medium‑low, low), with higher scores indicating greater levels of social support.

Health care or medical interventions factors

Early skin-to-skin contact (early skin-to-skin contact, carried baby against clothes, no early skin-to-skin contact) [1 month after childbirth]; early nursing (within 1 h of childbirth, ≥ 1 h after childbirth, not nursed until 1 month after childbirth) [1 month after childbirth]; rooming-in (roomed-in for half a day or less, roomed-in for three-quarters of a day, roomed-in all day) [1 month after childbirth]; infertility treatment (yes or no) [early pregnancy]; epidural analgesia during delivery (yes or no) [after childbirth].

Outcome

The outcome variable was MIBD without prior postnatal depression 1 year after childbirth. MIBD was assessed using MIBS-J (Yoshida et al. 2012), and postnatal depression was assessed using the EPDS (Cox et al. 1987) administered 1 month after giving birth and 6 months after childbirth. The Japanese version of the EPDS was developed by Okano et al. (1996) and has been shown to have good psychometric properties and an optimal depression screening cut-off score of 8/9. Prior postnatal depression was defined as an EPDS score ≥ 9 at both 1 month and 6 months postpartum. Mothers who met this criterion were excluded. Therefore, only mothers with an EPDS score < 9 at both time points were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

MIBS has been shown to have good validity as a screening measure for MIBD (Bienfait et al. 2011). The present study used the MIBS-J cut-off of 4/5 points from Matsunaga et al. (2017). The higher the MIBS-J score, the worse the mother–infant bonding. MIBS-J comprises 10 items, answered using a 4-point scale (0 = strongly agree, 1 = somewhat agree, 2 = somewhat disagree, 3 = strongly disagree); some items were reverse-scored to reduce response bias. The absolute score ranges from 0 to 30. MIBS-J has two subscales: LA and AR. Each subscale comprises four items (LA: items 1, 6, 8, and 10; AR: items 2, 3, 5, and 7) (Yoshida et al. 2012). The MIBS‑J is a questionnaire designed to assess the quality of maternal bonding; as it is not a diagnostic tool, no cut‑off values have been established for LA or AR. However, previous studies on father‑to‑infant bonding failure have used a score of 3 points to identify approximately the top 10% of individuals with high total scores on these subscales (Nishigori et al. 2020; Suzuki et al. 2023). In the present study, participants who scored ≥ 3 on the LA and AR items were assigned to the LA and AR groups, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association of 30 exposures with MIBD, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were calculated. Multivariable models were adjusted for potential confounders, including 30 exposures. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) were analysed for all 30 variables. The threshold for statistical significance was 5%, and we performed two-tailed tests. Risk indices are listed with ORs, AORs, and 95%CI in the results tables. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Among the 64,938 pregnancies, the missing-data rate was < 1% for most possible confounders and exposure factors, except for parity, obstetrical complications, nutrition methods, maternal employment status, household income, social support, and analgesics during childbirth, for which the missing-data rates were 2.4% (n = 1,548), 2.0% (n = 1,322), 3.7% (n = 2,425), 1.2% (n = 784), 6.8% (n = 4,421), 2.1% (n = 1,344), and 3.3% (n = 2,128), respectively. Missing values for the 30 exposure factors were imputed using a value of 99 and included in the logistic regression analysis (data not shown).

Results

A total of 64,938 mothers (mean age, 31.4 ± 4.9 years; mean body mass index before pregnancy, 21.1 ± 3.2 kg/m2) were analysed; 26,525 (41.8%) were primiparas and 63,770 (98.6%) were married.

The item with the next highest frequency of adverse responses was Item 6, ‘I enjoy taking care of this child’, to which 36.6% of mothers responded negatively. Prevalence of MIBD without prior postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months after childbirth

MIBD was identified in 10,991 women (11.6%) out of a total population of 94,750 before excluding those with postnatal depression or missing EPDS or MIBS-J data. Among the 64,938 women who did not experience postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum, 5,030 (7.7%) had MIBD (Table 2). Thus, 5,030 (45.8%) of mothers with MIBD did not experience postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum among 10,991 women who were identified as having MIBD.

The prevalence of LA without prior postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum was 24,800 (38.2%), 5,832 (9.0%), and 2,552 (3.9%) when the cut-off values were set at 0/1, 1/2, and 2/3, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, the prevalence of AR without prior postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum was 33,655 (51.8%), 16,448 (25.4%), and 7,551 (11.6%) when the cut-off values were set at 0/1, 1/2, and 2/3, respectively (Supplementary Table 4).

‘Difficulty holding the baby when they are cranky or arching their back’ had the strongest association with MIBD

To clarify the factors associated with MIBD without prior postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum, we performed logistic regression analysis with the comparison group as a normal group having neither postnatal depression nor MIBD. Figure 2 shows that mothers who felt it was difficult to hold their baby when the baby is cranky or arching their back had a significantly higher rate of overall MIBD compared with mothers who did not; this was the most strongly associated factor among the 30 exposure factors (AOR: 3.45 [2.96–4.03]). In addition, higher levels of social support (AOR: 0.45 [0.41–0.49]) were more strongly associated with the absence of MIBD. Furthermore, even when pregnancy‑related feelings were negative (AOR: 2.42 [2.20–2.65]), unintended but happy at the time of pregnancy confirmation (AOR: 1.50 [1.39–1.60]) was strongly associated with MIBD without prior postnatal depression.

Discussion and conclusions

Of the mothers with MIBD, 5,030/10,991 (45.8%) did not experience postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum. Previous studies have primarily focused on the co-occurrence of postpartum depression and MIBD (Kerstis et al. 2016; Slomian et al. 2019; Taylor et al. 2005), but the present study demonstrates that nearly half of the mothers with MIBD had not experienced postnatal depression. However, it is evident that postnatal depression at 1 month after childbirth predicts MIBD 1 year later, and early intervention for mothers with postnatal depression is crucial (Kasamatsu et al. 2019). Notably, 5,030/64,938 (7.7%) of mothers have MIBD among those who did not experience postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum, and they are at high risk of being missed by postnatal depression screening efforts such as the EPDS.

No previous studies were found regarding the prevalence of the MIBD subscales LA and AR among mothers. There were, however, several reports focusing on fathers (Suzuki et al. 2023; Nishigori et al. 2020). Regarding the prevalence of LA and AR in fathers, one report indicates rates of 4.9% for LA using a cut-off of ≥ 3 and 6.3% for AR using a cut-off of ≥ 3 (Suzuki et al. 2023). The results in this study cannot be directly compared with those of the previous report. However, when applying a 3‑point cut-off, the observed prevalences —AR: 7,551 (11.7%) and LA: 2,552 (3.9%)—suggest that mothers show a higher prevalence of AR but a lower prevalence of LA compared with fathers.

Responding ‘Often’ to the question ‘Do you find it difficult to hold your baby, such as when they are cranky or arching their back?’ was identified as the factor most strongly associated with MIBD without prior postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months postpartum, as well as with AR without prior postnatal depression at the same time points. Irritability and back arching are common in infants who are preterm or have neurologic injury or chronic lung disease (DeRegnier 2024). In this study, no significant association was found between preterm birth and MIBD. Although data regarding neurologic injury or chronic lung disease could not be analysed in this study, it is thought that irritability and back arching are also associated with the infant’s temperament and disposition. Furthermore, poor mental health in the mother may have a negative impact on both the formation of the maternal bonding and the infant’s temperament (Davies et al. 2021). This study cannot determine whether the infant’s inherent tendency towards strong irritability and back arching is directly linked to MIBD. However, the fact that mothers often find it difficult to hold infants who are irritable, colicky, or arching their backs is likely to be an early sign of possible MIBD. Thus, it is important to ask mothers whether they are having any difficulty holding their baby when the baby is cranky or arching their back during the 1-month check-up in order to identify MIBD without prior postnatal depression. An association between parents with high levels of infant carrying and good maternal bonding has been reported (Firk and Großheinrich 2024). In addition, carrying the infant is effective for promoting secure attachment in young mothers (Williams and Turner 2020). Therefore, carrying an infant may have a positive impact on the development of maternal bonding (Firk and Großheinrich 2024). To improve MIBD, it is considered important for mothers to feel comfortable holding their babies.

The second strongly associated factor was low social support, a finding in line with previous reports (Chen et al. 2023). According to government statistics for 2022, compared with 2008, the proportion of households living with the husband’s parents has fallen significantly, indicating a trend away from multigeneration households towards nuclear families. Furthermore, although 38.5% of wives cited their ‘parents’ as the person they turn to for advice when facing difficulties with childbirth or childcare, 48.7% cited their ‘husband’, marking the first time since 2008 that ‘husband’ has surpassed ‘parents’ (National Institute of Population and Social Security Research 2022). Against this backdrop, the focus of social support for pregnant women has shifted towards the ‘husband’, and the relationship with the husband is increasingly becoming a central issue in social support. A loss in social support, particularly in emotional aspects, carries high risk, especially for mental health (Matsumura et al. 2023). The present study suggested that the absence of people who show affection or kindness towards pregnant women, a lack of emotional support, and a lack of regular contact with trusted individuals may increase the risk of MIBD. In contrast, mothers with high levels of social support are less likely to experience postnatal depression (Matsumura et al. 2022), and their children are less likely to have developmental delays at the age of 3 years (Imanishi et al. 2024). It is clear that social support is important for both the postpartum mental health of mothers and the future development of their children.

The third factor that was strongly related to MIBD without prior postnatal depression was the ‘Feeling when you found out you were pregnant’ being ‘negative’ or ‘unexpected’. Negative feelings towards the pregnancy have previously been suggested as a risk factor in MIBD (Kokubu et al. 2012; Tokuda et al. 2021), and the same result was obtained in the present study. However, ‘unintended but happy’ was also related to MIBD. Many studies have investigated the negative impact of MIBD on child development (Winston and Chicot 2016), and feelings about pregnancy other than ‘very happy’ need to be carefully monitored in order to provide appropriate support. In particular, for pregnant women who have feelings other than happiness when they find out they are pregnant, there is a strong link to MIBD and LA. Therefore, it is important to actively target them for support.

Repeated analyses of AR and LA using higher cut-off values clarified the distinct characteristics of each. The factors associated with AR were very similar to those associated with MIBD, whereas the factors associated with LA were unique. The most notable feature of LA was that having previously given birth emerged as an associated factor. In both AR and MIBD, previous reports have indicated prior childbirth experience promotes better maternal-infant bonding (Tsuchida et al. 2019; Yoshida et al. 2020); however, for LA, the opposite was observed. This suggests that LA, in which prior childcare experience functions as an associated factor, represents a heterogeneous subtype within MIBD. This suggests that LA is more common among mothers with multiple children, suggesting that having multiple children increases the likelihood of experiencing LA. Furthermore, the factors associated with LA were maternal age, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) intake, hyperemesis gravidarum, and marital status. Previous studies have identified hyperemesis gravidarum as a potential inhibitory factor for MIBD (Yang et al. 2023), and our findings also suggest that it may be associated with a protective effect against LA. Intake of omega‑3 PUFAs has been shown to have an inhibitory effect on postnatal depression (Matsumura et al. 2024), and it has also been suggested that it may show a similar inhibitory tendency towards LA. However, this study excluded mothers who had experienced postnatal depression from the analysis, potentially introducing collider bias. Whether omega-3 PUFAs intake truly aids in improving LA remains a topic for future research. The finding that mothers who had lost their partner had lower LA levels was also novel. The absence of a partner is a risk factor for postnatal depression (Zacher et al. 2025), and postnatal depression is a risk factor for MIBD (Kasamatsu et al. 2019; Ohara et al. 2017). There may be collider bias in the association between absence of a partner and protective effect against LA (Holmberg Mathias and Andersen Lars 2022). As this analysis grouped unmarried, divorced, and widowed mothers together, to properly understand this phenomenon, separate analyses for each group should be conducted.

To our knowledge, this is the first large cohort study to investigate factors associated with MIBD without prior postnatal depression at 1 and 6 months after childbirth. These results are representative of the situation in Japan because the survey involved samples taken from 15 regional centres nationwide. Furthermore, we performed logistic regression analysis, using various factors from the pregnancy period to the postpartum period as covariates, and excluded the influence of confounding factors. In addition, the probability of β error occurring is very low because we analysed 64,938 mother–child pairs in this study.

This paper has several limitations. First, postnatal depression was examined in the first and sixth months after childbirth, whereas MIBD was examined in the first year after childbirth, with varying response times in each questionnaire. Therefore, the timing of the EPDS and MIBD surveys differs by 6 to 11 months. There was a lack of data for determining whether any depressive symptoms were present at the time of the MIBD assessment at 1 year after childbirth. Therefore, although MIBD without postnatal depression at 1 year postpartum was used as the outcome measure in this study, there was nonetheless the possibility of depressive symptoms being present at the 1-year time point. Second, this is an observational study, so it was not possible to conclude causality between MIBD and the associated factors. Furthermore, the MIBS-J is a self-administered questionnaire, and the researcher did not directly ask the mother in person about her feelings towards her baby. Hence, there is a possibility of discrepancy with clinically observed MIBD. Lastly, it should be noted that there is a possibility of collider bias occurring because only mothers who did not experience postnatal depression were extracted from among the mothers with MIBD.

In conclusion, our findings revealed an association of MIBD without prior postnatal depression with ‘difficulty holding the baby when they are cranky or arching their back’, ‘low social support’, and ‘negative feelings towards the pregnancy’. Clinicians should be aware that mothers who seem to have difficulty holding their baby when the baby is cranky or arching their back may be struggling with mother-to-infant bonding, even if they have not experienced postnatal depression. In addition, we found that mothers with MIBD who had not experienced postnatal depression at both 1 and 6 months after childbirth accounted for nearly half of all MIBD cases.
