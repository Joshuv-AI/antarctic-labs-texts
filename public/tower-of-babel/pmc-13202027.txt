Development and validation of three risk prediction models for clinical deterioration of patients after craniotomy: a retrospective cohort study in China
Authors: Laiyu Xu, Zhen Luo, Huilan Xu, Yunhong Tang, Ling Wang, Lingli Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to identify a deterioration prediction tool for patients after craniotomy.DesignA retrospective cohort study.SettingThree large tertiary hospitals in Hunan Province, China.ParticipantsBetween January 2018 and March 2020, 1576 patients who underwent craniotomy at three tertiary hospitals in Hunan Province were selected and randomly allocated to either the training or validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. Comprehensive demographic and disease-specific data were collected. Logistic regression, Bayes classification and back propagation neural network were used to construct the models. The Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to an Ideal Solution method was used to evaluate the efficiency of the models.ResultsThe performance of all three models was commendable. In both the training and validation datasets, the back propagation neural network model demonstrated the highest efficiency, achieving a sensitivity of 77.1%, specificity of 91.7%, correctness of 86.8%, positive predictive value of 82.3% and negative predictive value of 88.9%. Conversely, the Bayes classifier exhibited the lowest predictive efficiency among the models evaluated.ConclusionsWe developed three models to predict clinical deterioration in patients following craniotomy. Among these, the back propagation neural network model demonstrated superior predictive performance. This model serves as a valuable reference for clinical nurses, aiding them in identifying high-risk patients who may deteriorate post-craniotomy.Keywords: China, Risk management, Machine Learning, Nursing research, Adult surgery, Neurosurgery

Background

Clinical deterioration refers to the change in patients’ condition from one clinical state to a worse clinical state.1 Many ward patients may deteriorate to the point of serious adverse events, such as unplanned transfers to the intensive care unit (ICU) and even death.2 3 Patients often show some identifiable negative changes in physiological parameters for several hours before serious adverse events occur,4 and timely detection of deterioration is key to reducing the rate of potentially preventable morbidity and mortality among ward patients.5 As healthcare professionals who provide direct care to patients, nurses are expected to identify deterioration based on their clinical judgement or by following hospital protocols.6

Craniotomy is a neurosurgical technique involving a wide cranial opening that facilitates surgical treatment in the intracranial space.7 According to an American database analysis of 38 058 neurosurgical patients, postoperative complication rates were significantly higher in patients undergoing craniotomy than in those undergoing spinal surgery.8 The in-hospital mortality rate associated with unplanned transfer to a neurological intensive care unit (NICU) can be as high as 17%.9 Early identification and timely intervention are essential for decreasing critical events after craniotomy.

At present, the early identification of deterioration after craniotomy is mainly based on clinical experience, and differently experienced nurses make different judgements, which may easily lead to inaccurate judgements and delayed interventions. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score is the most commonly used predictor of prognosis for neurosurgical patients,10 but it reflects only eye-opening response, verbal response and motor response, without the patient’s physiological parameters. Moreover, the GCS score cannot be used in aphasic patients. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) score analyses the degree of abnormality in physiological parameters and is widely used to assess prognosis,11 12 but the APACHE score mainly predicts the prognosis of patients, such as mortality, rather than deterioration. To speed up the identification of critically ill patients, the Early Warning Score (EWS) has been proposed, using a simple algorithm based on the physiological measurement of patient vital signs, such as heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), respiratory rate (RR), temperature (T) and level of consciousness. However, the relationship between the EWS and the prognosis of patients after craniotomy is not well understood.13 This tool also omits some necessary indicators, such as age and underlying conditions. In general, there are several deficiencies in the prediction of deterioration: (1) limited risk factors, (2) a focus on prognosis rather than deterioration and (3) insufficient specificity for patients after craniotomy. Therefore, it is urgent and significant to establish a model for predicting deterioration after craniotomy, so as to facilitate medical resource allocation and help nurses provide optimal care.

Methods

Study subjects

We enrolled patients undergoing craniotomy in three large tertiary hospitals in Changsha, China, between January 2018 and March 2020. All hospitals are affiliated with Central South University and are directly administered by the National Health Commission. Data on patients (age≥18 years) who underwent craniotomy and were admitted to general wards for more than 48 hours were extracted from the electronic medical records. Patients with a history of stroke, a history of craniotomy or unplanned transfer from the operating room or post-anaesthesia care unit to the ICU were excluded. This study adopts the definition of disease deterioration proposed by Suero Molina colleagues.3 14 Participants from the three hospitals involved in the study were classified as experiencing disease deterioration if they met any of the following criteria: (1) unplanned transfer to the ICU: this refers to an unplanned transfer to the ICU for further monitoring and treatment due to a worsening of their condition. In this study, such transfers were determined through a joint assessment by ICU physicians and neurosurgeons. (2) Unplanned reoperation: patients in the specialist ward who required additional surgery due to disease progression or significant postoperative complications during the same hospital stay were considered to have undergone an unplanned reoperation. In this study, the classification of unplanned reoperations was based on the evaluations of neurosurgeons with attending-level experience or higher.

Given that postoperative deterioration in craniotomy patients is a low-probability event, and the number of concurrent craniotomy patients without deterioration is relatively high, to ensure the total sample size and enhance the precision of the study hypothesis, this research randomly selected patients undergoing concurrent craniotomy without deterioration as control subjects. The number of controls was approximately double the number of cases experiencing deterioration. Using the sample size estimation formula for unmatched case–control studies, with the minimum OR value selected as the factor of surgical duration,15 substituting P0=0.185 and P1=0.282, and setting α=0.05 and POWER=0.90, the estimated sample size for the case group was calculated as 294 subjects, with 588 subjects for the control group. Considering potential data loss, an additional 20% was added, requiring a minimum sample size of 353 subjects for the case group and 706 subjects for the control group.

Data collection

Through literature review and research team discussions, the patient information questionnaire was determined to include patients’ demographic information (eg, gender, age, education level, marital status and ethnicity), disease-related factors (eg, existing cranio-cerebral disease and other prior conditions), lifestyle habits (eg, smoking and drinking), surgical factors (eg, type of surgery, name of craniotomy, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, duration of surgery, amount of intraoperative blood loss and whether blood products were used during surgery), laboratory test indicators (eg, total protein, albumin, blood potassium, blood chloride, blood sodium, blood glucose, white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophils, platelet (PLT) count), physiological indicators and related symptoms (eg, T, HR, RR, BP, SpO2, pupil, urine volume, 24-hour fluid intake and output, modifined early warning score (MEWS), convulsion, vomiting and GCS score), and imaging findings (eg, postoperative CT findings for displacement of midline intracranial structures and for intracranial haematoma). To improve the sensitivity of the constructed model for future clinical applications, for the case group data, the worst value within 7 days post-craniotomy was selected for repeated measurements; for example, for vital signs and physiological observation data, the worst single reading within 7 days post-craniotomy was selected, excluding the last data point recorded before an unplanned transfer to the ICU or an unplanned reoperation. For single measurements, the single data point was selected as the worst value; for the control group, the worst value among repeated measurements taken within 7 days post-craniotomy was selected. For example, for vital signs and physiological observation data, the worst single reading within 7 days post-craniotomy was chosen, while for single-measurement data, the single data point itself was selected as the worst value.

Prior to data collection, staff from the Medical Records Department and the Network Information Department provided researchers with training on paper medical record retrieval and usage, electronic medical record system operation and electronic medical record query procedures. Medical records were selected strictly according to inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure data collection quality.

Theoretical basis

Logistic regression (LR) is a classic statistical modelling method that has the advantage of being simple and easy to understand.16 This study employed binary LR analysis using a stepwise forward LR method. Clinical variables identified through univariate analysis served as independent variables, while disease progression status acted as the dependent variable. With entry at α=0.05 and removal at α=0.10, a LR equation for disease progression in patients following craniotomy was constructed to establish a predictive model.

Bayes classification is an important method for establishing a prediction model. According to certain criteria, one or more discriminant functions are formed, and the discriminant coefficients are corrected by using a large amount of data, so as to determine the classification of the samples.17 This study employed Bayesian discriminant stepwise analysis, using univariate analysis results as independent variables and disease progression as the dependent variable. Based on training samples, a discriminant function was established to form a Bayesian discriminant analysis prediction model.

Back propagation neural network (BPNN) is a widely used and relatively mature artificial neural network (ANN) method. As an artificial intelligence technology (AIT), BPNN consists of numerous interconnected processing units that mimic the structure and function of biological neurons in the brain.18 This architecture leverages computational power to simulate the information transmission process of biological neural networks, featuring a three-layer structure comprising input, hidden and output layers. The difference between the output and the actual value is continuously transmitted back to the network in the process of model training, and the system adjusts the weights of each layer to minimise the difference between the output and the actual value.19 In this study, a BPNN model was constructed using a multilayer perceptron based on training set data. The results of univariate analysis served as the input layer, while the hidden layer could adopt a multilayer composite structure. The number of layers and neurons in the hidden layer was determined through automatic network optimisation. The output layer predicted whether intracranial patients experienced clinical deterioration. The ReLU activation function is selected for the hidden layer of the BPNN model to accelerate convergence and mitigate gradient vanishing. The Sigmoid function is employed for the output layer, mapping outputs to predicted probabilities of disease deterioration. Model training uses the Adam optimiser with an initial learning rate of 0.001, aiming to minimise the binary cross-entropy loss function. The maximum training iterations are set to 1000, with early stopping employed to terminate training when model performance plateaus. To enhance generalisation, dropout layers are introduced after each hidden layer. This technique randomly discards a proportion of neurons during training, reducing complex co-adaptive relationships between neurons.

The Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to an Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) entropy weighting method is employed to rank and compare different alternative options. This approach uses the entropy weighting technique to objectively calculate weights based on indicator information entropy, thereby avoiding subjective weighting biases. Subsequently, the TOPSIS method is applied for comprehensive evaluation. This methodology asserts that the selected alternative should exhibit the shortest geometric distance from the optimal state and the greatest geometric distance from the worst state. This enables a comprehensive analysis of different model performances while effectively minimising the influence of subjective factors.20

Data analysis

The data were statistically analysed using the SPSS V.24.0. In this study, the descriptive data were presented as frequencies (percentages) for the categorical variables. The χ2 test and univariate LR analysis were used to compare the rates or constituent ratios between the deterioration group and the non-deterioration group. The prediction models were established using the LR method, Bayes classification and BPNN. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and the Youden index were used to evaluate the predictive efficiency. The TOPSIS method was used to compare the efficiency of the three models.

Patient and public involvement

None

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 1576 patients were enrolled and randomly divided into the training set (1140 patients) and validation set (436 patients) in a 7:3 ratio. The specific framework for case data results from craniotomy patients across the three hospitals is presented in online supplemental appendix 1. A total of 553 patients across the three hospitals experienced postoperative deterioration. Among these, 10 were under 18 years old, 1 was a pregnant woman, 2 had prior craniotomy history, 1 had a history of intracranial disease and 5 were unplanned transfers to the ICU from the operating room or anaesthesia recovery room. Clinical records were incomplete for two patients. Ultimately, 532 cases met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. This cohort comprised 198 cases of unplanned ICU transfers and 334 cases of unplanned reoperations. An additional 26 056 craniotomy patients across the three hospitals did not experience deterioration. A total of 1098 control patients were randomly selected from the cohort at approximately twice the case group ratio. Among these controls, 28 patients were<18 years old, 2 were pregnant women, 5 had history of craniotomy, 7 had history of intracranial disease, 8 discontinued treatment or were discharged within 7 days post-craniotomy and 4 patients had missing clinical records. Ultimately, 1044 patients were included. The sample inclusion process is detailed in figure 1. A total of 44 categorical variables were adopted in this study, of which 21 variables were statistically significant (p<0.05), as shown in table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the case group and the control group in terms of gender, educational attainment, marital status, ethnicity and other indicators. Detailed data comparison between the case group and the control group can be found in online supplemental appendix 2.

BPNN model development

A neural network with a multilayer perceptron was used to build a prediction model for the risk of deterioration in patients after craniotomy. The model continued to use 21 clinical variables from the univariate analysis as independent variables, with patient deterioration serving as the dependent variable. Although increasing the number of network layers could improve accuracy, it would also lead to higher complexity, making model training more difficult. Therefore, a typical three-layer BPNN with a stable structure and high training efficiency was selected. Through a comprehensive evaluation of model performance on both training and validation datasets across different numbers of hidden layer neurons, a model with 61 neurons in the hidden layer was ultimately determined to exhibit optimal generalisation performance and selected as the final risk prediction model (table 4). Discussion

Early identification of deterioration in patients after craniotomy can help maintain the safety of critically ill patients, ensure rational allocation of medical resources, and improve the quality of patient safety management. Thus, based on electronic medical records, we developed three prediction models, providing clinical nurses with an objective reference for identifying deterioration in patients after craniotomy.

Identifying the indicators of deterioration is the first step in establishing a prediction model of deterioration. Some studies have revealed that SBP,21 diabetes mellitus,22 MEWS,23 GCS,24 pupil,25 urine volume26 and postoperative CT showing intracranial haematoma13 are indicators of deterioration after craniotomy, consistent with the current study. The differences in the variables among the three models may be attributed to the different algorithms. Based on the BPNN model, postoperative CT showing intracranial haematoma was the most important factor of deterioration in patients after craniotomy, similar to previous studies.27 With limited intracranial space to compensate, haematoma can cause increased intracranial pressure, oedema, cerebral ischaemia and life-threatening cerebral herniation.28 Therefore, a CT showing an intracranial haematoma is an important early warning indicator of deterioration.

All models achieved good predictive performance in terms of their ability to predict deterioration. The BPNN model had the best performance, followed by the LR model, similar to prior studies in other areas. One study revealed that the ANN exceeded LR and Bayes classification, providing the best prediction of mortality for patients with isolated moderate and severe traumatic brain injury.29 In a study of passenger satisfaction with airlines, BPNN was shown to be a better model than LR, and Bayes classification was the worst model.30 One possible explanation could be the unique characteristics of BPNN, including good fault tolerance, strong nonlinearity, self-learning mechanisms, self-organisation and self-adaptability.31 32 As a simulation of the human brain’s working mechanism, BPNN can handle complex fuzzy mapping relationships and identify complex nonlinear relationships between variables.32 In contrast, the LR model provides only OR estimates for risk factors when the number of variables is less than 20.33 A complex dataset with many predictors makes it difficult for LR to specify all possible interactions.34 Notably, we used existing data from electronic medical records, and LR commonly demonstrates better performance than Bayes classification in the presence of complete data. Therefore, if accuracy is paramount, then the BPNN model should be strongly considered. However, the BPNN model is a black box with relatively low explanatory power, which may lead to hesitancy in deploying it in clinical care.35 More advanced prediction algorithms are expected to solve these problems.

Study limitations

In this study, patient data for craniotomy procedures were extracted from the medical records of three provincial-level regional medical centres in Changsha, China, spanning January 2018 to March 2020. Consequently, the enrolled cohort captures a broad spectrum of disease conditions and severity levels relevant to craniotomy. Although all three models demonstrated excellent internal validation, their generalisability requires external validation using other databases. Future studies will use prospective patient datasets to validate the models across diverse hospital systems, thereby mitigating potential biases stemming from hospital-specific or temporal factors. Additionally, this study is in the discriminant validation phase and lacks calibration analysis of the model. Previous models predicting the risk of deterioration following craniotomy have demonstrated excellent calibration, indirectly supporting the calibration validity of similar models.36 In subsequent multicentre prospective validation studies, we will prioritise reporting calibration curves and Hosmer–Lemeshow test results.

Conclusion

Quantitative prediction and timely identification of the patient’s deterioration after craniotomy can help to provide targeted interventions in the early stage, which is important for improving patient outcomes. In this study, the effectiveness of the BPNN model was superior to that of the LR and Bayes classification models. The findings indicate that the BPNN model can be applied as a useful method for identifying deterioration after craniotomy and for helping with precise decision-making in practice.
