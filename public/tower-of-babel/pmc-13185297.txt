Barriers of spiritual care in nursing: a scoping review
Authors: K VGSG Vithana, T A Asurakkody, S SP Warnakulasuriya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpirituality is regarded as a fundamental aspect of humanity and is acknowledged by numerous healthcare professionals as a vital element in health and healing. Researchers have emphasized the importance of spiritual nursing care as a core aspect of nursing practice, highlighting the need to nurture the human spirit through the establishment of relationships and connections between nurses and patients. Nevertheless, despite the acknowledged significance of spiritual practices for health, there has been limited focus on spirituality within nursing practice and education in existing literature. This review aimed to identify the barriers that hinder the integration of spiritual care into nursing practice. Findings were organized using the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM) as a post-hoc analytical framework to systematically categorize barriers across multiple levels of influence.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and the Arksey and O’Malley framework. A total of 26 studies were identified from the existing literature concerning the barriers to providing spiritual care in nursing. These barriers are categorized into five main themes using the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM) as a post-hoc analytical organizing framework. The scoping review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.ResultsFive primary themes were identified across the Socio-Ecological Model levels. Individual barriers enclosed nurses’ personal knowledge gaps, discomfort, and spiritual self-awareness deficits, as well as clients’ reluctance to engage. Interpersonal barriers included religious sensitivity, cultural competence challenges, and communication difficulties. Organizational, community, and policy-level barriers further hindered the integration of spiritual care into nursing practice.ConclusionsThe primary barriers to providing spiritual care in nursing stem from the lack of visibility associated with this aspect of care within the profession. This review highlighted barriers faced by nursing professionals in delivering spiritual care to clients with concept of SEM, which need to be addressed in order to enhance the level and quality of spiritual care nursing. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating multilevel interventions targeting barriers at individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy levels, with particular emphasis on integrating spiritual care education into nursing curricula and establishing supportive institutional policies across diverse cultural and healthcare contexts.Keywords: Barriers, Nursing, Scoping review, Spiritual care

Introduction

Spirituality is a broad and multidimensional concept that has been defined in various ways across disciplines. For the purposes of this review, spirituality is understood as a dynamic and intrinsic dimension of humanity through which persons seek ultimate meaning, purpose, transcendence, and connection to self, to others, to nature, and to the significant or sacred, as articulated by Puchalski et al. [1]. This definition is widely used in healthcare and nursing literature and encompasses both religious and non-religious expressions of spiritual experience, making it applicable across diverse cultural and clinical contexts.

Spiritual care is a multifaceted notion that encompasses things like respecting patients’ privacy and dignity, paying close attention to what they have to say, and assisting them in understanding the course of their illness [2]. In order to obtain full and comprehensive health, spiritual care in conjunction with other nursing care will create a balance between the body, mind, and spiritually. To put it another way, spiritual and religious beliefs are strongly linked to mental health and mental well-being symptoms like lowering anxiety and depression, boosting self-control and trust, and hastening the healing process. They can also lessen physical pain, foster a closer bond between the nurse and the patient, and give life meaning and purpose [3]. As a mother of nursing great lady Florence Nightingale, in 1859 she voiced, “And remember every nurse should be one who is to be depended upon. she must have a respect for her own calling, because God’s precious gift of life is often literally placed in her hands.” However, this apparent gap between theory and practice has been established by nurse researchers: despite their belief that spiritual care is very significant, nurse clinicians report not offering it frequently [4, 5].

Spiritual care is being taught in basic nursing educational programs and is becoming more and more popular in the literature as a way to ensure holistic care [6, 7]. It is important to distinguish between spiritual care and holistic care: holistic care is a broad concept encompassing the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions of a patient’s wellbeing, whereas spiritual care specifically addresses the patient’s spiritual needs, meaning-making, values, and connection to a higher purpose. Spiritual care is therefore one essential component within the broader framework of holistic nursing care. However, nurses’ capacity to offer spiritual care is sometimes impacted by their comfort levels and time constraints. The nurse must conduct a spiritual assessment, distinguish between religious and spiritual needs, identify appropriate spiritual care interventions, be aware of their own and their patients’ and significant others’ perceptions of death and dying, and decide when it is appropriate to provide spiritual care in order to provide effective spiritual care [8].

There are a number of reasons why nurses may be unclear about their responsibilities when it comes to spiritual care. Among these are a lack of interest in the nursing profession, misinterpretations of religious beliefs, communication problems with family members, nursing sustenance issues, low motivation, a lack of time, a lack of fundamental communication skills, and a lack of training in effective communication [9–11].

The Socio-Ecological Model (SEM) was adopted in this review as a post-hoc analytical organizing framework. While not used to guide the search strategy, the SEM provided a systematic lens through which identified barriers were categorized and interpreted. Although the SEM originated within psychological and public health disciplines, its multilevel framework is directly relevant to nursing: care is shaped simultaneously by factors at the individual nurse level (knowledge, attitudes, beliefs), the interpersonal level (nurse-patient relationships), the organizational level (hospital policies and resources), the community level (cultural and religious norms), and the policy level (national standards and curricula). Therefore, the current review applies the SEM as a post-hoc structure to map the barriers that spiritual care nursing faces in current practice [12, 13].

Method

Aim

The aim of this study was to identify barriers of spiritual care nursing practice with application of socio ecological model; which is common for nurses and anyone starting out in spiritual caregiving to give an overview of the current condition.

Research questions based in this review

The following major questions were addressed: (1) What are the barriers of spiritual care in nursing practice? (2) How can the identified barriers to spiritual care in nursing be organized and understood using the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM) across individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy levels? The scoping review followed six methodological steps as outlined by Arksey and O’Malley [14] and adapted by JBI [15]: (1) identifying the research question; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) study selection; (4) charting the data; (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting results; and (6) consultation exercise. The process of scoping reviews was thought to be appropriate for methodically mapping the variety of literature, identifying important concepts, and determining the kinds of evidence that were accessible [16]. The methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley was used in current scoping review [14]. The Preferred Reporting Items for System article reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist was used to report the review, which was carried out in compliance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews [15].

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined using the JBI Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework, which is recommended for scoping reviews conducted under JBI methodology [15]. The PCC framework provides a structured approach to formulating and refining the scope of a scoping review by specifying the target population, the concept under investigation, and the context in which the phenomenon occurs [15, 16]. Each component is described below.

Population

Studies involving obstacles to nurses’ spiritual care nursing were considered in the current evaluation. Hospital nurses who participated fully or partially in the study were given consideration for inclusion, as were hospice care nurses. Occupational therapists, clinical pastoral care providers, and unpaid carers were among the settings that were not included. The review will refer to the participant role as “nurses” for reading purposes, and research that concentrate on other healthcare workers will not be included.

Concept

Studies that are eligible concentrate on examining obstacles to religiously based care and spiritual care nursing.

Context

Studies pertaining to spiritual care nursing difficulties were considered in the current scoping review. Research that qualifies should examine the obstacles to religiously based care and spiritual care nursing. Studies look at works published in English throughout the last 16 years (2010–2025). The year 2010 was selected as the starting point because it marks a significant period of growth in the empirical literature on spiritual care in nursing, coinciding with increased scholarly attention to holistic care frameworks and the publication of foundational studies in this domain (Carr, 2010). An additional search was conducted in March 2026 to capture any relevant publications from May 2025 onwards; no new studies meeting the inclusion criteria were identified.

Types of sources

Studies using quantitative, qualitative, and/or mixed methods designs, theses and dissertations, text, and opinion papers were all taken into consideration for the current scoping review. Book chapters, protocols, and some grey literature (theses, preprints, guidelines, policy documents, or any other type of report) that explains an approach actually delivered or intended for spiritual care nursing or religious-based care nursing were all included in the inclusion criteria, as were studies written in the English language, regardless of study design. Studies that address spiritual care barriers in nursing practices were included in the list of eligible studies. Books, conference proceedings, newspaper and web articles, expert letters, presentations, and duplicates were not included in this study. The goal of this review was to identify any obstacles that nurses might face when providing spiritual care.

Information sources

Five databases, PubMed, CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Scopus (Elsevier), PsycINFO and Research Gate were thoroughly searched to find published articles in health-related fields through May 2025. To find more related studies, the reference list of the included study articles was searched.

Search strategy

The search strategies using subject headings combine with free text terms: (‘Spiritual care nursing barriers OR ‘Religious care barriers OR spiritual care nursing experiences OR in combined with the title word nursing) by holding counsel with a more experienced librarian. The search was run on March to May 2025.

A further search was also conducted from the references of all included studies related to spiritual care and relevant terms, keywords, and synonyms. This search also included different terminology, word plurals, different word forms, various word spellings, acronyms, and specific names. In addition, studies were manually recognized through the reference lists of selected articles. The PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist was used [15]. The flowchart of PRISMA-ScR (Fig. 1) was integrated.

Study selection

Two researchers (KVGSG, TA) manually recorded the data search selection procedures in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis-2020 guidelines [17, 18]. Records needed for full-text examination had to be identified for eligibility in a thorough way. To demonstrate that a study specifically addressed the obstacles to spiritual care nursing among nurses, the complete text of the study needs to be reviewed. Consequently, a large number of publications were read in full to properly assess their applicability. All of the researchers discussed and decided on any discrepancies in the selection procedure.

Data extraction

Two-step assessment was used in the review. In order to access full texts where the titles and abstracts satisfied the inclusion criteria or where there was any doubt, two researchers (KVGSG and TA) first independently reviewed the titles and abstracts based on the inclusion criteria. The two researchers next looked at the barriers to spiritual care that were found in possibly relevant papers based on the study’s eligibility requirements. If there were any questions or concerns, a senior researcher (SSPW) was consulted. Using an extraction spreadsheet form created specifically for this review, the two researchers (KVGSG and TA) separately extracted the data. After that, the data extraction spreadsheet was revised to make the specifics of the data that needed to be extracted in each column clearer. Finally, to get the relevant data, the study’s researchers compared two data types to see whether there were any differences.

Data items

Data were extracted that contained details included studies were (1) author, (2) year of publication, (3) study design (4) settings (5) type of participants (Nurses) (6) size of samples, (7) Identified Barriers.

Data analysis

Because the included studies’ designs varied, no statistical pooling of results was carried out [19]. In order to convey the outcomes of the studies that were included, a narrative summary was chosen. A comprehensive synopsis of the features of the included studies was created at the outset. Following that, a narrative synthesis of the barriers of spiritual care with application of SEM and themes was conducted. Theme derivation proceeded through the following steps: First, two reviewers (KVGSGV and TAA) independently read all included studies and extracted reported barriers verbatim onto a standardized data extraction form. Second, extracted barriers were inductively grouped into preliminary categories based on conceptual similarity. Third, these preliminary categories were deductively mapped onto the five levels of the Socio-Ecological Model (individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy) by consensus among all three authors, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and input from the senior researcher (SSPW). This two-step process - inductive categorization followed by deductive SEM mapping -ensured that themes were both grounded in the data and systematically organized within the analytical framework.

These barriers can impact the effectiveness of spiritual care and the overall well-being of patients. Barriers of spiritual care in nursing: an application of socio ecological model

Theme 1:Individual barriers

Individual barriers have two sides: one from the nurses’ perspective and the other from the patients’ perspective. When considering the nurses’ perspectives, the following impediments can be identified: lack of training and education, lack of awareness of spiritual needs, time constraints, personal beliefs and values, and own psychological challenges from the patient perspectives, patient reluctance and emotional and psychological causes are presented.

In view of the lack of training and education, nurses may feel unprepared to address spiritual needs because they have not received adequate training in this area or lack formal education or training in spiritual care. Without proper guidance or knowledge about different spiritual beliefs and practices, nurses may be hesitant to engage in spiritual discussions or offer appropriate support. Most nurses may be unsure of how to assess spiritual needs or provide support. They not having proper training, self-motivational barriers, lack of knowledge, not having confidence in team members, lack of training and lack of language has been reported in several studies [10, 20, 24, 26, 28–31, 33, 37, 38, 42, 45]. Additionally, another study reported inadequate skills and competencies (73.7%) and insufficient knowledge (71.3%) [31].

Other study results highlight the importance of advanced training in spirituality and intervention strategies to reduce barriers for spiritual care [41] nursing experience also important factor for increase awareness of spiritual care. One study reported The level of spiritual care was positively correlated with spiritual care frequency and number of years working as a registered nurse [33].

Next one was a lack of awareness of spiritual needs. Nurses might not recognize when a patient is experiencing spiritual distress or may overlook the importance of spiritual care in holistic healing [25, 37, 43]. Previous studies have identified time constraints as a major barrier to providing spiritual care, as nurses often face heavy workloads and tight schedules, which make it difficult to dedicate time to spiritual care. Spiritual care nursing, which may require active listening and thoughtful interaction, is often seen as secondary to immediate physical care tasks. Nurses often work in busy healthcare settings with high patient volumes and tight schedules. Time limitations can make it difficult for nurses to provide individualized spiritual care, especially when addressing complex or sensitive spiritual issues that require careful listening and emotional support. One study on spiritual care from nurses for hospitalized patients in Jordan reported insufficient time (78.8%) [31]. Time availability issues are evidenced by several previous research studies [10, 20, 23, 26, 28, 32, 39, 40].

Nurses reported that they struggle to provide spiritual care if their own personal beliefs conflict with the patient’s spiritual or religious needs. Therefore, personal beliefs and values were identified as another individual barrier to providing spiritual care nursing. Some nurses felt uncomfortable focusing on spiritual matters if they lacked a shared faith or spiritual perspective with the patient. Nurses’ own personal beliefs, and biases can create barriers to providing spiritual care. If nurses feel uncomfortable with or judgmental toward a patient’s religious or spiritual practices, they may avoid engaging in spiritual-related conversations or may unintentionally impose their own beliefs on the patient. This issue is evidenced by some past studies [20, 25–27, 39–42].

Emotional or psychological challenges were influenced as personal hindrance for providing spiritual care nursing. Some nurses struggled with their own spiritual beliefs or may have difficulty dealing with existential issues like death, suffering, or the meaning of life, which are often central to spiritual care [30, 46]. The emotional toll of working with patients facing terminal illness or major life challenges can make it difficult for nurses to engage in spiritual care. Some nurses found spiritual issues to be emotionally challenging or difficult to discuss, particularly when dealing with patients at the end of life or those experiencing suffering. Nurses might avoid spiritual conversations because of personal discomfort with death, grief, or existential questions and their own psychological stress. Offering spiritual care can be emotionally demanding, especially when clients are facing end-of-life issues, chronic illness, or other significant challenges. Nurses had experienced burnout, compassion fatigue, or emotional distress, which can make it difficult to provide spiritual support to patients in need [24].

Next barrier was fear of overstepping boundaries during care. Some nurses had worries about overstepping their professional boundaries when discussing and addressing spiritual needs issues, particularly if they are unsure about how to appropriately integrate spiritual care nursing into their clinical role. This concern may cause them to avoid addressing spiritual matters altogether [21, 25].

When considering patients’ perspectives can identified patient reluctance as a significant barrier in spiritual care nursing. Some patients reported reluctant to discuss their spiritual beliefs, values or needs with healthcare providers, due to fear of judgment, misunderstanding, or perceived irrelevance. Spiritual needs may not be expressed if the patient feels that spiritual care nursing is not a priority in the healthcare setting. Patients may be hesitant to discuss their spiritual needs or concerns with a nurse due to fear of judgment, feeling uncomfortable, or not believing that spiritual care is a relevant part of their medical treatment. Some patients may also prefer to address spiritual matters with clergy or other religious figures rather than a nurse. This situation results were reported in previous studies [23, 40, 45].

Theme 2 : Interpersonal barriers

Interpersonal barriers have religious sensitivity issues, cultural competence issues and communication issues from nurses. When working with clients who need spiritual care, religious and cultural competency is required for nurses.

In view of religious sensitivity issues, nurses may face challenges in addressing spiritual needs if they are not familiar with or do not understand the patient’s religious background. Misunderstandings and unintentional insensitivity can arise when nurses are unaware of diverse spiritual practices. Nurses should identify religious differences, and they may encounter clients with beliefs and practices that differ from their own, which can lead to discomfort or confusion. The nurse’s own personal spiritual or religious views may also influence their approach to spiritual care, leading to potential biases. Current review has identified some previous studies on this issue [23, 25].

When considering cultural competence issues of nurses, a lack of cultural competence can hinder a nurse’s ability to offer effective spiritual care. Nurses may not be familiar with the individual spiritual practices or beliefs of clients from various cultural or religious backgrounds, leading to misunderstandings or discomfort when trying to focus on spiritual concerns. Nurses may not have the cultural competency necessary to understand or respect the individual spiritual beliefs of patients from diverse cultural or religious backgrounds. Less cultural sensitivity may lead to misunderstandings or even inadvertent disrespect towards the patient’s spiritual practices. Some previous researchers have reported the importance of cultural awareness for spiritual care nursing delivery [11, 23, 25].

Inadequate communication about spiritual needs between the healthcare team and the patient may prevent spiritual concerns from being addressed. Language and communication barriers, especially in diverse patient populations, can hinder spiritual care. Misunderstandings in communication about spiritual needs may lead to inadequate support or discomfort on both the patient’s and nurse’s parts. These two content areas were supported in several studies that have been done previously [25, 27, 37, 40, 42–44].

Theme 3: Organizational barriers

Organizational barriers have several concerning barriers regarding spiritual care nursing delivery. In view of less organizational support, some health sector environment settings may not prioritize spiritual care, and there may be fewer policies or systems in place to guide trained nurses in providing it. Most hospitals and health systems haven’t dedicated spiritual care providers, such as chaplains, leaving nurses to address these needs alone. Some institutions may not prioritize or adequately support spiritual care nursing. This can manifest in insufficient resources, lack of training programs, or minimal inclusion of spiritual care in patient care protocols. In some healthcare settings, there may be a disconnect between nursing staff and chaplaincy or pastoral care services. This lack of collaboration can result in missed opportunities for holistic care, as spiritual concerns may go unaddressed or not be integrated into the overall care plan. Limited access to chaplains, religious materials, or other spiritual resources in healthcare settings may make it more difficult for nurses to address spiritual concerns adequately. Nurses may find it difficult to advocate for or provide spiritual care if the organization does not recognize its importance. Organizational managers’ disregard for the value of comprehensive care, with environmental obstacles cited as obstacles affecting nurses [40]. According to certain research, palliative care does not fundamentally contain spiritual support [43], and it lacks the organizational and clergymen’s support to address patients’ spiritual distress [25]. Less private space in institution also affected [21]. Spiritual care is often seen as less tangible than physical care, which may lead to its neglect in practice. There may be a lack of clear documentation or recognition of spiritual needs in patient records, leading to an underemphasis on spiritual care due to lack of resources. Some other previous studies were reported institutional support requirements within their studies [23, 27].

Theme 4: Community level barriers

When considering community level barriers, interdisciplinary collaboration, lack of collaboration with chaplains or other spiritual care professionals can result in missed opportunities for holistic nursing care. Some healthcare teams may not recognize the value of integrating spiritual care with physical, psychological, and emotional care. Lack of support or collaboration always affects the nurses, and they may not always have access to a team of chaplains or spiritual care specialists.

A study with 340 (80.8%) participants completed the survey, which was conducted to better understand spirituality and the possible role of spiritual care in palliative and end-of-life care. The median age of the participants was 35 years (IQR: 31–48), and the majority were female (82.1%) and Caucasian (82.6%). Physicians (17.9%), nurses (64.7%), and “other” professionals (17.4%) were among the providers. on the discussion of patient concerns on religion and spirituality (R&S), provider observations varied. In particular, doctors were less likely to ask about R&S (41.4%) compared to nurses (60.3%) (p = 0.028). Additionally, doctors and other healthcare professionals consulted psychology and psychiatry more frequently (62.7%, p < 0.001), but nurses referred to chaplaincy/clergy more frequently (71.8%) [39].

Without this support, it can be difficult to focus on complex spiritual concerns that arise during patient care. According to one study participant, Iran lacks a culture of teamwork, and physicians’ strong territorialism, power, and dominance have prevented them from comprehending the talents, constraints, and duties of nurses [40]. One study illustrated an unprofessional view and less support in spiritual care delivery [25].

Theme 5: Policy barriers

Nurses may be concerned about crossing professional boundaries or inadvertently imposing their own beliefs on patients. Legal constraints in some areas may make it difficult to provide certain types of spiritual care, especially if it involves religious practices or rituals. Nurses may be concerned about respecting patient autonomy, maintaining professional boundaries, and adhering to legal and ethical standards. Spiritual and other care were impacted by logistical and ethical concerns [44]. Some may worry about crossing boundaries in religious discussions or introducing topics that could lead to ethical dilemmas. Nurses may be hesitant to engage in spiritual care due to concerns about crossing professional boundaries or infringing on patient autonomy. Ethical dilemmas can arise, particularly when a patient’s spiritual beliefs conflict with medical recommendations or institutional policies. Some healthcare settings may prioritize physical and medical care over spiritual needs. There might also be restrictions on the involvement of chaplains or spiritual care professionals.

The one study results of this study provide evidence that can be used to elaborate for enhancement in the infrastructure, sufficient funding allocation and policies that would allow for improved aspects of psychological, social, and spiritual care [20].

Discussion

Barriers in spiritual care nursing refer to the challenges that nurses face when addressing the spiritual needs of patients. More education and awareness, institutional support for spiritual care, and open communication with patients, families, and interdisciplinary teams are all necessary to overcome these obstacles. In order to overcome these obstacles and adopt a comprehensive, patient-centered approach, nurses can gain from continual training in spiritual care, cultural competency, and self-awareness. This will assist in establishing an environment where spiritual care is integrated into nursing practice and backed by institutional policies, education, and training. Individual, cultural, and institutional factors are among the variables that can affect obstacles in spiritual care nursing according to SEM. Researchers investigating the role of spiritual care in nursing practice have discovered that a significant proportion of nurses regard the provision of spiritual care as a vital aspect of their responsibilities. However, they also report facing challenges in implementing these practices in clinical settings [47]. Carr was identified three type of barriers that hinder the integration of spiritual care into daily nursing routines: individual, professional, and organizational. Nurses who perceive spirituality solely as addressing patients’ religious needs are less inclined to incorporate spiritual care into their practice [11]. An earlier study by Narayanasamy and Owens [48] found that nurses who limited their understanding of spiritual care to religious activities or viewed a patient’s spirituality as a private matter outside their professional scope often struggled to provide spiritual care, opting instead to refer patients to clergy. On a professional level, Carr [11] noted that insufficient nursing education regarding the concept of spirituality and its distinction from religion serves as a barrier to the practice of spiritual care. A lack of understanding of the elements of spiritual nursing care leads to challenges in conducting spiritual assessments and addressing the spiritual needs of patients and their families. This educational gap leaves nurses ill-equipped to incorporate spirituality into their everyday nursing practices [11, 47, 49–51]. Additionally, organizational barriers contribute to obstacles that restrict nurses’ capacity to deliver spiritual care. Nurses employed in organizations that adhere to a medical model of care [52] often find themselves in environments that prioritize economic considerations and task completion over holistic care, leading to feelings of undervaluation and lack of support in providing spiritual nursing care [11, 47].

Spiritual care in nursing is indeed a key aspect of holistic care, and while it’s especially emphasized in oncology due to the emotional and existential challenges faced by cancer patients, it extends far beyond that. Spiritual care can be found across various healthcare settings, including not only oncology but also in psychiatry, geriatric, end of life care, neonatal and pediatric Care.

According to previous studies results, patients with cancer often face significant emotional and existential distress, including fear of death, uncertainty, and a search for meaning. Nurses in oncology often provide spiritual care by helping patients explore their beliefs, offering emotional support, and respecting their spiritual needs during treatment and end-of-life care. According to a previous study, nurses beyond from these barriers and they discussed the difficulties they face in addressing patients’ faith and religion as well as their patients’ problems with difficult medical circumstances. As they gained experience, nurses learned how to have meaningful conversations with patients about spirituality and religion, which had a significant impact on their own lives and led to personal development [35]. Similarly, some studies identified ways to overcome barriers to spiritual care with better approaches in various settings [21, 40]. A spiritual care training protocol for nurses in oncology based on the concept of mutual growth with patients enhances nurses’ spiritual well-being and spiritual care competencies [45].

In view of psychiatric settings, spiritual care can support patients focus on the emotional and existential part of their mental health struggles. This could entail helping patients connect with their faith or values, understand and make sense of their experiences, or cope with the hopelessness and despair that frequently accompany mental illness. According to Neathery et al. [32], there have been reports of spiritual care barriers in psychiatric and mental health nursing as fear of aggravating psychiatric symptoms and a lack of education were the most common obstacles to offering spiritual care nursing. Nurses must receive training on how to provide spiritual care to patients with psychiatric mental health issues.

When considering older adults, especially those in palliative or end-of-life care, often experience spiritual distress related to issues of mortality, loss of independence, and legacy. Nurses in geriatric care may provide spiritual care by listening to patients’ concerns, helping them explore life meaning, and offering comfort as they navigate their final stages of life [1, 46]. Communication approaches including active listening and therapeutic lying can support maintain self-esteem of older people’s and their cognitive abilities [53].

In part of palliative and end of life care, spiritual care is essential to address patients’ needs for meaning, dignity, and peace as they near the end of life. Nurses are key in providing comfort and helping patients and families find closure through their spiritual or religious practices. Nurses need a better training and knowledge regrading approach of spiritual care in palliative setting. A study found that barriers towards providing spiritual care to palliative patients in hospices [37, 54].

While children may not have fully developed spiritual needs, parents often experience spiritual distress when their child is critically ill. Nurses in neonatal and pediatric care can support families in finding comfort and meaning during difficult times [55]. The nurses who were interviewed acknowledged the significance and worth of spiritual care, even though they were aware that physical therapies are thought to be far more important than spiritual requirements. The primary focus of spiritual care was on the families of the children; the nurses defended not providing spiritual care to children by citing time constraints as well as the age and consciousness of the children [56]. One of another previous study reported in mothers’ spiritual practices and lack of pastoral care services in health care setting in child with ADHD [57].

Further organizational obstacles to providing spiritual nursing care were noted by nurses as being the heavy patient load, insufficient staffing, and pressures in the workplace [11, 47].

Additionally, educational institutions erect obstacles to the provision of spiritual nursing care. Lemmer [58] discovered content deficiencies in the curriculum of universities that offered spirituality courses to their undergraduate nursing students in the most current thorough investigation into spiritual content in nursing curricula. Study conducted a survey of 132 baccalaureate nursing programs in the US and discovered that while the majority of universities offered elective spiritual courses or included spirituality in the curriculum, the key elements of spiritual care nursing connection, relationships, and the pursuit of meaning and purpose were only briefly covered. In additionally study found that a majority of faculty members felt inadequately prepared to teach spiritual care. Additionally, open-ended questions suggested that nursing faculty perceived spiritual care as a personal matter, often depending on their individual beliefs when instructing on the subject.

Spiritual care in nursing is based on respect for the patient’s particular beliefs and values and attempts to treat the patient as a whole, not only their physical or mental health. It necessitates a caring approach to treatment that, regardless of a patient’s illness or stage of life, helps them find comfort and support. However, nurses may need to overcome obstacles while implementing spiritual care in a practical situation. Effective integration of spiritual concepts into nursing practice necessitates awareness, training, and supporting institutional structures that value holistic care.

Implications for clinical practice and research - Nurses are essential in delivering holistic care, particularly in assisting clients to manage spiritual distress associated with illness. Positioned uniquely within the multidisciplinary care team, nurses develop strong professional relationships with clients, enabling them to effectively address spiritual needs. Using the Socio-Ecological Model as a framework, the provision of spiritual care can be examined at multiple levels. When considering the individual level, nurses must develop a solid foundation of knowledge, attitudes, and spiritual care in nursing practices, along with self-awareness of their competencies and personal beliefs. In view of the interpersonal level, trust-based nursing relationships with patients support the delivery of culturally sensitive and respectful spiritual care. When focusing Organizational barriers, such as time constraints, lack of training, and institutional policies, often hinder spiritual care practices in nursing; addressing these requires systemic change and integration of spiritual care into nursing curricula and unit protocols. In view of the community level, cross-cultural changes and religious diversity necessitate adaptable approaches tailored to the spiritual traditions and preferences of diverse patient populations. Finally, the policy-level support through national healthcare standards and nursing educational reforms is also crucial to institutionalize spiritual care nursing practices. Recognizing and addressing challenges across all SEM levels allows nurses to implement effective, evidence-based nursing strategies that enhance patient-centered spiritual care nursing. By evaluating studies and best nursing practices across different cultural contexts, nurses can creatively adapt their nursing interventions, thereby fostering professional development and autonomy in spiritual caregiving in nursing.

Post-hoc analytical framework application with the Socio-Ecological Model helps identify the multi-level factors influencing spiritual care in nursing. Addressing barriers at the individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy levels enables nurses to deliver culturally competent and holistic spiritual care, supported by education, institutional commitment, and evidence-based practices.

Rigor

Methodological rigour in this scoping review was increased through several strategies. The review used to the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and followed the PRISMA-ScR reporting guidelines, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. A structured eligibility criteria framework based on the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) components guided both study selection and data extraction. To minimize selection bias, two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consultation with a senior researcher (SSPW). A standardized data extraction form was used to ensure consistency in data extraction across all included studies. The use of multiple databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, PsycINFO, and ResearchGate) and supplementary reference list searching further strengthened the comprehensiveness of the search.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the most vital concept is that nurses can overcome these barriers to spiritual care nursing delivery and will be able to deliver effective and holistic nursing care to their clients by addressing their spiritual nursing care problems. Future research should priorities the development and evaluation of interventions that target barriers simultaneously at individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy levels. Longitudinal studies examining the effectiveness of spiritual care training programmes, as well as cross-cultural investigations across several healthcare systems, would further advance the evidence base and support the integration of spiritual care as a standard component of nursing practice globally.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. First, it followed a rigorous and transparent methodological framework, integrating to JBI methodology and PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Second, a comprehensive search across five major databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, PsycINFO, and ResearchGate) was conducted to minimize publication bias. Third, the application of the Socio-Ecological Model as a post-hoc analytical framework showed a systematic and theoretically grounded lens for categorizing barriers through multiple levels of influence.

Regarding limitations, due to the potential for bias stemming from the variability among the studies, it is essential to interpret and generalize the findings with caution. The barriers to offering spiritual care among nurses from various cultural backgrounds may lead to differing outcomes, which could affect the applicability of the findings. Additionally, restricting the language to English may introduce language and publication bias, as omitting studies in other languages could result in the loss of noticeable insights published elsewhere. Furthermore, this review focused merely on barriers encountered by nurses, excluding those integrated to interdisciplinary research on spiritual care.
