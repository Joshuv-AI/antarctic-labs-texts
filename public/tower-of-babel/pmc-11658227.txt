Bexarotene ameliorated the pulmonary inflammation and M1 polarization of alveolar macrophages induced by cigarette smoke via PPARγ/HO-1
Authors: Haoshen Feng, Zhe Li, Rui Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlveolar macrophages (AMs) modulate pulmonary inflammation in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), contributing to its progression. The PPARγ/RXRα heterodimer influences AM polarization induced by cigarette smoke (CS). Although PPARγ agonists suppress CS-induced M1 macrophage polarization, the impact of RXRα agonists on this process has not been determined. This study explored the effects and mechanisms of the RXRα agonist bexarotene on macrophage polarization in a COPD mouse model.MethodsC57BL/6 mice were assigned to the control, model, bexarotene, or model + bexarotene group. The COPD model was induced by CS exposure and intraperitoneal injection of cigarette smoke extract (CSE), followed by intraperitoneal administration of bexarotene. Additionally, MH-S cells were exposed to CSE and bexarotene. Lung tissues were subjected to hematoxylin-eosin staining, and emphysema and inflammatory scores were assessed. Cytokine levels and cell differentials in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were measured, and macrophage polarization was evaluated using immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, and qPCR.ResultsBexarotene effectively reduced inflammatory scores, cytokine levels, and neutrophil counts and ameliorated emphysema and M1 polarization of AMs in COPD model mice. Furthermore, while CSE exposure reduced PPARγ expression and the transcriptional activity of AMs, bexarotene enhanced the transcriptional response of PPARγ to CSE. HO-1 was identified as a potential target of PPARγ; its levels were assessed in AMs, revealing that bexarotene mitigated the CSE-induced reduction in HO-1. Notably, the effect of bexarotene was partially inhibited by the PPARγ inhibitor.ConclusionsOur results indicated that bexarotene may curb inflammation and M1 polarization in COPD through activation of the PPARγ/HO-1 pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-024-03064-x.Keywords: COPD, Cigarette smoke, Macrophage polarization, Bexarotene, PPARγ

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a pulmonary inflammatory disorder characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and irreversible airflow limitation. COPD remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, underscored by the projection of the World Health Organization as the third leading cause of death globally by 2030 [1]. The etiology of COPD is multifactorial, with cigarette smoke (CS) being the most well-documented risk factor. The pulmonary inflammation induced by CS facilitates the development and progression of COPD, which makes it crucial to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and identify anti-inflammatory targets.

Alveolar macrophages (AMs) are at the forefront of pulmonary innate immune defense and play a pivotal role in the pulmonary inflammation induced by CS in patients with COPD [2]. Macrophages display remarkable plasticity and are capable of polarizing into distinct phenotypes—namely, proinflammatory M1 macrophages and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages. The polarization of AMs induced by CS significantly influences disease progression and the pulmonary inflammatory status of COPD patients [3–5]. Our prior research demonstrated that CS induced M1, M2b and M2d polarization of AMs [6]. Furthermore, we found that inhibiting M1 polarization could mitigate emphysema and pulmonary inflammation triggered by CS [7, 8], suggesting that modulating AM polarization might be a viable anti-inflammatory therapeutic strategy for COPD.

Retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα) is a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily and a cadre of transcription factors that modulate gene expression in response to ligands. It acts as a universal heterodimeric partner for various nuclear receptors. These heterodimers are pivotal in the transcriptional regulation of downstream genes, and their transcriptional activity is sometimes distinctly modulated by two different ligands [9]. Our prior research revealed that the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone mitigates inflammation and M1 polarization of AMs by activating the PPARγ/RXRα pathway [7], which suggests that the PPARγ/RXRα heterodimer plays a role in cigarette smoke-induced macrophage polarization. Since the transcriptional activity of the PPARγ/RXRα heterodimer can be modulated by both PPARγ and RXRα ligands, RXRα ligands may have a regulatory effect on cigarette smoke-induced M1 polarization of macrophages. RXRα ligands include 9-cis Retinoic Acid and selective agonists like bexarotene. Interestingly, RXRα ligand bexarotene has been reported to alleviate pulmonary emphysema and airway inflammation in mice exposed to intraperitoneal cigarette smoke extract (CSE) [10]. Therefore, we hypothesize that bexarotene may participate in cigarette smoke-induced macrophage polarization through activation of the PPARγ/RXRα pathway, and this study mainly investigated the impact and underlying mechanisms of bexarotene on AM polarization in a mouse model of COPD.

Materials and methods

Animal model and bexarotene treatment

Forty-eight male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were acquired from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, Liaoning Province, China) and were randomly allocated into four groups: control, model, bexarotene, and model + bexarotene (Fig. 1A). The COPD mouse model was generated according to a previous study with some modifications [11, 12]. In the model and model + bexarotene groups, the mice were subjected to passive exposure to mainstream CS for 30 min twice daily, with an interval of 6–8 h, for 30 consecutive days. This exposure was conducted using a HOPE-MED 8050 inhalation exposure system (HOPE Biotechnology, Tianjin, China) with 20 commercial cigarettes (Daqianmen brand, containing 0.8 mg of nicotine, 12 mg of CO, and 10 mg of tar per cigarette) each session [13]. Additionally, on days 1, 11, and 21, these mice received a 0.3 ml intraperitoneal injection of 10% CSE. The control and bexarotene groups were exposed to fresh air and received a 0.3 ml intraperitoneal injection of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) on days 1, 11, and 21. Mice in the bexarotene and model + bexarotene groups were administered an intraperitoneal injection of bexarotene (Selleck Chemicals, 1 mg/kg, dissolved in a 1:9 ratio of dimethyl sulfoxide to normal saline) 30 min before each daily CS exposure [14]. Mice in the other groups received an equivalent volume of solvent via intraperitoneal injection. All animal experiments were conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee for Animal Use and Care at Shengjing Hospital under protocol number 2022PS202K.

Experimental grouping and emphysema assessment. C57BL/6 mice were divided into four groups: control, model, bexarotene, and model + bexarotene (A). Lung tissue histopathology was analyzed via H&E staining at 100x magnification. The mean linear intercept (MLI) and destructive index (DI) were quantified across the groups (B). The data are presented as the means ± SDs of six replicates. The experiments were conducted three times. Statistical significance is denoted by * for p < 0.05 compared to the control group and # for p < 0.05 compared to the model group

Lung tissue morphometric analysis and inflammatory score

The lungs of the mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde at 25 cm H2O pressure and subsequently fixed in the same solution for 48 h. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin, and 4 μm thick sections were prepared for staining. These sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and imaged using light microscopy. The mean linear intercept (MLI) was measured across 10 random fields per sample to assess emphysema severity [7]. The destructive index (DI) was calculated based on the method described [15], involving counting the number of destroyed versus normal alveoli under microscopy, and calculating the DI as follows: DI = (number of destroyed alveoli/total number of alveoli) × 100%. Pulmonary inflammation was evaluated using a 4-point scale (1: minimal, 2: mild, 3: moderate, 4: severe) across four criteria: alveolar septal, peribronchiolar, perivascular infiltrates, and bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue hyperplasia. The scores from each criterion in every sample were aggregated to quantify inflammation [8].

Total and differential cell counts in BALF

Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed with 1 mL PBS. The bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was centrifuged, and cell pellets were resuspended in PBS. Total cell count was determined using a hemocytometer. For differential counts, 100 µL of the cell suspension was placed on glass slides, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, and subsequently stained with Wright‒Giemsa for microscopic examination.

ELISA for measurement of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6

For the measurement of the cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, the supernatant from the BALF was collected after centrifugation and stored at -80 °C. The levels of these cytokines were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) following the manufacturer’s protocol (Boster, Wuhan, China). Optical density (OD) values at 450 nm were measured using an Infinite® 200 PRO spectrophotometer (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland).

Immunohistochemistry evaluation of iNOS and CD206

To assess the polarization status of lung tissue macrophages, the M1 and M2 polarization markers iNOS and CD206, respectively, were detected via immunohistochemistry as described in our previous study [7, 8]. Lung tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies against iNOS (ab15323) at a 1:200 dilution and CD206 (ab300621) at a 1:1000 dilution (both from Abcam, UK) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the sections were treated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Maixin, Fuzhou, China) at a 1:5000 dilution for 2 h at room temperature. DAB was used for visualization of positive cells, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Images were captured using a light microscope at 400x magnification, and positive cells were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0. Four random fields per section and five sections per animal were analyzed independently by two well-trained investigators. To ensure inter-rater reliability, they used the same equipment and performed the double-blind assessments under the same conditions. If there was a significant discrepancy (greater than 15%) in the evaluation of a sample between the two raters, the sample would either be discarded or reassessed.

Flow cytometry

The BALF was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, and the resulting cell pellet was prepared for flow cytometry. Each tube contained 1 × 106 cells, and Fc receptors were blocked using FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). The cells were then incubated with a FITC-labeled F4/80 monoclonal antibody (11-4801, eBioscience, USA) at 4°C for 40 min in the dark. For intracellular staining of iNOS and CD206, the Fix and Perm Kit (BD Bioscience, USA) was utilized per the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by incubation with PE-labeled iNOS (12-5920) and APC-labeled CD206 (17-2061) monoclonal antibodies (eBioscience, USA) at 4°C for 40 min in darkness. All antibodies were used at the concentrations recommended by the manufacturer. Cells were acquired using a BD Celesta instrument, with F4/80-positive cells gated as AMs and at least 1 × 104 events gated for each sample, consistent with our previous methodology. Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland).

Preparation of the CSE

CSE was prepared as follows [16]: Smoke from two unfiltered Daqianmen cigarettes (0.8 mg nicotine, 12 mg CO, 10 mg tar each) was drawn into 20 ml RPMI 1640 medium using a vacuum pump. The extract was filtered through a 0.22-µm filter to remove particles and bacteria, then standardized to a 10% concentration. OD measurements at 320 nm ensured consistency in CSE preparation.

Cell culture and drug treatment in vitro

MH-S cells (Pricella Life Science, Wuhan) were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). Cells were passaged at 70-80% confluence using a 1:3 ratio. For the experiments, MH-S cells were exposed to 1% CSE for 8 h to assess their polarization status and PPARγ/HO-1 expression. Cells were treated with bexarotene at concentrations of 500 nM and 1 µM one hour prior to CSE exposure. To examine the roles of RXRα, PPARγ and HO-1, cells were pretreated with a RXRα inhibitor (HX531, 1 µM; Cayman, USA), a PPARγ inhibitor (GW9662, 10 µM; Cayman, USA), a PPARγ inhibitor (rosiglitazone, 30 µM; Cayman, USA) and an HO-1 inhibitor (tin protoporphyrin IX dichloride [SnPPIX], 50 µM; MedChemExpress, USA) one hour before CSE or bexarotene application.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. The cells were plated in quintuplicate in 96-well plates (Nest Biotechnology, Wuxi, China). Cells were treated with 0, 50 nM, 100 nM, 200 nM, 500 nM, 1 µM, 2 µM, 5 µM or 10 µM bexarotene for 12 h, or treated with 1 µM bexarotene for 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h and 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, after which the absorbance at 450 nm was measured. Cell viability is expressed as a percentage of that of the control group.

PPARγ transcription activity detection

The nuclear protein of MH-S cells was extracted with a Nuclear Extraction Kit (Cayman, No. 10009277, USA). The transcriptional activity of PPARγ was detected with a PPARγ Transcription Factor Assay Kit (Cayman, No. 10006855, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance at 450 nm was determined and used to calculate the transcriptional activity of PPARγ.

Real-time qPCR assay

Total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, achieving an OD 260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0. cDNA synthesis was performed with a reverse transcription kit (Takara, China). Primers for iNOS, TNF-α, Arg-1, CD206, and β-actin were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software, with the following sequence:

iNOS forward: TGGAGCGAGTTGTGGATTGT.

iNOS reverse: GTAGTGATGTCCAGGAAGTAGGT.

TNF-α forward: CCACGCTCTTCTGTCTACTGA.

TNF-α reverse: GATGATCTGAGTGTGAGGGTCT.

Arg-1 forward: GAGAAGGTCTCTACATCACAGAAG.

Arg-1 reverse: TTCACAGTACGAGTCACCTCC.

CD206 forward: GTTTCCATCGAGACTGCTGC.

CD206 reverse: GCCACTTTCCTTCAACATTTCG.

β-actin forward: ACGGTCAGGTCATCACTATCG.

β-actin reverse: GTTTCATGGATGCCACAGGATT.

Quantitative PCR was conducted using a qPCR kit (Takara, China) on a Roche 480 LightCycler (Basel, Switzerland). The protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were quantified using the 2-ΔΔ CT method and normalized to β-actin expression.

Western blotting

MH-S cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and 1 mM PMSF. The lysates were sonicated and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min. The protein concentrations in the supernatants were determined using the BCA method. Proteins (30 µg) were resolved on a 10% polyacrylamide gel and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked with milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies at a dilution of 1:1000. Primary antibodies against PPARγ (16643-1-AP), HO-1 (10701-1-AP), and GAPDH (10494-1-AP) were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The following day, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 23.0). Differences between groups were assessed using either Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA. The experiments were replicated three times, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Bexarotene treatment ameliorated emphysema in COPD model mice

After CS exposure and intraperitoneal injection of CSE, COPD model mice were successfully established, as reflected by the increased MLI and DI (Fig. 1B). The MLI and DI were significantly lower in the model + bexarotene group than in the model group (p < 0.05), whereas no difference was found in the MLI or DI between the control group and bexarotene group (Fig. 1B). Our results indicated that bexarotene treatment could mitigate emphysema in COPD model mice.

Bexarotene treatment ameliorated pulmonary inflammation in COPD model mice

To evaluate pulmonary inflammation in the mice, the lung histopathological inflammatory score was determined according to our previous study [8], and the cell counts and cytokine levels in the BALF were also determined. Our results showed that the inflammatory scores of lung tissues were greater in the model group than in the control group, as reflected by the significant increase in the number of inflammatory cells in the alveoli and bronchi, and bexarotene treatment ameliorated the increase in inflammatory scores in the COPD model mice (p < 0.05, Fig. 2A). In addition, the total cell, neutrophil and macrophage counts were greater in the model group than in the control group, whereas bexarotene treatment partly attenuated the increase in total cell and neutrophil counts in the BALF (p < 0.05, Fig. 2B). TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 are important proinflammatory cytokines in COPD. The levels of these cytokines were also greater in the model group than in the control group, and bexarotene treatment partly ameliorated the elevation of cytokine levels in the model group but not in the control group (p < 0.05, Fig. 2C). Our results showed that bexarotene treatment could partly retard the pulmonary inflammatory response in COPD model mice.

Bexarotene treatment inhibited the M1 polarization of AMs in COPD model mice

According to our previous studies [7, 8], the polarization status of AMs was determined by immunohistochemistry in vivo, and iNOS- and CD206-positive AMs were used to indicate M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively. Consistent with our previous studies, our results showed that the numbers of iNOS- and CD206-positive AMs were greater in the model group than in the control group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3), which indicated that dual M1 and M2 polarization was induced in the AMs of the COPD model mice. Bexarotene treatment significantly decreased the number of iNOS-positive AMs (p < 0.05, Fig. 3A) but not the number of CD206-positive AMs in COPD model mice (Fig. 3B), which indicated that bexarotene treatment could inhibit the M1 polarization of AMs in COPD model mice. We also detected the percentages of iNOS- and CD206-positive AMs in the BALF by flow cytometry to further confirm the polarization status of the AMs. Similarly, the percentages of iNOS- and CD206-positive AMs were greater in the model group than in the control group, whereas bexarotene treatment partly attenuated the increase in iNOS-positive AMs in the COPD model mice (p < 0.05, Fig. 4). However, bexarotene treatment also increased the percentage of CD206-positive AMs in COPD model mice (p < 0.05, Fig. 4), which was different from the results of the immunohistochemistry. Overall, our results indicated that bexarotene treatment inhibited the M1 polarization of AMs in COPD model mice.

Bexarotene treatment inhibited the M1 polarization of AMs induced by CSE in vitro

The doses and duration of CSE were selected according to our previous in vitro studies [6], and the proper concentrations of bexarotene used in vitro were determined by a CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5A). We found that 1% CSE treatment for 8 h increased the mRNA expression of iNOS, TNF-α and Arg-1 and decreased the mRNA level of CD206 in MH-S cells. Bexarotene treatment partly ameliorated the increase in iNOS and TNF-α but increased the levels of Arg-1 and CD206 compared with those in the CSE group (p < 0.05, Fig. 5B). Thus, our results indicated that CSE induced M1 polarization of AMs and that bexarotene treatment inhibited the M1 polarization of AMs induced by CSE in vitro.

Bexarotene treatment improves PPARγ transcriptional activity in AMs

PPARγ is the core transcription factor regulating macrophage polarization and functions as a transcription factor by forming heterodimers with RXRα [17]. As the transcriptional activity of PPARγ/RXRα could be regulated by the ligands of RXRα, we detected the expression of PPARγ to clarify the mechanism by which bexarotene modulates macrophage polarization. The results showed that CSE decreased the expression of PPARγ in MH-S cells, and bexarotene treatment did not ameliorate the decrease in PPARγ expression (Fig. 6A), which was unexpected. We further detected the DNA binding activity of PPARγ to determine its transcriptional activity. CSE decreased the DNA binding activity of PPARγ in MH-S cells, whereas bexarotene treatment ameliorated the decrease in PPARγ transcriptional activity (p < 0.05, Fig. 6B). In addition, when MH-S cells were pretreated with the RXRα inhibitor HX531 and PPARγ inhibitor GW9662, the decrease in iNOS caused by bexarotene was partly attenuated (p < 0.05, Fig. 6C). Our results indicated that the M1 macrophage polarization induced by CSE might be related to the dysregulation of PPARγ, and bexarotene treatment inhibited the M1 polarization of AMs by activating PPARγ/RXRα.

Bexarotene treatment modulated the polarization of AMs induced by CSE via PPARγ/HO-1

Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) is an inducible enzyme that plays a pivotal role in cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative stress and inflammation. HO-1 is a potential downstream target of PPARγ, and its expression is modulated by the activity of PPARγ [18]. It has been reported that HO-1 is involved in the process of macrophage polarization [19], and CS exposure causes biphasic HO-1 expression in macrophages [20]. Based on the above findings, we speculated that HO-1 might also participate in macrophage polarization in COPD and that bexarotene treatment might modulate macrophage polarization via PPARγ/HO-1. We found that CSE decreased the protein level of HO-1 in MH-S cells, and bexarotene treatment improved the expression of HO-1 in MH-S cells treated with CSE (p < 0.05, Fig. 7A). To clarify the role of HO-1, cells were pretreated with SnPPIX, an HO-1 inhibitor, to block HO-1 before bexarotene was added, and the results showed that the decrease in iNOS and TNF-α caused by bexarotene was partly inhibited by SnPPIX (p < 0.05, Fig. 7B). To clarify the relationship between PPARγ and HO-1, we administered the PPARγ inhibitor GW9662 in conjunction with bexarotene and CSE. GW9662 inhibited the upregulation of HO-1 expression induced by bexarotene (p < 0.05, Fig. 7C). Additionally, the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone was found to enhance HO-1 expression in the presence of CSE (p < 0.05, Fig. 7C). Our study indicated that bexarotene might inhibit M1 polarization of AMs induced by CSE via PPARγ/HO-1.

Discussion

In this study, we found that bexarotene treatment ameliorated pulmonary inflammation in COPD model mice and inhibited the activation of M1 polarization in AMs via PPARγ/HO-1 (Fig. 8). Our study helped to clarify the mechanisms by which the RXRα agonist bexarotene affects macrophage polarization in COPD patients and proposed a potential anti-inflammatory treatment for COPD.

Bexarotene might ameliorate the M1 polarization of AMs induced by cigarette smoke via PPARγ/HO-1

Pulmonary inflammation is a key factor in the development and progression of COPD, and AMs, the first line of innate immunity in the lungs, play a significant role in COPD-associated pulmonary inflammation. Macrophages exhibit a high degree of heterogeneity and can polarize into proinflammatory M1 types and immune-regulating M2 types. Previous studies have reported that CS can induce dual polarization of macrophages into M1 and M2, and with prolonged CS exposure, the polarization of macrophages tends to shift from predominantly M1 to predominantly M2 [4, 6, 21]. This shift in macrophage polarization corresponds to the transition from early-stage acute lung inflammation to late-stage airway remodeling and emphysema formation in the course of COPD. Our previous studies showed that inhibiting CS-induced M1 polarization of macrophages can improve CS-induced pulmonary inflammation and thereby control the development of emphysema [7, 8], suggesting that modulating macrophage polarization could be a potential anti-inflammatory therapeutic approach for COPD. In this study, we found that bexarotene inhibited M1 polarization of AMs in a COPD mouse model, thereby delaying the development of emphysema in COPD mice. Although bexarotene may not be applied in the short-term treatment of COPD patients, our research provides a new potential drug for the anti-inflammatory treatment of COPD, marking a significant innovation and clinical relevance. Currently, bexarotene has been approved for the treatment of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL), where the recommended dosage is 300 mg/m²/day orally. Although the administration route differs, the dosage used in our study is relatively lower compared to the treatment dose for CTCL. The main side effects of bexarotene include hyperlipidemia (elevated serum triglycerides and cholesterol), hypothyroidism, skin reactions, and gastrointestinal discomfort. If bexarotene were to be applied in the treatment of COPD, we should focus on monitoring patients’ blood lipids, thyroid function, and observe for skin and gastrointestinal symptoms.

According to previous studies, PPARγ, a nuclear receptor transcription factor, is expressed at lower levels in the lung tissues of mice exposed to CS than in those of normal mice [22, 23]. PPARγ activation could inhibit pulmonary inflammation and delay the development of emphysema through mechanisms such as the suppression of TLR2/4 pathways [22, 23]. In our previous studies, we observed that CS reduced the expression of PPARγ in AMs, while the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone activated the PPARγ/RXRα dimer, inhibiting CS-induced M1 polarization of AMs and ameliorating lung inflammation [7]. Both PPARγ and RXRα agonists can activate the PPARγ/RXRα dimer, and Daisuke Morichika et al. reported that the RXRα agonist bexarotene improved pulmonary emphysema in mice induced by intraperitoneal injection of CSE [10]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that RXRα agonists might have effects on COPD similar to those of PPARγ agonists. As anticipated, in this study, we found that the RXRα agonist bexarotene reduces pulmonary inflammation and the development of emphysema in a COPD mouse model. Upon ligand activation, PPARγ forms a heterodimer with RXRα, and the regulation of downstream molecules relies on the activation of both ligands. In this study, we observed that cigarette smoke reduced the transcriptional activity of PPARγ in AMs, while bexarotene partially alleviated this decrease in transcriptional activity. To further clarify the role of PPARγ/RXRα in this process, we added additional experiments in which AMs were pre-treated with RXRα and PPARγ inhibitors (HX531 and GW9662) before the addition of bexarotene. The results showed that both HX531 and GW9662 inhibited the effect of bexarotene on M1 polarization in AMs, suggesting that bexarotene suppresses cigarette smoke-induced M1 polarization by activating the PPARγ/RXRα heterodimer. However, whether bexarotene can inhibit TLR2/4 activation in COPD as effectively as PPARγ activation remains to be explored in future studies.

HO-1 plays a significant role in macrophage polarization, a crucial process in regulating immune responses and inflammation. As an inducible enzyme that catalyzes the degradation of heme into biliverdin, carbon monoxide, and iron, HO-1 exerts various biological effects that influence the functional state of macrophages [17, 24]. Studies have demonstrated that the upregulation of HO-1 in macrophages could inhibit the expression of M1 cytokines and enhance the expression of M2 markers, thus shifting the balance toward an anti-inflammatory state that is crucial for managing conditions such as diabetic nephropathy, hypertension and interstitial lung disease [25–27]. Thus, the activity of HO-1 in macrophage polarization may represent a vital mechanism for controlling the inflammatory response and facilitating tissue healing, making it a potential target for therapeutic interventions in various inflammatory diseases. In addition, the effects of HO-1 on COPD have gradually attracted the attention of researchers. For example, HO-1 is involved in the ferroptotic death of AMs induced by CS [28], and HO-1/CO protects the lung from CS-induced injury [29]. In this study, we found that HO-1 is a downstream molecule of PPARγ and is involved in the process of macrophage polarization in COPD model mice,. Although our study did not provide direct evidence of binding between PPARγ and the HO-1 promoter, we have clarified that PPARγ is upstream of HO-1 in the process of bexarotene-mediated regulation of macrophage polarization through the use of PPARγ agonists and antagonists. Previous studies have shown that the HO-1 promoter region contains a peroxisome proliferator response element, and that PPARγ can directly participate in the transcriptional regulation of HO-1 in human vascular cells [30], which indirectly suggests the possibility of PPARγ regulating HO-1 transcription in this study. Combined with the finding that bexarotene improved the transcriptional activity of PPARγ and that bexarotene increased HO-1 levels and was rescued by a PPARγ inhibitor, we concluded that bexarotene inhibited the M1 polarization of AMs induced by CSE via PPARγ/HO-1.

Our study has several limitations. First, although we employed inhibitors of PPARγ and HO-1 to clarify their roles in the regulation of macrophage polarization by bexarotene, we did not perform specific knockouts of PPARγ and HO-1. Second, while we assessed the transcriptional activity of PPARγ, we did not determine whether the mRNA promoter regions of PPARγ and HO-1 could bind directly. Besides, due to the lack of blood drug concentration measurements, the corresponding concentrations of bexarotene in vivo could not be fully determined. Finally, our research was confined to animal and cellular models, and its implications for humans remain to be elucidated in future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that bexarotene ameliorated pulmonary inflammation and delayed the progression of emphysema in a mouse model of COPD, which might be associated with its inhibition of M1 polarization in the AMs of COPD model mice. Through in vitro experiments, we further discovered that bexarotene inhibited CS-induced M1 polarization of AMs by activating the PPARγ/HO-1 pathway. Our research contributed to the development of new anti-inflammatory therapeutic approaches for COPD.
